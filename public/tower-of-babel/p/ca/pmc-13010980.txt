Association between triglyceride glucose-body roundness index and incidence diabetes mellitus: a cohort study
Authors: Xingjie Huang, Feihuang Han, Jiquan Xiao, Qiheng Wan, Dunliang Ma, Nan Chen, Song Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetes mellitus (DM) is a major global health burden. Insulin resistance (IR) is a key driver, but its direct assessment is often impractical in large-scale settings. The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index and body roundness index (BRI) have emerged as accessible markers for IR and obesity, respectively. However, their combined role in predicting DM incidence remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the relationship between the TyG-BRI and DM risk, to provide a practical tool for early identification of high-risk individuals.MethodsThis study included 15,310 Japanese adults who participated in the NAGALA Physical Examination Project from 2004 to 2015. To assess the association of TyG-BRI with DM incidence, Cox proportional-hazards regression model, restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression analyses, subgroup analyses, and sensitivity analyses were utilized. Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to assess the ability of TyG-BRI to predict DM. To investigate the relationship between combined exposure to each component of TyG-BRI and DM incidence, weighted quantile sum regression analysis was employed.ResultsDuring the median 5.4-year follow-up duration, 350 (2.3%) participants developed DM. With TyG-BRI as a continuous variable, the HR (95% CI) for DM incidence was 1.38 (1.19-1.61) in the fully adjusted model. Participants with the highest TyG-BRI quartiles exhibited a 98% (HR 1.98, 95% CI 1.10-3.57) increased risk of DM compared to those with the lowest quartiles. RCS analysis indicated a positive linear relationship between TyG-BRI and DM incidence (p for overall<0.001; p for nonlinear = 0.465). FPG emerged as the primary contributor when the weights were assigned to the constituent elements of the TyG-BRI (weight = 0.751). Time-dependent ROC analyses suggested that TyG-BRI demonstrated superior and more stable predictive capacity for incident DM compared with the TyG index and BRI over mid-to long-term follow-up.ConclusionsThe TyG-BRI was positively associated with the risk of DM, demonstrating a dose-response relationship. Maintaining lower TyG-BRI levels may be beneficial to reduce the burden of DM.Keywords: Diabetes mellitus, triglyceride−glucose index, Body roundness index, TyG-BRI, Insulin resistance

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) ranks as the third leading non-communicable disease following cardiovascular diseases (CVD) and tumors, posing a significant global healthcare challenge [1]. As the most rapidly growing disease of this century, its morbidity and mortality rates are on the rise. By 2021, approximately 537 million adults worldwide had been diagnosed with DM, resulting in 6.7 million deaths. Additionally, a considerable number of diabetic individuals remain undiagnosed due to various reasons [2,3]. In light of these alarming statistics, it becomes imperative to highlight the necessity of developing a convenient and effective method for the early detection and prediction of DM.

Insulin resistance (IR) is a pivotal factor in the pathogenesis of DM. The homeostasis model assessment of IR (HOMA-IR), calculated based on fasting insulin levels and blood glucose, is widely recognized as a common measure for assessing IR [4]. However, its practicality is often compromised in widespread clinical practice and epidemiological investigations due to being time-consuming, complex, and expensive. In contrast, the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, derived from routinely used clinical indicators (fasting blood glucose and triglycerides), has demonstrated a significant correlation with IR and is closely associated with the incidence of DM [5,6]. Furthermore, obesity exacerbates IR and plays a substantial role in the progression of DM. Various obesity-related indicators, such as the body roundness index (BRI), have been shown to be closely linked to DM incidence in several studies [7,8]. Despite this, the combined impact of the TyG index and BRI on DM risk remains underexplored.

On this background, the present study aims to investigate the relationship between the TyG-BRI and DM risk. By integrating these indices, this study endeavors to provide valuable insights into more accessible and practical approaches for identifying individuals at high risk of DM. Ultimately, it is hoped that the findings will contribute to the development of targeted preventive strategies to delay or mitigate the onset of DM, thereby addressing a significant public health challenge.

Methods

Study design and population

Data for the present study were derived from the NAGALA (NAfld in the Gifu Area, Longitudinal Analysis) cohort study. Initiated in 1994, this ongoing cohort has continuously recruited individuals undergoing health checkups at the Murakami Memorial Hospital. Its primary objective is to investigate prospectively the incidence and associated factors of chronic diseases, including diabetes and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). A detailed description of the cohort can be found in a previous study by Okamura et al. [9], and the dataset is publicly accessible in the Dryad database at http://www.Datadryad.org/. For this analysis, we included data from 20,944 subjects enrolled in the NAGALA cohort between 1994 and 2016. The exclusion criteria were as follows:

(1)participants with a diagnosis of alcoholic fatty liver disease or viral hepatitis (n = 416), and those with DM (n = 323);

participants with a diagnosis of alcoholic fatty liver disease or viral hepatitis (n = 416), and those with DM (n = 323);

(2)individuals taking medications at the baseline examination (n = 2321);

individuals taking medications at the baseline examination (n = 2321);

(3)subjects with fasting plasma glucose (FPG) levels ≥6.1 mmol/L (n = 808);

subjects with fasting plasma glucose (FPG) levels ≥6.1 mmol/L (n = 808);

(4)those with a heavy drinking habit, defined as ethanol consumption exceeding 60 g/day for men and 40 g/day for women (n = 739);

those with a heavy drinking habit, defined as ethanol consumption exceeding 60 g/day for men and 40 g/day for women (n = 739);

(5)participants with missing covariate data (n = 863);

participants with missing covariate data (n = 863);

(6)individuals with BRI values classified as extreme values (defined as mean ±3SD) (n = 154) [7].

individuals with BRI values classified as extreme values (defined as mean ±3SD) (n = 154) [7].

Finally, 15,310 subjects (8364 male and 6946 female) included in this study. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Murakami Memorial Hospital, and all participants provided written informed consent. The research adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Definition of the TyG-BRI

The TyG-BRI is derived through the multiplication of the TyG index and the BRI. Specifically, the BRI is calculated utilizing the formula: BRI = 364.2−365.5 × (1-[WC (m)/2π]2/[0.5 × height(m)]2)½, where WC denotes waist circumference and height is measured in meters. The TyG index is computed according to the formula: TyG index = Ln[1/2 × FPG (mg/dL) × TG (mg/dL)], with FBG representing fasting plasma glucose and TG denoting triglycerides. Consequently, the TyG-BRI is obtained by multiplying the TyG index by the BRI, as expressed in the equation: TyG-BRI = TyG index × BRI [10].

Diabetes diagnosis

The diagnosis of DM in this study adhered to the American Diabetes Association guidelines, including self-reported DM in subjects during follow-up and HbA1c ≥ 6.5% or FPG≥7.0 mmol/L measured during follow-up [11].

Covariables

At baseline, standardized questionnaires were administered to collect demographic data, medication use, smoking and drinking status, disease history, and exercise habits of the subjects. Anthropometric measurements, including height, weight, waist circumference (WC), systolic blood pressure (SBP), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP), were obtained using established standardized methods. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height (m) squared. Exercise habits were categorized as participation in physical activities more than once a week. Smoking status was classified as never, former, or current, based on smoking history. Drinking status was stratified into non/small, light, moderate, and heavy categories, determined by the subjects' weekly alcohol consumption in the last month [9]. Obesity was defined as a BMI of ≥25 kg/m2 [12], and visceral fat obesity was determined by a WC of ≥90 cm in men or ≥80 cm in women [13].

For the measurement of biochemical parameters, venous blood samples were collected after the subjects had fasted for at least 8 h. Alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c), FPG, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), triglycerides (TG), and total cholesterol (TC) concentrations were assessed in a standardized laboratory using an automated biochemical analyzer. Fatty liver was diagnosed using color Doppler ultrasound. Following the ultrasound examination performed by a technician, a gastroenterologist evaluated four sonographic features: deep attenuation, hepatorenal echo contrast, vascular blurring, and liver brightness, to score and diagnose fatty liver [14].

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (n) and percentages (%), with intergroup comparisons performed using the chi-square test. Continuous variables conforming to a normal distribution were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), with comparisons between groups conducted using the One-way ANOVA. For continuous variables that did not follow a normal distribution, they were presented as the median (P25, P75), with group comparisons performed using the Kruskal-Wallis test.

Survival estimates and time-to-event variables were calculated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and the log-rank test was employed to compare the Kaplan-Meier probability of DM-free survival among TyG-BRI groups. Multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models were utilized to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association of the TyG index, BRI, and TyG-BRI with DM risk. Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 was adjusted for age and sex. Model 3 included adjustments for age, sex, SBP, DBP, obesity, smoking status, alcohol consumption, fatty liver, exercise habits, ALT, AST, GGT, HbA1c, HDL-C, and TC. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) analysis was used to evaluate potential nonlinear associations between the TyG-BRI and DM risk, with knots placed at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles. Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore the potential associations between the TyG-BRI and DM risk under different stratifications, including age, sex, smoking, alcohol consumption, BMI, fatty liver, and exercise habits. To assess the predictive performance over time, time-dependent ROC curves and their corresponding AUCs were computed at 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 years of follow-up using the timeROC package. Variance and covariance estimates for the AUCs were derived via an independent and identically distributed (IID) decomposition-based method, and comparisons between AUCs were performed using the compare() function. A weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression model was employed to clarify the overall relationship between exposure to each component of the TyG-BRI (FPG, TG, and BRI) and DM incidence, and to determine the relative contribution of each component to DM risk. In the WQS regression, weights assigned to exposure variables ranged from 0 to 1, with the total weights summing to 1. A higher weight value indicated a greater contribution of the component exposure to the overall risk.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software, version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and a two-sided p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics: higher TyG-BRI quartiles associated with adverse metabolic profile

The investigation comprised 15,310 participants, of whom 55% (8364) were male, with a mean age of 43.70 ± 8.89 years. The mean TyG-BRI was 21.81 ± 7.61. During an average follow-up of 6.05 ± 3.78 years, DM developed in 350 (2.3%) participants. Baseline characteristics of the study population stratified by TyG-BRI quartiles were displayed in Table 1. In terms of anthropometric indices, participants in the highest TyG-BRI quartile (Q4) exhibited significantly higher age, BMI, WC, SBP, and DBP compared to the lower quartiles (Q1-Q3). Notably, the Q4 group also included a higher proportion of men, drinkers, smokers, and individuals with fatty liver disease. Regarding laboratory indicators, participants in the higher TyG-BRI groups demonstrated elevated levels of FPG, HbA1c, TC, TG, ALT, AST, and GGT, but lower levels of HDL-C. TyG-BRI showed a progressive increase from Q1 to Q4 (13.05 ± 2.05 vs. 18.53 ± 1.44 vs. 23.79 ± 1.66 vs. 32.36 ± 4.57, p < 0.001), paralleled by a corresponding rise in DM incidence across the quartiles (0.4% vs. 1.1% vs. 1.9% vs. 5.7%, p < 0.001). TyG-BRI independently associated with increased DM risk

In the analysis with TyG-BRI as a continuous variable, the HR for DM risk in the crude model (Model 1) was 2.38 (95% CI 2.17-2.60, p < 0.001). This significant association persisted in the partially adjusted model (Model 2: HR = 2.19, 95% CI = 1.99-2.42, p < 0.001) and the fully adjusted model (Model 3: HR = 1.38, 95% CI = 1.19-1.61, p < 0.001). Furthermore, when categorized into quartiles, participants in the higher TyG-BRI quartiles exhibited a greater incidence of DM compared to those in the lowest quartile. In Model 3, the fully adjusted HRs (95% CIs) for participants in Q2, Q3, and Q4 of TyG-BRI were 1.95 (1.10-3.45), 1.45 (0.82-2.57), and 1.98 (1.10-3.57), respectively, compared to Q1. Trend tests indicated a significant increase in DM occurrence with higher TyG-BRI (p = 0.013) (Table 2).

Linear dose-response relationship between TyG-BRI and incident DM confirmed by RCS analysis

Fig. 2A illustrated the fully adjusted RCS model, demonstrating a positive linear association between the TyG-BRI and the risk of incident DM (p for overall <0.001; p for nonlinearity = 0.465). Consistently, subgroup analyses stratified by sex revealed linear relationships in both male (Fig. 2B) and female (Fig. 2C) participants (all p for nonlinearity >0.05). These findings indicated that the TyG-BRI served as an independent risk factor for DM, exhibiting a linear dose-response relationship, thereby supporting its potential clinical utility as a continuous risk indicator.

Time-dependent ROC analysis of the TyG-BRI、TyG index、and BRI for predicting the onset of DM in different future periods

To systematically evaluate the predictive value of TyG-BRI, TyG index, and BRI at various time points, time-dependent ROC curve analyses were performed in the overall population, as well as in sex-stratified subgroups, to assess the performance of these indices in predicting incident DM at years 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 of follow-up. In the overall population (Fig. 2D), the time-dependent area under the curve (AUC) for TyG-BRI followed a dynamic pattern, increasing progressively to a peak at year 8 and gradually declining thereafter. Specifically, at years 2, 4, and 6, TyG-BRI exhibited numerically higher predictive performance compared with BRI, with statistically significant differences (all P < 0.05); however, its performance was comparable to that of TyG index, with no statistically significant differences observed (all P > 0.05). In contrast, at years 8, 10, and 12, TyG-BRI demonstrated significantly superior predictive performance relative to both TyG index and BRI (all P < 0.05). A similar temporal trend was observed in the male subgroup (Fig. 2E), with TyG-BRI peaking at year 8 and maintaining a significant predictive advantage over the other indices during mid-to long-term follow-up. In the female subgroup (Fig. 2F), the predictive superiority of TyG-BRI was relatively more limited, with statistically significant improvements over both TyG and BRI observed only at year 8 (P < 0.05); at years 2, 4, 6, 10, and 12, TyG-BRI failed to demonstrate simultaneous significant superiority over both comparator indices. Further analyses revealed that, across the overall population and both sex-specific subgroups, TyG-BRI, TyG, and BRI all achieved time-AUC values exceeding 0.7 at years 8, 10, and 12 (Fig. 4), indicating robust medium-to long-term predictive accuracy for all three indices, with TyG-BRI demonstrating the most consistent and superior predictive performance.

FPG dominates the association between TyG-BRI and DM incidence in WQS analyses

The WQS regression model was employed to comprehensively evaluate the contributions of individual components of the TyG-BRI to DM incidence. It revealed that FPG had the highest relative contribution weight (0.751) among the three variables, followed by TG (Fig. 3).

Sensitivity analysis confirms robustness of association between TyG-BRI and DM incidence

To assess the robustness of these findings, participants with less than average follow-up (6.05-year) were excluded. With TyG-BRI as a continuous variable, the HR (95% CI) for the risk of DM was 2.29 (2.01-2.61) (p < 0.001) in Model 1. This association persisted across Model 2 (HR = 2.21, 95% CI = 1.93-2.54, p < 0.001) and Model 3 (HR = 1.65, 95% CI = 1.32-2.06, p < 0.001). Additionally, when TyG-BRI was categorized into quartiles, participants with higher quartiles had a higher DM incidence, compared with the lowest quartile in the TyG-BRI group. Model 3 showed that the fully adjusted HR (95% CI) of participants in Q2, Q3, and Q4 of TyG-BRI for the risk of DM were 1.84 (0.84-4.04), 1.91 (0.89-4.10), and 3.19 (1.44-7.04), respectively, compared to Q1. Trend tests also showed a substantial rise in the occurrence of DM with higher TyG-BRI (p = 0.013) (Table S1).

Discussion

In this study, we found that the TyG-BRI was positively associated with the risk of DM, with a dose-response relationship. In time-dependent ROC analyses, the TyG-BRI demonstrated superior and more stable predictive capacity for incident DM compared with the TyG index and BRI over mid-to long-term follow-up, with this enhanced performance being particularly evident in male participants. The WQS regression analysis identified FPG as the primary contributor to the observed correlation. Our findings suggested that monitoring TyG-BRI may help identify individuals at elevated risk of DM early on, and managing FPG levels could mitigate DM risk.

Numerous studies have shown that the TyG index is closely related to DM incidence and prognosis. A study of 179,541 adults over 20 years during a median follow-up of 3.01 years found that the TyG index was positively correlated with incident pre-DM/DM (HR 1.67, 95% CI 1.62-1.71), with a nonlinear positive relationship [15]. Another study of 201,298 non-diabetic individuals during an average follow-up of 3.12 years found that the TyG index was independently associated with a higher risk of incident DM (HR 3.34, 95% CI 3.11-3.60) [16]. A retrospective analysis of 568 patients with T2DM during an average follow-up of 14 years found that the TyG index predicted all-cause mortality (HR 1.39, 95% CI 1.07-1.79) and was associated with the onset of complications such as MACE, DKD, and neuropathy, independent of other conventional predictors [17]. Additionally, obesity exacerbates IR and plays a significant role in DM progression. Some studies have shown that combining the TyG index with obesity-related indicators, such as TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, and TyG-WHtR, can enhance its ability to predict DM risk. The BRI was developed in 2013 to predict body fat and visceral adipose tissue volume. Wu et al. used data from the NAGALA database and found that baseline BRI levels were positively associated with incident DM (HR 1.570, 95% CI 1.36-1.81), with a nonlinear relationship [7]. An analysis of 15,848 patients with DM and prediabetes from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) during an average follow-up of 92.9 months found that BRI exhibited a U-shaped relationship with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, with threshold values of 5.54 and 5.21, respectively [18]. A cross-sectional study of 11,980 adults aged ≥20 years from NHANES found that each unit increase in BRI was associated with a 17% increased risk of DM and prediabetes (OR 1.17, 95% CI 1.07-1.27), with a nonlinear trend [19]. However, the combined effect of the TyG index and BRI on DM risk has not been thoroughly investigated.

Our study revealed that TyG-BRI was positively associated with a higher risk of DM, with a dose-response effect. While quartile-based analyses revealed a superficially inconsistent, non-monotonic pattern, with a non-significant hazard ratio in Quartile 3, this finding was likely attributable to several factors, including a limited number of events within that stratum, potential unmeasured confounding, or sex-specific effects. Notably, in our sex-stratified RCS analyses, we found that while TyG-BRI showed a linear relationship with DM risk in both men and women, the RCS curve in men approached non-linearity with an inflection point at a TyG-BRI of approximately 24, which fell within the Q3 range (21.02-26.76). This sex-specific pattern may partially explain the non-monotonicity observed in the overall quartile analysis. However, the positive linear relationship shown by RCS analysis support a consistent dose-response association when TyG-BRI was treated as a continuous variable. Thus, the continuous analysis offered a more stable interpretation, while the quartile analysis remained useful for identifying the substantially elevated risk in the highest quartile (Q4). Time-dependent ROC analyses suggested that TyG-BRI demonstrated superior and more stable predictive capacity for incident DM compared with the TyG index and BRI over mid-to long-term follow-up. Similar to our study, a cross-sectional analysis of 1756 T2DM patients found that the TyG-BRI was significantly associated with CKD risk in T2DM patients, showing a dose-response relationship [10]. A cohort study of 60,685 participants (6980 from China and 53,705 from the UK) found that TyG-BRI had the best predictive performance for ischemic stroke in Chinese rural residents and exhibited an almost linear relationship with ischemic stroke occurrence. Additionally, TyG-BRI showed a U-shaped relationship with the risk of hemorrhagic stroke incidence in the UK [20]. A nationwide prospective cohort study of 8113 Chinese adults from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) found that individuals in the highest quartile (Q4) of the TyG-BRI had a 59.1% increased risk of developing incident CVD compared to those in the lowest quartile (HR 1.59, 95% CI 1.33-1.90), with a significant linear association [21]. Notably, the predictive advantage of TyG-BRI over the TyG index and BRI was not immediate but became evident after 8 years of follow-up, at which point it significantly outperformed both TyG index and BRI. This finding underscores the synergistic value of combining markers of insulin resistance and visceral adiposity; the composite index appeared to more faithfully reflect the chronic, progressive pathophysiology of DM development than either component alone. However, the sex-specific divergence was a critical nuance. This highlighted the need for sex-specific approaches in metabolic risk prediction and suggested that the TyG-BRI may be particularly valuable for risk communication and early intervention in males.

The WQS regression model identified FPG as the primary contributor to the observed association between the TyG-BRI and DM risk. It was important to note that this finding was partly inherent to the index structure, as FPG was a direct component of the TyG-BRI formula. Thus, while confirming the central role of glycemia within this composite metric, it does not represent an independent mechanistic insight. Hyperglycemia is central to diabetes diagnosis. Sustained hyperglycemia can directly damage islet beta cell function, reduce insulin synthesis and secretion, damage vascular endothelium, and exacerbate IR in peripheral tissues, forming a vicious cycle [22]. Hyperglycemia leads to blood lipid metabolism disorders through multiple pathways, resulting in increased free fatty acids, triglycerides, and LDL-C, and decreased HDL-C, which are closely related to diabetes and its complications [23]. The potential mechanisms linking increased DM risk with hyperglycemia also include chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction [24,25]. The WQS analysis provided significant clinical value. For individuals at high risk of DM (elevated TyG-BRI), controlling blood glucose should be a top priority, even if it is pre-diabetic or high normal. For diabetic patients, these results further support the central importance of strict glycemic control to delay complications. It also suggests that lowering TyG-BRI (reducing visceral fat through weight loss, improving IR, and thus lowering blood glucose and TG) should be part of management goals.

In sensitivity analyses excluding participants with shorter follow-up, the graded association between TyG-BRI and diabetes risk persisted, though statistical significance was limited to the highest quartile (Q4). This attenuation reflects the substantial reduction in statistical power due to fewer total participants and incident cases (n = 166 vs. 350), which widened confidence intervals and precluded detection of moderate effect sizes in Q2 and Q3. Importantly, the robust, and even stronger association for Q4 across both analyses suggested that the relationship at very high TyG-BRI levels was particularly stable and less susceptible to dilution over time, reinforcing its clinical utility for identifying the highest-risk subgroup. Critically, the fundamental linear dose-response relationship was reaffirmed by a significant trend across quartiles (p = 0.013) and a consistently positive continuous association in both the primary (HR 1.38 per SD) and sensitivity (HR 1.65 per SD) models. Furthermore, time-dependent ROC analyses demonstrated that the predictive capacity of TyG-BRI remains stable and improves with longer follow-up, further supporting its value for long-term risk stratification.

The present findings have significant value for DM prevention and management. The TyG-BRI is widely used clinically as a simple, cost-effective, and highly sensitive surrogate indicator of visceral obesity, insulin resistance, and metabolic disorder, further refining DM risk assessment. The advent of electronic medical records allows easy access to TyG-BRI data, facilitating the identification of individuals at elevated DM risk in large and long-term follow-up cohorts. For the general population, maintaining WC, FPG, and TG levels within ideal ranges and better control of TyG-BRI are crucial to mitigate DM risk. For individuals at high risk of DM, in addition to effective FPG control, interventions for visceral obesity (e.g., through lifestyle changes such as diet control, exercise-induced weight loss, especially abdominal fat reduction) must also be implemented. Effective weight loss (especially visceral fat reduction) can significantly improve insulin sensitivity, leading to a more effective reduction of FPG and TG, fundamentally reducing TyG-BRI and DM risk.

The present study had several strengths. First, it utilized extensive and reliable medical data from the NAGALA cohort with a prolonged follow-up period. Second, it adjusted for underlying confounding factors in the analysis and conducted subgroup analyses to control for bias, ensuring the robustness of the results. Third, it drew ROC curves to measure the ability of BRI, TyG, and TyG-BRI to predict DM risk. Fourth, it conducted WQS regression analysis to identify FPG as the main factor responsible for the observed correlation between TyG-BRI and DM incidence. Fifth, it provided new evidence for primary DM prevention, suggesting that early recognition and intervention in populations with elevated TyG-BRI could potentially reduce DM incidence.

Although the study has notable strengths, it also has limitations that require careful consideration. First, some DM diagnoses were based on self-reported information from participants, which may introduce information bias. This limitation underscores the need for future large-scale, randomized controlled trials. Second, although potential confounding variables were meticulously adjusted for, the impact of residual factors such as genetic predisposition and environmental changes on the outcomes cannot be entirely ruled out. Third, the exclusion of participants taking any medication at baseline was implemented to minimize confounding by treatment effects and enhance internal validity. However, this criterion necessarily restricted the applicability of our results to individuals not receiving pharmacological interventions for metabolic conditions at baseline. In real-world populations that included medicated individuals with hypertension, dyslipidemia, or other metabolic disorders, the association between TyG-BRI and incident DM may differ in magnitude or pattern. While we hypothesize that the relationship might be even more pronounced in higher-risk populations due to greater underlying insulin resistance, this remains speculative and warrants empirical investigation in future studies specifically designed to include medicated cohorts. Therefore, caution is warranted when extrapolating our findings to broader, real-world populations with prevalent medicated comorbidities. Finally, the study data were from the Japanese population, which may represent a more health-conscious cohort. Therefore, specific cutoff values and the magnitude of association for TyG-BRI observed here require validation in independent, multi-ethnic cohorts before broader clinical application. Despite these limitations, further research is needed to clarify the role of the TyG-BRI in DM risk, which will enhance our understanding of DM risk and facilitate the development of more effective treatment modalities.

Conclusion

The TyG-BRI was positively associated with the risk of DM, demonstrating a dose-response relationship. Maintaining lower TyG-BRI levels may be beneficial to reduce the burden of DM.
