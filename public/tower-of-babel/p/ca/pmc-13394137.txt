Pathophysiological and Molecular Features of DXd ADC‐Related Interstitial Pneumonitis in Cynomolgus Monkeys
Authors: Kazuyoshi Kumagai, Takuma Iguchi, Kentaro Kubota, Ken Sakurai, Yoshimi Tsuchiya, Katsuyoshi Chiba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTInterstitial lung disease (ILD) is an important adverse event related to deruxtecan‐based antibody‐drug conjugates (DXd ADCs). Understanding mechanisms and translationally relevant preclinical models remain incompletely defined. Here, we established a reproducible cynomolgus monkey model of DXd ADC‐related ILD and performed the first integration of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) extracellular vesicle (EV) proteomics with lung single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq). Weekly intravenous administration of a target‐nonbinding human IgG1 DXd ADC to cynomolgus monkeys for 12 weeks consistently induced interstitial pneumonitis in all animals, with radiologic abnormalities emerging by Week 6 and progressing thereafter. Longitudinal SomaScan‐based proteomic analyses revealed a pronounced and selective increase in differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) in BALF‐derived EVs from Week 6 onward, exceeding changes observed in plasma or bulk BALF. Gene Ontology analysis showed enrichment of inflammatory, wound repair/regeneration, and extracellular matrix remodeling pathways within the BALF EV compartment. Integration of BALF EV proteomics with lung scRNA‐seq demonstrated strong concordance between EV‐associated signatures and transcriptional programs in alveolar epithelial, mesenchymal, and immune cell populations. Core injury‐associated genes, including FN1, TIMP1, and COL3A1, were broadly upregulated across cell types, along with cell‐type‐restricted signatures including AT1 cells (BMP4, HMOX2), AT2 cells (CXCL2, SIRPA), fibroblasts (TNC, IGF1), endothelial cells (LCP1, CXCL12), neutrophils (ADAM17), and macrophages (CD84, HAVCR2, CXCL10). Together, this study defines a robust cynomolgus monkey model of DXd ADC‐related ILD and identifies BALF EVs as a sensitive, lung‐localized molecular readout and a promising translational source of mechanistic insights.

Introduction

Anti‐cancer drugs, including cytotoxic agents such as antibody‐drug conjugates (ADCs), molecular targeted therapies, and immune checkpoint inhibitors, have been implicated in the development of interstitial lung disease (ILD) [1, 2, 3]. Despite growing awareness, drug‐induced ILD continues to pose multiple clinical challenges. These include the difficulty of differential diagnosis due to nonspecific symptoms, the lack of reliable risk prediction models, the heterogeneity of radiologic and histopathologic patterns, and the uncertainty surrounding decisions on drug rechallenge [4]. Proposed mechanisms of drug‐induced ILD include direct cytotoxic injuries to pulmonary tissues and immune‐mediated lung damage [1]. However, the precise mechanisms often remain unclear for individual drugs.

Deruxtecan‐based ADCs (DXd ADCs) are composed of a monoclonal antibody that targets cancer cells, a protease‐cleavable maleimide tetrapeptide‐based linker, and an exatecan derivative topoisomerase I inhibitor DXd [5]. ILD is one of the significant adverse events associated with DXd ADCs including trastuzumab deruxtecan [6, 7, 8]. The detailed mechanisms underlying DXd ADC‐related ILD remain elusive. Advancing understanding of its pathogenesis and phenotypic characteristics may provide critical insights into risk mitigation and therapeutic management strategies.

Interstitial pneumonitis related to DXd ADCs has been observed in cynomolgus monkeys [9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. Pulmonary toxicity appears to occur independently of target antigen expression in the lungs, as demonstrated by cross‐evaluation of DXd ADCs in cynomolgus monkeys. Similar pulmonary lesions could not be reproduced in rodents such as rats, highlighting the cynomolgus monkey as a valuable nonclinical model for investigating the pathogenesis of DXd ADC‐associated ILD in humans. When trastuzumab deruxtecan was administered every 3 weeks, which was the same clinical dosing regimen, mild interstitial pneumonitis was detected in approximately half of the monkeys [14]. Given ethical constraints, expanding the number of non‐human primates in such studies is not feasible. Ideally, as seen with well‐established drug‐induced ILD models like bleomycin, a robust animal model would consistently induce pulmonary injury in all animals evaluated.

Small extracellular vesicles (EVs) encompass vesicles with a size of less than 200 nm in diameter. Within this category, exosomes, historically defined as EVs ranging from 30 to 150 nm, are thought to originate from intraluminal vesicles of multivesicular bodies formed through the endocytic pathway [15]. Recent studies have demonstrated that exosomal cargo including proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids can modulate immune responses, epithelial cell injuries, and fibrotic remodeling in lung diseases including ILD [16, 17]. The characterization of small EVs derived from plasma and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) might provide valuable biomarkers for ILD diagnosis and prognosis, while also offering insights into their role in intercellular communication.

In the study, we aimed to establish a DXd ADC‐related ILD model in cynomolgus monkeys through repeated intravenous administration of human IgG1 DXd ADC (IgG‐DXd) lacking binding affinity to any endogenous proteins in cynomolgus monkeys. Plasma and BALF were collected for comprehensive proteomic profiling, including analysis of small EVs derived from BALF. The lung tissues were subjected to single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq) to characterize the disease phenotype and explore underlying mechanisms. Materials & Methods

Test Article

IgG‐DXd was manufactured for nonclinical studies in Daiichi Sankyo Co. Ltd. The ADC is composed of a wild‐type human IgG1 antibody against a bacterial antigen and deruxtecan, lacking on‐target binding in mammalian tissues. The formulation buffer was 10 mM acetate buffer and 5% sorbitol (pH 5.5). The average drug‐antibody‐ratio was 7.9.

Study Designs

Cynomolgus monkeys (3 animals/sex, 3 to 4 years of age) were obtained from a commercial supplier. IgG‐DXd was administered intravenously at a dose level of 30 mg/kg (dose volume: 4 mL/kg) once a week for 12 weeks. The pulmonary toxicity dose for DXd ADCs in cynomolgus monkeys is generally 30 mg/kg or higher [9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. This dose was selected to assess whether weekly intravenous administration induces alveolar injury across multiple lobes.

Chest Computed Tomography (CT) Scan

Animals were examined under anesthesia with intramuscular injections of 0.08 mg/kg medetomidine hydrochloride (Domitor, Orion Corporation) and 10 mg/kg ketamine (Ketalar for Intramuscular Injection 500 mg, Daiichi Sankyo Propharma Co. Ltd.) on Day −6 (with Day 1 defined as the 1st dose) and every 3 weeks following the 3rd dose (Day 16), 6th dose (Day 39), 9th dose (Day 60), and 12th dose (Day 79 or 80) during the IgG‐DXd dosing. Chest CT scans were performed using an X‐ray CT scanner (Canon Medical Systems Corp.) at 120 kV and 170 mA, with a helical pitch of 5.5, 2 mm slice thickness, and images reconstructed using the FC52 kernel optimized for lung field evaluation. The acquired images were analyzed using OsiriX MD (Pixmeo SARL). Lung regions of interest were segmented using the region‐growing tool at a CT attenuation threshold of −600 to −950 HU, and the normal lung volume was estimated.

Plasma and BALF Collection

Approximately 2 mL of blood was collected from the femoral vein on pre‐dose (Days −13 and −7), and every 3 weeks thereafter (Day 22, Day 43, Day 64, and Day 85, prior to the subsequent dose or necropsy). Collected blood was treated with EDTA‐2 K as an anticoagulant. Plasma was separated by centrifugation at 1700 × g for 10 min at 4°C, followed by an additional centrifugation at 10000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. Plasma samples were stored at −80°C until use.

BALF was collected during anesthesia on the same days as chest CT scans. Using a fiberscope (MAF‐TYPE GM, Olympus Medical Systems Corp.), 5 mL of saline at approximately 39°C was instilled into the right middle lobe, left posterior lobe, or right posterior lobe. BALF was retrieved 3 times after instillation and pooled into a single tube. Samples were centrifuged at 200 × g for 10 min at 4°C. Approximately 0.5 mL of the supernatant was aliquoted into a tube and stored refrigerated as bulk BALF. The remaining supernatant was further centrifuged at 2000 × g for 10 min at 4°C until further processing for small EV isolation.

Lung Sample Collection

Animals were euthanized by exsanguination under anesthesia by 0.08 mg/kg medetomidine hydrochloride and 15 mg/kg ketamine, either at the time of early necropsy for animal welfare considerations or at necropsy conducted 1 week after the final dose. Lung tissue samples were collected for scRNA‐seq from both posterior lobes and from any gross lesions in each animal, and stored frozen in the vapor phase of liquid nitrogen until use. The remaining lung tissues including the bronchus were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin. All lung lobes were sectioned, embedded in paraffin, and stained with H&E.

Proteomic Profiling by SomaScan Platform in Plasma and BALF (Bulk and EV Fractions)

To collect small EVs, BALF samples were brought to a final volume of approximately 12 mL by adding D‐PBS (−) and then passed through a 0.2 μm filter (Merck Millipore). After ultracentrifugation (35,000 rpm, 120 min, 4°C), the supernatant was removed, and filtered D‐PBS was added to adjust the volume to approximately 12 mL, followed by a second ultracentrifugation under the same conditions. The supernatant was discarded, and 500 μL of D‐PBS was added. BALF EVs were stored at −80°C. Particle size distribution of the isolated small EVs were assessed by nanoparticle tracking analysis using a NanoSight NS300 system (Malvern Panalytical). The protein concentrations of BALF and BALF EVs were determined using the Micro BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Plasma (130 μL) and BALF and BALF EV samples (180 μL, adjusted to 40 ng/μL) were subjected to the comprehensive proteomic profiling using the SomaScan 7 K platform (Illumina Inc.) [18]. Proteins showing a > 1.5‐fold change relative to pre‐dose with a p‐value < 0.05 were identified as differentially expressed proteins (DEPs). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was performed using the Illumina Correlation Engine platform (Illumina Inc.). Enrichment was evaluated separately for up‐regulated and down‐regulated proteins using a hypergeometric test, in which the observed overlap between DEPs and each GO category was compared against the expected overlap based on all proteins measured by the SomaScan platform.

scRNA‐Seq in the Lungs

Based on histopathological findings adjacent to lung tissue sampling sites, 2 regions were selected from each of 6 animals: one with mild, localized lesions and one with more severe, widespread lesions in a different lobe. Frozen lung tissues from vehicle controls in a separate study (3 males, 1 female), stored in the vapor phase of liquid nitrogen, were processed similarly. Single‐cell partitioning and barcoding were performed at Takara Bio Inc. using the Chromium X platform (10 × Genomics Inc.) with the Chromium Fixed RNA workflow (Chromium Next GEM Single Cell Fixed RNA Sample Preparation Kit and Chromium Fixed RNA Kit, Human Transcriptome; 10 × Genomics Inc.), followed by sequencing on a NovaSeq X Plus system (Illumina Inc.). Data were processed with Seurat (v5.4). Cells with 200–7500 detected genes were retained. Pseudo‐bulk profiles were generated for each sample across all cells or within each annotated cell type, and differential expression was assessed using DESeq2. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as fold change > 2, adjusted p < 0.05, and detection rate ≥ 10% in either group.

Integration of BALF EV Proteomics With Lung scRNA‐Seq

To infer potential cellular origins, BALF EV proteomics data were descriptively integrated with lung scRNA‐seq data. This integration involved qualitative alignment of enriched GO terms and the corresponding DEGs and DEPs, rather than the use of specialized computational algorithms and was intended as a hypothesis‐generating framework linking EV proteomics with cell‐type‐resolved transcriptomics.

Results

Clinical Observations

One male exhibited tachypnea and coughing from Days 61 to 66, reduced spontaneous activity from Days 63 to 65. Another male showed tachypnea, coughing, decreased spontaneous activity on Day 68. For animal welfare considerations, the males were euthanized on Day 66 or Day 68 following the 10th dose. Another male exhibited coughing from Days 78 to 85. The remaining male and 3 females were necropsied on Day 85, 7 days after the final (12th) dose.

Chest CT Scans

No abnormalities were observed in any animals in pre‐dose or following the 3rd dose. From the 6th dose onward, all males and 1 female exhibited pulmonary abnormalities (consolidation) in both lungs. From Week 9 onward, the remaining 2 females also showed bilateral abnormalities (Figure 2). CT‐based volumetric analysis of the normal lung fields revealed substantial reductions (≥ 20% compared with pre‐dose) in 1 male at Week 6 and in all males and 1 female from Week 9 onward (Table 1).

Lung Histopathology

In all lung lobes in all animals, pulmonary alveolar edema characterized by eosinophilic surfactants was observed (Figure 3A). Acute to sub‐acute lesions demonstrated hyaline membranes composed of eosinophilic material lining the alveolar wall, indicative of early‐phase alveolar injuries. Regenerative changes in alveolar epithelial cells were evident within these lesions, accompanied by enlarged cytoplasm/nucleus, and similar changes were suggested in some airway epithelial cells (Figure 3C). Inflammatory changes with mixed neutrophil and lymphocyte infiltration were noted in both the alveolar and interstitial regions, along with prominent infiltration and clustering of foamy macrophages (Figure 3D). These lesions also exhibited intra‐alveolar fibrosis and mildly hyalinized alveolar septa (Figure 3D). Collectively, these histopathological findings suggest that IgG‐DXd is implicated in the development of interstitial pneumonitis in cynomolgus monkeys.

Distinct and Dynamic Proteomic Signatures in Plasma, BALF, and BALF EVs Following Repeated Dosing

In SomaScan‐based proteomic analysis of plasma, an increase in DEPs was observed from Week 3 onward relative to pre‐dose (Table 2). Measurement of total protein levels in BALF revealed a marked increase from Week 6 onward (data not shown). Consistent with the trend, SomaScan analysis demonstrated substantial increases in DEPs in BALF and BALF EVs from Week 6 onward relative to pre‐dose levels (Table 2). While plasma and BALF showed limited numbers of uniquely increased DEPs throughout the dosing period, BALF EVs exhibited a markedly higher number of unique DEPs emerging from Week 6 onward (Figure 4).

Given the histological features suggestive of interstitial pneumonitis, we additionally examined changes in ILD biomarker candidates [19, 20]. This analysis showed increased plasma levels of C‐X‐C motif chemokine 13 from Week 3 onward and matrix metalloproteinase‐7 from Week 6 onward, whereas plasma surfactant protein D and stratifin showed decreasing trends during IgG‐DXd dosing (Figure S1). These observations may be influenced by the study design involving repeated weekly administration of a pulmonary toxic dose in cynomolgus monkeys, rather than reflecting biomarker specificity for ILD.

Gene Ontology Pathway Analysis of Proteomic Profiles

DEPs identified by SomaScan of plasma, BALF, and BALF EVs from IgG‐DXd‐treated cynomolgus monkeys that showed sustained changes from Week 6 through Week 12 were subjected to GO analysis. The top 10 GO terms were visualized using circular enrichment plots for each compartment, where color indicates direction of change (Figure 5). The complete results of all GO analyses are provided in Table S1.

GO analysis of plasma, BALF, and BALF EV proteomic profiles. The top 10 GO terms identified in plasma, BALF, and BALF EVs were visualized using circular enrichment plots. For each GO term, the proportion of the red and blue segments represents the relative proportion of up‐regulated and down‐regulated DEPs, respectively. Numbers shown within each circle indicate the number of associated DEPs, with red and blue values denoting up‐ and down‐regulated proteins. In plasma, the top GO terms were predominantly related to ECM and structural components, and all corresponding DEPs showed a decreasing trend. BALF GO terms were mainly associated with inflammatory and immune responses as well as ECM organization, with all DEPs exhibiting an increasing trend. Similarly, most BALF‐EV GO terms showed increased DEPs, with the exception of vesicle organization and viral life cycle.

In plasma, the majority of the top 10 GO terms were related to extracellular matrix (ECM) and structural components, and the associated DEPs consistently showed a decreasing trend, suggesting systemic reduction of ECM‐related proteins. In contrast, BALF proteomic analysis revealed enrichment of GO terms associated with inflammatory response, immune activation, and ECM organization, with all corresponding DEPs increased, indicating robust lung‐localized inflammatory and remodeling responses.

BALF EV proteomic profiles showed a similar but more pronounced enrichment of GO terms related to inflammation, leukocyte activation, wound healing, and extracellular structure organization. With few exceptions, DEPs associated with these GO terms exhibited increasing trends, suggesting that BALF EVs capture lung‐localized pathological processes also reflected in BALF.

BALF EVs contained a greater number of DEPs and encompassed a broader range of functional categories than bulk BALF, indicating heightened sensitivity to lung pathology and a potential role in intercellular communication. Based on these findings, subsequent analyses focused on BALF EVs to explore lung‐localized molecular programs and their association with lung scRNA‐seq data.

scRNA‐Seq in the Lungs

Hierarchical clustering of pseudo‐bulk expression profiles from lung samples identified two biologically distinct groups among IgG‐DXd‐treated animals: a control‐like stable group (3 samples) and a progressive group with pronounced activation of pathological signaling pathways (9 samples) (Figure S2A). To focus on molecular determinants associated with interstitial pneumonitis progression, subsequent single‐cell analyses distinguished the stable and progressive groups.

Cellular clusters were annotated based on uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) embeddings in combination with canonical marker gene expression (Figures 6A and S2B). Major lung cell populations were identified, including alveolar type I (AT1) and alveolar type II (AT2) epithelial cells, capillary endothelial cells, arterial endothelial cells, fibroblasts, vascular smooth muscle cells, monocytes/macrophages, neutrophils, mast cells, T/NK cells, and B cells. UMAP visualization split by condition showed that these annotated cell populations were broadly represented across control, stable, and progressive samples, whereas progressive samples exhibited marked alterations in cellular composition (Figures 6A and S2C,D). Quantification of sample‐level cell‐type proportions revealed inflammatory remodeling in the progressive group relative to controls (Figure 6B), with monocytes/macrophages and neutrophils each increased 2.9‐fold. Subclustering of monocytes/macrophages indicated that this expansion in the progressive group was accompanied by a shift toward interstitial macrophage subsets (Figure S2E,F).

Differential expression analysis was performed to identify cell‐type‐specific transcriptional changes in progressive samples relative to controls. Using thresholds of fold change > 2 and adjusted p < 0.05, the largest numbers of upregulated DEGs were observed in AT1 and AT2 epithelial cells, followed by fibroblasts, monocytes/macrophages, and neutrophils (Figure 6C). These findings indicate that progression of IgG‐DXd‐related interstitial pneumonitis is associated with inflammatory cell accumulation together with extensive transcriptional remodeling of epithelial, mesenchymal, and myeloid compartments in the lung.

Differential expression analysis in the 3 categories across alveolar epithelial cells (AT1 and AT2), mesenchymal cells (fibroblasts, arterial endothelial cells, capillary endothelial cells, and vascular smooth muscle cells), and immune cells (B cells, T cells, neutrophils, monocytes/macrophages, and mast cells) identified both shared and cell‐type‐specific transcriptional responses. In the category for cell repair and regeneration, FN1, S100A8, TIMP1, COL3A1, PLAUR, and IL6 were upregulated across multiple cell types, consistent with a broadly activated injury‐associated transcriptional program. Cell‐type‐restricted induction was observed for IGF1 and TNC in fibroblasts, LCP1 in capillary endothelial cells, PLEK and ADAM17 in neutrophils.

As for the inflammatory response, genes including FN1, S100A8, S100A9, CRISPLD2, TIMP1, COL3A1, PLAUR, IL1RAP, PTGS2, FCAR, ICAM1, IL6, and CSF3R were upregulated across multiple cell types, consistent with a broadly activated response. Cell‐type‐restricted induction was observed for BMP4 and HMOX2 in AT1 cells, HP, PRKCZ, SIRPA, CXCL2, and CFP in AT2 cells, IGF1 in fibroblasts, CXCL12, CHST2, and LCP1 in capillary endothelial cells, CR1, ZAP70, and LST1 in B cells, CD84, HAVCR2, and CXCL10 in monocytes/macrophages, GNS and ADAM17 in neutrophils.

In the ECM organization, FN1, CRISPLD2, TIMP1, COL3A1, PLTP, MMP7, APOE, CCDC80, and ICAM1 were upregulated across multiple cell types. Cell‐type‐restricted induction was observed for SPINT1 in AT2 cells, TNC in fibroblasts, and LCP1 in capillary endothelial cells.

Across 3 categories, core genes (FN1, TIMP1, COL3A1) were consistently upregulated across multiple cell types, while each category showed cell‐type‐restricted gene induction, indicating a combination of shared and cell‐specific transcriptional responses during IgG‐DXd‐related interstitial pneumonitis.

Discussion

In the context of modeling drug‐induced ILD including DXd ADCs, animal models must fulfill several key criteria such as high reproducibility, mechanistic relevance to human pathophysiology, and pathological similarity to clinical ILD. Efforts are underway to explore the utility of advanced in vitro systems such as lung spheroids/organoids, microphysiological systems, and precision‐cut lung slices [21]. While these platforms are valuable for testing specific mechanistic hypotheses, they still face limitations in replicating the complex interplay among epithelial, stromal, and immune cells, as well as challenges related to exposure duration and susceptibility.

In the present study, despite multiple barriers including a limited sample size and the inherent challenges in non‐human primates, weekly dosing resulted in the induction of interstitial pneumonitis in all animals. In some animals, the disease was accompanied by respiratory symptoms such as coughing and involved multiple lung lobes with relatively severe pathology. In contrast, the clinically relevant 3‐week dosing schedule has been associated with only mild or variable pulmonary findings in cynomolgus monkeys [14]. Therefore, the monkey ILD model represents a highly valuable tool for understanding the pathogenesis and mechanisms of DXd ADC‐related ILD in humans.

CT abnormalities emerged from Week 6 and progressed by Week 9, paralleling a marked increase in DEPs in BALF EVs. EV‐associated changes were more pronounced than in plasma or bulk BALF and coincided with lung injury after repeated IgG‐DXd dosing. Recent studies have increasingly highlighted the roles of EVs in ILD beyond DI etiologies, implicating them in disease pathogenesis, biomarker discovery, and therapeutic modulation [17, 22]. Pathogenic EVs have been implicated in key processes driving ILD progression, including alveolar epithelial cell senescence, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, myofibroblast‐mediated fibrosis, and amplification of inflammatory responses. These EVs are qualitatively distinct from those involved in physiological cellular turnover and are released from multiple lung‐resident cell types under pathological conditions, thereby promoting aberrant intercellular communication.

Consistent with this concept, analyses of human idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis have demonstrated that BALF EVs are more strongly enriched in pathways related to intercellular communication, including antigen presentation and cytoskeletal remodeling, than bulk BALF, indicating preferential concentration of disease‐relevant signals within the EV fraction [23, 24]. In a bleomycin‐induced mouse model of pulmonary fibrosis, fibroblast‐derived BALF EVs were shown to function as active drivers of fibrotic progression, providing experimental evidence that pathogenic EVs play a causal role rather than merely reflecting secondary tissue injury [25].

In a rhesus macaque model for COVID‐19 pathogenesis, SomaScan‐based proteomic profiling was applied to BALF [26, 27]. Proteomic analyses specifically focused on EVs derived from BALF in non‐human primate models of lung injury remain limited. The present dataset highlights BALF EVs as a highly sensitive readout of lung‐localized pathological responses in drug‐induced ILD, with greater proteomic changes than observed in plasma or bulk BALF. This heightened sensitivity is further supported by the substantial overlap between proteomic changes observed in BALF EVs and those detected in BALF.

Changes in several EVs identified in the present study have also been reported in BALF‐derived or lung‐associated EVs across human lung diseases including ILD and animal models. These include ECM‐related proteins such as FN1 [25, 28], COL3A1 [25], and TIMP1 [29], which are consistently enriched in lung EVs and are thought to reflect ongoing matrix remodeling and fibroblast activation in fibrotic lung injury. Similarly, inflammatory mediators such as S100A8/A9 [29, 30, 31] and IL‐6 [32] have been detected in lung‐derived EVs and implicated in neutrophil recruitment, macrophage activation, and amplification of inflammatory responses. In addition, an adhesion molecule ICAM‐1 [33] are reported as EV cargos in lung inflammation and injury, consistent with endothelial and epithelial activation and enhanced leukocyte‐tissue interactions. The repeated identification of these molecules across human disease samples and experimental models underscores their role as canonical EV‐associated markers of pulmonary inflammation and fibrosis. While their detection in BALF EVs supports the biological validity of the present dataset, these proteins are less likely to represent novel EV cargos, and instead serve as internal benchmarks against which newly identified, less characterized EV‐associated pathways can be interpreted.

Alveolar epithelial cells exhibited subtype‐specific transcriptional changes that were concordantly reflected in BALF EV profiles, including AT1‐associated stress and oxidative response genes (BMP4, HMOX2) and AT2‐associated innate immune and chemokine‐related genes (CXCL2, SIRPA), consistent with established epithelial responses in lung disease. Although epithelial‐derived BALF EVs have been reported in acute and inflammatory lung conditions, representation of AT1‐ and AT2‐specific epithelial states within small EVs remains limited [33, 34]. Fibroblast‐associated transcriptional changes were characterized by induction of IGF1 and TNC, supporting activation of mesenchymal programs involved in tissue repair and ECM remodeling, as previously described for EV‐associated TNC in fibrotic settings [35]. While IGF1 is a well‐recognized regulator of fibrosis [22], direct evidence for its incorporation into BALF EVs and EV‐mediated signaling remains lacking. Endothelial cells displayed distinct activation signatures involving LCP1 and CXCL12 that were also reflected in BALF EV proteomes, consistent with roles of endothelial activation in leukocyte trafficking and vascular‐immune crosstalk during lung inflammation [36, 37], although direct evidence for endothelial‐derived BALF EV release remains limited. In parallel, neutrophil‐associated expression of ADAM17, a key regulator of cytokine and receptor shedding [38], and monocyte/macrophage activation markers (CD84, HAVCR2, CXCL10) were identified, highlighting coordinated representation of heterogeneous immune activation programs within BALF EVs. Collectively, these findings suggest that BALF EV proteomics reflect epithelial, mesenchymal, endothelial, and immune activation states during IgG‐DXd‐induced lung pathology, while underscoring the need for further studies to define EV cellular origins at the vesicle level.

One of the limitations in this study is that EV‐associated microRNAs (miRNAs), one of the most extensively studied classes of EV cargo [39, 40], were not evaluated. Given that many EV‐enriched miRNAs are highly conserved across species [41], profiling EV‐associated miRNAs could have provided additional mechanistic insights and potentially enhanced the translational relevance of the findings across species. Such analyses may also have offered clues toward preventive or therapeutic strategies for DXd ADC‐related ILD such as trastuzumab deruxtecan. Integration of EV‐associated miRNA profiling in BALF EVs and lung tissues from cynomolgus monkeys treated with DXd ADCs warrants consideration in future studies.

The cellular origins of EVs were not directly evaluated using vesicle‐level phenotyping approaches, such as flow cytometry or immuno‐capture, which remain technically challenging for robust single‐vesicle analysis in complex biological samples [42]. In this study, inference of EV origin relied on integration of EV proteomic profiles with lung scRNA‐seq data. This integrative strategy supports functional attribution of EV‐associated molecular programs to epithelial, mesenchymal, and immune compartments, but does not provide direct evidence of EV biogenesis from specific cell types. The integration with scRNA‐seq should be interpreted as reflecting convergence between EV cargo composition and cellular transcriptional states, rather than definitive proof of vesicle origin. Further methodological advances enabling reliable vesicle‐level phenotyping will be required to directly resolve the cellular sources of EVs in future studies.

The other limitation of the present analysis is the lack of independent assessment of EV particle number. Consequently, it remains unclear whether the observed proteomic changes primarily reflect increased EV release, qualitative alterations in cargo composition per vesicle, or a combination of both [43, 44]. This distinction is essential to determine whether EV changes reflect signal amplification or altered vesicle content during lung injury.

Collectively, our findings indicate that BALF EVs capture lung‐localized and sustained injury‐associated programs in the monkey ILD model for DXd ADCs. While causality remains to be determined, the distinct molecular features of BALF EVs highlight their potential relevance to the pathophysiology of DXd‐related ILD and support further investigation of EV‐mediated mechanisms in lung diseases.
