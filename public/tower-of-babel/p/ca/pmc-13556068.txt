Automated forensic human identification on dental panoramic radiographs using transformer-based detection with supervised metric learning
Authors: Hye-Ran Choi, Sang-Heon Lim, Ji Yong Han, Sujeong Kim, Su Yang, Jo-Eun Kim, Kyung-Hoe Huh, Sam-Sun Lee, Won-Jin Yi, Min-Suk Heo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDental radiographs are a primary modality for human identification in disaster victim identification, when fingerprint and visual recognition are not feasible. As antemortem (AM) databases grow, exhaustive AM–postmortem (PM) comparisons by expert visual inspection scale poorly to large reference databases. An automated method that pre-screens an AM database into a ranked shortlist of candidates for expert verification would address this bottleneck.MethodsThe framework integrates a Detection Transformer (DETR) with a Circle Loss objective. DETR detects individual teeth and outputs 256-dimensional embeddings from the detection queries. Circle Loss then optimizes these embeddings to minimize same-individual pair distances and maximize different-individual pair distances. Evaluated on 1,029 individuals with paired AM–PM DPRs, identification performance was assessed by deriving the retrieval indices including Rank-K accuracy, mean average precision (mAP), and normalized discounted cumulative gain (nDCG). This detection-based approach was benchmarked against representative convolutional neural network (CNN)-based detectors, including Faster Region-based CNN (Faster R-CNN), RetinaNet, and You Only Look Once (YOLOv9), as well as image-level representations that bypass tooth localization.ResultsThe framework achieved 65.4% Rank-1 and 87.4% Rank-10 accuracy, 72.8% mAP, and 75.3% nDCG, consistently outperforming CNN-based detection architectures. Detection-based structural representations improved Rank-1 accuracy by 44.4% over image-level counterparts, with embeddings capturing fine-grained anatomical details such as root curvature and arch configuration.ConclusionsThe proposed detection-based metric learning framework provides a practical pre-screening tool that compresses exhaustive AM–PM database comparisons into a ranked shortlist of candidates for forensic expert verification. Introduction

Forensic human identification in disaster victim identification relies on three primary modalities such as fingerprints, DNA, and dental records, each with distinct operational constraints [1, 2]. Dental evidence becomes particularly valuable when remains are fragmented, burned, or severely decomposed, as dental tissues and restorative materials resist environmental degradation far better than soft tissue [3–6]. Antemortem (AM) dental radiographs are also widely archived as part of routine clinical care, which makes dental panoramic radiographs (DPRs) a practically accessible source of reference data for postmortem (PM) comparison. In current forensic practice, AM–PM matching is performed manually by trained odontologists, a process that is labor-intensive, subject to inter-observer variability, and poorly scalable to large reference databases [7, 8]. Automated DPR-based identification systems that narrow candidate pools for expert verification would therefore address a practical bottleneck in forensic workflows [9].

In recent years, deep learning has been increasingly applied to the automated detection and classification of anatomical structures in dental imaging [10–12]. Early work on dental identification used convolutional neural networks (CNNs) built for isolated subtasks including tooth numbering, lesion detection, age estimation rather than for identity matching itself [13, 14]. Such pipelines first segment dental structures and then feed handcrafted or weakly optimized features into downstream models, which introduces error propagation between stages and precludes end-to-end optimization for identification. CNN backbones also rely on local receptive fields, limiting their capacity to model the long-range spatial relationships and global arch configurations that forensic identification depends on [15, 16].

Transformer architectures have been adopted for medical image analysis in recent years [17]. The Detection Transformer (DETR) uses global self-attention to capture long-range dependencies across the entire image rather than aggregating local features [18]. Unlike CNN detectors that regress overlapping candidate boxes and rely on non-maximum suppression, DETR formulates detection as a set prediction problem: a fixed set of learnable object queries attends to the image through transformer decoder layers, and each query directly outputs a single object or a discrete tooth in our setting. This design allows the model to be trained end-to-end, and the object queries themselves carry per-tooth representations that can be repurposed for downstream identity matching. Liang et al. [19] introduced a transformer-based framework with clinically informed attention localization and domain-specific augmentation, which improved Rank-1 accuracy by at least 22.98% over traditional approaches. Wu et al. [20] reported high Rank-1 accuracy on a large-scale DPR cohort using an attention-based multi-supervision network. These results indicate that attention-based deep networks can identify heterogeneous restorative patterns and dental arch configurations across temporally separated radiographs, conditions under which forensic matching must operate. Such automated retrieval is intended to produce a ranked shortlist of candidates for forensic expert verification, complementing rather than replacing the rule-based discrete-feature matching used in established forensic odontology workflows.

These developments notwithstanding, most deep learning studies have focused on detection accuracy rather than optimizing representations for identity matching. Forensic records exhibit significant domain shifts due to acquisition variability and long-term anatomical changes. Although dental pattern analysis systems have been proposed to account for the chronological progression of dental treatments [21], the integration of contrastive metric learning with attention-driven detection remains unexplored for bridging the gap between AM and PM embeddings.

The purpose of this study was to develop and comprehensively evaluate an automated framework for AM–PM matching of DPRs, by integrating attention-based dental detection with supervised metric learning. The key contributions are: (1) A new framework integrating transformer-based per-tooth detection with supervised metric learning is proposed for forensic AM–PM candidate retrieval, extracting identity-discriminative representations from the per-tooth detection queries, rather than from raw images alone. (2) Comprehensive empirical evaluation across seven detection backbones (four attention-based and three CNN-based), six metric-learning losses, and seven image-level encoder baselines is provided on a temporally heterogeneous, two-scanner cohort, characterizing the contribution of each design component. (3) The system produces a short, ranked shortlist of candidates, intended to operate upstream of expert verification in large-scale forensic identification workflows. The contribution lies in the proposed framework and its evaluation, rather than in a fundamentally new neural-network architecture.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and preparation

This study was performed with approval from the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Dental Hospital (ERI25045). The ethics committee approved a waiver of informed consent because this was a retrospective study. All procedures followed the Declaration of Helsinki. The present study retrospectively obtained 2,058 dental panoramic radiographs (DPRs; also called orthopantomographs or orthopantomograms) from 1,029 patients (465 males and 564 females; mean age 35.5 ± 15.3 years) who visited Seoul National University Dental Hospital from January 2000 to November 2020. DPRs were obtained from two digital panoramic systems: the OP-100 (Instrumentarium Dental, Tuusla, Finland) at 70 kVp and 8–10 mA with a native resolution of 1976 × 976 pixels, and the RayScan alpha-P (Ray, Seoul, Republic of Korea) at 71 kVp and 12–14 mA with a native resolution of 2988 × 1468 pixels (resized to 1976 × 976 pixels for consistency).

This study applied the following exclusion criteria: (1) primary or mixed dentition; (2) complete or partial edentulism; (3) premolar extraction for orthodontic treatment; (4) impacted teeth other than third molars; or (5) noise, haziness, or distortion that compromised image quality.

The metric-learning dataset was assembled using anonymized, longitudinal DPR pairs from 1,029 individuals. Due to the unavailability of authentic PM radiographs for these subjects within our institutional archives, each pair consists of two temporally separated, in vivo DPRs captured from the same patient. These temporally distinct images serve as a practical proxy for the AM–PM domain shift encountered in forensic practice. For clarity, the baseline scan is designated as the AM record and the subsequent follow-up as the PM counterpart. The imaging time interval between the two acquisitions varied considerably among individuals (mean 2,199 days, approximately 6.0 years; maximum 7,732 days, approximately 21.2 years), thereby illustrating the long-range temporal variability under which forensic matching must operate.

The present 1,029-subject metric-learning cohort is identical to the one previously used in [22], and the 1,638-image detection-annotation subset is re-used from [23]. Both datasets were partitioned into training, validation, and test subsets in a 6:2:2 ratio at the patient-identifier level, with the splits re-drawn independently of [22, 23]. To prevent identity leakage between the detection-training and identification-test cohorts, patient identifiers were used as the grouping key across all splits, so that subjects used to train the detector never appear as queries or candidates in the identification test set. The detection-annotation subset was used only to train and validate the detection prior, never for AM-PM metric-learning training. A patient-identifier overlap audit confirmed zero overlapping subjects between the detection training set and the 206-subject identification test gallery, and the subject-level partitioning further ensures that no metric-learning training subject appears in the identification test gallery. For these datasets, the detection annotations were prepared by trained dental professionals, who labeled bounding boxes for natural teeth, prostheses, treated root canals, and dental implants.

Image preprocessing

All DPRs were resized to 512 × 1024 pixels, preserving anatomical proportions, and pixel intensities were normalized to reduce inter-device variability. For the detection models, bounding box coordinates were transformed accordingly. Geometric augmentations such as rotation were deliberately omitted, since identity matching depends on the spatial configuration of dental structures that such transformations would distort. Horizontal flipping was also omitted, because FDI tooth numbering is side-specific (e.g., upper-right second molar 17 vs. upper-left second molar 27) and a left–right mirror would swap the detection label assignment. The same preprocessing steps were applied across all models.

Automated identification framework

The framework combined DETR-centric detection with supervised metric learning (Fig. 1). This study evaluated seven detection architectures: four attention-mediated models, namely the original DETR [18], Deformable DETR [24], multi-scale DETR (MS-DETR) [25], and denoising DETR (DN-DETR) [26], as well as CNN-based baselines such as RetinaNet [27], Faster Region-based CNN (Faster R-CNN) [28], and You Only Look Once (YOLO) v9 [29]).

Overview of the proposed framework for automated forensic human identification using dental panoramic radiographs. AM and PM DPR images are processed through identical branches consisting of a detection model, an attention pooling module, and a projection head. The two branches share weights to produce identity-discriminative embeddings, which are optimized using supervised metric learning with Circle Loss. *AM, antemortem; DPR; dental panoramic radiograph; PM, postmortem

All transformer-mediated detectors used a ResNet-50 backbone [30] feeding an encoder–decoder with 100 learnable object queries and a hidden dimension of 256.

Identity representations were derived from the final decoder layer of each detection model. The decoder output tensor (where L is the number of layers, B is the batch size, Q = 100 is the number of queries, and D = 256 is the embedding dimension) was taken from its last layer to yield query-level embeddings . The Q query embeddings were aggregated using a learnable attention pooling mechanism in which a linear layer produced attention scores for each query, followed by SoftMax normalization. The pooled 256-dimensional representation, computed as the attention-weighted sum of the query embeddings, was then projected into a 128-dimensional retrieval space by a two-layer multilayer perceptron (256 → 512 → 128) with BatchNorm, ReLU, and Dropout (p = 0.1), followed by L2 normalization. Retrieval is performed by cosine similarity in this normalized space.

As a detection-free baseline, raw DPRs were converted into token representations by a lightweight convolutional adapter, pooled by the same attention mechanism, and projected to the embedding space. This baseline was optimized with the same metric learning objective as the detection-based models, isolating the contribution of tooth-level detection to identification performance.

Supervised metric learning

A shared-encoder Siamese architecture with two identical branches and tied weights was adopted, mapping AM and PM images into a common embedding space. Embeddings were trained with Circle Loss [31], which assigns larger gradients to difficult positive pairs and difficult negative pairs. For each mini-batch with B=64 AM–PM pairs, with each subject contributing at most one pair per batch, a cosine-similarity matrix is constructed between AM and PM embeddings. The B diagonal entries correspond to positive AM–PM pairs from the same subject, whereas the off- diagonal entries correspond to pairs from different subjects and are treated as negatives, yielding K=B positive scores and L=B(B-1) negative scores per mini-batch.

Training was performed in two stages. First, the object detection models were trained to convergence. The detection parameters were then frozen, and the embedding projection layers were optimized using the metric learning objective. This strategy preserves detection performance while adapting the representation space for AM–PM identity matching. Only the attention-pooling and projection-head parameters were updated during the metric-learning stage; the detection backbone was held frozen throughout.

For broader characterization, image-level encoder baselines (ResNet-50 [30], DenseNet-121 [32], EfficientNetV2-S [33], ConvNeXt-Tiny [34], ViT-B/16 [35], Swin-T [36], and DINOv2 ViT-B/14 [37]) were fine-tuned with the same Siamese head and Circle Loss objective described above; detailed results are reported in Supplementary Table S1. The choice of metric-learning loss was separately ablated on the DETR backbone against Triplet [38], NT-Xent [39], ArcFace [40], CosFace [41], and SupCon [42] under the identical Siamese head, split, and hyperparameters; detailed results are reported in Supplementary Table S4.

All models were implemented in PyTorch ver. 2.1.0 (Meta Platforms, Inc., Menlo Park, CA, USA). For the detection stage, the DETR-centric detection networks used a ResNet-50 backbone with 6 encoder layers, 6 decoder layers, 100 object queries, an embedding dimension of 256, 8 attention heads, and a dropout rate of 0.1. The detectors were optimized using AdamW with a learning rate of 2 × 10⁻⁴ for the transformer parameters and 2 × 10⁻⁵ for the backbone, and a weight decay of 1 × 10⁻⁴. The batch size was set to 64.

For CNN-based detection architectures (RetinaNet, Faster R-CNN, and YOLOv9), feature representations were derived from the detector feature maps. Specifically, feature maps from the feature pyramid network were used for Faster R-CNN and RetinaNet, whereas a feature map from the detection head was used for YOLOv9. Each feature map was spatially averaged using global average pooling to produce 256-dimensional embeddings, matching the embedding dimensionality of the transformer-mediated detectors. These CNN-based networks were optimized using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 1 × 10− 4 and a batch size of 64.

For the metric learning stage, the embeddings extracted from each detection model were used as inputs to the Siamese network. The Siamese network consisted of an attention pooling module and a projection head with a dropout probability of 0.1. Training was performed with the Adam optimizer using a learning rate of 1 × 10− 4 and a batch size of 64 for up to 500 epochs. The early stopping strategy was applied when the training loss did not improve for 30 epochs.

Retrieval protocol

To ensure the reproducibility of the identification and evaluation processes, retrieval was performed from PM to AM, mirroring the operational forensic setting in which a PM record is presented as the query. For each of the 206 test subjects (with no training overlap), the PM image served as the query, and the gallery comprised the AM images of all 206 test subjects.

This setup excluded every training-set subject, so that no training identity was present as a candidate at evaluation time. Each PM query and AM gallery image was passed through the trained detection and Siamese pipeline to produce a 128-dimensional L2-normalized embedding, and candidates were ranked by cosine similarity to the query. The primary metric was Rank-K accuracy (K = 1, 5, 10), defined as the proportion of queries for which the correct AM record appeared within the top-K candidates. Retrieval mean average precision (mAP) and normalized discounted cumulative gain (nDCG) were reported as secondary metrics.

Performance evaluation

Detection performance was evaluated using mean average precision (mAP) at an intersection-over-union threshold of 0.5. For identification, this study employed retrieval metrics: Rank-K accuracy (K = 1, 5, 10), retrieval mAP, and normalized discounted cumulative gain (nDCG).

Rank-K accuracy reports the proportion of queries whose correct identity appears within the top-K retrieved candidates. Retrieval mAP averages, across all queries, the precision computed at the ranks where correct identities are retrieved. Feature embeddings were visualized using t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) [43] to assess the spatial distribution of identity representations. All analyses were performed using Python ver. 3.9.10.

Discussion

The present study established an automated framework for AM–PM matching of DPRs that combines transformer-based per-tooth detection with supervised metric learning. The framework attained 65.4% Rank-1 and 87.4% Rank-10 accuracy on the 206-subject test set, with detection-based structural embeddings demonstrating a substantial margin of superiority over image-level representations. The framework’s performance was found to be reproducible across random seeds, as evidenced by five-replicate training analyses (Supplementary Tables S1 and S4). The framework is not intended to replace the rule-based, discrete-feature matching employed in conventional forensic odontology workflows; rather, it is designed to serve as an alternative that can be utilized for high-throughput pre-screening.

Its primary function is to condense the exhaustive AM–PM comparison into a concise, ranked list of candidates for subsequent forensic expert verification. The two roles are complementary: the proposed framework compresses the AM database into a tractable shortlist, while rule-based discrete-feature matching provides transparent per-feature evidence for the expert verifying that shortlist.

In this study, a core design choice was the use of transformer-mediated detection rather than CNN baselines. Global self-attention allowed the network to model spatial relationships across the full dental arch, beyond the scope of local convolutional receptive fields, and to encode identity-discriminative features from natural dentition and restorative patterns alike. This architectural property is reflected in the current empirical results: DETR-centric detectors produced embeddings that outperformed image-level representations and CNN-based baselines across all retrieval metrics.

Recently, various deep learning architectures have been explored for automated DPR-based human identification, reporting a wide range of identification performance. Kim et al. [44] achieved 82.84% Rank-1 accuracy with a modified Visual Geometry Group (VGG)-16 on 746 individuals; Oktay [45] reported 81.0% Rank-1 accuracy using support vector machine classification; Chen et al. [46] reached 88.62% with a mask-based bilateral network. Choi et al. [22] later introduced the DENT-net framework with 85.16% Rank-1 and 97.74% Rank-5 accuracy. Liang et al. [19] reported that transformer-derived features exceeded CNN baselines by approximately 22.98% in Rank-1 accuracy. Comparative evaluations of CNN variants (VGG, ResNet, EfficientNet) on 1,663 individuals produced 80–90% identification precision under intra-institutional conditions [47], and multi-task/deep metric learning approaches reported 86–92% Rank-1 accuracy [48, 49].

In addition to identification-oriented work, recent CNN-based encoder–decoder networks have advanced tooth segmentation and numbering on panoramic radiographs, including the attention-gate encoder–decoder [50] and the multi-scale DentifyNet [51]. It is conceivable that both could function as alternative feature extractors, providing the same Siamese and Circle Loss head used in this study with the same functionality. Such a substitution would represent a natural extension of the proposed framework.

Transformer-based mask-segmentation models, such as MorphMaskFormer [52], represent a complementary line of research that supervises pixel-level masks rather than per-tooth query slots. In principle, both representations are compatible with the proposed Siamese and Circle Loss head. The primary differences between the two are in terms of cardinality (Q = 100 learned slots via Hungarian matching versus pixel-level assignment) and whether a compact per-tooth feature vector is yielded directly or requires mask-pooling. A natural extension of the present framework would be the incorporation of segmentation-mask priors.

Direct comparison with the present results is not straightforward. Many of the cited studies used images collected within a single imaging protocol and a limited AM–PM interval, whereas the test set spanned AM–PM intervals of up to 7,732 days. Systematic reviews have reported that identification accuracy remains high in static datasets [53] but declines under long temporal intervals or sex-specific dentition patterns [54]. The lower absolute Rank-1 accuracy should therefore be interpreted in the context of the broader temporal and structural variability of the evaluation cohort rather than as a direct performance deficit.

Retrieval directly from raw DPRs is vulnerable to acquisition-level variation driven by differences in device, vendor-specific processing, and image quality. Detection-based representation learning anchors the embedding to anatomically meaningful dental structures and restorative patterns, attenuating this acquisition noise. The rank histogram (Fig. 4) reflects this: models without detection distribute correct matches broadly across the top-10 ranks, while detection-based models concentrate correct matches at earlier ranks.

Transformer-centric detection produced the tightest concentration at Rank-1, indicating that its structural embeddings discriminate identity more effectively than CNN-based or image-level alternatives.

Although DN-DETR achieved the highest detection performance (mAP = 0.983), standard DETR produced the best identification accuracy, with Rank-1, Rank-5, and Rank-10 values of 65.4%, 82.9%, and 87.4%, respectively. Cross-architecture correlation analysis (Supplementary Table S3) demonstrated that overall detection mAP@0.5 exhibited no association with retrieval performance (Spearman r = − 0.29, p = 0.53). However, Prostheses AP emerged as the sole per-class metric that demonstrated a significant correlation with Rank-1 accuracy (Spearman r = + 0.76, p = 0.049). The findings of this study indicate that the efficacy of retrieval may be contingent on the encoding of restorative and treatment-related structures to a greater extent than on the accuracy of tooth localization in general. This interpretation is consistent with the hypothesis that DN-DETR’s query-denoising mechanism may encourage representations that are optimized for localization performance while preserving less identity-discriminative structural information. In contrast, although Deformable-DETR and MS-DETR achieved high detection accuracy for natural teeth, their performance on treatment-related structures, including prostheses and treated root canals, was comparatively lower. This may partly explain their reduced retrieval accuracy. Collectively, these observations indicate that the utility of detector-derived embeddings for forensic identification depends not only on overall detection performance but also on the consistent representation of treatment-related structures across radiographs.

A consolidated per-architecture profile of parameter count, FLOPs, and single-image inference latency (Supplementary Table S2) showed that DETR required the fewest FLOPs (93.8 G at 512 × 1024) and achieved the fastest inference time among the seven evaluated detectors (16.4 ms per image). Combined with its superior Rank-1 retrieval performance, these results support the use of DETR as the practical default detector within the proposed framework.

One notable advantage of the framework is its ability to distinguish identities even in cases where the discrete restorative cues commonly used in forensic dental comparison are limited. Whereas human examiners often rely on features such as restorations, prostheses, or missing teeth, the framework successfully retrieved the correct AM match in many cases dominated by natural dentition. This observation suggests that the learned embedding captures subject-specific structural information beyond restorative patterns alone. However, the framework does not currently provide case-level explanations for individual matches, and the anatomical features contributing most strongly to retrieval performance remain to be determined through future interpretability studies.

Quantitative analysis of the 28 Rank-10 failures (Supplementary Tables S17–S18) identified extensive AM-to-PM restorative change as the predominant failure mode, accounting for 53.6% of cases. In contrast, low dental complexity (3.6%) and extensive tooth loss (3.6%) contributed relatively little under the present exclusion criteria. The remaining 39.3% of failures were classified as a residual category characterized by coincidental similarity and/or mild restorative changes, in which AM and PM radiographs remained broadly concordant in restorative profile and tooth presence despite incorrect retrieval.

Collectively, these findings indicate that extensive longitudinal restorative change represents the principal clinical challenge for AM–PM identification within the proposed framework.

They further suggest that a substantial proportion of residual failures cannot be readily attributed to a single identifiable clinical factor, highlighting opportunities for future improvements in representation learning and retrieval robustness.

The integration of supervised metric learning with hard negative mining distinguished the developed framework from existing detection-only models. Unlike models that depend solely on categorical classification, the current approach benefited from representation learning that identified deep-seated morphological patterns. As illustrated in Fig. 5, AM and PM images from the same individual formed tight, cohesive clusters even in cases with significant restorative changes, and separation between clusters of different individuals became more pronounced after supervised metric learning. The 44.4% improvement in Rank-1 accuracy compared to detection-only embeddings validates the importance of incorporating identity-oriented metric learning objectives in forensic applications.

Stratification by AM–PM acquisition interval (Supplementary Table S9) showed that retrieval accuracy remained stable for intervals up to approximately 3 years (Rank-1 = 0.857 at ≤ 3 years) and declined progressively thereafter, reaching Rank-1 = 0.356 at > 10 years.

This temporal pattern provides quantitative evidence of the longitudinal-change effect that has previously been described only qualitatively in AM–PM forensic comparisons. Direct comparison of the present Rank-1 accuracy (0.654) with values reported in previous DPR-based identification studies should be interpreted with caution because evaluation settings differ substantially across studies. Many earlier investigations were conducted under less challenging conditions, including shorter AM–PM intervals, a single imaging protocol, smaller galleries, or classification-based top-1 evaluation rather than retrieval-based Rank-1 assessment. In contrast, the present framework was evaluated on a temporally heterogeneous two-scanner cohort spanning intervals of up to approximately 21 years, with a 206-subject retrieval gallery, conditions that more closely reflect the variability encountered in real-world forensic deployment.

The framework offers potential utility in disaster victim identification workflows. A Rank-10 accuracy of 87.4% indicates that the correct identity is retrieved within the top 10 candidates in nearly nine out of ten cases. In practice, this may substantially reduce the search burden placed on forensic experts by narrowing a large AM database to a small set of candidate matches for subsequent manual verification. Such a retrieval-based workflow is particularly advantageous when AM databases are large and exhaustive manual comparison is impractical.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the framework operates on two-dimensional DPRs, which are inherently affected by geometric distortion and structural superimposition, potentially limiting feature extraction in anatomically complex regions [8]. Future studies should evaluate whether three-dimensional imaging modalities, such as cone-beam computed tomography, can improve structural representation and identification performance. Second, although the dataset was substantial, all images were obtained from a single institution. This limits the generalizability of the findings across patient populations, imaging devices, and acquisition protocols [55].

Consistent with previous reports showing performance degradation of deep-learning models on external datasets [56], multi-center external validation remains essential. Although the present study quantified an internal device-shift effect through scanner-wise validation (intra-scanner Rank-1 = 0.684 versus cross-scanner Rank-1 = 0.600; Supplementary Tables S11–S15), such analyses cannot substitute for true cross-institutional evaluation. Third, the framework does not explicitly model longitudinal dental change. Stratified analysis demonstrated a monotonic decline in Rank-1 accuracy from 0.857 at acquisition intervals ≤ 3 years to 0.356 at > 10 years (Supplementary Table S9), indicating that long-term restorative and anatomical changes remain a major challenge for AM–PM identification. Future work should investigate approaches for improving robustness to temporal variation and progressive dental change.

Two further limitations concern the clinical scope and interpretability of the framework. The present exclusion criteria, including edentulism, impacted teeth other than third molars, and orthodontic treatment involving premolar extraction, remove cases that carry highly discriminative identification cues, so that the framework has limited applicability for these clinically important subgroups. Future studies should evaluate whether explicit modeling of missing teeth, impacted teeth, and complex treatment histories can improve performance in such cases. Finally, the framework currently provides limited interpretability.

Exploratory attention-attribution analysis (Supplementary Table S19 and Supplementary Figure S1) suggested that a relatively small subset of DETR query slots consistently contributed to retrieval decisions. However, because query slots are learned through Hungarian matching rather than predefined anatomical assignment, they cannot currently be linked directly to specific teeth, restorations, or anatomical regions. Accordingly, the anatomical basis of the learned representations remains incompletely understood. In addition, end-to-end fine-tuning of the detector during metric learning was not explored because of the limited training-set size relative to the detector backbone. Both improved interpretability and joint detector–retrieval optimization warrant investigation in larger multi-center datasets.

These findings demonstrate that detector-derived embeddings from DPRs can support clinically meaningful AM–PM dental identification under realistic retrieval conditions. At the same time, broader validation, improved interpretability, and greater robustness to long-term dental change will be necessary before routine operational deployment can be considered.

Conclusions

This study proposed an automated forensic human identification framework that integrates transformer-based detection with supervised metric learning for antemortem–postmortem matching of dental panoramic radiographs. The framework learned identity-discriminative representations from dental radiographs and achieved Rank-1 and Rank-10 accuracies of 65.4% and 87.4%, respectively, on a temporally heterogeneous two-scanner test set comprising 206 subjects under retrieval-based evaluation. These findings demonstrate the feasibility of using detector-derived embeddings to support AM–PM dental identification in a realistic retrieval setting. The proposed framework is intended as a pre-screening tool that reduces exhaustive database comparison to a ranked shortlist of candidates matches for subsequent forensic expert verification. Further validation across multi-center cohorts, improved robustness to longitudinal dental change, and enhanced interpretability will be important steps toward future operational deployment.
