Aging effects on emotionality, cognition and brain mononuclear cells in Sprague-Dawley rats of both sexes
Authors: Jasmina Djuretić, Jana Ivanović, Kristina Jezdić, Vanja Todorović, Biljana Bufan, Anja Santrač, Jovana Aranđelović Ilić
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAging impairs the function of immune cells and increases susceptibility to diseases such as anxiety and dementia. Nevertheless, some individuals exhibit resilience to age-related impairments, although the mechanisms are still unclear. This study investigated alterations in brain mononuclear cells and their association with age-related behavioral deficits in male and female rats. Flow cytometry was used to analyze the expression of CD45, CX3CR1, and CD163 on brain CD11b+ cells. Aging rats demonstrated reduced anxiety-like behavior, spatial learning, and social play, with certain sex differences. Young adult females showed hyperactivity and higher cognitive flexibility than same-aged males. Aging increased CD45 and CD163 expression within CD11b+ cells. Furthermore, sex-dependent correlations were observed between expression of CD163 and CX3CR1 within CD11b+ cells and locomotor activity in aging. Females might appear to be more susceptible to aging, suggesting microglial activation as a compensatory mechanism. These results suggest immune cell dynamics underlying sex-specific aging behavior.Subject terms: Immunology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Aging is a complex process accompanied by a decline in cellular function, which weakens the organism and makes it susceptible to various diseases1. As an advanced course of asynchronous physiological deterioration, it is responsible for structural and functional damage to brain cells, such as loss of mitochondrial homeostasis, impaired intercellular communication and reduced regenerative capacity1, affecting both neuronal and microglial cells2. Aging is associated with a shift in the microglial phenotype towards a more activated state3,4, which may have behavioral implications5. Nonetheless, aging is a heterogeneous process that refers to the different rates at which organisms of different species and organisms within the same species age1, highlighting the phenomenon of resilience6.

The chemokine (C-X3-C) motif receptor 1 (CX3CR1) is a highly specific microglial marker in the rodent brain. It plays a key role in the regulation of microglial function under both normal and pathological conditions7,8. Moreover, loss of CX3CR1 signaling in young mice was recently shown to result in a microglial transcriptome reminiscent of that of aged mice, suggesting premature aging9. Indeed, CX3CR1 has been found to be downregulated in aging, and consequently, reduced phagocytosis of myelin and oligodendrocytes as well as declined neuronal-microglial communication via the CX3CL1-CX3CR1 pathway has been associated with cognitive impairment5,10. On the other hand, CD163 expression has been hypothesized to be restricted to perivascular and meningeal macrophages, but CD163 has also been shown to be expressed on activated microglia11. In aging, CD163 expression is increased in brain regions critical for higher cognitive function, with a tendency for continued upregulation in Alzheimer’s disease (AD)12.

Aging may be an accelerating risk factor for the onset of mental disorders in older people such as depression and anxiety, often co-occurring and limiting a person’s ability to everyday function13. The dysfunction of brain circuitry and neuroinflammation associated with the brain atrophy that underlies aging14,15 can trigger the affective symptoms16. Anxiety occurs in both young and older populations, but generalized anxiety disorder and agoraphobia are phenomena that are more common in adulthood and the elderly17. However, preclinical data on the effects of aging on anxiety-like behaviors are inconsistent, suggesting both an increase18 and a decrease in anxiety19, possibly depending on sex20, but also on the age of the animals18 and the rodent species18,20,21, so these influences need to be further investigated.

Aging leads to changes in brain volume and structure, such that the total length of dendrites in the medial amygdala and medial prefrontal cortex increases and decreases with age, respectively, which could be the basis for increased anxiety-like behavior in older rats18, and depression-like behavior22. Further, the dark microglia found in aging with suppressed gene expression of CX3CR1 and P2RY12 showed decreased phagocytic activity10, and may lead to the aberrant synaptic pruning as found in depression23. The effect of anxiolytics and antidepressants decreases with age, so that not only studies on the influence of various factors such as age and sex on emotionality, but also on microglial changes, would provide new insights into such conditions and open new avenues for novel treatment options16,17.

Clinical studies have shown that the aging process affects individuals differently, and a group of people have been identified who age healthily and show no cognitive impairment (super-aging)6, indicating the importance of the phenomenon of individual resilience to multifactorial influences24. Interestingly, a positive correlation between preserved mental health and speed of movement and better cognitive status has been found in people who age healthily6. On the other hand, cognitive impairment, which is very common in old age, can lead to AD and microglia have been identified as a very attractive pharmacological target for treatment25. In addition, social engagement has been identified as an important non-pharmacological intervention to fight cognitive impairment in aging, but this type of behavior has not been well explored in preclinical research26.

Therefore, the aim of our study was to investigate the sex-dependent effects of aging on cognition, anxiety-like and depressive-like symptoms, and social behavior and their potential relationship with function of microglia as the most dominant brain mononuclear cell in Sprague-Dawley rats. In addition, we aimed to determine the microglial changes in a subgroup of aging animals that exhibit an adult-like behavioral phenotype (a resilient group that potentially ages healthily) and a subgroup that exhibits an altered phenotype (a susceptible group) using translational markers for locomotion and mental health in humans (cf6.).

Results

Results of the spontaneous locomotor activity test and elevated plus maze

Total distance did not differ between experimental groups in SLA (Fig. 1a), but aging animals had a lower percentage of active time compared to young adult rats, regardless of sex (Fig. S1a). In the EPM, the sex factor affected the distance traveled (Fig. S1b) and the number of entries into the closed arms (Fig. 1b), such that females traveled longer distances and entered more closed arms than males, respectively. The age factor also showed a significant main effect on the number of entries into the closed arms (Fig. 1b) and the percentage of entries into the open arms (Fig. 1c), with older animals showing decreased and increased values of these parameters, respectively, compared to young adult animals. Young adult females covered a greater distance and had a higher number of closed arm entries in the EPM than males of the same age, as well as in relation to older males (Fig. S1b, Fig. 1b, respectively).

Results of the sucrose preference test

The main effect of the interaction of age and sex in the sucrose preference test was at the level of the trend effect (p = 0.0702, Fig. 1d), and no change in sucrose intake was observed (Fig. S1d).

Results of the forced swim test

Swimming and immobility time were not affected by the groups studied (Figure. S1e, S1f), but the main effect of sex and the age x sex interaction were found to be significant for struggling time in the FST (Fig. 1e). Young adult females had increased struggling time compared to males of the same age (Fig. 1e). The type of behavioral strategy was significant as a main effect in the FST (Fig. 1f). Young adult males and aging females spent more time in swimming compared to struggling, which was not observed in aging males and young adult females (Fig. 1f). When analyzing the time animals spent using the active or passive strategy, the main effects of strategy and the interaction of strategy x sex were significant (Fig. 1g). Only young adult females showed a trend effect toward spending more time using the active strategy compared to the passive strategy, with the active strategy being more prominent (Fig. 1d).

Results of the resident-intruder test and the three-chamber test

The main effects of sex and age were found for social exploration, which manifested as allogrooming of conspecific in RIT, with females and aging animals showing lower values compared to their respective controls (Fig. 2a). These main factor effects were also found for social play in addition to their main interaction (age x sex) (Fig. 2b). Aging animals of both sexes and young adult females showed reduced social play compared to young adult males (Fig. 2b). The results for the overall social interaction in the RIT test showed significant main effects of age and sex and in the same direction (in Fig. 2c). The main effect of sex was evident in the SIT phase of 3CT test, with females spending less time in the chambers than males (Fig. 2d). Aging male animals discriminated between chambers with animals and empty cages, with spending more time in the chamber with conspecific, whereas the other groups did not show this behavior (Fig. 2d). The main effect of sex was shown in the SRT phase of 3CT test, with females spending less time in the chambers than males (Figure. S1g). In addition, the main effect of factor chamber was at the trend level and tested animals spent more time with the familiar conspecific compared to the novel (Figure. S1g).

Results of behavior in Morris water maze

The main effect of age and its interaction with sex or training day (day) were found to be significant for the distance required to find the platform in the training phase of MWM (Fig. 3a), while two former main effects and interaction age x sex x day were at trend level for the latency to reach the platform (Fig. S2a). On the second training day, the aging males covered a greater distance compared to the young adult males, while the difference with the aging females was at trend level with the same direction (Fig. 3b). At the same time point, the latency to find the platform was higher in the aging males than in the young adult males (Fig. S2b). The main effect of age x sex and sex x day were found for the percentage of path efficiency (Fig. 3c) and percentage of peripheral time (Fig. S2c) during the training phase of MWM, respectively. For the latter parameter, the main effect of sex was significant, with females showing higher values compared to males (Fig. S2c). The latency to reach the platform (Fig. 3d) and the number of entries into the platform zone (Fig. S2e) in probe trial of MWM did not differ between groups, but the main effect of sex was significant for the time spent in the circular target zone around the platform zone, with females spending less time in it than males (Fig. 3e). The sex effect was detected for the total distance traveled during probe trial, with females showing higher values than males (Fig. S2d).

Age-related changes in microglia/macrophage phenotype

The main effect of age was observed in the proportion of CD45high cells within the CD11b+ population. Specifically, young adults exhibited a negligible number of CD45high cells among CD11b+ cells (0.19 ± 0.13% and 0.27 ± 0.05%, for young adult males and females, respectively), whereas aging animals showed a markedly increased (p < 0.01) percentage of CD45high cells within this population (5.21 ± 2.13% and 6.58 ± 2.42%, for aging males and females, respectively) (Fig. 5a, b). The main effect of age did not reach significance for the proportion of CX3CR1+ cells within the CD11b+ population (p = 0.0872, Fig. 5c, d). Nevertheless, aging females exhibited an approximately 15% reduction in the proportion of CX3CR1+ cells among the CD11b+ population compared with female young adults, whereas aging males showed only a 3.5% decrease relative to sex-matched young adults (Fig. 5c, d). The main effects of age and sex, as well as their interaction age x sex were significant for CD163+ cells within CD11b+ cells (Fig. 5e, f). Namely, aging females exhibited a 2.5-fold increase in the frequency of CD163+ cells among the CD11b+ population compared with sex-matched young adults (p < 0.0001), whereas aging males showed a 67% increase in these cells relative to sex-matched young adults. However, this increase in male rats did not reach statistical significance. Furthermore, aging females displayed a significantly higher proportion of CD163+ cells (7.44 ± 1.89%) within the CD11b+ population compared to aging males (3.39 ± 0.6%; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 5f).

Correlations between behavioral parameters and brain mononuclear cell levels and a pilot study on phenotypes in aging rats

Spearman correlation between behavioral parameters and brain mononuclear cell parameters was performed for each experimental group (Fig. S4a–d). Pearson correlation between EPM parameters of the closed arms revealed a negative and positive correlation with the percentage of CD11b+ cells expressing CD163+ and CX3CR1 + , respectively, only in aging females (Fig. 5g). Furthermore, a negative correlation was observed between the latency to reach the old platform zone in the MWM reversal probe and the percentage of CD11b+ cells expressing CX3CR1+ in aging males (Fig. 5h).

Based on EPM parameter phenotyping, “susceptible” aging males showed a higher percentage of closed arm entries compared to “resilient” aging males and the young adult males (Fig. S5a). Aging females categorized as “susceptible” had a reduced number of closed arm entries and an increased percentage of open arm entries, compared to the other two female groups (Fig. S5b, S5c). “Susceptible” aging males showed an increased sucrose preference compared to the “resilient” aging males (Fig. S5d), while the latter group showed an increased distance on day 2 of Morris water maze training compared to “susceptible” aging males and young adult males (Fig. S5e). Regarding the percentage of CD11b+ cells expressing CD163 + , the “resilient” aging male group had a higher percentage of such cells than the young adult group (Fig. S6a), whereas in females, the “susceptible” aging group had higher levels than the “resilient” aging and the young adult group, and the “resilient” group had higher levels than the young adult group (Fig. S6b). Regarding the percentage of CD11b+ cells expressing CX3CR1, no difference was observed in males (Fig. S6c), while in females, “susceptible” aging group had lower values compared to the “resilient” aging group and the young adult group (Fig. S6d). In both the “susceptible” and the “resilient” aging groups, the levels of CD45high CD11b+ cells were increased in both sexes compared to the young adults (Fig. S6e, S6f).

Discussion

In the current study, different domains of emotionality, memory and social behavior were investigated in relation to the function of brain microglial/macrophages in middle-aged Sprague-Dawley rats of both sexes. In addition, a pilot study was conducted to phenotype aging animals using levels of anxiety and/or locomotion to assess the potential molecular link with resilience to aging. Aging animals showed a sex-independent reduction in anxiety and locomotion activity compared to young adult animals in EPM. Consistent with previous research20, young adult females showed hyperactivity compared to same-age and/or aging males, manifested in different contexts from hyperlocomotion in EPM to increased struggling time and the tendency to choose an active versus a passive strategy in FST. The latter was violated by aging process. Social play decreased with age in males, while females were generally less engaged in this type of behavior in the RIT. However, the social interaction in a 3CT-related environment was promoted in aging males compared to young adult males, suggesting the multifaceted social behavior27. Aging males showed lower spatial learning ability than young adult males. Females showed lower spatial learning behavior associated with increased anxiety compared to males in MWM regardless of age. Interestingly, young adult females showed higher cognitive flexibility in reversal probe MWM test compared to other groups studied. Aging was accompanied by an increase in the brain expression of CD45 within CD11b+ cells in both sexes, suggesting inflammaging. Furthermore, the increase in the expression of anti-inflammatory CD163 + CD11b+ cells, especially in the aging female brain, suggests a compensatory mechanism possibly related to hypolocomotion and likely ineffective in the long term. Furthermore, the decrease in expression of CX3CR1+ within CD11b+ cells in aging females correlated with hypolocomotion.

Aging animals covered a comparable total distance to young adults during the 10-min SLA test sessions, but showed less activity time than young adults. The former results may suggest preserved motor function, while the latter may be associated with declining motivation to explore such environment28. Similarly, the aging induced hypolocomotion in EPM. Furthermore, female young adult animals were found to be hyperactive, as reflected by the number of closed arm entries compared to same-aged or aging male animals in EPM, which is consistent with previous findings20 and points to the importance of studying sex differences. As Kalynchuk et al. have shown29, prominent struggling in a stressful environment such as the FST was a key aspect of young adult female phenotype. Here, their tendency to prefer active over passive coping strategy was observed, and is in line with previously reported sex differences (cf.30). It is noteworthy that the direction of the most variable parameter, struggling time, tended to move in the opposite course with age in both sexes, but significance was only reached for the main interaction between age and sex. This suggests a possible imbalance in the effectiveness of sex-dependent coping strategies or a sign of behavioral maturity, reflecting a preference for strategies that optimize energy expenditure under stress, during aging that warrants further investigation30.

In our study, aging animals were found to be less anxious than young adult animals in the EPM test when tracking the percentage of entries into the open arms, and this result was independent of sex. Our findings are consistent with a study conducted with a significantly larger number of aged mice21, and when the results are translated, they are consistent with a recent human study examining anxiety31. In a study with 12-month-old male Wistar rats, it was shown that, anxiety increased in middle-aged males, at the first exposure to the EPM device, but paradoxically decreased at the next exposure compared to adult animals, which may be related to disturbances in GABAergic transmission during aging, which plays a role in the regulation of anxiety32 and may be related to our results. Furthermore, reduced anxiety in aging was associated with stronger connections between the medial prefrontal cortex and medial amygdala in rats33. Because changes in these structures may also be associated with impairment in cognitive function in aging34,35, decreased emotionality and cognitive deficits may be part of the aging phenotype as shown here (Fig. S8).

In this study, no development of anhedonia was observed in older animals compared to young adults, as previously shown36, although a 3% sucrose solution was used, which can even lead to binge eating37.

In our study, spatial learning ability decreased slightly during the 4-day MWM acquisition phase in an age-related manner, as previously shown in rats, suggesting a hippocampus-dependent change38, while spatial memory was comparable. Distance to reach platform on day 2 of MWM was found to be critical for the detection of such cognitive impairment in aging males compared to matched young controls, but this difference was not found in aging females39. However, females showed a weaker ability to navigate through the MWM regardless of age, as previously confirmed in a large cohort of rats40,41, which may be related to the sex differences including hormonal changes and/or increased anxiety-like behavior reflected as thigmotaxis. The cognitive flexibility as reflected by the preference for the new platform zone over the old one during memory retrieval in the reversal MWM probe task, was impaired in the aging animals in our study as shown previously42,43. Moreover, in our hands, young adult females exhibited increased cognitive flexibility which was not the case in young adult males. Female rats showed better performance in the MWM compared to males, when non-stationary pretraining with a hidden platform was performed, which allowed their habituation to the maze and a reduction in endpoint anxiety44, which might be related to the change in the position of the platform zone in the reversal MWM phase in our study. This may be linked to their increased responsiveness to environmental changes under stressful conditions compared to males (cf45.). However, due to protocol variations for the reversal Morris water maze task across literature, this specific type of behavior is rarely studied (cf46.). Nonetheless, males spent more time in the target zones around the platform zones during the reversal probe than females, which is comparable to previous findings47, so the behavior in this part of the MWM needs to be investigated further.

Social interaction in aging is declined in healthy rodents48,49. Thus, we delineated that age affects overall direct social interaction in RIT, and found that the main contribution to this is the reduction in social play, rather than social exploration which manifests as allogrooming. The effect of aging in males may be related to lower motivation for a novel stimulus and the choice of more passive social behavior49. As expected, young adult female rats engaged less in social play behavior than males of similar age50,51, possibly due to increased anxiety-like behavior in such test52.

However, in the SIT phase of 3CT, aging male rats showed a higher preference for cages with conspecifics over empty cages and outperformed respective young adults, as recently shown in mice53, highlighting the complexity and domains of the construct of social behavior. A similar trend was observed in females, but further studies are needed due to the limited evaluation of their results in this study. In addition, the rats in our study tended to prefer the chamber with a familiar conspecific over the chamber with a novel conspecific during the SRT phase of the 3CT, which could be a rodent species-dependent cause and/or due to the socio-emotional status of a rat toward a familiar captive conspecific54. Although this part of the study has limited robustness due to the small sample size, it suggests that other tests for social memory may be more applicable.

Our results revealed two distinct subgroups within the aging groups based on EPM parameters: the resilient and the susceptible. However, because the subgroup sizes are small, this part of the study has lacking robustness and may serve as a pilot study that requires further evaluation in the future. The resilient group maintained anxiety levels similar to young adults, reflecting age-related resilience that may be comparable to the link between exploratory behavior and resistance to chronic stress55. In contrast, the susceptible group showed reduced anxiety, possibly related to disinhibition as observed in AD transgenic mouse models56. In females, this susceptible subgroup also displays hypolocomotion, which here probably indicates emotional hypoactivity rather than impaired motor ability and likely influence the percentage of open arm time as parameter of anxiety-like behavior. This is consistent with the findings of super-aging studies, where improved motor capacity and mental health are positively correlated with resilience6. In addition, we also observed a difference in sucrose preference between susceptible and resilient aging males. This difference could represent a binge-eating as a compensatory coping mechanism under aging, similar to stressed susceptible rats57. More interestingly, the reduced spatial learning in resilient aging males compared to susceptible aging males suggests sex-dependent differences in the aging trajectory and raises the question what is the more optimal behavior regarding anxiety-like behavior during aging per each sex.

Flow cytometry analysis revealed a greater proportion of CD45high cells among CD11b+ cells (microglia/macrophages) isolated from the brains of aging rats than in young adults. Microglia typically express low- to-intermediate levels of CD45, while macrophages express higher levels then microglia58. However, it has been demonstrated that CD45highCD11b+ cells comprise not only infiltrating immune cells, but also resident microglia in models of stroke, cerebral amyloid angiopathy, and aging59. Furthermore, it has been suggested that this microglial subpopulation within the CD45highCD11b+ compartment may contribute to the maintenance of active immune surveillance in the brain59. Inflammatory signaling can also increase microglial CD45 levels60, suggesting a possible association with inflammaging. The blood-brain barrier is significantly altered by aging. Decreased expression of tight junction proteins and morphological changes in aged endothelial cells lead to increased permeability of the barrier, which may contribute to infiltration of peripheral immune cells, including macrophages, into the brain parenchyma61. In this context, we cannot exclude the possibility that infiltration of peripheral myeloid cells also contributes to the increased proportion of CD45high cells within the CD11b+ population observed in aging animals compared with young adults.

Our results showed a tendency to decrease microglial CX3CR1 expression, but without reaching statistical significance with aging. When phenotyping was taken into account, this decrease was not statistically significant in males but was significant in aging-susceptible females compared to both sex-matched young adults and age-matched aging-resilient rats. Moreover, the positive correlation between microglial CX3CR1 expression and the number of closed arm entry in EPM was found exclusively in the aging female group. We therefore hypothesize that hypolocomotion may be an early marker associated with further cognitive decline in females62, and that the downregulation of microglial CX3CR1 expression may be related to this process. It has been suggested that CX3CR1 signaling in microglia is required for the maintenance of brain homeostasis63 and neuronal survival and that the absence of signaling through CX3CR1 could augment the deleterious inflammatory microglial subtype64 with increased phagocytosis63. In the context of AD, a reduction in CX3CR1 expression increased amyloid beta clearance but also neuronal loss63, while in the aging, it delayed recovery from sickness behavior (i.e., anorexia, lethargy, and social withdrawal)65, suggesting that these concurrent changes are consistent with a compensatory mechanism with limited effectiveness. At the behavioral level, CX3CR1 deficiency in mice was associated with decreased exploratory behavior66, a pattern we observed here. However, this should be further investigated due to the presence of peripheral long-lived CX3CR1+ cells, which may be associated with hypolocomotion, but an increase in anxiety-like behavior, which was not the case here67.

On the other hand, our results revealed that aging increases the proportion of CD163+ cells among brain CD11b+ cells, with the increase being particularly pronounced in aging female rats compared to aging male rats. The hemoglobin scavenger receptor CD163 mediates the endocytosis of hemoglobin-haptoglobin complexes, thereby preventing hemoglobin-induced oxidative stress. It has been demonstrated that CD163 exerts anti-inflammatory effects by inducing IL-10 secretion and upregulating heme oxygenase-1 expression68. Increased CD163 expression with aging may, at least in part, represent a protective response to age-related oxidative stress in brain tissue and chronic low-grade neuroinflammation. In aging, CD163 expression is increased in the hippocampus, entorhinal cortex and superior frontal gyrus, brain regions critical for higher cognitive function12, and is associated with an enhanced response to immunological challenges resistant to microglial depletion or repopulation69. Moreover, persistent upregulation of CD163 in the superior frontal gyrus has also been found in AD12. In the study by Pey et al.70, parenchymal microglia were immunoreactive for CD163 in all AD cases. It was suggested that this may be related to the tendency of CD163+ microglia to respond to extracellular protein pathology70. Indeed, CD163 is a marker of alternatively activated (M2) microglia expressed in both murine and human microglia71. M2 microglia have been shown to produce antiinflamatory cytokines and neurotrophic growth factors, as well as to promote the clearance of cell debris and misfolded proteins72. Consequently, an inadequate or impaired M2 microglial response may represent one of the mechanisms contributing to neurodegeneration. Furthermore, CD163 was determined as the critical white-matter hyperintensities related gene in mild cognitive impairment. Small-vessel injury presented in cortical tissue was related to the upregulation of CD163 expression73. Therefore, it is proposed that age-related upregulation of CD163 plays a compensatory role by mitigating the effects of proinflammatory gene alterations and may be also associated with the population of the perivascular macrophages that phagocyte amyloid beta, but also damage the nearby neurovascular function74. Therefore, our finding of age-related increase of CD163+ cells among CD11b+ brain mononuclear cells may reflect protective or compensatory aspects of microglial activation, which was also observed in neurodegenerative disease models75. However, the observation that this increase was more pronounced in female aging susceptible group than in female aging resilient group suggests that increased CD163 expression may not be sufficient to counteract age-associated neuroinflammatory or neurodegenerative processes and could also reflect a maladaptive or dysregulated immune response. Moreover, given that CD163+ blood monocytes/macrophages are considered a source of brain perivascular macrophages76, it is possible that the increased CD163+ population observed in aging animals reflects contributions from both resident and infiltrating mononuclear immune cells.

In conclusion, middle age in rats is associated with distinct, sex-dependent behavioral decline in anxiety-like behavior and spatial learning. Among the various multidimensional social behaviors assessed, social play emerged as the most profoundly affected domain in aging males. Neuroinflammatory processes in aging were characterized by potential changes in microglial phenotypes and/or marker expression such as CD45 and CD163. In addition, the latter was inversely correlated with locomotor activity in aging females, suggesting a possible link between microglial function and hypolocomotion. Hence, females appeared to be more susceptible to age-related neuroimmune and emotionality-related behavioral alterations, possibly reflecting differential vulnerabilities in terms of compensatory mechanisms of microglial activation.

Methods

Experimental animals

Sprague-Dawley rats were born and raised in the vivarium of the Faculty of Pharmacy, University of Belgrade. The animals were reared under standard laboratory conditions (constant temperature (21 ± 2 °C), relative humidity 50–60%, daily cycles: 12 h light/12 h dark, with 3-5 animals per cage). A total of 48 animals underwent behavioral protocol, which were divided into 4 groups of 12 animals each. The aging model animals (aging groups) of both sexes were 14-month-old at the beginning of the behavioral battery, while group of young adults (young adult groups) of both sexes were 3-month-old at the beginning of the behavioral battery. Food and water were provided ad libitum to the animals. All animals were habituated to the researchers for one week prior to the start of the experiment (Fig. 6 -habituation). Behavioral experiments were conducted during light phase of the day except for resident-intruder test. All experiments were in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines and were ethically approved by the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Water Management of Republic of Serbia (approval number 000085842 2025). The randomization and blinding were performed for all experiments.

Experimental design of the study.

The timeline shows the phases of the experimental protocol, consisting of the habituation of the animals to the researchers, the behavioral battery, the sampling and the molecular analysis of the mononuclear cells of the brain. Four experimental groups underwent the behavioral battery, consisting of the elevated plus maze (EPM), the spontaneous locomotor activity assessment test (SLA), the sucrose preference test (SPT), the three-chamber test (3CT), the resident-intruder test (RIT), the Morris water maze (MWM), the reversal MWM and the forced swimming test (FST).

Elevated plus maze test

The elevated plus maze test (EPM) was used to assess anxiety in laboratory animals77,78. The apparatus for the EPM consists of 2 open arms (50 × 10 cm, without walls), 2 closed arms (50 × 10 x 40 cm) with walls and a central square (10 × 10 cm). The apparatus is placed on a plastic stand 50 cm from the floor, while the test monitoring camera is mounted above the apparatus. All animals participating in the test spent 10 min in the adjacent room to acclimatize to the environment. The maze room was illuminated with subdued indirect light (25 W). The animal was lowered into the central part of the apparatus with its head facing the closed arms. The animal’s behavior was recorded with a camera and processed in the Anymaze software. Each individual spent 5 min in the test, and at the end of each test the apparatus was disinfected with 70% ethanol.

Spontaneous locomotor activity

Spontaneous locomotor activity (SLA) approach to assess the locomotor activity of the animal78 is conducted in a rectangular arena (60 cm × 50 cm × 35 cm) under dim indirect light. Behavior is tracked by Animaze software during 10 min.

Sucrose preference test

The sucrose preference test (SPT) is a well-established method for evaluating anhedonia in rats79. In this study, the test was performed over four days using a 3% sucrose solution. The rats were housed individually, and the initial training took place on the first two days: on the first day, each cage contained two bottles of sucrose solution, while on the second day, one sucrose bottle was replaced with water, so that the animals could choose between water and sucrose. The positions of the bottles were randomized to control for any side preference. On the third day, the animals were deprived of food and water for 18 h. On the fourth day, the SPT was performed by offering both bottles (one with sucrose, one with water) for one hour, and the intake of each solution was measured to quantify sucrose preference and sucrose intake.

Three-chamber test

The three-chamber test (3CT) is a widely used paradigm for assessing social interaction and social memory in rodents54. In brief, the test animal was first habituated to the central chamber, for 5 min while the side chambers remain closed. In the social interaction phase (SIT), the animal was allowed to explore the entire apparatus (each chamber was 19 × 45 cm) for 10 min; one of the side chambers contained a cage with a conspecific, while the other side chamber contained an empty cage. For the subsequent social recognition phase (SRT, 10 min), a novel conspecific was placed in the previously empty cage (new animal), while the previous conspecific remained in the other cage (old animal). All intruder animals were completely unfamiliar to the test animals and were housed in different rooms of the vivarium.

The resident-intruder test

The resident-intruder test (RIT) was used to assess direct social behavior with the unfamiliar conspecific (1.5-month-old rat of the same sex)80. In brief, the experimental animal was socially isolated in the home cage for three days, with no change of bedding to allow its establishment of a territory. On the test night, an intruder was placed in the home cage and behavior was recorded for 10 min. The social exploration (allogrooming) and social play (including wrestling, chasing, following, boxing, pinning down) of the test animal were later tracked manually via the Anymaze software by experienced researchers blinded to the experimental group.

Morris water maze followed by reversal protocol

Spatial learning and memory were assessed using the Morris water maze test (MWM)81. Each animal was trained to reach the hidden platform (2 cm below the water surface) in the pool (diameter 2 m) for 4 days in 4 × 2 min consecutive trials per day. The animal starting position in the pool was changed in a pseudo-random order to ensure that the last position used on a given day became the first position on the following day (for working memory). On probe day 5, the platform was removed, and each animal was allowed to explore the apparatus for 1 min. In addition, the 4-day reversal MWM protocol was performed, in which the platform was moved to the opposite quadrant of the pool. During the reversal MWM training, which consisted of 3 days with 4 trials each, the animals were allowed to learn the position of the new platform zone, which was further tested on the day of the reversal trial. This task was designed to test cognitive flexibility in aging and young adult animals81 which could potentially detect changes found in humans82. The behavior of the animals during MWM and reversal MWM was tracked using Anymaze software.

The forced swim test

To assess emotionality in rats, the forced swim test was performed with an inescapable transparent glass cylinder filled with water83. The water level was set at approximately 30 cm to accommodate the size of the animals and the temperature was maintained at 23 ± 1 °C, with the water being changed between test sessions to minimize any interference with subsequent rats. The animals were acclimatized to the environment in a 15-min training session (FST training). The FST test was conducted 24 h later, during which the animals’ behavior was tracked for 5 min using Anymaze (Stoelting, Wood Dale, IL). FST variables (amount of time the test animal spent swimming, immobile, and struggling) were manually scored by experienced, experimental group-blinded researchers.

Isolation of brain mononuclear cells and cell-surface antigen immunostaining

Animals were anesthetized intraperitoneally with ketamine. After perfusion of the animals, the brains were extracted, then mechanically dissociated through a 70-μm nylon cell strainer (BD Biosciences, Erembodegem, Belgium) and resuspended in ice-cold RPMI-1640 medium (Sigma-Aldrich Chemie GmbH) supplemented with 5% fetal calf serum. The resulting cell suspension was subjected to density gradient centrifugation with a discontinuous 40%/70% Percoll gradient (Sigma-Aldrich Chemie GmbH). Mononuclear cells were subsequently collected from the interphase. Cell count and viability were assessed using a Neubauer hemocytometer in combination with 0.2% trypan blue exclusion. For cell-surface antigen immunostaining cells were incubated with saturating concentrations of fluorochrome-conjugated monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. After incubation, the cells were washed and prepared for flow cytometric analysis (FCA). For biotin-conjugated or unconjugated primary antibodies, a secondary incubation step of 30 min was performed using the appropriate secondary reagents. Sample acquisition was carried out using a FACSAria flow cytometer (Becton Dickinson, Mountain View, CA, USA). For flow cytometric analysis, 50, 000 events per sample were acquired using a flow cytometer, and compensation was applied using single-stained control samples. Data were analyzed with FlowJo software, version 7.8 (TreeStar Inc., Ashland, OR, USA). Appropriate fluorescence minus one (FMO) or isotype controls were used to define gating strategies and to determine marker-specific positivity (Fig. S7). The following monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) were used for immunolabelling: biotin-conjugated anti-CD45 (clone OX-1) and phycoerythrin (PE)-conjugated anti-CD163 (ED2; clone HIS36). These antibodies, along with PerCP-conjugated streptavidin and PE-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit F(ab’)₂ fragments used as secondary reagents, were obtained from BD Biosciences Pharmingen (Mountain View, CA, USA). Fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated anti-CD11b (clone ED8) was purchased from Serotec (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Oxford, UK), and the polyclonal rabbit anti-CX3CR1 antibody was sourced from Abcam (Cambridge, UK) (Table S1).

Statistical analysis

The behavioral and mononuclear cell parameters were analyzed using two-way ANOVA (between-subject factors: age and sex) followed by Tukey-fast hoc test (for differences between all tested groups). In addition, three-way ANOVA with repeated measures and Tukey post hoc tests were used for analysis of parameters in 3CT, reversal MWM and FST (between-subject factors: age and sex; within-subject factor: chamber, platform or strategy, respectively). Additional age- and sex-wise comparisons with Sidak post-hoc test after two-way ANOVA were performed (Table S2). Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to examine the relationship between relevant behavioral parameters and FACS results, using data from individual animals with corresponding measurements from both techniques within each experimental group. Similarly, Pearson correlation analysis was performed for the previously identified significant correlations, as these data followed a normal distribution. A pilot phenotyping was performed using data-based animal classification with descriptive statistics for the parameters number of entries in closed arms and/or percentage of entries in open arms84,85. Specifically, the 15th percentile for the number of entries into the closed arms in each group of young adult animals was used as a threshold to categorize aging animals as either susceptible or resilient. In addition, the 85th percentile for the percentage of entries into the open arms within each group of adult animals was used as a separate threshold for assessment. Aging animals whose values fell below this threshold were classified as susceptible, while those with a higher number of entries were classified as resilient and/or vice versa for percentage of open arm entries.

F and p values are indicated in the upper right corner of each graph if they were significant. Asterisks in the graph represent significant differences between the groups (* for 0.01<p < 0.05, ** 0.001<p < 0.01, ***p < 0. 001).

Two aging females died after the first week of behavioral experiments, while two aging males were excluded from FST testing due to poor performance during FST training day. In addition, sporadic technical outliers occurred in SLA, EPM, MWM probe, and 3CT testing due to inaccurate animal tracking by the Anymaze software. These results were excluded from the analysis for the test and did not systematically bias the groups, except for the SRT phase of 3CT testing, where more animals were excluded. N per group in each experiment and outliers are stated in the Table S3.
