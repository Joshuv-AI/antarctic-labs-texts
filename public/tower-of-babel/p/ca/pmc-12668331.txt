The integration of community engagement into emergency medical Care education in South Africa
Authors: Kubendhren Moodley, Raisuyah Bhagwan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:The health science education landscape has evolved to recognize the importance of community engagement in advancing educational outcomes. This study sought to explore how community engagement has been integrated into health science education and its benefits for students in the Western Cape.MATERIALS AND METHODS:Two samples of academics were recruited: those from emergency medical care (EMC) departments specifically and academics with community engagement experience in health sciences. Sample 1 consisted of 16 EMC academics, while sample 2 comprised of 13 academics from health sciences. Data was gathered using two focus group discussions with sample 1 and in-depth interviews with sample 2.RESULTS:Four broad themes emerged: student preparedness for community engagement, pathways to engagement, assessment of projects, and benefits of the engaged experience.CONCLUSION:The study highlighted ways to prepare students for engagement and integrate these experiences into the classroom context, thereby enhancing the value of learning about social and health issues. It found that creating assessments around engaged experiences can entrench community engagement in EMC education, enriching students with opportunities to work collaboratively with disadvantaged communities.

Background

Contemporary societies have become increasingly complex and dynamic, compelling higher education institutions to interweave community engagement into their curriculum and to become more progressive in preparing students to be socially responsible citizens.[1] The Council on Higher Education of South Africa[2] asserted that universities should aim to produce socially responsible graduates beyond equipping graduates with skills to contribute to their disciplines. Despite embedding community engagement within the Higher Education Quality Committee documents, community engagement has remained at the periphery of academic activities at traditional universities in South Africa.[3]

The South African National Health Insurance Bill has mandated Departments of Health to encourage the participation of local communities in the planning, provision, and evaluation of health services; to establish a society based on democratic values, social justice, and respect for human rights; and to improve the quality of life of all citizens.[4] It is within this context that Ross and Kabidi[5] argued that emergency medical care (EMC) graduates need to be empowered with core attributes that can advance this engagement with communities once they have completed their education and training.

The National Department of Health designed and implemented a three-tier Emergency Care Qualification Framework, which reflected the calls to embed community engagement within curricula[6] and to focus on the social responsibility of EMC graduates as a core attribute. Due to the required competencies, workplace clinical practice forms a significant portion of the curriculum underpinning all EMC programs. Hence, EMC students engage in various work-integrated learning activities within the community space. However, these activities focus primarily on accumulating clinical skills.[7]

Renwick et al.[8] defined community engagement as “a systematic relationship between higher education institutions and their ecological communities, characterised by mutually beneficial interaction, in that it enriches learning, teaching and research whilst simultaneously addressing societal issues and challenges.” Community engagement is characterized by a collaborative relationship between higher education institutions and their larger communities for the mutually beneficial exchange of knowledge and resources in partnership and reciprocity.[910] The social responsibility of institutions is mirrored through their commitment to the common good by making their expertise and infrastructure available for community service programs.[11]

The notion of cultivating social responsibility among students is achieved by enabling them to become more empathetic toward the needs of disadvantaged communities.[12] Promoting and supporting social responsibility among students has become a key focus of Health Science faculties.[13] Bidandi et al.[14] opined that engagement is enabled through the provision of intellectual leadership in areas of community interest, while offering resources and facilities for community use and collaborating with them to achieve mutually productive outcomes.

A study by Talberg and Van Wyk[15] found that at a South African university integrating authentic community-based experiences within the curriculum for medical students improved their social responsibility. They further found that engagement played a crucial role in increasing students’ feelings of responsibility and responsiveness and helped them develop a sense of accountability through involvement in the community. Moreover, students’ knowledge and understanding of disadvantaged communities and social determinants of health were enhanced, and students were found to be more committed to playing a proactive role in making a difference in local communities.

Paramedic students can therefore, develop valuable skills and knowledge to understand and respond to the healthcare needs of the communities they serve through engaged initiatives. Experiential learning seeks to offer a range of experiences that can effectively capacitate paramedic students to provide emergency care across multiple healthcare situations and settings. Moodley[16] opined that the practical exposure of paramedic students to diverse real-world environments offers them greater opportunities for reflection and learning. Bhagwan[17] further argued that community engagement can be strengthened through teaching and learning activities, including service learning and community-based research. Tolken and McKay[18] identified volunteerism, community outreach, internships, cooperative education, and service-learning as typologies for student community engagement.

Several studies have documented the value of engagement. Ross and Kabidi[5] reported that paramedic students found volunteering extremely valuable in developing various interpersonal and communication skills which are necessary for paramedic practice in Australia. Johnston et al.[19] conducted a study involving 191 paramedic students from Charles Sturt University who were requested to complete 50 hours of health screening volunteer work and work-integrated learning in local communities. The study found that 7.3% of respondents felt a sense of civic responsibility and contribution to the community through these experiences. They concluded that the graduate attributes of undergraduate paramedicine students had improved while the health needs of communities were given priority. Bhagwan[20] concurred that engagement fosters a deeper understanding of real-world issues, embraces cultural diversity, and heightens social and civic responsibility.

Within the current South African health context and the sociopolitical landscape of higher education, critical elements from community engagement frameworks could be incorporated into community engagement strategies.[21] Despite the potential of community engagement models, some scholars have raised concerns about their limited ability to address the complex social and economic disparities in specific contexts.[21]

Building on this idea and following an extensive review of the diverse philosophies, theories, and models of community engagement, it became evident that Boyer’s model of the scholarship of engagement, is a suitable theoretical framework for the present study. It aligns well with the aim of the study, providing a comprehensive and practical approach to community engagement in paramedic education. Furthermore, the model emphasizes the importance of collaboration and partnership between universities and communities to address real-world issues and create new knowledge while promoting mutually beneficial relationships. Moreover, the model’s focus on research, teaching, and service aligns with the goals of effective community engagement in paramedic education, advancing the development of effective interventions that meet the unique needs of the local communities that paramedics serve.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This study was guided by a qualitative exploratory approach,[22] which enabled rich insights into the experiences and views of academics from health sciences and emergency medical care specifically. An exploratory design allowed the researchers to build upon the data by gaining a piece of richer and more descriptive information from participants[23] regarding their insights and experiences of community engagement in EMC education.

The Western Cape Province served as the study setting, where three educational institutions deliver the Diploma in Emergency Medical Care curriculum. Due to their geographic proximity, all three institutions offering EMC undergraduate training, share work-integrated learning and community engagement platforms for prehospital clinical student placement, which includes emergency medical services, healthcare facilities, and Western Cape communities.

Study participants and sampling

Two samples were recruited for this study: academics from health science faculties involved in community engagement in the Western Cape and academics from EMC departments specifically. This strategy enabled the researchers to target participants who could enhance an understanding of the phenomenon being researched.[24]

Purposive sampling was used in this study to select participants based on the study’s intent, with the goal that each participant would contribute unique and valuable information to its objectives. Data saturation was achieved after 13 interviews were conducted with academics from health sciences from four institutions. With the second sample, 16 EMC academics were used in two focus group discussions, with eight participants in each focus group. Participants from both samples were recruited through the Heads of Departments at their respective institutions after the researcher received ethical clearance from the institutions. These Heads served as gatekeepers, and those academics who met the eligibility criteria and were interested, were requested to contact the researcher to secure details related to participation in the interviews or focus groups.

Data collection tools and technique

After obtaining ethics approval from the University of Cape Town Human Research Ethics Committee (624/2022), consent was sought from the participants who volunteered to participate in the study. Permission to recruit the academics through their Heads of Departments was obtained from stakeholders from the Western Cape Government College of Emergency Care, MediClinic EMS College, the University of Cape Town, the University of Stellenbosch, and the University of Western Cape. Due to the COVID-19 pandemic, interviews and focus group discussions were slow but occurred between March 2020 and February 2021.

The semi structured interviews and focus group discussions were audio-recorded with the permission of the participants. They were 45 to 90 min long and were guided by an interview guide. These conversations explored the views and experiences of academics related to community engagement using open-ended questions.[25] The table with the interview questions is referenced below [see Table 1].

Data analysis

The data were analyzed after the verbatim transcriptions of the interviews using thematic analysis. Thematic data analysis is a method of analyzing qualitative information that begins with searching for broad categories or themes. Hennink, Hutter, and Bailey[26] encouraged thematic analysis as a data analysis method for exploratory descriptive qualitative research, stating it identifies the essential aspects at the core of the participants’ experiences.[27]

Trustworthiness

To achieve rigor and trustworthiness, the researcher was guided by the four main criteria of trustworthiness[28]: credibility, transferability, dependability, and conformability. This was achieved through deep engagement with the literature on community engagement, repeated reading of transcripts, and member checking with participants to ensure the accuracy of the data collected. Transferability was achieved by providing a comprehensive description of the study context, recruitment of participants, and data collection and analysis methods. Co-coding of the data occurred with a peer who is an expert in qualitative research. The data were further authenticated using an expert validation committee.

Results

Four main themes emerged from the analysis: student preparedness for community engagement, pathways to engagement, assessment of projects, and benefits of the engaged experience.

Theme 1: Preparing students for community engagement

Academics described several ways in which students could be prepared for community engagement, saying:

“You prepare them to be able to work in a collaborative way with them, and to understand what they do. That would very much be part of that teaching about how do you engage with and enter communities. Part of teaching students is preparing them how do you enter and engage with the community.” (A8).

“We used to have a function which we called ‘introducing the students to the community’, where we had representatives from the police services, the municipality, the education department, and social welfare, and they would all come, and we would introduce the new students to the community.” (A2)

“Community engagement must have a purpose, [students] need to have skills in determining what the community’s health needs are, how you would prioritise those health needs with the community, and how you would develop interventions…preparing people [students]… for the role that they will have to take” (A13)

Academics believed that students should be prepared for engagement, with one suggesting an event involving “introducing students to the community” where they could meet important stakeholders. Another suggested that students be taught the principles of collaboration and preparedness on how to enter and engage with communities. This was supported by Armien,[29] who opined that successful engagement requires early planning with students and community partners. Musesengwa and Chimbari[30] stated that without proper preparedness to engage key stakeholders, students are at risk of not identifying community needs and may plan interventions that lack community support and participation. Moreover, extensive research has highlighted several key aspects of a learning experience that are likely to improve student learning. These include collaboration, open-ended exploration, and problem-based learning in real-life situations.[31]

Theme 2: Pathways to engagement

Academics suggested the following strategies to enable the preparedness of health science students:

“We have a facilitator, an interprofessional group of students, and then they’ve got to collaborate on how to solve this issue.” (A1)

“We’ve developed a starter pack that can would be used for those who don’t have community organising skill[s] and are a little bit uncertain of words to use, when to start or where to start, and how to go about it.” (A9)

One of the academics recommended a starter pack to prepare emergency care students, specifically on community organizing skills. This can be similar to the one utilized by the Community Action Network in the Western Cape. Support for these findings is evidenced in the scholarly literature, which indicates that community engagement should become an integral part of patient care and clinical education, rather than an add-on or part of the “hidden curriculum”.[13] Local scholars have also supported the importance of preparing students for community engagement by teaching trust, mutual respect, and respect for diversity and culture as part of preparatory learning modules for community engagement.[2032] Ramsbottom et al.[33] concluded that preparation should equip students with an understanding of the purpose of community engagement and guide how students should establish relationships, build trust, and listen to communities.

One of the academics further suggested that the engagement experience can be brought into the classroom space as follows:

“Exposing our students to that reality and coming together to reflect gives students insight … there’s a better understanding, less self-doubt, and a bit more compassion for themselves and for their patients.” (A2)

“Just have a round table talk about your experience. What was your expectation, what have you learned?” (A5)

“When we are doing some simulation or creating a storyboard for our students. I experienced and created that scenario for the student … one way of actually getting into community engagement and teaching is sending various groups and creating that feedback system where students can say what their experience in different cultures was.” (A9)

Narrated reflections of community experiences brought into the classroom space create enhanced opportunities for students to learn about patient encounters, cultural diversity, and community expectations and challenges. Service learning effectively fosters socially responsive knowledge by exposing students to social issues and equipping them with the experience and skills needed to address these challenges.[34] Moreover, civic engagement, when underpinned by successful campus–community partnerships, can heighten students’ awareness of community strengthen capacities and encourage socially responsive behaviours through collaboration.[35]

Theme 3: Assessment of engagement

Academics raised the importance of assessing engagement:

“It is important to give students a certain number of hours for community work.” (A9)

“There’s much space for reflections, especially among the allied health students … As part of their assessment, they’ve got to submit reviews of how they’ve experienced it … it could be informal reflections, write-ups, or interviews … how they experienced it [community engagement], what it means for them and how they will do things differently in [the] future.” (A2)

“As part of their assessment, they’ve got to submit reflections of their experience. Medical students do more informal reflections … we get them to write up patients and they are assessed with interviews, and often it comes out how they experienced those home visits, and what it meant for them, and how they will do things differently in future.” (A11)

“After engagement activity, you do a course evaluation and review …… I realised there’s certain things in the curriculum that need to change or revision … and put it back into teaching.” (A10)

Academics believed that by intertwining community engagement within the formal assessment process, students would become more exposed to clinical scenarios and other health issues, which could further inform the curriculum. Benneworth et al.[36] supported this by saying that the evaluation of community engagement within curricula, closes the loop of community engagement processes and determines areas for improvement.

One participant suggested that students serve a specific number of hours in community spaces, while another described how written and verbal reflections could strengthen their learning and entrench engagement within classroom dialog. Bhagwan[20] wrote that service learning involves systematic and regular reflection, reporting, and assessment of service experiences, with both academics and students. This includes reading assignments or oral presentations that combine service learning and the impact thereof. Additionally, service learning should demonstrate reciprocity and reflection before, during, and after service by connecting service-learning objectives to the learning objectives and goals of the community partners.[37]

Benneworth et al.[36] further shared that assessing student engagement provides critical data that can inform teaching and program initiatives and issues related to students’ psychosocial growth, cognitive understanding, and professional development by reflecting on knowledge from informal and formal sectors. Muller and Couper[13] echoed their sentiments, saying that academics who want students to adopt a collaborative approach to patient management should provide possible strategies that allow for a cycle of decision-making, group inquiry, reasoning, and planning, where members of the team adopt a self-correcting dialog as part of their advocacy to improve patient care.

Goemans et al.[38] opined that reflective journal writing shares critical processes such as awareness of self-efficacy, self-reflection on activities, and judgements and feelings related to such activities. These reflections provide a feedback loop in addressing the positive and negative impacts on the engagement process.

Theme 4: Benefits of the engaged experience

The benefits of the engaged experience emerged as theme four. The academics described this as follows:

“They are in contact with patients from the first year, because we want them to be comfortable engaging with patients. They learn the anatomy, physiology, all of those things on patient encounters and linking [knowledge] to patients …we get them out into the community early.” (A3)

“Walking with the community healthcare worker to somebody’s house. Then you get those students to come back from that experience … the patient doesn’t just have HIV. This patient is being molested by their father. it gets more complex. In the reflection, students have experienced a little bit broader what the patients are exposed to.” (A11)

“They go into patients’ homes with the community healthcare worker, and use a biopsychosocial framework to assess the household. They use the international classification framework to assess for function, disability and health around environmental factors, personal factors, access to care, and then get those students to come back from that experience and reflect.” (A11)

Placing students in a clinical context enhances their opportunity to integrate theoretical knowledge with real-life patient encounters. Engagement enables students to enter patients’ homes, to assess how their physical and socioeconomic circumstances influence their health. Reflection on this experience can facilitate classroom discussion on social justice issues. Another academic suggested the need for interdisciplinary clusters of health science students to be developed to facilitate collaborative work with the community. In this vein, Taffere, Abebe, Zerihun, Mallen, Price, and Mulugeta[39] argued that civic knowledge is acquired through engagement as students learn about the complexity of societal issues and how to contribute to solving societal ills through collaboration. Community engagement has been associated with fostering attributes such as altruism and dutifulness, as well as improving academic outcomes among medical students.[404142] Similarly, Ceasar et al.[43] highlighted the importance of establishing support and cooperation during initial meetings with stakeholders as a strength of community engagement. Additionally, Askari et al.[44] stated that community engagement fosters trust, transparency, communication, and commitment across various contexts by strengthening the relationships between researchers and communities/stakeholders, thereby enhancing the acceptability and applicability of findings.

Strengths and limitations of the study

The research was specific to the context of the Western Cape Province of South Africa. Although the study was context-specific, the framework has naturalistic and inferential generalizability, which could be transferable to EMC academics or allied health professions training in similar settings.

Conclusion

This study highlighted the benefits of harnessing local community experiences to strengthen student learning through engagement. Engagement within health science education also represents a cornerstone for social and health improvement, thereby nurturing collaborative partnerships between communities and health science students to become socially responsible citizens equipped to deal with complex health and social issues. This study underscored the importance of engagement in developing community partnerships and the holistic development of students through leveraging their strengths to learn in community spaces.

In South Africa, the paramedic profession is undergoing a transformation that acknowledges the uniqueness and complexities of local contexts and the challenges related to health and social issues that paramedics confront in their daily clinical environments. Therefore, it is crucial that paramedic training in EMC education includes community engagement for students so that they develop greater social responsiveness, reflective practice, and a focus on social justice in a way that meets the needs of communities that supports the healthcare system in achieving healthcare plans. It would be valuable to explore strategies for implementing community engagement, as mandated by national policies and higher education legislation, for health promotion, social justice, and social responsibility.
