Therapeutic VEGFC treatment provides protection against traumatic-brain-injury-driven tauopathy pathogenesis
Authors: Ana Royo Marco, Katherine R. Bruch, Maureen N. Cowan, Julia G. Dill, Katelyn A. Moore, Ashley C. Bolte, John R. Lukens
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYTraumatic brain injury (TBI) increases one’s risk of developing Alzheimer’s disease and tauopathy. Yet, the mechanisms linking TBI to neurodegenerative disease remain poorly defined. Mounting recent evidence indicates that defects in brain lymphatic drainage contribute to multiple neurodegenerative diseases. Here, we investigated whether promoting brain lymphatic drainage recuperation following TBI via treatment with the lymphangiogenic factor vessel endothelial growth factor C (VEGFC) mitigates the ability of TBI to exacerbate tauopathy. In this study, we show that a single mild TBI leads to worsened neuropathology, brain macrophage activation, and neurodegeneration in the PS19 mouse model of tauopathy. Moreover, we find that viral-vector-based delivery of VEGFC into the meningeal compartment 24 h post-TBI ameliorates tau-mediated neurodegenerative disease pathogenesis. Findings from these studies offer new insights into how TBI leads to the development of tauopathy later in life and suggest that VEGFC-based treatments might offer a therapeutic strategy to limit tauopathy after sustaining a head injury.

INTRODUCTION

One of the greatest environmental risk factors for developing Alzheimer’s disease (AD), tauopathy, and chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE) later in life is traumatic brain injury (TBI).1–4 TBI not only significantly increases one’s risk of developing neurodegenerative disease but has also been linked to earlier onset and more aggressive disease progression.5,6 Despite being a major risk factor for multiple neurodegenerative diseases, how brain injury mechanistically contributes to these conditions later in life has not been formally established.

Emerging evidence suggests that defects in central nervous system (CNS) drainage via the meningeal lymphatic system can result in impaired clearance of neurotoxic agents involved in driving AD, tauopathies, and other forms of neurodegenerative disease.7–18 For instance, it has been reported that disruption of the brain lymphatic drainage system can impair clearance of amyloid-beta aggregates,13,14 extracellular tau,16 and alpha synuclein from the brain.17,18 Moreover, recent studies also indicate that aging is associated with massive deterioration of brain lymphatic drainage function, and this has been proposed to contribute to the buildup of toxic cellular byproducts in the brain during aging.12 Despite these recent findings linking brain lymphatic dysfunction to the neuropathology underlying multiple neurodegenerative diseases, it remains to be formally established whether brain-injury-induced impairments in lymphatic function contribute to TBI being a significant risk factor for developing a tauopathy later in life.

Recent studies from our lab and others have revealed that even mild forms of TBI can lead to disruption of brain lymphatic drainage function that persists for weeks after the initial head injury.19–21 Building on these findings, we next asked whether TBI-induced impairment in the brain lymphatic system was associated with accelerated tauopathy pathogenesis and whether promoting the recuperation of this drainage defect could limit the ability of head trauma to potentiate the development of tauopathy later in life. Here, we show that a single mild closed-skull head injury leads to more aggressive neurodegenerative disease progression in the PS19 mouse model of tauopathy. Moreover, we demonstrate that targeting the recovery of TBI-induced lymphatic drainage dysfunction with viral-vector-based delivery of the lymphatic vessel endothelial growth factor C (VEGFC) 1 day following head trauma mitigates neurodegenerative disease severity in this tauopathy model.

RESULTS

The ability of TBI to provoke exacerbated tauopathy is blunted with therapeutic VEGFC treatment

To explore whether VEGFC treatment can protect against the adverse effects of TBI on tauopathy pathogenesis, we subjected PS19 mice to a mild closed-skull impact injury or sham treatment at 3 months of age, which is a time point well before the onset of appreciable tauopathy, neuroinflammation, or behavioral abnormalities in this tau mouse model.22 One day following either mild TBI or sham treatment, mice received an intra-cisterna magna (i.c.m.) injection of either adeno-associated virus AAV1-CMV-mVEGFC (abbreviated as AAVVEGFC), to promote VEGFC expression and lymphangiogenesis in the meningeal compartment, or control AAV1-CMV-EGFP (abbreviated as AAVGFP) (Figure 1A). Consistent with previously published work,13 we saw that i.c.m. delivery of AAVGFP promoted GFP transgene expression predominantly in the meninges, pineal gland, and the pia surrounding the brain at 3 weeks post-injection (Figures S1A–S1E). Importantly, mild TBI did not appreciably alter the localization of AAV-driven GFP expression, and we also failed to observe expression of GFP by NeuN+ neurons or IBA1+ cells in the brain parenchyma (Figures S1B–S1E). As expected, AAV-driven VEGFC expression following i.c.m. delivery led to increased LYVE-1 expression in the meninges of both sham+AAVVEGFC and TBI+AAVVEGFC mice at 3 weeks post-TBI when compared to sham+AAVGFP and TBI+AAVGFP mice (Figures S2A–S2C). Consistent with previous work from our lab19 and others,20,21 we found that mild head trauma leads to impaired brain lymphatic drainage at 3 weeks post-injury (Figures S2D and S2E). Moreover, we observed that TBI mice treated with AAVVEGFC 24 h post-TBI show rescued bead drainage comparable to the levels of the uninjured mice at this 3-week time point (Figures S2D and S2E).

Following TBI induction and AAV administration, PS19 mice were aged out to 9 months, and then the ability of VEGFC to impact tauopathy was evaluated by performing immunofluorescence staining for phospho-tau and tau tangles using the AT8 and MC1 antibodies, respectively (Figure 1A). In line with previous studies,23 we found that mild TBI leads to more severe tau pathology in PS19 mice, as evidenced by the 100%–150% increase in AT8 percentage of area coverage in the ipsilateral hippocampus (Figure 1B and 1C) and piriform/entorhinal cortex (Figures 1D and 1E) of TBI+AAVGFP mice when compared to sham+AAVGFP controls. In comparison to TBI+AAVGFP mice, TBI+AAVVEGFC-treated mice showed a pronounced reduction in the AT8 percentage of area staining in both the hippocampus and the cortex (Figures 1B–1E). In agreement with these AT8 phospho-tau findings, we also observed similar outcomes using the MC1 antibody, which detects highly pathogenic conformations of tau tangles.24 More specifically, we observed exacerbated levels of tau tangles after mild TBI in PS19 mice, which were protected against with AAVVEGFC treatment (Figures 1F–1I). Taken together, these findings suggest that therapeutic administration of VEGFC 24 h post-TBI restores functional brain lymphatic drainage and further show that this is associated with an attenuation of progressive tauopathy in PS19 mice.

The ability of TBI to promote exacerbated CNS macrophage activation in tauopathy is attenuated with therapeutic VEGFC treatment

Aberrant activation of CNS macrophages has been extensively linked to neurodegenerative disease pathogenesis in human AD and in people with tauopathy.25 Moreover, reducing brain macrophage activation with a wide variety of approaches has been shown to be effective in limiting the seeding and spread of pathogenic tau in mouse models.26–29 Therefore, we were next interested in delineating whether the amelioration of tauopathy observed with therapeutic VEGFC administration following mild TBI in PS19 mice was also associated with dampened activation of CNS macrophages. To this end, we first evaluated IBA1, which is a commonly used marker of microglia and macrophages in the brain. In these studies, we observed that mild TBI increased the coverage of IBA1 in PS19 mice compared to uninjured sham controls (Figures 2A and 2B). Moreover, mild head trauma in PS19 mice shifted these IBA1+ brain macrophages to take on a rod-like morphology characterized by a decreased filament process length in the CA1 region of the hippocampus (Figures 2A–2C). Of note, the presence of rod-shaped macrophages/microglia in the CNS has been increasingly linked to more severe inflammation and neuropathology in various TBI models and tauopathies.30–33 In line with these morphology findings, mild TBI in PS19 mice was also found to result in increased expression of the microglial activation marker major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II and decreased expression of the homeostatic marker P2RY12 by IBA1+ macrophages (Figures 2D–2F).

Notably, treatment with VEGFC 24 h post-head injury in PS19 mice reduced IBA1 coverage to levels comparable to those observed in uninjured PS19 mice (Figures 2A and 2B). Consistent with VEGFC treatment reducing the CNS macrophage area coverage following mild TBI in PS19 mice, IBA1+ macrophages in the hippocampus of TBI+AAV-VEGFC mice displayed a less rod-like and more ramified morphology compared to those in mild TBI mice that received control AAV (Figures 2A–2C). To further assess the impact of therapeutic VEGFC treatment on microglial responses following head trauma, we likewise evaluated the expression of the activation and homeostatic markers MHC class II and P2RY12, respectively. In line with the beneficial effects of VEGFC treatment post-TBI on IBA1 expression and macrophage morphology, therapeutic administration of VEGFC 1 day after injury reduced MHC class II coverage and restored P2RY12 expression to levels comparable to those observed in uninjured PS19 mice (Figures 2D–2F). Collectively, these findings suggest that therapeutic administration of the lymphangiogenic growth factor VEGFC 24 h after TBI is effective in blunting CNS macrophage activation in PS19 mice.

VEGFC treatment following TBI results in increased expression of neuronal health and synaptic genes in PS19 mice

Next, we leveraged single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) to gain a more comprehensive and unbiased evaluation of the effects of VEGFC treatment on glial and neuronal biology following TBI in PS19 mice. We used the same experimental setup in these studies, whereby PS19 mice underwent either a mild TBI or sham treatment at 3 months of age, followed by i.c.m. injection of AAVGFP or AAVVEGFC 1 day later. Hippocampal nuclei were then isolated and sequenced from the four experimental groups at 9 months of age (Figure 3A). Unsupervised clustering revealed 32 distinct Leiden clusters corresponding to astrocytes, excitatory and inhibitory neurons, oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPCs), oligodendrocytes, fibroblasts, microglia, and choroid plexus epithelial cells (Figure S3A). Differential expression analysis across cell types and in the different treatment groups identified the most robust transcriptional changes within Slc17a6+/Slc17a7+ excitatory and Gad1+/Gad2+ inhibitory neuronal populations, which accounted for a large portion of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) that reached statistical significance (Figure S3B). In contrast, fewer transcriptional changes were detected in glial cells (Figure S3B).

Given that the majority of DEGs identified between the TBI+AAVGFP and TBI+AAVVEGFC comparison were found in excitatory and inhibitory neurons, we next performed a secondary re-clustering of neuronal subpopulations to more closely interrogate the transcriptional patterns seen in neuronal clusters across experimental groups. To this end, we employed a standardized taxonomy approach for neuronal cell-type labeling,34 which yielded glutamatergic hippocampus neurons of the dentate gyrus (DG Glut), the CA1 and prosubiculum (CA1-ProS Glut), and the CA3 (CA3 Glut), as well as GABAergic interneurons such as somatostatin GABAergic neurons (Sst Gaba) and parvalbumin GABAergic neurons (Pvalb Gaba), among others (Figures S3C, S3D, and 3B). Clusters were then anatomically assigned as originating from the hippocampus or cortical border/input cells based on label mappings. Subsequent DEG analysis was performed using the purified hippocampal fraction alone. While DG Glut neurons represented the largest neuronal population in this dataset (40%) (Figures S3C and S3D), CA1 ProS Glut neurons exhibited the greatest transcriptional changes between the TBI+AAVGFP and sham+AAVGFP groups (Figure 3C). Several genes associated with neuronal health, synapse formation, and synaptic transmission, including Lrrc4c, Il1rapl1, Cntn5, Ptprd, and Sorbs2, were found to be significantly downregulated in TBI+AAVGFP mice relative to sham+AAVGFP controls (Figure 3D). Consistent with these individual gene findings, pathway enrichment with Gene Ontology (GO) terms identified presynaptic membrane assembly, synaptic membrane adhesion, and dendritic spine morphogenesis as three of the most depleted pathways in the TBI+AAVGFP group compared to sham+AAVGFP (Figure 3E). Taken together, these findings suggest that TBI leads to decreased expression of neuronal health and synaptic genes in PS19 mice.

When comparing TBI+AAVVEGFC mice to TBI+AAVGFP controls, we observed elevated expression of genes linked to synaptic and neuronal health, including Grin2a, Camk2a, Homer1, Prkcb, Ptpn1, and Tcf4 in CA1 ProS Glut neurons (Figures 3F and 3G) as well as Synpr, Disc1, Negr1, Clstn2, and Glis3 in DG Glut neurons (Figures 3H and 3I). GO pathway analysis comparing TBI+AAVVEGFC and TBI+AAVGFP mice further revealed enrichment of transmembrane transport, long-term synaptic potentiation, and anterograde trans-synaptic signaling in CA1 ProS Glut neurons (Figure 3G), along with elevated expression of neuroblast and neural precursor cell proliferation pathways in the DG Glut neuronal subset comparison (Figure 3I). To further interrogate the synaptic gene expression differences uncovered in our snRNA-seq studies at the protein level, we conducted immunofluorescent staining of the presynaptic molecule synaptophysin in the hippocampus. Consistent with our GO terms findings, we observed a significant reduction in synaptophysin density adjacent to the hippocampus CA1 neuronal cell layer in TBI+AAVGFP mice relative to uninjured sham+AAVGFP controls (Figures 3J and 3K). Likewise, we found that treatment of PS19 mice with VEGFC 1 day post-TBI led to a rescue in the percentage of area of synaptophysin staining when compared to TBI+AAVGFP mice.

Collectively, these findings suggest that treating PS19 mice with VEGFC 24 h post-head trauma has a distinct effect on neuronal gene expression. More specifically, we found that the protection against tauopathy observed in TBI+AAVVEGFC mice is associated with increased expression of synapse-related genes and rescue of synaptophysin protein levels in hippocampal neurons.

VEGFC treatment after head trauma provides protection against tau-mediated neurodegeneration and cognitive decline

Given our transcriptomics data, which suggest an improvement in neuronal health with VEGFC treatment following mild TBI in PS19 mice, we next asked whether VEGFC intervention also protects against neuronal loss and cognitive decline. To this end, we first evaluated hippocampal loss and ventricle enlargement by T2-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) at 8.5 months of age. Here, we found that mild TBI accelerates neuronal loss in PS19 mice, as evidenced by a nearly 3-fold increase in ipsilateral ventricle size and the ~25% reduction in hippocampal volume seen in TBI+AAVGFP mice when compared to sham+AAVGFP controls (Figures 4A–4C). In contrast, the introduction of AAV-mediated VEGFC expression 1 day post-head trauma in PS19 mice was effective in preventing the exacerbation of hippocampal loss and ventricle enlargement by TBI (Figures 4A–4C). To further corroborate our brain atrophy findings by a complementary approach, we next performed fluorescent Nissl staining to evaluate the DG granule cell layer thickness. Consistent with our MRI-based assessment of hippocampal atrophy (Figures 4A–4C), mild TBI induced a pronounced reduction in neuronal density in the DG of TBI+AAVGFP mice compared to uninjured sham+AAVGFP controls, which was ameliorated with the introduction of AAV-induced VEGFC expression in TBI+AAVVEGFC mice (Figures 4D and 4E).

We next assessed whether therapeutic introduction of VEGFC 24 h post-TBI also provides protection against the ability of TBI to aggravate cognitive decline in PS19 mice. To this end, we evaluated the effects of TBI and AAV-driven VEGFC expression on learning and memory in the Morris water maze (MWM) at 7 months of age. Consistent with the exacerbated tauopathy and brain atrophy observed following mild TBI in PS19 mice, TBI+AAVGFP mice showed impaired learning of the platform location in the MWM test, swimming significantly farther from it, particularly on days 3 and 4 of the training phase (Figures 4F and 4G). In contrast, TBI+AAVVEGFC mice performed similarly to uninjured sham+AAVGFP and sham+AAVVEGFC mice in the learning phase of the MWM test (Figures 4F and 4G). To test for memory deficits, the platform was removed on day 5, and mice were allowed to freely explore the pool. Here, we saw that TBI+AAVGFP mice spent significantly less time inside the target quadrant (top right quadrant) when compared to uninjured sham+AAVGFP controls as well as TBI+AAVVEGFC mice (Figures 4G and 4H), which suggests that VEGFC treatment post-TBI in PS19 mice can provide protection against spatial memory loss. Importantly, these differences in MWM performance were not related to deficits in locomotor activity or alterations in anxiety-related behaviors, as mice across all four experimental groups exhibited comparable swim speeds in the MWM and performed similarly in the elevated plus maze (Figures S4A and S4B). Taken together, these findings indicate that mild TBI in PS19 mice provokes accelerated neuronal loss and exacerbated cognitive decline. Moreover, our results further suggest that therapeutic VEGFC treatment post-head trauma can limit the extent of brain atrophy and cognitive dysfunction that unfolds in PS19 mice after mild TBI.

DISCUSSION

The current study demonstrates that therapeutic VEGFC treatment protects against the adverse effects of TBI on tauopathy severity. Here, we found that treating PS19 mice with an AAV overexpressing the lymphatic growth factor VEGFC 1 day after TBI was effective in recuperating brain lymphatic drainage as well as mitigating tau pathology, brain macrophage activation, and neurodegeneration. Furthermore, we showed that AAVVEGFC treatment following mild TBI in PS19 mice protects against severe ventricle enlargement, hippocampus atrophy, DG thinning, and cognitive decline.

Our studies exploring the effects of TBI on tauopathy are in line with previously published work demonstrating that TBI can promote exacerbated tauopathy pathogenesis in PS19 mice.23 Similarly, we found that phospho-tau burden and brain macrophage activation were elevated in the hippocampus and piriform/entorhinal cortex of PS19 mice at 6 months post-TBI. While these brain regions are some of the first affected by tau pathology in the PS19 model, they are distant from the impact injury site in the right anterior somatosensory cortex. This suggests that tau pathology might not just be localized to the region of the injury but could affect distant areas, consistent with the idea that tau spreads through neuronal networks via synaptic connections.35

The brain lymphatic vessels are located within the outermost layer of the meninges, and through their direct connection with the deep cervical lymph nodes, these vessels play critical roles in maintaining tissue fluid homeostasis7,8 and immune surveillance.9–11 Meningeal lymphatic vessels are thought to serve as a continuation of the glymphatic system by providing a conduit for CNS interstitial fluid to exit the brain parenchyma.13,15 Mounting evidence over the last few years has begun to implicate prominent roles for meningeal lymphatic dysfunction in multiple neurodegenerative diseases and aging-associated dementia.12–18 For instance, some of the earliest work on this topic demonstrated that ablating the meningeal lymphatics in a mouse model of AD resulted in increased amyloid-beta deposition in both the meninges and the brain parenchyma.13,14 Conversely, rescuing aging-associated meningeal lymphatic compromise with AAV-based VEGFC overexpression has been reported to limit cognitive decline in aged mice.12 In further alignment with the idea that brain lymphatic demise might be an overarching culprit in various neurodegenerative diseases, the brain lymphatics have also been shown to be critically involved in alpha synuclein clearance from the brain and Parkinson’s disease progression in mouse models and people with Parkinson’s disease.17,18 It is also notable that many of the major environmental risk factors linked to AD and tauopathy development—including aging and TBI—are also associated with brain lymphatic dysfunction.

VEGFC treatment 1 day after a mild TBI was effective in rescuing the potentiating effects of head trauma on tauopathy progression. In contrast, VEGFC treatment was not found to significantly impact tau pathology, macrophage activation, neurodegeneration, or cognition in the absence of TBI at 9 months of age. These findings are consistent with recently published results indicating that VEGFC improves behavioral outcomes in aged mice with impaired drainage but does not appreciably affect behavior in young mice that possess fully functioning meningeal lymphatic vasculature.13 Collectively, this suggests that there is likely a ceiling effect where VEGFC administration cannot further boost homeostatic brain drainage.

Findings from our work are also in line with previous studies demonstrating that genetic deletion of the water channel protein and glymphatic regulator aquaporin-4 (AQP4) leads to elevated phospho-tau generation following TBI.36 However, there are a few notable distinctions between these previous TBI studies conducted in AQP4 germline knockout mice and the work presented in this manuscript. For one, our work sought to define the impact of mild TBI and therapeutic VEGFC treatment on tauopathy, whereas the previously published study investigated how germline deletion of AQP4 impacted acute tau phosphorylation at the injury site following moderate-to-severe TBI. Moreover, our work aimed to elucidate the progressive effects of mild TBI on tau-mediated neurological disease at 6 months posthead trauma in an established model of tauopathy that incorporates human tau, whereas the previous TBI work in AQP4-deficient mice evaluated changes in mouse tau at 4 weeks post-head trauma. This is an important distinction given that hyperphosphorylation of mouse tau does not lead to tau tangles or associated downstream neurodegenerative disease in mice.36 As a result, the phospho-tau detected in this previously published AQP4 knockout TBI study was likely more of a direct readout of the injury and not reflective of bona fide tauopathy disease pathogenesis. In contrast, hyperphosphorylation of human tau, which was studied in our work, can lead to progressive neuroinflammation, neuronal loss, and cognitive decline.

In summary, our findings demonstrate that even mild forms of TBI can lead to accelerated tauopathy progression. Moreover, we show that efforts to recuperate this injury-associated drainage dysfunction with AAV-based delivery of the lymphatic vessel growth factor VEGFC limit the ability of TBI to potentiate tauopathy development.

Limitations of the study

While these findings suggest that VEGFC-based therapeutics can potentially offer a strategy to reduce one’s risk of developing tauopathy following mild TBI, future studies are needed to ascertain how this intervention mechanistically exerts its neuroprotective effects. For instance, it will be interesting to explore if VEGFC treatment influences tau clearance from the brain via increased meningeal lymphatic flow. Further studies are also needed to more deeply characterize the impact of head trauma on tauopathy using additional approaches and to investigate potential effects of VEGFC treatment on cell types other than those that make up the brain lymphatic vasculature. Moreover, it will be of value to explore potential roles for brain-resident macrophages, rod-like microglia, and infiltrating myeloid cells in driving the exacerbated tauopathy and meningeal lymphatic dysfunction that unfold following TBI.

STAR★METHODS

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND SUBJECT DETAILS

Mice

All mouse experiments were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations of the University of Virginia and approved by the University of Virginia Animal Care and Use Committee. Mice were housed in specific pathogen-free conditions under standard 12-h light/dark cycle conditions in rooms equipped with control for temperature (21 ± 1.5°C) and humidity (50 ± 10%). Tau P301S mice (PS19, #008169) were originally purchased from the Jackson Laboratory and then bred and maintained within our animal facility. Unless stated otherwise, relatively equal numbers of age-matched female and male mice were used in all experiments. Male mice were used exclusively for snRNA-seq and behavioral studies to avoid sex as a confounding variable. TBI procedures were conducted at 12 weeks of age followed by AAV injections 24 h later.

METHOD DETAILS

Traumatic brain injury

Closed-skull mild TBI was induced as previously described by our group.19 Briefly, each mouse was anesthetized by 4% isoflurane with 0.3 kPa O2 for 2 min and then the right preauricular area was shaved. The mouse was then placed prone on an 8x4x4-inch foam bed with its nose in a nose cone delivering 1.5% isoflurane. The head was otherwise unsecured. The device used to deliver TBI was a Controlled Cortical Impact Device (Leica Biosystems, 39463920). A 3 mm impact probe was attached to the impactor device which was secured to a stereotaxic frame and positioned at 45° from vertical. In this study, we used a strike depth of 2 mm, 0.1 s (s) of contact time, and an impact velocity of 5.2 m/s. The impactor was positioned at the posterior corner of the eye, moved 3 mm toward the ear, and adjusted to the specified depth using the stereotaxic frame. The impact was delivered to the right somatosensory cortex at approximately between +0.35 and −1.35 bregma. Following impact, the mouse was placed supine on a heating pad and allowed to regain consciousness. The time elapsed until the mouse returned to the prone position was recorded as the righting time. Upon resuming the prone position, mice were returned to their home cages to recover on a heating pad for two hours with soft food. For sham procedures, mice were anesthetized with 4% isoflurane and 0.3 kPa O2 for 2 min and then the right preauricular area was shaved. The mouse was placed prone on a foam bed with its nose secured in a nose cone delivering 1.5% isoflurane for the same amount of time as the injured before being removed.

Intra-cisterna magna injections

Mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of a saline solution containing 10% ketamine (100 mg/Kg body weight) and 10% xylazine (10 mg/Kg body weight). The head of the mouse was secured in a stereotaxic frame and the neck skin was sanitized with 70% ethanol followed by 4% iodine. This was repeated three times before an incision was made at the base of the skull and the muscle layers were retracted exposing the cisterna magna. We used a 33-gauge Hamilton syringe (80308) length of 1.5 inches and 12° to puncture the dura and to manually inject the desired solution into the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) at a rate of 2 μL/min. After injection, the syringe was left in place for another 1-2 min to prevent back-flow of the injected solution. After injection, the skin was closed using 5-0 nylon sutures, and then mice received a subcutaneous (s.c.) injection of ketoprofen (1mg/kg). Mice were then allowed to rest and wake up on a heating pad. Mice were monitored daily for three consecutive days for visual signs of distress. Ketoprofen injections (1mg/Kg) were provided for pain management at least for two to three consecutive days starting the day after surgery. Induction of meningeal lymphangiogenesis was performed as previously described.13 Briefly, 2 μL of AAV1-CMV-VEGFC (Vector BioLabs, Cat. AAV-275994) or AAV1-CMV-EGFP (Vector BioLabs, Cat. 7002) was injected directly into the cisterna magna of 12-week old mice at a concentration of 1 × 1013 GC/mL diluted in artificial CSF (597316, Harvard Apparatus UK). Meningeal lymphatic drainage function was assessed by injection of 2 μL of FluoSpheres carboxylate 0.5μm (beads) 505/515 (Invitrogen, F8813).

Behavioral testing

Male mice were handled twice a day for two days leading up to the behavior tasks. Before the start of each testing day, mice were left in the room for 45-60 min to habituate to the lighting, sound, and overall conditions of the new environment. Only one behavior task was performed per day. The elevated plus maze was conducted first to measure baseline anxiety followed by 5 days of Morris water maze testing. All behavior testing was conducted in a blinded fashion.

Elevated plus maze

To evaluate anxiety-like behaviors, mice were placed on an elevated platform with four arms, in the shape of a ‘‘plus’’ sign. Two opposing arms were covered by high walls (20 cm tall opaque black Plexiglass, 35 × 6 cm2), while the other two arms remained open to the air (uncovered, 35 × 6 cm2) extending from a common center square, elevated 121 cm above the floor. Mice were placed onto the opaque white Plexiglass floor in the center facing a closed arm, and then allowed to explore the entire raised platform for 5 min. The total time spent in both open arms was then normalized as a percentage of the total trial time. Data were recorded using an overhead camera and with the EthoVision automated tracking system (Noldus Information Technology) which provided the time spent in the open arms.

Morris water maze

To evaluate cognitive function in the MWM, mice were placed in a large 1m diameter pool of opaque water. The water was made opaque by adding nontoxic tempera white paint and the water was kept warm at 23°C ± 1. A hidden 10-cm diameter platform was placed at approximately 1 cm underneath the surface of the water on which the mice can stand on. Four trials were performed each day for four days of ‘‘learning’’. Mice were placed in the pool at different starting locations and were given 60 s to explore the water to find the platform. Four large markers (a square, circle, triangle, and X) were placed on the tub walls which provide visual cues to allow the mice to determine where the platform is. Each mouse was allowed to remain on the platform for 20 s and was then moved from the maze to its home cage. If the mouse did not find the platform within 60 s, it was manually placed on the platform and returned to its home cage after 20 s. In between same day trails, mice were allowed to rest for at least 15–20 min. After four days of learning, a ‘‘probe’’ day was completed to assess memory function. Probe day measures the memory of the mice. On this day, the platform was removed from the water, and the mice were again placed in the tub, this time for a 60 s trial. All MWM testing was performed between 9 a.m. and 2 p.m., during the lights-on phase, by a blinded experimenter. During the acquisition, and probe, data were also recorded using an overhead camera and with the EthoVision automated tracking system (Noldus Information Technology) which provided the cumulative distance to platform, average distance to platform, as well as swimming time in the target vs. opposing quadrants.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and image analysis

All mice were anesthetized at an initial rate of 3% isoflurane for 2–3 min and maintained within 1.0–2.0% isoflurane in oxygen air while inside the MRI device. Body temperature was recorded and maintained at 36°C–37.5°C using an air-heater system. Respiratory rate and body temperature were monitored continuously. All MRI imaging was conducted on an actively shielded 9.4 T Bruker Biospec 94/20USR magnet with a 20-cm horizontal core (Bruker-Biospin, Billerica, USA). An RF RES 400 1H 11 coil was used as the RF transmitter, and a Bruker mouse brain RF ARR 400 1H M coil was used as the receiver. For structural imaging of brain ventricles, we used T2-weighted 2D gradient echo rapid acquisition with relaxation enhancement images adapted from a previously published protocol for a faster total scanning time.22 In brief, the setup parameters were the following: field of view 20 mm × 20 mm with 256 × 256 dimensions, slice thickness of 0.350mm, a repetition time of 3000 ms and echo time of 31.9ms with two averages and 1 repetition for a total imaging time of 3min12s. Lateral ventricle and hippocampal quantification were conducted in these 2D images drawing regions of interest (ROIs) utilizing the Horos project software (version 3.3.6). Images were collected and MRI data were analyzed in a blinded manner.

Immunofluorescence and confocal microscopy

Mice were euthanized with CO2 and perfused with ice-cold 1X PBS. For sham mice, whole brains were then harvested and bisected along the sagittal midline, with the right hemisphere stored in 4% PFA at 4°C for 24 h. For TBI brains, both ipsilateral (right) and contralateral (left) hemispheres were placed in 4% PFA at 4°C for 24 h. Brains were cryoprotected in 30% sucrose for 48 h at 4°C, embedded in OCT (Tissue Tek, 4583), and frozen on dry ice. 50 μm sections were cut using a CM 1950 cryostat (Leica) and stored at 4°C in 1X PBS with 0.05% sodium azide until staining. Tissue sections were immunostained free floating. Tissue sections were incubated in a blocking solution (2% normal donkey serum, 1% BSA, 0.1% Triton X-100, and 0.05% Tween 20 in 1X PBS) at room temperature on a rocker for 1hr. For anti-mouse antibodies, 2 drops of IgG blocking reagent were used per 2.5 mL of blocking solution according to the manufacturer’s instructions (VectorLabs, MKB-2213-1). Sections were then incubated with primary antibody in blocking solution overnight at 4°C (~16hrs). Samples were washed three times in a solution of 0.05% Tween 20 (in 1X PBS) and incubated with a secondary antibody in blocking solution for 2 h at room temperature. Finally, tissues were washed three times at room temperature (RT) for 10 min each in a solution of 0.05% Tween 20 (in 1X PBS) and counter-stained with DAPI in 1X PBS (Sigma Aldrich, D9542-1MG) for 10 min at RT. The NeuroTrace/Nissl (Thermo Scientific, N21480) staining was conducted after the secondary stain step and right before the DAPI counter-stain. Briefly, sections were incubated for 20 min at 1:100 (in 1X PBS), washed one time with 0.05% Tween 20 (in 1X PBS) for 10 min, and washed again with 1X PBS overnight at 4°C. After placing sections on slides, sections were allowed to dry completely and then 200 μL of 1XTrue Black solution (Biotium, 23007) was applied to quench autofluorescence. After drying, mounting media ProLong Gold (Thermo Scientific, P36930) was applied and sections were coverslipped. The following antibodies/fluorescent stains were used: AT8 biotin (Thermo Scientific, MN1020B 1:500), MC1 (Peter Davies, Albany, NY, 1:500), anti-Iba1 (Abcam, ab5076, 1:300), anti-P2RY12 (Abcam, ab300140, 1:1000), anti-MHC class II (Thermo Scientific, 14-5321-82, 1:100), anti-synaptophysin (Abcam, ab32127, 1:1000), anti-LYVE1 (Thermo Scientific, 50-0443-82, 1:200), anti-GFP (Abcam, ab6556, 1:1000), anti-NeuN (Millipore Sigma, MAB377, 1:500), NeuroTrace500/525 (Thermo Scientific, N21480, 1:100). NeuroTrace (Nissl) stains, meningeal lymphatics, deep cervical lymph nodes (dcLNs), and brain tau pathology (AT8, MC1) were imaged at 10x with the Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope while high-magnification images of IBA1+ brain macrophages and synaptophysin staining were acquired at 63x with the Stellaris 5 confocal.

To evaluate lymphatic vessel growth in the dura mater layer of meninges, the dorsal aspect of the skull was harvested and the dural meninges were fixed, while still attached to the skull bone, in 4% PFA for 3–4 h at RT. Whole mount dural meninges were carefully dissected and stored in 1X PBS at 4°C for a few days. To immunostain whole mount meninges, the fixed tissues were first incubated in the blocking solution (as above) at RT for 90 min. The meningeal tissue was then stained for 3 h at room temperature with directly conjugated primary antibody (LYVE-1-EF660 at 1:200, eBioscience 50-0443-82). Tissue was washed three times in a solution of 0.05% Tween 20 (in 1X PBS), DAPI in 1X PBS (Sigma-Aldrich, D9542-1MG) also for 10 min at RT and mounted onto glass slides with coverslips using ProLong Gold (Thermo Scientific, P36930). Images were acquired using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope at 10× magnification by an experimenter blinded to the groups.

To evaluate bead drainage, two dcLNs were harvested per mouse and fixed in 4% PFA for 2hrs. The dcLNs were cleared and counter-stained with DAPI over a 5 day protocol (see the dcLN clearance section) and imaged with the Z compensation function with the Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope at 10× magnification by an experimenter blinded to the groups.

dCLN clearance

The dCLN clearance protocol was performed as previously published.19,37 In brief, reagent 1 was composed of 80% Quadrol (Sigma-Aldrich, 122262) which made up 25% of the final concentration, urea (Sigma-Aldrich, U5128) which was another 25%, and 15% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich, X100) in d2O. Reagent 2 was made with sucrose (Sigma-Aldrich, S0389) which made up 50% of the final concentration, 25% of urea, and 10% triethanolamine (Sigma-Aldrich, 90279) in dH2O. Nodes were incubated in 50% concentrated reagent 1 (prepared 1:1 with dH2O) for 1 day at 37°C, shaking, and with DAPI counterstain (1:1000). Nodes were then transferred to 100% reagent 1 for 1 day at 37°C, shaking, with DAPI (1:1000). Nodes were washed two times in PBS +0.01% sodium azide for 2 h and overnight with DAPI (1:1000) at 37°C. Then nodes were incubated with 50% reagent 2 (prepared 1:1 with PBS) for 1 day at 37°C with DAPI (1:1000). Finally, nodes were incubated with reagent 2 for at least 1 day at 37°C without DAPI. Nodes were stored in reagent 2 for a 1–2 days at 37°C until imaged (if removed from reagent 2, the nodes might revert to being opaque). Next, they were placed in eight well chambers (155411, Thermo Fisher) covered with mineral oil (Sigma-Aldrich, 330779) and imaged straight away with confocal microscopy.

Confocal image analysis

Tau burden quantifications

Areas covered by the fluorescent antibodies AT8 and MC1 were quantified on thresholded images. ROIs were drawn around the whole hippocampus and the piriform cortex respectively using ImageJ (v2.14.0/1.54f). To quantify the tau burden per mouse, we stained 2 to 3 comparable brain sections per animal and then plotted the per mouse average. These sections were roughly 300 μm apart and obtained between the bregma coordinates − 1.4 mm to − 3.1 mm.

Nissl staining

Five straight lines were drawn on the top layer and three on the bottom layer of the dentate gyrus (DG) using the ImageJ. Then, these eight measurements were averaged to acquire the mean DG diameter per mouse. We took 2 to 3 sections 300 μm apart starting at bregma coordinates − 1.4 mm to − 3.1 mm and plotted averaged values per mouse.

Quantification of CNS macrophage activation

The area covered by IBA1, P2RY12, and MHC class II stain was quantified using thresholded images in ImageJ. We stained 2 to 3 comparable brain sections per animal and plotted the average value. These sections were roughly 300 μm apart and obtained between the bregma coordinates − 1.4 mm to − 3.1 mm. For the CNS macrophage morphology, we leveraged Imaris Software (v9.9.1) filament function to render 3D models. Briefly, we used the following protocol: autopath (no loops), used the ROI tool to quantify 4 macrophages near the center of each image, starting points 12 μm, seed points 0.550 μm with an automatic threshold, disconnected segments threshold 22.0 μm and a gap length of 10 μm, dendrite threshold (local contrast) 2.45 μm, and the algorithm is shortest distance from map. Researchers performed this analysis blinded to the treatment groups and individual macrophages were randomly chosen for each field of view (FOV). For the IBA1 cell total filament process length analysis, a total of 12 macrophages per mouse (4 macrophages per FOV times 3 sections per mouse) were averaged to get the per mouse value.

Quantitative analysis of synaptophysin index

Area covered by anti-synaptophysin staining within each ROI drawn was quantified on a thresholded image using ImageJ. Synaptophysin index was calculated as the percent area Syn+ staining normalized to the percent area of DAPI+ nuclei in the CA1 region of the hippocampus as a proxy for neuron counts. We took 2 to 3 sections 300 μm apart starting at bregma coordinates − 1.4 mm to − 3.1 mm and averaged values per mouse were plotted.

Quantitative analysis of lymphatic vessel growth

Using ImageJ, the area coverage for each mouse was calculated at the confluence of sinuses: (area of LYVE-1 signal/FOV area) x 100.

Quantitative analysis of bead drainage

Using ImageJ, we created 3D projections and analyzed them using the 3D object counter function with a fixed threshold for both beads and for DAPI to normalize the volume of beads to the size of the dcLNs. Each mouse had their 2 dcLNs averaged to get a single averaged value per mouse.

Single-nucleus RNA-sequencing (snRNA-seq)

Male 9-month-old PS19 mice were euthanized with CO2 and perfused with cold 1X PBS. Brains were harvested and their right hippocampus (ipsilateral) and the surrounding cortical tissue was microdissected with a razor. The tissue was then flash frozen in dry ice and stored at − 80C. Samples were sent overnight on dry ice to Azenta Life Sciences to handle the sequencing process.

Five single hemisphere hippocampi plus their surrounding cortical tissue were pooled to increase number of nuclei sequenced per group. Nuclei extraction was performed using the Miltenyi Nuclei Extraction Buffer (Miltenyi Biotec, Auburn, CA, USA) following manufacturer’s guidelines with gentle MACS Dissociation and C tubes. Upon isolation, the nuclei were counted using AO/PI dye on the Nexcelom Cellaca MX. Single nuclei RNA libraries were generated using the Chromium Single Cell 3′ kit (10X Genomics, CA, USA). Loading onto the Chromium X was performed to target capture of ~6,000 GEMs per sample for downstream analysis and processed through the Chromium X following the standard manufacturer’s specifications. The sequencing libraries were evaluated for quality on the Agilent TapeStation (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA), and quantified using Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA). Libraries were quantified using qPCR (Applied Biosystems, Carlsbad, CA, USA) prior to loading onto an Illumina HiSeq (4000 or equivalent) instrument. The samples were sequenced at a configuration compatible with the recommended guidelines as outlined by 10X Genomics.

Fastq files were processed with Cell Ranger 7.0.1 using the Cell Ranger ‘count’ pipeline to align reads to the mm10 Mus musculus reference genome. This was performed with 80 GB of memory and 16 processing cores, with sequencing reads truncated to 28 bp (read 1) and 98 bp (read 2) according to the 3′ chemistry protocol used during library preparation. The output cell by gene matrix files from Cell Ranger were imported into a Python (v3.9.18) environment using the Scanpy38 (v1.9.8) framework for further analysis. Quality control (QC) filtering was applied to the four pooled samples to remove low-quality nuclei. Nuclei with fewer than 1,000 total counts or fewer than 500 genes were excluded. Cells with total counts above the 95th percentile or detected gene counts above the 97th percentile were also filtered out. Additionally, cells with mitochondrial counts greater than 1% or ribosomal content above the 99th percentile were removed. Doublet removal was performed using Scrublet39 (v0.2.3), and SOLO (scVI-tools40). A median-based library size normalization and log1p-transformation was applied to the gene expression matrix in Scanpy for downstream analysis, apart from integration with scVI-tools40 (v1.1.2) of the four pooled samples, which utilized the raw counts layer as input for both the full dataset and neuronal recluster. Highly variable genes (HVGs) were identified using the Seurat v3 methodology in Scanpy, and the top 3,000 HVGs were used for unsupervised clustering with the Leiden algorithm within the scVI latent space.

Cells belonging to each Leiden cluster were annotated based on marker gene expression (Wilcoxon rank-sum test) to identify broad population identities (e.g., Excitatory vs. Inhibitory neuron), and initial differential expression (DE) across treatment groups was performed via Wald test, with size factor normalization at the cell type level using a model formula of ~1 + group. After initial processing, cell fractions were split by lineage and a neuronal subset was processed for further downstream analysis. Ambient RNA contamination was corrected using SoupX (v1.6.2). Neuronal subtypes (e.g., CA1-ProS Glut, etc.) were identified and labeled using a standardized taxonomy of whole mouse brain from MapMyCells.34 Neuronal subtypes were anatomically assigned as derived from hippocampal vs. possible border/input cells from the sample prep based on label mappings, using majority voting of per-cell mapped annotations within each cluster. Differential expression testing (Wilcoxon rank-sum) of neuronal subtypes was performed and significant genes were used as input for overrepresentation analysis of GO Biological Processes using EnrichR. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were reported based on a log2FC magnitude cutoff of 0.25 and adjusted p value (FDR) cutoff of 0.05.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Statistics

All data acquisition and analyses were conducted in a blinded fashion. Statistical analyses of the confocal imaging studies involving multiple brain slices per mouse were performed in RStudio (2024.09.1 + 394). Generalized linear mixed-effects models (GLMMs) with a log link and negative binomial error distribution (DHARMa package) were used to account for overdispersion. Fixed effects included TBI and AAV treatment and their interaction, while random effects accounted for mouse ID, sex, and slice number. The p values were obtained from R based on the raw data. two-way ANOVA was used for our analysis of LYVE-1 area coverage, bead drainage, MRI image quantification, and MWM behavior data. Graphs were generated using Prism software (v10.2.3) and data was plotted as mean values ±SEM with each data point representing an individual mouse. The statistical significance test used for each experiment is defined in the Figure legends, and p values are indicated p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), p < 0.001 (***), and p < 0.0001 (****).
