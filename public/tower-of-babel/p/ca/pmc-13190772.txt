Radiolucent lines after robotic-assisted surface-matching kinematically aligned UKA: short-term incidence and lack of association with tibial alignment
Authors: Junya Itou, Ayano Kuwasawa, Kotaro Nihei, Ken Okazaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study investigated the clinical outcomes of robotic-assisted unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA) performed using a surface-matching kinematic technique that restores native joint morphology without alignment constraints. The primary aim was to determine the incidence and distribution of radiolucent lines (RLLs), and the secondary aim was to evaluate short-term revision rates and clinical outcomes.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study reviewed 242 medial UKAs performed between November 2021 and August 2023 by the same high‑volume surgeon using a surface‑matching technique. Radiographic parameters and patient‑reported outcome measures (PROMs) obtained before and 1 year after surgery were assessed, with radiographic evaluation performed independently of the clinical outcome assessment. A cemented JOURNEY II Uni implant was used in all cases, with intraoperative surface mapping used to guide kinematic placement of components without alignment constraints.ResultsRLLs were seen in 21.9% of knees, exclusively on the tibial side and mainly in the most medial (zone 1: 14.8%, 95% confidence interval [CI] 10.9–19.9) and posterior regions (zone 9: 15.3%, 95% CI 11.3–20.3). A larger tibial component was the only independent predictor of RLLs (odds ratio 1.54, 95% CI 1.27–1.88; p = 0.04). All PROMs improved, with no differences between knees with and without RLLs. No revisions, infections, or major complications occurred during the short‑term (1‑year) follow‑up.ConclusionsIn kinematically aligned UKA performed with a surface‑matching technique without restricting tibial varus or posterior slope, RLLs appeared in 21.9% of cases and were concentrated medially on the anteroposterior view and posteriorly on the lateral view. Tibial component size was the only independent risk factor. Short‑term clinical outcomes improved similarly regardless of RLL presence.Level of evidenceLevel IV.

Introduction

One of the goals of knee arthroplasty is to preserve constitutional limb alignment, and kinematically aligned total knee arthroplasty (TKA) has been advocated as one approach to achieve this [1, 2]. Studies using the Coronal Plane Alignment of the Knee (CPAK) classification have demonstrated how constitutional alignment changes after surgery [3–5], reinforcing the importance of individualized alignment for optimizing TKA outcomes [6, 7]. Unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA), however, is inherently a surface‑replacement procedure [8]. Consistent with this concept, kinematically aligned UKA aims to position components to match the native joint morphology rather than the mechanical axis [9–12].

Robotic‑assisted UKA has been shown to yield superior functional outcomes compared with conventional manual techniques [13–15], and several robotic platforms are now available [16, 17]. The CORI™ฏ surgical system (Smith & Nephew, Pittsburgh, PA, USA) is an image‑free robotic platform that enables intraoperative mapping of both bone and cartilage surfaces [18]. This capability allows true surface‑matching implantation, in which components are positioned to reproduce the three‑dimensional native joint contour. Unlike conventional kinematic alignment, which often relies on two‑dimensional restoration and does not require robotic assistance, the surface‑matching technique leverages robotic precision to restore the original articular morphology without imposing constraints on tibial varus, posterior slope, or femoral component orientation. This represents a distinct approach to UKA implantation [18].

There has been ongoing debate regarding the optimal placement of tibial components in UKA. Historically, tibial implants were positioned perpendicular to the tibial axis, and implant designs did not replicate native tibial plateau morphology [19]. More recent reports have suggested that slight varus alignment may better restore natural knee kinematics, preserve the joint line, and improve clinical outcomes [20, 21]. However, these studies also acknowledged the limitations of conventional instrumentation in achieving precise kinematic alignment and appropriate posterior slope, contributing to growing interest in robotic‑assisted UKA. Despite these developments, the risk of implant loosening associated with excessive varus or posterior slope cannot be dismissed.

Importantly, no previous study has specifically examined implant positioning accuracy or the behavior of radiolucent lines (RLLs) in robotic‑assisted UKA performed with a surface‑matching technique. Consequently, the radiographic characteristics and clinical relevance of RLLs in this setting remain unclear. This is noteworthy because surface‑matching implantation differs in several respects from both mechanical‑axis–based and conventional kinematic alignment approaches. The present study addresses this gap by describing RLL patterns and short‑term clinical outcomes in robotic‑assisted UKA using a surface‑matching technique.

The primary objective was to investigate the incidence of RLLs, and the secondary objective was to determine the short‑term revision rate and clinical outcomes associated with this technique. We explored the possibility that RLLs might be more frequently observed on the most medial aspect of the tibia due to varus placement, and on the most posterior aspect due to the posterior slope. Based on previous reports [22], RLLs on the femoral side were considered less likely.

Materials and methods

Study subjects

This retrospective cohort analysis included a consecutive series of patients who underwent primary medial UKA for isolated medial femorotibial osteoarthritis or osteonecrosis [10] at our institution between November 2021 and August 2023. All procedures were performed by the same high-volume knee surgeon (performing more than 50 UKAs annually) using the surface-matching technique. A lower limb coronal plane deformity exceeding 20° of varus, flexion of less than 90°, flexion contracture of greater than 20°, and inflammatory arthritis were contraindications for UKA. An additional exclusion criterion was cases with missing radiographic or clinical data. There were no restrictions based on age, body mass index (BMI, calculated as kg/m2), or degree of physical activity. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (approval number 16-03-02, February 3, 2016). All study participants provided written informed consent for their clinical data to be used for research purposes.

Outcome measures and collection time points

The attending surgeon instructed patients to complete the Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS) [23, 24] questionnaire before surgery and at the 1-year postoperative follow-up for evaluation of patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs). The completed KOOS questionnaires were collected by a staff member who was not involved in the study and entered into a designated database. The minimal clinically important differences in the KOOS subscale scores following knee arthroplasty are as follows: pain, 18; symptoms, 7; activities of daily living, 16; quality of life, 17 [25].

The radiological evaluation using weight‑bearing radiographs consisted of measurement of angles around the knee joint and the presence of RLLs. Specifically, plain radiographs obtained immediately after surgery and at 1 year postoperatively were used to evaluate RLL. The lateral distal femoral angle (LDFA) was assessed on the anteroposterior (AP) view and the posterior condylar offset ratio on the lateral view to evaluate placement of the femoral and tibial components using the surface-matching technique [26]. Similarly, the medial proximal tibial angle (MPTA) was evaluated on the AP view and the medial posterior tibial slope (PTS) on the lateral view [27]. On the AP and lateral views of the tibia, the implant was divided into nine general zones for evaluation. Similarly, on the lateral view of the femur, the implant was divided into eight zones. Each zone was assessed for the presence of RLLs [28, 29] (Fig. 1), defined as radiolucent lines that are more than 2 mm and cover at least one zone [30]. A radiolucent line greater than 2 mm was more likely to progress over time and may serve as a predictor of aseptic loosening [31]. All radiological parameters and RLLs were evaluated by an orthopedic surgeon specializing in knee surgery (A.K). RLLs were recorded simply as present or absent in each zone. Alignment‑related parameters were treated as continuous variables.

Surgical procedures

All patients received the same implant, namely, the cemented JOURNEY II Uni (Smith & Nephew, Memphis, TN, USA). Our institution has consistently performed robot‑assisted UKA since 2019. Sub-vastus techniques were used in all cases. The cruciate ligaments and all three articular compartments were inspected carefully to ensure compatibility with UKA. The mechanical axis, range of motion, and laxity of the knee were evaluated following fixation of optical tracking arrays. A virtual model of the knee was then created by “painting” the articular surfaces using an optical probe to map the anatomy of the femoral condyle and tibial plateau. The surgeon used the same program to plan the intraoperative alignment, position, component size, and volume of bone resection required (Fig. 2). Although the robot suggested implant sizes, the final decision was made by the surgeon based on preoperative planning. In terms of placement of the femoral and tibial components, the implant was placed to match the individual joint surface morphology without angular constraints. Even in knees with advanced osteoarthritis, placement should consider the native contour, taking into account cartilage loss. The articular surface was elevated by a few millimeters according to the degree of cartilage wear (Fig. 3). Specifically, for both the femoral and tibial articular surfaces, a cartilage thickness of 2 mm was assumed in cases where the cartilage was completely worn or missing, and the traced articular contour was adjusted by shifting it 2 mm. Next, the height of the joint surface was adjusted to account for the gap. The handheld sculpting tool was then used to remove the bone and cartilage affected by arthritis [14]. Pulsed lavage was used to prepare the bone surface in all cases immediately before cementation [32]. Debris was also removed using pressurized CO₂ lavage (CarboJet; Kinamed, Camarillo, CA, USA) [33, 34]. High-viscosity bone cement (Biomet Bone Cement R; Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN, USA), was used in all cases [35]. Cementation was completed after confirming joint balance and removing the excess cement (Figs. 3 and 4).

All patients were allowed full weight-bearing ambulation from the first postoperative day onwards, with no restrictions on range of motion during their rehabilitation.

Statistical analysis

The data are presented as the median (range), number (percentage), or mean ± standard deviation as appropriate. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess whether the data were normally distributed. Differences were evaluated using the chi-squared test for categorical variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables. Explanatory variables were selected based on the published literature, with consideration given to the incidence of RLL to avoid overfitting, and only clinically important variables were included while taking the event count into account. Implant size was analyzed as an ordinal variable. Additionally, alignment‑related parameters were treated as continuous variables.

To assess the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), tibial RLL measurements were performed twice by a single orthopedic surgeon (A.K), with an interval of at least one month between assessments. In addition, interobserver reproducibility for the RLLs was examined based on independent evaluations conducted by two observers. The ICC was rated as poor (< 0.5), moderate (0.5–0.75), good (0.75–0.9) or excellent (> 0.9). All statistical analyses were performed using JMP version 18.2.2 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Incidence of RLL

A total of 270 knees (in 204 patients) met the study inclusion criteria. Twenty-eight knees were excluded from the analysis because of loss to follow-up during the first postoperative year, leaving data for 242 knees for analysis (Fig. 5). The demographic data are presented in Table 1. The mean patient age was 72.2 ± 7.7 years, and the mean BMI was 25.7 ± 3.2; 182 knees (75.2%) were in women, and 60 (24.8%) were in men.

At least one RLL was observed on the tibial side in 53 knees (21.9%), with none detected on the femoral side. Given the timing of postoperative imaging assessments in this study, it remained unclear whether the RLL represented a progressive or a stable finding. Details regarding the location of the RLLs are shown in Table 2. Of the 4356 tibial zones (18 in each of the 242 knees) examined on AP and lateral radiographs, RLLs were detected in 154 (3.5%) during the first year following UKA (Fig. 6). Multiple RLLs were observed in 40 knees (14.8%), most of which were in the medial (zone 1) and posterior (zone 9) regions. Of these, the mean number of affected zones per knee according to RLL was 2.91. The heatmap showed focal accumulation of radiolucent lines in the medial region (AP zone 1) and the posterior region (lateral zone 9), with low frequencies in the remaining zones (Fig. 7).

The ICC for intra‑observer agreement in the assessment of RLLs was 0.92, indicating excellent reliability. Similarly, the ICC for inter-observer agreement in the assessment of RLLs was 0.81, indicating good reliability.

Risk factors for RLLs on the tibial side

Logistic regression analysis that included age, weight, postoperative MPTA, postoperative PTS angle, and tibial implant size (ordinal variables) identified a larger tibial implant to be independently associated with the incidence of RLLs (p = 0.04), with an odds ratio of 1.54 (95% CI = 1.27–1.88) (Table 3).

The receiver operating characteristic analysis showed an area under the curve of 0.74, and all variance inflation factors were below 5, indicating acceptable discrimination and no evidence of problematic multicollinearity.

Clinical outcomes

All PROMs improved significantly after surgery (Table 4). The change in each subscale of the KOOS exceeded the minimal clinically important difference. There was no significant difference in the results of the KOOS according to the presence or absence of RLLs (Table 5).

Complications

No additional surgeries, including revision TKA, were required during the study period. There were no superficial or deep infections, no pin‑site fractures, and no minor complications were observed.

Discussion

The most important finding of this study was that postoperative tibial varus and posterior slope were not identified as short‑term risk factors for incidental tibial RLLs, even when components were placed using the surface‑matching technique without restricting alignment. A larger tibial implant was the only factor associated with RLLs. This study is the first to describe clinical outcomes of UKA performed with the surface‑matching method and demonstrates the potential utility of robotic‑assisted UKA using the CORI system. Although RLLs developed in 21.9% of cases during the first postoperative year, no short‑term revisions were required, and PROMs did not differ according to the presence or absence of RLLs.

Use of a larger tibial implant could potentially be associated with an increased risk of RLL. Although several mechanisms may contribute, these explanations remain speculative. Accurate registration of the posterior tibia is inherently difficult when using the CORI image‑free robotic system, and preservation of the anterior cruciate ligament limits exposure of the medial tibial plateau [33], which may contribute to slight postoperative changes in PTS. Inadequate posterior tibial resection could therefore create an uneven bone surface that predisposes to RLL formation. With the JOURNEY II Uni system, each increment in implant size adds 2 mm in the AP dimension and 1 mm mediolaterally, meaning that larger sizes require more extensive bone resection, which may increase the likelihood of minor irregularities at the bone–implant interface. Conversely, smaller tibial components may make RLLs more difficult to detect on plain radiographs. In addition, proximal tibial bone density is high in varus medial osteoarthritis [32], and sclerosis may cause the CORI burr to be subtly repelled, potentially leaving a microscopically plasticized surface. These hypotheses warrant further investigation in studies specifically designed to evaluate the biomechanical implications of implant size.

In this study, RLLs were mainly observed along the medial and posterior tibial edges, a distribution consistent with the rim effect [36]. Sawatari et al. [36] demonstrated in three-dimensional models that tibial varus and posterior tilt concentrate stress at the baseplate margins. Considering that the surface‑matching technique does not limit tibial varus or the PTS, stress may have focused on these regions, explaining the RLL pattern. As reported elsewhere [37], RLLs after UKA are typically confined to the tibial side, and indeed no femoral RLLs were identified in this study. However, there are conflicting reports regarding tibial RLLs and varus or PTS, with Barbadoro et al. stating that excessive varus (exceeding 5°) should be avoided [38].

Polt et al. found no association between PTS and RLLs in knees that had undergone cemented medial UKA [39]. Although RLL and implant loosening are not synonymous, an analysis of 337 fixed-bearing medial UKA knees reported by Hung et al. found no association between implant loosening and PTS [27]. In contrast, there is some evidence indicating that an increase in PTS is associated with a higher incidence of RLLs [40]. Hernigou and Deschamps retrospectively reviewed 99 UKAs with an average follow-up of 16 years and found that a PTS of > 7° was associated with an increased risk of loosening [41]. Perhaps the issue is not the absolute PTS angle but rather the magnitude of its change. Chatellard et al. reported that a postoperative increase in PTS of more than 2° was associated with shortened survival of the prosthesis after UKA [42]. Even within the framework of kinematic alignment, preserving varus and PTS has been regarded as important. The findings of the present study demonstrate that neither varus alignment nor PTS contributes to the development of RLLs when the surface-matching technique is used. However, there is a need for accumulation of further evidence.

The clinical interpretation of RLLs remains controversial, particularly with respect to whether they indicate adaptive remodeling or early loosening [43]. A thin, well‑defined RLL measuring approximately 1–2 mm that appears early after surgery and remains non‑progressive is generally considered a “physiological radiolucency,” reflecting adaptive bone remodeling. In contrast, an ill‑defined RLL exceeding 2 mm, especially when progressive or accompanied by component subsidence or tilting, is classified as a “pathological RLL” and is indicative of true aseptic loosening [30]. Thus, the distinction between adaptive remodeling and early loosening depends not on the presence of an RLL alone, but on its evolution over time and the accompanying clinical presentation. However, the 2‑mm value is not an absolute threshold; it represents only one component of a broader clinical evaluation. In the current study, a radiolucent line greater than 2 mm was used as a threshold to aid detection rather than to diagnose aseptic loosening. Although an RLL exceeding 2 mm does not in itself define loosening, previous studies have consistently shown that lucencies of this magnitude are more likely to represent distinct or potentially progressive changes at the bone–implant interface, whereas thinner lines are typically non‑progressive and clinically insignificant. Using a > 2 mm threshold therefore provides a robust and literature‑supported criterion for identifying radiographic changes that may warrant closer observation, without equating them with mechanical failure. In addition, this study included only a one‑year short‑term follow‑up and lacked longitudinal assessment; therefore, it remains necessary to determine whether the RLL is progressive and whether any component changes occur over time.

This study has several strengths. First, it investigated a consecutive series of UKA procedures performed by a single experienced surgeon. Second, it evaluated clinical outcomes of UKA performed using the surface‑matching technique, which aims to reproduce the native joint surface and theoretically minimizes strain on the preserved knee ligaments. Migliorini et al. reported that changes in the femoral valgus angle, LDFA, and MPTA increase the likelihood of needing revision TKA after UKA [44]. In this context, our findings suggest that the surface‑matching technique may offer advantages in restoring native morphology, although definitive conclusions regarding its superiority cannot be drawn. Zhao et al. reported that fixed‑bearing medial UKA can restore native coronal alignment and yield satisfactory PROMs [45]. Although the surface‑matching technique inherently preserves lower limb alignment, previous studies have also shown that residual varus alignment after UKA does not adversely affect PROMs [46].

Some limitations of this study must also be acknowledged. First, the observation period was only one year, and the long-term results are unknown. The short follow‑up period also represents a major limitation, as RLLs typically require longer-term monitoring to evaluate their onset, progression, and clinical significance. In particular, RLLs have been reported to appear later on the femoral side than on the tibial side [30], so the possibility of development of RLLs on the femoral side in the future cannot be excluded. In addition, the absence of revision does not necessarily guarantee long‑term implant stability. Second, there was no control group of knees that underwent UKA used techniques other than surface-matching. Although a formal a priori power analysis was not conducted, the sample size of 242 knees (53 with RLLs) was judged sufficient for the multivariable analyses performed. Although 47% of patients had bilateral involvement, the primary outcome (presence of RLLs) was assessed independently for each knee; therefore, any cluster effect from bilateral cases is expected to be minimal. Third, most of the study participants were East Asian, so an influence of ethnicity on outcomes cannot be excluded. East Asians generally have more pronounced tibial varus and a high prevalence of CPAK type I [47]. Given the rarity of neutral alignment in medial compartment osteoarthritis in this ethnic cohort, caution is advised when extrapolating these findings to other populations. Fourth, this study did not include CT‑based or radiostereometric assessment of component fixation, and therefore subtle changes in implant migration or fixation quality may not have been fully captured. Furthermore, implant overhang was not assessed in this study. Because overhang can influence stress distribution at the bone–implant interface, this remains an important factor that warrants further investigation. Fifth, it is undeniable that the cementation technique may have influenced the results. In this study, pulsed lavage followed by pressurized CO₂ lavage was used to remove debris, and high‑viscosity bone cement was applied. These factors should be considered as potential influences on the outcomes. Sixth, joint gaps were not fully evaluated. Gaps of 2–3-mm were created during some of the surgeries and may have affected the clinical outcomes. Seventh, this study also has statistical limitations, including its retrospective design and the absence of adjustment for clustering. Finally, because all procedures were performed by a single high‑volume surgeon, the findings should be interpreted with caution when considering broader generalizability.

Conclusion

In this study of short‑term clinical outcomes at a single center of kinematically aligned UKA performed with the surface‑matching technique without restriction of tibial varus or PTS, RLLs were observed in 21.9% of cases. These RLLs were observed predominantly along the medial edge on the AP view and along the posterior edge on the lateral view. No statistically significant association between postoperative varus or PTS and the development of RLLs was detected in this short‑term cohort, whereas a larger implant was a significant risk factor. Because the findings are limited to one‑year follow‑up, they should not be interpreted as validation of unrestricted alignment, and longer‑term outcomes remain uncertain and require continued monitoring.
