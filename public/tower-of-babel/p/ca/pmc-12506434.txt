Size-dependent ultrasound enhancement of nanoparticle endocytosis in tumor cells☆
Authors: Zhihuan Liao, Zihao Wen, Junliang Chen, Jingya Gu, Huan-Zhong Su, Xiao-Dong Zhang, Shuaidong Huo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Efficient cellular endocytosis is crucial for the therapeutic success of nanodrug delivery. Although ultrasound (US)-mediated sonoporation has been widely exploited to enhance drug delivery efficiency, the interplay between US exposure and nanoparticle endocytosis remains poorly understood. In this work, we systematically investigate the tumor endocytosis patterns and underlying mechanisms of multi-sized silica nanoparticles (SiO2 NPs) US stimulation. Our experimental results demonstrate that US exposure can significantly enhance the cellular uptake of SiO2 NPs within the 20–80 nm range in cancer cells. Notably, US exposure led to a statistically significant increase in the internalization of 40 nm nanoparticles, resulting in a 3.8-fold increase in cellular uptake efficiency. Mechanistic studies further demonstrated that US-induced sonoporation promoted energy-independent cellular internalization of 20 nm particles. US exposure not only counteracted the inhibitory effects of caveolae-mediated, clathrin-mediated, and phagocytic endocytosis blockers but also synergistically amplified nanoparticle uptake via multiple endocytic pathways. These findings elucidate the critical role of nanoparticle size in modulating US-enhanced endocytosis, providing fundamental guidance for engineering stimulus-responsive nanocarriers optimized for US-actuated drug delivery systems. These advancements pave the way for more effective and precise nanomedicine strategies, holding significant implications for clinical translation.

Introduction

Efficient cellular endocytosis is pivotal for the effective delivery of nanodrugs and the subsequent realization of therapeutic efficacy[1]. Over the years, researchers have dedicated significant effort to the rational design and rigorous evaluation of drug carriers, focusing on optimizing targeting specificity, physicochemical properties, and cellular internalization capacity to enhance overall drug delivery efficiency[[2], [3], [4]]. The intrinsic characteristics of nanoparticles, including size, surface ligands, surface charge, and topographical features, exert a profound influence on endocytosis efficiency[[5], [6], [7], [8]]. Among these parameters, nanoparticle size emerges as a critical determinant, governing biodistribution patterns and influencing cellular processing kinetics[[9], [10], [11]]. However, conventional passive delivery strategies, which rely solely on the inherent properties of nanoparticles, often encounter limitations in overcoming sequential biological barriers, ultimately compromising the bioavailability of therapeutic payloads[12,13]. This limitation has sparked considerable interest in the development of stimuli-responsive nanoplatforms that can actively modulate endocytic pathways, representing a paradigm shift toward precision-controlled intracellular delivery, which is essential for the clinical translation of nanomedicine innovations[14,15].

Emerging strategies that employ exogenous physical stimuli, including optical, ultrasound, magnetic, and electric modalities, have garnered attention for their potential to enhance therapeutic outcomes by modulating cellular internalization mechanisms [[16], [17], [18], [19], [20]]. Within this context, ultrasound (US) stands out due to its non-invasive nature, capacity for deep tissue penetration, and established clinical utility. US enables precise, localized drug release, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy while minimizing off-target effects [21]. Its versatility facilitates the design of responsive drug carriers, such as microbubbles, liposomes, and nanoparticles, capable of encapsulating a diverse array of therapeutic agents—including small molecules, proteins, nucleic acids, and combination therapies—thus supporting the principles of personalized precision medicine[[22], [23], [24], [25]]. These carriers are engineered to undergo structural modifications under US exposure, triggered by mechanical or thermal effects, leading to the release or activation of their therapeutic payloads.

The cell membrane, a primary target of US, undergoes morphological and functional alterations in response to mechanical effects, such as microstreaming and shear forces[26,27]. These forces can disrupt lipid and protein structures, increase membrane fluidity, and induce transient pores via a process termed sonoporation, thereby facilitating drug uptake[28]. US also influences membrane potential by modulating ion channel activity and affecting the cytoskeleton, potentially reducing drug efflux by interfering with active transport mechanisms, such as ion pumps[29]. Furthermore, the incorporation of microbubbles can lower the threshold for US-induced cavitation, further promoting endocytosis and enhancing its efficiency[30,31]. While recent studies have highlighted the potential of sonoporation to improve cellular uptake, the size-dependent effects of US-induced nanoparticle internalization remain underexplored.

To fully capitalize on the advantages of US in stimulating drug release and to optimize nanodrug size for enhanced cellular uptake, our study systematically investigates the endocytosis patterns and underlying mechanisms of multi-sized silica nanoparticles (SiO2 NPs) by tumor cells under US stimulation. In this work, monodisperse SiO2 NPs of varying sizes (20 nm, 40 nm, 60 nm, 80 nm, and 100 nm) were synthesized as model nanocarriers (Fig. 1). HeLa cervical cancer cells were employed as a tumor cell model, and a controlled ultrasonication experimental setup (1 MHz) was successfully established for US-regulated cell endocytosis studies. Our findings demonstrate that US significantly enhances the internalization of SiO2 NPs within the 20–80 nm range, with the magnitude of enhancement exhibiting clear size dependence. Notably, 40 nm SiO2 NPs exhibited a 3.8-fold increase in cellular uptake efficiency, representing the optimal size within the tested range. US-induced sonoporation facilitated the direct cytoplasmic entry of 20 nm SiO2 NPs via transient membrane permeabilization, further augmenting internalization. Remarkably, US not only mitigated the inhibitory effects of pharmacological inhibitors targeting caveolae-mediated, clathrin-mediated, and macropinocytosis pathways but also amplified nanoparticle uptake across multiple endocytic routes. This study provides a comprehensive understanding of the patterns and mechanisms governing US-enhanced nanoparticle endocytosis by tumor cells. By elucidating the size-dependent ultrasound enhancement of nanoparticles on cellular uptake, our findings address a critical research gap and offer valuable insights for the rational design of effective US-responsive nanodrugs.Fig. 1Schematic illustration of size-dependent ultrasound enhancement of nanoparticle endocytosis in tumor cells. Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of different-sized nanoparticles

To investigate whether ultrasound (US) affects the behavior of cancer cells in uptaking nanoparticles with different sizes, we prepared a series of silica nanoparticles (SiO2 NPs) of varying sizes as research models, which balance between systemic circulation longevity and efficient cellular uptake under ultrasound exposure, and concurrently labeled them with fluorescent Cy5 molecules (SiO2-Cy5 NPs) for subsequent visual observation (Fig. 2a). The morphology of these nanoparticles was initially characterized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). As depicted in Fig. 2b, the five different-sized nanoparticles exhibited a uniform spherical shape, with diameters of approximately 20, 40, 60, 80, and 100 nm, respectively. Notably, SiO2 NPs and SiO2-Cy5 NPs showed highly similar size distributions. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) revealed hydrodynamic diameters of approximately 35, 60, 75, 90, and 120 nm for the five SiO2 NP sizes and their SiO2-Cy5 counterparts (Fig. 2c). These measurements are slightly larger than the TEM-determined sizes, attributable to the expansion of the hydration layer in solution, as TEM imaging is performed in a dry state. The abundant hydroxyl groups on the nanoparticle surfaces induced a negative zeta potential (Fig. 2d), whose absolute value rose proportionally with particle size, indicating a consistent charge density on the surface of each size particle. The nearly identical charges of SiO2 NPs and SiO2-Cy5 NPs indicate that fluorescent labeling did not compromise the nanoparticles’ intrinsic properties. Additionally, fluorescence spectroscopy was used to assess the fluorescence properties of SiO2-Cy5 NPs. As shown in Fig. 2e, the emission spectra of nanoparticles across all sizes displayed similar peaks, with a maximum at approximately 659 nm. US condition optimization for cancer cellular endocytosis of nanoparticles

Before investigating the impact of US on cancer cellular endocytosis, we developed a cellular US experimental setup (Fig. 3a) and identified safe US treatment parameters on cancer cells. The selection of ultrasound frequency necessitates a critical balance between enhancing endocytosis and preserving cellular viability. For this study, we selected 1 MHz as it represents an optimal compromise, providing sufficient mechanical stimulation to boost endocytic activity without causing significant cytotoxicity. To evaluate the safety of high-frequency US (1 MHz) for endocytosis studies, we performed cell viability assays on HeLa cells across a range of treatment durations (0–120 s) and power intensity levels (0.5–2.5 W/cm2). Our results indicated that at 0.5 W/cm2, US exposure up to 60 s had minimal effect on cell viability (Fig. 3b). However, at 1.5 W/cm2, viability significantly decreased, reaching approximately 80 % after 50 s and 20 % after 70 s, suggesting notable toxicity (Fig. 3c). Further increasing the power intensity to 2.5 W/cm2 led to a further decline in viability, to about 12 % after 60 s of sonication (Fig. 3d). These data demonstrate that higher-intensity US and longer exposure times substantially enhance cytotoxicity (Fig. 3e). To rule out potential thermal effects induced by US, we monitored the temperature of HeLa cell cultures in real-time using infrared thermography throughout the sonication process. The results confirmed that exposure to lower intensity US (0.5 and 1.5 W/cm2) resulted in a negligible temperature increase, posing no risk of thermal damage to the cells (Fig. S1). Furthermore, we assessed the long-term cytotoxicity of US by performing colony formation assays. These assays revealed that while lower intensity US exposure did not impair the proliferative capacity of HeLa cells, treatment at a higher intensity (2.5 W/cm2) significantly suppressed their clonogenic potential (Fig. S2). The good biocompatibility of nanoparticles is crucial for their endocytosis research. Our subsequent study revealed that the cytotoxicity of SiO2 NPs on HeLa cells is dependent on both their size and concentration. The results (Fig. S3a) indicate that HeLa cells maintained > 90 % viability after a 3-hour exposure to 0.2 nM SiO2 NPs, irrespective of size. However, at a concentration of 1 nM, larger NPs (80–100 nm) significantly reduced viability to below 50 %, likely due to the increased mass burden of larger particles at equal number concentrations. Notably, 100 nm SiO2 NPs represented a ∼125-fold higher mass load than 20 nm particles at the same particle count. To distinguish size effects from mass-related toxicity and determine the safe treatment concentration of fluorescently labeled nanoparticles, we treated HeLa cells using mass-based dosing with fluorescent SiO2-Cy5 NPs (Fig. S3b). The cell viability remained >80 % at concentrations up to 1 μg/mL, suggesting no toxicity threshold was breached. These findings led us to establish 0.2 nM (number-based) and 1 μg/mL (mass-based) as safe, optimal concentrations for further endocytosis studies of different-sized NPs on HeLa cells with US treatment.

US-enhanced endocytosis of different-sized nanoparticles on cancer cells

To ascertain the influence of US on the uptake of nanoparticles of varying sizes by HeLa cells, we initially employed inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) to quantify the internalization after nanoparticle incubations with or without US irradiation. The uptake efficiency was determined as the percentage of intracellular silicon content relative to the total nanoparticle mass, expressed as a percentage of 100 %. Our findings revealed that US irradiation significantly modulates the uptake efficiency of HeLa cells towards nanoparticles in a size-dependent manner (Fig. 4a). Notably, the 40 nm NPs exhibited a remarkable 3.8-fold increase in uptake efficiency (from 12 % to 58 %) under US exposure. In contrast, the 60 nm NPs showed a 1.2-fold enhancement (from 27 % to 63 %), while the 20 nm and 80 nm NPs demonstrated moderate increases of 0.5-fold and 0.2-fold, respectively. Interestingly, the 100 nm NPs did not exhibit a significant change in internalization efficiency, suggesting a potential upper-size limit for US-mediated endocytic enhancement. Further, we established a quantitative curve fitting of the size-uptake relationship (R2 > 0.999), revealing an unimodal distribution in the US-mediated enhancement of nanoparticle cellular uptake (Fig. 4b). To further elucidate the nanoparticle uptake behavior under US stimulation, we next incubated HeLa cells with various-sized SiO2-Cy5 NPs and utilized laser scanning confocal microscopy (LSCM) to observe their endocytosis. As depicted in Fig. 4c, the HeLa cell nuclei were stained with DAPI (blue), the cell membrane was stained with MemGlow™ (green), and the red color indicated the location of the SiO2-Cy5 NPs. LSCM analysis confirmed the enhanced uptake of 20–80 nm particles under US irradiation, with the 40 nm NPs exhibiting the most pronounced fluorescence intensification (Fig. 4c). Semi-quantitative analysis using ImageJ further corroborated the significant US-mediated uptake increases (Fig. S4). Additionally, flow cytometry was employed to quantify intracellular fluorescence, revealing substantial uptake increases for the 40 nm and 60 nm NPs post-US treatment, with smaller increases observed for other sizes (Fig. 4d). These results are consistent with the ICP-MS analysis and LSCM observations, collectively demonstrating the profound impact of US on cancer cell’s endocytosis of different-sized NPs, particularly the selective enhancement of internalization efficiency for 40 nm and 60 nm NPs (Fig. 4e).

US-mediated nanoparticle endocytosis pathway change of cancer cells

The cellular uptake of nanoparticles is primarily governed by several key endocytic pathways, including caveolin- or/and clathrin-mediated endocytosis, as well as micropinocytosis[32]. To elucidate the impact of US on these endocytic pathways and its potential to enhance nanoparticle uptake, we employed specific inhibitors targeting each pathway for mechanistic studies. We initially employed methyl-β-cyclodextrin (MβCD), a caveolin inhibitor, to selectively block the caveolin-mediated endocytic pathway by binding cholesterol within the phospholipid bilayer. As depicted in Fig. 5a, our findings revealed that nanoparticle uptake was inhibited to varying degrees across all sizes. However, upon sonication, the proportion of 40 nm particles increased from 1.78 % to 31.45 %, while the 60 nm particles also saw an increase from 2.31 % to 8.23 %. For 80 nm nanoparticles, the endocytosis rate rose from 6.64 % to 14.43 %. It is noteworthy that the application of US significantly augmented the cellular uptake of 40 nm particles, with an increase of 16.6 times. Notably, for 20 nm particles, ultrasonic exposure did not result in a significant change in uptake. Hypertonic sucrose (Suc) was employed as a potent inhibitor of clathrin-mediated endocytosis, owing to its ability to prevent clathrin cage depolymerization and function. Co-incubation of cells with nanoparticles and Suc resulted in the inhibition of endocytosis for nanoparticles ranging from 40 to 100 nm (Fig. 5b). However, the application of US significantly enhanced the uptake of nanoparticles of all sizes, with increases of 3, 6.9, 15.2, 12.6, and 11.8 times for nanoparticles ranging from 20 to 100 nm, respectively. Surprisingly, the uptake efficiency of 20–60 nm particles after US enhancement exceeded that observed before the addition of the inhibitor. These results indicate that US treatment not only reverses the inhibitory effects of Suc on clathrin-mediated endocytosis but also opens up alternative pathways for enhanced nanoparticle uptake.

To assess the effects of US on the macropinocytosis pathway, we treated cells with a micropinocytosis inhibitor—Cytochalasin D (CytD). Under CytD treatment, the uptake rates of SiO2 NPs ranging from 20 to 100 nm were 13.8 %, 25.6 %, 34.9 %, 19.7 %, and 17.9 %, respectively, when exposed to US (Fig. 5c). This suggests that US can partially enhance other endocytic pathways, thereby mitigating the effects of CytD on the macropinocytosis-mediated endocytic pathway. To determine whether SiO2 NPs internalization is energy-dependent, we co-incubated nanoparticles with HeLa cells in the presence of NaN3, an oxidative phosphorylation inhibitor that disrupts cellular energy supply. As shown in Fig. 5d, endocytosis of 40–100 nm particles decreased both before and after US exposure. However, 20 nm SiO2 NPs showed less pronounced inhibition, with a slight uptake increase post-US.

The application of US can facilitate the formation of pores in the cell membrane, thereby enabling the unimpeded passage of various ions and even small particles. This US-induced transient enhancement in membrane permeability frequently results in an elevated intracellular concentration of Ca2+. To further investigate this phenomenon, we employed the Fura-2/AM probe to monitor intracellular Ca2+ levels and subsequent morphological alterations following US exposure (Fig. 5e). The LSCM observation revealed a significant increase in Ca2+ influx, as indicated by enhanced green fluorescence, within HeLa cells after a 20 s of US treatment. This observation is attributed to US-induced modifications in the cytoskeleton and membrane structure. Such alterations may account for the enhanced endocytosis of relatively small 20 nm particles observed during sonication, independent of energy-dependent processes.

Experimental data revealed that US modulates the endocytosis of nanoparticles of varying sizes in tumor cells. Systematic analysis, supplemented by heat map analysis of SiO2 NPs uptake inhibition/enhancement rates under various endocytic inhibitors with and without US, demonstrated a size-dependent cellular uptake pattern. Specifically, smaller SiO2 NPs (20–60 nm) exhibited significantly greater US-induced enhancement across all endocytic pathways, whereas larger NPs (e.g., 100 nm) showed diminished enhancement effects (Fig. 5f). Radar plot analysis further confirmed size-dependent regulation of uptake efficiency across three canonical endocytic pathways under US stimulation (Fig. 5g). Although uptake increased for all nanoparticle sizes, 80 nm and 100 nm particles demonstrated lower efficiency (smaller polygonal areas), whereas 40 nm and 60 nm particles showed superior performance (larger areas). Notably, the uptake of 40 nm particles increased from 7.3 % to 31.5 % via caveolin-mediated endocytosis and from 10.9 % to 86.1 % via clathrin-mediated endocytosis, while 60 nm particles rose from 14.1 % to 34.9 % via macropinocytosis. Collectively, these findings indicate that US-enhanced nanoparticle uptake is a multifaceted process involving the synergistic activation of multiple endocytic pathways and sonoporation-mediated nanoparticle influx (Fig. 5h).

Conclusions

In summary, our comprehensive study of US-modulated nanoparticle endocytosis in HeLa cells reveals a size-dependent paradigm governing internalization dynamics. Sonication overrides single-pathway inhibition by activating compensatory routes, enhancing uptake via clathrin-, caveolin-, and macropinocytosis-mediated mechanisms. Optimal performance occurred at 40 nm, with a 3.8-fold uptake increase, while smaller and larger sizes showed reduced responses. Mechanistically, US acoustic forces and cholesterol redistribution create size-selective endocytic hotspots. These results underscore the importance of considering the interactions between nanoparticle size and US stimulation when investigating endocytic mechanisms, providing crucial insights for the design of US-responsive nanocarriers with optimized delivery performance.
