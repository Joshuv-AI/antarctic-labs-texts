Risk factors of abdominal abscess after laparoscopic appendectomy
Authors: Liyang Liu, Wenjun Feng, Kuanyong Yu, Chuanyang Cao, Guanghui Qiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo explore the risk factors of intra-abdominal abscess (IAA) after laparoscopic appendectomy (LA).MethodsA total of 839 patients who underwent LA in Nanjing Jiangbei Hospital from July 2021 to November 2024 were retrospectively analyzed, and their clinical data were collected. Through univariate and multivariate analysis, the risk factors affecting the formation of IAA after operation were screened, and the predictive efficacy was evaluated by ROC curve.ResultsAfter screening, there were 25 cases with IAA after operation, 741 cases were cured and 73 cases were excluded. Multivariate analysis showed that appendix perforation (OR = 4.763, 95%CI: 1.827 ~ 12.414, P = 0.001) and operative time (OR = 1.024, 95%CI: 1.002 ~ 1.046, P = 0.029) were related to the formation of IAA after operation. The area under ROC curve (AUC) of appendix perforation was 0.623, and the operative time was 0.683.ConclusionFor patients with LA, perforation of appendix and longer operative time are independent risk factors for the formation of IAA after operation, which can help predict whether IAA will occur after operation.

Introduction

Acute appendicitis represents one of the most prevalent causes of acute abdominal pain, with an annual incidence ranging from 90 to 100 cases per 100,000 population [1]. While numerous studies have established the efficacy of antibiotic therapy for appendicitis - demonstrating a success rate of 72.7% in uncomplicated cases [2] - and emerging interventions like endoscopic retrograde appendicitis treatment (ERAT) have been introduced, appendectomy remains the gold standard for acute appendicitis management [3]. Current surgical approaches primarily include open appendectomy (OA) and laparoscopic appendectomy (LA). Compared to conventional open surgery, LA offers distinct advantages including reduced surgical trauma and accelerated postoperative recovery. With increasing availability of laparoscopic equipment and advancements in minimally invasive techniques, LA has become the preferred surgical option for many patients with acute appendicitis. Despite the widespread adoption of LA, some technical details—such as optimal management of the appendiceal stump—remain topics of discussion and ongoing refinement [4]. Although laparoscopic appendectomy has been performed for over four decades, certain technical aspects—such as optimal management of the appendiceal stump—remain under discussion, particularly as LA has become the predominant approach in many centers.

Postoperative complications following LA primarily include surgical site infection and intra-abdominal abscess (IAA) formation. While LA demonstrates a significantly lower incidence of incisional infections compared to OA [5], considerable controversy exists regarding IAA rates and associated risk factors across different studies. The literature reveals evolving perspectives on IAA incidence after LA. Early international studies suggested higher IAA rates following LA compared to OA [6], while more recent reports indicate comparable rates between the two approaches [7]. Notably, large-scale Chinese studies have demonstrated lower IAA incidence with LA versus OA [8]. The identification of IAA risk factors remains particularly contentious, with conflicting evidence regarding the roles of complicated appendicitis and peritoneal irrigation. IAA development represents a clinically significant complication, often necessitating prolonged treatment durations and invasive interventions such as percutaneous drainage. These consequences substantially increase healthcare costs and contribute to unplanned readmission rates. Given these clinical implications, elucidating the precise risk factors for postoperative IAA formation is of paramount importance.

Although existing studies, predominantly conducted in Western populations, have identified appendiceal perforation as a potential risk factor for IAA [9], regional variations remain underexplored. This study aims to investigate the incidence of IAA and identify associated risk factors specifically within the patient population of Nanjing Jiangbei Hospital.

Data and methods

General data

The clinical data of 839 patients who underwent laparoscopic appendectomy in Nanjing Jiangbei Hospital from July 2021 to November 2024 were retrospectively analyzed. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Nanjing Jiangbei Hospital (Ethics No.: 2024022). Since de-identified patient data is used, informed consent is not required. All the data were extracted from the electronic medical record system (EMR) of Nanjing Jiangbei Hospital and independently checked by two researchers to ensure the accuracy of the data.

Inclusion criteria: 1). Laparoscopic appendectomy; 2). Postoperative pathology confirmed acute appendicitis; 3). Standardized diagnosis and treatment during hospitalization and detailed clinical data.

Exclusion criteria: 1). Chronic appendicitis, periappendiceal abscess and appendiceal tumor confirmed by postoperative pathology; 2). Other diseases (such as ovarian cyst torsion, Meckel diverticulitis, etc.) were found during the operation; 3). Collateral injury during operation, change of operation mode, and other operations (such as intestinal injury during operation, ileocecal resection, etc.); 4). Complications other than IAA (such as incision infection, lung infection, urinary tract infections, deep vein thrombosis, and pulmonary embolism etc.) identified based on clinical symptoms, laboratory findings, and imaging results as per standard diagnostic criteria.

Diagnostic criteria of IAA

The diagnosis of IAA was based on clinical signs and imaging findings (ultrasound or CT). For imaging, all patients underwent standardized ultrasound or CT scans using the same protocol. The imaging findings were reviewed by radiologists who were blinded to the clinical outcomes of the patients. The criteria for IAA diagnosis included: 1). Abdominal pain and fever that do not conform to the normal prognosis occur within 30 days after LA; 2). Imaging (ultrasound or CT) confirmed hydrops in the operation area.

Diagnosis and treatment process

The patient underwent emergency laparoscopic appendectomy after admission, and all the operations were performed by qualified attending doctors and above. After general anesthesia, routine disinfection was carried out, and pneumoperitoneum was constructed by puncture, with pneumoperitoneum pressure of 12 mmHg and a CO₂ flow rate of 20 L/min. The surgical procedure commenced with placement of a 10 mm trocar at the superior umbilical margin as the observation port, followed by insertion of a 10 mm trocar at the umbilical plane and a 5 mm trocar 5 cm below the umbilicus along the lateral border of the right rectus abdominis as working ports (with adjustments made according to patient anatomy). Systematic exploration of the abdominopelvic cavity was performed to confirm appendicitis diagnosis and exclude other pathologies. The operative steps included aspiration of purulent fluid, elevation of the appendix, dissection of the mesoappendix with ligation of the appendicular artery, and transection of the appendiceal base. The stump was managed using a single endoloop ligature placed at the base, followed by transection with electrocautery. Routine stump inversion was not performed. The specimen was retrieved using an endobag through the 10 mm port for pathological examination. The decision regarding peritoneal irrigation and drain placement was made intraoperatively based on surgical findings and surgeon’s experience. Following instrument removal, port sites were closed. Postoperative management involved empiric antibiotic therapy subsequently adjusted according to culture results, with drain removal when output was < 40mL of clear fluid. Patients were discharged after symptom resolution, diet tolerance, and normalization of blood parameters without significant abnormalities. Covariates: Covariates include age, gender (male and female), time from symptom onset to surgery (≤ 3 days vs. >3 days), peritonitis was assessed based on preoperative CT imaging, with radiological signs such as peritoneal enhancement, free fluid, or inflammatory stranding used to classify patients into ‘yes’ or ‘no’ categories, CT fecal stone (no, yes and not applicable), preoperative fever (no and yes), appendicitis type (non-gangrenous appendicitis and gangrenous appendicitis), perforation (no and yes), antibiotic use was classified as monotherapy (a single antibiotic agent) or combination therapy (two or more antibiotics administered concurrently), discharge with antibiotics (no and yes), operative time (the total time from skin incision to skin closure), preoperative WBC, preoperative N%, postoperative WBC, and postoperative N%.

Follow-up

Postoperative follow-up was conducted within 30 days after surgery. Patients who developed abdominal pain or fever during this period were advised to return for clinical assessment. Comprehensive imaging examinations were performed to evaluate the potential formation of postoperative IAA in accordance with established diagnostic criteria.

Statistical method

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp). Normality of distribution for continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Non-normally distributed data were presented as median with interquartile range [M (P25, P75)], while categorical variables were compared using the χ² test. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were employed to identify independent risk factors for postoperative IAA formation following LA operation. ROC curve analysis was conducted to assess the predictive power of the identified risk factors for IAA. The AUC value was calculated to determine the overall predictive performance of the model. Confidence intervals for the AUC were computed using the bootstrap method. The optimal cutoff value for predicting IAA was determined using the Youden index method. P < 0.05 was considered as a statistical difference.

Results

Screening results

Of the 839 patients, 73 were excluded (chronic appendicitis, periappendiceal abscess, appendix tumor in 42 cases, ileocecal resection in 2 cases, intestinal injury in 1 case, other diseases found in 4 cases during operation, and other complications occurred in 24 cases after operation), 741 cases were included in the cure group, and 25 cases were included in the IAA group. Among the 25 patients who developed postoperative IAA, 21 were successfully treated with antibiotics alone, while only 3 required image-guided percutaneous drainage (either CT- or ultrasound-guided), suggesting that routine diagnostic or therapeutic puncture was not necessary in most cases.

Univariate analysis

In patients with acute appendicitis who underwent LA, univariate analysis revealed no statistically significant differences in age, sex, time from symptom onset to surgery, presence of peritonitis, presence of fecaliths on CT, preoperative fever, gangrenous appendicitis, antibiotic combination therapy, discharge with antibiotics, preoperative white blood cell count (WBC, ×10⁶/µL), or preoperative neutrophil percentage (N%) between the IAA group and the cured group (all P > 0.05). However, significant differences were observed in appendiceal perforation (P < 0.001), operative time (P = 0.002), postoperative WBC (P = 0.004), and postoperative neutrophil percentage (P = 0.013). Multivariate analysis

We assessed potential multicollinearity among the independent variables using variance inflation factors (VIF) in the logistic regression model. The VIF values for postoperative WBC and N% were 12.5 and 11.8, respectively, suggesting high multicollinearity with the perforation status. To address this issue, we included only postoperative WBC in the final model, as it had a slightly higher correlation with the outcome variable.

Variables that demonstrated statistically significant differences in the univariate analysis were incorporated into the multivariate analysis. The results revealed that appendiceal perforation (P = 0.001) and operative time (P = 0.029) were statistically significant predictors (Table 2). These findings indicate that appendiceal perforation and prolonged operative time are independent risk factors for IAA formation.

ROC curve prediction model

ROC curve analysis was conducted to evaluate the predictive power of the identified risk factors for IAA. Using ROC curve to construct the prediction model, the results showed that the AUC of appendix perforation was 0.623 (95%CI: 0.477 ~ 0.712), the optimal cutoff value was 0.52 (sensitivity: 72%, specificity: 54%), the AUC of operative time was 0.683 (95%CI: 0.511 ~ 0.747), and the optimal cutoff value was 60 min (sensitivity: 68%, specificity: 62%). AUC was 0.725 when combined with independent factors to predict the risk of IAA (Hosmer-Lemeshow P = 0.567). See Fig. 1 for details.

Discussion

In this study, 839 patients who underwent LA were analyzed retrospectively, in order to explore the risk factors of postoperative IAA. The results showed that perforation of appendix and prolonged operative time were independent risk factors for postoperative IAA.

Appendectomy remains the gold standard for the management of acute appendicitis. While open appendectomy is constrained by limited surgical visualization, often restricting intervention for abdominal infections to empirical measures (e.g., pelvic swabbing), laparoscopic techniques have transformed the intraoperative assessment and management of infectious foci, leading to a significant reduction in both surgical site infection rates and hospital readmission rates [10]. Recent advancements, such as single-incision laparoscopic surgery (SILS) and NOTES, have further minimized surgical trauma and enhanced patient outcomes, though their technical complexity currently hinders widespread implementation [11].

Notably, the reported incidence of postoperative IAA following LA varies considerably, ranging from 1.26% in large Chinese cohorts [8] to 3.65% in international studies [12]. This variability is likely attributed to differences in IAA diagnostic methodologies—with some studies mandating peritoneal aspiration for confirmation and others relying on imaging findings. In the present study, radiological confirmation was adopted as the primary diagnostic criterion (without requiring mandatory pus aspiration), yielding an IAA incidence of 2.98% (25/839 cases), which aligns with global data. Importantly, 84% (21/25) of IAA cases achieved resolution with antibiotic therapy alone, and only 12% (3/25) required percutaneous drainage, indicating that most cases can be managed non-invasively.

The detection of IAA is critically influenced by follow-up protocols, as evidenced by our finding that 64% (16/25) of IAA cases presented post-discharge. In terms of risk factors, while previous studies have inconsistently linked complicated appendicitis and peritoneal lavage to IAA, our multivariate analysis identified appendiceal perforation and prolonged operative time as independent predictors of postoperative IAA following LA.

Appendiceal perforation directly results in leakage of purulent fluid and appendiceal luminal contents, exacerbating intra-abdominal infection. Although laparoscopy facilitates more effective evacuation of purulent material and peritoneal lavage, microscopic infectious foci may persist within the intestinal wall or concealed anatomical spaces, thereby increasing the risk of postoperative IAA.

Domestic studies have identified complicated appendicitis (encompassing gangrenous, perforated appendicitis, and periappendiceal abscess) as a risk factor for IAA [13], whereas international reports indicate that the incidence of IAA following complicated appendicitis can reach 12.3% [7]. In the present study, gangrenous appendicitis and perforated appendicitis were analyzed separately: gangrenous appendicitis showed no association with postoperative IAA, while appendiceal perforation emerged as a significant risk factor, consistent with prior research [9].

Among the 839 patients enrolled, 70 had appendiceal perforation, of whom 8 developed postoperative IAA (11.4% incidence), and 7 experienced other complications (including septic shock and surgical site infection), resulting in an overall complication rate of 21.4%. Notably, some studies report that the incidence of IAA after laparoscopic appendectomy for perforated appendicitis reaches 24%, with a 5.6-fold higher risk of abscess formation compared to open surgery [14]. The relatively lower IAA rate (11.4%) observed in our perforated cases may be attributed to factors such as stricter patient selection (fewer severe cases), advancements in surgical techniques, and optimized perioperative management.

Prolonged operative time correlates with increased abdominal cavity exposure time, elevating the risk of intra-abdominal infection. Additionally, extended surgery may reflect greater surgical complexity (e.g., severe abdominal adhesions, ectopic appendix), which could independently increase the risk of postoperative infection. Therefore, minimizing unnecessary procedural steps and reducing operative time may help lower the incidence of postoperative IAA.

Controversy remains regarding several intraoperative practices, including peritoneal lavage, drainage placement, and appendiceal stump management. Some studies suggest that extensive peritoneal lavage may promote the spread of intra-abdominal infection [15], while domestic research identifies intraoperative peritoneal lavage as an independent risk factor for IAA after LA [16]. Recent meta-analyses confirm that routine drainage during LA significantly prolongs operative time, delays postoperative recovery, extends hospital stay, and increases surgical site infection rates [17]. These findings align with the guidelines from the World Society of Emergency Surgery, which explicitly recommend against routine postoperative drainage [18], confirming that unwarranted peritoneal irrigation and prophylactic drainage offer no clinical benefit.

Regarding the management of the appendiceal stump, existing literature presents conflicting evidence: some studies indicate comparable postoperative complication rates (including IAA) among purse-string suturing, Hem-o-lok clipping, and double ligation techniques [4], while others suggest that Hem-o-lok application may be associated with an elevated risk of IAA [19]. Based on our findings, we suggest the following clinical considerations: (1) enhanced postoperative surveillance for patients with prolonged operative times, as longer operative time was significantly associated with postoperative IAA; (2) optimization of surgical efficiency to minimize unnecessary intraoperative delays; (3) meticulous intraoperative evacuation of purulent material to reduce residual infectious burden; and (4) cautious use of peritoneal irrigation, given its potential influence on postoperative outcomes. These recommendations are exploratory and should be interpreted in the context of our retrospective design and the need for further prospective validation. These evidence-based strategies may collectively improve clinical outcomes by reducing unplanned reintervention rates, decreasing 30-day readmissions, and alleviating patient healthcare burdens. The use of antibiotics can effectively control the abdominal infection caused by acute appendicitis [20], and the use of antibiotics in perioperative period can effectively reduce the infection of surgical site [21]. Generally speaking, the choice of antibiotics depends on the local microbial population and drug resistance mode. In the treatment of acute appendicitis in our hospital, antibiotics are routinely used, broad-spectrum antibiotics are the first choice, and anti-anaerobic antibiotics are added empirically. However, the combined use of antibiotics has no obvious advantage in preventing postoperative IAA. Some studies think that the effect of intravenous antibiotics after operation is the same as that of oral antibiotics [22], and prolonging the time of antibiotic use may not necessarily reduce the infection rate of surgical site [23, 24]. In this study, 17 of the 25 IAA patients were treated with IAA again after discharge. The analysis of the clinical data of these patients found that whether they were discharged with drugs or not had nothing to do with the occurrence of IAA. In other words, prolonging the antibiotic treatment time by oral antibiotics could not prevent the occurrence of IAA. However, it is important to note that our study was retrospective and non-randomized, which introduces potential biases and limits the strength of this conclusion. The decision to continue antibiotics postoperatively may have been influenced by clinical judgment and patient-specific factors, rather than strictly protocol-driven.

Collectively, these evidence-based strategies hold the potential to enhance clinical outcomes by lowering the rates of unplanned reinterventions, reducing 30-day readmissions, and mitigating the healthcare burdens on patients. Antibiotics can effectively control abdominal infections arising from acute appendicitis [20], and their perioperative administration significantly reduces the incidence of surgical site infections [21]. Generally, the selection of antibiotics is determined by the local microbial flora and patterns of drug resistance. In the management of acute appendicitis at our hospital, antibiotics are used routinely, with broad-spectrum antibiotics being the first-line choice, and anti-anaerobic antibiotics added empirically. Nevertheless, the combined use of antibiotics offers no distinct advantage in preventing postoperative IAA. Some studies have indicated that the efficacy of postoperative intravenous antibiotics is comparable to that of oral antibiotics [22], and prolonging the duration of antibiotic use does not necessarily lead to a reduction in surgical site infection rates [23, 24]. In the present study, 17 out of the 25 patients with IAA developed recurrent IAA after discharge. An analysis of the clinical data of these patients revealed that the occurrence of IAA was unrelated to whether they were discharged with medications. In other words, prolonging antibiotic treatment through oral administration failed to prevent the development of IAA. However, it is crucial to note that this study was retrospective and non-randomized, which introduces potential biases and limits the robustness of this conclusion. The decision to continue postoperative antibiotic therapy may have been influenced by clinical judgment and patient-specific factors, rather than being strictly protocol-driven.

In the present study, notable discrepancies were also observed in the clinical data between patients who developed IAA during hospitalization and those who developed IAA after discharge. Nine patients experienced IAA during hospitalization, with a median onset time of 5 days postoperatively; among these, 66.7% had perforated appendicitis. In contrast, the median onset time of IAA after discharge was 11 days postoperatively, with only 18.8% of these cases involving perforated appendicitis. The authors hypothesize that in-hospital IAA development is predominantly attributed to severe abdominal contamination, where complete surgical eradication of the infectious focus is challenging. Conversely, post-discharge IAA may arise from alternative factors, such as poor patient compliance with postoperative care regimens or non-adherence to antibiotic prescriptions. These findings underscore the need to enhance patient education, improve treatment compliance, and implement individualized follow-up management strategies for discharged patients.​.

The ROC curve analysis of the predictive model revealed that the AUC for appendiceal perforation was 0.623, while that for operative time was 0.683. The combined AUC of these independent factors was 0.725. However, it is important to highlight that these AUC values are relatively modest and close to 0.5, indicating limited clinical predictive utility. Despite statistical significance, these results suggest that appendiceal perforation and operative time alone may not suffice as robust predictors of IAA occurrence. Consequently, these findings should be interpreted cautiously in clinical practice. Future investigations with larger sample sizes and more comprehensive predictive models are warranted to enhance the accuracy of IAA risk prediction.

Innovation and limitations of the study

The innovation of this study is to explore the risk factors of IAA after laparoscopic appendectomy, aiming at the patients in Nanjing Jiangbei Hospital, which fills the gap in related research in this area. The results show that appendiceal perforation and long operative time are independent risk factors for postoperative IAA in our hospital. These findings provide important guiding significance for clinical practice, suggesting that we should strengthen postoperative monitoring of patients with appendiceal perforation and long operative time. Besides, there are still many shortcomings in this study. While we acknowledge the retrospective nature of this study, it is also important to recognize the absence of detailed surgical technical variables, such as the volume of peritoneal lavage, the use of drains, and the type of stump closure. These factors have been reported in the literature as potentially influencing the development of postoperative complications, including IAA. The lack of data on these variables limits our ability to fully assess their impact on IAA formation in our cohort. For instance, the volume of lavage fluid used during surgery could influence residual contamination, while the placement and management of drains may affect postoperative infection rates. Similarly, different methods of stump closure might influence the risk of abscess formation. Our study did not collect data on surgeon experience or the time of surgery (emergency vs. elective hours). These factors have been shown in previous studies to influence surgical outcomes, including the risk of postoperative complications such as intra-abdominal abscesses. Surgeons with more experience may have better outcomes due to refined techniques and decision-making, while surgeries performed during emergency hours might be associated with higher stress and potentially different outcomes compared to those performed during elective hours. Future research should aim to collect and analyze these surgical technical variables to provide a more comprehensive understanding of their role in IAA development. This would help in developing more precise recommendations for surgical practice to minimize postoperative complications.

Conclusion

For patients undergoing LA, our study suggests that appendiceal perforation and longer operative time are independently associated with an increased risk of developing IAA after surgery. These findings highlight the importance of considering these factors when planning and performing the surgery. However, given the observational nature of our study, the results should be interpreted with caution, as they do not establish a causal relationship. Further prospective or randomized controlled trials would be necessary to definitively assess the impact of these factors on IAA development and to develop more precise predictive and preventive strategies.
