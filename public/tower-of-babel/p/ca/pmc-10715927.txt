Congenital Long QT Syndrome, Coinciding With Cavitary Mycobacterium avium Lung Infection, Led to Cardiac Arrest
Authors: Muhammad Ghallab, Nazaakat Ahmed, Zakaria Alagha, Daniel Miller, Nso Nso, Most Sirajum Munira
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractCongenital long QT syndrome is a cardiac disorder leading to arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death. We present a case of a 55-year-old woman with altered mental status experiencing cardiac arrest caused by congenital long QT syndrome, coincidentally found with Mycobacterium avium complex lung infection. Genetic testing identified a pathogenic mutation in the KCNH2 gene. (Level of Difficulty: Intermediate.)Key Words: cardiac arrest, congenital long QT syndrome, MAC infection, Mycobacterium avium complex, QT prolongation, torsades de pointes

Congenital long QT syndrome (LQTS) is a cardiac disorder affecting the repolarization of the ventricles, potentially leading to arrhythmias, syncope, and sudden cardiac death.1 Although its typical onset occurs during childhood or adolescence, there have been rare presentation cases in late adulthood, as observed in this particular instance. Notably, 17 genes have been identified as associated with congenital LQTS syndrome; however, the majority of cases (approximately 80%) are attributed to mutations in the KCNQ1, KCNH2, and SCN5A genes.2 The clinical manifestations of congenital LQTS exhibit significant variability and include LQTS-related syncope or seizures, aborted cardiac arrests, and sudden cardiac death. Nevertheless, it is worth noting that many individuals with congenital LQTS may remain asymptomatic throughout their lifetime.3

History of Presentation

A 55-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with altered mental status after her friend noted that she became unresponsive, with seizure-like activity for 10 minutes. In the emergency department, the patient had a temperature of 98.7 °F, a pulse of 76 beats/min, blood pressure of 105/53 mm Hg, and oxygen saturation of 100%. The physical examination was notable for mild lethargy.

Past Medical History

The patient was recently discharged from the hospital for a history of weight loss, fatigue, acute kidney injury, and urinary tract infection, for which she was prescribed trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole. The patient denied a prior family history or personal history of syncope.

Differential Diagnosis

Congenital LQTS was the first differential vs acquired etiology with an underlying genetic syndrome unmasked by recent antibiotic use of trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole.

Investigations

The patient was found to have a prolonged QTc of 592 milliseconds (Figure 1). A review of the patient’s chart revealed that her electrocardiogram 1 week earlier showed a normal sinus rhythm with a normal QTc of 440 milliseconds (Figure 2). On the second day of admission, 3 hours after receiving the trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole dose, the patient was found unresponsive and pulseless for 30 seconds and recovered with a cardiac thump. The initial rhythm was ventricular fibrillation/torsades de pointes. Initial blood work showed hypomagnesemia of 1.2 mg/dL and hypokalemia of 3.1 mmol/L.

The troponin level was 0.187 ng/mL (normal range: <0.010 ng/mL) and then elevated to 0.191 ng/mL. QTc is documented as up to 667 milliseconds. During the hospital course, the patient was also noted to have intermittent low-grade fevers. A chest x-ray (Figure 3) was done and revealed a right upper lung lobe multifocal cavitary lesion. Chest computed tomography with contrast (Figure 4) was done and revealed a 6.7-cm right upper lobe cavity. Blood cultures showed no bacterial growth. Sputum analysis revealed a positive acid-fast Bacillus result with negative Mycobacterium tuberculosis findings on polymerase chain reaction. The Interferon-γ release assay test result was indeterminate twice, and the HIV test result was negative. Acid-fast Bacillus sputum cultures have been speciated as Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC).

The patient then underwent a transthoracic echocardiogram, which demonstrated a left ventricular ejection fraction of 45%. A left heart catheterization revealed normal coronary arteries. Cardiac magnetic resonance was done and showed no areas of abnormal late gadolinium enhancement.

Management

The patient was started on intravenous magnesium and isoproterenol drip. A transvenous pacemaker was placed. She was continued on propranolol 30 mg 3 times daily for presumed LQTS. The patient then received 7 days of intravenous antibiotic therapy for a possible superimposed bacterial infection. Given no clear etiology for the patient’s prolonged QT, an underlying genetic syndrome was suggested, unmasked by her recent metabolic derangements with questionable recent antibiotic use of trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole. An implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) was placed a few days later. The patient was started on rifampin, ethambutol, azithromycin, and amikacin for MAC infection treatment. QTc was closely monitored.

Discussion

The prevalence of LQTS is approximately 1:2,500 births, with 3 major genes being responsible for more than 80% of the genetic types of LQTS.4 Congenital LQTS is a potentially life-threatening condition secondary to mutations of genes encoding sodium or potassium channels. Most of these gene mutations are on the short arm of chromosome 11.5 More than 90% of the gene mutations are associated with 3 genes: KCNQ1, KCNH2, and SCN5A. LQTS type 2 specifically is caused by a mutation in KCNH2 that primarily decreases the number of rapid delayed rectifier potassium (Ikr) channels in the cell membrane either by disturbance of channel synthesis or trafficking.6 In some cases, we can differentiate the 3 types of LQTS based on the electrocardiogram. To illustrate, whereas type 1 is characterized by broad-based T waves, type 2 exhibits a low-amplitude notched or biphasic T-wave. There is a long isoelectric portion followed by a narrow T wave in type 3.7 Presentation of congenital LQTS may remain asymptomatic for years or may present with syncope, aborted cardiac arrest, or sudden cardiac death. The physical examination can be helpful in the diagnosis of certain syndromes, such as congenital deafness in Jervell-Lange-Nielsen syndrome or scoliosis, and short stature can present as part of Andersen-Tawil syndrome.1

Identifying the mutation is also helpful in management. Patients with type 1 LQTS are at high risk of malignant arrhythmias during exercise, intense emotions, and swimming; thus, they are prohibited from participating in competitive sports and swimming.3

Management is focused on preventing syncope and, ultimately, sudden cardiac death. Available treatment options are lifestyle modifications; pharmacologic therapy; device therapy; and, rarely, surgical treatment. Beta blockers—specifically, nadolol and propranolol—are considered first-line therapy in all asymptomatic and symptomatic patients with congenital LQTS. In patients with KCNH2 trafficking defects, lumacaftor has been shown to reduce the QT interval significantly. An ICD is highly recommended in survivors of cardiac arrest, individuals at high risk for malignant arrhythmias, and those in whom beta-blocker therapy is ineffective. In patients with failure, intolerance, or contraindication of the previous lines of management, left cardiac sympathetic denervation can be considered.1,4,8

The subcutaneous ICD (S-ICD) eliminates the potential for vascular injury or pneumothorax because of its subcutaneous lead placement. Thus, in the case of younger individuals with active lifestyles and prolonged life expectancies like our patient, the S-ICD would be a better option in favor of a transvenous ICD. One particular concern in patients with both S-ICDs and LQTS is the potential for inappropriate shocks triggered by T-wave oversensing. However, this risk could be mitigated by exercise-based templates and skilled programming.9, 10, 11

Follow-Up

A genetic test for inherited arrhythmias and cardiomyopathies revealed a pathogenic mutation on the KCNH2 gene consistent with LQTS type 2. The patient was kept on oral propranolol 30 mg 3 times daily. Her sister was advised to cascade screening further as needed.

Conclusions

Congenital LQTS may only become apparent following insult with QT-prolonging drugs or electrolyte abnormalities. Symptomatic patients with arrhythmias should undergo genetic testing for correct diagnosis and long-term treatment with ICD.
