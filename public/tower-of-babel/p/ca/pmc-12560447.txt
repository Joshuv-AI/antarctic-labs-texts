Long-term BMI trajectories and epigenetic age acceleration: the role of genetic risk for obesity
Authors: Yue Wen, Yi Chen, Yanling Ma, Chunyan Lu, Yizhuo Liu, Ruiyuan Zhang, Jing Liao, Dongmei Wu, Changwei Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrevious studies reported mixed findings in the relationship between body mass index (BMI) and epigenetic age acceleration (EAA), while none considered long-term trajectory of BMI and individuals’ genetic background. Therefore, we aimed to evaluate the joint effects of long-term BMI trajectory and genetic risk for obesity on EAA among older US adults.MethodsWe performed secondary analyses of panel survey data from 3312 participants of the Health and Retirement Study. BMI was assessed biennially from 1996 to 2016. Latent variable mixture modeling was used to identify distinct BMI trajectories over the 20-year period. Thirteen epigenetic clocks were used to calculate EAAs based on DNA methylation data from the 2016 Venous Blood Study (VBS). Genetic risk for obesity was quantified by a polygenic risk score (PRS) and categorized into low, moderate, and high risk based on quartile cutoffs. Multivariable linear regression models were used to test associations between BMI trajectories and EAAs overall and stratified by genetic risk, while adjusting for age, sex, ancestry, education, cigarette smoking, alcohol drinking, and physical activity. Bonferroni method was used to correct for multiple testing.ResultsThe HRS participants demonstrated stable BMI trajectories over time and were classified into three groups: consistently normal weight, overweight, and obese. Overall, 41.0% had a BMI trajectory consistent with their genetic risk levels, 33.2% deviated to a worse trajectory, and 25.7% to a better trajectory. BMI trajectories were significantly associated with 6 of the 13 EAAs. BMI started to predict EAAs in at least 8 years ahead of EAA measurement. Consistently obese participants, but not those consistently overweight, exhibited significantly accelerated epigenetic aging compared to those with consistently normal weight. These associations were most pronounced among individuals with low or moderate genetic risk for obesity.ConclusionsLong-term obesity but not overweight was associated with EAA in older adults, particularly among individuals with low or moderate genetic risk for obesity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-025-04410-6.

Background

Obesity is a major risk factor for many aging-related diseases, including diabetes, stroke, and cardiovascular disease [1]. However, the relationship between body mass index (BMI) and both all-cause and disease-specific mortality follows a U- or J-shaped curve, with increased risks associated with both too high or too low BMI [2, 3]. Similarly, the association between BMI and molecular biomarkers of biological aging is mixed [4, 5]. While significant associations were observed in middle-aged and young adults, such links are less evident in older populations [4, 6]. Notably, these studies have largely overlooked individuals’ genetic predisposition to obesity.

From an evolutionary perspective, moderate adiposity may have conferred a survival advantage by providing energy reserves during periods of food scarcity while avoiding the health risks of extreme thinness or obesity [7]. Maintaining a BMI aligned with one’s genetically determined set point may therefore optimize health and longevity. However, actual BMI often deviates from genetically predicted levels due to environmental and lifestyle influences [8]. The health consequences of such deviations, particularly in the context of aging markers, remains poorly understood.

Epigenetic age acceleration (EAA) has emerged as a robust biomarker of biological aging, representing the gap between chronological and DNA methylation estimated age. A variety of epigenetic clocks have been developed to estimate epigenetic age. Early-generation clocks, such as the Horvath [9] and Hannum [10] clocks, were trained to predict chronological age or mortality risk based on DNA methylation profiles. Intuitively, a clock optimized for predicting chronological age may be less informative for biological aging, whereas one trained on mortality outcomes may be less accurate for estimating chronological age [11]. To address this limitation, the second-generation clocks were developed to predict biomarkers of aging, such as inflammatory and metabolic markers in plasma, or unhealthy lifestyles, such as smoking. These efforts led to the development of PhenoAge [12] and GrimAge [13], which estimate epigenetic age on biomarker profiles at a single timepoint, and more recently, the DunedinPoAm [14] and DunedinPACE [15] clocks, which incorporate longitudinal changes in aging-related biomarkers. These clocks substantially enhanced aging research. Several epigenetic clocks, including Horvath [9], Hannum [10], PhenoAge [12], and GrimAge [13], have been linked to age-related cardiometabolic disorders and mortality [16, 17]. Obesity has been associated with DNA methylation changes in genes related to inflammatory and metabolic pathways [18], potentially contributing to accelerate epigenetic aging. However, findings have been inconsistent, with results varying by epigenetic clocks, BMI categories, and population subgroups [4, 19, 20]. For example, BMI has been associated with EAA in liver tissue but not in blood or subcutaneous adipose tissue [21], and stronger associations have been observed among men and those with obesity [22–24].

A key limitation of prior research is the reliance on single-timepoint BMI measurements, which failed to capture the dynamic nature of weight changes over time. Long-term BMI trajectories may better reflect cumulative weight exposure and provide stronger predictive value for EAA [25, 26]. Moreover, individuals with high genetic risk for obesity may follow distinct BMI trajectories that differentially influence their biological aging [25, 26]. However, the joint effects of genetic susceptibility, longitudinal BMI patterns, and EAA remain largely unexplored.

To address these gaps, the present study aims to characterize long-term BMI trajectories in older adults and examine their associations with EAA, stratified by polygenic risk scores (PRS) for obesity. By integrating genetic data with longitudinal BMI and epigenetic aging measures, this study seeks to uncover novel insights into the interplay between genetic predisposition, weight history, and biological aging, contributing to our understanding of gene-environment interactions in aging-related health outcomes.

Methods

Study design

This study is a secondary analysis of data from the Health and Retirement Study (HRS), an ongoing, nationally representative longitudinal cohort of US adults aged 50 and older. The HRS was designed to assess health, social, and economic factors associated with aging and employs a multistage probability sampling strategy [27–29]. To enhance representativeness, the study oversamples key demographic groups, including African Americans and Hispanics. Since 1992, participants have been surveyed biennially on socioeconomic status, health, and biomarkers [30]. Participants are generally followed until death with new cohorts added every 6 years to maintain national representativeness.

Participants

In 2016, the HRS began collecting venous blood samples from 9934 participants as part of the Venous Blood Study (2016-VBS). As shown in Additional file 1: Fig. S1, a subsample of 4101 participants underwent genome-wide DNA methylation (DNAm) profiling [31]. This subsample is weighted to represent the broader US population [32, 33]. After excluding participants who failed DNAm quality control (n = 86) [32] and those with BMI measured only in one wave (n = 288) or having missing covariables in the 2016 wave (n = 415), the final analytic sample included 3312 (80.8%) participants. Additionally, in the analyses incorporating PRS for obesity, due to missingness in the genetic data (n = 481), a total of 2831 participants were included. Finally, to trace the correlation of BMI with EAA across waves, we included 1600 participants with BMI measured in all waves.

Measures

BMI

Height and weight were self-reported across 12 survey waves from wave 2 in 1994 to wave 13 in 2016. BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters. In the USA, self-reported BMI is highly correlated with measured BMI (ρ > 0.9) [34, 35]. To model trajectories, only participants with BMI measured in at least two waves were included. To describe the BMI trajectories, we applied the World Health Organization classification criteria of BMI for individuals of European and African ancestries [36]. Specifically, overweight was defined as a BMI of 25.0–29.9 kg/m2, and obesity as a BMI greater than or equal to 30 kg/m2 [37].

Genome-wide DNAm

DNAm was profiled once from blood samples collected for 4101 participants in the 2016-VBS using the Infinium Methylation EPIC BeadChip v1.0 at the University of Minnesota and is available directly from NIAGADS (data product #: NG00153) [32]. Samples were randomized across assay plates while balancing key demographic factors and included 40 pairs of blinded duplicates. Data processing and quality control were performed using the minfi R package. Probes with detection P values > 0.01 were removed (n = 29,431; 3.4%), followed by sample-level exclusions for > 5% failed probes, sex mismatches, or control samples. High-quality DNAm data were retained for 97.9% of the samples (N = 4018).

Epigenetic clocks and EAAs

Based on the genome-wide DNAm data collected for the 2016-VBS, the HRS team calculated 13 publicly available epigenetic clocks (https://hrsdata.isr.umich.edu/data-products/epigenetic-clocks) [38], categorized into three groups (Additional file 1: Table S1) predicting: (1) chronological age (e.g., Horvath [39], Hannum [10], Weidner [40], VidalBralo [41], Bocklandt [42], and Garagnani [43]), (2) lifespan/mortality (e.g., PhenoAge (12), HorvathSkin (9), Lin [44], Zhang [45], and GrimAge [13]), and (3) aging rates (e.g., Yang [46] and DunedinPoAm [14]). EAAs were defined as the residual from regressing epigenetic clocks on chronological age measured in 2016, when the mean age of participants was 69.9 years with a standard deviation (SD) of 9.7. The epigenetic clocks data is available as HRS public data [38].

Genotyping and PRS calculation

Genome-wide genotyping was performed using the Illumina’s Human Omni2.5-Quad arrays [47]. After stringent quality control, genotypes were imputed to the 1000 Genome Project Phase 3 reference panel. A PRS for obesity was calculated using genome-wide association studies (GWAS) meta-analysis results from the GIANT consortium (n = 681,275, European ancestry) [48]. The PRS was calculated by combining BMI increasing alleles of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) weighted by reported effect sizes using PGSice and PLINK software [49, 50]. SNPs was not trimmed by linkage disequilibrium or filtered by P value thresholding [50]. SNP weights were estimated after removing HRS from the GWAS summary statistics. The final PRS included 711,273 SNPs and was standardized within ancestry to have a mean = 0 and standard deviation (SD) = 1. The PRS was categorized into low (bottom quartile), moderate (middle two quartiles), and high (top quartile) genetic risk groups. The PRS data is available as an HRS Sensitive Health Data Product [50].

Covariates

Important covariables collected in the 2016 survey were selected a priori based on prior literature and included age, sex, ancestry (European Americans or African Americans), years of education [5, 33], cigarette smoking (never, former, current) [5, 51], alcohol drinking (current, non-current) [5, 52], and physical activity (PA) [53]. All covariables were self-reported. For PA, participants responded to questions about work-related and leisure-time vigorous, moderate, and light PA over the past 12 months. Participants indicated how often they participated in light, moderate, and vigorous PA, with 5 response options ranging from 1 = more than once a week, 2 = once a week, 3 = 1–3 times a month, 4 = hardly ever or never, and 5 = every day. In line with a previous study [54], the PA frequency was recoded as follows: 4 = every day, 3 = more than once a week, 2 = once a week, 1 = 1–3 times a month, and 0 = hardly ever or never. Three index scores were created to represent combined PA frequencies, including all types of PA, light to moderate PA, and moderate to vigorous PA. Each index score was the sum of frequencies of its PA components.

Statistical analyses

Latent variable mixture modeling implemented by the SAS Proc Traj procedure was used to identify BMI trajectories over 20 years (1996–2016) [55, 56]. Latent variable mixture modeling is a statistical approach that models heterogeneous longitudinal data by classifying individuals into groupings with similar patterns, called latent classes [55]. We fitted all trajectory models in quadratic form, starting with a 1-trajectory model and incrementally increasing the number of trajectories. The optimal number of trajectories was determined by comparing the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) across models, with the model yielding the lowest BIC selected as optimal. Ages at survey waves were used as the timescale, with mean ages ranging from 60.5 years at the first BMI measurement to 79.4 at the last. After identifying the 4-trajectory model as the best-fitting based on BIC, we further compared models with different forms. The final selected models included 4 BMI trajectories, each modeled using a quadratic form. The third and fourth trajectory groups both had BMI above 30 kg/m2. Due to small sample size of the fourth group, we merged them into one group. Therefore, a total of three groups: consistently normal weight, consistently overweight, and consistently obese were identified.

Characteristics of the study participants in the 2016 survey were summarized overall and by BMI trajectory groups. Continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations or medians and interquartile ranges, and categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons among the three BMI trajectory groups were conducted using one-way ANOVA (analysis of variance) for continuous variables or chi-square tests for categorical variables. Continuous variables were assessed for normality and log-transformed if necessary.

Associations between BMI trajectories and each EAA were modeled using multivariable linear mixed effect models with ancestry treated as random intercept, while controlling for age and sex in a base model and additionally controlling for education, cigarette smoking, alcohol drinking, and physical activity in a full model. SAS 9.4 software was used to perform the analyses. Two-sided P values were computed, and a Bonferroni corrected q value < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

To evaluate whether BMI trajectories impact EAAs differently by genetic susceptibility to obesity, we tested interactions between BMI trajectories and PRS for obesity on EAAs and presented associations between BMI trajectories and EAAs by PRS categories.

Results

The analyses sample included a total of 2722 individuals of European ancestry and 580 participants of African ancestry from the 2016-VBS (Table 1). The mean age of participants was 69.9 years, and 41.5% were men. Alcohol consumption was prevalent, with 56.0% reporting current use, while 10.8% were current smokers. On average, participants were overweight, with a mean BMI of 28.8 kg/m2. BMI trajectories and deviation from genetic risk

As shown in Fig. 1, BMI remained relatively stable over the past 20 years, forming four distinct trajectories: consistently normal weight (33.0%), consistently overweighted (41.6%), consistently obese (20.3%), and consistently extreme obese (5.1%). Average posterior probabilities of membership are 0.945, 0.930, 0.950, and 0.976 for the four trajectory groups. Due to sample size considerations, we combined the obese and extremely obese groups into a single “high BMI trajectory” group. In 2016, the mean BMIs for the low, moderate, and high BMI trajectory groups were 22.8, 27.7, and 35.6 kg/m2, respectively (Table 1). Overall, 41.0% had a BMI trajectory consistent with their genetic risk levels, including 37.8% of the low genetic risk, 38.3% of the moderate genetic risk, and 49.8% of the high genetic risk group (Table 1). However, 33.2% deviated to a worse trajectory, and 25.7% to a better trajectory.

Correlations between BMI with epigenetic clocks over time

Figure 2 and Additional file 1: Table S2 display correlations between BMI across 10 survey waves spanning 20 years and the 13 epigenetic clocks measured in 2016. DunedinPoAm (ρ: 0.05 in wave 6 to 0.07 in wave 12), Bocklandt (ρ: 0.01 in wave 2 to 0.10 in wave 13), and Zhang (ρ: 0 in wave 12 to 0.1 in wave 2) clocks consistently showed positive correlations with BMI. In contrast, the Yang clock showed no association with BMI, while the remaining nine clocks were negatively correlated with BMI. Among these, HorvathSkin and Hannum clocks exhibited the strongest negative correlations (both ρ = − 0.17 in wave 13), followed by GrimAge (ρ = − 0.16 in wave 13), Horvath (ρ = − 0.14 in wave 13), and Garagnani (ρ = − 0.14 in wave 13) clocks. Significant correlations for these clocks emerged beginning at wave 6. Four additional clocks, PhenoAge (ρ = − 0.04), Lin (ρ = − 0.09), VidalBralo (ρ = − 0.05), and Weidner (ρ = − 0.05), showed significant correlations beginning at wave 9. Notably, DunedinPoAm and Bocklandt clocks showed positive, significant correlations with BMI across all waves. BMI trajectories and EAAs in 2016

After adjusting for age, sex, and ancestry and Bonferroni correction for multiple testing, BMI trajectories were significantly associated with six EAA measures, including Hannum, PhenoAge, HorvathSkin, Zhang, GrimAge, and DunedinPoAm (Table 2 and Additional file 1: Table S3). Of the six EAA measures, except for Hannum EAA, the other five were all developed based on epigenetic clocks predicting lifespan and mortality. Participants in the high BMI trajectory group had significantly faster epigenetic aging, particularly for PhenoAge with a difference of 1.62 years, compared to those in the low BMI trajectory group (P < 0.001). The moderate BMI trajectory was also associated with accelerated epigenetic age, but only nominally for the HorvathSkin clock (P = 0.04). These associations remained consistent after additional adjustment for education, smoking, drinking, and physical activity. When stratified by ancestry, BMI trajectories were more strongly associated with EAAs among African Americans compared to European Americans (Additional file 1: Table S4). For example, in the fully adjusted model, the difference in Levine EAA between consistently high and consistently low BMI trajectories was 2.02 (SE = 0.9; P = 0.02) among African Americans, compared to 1.27 (SE = 0.37; P = 0.001) among European Americans. These differences were even more pronounced among individuals with low or high genetic risk (Additional file 1: Table S5). Notably, association between Yang EAA and BMI trajectories showed opposite directions in European Americans versus African Americans. BMI trajectories and EAAs by levels of BMI PRS

BMI trajectories showed significantly different associations with four EAA measures, including VidalBralo (P for interaction = 0.03), Yang (P for interaction = 0.01), Bocklandt (P for interaction = 0.001), and GrimAge (P for interaction = 0.004), by levels of BMI PRS. Overall, these associations were stronger among those with low or moderate genetic risk for obesity compared to those with high genetic risk.

Among the 705 participants with low genetic risk for obesity, 37.8%, 39.0%, and 23.1% were in the low, moderate, and high BMI trajectories, respectively. BMI trajectories were nominally associated with five EAAs (Table 3 and Additional file 1: Table S6), including Hannum (P = 0.02), Bocklandt (P = 0.01), Levine (PhenoAge, P = 0.01), GrimAge (P = 0.01), and Yang (P = 0.04). Compared to participants with consistently low BMI, those with a consistently high BMI showed significantly accelerated epigenetic age as measured by Hannum (beta = 1.39, SE = 0.55, P = 0.01), PhenoAge (beta = 2.13, SE = 0.72, P = 0.003), Yang (beta = 0.004, SE = 0.002, P = 0.02), Bocklandt (beta = 0.02, SE = 0.01, P = 0.004), and GrimAge (beta = 1.48, SE = 0.47, P = 0.002). No significant differences in EAA were observed between the moderate and low BMI trajectory groups in this stratum. In the moderate genetic risk group (n = 1407), 26.3%, 38.3%, and 35.4% fell into the low, moderate, and high BMI trajectories, respectively. As shown in Table 3 and Additional file 1: Table S6, BMI trajectories were significantly associated with Zhang EAA (P < 0.001) and nominally associated with Horvath (P = 0.02), Levin (PhenoAge, P = 0.01), GrimAge (P = 0.02), and DunedinPoAm (P = 0.01). A consistently high BMI trajectory was significantly associated with accelerated epigenetic age measured by PhenoAge (beta = 1.48, se = 0.50, P = 0.003), Zhang (beta = 0.10, se = 0.03, P = 0.001), GrimAge (beta = 0.70, se = 0.33, P = 0.03), and DunedinPoAm38 (beta = 0.02, se = 0.01, P = 0.01) clocks. Interestingly, participants in the moderate BMI trajectory had decelerated epigenetic aging, as measured by the Horvath clock (beta = − 0.92, se = 0.44, P = 0.04).

Among the 703 participants with high genetic risk of obesity, 19.5%, 30.7%, and 49.8% were in the low, moderate, and high BMI trajectory groups, respectively. Overall, BMI trajectories were not associated with any EAAs. However, compared to those in the low BMI trajectory, the high BMI trajectory group showed nominal associations with EAAs measured by Horvath (beta = 1.28, se = 0.68, P = 0.06), HorvathSkin (beta = 0.86, se = 0.50, P = 0.08), Lin (beta = 1.58, se = 0.83, P = 0.06), and Weidner (beta = 2.19, se = 1.16, P = 0.06). Surprisingly, the moderate BMI trajectory group in this stratum was significantly associated with EAAs measured by Horvath (beta = 1.67, se = 0.70, P = 0.02), HorvathSkin (beta = 1.04, se = 0.52, P = 0.04), and Yang (beta = 0.004, se = 0.002, P = 0.04).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the impact of long-term BMI trajectories on a comprehensive panel of EAA measures, stratified by genetic risk for obesity, among older US adults. We identified four predominant BMI trajectories, consistently normal weight, overweight, obese, and extreme obese, highlighting the relative stability of BMI in this population. Compared to those with a consistently normal weight trajectory, individuals with consistently obese or extremely obese trajectories exhibited significantly accelerated epigenetic aging based on six of the 13 epigenetic clocks examined. In contrast, those with a consistently overweight trajectory showed nominally accelerated aging for only one clock. When stratified by genetic predisposition to obesity, we found that the effect of persistent obesity on EAA was most pronounced among individuals with low or moderate genetic risk. In particular, consistently obese individuals with low genetic risk experienced the greatest acceleration in biological age. These findings suggest that obesity, but not overweight, contributes to biological aging, especially among those not genetically predisposed to obesity. This highlights an opportunity for precision prevention strategies tailored to genetic profiles.

BMI trajectories were most strongly associated with EAAs designed to predict lifespan or mortality. Of the five clocks significantly associated with BMI trajectories in the fully adjusted models, four, including Levine (PhenoAge), HorvathSkin, Zhang, and GrimAge, were developed as predictors of mortality or lifespan. This pattern is biologically plausible given the well-established link between obesity and premature death [57, 58]. For instance, recent modeling estimates suggest that excess weight accounts for more than 1300 excess deaths daily and reduces life expectancy by approximately 2.4 years [57]. However, a consistently overweight trajectory was associated with only one EAA measure, suggesting that being overweight may not be strongly linked to aging-related outcomes. This is consistent with findings from a meta-analysis showing that overweight status does not increase all-cause mortality [59].

Stratified analyses by genetic risk further revealed that consistently obese individuals with low or moderate genetic risk exhibited more EAAs than those with high genetic risk. This is in line with findings from a recent publication showing that obesity was more strongly associated with cardiovascular disease among individuals with low genetic predisposition to obesity compared to those with a high genetic risk [60]. One possible explanation is that individuals with high genetic risk may have developed compensatory biological adaptations that help mitigate some of the harmful consequences of excess adiposity, such as metabolic flexibility or altered fat distribution [60, 61]. Therefore, individuals with high genetic predisposition to obesity may be more resilient to the adverse effects of excess weight on biological aging. It is also possible that weight gain is even physiologically necessary for those with a high genetic risk. Notably, maintaining a normal body weight was not significantly associated with slow aging among these individuals. In contrast, for individuals with low genetic risk, obesity may result primarily from unfavorable lifestyle or environmental exposures (e.g., poor diet, physical inactivity, chronic stress), which themselves may drive additional metabolic or inflammatory insults. Furthermore, obesity in low-risk individuals could represent a greater deviation from their genetically “expected” body weight, potentially leading to increased physiological stress. This mismatch may accelerate biological aging processes, as reflected by greater epigenetic age acceleration and higher risk of obesity-related diseases, such as cardiovascular disease. Further mechanistic studies are warranted to uncover biological pathways that may buffer the negative impact of obesity in genetically susceptible individuals.

Despite BMI stability in this older cohort, substantial deviation from genetically predicted weight was observed. Among those with a genetically low predisposition for obesity, up to 23.1% were classified as obese. Similarly, nearly 20% of those genetically predisposed to obesity maintained a consistently normal BMI. These discrepancies underscore the influence of environmental and lifestyle factors in modulating genetic risk, emphasizing that body weight is shaped by both intrinsic and extrinsic determinants.

Finally, we observed that epigenetic age was impacted by BMI measured at least 8 years before the epigenetic age was measured. Previous studies have shown that obesity influenced DNA methylation up to 17 years in adolescents. Our study provided the first evidence that in older adults, BMI has long-term impact on epigenetic age [62].

This study has several notable strengths. First, we examined a comprehensive panel of 13 epigenetic clocks, offering a multidimensional assessment of epigenetic aging. Second, the use of a nationally representative sample of older US adults enhances the generalizability of our findings. Third, we utilized up to 20 years of BMI data, enabling the characterization of long-term weight burden with reduced measurement error compared to single-timepoint assessments. Several limitations merit consideration. First, BMI was based on self-reported height and weight, which may introduce misclassification. However, self-reported and measured BMI are highly correlated in older populations, and any misclassification is likely non-differential with respect to EAA, thus biasing results toward the null. Importantly, our study still identified robust associations between BMI trajectories and EAA. In addition, BMI does not differentiate between fat and lean mass, nor does it account for fat distribution [63–65]. As a result, it may misclassify muscular individuals as overweight or underestimate obesity in people with high body fat but normal BMI, limiting its accuracy in assessing true adiposity and related health risks. Future studies using better measures of obesity, such as body fat percentage, are warranted to confirm our findings. Second, there is a lack of baseline EAA data, which prevents us from estimating changes in EAA over time. Future longitudinal studies are needed to address this gap. Third, while DunedinPoAm38 clock was originally developed in participants aged 26–38 years, our study population consists of middle-aged and older adults. However, this clock has since been validated in individuals aged 28–95 years, demonstrating good performance across a broader age range. Finally, most epigenetic clocks were developed using data from populations of European ancestry, and their performance among African Americans may be suboptimal. This is supported by our observation that some EAA measures showed opposite associations with BMI trajectories in African Americans compared to European Americans. Nevertheless, many associations were consistent across racial groups. The development of ancestry-specific epigenetic clocks may be necessary to improve accuracy and relevance in future aging research.

Conclusions

In summary, BMI was relatively stable over two decades among older US adults. Long-term obesity, but not overweight, was significantly associated with accelerated aging as measured by epigenetic clocks predicting lifespan or mortality. These associations were more pronounced among individuals with low genetic risk for obesity. These findings highlight the potential value of integrating genetic risk profiling into obesity prevention and healthy aging strategies to advance precision health.
