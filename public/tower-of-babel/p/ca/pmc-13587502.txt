Analysis of whole-exome sequencing data from nearly 10,000 Iranian individuals: identification of recessive mitochondrial disease variants and proposal of a population-specific carrier screening panel
Authors: Mohadeseh Fathi, Parvaneh Karimzadeh, Farzad Ahmadabadi, Damoun Nashta Ali, Narjes Jafari, Raheleh Tangestani, Shadab Salehpour, Toktam Moosavian, Soudeh Ghafouri-Fard, Mohammad Miryounesi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMitochondrial diseases, often stemming from recessive nuclear gene mutations, represent a heterogeneous group of disorders with significant morbidity and mortality. Carrier screening for these conditions is population-specific, yet data on the pathogenic variant burden in the Iranian population remain limited. This study aimed to analyze whole-exome sequencing (WES) data from 9989 Iranian individuals to identify the spectrum and frequency of recessive mitochondrial disease variants and to develop a population-specific carrier screening panel.MethodsWe analyzed WES data from 9989 unrelated Iranian individuals. Variants in 1,564 nuclear genes associated with mitochondrial function were filtered for rarity (minor allele frequency < 0.01 in public databases), predicted pathogenicity, and recessive inheritance patterns (homozygous or compound heterozygous). Clinically relevant variants were manually curated, and carrier frequencies for significant recessive mitochondrial conditions were calculated.ResultsOur analysis identified variants across 15 groups of mitochondrial-related nuclear genes in 345 individuals recognized as carriers. Of these, 123 variants (35.6%) were classified as Pathogenic, and 154 variants (44.6%) were classified as Likely Pathogenic according to ACMG guidelines.ConclusionsThis study provides the first large-scale WES-derived assessment of recessive mitochondrial disease carrier burden in the Iranian population. The high estimated carrier rate supports implementing population-specific preconception screening. The results of this study can be used for design of targeted panels of nuclear mitochondrial genes to identify at-risk couples, facilitating genetic counseling and reproductive decision-making in Iran.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40246-026-01011-z.

Introduction

Mitochondrial diseases are a clinically and genetically heterogeneous group of disorders caused by dysfunction of the oxidative phosphorylation system [1]. They can result from mutations in either the mitochondrial genome (mtDNA) or, more commonly, in nearly 1,500 nuclear genes encoding mitochondrial proteins [2]. While mtDNA mutations are often sporadic or maternally inherited, nuclear gene defects typically follow Mendelian patterns [3], with autosomal recessive inheritance being particularly prevalent in consanguineous populations.

Iran, characterized by a high rate of consanguineous marriage (approximately 30–40% of unions) [4], exhibits a heightened incidence of autosomal recessive disorders, including mitochondrial diseases. These conditions collectively impose a substantial health and economic burden due to their often early onset, multisystem involvement, and lack of curative treatments [5]. Preconception carrier screening has proven effective in reducing the incidence of recessive disorders in other populations [6]. However, existing commercial or international screening panels are largely derived from European or North American datasets [7] and do not necessarily capture the unique pathogenic variant spectrum present in the Iranian gene pool.

Whole-exome sequencing (WES) has become a powerful tool for both disease diagnosis and population genomics. By leveraging existing WES data from unaffected or undiagnosed individuals, it is possible to ascertain the background carrier frequency of severe recessive mitochondrial variants. Despite the high disease burden, no systematic study has characterized the landscape of recessive mitochondrial disease variants in the general Iranian population.

Here, we analyze WES data from 9989 Iranian individuals to: (1) identify the spectrum and frequency of pathogenic recessive variants in nuclear mitochondrial genes; (2) estimate the population carrier rate for severe, actionable mitochondrial disorders; and (3) propose a population-specific carrier screening panel for recessive mitochondrial diseases tailored to the Iranian population. Our findings aim to inform public health strategies for preconception genetic screening and genetic counseling in Iran.

Methods

Study population and data source

We retrospectively analyzed WES data from 9989 unrelated Iranian individuals. The cohort comprises individuals who underwent genetic testing at the Center for Comprehensive Genetic Services, Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran from 2016 to 2024. Within this group, 1450 participants were screened for carrier status, either pre-marriage or post-marriage, while the remaining participants were tested for diagnostic purposes related to specific diseases. All samples were obtained from peripheral blood following informed consent for genetic analysis. The study protocol was approved by ethical committee of Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences (IR.SBMU.MSP.REC.1403.346).

Whole-exome sequencing and quality control

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood. Whole Exome Sequencing (WES) was then employed to enrich all protein-coding exons and other critical genomic regions. Subsequently, Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS) was performed using Illumina sequencers, generating approximately 100 million reads. The platform achieved coverage of over 95% of targeted regions with a sensitivity exceeding 99%. This assay is capable of simultaneously detecting point mutations, micro-insertions/deletions, and duplications (< 20 bp). Finally, the sequencing results underwent bioinformatics analysis, utilizing international databases and standard bioinformatics software.

Variant filtering and annotation

We curated a list of 1,564 nuclear genes associated with mitochondrial function, including structural subunits of oxidative phosphorylation complexes (I-V), assembly factors, mtDNA replication/maintenance factors, mitochondrial translation components, dynamics regulators, and metabolic enzymes (fatty acid oxidation, TCA cycle, CoQ10 biosynthesis, iron-sulfur cluster biogenesis). Variants were filtered for minor allele frequency (MAF < 0.01 in public databases), predicted functional impact (non-synonymous, splice-site (± 2 bp), or frameshift indels) and inheritance pattern (homozygous or compound heterozygous recessive).

Pathogenicity assessment

Variant pathogenicity was evaluated using a combination of ACMG guidelines (classes: Pathogenic, Likely Pathogenic, Variant of Uncertain Significance [VUS]), in silico prediction tools (SIFT [deleterious threshold: ≤0.05], PolyPhen-2 [probably damaging: ≥0.95, possibly damaging: 0.85–0.94], or CADD score [≥ 20 as deleterious]), population frequency comparison (Iranome vs. gnomAD).

Carrier frequency calculation

For each identified pathogenic or likely pathogenic recessive variant, carrier frequency was calculated as the number of heterozygotes divided by the total cohort (N = 9989).

Panel design

A population-specific carrier screening panel was proposed by selecting genes with: Carrier frequency ≥ 1 in 1000 in the Iranian cohort, known association with severe, early-onset recessive mitochondrial disease and actionability (availability of reproductive options or preventive measures). Results

Overview of identified variants

Analysis of WES data from 9989 Iranian individuals identified 345 individuals as carriers of recessive variants across 15 groups of nuclear genes involved in mitochondrial function (Tables S1-S15). Of these, 123 variants (35.6%) were classified as Pathogenic, and 154 variants (44.6%) were classified as Likely Pathogenic according to ACMG guidelines. The remaining variants (19.7%) were classified as Variants of Uncertain Significance (VUS). Of note, seven of the identified VUS variants had previously been documented in the literature concerning patients with mitochondrial disorders [8–14]. Consequently, these seven variants were selected from the VUS variants for inclusion in the final analysis. Among Likely Pathogenic variants, five variants were missense variants with no clinical report. These variants were also omitted from further analysis.

Distribution by functional category

Table 1 summarizes the distribution of pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants across key mitochondrial functional categories. Among all categories, Complex I (NADH Dehydrogenase) harbored the highest mutational burden, with 24 genes containing a total of 93 variants, accounting for the largest share of genetic heterogeneity. Representative genes included NDUFS1, NDUFV1, ACAD9, NDUFAF1, NDUFAF5, and NDUFAF6. Complex IV (cytochrome c oxidase) followed with 9 genes and 36 variants, represented by SURF1 and TACO1. Complex III (BCS1L, UQCRB) involved 4 genes and 12 variants, while Complex II (SDHA, SDHB) and Complex V (ATPAF2) each involved 2 genes, with 15 and 4 variants, respectively.

Beyond oxidative phosphorylation, mtDNA replication and maintenance showed a high variant count: 11 genes with 46 variants, including DGUOK and POLG. Mitochondrial dynamics accounts for 5 genes and 21 variants (MFF, MSTO1), and mitochondrial translation (MRM2) includes 4 genes with 5 variants.

Other functional categories with moderate contributions include: TCA cycle and intermediate metabolism (PCK2; 4 genes, 17 variants), iron-sulfur cluster biogenesis (IBA57; 4 genes, 9 variants), fatty acid oxidation (HADHA; 3 genes, 7 variants), and protein processing & apoptosis (PMPCB; 3 genes, 7 variants). Notably, coenzyme Q10 biosynthesis shows the smallest burden (COQ6; 1 gene, 1 variant), while mitochondrial transporters (SLC25A21) and other functions (FBXL4) contribute 2 genes/4 variants and 3 genes/12 variants, respectively.

Overall, Complex I and mtDNA maintenance pathways represent the most frequent targets of variation, highlighting their central role in mitochondrial disease etiology.

Subsequently, we evaluated how varying the population carrier frequency threshold (upper limit for inclusion) affects the number of genes included in a targeted mitochondrial disease panel, the representative genes selected, and the expected diagnostic capture rate. At a threshold of 1 in 1000, the panel includes 5 genes (NDUFB3, NDUFS1, SURF1, POLG, PCK2) with a capture rate of 19.1%. Relaxing the threshold to 1 in 1250 expands the panel to 9 genes (adding ACAD9, SDHA, MSTO1, FBXL4), increasing the capture rate to 28.6%. Further lowering the threshold to 1 in 1650 is projected to include 16 genes and achieve a capture rate of approximately 41%, while a threshold of 1 in 2500 would expand the panel to 24 genes with an estimated capture rate of 51%. As expected, lowering the carrier frequency threshold progressively increases panel size and diagnostic yield (Table 4).

Discussion

Since the initial discovery of mitochondrial disease mutations in the late 1980s [15, 16], both the genetic diagnosis of these disorders and the identification of the number of implicated genes have progressed rapidly. Particularly, in recent decades, this rapid advancement has been made possible primarily by the adoption of NGS approaches. These strategies have been applied to design targeted panels for carrier detection or diagnostic approaches in suspected patients [17, 18].

Here, we analyzed WES data from nearly 10,000 Iranian individuals to identify the spectrum and frequency of pathogenic recessive variants in nuclear mitochondrial genes and propose a population-specific carrier screening panel for recessive mitochondrial diseases.

Based on the above-mentioned data, approximately 3% of Iranian individuals carry at least one pathogenic or likely pathogenic recessive variant for a mitochondrial disease. Moreover, our data showed population-specific enrichment of some variants. In fact, several variants such as NDUFA9 c.394 C > T, PCK2 c.577 C > T and SURF1 c.816_817insAGCAGCG were significantly enriched in Iranians compared to global populations, indicating founder effects. The identification of population-specific founder variants has important implications for the design of an efficient carrier screening panel in Iran. Founder effects, driven by high rates of consanguinity and geographic isolation of certain subpopulations, lead to the recurrence of specific pathogenic alleles that may be rare or absent elsewhere. Incorporating these variants into a targeted genotyping panel allows for cost-effective first-tier screening, particularly in regions where access to comprehensive NGS is limited. For instance, the SURF1 frameshift insertion, which we observed exclusively in the Iranian cohort, was associated with a carrier frequency of approximately 1 in 3,300. Although individually below the 1 in 1,000 threshold for inclusion in the first-tier panel, such variants may be prioritized in a complementary second-tier assay or in regional screening programs with higher baseline consanguinity rates. Furthermore, the presence of multiple distinct pathogenic alleles in genes such as POLG and NDUFS1 underscores the necessity of sequencing-based approaches rather than simple genotyping of a handful of known founder mutations. Therefore, a hybrid model combining targeted sequencing of high-priority genes with genotyping of common founder variants may offer the optimal balance of cost, coverage, and clinical sensitivity for the Iranian population.

Our study provides the first large-scale WES-derived assessment of recessive mitochondrial disease carrier burden in the Iranian population. The results of this study can be used for design of targeted panels of nuclear mitochondrial genes to identify at-risk couples, facilitating genetic counseling and reproductive decision-making in Iran. However, this study had some limitations. First, our cohort, while large (n = 9,989), is not a true population-based random sample; it includes individuals referred for genetic testing for various indications, which may introduce ascertainment bias and potentially overestimate carrier frequencies for certain disease-associated genes. Second, the majority of identified variants were classified based on in silico predictions and ACMG criteria without functional validation. Although we excluded most VUS from our analyses, seven VUS with prior literature support were retained, yet their definitive pathogenicity remains to be confirmed through segregation studies or biochemical assays. Third, our analysis focused exclusively on nuclear genes with recessive inheritance patterns and did not include mtDNA variants or dominant mitochondrial disorders, which together account for a non-negligible proportion of mitochondrial disease burden. Fourth, the Iranian population is ethnically and geographically diverse (e.g., Persians, Azeris, Kurds, Baloch, Arabs), and the combined analysis may mask significant interethnic differences in carrier frequencies. Future studies should stratify by ethnicity and consanguinity status. Finally, the capture rate estimates provided in this study represent theoretical projections based on known pathogenic variant distributions; prospective validation in a prenatal or preconception cohort is required to establish real-world clinical utility.
