Harnessing ultrasound in photocatalysis: Synthesis and piezo-enhanced effect: A review
Authors: Chunyan Li, Xiaozhuo Wang, Jianhao Wu, Jingyang Gao, Rixu Zhao, Sasa Xia, Hua Yang, Zhi Chen, Lan Li, Wen Wang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractThis review summarizes the recent advance in ultrasonic-assisted photocatalysis with a particular emphasis on photocatalyst preparation and sonophotocatalysis/piezoelectricity. The impact of ultrasound on the crystal morphology and grain size of the synthesized photocatalyst is delved. Additionally, the synergism of sonophotocatalysis and piezoelectricity are discussed, and their potential to expedite pollutant degradation, water splitting, and bacterial sterilization is highlighted.Keywords: Sonophotocatalysis, Piezoelectric enhancement, Synergism of ultrasound and light irradiation, Photocatalyst preparation, Enhanced photocatalytic performance

Introduction

Carbon peak and carbon neutrality is a major strategy to face the global challenge of climate change. Utilizing solar energy is an effective way to lessen the carbon emissions caused by fossil fuels and face current environmental problems in the meantime. Photocatalysis can convert solar power into chemical energy with the assistance of a semiconductor photocatalyst, which is known as a green, high-efficiency, and cost-effective process [1], [2]. Photocatalysis, as a promising green technology, that has been intensively investigated for decades, has made significant achievements in several key areas, such as CO2 reduction, new energy production, environmental treatment, and others [3]. However, the low visible-light absorption and rapid carriers' recombination rate are two bottlenecks that need to be resolved for the practical application of photocatalysis.

Multitudinous photocatalysts, according to the composition, can be mainly categorized into oxide photocatalysts such as TiO2 [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9] and Bi2O3[10], [11], and non-oxide photocatalysts such as CuS [12], [13], ZnS [14], CdS [15], MoS2 [16], [17] and carbon nitride [18], [19], [20]. Additionally, morphology control strategies have been employed to change the shape of photocatalysts, leading to the development of 0D, 1D, 2D, and 3D photocatalysts with/without hollow structures [21], [22], [23]. Enhancing visible-light absorption and reducing carrier complexation are crucial for boosting photocatalytic performance. To achieve such goals, several synthetic approaches such as hydrothermal synthesis [24], [25], sol–gel process [26], [27], chemical vapor deposition (CVD) [28], [29], pyrolysis [30], physical vapor deposition (PVD) [31], [32], [33], and electrochemical oxidation [34], [35] have been explored. Furthermore, assisted techniques have also been introduced to synthesize target photocatalysts with specific morphology. Among these techniques, the introduction of assisted techniques is a promising method. The microstructure and morphology of a catalyst may be significantly modified during the synthetic process by using non-traditional methods such as ultrasound, microwave, and UV radiation. These methods also aid in the realization of specific morphological features to enhance performance [36]. Ultrasound is an acoustic wave that can be categorized into low frequency (20 kHz–2 MHz) and high frequency (2–10 MHz). Low-frequency ultrasound may affect chemical processes by creating cavitation in liquid, while high-frequency ultrasound is commonly used in the medical field [37]. Ultrasound waves can be focused on narrow beams and may propagate easily in strong directions. When the intensity of ultrasound waves reaches a certain level, cavitation may occur in liquid media due to the high acceleration of mass vibrations in the propagating medium. The formed cavitation plays an essential role in material synthesis.

In addition to material preparation, ultrasonic contributes to photocatalytic efficiency by combining ultrasound with light irradiation which spurs an enhanced photocatalytic effect. However, it should be pointed out that piezoelectric enhanced photocatalysis is only applicable to photocatalysts with piezoelectric properties. The piezoelectric material may undergo deformation due to external mechanical forces such as ultrasound and microwave, generating an internal electric field. This internal electric field plays a critical role in facilitating the separation of photogenerated carriers which spurs an enhanced photocatalytic efficiency [38]. In the photocatalytic process, the reactivity parameters may be optimized due to the synergism between ultrasound and light irradiation, which offers an alternative to boost the property remarkably with an additionally simple and cost-effective ultrasonic operation [39]. The prepared photocatalysts have wide applications in disinfection, hydrogen production, and wastewater treatment. Moreover, the photocatalytic behavior may be optimized with the help of ultrasound. Fig. 1 illustrates that the number of studies investigating the cross-application of ultrasound and photocatalytic technologies was below 50 per annum before 2018. Significant accomplishments have been achieved in this field over the past 5 years, leading to a twofold increase in academic publications. Several reviews about photocatalysis combined with ultrasound have been published recently [40], [41], [42]. They mainly focused on the academic introduction of techniques and paid little attention to the applications. However, a comprehensive overview of recent advances in ultrasonic-assisted photocatalysis including the photocatalyst preparation and photocatalytic process is still relatively scarce.

Herein, this review concentrates on summarising the utilization of ultrasound in photocatalysis including the preparation of photocatalytic materials and piezoelectric enhanced effects. Initially, the fundamental principles of ultrasound-assisted synthesis, sonophotocatalysis, and piezoelectric enhanced photocatalysis are introduced. Then, the preparations of photocatalysts using ultrasound-assisted techniques were delved into, highlighting its impacts on controlling the size and morphology. Moreover, the photocatalytic process incorporated with ultrasound was investigated, and particular attention was placed on the synergism of sonophotocatalysis and piezoelectric enhanced photocatalysis. The applications of sonophotocatalysis and piezoelectric enhanced photocatalysis pollutant degradation, water splitting, and sterilization were reviewed. In the end, a conclusion and outlook/challenge were provided to boost ultrasonic-assisted photocatalyst preparation, sonophotocatalysis, and piezoelectric enhanced photocatalysis with diverse applications.

Principles of ultrasound technology

Ultrasound-assisted synthesis

Ultrasonic synthesis is an unconventional chemical synthesis technology that utilizes high-intensity ultrasonic radiation to promote chemical reactions of the synthesis. This technique relies on the sound waves' mechanical vibration to steadily increase the temperature and local high pressure of the reactants, facilitating rapid activation of the reaction system and achieving the reaction. Ultrasound stimulation induces, enhances, or even weakens one or more chemical processes within a reaction system [43]. The principle of ultrasound-assisted synthesis is the formation of acoustic cavitation, which involves a physical effect rather than a direct chemical interaction of sound waves with a substance [44]. When the strength of ultrasonic waves surpasses the medium's tensile strength, the molecular structure connected by intermolecular forces cannot maintain its integrity, which induces the formation of void within the media [45]. This provides a different condition for the synthesis of photocatalysts in comparison with the conventional ones, which may facilitate the synthesis of photocatalysts.

During the ultrasonic reaction, gas bubbles in the liquid environment will be formed and gradually grow until they reach a critical size. Subsequently, they undergo a collapse process known as the “acoustic cavitation” phenomenon (Fig. 2) [46]. This phenomenon occurs when ultrasound at a frequency of 20 kHz propagates in a liquid environment containing gas or particles. Cavitation bubbles typically nucleate in the dilution phase and grow with gas inflow during the acoustic pressure cycle. However, the bubbles become unstable and collapse during the growth process due to the gradual accumulation of ultrasonic energy. Once the bubbles collapse, the accumulated energy within the bubbles explodes quickly, producing a localized high temperature and pressure hot spot in the liquid phase [43], [47].

The collapse of the cavitation bubbles is associated with some physical and/or mechanical effects, such as shock waves and microjets, along with heat generation. In a non-homogeneous system, the cavitation bubbles near the boundary experience constrained growth and eventually undergo an asymmetrical break. This uneven breakage induces fluctuations in the surrounding acoustic field, giving rise to the formation of microjets. Microjets with high velocities can break molecular bonds within the solution, activating partial materials. Microjets exceeding velocities of 100 m·s−1 also induce superficial deformation, and corrosion or even facilitate the formation of nanostructure [49].

Sonophotocatalysis

Sonophotocatalysis, which combines ultrasonic irradiation and photocatalysis, has demonstrated the ability to enhance photocatalytic performance. This process involves sonolysis, photolysis, and sonophotolysis. The cavitation mechanism explains how ultrasound triggers the breakdown of water in the liquid phase, leading to more reactive radicals. Steady cavitation involves bubbles oscillating around their critical values and typically have a lifetime exceeding one cycle. In contrast, instantaneous cavitation is characterized by bubbles that last for less than one cycle before collapsing violently. The collapse of bubbles releases sufficient kinetic energy to initiate a chemical reaction [50]. The reactive radicals formed in the photocatalyst through photodegradation reactions are crucial to the photocatalytic decomposition of organic compounds.

The cavitation mechanism (Fig. 3) explains how ultrasound triggers the breakdown of water in the liquid phase, leading to more significant amounts of reactive radicals. In sonolysis containing semiconductor photocatalysts, the collapse of cavitation bubbles produces acoustic luminescence, which involves the emission of light energy beyond the forbidden bandwidth of the catalyst. These results induce the migration of electrons from the valence band (VB) to the conduction band (CB) while the holes (h+) remain in the VB. Consequently, the photogenerated electron-hole pairs react with the system's oxidant to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS), similar to the proposed photocatalytic process. (Eqs. The generation of additional nuclei during ultrasound promotes the acceleration of the acoustic cavitation process and stimulates the production of •OH radicals in a liquid phase, thereby accelerating the photocatalytic degradation rate [52]. Deaggregation induced by ultrasonic irradiation may increase the mass transportation of contaminants to the photocatalyst surface [48]. Furthermore, the physical effect of ultrasound prevents photocatalysts from aggregating in a liquid phase and increases the active surface area, thus enhancing photocatalytic efficiency.

Piezoelectric enhanced photocatalysis

Piezoelectric materials respond to mechanical vibrations by external deformation, which results in piezoelectric effects. Piezoelectric materials often possess non-centrosymmetric structures which can promote enhanced electrochemical and photochemical behaviors [54]. Piezoelectric materials such as ZnO [55], BaTiO3 [56], bismuth-based materials [57], and transition metal sulfides [58] exhibit the piezoelectric effect. When a piezoelectric material experiences an external force in a fixed direction, an internal electrode phenomenon is present, resulting in a charged surface. Furthermore, the direction of the external force may influence the polarity of the charge. Once the external force is withdrawn, the material returns to the uncharged state. The amount of charge generated by the material is straightly proportional to the magnitude of the external force [59]. The induced internal electric field by the piezoelectric effect can facilitate the separation of photoinduced electron-hole pairs and enhance the catalytic performance. The construction of heterojunctions is the primary strategy to improve the piezoelectric effect. For instance, Meng et al. [60] fabricated a heterojunction of Pt/BaTiO3 for piezoelectric enhanced photocatalysis, showing a 1.32-fold increase of methyl orange photodegradation than that under normal light conditions, which confirmed the enhancement of piezoelectric effects on the photocatalytic activity. Liu et al. [61] fabricated BaTiO3@ReS2 Schottky heterostructure to facilitate electron transport, demonstrating a 2.44 times higher activity than conventional photocatalysis. Morphology control is another strategy to enhance the piezoelectric effect, and diverse nanostructures including nanorods, nanosheets, and nanowires have been synthesized, which facilitates the conversion of mechanical stress into polarized charge due to the notable bending compliance and flexibility.

Photocatalysis is a process that utilizes solar energy for a range of applications, and the efficiency is governed by light absorption, the rate of charge separation, as well as surface reactions. However, the rapid reconfiguration of electron-hole pairs severely reduces its photocatalytic performance. Using piezoelectric catalyst in photocatalysis efficaciously inhibits the reconfiguration of photoproduced electron-hole pairs. Ultrasonic waves induce piezoelectric photocatalysis by generating cavitation bubbles that create pressure on dispersed piezoelectric materials, causing periodic electric potential [62], [63]. The piezoelectric potential in the piezoelectric catalytic electrode enhances the piezoelectric photocatalytic coupling effect, allowing the energy band bending to promote photogenerated charge separation in the piezoelectric photocatalyst. Redistributing polarized charges also changes the carrier transportation channels within the materials and at the interfaces. When a piezoelectric material is subjected to a piezoelectric field generated by mechanical deformation, electron jumps occur in the VB and CB of the material. This process provides an additional electron-hole source for the flowing separation and transport [59], [63], [64].

As a generalized example, Dong et al. [65] presented the internal electric field variation of Bi-MOFs (CAU-17) materials with piezoelectric properties (Fig. 4), which were prepared by the ultrasonic method. Fig. 4a illustrates the internal structure of the catalyst without ultrasonic vibration, where the forbidden bandwidth is larger than the redox potential of O2/–O2 and OH/–OH. Upon ultrasonic vibration, the internal deformation of the catalyst generates the piezoelectric field, causing the flow of independent electrons and holes from the CB and VB toward the crystal surface (Fig. 4b). As the charge accumulates, the surface redox reaction slows down (Fig. 4c). Subsequently, upon a decrease in the ultrasonic induction force, the potential equilibrium is disrupted, allowing the charge to be transferred back and initiating a new redox reaction (Fig. 4d).

Application of ultrasound in the preparation of photocatalytic material

There are various ultrasonic techniques. Ultrasonic baths, ultrasonic horns, and ultrasound probe techniques (Fig. 5a, b, c) can be employed as indirect high-intensity ultrasonic treatments for the synthesis of photocatalysts. Ultrasonic baths are commonly utilized due to their easy and simple operation. However, ultrasonic baths cannot guarantee the uniform of acquired energy, which heavily relies on the probe and its placement [66]. Additionally, the bath's size may also affect the particle size of the synthesized material. Ultrasonic horns have a variety of vibration modes categorized as longitudinal, bending, and torsional vibration, which is fixed once the size and shape of the horn are decided. Nevertheless, the tip of the ultrasonic horn is susceptible to corrosion. The ultrasound probe technique provides high-intensity ultrasonic energy, which can directly synthesize specific photocatalysts due to the huge power except for the dispersion of built-up material. Whereas, the energy released from the probe tends to focus on a limited area. Moradi et al. [67] demonstrated the preparation of FeTiO3/GO composites for photocatalytic degradation of phenol. They utilized a high-intensity ultrasonic horn to obtain FeTiO3, while the ultrasonic bath facilitated the incorporation of carboxyl functional groups onto graphene oxide (GO). Under the influence of the ultrasonic environment, covalent bonds were formed between FeTiO3 and GO, giving a composite structure. Balu et al. [68] used the probe technique to exfoliate g-C3N4 for better integration with other materials. In addition, the ultrasonic spray atomization technique (Fig. 5d) allows the solution to be atomized into droplets with a narrower particle size distribution. Yudin et al. [69] employed this technique to prepare nickel oxide microspheres with hollow nanostructures (Fig. 6a).

As an efficient and environment-friendly material-assisted technology, ultrasound is frequently employed in conjunction with other preparation methods for synthesizing photocatalysts. Ultrasonic-assisted hydrothermal method as a ready method is frequently used to synthesize photocatalysts. Mahmoodi et al. [71] employed this technique during the synthesis of GO to prevent material agglomeration and increase the specific surface area. The pollutant degradation efficiency is highly improved because of the increased adsorption ability. Tian et al. [7] employed ultrasound to disperse g-C3N4 and TiO2 nanoparticles uniformly in the solution, ensuring a complete reaction for the hydrothermal synthesis of rod-like TiO2 and g-C3N4 composites (RT-CN). Zargazi et al. [72] utilized a cup horn to regulate the morphology of bismuth tungstate (BWO) nanomaterials, resulting in hollow microspheres with special flower shapes (Fig. 6b), which thereby increased the specific surface area to boost the photocatalytic degradation efficiency. Yadav et al. [73] realized the nanoflower-like FeCo2O4 from a probe ultrasound-assisted hydrothermal method (Fig. 6c).

Sol-gel methods are widely used in preparing photocatalytic material due to their chemical simplicity and versatility. The ultrasound-assisted sol–gel method provides an avenue for process intensification with boosted photocatalytic performance. Ambati et al. [36] synthesized Fe(III)-doped TiO2 catalysts by combining ultrasonic bath and conventional sol–gel method. They compared the ultrasonically synthesized catalysts (Fig. 6d) with the conventionally synthesized catalysts (Fig. 6e) and found that ultrasonic assistance results in smaller catalyst particle size and more uniform surface. This indicates that the ultrasound-assisted method is superior to the conventional method. Not coincidentally, another interesting example is that Yankovych et al. [77] also used an ultrasound-assisted method to prepare TiO2 and granular activated carbon composite to degrade 4-halogenated phenols. The ultrasound treatment resulted in a reduced size of the photocatalyst particles.

Indeed, ultrasound assistance can also increase vacancy defects and tune the size of the material. Guan et al. [74] proposed that the ultrasound-assisted solvothermal method not only induces oxygen vacancies on the surface of BiOBr(0 0 1) but also regulates its structural morphology and component composition. The intensity of the characteristic peak of BiOBr increased with longer sonication time (Fig. 6f), and the CO generation rate reached the maximum of 234.05 μmmol·g−1·h−1 after 20-min sonication. This value is 3.3 times higher than the sample without sonication (Fig. 6g). Veisi et al. [75] used ultrasonic activation to promote the in situ reduction of Pd ions to Pd nanoparticles, enabling their attachment to the surface of the Fe3O4 nanocomposite (Fig. 6h).

In addition, researchers have investigated the preparation of photocatalysts using ultrasound-assisted UV photolysis co-precipitation. For instance, Han et al. [78] successfully fabricated a nanocomposite with excellent photocatalytic properties by co-precipitating a CdS and Ag2S precipitate suspension via ultrasonic treatment. Similarly, Wang et al. [76] prepared composites of Bi2NbO5F with rectorite through the ultrasound-assisted molten salt method (Fig. 6i), which provides a direction for synthesizing responsive clay and Bi-based tunable structural material.

Several factors, such as ultrasound frequency, irradiation time, and the size of the ultrasound equipment reactor, can influence ultrasound-assisted results. Ambati et al. [36] and Guan et al. [74] stated that the particle size decreased as the ultrasonic irradiation time increased.

Ultrasonic devices may be divided in different types such as bath and horn types, and each type has certain size specifications. Neppolian et al. [79] undertook a study examining reactor size's implication on photocatalyst morphology. They found that particle size and pore size tend to increase with reactor size, but the specific surface area and pore capacity decrease. Proper precautions should be taken to prevent tip erosion and minimize potential contamination, ensuring the purity and integrity of the final photocatalyst.

Combination of ultrasound and light for photocatalytic applications

Photocatalytic technology is a promising and widely used technology, and how to boost the efficiency of the photocatalytic process is also a question that has been pondered. Therefore, some studies have begun to combine ultrasound and light and utilize the synergistic effect between them to enhance photocatalytic efficiency. Sonophotocatalysis and piezoelectric enhanced photocatalysis have been developed, which have a broad applications in environmental control, water splitting, and bacterial disinfection [93], [94], [95].

Sonophotocatalysis

Ultrasound is an effective tool to facilitate photocatalytic reactions in conjunction with sunlight, resulting in hybrid advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) technology. The “Advanced Oxidation Processes” concept was first introduced in 1987 [96]. AOPs encompass various techniques such as Fenton oxidation, ozone oxidation, ultrasonic oxidation, photocatalytic oxidation, and others. Among them, ultrasound can be effectively combined with other AOPs to improve the behavior. The enhanced property may be resulted from the increased reactive species which is related to the formed cavitation bubbles produced by ultrasound. This combination is attributed to the formation of an increased number of cavitation bubbles as the ultrasound frequency increases, resulting in the generation of a large quantity of hydroxyl radicals. Sonophotocatalysis, on the other hand, is a hybrid oxidation process that integrates ultrasound and photocatalysis [96]. Sonophotocatalysis has been extensively studied in environmental management, particularly in the degradation of pollutants. This technique offers great potential for addressing environmental challenges and achieving efficient pollutant degradation.

Sonophotocatalysis harnesses the generation of reactive radicals for redox reactions through the generation of cavitation bubbles. The synergy influence of ultrasound and photocatalysis results in higher photocatalytic performance. Isari et al. [97] developed a novel ultrasonically coupled photocatalyst, which first synthesized Cu co-doped TiO2 and modified a single-walled carbon nanotube. The obtained composite was utilized for the treatment of pharmaceutical effluents. The results indicated that the bubble content increased with ultrasound intensity increased, which in turn accelerated the production of active radicals. Ultrasonic photocatalysis degraded 93% of COD and 89 % of TOC within 180 mins. This area of such advanced oxidation techniques is still relatively unexplored for removing wine lees. Using ultrasound and non-homogeneous photocatalysts to remove wine lees has been investigated by Poblete et al. [98]. They found that sonophotocatalysis exhibited superior performance for removing polyphenols from wine lees, achieving a removal rate of up to 68%. The synergy effect between ultrasound and photocatalysis outperformed either approach alone. Xu et al. [99] conducted extensive research on the sonophotocatalytic degradation of Rhodamine B by TiO2 and explored the influence s of a variety of factors on the degradation of this pollutant. Their findings highlighted that ultrasound induced more hydroxyl radicals under the cavitation effect, which played a crucial role in accelerating the degradation efficiency of Rhodamine B in water (Fig. 7a). Similarly, Kucukcongar et al. [100] used a similar approach to identify the optimal conditions for the degradation of reactive dyes (Reactive Red 195) by considering various influencing factors. Another notable example is the development of a new sonophotocatalytic wastewater treatment system using TiO2 thin films by Hu et al. [101], which demonstrated excellent results in the degradation of Rhodamine B.

The combination of ultrasound shows enhanced efficiency of photocatalytic water splitting. However, the current research is limited in this area. Kharissova et al. [102] used a homogeneous photocatalyst (Theraphthal) for water splitting. The ultrasound-assisted photocatalysis process generated reactive oxygen species, and the synergies between ultrasound and light in sonophotocatalysis produced excess oxygen-saturated radicals. These radicals integrate with hydrogen atoms to form water and hydrogen peroxide, promoting O2 production and inhibiting the formation of H2 (Fig. 7b). Another interesting example of water splitting is the sonophotocatalytic water splitting for hydrogen formation, as reported by Singh and Sinha et al. [103]. It was found that higher H2 efficiencies could be obtained with sonication comparing the conditions without sonication. Ultrasound facilitated faster desorption of the H2 on the catalyst surface.

Ultrasound has been investigated extensively for the photocatalytic decomposition of pollutants and has recently been applied to sterilization treatments. Fig. 7c illustrates the reaction mechanism of sonophotocatalytic sterilization. The sonosensitizer is activated through a photochemical-like pathway, producing reactive oxygen species (ROS). These ROS and highly reactive substances can react with miscellaneous biological molecules, including proteins and nucleic acids. In the sonophotocatalysis-induced bactericidal process, the acoustic sensitizer with photocatalytic activity attaches to the surface of the bacterial cell. It is excited by the corresponding wavelength of sonoluminescent light. The energy released is then transferred to the native fluid to generate ROS as the sonosensitiser returns from the excited state to the ground one. These ROS further mediates photo-oxidative damage to the bacterial cells [104]. Yan et al. [105] researched the inactivation rate of E. coli by ZnO under the combined effect of ultrasound and light. They found that sonophotocatalysis increased the content of hydroxyl radicals, which in turn increased the dissolved oxygen in the solution (Fig. 7d).

Piezoelectric enhanced photocatalysis

Besides, many photocatalyst materials have piezoelectric properties. These piezoelectric materials are induced by externally applied ultrasonic waves, which alter the internal piezoelectric potential of the material and promote electron leaping, thus enhancing photocatalytic performance. Unlike sonophotocatalytic degradation of pollutants, piezoelectric enhanced photocatalysis uses ultrasound waves to transfer external pressure during the photocatalytic process. This pressure-induced deformation of the material structure generates a large number of polarized charges, leading to a change in piezoelectric potential. The resulting steeper bending of the energy band favors the enhancement of electron and hole transfer rates, thereby boosting the effect of photocatalysis [106], [107]. Liu et al. [108] utilized TiO2-coated BaTiO3 piezoelectric material (BT-TOAR) to generate piezoelectric fields under ultrasound to enhance the photogenerated photocatalytic efficiency. The continuous formation of the piezoelectric field during the repeated deformation process led to the generation of a large induced charge, which in turn promoted the photogenerated carrier separation (Fig. 8a). Yu et al. [109] reported the degradation of organics using Ag3PO4/ZnO nanowires. The complexes achieved 98.2% degradation of methylene blue (MB) under the synergism of light and ultrasound, which increased the intrinsic piezoelectric effect of the material by ultrasound, thus reducing the electron-hole recombination rate (Fig. 8b). Similarly, Chen et al. [110] proposed that ultrasound-induced change in piezoelectric potential causes free electrons and holes to diffuse in the counter direction during the degradation of organic pollutants with BiOIO3. In this work, photocatalysis is coupled with a built-in voltage field to increase the charge separation efficiency of the photocatalysis [111], [112]. Likewise, Li et al. [113] reported similar findings for the degradation of Rhodamine using BiOCl/NaNbO3 heterojunction piezoelectric composites. The efficiency of pollutant degradation in sonophotocatalytic and piezoelectric enhanced photocatalytic processes can be influenced by several factors including ultrasound frequency, ultrasound power, irradiation time, and initial pH. While Hu et al. [114] used g-C3N4 for hydrogen production through water splitting, a piezoelectrically enhanced photocatalytic approach was adopted to improve the hydrogen production yield. Ultrasonically induced carbon vacancy enrichment in g-C3N4 retained more charged polarization charges at the surface reaction sites, leading to hydrogen production efficiency of 12.16 mmol·g−1·h−1 without co-catalyst (Fig. 8c). Zhao et al. [115] have successfully produced single-crystal CdS nanosheets, which are piezoelectric materials capable of splitting water to produce hydrogen. The mechanism revealed that the single-crystal CdS nanosheets would be deformed to enhance their intrinsic piezoelectric effect under ultrasonic vibration. There is a positive correlation between the catalytic activity of the catalyst and the piezoelectric field or deformation; meanwhile, the intensity of ultrasound directly influenced the degree of catalyst deformation.

Piezoelectric materials have also shown potential for sterilization application. The intrinsic piezoelectric characteristics of piezoelectric materials play a major role in disrupting bacterial cell membranes. A notable example is the work conducted by Gazvoda et al. [116], where poly-L-lactide (PLLA) films were used for the degradation of Gram-positive bacteria and Escherichia coli (E. coli). In saline solution, E. coli appeared intact and uniformly stained, while upon contact with PLLA films, E. coli exhibited unevenly stained, indicating obvious damage and rupture of the cell membrane. This demonstrates the effectiveness of piezoelectric materials in promoting bacterial cell membrane disruption, leading to sterilization.

In conclusion, sonophotocatalysis and piezoelectric enhanced photocatalysis have great potential in applications due to the improved photocatalytic efficiency. However, they may also induce some shortcomings. Firstly, the introduction of ultrasound in the photocatalytic process requires additional apparatus and increases the operational complexity. Secondly, the efficiency of sonophotocatalysis and piezoelectric enhanced photocatalysis can be influenced by several factors including ultrasound frequency, ultrasound power, irradiation time, and initial pH which needs more precise operation. Thirdly, the current applications of sonophotocatalysis and piezoelectric enhanced photocatalysis mainly focus on the heterogeneous process, and little attention is paid to homogeneous one [102]. Finally, piezoelectric enhanced photocatalysis can be only applicable to specific materials with piezoelectric effect.

Summary

Ultrasound has been integrated into photocatalysis to enhance its efficiency, which has been divided into ultrasound-assisted photocatalyst synthesis, sonophotocatalysis, and piezoelectric enhanced photocatalysis. The introduced ultrasound has a prominent role in increasing the specific surface area, dispersion, and particle size of the synthesized photocatalyst. Sonophotocatalysis may generate more active radicals in the photocatalytic system to boost redox reactions, and piezoelectric enhanced photocatalysis promotes the electron transition and reduces the recombination of photogenerated charges, thus enhancing the photocatalytic efficiency.

However, ultrasound needs additional apparatus which also increases the complexity of operation. During ultrasound-assisted synthesis, new impurities may be produced since the introduction of the ultrasound facility. Sonophotocatalysis may be influenced by diverse experimental parameters, and a comprehensive study of them is still insufficient. Piezoelectric enhanced photocatalysis can be merely applicable for those photocatalysts with a piezoelectric effect. Additionally, the current applications of sonophotocatalysis and piezoelectric enhanced photocatalysis are limited.

Efforts may be made to address the above issues, and the strategies are below: 1) integrating the ultrasound unit with photocatalytic equipment to simplify the operation. 2) the parameters including the quantity of photocatalyst, light intensity, reaction temperature, ultrasound frequency and intensity, pH, and initial concentration may be comprehensively investigated to clarify the factor influencing the photocatalytic behavior. 3) Additionally, the energetic efficiency and direct measurements of the cavitation concentration may be strengthened. 4) Similar to sonophotocatalysis, the factors influencing piezoelectric photocatalysis may be decided precisely. Additionally, engineering new semiconductors with piezoelectric properties is crucial for improving the performance. 5) The current application of sonophotocatalysis and piezoelectric enhanced photocatalysis primarily concentrates on water splitting and organic degradation reactions. Their potential applications in CO2 reduction, H2O2 generation, nitrogen fixation, and other fields need to be developed.

Anyway, this overview offers notable strategies for improving the properties by the combination of ultrasound and photocatalysis, which overcomes the limitation of a sole technique. This overview provides an alternative to tune the photocatalytic properties, thereby promoting the practical application of the photocatalytic technique.
