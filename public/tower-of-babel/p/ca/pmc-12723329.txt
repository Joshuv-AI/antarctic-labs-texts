Chemoresistive and Catalytic Dual‐Signal Pd‐WO3 MEMS Sensor for Reliable H2 Monitoring
Authors: Seon Ju Park, Soo Min Lee, Sung Hwan Cho, Hee Ryeong Kwon, Youngmin Kim, Do Joon Yoo, In‐Sung Hwang, Ho Won Jang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe demand for H2 monitoring in its production and utilization grows rapidly to ensure operational efficiency and safety. Given the high flammability of H2, it is crucial to detect levels below the lower flammability limit (4%) in a sensitive, rapid, and reliable manner. Although integrating multiple sensor types can improve selectivity and accuracy, such systems often involve bulky architecture and time‐consuming operation. This study presents a dual‐signal H2 sensor based on a single microelectromechanical system (MEMS) platform, enabling miniaturization, low power consumption, and robust performance. Pd nanoparticle‐decorated WO3 nanorods (Pd‐WO3 NRs) are employed as sensing material and integrated into the MEMS substrate. The catalytic Pd promotes H2 oxidation, enabling the sensor to simultaneously monitor resistance changes in the Pd‐WO3 NRs and temperature variations in the microheater. This dual‐signal approach corresponds to concurrent chemoresistive‐ and catalytic combustion‐type gas sensing. The sensor exhibits high sensitivity and selectivity toward H2, with detection limits of 0.01%–0.02% for dual signals, and demonstrates excellent reliability under varying humidity levels, repetitive cycles, and static gas exposure conditions. This work provides a practical foundation for the development of single gas sensors with dual output signals, enabling robust and energy‐efficient H2 detection in diverse environmental and industrial scenarios.Keywords: H2 , MEMS, Pd‐WO3 , catalytic combustion, chemoresistive, dual‐signal

Introduction

With the growing interest in hydrogen (H2)‐based technologies, including green H2 production, storage, and utilization in petroleum refining, ammonia synthesis, and fuel cell applications, reliable H2 monitoring has become increasingly important for ensuring both operational efficiency and safety.[ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 ] H2 is an odorless, colorless, and highly flammable gas with a lower explosion limit of 4%, making it prone to explosion even at low concentrations.[ 6 ] Consequently, the development of highly sensitive, selective and reliable H2 detection systems is essential to mitigate potential hazards in industrial environments.

Conventional gas analytical techniques, such as gas chromatography and mass spectroscopy offer high accuracy and selectivity.[ 7 , 8 ] However, their bulky instrument, high cost, and complex, time‐consuming operational requirements limit their suitability for real‐time and on‐site gas monitoring. As an alternative, gas sensors, including chemoresistive, catalytic combustion, potentiometric and colorimetric types, have attracted significant interest owing to their simple operation, cost‐effectiveness, rapid response/recovery kinetics, and ease of miniaturization.[ 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 ] These sensors function by transducing the physiochemical interactions between the target gases and sensing materials into electrical, optical, or mechanical signals. Despite these advantages, single‐signal gas sensors often suffer from poor selectivity and limited reliability under harsh interfering conditions, hindering their broader deployment in real‐world applications.

To address these challenges, various approaches have been proposed, including the design of highly selective sensing materials, the implementation of sensor arrays, and the development of multivariable sensing platforms.[ 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 ] Among these, dual/multi‐signal sensors, which extract multiple, partially independent signals from a single sensing material, have emerged as a promising platform toward the next‐generation sensing technologies, particularly in the context of the Internet of Things (IoT). By enabling internal self‐validation through signal correlation, such sensors improve recognition accuracy and reduce false positives without significantly increasing system complexity or size. In particular, chemoresistive signals have been combined with optical, capacitive, gravimetric, and potentiometric transduction modes to enhance sensor reliability.[ 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 ] For instance, Zhang et al. fabricated a dual‐signal sensor that combined chemoresistive and potentiometric responses by depositing SnO2 onto a solid electrolyte substrate.[ 14 ] The sensor provided two distinct outputs derived from the resistance change of SnO2 and the potential variation at the electrolyte electrode upon gas exposure. Similarly, Gao et al. developed a dual‐signal sensor that simultaneously measured chemoresistive and gravimetric responses of a sensing material to discriminate alcohols.[ 24 ] While these dual‐signal approaches enhance sensing reliability through self‐validation, their complexity and fabrication cost often hinder practical scalability.

Microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) present an attractive platform for realizing compact, low‐cost, and energy‐efficient dual‐signal sensors.[ 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 ] The microheater in MEMS not only acts as a heat source but can also serve as a resistance temperature detector (RTD), wherein the temperature‐dependent resistance reflects the exothermic heat generated from catalytic gas reactions; thereby enabling catalytic combustion sensing. In this work, we report a chemoresistive‐catalytic combustion dual‐signal sensor using MEMS platform. The device comprises interdigitated electrodes (IDEs) and a microheater on a compact MEMS, onto which Pd‐WO3 nanorods (NRs) are deposited. Pd has been widely employed as a catalyst to improve H2 sensitivity and selectivity of metal oxide‐based gas sensors through its catalytic activity and H2 spillover effect.[ 29 , 30 ] WO3 is also extensively utilized as a sensing material due to its high sensitivity to reducing gases such as H2.[ 31 ] Accordingly, Pd‐WO3 was selected as a sensing material for proposed dual‐signal H2 sensor. Upon H2 exposure, the Pd‐WO3 layer generates simultaneous signals: a change in electrical resistance of Pd‐WO3 NRs (chemoresistive response) and a temperature‐induced resistance shift in the Pt‐based microheater (catalytic combustion response). Through the generation of these two distinct outputs that remain stable even in humid or interfering environments, the proposed sensor offers potential for enhanced reliability and selectivity through self‐validation. The dual‐signal sensor exhibits rapid response/recovery dynamics, ultra‐low power consumption, and good stability, making it suitable for real‐time, on‐site hydrogen safety monitoring. We believe that our findings could pave the way for the design of simple, low‐cost, energy‐saving and highly efficient dual‐signal sensors.

Results and Discussion

Fabrication and Characterization of Dual‐Signal Pd‐WO3 MEMS Sensor

The fabrication process of dual‐signal Pd‐WO3 MEMS sensor is illustrated in Figure 1a. The WO3 NRs were grown on the MEMS platform via a glancing angle deposition (GLAD) method using electron‐beam evaporator (Figure 1a1). During deposition, the incident angle of vapor flux induces a self‐shadowing effect, resulting in a porous thin film composed of vertically‐aligned NRs. This nanostructure morphology was chosen as the sensing layer due to its large surface‐to‐volume ratio, which facilitates effective interaction with analyte gases.[ 32 , 33 ] The MEMS platform, consisting of a microheater and IDEs, was utilized to enable the extraction of two independent sensing signals (Figure 1b). The microheater surrounded the IDEs to minimize heat loss and improve power efficiency. The detailed mechanism for dual‐signal acquisition is discussed in Section 2.2. The as‐deposited WO3 NRs were annealed at 500 °C for 2 h to promote crystallization. Subsequently, Pd nanoparticles (NPs) were decorated onto the WO3 NRs via a facile solution‐based method (Figure 1a2).

Schematic illustration of a1) E‐beam GLAD process and a2) Pd‐WO3 NRs fabrication process. b) False‐colored SEM image of MEMS platform after deposition of Pd‐WO3 NRs. c) Cross‐sectional and d) Top‐view SEM images of Pd‐WO3 NRs deposited on SiO2/Si substrate. e) TEM, f) EDS mapping, and g) HR‐TEM images of Pd‐WO3 NRs.

The structural characteristics of the Pd‐WO3 NRs were analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). The cross‐sectional and top‐view SEM images confirmed that the highly porous Pd‐WO3 thin film was composed of well‐oriented NRs with a thickness of 430 nm, forming a highly gas‐accessible nanostructure (Figure 1c,d). The TEM image revealed that Pd NPs were uniformly distributed on the surface of the WO3 NRs, with an average diameter of 6.87 nm (Figure 1e; Figure S1, Supporting Information). Furthermore, energy‐dispersive X‐ray spectroscopy (EDS) analysis confirmed the well distribution of W, O, and Pd elements throughout the NRs array, with the Pd content determined to be 0.84 wt.% (Figure 1f; Table S1, Supporting Information). The high‐resolution TEM (HR‐TEM) image confirmed the crystallinity of WO3, exhibiting lattice fringes with interplanar spacings of 3.66 and 2.66 Å, corresponding to (200) and (2¯02) planes of monoclinic WO3 (Figure 1g).[ 34 ] Moreover, crystal lattice plane spacings of 2.25 and 1.96 Å, which correspond to the (1¯11), (1¯11¯), and (002) planes of cubic Pd, were observed (Figure S2, Supporting Information).[ 35 , 36 ] The crystal structures of Pd‐WO3 and pristine WO3 were investigated by X‐ray diffraction (XRD) (Figure 2a). Both samples exhibited a monoclinic WO3 phase (PDF 01‐089‐4476), indicating that the crystal structure of WO3 remained after Pd loading. No Pd‐related peaks were observed in the XRD pattern due to the extremely low amount of Pd, which is below the detection limit of instrument. The chemical compositions of Pd‐WO3 and WO3 were examined by X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). The XPS survey spectrum confirmed the presence of Pd, W, and O in Pd‐WO3 (Figure S3, Supporting Information). The Pd 3d spectra of Pd‐WO3 were deconvoluted into two pairs of doublets with a spin‐orbit doublet separation value of 5.2 eV (Figure 2b). The Pd 3d 5/2 spectra had two peaks at 337.0 and 335.2 eV, which are identified as Pd2+ (PdO) and metallic Pd0, respectively.[ 37 ] These results demonstrate that Pd exists in the form of metallic Pd and PdO after the heat treatment of the sample. In the W 4f spectra of WO3, two peaks corresponding to W 4f 5/2 and W 4f 7/2 with binding energies of 37.3 and 35.2 eV were observed, indicating the W6+ oxidation state (Figure 2c).[ 38 , 39 , 40 ] For Pd‐WO3, these peaks shifted to 37.5 and 35.4 eV, respectively, indicating electron transfer from WO3 to Pd/PdO due to the work function difference (ΦPd = 5.1 eV, ΦPdO = 5.2 eV, and ΦWO3 = 4.9 eV).[ 41 , 42 , 43 ] The O 1s spectra were deconvoluted into three components at 530.1, 531.1, and 531.9 eV, corresponding to lattice oxygen (O2−, denoted as Olat), oxygen vacancy‐related species (O−, denoted as Ovac), and chemisorbed oxygen (H2O, denoted as OH2O), respectively (Figure 2d).[ 44 , 45 ] The Ovac component is generally associated with adsorbed oxygen species near vacancy sites, which serve as active centers for oxygen adsorption. A notable increase in the Ovac/Olat ratio was observed for the Pd‐WO3 (Ovac/Olat = 0.37) compared to pristine WO3 (Ovac/Olat = 0.20), demonstrating the enhanced oxygen adsorption by Pd decoration.

Gas‐Sensing Characteristics of Pd‐WO3 MEMS Sensor

The as‐fabricated Pd‐WO3 MEMS sensor was placed in a sensor measurement system with probe station, and analyte gases were injected using a mass flow controller to evaluate its gas‐sensing properties (Figure 3a). The MEMS platform comprises two electrically isolated electrode components—Pt IDEs and an Pt microheater, each serving a distinct sensing function. The Pt IDEs are used to measure the resistance of the Pd‐WO3 NRs (R chem), functioning as the chemoresistive gas‐sensing channel. The Pt microheater provides the thermal activation energy required for gas oxidation on Pd‐WO3 via Joule‐heating and simultaneously functions as a catalytic combustion‐type sensing channel (RTD sensor), where its own resistance (R comb) varies with temperature. The Pd‐WO3 NRs serve dual roles—as the gas‐sensing material in the chemoresistive channel and as the catalyst for surface combustion reactions in the catalytic combustion channel. During gas exposure, R chem and R comb were measured simultaneously.

Schematic illustrations of a) a sensor measurement system with probe station, b) sensing mechanisms of dual‐signal MEMS‐based sensor. Dynamic c) R chem and d) R comb transients of Pd‐WO3 NRs MEMS under different V h toward 0.4% H2, under 50% RH.

The changes in R chem and R comb arise from the surface oxidation reactions of Pd‐WO3 NRs, leading to dual gas‐response signals originating from electron transfer and exothermic heat release (Figure 3b). At elevated temperatures (>100 °C), oxygen molecules in the ambient atmosphere adsorb onto the Pd‐WO3 surface and form oxygen ion species—O2 − (<150 °C), O− (150–400 °C), and O2− (>400 °C)—creating an electron depletion layer (EDL).[ 46 ] When H2 is introduced, they undergo exothermic oxidation reactions with surface ionized oxygen, thereby injecting electrons into the EDL of Pd‐WO3 and increasing the temperature of the microheater. These result in a variation of R chem and R comb, respectively. This dual‐channel sensing platform enables concurrent acquisition of chemoresistive and catalytic combustion gas‐sensing signals, enhancing the reliability of H2 detection.

The gas‐sensing characteristics of the Pd‐WO3 MEMS sensor toward H2 were first evaluated under different voltages (V h) applied to the microheater: 0.5 V (≈109.1 °C), 1.0 V (≈232.5 °C), and 1.5 V (≈355.9 °C). The corresponding heater temperatures were estimated by correlating the resistance of microheater (R h) obtained under external heating and voltage‐biased heating conditions (Figure S4, Supporting Information). Notably, the MEMS sensor achieved a working temperature of 232.5 °C with only 19.1 mW of power, indicating its significant low‐ power operation. As depicted in Figure 3c, R chem decreased upon exposure to 0.4% H2 (relative humidity = RH = 50%), which is a typical sensing characteristic of n‐type semiconductors. Accordingly, the gas response based on R chem is defined as S chem = R a/R g −1, where R a and R g indicate the resistances in air and gas, respectively. In the case of microheater, R comb exhibited an increase upon exposure to 0.4% H2, attributed to additional heat generated by the exothermic oxidation of H2 on the catalytic surface (Figure 3d). Therefore, the gas response based on R comb is defined as S comb = (R g/R a−1) × 100 (%). Since both chemoresistive and catalytic combustion type sensors showed the highest H2 response at V h = 1.0 V, the optimal operating V h was determined to be 1.0 V.

To demonstrate the feasibility of the Pd‐WO3 MEMS sensor for monitoring flammable H2 accumulation, we assessed its dual‐mode sensing behavior under various H2 concentrations ranging from 0.03% to 0.8% (50% RH) at V h = 1.0 V (Figure 4a). Both R chem and R comb simultaneously exhibited distinct and reproducible sensing transients, with responses increasing proportionally to the H2 concentration. At 0.4% H2, the Pd‐WO3 MEMS sensor exhibited responses of 14900.17 (S chem) and 1.26% (S comb), respectively. The superior H2 response is attributed to the synergistic effect of Pd decoration and highly porous NRs structure. First, the catalytic effect of Pd and PdO promotes the H2 dissociation and increases the amount of adsorbed oxygen species on the sensing materials, thereby enhancing oxidation reactions.[ 29 ] In addition, the Pd/PdO decoration induces electronic sensitization by reducing the concentration of background charge carrier due to work function differences between Pd/PdO and WO3, resulting in enhanced chemoresistive variations.[ 47 , 48 ] In contrast, the pristine WO3 MEMS sensor showed relatively low responses of 17.07 (S chem) and 0.46% (S comb) to 0.4% H2, confirming that Pd NPs served a vital role in achieving sensitive H2 detection (Figure S5, Supporting Information). Additionally, the highly gas accessible microstructure of NRs could facilitate the gas adsorption on the surface, further enhancing sensor response.[ 32 ] As shown in Figure S6 (Supporting Information), highly porous Pd‐WO3 NRs sensors showed 1.98‐fold and 1.54‐fold higher S chem and S comb compared to dense Pd‐WO3 thin film sensors (S chem = 7789.0, S comb = 0.81% to 0.4% H2 under 50% RH), highlighting the vital role of gas‐accessible microstructure in sensing properties. As shown in Figure 4b, both sensing signals exhibited good linearity with respect to H2 concentration, described by the equations y = 0.90x + 4.48 and y = 1.12x + 0.55, for the chemoresistive and catalytic combustion channels, respectively. Based on a signal‐to‐noise ratio threshold of 3, the calculated detection limits were 0.02% and 0.01% for S chem and S comb, respectively, demonstrating its capability for sub‐percent‐level hydrogen detection.[ 49 , 50 ] The Pd‐WO3 MEMS sensor also exhibited rapid, reversible response dynamics in both transduction modes. In the chemoresistive channel, R chem dramatically drops upon exposure to H2, then gradually increases and stabilizes. Therefore, we defined the response time (τ res,chem) as the time required from gas injection to the point of R chem reach 90% resistance change from minimum to stabilized R g. In contrast, R comb continuously increases during gas exposure, thereby its τ res,comb is defined as the time required to reach 90% of the maximum response. The recovery times (τ rec,chem and τ rec,comb) for both modes are defined as the time required to return to 90% of the baseline resistance after gas removal. The measured τ res/τ rec of Pd‐WO3 MEMS sensor toward 0.4% H2 were 118/209 s for chemoresistive channel, and 25/29 s for catalytic combustion channel, respectively (Figure 4c,d). Notably, both τ res and τ rec were significantly shorter than those of the dense Pd‐WO3 thin film MEMS sensors (184/1281 s for chemoresistive channel, and 75/36 s for catalytic combustion channel), further confirming the advantage of the gas‐accessible NR structure in facilitating rapid gas diffusion and desorption (Figure S6, Supporting Information). These results highlight the dual‐signal functionality of the Pd‐WO3 MEMS sensor, enabling reliable detection of H2 at concentrations below the lower explosion limits with fast, stable, and fully reversible behavior.

Dynamic c) S chem and d) S comb transients of Pd‐WO3 MEMS upon exposure to 0.4% H2 (50% RH). e) S chem and S comb to 0.4% H2 under varying humid conditions (Dry and 30%, 50%, and 70% RH). f) S chem and S comb to 2% H2 under static flow conditions (50% RH). g) S chem and h) S comb upon exposure to various analyte gases (gas concentrations=0.1% for H2, C2H5OH, CO, NH3 and 0.01% for C7H8, NH3 under dry conditions).

The stability and selectivity are critical parameters for evaluating the practical applicability of gas sensors. To evaluate environmental robustness, the humidity tolerance of the Pd‐WO3 MEMS sensor was investigated by measuring the sensing characteristics to 0.4% H2 under varying RH levels (Figure S7a,b, Supporting Information). A slight decrease in both S chem and S comb was observed with increasing RH level, probably due to the formation of hydroxyl groups on Pd‐WO3 NRs surface (Figure 4e).[ 51 , 52 ] The decreases in R chem with increased RH levels imply the formation of the hydroxyl groups on the surface (Figure S7c, Supporting Information). Nonetheless, the sensor maintained consistently high responses across all humid conditions, confirming its excellent humidity tolerance. Moreover, the repeatability of the sensor was validated through 20 repetitive cycles of H2 exposure (gas concentration = 0.8%, 50% RH). As shown in Figure S8 (Supporting Information), the sensor exhibited stable and reproducible response patterns in both channels, highlighting its long‐term operational stability. To simulate real‐world environments, the sensing behavior was also evaluated under static gas conditions. After the stabilization in static chamber, injection of 2% H2 induced high and reversible response, confirming the applicability to real‐time H2 monitoring in confined or low‐flow environments (Figure 4f). Meanwhile, the selective detection capability was evaluated against interfering gases, including C2H5OH, CO, NH3, CH3COCH3, and C7H8 under dry condition (Figure S9, Supporting Information). The Pd‐WO3 MEMS sensor exhibited exclusively high selectivity to H2 for both chemoresistive and catalytic combustion sensing modes (Figure 4g,h). This is attributed to the Pd‐induced H2 spillover effect, whereby H2 molecules readily dissociate on the Pd and PdO surfaces, promoting the oxidation reaction with surface ionized oxygen species.[ 29 , 30 ] Furthermore, the high combustion enthalpy of H2 per mole of oxygen consumed, along with its relatively low catalytic combustion temperature, contributed to its ultra‐high H2 selectivity in the catalytic combustion channel (S H2,comb/S interferents,comb ≥ 10.37).[ 53 , 54 ] Moreover, the H2 sensing performance under mixed‐gas conditions was examined. Given that the Pd‐WO3 MEMS sensor exhibited the second highest S chem to C2H5OH among the tested interferents, the H2 response in the presence of C2H5OH was assessed. As shown in Figure S10 (Supporting Information), both chemoresistive and catalytic combustion responses preserved comparable despite the presence of C2H5OH, further confirming the robustness of the dual‐signal sensor. A comparison with previously reported WO3‐based H2 sensors is summarized in Table S2 (Supporting Information). The Pd‐WO3 MEMS sensor in this work exhibits comparable or superior chemoresistive sensing performance for H2 detection, while uniquely offering an additional catalytic combustion response. To the best of our knowledge, no prior reports have demonstrated WO3‐based sensors operating in this dual‐mode configuration, highlighting the distinctiveness of the present approach. Taken together, these results demonstrate that the Pd‐WO3 MEMS sensor possesses excellent environmental stability, strong humidity tolerance, and high gas selectivity, affirming its potential as a reliable, low‐power sensing platform for hydrogen leak monitoring in industrial environments.

The device packaging is essential to ensure practical and commercial viability of MEMS‐based sensors. For this, the fabricated Pd‐WO3 MEMS chip was attached to a stainless‐steel TO‐5 type 4‐pin stem and encapsulated with a stainless‐steel mesh cap, which protects the sensing element while allowing gas exposure (Figure 5a; Figure S11, Supporting Information). The packaged sensor exhibited stable and reproducible sensing transients to 0.1–0.8% of H2 under a constant V h of 1 V (Figure 5b). Although slight reductions in S chem and S comb were observed after packaging, the sensor maintained high H2 responses (S chem = 829.7 and S comb = 2.47% to 0.8% H2). The response/recovery times of the packaged sensor were determined from transient measurements at 0.8% H2. The chemoresistive‐sensing channel exhibited τ res,chem/τ rec,chem of 108.7/381.6 s, while the combustion‐sensing channel showed rapid τ res,comb/τ rec,comb of 4.2 7.0 s. Furthermore, the packaged sensor showed high stability during repeated H2 exposures, validating its robustness and reliability (Figure 5c). These results highlight the feasibility of integrating the Pd‐WO3 MEMS sensor into ultra‐low‐power commercial H2 leak detection systems.

Conclusion

A low power consuming Pd‐WO3 NRs MEMS sensor capable of dual‐signal H2 detection has been successfully developed. The chemoresistive and catalytic combustion dual‐signal sensing platform was realized by integrating porous film composed by Pd‐WO3 NRs with a microheater and IDEs on a MEMS platform. Especially, the Pd NPs served as active catalytic sites that facilitated H2 spillover and enhanced surface oxidation reactions. The vigorous oxidation process induced electron transfer and heat generation, enabling two distinct sensing signals through simultaneous measurement of R chem and R comb. The developed sensor exhibited high sensitivity, high selectivity, and low detection limits in both modes. It also demonstrated reliable sensing performance under various conditions, including high humidity environments and static gas exposure tests. Even after packaging with a stainless‐steel cap, the device retained excellent dual‐signal sensing characteristics, underscoring its potential for real‐world applications. The combination of dual‐signal H2 discrimination and low power consuming MEMS platform offers a practical approach for reliable detection of H2 in environmental, industrial, and energy fields.

Experimental Sections

Fabrication of Pd‐WO3 Sensors

Pd‐WO3 NRs were fabricated via GLAD using an electron‐beam evaporator. WO3 (Taewon Scientific Co., 99.99%) grains were used as source material. The WO3 was deposited on MEMS platform, constructed of a Pt‐microheater and Pt‐interdigitated sensing electrode (electrode gap: 2.5 µm), at a glancing angle of 65° with a substrate rotation speed of 80 rpm. During deposition, the electrical pads were masked to prevent material deposition. The base pressure was maintained in the range of 10−6 Torr, and the growth rate was set at 1.5 Å s−1. The as‐deposited WO3 NRs were annealed at 500 °C for 2 h in ambient air to promote crystallization. Subsequently, Pd NPs were decorated on to the WO3 NRs via a water‐bath method. A precursor solution was prepared by dissolving potassium tetrachloropalladate in distilled water at a concentration of 150 µm. The WO3 NRs substrate was immersed in the precursor solution, maintained at 70 °C for 5 min. After the reaction, Pd‐WO3 were rinsed and annealed at 200 °C for 2 h.

Gas Sensing Measurements

The gas sensing properties of the prepared sensor chip were evaluated in a custom‐designed gas measurement system equipped with a probe station chamber (inner diameter: 60 mm, height: 30 mm; VPX‐G400P8, WIT). For chip packaging, a TO‐5 4‐pin stem and a stainless‐steel mesh cap (SUS316, double layer of 100‐mesh gauze) were used. After packaging, the sensor measurement was conducted in a cubic quartz tube reactor (size: 1.14×1.14×1.14 cm3). The sensor resistance was measured using a dual channel electrometer (Kiethley 2612B). The constant voltage of 5 V was applied to the chemoresistive channel, while input voltage for MEMS heater was adjusted as 0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 V. The atmosphere in the chamber was adjusted by varying the mixing ratio between dry synthetic air and analyte gas using mass flow controller. The total gas flow was fixed at 500 mL min−1. The RH level was controlled by admixing humid air generated by bubbling distilled water.

Materials Characterization

The morphology of the fabricated Pd‐WO3 NRs was characterized using FE‐SEM (JSM‐IT1800SHL, JEOL). The microstructure and phase were investigated via TEM (JEM‐2100F, JEOL), equipped with scanning TEM (STEM), and energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS). The crystal structure was characterized via XRD (D8 advance, Burker). XPS (AXIS‐HSIS, KRATOS) was conducted to analyze the chemical states, and the binding energy scales were calibrated using the C 1s core level positioned at 284.5 eV.
