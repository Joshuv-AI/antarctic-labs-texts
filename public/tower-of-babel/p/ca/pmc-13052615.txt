PD-L1-targeted PET imaging for non-invasive assessment of immunotherapy response in lung cancer
Authors: Yang Chen, Xiufeng Liu, Yinting Hu, Ao Li, Baozhen Zeng, Peng Wang, Ganghua Tang, Benyuan Jiang, Hui Yuan, Lei Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmune checkpoint inhibitors have improved outcomes for several malignancies; however, there remains a lack of accurate, non-invasive methods to assess tumor PD-L1 expression levels and guide immunotherapy. This study aimed to evaluate the role of PD-L1-targeted positron emission tomography (PET) imaging in predicting immunotherapy response and prognosis in lung cancer.MethodsFour healthy volunteers and 22 treatment-naïve lung cancer patients were prospectively enrolled and underwent [68Ga]Ga-PDL1p PET imaging. All patients additionally completed paired baseline [18F]fluorodeoxyglucose ([18F]FDG) PET scans. Of the 22 patients, 17 received ≥3 cycles of immunotherapy combined with chemotherapy and underwent follow-up [18F]FDG PET or CT examinations. The correlations of baseline [68Ga]Ga-PDL1p and [18F]FDG uptake with tumor PD-L1 expression were evaluated. Furthermore, the associations of tumor [68Ga]Ga-PDL1p uptake, [18F]FDG uptake, and PD-L1 expression with immunotherapy response were analyzed, along with their predictive values for immunotherapy efficacy and outcomes.ResultsLesions with high PD-L1 expression exhibited significantly higher [68Ga]Ga-PDL1p uptake than those with low expression (p=0.007), whereas [18F]FDG uptake showed no significant difference (p=0.499). At baseline, [68Ga]Ga-PDL1p uptake was significantly higher in responders than in non-responders (p=0.008), with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.886. In contrast, neither [18F]FDG uptake nor PD-L1 expression levels differed significantly between the two groups. Disease progression occurred in 23.5% of patients (4/17) by the final follow-up. Patients with higher [68Ga]Ga-PDL1p uptake or higher [18F]FDG uptake demonstrated significantly longer progression-free survival (PFS) than those with lower uptake (p=0.033 and p<0.001, respectively). However, no significant difference in PFS was observed between patients with high and low PD-L1 expression, using either a 50% (p=0.487) or 1% (p=0.100) cut-off.Conclusions[68Ga]Ga-PDL1p PET outperforms conventional [18F]FDG PET and immunohistochemistry-based PD-L1 assessment in predicting immunotherapy response and prognosis. These findings offer new insights for evaluating immunotherapy efficacy and guiding individualized tumor treatment.Keywords: Immunotherapy, Lung Cancer, fMRI / PET

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study demonstrated that tumor uptake of [68Ga]Ga-PDL1p was significantly correlated with PD-L1 expression levels and effectively predicted immunotherapy response and progression-free survival in patients with lung cancer, outperforming [18F]fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (PET) and immunohistochemistry-based PD-L1 assessment.

This study demonstrated that tumor uptake of [68Ga]Ga-PDL1p was significantly correlated with PD-L1 expression levels and effectively predicted immunotherapy response and progression-free survival in patients with lung cancer, outperforming [18F]fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (PET) and immunohistochemistry-based PD-L1 assessment.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting the programmed cell death protein 1/programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-1/PD-L1) pathway have significantly improved outcomes in patients with malignancies, especially lung cancer, demonstrating durable disease control and survival benefits.1 2 Currently, PD-L1 expression quantified by immunohistochemistry on localized single or multiple tumor biopsies represents the most widely validated and clinically accepted biomarker for guiding immunotherapy treatment decisions and predicting efficacy.3 However, in real-world practice, there remains considerable variability in individual responses to immunotherapy, which may be attributed to intra- and inter-tumoral heterogeneity in PD-L1 expression.4,6 Additionally, due to the unique mechanism of action of ICIs in enhancing immune activity, patients may exhibit atypical response patterns during treatment, such as pseudo-progression, delayed response, or dissociated response. Conventional anatomical imaging criteria, including Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST version 1.1) and Immune-based RECIST (iRECIST), are often inadequate for accurately evaluating these types of immunotherapy response.7 8 Therefore, there remains an urgent need to develop improved methods for assessing tumor PD-L1 expression to predict therapeutic efficacy before treatment initiation and to enable real-time response monitoring throughout immunotherapy.

Positron emission tomography (PET) is a non-invasive molecular imaging technique capable of providing real-time visualization and quantification of target expression across both primary and metastatic sites in vivo. Parameters derived from [18F]fluorodeoxyglucose ([18F]FDG) PET/CT have been extensively studied for predicting or evaluating responses to immunotherapy across various malignancies and disease stages.9 10 However, during ICIs therapy, immune activation and infiltration within the tumor microenvironment (eg, increased T cell activity) can lead to elevated [18F]FDG uptake, resulting in false-positive findings. Consequently, semi-quantitative assessment based on [18F]FDG uptake suffers from low specificity and limited accuracy in predicting treatment efficacy and may even be misleading in certain clinical scenarios.11 12 With the continuous development of diverse novel PD-L1-specific radiotracers, PET imaging has demonstrated great potential for detecting spatiotemporal heterogeneity of PD-L1 expression in tumors in both preclinical and clinical studies.13,19 However, research on the ability of PD-L1-targeted PET to predict immunotherapy response and prognosis in patients with lung cancer remains limited. Several existing studies enrolled limited cases with lung cancer or lacked follow-up data of patients such as progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS).20,25

We have previously investigated the imaging performance of a novel peptide-based PD-L1-targeted PET tracer, [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-PDL1p. This tracer exhibited a favorable safety profile without adverse events reported, rapid clearance from the blood and non-target organs owing to its low molecular mass, and promising ability to noninvasively evaluate PD-L1 expression in patients with lung cancer.26 Notably, a significant non-specific renal uptake of [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-PDL1p was observed, which constitutes a critical consideration in radiopharmaceutical development. NOTA and DOTA are commonly used metal chelators for 68Ga-labeled peptide tracers. To address this limitation, we selected the NOTA chelator for structural modification of the tracer. Compared with DOTA, NOTA has distinct advantages: it forms more stable complexes with 68Ga, possesses a more balanced hydrophilic-hydrophobic profile, and is less likely to accumulate in the renal cortex, thus potentially reducing non-specific renal uptake while maintaining high targeting affinity for PD-L1. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate and compare the biodistribution and tumor imaging performance of [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-PDL1p and [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-PDL1p, as well as to further assess their value in predicting immunotherapy response and prognosis in patients with lung cancer.

Materials and methods

Human participants

This study on PD-L1-targeted PET/CT imaging enrolled participants aged 18 years or older, including healthy volunteers and patients with lung cancer, conducted in a prospective manner between September 2024 and June 2025. To preliminarily assess the safety, pharmacokinetics, and biodistribution of [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-PDL1p and [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-PDL1p, we initially recruited four healthy volunteers (two for each tracer). The inclusion criteria for patients were as follows: (1) pathologically confirmed lung cancer; (2) no prior antitumor therapy; (3) no history of autoimmune diseases; (4) absence of severe hepatic or renal dysfunction. Finally, 18 participants (2 healthy volunteers and 16 patients) in the [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-PDL1p group and 8 participants (2 healthy volunteers and 6 patients) in the [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-PDL1p group were consecutively recruited. Primary lung tumor specimens of patients for PD-L1 analysis were obtained via bronchoscopy or CT-guided biopsy and were blinded to [68Ga]Ga-PDL1p PET results. PD-L1 expression in lung cancer lesions was assessed using the Tumor Proportion Score (TPS) via immunohistochemistry.

PET/CT scan and image analysis

The synthesis of [68Ga]Ga-PDL1p, encompassing both [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-PEG2-Asp2-PDL1p and [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-PEG2-Asp2-PDL1p, was conducted following the similar procedure described in our previous study.26 All participants underwent baseline [68Ga]Ga-PDL1p PET/CT imaging, while patients with lung cancer additionally completed baseline [18F]FDG PET/CT scans within 1 week. [68Ga]Ga-PDL1p and [18F]FDG were intravenously administered at doses of approximately 1.85 MBq/kg and 2.96 MBq/kg, respectively, and PET/CT acquisition was initiated at 60±5 min post-injection. All patients were required to fast for a minimum of 6 hours prior to [18F]FDG injection on their serum glucose levels ≤11.0 mmol/L. Follow-up was conducted 2 weeks after imaging to evaluate any potential side effects or other concerns.

All PET/CT scans were performed using a total-body PET/CT scanner (uEXPLORER, UIH, Shanghai, China) with an axial field of view of 194 cm. A low-dose CT was performed prior to PET scan for attenuation correction and anatomical localization. Subsequently, PET imaging was acquired in list-mode with a 5 min duration, and all PET images were reconstructed employing a three-dimensional ordered-subset expectation maximization algorithm utilizing the following parameters: 3 iterations, 20 subsets, and a voxel size of 1.92×1.92×2.89 mm3, along with time-of-flight and point spread function modeling. A Gaussian filter with a full width at a maximum of 3 mm was applied to the reconstructed images. An extra chest high-resolution CT was performed after the PET scan using the following parameters: 120 kV, auto mA, 1.09 pitch, 0.5 s tube rotation, and slice thickness of 1 mm.

Images were analyzed independently using uWS-MI station (UIH, Shanghai, China) by two experienced nuclear medicine physicians (YC and HY), who were blinded to patient clinical information. The conflicting results and disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus. Regions of interest were manually delineated on tumor lesions and healthy tissues to obtain the maximum and mean standardized uptake value (SUVmax and SUVmean) for semiquantitative analysis. The tumor-to-background ratio (TBR) was calculated as SUVmax (tumor)/SUVmean (adjacent normal lung parenchyma).

Response evaluation and follow-up

As shown in figure 1, 5 out of 22 patients who did not receive immunotherapy were excluded from the follow-up: three received targeted therapy (either alone or in combination with chemotherapy), and two proceeded directly to surgical resection. The remaining 17 patients who received immunotherapy combined with chemotherapy were included in the treatment response evaluation. Treatment regimens were carefully documented. Patients were required to complete at least three cycles of treatment unless they experienced disease progression or intolerable adverse events. These patients underwent CT scans approximately every 3 months, using either standard radiological CT or the CT component of [18F]FDG PET/CT, along with subsequent clinical follow-up. Pretreatment contrast-enhanced brain MRI was performed in 14 patients with stage III or higher lung cancer evaluated by [18F]FDG PET/CT, while post-treatment brain MRI was conducted in 6 patients with baseline brain metastases or those who developed head discomfort after the initiation of treatment.

Overview diagram and flow chart of enrollment, imaging, and follow-up. [18F]FDG, [18F]fluorodeoxyglucose; PET, positron emission tomography.

Treatment response was assessed according to iRECIST criteria27 and categorized as immune complete response (iCR), immune partial response (iPR), immune stable disease (iSD), or immune progressive disease (iPD) which includes unconfirmed (iUPD, assessed for confirmation 4–8 weeks later) and confirmed progression (iCPD). Patients were subsequently classified into two groups: immunotherapy response (iCR or iPR) and non-response (iSD or iPD). Similarly, patients were classified into immunotherapy response (CR or PR) and non-response (SD or PD) groups according to the RECIST V.1.1 criteria.28

Clinical follow-up was performed, including evaluation of patient symptoms, laboratory test results, imaging findings, and histopathological data. Disease progression was defined as the primary endpoint event. PFS was defined as the interval from the date of diagnosis to the date of disease progression or death from any cause. For patients without disease progression or death, data were censored at the date of the last follow-up.

Statistical analysis

R (V.4.3.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and IBM SPSS Statistics (V.27) were used for statistical analysis. Continuous variables were expressed as median and range, and categorical variables were expressed as frequencies. Pearson correlation analysis was used to assess the association between the variables. Independent-sample t test or Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare continuous variables, while the Fisher’s exact test was used to compare rates, where appropriate. The specificity and sensitivity for predicting immunotherapy response was determined using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. Kaplan-Meier was applied to plot the survival curves, and survival estimates were compared by log-rank tests. The cut-off values for TBR_PDL1p and TBR_FDG were determined using the R package survminer, while the conventional thresholds of 50% and 1% were applied for PD-L1 TPS classification, respectively.29 30 A p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Additionally, the healthy volunteer group consisted of three males and one female, with a median age of 51 years (range, 50–70 years).

In both healthy volunteers and patients with lung cancer, [68Ga]Ga-PDL1p was predominantly cleared via the urinary system, with intense radiotracer accumulation observed in the kidneys and urinary bladder. Tumor uptake of [68Ga]Ga-PDL1p and PD-L1 immunohistochemistry

In all 22 patients with lung cancer, as well as in the subset of 14 patients with low PD-L1 expression (TPS<50%), there was no statistically significant difference in pulmonary tumor uptake (TBR) between the [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-PDL1p and [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-PDL1p tracers (p>0.05; online supplemental table S3). As illustrated in figure 3a, the patient with high PD-L1 expression (TPS=70%) demonstrated higher [68Ga]Ga-PDL1p uptake compared with the patient with low PD-L1 expression (TPS<1%), with TBR of 7.32 vs 2.32. Tumor uptake of [68Ga]Ga-PDL1p was significantly positively correlated with PD-L1 expression levels (R=0.60, p=0.011; figure 3b). When patients were stratified into high-PD-L1 and low-PD-L1 expression groups using a TPS cut-off of 50%, [68Ga]Ga-PDL1p uptake (TBR_PDL1p) was significantly higher in the high-expression group than in the low-expression group (p=0.007; figure 3c). In contrast, [18F]FDG uptake (TBR_FDG) showed no significant correlation with PD-L1 expression levels (R=0.25, p=0.329; figure 3d). Furthermore, no significant difference in [18F]FDG uptake was observed between the high-PD-L1 and low-PD-L1 expression groups (p=0.499; figure 3e).

Predictive value of [68Ga]Ga-PDL1p PET for immunotherapy response

According to iRECIST criteria, 58.8% (10/17) of patients were categorized as the response group (iCR or iPR), while 41.2% (7/17) were assigned to the non-response group (iSD or iPD). Baseline TBR_PDL1p was significantly higher in the response group than in the non-response group (p=0.008; figure 4a). In contrast, no significant differences between the two groups were observed in baseline TBR_FDG (p=0.079; figure 4b) or PD-L1 expression levels (assessed by TPS via IHC, p=0.278; figure 4c). ROC analysis further demonstrated the superior predictive performance of [68Ga]Ga-PDL1p PET for immunotherapy response, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.886 (p=0.008; figure 4d), outperforming that of [18F]FDG PET and PD-L1 expression level, which had an AUC of 0.757 (p=0.079) and 0.657 (p=0.283), respectively. Furthermore, based on the RECIST 1.1 criteria, 52.9% (9/17) of patients were categorized as the response group (CR or PR), while 47.1% (8/17) were assigned to the non-response group (SD or PD). Notably, one patient (figure 5) was classified as non-responding under RECIST 1.1 criteria but as iUPD under iRECIST. The patient was subsequently reclassified as responding following surgical resection, with confirmation of a pathological CR (pCR). The status of TBR_PDL1p, TBR_FDG, and PD-L1 expression levels between the two groups, as well as the ROC analysis results, was similar to those obtained when using the iRECIST criteria for evaluation (online supplemental figure S1).

In a case of pseudo-progression (figure 5), baseline [68Ga]Ga-PDL1p PET showed high tracer uptake (TBR=5.62), indicating elevated PD-L1 expression. After three cycles of immunotherapy combined with chemotherapy, follow-up [18F]FDG PET/CT revealed no reduction in the size of the primary lung lesion; additionally, several lymph nodes and pleural lesions exhibited increased size and [18F]FDG uptake. The patient underwent surgical resection 11 days after this follow-up scan, and pathological examination confirmed a pCR in all resected lesions. In a case with true disease progression (figure 7), baseline [68Ga]Ga-PDL1p PET imaging showed moderately low tracer uptake (TBR=3.21). After five cycles of immunotherapy combined with chemotherapy (~4 months from start of treatment), follow-up contrast-enhanced brain MRI detected new metastatic lesions, indicating disease progression and poor prognosis.

Discussion

Due to tumor heterogeneity, conventional single-site biopsy-based immunohistochemistry assessment of PD-L1 expression reflects only localized information and often fails to accurately identify patients who may benefit from immunotherapy. This limitation is further highlighted by clinical studies, which report that only 20%–40% of treated patients exhibit a response to ICIs, underscoring the challenge posed by low response rates.31 Here, we present a peptide-based PD-L1-targeted PET imaging approach that enables comprehensive macroscopic evaluation of PD-L1 expression across entire tumor burdens, which is critical for improving the prediction of immunotherapy efficacy and identifying patients most likely to benefit from treatment.

In vivo evaluation of [68Ga]Ga-DOTA/NOTA-PDL1p demonstrated a favorable safety profile, with no tracer-related adverse events observed during follow-up. Owing to its low molecular mass, [68Ga]Ga-PDL1p was rapidly cleared from the blood and other non-target organs, with low background uptake in organs and tissues including the liver, spleen, bone marrow, and gastrointestinal tract, which results in minimal radiation exposure. Furthermore, no significant differences in biodistribution were observed between the two tracers employing DOTA and NOTA chelators across different tissues, except in the renal cortex. As a peptide-based tracer, [68Ga]Ga-PDL1p is primarily filtered by the glomeruli. The selection of chelator influences the overall charge, hydrophilicity, and protein-binding properties of the tracer, thereby modulating its renal clearance kinetics.32 For instance, DOTA possesses one more carboxyl group than NOTA. On one hand, this enhances the hydrophilicity of the tracer, potentially promoting renal uptake; on the other hand, the additional negative charge may reduce filtration efficiency, as evidence suggests that cationic and neutral molecules are filtered more effectively than anions.33 These opposing effects contribute to the prolonged renal retention observed with the DOTA-conjugated tracer. Consistent with these mechanisms, the renal cortical uptake of [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-PDL1p was significantly lower than that of [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-PDL1p in our results. This property may enhance imaging contrast and reduce renal radiation exposure, indicating that [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-PDL1p offers superior imaging performance. Furthermore, consistent with prior studies using DOTA and NOTA chelators for 68Ga-labeling of the same target,32 34 no significant difference in tumor uptake was observed between [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-PDL1p and [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-PDL1p, indicating that the choice of chelator does not compromise the specific tumor targeting of this peptide.

In patients with lung cancer, the tumor uptake of [68Ga]Ga-PDL1p correlated with tumor PD-L1 expression levels, confirming the in vivo targeting specificity of the probe for PD-L1. Among patients receiving immunotherapy, baseline tumor [68Ga]Ga-PDL1p uptake was significantly higher in the response group than the non-response group, suggesting its predictive potential for immunotherapy response. In contrast, no significant difference in baseline tumor [18F]FDG uptake was observed between responders and non-responders, indicating [18F]FDG PET unsuitable for pretherapeutic patient selection. Furthermore, no significant difference in tumor PD-L1 expression levels was noted between the two groups, underscoring the limited predictive value of conventional biopsy-based PD-L1 assessment for immunotherapy outcomes. Intratumoral heterogeneity may be one reason for the inaccuracy of PD-L1 evaluation by localized biopsy, thereby failing to accurately predict immunotherapy response. As consistent with our previous findings,26 this study again observed uneven tracer uptake of [68Ga]Ga-PDL1p within larger tumor lesions, highlighting the intratumor heterogeneity of PD-L1 expression. The inaccuracy of PD-L1 evaluation by biopsy may also explain why we observed a correlation between [68Ga]Ga-PDL1p uptake and both PD-L1 expression and immunotherapy response, but not between PD-L1 expression and response. Considering the intra-tumor heterogeneity of PD-L1 expression in future research could help understanding of immunotherapy response and contribute to the development of more personalized treatment strategies.

During follow-up, patients with high baseline [68Ga]Ga-PDL1p uptake showed prolonged PFS, highlighting the potential of this tracer to predict both therapeutic response and prognosis. In contrast, tumor PD-L1 expression measured by immunohistochemistry, despite its widespread clinical use and acceptance, was not associated with prolonged PFS in our cohort. Additionally, although [18F]FDG PET is one of the established modalities for evaluating immunotherapy response,35 it falsely judged disease progression in one patient who achieved pCR after immunotherapy (figure 5). By comparison, predictive efficacy of immunotherapy efficacy by baseline [68Ga]Ga-PDL1p imaging (AUC=0.886, p=0.008) was higher than that of [18F]F-FDG imaging (AUC=0.757, p=0.079) in this study. In fact, the role of [18F]FDG in predicting immunotherapy response in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) remains controversial.36 It is reported that lower baseline [18F]FDG PET/CT parameters were associated with favorable immunotherapy responses in NSCLC,37 whereas a previous study found that the average SUVmax of the responders was significantly higher than that of the non-responders.38 In the present study, we observed that patients with higher baseline [18F]FDG uptake derived greater benefit from immunotherapy than those with lower uptake. Notably, [18F]FDG can be taken up by tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) and correlates with the abundance of CD8+ and PD-1+ TILs.38 39 We therefore hypothesize that tumors with high [18F]FDG uptake may be categorized as “hot” tumors, potentially explaining their enhanced response to immunotherapy observed in this subgroup of our cohort.

Several previous studies have explored the predictive value of PD-L1-targeted PET imaging in patients undergoing immunotherapy, using radiotracers based on antibodies, nanobodies, or peptides. Among them, studies on antibody-based tracers20 21 25 have enrolled only a limited number of patients with lung cancer (5–9 cases). Although these agents exhibited high tumor targeting affinity, their large molecular size results in prolonged metabolic clearance and poor tumor penetration, which limits their clinical utility. They also carry a potential risk of tracer-related adverse events. Studies involving lower molecular-weight agents, such as nanobodies and peptides, remain scarce. Some studies reported responses only in individual lesions rather than assessing treatment efficacy across all enrolled patients23 or used major pathological response as an endpoint in neoadjuvant settings without subsequent follow-up,24 40 thereby failing to demonstrate the ability of these tracers to predict overall prognosis. Additionally, these tracers also showed prominent retention in the liver, spleen, or gastrointestinal tract, which complicates image interpretation of primary or metastatic tumors in these regions and increases patient radiation exposure. Our tracer [68Ga]Ga-PDL1p showed minimal to mild accumulation in most tissues and organs except for the kidneys, which can overcome these drawbacks. For instance, [68Ga]Ga-PDL1p PET detected more lesions with higher tumor-to-background contrast than [18F]FDG PET in a patient with lung cancer with multiple brain metastases, which was confirmed by contrast-enhanced MRI.26

Similar to other studies in the field, our research is limited by its relatively small sample size. Future validation in larger prospective cohorts is necessary to confirm these findings. The limited cohort size also precluded a comprehensive analysis incorporating additional clinical variables to improve predictive accuracy. Additionally, the analysis was restricted to primary tumors. It would be beneficial to investigate the correlation between clinical efficacy and metastatic tumors located outside the lungs. Furthermore, the absence of post-treatment [68Ga]Ga-PDL1p PET imaging prevented a direct longitudinal assessment of treatment-induced changes, which would have further validated its role in monitoring immunotherapy responses. Finally, limited accessibility to PET/CT scanners in some regions may challenge the widespread clinical implementation of this approach.

Conclusions

[68Ga]Ga-PDL1p PET outperforms conventional [18F]FDG PET and immunohistochemistry-based PD-L1 assessment in predicting immunotherapy response and prognosis. These findings offer new insights for evaluating immunotherapy efficacy and guiding individualized tumor treatment.
