Jamming as a topological satisfiability transition with contact number hyperuniformity and criticality
Authors: Jin Shang, Yinqiao Wang, Deng Pan, Yuliang Jin, Jie Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceIn liquids and gases, particles are distributed uniformly in space due to the absence of structural order. In contrast, particles in crystals are arranged in a “hyperuniform” manner, exhibiting long-range order with suppressed large-scale fluctuations. Surprisingly, amorphous packings at the jamming transition—despite lacking obvious structural order—display hyperuniformity. What is even more puzzling is that this hyperuniformity, characterized by suppressed fluctuations, coexists with criticality, which typically implies diverging fluctuations. We find that the hyperuniformity in contact number distributions at the jamming transition originates from constraints on mechanical stability and isostaticity. Based on a minimal network model, we characterize jamming as a topological satisfiability transition that simultaneously displays hyperuniformity and criticality.

Hyperuniformity, with widespread observations in classical (1–8), quantum (9, 10), biological (11, 12), mathematical (13, 14), and cosmological systems (15), describes the suppression of large-scale fluctuations in the spatial distribution of physical quantities (16, 17). A physical quantity A in d dimensions is hyperuniform if its variance scales subextensively with observation radius R,[1]σA2∝Rd−γ,

with γ∈(0,1]. In Fourier space, this corresponds to a structure factor[2]SA(q)∝qα,

as q→0, with α>0. Three hyperuniform classes are defined according to the values of exponents - Class I: γ=1 and α>1; Class II: γ=α=1 with logarithmic corrections; Class III: γ=α∈(0,1) (16). Recently, interest in hyperuniformity has been expanded beyond crystals and quasicrystals to disordered materials, owing to their unique optical and mechanical properties (9, 18, 19).

The particles in equilibrium fluids are distributed uniformly at large distances (α=0) in order to have a finite isothermal compressibility, while crystals are typically hyperuniform (γ=1) due to the ordered arrangement of particles. For packings of randomly jammed particles, it was conjectured that the distribution of particle positions is hyperuniform (density hyperuniform, or DH) (16, 20), although this conjecture has been debated (21–25). More robust hyperuniformity is observed in the distribution of mechanical contacts between particles (contact number hyperuniformity, or CNH) at the jamming transition, with γ≈1 and α≈1.5 (19, 26–29). Accompanied with CNH is two distinct diverging lengths ξZ and ξf characterized by different critical exponents νZ and νf (26, 27). The diverging lengths, in contrast to a finite nucleation length at typical first-order phase transitions, imply jamming as a critical point. However, the diverging fluctuations (γ<0) in standard critical phenomena seem incompatible with hyperuniformity (γ>0). Although it has been assumed that the hyperuniformity at jamming is related to its criticality (24), the paradox is still unsolved.

Great efforts have been made to uncover the origin of hyperuniformity. In systems with long-range interactions, density fluctuations are suppressed by a conserved field (30–32), which however does not show critical behavior. Hyperuniformity and criticality emerge in a class of nonequilibrium phase transitions, such as the conserved directed percolation, which belong to the Manna universality class (MUC) (1, 32–34). Recent works (6, 35) propose to connect DH at jamming (7, 20, 36–41) to a dynamical absorbing phase transition in the biased random organization model (6, 35, 40, 42) in the MUC. However, the MUC exponents γMUC=αMUC=0.45 in 2D and γMUC=αMUC=0.25 in 3D (Class III) are clearly different from the CNH exponent γ≈1 and α≈1.5 (Class I) observed in simulations (26, 27). Thus the origin of CNH at the jamming transition remains elusive.

Here, we propose a mechanism for CNH, rooted in two mechanical constraints: a global equality due to isostaticity at jamming and a local inequality ensuring mechanical stability of any subsystems. Together, they suppress contact number fluctuations to scale with the surface area (Rd−1) rather than the volume (Rd) of an observation window, closely paralleling DH in crystals. Based on this mechanism, we propose a minimum network model with a topological satisfiability transition, which exhibits simultaneously CNH and critical behavior as in the jamming transition. As illustrated in Fig. 1, this hyperuniformity is subtle and difficult to distinguish from random noise in real space (see the comparison between the hyperuniform distribution of contact numbers in Fig. 1A and the uniform distribution in Fig. 1B). However, the CNH is manifested clearly in the Fourier space through suppression of the structure factor SZ(q) at small wavenumbers q (Fig. 1D). Importantly, our minimum network model (Fig. 1 C and F) successfully reproduces these signatures of contact networks, yielding patterns remarkably analogous to those observed in jamming. The CNH and critical exponents are in agreement in the minimum model and granular packings, suggesting a universality class of nonequilibrium phase transitions. Our results show that CNH can be decoupled from DH, as it arises even with uniformly distributed particle positions.

An Intuitive Argument for the Origin of Contact Number Hyperuniformity.

We first revisit the DH in crystals. As shown in Fig. 2A, the fluctuation σn2(R) of the particle number n(R) within a window of a radius R scales with the surface area Rd−1, not the volume Rd. Analogously, we show below that, at the jamming transition, contact number fluctuations σZ2(R) scale as Rd−1. The derivation starts from the following two types of constraints imposed on the contact network at the jamming transition.

As can be seen in Fig. 2B, m(R)=mI(R)+mB(R), where mI(R) is the number of contacts inside and mB(R) the number of contacts crossing the boundary (Fig. 2B is an example of the minimum network model defined below). Applying Eq. 4 to the complementary set of m(R) yields m¯(R)≡M−mI(R)>d[N−n(R)], which, combined with Eq. 4, gives[5]dn(R)−mB(R)<mI(R)<dn(R).

Eq. 5 shows that the bulk contact number is bounded from two sides, and its fluctuation depends on the surface area, δm(R)∼δmI(R)∼mB(R)∼Rd−1.

Following refs. 26 and 27, we define the local coordination number z(R)=∑i∈s(R)Zi for particles inside the subsystem s(R). By definition, z(R)=m(R)+mI(R) (note that the mI contacts should be double counted). Following a similar strategy as discussed above for m(R), it can be shown that, 2dn(R)−dnE(R)<z(R)<2dn(R)+dnO(R), where nE (inside) and nO (outside) denote the number of boundary particles forming the mB boundary contacts (Fig. 2B). Thus the fluctuation of z also scales with the surface area, δz(R)∼nE(R)+nO(R)∼Rd−1.

The above analysis demonstrates that combining the local inequality constraints Eq. 4 and the corresponding global equality constraint Eq. 3 naturally results in a hyperuniform distribution. At the jamming transition, both constraints arise from mechanical reasons. More generally, one can consider systems governed by i) A=cN+k and ii) a(R)>cn(R)+k (or a(R)<cn(R)+k) for any subsystems, where A is a physical quantity and c,k are constants. Any system with such constraints—regardless of the underlying physics—should exhibit hyperuniformity. The key is that the complement of a subsystem must also satisfy the inequality; together with the global constraint, this bounds a(R) on both sides, limiting its fluctuations to scale with the surface area.

A Minimum Network Model with Contact Number Hyperuniformity.

To further reveal the origin of CNH, we introduce a minimal network model composed of sites distributed according to given geometric patterns. The sites are randomly connected to their neighbors under constraints analogous to Eqs. 3 and 4. Identifying networks that satisfy these constraints poses a complex optimization problem; even verifying the satisfiability of a given configuration is with time complexity scaling as ∝2N. To maintain tractability, we focus on models solvable via polynomial-time algorithms.

The model, called Net-I, satisfies global and local constraints:[6]M=N−1,

and[7]m(s)>n(s)−1,foranys,

where s denotes an arbitrary subsystem. It forms a spanning, acyclic tree with each node connected to at least one neighbor, generated via an optimized Monte Carlo algorithm. We systematically explore various base networks in 2D, 3D, and 4D (Generation of Net-I). A close-up view of the Net-I on a triangular lattice is shown in Fig. 2B. Additional examples of 2D Net-I configurations are provided in SI Appendix, Fig. S2. For comparison, we also analyze packings of frictionless spheres generated via rapid quench and swap algorithms (Fig. 2C and Generation of Frictionless Sphere Packings), representing generic and ultrastable jammed states, respectively.

Spatial fluctuations in contact numbers are quantified by the variance σZ2(R) within a circle of a radius R (Contact Number Fluctuations). As shown in Fig. 3A, σZ2(R)∝Rd−γ, with γ≈1 in multiple Net-I networks—a scaling also observed in rapidly quenched and ultrastable granular packings (Fig. 3D). This exponent γ=1 holds across dimensions d=2 to 4, in contrast to the MUC exponents: γMUC=0.45 in 2D and 0.25 in 3D. Fig. 3A indicates that CNH and DH are not necessarily correlated. In particular, random triangulation networks exhibit a uniform site distribution (the pair correlation function g(r)=1), yet CNH can still arise (for a more rigorous formulation, see Contact Number Fluctuations).

We have argued that the contact number fluctuations inside a subsystem depend on the number of sites near the boundary of the subsystem (Eq. 5). Simulation data from network models and granular packings can directly verify this insight. Because nE(R)≈nO(R) for large windows, for the scaling analysis, we focus on the σZ2(R) vs. nE(R) data (the σZ2(R) vs. nO(R) data give the same conclusion). Fig. 3 B and E confirm a linear relationship in the large-R limit,[8]σZ2(R)=XnE(R).

The prefactor X is obtained by linear fitting: X=0.49(5) for Net-I; X=1.01(1) and 1.6(3) for granular packings in 2D and 3D respectively. The errors are given by the SD between the fitting for different curves plus the range of the 95% CI. Interestingly, we observe a universal relation, X≈c/2, where c is the prefactor in the constraints (c=1 for Net-I and c=d for granular packings).

Assuming contact changes (connection or disconnection events) are independent events with a probability p=1/2, it can be shown that X=c/2 (Derivation of X=c/2), which is consistent with our numerical results. In more general cases (see Net-II in SI Appendix), contact changes are dependent and X≠c/2; the value of X^≡2X/c then characterizes more delicate correlations between contacts beyond hyperuniformity.

Hyperuniformity can also be characterized in Fourier space by the structure factor of the contact number, SZ(q) (defined in Structure Factor of the Contact Number), through its scaling behavior Eq. 2 (16, 20, 26). For Class I hyperuniform systems (γ=1 and α>1), the precise value of α can reveal subtle structural features. In Net-I and granular packings (Fig. 3 C and F), α≈1.5 is universally observed, independent of the dimensionality, the site patterns in Net-I, and the preparation protocol of packings. From fitting, we obtain α=1.51(7) in 2D and α=1.48(8) in 3D for Net-I. The robustness of hyperuniformity with respect to packing preparation protocols is illustrated in SI Appendix, Fig. S1. Our results imply a potential correspondence between α and X^: Both of them might be related to subtle spatial fluctuations that cannot be captured by the hyperuniformity scaling σZ2∝Rd−1.

Critical Scalings in the Minimum Model Near a Topological Satisfiability Transition.

In the above, we have demonstrated that when the two constraints are satisfied, the Net-I displays CNH. If the total number of contacts M is treated as a variable, then it is easy to see that the condition M=N−1 corresponds to a topological satisfiability transition (TST). i) When M<Mc≡N−1, the number of contacts is fewer than the number of sites, and it is impossible to satisfy all local constraints Eq. 7. ii) When M=Mc, Eq. 7 is satisfied marginally and the system has strict CNH. Meanwhile, we will show below that the system is also critical, as revealed by a power-law distribution of unstable clusters with one contact swapped. iii) When M>Mc, the network has redundant sites to satisfy Eq. 7, and it can be shown that the system has CNH up to a finite length scale.

The above picture has an analogue in frictionless granular packings near the jamming transition. i) When Z<Ziso≡2d, the unjammed system is unstable, meaning that the local stability condition Eq. 4 cannot be satisfied for arbitrary subsystems. ii) At the jamming transition Z=Ziso, the system has CNH as shown in refs. 26 and 27. Meanwhile, isostaticity manifests that the system is in a marginally stable state (45, 46), i.e., it becomes unstable upon removing one contact or applying an infinitesimal perturbation. In this case, perturbations, such as shear deformations, typically induce avalanche behavior, leading to power law distributions of physical quantities such as the stress drop. iii) Away from the jamming transition, Z>Ziso, the system is hyperstatic, i.e, Eq. 4 is satisfied with redundant contacts. Ref. 26 shows that the deviation from the perfect CNH is captured by two distinct length scales ξZ and ξf, correspondingly associated with the two-point correlations of the local contact numbers and the contact-number fluctuations σZ2(R). The exponents in the scalings ξZ∝ΔZ−νZ and ξf∝ΔZ−νf are numerically determined in ref. 26 by collapsing SZ(q) data following a scaling function SZ(q)=ΔZβf(qξZ) for different ΔZ and using the relation νf=β=ανZ (Table 1).

In the following, we will first show the criticality in Net-I at the TST (M=N−1). We apply a perturbation by randomly breaking a connection and then randomly connecting an unconnected pair of sites. Applying the perturbation induces subsystems that violate the given constraint m(s)>n(s)−1, as shown in Fig. 4A. An unstable cluster is defined as the connected component with m(s)<n(s), or equivalently the minimum subsystem violating the constraint m(s)>n(s)−1, for a given random realization of perturbation. The size S of the unstable cluster is defined by the number of sites within it. Adding a single connection to the unstable cluster can make the entire system satisfying all constraints. Fig. 4B shows the probability distributions P(S) across various dimensions and site patterns, exhibiting power-law behavior, P(S)∝S−τ, with an exponent τ2D=1.35(1), τ3D=1.45(1), and τ4D=1.49(2). Assuming P(S) is analogous to the distribution of avalanche sizes under perturbations, we can compare our values to those reported in the literature. Mean-field theories have been largely developed to describe a class of phenomena called “cracking noise,” which generally give τMF=3/2 (48, 49). Based on a numerical elastoplastic model and theoretical scaling relations, Lin, et al. obtain respectively τ2D−packing=1.36 and 1.34 in 2D, and τ3D−packing=1.45 and 1.48 in 3D (47, 50). The mean-field glass theory with a full-replica symmetry breaking ansatz predicts τJ≈1.42 in large dimensions (51). Although the exact value of τ remains indecisive, the values measured in Net-I are nevertheless close to those of packings reported in the literature.

Next we analyze the diverging length scales near the TST. Following ref. 26, we compute SZ(q) at different ΔZ≡Z−Zc, where Z≡2M/N and Zc≡2Mc/N=2(N−1)/N, and collapse the data according to the scaling form SZ(q)=ΔZβf(qξZ) (Fig. 5 A and B), where f(x) is a constant for x≪1, and f(x)∝xα for x≫1. To recover the small-q behavior SZ(q)∼qα, the relation β=ανZ is required (26). The best collapse yields νZ2D=0.73(9) and β2D=1.09(13) (Fig. 5B).

Taking into account the asymptotic relationship between the fluctuation and the structure factor (16, 26), limR→∞σZ2(R)/ ⟨n(R)⟩=limq→0SZ(q)∝ΔZβ, we can express the fluctuation as σZ2(R)/Rd=ΔZβg(R/ξf), where g(x) is constant for x≫1 and g(x)∝x−1 for x≪1. On the other hand, limR→∞σZ2(R)/Rd∼(R/ξf)−1. Matching ΔZβ with (R/ξf)−1 implies a relation νf=β, i.e., νf2D=1.09(13). Fig. 5 C and D show that the σZ2(R) curves and their excellent collapse after rescaling using νf2D=β=1.09, except for deviations at large scales due to finite-size effects. The obtained exponents, νZ2D=0.73(9) and νf2D=1.09(13), are independent of site patterns (SI Appendix, Fig. S2), and are consistent with the reported values for 2D packings, νZ2D−packing=0.7(1) and νf2D−packing=1.07(18) (26).

In 3D, the fitted exponents are νZ3D=0.65(9) and νf3D=0.98(14) in Net-I (SI Appendix, Fig. S3), which are slightly different from νZ3D−packing=0.85(15) and νf3D−packing=1.29(27) in 3D packings (26). We emphasize that it is very challenging to measure accurate exponents in 3D, due to strong finite-size effects in data collapsing (26).

Combining our numerical results with several scaling arguments, we provide below theoretical conjectures of these exponents (Table 1). Both Net-I and packing data suggest that γ=1 and α=3/2, independent of d. The exponent τ is close to the mean-field value τ=3/2, with weak d-dependence on the tail of P(S) (Fig. 4B). To derive the exponent νf, we apply a scaling argument similar to the “cutting argument” by Wyart, et al. (52). When ΔZ>0, the entire system does not satisfy the global constraint Eq. 6, but one can ask up to what length scale, the subsystem can satisfy Eq. 6. In a subsystem of a linear size ξf, the increase of contact numbers in the bulk ΔZξfd should be compensated by the surface effect Zξfd−1, yielding ΔZξfd∼Zξfd−1, or ξf∼ΔZ−1 (νf=1). Thus ξf is a crossover length: When R<ξf, the contact distribution σZ2(R)∼Rd−1 is hyperuniform; when R≫ξf, it becomes uniform σZ2(R)∼Rd. Finally, using the relation νf=ανZ, we obtain νZ=2/3. To validate these theoretical predictions, we analyze the scaling behaviors of 2D frictionless disk packings in Fig. 6 (see SI Appendix, Fig. S5 for the 3D case). Remarkably, the data for both SZ(q) and σZ2(R) collapse excellently when rescaled using these expected TST exponents. As compared in Table 1, the theoretical expectations agree reasonably well with numerical values considering numerical errors, in both Net-I and packings. Moreover, the exponents do not seem to be dependent on d, implying that both Net-I and packings are mean-field-like, consistent with the previous proposal of an upper critical dimension du=2 (43). On the other hand, it is clear that these exponents do not belong to the Manna universality class.

Discussion.

We have presented a simple and intuitive physical picture of CNH at the jamming transition of frictionless spheres: It arises due to the combined effects of global and local constraints required by isostaticity and mechanical stability. The minimum network model (Net-I) reproduces the exponents for CNH and critical scalings near the jamming transition, suggesting jamming as a TST. Our network models are mathematically well-defined and could be analytically tractable in future studies.

It is important to discuss the difference between the TST in Net-I and the SAT-UNSAT transition in random constraint satisfaction problems (rCSPs) including k-satisfiability and q-coloring problems (53). The SAT-UNSAT transition in rCSPs has been considered sharing the universality classes of hard sphere jamming (54, 55), based on the following analogue: The satisfiable (SAT) phase corresponds to the unjammed phase where hard spheres can be positioned without overlapping, and the unsatisfiable (UNSAT) phase corresponds to the overjammed phase where overlapping cannot be avoided (i.e., hard sphere solutions do not exist). In this sense, the SAT-UNSAT transition in rCSPs can be referred to as a “geometrical” satisfiability transition. In contrast, in this study, we consider a “topological” satisfiability transition, where the constraints are imposed on the contact network topology instead of particle positions. In our case, the overjammed phase is SAT and the unjammed phase is UNSAT. Our results show that CNH is originated at topological constraints, which can be decoupled from geometrical constraints (28, 56).

The TST is different from the rigidity percolation (RP) (44, 57). The RP refers to the emergence of a system-spanning rigid cluster—the other smaller clusters do not have to be rigid. The largest cluster has a heterogeneous, fractal shape, typical for a second-order phase transition. In contrast, at the TST, not only the largest cluster, but also any subsystem is required to satisfy the local stability constraint. In Net-I, the system is percolated at the TST (M=Mc), but does not have to be percolated either below or above. In other words, the TST does not correspond to a percolation transition. The essential difference between the TST and RP perhaps explains the dramatic differences in the nature of the marginal states at the jamming transition and the RP (44).

Our Net-I model combines hyperuniformity and criticality (as evidenced by the power-law divergence of length scales ξZ and ξf and the scale-free distribution of unstable clusters P(S)) at the TST. In standard equilibrium phase transitions, hyperuniformity and criticality cannot occur simultaneously. For example, in ordering transitions like crystallization, hyperuniformity emerges in the ordered phase, but the transition itself is not critical, and the correlation length jumps discontinuously. In contrast, while second-order phase transitions are critical, their fluctuations diverge in the thermodynamic limit, rather than being suppressed as in hyperuniform systems. In nonequilibrium transitions, hyperuniformity and criticality can coexist, particularly in self-organized criticality models like the Manna model. However, the exponents of the Manna universality class are inconsistent with those observed in the jamming transition, as shown here and in a recent work (58). We therefore propose that the jamming transition and the Net-I model represent a universality class of nonequilibrium phase transitions characterized by the coexistence of hyperuniformity and criticality and a new set of exponents.

Our study opens the door to several future directions. i) Experimental verification. It remains unclear if CNH would emerge in experimental packings, since frictional or nonspherical particles can induce uncertain constraints and exhibit strong dependence on preparation protocols (52, 59). In addition, boundary conditions can significantly affect the results (27), requiring careful treatment for nonperiodic systems. ii) Dynamics. To capture the mechanical and rheological properties in the unjammed phase, one needs to extend the current static network models to dynamic ones. iii) Extension to other systems. It is very interesting to seek other systems, e.g., cosmological models (15), with similar global and local constraints, and examine the universality of the discovered hyperuniformity (15).

Materials and Methods

Generation of Net-I.

We begin by selecting a base network structure and randomly generating an initial configuration of M connections. This configuration may not satisfy the local inequality conditions required by all subsystems. An optimization algorithm is then used to iteratively adjust connection positions, progressively improving the satisfaction of these conditions. When M=N−1, the local conditions require the network to form a spanning tree. To expedite convergence, we prioritize breaking connections within cycles and reconnecting disjoint components. For M≥N, each connected component sc must satisfy m(sc)>n(sc)−1, where m(sc) and n(sc) denote the number of connections and sites in component sc, respectively. Consequently, the algorithm removes excess connections from components with m(sc)>n(sc) and adds connections to those with m(sc)<n(sc). To reduce bias from the initialization process, once all local conditions are met, the algorithm continues to adjust connections while preserving the constraints, thoroughly exploring the configuration space.

We systematically investigate the following site patterns: in 2D, 1) square lattices, 2) triangular lattices with nearest neighbor connections (NNCs), 3) Union Jack lattice (square lattice with diagonal next NNCs), and 4) random triangulation network (Delaunay triangulation of a Poisson point process); in 3D, 5) cubic lattice and 6) face-centered cubic (FCC) lattice with NNCs; in 4D, 7) hypercubic lattice with NNCs.

Generation of Frictionless Sphere Packings.

We generate random configurations with a packing density φ=0.70 that is far above the jamming density. Then, the systems are decompressed, under quasistatic athermal conditions, to a state very close to the jamming transition (δφ≈10−5). For overjammed systems, we employ harmonic interaction and minimize the total potential energy. Packings of frictionless particles with different size dispersities are prepared. Three representative size distributions are generated in 3D: 1) monodisperse, 2) binary distribution of a diameter ratio 1.4 with equal number of large and small particles, 3) polydisperse with an inverse power-law distribution, P(r)∼r−3 with a size ratio rmax/rmin=0.45. In 2D, bidisperse configurations are generated.

Jammed states with a varying stability are prepared by quenching the thermally equilibrated configurations (60, 61). Well equilibrated samples with φeq=0.598,0.609,0.619,0.630 are generated via the SWAP algorithm (62). We focus on systems with inverse power-law size distribution, due to its high efficiency (62). The systems are composed by N=192,000 and N=16,000 particles for random and stable configurations, respectively.

When calculating the contact number fluctuations σZ2(R) and structure factor SZ(q), all rattlers are excluded, and the length scale is normalized by the average distance between contacting particles.

Contact Number Fluctuations.

We define the variance of contact numbers within a spherical window as[9]σZ2(R)=∑iδZiwri−x0;R2,

where δZi=Zi−Z denotes the deviation of the contact number Zi at a site i from the average Z, ri represents the spatial coordinate of the site, and w represents a spherical window function of radius R centered at a randomly sampled point x0. The ensemble average ⟨·⟩ is taken for all x0 of a given network and for different random network realizations.

With this definition, CNH and DH can be independent. To show that, consider the contact number correlation function,[10]CZ(r)=∑i,jδ(|ri−rj|−r)δZiδZj∑i,jδ(|ri−rj|−r),

where δ(r) is a radial Dirac delta function (we assume isotropic systems for convenience). The contact number fluctuations can be expressed as[11]σZ2(R)=ρV(R)δZi2+ρ∫drCZ(r)g(r)α2(r;R).

Here, ρ denotes the mean density of the site number, δZi2 is the single-site fluctuation, and α2 quantifies the overlap of windows, α2(r;R)=∫dx0w(x0;R)w(x0+r;R)/V(R). When the two-point correlation function g(r)=1, i.e., the absence of DH, as in random triangulation networks, σZ2(R) is controlled by CZ(r) – in this case, CNH is obviously independent of the site distribution.

Derivation of X = c/2.

As mentioned in the main text, contact number fluctuations in subsystems exhibit linear scaling with the number of boundary sites, Eq. 8. The prefactor X reflects the space correlations in contact numbers to a certain extent. To see that, we start with the simplest case, Net-I. For Net-I, the limitation of z(R) is imposed by 2n(R)−nE(R)≤z(R)≤2n(R)+nO(R). We assume that each time the contact number within a subsystem changes by one with an equal probability p=1/2 and the nB=2nE events are created as independent events. Then the distribution P(z) is a binomial distribution[12]P(z)=nB!z!(nB−z)!pz(1−p)nB−z,

whose variance is σZ2=nBp(1−p)=nE/2 (i.e., X=1/2), consistent with the numerical result X=0.49(5) for Net-I in Fig. 3B. Generalizing the above analysis to granular packings, where nB=2dnE due to 2dn(R)−dnE(R)<z(R)<2dn(R)+dnO(R) for packings, one expects σZ2=dnE/2 (i.e., X=d/2), consistent with the numerical results, X=1.01(1) in 2D X=1.6(3) in 3D (Fig. 3E).
