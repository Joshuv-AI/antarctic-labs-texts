Challenges and methodological considerations for research on the role of sleep stage transitions in altered affective processing
Authors: Maia ten Brink, Haimei Yu, Jin-Xiao Zhang, Sylvia D Kreibig, Rachel Manber, Andrea Goldstein-Piekarski, James J Gross
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFrequent sleep stage transitions and abnormal sleep stage distribution are features of sleep disorders, including insomnia, sleep apnea, and narcolepsy, and have been associated with altered affective processing, including mood disorders. Research on the role of sleep stage transitions is nascent, and mixed operationalizations abound. In this comparative methods study, we overview the ways that prior research has operationalized sleep stage transitions, propose guidelines for four types of metrics, and compare the relevance of each for different analytic purposes. We then discuss three definitional and methodological “hard problems” for research on sleep stage transitions: bias due to scoring discrepancies, low temporal resolution, and opposing definitions of transitions. We discuss the pros and cons of several solutions that use machine learning (ML) scoring algorithms, with examples derived from the Stanford Sleep and Affect polysomnography dataset scored with validated ML algorithms (Stanford STAGES, U-Sleep, and YASA), and conclude with a call to return to descriptive physiological studies to shift the current framing of sleep stage transitions away from categorical state changes. This intends to lay the foundation for further insight into the role of sleep stage transitions in affective function and in clinical dysfunction. Statement of SignificanceThis comparative methods study examines four common approaches to operationalizing sleep stage transitions. In doing so, it lays out the difficult definitional and methodological challenges that face researchers interested in transitions among sleep stages, both in the context of affective functioning and in the context of clinical sleep disorders more broadly. By taking a novel comparative approach to describing transitions via applying machine learning sleep staging algorithms using both 30- and 6-second epochs, this paper helps clarify potential routes and roadblocks to greater understanding of the role of sleep stage transitions in explaining affective states. Keywords: transitions, sleep stages, comparative methods, exploratory, automatic sleep scoring, 6-second epochs

Introduction

Researchers and clinicians have long been interested in understanding the role of sleep stages in affective function [1–7]. This work has primarily focused on the amount of a certain sleep stage (e.g., percentage, duration) throughout the night [8–14]. However, less attention has been paid to the role of transitions between stages—the patterns of distribution and consolidation within and across sleep states. Transitions between sleep stages occur fluidly and dynamically throughout the night as the brain cycles between distinct but linked states of rapid eye movement (REM) sleep and non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep stage 1 (N1), NREM stage 2 (N2), and NREM stage 3 (N3) [15]. According to current practices, these sleep states are determined at the resolution of 30-second epochs of polysomnography (PSG) based on the neurophysiological and muscular characteristics during that epoch [16]. Nascent research examining transitions among these states using this 30-second staging method suggests that there may be a connection between affect functioning and sleep stage transitions.

Sleep Stage Transitions May Play a Role in Affective Function

A small handful of studies in clinical and non-clinical samples, using a variety of metrics, implicate sleep stage transitions in affective dysfunction. Numerous sleep disorders, including insomnia, obstructive sleep apnea, narcolepsy, and REM sleep behavior disorder, are marked by abnormal shifts between sleep stages [17–20], and many of these same sleep disorder patients also demonstrate affective dysfunctions [13, 21–25]. Insomnia patients, who have higher levels of anxiety and depression than healthy sleepers, tend to have abnormal transitions [13, 22, 26, 27]. Individuals with insomnia disorder have been shown to have a higher probability of transitioning from N2 to N1 or wake (i.e., from deeper to lighter stages), and increased probability of transitioning from N1 into N2 discriminates insomnia vs. non-disordered individuals better than conventional stage duration and continuity metrics [18]. REM sleep instability—measured as a greater frequency of transitions out of REM—in particular is theorized to be a crucial component of insomnia, contributing to mood disorders via disrupted locus coeruleus quiescence and excessive orexin [28]. Another study showed that the number of transitions out of REM into wake was associated with depressive symptoms, in particular lack of positive emotions, among adolescents [29]. Finally, an early study of mood and sleep architecture in a non-clinical sample by Cluydts and Visser [30] found a link between the number of sleep stage transitions and tension.

Importantly, studying transitions provides additional insights beyond studying the overall duration of the night spent in a given state or overall sleep fragmentation [26]. Recently, an experiment from Wassing et al. [31] suggested the potential relevance of sleep transitions in overnight affect processing, which involved participants listening to their own karaoke singing as a shame induction before and after sleeping, while their amygdala responses were measured with functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI). Wassing et al. [31] reported that the degree of overnight amygdala change (reduced amygdala adaptation interpreted as an indicator of worse overnight affective recovery) depended on transitions from REM into NREM or wake. That is, the higher the frequency of transitions out of REM, the weaker the association was between REM episode duration and overnight change in amygdala reactivity. However, the main effect linking REM transitions and overnight amygdala reactivity change was only marginally significant.

Wassing et al. [31] also investigated the period of transition to REM, which they defined as the period following the last two consecutive epochs of N3 sleep before a REM episode. They found that longer periods of transition from NREM into REM increased the association of the subsequent REM period with overnight amygdala reactivity change. Wassing et al.’s results hint at the importance of focusing on transitions themselves to understand the role of sleep in affective functioning, as well as highlight the current challenges in defining the relevant characteristics of sleep stage transitions.

Rationale and Aims of the Present Paper

Despite initial promising findings, it is difficult to have confidence in the link between sleep transitions and affect, both because (1) there are a relatively small number of studies and (2) because these studies have employed such a diverse array of approaches to quantifying sleep transitions. For example, quantifications have included the “number of stage shifts” [30], the number of transitions per hour out of specific stages [31], the number of intervals occurring after the last two consecutive epochs of N3 sleep and before a REM episode [31], and the probability of transitions into and out of sleep stages estimated with a first-order continuous Markov model [19]. The inconsistency in definitions and metrics represents a key barrier to making progress in investigating the potential link between sleep stage transitions and affective processing further. Developing a consistent definition to characterize transitions is important for uncovering the underlying neurophysiological mechanisms in adaptive affective functioning, both in clinical psychiatric and sleep-disordered as well as non-clinical populations.

The goal of this paper is therefore to review four challenges of operationalizing and defining sleep stage transitions (see Table 1), compare ways to quantify sleep stage transitions, and propose potential solutions using both human and machine learning (ML) algorithm-scored stages. In the first part, we address the methodological challenge of inconsistent operationalization across studies and, as a potential solution, define four metrics that can be used to summarize the overall degree of transitions across the night and discuss the information carried by each. We give examples and compare each method using 76 PSG records from the Sleep and Affect Study at Stanford University (see Supplement for full Methods) [32, 33]. These PSG records were collected for a separate purpose and scored by a registered PSG technician following American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) guidelines [31] in a sample of healthy adults from the San Francisco Bay Area community (Mage = 30.59 (7.10), 74 per cent female) who completed a single night of home PSG in order to study relationships between sleep bruxism (teeth grinding), affect, and affect regulation (see Supplementary Table S1 for full demographic characteristics of the sample and Supplementary Table 2 for PSG descriptive statistics).

Challenges of operationalizing and defining sleep stage transitions

In part two, we discuss two methodological challenges with operationalizing, defining, and determining the presence of true sleep stage transitions given current sleep scoring methods (see Table 1): the amplification of transition scoring disagreements using traditional AASM scoring methods and the temporal mismatch between scoring with 30-second epochs and neurophysiologically relevant stage transitions. We propose two potential solutions taking advantage of ML scoring, demonstrate one of them using the Stanford Sleep and Affect Study dataset to score 6-second epochs, and discuss drawbacks to both methods.

Finally, in part three, we describe a fundamental conceptual challenge to studying transitions—namely, the lack of a definition for transitions as discrete state changes vs. transitional periods—and call for further descriptive research using unsupervised ML models to understand and define the neurophysiological signatures of transitions.

Part 1: Methodological Heterogeneity in Quantifying Sleep Stage Transitions

One key challenge to studying sleep stage transitions is that different papers and research groups have calculated summary metrics of transitions in various ways. We propose four categories distinct metrics based on prior literature—transition count [17], rate [29, 31, 34], ratio [35], and probability [18, 19, 36] —and suggest that researchers adopt one of these metrics as a solution to the heterogeneity found in the literature. See Table 2 for a definition and description of each transition metric, as well as an example of how to interpret the metric for comparing between individuals. We use the term transition metrics to refer to these four categories, while transition type refers to possible transitions from state i into state j or from state i into state j.

Transition metrics

First, we can calculate a summary metric of sleep stage transitions as a total count by counting the number of transitions across the entire night. We can then bin transitions according to the analysis, which we are terming transition types. If investigating which stage an epoch transitioned into, one would calculate transitions to REM from any other stage besides itself (“→ R”). If investigating specific transition types, for example, one could calculate from NREM to REM (“N → R”) and Wake to N1 (“W → N1”). This metric was employed in an early study of mood and sleep architecture by Cluydts and Visser [30], where they operationalized transitions as the “number of stage shifts.”

Two further metrics can be calculated from total count. Transition rate for each transition type can be determined by calculating the number of transitions per unit of time, as in Wassing et al. [31], which examined the number of transitions per hour (i.e., rate) from REM. [31] They had transition count as a predictor but adjusted for REM duration in their models, which could be considered a transition rate metric, as it quantifies transition count relative to stage length.

We can also calculate the transition ratio for each transition type. Transitions could be impacted by likelihood of consolidated sleep stage, which could be influenced by prior sleep deprivation or sleep disorder phenotype. This normalizes a given transition count by the total number of transitions, allowing researchers to capture the specific role of a given type of transition (e.g., “W → R”) as opposed to an individual’s general tendency to experience stage transitions. Because Markov probability only takes the current epoch into account when predicting the likelihood of the next epoch, it does not require normalization for either duration of sleep episode or number of transitions. In essence, it combines the benefits of using rate or ratio for interpreting transition counts into a single metric. This metric can also index sleep stage consolidation, since it can determine the transition probability from a given stage into itself. This is in line with Bianchi et al.’s [19] operationalization of fragmentation in obstructive sleep apnea patients using a first-order continuous time Markov model to calculate transition probabilities that accounted for observed distributions as well as the probabilistic transitions that reflect the inherent distributions given sleep cycle dynamics. Stevner et al. [36] also mapped Markov probabilities of wake and N1, N2, and N3 transitions to topographic brain states using functional connectivity analysis to extract 19 whole-brain network states defined according to temporal probability.

Testing Overlap in Variance among Different Transition Metrics

Importantly, the four transition metrics described above capture different aspects of variance. Although we expected relationships among the metrics because they are all based on transition count, we examined the observed degree of covariance using the 76 PSG records from the Stanford Sleep and Affect Study (see Supplementary Materials for full description of methods and demographic characteristics). Pearson’s correlations (see Figure 1) were run for all four human scoring-derived transition metrics and all 25 possible transition types (total tests n = 4095). False discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted p-values were calculated with a threshold of 0.05 using the “p.adjust” function of the stats package in R [37]. Overall, the sparsity of significant correlations after FDR correction and lack of visibly structured clustering of patterns either by metrics or by transition types in Figure 1 indicates that there is not a highly predictable pattern of correlation. There are also several correlation values of 1.00 (e.g., W → N3 rate and ratio), which may be due to extremely low base rates of certain transition types leading to nearly identical rate, ratio, and probability values. However, there does appear to be some consistency in positive correlation values forming an approximate diagonal stripe. Specifically, rate and ratio, as well as ratio and Markov probability, appear to have a stripe of higher correlation values (darker red) at the diagonal of sub-sections delineated by dashed lines, indicating that those metrics may travel together. Finally, there appear to be several negative correlations on the off-diagonal between ratio and Markov probability metrics, indicating that having a higher transition ratio for specific transition types may decrease the probability of other transition types occurring. We can also see that the ratio values (i.e., relative to total number of transitions) show more significant correlations with Markov probability values than rates (i.e., transitions per hour). This pattern can be understood as capturing the natural cycling through ordered stages (e.g., through gradually deeper stages of NREM) over the course of a night; however, this is not as apparent when examining rate, which does not necessarily match with the length of an individual’s sleep cycle. Potentially, if rate metrics were calculated with the denominator as cycles instead of sleep duration in hours, the pattern of correlations with Markov probability metrics may be more similar to ratio metrics.

Standardizing a Transition Metric for Comparison across Studies

In conclusion, both mathematically and in terms of observed correlations, we find that these different metrics will carry somewhat information about different aspects of transitions. We suggest that Markov probabilities could be adopted for the purposes of comparing individuals and meta-analyzing findings across different studies. Markov probabilities are also the only metric that incorporates baseline probability distributions, inherently part of the sleep cycle. However, operationalizing transitions using total transition count is an option if a researcher wants to examine independent sources of variance due to tendency to experience transitions, duration of the entire night or a specific stage, or other individual phenotypes.

Part 2: Methodological Challenges for Determining a True Stage Transition Using AASM Scoring

A more difficult issue to resolve is the fact that transitions have traditionally been described by the presence and classification of stages, not by their transitions. PSG scoring has historically determined the stages of sleep via human visual pattern recognition in 30-second epochs [16]. Human scorers usually have only around 83 per cent agreement (lower for certain stages, e.g., 74.6 per cent agreement for N1, N2, or wake during a sleep period [39]), with scoring disagreements occurring especially during transitions between sleep stages [40]. Recently, automated sleep staging with ML has gained popularity and accuracy. Published ML algorithms are trained on large datasets of multiple thousands of records, demonstrate accuracy comparable to human scorers, and are cost- and time-efficient. As a demonstration of the generally high agreement of validated ML scoring algorithms with human scorers, we re-scored the PSG records using three algorithms developed by sleep experts and validated on thousands of PSG records from a variety of samples: Stanford STAGES [41], YASA [42], and U-Sleep 2 [43]. In Figure 2, Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves [44] show comparisons for each of the three ML algorithms with the human scoring in our Stanford Sleep and Affect Study dataset [41, 42, 45]. STAGES performed closest to human scoring, followed by YASA and then U-Sleep. Higher area under the curve (AUC) values indicated that, while all three algorithms had high agreement for wake epochs, there was more disagreement for other stages, with least agreement for N1. Using algorithms for scoring may offer some solutions to the following methodological and conceptual challenges faced by researchers investigating sleep stage transitions.

A‌ASM Scoring Amplifies Disagreements when Scoring Transitions

Crucially, the consequences of scoring disagreement become magnified when investigating transitions. A hypothetical 1000-epoch recording would have 999 possible transitions between each 30-second epoch, each of which has 25 possible transition states (five stages for epoch n and five stages for epoch n + 1). If two scorers had 83 per cent agreement, then 17 per cent of epochs (170 epochs) would switch from stage i to stage j. Therefore, 169 transitions would change from one stage to one of 24 other possible stages, leading to 169 × 24 = 4056 possible altered transitions at 999 possible temporal locations in a hypnogram. This mathematical fact amplifies the potential for bias in metrics for specific transition types from single scorers exponentially more than stage classification disagreement.

To examine a more ecologically valid probability distribution of sleep stages, given the nature of sleep cycles, we therefore used four scoring methods (one human; three ML algorithms: STAGES [41], YASA [42], and U-Sleep 2 [43]) to score the PSG records from our Stanford Sleep and Affect dataset and to calculate the transition count for each transition type. The Fleiss’ κ [46] indicating agreement across the four scoring methods overall for each of the five different stages (Wake, REM, N1, N2, and N3) was 0.64 (considered substantial agreement), but for each of the 25 different transition types was only 0.58 (considered moderate agreement; Table 3). Furthermore, the four metrics in Part 1 vary in their sensitivity to scorer disagreements. For the count metric, each scoring disagreement potentially removes one transition type (i → j) and replaces it with another (i’ → j’), altering counts for up to two transition types. This means that the raw count shifts linearly with each disagreement. However, the impact depends on the base rate of each transition type; common transition types (e.g., N2 → N3) would be less affected than uncommon transitions (e.g., W → R). The rate metric, using sleep duration as the denominator, is only sensitive to wake/sleep disagreements, but preserves within-sleep transition relative proportions. The ratio metric, which uses the total transition count as the denominator, is similarly more affected by changes to rare transitions than common ones. Finally, Markov probabilities are highly sensitive to scorer disagreements, as they impact both the numerator (i.e., count of i → j) and denominator (i.e., count of any j given a prior epoch i). This means that scoring disagreements can alter the entire conditional probability matrix, amplifying bias across all transition types, but especially uncommon transition types. In summary, normalized metrics are more sensitive to scorer disagreements, with Markov probabilities most subject to amplified bias. Across all metrics, the lower the base rate of a transition type, the more prone it is to distortion.

A Temporal Mismatch between AASM Scored Epochs and Neurophysiological Stage Transitions

Another methodological challenge lies with the inherent classification bias involved in scoring according to AASM principles. According to AASM scoring criteria, human scoring requires categorizing sleep stages based on visually apparent characteristics of a given sleep stage occurring for certain proportions of a 30-second epoch. For example, for scoring N2, k-complexes must occur in the 50 per cent of the record for that epoch to count as N2, and scoring N3 requires at least 20 per cent of the epoch to contain slow wave activity, but the first slow wave must occur before the 50 per cent mark [47, 48]. This means that traditional AASM scoring may be erasing relevant stage transitions based on where epochs fall relative to the recording. It also means that trying to research transitions as a time series vector of stage classifications based on human scoring places the moment of transition between the epochs, but that may not accurately and precisely represent the timing of the neurophysiological states transitioning. As a result, the “ground truth” for determining the precise timing of when a stage ends or begins is influenced not only by the neurophysiology but also by where neurophysiological markers fall within an arbitrary scoring epoch length. Thus, using different epoch lengths or different starting times of the PSG record could result in potentially different scores. We will discuss the potentials and pitfalls of two potential proposals, both using ML, for resolving this temporal precision issue.

ML algorithms provide the opportunity for much more complex characterization than a categorical choice every 30 seconds. Some algorithms allow scoring across shorter epochs than the traditional 30 seconds [41, 43, 47]. This could potentially allow automated scoring to capture transitions more precisely than a human scorer, who typically determines stages based on more than a certain proportion of a 30-second epoch meeting particular classification criteria [47, 48].

To explore this option, we re-scored our dataset using 6-second epochs with two published scoring algorithms capable of changing the epoch length, STAGESb and U-Sleep 2. The choice of 6-second epoch lengths was based on wanting an evenly divisible epoch length for comparison to 30-second epochs that would be shorter than the 50 per cent of epoch AASM stage classification rule, short enough to capture very brief states, and long enough that ML algorithms could effectively classify stages given prior training on 30-second epoch data. Figures 4 and 5 show ROC curves for 6-second epochs scored by STAGES vs. U-Sleep 2 for both sleep stages (Figure 4) and transitions (Figure 5), displaying the agreement between two different ML algorithms as if they were two different scorers. The overall agreement between the 6-second epoch sleep staging for these two ML algorithms was Cohen’s κ = 0.61, and for the transition types was κ = 0.55. Scoring of stages with 6-second epochs by two ML algorithm “scorers” showed AUC values ranging from 0.75 to 0.94, while AUC values of different ML algorithms relative to a human scorer using 30-second epochs (Figure 4) ranged from 0.81 to 0.99 (Figure 2). Likewise, for scoring transition types, the range of AUC values for agreement between two ML algorithm “scorers” was higher (range 0.26–0.94; Figure 5) compared to transition types scored with 30-second epochs (range 0.16–0.98; Figure 3). Generally, scoring agreement between the two ML algorithms is comparable or slightly worse when using 6-second epochs relative to ML algorithms’ agreement with human scoring with 30-second epochs displayed in Figures 2 and 3.

Despite the potential promise of using shorter epochs, a fundamental conundrum remains: we do not currently have a ground truth to compare against, as there is no consensus across the field about the appropriate epoch length to categorize an epoch as entirely one stage or entirely another. Indeed, empirical research is needed to determine whether this varies significantly between individuals, within individuals, or follows a stereotypical pattern across people.

Another way we propose that ML algorithms could address the issue of AASM’s proportional (e.g., 50 per cent) epoch classification rule takes advantage of the fact that some ML algorithms calculate confidence ratings for each stage in order to make the final classification. This property could be leveraged to examine the algorithm’s second choice for classification to determine when stage transitions may be occurring within a given 30-second epoch. That is, epochs where transitions occur could be identified when the first-choice classification and the second-choice classification have relatively similar confidence intervals. Unfortunately, confidence ratings are unlikely to be interpretable; instead, they capture the weights created at the penultimate classification step after a series of “black box” computations. STAGES and U-Sleep are based on convolutional neural networks [41, 43] while YASA is based on a tree-based gradient boosting classifier, LightGBM [49]. Those weights may not be picking up on the relevant properties for a second-best estimate, but on some other unknown properties most likely to reproduce human 30-second staging (Vallat, personal communication, October 11, 2022). Moreover, because the algorithms have been trained on human scoring, which provides only one stage (not a second-best estimate), and because the algorithms are not trained to optimize the second-best estimate, the next-highest confidence rating may not reflect a second-best stage estimate. However, if future ML algorithms are built and trained on data specifically for the purposes of detecting transitional epochs—for example with a dataset of human-rated confidence in each stage categorization, a human-generated dataset of second-best stage estimates, or an optimization algorithm that specifies particular neural patterns and spectral activity as markers of a given stage, regardless of epoch length—then perhaps a method of this kind could be employed.

Part 3: Are Sleep Stage Transitions Discrete State Changes or Transitional Periods?

The last, and perhaps most fundamental, challenge to investigating transitions involves settling on a definition. Some researchers have defined transitions as a period with a start and end, not just as the existence of a discrete shift in the type of sleep stage. Benington et al. [50] identified non-REM to REM transitions (termed “NRTs”) in rats by identifying and scoring a single REM episode for each NRT in 10-second epochs, independent of subsequent duration and timing of REM episodes. Although they described these brief REM sleep episodes as “true REM sleep episodes,” they noted that the brief episodes were too short to count when using a typical 10-minute rat sleep cycle criterion. Benington et al. showed that the characteristics of NREM sleep alter leading up to REM sleep, such that there is a roughly 30–60-second period of decreased slow wave activity, increased theta (6–9 Hz) and sigma (10–14 Hz) activity, gradually decreasing electromyography tone, and brief, large ponto-geniculo-occipital wave spikes measured in the cerebellum or pontine nucleus. Importantly, like Wassing et al. [31], they suggested that it was not only the REM characteristics that determined the REM episodes, but the characteristics of a prior NREM period.

In humans, transitional spectral activity leading up to a stage shift may contain more distinctions than are traditionally captured in AASM scoring, even if the scorer would classify epochs as belonging to the same stage. Indeed, Decat et al. [51] used feature-based clustering to identify distinctive sets of features in PSG records that had partial overlap (43–77 per cent) with traditional AASM scoring. Stage N2, in particular, showed a diversity of matches with different ML-identified clusters. Although Decat et al. did not focus specifically on transitions, their findings suggest that clusters with consistent features occur between more stereotypical AASM stage episodes. This definition of stage transitions lies in contrast with other researchers, including Yakunina et al. [35], Bianchi et al. [19], and Stevner et al. [36], who define transitions as discrete state changes between stages, such that there is no in-between state of “NREM to REM” (similar to Benington et al.’s finding in rats [50]), only the state transition from NREM to REM. The conflicting definitions of stage transitions are a result of methodological constraints that followed from assumptions about biological relevance—e.g., the limitations of human visual PSG scoring, the field’s early focus on categorical sleep stages as opposed to spectral activity over continuous periods—and therefore require both empirical evidence and methodological innovation to find potential solutions. Because this definitional challenge cannot be resolved with current AASM-based (human or ML) classification systems, more descriptive research is needed in human and comparative biology research to determine how we should define transitions between stages. Importantly, the ML methods described in this paper are all supervised models trained on AASM stage labeling classification as ground truth. We suggest that unsupervised ML models like those employed by Decat et al. [51] have an important role to play in describing sleep neurophysiology beyond traditional AASM scoring. The findings may recapitulate some traditional sleep stage transitions but might also spark new classifications. Whether the methods employed to harness ML (e.g., clustering, Hidden Markov Models, feature extraction, prediction of subsequent neural states) are supervised or unsupervised, the goals remain descriptive because we still have not defined what a transition is, how long it lasts, what features characterize it, how many forms it can take, and is built on the assumption that AASM sleep stages themselves are the best functional and structural organization of neurophysiological states. This will require researchers to explicitly shift focus from the consistency of states to the shifts between and variation within states and, accordingly, to employ methods optimal for detecting unstable and transitional states.

Discussion

Implications

The implications of resolving the methodological and definitional challenges outlined here are crucial for a greater understanding of the etiology and consequences of sleep disorders, including insomnia, obstructive sleep apnea, narcolepsy, and REM sleep behavior disorder, which are characterized by abnormal shifts between sleep stages [17–19]. Insomnia patients display abnormal wake–sleep transitions and have higher levels of anxiety and depression [13, 22, 26, 27]. Altered sleep stage transitions, particularly in REM, have been identified as diagnostic markers of narcolepsy [17, 52]. Additionally, idiopathic REM sleep behavior disorder patients exhibit more frequent transitions between sleep and wake, as well as between NREM and REM [35]. Refining definitions and consistent metrics can help clinical researchers develop diagnostic biomarkers for abnormal alterations in transitions.

Further, as our understanding of the functional importance of different aspects of sleep architecture for affective processing in mood disorders is still incomplete, comparative methods studies can support further research on the connection between sleep stage transitions and affective processing. Exploring different characterizations of transitions may uncover patterns and mechanisms among shared neural circuitry regulating transitions and implicated in adaptive affective functioning. Moreover, adopting consistent definitions and metrics will facilitate efforts to reproduce initial results that hint at transitions playing an underappreciated role in overnight affective processing.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that bear mentioning. First, we used our own dataset in healthy community adults rather than large freely available PSG datasets such as Sleep Heart Health Study PSG Database, the Human Sleep Project, and the Sleep EDF Database [53] to be able to compare methods in a completely new sample, as many of the ML-algorithms have been trained on these same freely available datasets [41–43]. Second, much remains unknown about why and when stage transitions occur, so we prioritized describing patterns using our data over statistical significance. Independent replication and confirmation in future work are essential. Third, our operationalization of sleep stage transitions did not explicitly consider adjustment for homeostatic and circadian factors. Future research should be done to consider operationalization that accounts for factors including sleep timing and prior sleep loss. Fourth, this study’s findings may not be generalizable to other populations. Our recruitment excluded participants with comorbidities that might confound associations between affect and sleep, resulting in a majority female, highly educated, and atypically healthy sample with sleep bruxism (teeth grinding) overrepresented [54] compared with the general population. Fifth, this study did not involve a habituation PSG night, so the transition measures observed may not be generalizable to a night of typical sleep. Indeed, hemispheric cortical arousal has been shown to occur for the first night of PSG [55], suggesting that the particularities of this single night’s comparisons of transition metrics and scoring results should not necessarily be generalized to multiple nights of PSG. However, ecological validity was preserved, as participants maintained their own bedtimes, wake times, and sleep rituals within their own habitual sleep environment. Lastly, the staging algorithms we used are continuously improving; this work reflects present (2023–2024) ML capacities for sleep staging. We only applied two staging algorithms using 6-second epochs, as the other algorithms only allow predictions on 30-second or longer epochs. With larger datasets and updated ML algorithms, future research can use different and updated sleep scoring algorithms, with shorter and different epoch lengths.

Conclusions

Transitions between and interruptions of sleep stages, which can occur with excessive frequency and lead to abnormal stage distributions in disorders such as insomnia, sleep apnea, and narcolepsy, may play a role in dysfunctional affective processing. This research is still nascent and limited by highly variable methodology as well as definitions.

Given existing sleep staging methods, we proposed consistent guidelines for operationalizing sleep stage transitions—number, rate, ratio, and probability of transitions into, out of, or between different stages—and discussed the information carried by each. For the purposes of comparing findings across study designs and samples, we suggest that researchers use the Markov probability metric, with the caveat that scoring disagreements will strongly amplify bias in this metric. For the purposes of comparing transition metrics across scorers, we recommend the ratio metric, as it is conceptually similar to Markov probability and showed strong correlations with Markov probability in our empirical data, but it is only moderately sensitive to bias from scoring disagreements. Other metrics may be relevant to specific clinical or research purposes, and further research is needed to understand the predictive relevance of these metrics. Finally, researchers should be aware that bias related to scoring disagreements will be worse if they are interested in transition types that occur infrequently; if those transition types are of particular interest, and then we recommend using transition count, as it is not a normalized metric.

However, definitional and methodological challenges remain with operationalizing sleep stage transitions, specifically related to inflated bias due to staging disagreements, problems of temporal resolution, and varying definitions of transitions as state changes vs. separate transitional states. We discussed the pros and cons of several solutions using ML scoring algorithms, with examples using the Stanford Sleep and Affect PSG dataset. In this dataset, ML scoring of stages and transitions using 6-second epochs showed comparable agreement among two ML algorithms, and agreement did not appear to diminish much relative to scoring with 30-second epochs, suggesting that shorter epochs perform no worse than traditional methods. Therefore, we propose that 6-second epochs scored by ML could be used to investigate transitions with finer temporal resolution. We further suggest that ML algorithms should be built and trained on data specifically for the purposes of detecting transitional epochs. Ultimately, however, we argue that resolving these three definitional and methodological “hard problems” is impossible without further descriptive neurophysiological research and shifting the framing of sleep stage transitions away from categorical state changes.
