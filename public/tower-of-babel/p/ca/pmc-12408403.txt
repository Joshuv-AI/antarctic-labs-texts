H-silicene nanosheets as a novel therapeutic approach for disuse muscle atrophy by modulating macrophage polarization
Authors: Lubing Cai, Zhiqiang Zhang, Yangyang Chen, Yuqi Li, Ya-Xuan Zhu, Jiwu Chen, Yaying Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Disuse muscle atrophy (DMA) is characterized by progressive loss of muscle mass and strength, often accompanied by inflammation and macrophage imbalance. Here, we introduce hydrogenated silicene nanosheets (H-silicene) as a novel nanotherapeutic strategy to mitigate DMA through modulating macrophage polarization. H-silicene exhibited good biocompatibility and sustained hydrogen release. In vitro, H-silicene suppressed LPS-induced M1 macrophage activation while promoting M2 polarization, and alleviated myotube atrophy in co-culture assays. In a murine immobilization model, intramuscular H-silicene administration significantly mitigated muscle wasting, reduced fibrosis, and improved functional outcomes. Immunofluorescence staining confirmed a decrease in iNOS+/TNF-α+ cells and an elevated CD206+/IL-10+ populations in treated muscle. Integrated transcriptomic and proteomic analyses revealed H-silicene-mediated modulation of pathways related to inflammation, oxidative stress, and myogenesis. These findings highlight H-silicene as a promising immunoregulatory nanomaterial for the treatment of disuse-induced muscle atrophy.MethodsH-silicene nanosheets were synthesized from CaSi2 under argon-protected acid treatment and probe-sonicated to obtain dispersible nanosheets. Cytocompatibility was assessed in RAW264.7 and C2C12 cells. Macrophage polarization was analyzed by flow cytometry, ELISA, and immunofluorescence after LPS stimulation ± H-silicene. Conditioned media were applied to differentiated C2C12 myotubes to assess paracrine effects. In vivo, a mouse hindlimb immobilization model was used to induce DMA. Mice received intramuscular injections of H-silicene (250 ppm). Muscle tissue was analyzed by histology, immunofluorescence, behavioral assays, and RNA-seq and proteomics.ResultsH-silicene exhibited low cytotoxicity and dose-dependently suppressed LPS-induced M1 polarization while enhancing M2 polarization. It reduced proinflammatory cytokines and preserved C2C12 myotube morphology in co-culture models. In vivo, H-silicene improved muscle fiber area, reduced collagen deposition, restored grip strength , and improved rotarod performance in DMA mice. Immunostaining confirmed reduced iNOS+/TNF-α+ and increased CD206+/IL-10+ macrophages. Multi-omics analysis revealed regulation of inflammatory and regenerative signaling, including NF-κB, IL-17, and myoblast differentiation pathways.ConclusionThis study demonstrates that H-silicene alleviates disuse-induced muscle atrophy by promoting the transition from pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages to anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages and remodeling the local immune microenvironment, making it a promising nanotherapeutic for muscle-wasting disorders.

Introduction

Disuse muscle atrophy (DMA), characterized by the progressive loss of muscle mass, volume, and strength, is a common degenerative condition of the musculoskeletal system. It often occurs following prolonged immobilization, such as extended bed rest or postoperative rehabilitation [1]. The clinical consequences of DMA are severe, including increased risks of falls, delayed postoperative recovery, and reduced quality of life [2]. Despite considerable research into therapeutic strategies—such as physical therapy and nutritional supplementation aimed at balancing protein synthesis and degradation—current interventions remain suboptimal [[3], [4], [5]]. Therefore, understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying DMA and identifying novel therapeutic targets have become critical in orthopedics and sports medicine.

Recent studies have increasingly focused on the role of the local immune microenvironment in skeletal muscle, particularly the involvement of macrophages, in the pathogenesis of muscle atrophy [[6], [7], [8]]. Macrophages, essential regulators of skeletal muscle homeostasis, exist in two primary phenotypes: pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages and anti-inflammatory, tissue-repairing M2 macrophages [9]. In DMA, the skeletal muscle microenvironment undergoes significant disruption, including hypoxia due to reduced blood flow and excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [10,11]. These conditions suppress mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation in M2 macrophages [12] while promoting glycolysis-dependent M1 polarization [13], leading to excessive secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines and muscle wasting [13,14]. Moreover, the elevated ROS levels induced by disuse drive M1 polarization [13]. Thus, targeting macrophage polarization to inhibit the M1 phenotype and promote M2 transition may offer a promising therapeutic approach to break the vicious cycle of disuse-induced inflammation and muscle atrophy [15]. Although pharmacological agents like bezafibrate and celecoxib have shown partial efficacy in mitigating M1 polarization and alleviating atrophy in animal models [6,7], their therapeutic potential in DMA remains uncertain. Hence, innovative strategies for macrophage modulation are urgently needed.

Two-dimensional (2D) nanomaterials have shown increasing potential in biomedical applications, including drug delivery, tissue repair, and immune modulation, due to their unique physicochemical properties and high surface-to-volume ratio. Recent studies have highlighted that certain functional 2D materials can actively modulate immune responses, eliminate excess ROS via valence state–dependent redox reactions, suggesting their utility in inflammation-related diseases [[16], [17], [18], [19]]. Meanwhile, hydrogen gas (H2), known for its antioxidant and immunomodulatory properties, has emerged as a promising therapeutic candidate for inflammatory diseases [[20], [21], [22]]. As a small molecule with high membrane permeability, hydrogen can rapidly diffuse into tissues and cells, scavenge excess ROS, and alleviate oxidative stress [23]. It also influences macrophage polarization, showing potential in treating inflammatory conditions and promoting wound healing [24]. However, the clinical application of hydrogen is limited by its low solubility and rapid dissipation in biological environments [25]. To address this, we have developed the two-dimensional hydrogen-silicene nanosheets designed to overcome the limitations of conventional hydrogen delivery. These nanosheets react with local water molecules to generate hydrogen in situ, providing a localized and sustained antioxidant and immunomodulatory effect. Outcomes in vitro and in vivo together demonstrated the excellent protective effects of H-silicene as a H2 supplier against DMA, where intramuscular injection of the nanosheets significantly alleviated muscle atrophy and modulated macrophage polarization. Transcriptomic and proteomic analyses revealed coordinated regulation of inflammation, metabolism, and regenerative pathways at both gene and protein levels after H-silicene intervention.

Methods

Preparation of H-silicene nanosheets

To synthesize H-silicene nanosheets, 1 g of CaSi2 was dispersed in 100 mL of HCl precooled under argon protection and kept stirring for 7 days at −20 °C. Then, the H-silicene sheets were centrifuged (12000 rpm, 10 min) and sequentially washed with acetone and ethanol for 2 times. The product was dispersed in 100 mL of ethanol and subjected to tip sonication (600 W, on: 2 s, off: 4 s) for 8 hours under ice bath to obtain H-silicene nanosheets. Calcium silicide (CaSi2, tech-95, 95 %, powder, Gelest) was purchased from Alfa Aesar (MA, USA). Hydrochloric acid (HCl) was purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Ethanol was bought from Shanghai Lingfeng Reagent Chemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Characterizations of H-silicene nanosheets

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM-F200, JEOL) was applied to evaluate the morphology of nanosheets. Phase composition of H-silicene was characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD; Ultima IV, Rigaku). The thickness of H-silicene was determined using atomic force microscopy (AFM; Dimension ICON, Bruker). The hydrodynamic diameters and polydispersity index (PDI) value (characterized via dynamic light scattering, DLS) of H-silicene nanosheets were measured using a zetasizer (Nano ZS, Malvern Instruments, UK). To examine the hydrogen release behavior of H-silicene, 20 mg of nanosheets were dispersed in 40 mL of PBS (pH 7.4) and incubated in a sealed 100 mL glass reaction vessel at room temperature. At predetermined time points, 5 mL of gas from the headspace was carefully collected using a gas-tight syringe and analyzed for hydrogen content using gas chromatography (GC2010, Shimadzu, Japan). Each measurement was performed in triplicate (n = 3). The cumulative hydrogen release was calculated based on the standard hydrogen calibration curve over time.

Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data used in this study were acquired from Tan J et al. upon reasonable request, as stated in the original publication [26]. Raw sequencing data were processed using fastp for quality control and aligned to the mm10 reference genome with STARsolo. Downstream analysis was conducted using Seurat version 4.4.0 R for quality filtering, clustering, and differential expression analysis, with mitochondrial gene percentage <10 % and nFeature_RNA between 200 and 6000 as quality thresholds. Cell subpopulations were characterized through differential gene expression analysis (|log2FC|>0.25, adj. p < 0.05). Macrophages in single-cell data were extracted, and the process of dimensional reduction and clustering was reiterated using Seurat software. Subsequently, the Seurat “FindAllMarkers” was applied to discover differentially expressed genes that characterize the subpopulations, and “FindMarkers” was used to identify upregulated genes in the IM group. Functional enrichment analysis used clusterProfiler for GO and KEGG pathway analysis.

Cell culture

RAW264.7 and C2C12 cell lines were obtained from Chinese Academy of Science (Shanghai, China) and cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (Gibco, C11995500BT) with 10 % fetal bovine serum (Yeasen, China) and 1 % penicillin–streptomycin (NCM Biotech, China) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5 % CO2.

For M1 polarization, RAW264.7 cells were stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 100 ng/mL, Sigma-Aldrich, L2880) for 24 h. Untreated cells served as non-polarized controls.

For C2C12 differentiation, when cells reached 60–80 % confluency, the medium was replaced with highglucose DMEM with 2 % horse serum (Gibco, 26050070), and then cultured for another 7 days.

Cytotoxicity assay

RAW264.7 and C2C12 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at 1 × 104 cells/well and incubated with or without H-silicene. After treatment, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent (Beyotime, China) was added to each well, followed by incubation for 1–4 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA).

Flow cytometry

RAW264.7 cells were collected, blocked, and incubated with anti-CD86 (BioLegend, 105011) and anti-CD206 (BioLegend, 141705) antibodies for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. After washing with PBS, cells were analyzed using a flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA).

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min and blocked for 30 min, followed by incubation with primary antibodies against CCR7 (Abcam, ab32527) or Arg-1 (Abcam, ab91279) overnight at 4 °C. Secondary antibody (Abcam, ab150080) was applied for 1 h, followed by nuclear staining with DAPI (Servicebio, China). Images were captured using a DMI8 fluorescence microscope (Leica, Germany).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

RAW264.7 cells were pretreated with LPS (100 ng/mL) for 2 h, followed by H-silicene (100 or 250 ppm) for 24 h. Supernatants were collected, centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min, and stored at −80 °C. Cytokine levels (TNF-α, IL-1α, IL-10, TGF-β) were quantified using commercial ELISA kits (Enzyme-linked Biotechnology, Shanghai) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Absorbance was read at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA). Each condition was tested in triplicate, and cytokine concentrations were calculated based on standard curves. For ELISA and flow cytometry analyses, each experimental condition was tested in triplicate biological replicates.

Giemsa staining

Differentiated C2C12 myotubes were washed with PBS, fixed in pre-cooled methanol for 10 min, and stained with Giemsa solution (1:10 dilution; Servicebio, China) for 15 min. After rinsing with distilled water and drying, stained myotubes were imaged under a bright-field microscope. Myotube diameters were measured in 10 random fields per well using ImageJ software.

Animal

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Shanghai Chengxi Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Approval No. CX052503047). Eighteen male C57BL/6 mice (10 weeks old, ∼20 g) were housed under SPF conditions with controlled temperature (22 ± 1 °C), humidity (50 ± 10 %), and a 12-h light/dark cycle. Mice were randomly assigned to three groups (n = 6 each): (1) control, (2) immobilization for 7 days (model), and (3) immobilization with H-silicene treatment (intramuscular injection into the gastrocnemius on days 2 and 5).

Hindlimb immobilization

Bilateral hindlimb immobilization was performed as previously described. Mice were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg, i. p.), and both hindlimbs were restrained in an extended position using surgical tape and shortened cotton swab sticks.

Tissue collections and histological analysis

At the end of experiments, mice were euthanized under 3 % isoflurane followed by cervical dislocation. Gastrocnemius, tibialis anterior, and quadriceps muscles were harvested, weighed, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, or fixed in 4 % neutral buffered formalin (Servicebio, China). Tissues were embedded, sectioned (5 μm), and stained with hematoxylin-eosin (H&E), Masson's trichrome, or used for immunofluorescence. Major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys) were also collected for H&E staining. Semi-quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ. Antibodies are listed in Table S1.

Behavioral assessments

For grip strength test, gently lower the mouse over the top of the grid of the grip strength meter, so that both its front paws and hind paws can grip the grid. Allow the mouse to attach to the grid properly before pulling it away. Keep the torso parallel to the grid and pull the mouse back horizontally, slowly and steadily increase the pulling force until the hind paws are released. Record the value and repeat this procedure twice to obtain an average grip strength measurement of one mouse.

The rotarod test was used to assess motor coordination. Mice were placed on a rotating rod accelerating from 4 to 60 rpm. The latency to fall, speed at fall, and distance traveled were recorded. All assessments were performed by a blinded investigator. Mice were pre-trained three times per day for three consecutive days before modeling.

RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). RNA-seq and data analysis were performed by NovelBio Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) using the CytoNavigator Analysis Platform (sc.novelbrain.com). Differentially expressed genes were identified using DESeq2 [27] with the following thresholds: |log2FC| > 1, p < 0.05, and FDR <0.05.

4D label-free proteomics

Proteins were extracted in SDT buffer, homogenized with quartz sand using an MP Fastprep-24 homogenizer, then sonicated, boiled, centrifuged, filtered (0.22 μm), and quantified via BCA assay (Beyotime, P0012). Proteomic analysis was conducted by Genechem Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). MS data were processed using MaxQuant (v1.6.17.0) with an FDR <1 % at the peptide and protein levels. Label-free quantification (LFQ) intensities were used to calculate protein abundance. Proteins with fold change >2 or <0.5 and p < 0.05 (Student's t-test) were defined as differentially expressed.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10.4.1. Student's t-test or one-way ANOVA was applied as appropriate. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All image-based quantifications (e.g., immunofluorescence, histological sections) using ImageJ were performed by two independent researchers in a blinded manner to minimize potential bias.

Results

Synthesis and characterization of H-silicene nanosheets

Further, the structural analysis of H-silicene was performed by X-ray diffraction (XRD, Fig. 1D), and the presence of Si–Si and Si–H bonds was demonstrated by Raman spectrum. Macrophage polarization profile in disuse-induced skeletal muscle atrophy

To elucidate the cellular landscape of disuse-induced skeletal muscle atrophy, single-cell RNA sequencing was performed on gastrocnemius muscles from immobilized (IM) and normal control (NC) mice. H&E staining of gastrocnemius cross-sections from normal control (NC) and immobilization (IM) groups showed myofiber atrophy and increased interstitial space following 7 days of hindlimb immobilization (Fig. 2A and B). Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) analysis revealed distinct clustering of multiple cell populations, including B cells, endothelial cells, fibro-adipogenic progenitors (FAPs), fibroblasts, glial cells, macrophages, muscle stem cells (MuSCs), neutrophils, smooth muscle cells, and T cells (Fig. 2C). Notably, a pronounced shift in the distribution was observed in the IM group, showing a significant increase in macrophages compared to the NC group. Unsupervised clustering confirmed that a higher proportion of cells in the IM muscle samples formed macrophage clusters (Fig. 2D). This result was further confirmed by immunofluorescence staining of the gastrocnemius muscles (Fig. 2E and F). Feature plots of macrophage polarization markers indicated that CD86, a classical M1 marker, was predominantly expressed in the IM group, suggesting enhanced pro-inflammatory activation, while CD163, an M2 marker, was more enriched in the NC group, indicating an imbalance favoring M1 polarization in the disused muscle (Fig. 2G). In vitro biocompatibility and cytotoxicity of H-silicene nanosheets

Prior to evaluating therapeutic efficacy, we assessed the cytotoxicity of-H-silience nanosheets on RAW264.7 macrophages and C2C12 myoblasts using CCK-8 and live/dead staining assays. No significant reduction in cell viability was observed at concentrations up to 250 μg/mL after 24 and 48 h of co-culture (Fig. S3–4), whereas higher doses (500 and 1000 μg/mL) induced moderate cytotoxicity, indicating good biocompatibility of H-silicene in vitro. Based on these results, 250 μg/mL was selected for subsequent animal experiments.

Regulation of macrophage polarization by H-silicene

We next explored the immunomodulatory effects of different interventions on macrophage polarization. RAW264.7 cells were stimulated with LPS (100 ng/mL) to induce a proinflammatory M1 phenotype, followed by treatment with H-silicene. Flow cytometric analysis revealed that H-silicene attenuated M1 polarization in a dose-dependent manner, significantly reducing CD86+ cells (from 65.2 % ± 2.98 % in the LPS-only group to 30.4 % ± 2.69 % with 250 μg/mL H-silicene; p < 0.001). Concurrently, H-silicene promoted M2 polarization, as evidenced by an increase in CD206+ cells (from 0.68 % ± 1.12 % to 37.6 % ± 2.76 %; p < 0.001) (Fig. 3A and B). We also measured cytokine secretion in RAW264.7 cells following LPS stimulation with or without H-silicene treatment. As shown in Fig. 3C, LPS significantly increased TNF-α and IL-1α production compared to the negative control, confirming successful induction of inflammation. H-silicene treatment reduced TNF-α secretion in a dose-dependent manner, with the 250 μg/mL group showing a significant reduction (p < 0.01). A similar reduction in IL-1β was observed (p < 0.001). Conversely, LPS exposure suppressed IL-10 and TGF-β secretion, while H-silicene treatment restored both cytokines, with 250 μg/mL significantly enhancing IL-10 and TGF-β levels (p < 0.01, p < 0.0001, respectively).

These findings were further validated by immunofluorescence staining. H-silicene treatment led to decreased expression of the M1-associated marker CCR7 and enhanced expression of the M2-associated marker Arg1 (Fig. 3D). Notably, treatment with H-silicene alone, in the absence of LPS stimulation, did not significantly affect the expression levels of CCR7 or Arg1 compared with untreated controls (Fig. 3E), suggesting that H-silicene selectively modulates macrophage polarization in an inflammatory context.

Together, these results indicate that H-silicene can effectively reprogram LPS-activated macrophages from a proinflammatory M1 phenotype toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, thereby highlighting its potential as a macrophage-targeting immunoregulatory agent.

H-silicene alleviates the effects of proinflammatory macrophages on C2C12 myotubes

To evaluate the impact of H-silicene on macrophage-mediated effects on myotubes, we established an indirect co-culture model using RAW264.7 macrophages and differentiated C2C12 myotubes. RAW264.7 cells were exposed to LPS (100 ng/mL) with or without H-silicene (250 μg/mL) for 24 h, and the conditioned media were mixed with C2C12 differentiation medium at a 1:1 ratio and applied to mature myotubes for 48 h (Fig. 3F).

Morphological analysis revealed that conditioned medium from LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 cells caused significant myotube atrophy, as indicated by a reduction in myotube diameter. However, conditioned medium from RAW264.7 cells exposed to both LPS and H-silicene significantly attenuated the LPS-induced atrophy, preserving myotube morphology similar to that of untreated controls. Importantly, no significant changes were observed when myotubes were cultured in conditioned medium from H-silicene-treated RAW264.7 cells alone, in contrast to the effects seen with LPS-conditioned media. This implies that H-silicene does not directly affect myotubes but may instead exert its function via altering macrophage polarization under inflammatory conditions (Fig. 3G, Fig. S6). These findings suggest that H-silicene mitigates the detrimental effects of proinflammatory macrophages on skeletal muscle cells, likely through modulation of macrophage polarization and secretory phenotype.

Histological analysis of muscle cross-sections demonstrated pronounced myofiber atrophy and increased collagen deposition in the model group compared to NC. In contrast, H-silicene treatment significantly increased the muscle fiber cross-sectional area and reduced collagen fraction (Fig. 4F and G), indicating attenuation of muscle degeneration and fibrosis.

In line with histological changes, the muscle mass of quadriceps, tibialis anterior, and gastrocnemius muscles was significantly reduced in the model group compared to controls (Fig. 4B–C, Fig. S8A–C), whereas H-silicene treatment significantly restored the mass of gastrocnemius. Notably, quadriceps and tibialis anterior mass showed a trend of improvement but did not reach statistical significance (Fig. S8A–B). Immobilization also resulted in body weight loss, which was also partially reversed by H-silicene administration.

Functional assessments further supported these findings. Hindlimb grip strength tests showed a significant reduction in the model group but was partially restored following H-silicene treatment (Fig. 4D, Fig. S8D). Similarly, rotarod tests revealed that H-silicene significantly improved motor coordination and balance, as evidenced by increased maximum speed and time on the rotarod, though changes in total distance remained insignificant (Fig. 4E).

Furthermore, serum biochemical analyses showed no significant differences in ALP, UREA, or CREA levels among groups (Fig. S8C–E), suggesting minimal hepatotoxicity or renal burden associated with H-silicene administration. Surprisingly, ALT and AST levels were elevated in the model group but reduced by H-silicene treatment (Fig. S8A–B). H&E staining of major organs showed no histological abnormalities across all groups, supporting the biosafety of H-silicene in vivo (Fig. S9).

Collectively, these results indicate that H-silicene mitigates disuse-induced muscle loss and dysfunction in immobilized mice, without inducing systemic toxicity.

Discussion

In this study, we explored the therapeutic potential of hydrogenated silicene (H-silicene) in alleviating DMA, with a particular focus on its role in modulating macrophage polarization and the immune microenvironment. Our findings demonstrate that H-silicene significantly attenuates the pathological processes of DMA, particularly by reducing pro-inflammatory response. Via its immunomodulatory effects, H-silicene protected muscle fibers from atrophy in a murine model, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic agent in muscle-wasting disorders.

Compared to other emerging two-dimensional materials, H-silicene offers distinct advantages for inflammation-targeted applications. While black phosphorus has demonstrated osteogenic and antimicrobial bioactivities, its rapid degradation in aqueous environments and high-dose cytotoxicity significantly limit its clinical translation [37]. MXene-based nanoplatforms, though effective in tumor therapy and immunomodulation, typically require external triggers such as light to initiate therapeutic effects, which may complicate in vivo applications [38]. In contrast, H-silicene enables spontaneous hydrogen release upon exposure to water—a condition inherently present in biological systems. This intrinsic responsiveness allows for passive and sustained reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging without external stimulation. Moreover, H-silicene degrades into silicon-based byproducts with proven biosafety and is gradually cleared from major organs [25]. These properties collectively underscore the translational potential of H-silicene as a practical, stimulus-free nanoplatform for regulating inflammation and modulating immune cell phenotypes in situ.

DMA remains a prevalent clinical challenge, often resulting in significant muscle mass loss, functional impairment, and prolonged recovery time [2]. Although the pathophysiology of DMA is multifactorial, increasing evidence highlights the pivotal role of inflammation, particularly the proinflammatory activity of macrophages, in mediating muscle degeneration [39]. In this study, we, for the first time, demonstrated that H-silicene effectively ameliorates DMA by reprogramming macrophage phenotypes, both in vitro and in vivo.

Mechanistically, H-silicene suppressed LPS-induced M1 polarization in RAW264.7 macrophages while promoting M2 phenotypes. M1 macrophages typically express iNOS and CD86, while M2 macrophages are marked by Arg1, CD163, YM1, and Fizz1 [40]. Flow cytometry and immunofluorescence staining confirmed a significant decrease in CD86+ and CCR7+ cells, and a concurrent increase in CD206+ and Arg1+ cells upon H-silicene treatment, suggesting a phenotypic shift toward a tissue-repairing, anti-inflammatory state. Our observation that H-silicene selectively modulates M1/M2 macrophage polarization under inflammatory conditions aligns with emerging strategies targeting immune plasticity in muscle disorders [48]. Unlike broad-spectrum anti-inflammatory agents, H-silicene's stimulus-dependent activity avoids excessive immunosuppression, a feature crucial for clinical translation. This specificity contrasts with previous nanomaterials (e.g., graphene oxide) that indiscriminately suppress macrophage activation, risking secondary infections [3]. The phenotypic shift toward CD206+/Arg1+ macrophages suggest H-silicene promotes a regenerative microenvironment, consistent with studies showing M2-derived exosomes enhance muscle repair [4]. However, our work uniquely demonstrates that this polarization directly correlates with myotube preservation, bridging immune dynamics to structural outcomes.

Encouraged by the in vitro results, we further assessed the therapeutic potential of H-silicene in a murine model of disuse muscle atrophy. Mice subjected to hindlimb immobilization exhibited typical features of disuse atrophy, including muscle wasting, reduced grip strength, impaired motor performance, and increased fibrosis. Remarkably, twice-weekly intramuscular injection of H-silicene reversed these changes. Treated mice displayed improved gastrocnemius muscle mass, increased myofiber cross-sectional area, and decreased collagen deposition, alongside restored functional performance in behavioral assays. Importantly, we observed no histopathological abnormalities in major organs by H&E staining, and blood biochemical indices remained within normal limits after H-silicene treatment, indicating good short-term biocompatibility and the absence of systemic toxicity. These findings support the safety of H-silicene in vivo, though we agree that further long-term studies are warranted to monitor its biodistribution, clearance, and structural stability.

To gain a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms underlying H-silicene–mediated muscle protection, we performed integrated transcriptomic and proteomic analyses. Transcriptomic data revealed H-silicene-responsive genes involved in inflammation, metabolism, and muscle regeneration, while proteomic profiling identified consistent changes in pathways related to immune regulation, myoblast differentiation, and cellular homeostasis. Specifically, Nmrk2 and Smox were two of the differentially expressed genes based on fold change and statistical significance. Nicotinamide riboside kinase-2 (or NMRK2) is a muscle-specific β1-integrin binding protein and is highly expressed in skeletal muscle [41]. It is also one of the previously identified inactivity-related genes in aged skeletal muscle [42]. Other studies have indicated that when SMOX was overexpressed, muscle fiber size increased, accompanied by a decrease in muscle atrophy-related genes and an increase in genes that support muscle mass maintenance [43]. These multi-omics findings together highlight the coordinated modulation of gene and protein networks by H-silicene in disuse-induced muscle atrophy.

Despite these promising findings, several questions remain. First, the precise molecular targets of H-silicene in macrophages except hydrogen require elucidation—whether it acts via surface receptor interaction, intracellular signaling modulation, or epigenetic regulation. Second, the biodistribution and long-term safety of repeated H-silicene administration need investigation in higher-order species. Third, exploring the potential synergy of H-silicene with mechanical stimulation or nutritional interventions may enhance its translational value, as these strategies could act additively to promote muscle preservation and regeneration [5,[44], [45], [46], [47]]. It is worth noting that this study primarily aimed to prevent muscle loss in a disuse atrophy model, rather than to stimulate regeneration following muscle injury. As such, our assessments focused on structural preservation and functional maintenance, future studies could investigate whether it also exerts indirect effects on muscle regeneration pathways or satellite cell activation. It is also important to note that the current evidence supports a correlative, rather than causative, relationship. Studies employing macrophage-depletion models or conditional knockout systems will be necessary in the future. Finally, in this study, H-silicene nanosheets were prepared through a multi-step procedure involving the reaction of calcium silicide (CaSi2) with hydrochloric acid under low temperature and inert atmosphere, followed by prolonged ultrasonication and solvent purification, limiting its scalability and cost-effectiveness. For H-silicene to become a viable therapeutic material, future work should focus on developing more efficient, economical, and industrially scalable synthesis methods while maintaining consistency in nanosheet quality and biological performance.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study identifies H-silicene as a promising therapeutic nanomaterial for disuse-induced skeletal muscle atrophy. Through its modulation of macrophage polarization and alleviation of inflammation, H-silicene protects against muscle degeneration and functional loss. Further research into its molecular mechanisms, as well as its potential in combination with other therapeutic strategies, will be crucial for advancing H-silicene as a viable treatment for muscle atrophy and related disorders.
