Unraveling a new Klebsiella phage KP47 against Klebsiella spp.
Authors: Giovane Böerner Hypolito, Caio Felipe Cavicchia Zamunér, Layla Farage Martins, Mark Enright, Henrique Ferreira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe increase in cases of infectious diseases related to multidrug-resistant bacteria and the spread of multidrug-resistant genes have driven the search for therapeutic alternatives that can circumvent this global phenomenon. One of these alternatives is the use of bacteriophages, which are viruses capable of infecting and killing specific bacteria. Klebsiella pneumoniae species complex (KpSC) is an important opportunistic group associated with multidrug resistance. This group includes Klebsiella quasipneumoniae subsp. similipneumoniae, a species recognized as a clinically relevant pathogen, and that was used as a host (designated Klebsiella KH1) to isolate the phage characterized in the present study. The characterization of new phages is essential to expand the therapeutic arsenal and to improve our understanding on phage diversity. In this study, we evaluated a Klebsiella bacteriophage named KP47 that was isolated from sewage in the United Kingdom. We performed phage biological and genomic characterization in order to assess its therapeutic attributes. KP47 represents a newly isolated Klebsiella phage, being able to infect both K. pneumoniae and K. quasipneumoniae subsp. similipneumoniae, which also shows evidence of depolymerase activity and an efficient bacteriolytic performance.ResultsThe phage KP47 showed a typical morphology of the Caudoviricetes class, with a capsid of 48 nm and a tail of 161 nm. Its genome has 47,396 bp, 63 CDSs, and a GC content of 57.52%, which apparently encodes a depolymerase. ViPTree analysis placed KP47 near Drexlerviridae-related phages, while comparative analyses using representative RefSeq genomes revealed low intergenomic similarity. This phage demonstrated strictly lytic behavior and fast adsorption (90% in 6 min). MOI experiments indicated complete inhibition of bacterial growth in MOIs ≥ 1. The phage infected two out of nine tested strains belonging to the strains of KpSC.ConclusionsIn this study, we described the Klebsiella phage KP47, likely representing a new viral genus, that exhibits a strictly lytic lifestyle, rapid adsorption (90% within 6 min), and an effective bacterial growth inhibition at MOIs ≥ 1. KP47 was able to infect clinically relevant members of the Klebsiella pneumoniae species complex. Background

Fatalities associated with infectious diseases caused by antibiotic-resistant bacteria are a significant global issue nowadays. Allied to this is the rapid proliferation and spread of these microorganisms and the escalating antibiotic resistance, particularly to antibiotics employed as a last resort in infections. Given its importance, in 2014, the World Health Organization (WHO) identified this problem as a global health threat requiring immediate attention [1]. Among the bacterial groups involved, the Klebsiella pneumoniae species complex (KpSC) stands out because of its significant resistance to many antibiotics. A considerable proportion of infections caused by KpSC originate from two main pathotypes: multidrug-resistant (MDR) and hypervirulent (hv) clones [2].

Klebsiella pneumoniae was identified by Edwin Klebs in 1875 during the examination of the airways of pneumonia victims; however, the species was formally named later by Friedlaender [3]. K. pneumoniae is an important etiological agent of opportunistic infections in hospital settings, associated with urinary tract infections, neonatal sepsis, and community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) [4–6]. The Klebsiella pneumoniae species complex can be separated into three phylogenetic groups: Klebsiella pneumoniae, Klebsiella variicola and Klebsiella quasipneumoniae [7–12]. The K. quasipneumoniae and K. variicola variants are described as opportunistic pathogens with lower virulence in humans compared to K. pneumoniae, however, it has been observed that K. quasipneumoniae and K. variicola can be as virulent as K. pneumoniae [13].

Bacteriophages, or phages, are viruses that target bacteria and, consequently, are the natural predators of bacteria. Thus, an alternative to antibiotics is the use of phages as antimicrobial agents for the treatment of bacterial infections, a practice known as phage therapy. Phage therapy development was largely delayed by the widespread adoption of antibiotics [14–18]. However, it is experiencing a global revival due to its therapeutic potential. Not only that, but phages also produce enzymes of biotechnological interest that can be used in conjunction with phage therapy or antibiotics to fight bacterial diseases. These enzymes are depolymerases and endolysins that target the bacterial biofilm and the peptidoglycan cell wall, respectively [19–26].

Effective phage therapy requires bacteriophages with specific and desirable characteristics. Consequently, the isolation of novel phages, along with comprehensive genomic and biological characterization, is essential for expanding and refining existing databases [27]. In this study, we characterized phage KP47, a Klebsiella bacteriophage isolated from sewage in the United Kingdom. Through comprehensive biological and genomic analyses, we assessed its therapeutic potential and confirmed it as a previously unreported Klebsiella phage.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains, growth conditions and isolation of phage KP47

This study used 8 isolates of Klebsiella pneumoniae and one of Klebsiella quasipneumoniae subsp. similipneumoniae NCTC 13368 (designated Klebsiella KH1). The strains were obtained from different sources, including the National Collection of Type Cultures (NCTC, UK), the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine (LSHTM, UK), and the bioMérieux European MDR collection. Detailed information regarding strain origin, sequence type (ST), capsular locus (KL), and clinical characteristics is provided in Supplementary Table S1. All strains were grown at 37 °C for 16 h in TSB (Tryptic soy broth), for solid medium agar was added to 1.5% final concentration (TSB-agar). The strains were preserved in TSB supplemented with 40% glycerol at -80 °C. Phage KP47 was isolated from an unfiltered sewage sample collected from a wastewater treatment plant in Manchester. It was propagated in Klebsiella KH1 using the double-layer agar technique [28].

DNA extraction and sequencing

Total DNA was extracted using a phenol/chloroform/isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1 [v/v]) method as described in [29]. One and half mL of the lysate (109 PFU/mL) was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. One mL of the lysate was transferred to a new tube and treated with DNase I (10 µL of a 1 mg/mL stock) and RNase A (4 µL of 12.5 mg/mL RNase A stock) for 30 min. After that, 1 mL of phenol was added, vortexed for 30 s, and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The aqueous phase was collected, and 1 mL of phenol/chloroform/isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1) was added, vortexed for 30 s, and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. One more repetition of the washing step with phenol/chloroform/isoamyl alcohol was performed, and then the DNA was precipitated with 2 volumes of 100% chilled ethanol and 1/10 volume of 7.5 M ammonium acetate, incubated overnight at -20 °C. After that, the sample was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the pellet was washed twice with 70% ethanol (v/v) and resuspended in 100 µL of nuclease-free water. The genome was sequenced with Illumina Nextera XT technology, and the libraries were prepared using the Nextera XT DNA Library Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Phage DNA sequencing was carried out with paired end 2 × 150 bp (300 cycles) using the Illumina NextSeq500 platform at Manchester Metropolitan University, UK.

Genome assembly

Raw reads were assembled using SPAdes v3.10.1 [30, 31] with the --careful option and k-mer sizes of 71, 91, and 111. Assembly resulted in a single phage genome contig of 47,396 bp with an average coverage of 1,204×. An additional 112-bp low-complexity contig composed entirely of cytosine residues was generated by the assembler and was excluded from downstream analyses. All parameters can be viewed in Table S6 in the supplementary materials.

Bioinformatics analysis

The phage annotation utilized Pharokka v 1.7.4 [32] to identify coding sequences (CDS) through PHANOTATE [33], tRNAs via tRNAscan-SE 2.0 [34], tmRNAs with Aragorn [35], and CRISPRs using CRT [36]. Functional annotation was achieved by linking each CDS to databases such as PHROGs [37], VFDB [38], and CARD [39], employing MMseqs2 [40] and PyHMMER [41] for this process. The data were matched to the INPHARED database [42] using mash [43]. Visual representations were created with pyCirclize, while further annotation was enhanced through the Phold tool [44], which leverages structural protein homology. For phylogeny and taxonomy, the ViPTree tool was used, which generates a proteome tree [45], and intergenomic similarity was performed in VIRIDIC [46]. To assess the phylogenetic relationship of the isolated KP47 with related organisms, the 30 most similar complete genomes were retrieved from the NCBI GenBank database using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLASTn). Selection criteria were based on the highest query coverage and percentage of identity. The phylogenomic tree was constructed using Mashtree (v1.4.6), a tool that utilizes the Mash (MinHash) algorithm to estimate genomic distances. This approach allows for rapid comparison by reducing large genomic datasets into compact “sketches.” For this analysis, each genome was processed as an individual input file. To ensure the statistical robustness of the tree topology, 100 bootstrap replicates were generated using the mashtree_bootstrap.pl script. The final tree was visualized and edited using FigTree v1.4.4 (http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree/) [47]. To identify potential depolymerase targets in the genome, we performed an analysis using DepoScope v1.0 [48] and HHpred [49].

Host range

To determine the host range of phage KP47, a spot test was performed according to Clokie, Kropinski [28] (Chap. 14, pp. 141–149). From an overnight culture of bacteria, 100 µL of it was mixed with 3 mL of TSB top-agar 0.6% and poured into a petri dish with TSB-agar. After solidification of the top-agar, 5 µL of phage lysate was dropped on the surface of the agar, and the plate was incubated at 37 °C overnight for observation of the lysis plate. The strains used for host range were Klebsiella KH1, K. pneumoniae strains KH2, KH3, KH4, KH7, KH11, KH17, KH55, KH92 (Table S1 – Supplementary). The KH1 and KH11 strains were selected for the Efficiency of Plating (EOP) assay following the methodology described by Martins et al. [50] with modifications. Overnight bacterial cultures were diluted to an OD600nm of 0.4 (~10⁸ CFU/mL). Subsequently, 100 µL aliquots from eight serial 10-fold dilutions of phage stocks (10⁸ PFU/mL) were mixed with 100 µL bacterial suspensions. Next, 5 mL of molten 0.7% TSB soft agar was added to the phage–bacteria mixture, which was then poured onto TSA plates and incubated overnight at 37 °C.

EOP values were calculated by dividing the number of plaques formed on each susceptible strain by the number of plaques formed on the original host strain at the corresponding phage dilution. EOP values greater than 0.1% were considered moderate, whereas values greater than 50% were considered high. The EOP assay was performed in duplicate.

Transmission electron microscopy

Approximately 3 µL of purified phage suspension was deposited on glow-discharged carbon-coated copper grids, blotted after 1 min, and stained with 2% uranyl acetate before air-drying. The negatively stained phage particles were examined using a JEOL JEM 2100 transmission electron microscope at 100 kV, and images were digitally recorded following protocols established by the Chemistry Institute Analytical Center (CA-USP, https://ca2.iq.usp.br/) [50].

Time-killing curve

The antibacterial activity of phage KP47 against Klebsiella KH1 was evaluated by a time-kill assay, monitoring absorbance over time, and further confirmed by a resazurin microtiter assay (REMA) [51]. A stock of phage KP 47 was diluted in TSB in a 96-well plate for a range of MOI 100 to 0.02. The negative control (NC) was only the TSB culture medium. A culture of bacteria was added to each well at a final concentration of 104 cells per well. The plate was incubated in a Synergy H1 plate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT) at 37 °C in orbital rotation for 16 h and measured at OD600nm every 5 min. After incubation, resazurin solution was added to a final concentration of 0.015 µg.mL-1/well and the plates were incubated for 10 min at 37 °C. The reduction of resazurin to the fluorescent compound resorufin was quantified in a fluorescent plate reader Synergy H1 (BioTek, Winooski, VT) set to excitation at 530 nm and emission at 590 nm.

Adsorption curve of phage KP47

The adsorption assay was performed following the protocol described by Clokie, Kropinski [28] (Chap. 15, pp. 151–155). First, 12 microtubes of 1.5 mL were separated and 0.95 mL of TSB was added, numbered A1-A10 and C1 and C2. Then, 3 drops of chloroform were added with a Pasteur pipette to each microtube. The tubes were left on ice for 10 min. From a cell inoculum in the mid-log, a new inoculum containing 10 mL in OD600nm 0.1 was made. Two 80 mL sterile polypropylene specimen container were separated and marked “A” and “C”. In container “A”, 9 mL of diluted inoculum was added, and in container “C”, 9 mL of TSB was added. At time 0, 1 mL of phage stock was added at 105 PFU/mL titration in container “A” and “C” and incubated at 37 °C. Every minute for 10 min, 50 µL of container “A” was collected and added to the microtubes marked with the letter “A” (the number corresponds to the minute of collection). Mix for 10 s and place the microtube on ice again. At the end of the 10 min, repeat the same procedure with container “C”. For quantification, 100 µL of each sample was pipetted using the single-layer agar method. The experiment was carried out in triplicate in independent events. For the statistical analysis, the time of 1 min was normalized as 100% free phages, using the equation: % free phage = ([PFU/mL A1 – PFU/mL sample)/PFU/mL A1])*100.

Chloroform stability assay of phage KP47

The chloroform stability assay was performed to evaluate virion structural stability and potential tolerance to organic solvent exposure commonly used during phage purification procedures. This assay was performed following the protocol of Rahimi‑Midani et al. [52]. Phage particles at a titer of 107 PFU/mL were incubated in a mixture containing TSB with 10% chloroform for one hour, while the control sample contained only TSB. After incubation, phages were propagated with the single-layer agar method [53] and lysis-forming plates counted [28]. The tests were carried out in triplicate.

Results

Isolation and morphology of phage KP47

The phage was isolated from sewage using K. quasipneumoniae subsp. similipneumoniae NCTC 13368 (designated KH1) as the host strain. Following successive purification steps, the phage produced clear and uniform plaques of approximately 0.4 mm in diameter on double-layer agar, surrounded by a diffuse halo measuring about 0.9 mm which is evidence of a putative depolymerase activity (Fig. 1A). Transmission electron microscopy revealed a polygonal capsid with an estimated diameter of 48 nm and a tail measuring approximately 161 nm in length (Fig. 1B). The phage was designated KP47 (GenBank accession number: PX906587) and classified within the class Caudoviricetes, displaying a siphovirus-like morphology.

The Phage KP47 is classified within a novel genus

Genomic analysis of phage KP47 revealed a 47,396 bp genome with a GC content of 57.52%. A total of 63 coding DNA sequences (CDSs) were identified, of which 36 (57%) were assigned to known functional categories, while 27 remained as hypothetical proteins with unknown functions. Classic phage genes were identified, such as structural genes and the endolysin (CDS_0047). Although it was not detected by genome annotation (Fig. 2), phage KP47 presents a diffuse halo (Fig. 1A), which indicates the presence of depolymerase enzymes. To investigate further, we employed the DepoScope tool and HHpred to identify a potential depolymerase candidate. The DepoScope tool did not identify any candidates with potential depolymerase activity (Supplementary Table S3). However, HHpred results revealed hits in CDS 24 and CDS 25 that may indicate the presence of depolymerases (Endo-1,4-beta-xylanase hit 99 and Endo-pectate lyase hit 231 for CDS 24; Endo-N-acetylneuraminidase hit 9 for CDS 25).(supplementary Table S4 and S5) [54]. No genes associated with anti-CRISPR systems or antibiotic resistance were found (Fig. 2). The complete annotation can be found in supplementary Table S2.

The analysis provided by ViPTree positioned KP47 near members of the Drexlerviridae family (Fig. 3A). Homology analysis with other Klebsiella phages showed minimal similarity, with only limited homology to Klebsiella phage Sweeny (NC_049839) (Fig. 3C). Comparative analysis using representative RefSeq genomes in VIRIDIC showed low intergenomic similarity (<1.7%) among the selected reference dataset (<1.7%) (Fig. 3B). We performed an additional Mash distance–based analysis, which produced clustering patterns similar to the proteomic tree, forming a distinct branch relative to closely related reference genomes (supplementary Fig S3). Collectively, these findings support the classification of phage KP47 as a member of a novel bacteriophage genus.

Biological characterization of phage KP47

Phage KP47 forms a clear lysis plaque, which is a characteristic of a typical lytic phage, and a huge halo surrounding the lysis plaques, that can indicate depolymerase activity (Fig. 1A). KP47 exhibited efficient adsorption to its host, with approximately 90% of phage particles adsorbed after 6 min of incubation (Fig. 4C). Regarding the stability of the phage KP47 at 10% chloroform for 1 h, it was noticed a survival of 60% (Fig. 4B). We investigated the bacteriolytic effect of the phage in different MOIs and we found it to be more efficient in MOIs greater than 1 (Fig. 4A), with complete inhibition of bacterial growth observed for up to 16 h under these conditions (Supplementary Fig. S1). Phage KP47 was able to infect only 2 of the 9 KpSC strains tested: Klebsiella quasipneumoniae subsp. similipneumoniae NCTC 13368 (KH1), the isolation host, and Klebsiella pneumoniae KH11 (Supplementary Fig S2). The results of efficiency of plating (EOP) showed a value of 340% for strain KH11, which indicates a high productive infection (Supplementary Fig S4).

Discussion

Research on bacteriophages as tools for treating infectious diseases and improving food quality control in industry is attracting increasing global interest [23, 55, 56]. Thus, this work described the biological and genomic characterization of the bacteriophage KP47, which is a novel lytic phage able to infect Klebsiella spp.

The analyses of the proteome tree, phylogenetic tree and intergenomic similarity showed that the Phage KP47 shares low homology with already described phages of Klebsiella, in addition to not fitting into any described genus deposited on NCBI so far. According to the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV), phages sharing more than 95% nucleotide sequence identity across their genomes are classified as the same species, while those sharing more than 70% are assigned to the same genus [57]. In this context, the extremely low intergenomic similarity observed for KP47 (<1.7%) strongly indicates that it does not belong to any currently described genus. Taken together, these findings support the classification of phage KP47 as a representative of a novel bacteriophage genus.

The biological characterization of phage KP47 revealed several features of interest for phage therapy [27]. The plaque morphology, characterized by clear lysis zones, and its strict lytic nature are desirable traits for therapeutic applications. Additionally, KP47 exhibited rapid adsorption kinetics, with approximately 90% of viral particles adsorbing to the host cells within 6 min. This adsorption rate was faster than those reported for previously characterized Klebsiella phages, which required approximately 10–12 min to reach the similar adsorption level of 90% [58, 59]. Furthermore, KP47 is still effective at MOI values frequently investigated in experimental phage therapy investigations due to its ability to achieve complete suppression of bacterial proliferation at multiplicities of infection greater than one. For instance, in Galleria mellonella infection models of Klebsiella pneumoniae, phage therapy at MOIs of 1 and higher has been linked to better survival [60, 61]. All these findings point to KP47 as a viable option for phage therapy.

Phage KP47 also exhibits features that may be relevant for future biotechnological applications. Genome annotation identified an endolysin gene, and additional analyses were performed to investigate the possible presence of depolymerase-associated genes. Although DepoScope did not identify any depolymerase candidates, HHpred identified hits to Endo-1,4-beta-xylanase and Endo-pectate lyase in CDS 24 (tail protein), as well as a hit to Endo-N-acetylneuraminidase in CDS 25 (tail fiber protein) [54]. These findings, together with the presence of diffuse halos around the lysis plaques, suggest that phage KP47 may encode depolymerases [21, 54]. It is important to emphasize, however, that these genes should be considered only as putative depolymerase candidates. Definitive confirmation of depolymerase activity would require dedicated biological assays to experimentally validate their enzymatic function.

The host range analysis showed that KP47 has a limited spectrum, with susceptibility observed for two of the examined bacterial isolates. Significantly, none of the ST258 lineages exhibited susceptibility to the phage. The EOP obtained for KH11 was higher than that observed for the original host strain, suggesting more efficient phage propagation in the host. Despite this limitation, KP47 continues to be a promising candidate for phage therapy, as therapeutic cocktails can be designed to expand bacterial coverage and improve treatment efficacy [62, 63]. A significant finding of this study is that KP47 can infect strains of both Klebsiella pneumoniae and Klebsiella quasipneumoniae subsp. similipneumoniae, corroborating previous observations reported for other Klebsiella phages [64, 65].

Overall, KP47 expands the known diversity of Klebsiella-infecting phages and contributes to the growing understanding of phage taxonomy, evolution, and application potential. Its features support both future therapeutic evaluation and possible biotechnological uses, particularly involving lysis-associated enzymes. Future work should focus on identifying host receptors, stability tests on diverse environmental conditions, and evaluation of KP47 efficacy in vivo infection models to determine its clinical feasibility.

Conclusions

In this study, phage KP47 was isolated and characterized, revealing key attributes that support its therapeutic potential. KP47 represents an undescribed bacteriophage, likely constituting a new viral genus, with a strictly lytic lifecycle, rapid adsorption kinetics (90% within 6 min), and effective suppression of bacterial growth at MOIs ≥ 1. Importantly, its ability to infect both Klebsiella pneumoniae and Klebsiella quasipneumoniae subsp. similipneumoniae highlights its relevance against clinically significant members of the Klebsiella pneumoniae species complex. Collectively, these findings position KP47 as a promising candidate for future therapeutic applications, particularly in the context of infections caused by multidrug-resistant Klebsiella spp.
