Epigenetic-Metabolic interplay in chronic kidney disease mortality: insights from grimage acceleration and transcriptomic profiling
Authors: Xiangyun Dang, Zhongxin Jin, Yafeng Zhao, Yu Wang, Xiancheng Li, Xiaoyuan Liu, Pei Yan, Wei Xu, Yuhui Liu, Xiaoyong Yu, Nan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic kidney disease (CKD) is associated with a substantially elevated risk of mortality. Although GrimAge acceleration (GAA) and metabolic syndrome (MetS) are both implicated in this risk, their combined impact and the underlying biological mechanisms remain poorly understood.MethodsThis study integrated data from National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES; n = 2,529), Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), and public aging-related genes. Participants with CKD were divided into four groups: low GAA without MetS (reference), high GAA without MetS (GAA), low GAA with MetS (MetS), and high GAA with MetS (GAA-MetS). Weighted Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate associations with all-cause mortality. Independent transcriptomic analyses of CKD (GSE66494), MetS (GSE98895) and aging-related genes datasets included differential expression analysis, weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), and machine learning (LASSO and SVM-RFE) to identify hub genes. Immune cell infiltration was estimated using CIBERSORT.ResultsPatients with CKD in the GAA (HR = 2.331, 95% CI: 1.785–3.043, p < 0.001), MetS (HR = 1.314, 95% CI: 1.057–1.635, p = 0.014), and GAA-MetS (HR = 2.112, 95% CI: 1.525–2.927, p < 0.001) groups exhibited significantly higher mortality risks compared to the reference group. High GAA (HR = 2.083, 95% CI: 1.533–2.831, p < 0.001) independently predicted mortality in patients aged ≥ 72 years. Sex subgroup analyses revealed elevated risks for males in both the GAA (HR = 2.440, 95% CI: 1.471–4.048, p < 0.001) and GAA-MetS (HR = 2.320, 95% CI: 1.395–3.859, p = 0.001) groups, and for females in the GAA-MetS group (HR = 1.763, 95% CI: 1.054–2.950, p = 0.031). Transcriptomic analysis identified five hub genes (ZMPSTE24, RELB, STAT6, DKC1, E2F3) implicated in CKD pathogenesis, cellular senescence, and metabolic pathways, whose expression profiles were correlated with distinct alterations in immune cell infiltration within CKD tissues.ConclusionGAA is an independent predictor of all-cause mortality in CKD. The GAA-MetS identifies a particularly high-risk phenotype. Complementary transcriptomic analyses offer a testable hypothesis for the interplay between epigenetic and metabolic dysregulation of CKD pathogenesis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13148-026-02071-7.

What was known

GrimAge acceleration (GAA) and metabolic syndrome (MetS) are independently associated with adverse outcomes in chronic kidney disease (CKD). However, their synergistic interaction and the underlying molecular mechanisms were not well understood.

This study adds

This study demonstrates that GAA is an independent predictor of all-cause mortality in CKD. The GAA-MetS identifies a particularly high-risk phenotype. By integrating epidemiological data with independent transcriptomic profiling, we further identify five hub genes (ZMPSTE24, RELB, STAT6, DKC1, E2F3) whose expression patterns suggest a potential link between this epigenetic-metabolic interplay in CKD.

Potential impact

Our findings provide a framework for refined risk stratification in CKD by highlighting the high-risk GAA-MetS phenotype. This work may guide the development of medicine strategies aimed at moderating epigenetic aging or its downstream consequences in high-risk CKD populations.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is defined by a progressive and irreversible decline in renal structure and function, representing a major global health burden [1]. Currently, it ranks as the third fastest-growing cause of death worldwide and affects approximately 850 million individuals [2], highlighting the need for novel biomarkers that reflect its underlying pathological mechanisms.

Aging is a central driver of renal functional decline, involving processes such as cumulative cellular damage, mitochondrial dysfunction, and progressive nephron loss. Recent advances in epigenetics allow for the quantification of biological aging independently of chronological age. The DNA methylation-based estimator GrimAge integrates the methylation profiles of seven plasma proteins (adrenomedullin, beta-2 microglobulin, cystatin C, HbA1c, GDF-15, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein, and leptin) into an algorithm predictive of mortality and associated with various disease risks [3–5]. GrimAge acceleration (GAA), derived from the residual difference between GrimAge and chronological age, reflects a systemic aging burden and has been linked to multiple conditions. Concurrently, metabolic syndrome (MetS), characterized by abdominal obesity, dyslipidemia, hypertension, and hyperglycemia, is highly prevalent in CKD and accelerates the decline of renal function through inflammatory and oxidative stress pathways. The hyperglycemia and lipotoxicity can disrupt DNA methylation patterns, resulting in the hypomethylation of inflammatory genes concurrent with the hypermethylation of renoprotective factors.

Although both GAA and MetS are established independent risk factors for adverse outcomes in CKD, their potential synergistic interaction and the molecular mechanisms remain poorly understood. Moreover, it remains unclear whether their coexistence defines a distinct high-risk phenotype and through which specific biological mechanisms this risk is mediated. This study therefore aimed to clarify the associations of GAA and MetS with all-cause mortality in CKD. By exploring how metabolic dysfunction interfaces with senescence pathways, we sought to provide deeper mechanistic insight into CKD pathogenesis and inform the development of targeted therapeutic strategies for this growing global health burden.

Materials and methods

Study design

This observation analysis used data from the 1999–2002 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES, https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes) and the public repository NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo). After excluding participants without DNA methylation data, pregnant individuals, and those with incomplete information for GAA or CKD definition, a total of 2,529 participants from the NHANES database were included in the final analysis. Additionally, datasets GSE66494 (8 controls, 53 CKD samples), GSE98895 (20 controls, 53 MetS samples), and GSE104948 (21 controls, 175 CKD samples) were obtained from GEO for transcriptomic analyses. A total of 104,948 aging-related genes were sourced from the Human Ageing Genomic Resources (HAGR, https://genomics.senescence.info/cells/) and Ageing Atlas (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/ageing/age_related_genes). Variables

Covariates

Age, gender, race-ethnicity, education, and poverty-income ratio (PIR) were acquired from self-reported questionnaires. Systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) readings represent the average of three measurements taken during examinations. Body mass index (BMI), waist circumference, fasting blood glucose (FBG), glycohemoglobin (HbA1c), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), serum creatinine, urine creatinine, urine albumin, and the percent of CD8 + T Cell, CD4 + T cell, natural killer (NK) cell, B cell, monocyte cell were measured in blood and collected at mobile examination centers. The follow-up time and all-cause mortality were obtained from mortality files of NHANES.

DNA methylation sample processing and GrimAge calculation

Whole blood samples from NHANES (1999–2002) participants aged ≥ 50 years underwent DNA extraction, bisulfite conversion, and hybridization to Illumina EPIC BeadChips. Standard preprocessing included quality control, normalization, and batch correction. GrimAge was calculated using a validated algorithm incorporating DNA methylation signatures of seven plasma proteins (adrenomedullin, beta-2 microglobulin, cystatin C, GDF15, leptin, PAI-1, TIMP-1) and smoking exposure. GAA was calculated as the residual difference between GrimAge and chronological age: GAA = GrimAge (methylation-derived age) – age. The low and high GAA was defined as GAA ≤ 0 and GAA > 0, respectively.

Definition

The CKD was defined as urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) ≥ 30 mg/g and / or estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 according to the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) 2021 Guidelines. The eGFR was calculated by the 2021 CKD-EPI equation.

MetS was defined by the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) criteria (≥ 3 of): (a) abdominal obesity (waist circumference: male > 102 cm, female > 88 cm); (b) triglycerides ≥ 150 mg/dL; (c) HDL-C < 40/50 mg/dL in male/female; (d) hypertension (SBP ≥ 130 mmHg or DBP ≥ 80 mmHg and/or use of antihypertensive medications); (e) hyperglycemia (FBG ≥ 100 mg/dL, or HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, or previously diagnosed diabetes).

Subgroups covariates

The exposure groups were defined by integrating of GAA and MetS status. Four mutually exclusive exposure categories were created: (a) Reference: Low GAA without MetS; (b) GAA: High GAA without MetS; (c) MetS: Low GAA with MetS; (d) GAA-MetS: High GAA with MetS. For subgroup analyses, participants were stratified by the weighted median age of the study population and by sex (male or female).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics

All analyses accounted for the complex NHANES survey design using appropriate weights. Continuous variables were presented as weighted means ± standard deviations, and categorical variables as weighted percentages. Inter-group comparisons were performed using weighted Student’s t-tests or Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables, and weighted chi-square tests for categorical variables.

Survival analysis

Survival analysis employed weighted Cox proportional hazards models to assess associations between exposure groups and all-cause mortality, with adjustment for age, gender, race, education, and PIR. Effect sizes (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were recorded.

Sensitivity analysis

Three sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of primary findings: (1) residual-adjusted GAA model to account for chronological age effects; (2) multiplicative interaction testing between continuous GAA and MetS was formally tested by including an interaction term in the weighted cox proportional hazards regression model; and (3) collinearity diagnostics using variance inflation factors (VIF), with VIF < 5 considered acceptable.

Transcriptomic analysis

Differential expression analysis of the CKD (GSE66494) was performed using the “limma” package in R software with criteria of adjusted p < 0.05 and |logFC| > 0.585. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) [6] was conducted to identify modules associated with MetS (GSE98895). Functional enrichment analysis of Gene Ontology terms and KEGG pathways was performed using package “clusterProfiler” [7]. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed using the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/).

Machine learning and hub gene identification

Two machine learning approaches were employed to identify hub genes: least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression and support vector machine-recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE) [8, 9]. Genes identified by both methods were considered hub genes. Diagnostic performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and area under the curve (AUC) values.

Immune cell infiltration analysis

To further identify the differences in immune cell infiltration between the health population and CKD patients, The CIBERSORT deconvolution algorithm was performed to identify the differences in 22 immune cell types between the health controls (HC) and CKD based on the dataset GSE66494 [10]. The correlation between the expression of hub biomarkers and immune cell infiltration level was explored by Spearman correlation analysis. The R “ggplot2” packages was used to visualize the results.

Results

Characteristics of participants in CKD and Non-CKD subgroups

A total of 2,529 participants were enrolled, including 642 (25.4%) with CKD and 1,887 (74.6%) without CKD. Compared to non-CKD individuals, those with CKD were more frequently aged ≥ 72 years (p < 0.001) and male (p < 0.001), had lower education levels (p = 0.003) and higher PIR (p < 0.001). Daily smoking was less prevalent among the CKD participants (p = 0.047), whereas hypertension and diabetes were significantly more common (both p < 0.001). Individuals in the MetS and GAA-MetS subgroups were more frequently represented in the CKD group (p = 0.021). The CKD group also had shorter follow-up times and higher all-cause mortality rates (both p < 0.001). Biochemically, individuals with CKD showed elevated waist circumference, FBG, HbA1c, neutrophil, monocyte, and NK cell (all p < 0.05), along with reduced levels of HDL-C, CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, and B cells (all p < 0.05) (Table 1).

Characteristics of participants in exposure subgroups within CKD

The study involved 119, 76, 223, and 224 participants with CKD in the reference, GAA, MetS, and GAA-MetS groups, respectively. Individuals aged < 72 years were more prevalent in the GAA-MetS group (p < 0.001). The GAA group consisted predominantly of males, while females constituted the majority in the MetS group (p < 0.001). Mexican Americans exhibited a higher prevalence across all exposure groups (p = 0.023). Diabetes and hypertension were most frequently observed in the MetS group, whereas daily smoking was most common in the GAA group (all p < 0.001). Trends of increasing BMI, waist circumference, FBG, HbA1c, and TG were observed from the reference to the GAA-MetS group (all p < 0.001). The GAA-MetS group presented with the lowest HDL-C (p < 0.001), while the MetS group exhibited the highest LDL-C levels (p < 0.001). Significant differences were also observed in the proportions of CD4+ T cells (p = 0.029), B cells (p < 0.001), and NK cells (p < 0.001). No significant differences were found in all-cause mortality or follow-up duration among the groups (Table 2).

Characteristics of participants in age subgroups within CKD

Participants with CKD were stratified by the weighted median age of the population into two groups: <72 years and ≥ 72 years. Compared to the elderly, younger participants had a higher proportion of Mexican Americans (p = 0.001), a greater prevalence of daily smoking (p < 0.001), an increased incidence of diabetes (p = 0.005), and a larger percentage with GAA-MetS (p < 0.001). The younger group also showed significantly higher levels of BMI, waist circumference, FBG, HbA1c, TG, eGFR, CD4+ T cells, and B cells (all p < 0.05). In contrast, levels of NK cells (p < 0.001), monocytes (p = 0.003), and neutrophils (p = 0.048) were lower in younger participants (Supplement Table 1).

All-cause mortality risk in different subgroups with CKD

Mortality risk varied significantly across exposure groups in CKD patients. Compared with the reference group, the GAA, MetS, and GAA-MetS groups each exhibited a significantly elevated risk, with HRs of 2.331 (95% CI: 1.785–3.043, p < 0.001), 1.314 (95% CI: 1.057–1.635, p = 0.014), and 2.112 (95% CI: 1.525–2.927, p < 0.001), respectively (Fig. 2a). Participants with high GAA showed the poorest overall survival, with a marked decline observed after 5 years (Fig. 2b).

Sensitivity analysis

In a sensitivity analysis accounting for chronological age using residual-adjusted GAA, a significant association with increased mortality risk was confirmed (HR = 2.322, 95% CI: 1.560–3.455, p < 0.001; Supplemental Fig. 1). A significant multiplicative interaction was identified between continuous GAA and MetS (p = 0.033). Each standard deviation increase in GAA was associated with elevated mortality risk in participants without MetS (HR = 1.746, 95% CI: 1.473–2.070, p < 0.001), whereas this association was attenuated in those with MetS (HR = 0.815, 95% CI: 0.674–0.984, p = 0.034; Supplemental Fig. 2). Variance inflation factor (VIF) values for all covariates ranged from 1.02 to 1.35 (all < 5), indicating no substantial multicollinearity and supporting the reliability of the fitted models (Supplemental Fig. 3).

Identification of aging-related DEGs and functional enrichment analysis

In the independent CKD transcriptomic dataset (GSE66494), 2,850 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified, including 1,634 upregulated and 216 downregulated genes (Fig. 5a). A heatmap visualizing the top 50 DEGs was presented in Fig. 5b. To investigate pathways associated with metabolic dysfunction, weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was applied to the MetS dataset (GSE98895), revealing 27 distinct co-expression modules (Figs. 6a–c). Among these, the MEyellow module exhibited the strongest positive correlation with MetS (cor = 0.82, p = 1e-10), while MEbrown (cor = − 0.64, p = 7e-06) and MEturquoise (cor = − 0.63, p = 1e-05) showed significant negative correlations. A total of 2,116 genes from these MetS-associated modules were selected for further integration. By intersecting them with an aging-related gene set and the CKD DEGs, 34 overlapping aging-related DEGs were identified (Fig. 7a). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis indicated that these genes were primarily involved in R-SMAD binding, histone deacetylase activity, the ESC/E(Z) complex, circadian rhythm, and rhythmic processes (Fig. 7b). KEGG pathway analysis highlighted their association with human T-cell leukemia virus infection, apoptosis, and cellular senescence (Fig. 7c). PPI network analysis further illustrated functional associations among these genes (Fig. 7d).

Identification of the hub biomarkers and validation

To identify biomarkers from the 34 candidate genes, we employed two machine‑learning algorithms. LASSO regression selected 10 genes with non‑zero coefficients (Fig. 8a), and SVM‑RFE identified 12 genes with optimal predictive accuracy (Fig. 8b). Five overlapping genes—ZMPSTE24, RELB, STAT6, DKC1, and E2F3—were identified as hub biomarkers (Fig. 8c). ROC analysis revealed high diagnostic performance of these biomarkers in the CKD dataset, with AUC values ranging from 0.922 to 0.991 (Fig. 9a). External validation using the independent CKD dataset GSE104948 produced AUC values between 0.551 and 0.810 (Fig. 9b). All five hub genes were differentially expressed between CKD and control samples (p < 0.05; Fig. 9c), and their expression correlations are displayed in a heatmap (Fig. 9d).

Immune infiltration analysis

The CIBERSORT algorithm was employed to assess immune cell infiltration in CKD from dataset GSE66494. The proportions of 22 immune cell types in CKD and healthy controls were shown in Fig. 10a. Significant differences were observed in several cell populations: naïve B cells, CD8⁺ T cells, M0 macrophages, and M2 macrophages were elevated in CKD, whereas memory B cells, resting memory CD4⁺ T cells, follicular helper T cells, and regulatory T cells (Tregs) were reduced. Correlation analysis further revealed a network of interactions among these immune cells (Fig. 10b). The identified hub biomarkers exhibited correlations with the abundance of specific immune subsets (Fig. 10c).

Discussion

This comprehensive study of 2,529 participants from the NHANES (1999–2000, 2001–2002) identified that GAA as an independent predictor of all-cause mortality among adults with CKD. The risk associated with GAA was particularly pronounced in patients aged ≥ 72 years. Higher GAA levels were linked to increased mortality risk among males, whereas both males and females with GAA-MetS exhibited a higher risk compared to those without. Furthermore, transcriptomic analysis based on the independent GEO datasets identified five hub genes (ZMPSTE24, RELB, STAT6, DKC1, and E2F3) whose expression was associated with CKD and involved in biological processes related to cellular senescence and metabolic disorder. The study underscored the importance of GAA as a critical risk factor and suggested new metabolic-aging networks that may be involved in CKD progression.

Our results indicated that metabolic dysfunction and cellular senescence represent potential pathological mechanisms in CKD progression. Patients with CKD exhibited elevated waist circumference, increased fasting blood glucose and HbA1c levels, and reduced HDL-C. This metabolic profile likely promotes a pro-inflammatory state during disease advancement. Sustained hyperglycemia may accelerate the formation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs), which can activate the NF-κB signaling pathway and enhance neutrophil infiltration [11, 12]. Concurrently, dyslipidemia may induce lipotoxicity, driving monocytes toward pro-inflammatory M1/M2 polarization [13–15]. Abdominal obesity could further promote leptin resistance, impair T-cell regulation, and contribute to CD4+ T-cell depletion [16]. Notably, these metabolic and inflammatory changes appear to be associated with accelerated epigenetic aging, as evidenced by the higher mortality observed in CKD patients with high GAA. Metabolic dysfunction was more pronounced in CKD patients with GAA-MetS. In particular, growth differentiation factor-15 (GDF-15), a component of GrimAge, shows a strong correlation with renal fibrosis and endothelial dysfunction [17], suggesting a potential feedback loop in which metabolic stress accelerated epigenetic aging, thereby possibly worsening renal impairment. Moreover, this process could be aggravated by cellular senescence mechanisms, whereby telomere shortening in CD8 + T cells and NK-cell exhaustion could promote an immunosenescent microenvironment that favors renal deterioration [18, 19].

Sex subgroup analyses revealed that the association between elevated GAA and mortality was stronger in males than in females (HR = 2.440 vs. 1.933), which may reflect sex‑specific inflammatory responses. Experimental evidence suggests that testosterone could amplify renal TLR4/NF-κB signaling, promote IL‑6/STAT3 activation, and accelerate fibrotic processes, potentially synergizing with GAA-associated epigenetic drift [20]. In contrast, 17β-estradiol upregulates miR-148a, which suppresses DNMT1 activity and may help preserve renal methylation patterns while mitigating oxidative stress [21, 22]. Notably, the GAA-MetS phenotype was associated with a significantly increased all-cause mortality risk in both sexes, indicating that this combined epigenetic-metabolic risk factor operates independently of sex. The lower survival observed in the female subgroup likely resulted from its higher proportion of older individuals, underscoring advanced age as a key independent predictor of mortality (Supplemental Table 2). Furthermore, socioeconomic factors may further exacerbate these biological risks by limiting healthcare access and delaying metabolic management, potentially accelerating epigenetic aging.

The GAA-MetS phenotype was more evident in younger CKD patients. In this younger subgroup, metabolic dysfunction may act with epigenetic aging to accelerate renal decline. All-cause mortality was lower in patients aged < 72 years compared with those aged ≥ 72 years. And GAA demonstrated the most strongest association with mortality in patients aged ≥ 72 years. Elevated GAA may foster a microenvironment conducive to progressive epigenetic toxicity during aging, leading to accumulated methylation abnormalities. Uremic toxins have also been reported to inhibit TET-mediated DNA demethylation, resulting in hypermethylation of pro-senescence genes and hypomethylation of inflammatory factors [23, 24]. This process could initiate a self-reinforcing cycle that promotes uncontrolled inflamm-aging. In the younger subgroup, MetS represented the most prominent risk factor for all-cause mortality, although the difference did not reach statistical significance relative to the reference group. Sensitivity analysis indicated that MetS was associated with a reduction in GAA-related mortality risk (HR = 0.815), especially among patients aged ≥ 72 years. While some experimental studies suggest that metabolic dysregulation may attenuate epigenetic toxicity through specific mechanisms, such as AMPK/SIRT1 signaling activation under chronic nutrient overload or PPARγ activation in regulatory T cells by elevated leptin and free fatty acids in MetS [25–27], these pathways remain speculative and warrant careful consideration in the present clinical context. First, the observed association in older adults may reflect survivor bias, as individuals reaching advanced age despite having both CKD and MetS likely represent a biologically resilient subgroup. Second, the apparent risk reduction may stem from differential treatment intensity and enhanced clinical surveillance following a MetS diagnosis, partly reflecting benefits of more aggressive risk-factor management rather than MetS itself. Moreover, the clinical and pathophysiological heterogeneity of MetS, which encompasses phenotypes ranging from predominant insulin resistance to dyslipidemia, may lead to variable organ damage and thus attenuate its overall detrimental effect. Therefore, this finding should be considered hypothesis-generating. Future prospective studies with detailed longitudinal data on treatment, disease progression, and ideally tissue-specific markers of aging and injury are needed to address these biases and clarify any potential causal relationships.

Independent transcriptomic analyses in this study identified distinct gene expression patterns associated with metabolic dysfunction and aging in CKD. The discovery of five hub biomarkers—ZMPSTE24, RELB, STAT6, DKC1, and E2F3—suggested that cellular senescence and metabolic pathways were dysregulated in CKD, providing a plausible mechanistic link to the clinical phenotype of GAA. In CKD, the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) may be promoted by impaired ZMPSTE24-mediated prelamin A processing, leading to a persistent DNA damage response via ATM/CHK2 activation [28, 29]. ZMPSTE24 overexpression correlates with elevated M2 macrophage levels, potentially modulating TGF-β1 expression and fibrotic repair [30]. DKC1, which is essential for telomerase RNA stability [31], connects telomere attrition with epigenetic aging. Reduced STAT6 expression is associated with decreased Treg populations, indicating a potential shift toward Th1 polarization in CKD [32, 33] that may compromise IL-10-mediated immune tolerance [34]. Activation of RELB upregulates pro-inflammatory factors through the NF-κB pathway, likely contributing to chronic inflammation and renal fibrosis [35], and may remodel the renal immune microenvironment by modulating immune cell activity [36]. The association between RELB and M1 macrophages could reflect compensatory NF-κB activation in response to mitochondrial stress [37, 38], indicating an early stage in the progression toward immune exhaustion. Furthermore, suppression of E2F3 can halt the cell cycle, induce cellular senescence, and impair DNA damage repair. In renal tubular cells, dysregulated E2F3 activity may drive aberrant cell cycle re-entry and facilitate senescence-associated heterochromatin foci (SAHF) formation via H3K9me3 deposition [39–41]. Collectively, the hub genes may serve as both biomarkers and potential mechanistic effectors in metabolic-aging-related CKD. However, these insights, derived from bioinformatic and literature analyses, require experimental validation to establish causality and a direct relationship with GAA.

This study presented several notable strengths that enhanced the validity and translational potential of its findings. First, it integrated a large, nationally representative epidemiological cohort (NHANES) with mortality and epigenetic data, providing robust population-level evidence for the prognostic value of GAA in CKD. Second, weighted Cox models and extensive sensitivity and subgroup analyses were employed to confirm the stability of the primary associations. Third, the interaction between GAA and MetS was evaluated, yielding more nuanced clinical insights than assessing either factor alone. Finally, by leveraging independent transcriptomic datasets, the study proposed a biologically plausible, hypothesis-generating framework focused on five hub genes(ZMPSTE24, RELB, STAT6, DKC1, and E2F3). This integrative approach linked population-level observations with potential molecular mechanisms, thereby providing a clear direction for future mechanistic research. Together, these strengths reinforced the role of our study in refining CKD risk stratification and establishing a foundational framework for investigating the epigenetic–metabolic–immune axis in renal aging and disease progression.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the DNA methylation data from NHANES were restricted to participants aged ≥ 50 years, which limited the generalizability of our findings to younger CKD populations where early epigenetic alterations may be particularly relevant. Second, the epidemiological analyses (NHANES) and the transcriptomic investigations (GEO) were conducted on entirely independent datasets that did not share overlapping variables. The proposed molecular framework remained a complementary and hypothesis-generating. Third, due to the limitation of sample sizes, the observed high diagnostic performance in the discovery set contrasted with more modest validation results. Further larger cohorts will be needed to validate the finding of the study. Fourth, the immune infiltration analysis performed using CIBERSORT was inherently exploratory. This computational deconvolution approach provided estimates of immune cell proportions from bulk transcriptomic data but cannot resolve the spatial architecture of immune cells within renal tissue or distinguish functionally distinct subsets. Finally, the cross-sectional design of the transcriptomic datasets precluded causal inference regarding the dynamics of gene expression in disease progression. Future longitudinal studies incorporating multi-omics profiling within unified cohorts are essential to validate these findings and elucidate the temporal and causal relationships within the proposed epigenetic-metabolic-immune axis.
