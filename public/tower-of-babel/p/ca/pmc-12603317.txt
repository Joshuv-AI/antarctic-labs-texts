Assessing the spatiotemporal heterogeneity of resource allocation effects on patient flow in shanxi’s County hospitals, China
Authors: Jing Liu, Boyu Li, Tao Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChina’s healthcare system faces significant challenges in resource distribution, with urban tertiary hospitals receiving disproportionate allocations. This imbalance has led to a substantial bypassing of county-level hospitals by rural patients seeking care at centralized urban facilities. This study examines the spatiotemporal effects of resource allocation in county-level hospitals on patient flow distribution between county and urban tertiary hospitals. We analyzed hospital-level data from 2013 to 2019, encompassing 41 tertiary and 189 county hospitals in Shanxi Province. Spatial autocorrelation was assessed using Moran’s I, while Geographically and Temporally Weighted Regression models were employed to examine the relationship between resource allocation (including physicians, beds, medical equipment, and hospital assets) and patient volumes at both hospital levels. Our analysis reveals a positive correlation between increased resource allocation in county hospitals and their patient volumes, coupled with a corresponding decrease in urban tertiary hospital utilization. Temporal analysis shows intensifying benefits for county hospitals and more pronounced adverse effects on tertiary hospitals over time. Spatial patterns indicate stronger positive impacts in northern and southern Shanxi, where tertiary hospitals are less concentrated. Conversely, in areas with dense tertiary hospital presence, increased county hospital resources paradoxically lead to higher service volumes at tertiary institutions. Strategic enhancement of county hospital resources can effectively redistribute patient flows and alleviate overcrowding in tertiary hospitals, particularly in regions with limited tertiary facilities. However, healthcare resource reallocation strategies must account for regional disparities to avoid unintended consequences, such as increased patient migration from county to urban tertiary hospitals. Introduction

Globally, multi-tiered healthcare systems are widely implemented to optimize medical resource allocation and ensure appropriate care delivery based on disease severity1,2. Primary healthcare services, demonstrating superior cost-effectiveness compared to higher-level facilities, play a crucial role in achieving universal health coverage3,4. Consequently, strengthening primary healthcare has become a central focus of health system reforms worldwide5,6.

In rural China, healthcare services are delivered through a three-tier network of county-township-village medical units, designed to function as a referral chain from primary to secondary care and to provide technical assistance from county-level facilities to village clinics7. The core principles of this system are that residents seek care at village clinics for minor illnesses, township hospitals for common illnesses, and county-level hospitals for general serious illnesses8. At the top tier of this rural healthcare system, county hospitals provide a range of services, including the diagnosis and treatment of common and critical diseases, delivery of public health services, and response to natural disasters and health emergencies. These facilities serve over 900 million residents, accounting for more than 70% of China’s total population9,10.

County-level hospitals also play a pivotal bridging role within the urban–rural healthcare system. On one hand, they serve as the primary destination for patients requiring specialized care beyond the capacity of village clinics or township health centers, referring them to urban hospitals when advanced procedures or specialized treatments are needed11. On the other hand, patients who complete treatment in urban hospitals may be transferred back to county-level hospitals for follow-up care and rehabilitation12.

However, China’s healthcare system faces significant challenges in resource distribution, with urban tertiary hospitals concentrating high-quality resources and attracting patients seeking perceived superior care13,14. This has led to a substantial ‘siphon effect,’ where patients bypass local facilities for urban hospitals15. Recent data indicate that tertiary hospitals account for 53% of healthcare services, far exceeding county-level facilities’ share16. This pattern has resulted in overutilization of tertiary services and escalating healthcare costs17,18.

In response, China has implemented comprehensive reforms to strengthen county-level hospitals, including infrastructure upgrades, equipment modernization, and personnel training19. The establishment of county-level integrated health systems aims to reduce urban–rural disparities through resource consolidation12,20. These measures align with the national “XIAO BING BU CHU CUN, DA BING BU CHU XIAN” initiative, targeting 90% local healthcare service provision21. As such, gaining insights into the intricate relationship between healthcare resource allocation and patient flow dynamics has become paramount for the ongoing reform of county-level public hospitals in China.

Although prior studies have offered insights into how county-level public hospital reforms affect patient distribution, their scope predominantly revolves around patient flows within local jurisdictions, neglecting cross-regional patient mobility, particularly the “siphon effect,” where patients bypass local facilities for urban tertiary hospitals12,20,22. This phenomenon is not unique to China. Similar patterns have been observed in India’s tiered healthcare system, where patients often bypass primary health centers (PHCs) in favor of private urban hospitals due to perceived quality gaps6. Enhancing resource allocation in lower-tier hospitals is a cornerstone of public hospital reform globally. Studies in Thailand demonstrate that strengthening district-level facilities can reduce unnecessary referrals to central hospitals, particularly when combined with financial incentives for local care utilization23. However, in systems with strong hospital competition, such as the U.S., increased resources in community hospitals do not always curb patient migration to academic medical centers, as branding and specialist availability dominate patient choice24,25. These international comparisons underscore that while strengthening lower-tier hospitals may mitigate patient bypassing in some contexts, the effectiveness of such reforms is highly dependent on local health system structures and patient preferences26. China’s healthcare system presents prevalent siphon effect resulting from the influence of multiple complex factors. It remains unknown whether bolstering resource allocation in lower-tier hospitals can effectively redirect patient flow.

Significant spatial and temporal variations in healthcare utilization, as seen in China2,27, are also evident in other large, decentralized systems. For example, Germany’s regional hospital planning faces similar disparities, with rural areas experiencing declining bed capacities despite policy efforts to promote equity1. Traditional analytical methods like ordinary least squares (OLS) often fail to capture these spatial autocorrelations and temporal variations28,29. Internationally, advanced approaches like Geographically Weighted Regression (GWR) have been employed to model hospital catchments, revealing how local socioeconomic factors interact with supply-side policies30. The results compellingly demonstrate that effective health resource optimization requires policymakers to account for substantial regional variations, demanding tailored policy solutions that transcend conventional one-size-fits-all paradigms. To realize this goal, a shift towards more rigorous multiple regression methodologies that incorporate both temporal and spatial scales is imperative.

This study aims to address these gaps by examining how county-level hospital resource allocation affects patient flow distribution between county and urban tertiary hospitals, with particular emphasis on temporal and spatial heterogeneity. Our findings offer valuable insights for optimizing resource allocation in lower-tier hospitals and promoting balanced patient distribution within China’s multi-tiered healthcare system.

Methods

Study area and data source

Our study focuses on Shanxi Province, located in north-central China (Fig. 1). The province covers 156,700 square kilometers and has a population of approximately 35 million. With a per capita GDP of 73,686 Chinese Yuan in 2022, Shanxi ranks 14th among China’s 31 provinces and municipalities in economic development. The province’s healthcare infrastructure includes 1376 hospitals, 186,300 hospital beds, and 189,334 healthcare professionals, distributed across 11 prefectures and 96 counties31.

Location of study area and sample hospitals. The figure was created by the ArcMap 10.4.1.

Shanxi Province’s healthcare system shares key structural and institutional features with many other Chinese provinces. Similar to the central provinces of China (e.g., Hebei, Shaanxi, Henan), Shanxi exhibits a pronounced urban–rural divide in healthcare access, with high-quality resources concentrated in major cities (Taiyuan, Datong) while county hospitals face persistent gaps in staffing and infrastructure. This spatial imbalance mirrors national trends, where tertiary hospitals in provincial capitals account for a disproportionate share of services despite government efforts to strengthen primary care. The “siphon effect” observed in Shanxi, where patients bypass local facilities for urban tertiary centers, is well-documented across China14. Moreover, Shanxi’s healthcare reforms, including establishing county-level integrated health systems, follow the national public hospital reforms. This province thus serves as a representative case study regarding its models of health resource allocation and service utilization offering valuable insights for other provinces in China or countries facing similar challenges like China.

We obtained hospital-level data from the Financial Network Reporting System maintained by the Shanxi Ministry of Health. The dataset included comprehensive information on healthcare resources, service utilization, and health expenditures. Our analysis focused on county-level and urban tertiary hospitals. After excluding cases with critical missing data, our final sample comprised 41 tertiary hospitals and 189 county hospitals. The study period spanned from 2013 to 2019, providing a seven-year longitudinal perspective.

We supplemented the hospital data with socioeconomic indicators at prefecture and county levels from the Shanxi Statistical Yearbook31. For spatial analysis, we obtained precise geographic coordinates (latitude and longitude) for each hospital using Baidu Maps, China’s leading digital mapping service. These coordinates enabled precise spatial visualization of hospital locations, as shown in Fig. 1.

Variable measurement

Our study employed a dual analytical approach, as illustrated in Fig. 2. The first analytical component examined the relationship between county-level hospital resource allocation and urban tertiary hospital service volumes. Using data from tertiary hospitals, we investigated whether increased resource allocation to county hospitals at the prefecture level was associated with reduced service volumes in urban tertiary hospitals.

Analysis framework and corresponding study variables.

The second component focused on county hospitals, analyzing how their internal resource allocation patterns influenced their own service volumes. This dual approach allowed us to examine both the potential competitive effects between hospital tiers and the internal dynamics of resource utilization within county hospitals.

Dependent variables

We examined both absolute and relative measures of healthcare utilization as dependent variables across both analytical components12,32,33. Specifically, we analyzed absolute service volumes including (1) annual outpatient visits and (2) annual inpatient admissions, and relative service proportions including (1) share of total prefecture-level outpatient visits and (2) share of total prefecture-level inpatient admissions. These dual metrics enabled comprehensive assessment of patient distribution patterns between county and tertiary hospitals (Table 1).

Independent variables

The study employed distinct sets of independent variables for each analytical component: county hospitals’ resource share within prefecture: (1) proportion of hospital facilities; (2) proportion of bed capacity; (3) proportion of doctors26,34, and core hospital resources: (1) doctors; (2) bed capacity; (3) medical equipment13,26,35.

Control variables

To effectively mitigate the confounding effects within our model, we have strategically incorporated diverse sets of control variables tailored specifically for two distinct analytical frameworks. In the first analysis, recognizing the direct influence of resource allocation of tertiary hospitals on their service volume, we have incorporated their number of doctors, beds, and medical equipment as control variables36. In the second analysis, based on the potential siphoning effect of tertiary hospitals on patient volume in county hospitals37, we calculated the proportions of tertiary hospitals, their doctors, and beds at the prefecture level, subsequently merging these data into the county hospital-level database based on the prefecture where each county hospital resides.

Additionally, to capture the broader contextual influence, we have incorporated hospital external environment variables as covariates. These encompass population size, the proportion of urban population, and GDP per capita24,38. Notably, we have ensured that these indicators are aligned with the analytical granularity of each analysis: prefecture-level indicators for the first analysis and county-level indicators for the second, thereby maintaining consistency and relevance. Their statistical summaries are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Methodology

In this study, we employed a sophisticated statistical model that integrates both spatial and temporal dimensions to delve into the relationship between resource allocation patterns within county-level hospitals and the subsequent distribution of patient flows. First, we leveraged Moran’s Index within a spatial autocorrelation framework to meticulously quantify the extent of spatial dependency inherent in our dependent variable. Upon confirming the existence of spatial autocorrelation, we progressed to construct a geographically and temporally weighted regression (GTWR) model. This analytical tool enabled us to dissect the intricate effects of independent variables, while simultaneously accounting for their temporal dynamics and spatial heterogeneity.

Descriptive analysis

Figures 3, 4 compare the average patient volumes and resource allocation between county-level and urban tertiary hospitals. Although county hospitals collectively possess a larger share of overall resources compared to prefecture-level tertiary hospitals, the average allocation of doctors, beds, and medical equipment per tertiary hospital significantly exceeds that of county hospitals (Fig. 4). These disparities have grown more pronounced over time. For instance, the average number of registered doctors in tertiary hospitals increased substantially from 792 in 2013 to 1113 in 2019, while the corresponding number in county hospitals rose only slightly from 64 to 72 during the same period.

In terms of patient volumes, tertiary hospitals consistently handled significantly higher numbers of outpatient visits and inpatient admissions than county hospitals, with both metrics showing a steady upward trend throughout the study period (Fig. 3). Notably, the share of patients served by tertiary hospitals has increased over time, while the share served by county hospitals has declined.

Spatial correlation test

The Moran’s I index was used to evaluate the spatial correlation of outcome variables from 2013 to 2019. The results of the global Moran’s I analysis for both service volume and its share in county and urban tertiary hospitals are summarized in Table S2. Our analysis indicated that the global Moran’s I indices were predominantly statistically significant, revealing a strong spatial correlation in the distribution of patient volume across the study area. Notably, a significant negative Moran’s I value was observed for inpatient admissions in county hospitals and their corresponding proportions, suggesting distinct negative spatial autocorrelation.

These findings demonstrate that the distribution of patient volume was not random but likely influenced by local factors. Traditional models that ignore spatial variation are therefore inadequate in this context. To address this limitation, the GTWR model, which accounts for spatial heterogeneity, was introduced.

Discussion

This study utilizes hospital-level data to explore the relationship between resource allocation strategies in county-level hospitals and their impact on the redistribution of patient flows between these facilities and urban tertiary hospitals. Our findings indicate that increasing resources in county hospitals not only enhances their ability to attract more patients but also reduces patient volumes at tertiary hospitals. This relationship demonstrates significant variations across both temporal and spatial dimensions.

From a global perspective, our analysis highlights a key finding: increasing resource allocation to county hospitals is positively associated with their patient volumes, while negatively correlated with patient volumes in tertiary hospitals. Traditionally, tertiary healthcare institutions in China, known for their abundant physicians, extensive bed capacities, and advanced medical technologies, have been widely regarded as benchmarks for quality improvement35,37. This perception has led many patients to believe that larger hospitals inherently provide superior healthcare services. As a result, a significant proportion of rural patients bypass county hospitals and seek care directly at urban tertiary facilities41, contributing to a steady decline in patient volumes at county-level institutions. To address this trend, expanding resources in county hospitals has been identified as a practical strategy to enhance their service capabilities.

Enhanced county hospital resources simultaneously increase local service volumes and decrease tertiary hospital utilization might stem from following mechanisms. Firstly, recruiting more medical professionals and increasing bed capacities at county hospitals directly elevate clinical capacity, enabling treatment of more complex cases that previously required referral. Secondly, resource upgrades alter patient perceptions through two pathways: (a) visible infrastructure improvements build trust in local facilities, and (b) the arrival of specialists reduces perceived quality gaps. Integrating the above mechanisms, the referral filter effect occurs when better-resourced county hospitals more accurately identify true tertiary cases. Patients were also encouraged to seek care within their local counties, thereby reducing the outflow of patients to tertiary hospitals34.

From the temporal perspective, the intensifying dual effect of county hospital resource allocation reflects the cumulative impact of China’s multi-pronged health system reforms since 2015. For example, integrated health systems through resource consolidation have been continuously advocated by the Chinese government in recent years. In this system, the mandatory specialist rotation program created enduring clinical capability transfers, with tertiary hospital physicians required to spend several months annually mentoring county teams12,20,42. Simultaneously, the brand integration initiative allowed county facilities to affiliate with prestigious tertiary centers, leveraging urban reputational capital while building local trust12. This hybrid branding strategy reduced perceived quality gaps and further influenced their healthcare-seeking behavior.

Additionally, the payment method reform also made potential contributions. For example, Capitation prepayment was introduced for the Integrated County Healthcare Consortium (ICHC). The total healthcare costs were prepackaged, including outpatient and inpatient services and referral services, to the county hospital. Those outside of the ICHC were deducted from the capitation funding pool. Unspent funds are kept by the ICHC, but overspending was not subsidized by the insurance programs43. This incentive mechanism is designed to motivate county hospitals to maximize the retention of patients for treatment within their own facilities. Moreover, diagnosis-related group payment reform intentionally set loss-inducing rates for tertiary hospitals treating basic conditions44. For those uncomplicated diseases (e.g., hypertension and diabetes), the reimbursement ceiling was set below tertiary hospitals’ actual costs. This approach incentivized tertiary hospitals to proactively decrease the admission rates of such patients.

Previous studies have demonstrated that these reforms have significantly enhanced the convenience and capacity of lower-tier hospitals, while also increasing patient loyalty and preference for these institutions. This has led to higher levels of patient satisfaction and trust12,42. These findings underscore a critical policy implication: investing in high-quality health resources at lower-tier hospitals can effectively address the imbalance in patient distribution across multi-tiered healthcare systems.

Our analysis of spatial heterogeneity revealed that the enhancement of county-level health resources, which aims to bolster service capacity and alleviate patient congestion in tertiary hospitals, primarily exhibits its effects in the northern and southern regions of Shanxi Province. Two key factors that change how patients make healthcare decisions may make the contributions. Firstly, in these peripheral regions, the sparse spatial distribution of urban tertiary hospitals delineates natural geographic service areas (Figure S1). Within these areas, county-level healthcare facilities function not merely as transient waystations but as indispensable anchors of healthcare delivery36,37,45. Previous studies also found that geographic remoteness significantly influenced care-seeking behavior since patients weigh accessibility more heavily when urban alternatives involve substantial travel burdens46,47. As county hospitals augment their capacities, patients are presented with authentic choices rather than hypothetical possibilities, thereby fundamentally reshaping their decision—making processes when seeking medical care. Secondly, the relatively modest economic development was observed in these regions. People in these less wealthy areas feel the pinch much more when traveling to distant city hospitals. This economic reality interacts powerfully with China’s tiered insurance system, where differential reimbursement rates make county hospital care become cheaper48. As a result, local residents are more inclined to prioritize affordable county-level healthcare services.

In contrast, central Shanxi exhibits a positive correlation between increased resource allocation to county hospitals and the service volume of tertiary hospitals. On the one hand, as the province’s most developed area, it hosts a dense concentration of high-quality tertiary hospitals in major urban centers. Accordingly, high-quality healthcare resources are predominantly directed toward urban tertiary hospitals, widening the perceived gap between these institutions and county hospitals49. This perception undoubtedly reinforces patients’ preference for prioritizing urban tertiary hospitals for seeking medical care. Additionally, the high concentration of tertiary hospitals in this region intensifies competition among them. Previous studies have indicated that heightened hospital competition can drive institutions to expand their scale and engage in a medical “arms race”37,50. As these hospitals grow in size, they tend to siphon off patients from surrounding counties. One the other hand, patients in this region, facing smaller relative cost burdens and more convenient transportation compared to northern and southern regions, continue to bypass county hospitals and seek care directly at urban tertiary facilities49.

Although the Integrated Delivery System was introduced in China to promote collaboration between urban tertiary hospitals and county hospitals, enabling the latter to enhance their service capabilities and establish cooperative networks, previous studies have revealed a paradoxical outcome. Instead of revitalizing and strengthening the county-level healthcare system, tertiary hospitals have used the integration as an opportunity for expansion, excessively admitting patients who could otherwise be managed at the county level51,52. For example, urban hospitals leverage their resource advantage to establish referral networks that channel complex cases from county hospitals while capturing the growing demand for advanced services. Under such circumstance, larger county hospitals have inadvertently facilitated the transfer of even more patients to urban tertiary institutions. Furthermore, the concentration of tertiary hospitals in Central Shanxi has intensified market competition, incentivizing these hospitals to attract patients from county hospitals to increase revenue37,50. To ensure a more equitable and efficient distribution of healthcare resources in future reforms, it is crucial to establish an incentive and constraint mechanism that promotes genuine resource reconfiguration and collaboration across different tiers of hospitals.

This study has several limitations. First, the scope of our analysis is strictly limited to data sourced from hospitals within Shanxi Province, precluding a direct generalization of our findings to broader regions within China. The inherent bidirectional relationship between hospital health resource allocation and service volume poses challenges for causal inference. Although we have leveraged longitudinal data spanning from 2013 to 2019, the observational design of this study inherently limits our ability to draw definitive conclusions about causality. Third, data unavailability has resulted in an incomplete inclusion of prefecture-level control variables that potentially impact patient distribution between county and tertiary hospitals, such as the proportion of elderly individuals and chronic disease patients in the general population. Fourth, the absence of data from village clinics and township health centers constitutes a crucial knowledge gap in our comprehensive analysis. This limitation prevents us from conclusively assessing whether the enhanced allocation of healthcare resources in county hospitals may inadvertently siphon patients from primary care setting, despite its apparent effect of alleviating patient volume in urban tertiary hospitals. Future study incorporating data from a wider range of healthcare facilities across different tiers is imperative to comprehensively analyze the systemic impacts arising from the allocation of healthcare resources. Fifth, due to data unavailability during and immediately after the COVID-19 pandemic, our analysis does not include recent data (past five years). While China’s healthcare resource allocation and service utilization patterns have largely returned to pre-pandemic levels, future studies incorporating post-pandemic data when they become available could enhance the generalizability of these findings.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that increasing health resource allocation to county hospitals significantly enhances their service capacity and reduces patient congestion in tertiary hospitals. However, these relationships exhibit notable temporal and spatial variations. Temporally, the positive effects of resource allocation in county hospitals have become more pronounced over time. Spatially, the analysis reveals a critical insight: strengthening county-level health resources substantially improves their service capacity and alleviates overcrowding in tertiary hospitals, particularly in regions with fewer tertiary hospitals and a higher density of county hospitals. In contrast, in areas with a high concentration of tertiary hospitals, these effects are reversed. These findings highlight the importance of directing high-quality health resources to county hospitals and implementing regulatory measures to prevent the excessive transfer of patients from county hospitals to urban tertiary institutions.

The data supporting the findings of this study are available from the Provincial Health Commission. However, restrictions apply to the availability of these data, which were used under license for the current study and are therefore not publicly available. Data are however available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request and with permission of the Provincial Health Commission.
