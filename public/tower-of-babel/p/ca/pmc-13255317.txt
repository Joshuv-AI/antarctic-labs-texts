Acceptability and factors influencing human milk banking among healthcare professionals in eastern Ethiopia: a mixed methods study
Authors: Ahmed Abadir, Sagni Girma, Fentahun Meseret, Hayat Hussen, Mulualem Keneni, Assefa Desalew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough human breast milk improves child survival, evidence on its acceptability among healthcare professionals in low-income settings such as Ethiopia remains limited. This study assessed the acceptability of human milk banking (HMBs) and identified factors influencing healthcare professionals’ acceptance of HMBs in public hospitals in eastern Ethiopia.MethodsA facility-based, multicenter, and mixed-methods study was conducted in four public hospitals from August to September 2024. The quantitative component used simple random sampling to select 361 healthcare professionals, while the qualitative section used purposive sampling to recruit seven participants for in-depth interviews. Quantitative data were analyzed using Binary logistic regression, with adjusted odds ratio (AOR) and 95% confidence interval (CI). Qualitative data were analyzed thematically based on the theoretical framework of acceptability (TFA).ResultsThe acceptance rate of HMBs was 38.62% (95% CI: 33.47–43.96%). Factors significantly associated with acceptance included adequate knowledge (AOR = 2.25; 95% CI: 1.03–4.93), good attitude (AOR = 3.11; 95% CI: 1.80–5.38), higher educational level (Master’s/MPH) (AOR = 12.84; 95% CI: 4.19–39.29), being a medical doctor (AOR = 2.70; 95% CI: 1.44–5.03), and having children (AOR = 4.38; 95% CI: 2.54–7.53). Qualitative findings indicated that knowledge of breast milk benefits, confidence in safety protocols, and training opportunities facilitated acceptance, while cultural beliefs, awareness gaps, and resource constraints were identfied as barriers.ConclusionFour in ten healthcare professionals accepted the concept of HMBs. Targeted interventions focusing on education, training, and resource allocation are needed to improve acceptance and support successful implementation, contributing to reduced child morbidity and mortality in low-income settings.Keywords: Acceptability, Human milk banking, Healthcare professionals, Mixed-methods, Eastern Ethiopia

Background

Optimal nutrition during the first 1,000 days of life is critical for the long-term health, growth, and development of infants and children [1]. Breast milk provides all essential nutrients and energy required during infancy and remains an important dietary component throughout the first two years of life [2]. The World Health Organization (WHO) and the United Nations International Children’s Emergency Fund (UNICEF) strongly recommend initiating breastfeeding within one hour of birth and maintaining exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months to reduce infant morbidity and mortality [2]. Despite these recommendations, numerous global challenges, such as maternal morbidity and mortality, inadequate lactation and breastfeeding support, aggressive formula marketing, and weak policy implementation, continue to hinder both the early initiation and sustained practice of breastfeeding, depriving many infants of its full benefits [3]. When maternal milk is unavailable or contraindicated, donor breast milk (DBM) is recommended as the preferred alternative, particularly for vulnerable infants such as those born preterm or with low birth weight [4]. Evidence indicates that human milk (HM) substantially reduces the risk of necrotizing enterocolitis, sepsis, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, retinopathy of prematurity, and severe intraventricular haemorrhage, while improving feeding tolerance, shortening hospitalization duration in neonatal intensive care unit (NICU), and lowering cardiovascular risk factors in adolescence [5–10].

Breastfeeding and human milk donation offer several maternal health benefits, including reduced risks of breast and ovarian cancers, promotion of lactation amenorrhea, and support for post-partum weight loss [7]. Human milk banking is are nonprofit facilities that collect, processes, stores, and distributes safe donor milk for infants in need [11]. Donors are healthy lactating mothers with surplus milk, ensuring their own infants’ nutritional needs are met first [9, 10]. Established over a century ago, with the first HMB founded in Vienna in 1909, more than 600 HMBs now operate globally predominantly in Europe and North America with successful examples also emerging in African countries such as South Africa, Kenya, and Uganda [12–14].

Furthermore, the Global Network of Human Milk Banks (rBLH), coordinated by Brazil’s Oswaldo Cruz Foundation (Fiocruz), brings together more than 20 countries across Latin America, Europe, and Africa. The network promotes international cooperation in breastfeeding protection and HMB through policy development, capacity building, and research collaboration. Recognized by the WHO and Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) as a cost-effective and impactful strategy, the rBLH has played a central role in expanding safe donor milk access and sharing technical expertise globally [15].

Despite global endorsements, the adoption of HMB programmes in Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs), including Ethiopia, remains constrained [16–19]. This limited implementation is a significant concern given Ethiopia’s high prevalence of preterm birth (10.48%) [20] and low birth weight (14.1%) [21].

The acceptance of HMBs among health care professionals exhibits considerable variability across different regions globally. For instance, research showed that acceptance rates range from 31% in Indonesia [16] and Zimbabwe [17] to 94.29% in India [18]. Furthermore, studies in Ghana and South Africa indicated widespread support for HMBs, although some hesitancy remained regarding actual usage [22, 23]. Several factors significantly influence the acceptance of HMBs: Positive influences include awareness of HMBs, good attitudes, and prior professional experience in environments where HMBs are utilized [16–18]. Conversely, various barriers can impede acceptance, such as religious and cultural beliefs, concerns about infection transmission, particularly human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), a lack of awareness, insufficient support systems, and logistical challenges [17, 23–25].

Currently, Ethiopia has no operational HMBs and limited evidence on their acceptability within the healthcare system, including among health professionals. While global studies have shown that knowledge, attitudes, and contextual factors significantly influence HMBs’ acceptance [17, 22, 26], a prior study in Addis Ababa indicated that approximately 53.7% of healthcare professionals lacked awareness regarding donor human milk [27]. This highlights a critical gap, which this study addressed by assessing the acceptability of HMBs and identifying enablers, barriers, and influencing factors among healthcare professionals in public hospitals of Harari Regional State and Dire Dawa City Administration, eastern Ethiopia.

Methods and materials

Study setting and population

A mixed-method design was employed, combining a cross-sectional quantitative study with qualitative in-depth interviews to obtain both measurable estimates of acceptance and detailed insights into the facilitators and barriers of HMBs among health care professionals. This study was conducted in four public hospitals: Hiwot Fana Comprehensive Specialized University Hospital (HFCSUH) and Jugal General Hospital in the Harari Regional State, and Dil-Chora Referral Hospital and Sabian General Hospital in the Dire Dawa City Administration, from August 15 to September 13, 2024. These hospitals were included they are the only public hospitals in the two regions and are the main institutions providing comprehensive maternal, neonatal, and child health services. Therefore they represent key settings for assessing the acceptability and potential implementation of HMBs initiatives in eastern Ethiopia.

Hiwot Fana Comprehensive Specialized University Hospital is located in Harari Regional State, 526 km from Addis Ababa. The 2024 health management information system reported that HFCSUH has 825 healthcare professionals, with 254 working in maternal and child health units. This facility manages 5,500 childbirths annually and has a Level III NICU. Jugal General Hospital, established in 1902, and located in Harari Regional State, it serves over 58,000 patients annually, employs 297 healthcare professionals, with 66 in maternal and child health units, and has a level II NICU. Dil-Chora Referral Hospital, established in 1952, and located in Dire-Dawa City Administration, 508 km from Addis Ababa. According to the hospital’s 2024 health management information system report, it had 570 healthcare professionals, with 107 in maternal and child health units, and manages 4,074 births annually, and has a Level II NICU. Furthermore, Sabian General Hospital, located in the Dire-Dawa City Administration, and had 237 healthcare professionals, with 68 specifically assigned to maternal and child health units. The hospital conducted 2,957 deliveries in 2024, and it operates a Level II NICU.

Population

Healthcare professionals working in maternal and child health units of the selected public hospitals were included. These include nurses, doctors, midwives, and public health professionals directly involved in maternal and child healthcare services. However, healthcare professionals on annual, maternity, or sick leave, those with less than one year of professional experience, and those who did not consent to participate in the study were excluded.

Sampling

The quantitative component of this study employed a single population proportion formula to calculate the required sample size, considering a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error, and a 10% non-response rate. The calculated sample size was proportionally allocated to the number of health care professionals working in the maternal and child health units of each selected hospital. A sampling frame was developed by listing all health care professionals working in the maternal and child health units of each selected hospital. The lists were obtained from official department or unit records, which included nurses, doctors, midwives, and public health professionals eligible for the study. Participants were then selected from this list according to the determined sample size. Study participants were selected using a simple random sampling technique based on the allocated sample size. After developing the sampling frame from department or unit lists of eligible health care professionals, each individual was assigned a unique number. Participants were then randomly selected using a computer-based random number generator application to ensure unbiased selection (Fig. 1).

For the qualitative study, the sample size was determined based on the principle of data saturation. Seven health care professionals were selected purposively based on their direct roles in maternal and child health and their ability to offer relevant insights into the acceptability of HMBs. They were not included in the quantitative survey and were chosen from the same professional backgrounds to ensure diverse and relevant perspectives for in-depth exploration of HMB acceptability.

Data collection

For quantitative data, the participants were recruited after providing written informed consent. Data were collected using a self-administered structured questionnaire adapted from previously published studies [17, 26, 28–31]. The questionnaire included socio-demographic characteristics. Acceptability of HMBs guided by the Theoretical Framework of Acceptability (TFA) [32]. The data collection was conducted by a team of three trained professionals holding Bachelor of Science degrees. The team comprised a pediatric and child health nurse and two midwives. The data collection process was overseen by an individual holding a Master of Science degree in health sciences with previous research experience.

For qualitative data, the research employed a semi-structured interview guide, adapted from previous studies and tailored to the specific local environment, to conduct in-depth interviews. This guide was developed using TFA to examine factors influencing acceptability and to identify the facilitators and barriers to the implementation of HMBs [17, 22, 29, 30]. The data were collected by one researcher holding a Master of Science degree in pediatrics and child health, while a second professional acted as a supervisor and facilitator to observe and support the process, ensuring data quality and consistency. All interviews were conducted in person, at a private place to uphold participant confidentiality. The duration of each interview ranged from 10 to 20 min. With the informed consent of the participants, the interviews were audio-recorded. These recordings were subsequently transcribed verbatim. To elicit more comprehensive responses and clarify any ambiguities, probing questions were employed throughout the interviews.

Outcome measurement

Acceptability of human milk banking was measured using the Theoretical Framework of Acceptability (TFA) proposed by Sekhon et al. [32]. This framework enables assessment of the perceived acceptability of health interventions among service providers or recipients before implementation. The questionnaire included one item for each of the seven TFA constructs: (1) affective attitude: how an individual feels about the intervention before taking part, (2) burden: The perceived amount of effort required to participate in the intervention, (3) ethicality: The extent to which the intervention has a good fit with an individual’s value system, (4) intervention coherence: participant’s understanding of how an intervention works, (5) opportunity costs: The extent to which benefits, profits, or values must be given up to engage in the intervention, (6) perceived effectiveness: The extent to which the intervention is perceived to be likely to achieve its purpose, and (7) self-efficacy: participant’s confidence that they can perform the intervention [20, 32]. Each question used a three-option response scale of “Yes,” “No,” and “Unsure.”

Two of the seven items were negatively worded and, therefore reverse reverse-coded. For positively stated items, a response of “Yes” was scored as 1 point, while “No” and “Unsure” were scored as 0. For negatively stated items, “No” was scored as 1 point, while “Yes” and “Unsure” were scored as 0. To minimize bias and improve analytical clarity, “Unsure” responses were treated as “No” for positively stated items and as “Yes” for negatively stated items. This approach assumes that uncertainty reflects a lack of clear agreement for positive statements but a potential inclination toward agreement for negative ones, consistent with previous studies on healthcare intervention acceptability [23, 24]. Responses for each construct were categorized as “accepted” or “not accepted.” Overall acceptability was then determined by summing the number of accepted constructs. Participants who accepted at least five out of the seven components were classified as having accepted human milk banking [29].

Adequate knowledge

Respondents who correctly answered at least eight of eleven HMB knowledge questions [16, 33].

Inadequate knowledge

Respondents who answered fewer than eight of the eleven HMB knowledge questions [16, 33].

Good attitude

Respondents who correctly answered at least eleven of the thirteen attitudes toward HMBs items [16].

Poor attitude

Respondents who answered fewer than eleven attitudes toward HMB items [16].

Data quality control

For the quantitative component, data were collected after obtaining written informed consent from all participants. A self-administered structured questionnaire was used, adapted from previously published studies [17, 26, 28–31]. The tool included items on socio-demographic characteristics and acceptability of human milk banking, which was guided by the Theoretical Framework of Acceptability (TFA) [32]. Data collection was conducted by a team of three trained Bachelor of Science professionals, including one pediatric and child health nurse and two midwives. The team was supervised by a Master of Science-level health professional with prior research experience to ensure consistency and adherence to the study protocol.

Before data collection, the tools were pretested on 5% of the total sample at Haramaya General Hospital, and necessary modifications were made for clarity and cultural relevance. The internal consistency of the acceptability section demonstrated a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.81, indicating good reliability. The data collectors and supervisors underwent two days of intensive training on study objectives, ethical conduct, questionnaire administration, data coding, and verification procedures. Completed questionnaires were reviewed daily by the supervisor and the principal investigator for completeness and accuracy. Data were entered into EpiData version 4.6, using double-entry verification and built-in range and consistency checks before export to Stata version 17 for analysis.

For the qualitative component, in-depth interviews were conducted using a semi-structured interview guide developed from previous studies and tailored to the local context [17, 22, 29, 30]. The guide, grounded in the TFA, aimed to explore factors influencing acceptability and to identify facilitators and barriers to the implementation of human milk banking.

Data were collected by a researcher holding a Master of Science degree in pediatrics and child health, while another Master’s professional acted as a supervisor and facilitator, ensuring procedural consistency and data quality. All interviews were conducted in person at a private location to maintain confidentiality. Each session lasted 10–20 min and was audio-recorded with informed consent. Probing questions were used to elicit deeper insights and clarify responses. The recordings were transcribed verbatim, reviewed multiple times for accuracy, and coded independently by two trained researchers. Triangulation with quantitative findings and peer debriefing with senior researchers were conducted to enhance credibility and confirm consistency across data sources.

Statistical analysis

The quantitative data were entered into EpiData version 4.6 and analyzed with Stata version 17. Descriptive statistical analysis was done using a cross-tabulation. Frequency distribution and summary statistics were calculated for both groups. The association between covariates and outcome variables was assessed using binary logistic regression model. The variance inflation factor (> 10) and standard error (> 2) were used to assess multicollinearity, the Hosmer‒Lemeshow test (> 0.05) was used to assess goodness-of-fit, and the Area Under the Curve (AUC) was 80.5% an excellent discriminatory ability in differentiating groups. The adjusted odds ratio (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) and a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered a statistically significant association with the outcome variable.

The qualitative data were analyzed by thematic analysis using ATLAS.ti7 software, based on TFA of healthcare interventions. Interview transcripts were read multiple times to ensure familiarity. Two MSc-level researchers independently coded the transcripts and identified initial codes, which were then grouped into themes and subthemes. Differences in coding were resolved through discussion until full inter-coder agreement was achieved. Themes were derived inductively and used to explore barriers and facilitators to human milk banking acceptability. Data triangulation was carried out by integrating qualitative themes with quantitative findings during the interpretation phase. Specifically, qualitative insights explaining healthcare professionals’ perceptions, motivations, and barriers were compared with statistically significant quantitative variables influencing acceptability, allowing for comprehensive contextual understanding and validation of results.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics

Of the 361 calculated sample, 347 healthcare professionals completed the questionnaire, resulting in a response rate of 96.1%. The remaining 3.9% were not included in the analysis due to unreturned or incomplete responses to ensure data completeness and reliability. Most participants were from HFCSH (n = 182; 52.45%). Regarding gender, (n = 177; 51.01%) were male. The age range of the participants was 20 to 50 years, with a median age of 30 and an interquartile range of 28 to 34 years. Concerning marital status, (n = 181; 52.16%) were married, and (n = 156; 44.96%) had children. Regarding the educational level, the majority of the participants (n = 209; 60.23%) possessed a bachelor’s Science degree, with nurses representing (n = 153; 44.09%) of the sample. Work experience ranged from 1 to 30 years, with a median of 5 years. Regarding their religion, (n = 155; 44.67%) participants identified as Orthodox Christians. Monthly incomes ranged from 3,600 to 50,000 Ethiopian Birr (ETB), with a median income of 9000 ETB (Table 1).

Factors influencing human milk banking acceptability

In multivariable logistic regression analysis, health care professionals who have children were 4.38 times (AOR: 4.38; 95% CI: 2.54–7.53; p = 0.000) more likely to accept HMBs compared to their counterparts. Professionals with MSc/MPH degrees were 12.84 times (AOR: 12.84; 95% CI: 4.19–39.29; p = 0.000) more likely to accept HMBs compared to those with a diploma and BSc holders. Moreover, medical doctors and above (general practitioners, residents, and specialists) were 2.7 times (AOR: 2.7; 95% CI: 1.44–5.03; p = 0.002) more likely to accept HMBs compared to those with a diploma and BSc holders. Health care professionals who possessed adequate knowledge of HMBs were 2.25-fold (AOR: 2.25; 95% CI: 1.03–4.93; p = 0.041) more likely to accept HMBs compared to those with inadequate knowledge. Furthermore, healthcare professionals with a good attitude towards HMBs were 3.11 times (AOR: 3.11; 95% CI: 1.80–5.38; p = 0.000) more likely to accept HMBs compared to those with a poor attitude (Table 4).

Facilitators to the acceptability of human milk banking

This study identified several facilitators that enhance the acceptability of HMBs among healthcare professionals. One important factor was a strong understanding of the scientific and health benefits of breast milk, which influenced positive perceptions toward donation and utilization. As one nurse explained:

We, the healthcare professionals, can understand the effect of donating. As I donate, the production of milk also increases, so knowing about the mechanism of milk production can be one factor for accepting the HMBs concept

Another facilitator was confidence in the safety and quality assurance protocols implemented by healthcare systems strengthened trust in the use of donor breast milk. As highlighted by a midwife:

If HMBs service operates within a healthcare facility, the issues that arise, such as hygiene, can be addressed and foster acceptance

Finally, the availability of training and continuous educational programs was perceived as crucial in raising awareness, dispelling misconceptions, and fostering greater utilization of HMBs. A pediatric resident emphasized this need, stating:

Since the HMBs concept is new, training and awareness for all stakeholders are crucial.

Barriers to the acceptability of human milk banking

Several factors have been identified as barriers to the acceptance of HMBs. These include limited awareness of the existence and purpose of HMBs, religious and cultural concerns. As pediatrician noted:

Community concerns and religious factors may influence the acceptability of HMBs among healthcare professionals, while lack of awareness among them represents a significant barrier. For example, in the Muslim context, there is a belief that sharing breast milk may create familial (milk) kinship, which can limit acceptance of HMBs

Additionally, infrastructural limitations were barriers for the acceptance of HMBs, as emphasized by one pediatric resident:

Insufficient infrastructure and resources can limit the implementation of HMBs

Discussion

Human milk banking provides a crucial alternative to infant formula, offering numerous benefits for infants who cannot receive their mother’s milk [34]. Globally, the acceptance of HMBs among healthcare professionals exhibits considerable variability across different regions [16, 19]. This study found that 38.62% (95% CI: 33.47–43.96%) of healthcare professionals accepted the concept of HMBs. In quantitative analysis, having adequate knowledge, a good attitude, having children, holding a master’s degree, being a medical doctor, and above were significantly associated with the acceptability of HMBs. Moreover, understanding of the nutritional and immunological benefits of breast milk and breast milk donation, confidence in the safety protocols of the healthcare system, training, and educational initiatives were facilitators of acceptability. Conversely, lack of awareness, cultural and religious concerns, and logistical challenges were barriers to the acceptability.

In this study, the acceptance rate of HMBs among healthcare professionals was moderately higher than that reported in Zimbabwe (31%) [17] and Indonesia (31%) [16]. This difference may be attributed to variations in participants’ educational attainment, professional mix, and cultural norms. For example, in the present study, more than half of the participants held bachelor’s degrees or higher, which could enhance awareness and openness toward new neonatal nutrition interventions. In contrast, studies from Zimbabwe and Indonesia included a higher proportion of general nurses and ancillary health staff, among whom awareness of HMBs practices was relatively limited.

Similarly, a study conducted in Ghana reported favourable views toward HMBs, where most healthcare workers expressed willingness to recommend donor milk if safety measures were ensured [22]. This finding aligns with the cautious optimism seen in our study, suggesting that professionals’ acceptance depends strongly on confidence in safety and screening protocols. In contrast, the acceptance rate in the present study was markedly lower than that reported in India (94.29%) [19]. This discrepancy could be linked to contextual differences, as India has a well-established HMBs network, national guidelines, and structured training programs that raise awareness among healthcare providers. Conversely, in Ethiopia, HMBs is not yet implemented, and no standardized policy or professional training exists to inform healthcare professionals about its process and safety assurance. These contextual limitations may explain the relatively lower acceptance rate observed in this setting.

The current study raises concerns about the potential burden that low awareness of HMBs might place on healthcare professionals; hence, in qualitative insights, participants believed HMBs could reduce the workload. This finding is aligned with a study in Ghana, which reported that healthcare providers recognized the efficiency of HMBs in reducing time spent on alternative feeding methods [22]. The current findings in the quantitative study showed that the ethical acceptability of HMBs was supported; however, qualitative interviews revealed underlying cultural and religious reservations. Several Muslim participants expressed that the concept of milk kinship (foster siblinghood) in Islam could complicate acceptance, especially if the identity of donors and recipients is not traceable. Others raised privacy concerns and preferred clearer documentation and transparency regarding milk donors. Similar cultural and religious considerations have been reported in Uganda and South Africa, where family traditions and beliefs around maternal bonding influenced acceptance [23, 35].

Regarding perceived readiness, the current finding highlighted confidence gaps; in qualitative findings, more positive dimensions showed an enthusiasm from experienced professionals, one healthcare professionals stating that “I am fully confident and eager to encourage others to support this initiative” while other healthcare professionals stated his readiness as “I am fully confident, despite the inexperience within our society and healthcare system.”

This study identified healthcare professionals with adequate knowledge were 2.25 times more odds to accept HMBs than their counterparts. This finding is consistent with studies in Malaysia [18], Indonesia [16], and Zimbabwe [17]. The findings highlight the importance of targeted educational intervention to improve knowledge and support for HMBs among healthcare professionals. Educational status has a strong association with acceptability, with those holding MSc/MPH and medical doctors being more likely to accept HMBs than diploma and BSc holders. Studies in Malaysia and Zimbabwe affirm that better knowledge about HMBs correlates with higher acceptability, emphasizing the need to improve awareness among healthcare professionals, particularly those with lower educational backgrounds [17, 18].

Present findings showed that healthcare professionals with a good attitude had a higher acceptance of the concept of HMBs than their counterparts; this finding is in line with a study in Indonesia, attitudes can significantly influence the individual recognition of HMBs’ benefits [16]. In this study, participants who had children were four times more likely to accept HMBs than those not have children. This suggests that parental experience influences perceptions of the importance of breast milk, awareness of breastfeeding challenges, and openness to donor breast milk for infants, which is supported by studies in India [19] and in Malaysia [18].

In this study, participants believed structured training programs and public education campaigns are essential to overcome deficits in knowledge and to correct erroneous cultural beliefs. This finding is consistent with the scoping review, which highlighted the necessity of training to enhance the healthcare professionals’ understanding and increase the acceptance of HMBs [25]. In contrast, the current findings showed that participants expressed worries about disease transmission, safety in screening, and storage methods. This finding is aligned with studies in Kenya and in Zimbabwe, where hygiene fears and concerns about HIV transmission limited the acceptance [17, 24].

In this study, several contextual and perceptual factors influenced healthcare professionals’ views on HMBs. The qualitative findings indicated that most participants expressed initial concerns about the safety of donor milk and the potential risk of disease transmission, reflecting limited exposure to milk banking practices. Similarly, logistical challenges, such as inadequate infrastructure, personnel shortages, and financial constraints, were raised as major barriers in this study. Approximately half of the participants also mentioned concerns about resource constraints, such as the availability of screening equipment and storage facilities, which they believed could affect the feasibility of implementation in public hospitals. This finding is supported by a systematic review study that identified HMBs as a global barrier to implementing HMBs [36]. Despite these concerns, many participants acknowledged that HMBs could reduce the workload of healthcare professionals by minimizing the time spent on preparing alternative feeds for neonates. This perception aligns with findings from Ghana, where health workers similarly viewed milk banking as a means to improve clinical efficiency [22].

The study was conducted in public hospitals in Harari and Dire Dawa, which are major centres for maternal and child health services in Eastern Ethiopia. None of these hospitals has an existing milk banking system, and awareness of HMBs remains limited. These contextual limitations may have contributed to the mixed perceptions observed, as healthcare professionals are still unfamiliar with the operational and ethical dimensions of milk banking. Additionally, the lack of comparable national data limited the ability to contextualize the findings within a broader Ethiopian framework. Despite these limitations, the study provides essential baseline evidence to inform future research, advocacy, and policy development related to HMBs in Ethiopia.

Conclusion

Four in ten of healthcare professionals accepted the concept of HMBs. The acceptability of HMBs was significantly associated to adequate knowledge, a good attitude, having children, attending medical education, and higher educational level holders. Qualitative findings revealed that understanding the nutritional and immunological benefits of breast milk, confidence in healthcare safety protocols, and training opportunities were facilitators of acceptance of HMBs. Cultural and religious beliefs, awareness gaps, and resource constraints were barriers to the acceptance of HMBs. These findings highlight the need for interventional strategies focusing on education, training, and addressing resource concerns to increase the acceptance and successful implementation of HMBs and reduce child morbidity and mortality.
