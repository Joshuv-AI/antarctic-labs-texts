Establishment of a Vancomycin Population Pharmacokinetic Model for Pediatric Patients Based on the Non-Linear Mixed-Effects Model
Authors: Biao Yu, Kangkang Mei, Didi Zhan, Qi Tang, Heping Cai, Runcong Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesVancomycin (VCM) pharmacokinetic parameters vary widely between individuals. Existing models developed domestically and internationally may not apply universally because of differences in patient populations and testing methodologies. Therefore, the present study aimed to address the clinical challenge of optimizing individualized VCM dosing in pediatric patients at the study institution by developing a VCM population pharmacokinetic model and identifying key factors influencing VCM clearance, thereby providing a reference for safe and effective clinical dosing strategies.MethodsData regarding 124 effective VCM concentrations and 100 pediatric patients who received intravenous VCM were retrospectively collected. We used a non-linear mixed-effects model with forward inclusion and backward elimination to create the final VCM population pharmacokinetic model. The model was internally validated using bootstrapping, goodness of fit, and visual predictive check. The Monte Carlo method was used to simulate the range of trough concentrations in pediatric patients with renal insufficiency and normal function under different dosing regimens.ResultsA one-compartment model adequately described the pharmacokinetic behavior of VCM in vivo. The typical values of clearance and volume of distribution were 8.22 L/h and 113 L, respectively. Renal function and body weight were the most important factors affecting the clearance of VCM. The model was validated as stable and reliable.ConclusionsThe VCM population pharmacokinetic model established during this study can assist physicians with the development and optimization of dosing regimens for pediatric patients.

Key Points

The study established a vancomycin population pharmacokinetic model of pediatric patients in the study institution and found that body weight and glomerular filtration rate were important covariates affecting clearance.Compared with the group with normal renal function, the trough concentrations in the mild and moderate renal insufficiency groups increased 0.8-fold and 1.78-fold, respectively, under the 20 mg/kg every 8 h dosing regimen.Under the dosing regimen of 15 mg/kg every 6 h, the trough concentrations were higher in all groups. In both dosing regimens, the trough concentrations in the groups with normal renal function were within the therapeutic window.

Introduction

Vancomycin (VCM) provides excellent antibacterial activity against infections caused by methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus and other pathogens in pediatric patients [1]. Although VCM has been clinically used for more than 60 years, information regarding its clinical use for pediatric patients has been obtained from studies involving adults. However, the body surface areas and extracellular fluid levels of pediatric patients are different from those of adults. Furthermore, pediatric patients have immature renal function compared with that of adults. Therefore, the pharmacokinetic (PK) parameters of VCM for pediatric patients significantly differ from those of adults and have greater inter-individual variability [2]. Studies have shown that the incidence of VCM nephrotoxicity among pediatric patients is approximately 5–20% [3–7]. Additionally, when the trough concentration is more than 15 mg/L, the incidence of nephrotoxicity can be as high as 30–40%. Therefore, maintaining the trough concentration within the therapeutic window is crucial to reducing the occurrence of adverse reactions [8, 9]. Given that VCM has a narrow therapeutic index and large individual variation, the use of a population PK (PPK) model to adjust VCM dosing has been supported by many studies [10]. The PPK model is a practical tool for individualized medicine. It requires limited sampling, which makes it particularly convenient for pediatric patients. Additionally, the model allows estimation of PK parameters by incorporating inter-individual variability and residual unexplained variability. These factors are crucial for optimizing VCM dosing regimens [11].

At many institutions, VCM concentrations are monitored. These data can be applied to estimate PK parameters and establish a PPK model that can be used to adjust the VCM dosage. However, these models cannot be universally applied to all scenarios because of differences in ethnic backgrounds, disease states, and drug monitoring methods of the modeled populations. Therefore, it is necessary to evaluate the applicability of a model to a specific population. During this study, we screened important covariates that affect PK parameters to provide a reference for formulating the initial VCM dosage and used a non-linear mixed-effects model (NONMEM) to establish a VCM PPK model for pediatric patients at the study institution.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

Data of pediatric patients who received intravenous VCM at the Pediatric Department of Anhui Children’s Hospital between September 2021 and November 2023 were retrospectively collected. The inclusion criteria were as follows: age younger than 18 years; confirmed or suspected infection caused by Gram-positive bacteria; intravenous administration of VCM; and at least one available steady-state trough concentration measurement of VCM. Patients were excluded if they received renal replacement therapy or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, had undetectable VCM concentrations (i.e., no numerical results reported by the assay), or had incomplete clinical data (e.g., demographics, pathophysiology). Samples with measurable VCM concentrations below the lower limit of quantification (2 mg/L) were included in the modeling.

Dosing Regimens and Blood Concentration Measurement Methods

Clinicians use empirical initial and adjustment plans based on the patient’s condition and renal function. Vancomycin (0.5 g/vial; Eli Lilly Pharmaceuticals Co., Ltd., Indianapolis, IN, USA or VIANEX S.A., Kifisia, Greece) was dissolved in a 0.9% saline or 5% glucose injection via an intravenous intermittent infusion over the course of 60 min. According to the recommendations of the Expert Consensus on Therapeutic Drug Monitoring in Children, trough blood samples should be collected 30 min before the fourth dose of medication, and 1–2 mL should be collected each time [12]. Accurate drug concentrations in the blood obtained at appropriate collection times were also included in this study. Approximately 2 mL of venous blood was collected with an EDTA anticoagulated blood collection tube and centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 10 min; then, at least 0.3 mL of the supernatant was collected. The drug concentration in plasma was determined using an enzyme amplification immunoassay and a fully automatic biochemical analyser (Viva-ProE; Siemens, Munich, Germany).

Data Analysis

Data Analysis Software

NONMEM (version 7.4.1; Icon Inc., Dublin, Ireland) was used for modeling and simulation. Perl-speaks-NONMEM (PsN version 5.1.2; Uppsala University, Uppsala, Sweden) was used for covariate screening and model validation [13]. R (version 4.3.1) and R Studio (version 2023.09.1) were used for graphing and statistical analyses.

Model Establishment and Analysis

An Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA) data file containing relevant data of 100 pediatric patients was established. NONMEM software was used to fit the compartment model and estimate the PK parameters of VCM.

Basic Model

In this study, retrospectively collected data on the steady-state trough concentrations of VCM from pediatric patients undergoing routine monitoring were used. Therefore, a one-compartment model with first-order elimination (ADVAN1 TRANS2 submodule) was adopted as the PK structural model. The selection criteria was based on the selected structural models. The best model was determined by comparatively analyzing OFV, goodness of fit (GOF), and magnitude of the PK parameters of different random-effects models.

Covariate Model

The covariates examined during this study were classified as continuous or categorical variables. Continuous variables included the eGFR, alanine aminotransferase (ALT, U/L), blood urea nitrogen (mmol/L), and age. The categorical variable was sex. Covariates that were more commonly used clinically or that had the highest correlation with PK parameters were selected for inclusion in the model through base model estimation. NONMEM designates the −2-fold log-likelihood value as the OFV, and it is used as an overall indicator of fit. Similar to the base model, the effects of covariates on PK parameters were examined using a stepwise method.

During the forward inclusion process, the covariates were individually added to the model. Assuming that the test level α = 0.05, if the decrease in OFV exceeded 3.84 (χ2 test; p < 0.05; df = 1) after adding a covariate, then we added the covariate to the model. Otherwise, it was excluded. The process was repeated until the OFV showed no significant change. At that time, a full regression model was established. The covariates in the full regression model were individually eliminated using the one-by-one backward elimination method. The test level was set at a more stringent α = 0.01, and the factor was retained in the model if the change in the OFV was more than 6.63 (χ2 test; p < 0.01; df = 1). The covariates in the model were selected based on the physiological plausibility of the parameter estimates, GOF plots, and statistical significance.

Model Evaluation

The model was evaluated using a combination of diagnostic plots and statistical tests. GOF, visual predictive check (VPC), and non-parametric bootstrapping were used to internally validate the final model. The GOF included the observed concentration versus the population-predicted concentration, the observed concentration versus the individual-predicted concentration, the conditional weighted residuals versus the population-predicted concentration, and the conditional weighted residuals versus the time after the dose. The VPC was used for 200 dataset simulations, and the estimated 5th, 50th, and 95th percentiles of the predicted concentrations obtained from the simulations were plotted against time with the observed concentration. The non-parametric bootstrap method was used to evaluate the robustness of the PPK model.1000 bootstraps were performed. The median and 2.5–97.5th percentile intervals were calculated and compared with the values obtained using NONMEM.

Model Application

Based on the final model, Monte Carlo simulations were performed by dividing the glomerular filtration rate (GFR) into the following three groups: moderate renal insufficiency group, 30–59 mL/min/1.73 m2; mild renal insufficiency group, 60–89 mL/min/1.73 m2; and normal renal function group, ≥90 mL/min/1.73 m2. The GFR was calculated using the Schwartz formula (Eq. 2). The design of the dosing regimen was based on the dose of 60 mg/kg/day recommended by the Guidelines for Drug Monitoring of VCM Therapy in China (2020 update) [referred to as the guidelines hereafter], and the dosing intervals were set at every 6 h or every 8 h [18]. Each scenario was simulated using the Monte Carlo method for 1000 virtual patients (R software and the ONLYSIMULATION and SUBPROBLEMS modules in the $SIMULATION package of NONMEM software). In these simulations, inter-individual variability (ωCL) was included to reflect realistic PK variability across pediatric patients. During our study, a steady-state trough concentration of 5–15 mg/L was selected to determine the optimal initial dosing regimen. Simulations were based on a median patient WT (24 kg), consistent with the study cohort’s central tendency. One thousand simulations were performed using the initial dataset. Subsequently, the steady-state trough concentration value of each simulated subject was calculated.

PPK Model

Basic Model

Most data used for this study were sparsely sampled; therefore, the obtained data were dominated by trough concentrations. By setting different initial parameter values, the CL and V of the model were calculated. Because the estimated value of the inter-individual variability in V was too small, it was omitted in subsequent calculations. This adjustment may help improve model convergence and ensures the stability of parameter estimation. A one-compartment model with first-order elimination (ADVAN1 TRANS2 module) was selected as the base model. The OFV of the base model was 632.217. The CL and V measurements were 7.52 L/h and 127 L, respectively. A comparison of the different residual models revealed that the fit of the proportional model was optimal.

Covariate Model

During this study, adding covariates to the CL resulted in a decrease of more than 3.84 in the OFV. Adding the GFR in an exponential form resulted in the greatest decrease in the OFV. By repeating these steps, the GFR was retained in the model. Then, the GFR was removed in reverse, and the model OFV increased by 52.62, indicating that the GFR had a significant effect on the model and should be retained in the final model (Table 2).

Model Stability

Repeat sampling of the bootstrap procedure was set to 1000, and 997 were performed successfully. The robustness rate was 99.7%. The median parameters that were estimated using the bootstrap procedure were similar to the values of the final model. Furthermore, the 95% confidence intervals were in agreement with the 2.5–97.5% bootstrap estimates, indicating that the estimates of the PPK parameters in the final model were precise and that the model was stable [19]. Predictive Capability of the Model

The VPC plot was used to validate the final model (Fig. 2). Overall, the 90% prediction interval effectively covered most of the observed VCM concentrations. At the median (50th percentile) and the 5th percentile, the observed data points were highly consistent with the simulated median and prediction intervals, showing similar distribution patterns. However, a significant deviation occurred at the 95th percentile: the observed values in this upper-tail region more frequently fell outside the 90% prediction interval. These wide prediction intervals can be attributed to sparse sampling and the use of a fixed residual error, which collectively limit the model’s precision in estimating high concentration values. Because of the limited number of samples in this range, the model struggled to fully characterize the variability of extreme values, thus leading to the observed mismatch. In the distributions of the median and the fifth percentile, no systematic time-dependent trends were detected, and their consistency with the simulated intervals remained stable throughout the time course, which indicated that the model had reasonable predictive power in typical and lower-tail exposure scenarios.

Predicting the Distribution of Trough Concentrations for Empirical Dosing Regimens

Under the condition of decreased renal function, the trough concentrations of patients exceeded the upper limit of the target concentration range when the drug was administered at 20 mg/kg every 8 h or 15 mg/kg every 6 h, as recommended by the guidelines [18]. The trough concentration was higher with the dosing regimen of 15 mg/kg every 6 h. The results of the Monte Carlo method suggested that it was necessary to formulate an individualized VCM dosing regimen based on the model to improve the treatment target rate in clinical practice (Table 4 and Fig. 3).

Discussion

We examined the VCM PPK profile for pediatric patients at the study institution. The structural model was exponential and the random-effects model was proportional. Endogenous creatinine, which was calculated using the Cockcroft–Gault formula, was an important indicator of renal function and had been used to estimate VCM doses for adults. However, because of the differences in muscle mass and tubular secretion of creatinine between children and adults, the Cockcroft–Gault formula is not applicable to children [20]. Therefore, the Schwartz formula was used to calculate the GFR, which was used to evaluate the renal function index.

Compared with the one-compartment model, the two-compartment model was better able to characterize the PK profile of VCM [21]. However, we chose the one-compartment model because the results of the two-compartment model did not satisfactorily fit the structural and random-effects models. Because of sparse sampling, predominantly of trough concentrations, estimation of V and intercompartmental parameters was limited. This constrained the feasibility of a two-compartment model, which might otherwise better reflect VCM pharmacokinetics in a certain pediatric subpopulation. The decision to fix V was both a pragmatic choice based on data characteristics and a necessary measure to enhance the reliability of model fitting. We estimated V from our own data rather than fixing it to literature values, as our pediatric cohort had unique physiology that can markedly alter V. Published V values derive from heterogeneous or disease-specific pediatric groups and may not represent our patients. We acknowledge that the literature we surveyed was limited and we did not formally compare the two approaches.

Regarding the fixing of residual variability, our main consideration stemmed from the limitation of the sample size in this study. During the initial stage of model development, we attempted to freely estimate the residual variability. However, the results of multiple iterations showed that the estimated values were poorly stable, and the model failed to converge properly. Therefore, we ultimately chose to fix the residual variability to prioritize ensuring the stability of the estimation of core parameters such as CL. Instead of directly using literature values, we conducted a systematic sensitivity analysis by testing three fixed residual error values (0.2, 0.3, and 0.4). Results showed that Omega, which reflects inter-individual variability, fluctuated minimally (85.7–80.3%) with stable relative standard errors (11–12%) and shrinkage values within a narrow range (2–6%), indicating insensitivity to fixed residual error values. For the key parameter CL, population typical values increased slightly (7.13–8.03) while relative standard errors remained controllable (11–10%). After comprehensive comparisons, the residual error was ultimately fixed at 0.3. This approach aimed to avoid undermining the robustness of the entire model due to unreliable estimation of residual variability. A similar approach was used in another small-sample study [22]. Other literature [23] also noted that fixing residuals may improve model stability with sparse data. Nevertheless, this method might to some extent reduce the model’s ability to capture random errors in the data and fail to fully reflect the specificity of the data in this study.

We examined the effects of covariates such as sex, age, WT, alanine aminotransferase, blood urea nitrogen, and GFR on the PK behaviour of VCM and found that WT had a significant impact on CL and V, consistent with previous studies [24]. In agreement with the findings of most previous studies [25], GFR was also an important factor that influenced CL in this study, possibly because VCM was mainly cleared through the kidneys. The typical population value of CL in the final model was 8.22 L/h. Based on a WT of 70 kg, the CL was approximately 0.117 L/h/kg, which was in agreement with that (0.071–0.125 L/h/kg) reported in the international literature [26].

During our study, the model was evaluated using GOF, bootstrapping, and VPC. The VPC suggested that the observed and simulated data had similar distribution characteristics at the median and fifth percentile. However, the distributions differed more at the 95th percentile. Clinicians should exercise caution when relying on predicted concentrations exceeding 15 mg/L, as these values stem from limited high-concentration samples and may be disproportionately influenced by sparse data and fixed residual errors [27]. Specifically, because of the scarcity of supporting data for concentrations above 20 mg/L, where prediction uncertainty increases significantly, the model is not recommended for predicting concentrations within this range.

Before using the final model for simulations, we selected therapeutic drug monitoring targets. Currently, there is no consensus regarding the correlation between the area under the concentration-time curve (AUC) and trough concentration [28, 29]. The prevailing view was that trough concentrations were associated with the development of nephrotoxicity, whereas the AUC was often used to predict clinical efficacy [30, 31]. Although the new version of the American Society of Health-System Pharmacists therapeutic drug monitoring guidelines (2020) recommends monitoring the 24-h concentration–time AUC [32], steady-state trough concentrations were often used as a surrogate measure in clinical practice because pharmacists and physicians had little knowledge of methods used to estimate the patient AUC, such as the Bayesian estimation or first-order PK formula methods. To enhance feasibility, the guidelines [18] recommend monitoring trough concentrations or 24-h concentration–time AUC. However, the guidelines do not specify a target range for the AUC of children. Therefore, we set a VCM trough concentration of 5–15 mg/L as our target.

During this study, the virtual population was divided into the normal renal function group (eGFR ≥90 mL/min/1.73 m2), the mild renal insufficiency group (eGFR: 60–89 mL/min/1.73 m2), and the moderate renal insufficiency group (eGFR: 30–59 mL/min/1.73 m2). Children with an eGFR ≤30 mL/min/1.73 m2 were excluded from the study because they often received renal replacement therapy. The dosing regimen was based on the guideline recommendations [18]. The final model was used to estimate the exposure of VCM trough concentrations with different renal function statuses and dosing regimens based on the Monte Carlo method. The simulations in this study were performed using a typical patient with a median WT of 24 kg, chosen to represent a clinically relevant WT stratum within the pediatric population and to enable a clear visualization of concentration profiles across different dosage regimens. However, it was important to acknowledge that this approach may, to some extent, restrict the direct extrapolation of results to patients with extreme WTs. Accordingly, dosage adjustments in clinical practice should be individualized based on each patient’s actual WT.

The simulation results showed that with the 20mg/kg every 8 h dosing regimen, the trough concentrations exceeded the therapeutic window of the mild and moderate renal insufficiency groups and were significantly higher than those of the normal renal function group. Compared with those of the normal renal function group, the trough concentrations of the mild and moderate renal insufficiency groups increased by 0.8-fold and 1.78-fold, respectively. With the 15mg/kg every 6 h dosing regimen, each group exhibited higher trough concentrations; this may have been attributable to the increased dosing frequency. The trough concentrations of the mild and moderate renal insufficiency groups increased by 0.83-fold and 1.67-fold, respectively, compared with those of the normal renal function group. With the two different dosing regimens, the trough concentrations of the normal renal function group were within the therapeutic window.

The results of this study suggested that when clinicians at the study institution referred to the guidelines [18] to determine appropriate clinical treatment, they may need to reduce the dose for patients with renal insufficiency to decrease the risk of adverse events caused by high trough concentrations. For example, in the moderate renal insufficiency group, when the original dose was 20 mg/kg every 8 h, we suggested adjusting it to 15 mg/kg every 12 h; for the 15mg/kg every 6 h regimen, we recommend 10 mg/kg every 8 h. However, our study did not investigate the probability of target attainment of different dosing regimens in different renal function groups. In the future, as the sample size increases, we will conduct further research based on this study. At that time, we will provide practical dose adjustment strategies for populations with different renal functions through a simulation.

Based on the PPK model established, we plan to further develop a Bayesian dose optimization tool combined with therapeutic drug monitoring data. This tool is expected to achieve more accurate individualized dose adjustments by integrating patient-specific covariates (such as WT and renal function) and real-time drug concentration data, thereby improving the effectiveness and safety of VCM therapy in pediatric patients. We believe that the application of such tools will be an important direction for future clinical practice.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size is relatively small. The limitation is the absence of external validation using an independent dataset, which may limit the generalizability of our model to other clinical settings. Secondly, the factors influencing the PK behavior of VCM had not been comprehensively investigated. Future studies with larger sample sizes and more extensive data collection are warranted to further explore these factors and validate the model. Thirdly, because of the retrospective nature of this study, some bias in the accuracy of the sampling times is possible. Fourthly, most data in this study consisted of single steady-state trough concentrations, with limited high-concentration samples. This might have introduced deviations in the model’s prediction of extreme concentration ranges. Notably, fixing residual variability impairs the model’s ability to capture random errors and reflect data specificity, hindering adaptation to scenario-specific random error characteristics. Combined with a small sample size, it widens parameter confidence intervals, restricting broad applicability across clinical contexts. Nevertheless, these limitations did not significantly affect the calculation of CL and screening of influencing factors [33]. Additionally, only the PK profile of VCM was examined during this study. Future studies should combine bacteriological examinations and clinical regression of pediatric patients to establish a PK–pharmacodynamic linkage model that could provide a better clinical basis for drug administration.

Conclusions

The VCM PPK model developed for pediatric patients at the study institution identified actual WT and GFR as important covariates that explain inter-individual PK variability of this specific population. This model can support clinicians in tailoring VCM therapy based on WT and renal function. With integration into therapeutic drug monitoring systems or Bayesian software platforms, it may further facilitate precision dosing in pediatric care. Nevertheless, significant external validation will be required before clinical implementation.
