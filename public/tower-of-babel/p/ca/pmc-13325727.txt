Pediatric urethral obstruction caused by Cowper's duct syringocele diagnosed and treated endoscopically: a case report
Authors: Rifang Pan, Zhaowei Zhou, Junzhong Li, Zhaopan Li, Zhihua Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cowper's duct syringocele is an uncommon and often overlooked cause of urethral obstruction in children. We report a 7-year-old boy who had experienced severe voiding difficulty, frequency, and urgency for three months and could void only while sitting. Ultrasonography, computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging, and voiding cystourethrography were nondiagnostic. Cystourethroscopy revealed a prominent posterior urethral crest and verumontanum, two ectopic pinpoint ostia in the bulbous urethra, and a blind membranous pouch. Incision of the bulbous ostia released abundant proteinaceous material, relieved the obstruction, and improved postoperative uroflowmetry. Syringocele should be considered in boys with unexplained infravesical obstruction.

Introduction

Cowper's glands are paired accessory glands located in the deep perineal space, with ducts that typically open into the bulbous urethra.1,2 They secrete an alkaline mucus that lubricates the urethra and helps neutralize residual urinary acidity.1 A syringocele is a cystic dilatation of the Cowper duct and may be congenital or acquired.3,4 Although once considered rare, it is increasingly recognized in both children and adults.1,5

Clinical presentation depends on whether the lesion behaves as an open or closed obstruction. Open lesions are more often associated with post-void dribbling, discharge, or hematuria, whereas closed lesions more commonly produce obstructive symptoms, urinary retention, or recurrent infection.4,5 Because these symptoms overlap with those of posterior urethral valves, urethral diverticulum, dysfunctional voiding, and other causes of infravesical obstruction, diagnosis may be delayed5, 6, 7.

We report a 7-year-old boy with severe voiding difficulty in whom ultrasonography, cross-sectional imaging, and voiding cystourethrography were unrevealing. The diagnosis was established only after endoscopic exploration, which also provided the cure.

Case presentation

A 7-year-old boy presented with a >3-month history of difficulty urinating. He could only void while sitting; standing and squatting were not possible. He also reported urinary frequency and urgency, without dysuria, gross hematuria, fever, or perineal pain. Physical examination, including abdominal assessment, renal angle percussion, and inspection of the external genitalia, was unremarkable.

Initial investigations included urinalysis, urinary tract ultrasonography, computed tomography, cranial and lumbosacral magnetic resonance imaging, and psychological assessment. None identified a structural, neurologic, or behavioral cause for the severity of symptoms. Because the child remained severely symptomatic, cystourethroscopy was performed under anesthesia. Two pinpoint ostia, each approximately 0.1 cm, were identified at the 12 o'clock and 3 o'clock positions in the bulbous urethra. A diverticulum-like opening was also noted in the membranous urethra. The posterior urethral crest and verumontanum appeared prominently elevated, causing functional obstruction, and mild bladder trabeculation was observed. The membranous diverticulum-like opening was first incised with a cold knife, revealing a blind-ending pouch with no improvement in outflow. The two bulbous urethral ostia were then incised. Abundant proteinaceous, plug-like flocculent material was released immediately, the obstructive bulging subsided, and urethral patency was restored (Fig. 3D). After the procedure, the patient voided smoothly in both supine and standing positions, and the family reported obvious early symptomatic relief. Postoperative uroflowmetry demonstrated marked improvement (Fig. 4), with a waiting time before voiding of 6 s, voiding time of 12 s, flow time of 12 s, time to maximum flow of 9 s, Qmax of 11 mL/s, Qave of 8 mL/s, a 2-s flow rate of 6 mL/s, and a voided volume of 101 mL (Table 1).Fig. 4Postoperative uroflowmetry curve showing improved voiding dynamics.

Discussion

This case highlights an important but easily overlooked cause of pediatric infravesical obstruction. Although open syringoceles are often associated with post-void dribbling, children with obstructing lesions may instead present with a weak stream, straining, urinary retention, recurrent urinary tract infections, or upper tract dilatation4, 5, 6,8,9. Our patient presented with severe obstructive symptoms and markedly poor preoperative uroflowmetry, although routine imaging failed to identify the underlying cause. The clinical improvement following endoscopic treatment was corroborated by objective postoperative uroflowmetry, with Qmax rising from 4 to 11 mL/s, Qave from 1 to 8 mL/s, and voided volume from 25 to 101 mL.

The diagnostic difficulty in this case aligns with prior reports. Retrograde or voiding urethrography may demonstrate communicating lesions, whereas closed lesions can appear merely as a smooth extrinsic impression on the bulbous urethra or necessitate magnetic resonance imaging for more precise morphologic evaluation5,7,10, 11, 12. Even so, imaging may remain nondiagnostic when the lesion is small, decompresses intermittently, or is obscured by complex urethral anatomy. When clinical suspicion persists, cystourethroscopy remains the most definitive test, as it enables direct visualization and immediate treatment.4,5,12

The endoscopic sequence in our patient proved particularly instructive. The blind-ending membranous pouch initially appeared to be the likely culprit, but incising this lesion did not improve outflow. Relief occurred only after unroofing the two subtle bulbous urethral ostia, which released abundant inspissated proteinaceous material. This pattern strongly supports obstruction due to a Cowper duct syringocele rather than an isolated membranous diverticulum. From a practical standpoint, careful inspection of the bulbous urethra for tiny ostia, translucent protrusions, or abnormal ventral openings is essential in boys with unexplained obstruction.

The anatomy also supports the diagnosis. Cowper ducts traverse the deep perineal tissues and typically empty into the bulbous urethra near the midline.1,2 Maizels et al. originally classified syringoceles as simple, perforate, imperforate, or ruptured.3 Later classifications were simplified into open versus closed, or nonobstructing versus obstructing, categories that better align with clinical presentation and treatment planning.4,5,8 Given the marked obstruction and decompression following incision, our case most closely resembled an obstructing lesion with ductal plugging.

Published pediatric series suggest that syringocele is more often underdiagnosed than truly rare. Campobasso et al. described 15 consecutive pediatric cases and highlighted the distinction between nonobstructing and obstructing lesions.8 In the largest pediatric cohort, Wagemans et al. reported 122 patients and found that urinary tract infection and obstructive voiding symptoms were the most common presentations; transurethral incision was effective, though repeat treatment was occasionally needed.9 These findings support minimally invasive endoscopic treatment as the first-line approach for symptomatic lesions.

This report has limitations. It represents a single case with only early postoperative follow-up, and the long-term durability of the uroflow metric improvement remains to be established. Histopathologic confirmation was also not obtained, as the lesion was managed endoscopically. Nonetheless, the case retains clinical value by illustrating a diagnostically challenging yet readily treatable cause of urethral obstruction in children.

Conclusion

Cowper's duct syringocele should be considered in the differential diagnosis of boys with unexplained infravesical obstruction, especially when symptoms are severe but standard imaging yields no clear findings. Careful cystourethroscopic examination of the bulbous urethra is essential. In this patient, endoscopic incision of abnormal ostia in the bulbous urethra released proteinaceous plugs and provided immediate relief of obstruction.
