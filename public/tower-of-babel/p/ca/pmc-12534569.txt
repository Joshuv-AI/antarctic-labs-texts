HSP90/PUS7/THUMPD1 promotes metastasis and cisplatin resistance in gastric cancer cells
Authors: Ziwei Ye, Junjie He, Renjie Zuo, Chenchun Ding, Zhenzhen Guo, Quan Liao, Xuan Zhu, Guoyan Liu, Li Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe metastasis and chemotherapy resistance of gastric cancer significantly contribute to treatment failure and mortality. Based on previous studies, we hypothesized that Heat Shock Protein 90 (HSP90) interacts with and upregulates Pseudouridine Synthase 7 (PUS7), which in turn promotes THUMP Domain Containing 1 (THUMPD1) expression, driving gastric cancer progression and drug resistance. Differential gene expression analysis revealed that HSP90, PUS7, and THUMPD1 are overexpressed in multiple tumor types and positively correlate with tumor mutational burden (TMB) and microsatellite instability (MSI). Mechanistically, HSP90 interacts with PUS7 to regulate THUMPD1 expression, enhancing tumor cell proliferation, migration, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), angiogenesis, and cisplatin resistance. Functional inhibition of HSP90 and THUMPD1 suppressed these oncogenic processes, while PUS7 overexpression exacerbated them. These findings highlight the HSP90/PUS7/THUMPD1 axis as a critical regulator of gastric cancer progression and a potential therapeutic target.Keywords: Gastric cancer, Mechanism, HSP90, PUS7, THUMPD1Subject terms: Cancer, Cell biology, Gastroenterology, Oncology, Pathogenesis

Introduction

Gastric cancer is a common and aggressive malignant tumor of the digestive tract, particularly prevalent in China, where it accounts for 23.02% of all cancer-related deaths1,2. Early-stage gastric cancer often presents with subtle symptoms, resulting in a diagnosis and treatment rate of less than 10%3, with approximately 80% of cases diagnosed at advanced stages, leading to poor prognoses4. Peritoneal metastasis significantly contributes to mortality in advanced gastric cancer, limiting surgical options and yielding a one-year survival rate of only 22.2%.5,6. Current treatments for peritoneal metastasis include chemotherapy, surgery, intraperitoneal chemotherapy, hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy, and radiotherapy, which may extend survival but face challenges due to tumor cell resistance to chemotherapy and decreased sensitivity7–9. Therefore, investigating the mechanisms of peritoneal metastasis and chemoresistance, along with identifying novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets, is essential for improving patient outcomes.

HSP90, an ATP-dependent molecular chaperone, is upregulated in gastric cancer and correlates with tumor growth, lymph node metastasis, and poor survival10. Elevated plasma HSP90 levels are associated with reduced short-term efficacy of platinum- and fluorouracil-based chemotherapy, with further increases observed after treatment failure11. Inhibition of HSP90 degradation promotes proliferation, migration, and invasion, underscoring its role in gastric cancer progression and resistance12. Importantly, HSP90 inhibitors show antitumor activity in multiple malignancies, including gastric and colon cancers, highlighting their therapeutic potential13,14.

PUS7, a pseudouridine synthase, regulates RNA stability and translation. It enhances ALKBH3 translation via mRNA pseudouridylation, thereby promoting gastric tumorigenesis15. High PUS7 expression also drives progression of non-small cell lung cancer and colorectal cancer through stabilization of oncogenic transcripts and activation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling16,17. Conversely, PUS7 inhibition suppresses glioblastoma and prolongs survival in vivo18. HSP90 interacts with PUS7, and PUS7 overexpression counteracts the anti-metastatic effects of HSP90 inhibition in colon cancer, suggesting that PUS7 mediates HSP90-driven oncogenesis19,20.

THUMPD1, a THUMP domain-containing RNA-binding protein, partners with NAT10 to mediate N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C) modification, enhancing RNA stability, export, and translation. Dysregulated THUMPD1 promotes proliferation, migration, invasion, and EMT. Its upregulation in gastric cancer is linked to chemoresistance, while targeting by miR-873-5p sensitizes cells to adriamycin, fluorouracil, and cisplatin21,22. Notably, PUS7 and THUMPD1 have been associated with MSI and TMB, which may have important predictive and regulatory value in tumor immunity and immunotherapy, potentially serving as biomarkers of response to immune checkpoint blockade. STRING database predictions further indicate a binding relationship between PUS7 and THUMPD1 (Fig. 1j), implying that PUS7 may promote metastasis and chemoresistance by upregulating THUMPD1.

Given the aforementioned findings, it is hypothesized that HSP90 may up-regulate PUS7 by binding to its client protein PUS7, which in turn may further enhance THUMPD1 expression to promote gastric cancer metastasis and chemoresistance. This study aims to explore the interactions among HSP90, PUS7, and THUMPD1, along with their effects and mechanisms on gastric cancer metastasis and chemotherapy resistance through cellular and animal experiments. Successful completion of this project is anticipated to offer a new target and theoretical foundation for the early diagnosis and treatment of gastric cancer.

Methods

Cell culture

The human gastric cancer cell lines MKN-45 and AGS were acquired from Procell Life Science and Technology (Wuhan, China) and validated through STR profiling. MKN-45 and AGS cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) in a humidified incubator set at 37 °C and 5% CO2.

Construction of lentivirus and stable cell lines

Custom-designed lentiviral vectors for overexpression and knockdown were provided by Genepharma (Suzhou, China). The specific shRNA sequences used for HSP90AA1 and THUMPD1 knockdown were as follows: HSP90AA1 shRNA - GTGGAGAAGGAACGTGATAAA and THUMPD1 shRNA - AGTGGAAAGTGGAGCAAATAA. Following transfection at an MOI of 100, the cells were subjected to a 2-week selection process using 15 µg/mL puromycin. Subsequent assays were carried out utilizing the selected cell pools.

Cell viability assay and proliferation assay

Cells from each group were seeded at a density of 5,000 cells per well in a 96-well plate and incubated for a set period. Following incubation, CCK-8 reagent was added and left to incubate for 2.5 h. The optical density (OD) of each well was evaluated using a microplate reader set to 450 nm.

Cell cloning assay

Cells from each experimental group were seeded at a density of 1,000 cells per well in 6-well plates. After 15 days of incubation, cell clone formation was observed. The cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 15 min, and the number of clones was evaluated microscopically to determine the clone formation rate.

Migration and invasion assays

The migration and invasion potential of all cell lines were assessed using 24-well Transwell plates with 8-µm pores. For invasion analysis, inserts were coated with 1 mg/mL Matrigel. The upper compartment was seeded with 6 × 104 cells in serum-free media, while the lower chamber contained 20% FBS as a chemoattractant. In migration assays, the upper compartment used a non-coated membrane with the same cell density. Cells were incubated at 37 °C for a designated time, then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with crystal violet, and counted microscopically at ×100 magnification. All experiments were performed in triplicate to evaluate migration and invasion based on cell count.

Western blot (WB) analysis

Whole-cell lysates were extracted using the RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The protein concentration was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit. Subsequently, proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to a PVDF membrane. A blocking solution consisting of 5% dry milk in TBST was applied for 1 h at room temperature to prevent nonspecific binding. The PVDF membranes were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with specific primary antibodies: HSP90 (cat# 13171-1-AP, 1:2000), THUMPD1 (cat# 14921-1-AP, 1:2000), MMP2 (cat# 10373-2-AP, 1:500), MMP9 (N-terminal) (cat# 10375-2-AP, 1:1000), E-cadherin (cat# 20874-1-AP, 1:20000), N-cadherin (cat# 22018-1-AP, 1:2000), CD34 (cat#14486-1-AP, 1:2000), β-actin (cat# 66009-1-Ig, 1:50000), and GAPDH (cat# 60004-1-Ig, 1:50000) were sourced from Proteinech (Wuhan, China), while PUS7 (cat#ab289857, 1:1000) was obtained from Abcam (Cambridge, USA). Following three washes with TBST, the membranes were treated with an HRP-labeled secondary antibody. Protein bands were visualized and quantified using the Bio-Rad ChemiDoc XRS + Imaging System along with Image Lab (Bio-Rad).

HE staining

The transplanted tumor samples were fixed meticulously in 10% paraformaldehyde for 48 h. Subsequent dehydration involved a series of ethanol solutions at concentrations of 70%, 80%, 95%, and finally 100%. After dehydration, the specimens were infiltrated with molten paraffin wax for embedding into paraffin blocks. Thin sections of 4 μm thickness were precisely cut from the paraffin-embedded blocks using a microtome. Tissue morphology was examined through HE staining, and the stained sections were viewed under a light microscope to enable histopathological analysis.

Immunohistochemical assay

Mouse xenograft tumors were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 μm. Sections were deparaffinized with xylene, rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and antigen retrieval was performed with citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at high temperature. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% H₂O₂, and nonspecific binding was blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibody, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. DAB was used for color development, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining, dehydration, and mounting. Images were captured under a microscope, and positive areas were analyzed using Image J.

Co-immunoprecipitation (CO-IP)

Co-IP assays were conducted using the rProtein A/G IP/Co-IP Kit (Yeasen, China, Cat# 36421ES40) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Total protein lysate was extracted using a non-denaturing lysis buffer. Subsequently, 500 µg of total protein was incubated with 1 µg of primary antibody or IgG overnight at 4 °C. The mixture was then combined with rProtein A/G MagBeads and gently agitated for 1 h at 4 °C. Following this, the beads underwent washing with a buffer, dilution in a 5× loading buffer, and denaturation by boiling for 10 min. The protein samples were subsequently analyzed using the WB technique.

In vivo model

Six-week-old BALB/c nude mice (Laboratory Animal Center, Xiamen University, China) were used. All procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Xiamen University (XMULAC20240197), and all methods were carried out in accordance with relevant regulations and reported following the ARRIVE guidelines. Mice were randomly assigned and subcutaneously injected with 1 × 10^6 MKN-45 or AGS cells. After tumor establishment, the MKN-45 group received cisplatin (2 mg/kg daily), whereas the AGS group was left untreated. Tumor volume was measured every 3 days by investigators blinded to group allocation. On day 30, mice were euthanized by CO2 inhalation followed by cervical dislocation, and tumors (≤ 1.5 cm³) were collected for analysis.

Databases

The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) (https://cancergenome.nih.gov) datasets, and GEPIA (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn) were employed to explore the expression of HSP90, PUS7, and THUMPD1 in human cancer samples in contrast to that in non-malignant tissues. STRING (https://string-db.org) was used to predict the binding relationship between PUS7 and THUMPD1.

Statistical analyses

The data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). To assess differences between groups, a statistical analysis was performed employing a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. In cases where the p-value was less than 0.05 (suggesting that the result is statistically significant with a 95% confidence level), it signified a statistically significant difference between the groups under consideration.

Results

HSP90, PUS7, and THUMPD1 as potential targets for the treatment of gastric cancer

Recent studies have highlighted the roles of molecular chaperones and RNA-modifying enzymes in tumor biology, with HSP90, PUS7, and THUMPD1 identified as key molecules. Our team performed differential gene expression analysis of HSP90AA1, PUS7, and THUMPD1 across various cancer types using TCGA and GEPIA, revealing significant upregulation in multiple tumors, including gastric cancer (Fig. 1a-c). Further analysis showed a significant positive correlation between the expression levels of these genes and TMB (Fig. 1d-f) as well as MSI (Fig. 1g-i). This association suggests that these genes may play crucial roles in tumorigenesis, progression, and acquired resistance by affecting microsatellite stability and genomic integrity. Additionally, increased TMB often correlates with a higher tumor antigenic load, potentially enhancing sensitivity to immune checkpoint inhibitors. Therefore, HSP90AA1, PUS7, and THUMPD1 are promising therapeutic targets for gastric cancer.

HSP90 interacts with PUS7 to regulate its abundance, while PUS7 binds to THUMPD1 to modulate its levels in gastric cancer cells

Immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry (IP-MS) has validated the binding and interaction between HSP90 and PUS7 in colon cancer cells19. Furthermore, predictions from the STRING website indicate potential interaction between PUS7 and THUMPD1 (Fig. 1j). To validate the interactions among HSP90, PUS7, and THUMPD1, endogenous protein CO-IP were conducted. Results demonstrate the presence of binding relationships between HSP90 and PUS7, as well as between PUS7 and THUMPD1, compared to IgG (Fig. 2a). Subsequently, we knocked down HSP90 and THUMPD1 in MKN-45 and AGS cells and overexpressed PUS7 (Fig. 2b, c), using WB to detect the regulatory relationships among these three proteins. We found that a decrease in HSP90 levels significantly downregulated the expression of PUS7 and THUMPD1 at the protein level. Conversely, overexpression of PUS7 noticeably increased the abundance of THUMPD1 without affecting HSP90. The absence of THUMPD1 had no significant effect on the expression levels of HSP90 and PUS7 (Fig. 2d, e). Overall, PUS7 may act as a downstream effector of HSP90, while THUMPD1 may serve as a downstream effector of PUS7.

The original blots are presented in Supplementary Material 1. All the data are shown as the mean ± s. d. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001.

HSP90/PUS7/THUMPD1 axis promotes gastric cancer cell proliferation

To elucidate the biological roles of HSP90, PUS7, and THUMPD1, we conducted experiments focused on cell proliferation and colony formation. The results indicated that decreased expression of HSP90 and THUMPD1 inhibited cell proliferation in MKN-45 and AGS cells, whereas the overexpression of PUS7 enhanced this proliferation. Subsequent rescue experiments demonstrated that overexpression of PUS7 could counteract the proliferation inhibition induced by HSP90 deficiency, while the absence of THUMPD1 diminished the proliferation-promoting effect of PUS7 (Fig. 3a-c). These findings suggest that HSP90, PUS7, and THUMPD1 work synergistically to promote the proliferation of gastric cancer cells.

Effect of HSP90/PUS7/THUMPD1 axis on cell proliferation. a-c The effects of HSP90/PUS7/THUMPD1 on the proliferation of MKN-45 and AGS cells were assessed using plate cloning and cell viability assays. All the data are shown as the mean ± s. d. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001.

HSP90/PUS7/THUMPD1 axis promotes migration, invasion and Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) of gastric cancer cells

To investigate the regulatory role of the HSP90/PUS7/THUMPD1 axis in cell migration, invasion, and EMT, we conducted in vitro experiments on migration and invasion (Fig. 4a, b). Expression levels of MMP9, MMP2, N-cadherin, and E-cadherin in MLN-45 and AGS cells were also examined (Fig. 5a, b). Results suggest that HSP90 and THUMPD1 suppress cell migration and invasion, leading to decreased levels of MMP9, MMP2, and N-cadherin, but increased E-cadherin expression. Conversely, PUS7 overexpression displays an opposing trend. Rescue experiments reveal that PUS7 overexpression alleviates the inhibition of cell migration, invasion, and EMT caused by HSP90 deficiency, while THUMPD1 deficiency mitigates the promotion of cell migration, invasion, and EMT induced by PUS7 overexpression. These findings indicate that the HSP90/PUS7/THUMPD1 axis facilitates migration, invasion, and EMT in gastric cancer cells.

All the data are shown as the mean ± s. d. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001.

The original blots are presented in Supplementary Material 1. All the data are shown as the mean ± s. d. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001.

HSP90/PUS7/THUMPD1 axis promotes angiogenesis and cisplatin resistance

We inoculated MKN-45 and AGS cells subcutaneously into mice to establish transplantation tumors and assessed the expression levels of HSP90, PUS7, THUMPD1, MMP9, MMP2, N-cadherin, and E-cadherin in these tumors (Fig. 6a-d). The results demonstrated that HSP90 and THUMPD1 were effectively knocked down, while PUS7 was successfully overexpressed. Meanwhile, the regulatory relationships among these three proteins and their effects on EMT are consistent with previous experimental findings. To ascertain whether the HSP90/PUS7/THUMPD1 axis is associated with angiogenesis, we employed WB and immunohistochemistry to evaluate the expression level of CD34 in the transplantation tumors derived from AGS cells (Fig. 7a-d). The findings indicated that the absence of HSP90 and THUMPD1 inhibited CD34 expression, whereas PUS7 overexpression promoted it. Moreover, PUS7 overexpression could counteract the inhibition of CD34 expression caused by HSP90 deficiency, while THUMPD1 deficiency diminished the promotion of CD34 expression induced by PUS7 overexpression. Furthermore, to explore whether the HSP90/PUS7/THUMPD1 axis affects cellular resistance to cisplatin, we conducted cell proliferation assays to assess the cytotoxic effects of various cisplatin concentrations (Fig. 7e). The results indicated that the IC50 values for the different cell groups were 2.758, 5.063, 1.380, 1.016, 2.695, and 2.186 µg/mL. In transplantation tumors inoculated with MKN-45 cells and treated with cisplatin, the tumor volume in the HSP90 and THUMPD1 deficiency group significantly decreased, accompanied by extensive necrosis of tumor cells. In contrast, the tumor volume in the PUS7 overexpression group significantly increased, with a marked reduction in tumor cell necrosis. Rescue experiments revealed that PUS7 overexpression alleviated the growth inhibition and cell death resulting from HSP90 deficiency, while THUMPD1 deficiency mitigated the proliferation enhancement and cisplatin resistance driven by PUS7 overexpression (Fig. 7f-i). These results indicate that the HSP90/PUS7/THUMPD1 axis plays a crucial role in both promoting angiogenesis and facilitating cisplatin resistance.

Discussion

Gastric cancer is characterized by strong invasiveness and metastasis, leading to high incidence and mortality rates that pose a significant global burden. EMT plays a critical role in promoting tumor metastasis and progression. Tumor cells initially detach from the primary tumor by reducing their adhesiveness, degrading the extracellular matrix (ECM), and penetrating the basement membrane. Subsequently, these cells enter the bloodstream, target distant organs, and achieve metastasis through angiogenesis. EMT, matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), and angiogenesis are essential components of this process23–27.

This study, for the first time, highlights the pivotal role of the HSP90/PUS7/THUMPD1 axis in the proliferation, metastasis, angiogenesis, and drug resistance of gastric cancer cells, while also exploring its potential regulatory mechanisms. We found that HSP90 regulates PUS7 abundance through direct interaction, while PUS7 subsequently interacts with THUMPD1, affecting its protein levels. In gastric cancer cells, the knockdown of HSP90 or THUMPD1 significantly inhibited cell proliferation, migration, invasion, EMT, angiogenesis, and cisplatin resistance, whereas PUS7 overexpression markedly promoted these malignant biological behaviors. These results suggest that HSP90 may mediate its oncogenic effects through PUS7, which further extends its signaling via THUMPD1, thereby driving the progression of gastric cancer.

HSP90 has long been recognized for stabilizing oncogenic proteins, including ER, HER2, EGFR, and KRAS, thereby supporting tumor growth and therapy resistance across cancer types28–31. This study confirms that HSP90 impacts the phenotype of gastric cancer cells by regulating PUS7 and THUMPD1, indicating that HSP90 may not only function as a molecular chaperone but also influence RNA modification processes, thereby regulating tumor progression, although the mechanism by which it affects RNA modification remains to be further elucidated.

This study demonstrates that overexpression of PUS7 significantly upregulates THUMPD1, while HSP90 knockdown reduces the expression levels of both PUS7 and THUMPD1, suggesting that PUS7 may serve as a downstream effector of HSP90 in promoting cancer. Furthermore, the absence of THUMPD1 does not significantly affect the expression of PUS7 and HSP90, further supporting the hypothesis that THUMPD1 resides downstream of the HSP90/PUS7 axis and may facilitate gastric cancer progression through RNA modification pathways. The interaction between PUS7 and THUMPD1 may involve RNA modifications such as pseudouridylation and N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C), thereby influencing RNA stability, splicing, and translation efficiency, which provides a mechanistic explanation for their role in driving gastric cancer malignancy and chemoresistance.

Notably, this study also discovered that the activation of the HSP90/PUS7/THUMPD1 axis enhances cisplatin resistance in gastric cancer cells. Cisplatin, a first-line chemotherapy drug for gastric cancer, has its efficacy severely limited by drug resistance32,33. This study demonstrates that knocking down HSP90 or THUMPD1 increases cells’ sensitivity to cisplatin, while PUS7 overexpression promotes drug resistance. This suggests that dual targeting of HSP90 and downstream components may overcome resistance. Future studies should explore combining HSP90 inhibitors with cisplatin to achieve synergistic effects.

In conclusion, this study elucidates the critical role of the HSP90/PUS7/THUMPD1 axis in gastric cancer progression and confirms its significant regulatory functions in cell proliferation, metastasis, EMT, angiogenesis, and drug resistance. These findings not only enhance our understanding of gastric cancer development but also provide a theoretical basis for the HSP90/PUS7/THUMPD1 axis as a potential therapeutic target. However, this study has certain limitations, including the need for further investigation into the specific mechanisms of action of this signaling axis, particularly the roles of PUS7 and THUMPD1-mediated RNA modifications in gastric cancer. Additionally, validation of this axis in large human gastric cancer cohorts and the exploration of specific inhibitors targeting PUS7 and THUMPD1 will be critical for clinical translation. Future research should integrate RNA methylation modifications, single-cell sequencing, and clinical big data analyses to thoroughly investigate the functional mechanisms of this signaling axis and explore its potential applications in precision therapy. Moreover, given the limited research on THUMPD1, it is necessary to further explore its potential context-dependent functions in different tumor types in future studies.

Conclusions

This study confirmed through cell and animal experiments that HSP90 can upregulate the expression of its client protein PUS7 by binding to it. PUS7 further induces gastric cancer metastasis and chemoresistance by promoting the expression of THUMPD1, aiming to provide important targets and theoretical basis for early diagnosis, treatment, and intervention of chemoresistance in clinical gastric cancer.
