Recent Synthetic Advances in C–H/N–H Functionalization of 1H‐Pyrazoles: Diverse Strategies Across Variously Substituted Scaffolds
Authors: Karolina Dzedulionytė Müldür, Asta Žukauskaitė, Algirdas Šačkus, Eglė Arbačiauskienė
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This review briefly and systematically overviews C–H and N–H functionalization reactions of pyrazoles, aimed at creating new C—C and C—heteroatom bonds on the pyrazole ring. It discusses various strategies, including traditional cross‐coupling reactions that necessitate prefunctionalized pyrazoles as well as direct functionalization reactions, which offer a more efficient approach to obtaining a diverse array of functionalized derivatives in only one step.

Introduction

Pyrazoles are common scaffolds in organic and medicinal chemistry, often used as starting materials for the preparation of more complex molecular systems with relevance in the pharmaceutical field. Numerous pyrazole derivatives possess various biological activities, for example, antibacterial,[ 1 ] antitubercular,[ 2 ] anticancer,[ 3 ] antiproliferative,[ 4 ] or antidiabetic.[ 5 ] These heterocyclic compounds have gained significant importance in academia and the pharmaceutical industry, where pyrazole containing molecules are employed as approved drugs or are currently under investigation for extended applications (Figure 1 ).[ 6 ] Broad relevance of pyrazoles has accelerated the development of diverse synthetic approaches to these compounds.[ 7 , 8 , 9 ]

Approved pyrazole‐containing drug molecules.

Pyrazoles are electron‐rich five‐membered heterocyclic systems comprising two vicinal N atoms – an acidic pyrrole‐like N atom with a lone electron pair conjugated with the aromatic system and a basic sp2‐hybridized pyridine‐like N atom (Figure 2a). Pyrazole can exhibit both acidic and basic properties; moreover, N‐1, N‐2, and C‐4 positions of the ring are nucleophilic, whereas C‐3 and C‐5 positions are electrophilic; therefore, electrophilic substitution is expected to preferably occur at the 4‐position, whereas nucleophilic attack takes place at the 3‐ or 5‐position of the pyrazole ring (Figure 2b). Sizes and electron densities of the substituents in the pyrazole ring can potentially exert substantial influence on the reaction regiochemistry and should also be considered.[ 10 ]

Highlights of pyrazole structure: a) properties of N atoms and b) nucleophilic and electrophilic positions of the ring.

To prepare structurally diverse pyrazoles, several strategies can be utilized depending on the synthetic goal. Desired substitution pattern can be achieved by synthesizing the pyrazole building block through various cyclization reactions.[ 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 ] Another pathway involves late‐stage C−H functionalizations, using either prefunctionalization or direct on‐site reactivity.[ 16 ] Functionalization of pyrazoles can afford mixtures of C‐5‐, C‐4‐, or C‐3‐substituted products, which can be problematic due to difficult separation of these products, low atom economies of these reactions, and production of undesired side products in large amounts.

In the case of N‐functionalization of substituted pyrazoles, regioisomeric mixtures can be obtained owing to prototropic tautomerism.[ 17 , 18 , 19 ] Hence, the development of regioselective reactions of pyrazoles remains a persistent challenge, and directing the reaction equilibrium toward the desired regioisomer often requires considerable efforts.[ 20 , 21 ] This review covers pyrazole functionalization reactions at various positions for the selective formation of new C—C and C—heteroatom, and N—C bonds on the pyrazole ring.

C−H Functionalization of Pyrazoles at C‐5 Position

Presence of electronegative adjacent N atoms reduces electron densities at the C‐3 and C‐5 positions of the pyrazole ring. Electrophilic attack preferentially occurs at the C‐4 position. Although the electrophilic nature of C‐5 of pyrazole increases reactivity at this position, regioselectivity issues between the C‐3 and C‐5 positions may still occur due to similar dissociation energies of these positions.[ 22 ]

To overcome the regioselectivity issues, various strategies can be applied, including transition‐metal catalysis and variation of ligands, solvents, or other reaction additives. Protecting, directing, or functional groups can also be introduced into specific positions of the ring. Consequently, effective functionalization at the C‐5 position of the pyrazole ring can be achieved.

Functionalization of 1‐Substituted Pyrazoles

N‐Substituted pyrazoles are commonly used for the functionalization of the C‐5 position. For example, N‐substituted pyrazoles can be readily lithiated and subsequently treated with acyl chlorides. As reported by Fuse et al.,[ 23 ] the first step involved the preparation of the 1‐aryl‐1H‐pyrazoles 3a−d via Ullmann‐type coupling between the pyrazole 1 and the aryl halides 2a−d (Scheme 1 ). Acquired N‐arylated pyrazoles underwent indirect C−H functionalization at the C‐5 position via nucleophilic acyl substitution of pyrazolyl lithium generated by lithiation of 3a−d with nBuLi. Resulting aryl(1‐aryl‐1H‐pyrazol‐5‐yl)methanones (5a−e) were subjected to further iodination and intramolecular annulation to obtain indeno[2,1‐c]pyrazolones as inhibitors against HIF‐1 transcriptional activity.

Functionalization at 5‐position via nucleophilic acyl substitution of pyrazolyl lithium.

Another example comprises the regioselective C−H functionalization of N‐functionalized pyrazoles.[ 24 ] In this case, 1‐phenyl‐1H‐pyrazole (6) was treated with nBuLi to form a 5‐lithiated pyrazole intermediate, which was further reacted with 2‐(methoxymethyl)oxirane (7) (Scheme 2 ). The epoxide 7 underwent ring‐opening and generated reactive species requisite for successful substitution at the C‐5 position of the pyrazole ring. For further modification Swern oxidation was performed using trifluoroacetic anhydride (TFAA) and triethylamine (Et3N) to obtain 1‐methoxy‐3‐(1‐phenyl‐1H‐pyrazol‐5‐yl)propan‐2‐one (9), which is a useful building block for the synthesis of 1,3,5‐trisubstituted pyridazin‐4(1H)‐one and its derivatives as phosphodiesterase PDE10A inhibitors.

Regioselective C−H functionalization at the C‐5 position of 1‐aryl‐1H‐pyrazole with 2‐(methoxymethyl)oxirane and successive Swern oxidation of the hydroxyl group.

Alvarez et al. reported a novel pathway for the late‐stage C−H functionalization of pyrazoles at C‐5 position (Scheme 3 ).[ 25 ] Corresponding study reveals an operationally simple protocol that does not necessitate a catalyst, irradiation, extremely low temperatures, or an inert atmosphere. Reaction occurs via the coupling of aryl sulfonium salts 11a−b with the 1‐methyl‐1H‐pyrazole 10 in the presence of sodium persulfate and nBu3N (Scheme 3). Reaction mechanism involves the generation of α‐amino alkyl radicals through reductant upconversion[ 26 ] and C—S bond cleavage of the aryl sulfonium salts. Finally, addition of formed aryl radicals to 5‐position of the pyrazole ring yields the target compounds 12a−b.

C‐5 Functionalization via the coupling of 1‐methyl‐1H‐pyrazole with arylsulfonium salts.

Thereafter, Fuse et al. described sequential multiarylation.[ 27 ] Regioselectively activating the C‐5 position without affecting other reactive centers of the pyrazole ring was the key goal in this synthetic pathway. At first, arylation was conducted at the N‐1 position of the 1H‐pyrazole 13 to obtain the N‐substituted pyrazoles 14a−c (Scheme 4 ). Subsequently, direct regioselective C−H arylation at the C‐5 position of the 1‐aryl‐pyrazoles 14 was performed. Therefore, numerous combinations of catalysts (Pd(OAc)2 and [Pd(dppf)Cl2]·CH2Cl2), ligands (PPh3, [(Cy3)PH]BF4, [(tBu3)PH]BF4, cataCXium A, JohnPhos, DavePhos, SPhos, and XPhos), solvents (water, acetonitrile, 1,4‐dioxane, dimethylformamide, dimethyl sulfoxide, and dimethylacetamide), bases (K2CO3, Cs2CO3 and Ag2CO3), and reaction additives (CuI and CuCl) were examined. After extensive investigation using various aryl halides, the most suitable approach was demonstrated to consist of a complex system comprising Pd(OAc)2, cataCXium A, PivOH and K2CO3 in dioxane. Electron‐donating, electron‐withdrawing, and heteroatom‐containing aromatic systems were introduced in moderate to good yields. Obtained 1,5‐diarylated pyrazoles 15a−i were used for further arylations at C‐4 and C‐3 positions.

Direct C−H arylation at the C‐5 position of 1‐aryl‐pyrazoles 14.

Yao et al. reported Ag‐mediated and Pd‐catalyzed domino Heck/intermolecular C−H functionalization of arenes and heteroarenes.[ 28 ] Investigation of the reaction scope revealed that this methodology is also compatible with azaheteroarenes such as pyrazoles. For instance, the reaction between 1‐methylpyrazole (16) and N‐ethyl‐N‐(2‐iodophenyl)methacryl‐amide (17) using a bimetallic Ag/Pd system resulted in a 5‐substituted pyrazole derivative 18 with high enantioselectivity (Scheme 5 ). Control experiments indicated a dual function of the ligand, where it assisted Ag in cleaving the aryl C—H bond and coordinated Pd to control the enantioselectivity of subsequent Heck cyclization.

Ag‐mediated and Pd‐catalyzed C‐5 functionalization of 1‐methylpyrazole.

Joe et al. described a scalable synthetic procedure for 2‐chloro‐5‐[1‐(tetrahydro‐2H‐pyran‐2‐yl)‐1H‐pyrazol‐5‐yl]‐aniline (21), a key intermediate in the preparation of an immuno‐oncology asset.[ 29 ] Corresponding development was focused on the construction of a C—C bond via metal‐catalyzed C(5)−H arylation of pyrazoles. Regioselective Pd‐catalyzed direct arylation was of particular interest. Small‐scale experiment revealed that although transformation was selective for the desired regioisomer 21, selectivity was in a 85:7:8 ratio, and other mono‐ (22) and di‐functionalized (23) side products were also acquired (Scheme 6 ). For scalable synthesis, removal of structurally similar side products can be difficult throughout the process; moreover, unwanted products in high levels leads to lower atom economy.

Cross‐coupling approaches to synthesize compound 21.

Pyrazole can undergo selective deprotonation at the C‐5 position in the presence of nBuLi; consequently, the heteroarylic Zn reagent 24 was generated in situ using ZnCl2. Thereafter, Negishi cross‐coupling of compound 24 with 5‐bromo‐2‐chloroaniline (20) was conducted on a gram scale to selectively achieve the targeted 5‐substituted pyrazole 21 without undesired over‐functionalization (Scheme 6).

Imran et al. reported ligand‐controlled Ni‐catalyzed tandem hydroheteroarylation of terminal alkenes with various aza‐heterocyclic derivatives, including benzimidaloles, thiazoles, indoles, and pyrazoles.[ 30 ] Under the optimal conditions, 1‐phenyl‐1H‐pyrazole (25) was treated with but‐3‐en‐1‐ylbenzene (26) in the presence of a Ni catalyst and NaOtBu. As shown in Scheme 7 , different N‐heterocyclic carbene (NHC) ligands can completely switch reaction regioselectivity and induce isomerization. Use of Ni complex 27 enables long‐distance isomerization and favors the formation of branched (B) 5‐substituted pyrazole 29 with excellent regioselectivity, where the ratio of 3‐ and 5‐substituted products was equal to 1/99, respectively. Employment of Ni complex 28 deactivates the isomerization of alkenes and results in a linear (L) product 30, in which the substitution pattern is regioselectively shifted to the C‐3 position of the pyrazole ring.

Impact of Ni complex on the substitution pattern in 1‐phenyl‐1H‐pyrazole (25).

Based on mechanistic experiments with pyrroline derivative and literature findings,[ 31 ] proposed reaction mechanism involves the formation of active Ni(II)‐H species via oxidative addition, which is followed by alkene insertion into the complex intermediate consisting of a heterocycle‐Ni‐alkene system. In the later stage, two pathways are possible depending on the bulkiness of the ligand. Sterically smaller ligand of the complex 27 promotes alkene isomerization, whereas the bulky ligand of the complex 28 disfavors alkene isomerization, leading to the linear structure. Finally, the reductive elimination step affords branched (B) or linear (L) products after regeneration of the Ni(0) species.

Rh catalysts can also be used to support the construction of new C—C bonds in pyrazoles. Meng et al. described Rh(III)‐catalyzed N‐directed C–H alkenylations.[ 32 ] They evaluated C–H alkenylation of 2‐(1H‐pyrazol‐1‐yl)pyridine (31) with various terminal alkenes 32 (Scheme 8 ). Application of [Cp·RhCl2]2 as a catalyst with AgOAc as an additive in either acetonitrile or methanol resulted in 5‐alkenylated 1H‐pyrazol‐1‐ylpyridines 33a−j in acceptable to good yields. Substrates bearing electron‐donating and electron‐withdrawing substituents on the phenyl ring were tolerated, which included methyl, methoxy, and trifluoromethyl groups. Further investigation of reaction additives revealed that a slight modification of the conditions, namely, addition of acetic acid, can lead to dialkenylation of 2‐(1H‐pyrazol‐1‐yl)pyridine (31) via rollover cyclometalation and yield (1H‐pyrazol‐1‐yl)pyridines 34a−j. Although the exact role of acetic acid is not yet fully understood, acetic acid indeed plays an important role in promoting C−H dialkenylation.

Acid‐controlled Rh‐catalyzed switchable C–H mono‐ and di‐alkenylation of 2‐(1H‐pyrazol‐1‐yl) pyridine.

In another study, the same Rh catalyst [Cp·RhCl2]2 was used to demonstrate solvent‐controlled functionalization of 2‐(1H‐pyrazol‐1‐yl)pyridine.[ 33 ] Pyrazole 35 was treated with several internal alkynes 36 in the presence of [Cp·RhCl2]2, AgTFA, Cu(OAc)2 and KOAc in 1,2‐dichloroethane to obtain 5‐substituted 1H‐pyrazol‐1‐yl‐pyridines 37a−k in 43−90% yields (Scheme 9 ). Interestingly, when 1,2‐dichloroethane was replaced with more polar dimethylformamide, a different selectivity was observed. In this case, 4,5‐substitution pattern was dominant, and fused pyrazole derivatives 38a−i were isolated as major products in 47−87% yields.

Solvent‐controlled C–H alkenylation of 2‐(1H‐pyrazol‐1‐yl)pyridine (35).

Kim et al. reported the synthesis of conformationally fixed pyrazolo‐pyridine compounds with fused ring systems.[ 34 ] Corresponding methodology comprises Rh‐ and Cu‐catalyzed C−H annulation using internal alkynes. At first, optimization of C−H functionalization of 2‐(1H‐pyrazol‐1‐yl)pyridine 39 with diphenyl acetylene was performed to acquire the annulated pyrazole 41 as the major product (Scheme 10 ). Use of a nonpolar solvent and pivalic acid additive was critical for the successful C−H activation of the pyridine core; nevertheless, it resulted in trace amounts of the unwanted products 42 and 43. Optimized conditions were applied to evaluate the reaction scope. Therefore, the activity of the diverse alkynes 40, such as aryl alkynes containing electron‐donating groups (EDGs) and electron‐withdrawing groups (EWGs), were examined. Consequently, the fused systems 41a−s annulated via the 5‐position of pyrazole were obtained in 28−96% yields.

Rh‐Catalyzed C−H annulation of 2‐(1H‐pyrazol‐1‐yl)pyridines 39.

Pd‐catalyzed [5 + 2] rollover annulation is another strategy for obtaining fused pyrazole derivatives annulated via the 5‐position of the ring. As depicted in Scheme 11 , reaction of 1‐benzylpyrazoles 44 with alkynes 45 in the presence of a Pd catalyst and AgOAc/PivOH as an oxidant in dimethylformamide afforded various tricyclic pyrazolo‐2‐benzazepines (46,47).[ 35 ] Analysis of reaction substrates implied, that EWGs and EDGs, including halogens, of the aryl substituent in benzylpyrazoles were well‐tolerated, which would be beneficial for further functionalization. Comparable results were acquired with symmetrical aryl alkynes having substituents at the meta‐ and para‐positions of the phenyl ring. Conjugated alkynes provided regioselective products in lower yields (47l and 47m); in contrast, the reaction of unsymmetrical diaryl alkyne yielded a 1.6:1 regioisomeric mixture (47n).

Pd‐Catalyzed [5 + 2] rollover annulation of 1‐benzylpyrazoles 44.

Additionally, 1‐benzylpyrazoles 44 can undergo [5 + 2] rollover annulation and subsequent lactonization when treated with 1‐hydroxy‐2‐alkynoates 48 under almost identical conditions (Scheme 11).[ 36 ] In this study, the reagents scope was extended to several acyclic and cyclic alkynoates, bearing five‐, six‐, seven‐, and eight‐membered rings. Application of this method resulted in a series of fused 1H‐benzo[e]furo[3,4‐c]pyrazolo[1,5‐a]azepin‐3(8H)‐one derivatives (49,50) in 68−87% yields.

Zhu et al. established a synthetic strategy for constructing N‐fused quinolines having SCF3 moiety and involving reactivity at the 5‐position of the pyrazole ring.[ 37 ] They conducted radical trifluoromethylthiolation using numerous N‐(ortho‐alkynyl)‐arylpyrazoles 51 (Scheme 12 ). To select the most effective reaction conditions, various photocatalysts, solvents, additives, and light sources were screened.

Photo‐induced radical trifluoromethylthiolation of pyrazoles 51.

Optimization results revealed N‐(trifluoromethylthio)‐phthalimide (Phth‐SCF3) 52 to be the best trifluoromethyl‐thiolation reagent; Ir[ppy]2(dtbbpy)PF6 and hexamethylphosphor‐amide (HMPA) were chosen as a photocatalyst and base additive, respectively. Pyrazoles 51 were treated with Phth‐SCF3 52 under the irradiation of 10 W blue light‐emitting diodes (Scheme 12). Radical trifluoromethylthiolation of N‐(ortho‐alkynyl)aryl‐pyrazoles afforded products 53a−r in moderate to good yields, demonstrating compatibility with several EWGs and EDGs at the para‐position of the phenyl ring. Pyridine, thiophene, and alkyl scaffolds were also moderately tolerated under these reaction conditions. Notably, this methodology extends to 1,3‐ and 1,4‐substituted pyrazoles, further highlighting its versatility.

Wu et al. reported Cu‐catalyzed synthesis of pyrazolo[1,5‐a] quinoline derivatives via sequential one‐pot cascade C(sp3)−H activation, radical addition, and cyclization.[ 38 ] Products were obtained by reacting various N‐(ortho‐alkynyl)arylpyrazoles with cyclic and acyclic ethers. For example, Scheme 13 exhibits the scope of the reaction between 1‐[2‐(phenylethynyl)phenyl]‐1H‐pyrazole (54) and different substrates 55 with respect to the C(sp3)—H bonds adjacent to a heteroatom. Reaction proceeded in the presence of CuCl2, di‐tert‐butyl peroxide (DTBP) and NaHCO3 at 110 °C, providing the products 56a−h in moderate to good yields. Usage of 1,2‐dimethoxyethane as a substrate led to a mixture of regioisomers 56h and 56h′, which were isolated in 41% and 24% yields, respectively. Reported method also demonstrates a broad substituent scope in the phenyl‐, alkyl‐, and pyrazole moieties of N‐(ortho‐alkynyl)arylpyrazoles.

Synthesis of pyrazolo[1,5‐a]quinolines 56 via Cu‐catalyzed cyclization reactions.

Functionalization of 1,3‐Disubstituted Pyrazoles

Stepan et al. synthesized a 1H‐pyrazol‐imidazo‐triazin‐4‐(3H)‐one derivative as a phosphodiesterase 2A inhibitor.[ 39 ] Their study emphasizes numerous C−H diversification methods as strategic tools to construct required intermediates and target molecules on a gram scale. To acquire the desired intermediate 60, a two‐step synthetic approach was described (Scheme 14 ). Initially, 1,3‐dimethyl‐1H‐pyrazole (57) was treated with Bu3SnCl in the presence of lithium diisopropylamide (LDA) and N,N,N′,N′‐tetramethyl ethylenediamine (TMEDA) to obtain 1,3‐dimethyl‐5‐(tributylstannyl)‐1H‐pyrazole (58). The presence of SnBu3 group at the C‐5 position of pyrazole 58 facilitated further Pd‐catalyzed Stille cross‐coupling with 1‐bromo‐4‐(trifluoromethyl)benzene (59), affording target pyrazole intermediate 60 in 89% yield.

Indirect C−H functionalization of 1,3‐dimethylpyrazole via Stille cross‐coupling.

Morita et al. synthesized triaryl‐substituted pyrazoles using readily available heteroaromatic scaffolds.[ 40 ] Preparation of multisubstituted pyrazoles involved straightforward S N A, Suzuki–Miyaura reaction, and direct C−H arylation reactions. One of the synthetic steps comprised direct C−H arylation at the C‐5 position of 1,3‐diarylpyrazoles 61 (Scheme 15 ). Reaction conditions were examined using a combination of different phosphine ligands and solvents to overcome undesired double arylation at both C‐4 and C‐5 positions. An experiment involving the use of Pd(OAc)2, PCy3·HBF4, PivOH, and K2CO3 provided the desired coupling products 62a−i with electron‐donating, electron‐withdrawing, and heteroatom‐containing groups in satisfactory to good yields.

Direct Pd‐catalyzed C−H arylation at the C5‐position of the 1,3‐diaryl‐substituted pyrazole 61.

Koidan et al. investigated diaminomethylation reactions of N‐methyl‐ and N‐phenyl‐pyrazoles.[ 41 ] Interestingly, 1‐substituted pyrazoles did not react with silylformamidine 64 even under heating for a prolonged time, whereas 1,3‐disubstituted pyrazoles 63a−e readily reacted with substrate 64 (Scheme 16 ). However, both the yields and reaction outcomes were considerably different. For instance, 3‐bromopyrazoles 65a,b were obtained in 24% and 87% yields, respectively. Nitropyrazoles reacted differently, as 1‐methyl‐3‐nitro‐1H‐pyrazole (63e) underwent silylation at the C‐5 position instead of insertion, affording the product 66e in 54% yield. Moreover, further hydrolysis and methanolysis of obtained products were investigated. Hydrolysis of pyrazole aminals 65a−d in 15% HCl aqueous solution provided corresponding pyrazole‐5‐carbaldehydes 67a−d, whereas methanolysis of the aminals 65c,d afforded N‐methylimines 68c,d in 68% and 80% yields, respectively.

Diaminomethylation at the C‐5 position of 1,3‐disubstituted pyrazoles.

1,3‐Functionalized pyrazoles can serve as scaffolds for the dehydrogenative synthesis of biazoles. Yu et al. presented a so called “prejoin” approach,[ 42 ] which is particularly useful for addressing the challenges of conventional methods for homo‐ and hetero‐coupling of azoles. Introduction of a “prejoin” group at the N−H position of the two pyrazoles promotes intramolecular dehydrogenative coupling to form a fused ring, which yields the desired bipyrazole derivative after the removal of the “prejoin” group. Their strategy offers key advantages, that is, improved regio‐ and chemoselectivities, the “prejoin” group also acts as a protecting group and enables straightforward synthesis of unsymmetrical biazole derivatives by attaching two different azoles to the suggested pyrazine “prejoin” group.

2,3‐Di(1H‐pyrazol‐1‐yl)pyrazines, bearing the pyrazine “prejoin” group, were prepared at first and thereafter subjected to Pd‐catalyzed intramolecular dehydrogenative coupling. During the first step, the pyrazoles 69 were treated with Pd(OAc)2/(nBu)3 P in the presence of Ag2CO3 as an oxidant and 4‐methylpyridine as an additive in dioxane (Scheme 17 ). Subsequently, the “prejoin” group was cleaved using NaBH4 in dimethyl ether, resulting in the final bipyrazoles 71a−l. This two‐step procedure is suitable for both homo‐ and hetero‐couplings of pyrazoles, bearing diverse functional groups. Furthermore, the reported method can be conducted as a one‐pot reaction; nevertheless, the two‐step approach generally provides higher overall efficiency.

Dehydrogenative synthesis of bipyrazoles via the “prejoin” approach.

Functionalization of 1,4‐Disubstituted Pyrazoles

Alonso et al. performed the synthesis of N‐heterocycle‐containing CRTh2 antagonists and examined the corresponding structure‐activity and structure‐kinetic relationships.[ 43 ] To synthesize pyrazole‐based antagonists, 1,4‐disubstituted pyrazoles 72 were regioselectively deprotonated at the C‐5 position using nBuLi followed by treatment with 5‐substituted 2‐bromo‐benzaldehydes (Scheme 18 ). Acquired benzylic alcohols 74a−b underwent Si‐based acid‐catalyzed reduction to yield the pyrazole derivatives 75a,b. The presence of Br substituent in the phenyl ring allows further modification of the products.

Indirect C−H functionalization of 1,4‐disubstituted pyrazoles at the C‐5 position.

To enable the selective synthesis of C‐5 arylated pyrazoles via direct Pd‐catalyzed C−H activation, protecting groups, for example, formyl, ester, or nitrile, may be incorporated at the C‐4 positions. For the same purpose, Romdhane et al. investigated the application of hydroxymethyl substituent as a blocking group.[ 44 ] In this case, the presence of this blocking group can also be beneficial for further synthetic transformations to obtain more complex molecules. As shown in Scheme 19 , (1‐methyl‐1H‐pyrazol‐4‐yl)methanol (76) was treated with various ortho‐, meta‐, and para‐substituted aryl bromides 77 in the presence of PdCl(C3H5)(dppb) as a catalyst and along with KOPiv as a base in dimethylacetamide. Reactions selectively proceeded at the 5‐position and afforded the compounds 78a−y in 48–76% yields. Additionally, reactions were conducted using 3‐bromoquinoline, 2‐bromonaphthalene and 1‐bromonaphthalene, leading to the products 79, 80, and 81, respectively. As reported, C‐5 arylation of pyrazole 76 proceeds via classical direct arylation mechanism.

Pd‐Catalyzed C‐5 arylation of the pyrazole 76 using 4‐hydroxymethyl substituent as a blocking group.

Bae et al. described a common approach for the construction of 5‐allylated and 5‐benzylated pyrazoles.[ 45 ] Electron‐withdrawing substituents at the C‐4 position render the Lewis basic N atom less basic and 5‐H position more acidic as compared to the case of parent unsubstituted pyrazole. This variation of the bond properties facilitates catalytic C−H allylation and benzylation at the C‐5 position of the pyrazole. Regarding the 5‐alkylation of the pyrazoles 82 (Scheme 20 ), several important factors had to be considered for the alkyl group source. With the progress of the reaction, the leaving group of alkyl donors can interfere with the activity of the Pd catalyst. Initial experimentation revealed that only allyl acetate and aryl chlorides were appropriate for the direct allylation and benzylation of EWG‐containing pyrazoles 82. Scope of the reactions was investigated under optimized conditions. Treatment of 1,4‐disubstituted pyrazoles 82 with allyl acetate 83 using a combination of Pd(OAc)2, PPh3, and K2CO3 afforded the products 84a and 84c−f in 74−88% yields. Interestingly, prolonged heating resulted in the isomerization of the terminal alkene and yielded the product 84b. Benzylation reactions were significantly promoted under the same conditions by the presence of pivalic acid. Pyrazoles 82 were coupled with a wide range of benzyl chlorides 85 to provide the products 86a−r in 38−97% yields. Notably, no overalkylation was observed.

Catalytic C−H allylation and benzylation of 1,4‐disubstituted pyrazoles 82.

To expand the access to C−H functionalization of pyrazoles by overcoming the limitations arising from phosphine‐ligated complexes, Han et al. investigated phosphine‐free catalytic systems to facilitate C5‐functionalization of pyrazoles (Scheme 21 ).[ 46 ] To achieve selective C5‐olefination, various reaction parameters had to be evaluated. At first, different oxidants were examined, indicating that Cu(OAc)2 was superior to Ag‐containing oxidants. Use of pyridine as a monodentate N ligand also improved the reaction yield. Catalyst, derived from Pd(OAc)2 and pyridine, in combination with Cu(OAc)2 as an oxidant, enabled oxidative C−H functionalization of 1‐methyl‐4‐nitro‐1H‐pyrazole (87) and afforded 5‐alkenylpyrazoles 89a−i as major products, which proved to be useful in further intramolecular cyclization reactions. As predicted from the reactivity of the Pd/pyridine system, pyrazoles substituted with a broad range of functional groups at the C‐1 and C‐3 positions, underwent C−H olefination.

Oxidative C−H alkenylation of the functionalized pyrazole 87.

Yao et al. reported another study involving cross‐coupling strategy for the regioselective olefination of arenes, which was facilitated by a dual Pd/Ag bimetallic system.[ 47 ] In this study, numerous simple arenes and heteroarenes, including pyrazole, were investigated. Functionalization of 4‐bromo‐1‐methyl‐1H‐pyrazole (92) at the 5‐position was performed with trifluoromethanesulfonate 93 in the presence of PdCl2, Ag2CO3, Ph2 tBuP and K2CO3 in dimethylformamide under Ar atmosphere (Scheme 22 ). This reaction led to 1,3,5‐trisubstituted pyrazole 94 in 83% yield. Control experiments revealed that the aryl‐Ag and vinyl‐Pd complexes might be key intermediates in the reaction mechanism.

Ag‐Mediated and Pd‐catalyzed nondirected olefination of 4‐bromo‐1‐methyl‐1H‐pyrazole (92) at the C‐5 position.

J. Q. Liu et al. reported a Cu‐catalyzed approach to the cyanation of 1,4‐disubstituted pyrazoles.[ 48 ] Several oxidizers, CN sources, solvents, and other additives were investigated to obtain the target 5‐cyanated pyrazole 96 (Scheme 23 ). Interestingly, when the reaction was conducted using Cu(OAc)2, KI, and CuCN in dimethylformamide (method a), the products 96, 97, and 98 were acquired in 56, 25, and 10% yields, respectively. Further addition of excess acetic acid (method b) greatly improved reaction selectivity and afforded pyrazole 96 in 92% yield. Under the optimal conditions, the reaction scope was investigated. Obtained results indicated that substituent variations at the N‐1 and C‐4 positions were well tolerated as the products 99a−i were obtained in 58−84% yields.

Cu‐Catalyzed regioselective oxidative C−H cyanation.

Z. Liu et al. developed enantioselective Pd‐catalyzed C−H arylation of functionalized azoles, resulting in a series of axially chiral heterobiaryls.[ 49 ] In this study, 1,4‐disubstituted pyrazoles were subjected to asymmetric C5‐arylation in the presence of a Pd(OCOCF3)2/CD3‐AntPhos/AdCOOH catalytic system and K2CO3 as a base (Scheme 24 ). During these reactions, differently substituted bromonaphthalenes or bromoquinolines reacted with the pyrazoles 100, generating axially chiral products in moderate to good yields and with good to excellent enantioselectivities. For example, functional groups, such as methoxy, ketone, ester, and aldehyde, in the naphthalene scaffold were well tolerated in the reactions with 1‐methyl‐4‐nitro‐1H‐pyrazole (100a), providing C5‐functionalized pyrazoles 102a−d in 81−95% yields. Although heterobiaryl 102e with a methyl substituent on the naphthalene ring led to lower yield, it maintained high enantioselectivity. With an increase in the size of the substituent at the 1‐position on the pyrazole ring, decreased yields were observed (the products 105a,b).

Enantioselective Pd‐catalyzed C−H arylation of 1,4‐disubstituted pyrazoles 100.

Asymmetric C5‐arylation of 1,4‐substituted pyrazole was similarly applicable to the 1‐methyl‐1H‐pyrazole‐4‐carbaldehyde (100b); however, the products 103a−f and 106 were obtained in lower yields. Reaction of 1‐methyl‐1H‐pyrazole‐4‐carbonitrile (100e) afforded the product 107 in a high 90% yield, yet with only 56% enantioselectivity. Reactions with 5‐bromo‐6‐methoxyquinoline generated both products 109 and 110 in 77% and 69% yields, respectively, and with high enantioselectivities.

Comparison of the obtained results implied that ortho‐alkoxy substituents (R3 = OMe, OBn, OiPr) on naphthalene were crucial for enantioselectivity. Other substituents (R3 = CHO, Me, CN) at this position substantially decreased enantioselectivities of the products 104c, 104 d, and 107, with ee values of 62%, 55%, and 56%, respectively.

Functionalization of 1,3,4‐Trisubstituted Pyrazoles

Polyfunctionalized pyrazoles, lacking a substituent at the C‐5 position, are prone to C−H functionalization. Utecht et al. investigated C‐5 functionalization of 3‐trifluoromethylpyrazole.[ 50 ] At first, the hydroxyl group in 4‐(2′‐hydroxyethyl)‐1‐tolyl‐3‐trifluoromethyl‐1H‐pyrazole (111) was activated with either TBS or Ts group (Scheme 25 ). The obtained ether 112 was treated with nBuLi to afford the organolithium intermediate 113, which was stabilized by the silyl ether group. Subsequent treatment with selected electrophiles resulted in the successful incorporation of methyl, iodine, amide, and O‐containing groups at the 5‐position of the pyrazole ring, affording the products 114a−g in good yields.

Functionalization at the C‐5 position of 3‐trifluoromethylpyrazoles.

Presence of the 2‐hydroxyethyl unit in the pyrazole 111 also allowed the preparation of polycyclic pyrazole derivatives. In this case, the Ts group bearing O‐activated pyrazole 112 was treated with a suitable ortho‐bromoarene 115 (Scheme 25). Nucleophilic displacement led to the pyrazole precursors 116a−c in 65−92% yields. Compounds 116a−c underwent intramolecular C−H arylation at the 5‐position of the ring based on the general procedure developed by Poh et al.[ 51 ] Cyclization of 116a−c resulted in fused pyrazoles 117a−c comprising oxepane, thiepane, and azepane units.

Smari et al. reported regioselective C‐5 arylation of pyrazole‐4‐carbaldehydes.[ 52 ] Formyl substituent can be further transformed into other functional groups such as imines, alcohols, or thiocarbonyls. Moreover, the formyl substituent may be used as a protecting group as it can be easily removed. At first, the formation of decarbonylated starting compound was observed. Side reactions could be expected as Pd‐catalyzed decarbonylation of azoles has already been described.[ 53 ] Therefore, reaction conditions needed to be optimized to suppress early‐stage deformylation. For this purpose, appropriate coupling agents, catalysts, bases, and solvents had to be carefully chosen. A combination of Pd(OAc)2 and KOAc in dimethylacetamide was determined to be the most suitable system for mild coupling of the pyrazoles 118 with aryl bromides (Scheme 26 ). Scope of the substrates was evaluated by performing arylations at the 5‐position of pyrazole ring with various (hetero)aryl‐ and ortho‐ and meta‐substituted aryl bromides, which led to the coupled products 119−123 in 35−94% yields. To demonstrate the application of formyl substituent as a protecting group, decarboxylation of 5‐arylpyrazoles 119g−i was conducted using Pd(OAc)2 and molecular sieves to afford the C‐4 unsubstituted products 124 in 69−90% yields.

Pd‐Catalyzed direct arylation at the C‐5 position of pyrazoles 118.

Recently, the use of ortho‐substituted atropisomeric (hetero)biaryl motifs has gained attention and is a current trend in drug discovery.[ 54 , 55 ] Atropisomerism is a type of chirality that originates from hindered rotation around a single bond. Nguyen et al. reported highly enantioselective C−H arylation of 1,3,4‐trisubstituted pyrazoles, providing efficient access to atropisomeric (hetero)biaryls.[ 56 ] Initial screening of several reaction conditions revealed that Pd(dba)2 with pivalic acid as a cocatalyst was an appropriate combination to reach the desired selectivity (Scheme 27 ). Atroposelective arylation proceeded with excellent enantioselectivity when the pyrazoles 125a−c with EWGs were employed, whereas C−H arylation of 1‐methyl‐4‐phenyl‐1H‐pyrazole‐3‐carbonitrile with 1‐bromo‐2‐methoxy‐naphthalene generated the product 127b in an excellent 97% yield and a 97:3 enantiomeric ratio.

Atroposelective arylation of pyrazoles.

Liu et al. described sustainable metal‐catalyst‐free C—N bonding for five‐membered N‐heterocycles.[ 57 ] Initially attempted C—N coupling between 3,4‐dinitropyrazole and substituted azoles was unsuccessful. Introduction of an EWG at the 1‐position of 3,4‐dinitropyrazole effectively activated the adjacent C—H bond for coupling reactions. At first, cross‐coupling of 1,3,4‐trinitropyrazole 128 with selected fully substituted azoles was investigated. Reactions were performed in refluxing ethanol in the presence of K2CO3, resulting in C−N‐bridged pyrazole potassium salts 130 (Scheme 28 ). Follow‐up acidification with 10% HCl aqueous solution led to bis‐heterocyclic derivatives 131a−d in 78−86% yields. Subsequently, further application of the proposed synthetic strategy was examined by expanding the scope of azole substrates. Reactions were conducted between 1,3,4‐trinitropyrazole 128 and various derivatives of pyrazole, imidazole, 1,2,3‐triazole, and 1,2,4‐triazole. Under the same reaction conditions, fifteen C−N cross‐coupling products 132a−o were obtained as potassium salts in 80−90% yields.

Direct C−N coupling between bis‐heterocyclic compounds in the absence of transition‐metal catalysts.

Interesting observation was made while analyzing the reactions with substrates having higher pKa values. When 1,3,4‐trinitropyrazole was treated with pyrrole, 3‐methyl‐1H‐pyrrole, pyrazole, 4‐methyl‐1H‐pyrazole, imidazole, 4‐methyl‐1H‐imidazole and 3‐methyl‐1H‐1,2,4‐triazole, only self‐coupling of 1,3,4‐trinitropyrazole 128 proceeded, resulting in potassium bipyrazolate 133 (Scheme 28). This may be attributed to the higher pKa values of the substrates, which increase their basicity, allowing them to act as bases and initiate self‐coupling of 1,3,4‐trinitropyrazole.

During the investigation of Rh‐catalyzed asymmetric dearomatization reactions, Li et al. achieved unexpected results, indicating the formation of tandem C−C/C−H activation/annulation products.[ 58 ] Therefore, they turned their attention to further analyze this phenomenon. During the reaction optimization stage, it was found that rhodacyclopentanone underwent hydrolysis, which is a competing pathway to activation/annulation processes. Established methodology was broadened and applied to numerous heterocycles, including pyrazoles. Thus, pyrazole‐4‐carboxamide 134 was treated with a [Rh(HQ)(cod)]SbF6/PPh3 catalyst system in dioxane at 150 °C to acquire an annulated pyrazole derivative 135 in 63% yield (Scheme 29 ). Reaction occured without any dearomatization products.

Cascade C−C/C−H activation/annulation of the pyrazole derivative 134.

Shalimov et al. reported a novel and simple method to construct pyrazolothiadiazines and pyrazolothiadiazepines incorporating an endocyclic sulfonamide fragment.[ 59 ] Proposed methodology can be applied to 1,3,5‐trisubstituted pyrazoles. As depicted in Scheme 30 , 1,3‐dimethyl‐1H‐pyrazol‐4‐amine (136) was treated with imidoyl chlorides 137a,b using N,N‐diisopropylethylamine (DIPEA) as a base in dichloromethane at room temperature. The reaction proceeded involving C‐5 of pyrazole in the annulation, and pyrazolo[4,3‐e][1,2,4]thiadiazines 138a,b were formed in 50−57% yields.

Synthesis of fused pyrazolo‐thiadiazines 138 using N‐chlorosulfoniltrihaloacetimidoyl chlorides.

C−H Functionalization of Pyrazoles at C‐4 Position

Pyrazoles are prone to electrophilic substitution reactions at C‐4 position due to maximum electron density at this position as compared to those at C‐5 and C‐3 positions. However, in some reactions, C−H functionalization can be more complicated, resulting in poor regioselectivity and affording mixtures of variously substituted products. To address the selectivity issue, various synthetic strategies have been developed.

Functionalization of 1‐Substituted Pyrazoles

Halogenation reactions are probably one of the most common functionalizations of pyrazoles at the C‐4 position, as an introduced halogen atom can either improve specific properties or open the way for further chemical transformations of the molecule. Numerous classical and novel halogenation strategies have been reported. For example, fluorination of 1‐phenyl‐1H‐pyrazole can be performed using N‐chloromethyl‐N′‐fluoro‐triethylene‐diammonium bis(tetrafluoroborate), an electrophilic fluorine donor, also known as Selectfluor (Scheme 31 ).[ 60 ] To incorporate Cl, Br, or I atoms, wider options are available.

C‐4‐Halogenation of N‐substituted pyrazoles.

For halogenation reactions succinimides are prevalently used owing to easy handling and high reactivity of the N—X bond, which acts as a convenient source of halogen radicals. For instance, chlorination of 1‐phenyl‐1H‐pyrazole can be conducted using N‐chlorosuccinimide in the presence of catalytic amount of [TEMPO][OTf], generating the desired product 141 in excellent yield (Scheme 31).[ 61 ] Another finding by Fagnani et al. revealed the possibility to synthesize chloroarenes using waste poly(vinyl chloride) (PVC).[ 62 , 63 ] Significance of this study is based on reduction and reuse of plastic waste as PVC is the least recycled polymer due to the release of plasticizers and corrosive HCl gas from it. In the reported case,[ 62 ] PVC is employed as a reagent to chlorinate 1‐phenyl‐1H‐pyrazole by paired electrolysis at room temperature under air‐ and moisture‐tolerant conditions. For the chlorination of arylpyrazoles, trichloroisocyanuric acid (TClCA) can also be used as a halogenating agent to obtain the pyrazole 142 in 95% yield.[ 64 ]

Bromination of pyrazoles can be similarly performed using N‐bromosuccinimide to achieve the product 143 in good yield (Scheme 31).[ 64 ] Another pathway was described by Song et al., involving oxidative bromination of arenes with HBr and dimethyl sulfoxide. This methodology was applied to obtain the brominated pyrazole 144 in 77% yield.[ 65 ] Additionally, Olsen et al. introduced Br atom into the pyrazole core under mild reaction conditions, where the use of NaBr salt and Oxone resulted in the pyrazole 145 in good yield.[ 66 ]

Similar to the cases of chlorination and bromination, iodination at the C‐4 position of pyrazole ring can be implemented using N‐iodosuccinimide.[ 64 ] Although N‐iodosuccinimide is a practical and convenient reagent for iodination, its utility can be severely limited for some substrates. Therefore, disulfide‐catalyzed electrophilic iodination of aromatic compounds was established.[ 67 ] In the specific procedure, bis(4‐methoxyphenyl) disulfide activates 1,3‐diiodo‐5,5‐dimethylhydantoin (DIH) as a Lewis base to promote iodination of aromatic compounds, leading to the pyrazole 147 in excellent yield (Scheme 31). Another methodology involved the application of Ag(I) sulfonates,[ 68 ] in which C−H iodination proceeded via putative sulfonyl hypoiodites and resulted in the pyrazole 148.

Other common functionalization of pyrazoles includes formyl group incorporation into the pyrazole core. Formylation at the C‐4 position of 1‐phenyl‐1H‐pyrazoles is often a key step in the synthesis of more complex compounds. Vilsmeier−Haack reaction is one of the classical approaches, where POCl3 and dimethylformamide are used to generate a formyl moiety.[ 69 ] Duff reaction using hexamethylenetetramine (HMTA) in trifluoroacetic acid is a milder and safer alternative.[ 70 ]

Oliveria et al. applied Duff reaction conditions to obtain starting materials for the preparation of compounds with antinociceptive and anti‐inflammatory activities.[ 70 ] For this purpose, the pyrazoles 149a−c were subjected to Duff reaction using HMTA in trifluoroacetic acid, leading to 1‐aryl‐1H‐pyrazole‐4‐carbaldehydes 150a−c in 83−95% yields (Scheme 32 ). Subsequently, formylated pyrazoles were converted to the pyrazole carbonitriles 151a−c, which were useful synthons for the formation of a tetrazole core to afford the desired pyrazole‐tetrazole derivatives 152a−c in 76−96% yields.

Formylation of N‐aryl‐1H‐pyrazoles via Duff reaction and successive modification of the formyl group.

Selective alkenylation reactions were also investigated to achieve C‐4 functionalization. Kim et al. developed regioselective C‐4 alkenylation of structurally simple pyrazoles by recognizing that an electrophilic Pd catalyst preferred the nucleophilic C‐4 position of the ring, whereas efficient deprotonation enabled C−H functionalization at the C‐5 position.[ 16 ] Using this finding and optimized conditions, substrate scope of alkenes was investigated (Scheme 33 ). 1‐Methyl‐1H‐pyrazole (153) was treated with various acrylates, acrylamides, styrenes, and vinyl phosphonates in the presence of 4,5‐diazafluoren‐9‐one (DAF), trifluoroacetic acid, and 1,4‐benzoquinone (BQ) using Pd(OAc)2 as catalyst to afford the C‐4 alkenylated pyrazoles 155a−g as major products in 48−86% yields.

Regioselective Pd‐catalyzed alkenylation of pyrazoles at the C‐4 position.

Direct arylation at the C‐4 position of N‐substituted pyrazoles has been reported.[ 71 ] Preliminary experiments indicated that regioselectivity of the reaction was promoted by a ligand‐free Pd catalyst and a base without oxidants or other additives. Determined optimal conditions were applied to investigate the tolerance of substituents in the aryl bromides 159 and at the N‐position of the pyrazole ring (Scheme 34 ). Substituents on aryl bromide significantly influence the regioselectivity of the reaction. For example, the use of 2‐bromonitrobenzene and 2‐(trifluoromethyl)bromobenzene resulted in the regioisomers 164a and 166a with 97% and 92% regioselectivity, respectively. In contrast, the application of 1‐bromo‐2‐fluorobenzene led to the pyrazole 167a with 78% selectivity. Overall, 70−98% regioselectivities were achieved using pyrazoles bearing iPr, cyclohexyl, benzyl, and 1‐phenylethyl substituents on the N atom of the pyrazole ring. Regioselectivity of the reported synthetic approach is also highly dependent on the nature of the solvent and base.

Regioselective arylation of N‐substituted pyrazoles at the C‐4 position.

Li et al. reported another highly selective arylation via Au/Ag dual‐catalyzed cross‐dehydrogenative coupling.[ 72 ] Under optimized reaction conditions, C‐4 selectivity was achieved using DMSAuCl, AgOAc and phenyliodide diacetate (PIDA) without production of homocoupling or other side products. Tolerance of the method for N‐substituents in the pyrazole ring was investigated (Scheme 35 ). N‐Aryl unit bearing both EDGs and EWGs in the compounds 192d−f demonstrated moderate tolerance as compared to N‐alkyl substitution, where products with N‐methyl and N‐ethyl groups (192b and 192c) were obtained in 96% and 95% yields, respectively. Mechanistic investigation of coupling reaction revealed that Ag(I) was responsible for C—H bond activation in electron‐poor fluoroarene species generating arylsilver, which further participated in transmetalation to Au center. Au(III) plays a role in C−H activation of the pyrazole ring. Selectivity of the reaction originates from orthogonal activation of Ag(I) and Au(III) on (hetero)arenes with different electronic properties.

Au/Ag‐Catalyzed cross‐dehydrogenative coupling between pyrazoles and fluoroarene.

Tian et al. described a novel and sustainable approach for C—C bond formation via mild oxidative Suzuki, Heck, and Sonogashira reactions in water using hydrogen peroxide as a terminal oxidant.[ 73 ] Optimized methodology was also demonstrated for pyrazole derivatives. As shown in Scheme 36 , the phenylpyrazole 193 underwent Suzuki cross‐coupling when treated with the boronic acid 194 using FeBr3/H2O system, Pd(PPh3)2Cl2 as a catalyst, H2O2, and Na2CO3. Reaction proceeded in a one‐pot manner; that is, the reactive brominated species, needed for successful cross‐coupling, were produced in situ from Fenton oxidation of Fenton‐bromide. Under specified conditions, 1,4‐diphenyl‐1H‐pyrazole (195) was isolated in 86% yield. Moreover, 1‐phenyl‐1H‐pyrazole was subjected to Sonogashira coupling by treating it with 2‐methyl‐3‐butyn‐2‐ol (196) under similar reaction conditions, except for substituting piperidine for Na2CO3. In this case, the coupling product 197 was acquired in 79% yield.

In‐water oxidative Suzuki and Sonogashira coupling of pyrazoles using H2O2 as a terminal oxidant.

Zhang et al. reported another advancement in Suzuki cross‐coupling reactions.[ 74 ] In their study, Suzuki coupling under acidic conditions was investigated, as it solves the issue of using acid‐sensitive reaction partners. For selective cross‐coupling, substrates must bear suitable functional groups, for instance, bromide and boronic acid. Thus, prefunctionalization of 1‐substituted pyrazoles needed to be performed. Aromatic C−H thianthrenation reaction at the C‐4 position of pyrazoles 198 was conducted using tetrafluorothianthrene (TFT) and thianthrene (TT) S‐oxide[ 75 ] to obtain the pyrazole TFT and TT salts 199a and 199b, respectively (Scheme 37 ). Thereafter, the obtained salts 199 were subjected to Suzuki cross‐coupling with the boronic acids 200 using Pd(tBu3P)2 and HBF4·Et2O in methanol. Generally, acid is not required for the catalytic mechanism of this reaction; nevertheless, acidic conditions allow the participation of substrates that otherwise would not efficiently participate. During the reaction, the 4‐arylated pyrazoles 201a and 201b were obtained in high yields (98% and 91%, respectively). Overall, the reported method extends the substrate scope to compounds that cannot be directly converted through standard Suzuki cross‐coupling from boronic acids without protection steps.

Selective aromatic C−H thianthrenation and subsequent Suzuki cross‐coupling under acidic conditions.

Almendros et al. presented a convenient method for controlled metal‐free functionalization of pyrazoles.[ 76 ] As depicted in Scheme 38 , refluxing of the pyrazoles 202a−d with 2‐(2‐fluoropyridin‐1‐ium‐1‐yl)‐1,1‐bis[(trifluoro‐methyl)sulfonyl]‐ethan‐1‐ide (203) in acetonitrile proceeded in a selective C‐4 ethylation of the pyrazole nucleus. The bis(triflyl)ethylated products 204a−d were isolated as N‐protonated zwitterions in excellent yields, which were easily transformed into sodium salts by treatment with sodium methoxide in methanol. Interestingly, when the reaction was performed between the pyrazole 202a and the compound 203 at room temperature, the kinetically favorable N‐alkylated product 205 was isolated. Successive heating of the product 205 resulted in its conversion to the 4‐substituted counterpart 204a in 96% yield.

Direct bis(triflyl)ethylation of N‐substituted pyrazoles 202.

4‐Thio‐ and 4‐seleno‐cyanated pyrazoles were synthesized using NH4SCN/KSeCN and PhICl2.[ 77 ] Wu et al. investigated the application of their previously reported PhICl2‐mediated electrophilic thiocyanation[ 78 ] to variously substituted pyrazole scaffolds. For example, treatment of phenylpyrazole 206 with either NH4SCN or KSeCN under inert conditions in toluene led to direct regioselective 4‐thiocyanation and 4‐selenocyanation, affording the products 207 and 208 in 87% and 79% yields, respectively (Scheme 39 ). Reactions proceeded via hypervalent iodine‐mediated electrophilic thio/selenocyanation and are likewise applicable to various 1,3,5‐trisubstituted pyrazoles.

Synthesis of 4‐substituted phenylpyrazoles via oxidative thio‐ and seleno‐cyanation.

Functionalization of 1,3‐Disubstituted Pyrazoles

Indirect approach can be employed for C‐4 functionalization of 1,3‐disubstituted pyrazoles. For instance, Kankala et al. synthesized fluoro‐phenylpyrazole‐picolinamide derivatives via cross‐coupling reaction catalyzed by in situ formed Pd‐N‐NHCs.[ 79 ] At first, Br atom was effectively introduced into the 4‐position of the pyrazole ring (Scheme 40 ). The obtained halogenated pyrazole 210 was further modified using isopropoxyboronic acid pinacol ester 211 in the presence of nBuLi to obtain fluorophenylpyrazole‐boran ester, which could subsequently participate in final cross‐coupling reaction. For new C—C bond formation, Pd(II)/NHC/PTC/Et3N system was successfully applied. 1,3‐Bis(2,4,6‐trimethylphenyl)‐imidazolinium chloride (Im‐Cl) was used as an NHC ligand, whereas tetrabutylammonium bromide (TBAB) was chosen as a phase‐transfer catalyst. Reaction under specified conditions resulted in the target trisubstituted pyrazole 214 in an excellent yield.

Indirect three‐step functionalization of the 1,3‐disubstituted pyrazole 209.

Yoshikawa et al. reported a similar strategy involving bromination as an initial step for indirect functionalization of pyrazoles.[ 80 ] For the synthesis of novel receptor interacting protein 1 (RIP1) kinase inhibitors, a multistep methodology was presented. As mentioned earlier, at first, bromination of the pyrazole 215 was conducted using Br2 in acetonitrile (Scheme 41 ). Further Suzuki–Miyaura coupling yielded the compound 217, in which the ethoxyvinyl group was transformed into the formylmethyl group, affording the pyrazole 218 in 94% yield. In the next step, reductive amination was performed with (S)‐3‐amino‐5‐methyl‐2,3‐dihydrobenzo[b][1,4]oxazepin‐4(5H)‐one hydrochloride (219) to give 1,3,4‐trisubstituted product 220. Finally, cyclization using AlMe3 in toluene provided the fused pyrazole derivative 221.

Multistep synthetic approach for the formation of fused pyrazole 221.

Similarly, Milišiūnaitė et al. described the iodination of pyrazole 222 ring at 4‐position with I2 in the presence of KOH in dimethylformamide (Scheme 42 ).[ 81 ] Successive Sonogashira coupling with (hetero)aryl‐ and alkylacetylenes 224 afforded 4‐ethynyl‐1‐phenyl‐1H‐pyrazol‐3‐ols 225 which were then utilized in Ag(I) ion‐mediated 5‐endo‐dig cyclization to afford the 2H‐furo[2,3‐c]pyrazoles 226a−i in fair to excellent yields.

Synthesis of 2H‐furo[2,3‐c]pyrazole ring systems 226.

Evidently, the incorporation of a formyl group into the pyrazole ring can play a crucial role in the generation of desired target compounds. Scheme 43 demonstrates another example, where the pyrazole 227 is formylated under classical Vilsmeier–Haack reaction conditions.[ 82 ] Thereafter, reduction of the aldehyde 228 in the presence of NaBH4 resulted in the primary alcohol 229. Compound 229 was chlorinated using thionyl chloride in refluxing benzene to afford 3‐[4‐(chloromethyl)‐1‐phenyl‐1H‐pyrazol‐3‐yl]‐2H‐chromen‐2‐one (230). Obtained product was used as an intermediate for the construction of new coumarin and pyrazole conjugates of benzimidazoles with an S linkage.

Formylation followed by reduction and chlorination at the C‐4 position of the pyrazole 227.

Vilsmeier–Haack reaction can also be employed to introduce a formyl group at the 4‐position of 1‐substituted 1H‐pyrazol‐3‐ols. In this particular case, protection, activation, or functionalization of the hydroxyl group can serve as a strategic tool for synthesizing diverse reaction products.[ 83 , 84 , 85 ] For example, sequential benzylation, formylation, and debenzylation can be followed by the activation of the hydroxyl group by treatment with triflic anhydride in the presence of triethylamine in dichloromethane (Scheme 44 ). The corresponding triflate 233 can be coupled with various alkyl and aryl acetylenes 234 using standard Sonogashira cross‐coupling conditions to give 3‐alkynyl‐1H‐pyrazole‐4‐carbaldehydes 235. Heating these intermediates in dry ammonia under pressure produces pyrazolo[4,3‐c]pyridines 236, some of which possess excellent anti‐mitotic activities.[ 86 ] Notably, the intermediates of this synthetic sequence enable further synthetic elaborations. For instance, the pyrazole‐4‐carbaldehyde 235b can be utilized in the preparation of compounds with photodynamic anticancer[ 87 ] and antiproliferative activities.[ 88 ]

Synthesis of the pyrazolo[4,3‐c]pyridines 236 from the 1‐substituted 1H‐pyrazol‐3‐ols 231.

Pyrazole functionalization can also be achieved using the Mannich reaction. It is a three‐component reaction involving the condensation of an amine, aldehyde, and active H‐containing compound. Originally, the third compound with acidic hydrogen next to the carbonyl group was an ester or a ketone; however, comprehensive investigation of the reaction revealed that other nucleophiles, such as nitroalkanes, acetylenes, and electron‐rich heterocycles, can be used as alternatives.[ 89 ]

Based on these findings, Mannich reaction was utilized to functionalize the 1,3‐substituted pyrazole 237 (Scheme 45 ).[ 90 ] Treatment of pyrazole 237 with methylamine and paraformaldehyde as a methylene source in refluxing ethanol afforded the pyrazol‐4‐yl‐N‐methylmethanamine 238 in 77% yield.

Pyrazole functionalization via Mannich reaction.

Tan et al. reported Ru‐catalyzed alkynylation using bromoalkynes.[ 91 ] The carboxylic acid derivative 239 was alkynylated using (bromoethynyl)‐triisopropylsilane (240) in tert‐amyl alcohol and [RuCl2(p‐cymene)] as a catalyst (Scheme 46 ). Reaction proceeded in the presence of a mild base without cyclization and resulted in the pyrazole 241 in high yield. Interestingly, this reaction can be applied to several carboxylic acid derivatives of many aza‐heterocyclic systems including pyridines, indoles, and quinolines.

Ru‐Catalyzed alkynylation of the pyrazole 239 with (bromoethynyl)‐triisopropylsilane.

Various methods have been reported for the synthesis of 4‐acylated pyrazole derivatives. Some classical approaches, such as Friedel–Crafts acylation, may not always be suitable for pyrazole functionalization. Other direct acylation methods comprise reactions with oxalyl chloride or the use of pyrazolic Weinreb amides, which require accurate precision and oxygen‐ and moisture‐free conditions.

Alternatively, using 1‐phenyl‐ and 1‐methyl‐1H‐pyrazol‐3‐ols 242 as starting materials, Milišiūnaitė et al. initially conducted O‐acylation with benzoyl chlorides 243 or benzoic acids 244 and obtained O‐acylated intermediates 245, which were subsequently subjected to Fries rearrangement with AlCl3 in carbon disulfide (Scheme 47 ).[ 92 ] Cyclization of the corresponding rearrangement products with K2CO3 in dimethylformamide provided the desired benzopyrano[2,3‐c]pyrazol‐4(2H)‐ones 247a−l, some of which were proven to possess anthelmintic activities.

Synthesis of the fused pyrazoles 247 via O‐acylation, Fries rearrangement, and subsequent cyclization reactions.

Poplavski et al. investigated a methyl sulfone‐vinamidinium reagent to access numerous heteroaromatic methyl sulfones, including bicyclo derivatives.[ 93 ] They performed experiments using a series of N,N‐ and N,C‐bis‐nucleophiles such as pyrazole derivatives. Treatment of 1‐methyl‐1H‐pyrazol‐3‐amine (248) with vinamidinium salt 249 in pyridine at 100 °C is one of these examples (Scheme 48 ). The reaction led to corresponding pyrazolo[3,4‐b]pyridine 250 in 92% yield. Notably, the proposed method for the construction of MeSO2‐derivatives worked well only for electron‐rich systems.

Reaction of aminopyrazole 248 with 1,3‐bis‐electrophile 250.

Functionalization of 1,5‐Disubstituted Pyrazoles

Efforts made in pyrazole chemistry were presented in the study reported by Merck Research Laboratories,[ 94 ] which covers several approaches for the synthesis of CHK1 inhibitor MK‐8776. Late‐stage indirect functionalization at the C‐4 position of the pyrazole ring is one of these synthetic routes (Scheme 49 ). After the preparation of the starting tert‐butyl 3‐(7‐aminopyrazolo[1,5‐a]pyrimidin‐5‐yl)piperidine‐1‐carboxylate (251), the amino group was protected using 2‐(trimethylsilyl)ethoxymethyl chloride (SEM‐Cl) followed by C‐4 iodination with N‐iodosuccinimide affording the pyrazole intermediate 252 in an overall 70% yield. In the next step, treatment with 1‐methylpyrazole boronate 253 under Suzuki conditions and subsequent deprotection provided 3‐(1‐methyl‐1H‐pyrazol‐4‐yl)‐5‐(piperidin‐3‐yl)pyrazolo[1,5‐a]pyrimidin‐7‐amine (254) in 73% yield. Finally, the pyrazole derivative 254 was subjected to chiral separation to afford a pure enantiomer, which was brominated in the last stage using N‐bromosuccinimide. Bromination occurred at the 6‐position of the pyrazolo[1,5‐a]pyrimidin‐7‐amine scaffold instead of at the unoccupied pyrazole ring positions, which possess lower reactivity. Therefore, the reaction resulted in the target inhibitor MK‐8776 in moderate yield.

Synthesis of pyrazole‐based MK‐8776 via late‐stage functionalization.

Mistico et al. described another interesting synthetic approach involving C‐4 functionalization of 1,5‐disubstituted pyrazoles.[ 95 ] Novel silylated pyrazole derivatives were obtained by Pd‐catalyzed C−H activation of a trimethylsilyl (TMS) group. To access target compounds, at first, C‐4 iodination of tetrahydropyranyl (THP)‐protected pyrazoles 255a,b with N‐iodosuccinimide and subsequent Suzuki‐Miyaura coupling with ortho‐bromophenyl boronic acids were conducted, and the intermediates 258a−d were isolated in 47−80% yields (Scheme 50 ). Thereafter, the bromoaryls 258a−c were converted by Li‐Br exchange and I2 trapping to acquire pyrazoles 259a−c with a rearranged TMS group. Halogenated pyrazoles 259 underwent cyclization reaction that relied on TMS group activation. Pd(OAc)2 and PCy3 were used as a catalyst and ligand, respectively, in the presence of PivOH and Cs2CO3 to obtain the silylated heterocycles 260a−c, which were deprotected and yielded the target compounds 261a−c.

Synthesis of 4,5‐fused silylated pyrazole derivatives by Pd‐catalyzed C−H activation of the TMS group.

Following the reported C‐5 > C‐4 > C‐3 activity trend for C—H bond activation against direct arylation,[ 96 ] Fuse et al. introduced aryl substituents at the 4‐position of 1,5‐disubstituted pyrazoles.[ 27 ] After the incorporation of aryl groups at N‐1 and C‐5 positions and achievement of the pyrazoles 262, a new C—C bond was regioselectively formed by treating the pyrazoles 262 with appropriate halogenated aryls in the presence of Pd(OAc)2 as a catalyst (Scheme 51 ). Although the introduction of strong EDG‐containing aryl substituents led to products 263c and 263i in low yields, EWG (263a,b,d,e,h), weak EDG (263 g), and heteroatom‐containing (263f) groups were adequately tolerated and resulted in moderate to good yields of products. Note that electronic factors of the substituents at 1‐ and 5‐positions exhibited slight influence on coupling reaction at 4‐position.

Direct arylation of 1,5‐disubstituted pyrazoles.

Kim et al. investigated ligand‐controlled regiodivergent C−H alkenylation of N‐methyl pyrazoles.[ 16 ] They demonstrated that by simply switching the catalysts, N‐methyl pyrazole can provide corresponding C‐4, C‐5, and C‐4,5 alkenylation products. Thus, after discovering the selective alkylation sequence between C‐4 and C‐5 positions, the obtained 5‐alkenylated pyrazole 264 was functionalized (Scheme 52 ). The latter was treated with styrene 265 using a Pd/Ag/PivOH system in dioxane to obtain dialkenylated pyrazole 266 in 74% yield, which was further successfully applied in a ring‐closure reaction to achieve the indazole 267.

Pd‐Catalyzed C‐4 alkenylation and subsequent cyclization of dialkenylated intermediate.

Kalshetti et al. examined Rh‐catalyzed cross‐dehydrogenative coupling of the pyrazole unit of the GBT‐440 scaffold with styrenes.[ 97 ] For the chosen approach, reaction conditions had to be optimized to achieve higher yields, which were affected by steric cluster around the pyrazole ring as well as carbaldehyde and unprotected hydroxyl groups. Under optimized conditions and in the presence of RhCp·Cl2, AsSbF6, AcOH, and Cu(OAc)2 in dichloroethane, the pyrazole 268 was separately treated with simple and conjugated olefins 269, resulting in the products 270a−n and 271a−f in moderate to good yields (Scheme 53 ).

Rh‐Catalyzed cross‐dehydrogenative coupling of the pyrazole 268 with olefins.

Shang et al. investigated Cu‐catalyzed C−H alkynylation at the 4‐position of pyrazole.[ 98 ] In the corresponding study, functionalization using terminal alkynes was prompted by Cu‐mediated reactions using amide‐oxazoline moiety as a directing group at the C‐5 position of the pyrazole ring. N‐Methyl pyrazole 272 was reacted with the 1‐ethynyl‐4‐methylbenzene coupling partner 273 under mild conditions utilizing Cu(OAc)2 and NaOAc in dimethyl sulfoxide (Scheme 54 ). The coupled product 274 was obtained in 63% yield. Reported method is also beneficial because the amide‐oxazoline group can be easily removed by treating the product under standard amide hydrolysis conditions to acquire a corresponding acid.

Cu(III)‐Mediated alkynylation of the 1,5‐disubstituted pyrazole 272.

Pyrazoles prefunctionalized with thiocyano functional group at C‐4 position can undergo one‐pot microwave‐assisted reactions, leading to pyrazolopyrimidines.[ 99 ] To prepare the starting compounds, the aminopyrazoles 275a,b were subjected to a two‐step synthetic route for transformation into the 5‐amino‐4‐thiocyanato‐pyrazoles 276a,b (Scheme 55 ). In the next step, the obtained thiocyanato‐pyrazoles 276 were treated with the coupling reagent PBr3 in formamide (Vilsmeier reagent) at 130 °C under microwave irradiation (15 W) for 15 min. These reactions resulted in the pyrazolopyrimidines 278 and pyrazolothiazoles 279, which were obtained as major and minor products, respectively.

Thiocyanation and subsequent one‐pot synthesis of pyrazolopyrimidines and pyrazolothiazoles.

Plausible mechanism via two different pathways was proposed. First suggested path involves four steps: acid‐catalyzed cascade dethiocyanation/cyclization, Vilsmeier reaction, ring expansion, and finally deamination, leading to pyrazolopyrimidines. In the second path, pyrazolothiazole formation proceeds via nucleophilic heterocyclization, comprising sulphur nucleophilic reaction and cyanamide elimination. Second pathway is less favorable, resulting in the formation of 279 as minor products.

Zhang et al. reported electrocatalytic C−H/S−H coupling of amino pyrazoles and thiophenols.[ 100 ] Developed method was suitable for the C‐4 functionalization of a wide range of 1,3,5‐trisubstituted pyrazoles. C−H/S−H Coupling was also effective for 1,5‐disubstituted pyrazole, extending its applicability beyond 1,3,5‐trisubstituted pyrazoles. As depicted in Scheme 56 , 5‐amino‐1‐phenyl‐1H‐pyrazole (280) was treated with 4‐methylthiophenol (281) using NH4I as a mediator and NH4BF4 as an electrolyte. Reaction was performed in an undivided cell with a constant 5 mA current, equipped with two Pt plate electrodes at room temperature, affording the pyrazole thioether derivative 282 in 86% yield. When the reaction was carried out under standard conditions without electrolysis, no desired product was noticed, indicating that electricity was essential for this C−H/S−H coupling reaction.

Functionalization of 1,3,5‐Trisubstituted Pyrazoles

During the past few years, Sawant's group achieved some interesting progress in the field of C‐4 functionalization of 1,3,5‐trisubstituted pyrazoles. In 2022, a sustainable protocol was established, covering halogenation of 3,5‐dimethyl‐1‐aryl‐1H‐pyrazoles (Scheme 57 ).[ 101 ] In the reported study, initially, 1‐aryl‐pyrazoles 283 were treated with tetrabutylammonium iodide (TBAI) in the presence of Oxone (potassium monopersulfate) as an oxidant under microwave irradiation. Unexpectedly, the reactions were rapid and highly efficient, providing the products 284a−m in good to excellent yields. TBAB and TBAC were also applied to expand the scope of this methodology, resulting in the 4‐halogenated pyrazoles 285a−l and 286a−j, respectively. This protocol is considered promising as it is in agreement with the principles of green chemistry and avoids the use of transition‐metals and toxic solvents.

TBAX/Oxone‐mediated halogenation of pyrazoles at the C‐4 position.

Same research group further explored the application of Oxone in pyrazole chemistry. New method for the insertion of methyl group during C—C bond formation was disclosed, resulting in divergent bis‐pyrazole molecules.[ 102 ] Methyl group of dimethyl sulfoxide was activated by Oxone and introduced between two C(sp2)—H bonds of two pyrazole moieties (Scheme 58 ). After establishing the optimal conditions, the scope of this method was analyzed to evaluate the generality of the reaction.

Oxone‐DMSO‐triggered methylene insertion to access bis‐pyrazole derivatives.

Various pyrazoles bearing diverse substituents on the phenyl ring were applied to access symmetric and unsymmetric pyrazoles 289a−v and 290a−j, respectively (Scheme 43). In this protocol, dimethyl sulfoxide was used as both a solvent and methylene source. Application of dimethyl sulfoxide is an advantageous option due to its low toxicity and possibility to produce the solvent in renewable processes. Obtained bis‐pyrazoles are attractive synthetic species as the inserted methyl bridge can be oxidized to access carbonyl functional group, which can subsequently undergo other transformations such as reduction or amination.

Interestingly, during control experiments, a 4‐methyl(thiomethyl) substituted pyrazole intermediate was isolated. After additional optimization, it was revealed that KOAc additive together with the Oxone‐DMSO system afforded pyrazoles with 4‐methyl(thiomethyl) substituents 292a−e in 70−82% yields (Scheme 59 ).

Oxone‐DMSO‐triggered methyl thiomethylation of pyrazoles.

More recently, Kour and coworkers reported their findings regarding Se incorporation into bis‐pyrazole systems.[ 103 ] Optimization revealed that by varying the amount of SeO2, mono‐ or di‐selenylation of pyrazoles can be achieved. Developed method was investigated using several 1,3,5‐trisubstituted pyrazole substrates 293 (Scheme 60 ). Simple 3,5‐dimethyl‐1‐phenyl‐1H‐pyrazole along with mono‐ and di‐substituted phenyl scaffolds were well tolerated under the optimized conditions without significant impact of different substituents on their ortho‐, meta‐, and para‐positions. When two equivalents of SeO2 was used, mono‐selenides 294a−q were obtained; in contrast, three equivalents of Se source led to diselenylated pyrazole systems 295a−m.

Controlled mono‐ and di‐selenylation of 3,5‐dimethyl‐1‐aryl‐1H‐pyrazoles.

Bonacorso et al. introduced a trifluoromethyl moiety at the 4‐position of the pyrazole ring.[ 104 ] Possible insertion of a trifluoromethyl group during ring formation or at a later stage results in the incorporation of CF3 substituent mostly at C‐3 and C‐5 positions. Therefore, the new approach was focused on the functionalization of pre‐synthesized 1,3,5‐trisubstituted pyrazole derivatives. At first, iodination of the starting pyrazoles 296a−h was performed (Scheme 61 ). As the pyrazole ring was highly deactivated by the CF3 group at 5‐position, iodination was based on the in situ formation of iodine trifluoroacetate species from a mixture of trifluoroacetic acid and N‐iodosuccinimide. Produced reactive electrophile allows iodination at C‐4 position in a relatively short time and provides the iodinated pyrazoles 297a−h in 75−87% yield.

Synthesis of the pyrazoles 298a−h through sequential iodination‐trifluoromethylation reaction.

Thereafter, iodine was exchanged using methyl fluorosulfonyldifluoroacetate (MFSDA). To successfully introduce a trifluoromethyl group, CuI and HMPA ligand had to be used. HMPA also acts as a cosolvent, which selectively solvates the formed cations and accelerates nucleophilic substitution. Under the optimized conditions, seven 4,5‐bis(trifluoromethyl)‐1H‐pyrazoles were obtained, and diaryl pyrazoles were the most tolerated substrates (298e−h) as compared to pyrazoles containing methyl and isopropyl substituents at C‐3 position (298a and 298c). Interestingly, not even traces of the tetrasubstituted pyrazole 298d with 3‐nBu were detected under these reaction conditions (Scheme 61).

Recently, Chen et al. reported another interesting finding regarding C(sp2)–H sulfonylmethylation of 5‐amino‐pyrazoles 299a−h (Scheme 62 ).[ 105 ] Sulfonylmethylation was conducted in the presence of glyoxylic acid and various sulfinates 301 in water to achieve the target pyrazoles 302a−p in good to excellent yields. This methodology is very versatile, as it can be used with diverse sodium benzene‐, naphthalen‐2‐yl‐, and alkylsulfinates; furthermore, phenyl groups with either EWGs or EDGs at the N‐1 position are also compatible. Reaction mechanism was proposed using the compound 299a as an example, where 5‐aminopyrazole reacts with glyoxylic acid to form the condensation product A, which further undergoes elimination of the starting compound 299a to generate the intermediate B. In the next step, reaction with sodium tosylate yields the intermediate C, which after subsequent decarboxylation gives the final derivative 302a.

Sulfonylmethylation of 5‐amino‐pyrazoles 299a−h and proposed reaction mechanism.

Another possible strategy for C‐4 functionalization of polysubstituted pyrazoles involves the use of orthogonally modifiable groups. Fricero et al. analyzed a combination of halogen and BF3K substituents at 4‐ and 5‐positions, respectively, to facilitate regiocontrolled derivatization of pyrazoles.[ 106 ] Initially, the trisubstituted pyrazoles 303 were treated with N‐chlorosuccinimide to insert Cl atom at the vacant position of the ring (Scheme 63 ). Introduction of Cl instead of Br was chosen because bromination of the respective pyrazoles resulted in the partial displacement of the required BF3K group.

Orthogonal functionalization of polysubstituted pyrazoles.

Using a selection of chlorinated pyrazole trifluoroborates 304, at first, Suzuki‐Miyaura cross‐coupling was applied to differentiate between borate and halide groups. Reaction conditions were investigated such that a competitive reaction at the Cl‐substituted position could be prevented. Combination of Pd(OAc)2, XPhos, and Et3N successfully led to the corresponding 5‐arylated products 305, leaving the Cl substituent intact (Scheme 63). Consecutive coupling of the chlorinated pyrazoles 305 with respective boronic acids proceeded in the presence of Buchwald's XPhosPdG2 as a catalyst and yielded the fully functionalized pyrazoles 306a−f in 59−91% yields.

Gao et al. described highly enantioselective arylation reactions using a chiral phosphoric acid (CPA) catalyst under mild conditions.[ 107 ] After performing optimization reactions to examine the impacts of catalyst, solvent, and temperature on stereoselectivity, appropriate reaction conditions were chosen. At first, 1‐(tert‐butyl)‐3‐phenyl‐1H‐pyrazol‐5‐amine was treated with various 1,4‐quinone esters using the CPA‐type catalyst 309 in dichloromethane at room temperature (Scheme 64 ). Direct arylations were completed in 30 min, affording the axially chiral products 310a−l in 82−95% yields with high enantioselectivities (91−98% ee). Additionally, when the ester group was replaced with an amide, the arylated pyrazole derivative 310 m was obtained; nevertheless, the yield and enantioselectivity were lower, 79% and 81%, respectively.

Enantioselective arylation of the 3‐aryl‐5‐aminopyrazoles 307 with quinone derivatives via CPA catalysis.

Further investigation involved reactions of different 3‐aryl‐5‐aminopyrazoles with 2‐(methoxycarbonyl)‐1,4‐benzoquinone. Under the same reaction conditions, the products 311a−g were isolated in good yields with 97−99% enantioselectivities (Scheme 64). 3‐Thiophene scaffold on the pyrazole ring was also well tolerated, resulting in the derivative 311h with excellent stereoselectivity. Subsequently, the suitability of different substituents at the N‐1 position of the pyrazole ring was examined, and the axially chiral pyrazoles 311i−o were acquired in moderate yields with high enantioselectivities. Moreover, the proposed methodology was proven effective for gram‐scale synthesis.

Similarly, Luo et al. investigated atroposelective construction of naphthylpyrazoles via CPA‐catalyzed cross‐coupling reactions.[ 108 ] In their study, 5‐aminopyrazoles were treated with a naphthoquinone esters using another CPA‐type catalyst 314, in the presence of 4 Å molecular sieves in xylene at 0 °C (Scheme 65 ). Specified conditions proved to be efficient for a broad range of 5‐aminopyrazoles with various substituents. N‐Aryl‐5‐aminopyrazoles bearing both EDGs and EWGs facilely reacted with methyl 1,4‐dioxonaphthalene‐2‐carboxylate, affording axially chiral naphthylpyrazoles 315a–r in 76–99% yields with typically high enantioselectivity (64–99% ee). Aminopyrazoles with aliphatic substituents at N‐1 position were also appropriate substrates, resulting in respective products 315s−u in 92–99% yields; however, electron‐donating aliphatic groups led to reduced reaction enantioselectivity. Replacing bulky tert‐butyl substituent with methyl resulted in the racemic product 315v, indicating the importance of steric effects.

CPA‐Catalyzed enantioselective cross‐coupling of the pyrazoles 312 with naphthoquinone esters.

Subsequent variation in the naphthoquinone ester group revealed that increasing the size of the substituent decreased both yield and enantioselectivity of the reaction. As shown in Scheme 65, the naphthylpyrazoles 316a−d were obtained in 59−74% yields with 73−96% enantioselectivities. Notably, naphthoquinone without an ester group still participated in the reaction, providing the product 316e in a considerably lower yield (56%), yet retaining a high enantioselectivity of 96%, suggesting that ester group may not be essential for the stereocontrol of the reaction. Furthermore, the applicability of the reported synthetic strategy was demonstrated by synthesizing the chiral naphthylpyrazole 315a on gram‐scale and subjecting it to further modifications.

Cu‐Promoted switchable synthesis of pyrazole‐fused pyridazines and pyrazines have been reported.[ 109 ] At first, the aminopyrazoles 317 were treated with benzoyl peroxide (BPO) as an oxidant in the presence of Cu(OAc)2 as a catalyst and K2S2O8 as an additive in toluene at 100 °C (Scheme 66 ). These reactions were suitable for the 3‐methylpyrazole substrates, having N‐methyl, N‐2‐naphthyl, and N‐phenyl substituents with various EDGs and EWGs, resulting in the dimerization products 318a−k in 50−79% yields. Additionally, changes in the starting pyrazole substituents at C‐3 from methyl to ethyl, phenyl, thiophen‐2‐yl, and 4‐fluoro‐phenyl groups were also acceptable, yielding the fused pyrazolo‐pyridazines 318l−o. Interestingly, when either R1 or R2 substituents on the pyrazole ring were tert‐butyl groups, the fused pyrazolo‐pyrazines 319a−c were isolated.

Cu‐Promoted dimerization of 5‐aminopyrazoles leading to the formation of pyrazolo‐pyridazines.

Motivated by unexpected findings, Chai et al. further investigated the proposed methodology to obtain fused pyrazolo‐pyrazines (Scheme 67 ). After screening various reaction conditions, the use of CuI2 as a catalyst together with 1,10‐phenanthroline as a ligand in the presence of tert‐butyl peroxybenzoate (TBPB) as an oxidant and Na2CO3 was determined to be the most effective for this purpose. Under given conditions, the aminopyrazoles 320 underwent dimerization, leading to the pyrazolo‐pyrazines 321a−m and 319a−c. Notably, the yields of the products 319a−c were lower than those achieved by the initial method.

Cu‐Promoted synthesis of pyrazolo‐pyrazines from 5‐aminopyrazoles.

C−H Functionalization of Pyrazoles at C‐3 Position

C‐3 position of the pyrazole ring possesses lower activity as compared to those of the C‐4 and C‐5 positions. Consequently, functionalization at this position is poorly investigated and only a limited number of examples are available in the literature. However, during the synthesis of polysubstituted pyrazoles, C−H functionalization can be achieved by utilizing different strategies, for example, indirect functionalization by switching the functional groups or blocking more reactive positions.

To overcome the low reactivity of the C‐3 position of the pyrazole ring, a SEM‐switch methodology was developed.[ 96 ] This protocol involves SEM‐group transfer from one N atom to another, which converts the unreactive 3‐position of the pyrazole ring to the reactive C‐5 position, enabling subsequent C−H arylation. As depicted in Scheme 68 , 4‐phenyl‐1‐SEM‐1H‐pyrazole (322) was directly arylated at the 5‐position of the ring to form 1,4,5‐trisubstituted pyrazoles 323a−d. After introducing the second aryl substituent, the goal was to perform successive arylation at the vacant C‐3 position. Achieving this goal by direct arylation is challenging due to the low reactivity of the C‐3 site. To address this issue, SEM‐group transposition was employed based on the findings that a mixture of N‐protected imidazoles can be equilibrated by heating with alkylating agents, leading to an isomerization product.[ 110 ] Application of this idea to the SEM‐pyrazoles 323a−d allowed to shift the SEM group by heating the corresponding pyrazoles with SEM‐Cl in acetonitrile, and affording the target products in 80−91% yields. Treatment with SEM‐Cl resulted in a pyrazolium salt, which enabled the equilibration of the two regioisomers, eventually producing less hindered and thermodynamically more favorable 1,3,4‐trisubstituted products 324a−d. This approach offers access to further functionalization at the more active C‐5 position, facilitating the attainment of fully functionalized pyrazole derivatives.

Pyrazole functionalization by SEM‐group transposition.

Use of an N‐THP protective group is another possible route to obtain 3‐functionalized pyrazoles. For example, Ahmed et al. described sequential one‐pot synthesis of 1,3‐disubstituted pyrazoles.[ 111 ] At first, the 1H‐pyrazole 325 was protected using 3,4‐dihydro‐2H‐pyran (DHP) to afford its tetrahydropyran derivative 1‐THP‐pyrazole 326 (Scheme 69 ). In the next step, the pyrazole 326 was treated with nBuLi followed by reaction with 1‐bromohexane. As mentioned earlier, the THP protective group promotes ortho‐deprotonation owing to the coordination of the Li countercation with the O atom present in the THP ring, leading to the unreactive 5‐substituted 1‐THP‐pyrazoles 327. Although isomerization of 5‐alkyl‐ to 3‐alkyl‐1‐THP‐pyrazoles can be accomplished by heating reaction mixture at high temperature,[ 112 ] this reaction can be greatly shortened by adding catalytic amount of I2. Corresponding reaction mechanism is similar to that proposed for SEM group translocation in SEM‐protected pyrazoles.[ 96 ] If required, additional C‐5 functionalization can be further accessed under the same reaction conditions, that is, using nBuLi and alkyl halides in tetrahydrofuran at −78 °C. The obtained 3,5‐dialkyl‐1‐THP‐pyrazole 329 can be deprotected by the treatment with aqueous concentrated HCl to yield the 3,5‐alkyl‐1H‐pyrazole 330.

Sequential one‐pot synthesis of the 3,5‐substituted pyrazole 330. Conversion was determined by 1H NMR.

Thermal rearrangement can also serve as an indirect route to acquire 3‐functionalized pyrazoles. Dufter‐Münster et al. investigated lithium nitropyrazolates as potential pyrotechnic colorants, and therefore, part of their study covered selective nitration of unsubstituted pyrazoles (Scheme 70 ).[ 113 ] As the nitration of 1H‐pyrazole occurs at C‐4 position,[ 114 ] the NO2 group was introduced at the C‐3 position via indirect functionalization.

Indirect introduction of NO2 group at the C‐3 position by thermal rearrangement and subsequent nitration reactions.

Initially, the pyrazole 331 was treated with a HNO3/AcOH mixture followed by Ac2O to obtain 1‐nitro‐1H‐pyrazole (332) (Scheme 70). As N‐nitropyrazoles are less stable than the C‐substituted analogs, thermal rearrangement can be achieved via a sigmatropic shift and tautomerization of 3H‐pyrazoles. Thus, pyrazole 332 can easily undergo thermal rearrangement in benzonitrile at 180 °C, resulting in the 3‐nitro‐1H‐pyrazole (333). After this stage, pyrazole can be further modified by inserting an additional nitro group either at the C‐4 position to yield the pyrazole 334 or again at the N‐1 position followed by rearrangement to acquire the 3,5‐dinitropyrazole 336.

Another example of C‐3 functionalization in polysubstituted pyrazoles involves a gram‐scale procedure reported by Tairov et al.[ 115 ] A set of 1‐methyl‐5‐(trifluoromethyl)‐1H‐pyrazoles containing boron pinacolate and carboxylic acid functional groups was synthesized. At first, electrophilic bromination was conducted to introduce a Br atom at the C‐4 position of the ring, which functioned as a directing group (Scheme 71 ). Direct ortho‐metalation was achieved by treating the brominated pyrazole 338 with LDA solution in tetrahydrofuran/hexane followed by trapping the organolithium intermediate 339 with dry ice to afford the corresponding pyrazole‐3‐carboxylic acid 340. Simultaneously, intermediate 339 was subjected to reaction with 2‐isopropoxy‐4,4,5,5‐tetramethyl‐1,3,2‐dioxaborolane (342), leading to the boronic acid pinacolate 343. Finally, Br was cleaved by hydrogenation, yielding the 1,3,5‐trisubstituted pyrazoles 341 and 344.

Gram‐scale synthesis of 1,3,5‐trisubstituted pyrazoles via sequential lithiation‐functionalization‐debromination.

Design and application of novel catalysts for the functionalization of N‐heterocycles are still only emerging. Perfluoroalkylation of heteroarenes using perfluoroalkyl bromides in the presence of nanocatalysts was recently developed.[ 116 ] Following the general interest in replacing standard catalysts, Zhang et al. established a convenient methodology using N‐doped C‐supported Co nanoparticles. Reaction optimization was performed using neat 4‐methylpyridine and a set of different heterocycles. Unexpectedly, the designed method was also applicable to the 1‐methyl‐1H‐pyrazole (345) (Scheme 72 ). Use of the specific Co‐based nanocatalyst Co/N‐C‐800 (N‐doped C‐supported Co) allowed selective perfluoroalkylation at C‐3 position, generating derivative 347 as the major product.

Selective perfluoroalkylation of the methylpyrazole 345 in the presence of Co/N‐C‐800 as a nano catalyst.

O’Sullivan et al. described an interesting finding regarding regioselective C‐3 functionalization of 4,5‐unsubstituted pyrazoles.[ 117 ] As the regioselective synthesis of C‐3 functionalized pyrazoles remains challenging due to the low reactivity at this position, this direct and mild approach is of significant importance. Regioselective path to 3‐hydroxyarylated pyrazoles was realized through the reaction of pyrazole N‐oxides 350 with 2‐TMS‐phenyl trifluoromethanesulfonates 351 (Scheme 73 ). Coupling reaction with N‐benzyl, N‐aryl, and N‐methyl pyrazole N‐oxides afforded the products 352a−j in 58−89% yields. Various functional groups, for example, CF3, OMe, CN, and CO2Et, in the phenyl ring were well tolerated. However, alkene at para‐position and bulky isopropyl at ortho‐position led to low product yields (58% and 60%, respectively). Furthermore, several experiments were conducted to evaluate the reactions with different arynes. For this purpose, 1‐benzyl‐1H‐pyrazole 2‐oxide was treated with various aryne precursors, successfully resulting in the 3‐functionalized pyrazoles 353a−f.

Regioselective C‐3 functionalization of N‐benzyl, N‐aryl, and N‐methyl pyrazole N‐oxides.

Donaldson et al. reported straightforward preparation of diverse heterocycles via Rh‐catalyzed C−H activation, using norbornadiene as an acetylene equivalent.[ 118 ] Their study began with benzoic acid derivatives and expanded toward the analysis of numerous heterocycles including pyrazoles. As demonstrated in Scheme 74 , pyrazole O‐pivaloyl hydroxamic acids 354 were treated with norbornadiene (355) using [Cp·RhCl2]2 as a catalyst and cesium pivalate in trifluoroethanol. Reactions were stirred under microwave‐irradiation for 1 h at 170 °C to achieve fused pyrazolo[4,3‐c]pyridin‐4‐ones 356a and 356b in 88% and 87% yields, respectively. Note that several protecting groups were examined in the reported methodology, revealing that groups such as Boc, THP and Ts were labile under the given reaction conditions. In these cases, unprotected products were obtained; in contrast, the p‐methoxybenzyl (PMB) protective group remained stable, as noticed in the fused pyrazole 356a.

Microwave‐assisted Rh‐catalyzed annulation of pyrazole O‐pivaloyl hydroxamic acids.

Another study reported N‐heteroaryl C−H functionalization via dearomative addition−hydrogen autotransfer.[ 119 ] This study covers a broad substrate scope including an important example for pyrazole C‐3 functionalization. In this work, 1‐(4‐fluorophenyl)‐1H‐pyrazole‐4‐carbaldehyde (357) was treated with isoprene (358) by catalyzing the reaction with RuHCl(CO)(PPh3)3 and using Xantphos as a ligand in toluene under an Ar atmosphere (Scheme 75 ). Reaction proceeded regioselectively, resulting in the 3‐functionalized pyrazole 359 in a high yield (93%).

Ru‐Catalyzed C−C Coupling of isoprene with the pyrazole 357.

N−H Functionalization of 1H‐Pyrazoles

Regioselective N‐functionalization of pyrazoles is a particular challenge owing to annular N,N‐prototropy.[ 10 , 120 ] Reaction outcome and ratio of regioisomeric products may depend on the use of protecting or directing groups, bases, catalysts, or solvents.

N‐Alkylation of 4‐bromopyrazole 360 with alkyl bromides 361 was performed using tetrabutylammonium hydrogen sulfate (TBAHS) as a catalyst under solvent‐free conditions in the presence of sodium hydroxide to afford N‐alkyl‐4‐bromopyrazole derivatives 362a−c (Scheme 76 ).[ 121 ] By this method, the reaction smoothly occurred and necessitated easy workup, affording C3‐C6 carbon chain successfully appended at the N1‐position of 4‐bromopyrazole. Long‐chain alkyl halides slowly reacted as compared to the cases of short‐chain alkyl halides.

4‐Bromo‐1H‐pyrazole alkylation with alkyl bromides.

Enache et al. described a general method for ultrasound‐assisted methylation of pyrazoles.[ 122 ] Pyrazole derivatives 363a‐d were pretreated with TBAB in water, followed by addition of KOH and sonication for 5 min. Subsequent addition of excess methyl iodide followed by stirring of the reaction mixture at room temperature afforded the compounds 364a−d in 45–66% yields (Scheme 77 ).

Methylation of 4‐styryl‐1H‐pyrazoles with methyl iodide.

Mitsunobu reaction of symmetrical ethyl 4‐pyrazolecarboxylate 366 with the D‐ribose derivative 365 in the presence of PPh3 and diisopropylazodicarboxylate (DIAD) in tetrahydrofuran provided the pyrazole ethyl ester nucleoside 367 in 92% yield (Scheme 78 ). Additionally, the high regioselectivity of the Mitsunobu reaction of unsymmetrical ethyl 3‐pyrazolecarboxylate 368 with D‐ribose derivative 365 led to the corresponding pyrazole ethyl ester nucleoside 369 in 85% yield.[ 123 ]

Mitsunobu reaction of symmetrical and unsymmetrical ethyl 1H‐pyrazolescarboxylates 366 and 368 with the D‐ribose derivative 365.

Iškauskienė et al. reported an efficient protocol offering easy access to highly functionalized heterocyclic compounds as novel amino acid building blocks, which were developed by coupling alkyl pyrazolecarboxylates with N‐Boc‐3‐iodoazetidine.[ 17 ] When ethyl 1H‐pyrazole‐4‐carboxylate 370 was coupled with N‐Boc‐3‐hydroxyazetidine 371a under Mitsunobu reaction conditions, the yield of the target 1‐(N‐Boc‐azetidin‐3‐yl)‐1H‐pyrazole‐4‐carboxylate 372 reached only 25% (Scheme 79 ). To improve the N‐functionalization outcome, the alcohol 371a was transformed into a good leaving group‐containing methyl sulfonate 371b and resulting active agent was reacted with pyrazole‐4‐carboxylate 370 in dimethylformamide in the presence of Cs2CO3 to obtain the desired compound 372 in 78% yield. After this result, alkyl iodide 371c was also successfully used to acquire the target product 372 in 84% yield.

Coupling of the ethyl 1H‐pyrazole‐4‐carboxylate (370) with the 3‐functionalized N‐Boc‐azetidines 371a−c.

Huang et al. extensively investigated N‐functionalization reactions of 3(5)‐substituted pyrazoles.[ 124 ] Initially, the scope of N‐substitution was evaluated using 3(5)‐nitropyrazole. Regioselective functionalization utilizing a K2CO3−DMSO system was examined using various alkyl and aryl halides as substrates. Consequently, n‐alkyl halides, which are more reactive and less sterically hindered electrophiles, provided higher yields and regioselectivity. Moreover, the secondary halide substrates resulted in low reaction rates, which implied that the reactions exhibited an S N 2 character. Under the same reaction conditions, N‐functionalization with benzyl bromide was conducted using the pyrazoles 373a−d with different substituents, that is, CO2Et, CF3, CH3 and NO2, at the 3(5) position to achieve the pyrazoles 375a−d and 376a−d (Scheme 80 ). Calculations were also performed on the corresponding pyrazoles to clarify the regioselectivity. For instance, optimization for the 3‐CO2Et pyrazolates indicates that the N‐1 and N‐2 atoms exhibit similar nucleophilicity, and therefore, the regioselectivity is expected to be low, which is consistent with experimental observations. Substantial difference between the regioselectivities of the 3‐nitro and 3‐ester pyrazoles suggests the major role of the electronic effect of R in regioselectivity when the R groups are similar in size (A‐values: −NO2 = 1.1 vs −CO2Et = 1.2 kcal/mol).

Regioselective N‐functionalization of 3(5)‐substituted pyrazoles.

Zagorskytė et al. reported the use of sulfonylated glycerol carbonate as an efficient N‐alkylation reagent for pyrazoles.[ 19 ] In this study, ethyl 1H‐pyrazole‐5(3)‐carboxylates 377a−e were treated with 3‐O‐tosylated glycerol‐1,2‐carbonate 378 using Cs2CO3 as a base in dimethylformamide (Scheme 81 ). Reactions led to N‐alkylated ethyl pyrazole‐5‐carboxylates 379a−e and pyrazole‐3‐carboxylates 380a−e in very good total yields (78−88%). In all the reactions, pyrazole‐5‐carboxylates were obtained as major products, and best regioselectivity was observed in the reaction with sterically hindered 5‐tBu‐pyrazole‐3‐carboxylate 377e.

N‐Alkylation of the pyrazole carboxylates 377a−e with the 3‐O‐tosylated glycerol‐1,2‐carbonate 378.

Khursan et al. analyzed the reactivity of 3‐poly‐fluoroalkylated pyrazoles.[ 125 ] They investigated unsymmetric polyfluoroalkylpyrazoles 381a−d and their 4‐substituted counterparts 381e−g as nucleophiles in methylation reactions (Scheme 82 ). At first, methylation was conducted under standard conditions using methyl iodide in the presence of K2CO3 in refluxing acetonitrile. The regioisomers 382a−g were acquired as major products with exceptional selectivity in the case of pyrazole 381c as 382c:383c product ratio was 93:7. Interesting results were obtained when reactions were performed using dimethyl sulfate as a methylating agent. By treating pyrazoles 381a−g with 5‐fold excess of dimethyl sulfate, regioselectivity of methylation was reversed to favor the formation of the isomeric products 383a−g, where the highest selectivity was achieved with the pyrazoles bearing phenyl substituent at the C‐5 position of the ring (383a,b,d,e). Additional experimental and theoretical investigations revealed that in the first case methylation proceeded via an anionic mechanism, whereas in the second case, it progressed via a cationic mechanism.

Regioselective methylation of the polysubstituted pyrazoles 381a−g.

Wright and coworkers addressed the regioselectivity problem by introducing removable triorganosilyl groups at the C‐3(5) position to direct the N‐alkylation of pyrazole.[ 18 ] Results of their study indicated that alkylation of the pyrazoles 384a−d with the PMB protected pyrazole derivative 385 using powdered K2CO3 in CH3CN at 20 °C, consistently favored the generation of the regioisomers 387a−d, whereas alkylation of 384e under the same reaction conditions preferred the formation of the regioisomer 386e (Scheme 83 ). Alkylation can be sterically directed using the triphenylsilyl group to provide 1H‐pyrazolecarboxylates, from which the triphenylsilyl group can be eliminated using Bu4NF, in synthetically useful yields.

N‐Alkylation of ethyl 3‐(triorganosilyl)‐substituted‐1H‐pyrazole‐5‐carboxylates.

As expected, during the development of the N‐alkylation of 3‐phenylpyrazole 388 with 2‐bromo‐N,N‐dimethylacetamide (389), the N1‐alkylated regioisomer 390 was selectively produced in 97:3 ratio (390/391) under basic conditions (1,8‐diazabicyclo[5.4.0]undec‐7‐ene (DBU) in tetrahydrofuran) in 60% yield.[ 20 ] Nevertheless, it was discovered that when using MgCl2 (50 mol%), the N2‐alkylated isomer 391 was preferentially generated in a 2:98 ratio (390/391) (Scheme 84 ).

N‐Alkylation of the 3‐phenyl‐1H‐pyrazole 388 with the 2‐bromo‐acetamide derivative 389.

Moraes et al. reported notable N‐derivatization of the CF3‐pyrazole ring 392 with 5‐bromo enaminones 393 and 5‐bromo enones 395.[ 126 ] Reaction of pyrazoles 392 with 5‐bromo enaminones 393 afforded the regioisomers 394 a−c via nucleophilic substitution (Scheme 85 ). Under the same conditions, that is, using K2CO3 as a base in refluxing acetonitrile, the active intermediate was formed by the conjugated addition of the pyrazole N1 to the β‐C atom of the 5‐bromo enone 395, rather than the standard nucleophilic substitution. Consequently, the cyclic hemiaminal ethers 396a−e were obtained. Interestingly, an increase in the size or number of substituents exhibited no impact on the reaction regio‐ and chemoselectivity.

Regioselective pyrazole alkylation using 5‐bromo enones and enaminones.

Interesting findings in pyrazole chemistry involve the application of enzyme engineering. In nature, heteroatom alkylation is conducted by enzymes with unprecedented precision. As pyrazoles are rarely found in nature,[ 127 ] pyrazole methyltransferases (MT) are presently unknown. Accordingly, MT was engineered to offer access to regioselective methyl group transfer; in this regard Bencel et al. set up the cyclic enzyme cascade for the alkylation of pyrazoles (Scheme 86 ).[ 128 ] Enzymatic selective alkylation of pyrazoles with haloalkanes was performed using the enzyme v36 with NSA‐synthase as a catalyst. Reactions were conducted using the pyrazole 397 as a substrate and 10 equiv. of haloalkane at 30 °C (for the iodoethane 398b and iodopropane 398c) and 37 °C (for the iodomethane 398a). Enzymatic alkylation of 3(5)‐cyclopropyl‐1H‐pyrazoles 397 generated 1‐methyl‐ (399a), 1‐ethyl‐ (399b) and 1‐propyl‐5‐cyclo‐1H‐propylpyrazole (399c) with very high regioselectivities of >99, >98, and >97%, respectively. To demonstrate that enzymatic pyrazole alkylation can be performed on a preparative scale (1.0 mmol), the methylated pyrazole 399a was synthesized using iodomethane as alkyl donor. Corresponding product was acquired in 37% isolated yield with a very high regioselectivity (97%).

Methylation of pyrazoles proceeding with the support of engineered enzymes.

Wang et al. described a general direct procedure for the catalytic construction of aminopiperidine‐azoles from phenylcarbamate‐protected 1,2‐dihydropyridine and azoles.[ 129 ] Pd‐Catalyzed N—H insertion into the double bond of 1,2‐dihydropyridine was a key step in this procedure. By determining the optimal reaction conditions, the use of [Pd(allyl)Cl]2 in combination with the DPEphos ligand was determined to be the most effective for obtaining pyrazole products with good regioselectivities. For example, the aminopiperidine‐pyrazoles (±)‐403a−c were acquired from the pyrazoles 401 in high yields with high regioselectivities ((±)‐403a−c/(±)‐404a−c, rr > 20/1) (Scheme 87 ). After evaluating different chiral ligands, Taniaphos was chosen for asymmetric catalysis. Selected pyrazoles participated in the same reaction with the Taniaphos‐based catalyst system; however, this system demonstrated lower reactivity as compared to that of the DPEphos‐based system. Therefore, N‐substituted pyrazoles (R)‐403a−c were achieved in moderate yields and exhibited high enantioselectivity. Nevertheless, compounds (R)‐403a and (R)‐403c showed reduced regioselectivities: rr = 3/1 and rr = 1.3/1, respectively.

Pd‐Catalyzed hydroamination of the 1,2‐dihydropyridine 402 with the pyrazoles 401a−c.

New method for the N‐alkylation of the pyrazoles 405 has been developed using the trichloroacetimidate electrophile 406 and camphorsulfonic acid (CSA), a Brønsted acid catalyst.[ 130 ] Numerous pyrazoles bearing 4‐position substituents, such as halogens, alkyl, aryl, and ester groups, could be employed to synthesize the compounds 407a−g in good to moderate yields (Scheme 88 ). Although 3,5‐dimethylpyrazole 408 was also successfully used, the yield of the 1,3,5‐trisubstituted pyrazole 409 was only 50%. Unsymmetrical pyrazoles can provide two different regioisomers depending on which N atom reacts with imidate. To evaluate the selectivity of imidate alkylation, 3‐methyl‐5‐phenyl‐1H‐pyrazole (410) was transformed using phenethyl trichloroacetimidate. This afforded the two regioisomers, pyrazoles 411 and 412, in 40% and 16% yields, respectively.

Alkylation of 1H‐pyrazole derivatives with phenethyl imidate.

Huang's research group presented a protocol for selective conjugate additions to synthesize N‐alkyl pyrazoles.[ 131 ] Initially, 3(5)‐nitro‐1H‐pyrazole and ethyl acrylate were used to optimize the reaction conditions. After the combination of an appropriate base, solvent, and reaction temperature was established, diverse pyrazoles were treated with Michael‐type acceptors in the presence of N,N‐diisopropylethylamine in dimethyl sulfoxide. This methodology is superior owing to the fact that it tolerates various pyrazole substitution patterns, including 3‐substituted‐1H‐pyrazoles (Scheme 89 ). The compounds 415a−f favor N1 alkylations in high yields (92−97%). High regioselectivity appears to be characteristic of all substituents on the pyrazole ring.

Selective conjugate additions to synthesize N‐alkyl pyrazoles.

Khachatryan developed an efficient synthetic route for the aza‐Michael addition of pyrazoles to crotonic acid, yielding a series of 3‐(1H‐pyrazol‐1‐yl)butanoic acids;[ 132 ] furthermore, Das et al. reported an effective and catalyst‐free protocol for the aza‐Michael addition of pyrazoles to 2‐aryl‐3‐nitro‐2H‐chromene derivatives.[ 133 ] Zhang et al. recently established pyrazole‐sulfone hybridization, which involves Ag2CO3‐catalyzed aza‐Michael coupling of pyrazoles and vinyl sulfones. This methodology offers efficient and regioselective synthesis of N‐sulfonylethylated pyrazoles in excellent yields with high regioselectivities.[ 134 ]

Lee and coauthors reported that cinchona‐based phase‐transfer catalysts promoted asymmetric aza‐Michael reaction between 1H‐pyrazoles and various α,β‐unsaturated ketones, forming chiral N‐substituted pyrazoles.[ 135 ] For instance, the pyrazole 417a was treated with (E)‐3‐alkyl‐1‐phenylprop‐2‐en‐1‐ones (418a−c) and Rb2CO3 as a base additive in tetrahydrofuran at −20 °C in the presence of the catalyst 419 (Scheme 90 ). In all cases, the desired products 420a−c were obtained in excellent yields (96−99%) with high enantioselectivities (91−94%). Applicability of the method was successively investigated using 3(5)‐methyl‐1H‐pyrazole (417b), yielding the compound 420d, which exhibited not only an excellent yield (99%) and a high enantioselectivity (92% ee), but also good regioselectivity (8:1 rr).

Asymmetric synthesis of chiral N‐substituted pyrazoles.

Liu et al. introduced an interesting approach to construct N‐functionalized pyrazoles.[ 136 ] In the protocol, variously substituted pyrazoles were obtained via direct site‐selective difunctionalization, utilizing readily available alkenyl sulfonium salts and N‐halosuccinimides. As shown in Scheme 91 , the unsymmetric pyrazoles 421a−d were treated with the styrene sulfonium salt 422 and N‐iodosuccinimide 423 using Cs2CO3 as a base in tetrahydrofuran under an Ar atmosphere. Reactions proceeded with dual functionalization by introducing halogen and N‐substituent into the pyrazole ring, resulting in the target products 424a−d in 51−91% yields. Selectivity of the reaction can be controlled by different substituent positions on the pyrazole ring. Moreover, mechanistic studies revealed the dual role of the sulfonium salt, functioning as both a reaction substrate and an acid donor.

Direct site‐selective difunctionalization of the pyrazoles 421a−d.

Pyrazoles can undergo N‐allylic alkylation in Pd‐catalyzed reactions with vinylcyclopropanes.[ 137 ] In case of unsymmetric pyrazoles, the proposed protocol provides high stereoselectivity and regioselectivity. As depicted in Scheme 92 , the pyrazoles 425a−d were treated with (1‐cyclopropylethenyl)benzene (426) using Pd(PPh3)4 as a catalyst with 5,5′‐dimethyl‐2,2′‐bipyridine as a ligand and trifluoroacetic acid as an additive. Reactions proceeded by ring‐opening amination and led to the stereoselective products (E)‐427a−d in 64–95% yields.

Pd‐Catalyzed N‐allylic alkylation of the pyrazoles (E)‐425a−d.

Chiral N‐substituted pyrazoles are valuable scaffolds in the development of drugs, such as the kinase inhibitor ruxolitinib,[ 138 ] driving ongoing efforts to develop efficient enantioselective methods for the synthesis of these scaffolds. Breit's group developed the Rh‐catalyzed asymmetric N‐selective coupling of pyrazole derivatives with terminal allenes that affords access to enantioenriched secondary and tertiary allylic pyrazoles.[ 139 ] Thus, the reaction of the unsymmetric NH‐pyrazoles 428 with the cyclohexylallene 429 in the presence of [Rh(cod)Cl]2, pyridinium p‐toluenesulfonate, and the chiral JoSPOPhos ligand in toluene at 80 °C generated chiral N‐allylated pyrazoles 430a−g and 431a−g (Scheme 93 ). The products 420a−g were formed in good yields with good to excellent regioselectivities and high enantiomeric excess. Only the pyrazole 430b, which comprised an electron‐withdrawing CN group at the 4‐position, was formed in a lower enantiomeric excess of 57%.

Rh‐catalyzed regio‐ and enantioselective addition of pyrazole derivatives to cyclohexylallene.

Gill et al. developed a new and efficient vinylcarbenoid method for the N‐functionalization of NH‐pyrazoles.[ 140 ] Rh(II)‐catalyzed reaction of the diazodiester 433 with 3,5‐diphenylpyrazole 432 yielded the carbenoid N–H insertion product 434 in an excellent yield of 94%. Reaction of 433 with the unsymmetrical pyrazole 435 provided the analogous insertion products 436a and 436b in a high combined yield (83%) (Scheme 94 ).

Rh(II)‐Catalyzed reactions of the pyrazoles 432 and 435 with diazoacetate.

Recently, Cui et al. investigated organocatalytic activation of alkynes, allowing remote control of atroposelectivity using vinylidene para‐quinone methides (p‐VQMs).[ 141 ] The pyrazoles 437a−f were treated with arylacetylene 438 in the presence of CPA‐type catalyst 439 in dichloromethane at room temperature (Scheme 95 ). During C—N bond formation, the axially chiral compounds 440a−f were obtained in good yields (80−91%) with high enantioselectivities (89−96%). Absolute configuration of 440e was confirmed by X‐ray crystallographic analysis, and configuration of the other products was also assigned. Reported methodology is also applicable to ambident nucleophiles, including 2‐mercaptobenzothiazoles and 2‐mercaptobenzoxazoles, that were effectively converted to S‐selective adducts with excellent enantiocontrol.

Enantioselective addition of p‐VQM to the substituted pyrazoles 437a−f.

Highly regioselective N‐benzoylation and N‐tosylation of substituted pyrazoles was described by Bawazir et al.[ 142 ] Synthetic goal was to improve known methodologies and present a more practical and efficient method to obtain N‐functionalized products with antimicrobial activities. The substituted pyrazoles 441a−c were treated with either aryl benzoyl chloride (442a) or sulfonyl chloride (442b) using three different combinations of reaction conditions at room temperature: a) pyridine in dichloromethane under an inert atmosphere, b) an aqueous solution of NaOH (0.6 M) in tetrahydrofuran, and c) an aqueous solution of K2CO3 (0.6 M) in tetrahydrofuran (Scheme 96 ). Although the desired pyrazole derivatives 443a−c and 444a−c were successfully obtained in all cases, highest regioselectivity and yield (82–93%) were achieved using K2CO3 as a base in tetrahydrofuran.

Highly regioselective N‐alkylation of disubstituted pyrazoles with aryl sulfonyl and benzoyl chlorides.

Diamanti et al. designed and synthesized pyrazole carboxamide‐based acid ceramidase inhibitors.[ 143 ] Synthetic strategy involved N‐functionalization reactions of 3(5)‐substituted 1H‐pyrazoles (Scheme 97 ). The pyrazoles 445a−j were coupled with hexyl isocyanate in the presence of 4‐dimethylaminopyridine (DMAP) and pyridine, yielding a set library of the regioisomeric products 447 and 448. Ratio of the two isomers was affected by the electronic factors of the substituents at the 3‐ and 5‐positions of the pyrazole ring. For instance, the presence of the strong EDG NH2 at 3‐position (445a) favored the formation of the regioisomer 448a. Selectivity increased when a weak EDG (methyl substituent) was present at C‐5 position (445f), providing the isomers 447f and 448f in a 6:94 ratio, respectively. When the EWG Br was present at 3‐position and 5‐position of the ring was left unoccupied (445c vs 445h), higher selectivity for 447 regioisomer was achieved, resulting in a regioisomeric ratio of 92:8, respectively.

Conclusions and Perspectives

Late‐stage C–H functionalization enables the systematic preparation of various pyrazoles. Numerous reported synthetic methods rely on transition‐metal catalysis, which is a versatile tool for the formation of new C—C and C—heteroatom bonds. In addition to well‐known classical strategies, novel methods to control reaction regioselectivity by creating new catalytic systems, using protecting or directing groups, or simply by appropriately positioning functional groups in the pyrazole ring or other reactive species used in functionalizations are emerging. New synthetic approaches facilitate the functionalization of readily available pyrazole derivatives, avoiding the difficult early‐stage preparation of starting materials.

Halogenation of pyrazoles is also briefly discussed in this review, as halogenated pyrazole intermediates are useful scaffolds for further chemical transformations.[ 101 , 144 ] Additionally, the introduction of halogen atoms can improve specific properties, for example, alter biological activity, of the molecule.[ 145 , 146 ]

N‐Functionalization is an alternative method for obtaining N‐substituted pyrazoles when the incorporation of desired substituents is not possible during ring construction reactions. Highly regioselective N‐derivatization of unsymmetric pyrazoles is a remaining challenge, as mixtures of regioisomeric products are obtained due to the relocation of a hydrogen atom. Several studies have been discussed to demonstrate the recent discoveries toward controlled regioselectivity.

Focus in this field is expected to shift toward the development of greener and more sustainable synthetic methods, minimizing the reliance on hazardous reagents and transition‐metal catalysts. Evolving approaches, such as photocatalysis and biocatalysis, hold significant promise for reducing environmental impact while improving reaction efficiency. Moreover, advancements in computational chemistry and machine learning may enable the rational design of catalysts and reaction conditions to achieve higher regio‐ and chemoselectivity. Continuous exploration of directing group strategies and innovative reagent systems will possibly facilitate more precise C–H and N–H functionalizations, which would address regioselectivity challenges and expand the scope of accessible pyrazole derivatives.
