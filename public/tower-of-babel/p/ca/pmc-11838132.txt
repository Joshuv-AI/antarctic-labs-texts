Impact of asthma and allergic rhinitis control on sleep disordered breathing in pediatrics
Authors: Catarina Isabel Medalhas Rebelo da Silva, Rosa Martins, Rosário Ferreira
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThe aim of our study is to assess the quality of sleep in children with asthma and/or allergic rhinitis, as well as the impact of the control of allergic respiratory diseases on sleep disturbances.Materials and methodsIt is an observational cross-sectional study on children attending pediatric allergy appointments at a portuguese tertiary hospital (G1), and compared to a control population (G2). The asthma and rhinitis control was determined using the Control of Allergic Rhinitis and Asthma Test (CARAT/CARATKids) questionnaires. Sleep was evaluated using the Pediatric Sleep Questionnaire (PSQ). G1 included 61 patients and G2 included 100 children.ResultsAbout a third of the children in the study group had sleep-related breathing disorders (SDB), compared to 9 % on the control group (p-value <0.05). There was a significant positive correlation between uncontrolled asthma and allergic rhinitis, and SDB (p-value <0.05).ConclusionSDB is more prevalent among children with asthma and/or allergic rhinitis. The absence of control of the allergic respiratory diseases has a negative impact on sleep. We recommend large cross-sectional studies to better comprehend the exact relationship between the two conditions, as well as the use of objective diagnostic tools. We believe it is essential to implement early screening for sleep disorders in children with allergic respiratory diseases, through validated questionnaires.Keywords: Asthma, Children, Rhinitis, Sleep

Introduction

Asthma is a chronic respiratory disease characterized by inflammation of the small airways and bronchial hyperreactivity, resulting in airflow obstruction. Allergic asthma is the most common phenotype [1], with a prevalence of 14 % globally [2] and about 7.2 % [3] in Portugal.

Asthma symptoms tend to have great diurnal variability, and include dyspnea, wheezing, chest tightness and cough of variable frequency, with a tendency to worsen at night, during exercise, or intense laughter [4].

Allergic rhinitis also presents with chronic inflammation. The most common manifestations are nasal congestion, watery rhinorrhea, sneezing, and nasal pruritus [5]. Its prevalence is between 24 % and 27 % in children and adolescents [6].

Sleep disorders are relatively common in children with asthma and allergic rhinitis, particularly insomnia and sleep disordered breathing (SDB). SDB is defined as a syndrome of upper airway dysfunction during sleep, characterized by snoring and increased respiratory effort, which can result in pharyngeal collapse or increased airway resistance [7]. It manifests with snoring, witnessed apneas or hypopneas, mouth breathing, abnormal postures, and paradoxical chest movements during sleep [8], that can lead to prolonged partial obstruction or intermittent complete obstruction of the airway, disrupting ventilation and normal sleep patterns. The gold-standard diagnostic test for SDB is the polysomnography [9], but questionnaires have been increasingly used as a method of tracking and diagnosis.

One of the primary pathophysiological mechanisms explaining the association between asthma, allergic rhinitis and SDB is the release of inflammatory mediators by mast cells, mediated by Immunoglobulin E (IgE). This release has daily variations resulting in peaks of symptoms at two preferred times: early morning and during the night. Simultaneously, there is a decrease in levels of other interleukins (IL-4, IL-6, IL-10), responsible for controlling the circadian rhythm, increasing REM sleep, and reducing sleep latency. This inflammatory process can directly affect the central nervous system, altering the sleep-wake cycle, fragmenting sleep, and causing daytime sleepiness. Similarly, cysteinyl-leukotrienes lead to the development of tonsillar hypertrophy, one of the main factors leading to SDB [10].

Inflammatory cytokines from the lower airways, condensed during expiration, can contribute to lymphoid tissue proliferation in the upper airways and promote SDB.

Another mechanism is the presence of allergic symptoms during the night, which awaken the individual and cause fragmented sleep. The nighttime exacerbation of perennial allergic rhinitis can be explained by increased exposure to dust mites when in contact with allergen-laden fabrics [11]. Additionally, the preferred position adopted by patients (lateral decubitus) may worsen nasal obstruction due to increased nasal turbinates’ edema. The coexistence of atopic dermatitis in children with asthma and/or allergic rhinitis is common, so itching can also lead to recurrent early awakenings [12].

Although not fully established, there are descriptions of possible effects of allergic rhinitis on autonomic nervous system dysfunction that influence the development of sleep disturbances [10].

Sleep disordered breathing can arise due to anatomical factors (tonsillar hypertrophy, micrognathia, macroglossia, obesity) or due to the previously discussed inflammatory factors, promoting narrowing and collapse of the upper airways [7].

In most children, many of these factors and pathophysiological processes coexist and lead to sleep disruption.

Changes in the quantity or quality of sleep have been shown to have cognitive, psychological and affective impact particularly during early stages of development. Sleep disturbances increase irritability, aggressiveness, anxiety and/or depressive syndromes, attention deficits, and cognitive deficits in verbal comprehension, reasoning and working memory. There are also cardiovascular, endocrine-metabolic and immunological consequences [8].

There is a higher incidence of SDB in allergic rhinitis patients, although some authors consider it is not necessarily a risk factor for SDB pathophysiology but rather as a symptom enhancer [13].

Asthma increases the likelihood of snoring, apneas, daytime sleepiness, fatigue, and morning headaches. The pattern is similar even in older children and adolescents, although the differences are not as substantial [14,15]. SDB is also associated with longer hospitalization periods in asthmatic children [14].

Objectives

The primary outcome of this study is to assess the impact of asthma and allergic rhinitis on pediatric SDB. It evaluates the presence of SDB symptoms in individuals diagnosed with asthma and/or allergic rhinitis, comparing it to those without these diagnoses. Additionally, it assesses the influence of asthma and allergic rhinitis control in the occurrence of SDB symptoms.

As for the secondary outcomes, this study intends to investigate the association between various individual demographic factors - age, obesity and overweight - and the prevalence and control of asthma, allergic rhinitis and SDB in pediatrics.

Materials and Methods

Study design

This is an observational, cross-sectional, and comparative study. It was conducted at the Pediatric Allergology Clinic of Hospital de Santa Maria, integrated into the Unidade Local de Saúde de Santa Maria (ULSSM), in Lisbon (Portugal), from September 2023 to January 2024. The sample consisted of children between 6 and 17 years and 364 days old, diagnosed with asthma and/or allergic rhinitis by a doctor at the time of the study (G1). The control group (G2) comprised children from schools of the same region, without these diagnoses.

Data collection for the study group was done through the application of questionnaires on demographic data, sleep, and asthma and/or allergic rhinitis control, handed out to the children's guardians during their clinic visits in the form of assisted self-completion. To complement the clinical data, information about the patients' pulmonary function was collected from electronic medical records.

Questionnaires on demographic and sleep data were distributed to children in the control group.

Individuals with comorbidities that can interfere with sleep (epilepsy, depression, anxiety), under treatment that can interfere with sleep, psychiatric pathology with the inability to understand the questionnaires and caregivers who refused to sign informed consent or did not authorize their child's participation, were excluded from the study, as well as children with incomplete questionnaires.

Ethical considerations

Approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Academic Medical Center of Lisbon. All participants and their respective guardians agreed to participate and signed the informed consent, ensuring the voluntariness and confidentiality of the process. There is no conflict of interest. This research did not receive any specific grant from funding agencies in the public, commercial, or not-for-profit sectors.

Materials

Demographic data. Data on age, sex, height, weight, race, family history of atopy, personal history of atopy, and previous diagnosis of asthma and/or allergic rhinitis, was collected.

Allergic respiratory disease data. Data on duration of symptoms, recent pulmonary function tests (Spirometry - Forced Expiratory Volume in 1 s (FEV1) and FEV1/Forced Vital Capacity (FVC) ratio), and asthma and rhinitis treatment, was also collected.

Spirometry. Data on the last spirometry prior to the study was collected (Jaeger Master Screen Body®–Sentrysuite V.3.20.7), performed according to the American Thoracic Society and the European Respiratory Society criteria [16]; The values were expressed in Z-scores according to the equations from the Growing Lung Initiative (growinglungs.org.uk).

Pediatric Sleep Questionnaire. The Portuguese version of the Pediatric Sleep Questionnaire (PSQ) [17] is validated for screening of children with SDB. The PSQ is divided into 22 questions organized into 3 different categories. The first (A) consists of 9 questions about the child's nighttime symptoms, the second (B) comprises 7 questions about daytime sleepiness, development, and overweight, and the third (C) includes 6 questions about attention deficit and hyperactivity. The responses to each question can be ‘Yes’ = 1, ‘No’ = 0, or ‘Don't know’. A mean score higher than 0.33 is considered positive and indicative of symptoms compatible with SDB [18].

CARAT and CARATKids Questionnaires. The Control of Allergic Rhinitis and Asthma Test (CARAT) questionnaire [19] and CARATKids questionnaire [20] simultaneously assess asthma and allergic rhinitis control in children over 12 years old and children aged 12 or younger, respectively. The CARAT is divided into 10 questions, organized into 2 different categories. The first consists of 4 questions about upper airway symptoms, while the second comprises 4 questions about lower airway symptoms, followed by 1 question about the impact of sleep symptoms and 1 question about the use of additional medication. In the first 9 questions, responses are scored as follows: ‘Never’ = 3, ‘Up to 2 days per week’ = 2, ‘More than 2 days per week” = 1, or ‘Almost every or every day’ = 0. In the last question, the scoring is: ‘I am not taking medication’ = 3, ‘Never’ = 2, ‘Less than 7 days’ = 1, or ‘7 days or more’ = 0. A total score on CARAT of more than 24 points is considered good overall disease control. A partial score of more than 8 points in questions 1 to 4, corresponding to the score of upper airways (CARAT-SUA), indicates good allergic rhinitis control. A partial score of 16 or more points in questions 5 to 10, corresponding to the score of lower airways (CARAT-SLA), indicates good asthma control. Summarizing, a higher score in CARAT means better control of the diseases. The CARATKids is a simplified version of the CARAT, with 13 questions. The first 8 questions are answered by the children (child score) and the last 5 by the adult (adult score). Responses to each question use a dichotomous scale (yes/no). A total score of 3 or less excludes uncontrolled disease, while a score of 6 or more identifies poor disease control. Within the child score, there are 3 questions related to upper airways control (SUA) and 4 related to lower airways control (SLA). The eighth question assesses the impact of the disease on the child's activities and daily life. Summarizing, a higher score in CARATKids means worse disease control.

Analysis

The data was collected and recorded in a database in Excel©, solely in the possession of the lead researcher. The analysis was performed and implemented using the R programming language© [21], and data processing was done in the LaTeX environment [22]. A pre-defined p-value of 5 % was established for rejecting the null hypothesis.

Discussion

The prevalence of symptoms of SDB in the study group - 34.4 % - was significantly higher than that of the control group - 9 % (p-value <0.05), indicating more disturbed sleep in children with allergic respiratory disease.

Moreover, and as described in literature, we observed that there is a significant correlation between CARAT, CARATKids and PSQ, which means a higher prevalence of symptoms of SDB in children with asthma and/or allergic rhinitis not controlled. This occurred for both age groups, although the correlation in the younger group was weaker.

Worryingly, we observed that a significant portion of children did not have their allergic respiratory diseases globally ‘Controlled’, specifically 62.3 % of the sample, which was also the case when both diseases were evaluated separately. The group of participants aged over 12 years showed less control of these diseases compared to the younger group. This is vastly supported by literature, that states that adolescents tend to have worse medication adherence [23].

Regardless of age, allergic rhinitis was less controlled (15 % ‘Controlled’) compared to asthma (39.1 % ‘Controlled’). These values are similar to those obtained in the literature, which mentioned, for example, asthma control in only 49 % of children [24].

We observed that 9 % of participants in the control group had symptoms of SDB, which falls within general recent reports [25]. More recently, the American Academy of Pediatrics reported a prevalence of OSA in pediatrics ranging from 1.2 % to 5.7 %, and a meta-analysis from the European Respiratory Society reported a range between 0.1 % and 13 %. Other studies recognize a prevalence of 4 %–11 % [25]. The wide variation between studies and assessments is due to the use of different clinical criteria, self-reporting bias, and cultural and perception differences regarding symptoms such as snoring [26].

The prevalence of overweight and obesity was higher in children with asthma and/or allergic rhinitis (36.1 % vs 20 %), and also higher in groups with SDB (p-value >0.05). According to the latest study from the Childhood Obesity Surveillance Initiative in 2022, 31.9 % of children in Portugal were overweight, of which 13.5 % had obesity, slightly lower values than those collected from our control group.

The high prevalence of obesity and overweight in the study group, which is a well-documented risk factor for SDB, constitutes a confounding variable when accounting for the true influence of allergic respiratory diseases, and their control, in SDB.

The influence of rhinitis control in SDB was statistically significant, but only in children with the age of 12 years or more. However, the influence of asthma control in SDB was not statistically significant for either age subgroup.

In this study, almost all participants were already under treatment. Most asthma patients were already in Step 3 of the Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) [1], and rhinitis patients with multiple pharmacological treatments. Even so, there was a poor control of the diseases, which can entail that the patients have particularly severe disease, poor inhalation technique, poor adherence to treatment, other untreated comorbidities or even polysensitization to multiple allergens.

Most individuals didn't show major changes in the pulmonary function. However, as an objective evaluation of this parameter was not possible, we can't reach any further conclusions.

Limitations and recommendations

Throughout the development of this study, it was possible to identify some limitations. As it was an observational and cross-sectional study, it does not allow for a definitive establishment of a causal relationship, that could be accomplished with a prospective cohort study.

The sample size was not sufficiently representative given the prevalence of asthma and rhinitis in Portugal. Similarly, the study area was only related to the ULSSM which limits the conclusions nationally. Therefore, the study should be replicated with a more significant sample size and from a larger geographic area.

It was not possible to objectively assess the impact of allergic disease and SDB on the development and academic performance, which may be of interest to explore in a subsequent study. With respect to the two groups (G1 and G2), they were statistically distinct (p-value <0.05) in terms of weight, height, sex and age, which raises an additional limitation in their comparison.

The PSQ questionnaire identified individuals with symptoms compatible with SDB, without an objective confirmation of diagnosis. However, the use of more objective diagnostic methods for sleep disorders, such as polysomnography, is limited by the technical difficulties of its use in a large population. Although it would be important to further confirm and categorize sleep disorders in these children, the PSQ questionnaire has sensitivity to OSA, so it can be useful as a screening tool [18].

It is worth mentioning that the fact that most individuals had already started treatment for allergic diseases at the beginning of the study does not allow for a pure correlation between baseline asthma or allergic rhinitis and sleep disorders. However, the correlation between poor control of asthma, and rhinitis, and SDB symptoms allows us to conclude for a positive relationship.

In the literature, the different methodologies of each study, with different age groups and diagnostic criteria, do not allow for a secure meta-analysis and comparison of data. Although many studies demonstrate a significant association between asthma, allergic rhinitis, and sleep disorders, many are still of low evidence, and there is little scientific consensus [27]. For this reason, the temporality and directionality factors are not yet proven regarding the causal relationship between asthma or allergic rhinitis and SDB.

Conclusion

This study corroborates previous findings, once symptoms compatible with SDB have a higher prevalence among children with asthma and/or allergic rhinitis, and that the absence of control of the allergic respiratory diseases has a negative impact on sleep. It is essential to implement early screening for sleep disorders (particularly SDB and insomnia) in children with allergic respiratory diseases, through the application of validated questionnaires, such as the PSQ. This procedure would facilitate quicker identification and referral of individuals with SDB for complementary diagnostic tests, as well as prompt initiation of treatment to reduce inadequate sleep and its consequences.
