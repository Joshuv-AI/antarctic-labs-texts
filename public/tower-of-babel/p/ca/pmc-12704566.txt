Addressing Pancreatic Exocrine Insufficiency and the Impact of Pancreatic Enzyme Replacement Therapy Shortages in Europe
Authors: Afrodita Panaitescu‐Damian, Ibrahim Gürcinar, Viorelia Stoica, Salvatore Paiella, Marcus Hollenbach, Ivonne Regel, Dawn Swibold, Gabriele Capurso, Alfredo Carrato, Patrick Michl, Luis Arnes
Published: 2025

Introduction

Across Europe, pancreatic enzyme replacement, a therapy that is essential for the nutrition of patients with pancreatic diseases has become unavailable for patients in several countries. We seek to provide insights into the impact of this supply crisis across European countries and to encourage discussions on potential solutions.

Patients after pancreatic resection following a diagnosis of pancreatic cancer or those with chronic pancreatitis are commonly affected by pancreatic exocrine insufficiency (PEI). PEI is defined as inadequate secretion and function of pancreatic enzymes which may lead to maldigestion and malnutrition [1]. However, not only patients with pancreatic diseases are affected by PEI, as up to 10% of the elderly population may also be facing PEI due to age‐related loss of pancreatic exocrine function [2]. Apart from aging, other risk factors associated with PEI include alcohol consumption, irritable bowel disease, diabetes, coeliac disease, and HIV [3]. Despite affecting so many people, PEI is frequently underdiagnosed and undertreated [4, 5].

Pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT) and dietary advice can significantly improve symptoms, enhance quality of life and have been associated with longer survival in patients with pancreatic cancer or chronic pancreatitis [6].

Currently, there is a shortage of pancreatic enzyme replacement drugs from several suppliers. This shortage affects several EU member states in which these products are marketed, including Austria, Belgium, Bulgaria, Cyprus, Czechia, France, Greece, Italy, Ireland, Latvia, Luxembourg, Malta, the Netherlands, Portugal, Spain, Sweden (www.ema.europa.eu), as well as the UK (www.cas.mhra.gov.uk). This problem is not expected to be resolved until the second half of 2026.

Addressing the challenges of diagnosing, treating, and managing PEI is essential to ensuring patient well‐being and healthcare resource sustainability.

By conducting and summarizing two online surveys among both patients and health care professionals across Europe, this paper aims to raise awareness of the consequences of the PERT shortage from the perspective of affected patients and healthcare providers.

Materials and Methods

Two online surveys, one targeting physicians and one targeting patients, were conducted online from May to November 2024 in order to evaluate the PERT supply shortage across Europe. The surveys were designed by the Public Affairs Committee of the European Pancreatic Club (EPC) in collaboration with Pancreatic Cancer Europe (PCE) and were hosted on Google Forms. The physician survey was designed and distributed in English, whereas the patient survey was distributed in multiple European languages. Both surveys were anonymous and respondents provided consent for data analysis and publication.

Results

Patient Survey

Demographics

144 patients from 12 European countries responded to the survey. Most respondents live in Spain, Sweden and Denmark.

PERT Availability and Financial Burden

Of note, a considerable proportion of respondents reported a significant financial burden caused by the PERT shortage, including 70% of the respondents from Italy, almost half of the respondents from Sweden, 24% of the respondents from Spain and more than one‐fourth of the respondents from Denmark who rated the overall individual financial burden caused by the lack of PERT supply as very high or high (Figure 1B).

When PERT was not available in local pharmacies, 39% of the patients stated that they had to take reduced PERT dosages.

PERT Availability and Professional Support

When asked to rate their satisfaction with the level of support by their healthcare providers regarding PERT unavailability on a scale from 1 (low) to 5 (high), half of the Spanish respondents rated it as “4” or “5.” Approximately one‐third of respondents from Denmark, the UK, Germany, Italy, and Sweden rated it as “3,” while one‐third of respondents from Italy, Germany, and Spain, along with more than half of those from the UK, were least satisfied with a rating of “1” or “2”.

Among the coping mechanisms, communication with the healthcare providers was deemed important for 43 respondents (30%). Only a minority of patients received assistance from patient advocacy groups or other organizations.

Physicians' Survey

Demographics

There were 106 responses from healthcare professionals, mostly from Italy, Spain, and Turkey.

PERT Availability

More than 90% of the responding health care professionals from Spain and Italy considered the PERT shortage to have been a significant problem for weeks or months. The severity of the shortage and the impact on patient care were assessed mainly based on patient reports (Figure 1C).

Regarding communication with pharmaceutical companies, 37% of respondents felt that the companies could have been more present and responsive in their communication with referring doctors; 29% felt that the companies were consistently present, available and clear in providing updates on the situation. In Italy, 40% felt the companies were initially present and proactive but then did not seem to prioritize areas promptly.

All respondents from Spain and 92% of respondents from Italy reported to be concerned about the impact of the PERT unavailability in their local practice (Figure 1D); 64% indicated that the lack of access to PERT negatively impacts patients' overall health outcomes. Additionally, 69% observed adverse health effects in patients due to the lack of access to PERT.

Conclusions and Call to Action

Despite the limitations of this study due to limited sample sizes and possible selection biases, these survey results clearly demonstrate that the shortage of PERT has a significant negative impact on the health and well‐being of patients with PEI. This underscores the urgent need to restore a stable supply and to ensure that patients receive adequate treatment.

Moreover, clear guidelines from health authorities and patient advocacy groups are essential to bridge the knowledge gap and provide direction for effectively managing PERT shortages. Transparency in communication from pharmaceutical companies regarding supply issues and available alternatives is beneficial for healthcare providers and patients.

Furthermore, the development of alternative enzyme formulations, such as non‐porcine PERT is needed in order to expand treatment options. This would help address supply shortages and collaterally provide solutions for patients with dietary restrictions related to porcine‐derived products.

Addressing these issues is critical to improving patient outcomes and ensuring that individuals with PEI receive the care they need. Solving PERT deficiency in Europe demands awareness, prompt prescribing and fair access.
