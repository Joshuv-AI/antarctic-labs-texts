Dose optimization and utilization trends of mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists in heart failure patients with reduced ejection fraction in Ethiopia
Authors: Minimize Hassen, Birhane Tilahun Worku, Tewodros Solomon, Mulat Belete Demessie, Teklehaimanot Fentie Wendie, Oumer Sada Muhammed, Mengistie Yirsaw Gobezie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNascent evidence-based guidelines advocate the use of Mineralocorticoid Receptor Antagonists (MRAs) in all heart failure patients with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) to abridge cardiovascular mortality and morbidity. This study assessed the utilization patterns and dose optimization of MRAs among patients with HFrEF at Dessie Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Ethiopia, from March to August 2024.MethodsA cross-sectional analysis of 260 adult patients with confirmed HFrEF, who had been under follow-up for at least six months, was conducted. MRA use and dose optimization were evaluated according to 2022 American Heart Association (AHA) guidelines. Logistic regression identified factors associated with MRA use, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsAmong the 260 patients, 168 (64.6 %) were prescribed MRA, all receiving spironolactone. Of these, 119 (69.1 %) used it to reduce cardiovascular mortality, while 30.9 % used it for its potassium-sparing effect. However, only 38 (22.6 %) patients received the optimal dose, with a mean daily dose of 22.3 mg. MRAs underuse was observed in 83 (31.9 %) patients. Factors positively associated with MRA use included female sex (AOR = 2.79, 95 % CI: 1.33–5.85), NYHA class III (AOR = 4.99, 95 % CI: 1.71–14.61), diuretic use (AOR = 7.58, 95 % CI: 3.02–19.05), and taking five or more medications (AOR = 3.87, 95 % CI: 1.46–10.29). Conversely, negative associations were found with secondary prevention agents (AOR = 0.04, 95 % CI: 0.01–0.22) and a baseline serum potassium level of 5–5.5 meq/L (AOR = 0.20, 95 % CI: 0.08–0.53).ConclusionWhile MRA use is common, optimal dosing remains suboptimal. Clinicians should adhere to guideline recommendations to ensure optimal dosing and maximize clinical benefits.Keywords: Dose optimization, Utilization trend, Spironolactone, HFrEF, Ethiopia

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a chronic, progressive condition affecting ~2 % of adults in developed countries [1]. Current international HF guidelines including the American College of Cardiology (ACC) and American Heart Association (AHA) [2], and European Society of Cardiology (ESC) [3,4], classify HF by ejection fraction (EF) into HF with reduced EF (HFrEF; ≤40 %), HF with preserved EF (HFpEF; ≥50 %), and HF with mildly reduced EF (HFmEF; 40–49 %). For HFrEF, four standard therapies are recommended to slow down progression and improve survival: Angiotensin Receptor Antagonist-Neprilysin Inhibitor (ARNI) or Angiotensin Converting Enzyme Inhibitors (ACEIs) or Angiotensin Receptor Blockers (ARBs), Beta-blockers, Mineralocorticoid Receptor Antagonist (MRA), and Sodium-Glucose Transporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors [[2], [3], [4]].

The latest (2022) ACC/AHA guideline recommends the use of an MRA in patients with HFrEF and New York Heart Association (NYHA) class II to IV symptoms to reduce cardiovascular morbidity and mortality if estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) is >30 mL/min/1.73 m2 and serum potassium is <5.0 mEq/L [2]. The top-notch criterion for MRA prescription is NYHA class II-IV patients with an EF of ≤35 % without contraindication [2,4]. Although it is not grounded on strong evidence, the updated AHA guideline advocates the use of MRAs even in patients with HFpEF [2,3].

Clinical trials [[5], [6], [7]] support the tremendous impact of MRA in HF. For instance, the Randomized Aldactone Evaluation Study” (RALES) trial reported a 30 % reduction in all-cause mortality in HF patients with an EF of <35 % and NYHA III-IV symptoms with spironolactone use [6]. Likewise, the EPHESUS (Eplerenone Post–Acute Myocardial Infarction Heart Failure Efficacy and Survival Study) trial showed that eplerenone reduced cardiovascular mortality by 15 % [5] while the EMPHASIS-HF (Eplerenone in Mild Patients Hospitalization and Survival Study in Heart Failure) revealed a 30 % decrease in hospitalization rate among NYHA class II HF patients [7].

Despite strong evidence supporting MRAs in reducing morbidity, mortality, and readmissions in HF, their use remains significantly underutilized [8], with only 33 %–42 % of HFrEF patients receiving them [[9], [10], [11]]. Contributing factors include impaired renal function, hypotension, abnormal labs, non-specialist care, absence of other HF therapies, and mild symptoms [[12], [13], [14]]. Additionally, many clinicians, especially in developing countries, misuse MRAs primarily for their potassium-sparing effect rather than their proven cardiovascular benefit, complicating efforts to assess appropriate prescribing patterns [[15], [16], [17]].

To maximize clinical benefit, MRAs should be used at guideline-recommended target or maximum tolerable doses [18,19], 25–50 mg for spironolactone and 50 mg for eplerenone [2]. Although clinical guidelines recommend these target doses in HFrEF, the optimal dose remains uncertain, as higher doses may increase the risk of hyperkalemia, while real-world practice often favors cautious titration. Comorbidities, side effects, and polypharmacy often hinder optimal dosing [20,21]. Both underuse and suboptimal dosing limit the proven cardiovascular benefits of MRAs in HF patients [22].

While ACEIs and beta-blockers have been studied in Africa [[23], [24], [25], [26]], research on MRA use and dose optimization remains scarce, particularly in Sub-Saharan countries like Ethiopia. Given MRAs' critical role in HF management, this gap highlights the need for focused investigation. This study aimed to assess MRA utilization, dose optimization, and associated factors among adult HFrEF patients at the ambulatory clinic of DCSH in Dessie, Northeast Ethiopia, to inform and improve clinical practice.

Methodology

Study setting, study design, and study period

A hospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted from March 01 to August 30, 2024, at the ambulatory care clinic of DCSH, Dessie, Northeast Ethiopia. Dessie is a town located in the Amhara region of northeast Ethiopia, 400 km away from Addis Ababa. The total population of the town is estimated to be 151,094, of which 78,203 are females. Annually, the hospital serves nearly 9 million people including patients who come from surrounding regions. The hospital has various wards and outpatient department clinics, one of which is the ambulatory care clinic, where different healthcare services are offered [27]. The clinic renders comprehensive service including treatment and follow-up. It provides cardiac-related services mostly two days per week (Monday and Thursday). On average, 30 HF patients came for follow-up each day.

Population

All patients with HFrEF who attended the ambulatory care clinic of DCSH were considered as a source population. All adult patients with HFrEF who fulfilled the stated inclusion criteria were considered as a study population.

Eligibility criteria

Adult patients aged ≥18 years with a confirmed echocardiographic diagnosis of HFrEF (EF ≤ 40 %) who had been on regular follow-up for at least 6 months were included in the study. Patients with precautions and contraindications to MRA use including fatal renal dysfunction, hypotension, hyperkalemia, severe liver dysfunction, hypersensitivity to any component of the drug, Addison's disease or other medical conditions associated with hyperkalemia, patients with critical illness, and incomplete medical records were excluded from the study.

Sample size calculation and sampling technique

The sample size was calculated based on a single population proportion formula by using a 95 % confidence level, 5 % margin of error, 50 % proportion of MRA use (since there is no prior study on the subject area in Ethiopia set-up). Accordingly, a sample size of 384 patients with HFrEF was calculated. Subsequently, further adjustment was performed based on the expected number of source population in the study period (N = 1440) and then a total of 303 patients were computed. Out of the 303 patients approached, 43 of them were excluded from the study due to the presence of precaution or contraindication to MRA [30] and incomplete medical records [13]. Therefore, a total of 260 patients were included in the final analysis.

A simple random sampling technique was employed to select study participants who fulfilled the posited inclusion criteria. The Medical Referral Clinic (MRC) registration logbook was used as a sampling framework. By doing so, patients were recruited randomly into the study.

Study variables

Dependent variables: 1) MRA utilization 2) Dose optimization of MRA.

Independent variables: Socio-demographic characteristics: age, sex, and residence. Disease-related characteristics: causes, stage and NYHA class of HF, duration of HF, family history, presence and type of comorbidities, hospitalization history, baseline and follow-up blood pressure value, serum potassium level, and creatinine clearance. Treatment-related characteristics: duration since starting HF therapy, total number of prescribed medications, and medication types.

Data collection procedures

The medical record number of HF patients who had follow-ups at least for the last 6 months was obtained from MRC and taken to the hospital's card room to be traced by the senior staff. Patients' medical records were exhaustively reviewed to gather necessary sociodemographic, disease, and treatment-related data. A data abstraction checklist developed after reviewing different related literature and guidelines was used to collect the data by two clinical pharmacists.

Data analysis

The data were entered into and cleaned in Epi Info version 4.6.0.2 and subsequently exported into and analyzed in Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 26. Frequencies and percentages were deployed for all categorical variables, while mean ± standard deviation and/or median (inter quartile range) for continuous variables, as appropriate. Initially, multicollinearity was assured to test correlation among the independent variables using the variance inflation factor (VIF). A VIF < 10 was applied as a cut point for excluding collinearity. Binary logistic regression analysis was carried out to assess the association between MRA use and all the independent variables and to identify candidates for multivariable analysis. Independent variables with p-value < 0.25 in the univariable binary logistic regression analysis were re-entered into a multivariable binary logistic regression model to identify predictors of MRA use. To declare statistical significance, a p-value of <0.05 was utilized.

Operational definitions

Patients were said to be optimally treated if they received an optimal dose of guideline-recommended MRA (spironolactone/eplerenone). MRA were said to be underutilized if they were not used by patients in the absence of contraindications. Patients were considered as receiving guideline-recommended MRA therapy if they received a prescription of spironolactone or eplerenone. The dose of MRA was said to be optimal if it was given at a guideline-recommended target dose or a maximum tolerable dose. Conversely, the dose was said to be suboptimal if the patient was taking any dose of MRA below the target dose in the absence of contraindications for up-titration. For spironolactone, the optimal dose was considered if the dose was 25–50 mg daily, and for eplerenone, the dose was said to be optimal if it was ≥50 mg daily.

Results

Socio-demographic and clinical characteristics of HFrEF patients

A total of 260 HFrEF patients were included in the study. The mean age of the patients was 54.5 ± 15.8 years. Out of the total studied patients, 135(51.9 %) were males, and 151(60.6 %) came for routine follow-up from areas outside Dessie. About 44.2 % had NYHA class III HF. The top three underlying etiologies of HFrEF were dilated cardiomyopathy, ischemic heart disease, and chronic rheumatoid heart disease accounting for 49.6 %, 40.0 %, and 7.3 %, respectively. Roughly, 151(58.1 %) patients had two or more comorbid conditions. Among these, more than half of them (53.5 %) presented with coronary artery disease (CAD) followed by hypertension (33.5 %), and atrial fibrillation (19.6 %) (Table 1).

Treatment related characteristics of HFrEF patients

Approximately half of the HFrEF patients (52.7 %) were prescribed five or more medications. Out of the total HFrEF patients studied, MRA use was reported only in 168 (64.6 %) patients. Expectedly, Spironolactone was the only type of MRA utilized (100 %) by these patients and thus none of them were on Eplerenone. Amidst MRA users, only 119 (69.1 %) patients utilized spironolactone for its cardiovascular mortality reduction benefit while the remaining (30.9 %) applied it for its potassium-sparing effect. The most frequently prescribed class of medications besides MRA among HFrEF patients were Beta-Blockers (88.8 %), secondary prevention agents (79.6 %), ACEIs (79.6 %), and loop diuretics (72.3 %). Although SGLT2 inhibitors are recommended as first-line therapy in HFrEF, none of the patients in our cohort received these agents due to limited availability and high cost in our setting. All patients received loop diuretics alone, with no use of thiazide or thiazide-like diuretics, minimizing potential confounding effects on potassium excretion and MRA prescribing (Table 3).

Utilization and dose optimization of MRA in HFrEF patients

Out of the total, 168 (64.6 %) patients received evidence-based MRA. All these patients received spironolactone, which is an evidence-based MRA. Among the patients who took spironolactone, only 38 (22.6 %) were receiving an optimal dose. MRA underuse was reported in 83 (31.9 %) patients. The minimum and maximum daily doses of spironolactone ingested by the MRA users were 12.5 mg and 25 mg, respectively. The mean daily dose of spironolactone that was taken by HFrEF patients was 22.3 mg (Table 4).

Factors affecting MRA use in HFrEF patients

Initially, univariable logistic regression analysis was performed on selected sociodemographic, clinical, and treatment-related characteristics to identify variables candidate for multivariable logistic regression, and a total of 12 variables were found to be candidates at a P-value of ≤0.25. According to multivariable logistic regression analysis, female sex (Adjusted Odds ratio (AOR) = 2.79, 95 % confidence interval (CI): 1.33–5.85), NYHA class III (AOR = 4.99, 95 % CI: 1.71–14.61), diuretic use (AOR = 7.58, 95 % CI: 3.02–19.05), taking five or more prescribed medications (AOR = 3.87, 95 % CI: 1.46–10.29), ingestion of secondary prevention agents (AOR = 0.04, 95 % CI: 0.01–0.22), and a baseline serum potassium level between 5 and 5.5 mEq/L (AOR = 0.20, 95 % CI: 0.08–0.53) were significantly associated with MRA use. While female sex, NYHA class III HF, diuretic use, and ingestion of five or more prescribed medications were positively associated with MRA use, taking of secondary prevention agents and a serum potassium level of 5–5.5 mEq/L were negatively associated with MRA use (Table 5).

Discussion

This is a pioneer study conducted in sub-Saharan Africa including Ethiopia that examined the overall utilization pattern and dose optimization practice of MRA in HFrEF patients. Moreover, it identified a multitude of sociodemographic, disease and treatment-related factors that are either positively or negatively associated with MRA use.

This study found evidence-based MRA utilization in two-thirds of patients (65 %), a figure that is higher than the result of seven studies that showed MRA utilization rate of 37 % in Charleston [8], 38.2 % in Michigan [28], 35 % in Memphis [29], 36 % in Colorado [20], 21 % in Texas [30], 25 % in New York [31], 40 % in Denmark and Sweden [14,32]. The discrepancy in MRA utilization rate among various studies could be explained by differences in sample characteristics, drug availability, and access to healthcare. The prominent MRA utilization noticed in this study could be the result of increased awareness and knowledge of most prescribers working in DCSH about the cardiovascular mortality benefit of MRA in HFrEF. It could also be ascribed to relatively better compliance of health professionals towards national treatment guidelines, increased healthcare access, and drug availability. Unfortunately, spironolactone is the only MRA used by patients, largely due to its increased accessibility and affordability compared to other options. This study suggests that the exclusive use of spironolactone among MRA users in Ethiopia, including Dessie town, may be attributed to the unavailability of Eplerenone in the region.

Although evidence-based clinical practice guidelines [[2], [3], [4]] recommend the use of MRA in all patients with HFrEF, about 35 % of the patients were not receiving MRA without known reason in our study. Consonant to our eye-opening finding, MRA was often underutilized in similar studies [8,13,28,29] done elsewhere. In the present study, beta-blockers and Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone-System (RAAS)-blockers like ACEI were the most commonly used drugs (ACEI in 78.5 % and β-blocker in 88 % of patients), which is comparable with a study conducted by Trujillo et al. [30] which reported 74 % β-blocker and 72 % ACEI use and another study done in Michigan [28] which found 75.0 % ACEI and 66.7 % β-blockers use. Among patients receiving guideline-recommended MRA, only one-fourth of them (22.6 %) were taking the optimal doses. The possible reason for sub-optimal dosing could be due to low physician inertia in up-titrating to the target dose, low awareness of dose titration protocol among physicians, physicians fear of encountering serious adverse effects, and patient refusal.

Out of the total MRA users, one-fourth of them were utilizing MRA for its potassium-sparing benefit, showing serious gaps among some prescribers in Ethiopia who didn't know the impressive benefit of MRA in HF beyond its potassium-sparing role. Surprisingly, no international clinical practice guideline [2,3] definitively advocates MRA use for potassium-sparing purposes in HFrEF and thus contradicts our crucial finding. Despite this incongruency, some national guideline [33] shallowly outlines the concurrent use of MRA in loop diuretic user HF patients, which is a misleading factor for higher utilization patterns of MRA in real Ethiopian healthcare practice.

In the present study, six variables were found to be independent predictors of MRA use. This includes female sex, NYHA Class III, diuretics use, taking ≥5 medications, using other pertinent drugs, and elevated serum potassium levels. Gender-wise, female HFrEF patients had three times higher odds of utilizing MRA as compared to their counterparts. Diuretic use was the strongest predictor of MRA use as loop diuretics users were eight times more likely to use MRA than non-users. Perhaps, this interesting finding strongly supports the higher proportion of implementation of MRA for the intent of the potassium-sparing effect noted in the present study, surmounting the fearful hypokalemic adverse effect of loop diuretics. Previous studies examining the use of ACEIs and beta-blockers reported a negative correlation between diuretic use and the use of ACEIs and beta-blockers [24,26]. In contrast, our study identified a positive association between diuretic use and MRA.

Patients presented with NYHA class III had five times higher odds of utilizing MRA than those with NYHA class II or IV. This result is consistent with evidence-based clinical practice guideline recommendation to use MRA for cardiovascular mortality benefit in NYHA class III patients with EF ≤35 % [2]. Likewise, a previous study in Jimma, Ethiopia [25] that explores ACEI utilization in HF patients similarly found higher ACEI utilization among NYHA class III patents. Moreover, as class III patients are usually very symptomatic, the probability of using symptom reversal agents like loop diuretics is very high which in turn triggers clinicians to deploy MRA as a preventive weapon to counteract hypokalemia. Additionally, MRA utilization was four times higher in patients taking five or more medications as compared to patients taking less than five medications. The fact that ingesting multiple medications has a positive association with MRA utilization may be attributed to the presence and occurrence of comorbidities that eventually act as a compelling indication of the use of polypharmacy incorporating MRA in the regimens. Perhaps, this finding is new.

Conversely, patients taking secondary prevention agents are 96 % less likely to utilize MRA compared to those who didn't take secondary prevention. The lower utilization habit observed in patients taking other pertinent drugs (secondary prevention agents like aspirin, clopidogrel, and warfarin) could be colligated to the lesser progression of the disease slowing the clinicians' urge to prescribe cardiovascular drugs having survival benefits. It could be due to clinicians' fear of encountering clinically apparent pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic drug interactions amidst these agents. Patients with serum potassium levels between 5 and 5.5 mEq/L were 80 % less likely to utilize MRA than patients with values <5 mEq/L. This shows higher cognizance of clinicians to the fact that massive hyperkalemia (≥5.5 mEq/L) is an absolute contraindication to MRA use. This result is consonant with international HF guidelines [[2], [3], [4]]. This result is coherent with a study done by Cooper et al., that revealed patients on MRA therapy with borderline or preserved ejection fraction had greater risks of readmission for hyperkalemia [34].

To improve drug compliance in our region, a multifaceted approach is essential. Strengthening patient counseling at the point of care can enhance understanding of treatment regimens and the importance of adherence. Implementing follow-up systems such as SMS reminders, telephone check-ins, or app-based alerts can support ongoing compliance, particularly for patients in remote areas. Additionally, community health workers can play a vital role in delivering home-based education and monitoring. Where feasible, written instructions or letter-based communication in local languages can reinforce verbal counseling and address literacy barriers. Together, these strategies can significantly enhance medication adherence and treatment outcomes. Furthermore, cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIEDs) play a crucial role in optimizing HF management by enhancing drug tolerability and enabling up-titration of guideline-directed medical therapy. By stabilizing heart rhythm and preventing bradyarrhythmias or conduction delays, CIEDs reduce the risk of adverse effects from medications such as beta-blockers, ACEIs, MRAs, thereby allowing safer dose escalation and improved treatment adherence.

Nevertheless, this study has some limitations that should be acknowledged. First, although various disease and treatment-related factors that might influence the utilization of MRA were attempted, some demographic characteristics were missed as the data collection was confined to medical chart review where the mode of healthcare access, marital, educational, and occupational status of patients is poorly documented in the study setting. Second, we acknowledge that in some cases the explicit rationale for prescribing MRAs was not directly documented in medical charts. However, we relied on routine annotations made by clinical pharmacists in patient records, supplemented by relevant clinical parameters (e.g., EF, diuretic use, serum potassium) to infer the most likely therapeutic indications. Future studies with prospective data collection may better capture physicians' explicit prescribing rationale. Third, the exact time lag between diagnosis and MRA initiation was not consistently recorded, limiting comparison with other HF therapies. Fourth, the current study did not assess the impact of health professionals' level of knowledge on the utilization and titration practice of MRA. Fifth, this study may not provide sufficient evidence pertaining to the cause-effect relationship of MRA utilization and its predictors due to the inherent characteristics of a cross-sectional study. Sixth, because it is a single-center study, it might not be generalized to the general population. Last, the findings of this study should be extrapolated to other countries with caution as it may be affected by differences in participants' characteristics, disease distribution, healthcare infrastructure, and methods employed. Therefore, future studies incorporating prospective and/or mixed-method designs involving patient interviews should be conducted to address some of these limitations.

Conclusion

While nearly two-thirds of patients used MRA intending to reduce cardiovascular mortality, only a quarter received the optimal dose. Female sex, NYHA class III, diuretic use, and taking five or more medications were positively associated with MRA use while the ingestion of secondary prevention agents and elevated serum potassium levels were negatively associated with MRA use. Therefore, physicians should attempt to provide guideline-recommended MRA at optimal doses taking into consideration the aforementioned factors to achieve the desired clinical benefits.
