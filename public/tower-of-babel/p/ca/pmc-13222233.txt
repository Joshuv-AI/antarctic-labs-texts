Comparative Study Assessing Multiple Switches Between Biosimilar ABP 501 (adalimumab-atto) and Adalimumab Reference Product in Patients with Plaque Psoriasis
Authors: Paul S Yamauchi, Vincent Chow, Daniel T Mytych, Muhan Zhou, Waldemar Radziszewski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionABP 501, now approved as AMJEVITA® (adalimumab-atto, USA)/AMGEVITA® (adalimumab, EU), is a biosimilar to adalimumab reference product (RP, Humira®) indicated for the treatment of various chronic inflammatory conditions. This multicenter, randomized, double-blind study aimed to investigate the impact of multiple switches between adalimumab RP and ABP 501 as compared with continued-use of adalimumab RP.MethodsThis study (NCT05073315) consisted of a 12-week lead-in period where adults with moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis received adalimumab RP subcutaneously every 2 weeks (Q2W), followed by a double-blind, two-parallel arm period in which patients were randomized to either the continued-use group (adalimumab RP Q2W, weeks 12–28) or switching group (ABP 501, weeks 12 and 14; adalimumab RP, weeks 16 and 18; ABP 501 Q2W, weeks 20–28). Adherence to dosing protocol and completion/discontinuation rates were similar between groups. There were no new or concerning safety signals.ConclusionThis study demonstrates PK similarity in patients with plaque psoriasis who underwent three treatment switches between adalimumab RP and ABP 501 as compared with those who received continuous treatment with adalimumab RP. Safety, immunogenicity, and efficacy profiles were comparable. Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Adalimumab-atto (AMJEVITA®, USA/AMGEVITA®, EU; Amgen Inc.), also known as ABP 501, is an approved biosimilar to adalimumab (HUMIRA®, AbbVie Inc.) to treat chronic inflammatory conditions.This study was conducted to support the interchangeability designation between the biosimilar adalimumab-atto and the adalimumab reference product (RP) and was designed in line with the 2019 US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) guidance regarding demonstration of interchangeability.The study investigated whether multiple switches (back and forth) between ABP 501 and adalimumab RP would result in similar pharmacokinetics (PK) compared with continuous treatment with adalimumab RP. Safety, immunogenicity, and efficacy were also evaluated. What was learned from the study? The study demonstrated that switching back and forth between ABP 501 and adalimumab RP resulted in similar PK profiles, as well as comparable efficacy, immunogenicity, and safety compared with continual use of adalimumab RP.The results support the conclusion that ABP 501 and adalimumab RP can be used interchangeably in accordance with the 2019 FDA guidance.

Introduction

Adalimumab is a recombinant, fully humanized monoclonal antibody that specifically binds to the inflammatory cytokine tumor necrosis factor (TNF) and is approved for treatment of several chronic inflammatory conditions. Adalimumab-atto (AMJEVITA®, Amgen Inc.), also known as ABP 501, was developed as a biosimilar to adalimumab (HUMIRA®, AbbVie Inc.). AMJEVITA® (adalimumab-atto) in the United States (USA) and AMGEVITA® (adalimumab) in the European Union (EU) and other countries is approved as a 50 mg/mL and a 100 mg/mL formulation [1, 2].

This study was designed and conducted to support the interchangeability between ABP 501 and adalimumab reference product (RP) in accordance with 2019 US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) guidance for interchangeability of biosimilars and a RP [3]. The primary objective was to demonstrate that the risk in terms of safety or diminished efficacy of switching between ABP 501 and adalimumab RP is not greater than the risk of using adalimumab without such a switch. More specifically, the study aimed to demonstrate that multiple switches between adalimumab RP and ABP 501 would not result in differences in predefined key pharmacokinetic (PK) parameters when compared with continued use of adalimumab RP. Safety, immunogenicity, and clinical efficacy measures were also assessed.

Methods

This switching study was developed in consultation with the US FDA and aligned with the 2019 FDA guidance entitled “Considerations in Demonstrating Interchangeability With a Reference Product,” which outlines the regulatory framework for establishing interchangeability between a biosimilar and its RP. The study was conducted at 85 centers in Canada, Estonia, Germany, Latvia, Poland, and the USA (NCT05073315; EudraCT 2021-000542-18; Fig. 1) and was conducted in accordance with International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) Good Clinical Practice (GCP) Guideline E6 (R1)/Integrated Addendum E6 (R2); 21 Code of Federal Regulations Parts 50, 56, and 312; requirements for the conduct of clinical studies as provided in the EU Directive 2001/20/EC; the consensus ethical principles derived from international guidelines including the Declaration of Helsinki and Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences International Ethical Guidelines; and all applicable laws and regulatory requirements. The study protocol, the informed consent form, and any accompanying materials provided to the patients were reviewed and approved by an institutional review board or independent ethics committee at each study center (Table S1).

Participants

Eligible patients were adults aged between 18 and 75 years with moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis for at least 6 months and stable disease for at least 2 months, with a Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) score ≥ 12, involvement of ≥ 10% body surface area (BSA), and static Physician’s Global Assessment (sPGA) score ≥ 3 at screening and at baseline, and candidates for phototherapy or systemic therapy. Key exclusion criteria were diagnoses of erythrodermic psoriasis, pustular psoriasis, guttate psoriasis, medication-induced psoriasis, or other skin conditions that would interfere with evaluations of the effect of investigational product on psoriasis; laboratory abnormalities at screening; previously received adalimumab RP or a biosimilar of adalimumab; received biologic treatment for psoriasis or any investigational agents within the previous month or 5 drug half-lives; received nonbiologic systemic psoriasis therapy or ultraviolet A phototherapy or excimer laser within 4 weeks prior to enrollment, or received ultraviolet B phototherapy or topical psoriasis treatment within 2 weeks prior to enrollment; or had prior use of two or more biologics for treatment of psoriasis. A population of patients with moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis was selected for this study as this population provides a sensitive and relatively homogenous cohort for detecting potential differences in immunogenicity, PK, safety, and/or efficacy in a repeat-dose setting.

Study Design

The study was composed of two periods: a lead-in period of treatment with adalimumab RP (week 1 to week 12), followed by a randomized, two-parallel arm period (week 12 to week 32). Following screening and enrollment, for the lead-in period, patients received a loading dose of 80 mg of adalimumab RP on day 1/week 1, 40 mg 1 week later on day 8/week 2, then 40 mg in 2-week intervals at weeks 4, 6, 8, and 10. At week 12, patients with at least 50% improvement in PASI (PASI 50 response) were randomized either to a continued-use group or a switching group in a 1:1 ratio, stratified by geographic region and prior biologic use at baseline (two-parallel arm period). Patients randomized to the continued-use group continued receiving adalimumab RP every 2 weeks, with the last dose at week 28. Patients randomized to the switching group switched at week 12 to receive ABP 501 at week 12 and week 14, then back to adalimumab RP for week 16 and week 18, and switched again to ABP 501 for weeks 20, 22, 24, and 26, with the last dose of ABP 501 at week 28. An end-of-study (EOS) visit was conducted at week 32. The study was conducted using ABP 501 100 mg/mL and adalimumab RP 100 mg/mL. Both investigational products were supplied as approved 40 mg/0.4 mL prefilled syringe.

Following randomization at week 12, the study was conducted in a double-blind manner; therefore, patients and investigators were blinded to patients’ treatment allocation. Randomization was conducted using an interactive web/voice response system (IXRS) and stratified by prior biologic use for psoriasis at baseline (week 1/day 1, yes vs no) and geographic region. At week 12, a unique patient randomization number was received by the contract research organization (CRO) to randomize the patient centrally to either the switching group or continued-use group in a 1:1 manner. All enrolled patients not randomized at week 12 were considered lead-in failures and documented. Randomization data was kept confidential, accessible only to the randomization statistician, an unblinded statistician, and unblinded programmers.

Endpoints and Study Assessments

PK and immunogenicity were assessed at baseline and weeks 6, 12, 16, 18, 20, and 28. Serum PK samples and blood samples for assessment of anti-drug antibodies (ADA) were collected prior to investigational product administration. Screening and confirmatory assay cut points were established statistically during assay validation, consistent with FDA recommendations for ADA assay validation [4]. Binding ADAs were assessed using a validated electrochemiluminescent bridging immunoassay with a two-tiered approach to detect binding antibodies and confirm specificity. Assay sensitivity was approximately < 25 ng/mL. Drug tolerance assessment demonstrated detection of 25 ng/mL of ADA in the presence of 25 µg/mL drug. Samples positive for binding ADAs were subsequently evaluated for neutralizing antibodies [5].

Additional serum PK samples were collected at 1, 3, 4, 7, 11, and 14 days after week 28 dosing. Serum PK samples were analyzed using a validated electrochemiluminescence assay. The primary study endpoints were the PK parameters of area under the concentration–time curve (AUCtau) and maximum serum concentration (Cmax) between week 28 and week 30. Serum samples were also analyzed for binding and neutralizing ADAs and tabulated descriptively.

Secondary endpoints included time to maximum serum concentration (tmax) between week 28 and week 30, trough serum concentration (Ctrough) between week 14 and week 28, improvement in PASI percent from baseline to week 30, as well as PASI 75 response at week 30. Safety endpoints included treatment-emergent adverse events (AEs), treatment-emergent serious adverse events (SAEs), and events of interest (EOIs) post randomization. Safety assessments conducted included physical examination, vital sign measurement, tuberculosis testing, chest radiography, and clinical laboratory tests. AEs were graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (version 4.03). Prespecified EOIs for this study included serious infections, malignancies, hypersensitivity reactions, demyelinating diseases, hematological reactions, heart failure, lupus-like syndromes, liver enzyme elevations, and injection site reactions. The EOIs were retrieved utilizing Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) system organ class (SOC) queries, standardized MedDRA queries, or Amgen MedDRA queries.

Statistics

A sample of 414 patients was planned to ensure approximately 342 patients would be randomized at week 12, considering 17% lead-in failures. This sample size was calculated to provide at least 90% power for similarity of primary PK endpoints using two one-sided tests at a 0.05 significance level. This assumed a between-patient variability of 53% for ABP 501 100 mg/mL and adalimumab RP 100 mg/mL, a geometric mean ratio (GMR) of 1, a similarity margin of (0.8, 1.25), and a 15% dropout rate from randomization through week 30. PK parameters were calculated from the serum concentration–time data using noncompartmental methods (Phoenix WinNonlin®, version 8.3, Pharsight Corp, St. Louis, MO) and actual sampling times. The area under the curves were calculated using the linear trapezoidal method. The point estimates and 90% confidence intervals (CIs) for the GMRs between ABP 501 and adalimumab RP for AUCtau and Cmax between weeks 28 and 30 were estimated using an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) model adjusting for stratification factors, weight, and PK trough concentration at the end of the lead-in period (week 12). Prior to statistical testing, the PK parameters were logarithmically transformed (natural log). Point estimates and 90% CIs for the mean difference in logarithmic PK parameters were estimated using the ANCOVA model, which were transformed back to the original scale to obtain the point estimates and 90% CIs for GMR. To support a demonstration of interchangeability, the 90% CIs of GMR were required to fall within the prespecified similarity margin (0.8, 1.25).

The protocol-defined statistical analyses were amended one time prior to unblinding. The PK parameter analysis set was revised to include all randomized patients who received at least one dose of the investigational product post randomization, provided they had an evaluable serum concentration–time profile between weeks 28 and 30. Accordingly, the per-protocol (PP) PK parameter analysis set was adjusted to comprise all patients within the PK parameter analysis set who did not have any important protocol deviations during the study that could impact the primary PK endpoints. Other analysis sets included the full analysis set (FAS; all randomized patients), PP efficacy analysis set (patients who received all assigned doses and had not experienced any important protocol deviations affecting efficacy), and safety analysis set (patients who were randomized and received at least one dose of IP). The analytical methods described in this section are based on the final protocol (version 1.0, dated 03 June 2021) and the final SAP (version 2.0; dated 10 February 2023). All statistical analyses were performed using SAS® (Version 9.4 or higher).

Results

Participants

A total of 425 patients were enrolled in the study (Fig. 2) and had a mean age of 44.7 years, with a range of 18 to 75 years. Prior to or at week 12 (open-label lead-in period), 45 patients discontinued the study. The most common reason for discontinuation was not meeting PASI 50 criterion at week 12 (23 [5.4%]). One (0.2%) patient discontinued due to death (the event was considered unrelated to the investigation product or study by the investigator) and one (0.2%) patient due to a COVID-19-related AE. A total of 380 patients completed the lead-in period and were randomized at week 12 (n = 186, switching group; n = 194, continued-use group). Post randomization, demographic baseline characteristics, and baseline disease characteristics for patients, were generally similar between the two treatment groups. Overall, 352 (92.6%) of the 380 randomized patients completed investigational product dosing at week 28 (28 patients discontinued; 9 [4.8%] in the switching group and 19 [9.8%] in the continued-use group); while 347 (91.3%) of the 380 randomized patients completed the study (33 patients discontinued; 12 [6.5%] in the switching group and 21 [10.8%] in the continued-use group). The most common reason for discontinuing the study was “consent withdrawn” (13 [3.4%]). In general, investigational product dosing protocol adherence and study completion/discontinuation rates were similar between the two treatment groups.

Pharmacokinetic Evaluation

The point estimates and 90% CIs for the GMRs of AUCtau and Cmax between the switching group and the continued-use group were 1.0516 (0.9010, 1.2273) and 1.0044 (0.8717, 1.1574), respectively. The 90% CIs were within the predefined similarity margin (0.8, 1.25), thus demonstrating PK similarity between the two groups (Table 1). To assess the robustness of the primary analysis result, sensitivity analysis was performed using the PP PK parameter analysis set and ANCOVA with gender covariate added. The point estimates and 90% CIs of the GMRs for AUCtau and Cmax between the groups in both analyses were fully contained within the prespecified similarity margin (0.8, 1.25). Subgroup analysis of neutralizing ADA-negative patients utilizing the PK parameter analysis resulted in point estimates and 90% CIs of the GMRs for AUCtau and Cmax were fully contained within the prespecified similarity margin (0.8, 1.25; Table S2). Secondary PK endpoints (tmax between week 28 and week 30 and Ctrough between week 12 and week 28) and associated descriptive statistics were also similar between treatment groups. Median (range) tmax between week 28 and week 30 was 72.30 (0.0, 266.5) hours in the switching group and 72.35 (0.0, 334.9) hours in the continued-use group. Resulting serum PK trough concentrations between week 12 and week 28 and the point estimates and 90% CIs of the GMRs for Ctrough (between week 16 and week 28) between the groups were similar (Table 2). Serum PK trough concentrations were similar between the switching group and continued-use group between week 12 and week 28 (Fig. S1). Mean (± STD) PK concentration–time profiles between week 28 and week 30 show a high degree of similarity between the switching group and continued-use group (Fig. S2).

Efficacy

Efficacy outcomes were assessed as secondary endpoints and analyzed descriptively. Overall, the efficacy endpoints were comparable between the two treatment groups through week 30. Mean PASI percent improvement from baseline at week 30 was 88.8% (95% CI 86.40, 91.14) in the switching group and 90.8% (95% CI 88.60, 93.03) in the continued-use group based on the FAS (observed data). The difference between the treatment groups was − 1.91 with the two-sided 90% CI of (− 4.59, 0.77). Mean PASI scores (SD) from baseline over time based on PP efficacy analysis set were similar between the treatment groups through week 30 (Fig. S3). PASI 75 response based on the PP efficacy analysis set (NRI) at week 30 was also similar between the two treatment groups (78.4% [95% CI 71.4, 84.4] and 81.7% [95% CI 74.9, 87.3] for the switching and continued-use groups, respectively). The difference between the treatment groups was − 3.6% with the two-sided 90% CI of (− 10.4, 3.2).

Immunogenicity

ADAs were tracked over time, with transient ADA defined as a positive result followed by a negative at the study’s last available assessment and ADA responses not meeting this criterion were considered persistent. At baseline, of the 419 patients with a result, 21 (5.0%) tested positive for pre-existing binding ADAs and none tested positive for pre-existing neutralizing ADAs. A total of 420 patients had a post-baseline result through the lead-on period, 356 (84.8%) tested positive for ADAs (29 [6.9%] were transient) and 37 (8.8%) tested positive for neutralizing ADAs. Post randomization and through the EOS, 186 and 194 patients had results in the switching group and continued-use group, respectively. Among patients who were ADA-negative or had no result prior to the first post-randomization dose, the incidence of developing binding and neutralizing ADAs was similar between the switching group and the continued-use group post randomization (Table 3). In both groups, most post-randomization ADA responses were persistent, with only a small proportion being transient. PK similarity was observed across all patients and those without neutralizing ADAs, indicating immunogenicity did not affect systemic exposure in the switching group.

Safety

In the lead-in period, all 425 patients were treated with adalimumab RP 100 mg/mL. AEs were reported by 176 (41.4%) patients, with grade ≥ 3 AEs reported in 9 (2.1%) patients; discontinuation of investigational product or of study was reported in 7 (1.6%) patients. The patient incidence of SAEs was 5 (1.2%) and 1 patient experienced a fatal AE (considered unrelated to investigational product or study by the investigator). In the post-randomization period, AEs were reported by 97 (52.2%) patients in the switching group and 106 (54.6%) patients in the continued-use group (Table 4). The majority of AEs were mild (CTCAE grade 1 or 2), with grade ≥ 3 AEs reported in 2 (1.1%) patients in the switching group and 5 (2.6%) patients in the continued-use group; discontinuation of investigational product or of study was reported in 3 (1.6%) and 5 (2.6%) patients, respectively. There was no notable difference in the frequency of AEs by treatment group.

For both treatment groups, the highest patient incidence rates of AEs were in the SOC of infections and infestations (52 [28.0%] patients in the switching group and 51 [26.3%] patients in the continued-use group). SAEs occurred in 3 (1.6%) patients in the switching group and 4 (2.1%) in the continued-use group (Table 4). There were no differences in the occurrence of SAEs by preferred term or SOC between the treatment groups and no fatal AEs were reported during this period. EOIs prespecified for this study were serious infections, malignancies, hypersensitivity reactions, demyelinating diseases, hematological reactions, heart failure, lupus-like syndromes, liver enzyme elevations, and injection site reactions. The distribution of type, frequency, and severity of the EOIs observed were similar between treatment groups (Table 4). Of note, two patients in the switching group and no patients in the continued-use group experienced hypersensitivity reactions. The hypersensitivity reactions observed in the switching group were limited to moderate, nonserious contact dermatitis and hand dermatitis, which resolved on treatment. There were no reports of systemic hypersensitivity reactions. The safety events observed were within the expected range for incidence and severity as described for adalimumab RP or ABP 501. No new or unexpected safety signals were identified.

Discussion

This study was conducted to demonstrate that multiple switches between ABP 501 and adalimumab RP would not result in differences in treatment outcomes, when compared to continued use of adalimumab RP. Previous clinical and observational studies in populations of moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis, rheumatoid arthritis, and inflammatory bowel disease have demonstrated similarity in clinical efficacy, safety, immunogenicity, and tolerability of ABP 501 with adalimumab RP [5–7]. A switching study is not intended to re-establish similarity, but to assess the potential impact of multiple switches between an RP and its biosimilar in line with the FDA guidelines issued in 2019 [3].

In this study, PK similarity was demonstrated between the switching group and the group treated continuously with the RP, based on the 90% CIs of the GMRs for the primary PK endpoints (AUCtau and Cmax between week 28 and week 30) falling within the pre-defined margins of PK similarity. Thus, the primary objective of the study was met. Secondary analyses of the primary PK endpoints and the analyses of the secondary PK endpoints support the conclusion of PK similarity between the two groups. This similarity was maintained across the neutralizing ADA-negative patient subgroup analyses. Safety profiles, incidences of developing binding and neutralizing ADAs, and efficacy measures were also similar between the treatment groups, further supporting the study conclusion.

Limitations

It should be acknowledged that this study was conducted as controlled switches within a clinical trial and thus may not account for every possible scenario that could arise in real-world clinical practice. Of note, the US FDA’s scientific approach to switching studies in support of biosimilar product interchangeability is evolving. According to the 2024 draft guidance, such studies may no longer be routinely required [7].

Conclusion

Results of this study demonstrated PK similarity in patients with moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis who underwent multiple back-and-forth switches between ABP 501 and adalimumab RP as compared with those treated continuously with the RP, with comparable safety, immunogenicity, and efficacy across treatment groups. This study, along with the totality of evidence supporting the conclusion that APB 501 is highly similar with adalimumab RP, supports the FDA approval of ABP 501 as an interchangeable adalimumab biosimilar [8].
