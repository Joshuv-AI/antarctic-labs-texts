Blood DNA methylation signature of cognitive reserve moderates the association between CSF tau pathology and memory in prodromal Alzheimer's disease
Authors: David Lukacsovich, Juan I Young, Lissette Gomez, Brian W Kunkle, Zhixin Mao, Wei Zhang, X Steven Chen, Deirdre M O'Shea, Tatjana Rundek, Eden R Martin, Lily Wang, for the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONCognitive reserve (CR) reflects variability in cognitive adaptability that modifies the impact of Alzheimer's disease (AD) pathology on cognition. However, blood‐based biomarkers of CR have not been established in prodromal AD. We operationalized CR as memory reserve, defined by the attenuation of the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) phosphorylated tau threonine 181 (pTau181)–memory association and aimed to identify blood DNA methylation (DNAm) loci involved in memory reserve.METHODSWe studied 92 amyloid‐positive participants with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) with blood DNAm, CSF pTau181, and memory (PHC_MEM) measured at the same visit. Memory was residualized after adjustment for age, sex, APOE 𝜀4 allele count, and estimated immune cell‐type proportions. For each CpG, linear models tested DNAm, pTau181, and DNAm×pTau181 interaction; inflation was corrected using the bacon method. In addition, we also identified differentially methylated regions (DMRs). Moreover, we constructed a methylation reserve score (MRS) from loci identified in this cohort at baseline and tested its associations with longitudinal memory using linear mixed‐effects models in 88 participants with follow‐up information.RESULTSAfter removing low‐variability CpGs, we identified six CpGs with suggestive DNAm×pTau181 interaction (p value < 1 × 10−5, none passed a 5% false discovery rate) and 11 DMRs passing multiple‐comparisons correction. The suggestive CpGs and significant DMRs mapped to genes implicating synaptic function, vascular/blood–brain barrier integrity, and immune regulation, with minimal marginal associations with pTau181 or memory, consistent with a moderation model rather than mediation. In this cohort, higher baseline MRS was associated with attenuation of the pTau181–memory association and with slower subsequent memory decline, independent of age, sex, education, APOE ε4, and baseline pTau181.DISCUSSIONBlood DNAm that moderates the pTau181–memory association may reflect epigenetic correlates of memory reserve (i.e., differential susceptibility to tau‐related memory impairment), rather than reflecting variations in pTau181 levels. These DNAm patterns can be summarized as a MRS that, in this cohort, was associated with longitudinal memory trajectories in MCI. Further validation in independent cohorts is warranted.Keywords: cognitive reserve, DNA methylation, CSF pTau181, mild cognitive impairment, Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative, ADNI

BACKGROUND

The pathological hallmark of Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the accumulation of aggregated amyloid beta plaques and tau neurofibrillary tangles in the brain. However, there is considerable heterogeneity in clinical presentation that cannot be fully explained by the extent of AD pathology. It has been estimated that age‐related neuropathological burden explains only about half of the variance in cognitive decline, 1 , 2 , 3 suggesting that additional factors are involved in the clinical manifestation of AD.

Cognitive reserve (CR) refers to differences in the adaptability of cognitive processes that modify the impact of AD pathology on cognitive performance. 4 , 5 It has been observed that higher CR is associated with preserved cognitive function, even after accounting for brain pathologies. 6 In this study, we operationalized CR as memory reserve (i.e., memory‐related CR), defined as attenuation of the CSF tau phosphorylated at threonine 181 (pTau181)–memory association. The moderation approach, widely regarded as the most direct evidence of CR, tests whether a candidate CR factor attenuates the pathology‐to‐cognition association. 4 , 7 Notably, in this moderation framework, a reserve factor may show weak or no marginal association with pathology or cognition individually yet still meaningfully alter the pathology–cognition association. 8

Despite its recognized importance, the biological underpinnings of CR remain poorly understood. While a number of studies investigated CR using genetic and proteomic approaches, 9 , 10 , 11 few have examined its epigenetic basis. DNA methylation (DNAm) is influenced by both genetic and environmental factors, including lifestyle factors such as smoking, diet, and exercise, 12 which have been shown to contribute to CR. 13

In this study, we analyzed samples from the mild cognitive impairment (MCI) subjects in the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) study. We used cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pTau181 as our index of tau pathology because it is sensitive to early disease‐related changes, significantly associated with clinical cognitive symptoms, and correlates with greater baseline tau‐positron emission tomography (PET) signal, as well as faster longitudinal tau accumulation, especially among amyloid‐positive subjects. 14 , 15 Importantly, we evaluated the feasibility of DNAm as a biomarker for individualized prognosis in MCI, a stage when disease trajectories may still be modifiable.

METHODS

Study participants

Blood DNAm data from 92 amyloid‐positive, self‐identified non‐Hispanic White MCI participants were obtained from the ADNI cohort. Each subject also had data on CSF p‐tau181 (PHC_pTau), memory composite scores (PHC_MEM), and amyloid positivity status at the same visit. Data were downloaded from adni.loni.usc.edu.

Cognitive measures and CSF biomarkers

The harmonized measures were generated by the Alzheimer's Disease Sequencing Project Phenotype Harmonization Consortium (ADSP‐PHC). 16 PHC_MEM is a memory composite derived primarily from verbal learning and delayed‐recall measures using confirmatory factor analysis. 16 The CSF AD biomarkers amyloid beta (Aβ) 42, total tau, and pTau181 were quantified using the Roche Elecsys platform 17 and subsequently co‐calibrated across ADSP‐PHC cohorts. Harmonization involved removing duplicate observations, excluding outliers, and standardizing the remaining values to z‐scores. 18 Amyloid‐positive (A+) status was then assigned by applying a two‐component Gaussian mixture model to the post‐quality‐control z‐scores of CSF Aβ42.

Identification of DNAm loci moderating the pTau–memory association

The details of DNAm data pre‐processing are described in Supplementary Text and Table S1. Using the quality‐controlled DNAm data, we estimated major immune cell‐type proportions using the EpiDISH R package. Granulocyte proportions were computed as the sum of neutrophil and eosinophil proportions. After quality control, a total of 776,649 CpGs were analyzed. We then regressed PHC_MEM scores on covariates (age, sex, number of APOE ε4 alleles, and estimated major immune cell type proportions) and used the residuals as covariate‐adjusted memory scores. A significant DNAm×pTau181 interaction (γ ≠ 0) indicates that the association between pTau181 and memory depends on DNAm at that CpG. Assuming higher pTau181 is associated with worse memory (β < 0), a positive γ indicates attenuation of the negative pTau181–memory association at higher DNAm, which corresponds to greater memory reserve. On the other hand, a negative γ indicates more negative pTau181– memory association at higher DNAm (i.e., less negative association at lower DNAm), so greater memory reserve corresponds to lower DNAm. The pTau181 slope at a given DNAm level is estimated by β^+γ^DNAm. Thus, DNAm moderates the pTau–memory association through the DNAm×pTau181 interaction term γ^, which determines how the pTau181–memory slope varies across methylation levels.

Secondary analyses were performed to evaluate whether CpGs identified in the memory reserve analyses were also associated with other cognitive domains. We tested associations between the 94 CpGs identified in the PHC_MEM analyses (six suggestive single‐site CpGs and 88 CpGs located in differentially methylated regions [DMRs]) with harmonized language (PHC_LAN) and executive function (PHC_EXF) composite scores using the same linear regression model and covariate adjustment described for PHC_MEM, replacing the outcome with the language or executive function score, respectively.

Inflation assessment and correction

Using the bacon method, 19 the estimated inflation factor was 1.06 and the estimated bias was −0.058. After genomic correction, the estimated inflation factor and bias were 1 and 9.90×10−7. To exclude CpGs with minimal variability, we required an interquartile range (IQR) greater than 0.02 in β values across all samples. We considered CpGs with p values less than 1×10−5 to have suggestive significance and computed false discovery rate (FDR).

Differentially methylated regions analysis

For region‐based analysis, we used the comb‐p method, 20 with bacon‐corrected p values of the DNAm×pTau181 interaction effect as input and parameter settings –seed 0.05 and –dist 750. 21 We selected DMRs with Sidak p values less than 0.05 after multiple‐comparisons correction, and having all the CpGs within the DMR with a consistent direction of change.

Computation of methylation reserve scores (MRS) and assessment of its association with future memory decline

The MRS was computed based on the six CpGs meeting the pre‐specified suggestive threshold (p < 1×10−5) and 88 CpGs located within significant DMRs (Sidak p < 0.05). In Model 1 described above, for each subject and at each CpG, the pTau effect on memory at a given DNAm level is estimated as β^pTau+γ^pTau×DNAmM, where β^pTau is the estimated main effect of pTau, γ^pTau×DNAm is the estimated interaction effect, and M is the methylation beta value. We then averaged the CpG‐specific values for pTau effect on memory across the six suggestive CpGs and 88 CpGs located within DMRs to obtain a subject‐specific raw score (MRSraw), so that lower values indicate greater pTau‐associated memory decline. It was then rescaled as a z‐score. Note that in each CpG‐specific regression model, the estimated main effect of pTau181 (i.e., β^pTau) is the same for all individuals and therefore contributes only a constant shift to MRS and not to between‐subject differences in MRS. In addition, the participant's measured CSF pTau181 value is not used to compute the MRS. Instead, subject‐to‐subject variation in MRS arises from individual DNAm levels through the DNAm×pTau181 interaction effect γ^, which quantifies how the pTau181–memory association varies across methylation levels. Because the MRS is derived from DNAm×pTau interaction terms and is then z‐scaled, it reflects DNAm‐dependent susceptibility/resilience to tau‐related memory impairment rather than DNAm associations with pTau181 levels.

RESULTS

Study cohort

The amyloid‐positive MCI participants had a mean age of 74.67 ± 7.51 years; 45% (41 subjects) were women, and the mean duration of education was 16.21 ± 2.78 years (Table S2). Approximately half (52%, 48 subjects) carried at least one APOE ε4 allele, and 43% (40 subjects) reported a history of smoking. Baseline CSF pTau181 biomarker concentration was 46.46 ± 27.32 pg/mL. The mean baseline memory score (PHC_MEM) was 0.13 ± 0.55. Participants were followed for 4.18 ± 2.48 years with 4.23 ± 2.13 visits on average.

The CSF pTau181–memory association varies by DNAm at individual CpGs and DMRs

We identified 11 CpGs with a DNAm×pTau181 interaction effect meeting the suggestive significance threshold (p < 1×10−5); none reached the 5% FDR significance. After excluding low‐variability CpGs (IQR < 0.02 in beta values across all samples), six CpGs remained (Table 1, Figures S1–S2). Across these six CpGs, four showed positive interaction estimates, such that higher DNAm was associated with a weaker (less negative) pTau–memory relationship. In contrast, for two CpGs (cg01602139 and cg11145222), the interaction estimates were negative, indicating that lower DNAm was associated with a weaker pTau181–memory relationship (i.e., greater memory reserve).

We next took a closer look at the six CpGs showing suggestive evidence of a DNAm×pTau181 interaction. At cg03281038 in the UNC13C promoter region, the pTau–memory association varied by DNAm level. When DNAm was fixed at the 25th percentile, higher pTau was significantly associated with poorer memory performance (β = −0.192, 95% CI: −0.285 to −0.100) (Figure 1). In contrast, when DNAm was fixed at the 75th percentile, the pTau–memory slope was near 0 (β = 0.008, 95% CI: −0.092 to 0.108), indicating attenuation of pTau–memory association and supporting a moderation effect. UNC13C encodes a presynaptic protein that primes synaptic vesicles and is essential for neurotransmitter release, a process supporting synaptic function and neural communication that has been repeatedly implicated in cognitive resilience. 22

In contrast, when DNAm was fixed at the 25th percentile, the pTau–memory association was not significant (β = 0.071, 95% CI: −0.041 to 0.182), indicating attenuation at lower DNAm. Biologically, TSPAN18 has been implicated in endothelial calcium signaling and thrombo‐inflammatory responses, processes contributing to vascular and blood–brain barrier (BBB) integrity. 23 Impairment of vascular and BBB integrity has been associated with cognitive decline, aging, and AD pathology. 24

Interestingly, we found that none of these CpGs showing a significant DNAm×pTau interaction had marginal associations with CSF pTau181 or memory that survived Bonferroni correction; the only nominal signal was for cg26869675 with memory (p = 0.038), which did not remain significant after multiple‐testing correction (Figure S3). This pattern is expected under a moderation model and supports interpreting these loci as modifiers of the pTau181–memory association rather than biomarkers of tau burden or current memory performance.

Using p values for individual CpGs as input, comb‐p software 20 identified 11 DMRs (Table 2). The number of CpGs in these DMRs ranged from 3 to 17. The majority (seven DMRs, 64%) showed hypermethylation associated with greater memory reserve. As with top‐ranked (suggestive) individual CpGs, we found that all DMRs, except one located in the gene body of JAKMIP3, showed no significant association with either CSF pTau181 or memory (Figure S4). Together with the CpG results, this further supports moderation rather than mediation or main‐effect associations with pTau181 or memory. Notably, the six suggestive single‐CpG findings did not overlap the 11 significant DMRs. This could happen because DMR detection prioritizes clusters of spatially correlated CpGs with consistent evidence, while single‐CpG testing can identify isolated peaks that may not be supported by neighboring CpGs and therefore do not form a region‐level signal.

In secondary analyses of other cognitive domains, we tested the 94 CpGs selected in the PHC_MEM analyses for association with harmonized language and executive function scores using the same regression framework (Table S3). For language, 24 CpGs showed nominal associations (p < 0.05), with a minimum p value of 3.5 × 10−4, and one CpG passed Bonferroni correction (p < 0.05/94 ≈ 5.3 × 10−4). For executive function, 21 CpGs were nominally associated, with a minimum p value of 1.19 × 10−4, and four CpGs passed Bonferroni correction.

Because 43% of participants reported a history of smoking, we refitted the models with additional adjustment for smoking status. Effect estimates and p values were very similar, and the main conclusions were unchanged (Table S4), indicating that these associations are unlikely to be explained by smoking history.

To provide context for the DNAm associations described above, we also evaluated the relationship between pTau and memory performance. We found that higher pTau was associated with poorer memory performance (unadjusted Spearman ρ = −0.31, p = 0.0028; Figure S5). After adjusting for age at visit, gender, APOE ε4, and education (using covariate‐adjusted memory residuals), the association remained in the same direction but was attenuated (Spearman ρ = −0.20, p = 0.0548).

Methylation‐based memory reserve biomarkers at baseline are significantly associated with subsequent longitudinal memory trajectories in MCI

To evaluate whether cognitive reserve‐related DNAm at baseline was associated with subsequent memory decline within ADNI, we developed a MRS from CpGs selected based on DNAm×pTau interaction evidence in this cohort, including six individual CpGs and 88 CpGs located within DMRs. For each subject and at each CpG, the pTau effect on memory was computed as β^pTau+γ^pTau×DNAmM, where β^pTau is the estimated main effect of pTau, γ^pTau×DNAm is the estimated interaction effect in Model 1, and M is methylation beta value for the particular subject at that CpG (Methods). For each subject, the MRS was then computed by averaging these CpG‐specific values across all 94 selected CpGs. Note that the MRS reflects the DNAm‐dependent effect of pTau on memory for each subject, and lower MRS values indicate greater pTau‐associated memory decline (i.e., a more negative pTau effect on memory), while higher values indicate attenuation of pTau's impact (i.e., less negative pTau effect on memory, thus more memory reserve). Because the CpG‐specific β^pTau terms are constants (i.e., they do not vary across individuals), they contribute only a constant shift to the MRS scores and cannot drive between‐subject differences or associations with longitudinal outcomes. Therefore, participant‐to‐participant variation in MRS is determined by the DNAm‐dependent interaction component γ^pTau×DNAmM.

Among 92 MCI subjects, 88 had at least one follow‐up memory assessment. We restricted the longitudinal dataset to follow‐up visits occurring after the baseline visit and fitted linear mixed‐effects models to assess the association between baseline MRS and longitudinal memory (PHC_MEM). Random intercept effects for each subject were included to account for within‐subject correlations among repeated measures. We adjusted for baseline pTau, age, sex, education, and APOE 𝜀4 and included interaction effects MRS×time, pTau×time, and pTau×MRS. The interpretations of the mixed‐effects model coefficients are presented in Table S5. Note that baseline age, pTau, and MRS were standardized (z‐scored), so that their mean baseline values were 0.

Our results showed that memory declined significantly over time (β time = −0.050 per year, p = 1.8 × 10−9) (Table 3). At baseline (time = 0, average MRS and pTau), older age and APOE 𝜀4 carrier status are significantly associated with lower memory (β age = −0.308, p = 6.1×10−6; β APOE4 = −0.480, p = 2.2×10−5), while sex, education, and the main effect of MRS were not significant. Higher baseline pTau is associated with lower baseline memory (β = −0.138, p = 0.025); it also predicted faster decline (β time×pTau = −0.026 per year per unit increase in pTau, p = 3.0 ×10−4). At baseline, MRS attenuates the harmful effect of pTau on memory (β MRS×pTau = 0.217, p = 2.4 × 10−4). Importantly, higher MRS at baseline is significantly associated with slower decline (β MRS×time = 0.034, p = 9.5 × 10−5). Taken together, these results suggest that higher MRS may indicate greater memory reserve in this cohort, reflected by reduced pTau‐related memory impairment at baseline and slower longitudinal memory decline.

Figure 3 shows changes from baseline in harmonized memory scores for the 88 MCI subjects, grouped by tertiles of their baseline MRS. Notably, subjects with higher MRS showed slower memory decline over time. To formally test group differences, we refit the mixed model described above, except by replacing MRS scores with MRS tertile indicators. Our results showed that compared to the high‐MRS group, the low‐MRS group declined significantly faster (β low MRS×time = −0.053, p = 0.008) and showed a stronger harmful baseline pTau‐to‐memory association (β low MRS×pTau = −0.520, p = 4.1×10−4); the mid‐ versus high‐MRS comparisons were not significant (Table S6). Overall, higher baseline MRS is associated with more attenuation in tau‐related effects on memory at baseline, as well as slower longitudinal memory decline, corresponding to greater memory reserve.

DISCUSSION

We performed an interaction‐based analyses to identify a blood DNAm signature of CR that modified the association between CSF pTau181 and memory in amyloid‐positive ADNI participants with MCI. After excluding low‐variability CpGs, we identified 6 CpGs showing a DNAm×pTau181 interaction at a suggestive significance threshold (p < 1×10−5), and 11 DMRs were significant after multiple‐comparisons correction. A composite baseline MRS derived from these loci was significantly associated with slower subsequent longitudinal memory decline in MCI, independent of baseline pTau181, age, sex, APOE ε4, and education. However, because the MRS was derived and evaluated within the same ADNI cohort, these results should be considered exploratory and require independent validation before clinical translation.

A key feature of these CR‐related loci is that, despite showing robust DNAm×pTau181 interaction effects, nearly all lacked strong marginal associations with pTau or memory (Figure S3 and S4). The lack of strong marginal effects argues against a mediating role of DNAm in the pTau–memory relationship, but instead supports a moderating role, in which DNAm at these loci, or the biological mechanisms they mark (e.g., transcription factor binding, chromatin accessibility, histone modifications), may be associated with variations in the impact of tau pathology on memory. Future mechanistic and longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether DNAm at these loci is causal or instead serves as a peripheral marker of other CR‐related regulatory processes. Functional annotations (expression quantitative trait methylations, methylation quantitative trait loci, dementia genome‐wide assocation study overlaps) and blood–brain concordance are detailed in Tables S7–S10.

A key contribution of this study is the construction and evaluation of a MRS that aggregates information across multiple CR‐related CpGs and DMRs into a single, interpretable measure. By definition, this score quantifies the DNAm‐dependent effect of pTau on memory, such that higher values represent greater attenuation of tau's harmful impact. Although the CpG‐specific slope expression (β^pTau+γ^DNAm) includes the estimated main pTau181 effect (i.e., β^pTau), that term is constant across individuals and thus does not contribute to between‐subject variability in MRS. The score's variability is driven by DNAm via the DNAm×pTau181 interaction component. In longitudinal mixed‐effects models, a higher MRS at baseline was significantly associated with slower subsequent decline in harmonized memory scores among MCI participants, even after adjusting for age, sex, education, APOE ε4, and baseline pTau. Moreover, individuals in the lowest MRS tertile experienced both a stronger pTau–memory association at baseline and a faster rate of memory decline compared with those in the highest tertile (Table S6). These findings suggest that CR‐related DNAm patterns may provide prognostic information about future clinical trajectories in prodromal AD, but the magnitude and generalizability of this association require validation in independent cohorts.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, because we operationalized reserve using PHC_MEM (a harmonized memory composite), our findings pertain to memory reserve and may not generalize to reserve in other cognitive domains. Second, the MRS was derived from baseline moderation effects and evaluated for association with longitudinal memory within the same ADNI cohort. Therefore, this internal evaluation does not constitute independent validation. Third, our analysis was conducted in a relatively small sample of amyloid‐positive MCI participants with concurrent CSF, DNAm, and memory data in ADNI, which limited statistical power, particularly for genome‐wide interaction models, and likely contributed to the fact that no single CpG interaction survived 5% FDR correction. Therefore, the CpG‐level associations should be considered suggestive until independently validated in external cohorts. Fourth, the ADNI cohort is highly educated and predominantly of European ancestry, and our results may not generalize to more diverse populations or to individuals at different stages of the AD disease course. Fifth, the use of CSF pTau181 as the pathology term in the interaction model focused our analyses on tau‐related processes that are closely associated with Aβ deposition and may not capture reserve mechanisms that primarily operate through other non‐AD pathologies. Finally, DNAm, CSF pTau181, and memory were measured concurrently at baseline. Therefore, we cannot infer causal directionality.

In conclusion, this study provides preliminary evidence that blood DNAm captures biologically meaningful variation in memory reserve in MCI. We identified CpGs and DMRs that moderate the association between CSF pTau181 and memory and integrated them into a MRS that, in this cohort, was associated with future memory decline. These findings highlight the promise of blood DNAm as a non‐invasive biomarker for resilience to AD pathology. Future work should validate and refine the MRS in independent and more diverse cohorts with matched DNAm, CSF biomarkers, and longitudinal cognitive follow‐up.
