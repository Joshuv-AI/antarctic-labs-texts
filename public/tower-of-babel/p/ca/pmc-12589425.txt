Olig1/2 Orchestrates Progenitor Cell Fates during Mammalian Cortical Gliogenesis and Gliomagenesis
Authors: Yu Tian, Ziwu Wang, Feihong Yang, Wen Zhang, Jialin Li, Lin Yang, Tongye Fu, Wenhui Zheng, Zhejun Xu, Tong Ma, Yan You, Xiaosu Li, Jiangang Song, Yunli Xie, Zhengang Yang, Zhuangzhi Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDuring cortical gliogenesis, tri-potential intermediate progenitor cells (Tri-IPCs) differentiate into oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs) or olfactory bulb interneuron intermediate progenitors (OBIN-IPCs) - a developmental program frequently co-opted in glioblastoma (GBM) to drive tumorigenesis. Here, we show that the transcription factors Olig1/2 coordinately regulate Tri-IPC fate specification through dual transcriptional mechanisms: they activate OPC specification while simultaneously repressing OBIN-IPC generation by directly suppressing Gsx2 expression. Genetic ablation of Olig1/2 redirects Tri-IPCs from producing proliferative OPCs to generating non-proliferative OBIN-IPCs, concomitant with Gsx2 upregulation. Mechanistically, Olig1/2 bind and silence multiple conserved enhancer elements of Gsx2. Remarkably, in proneural GBM models, Olig1/2 deletion reprograms glioma stem cells toward OBIN-IPC-like cells, potently inhibiting tumor growth and improving survival. Integrative multi-omics and immunohistochemical staining analyses further identify cortical Tri-IPCs as the likely cellular origin of human H3.3G34R/V gliomas. These findings establish Olig1/2 as master regulators linking normal gliogenesis to gliomagenesis, and reveal therapeutic opportunities through fate reprogramming of glioma cells.Subject terms: Developmental neurogenesis, Cell fate and cell lineage, CNS cancer, Cancer genomics, Cancer models

Introduction

The human neocortex, the seat of higher-order cognitive functions such as language and reasoning, is composed of a complex network of neurons and glial cells. Among these, glial cells—astrocytes and oligodendrocytes—play indispensable roles in supporting neuronal function and maintaining brain homeostasis1–3. These cells originate from tri-potential intermediate progenitor cells (Tri-IPCs) during gliogenesis, a process that also retains the plasticity to generate neurons under specific conditions4–6. The fate decisions of Tri-IPCs are tightly regulated by key signaling pathways (e.g., Wnt, Notch, and Shh) and transcription factors (e.g., Nfia, Oct4)4,7–10. However, in gliomas, particularly aggressive forms like glioblastoma (GBM), this finely tuned differentiation process is hijacked. Mutations and dysregulated signaling pathways drive Tri-IPCs or glioma stem cells (GSCs) to produce proliferative oligodendrocyte-like and astrocyte-like cells instead of neurons11–14. Understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying these fate switches could unveil therapeutic targets for glioma treatment.

One promising therapeutic strategy involves reprogramming glioma cells into post-mitotic neurons, thereby inhibiting tumor progression. Central to this approach are the bHLH transcription factors Olig1 and Olig2, which are critical for oligodendrocyte development and are markedly overexpressed in proneural GBM15–21. Although Olig1 and Olig2 are well-characterized in oligodendrogenesis, their functional interplay in cortical Tri-IPCs during gliogenesis and gliomagenesis remains poorly understood.

A unique subset of pediatric high-grade gliomas, histone H3.3G34R/V mutants, exhibits mixed neuronal and astroglial identities but lacks oligodendroglial markers, coinciding with low OLIG1/2 expression11,22–24. Intriguingly, these tumors are hypothesized to arise from GSX2-expressing progenitors in the ventral basal ganglia25,26. However, our recent findings challenge this view by demonstrating Gsx2 expression in cortical-derived Tri-IPCs4,27. This raises a critical question: Could H3.3G34R/V gliomas can also originate from cortical GSX2-expressing Tri-IPCs?

In this study, we employ a combination of Olig mutant mouse models and multi-omics approaches to dissect the roles of Olig1/2 in Tri-IPCs during gliogenesis and gliomagenesis. We reveal that Olig1/2 ablation shifts Tri-IPC fate from highly proliferative oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs) to low-proliferative olfactory bulb interneuron progenitor cells (OBIN-IPCs). Mechanistically, Olig1/2 orchestrate this fate switch by directly repressing Gsx2 expression through binding to its multiple enhancers. Furthermore, we provide compelling evidence that human H3.3G34R/V gliomas likely originate from cortical Tri-IPCs, complementing the prevailing ventral progenitor hypothesis. Our findings not only redefine the developmental origins of these tumors but also highlight Olig1/2 as pivotal regulators bridging normal gliogenesis and gliomagenesis, offering avenues for therapeutic intervention.

Results

Increased cortical-derived olfactory bulb interneuron production in Olig1/2 double-mutant mice

During cortical gliogenesis, bHLH transcription factors Olig1/2 are highly expressed in tri-potential intermediate progenitor cells (Tri-IPCs), which have the potential to generate astrocyte progenitor cells (APCs), oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs), and olfactory bulb interneuron intermediate progenitor cells (OBIN-IPCs)2,4,28. However, their specific roles in Tri-IPCs remain undefined. To address this, we generated multiple mutant mouse lines: conventional Olig1 knockout (Olig1-KO) mice; conditional Olig2 knockout (Olig2-CKO) mice; and double conditional Olig1/2 knockout (Olig1/2-CKO) mice (Supplementary Fig. 1a). For cortical-specific deletion, we crossed Emx1-Cre mice with Olig2F/F or Olig1/2 F/F lines to generate Emx1-Cre; Olig2F/F (Olig2-ECKO) and Emx1-Cre; Olig1/2 F/F (Olig1/2-ECKO) mice. Immunofluorescence staining confirmed that Olig1 was completely absent in the cortex following Olig1 mutation. Conditional knockout of Olig2 or Olig1/2 led to a significant reduction in OLIG1+ and OLIG2+ cells in the cortex, with residual OLIG1/2 double-positive (arrowhead) cells identified as ventral-derived OPCs (Supplementary Fig. 1b–d). Further supporting evidence is presented in subsequent sections, confirming the successful generation of these knockout mouse models.

To determine whether Olig1/2 influences OBIN fate specification, we examined the consequences of Olig1/2 deletion on OBIN development. Based on our previous identification of OBIN-IPCs in the dorsal subventricular zone (dSVZ) expressing DLX2 and SP829, we systematically analyzed these genes throughout development. Quantitative analysis demonstrated a significant expansion of both SP8+ and DLX2+ cells in the dSVZ of both Olig1-KO and Olig2-ECKO mice compared to controls at postnatal day 0 (P0) (Supplementary Fig. 2a–c). Notably, this expansion was substantially greater in Olig1/2 double knockout (Olig1/2-ECKO) mice at P0 compared to both control and single knockout groups (Supplementary Fig. 2a–c). The phenotype became even more pronounced in Olig1/2-ECKO mice by P7 (Fig. 1a, b). This expansion of OBIN-IPCs persisted into adulthood, with Olig1/2-ECKO mice maintaining significantly elevated numbers of SP8+ and DLX2+ cells in the dSVZ compared to the controls (Supplementary Fig. 2d–f). These findings demonstrate that Olig1 and Olig2 function cooperatively to suppress OBIN-IPC generation throughout development.

To comprehensively assess the impact of Olig1/2 deletion on cortical cell composition, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on P7 cortical tissues (n = 3 per group) from control and Olig1/2-ECKO mice (Fig. 1c). Following quality control (13,753 control cells; 13,621 mutant cells retained), UMAP analysis using Seurat 3.2 identified 17 distinct clusters (C0-C16). These clusters were annotated based on canonical marker genes into 10 major cell types (Fig.1c and Supplementary Data 1): Endothelial cells (C0 and C6), Astrocytes (C1 and C7), Oligodendrocyte-lineage (OL-lineage) cells (C2 and C10), OBIN-IPCs (C3 and C5), Immune cells (C4 and C12), Ependymal cells (C8), Mural cells (C9), PyN-lineage cells (C11, C13 and C15), Erythrocyte cells (C14), Leukocyte cells (C16) (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Data 1). Notably, OBIN-IPCs increased from 7.1% in controls to 26.9% in Olig1/2-ECKO mice (Fig. 1d), indicating that Olig1/2 ablation promotes OBIN-IPC production. Subclustering of OBIN-IPCs revealed five distinct populations (Fig. 1e, f): Type B (Clu+/Gfap+/Hopx+), Type C (Btg2+/Ascl1+/Egfr+), Type A (Dlx2+/Gad1+/St18+), immature N1 (Dcx+/Tubb3+/Map1b+), and immature N2 (Erbb4+/Prokr2+/Pbx3+) (Fig. 1e, f and Supplementary Data 2). Importantly, Olig1/2-ablated OBIN-IPCs maintained expression of migration markers Prokr2 and Pbx3 (Fig. 1g, h). In addition, we observed an increase in SP8+ cells within the periglomerular layer (PGL) and granule cell layer (GCL) of the olfactory bulb in Olig1/2-ECKO mice (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). This was further confirmed by in utero electroporation (IUE) experiments demonstrating normal migration of OBINs despite Olig1/2 deletion (Supplementary Fig. 3c, d). Together, these findings demonstrate that Olig1/2 loss increases OBIN-IPC production without compromising their migratory capacity.

Finally, the previous studies have identified Olig1/2 expression in Type C cells during adult neurogenesis30. To specifically examine their roles in this context, we generated Sox2-CreERT2; Olig1/2 F/F; ISF/+ (Olig1/2-SCKO) mice through genetic crossing (Fig. 1i). Following tamoxifen (TM) induction at P10 and P12, we observed a significant increase in the percentage of tdTomato+/SP8+ and tdTomato+/DLX2+ cells in the dSVZ of Olig1/2-SCKO mice compared to controls at P21 (Fig. 1j, k). Even in OB, the tdT/SP8 double-positive cells are also increased (Fig. 1l, m). Taken together, these results demonstrate that Olig1/2 function to suppress OBIN production not only during embryonic development but also throughout postnatal stages.

Distinct and overlapping functions of Olig1 and Olig2 in OPC development and myelination

Although both Olig1 and Olig2 are essential for oligodendrocyte development, their respective contributions to oligodendrocyte precursor cell (OPC) generation and cortical myelination remain debated17,19,20,31–35. To clarify their roles, we first examined markers of oligodendrocyte lineage progression in our mutant mice. Immunostaining demonstrated a pronounced reduction in SOX10+ and PDGFRA+ cells in Olig2-ECKO and Olig1/2-ECKO mice at P0, whereas Olig1-KO mice exhibited a modest decrease compared to controls (Fig. 2a, b and Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). We also found that the number of SOX10+ cells continuously decreased from P0 to P30 in Olig1/2-ECKO mice (Fig. 2c). These results indicate that Olig1 plays a limited role in the generation of cortical OPCs, while Olig2 plays a critical role in this process. Intriguingly, residual SOX10+ cells maintained OLIG2 expression, suggesting a non-cortical origin. This finding is consistent with our recent lineage-tracing studies, which revealed that cortical OPCs arise from both medial ganglionic eminence (MGE) progenitors and cortical RGCs36. Supporting this model, over 90% of SOX10+/OLIG2+ cells co-expressed the maturation marker S100B (Supplementary Fig. 4c, d), further confirming their MGE derivation.

To further investigate the origin of residual OPCs in mutant mice, we performed scRNA-seq on P7 brains. We found that Sox10 is primarily expressed in cluster 2, which corresponds to OL-lineage cells (Supplementary Fig. 4e). The differential expression gene analysis between the Olig1/2-ECKO and control groups showed that OL-lineage cells in Olig1/2-ECKO mice exhibited higher expression of Nkx2-1 and S100b, and lower expression of Emx1, compared to control mice (Fig. 2d, e and Supplementary Fig. 4f, g). These findings suggest that the SOX10+ cells in the Olig1/2-ECKO mice are derived from the MGE.

Next, to delineate the roles of Olig1 and Olig2 in myelination, we analyzed Mbp expression—a key regulator of myelin sheath formation and maintenance37,38. Our results show that the expression of the Mbp (arrowheads) is significantly decreased in both the cortex and corpus callosum (CC) of Olig1-KO, Olig2-ECKO, and Olig1/2-ECKO mice at P7, compared to control mice (Fig. 2f, g and Supplementary Fig. 5a, b). In adulthood, myelination partially recovered in Olig1-KO, Olig2-ECKO, and Olig1/2-ECKO mice compared to control mice, but it did not fully return to normal levels (Supplementary Fig. 5c, d). Therefore, our results suggest that Olig1 and Olig2 have similar functions in cortical myelination. Integrating these findings with our earlier results, the loss of Olig1/2 in Tri-IPCs leads to an increase in OBIN-IPCs and the elimination of cortical-derived OPCs during cortical gliogenesis (Fig. 2h).

scRNA-seq reveals fate switching in Olig1/2-deficient Tri-IPCs

To investigate whether Olig1/2 ablation redirects Tri-IPC fate specification, we performed lineage tracing combined with scRNA-seq. We electroporated Cre plasmids into cortical RGCs of ISF/+ or Olig1/2 F/F; ISF/+ mice at E15.5 and conducted scRNA-seq on FACS-sorted CRE recombined cells (tdT+, red) at P1 (Fig. 3a). Unsupervised clustering identified seven distinct populations: Glutamatergic pyramidal neurons (Neurod1+/Neurod6+/Bhlhe22+); OBIN-IPCs (Dlx1+/Nrxn3+/Tshz1+); RGCs (Slco1c1+/Sparc+/Gfap+); APCs (Aldh1l1+/Tnc+/Id1+); Tri-IPCs (Ascl1+/Egfr+/Rrm2+); Ependymal cells (Foxj1+/S100a11+/Ogn+) and OPCs (Gpr17+/Cspg4+/Martn4+) (Fig. 3b, c and Supplementary Data 3). Notably, Olig1/2-CKO mice exhibited a significant reduction in OPC populations compared to controls (red arrowheads, Fig. 3d). The residual OPC population maintained expression of both Olig1/2 and canonical OPC markers (Pdgfra+/Sox10+/Cntn1+), likely representing either incompletely CRE-recombined cells or minor FACS sorting contaminants (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 6a, b). In contrast, we observed a substantial expansion of OBIN-IPCs expressing definitive lineage markers (Dlx2+/Sp9+/Gad2+) (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 6c).

Using immunohistochemistry to validate the scRNA-seq results in vivo, we found that the number of tdT+/PDGFRA+ and tdT+/SOX10+ cells were significantly decreased in the Olig2F/F; ISF/+ and Olig1/2 F/F; ISF/+ mice at P7, compared to control and Olig1-KO; ISF/+ mice (Fig. 3e, f). In contrast, the number of tdT+/SP8+ cells were significantly increased in the Olig1-KO; ISF/+, Olig2F/F; ISF/+, and Olig1/2 F/F; ISF/+ mice, compared to control mice (Supplementary Fig. 6d, e). In addition, we also noted an increase in Tri-IPCs that highly expressed Egfr and Ascl1 (Supplementary Fig. 6f). The differentially expressed genes analysis of Tri-IPCs revealed a significant decrease in the expression of genes associated with OPC fate determination (including Bcas1, Chd7, and Sox10) and a significant increase in the expression of genes (including Stmn1, Hist1h4d and Gsx2) associated with OBIN fate determination (Fig. 3g). Taken together, these results indicate that Olig1/2 cell-autonomously regulates Tri-IPC fate decisions, promoting OPC generation while suppressing OBIN-IPC production.

Integrated multi-omics reveals a dual regulatory mechanism of Olig1/2 in Tri-IPC fate determination

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying Olig1/2-mediated fate specification of OPCs and OBIN-IPCs, we performed CUT&Tag-seq and scATAC-seq27 on FACS-sorted GFP+ cells from perinatal hGFAP-GFP mouse cortices, a stage coinciding with Tri-IPC proliferation and differentiation (Fig. 4a). CUT&Tag-seq data exhibited high reproducibility, with OLIG1/2 binding peaks enriched at transcription start sites (TSS), consistent with their roles as transcriptional regulators (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 7a, b). Co-binding analysis revealed substantial overlap between OLIG1 and OLIG2 target genes (Fig. 4c), supported by motif analysis showing shared recognition of bHLH transcription factor binding sites (e.g., Neurog2, Sox10, Sox3) (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 7c). These findings suggest cooperative regulation of downstream targets by OLIG1/2 during cortical development.

While Olig2 is known to regulate Pdgfra and Sox10 in oligodendrocytes18,39,40, Olig1’s role remained undefined. Our data demonstrate direct binding of both OLIG1 and OLIG2 to the Pdgfra promoter and a putative enhancer (Supplementary Fig. 7d). scATAC-seq further revealed elevated chromatin accessibility at these loci in Tri-IPCs and OPCs compared to OBIN-IPCs, APCs, or RGCs. Similarly, OLIG1/2 bound enhancers of myelination-critical HMG-domain genes (Sox8 and Sox10), with Sox10 enhancers U2/U3 exhibiting cluster-specific accessibility (Supplementary Fig. 7e, f). These results implicate OLIG1/2 in orchestrating OL-lineage progression via direct transcriptional control.

Notably, OLIG1 and OLIG2 directly bind to known enhancers of Gsx2, such as hs687 and DSG (downstream from Gsx2). The binding sites of the OLIG1 and OLIG2 show higher chromatin accessibility in the Tri-IPC, OPC, and OBIN-IPC clusters than in APCs and RGCs (Fig. 4e). We also found that OLIG1 and OLIG2 bind a putative enhancer (PE) near the Gsx2 gene. This binding site shows higher chromatin accessibility in the Tri-IPC cluster than in other clusters (Supplementary Fig. 7h). Luciferase assays in 293 T cells showed that Olig2 expression suppressed activity of Gsx2 enhancers (DSG3, hs687, PE) (Fig. 4h). Hi-C data41 confirmed evolutionary conservation of the DSG-GSX2 regulatory axis in humans (Fig. 4g). Consistent with epigenetic repression, scRNA-seq and immunohistochemistry revealed upregulated Gsx2 expression in Olig1/2-deficient mice (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. 7g), with GSX2+ cells significantly increased in the dSVZ of knockout models (Fig. 4i, j). Collectively, we identify an Olig1/2-mediated dual-control mechanism governing Tri-IPC fate: activating OPC differentiation through Pdgfra/Sox while repressing OBIN-IPC specification via Gsx2 enhancer silencing - an evolutionarily conserved regulatory paradigm.

Conserved roles of Olig1/2 in gliogenesis and gliomagenesis

The hijacking of early developmental programs is a hallmark of gliomas, where tumor cells resemble neurodevelopmental lineages and exhibit mechanisms of neural stem cell resilience. Glioblastoma (GBM), the most prevalent and malignant brain tumor, is notoriously resistant to conventional therapies42. Olig1/2 is present in various diffuse gliomas, and it is particularly enriched in the proneural (OL signature) GBM subtype. Therefore, we further investigated the roles of Olig1/2 in glioblastoma cells. First, we delivered three plasmids containing PBase, PB-Pdgfb-dn-P53, and Cre into the cortical RGCs of ISF/+ mice, or Olig1/2 F/F; ISF/+ mice to construct proneural GBM-like model at P0 by IUE (Fig. 5a). We found that the ISF/+ mouse has abnormal brain structure, whereas the Olig1/2 F/F; ISF/+ mice had normal brain structures, without tumor expansion (Fig. 5b). To systematically study the roles of Olig1 and Olig2 in glioblastoma, we applied the same method to ISF/+ mice, Olig1-KO; ISF/+ mice, Olig2F/F; ISF/+ mice, and Olig1/2 F/F; ISF/+ mice. Our results showed that in the Olig1-ablated glioblastoma model, the survival time of the mice was not significantly altered, and they died before P50. Strikingly, the Olig2-ablated and Olig1/2-ablated glioblastoma mice had significantly extended survival times, with some surviving for more than one year (Fig. 5c).

Next, to investigate why the loss of Olig2 or Olig1/2 significantly extends the mouse survival time, we analyzed mice of different genotypes at P28. In our system, the tdTomato (tdT)+ cells are Olig1-ablated, Olig2-ablated, or Olig1/2-ablated cells (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 8a). As observed during normal development, we found that the number of the tdT-positive cells significantly increased in the OB of the Olig2-ablated and Olig1/2-ablated glioblastoma mice, compared to glioblastoma mice (Fig. 5d–f and Supplementary Fig. 8a–c). We also found that the number of the tdT-positive cells increased in the OB of the Olig1-ablated glioblastoma mice, compared to glioblastoma mice (Supplementary Fig. 8a, b). On the contrary, the number of the tdT-positive cells are greatly reduced in the SVZ of the Olig2-ablated and Olig1/2-ablated glioblastoma mice, compared to glioblastoma mice (Fig. 5d, e and Supplementary Fig. 8a–c). A study indicates that Olig2 deletion in glioblastoma changes the gene expression profile from proneural (OL signature) to astrocyte-associated classical phenotype and leads to increased Egfr expression43. Indeed, we observed that Egfr expression was significantly upregulated in tdT-positive cells in the cortex and OB of Olig2-ablated and Olig1/2-ablated glioblastoma mice. However, these cells in the OB are OBIN-like cells, not astrocyte-like glioblastoma cells, as these cells exhibit the morphology of mature OBINs (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 8a).

To understand the molecular mechanisms behind this, we analyzed the glioblastoma model mice at P15, when the gliomas had just initiated (Fig. 5g). Many tdT-positive cells co-expressed with OL-lineage marker SOX10, and fewer co-expressed with GSX2 (OB-lineage) in the ISF/+ glioblastoma mice. In contrast, the number of tdT-positive cells co-expressing GSX2 are significantly increased, while those co-expressing SOX10 are significantly decreased in the Olig1/2-ablated glioblastoma mice (Fig. 5g–i). Altogether, our findings demonstrate that Olig1/2 ablation redirects GSCs from generating highly proliferative OPC-like progeny toward proliferation-restricted OBIN-IPCs, thereby suppressing tumor expansion and prolonging survival.

Cortical Tri-IPCs is a potential origin of human H3.3G34R/V gliomas

According to our above research, Olig1/2 can promote OPC generation and inhibit OBIN-IPC generation in glioblastoma. Among various types of gliomas, H3.3G34R/V pediatric high-grade gliomas have garnered significant attention due to their universally lethal nature in children11. These tumors have been recognized and are known to originate from GSX2-positive progenitors in the LGE/MGE25,44. However, our recent findings revealed that cortical-derived progenitors also express GSX24, prompting us to hypothesize that cortical-derived GSX2-positive progenitors could be the origin of H3.3G34R/V gliomas.

To explore this hypothesis, we first compared the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between H3.3G34R/V and H3.3K27M11 gliomas to elucidate the molecular features of H3.3G34R/V gliomas (Supplementary Fig. 9a). Our analysis showed that H3.3G34R/V gliomas exhibited higher expression levels of DLX2, PROKR2, and SP8, while displaying lower levels of GPR17, OLIG1, and OLIG2 compared to H3.3K27M gliomas (Fig. 6a). KEGG analysis of the DEGs in H3.3G34R/V gliomas revealed that the downregulated genes were primarily involved in the regulation of glial cell differentiation, gliogenesis, and oligodendrocyte differentiation. In contrast, the upregulated genes were mainly associated with OB interneuron differentiation, neural fate commitment, and cell fate specification (Fig. 6b), which is consistent with the phenotype observed in Olig1/2 mutant mice.

Next, we conducted an integrative analysis of the published human GE data45 and mouse cortex data27 to identify the molecular characteristics of LGE, MGE, CGE, and cortical-derived OB-lineage cells (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Fig. 9b, c). Using GSEA and Upset analysis, we found that the molecular features of H3.3G34R/V gliomas were more closely aligned with OB-lineage cells rather than with LGE, MGE, and CGE (Fig. 6d and Supplementary Fig. 9d). To further investigate the potential origin of H3.3G34R/V gliomas, we reanalyzed published human cortical scRNA-seq data41 and discovered that cortical-derived progenitors expressed PAX6, EOMES, NEUROG2, OLIG1, OLIG2, and EGFR (Fig. 6e and Supplementary Fig. 9e). It is worth noting that a subpopulation of these cortical progenitors also expressed GSX2 (red arrowheads, Fig. 6e).

Immunohistochemical staining confirmed high GSX2 expression in the cortex at GW23, with these GSX2-positive cells also expressing Tri-IPC markers such as EGFR and OLIG2 (arrowheads, Fig. 6f, g and Supplementary Fig. 9f). These findings indicated that cortical-derived progenitors can express GSX2 in humans. Moreover, PPI analysis of the DEGs highlighted the key regulatory roles of OLIG1/2, FOXG1, and DLX2 in H3.3G34R/V gliomas (Supplementary Fig. 9g). These results indicated that due to the lack of OLIG1/2, the H3.3G34R/V gliomas may upregulate GSX2 and generate more OBIN-like cells in the cortex. Taken together, our findings provide evidence that H3.3G34R/V gliomas may arise from cortical Tri-IPCs.

Discussion

Cortical gliogenesis is a tightly regulated process orchestrated by interplay between signaling pathways and transcription factors, yet the molecular mechanisms driving cell fate decisions—particularly between glial and neuronal lineages—remain incompletely understood. Our study uncovers a dual role for Olig1/2 in cortical gliogenesis: these factors not only promote OPC generation but also repress OB interneuron intermediate progenitor cell (OBIN-IPC) production by directly suppressing Gsx2 expression through evolutionarily conserved enhancers. Strikingly, loss of Olig1/2 redirects glioma stem cells (GSCs) from generating proliferative OPCs to proliferation-restricted OBIN-IPCs, suggesting a conserved mechanism underlying both normal gliogenesis and gliomagenesis. Furthermore, our data implicate cortical Tri-IPCs as a cellular origin for human H3.3G34R/V gliomas, thereby revising and expanding the prevailing view that these tumors derive exclusively from GE-derived IPCs. Therefore, our study not only revised the origin of H3.3G34R/V gliomas but also emphasizes the need for a paradigm shift in how we approach glioblastoma treatment. By integrating insights from developmental biology with glioma research, we can begin to unravel the complexities of these tumors and identify more effective therapeutic targets.

The balance between Olig1/2 and Gsx1/2 is pivotal for fate specification across brain regions. In the LGE, Gsx1/2 delays OPC production by inhibiting Olig246,47, whereas during interneuron development, Gsx1/2 activates Dlx1/2, which in turn repress Olig1/229,48–53. Although Olig2 loss shows no early interneuron defects (likely due to perinatal lethality)54,55, our work reveals that Olig1 and Olig2 act redundantly to suppress Gsx2 and Dlx1/2 in cortical Tri-IPCs, mirroring the functional cooperativity seen among other fate-determining factors (e.g., Nr2f1/2, Sp8/9)46,56–58. Mechanistically, Olig1/2 function as bifunctional transcriptional regulators: they activate OPC programs (Pdgfra, Sox8/10) while repressing OBIN-IPC genes (Gsx2, Dlx1/2). Notably, our study demonstrated that Olig1 and Olig2 synergistically inhibit Gsx2 to gatekeep Tri-IPC fate during cortical gliogenesis.

Gliomas often hijack developmental pathways, exhibiting aberrant plasticity and proliferative signatures reminiscent of IPCs42,59. Olig1/2 are ubiquitously expressed in gliomas and play critical roles in tumorigenesis and tumor phenotype plasticity43,60–62. Recent studies have shown that Olig1/2-expressing intermediate lineage progenitors are predisposed to PTEN/p53-loss–induced gliomagenesis and harbor specific therapeutic vulnerabilities14. Olig2, in particular, plays extensive and pivotal roles across various glioma subtypes43,63. Interestingly, one study reported Olig2 has the capacity to dictate glioma subtype, as a loss of Olig2 leads to a shift from a proneural transcriptional subtype towards a more astrocytic phenotype, including downregulation of Pdgfra and concomitant upregulation of Egfr63. However, in our Olig1/2-CKO glioma mouse model, we observed that tumor formation is disrupted, resulting in the generation of many OBINs, despite the high expression levels of Egfr in Olig1/2- ablated cells. Thus, the role of Olig1/2 in tumorigenesis is context-dependent, possibly involving the inhibition of Egfr expression in different glioma subtypes. It is worth noting that the functions of OLIG1/2 are also closely associated with their post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms. For example, phosphorylation of a highly conserved triple serine motif at the N-terminus of OLIG2 (S10, S13, and S14) is crucial for regulating glioma cell proliferation and invasion64–66.

Elucidating the developmental origins of gliomas represents a critical step in understanding their pathological mechanisms and developing targeted therapies. While pioneering studies have established that H3.3G34R/V gliomas may arise from GSX2-positive progenitors in the lateral ganglionic eminence (LGE) with PDGFR mutations25, several clinical and molecular observations challenge this exclusive ventral origin hypothesis. First, these tumors predominantly localize to the cerebral cortex rather than the basal ganglia67,68. Second, our recent work has identified GSX2 expression in cortical-derived tripotential intermediate progenitor cells (Tri-IPCs) during gliogenesis4,36, revealing a previously unappreciated dorsal source of gliomagenic precursors. The current study provides three key lines of evidence supporting a cortical origin hypothesis: (1) demonstration of GSX2 expression in human cortical Tri-IPCs, (2) significant transcriptional similarity between H3.3G34R/V gliomas and cortical-derived olfactory bulb interneurons (OBINs), and (3) the characteristic OLIG1/2-negative/GSX2-DLX1/2-PROKR2-positive molecular signature in these tumors. These findings collectively suggest that cortical GSX2-positive Tri-IPCs may represent a cellular origin for H3.3G34R/V gliomas.

However, we acknowledge important limitations in our experimental approach. While our glioma model effectively targets dorsal cortical progenitors through Pdgfb and dominant-negative p53 expression, it does not specifically isolate GSX2+ subpopulations or incorporate H3.3G34R/V oncohistone mutations - both crucial aspects of human gliomagenesis. Therefore, we propose a dual-origin model wherein both dorsal cortical Tri-IPCs and ventral LGE progenitors may contribute to tumorigenesis, with their relative contributions potentially depending on genetic context and microenvironmental factors. This interpretation reconciles our findings with previous studies while accounting for the complex cellular heterogeneity of gliomas.

In summary, our findings uncovered the molecular mechanisms by which the transcription factors Olig1/2 determine cell fate in Tri-IPCs and GSCs. These findings provide insights into potential therapies for gliomas, suggesting that interfering with the expression of Olig1/2 could transform proliferative OPC-like tumor cells into non-proliferative OB interneuron-like cells. Moreover, we also provide evidence that the H3.3G34R/V glioma may derive from the cortical-derived Tri-IPCs. These findings provide a bridge for future research to understand the relationship between gliogenesis and gliomagenesis.

Methods

Animals

All experiments conducted in this study were in accordance with guidelines from Fudan University (No. 20220228-140). Olig2F/+36, Rosa-IS36, hGFAP-GFP27, Sox2-CreER69, and Emx1-Cre36 mice were previously described. Olig1/2 F/+ mice were generated via the CRISPR/Cas9 strategy. Lox2272 sites flanked the coding region of two exons of Olig2 and LoxP sites flanked the one exon of Olig1. Olig1-KO mice were generated via the CRISPR/Cas9 strategy. LoxP sites flanked the one exon of OLIG1. Wild-type mice or littermates without the Cre allele were used as controls. All mice were maintained on a mixed genetic background of C57BL/6 J and CD1. The mice were allowed access to water and food ad libitum and maintained on a 12 h light/dark cycle. The day of vaginal plug detection was considered embryonic day 0.5 (E0.5), and the day of birth was defined as postnatal day 0 (P0). Both male and female mice were used in all experiments.

Brain tumor harvest and survival curves

Brain tumors were harvested from mice at postnatal day 50 (P50) or upon the onset of severe neurological symptoms—such as hunching or seizures—indicative of maximal tumor burden. This endpoint was strictly defined in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Fudan University Institutional Review Board and was not exceeded in any of the experiments.

Tamoxifen labeling

A single intraperitoneal injection of Tamoxifen (100 mg/kg) was administered at P10 or P12, and the mouse was analyzed 7 days after Tamoxifen injection.

Tissue preparation

Mice were deeply anesthetized and perfused intracardially thoroughly with ice-cold PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). All brains were fixed overnight in 4% PFA at 4 °C, cryoprotected in 30% sucrose for at least 24 h, embedded in optimal cutting temperature (O.C.T.) (Sakura Finetek) on dry ice and ethanol slush, and preserved at − 80 °C. Human tissue preparation was performed as previously described70.

Immunofluorescence

In this study, 20 μm thick frozen sections were used for immunostaining. The sections were washed with 0.05 M TBS for 10 min, incubated in Triton-X-100 (0.5% in 0.05 M TBS) for 30 min at room temperature (RT), and then incubated with blocking solution (10% donkey serum + 0.5%; Triton-X-100 in 0.05 M TBS, pH = 7.2) for 2 h at RT. The primary antibodies were diluted in 10% donkey serum blocking solution, incubated overnight at 4 °C, and then rinsed 3 times with 0.05 M TBS. Secondary antibodies (from Jackson, 1:500) matching the appropriate species were added and incubated for 2–4 h at RT. Fluorescently stained sections were then washed 3 times with 0.05 M TBS. This was followed by 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Sigma, 200 ng/ml) staining for 5 min, and then the sections were then cover-slipped with Gel/Mount (Biomeda, Foster City, CA). Sources of antibodies are described in Table 1.

In situ hybridization

ISH was performed as previously described36. Briefly, ISH was performed on 20 μm cryosections using digoxigenin riboprobes. Probes were made from P0 wild-type mouse brain cDNA amplified by PCR using the following primers:

Primer F (Mbp): cgcggatccgaagagacagccgctctggatctc; and

Primer R (Mbp): ccggaattcatggtgacatttggcggccac.

In utero electroporation (IUE)

In utero electroporation (IUE) of IS embryos was performed at E16.5 or P0. The pCAG-Cre, pCAG-PBase, or PB-Pdgfb-dnP53 plasmid was mixed with 0.05% Fast Green (Sigma) and injected into the lateral ventricle of embryos (0.5 μl per embryo) using a beveled glass micropipette. Five electrical pulses (duration: 50 ms) were applied at 42 V across the uterine wall with a 950 ms interval between pulses. Electroporation was performed using a pair of 5 mm platinum electrodes (BTX, Tweezertrode 45-0488, Harvard Apparatus) connected to an electroporator (BTX, ECM830).

Plasmid construction

The pCAG-Cre vector was constructed as reported. The coding sequence of Cre in the pCAG-Cre vector was replaced by the PiggyBac transposase to construct the pCAG-PiggyBac transposase vector71. The internal ribosomal entry site sequence was placed between the targeted CDS and the GFP reporter. For tumor-inducing vectors71, Pdgfb was cloned into the downstream of the CAG promoter, and GFP was replaced by an HA-tagged dnP53.

scRNA-seq

After anesthetizing the mice, make a T-shaped incision on the back of the head. Use forceps to peel back the skin and skull on both sides to expose the brain tissue. Insert the forceps into the base of the brain and remove the brain, placing it in HBSS. If needed, clip the tail for PCR identification. Under a dissecting microscope, peel off the meninges, cut the target tissue (cortex) into small pieces. Transfer the tissue pieces to a 15 ml centrifuge tube using a 1 ml pipette. After standing for 30 s, remove the excess HBSS. Add 2 ml of preheated (37 °C) cell dissociation solution to the tissue pieces. Incubate in a 37 °C incubator for 20 min, gently shaking every 5 min to mix. Add 2 ml of 10% FBS-DMEM to stop the reaction. Pipette up and down (10–15 times) until a single-cell suspension is formed. Rinse a 40 μm MACS SmartStrainer (filter) with 10% FBS-DMEM. Place the filter on a 15 ml centrifuge tube and filter the cell suspension through it. Centrifuge at 500 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. Remove the supernatant and resuspend the cells in an appropriate amount of 10% FBS-DMEM. Keep on ice until use. According to the experimental design, perform flow cytometry sorting on the concentrated cells to obtain positive target cells. After obtaining single-cell suspensions from the target mice (Olig1/2 F/F; ISF/+ n = 3), we used the Countstar instrument to count the cells and assess viability (trypan blue staining), ensuring the quality and viability of the cells. Once qualified, the library construction and sequencing were outsourced to an external company. The single-cell sequencing library was constructed using 10X Genomics technology, and high-throughput sequencing was performed on the Illumina Hiseq4000. After sequencing, we used CellRanger for quality control and preprocessing of the raw data to ensure the reliability of subsequent analysis. Through this series of steps, we obtained the cell expression matrix file, which was then used for single-cell bioinformatics analysis using the Seurat package in R.

CUT&Tag-seq

The CUT&Tag assay was conducted on FACS-sorted GFP+ cells, using the cortex dissected from hGFAP-GFP at perinatal stages. Initially, 1 × 10^5 cells were washed with 500 μl of wash buffer (10 × wash buffer (-), 50 × protease inhibitor cocktail, ddH2O) and centrifuged at 600 rcf for 3 min at room temperature (RT). The cell pellets were then resuspended in 100 μl of wash buffer. Concanavalin A-coated magnetic beads were washed twice with binding buffer (10 × binding buffer, ddH2O). Next, 10 μl of activated beads were added and incubated at RT for 10 min. Bead-bound cells were resuspended in 50 μl of antibody buffer (10 × wash buffer (-), 50 × protease inhibitor cocktail, ddH2O, 5% digitonin, 0.5 M EDTA, 30% BSA). Then, 1 μg of primary antibody (mouse polyclonal anti-Olig2 and anti-Olig1) or no antibody (control) was added and incubated at RT for 2 h. The primary antibody was removed using a magnetic stand. The secondary antibody was diluted in 50 μl of Dig-wash buffer (10 × wash buffer (-), 50 × protease inhibitor cocktail, ddH2O, 5% digitonin), and the cells were incubated at RT for 1 h. Cells were washed three times with Dig-wash buffer to remove unbound antibodies.

The Hyperactive pG-Tn5 Transposase adapter complex (TTE mix, 4 μM, Vazyme) was diluted 1:100 in 100 μl of Dig-300 buffer (10 × Dig-300 buffer (-), 5% digitonin, 50 × protease inhibitor cocktail, ddH2O). The cells were incubated with 0.04 μM TTE mix at RT for 1 hour and then washed three times with Dig-300 buffer to remove unbound TTE mix. The cells were resuspended in 300 μl of tagmentation buffer (10 mM MgCl2 in Dig-300 buffer) and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. To terminate tagmentation, 10 μl of 0.5 M EDTA, 3 μl of 10% SDS, and 2.5 μl of 20 mg/ml Proteinase K were added to the 300 μl sample and incubated overnight at 37 °C. DNA was purified using phenol-chloroform-isoamyl alcohol extraction and ethanol precipitation with RNase A treatment.

For library amplification, 24 μl of DNA was mixed with 1 μl of TruePrep Amplify Enzyme (TAE, Vazyme), 10 μl of 5 × TruePrep Amplify Enzyme Buffer, 5 μl of ddH2O, and 5 μl of uniquely barcoded i5 and i7 primers from the TruePrep Index Kit V2 for Illumina (Vazyme). A total of 50 μl of the sample was placed in a thermocycler. To purify the PCR products, 1.2 × volumes of VAHTS DNA Clean Beads (Vazyme) were added and incubated at RT for 10 minutes. The libraries were washed twice with 80% ethanol and eluted in 22 μl of ddH2O. The libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform, producing 150-bp paired-end reads. All raw sequence data were quality trimmed to a minimum Phred score of 20 using Trimmomatic. Apparent PCR duplicates were removed using Picard MarkDuplicates vl.107. All reads generated from the CUT&Tag-seq of OLIG1 and OLIG2 were aligned to the mm39 mouse genome using Bowtie2 version 2.3.4. Sequence tags were aligned to the genome and subsequently analyzed by MACS2 software version 2.1.4 to detect genomic regions enriched for multiple overlapping DNA fragments (peaks), which were considered putative binding sites. Peaks with a false discovery rate lower than 5% were retained for further chromosomal region analysis. Visualization of peak distribution along genomic regions of genes of interest was performed using the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV).

Dual-luciferase assay

The DNA fragments of the Gsx2-DSG-1, Gsx2-DSG-2, Gsx2-DSG-3, Gsx2-hs687, and Gsx2-PE near the Gsx2 gene were created by PCR. The primers used to amplify these Gsx2 enhancer regions are listed in Table 2. Each fragment was subsequently cloned into the pGL4.23 firefly luciferase vector (Promega) upstream (U) or downstream (D) of the Luc2 gene. Cells from 293 T were grown in DMEM (Gibco, 12571063) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, 10099141). For the luciferase assay, 293 T cell transfections were performed in triplicate in 24-well plates by using Lipo8000 transfection reagent according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Beyotime, C0533). Luciferase activity was quantified by a microplate luminometer (Turner BioSystems, Modulus microplate reader).

Publicly available datasets were utilized as follows: mouse cortex scRNA-seq data were obtained from our previous study GSE16113227[https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE161132]; mouse cortex scATAC-seq data were derived from our prior work GSE16113227[https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE161132]; human brain scRNA-seq data spanning gestational weeks 9–18 (GW9–GW18) were sourced from a published repository GSE13582745[https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE135827]; human cortical tissue scRNA-seq data from post-conceptual weeks 16–24 (PCW16–PCW24) were acquired from an earlier study GSE16217041[https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE162170]; RNA-seq data of human brain were obtained from a previous investigation GSE3482411[https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE34824]. Source data are provided in this paper.

This study did not generate any original code.
