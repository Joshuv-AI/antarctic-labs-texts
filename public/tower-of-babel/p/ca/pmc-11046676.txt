Exploring awareness and prevalence of chronic viral hepatitis in a UK based Nepali population – lessons learned for future models in engaging migrant communities
Authors: Claire Kelly, Sanju Mathew, Mihaela Petrova, Shuja Shafi, Margot Nicholls, Osman Dar, Jeewaka Mendis, Simon de Lusignan, Jane Hendy, Aftab Ala
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe burden of chronic viral hepatitis (CVH) in the UK Nepali population is unknown. We aimed to determine knowledge of liver disease (LD) and prevalence of CVH in this community.MethodsThis was a mixed method (qualitative and quantitative) study guided by a multidisciplinary stakeholder group. Focus groups (FG) led by Nepali community leaders explored LD knowledge. Thereafter, a prospective community-based cohort study utilising dried-blood spot testing was conducted. Thematic analysis explored FG data with categorical data analysed with Excel and R Studio.ResultsFG data showed a lack of LD knowledge, with conflict between the roles of traditional and modern practices; 1,005 participants (525 male, 480 female) were tested for CVH, with a mean age of 63 years (range:19–86). Rates of CVH infection were low: 0.3% had current hepatitis B, with no active hepatitis C.DiscussionKey drivers for enthusiastic participation were development of peer support networks and advisory groups to disseminate information, including hepatitis B vaccine recommendations.KEYWORDS: UK Nepali, hepatitis B, hepatitis C, liver disease, community programme, migrant health

Introduction

Migrants face inequalities in healthcare access, with health disparities widely reported.1 First-generation migrants and new migrant communities from countries with less established links to the UK may be at greater risk with unknown disease prevalence. This is further compounded by uncertainty over how to reach these communities.2, 3

The World Health Organization (WHO) aims to eliminate chronic viral hepatitis (CVH) as a public health problem by 2030. Previously, work focused on case-finding in CVH and healthcare engagement in at-risk groups, eg migrants from higher-prevalence countries.3, 4 Following settlement rights granted to ex-Gurkha servicemen there has been a substantial growth of the Nepali population in the UK.5, 6 Given their military links, the community has clustered around traditional army sites, often in south-east England.7

The United Nations has classified Nepal as one of the least developed countries, with an estimated 40% living in poverty and an average life expectancy of 68 years.8, 9 The prevalence of CVH, awareness of liver disease (LD), attitudes to community engagement, uptake of CVH testing and risk factors for infection among the UK Nepali population is unknown.10, 11, 12 This study was undertaken to improve this knowledge gap, to help develop wider models for future health engagement and service delivery.

Methods

This is a qualitative and quantitative mixed-methods study performed in two independent sequential phases. First, focus groups (FGs) were held in a local community centre familiar to the Nepali population. Second, large-scale community-based testing for CVH was performed with ethical approval from the health research authority (12/LO/1530).

Phase 1: focus groups

A multidisciplinary steering group was developed, comprising council members, healthcare staff, community liaison leads, Nepali nursing staff and a local independent charity, Rushmore Healthy Living (www.rhl.org.uk), who then identified community leaders. FGs were chosen through community links, as this approach allows participation of under-represented and marginalised groups in research.13

Participants identified by the steering group were invited to join a suitable session based on sex and age. Invitation to attend was by a standardised letter and by personal ‘word of mouth’ from our Nepali volunteers. Four FGs were held to allow representation of both men and women in younger and older cohorts. FG sessions were held in a community centre in Surrey, UK and delivered in Nepalese at the recommendation of the steering group.

FGs were chosen to explore baseline awareness, knowledge and perceptions of LD in our target population. Facilitators were trained as ‘peer researchers’ to conduct the FGs. The role and initial training of the facilitator was key to the success of discussions, with emphasis on cultural awareness and overcoming language barriers.

Clinical members of the team were not present during discussions to prevent bias. FG guides were used across all four group sessions. Facilitators used the generic term ‘liver disease’ in the discussions, with autonomy to phrase questions as appropriate for each group, particularly given the uncertainties over how specific terminologies (such as viral hepatitis or hepatitis B and C) would be understood, and to explore broader concepts of how LD was perceived, in order to identify broad factors that may influence healthcare engagement.

As we were uncertain of how specific terminology of viral hepatitis, LD, hepatitis B (HBV) and hepatitis C (HCV) may be understood, we used these terms interchangeably. Participants were given written information and consent sheets in Nepalese and English. FG sessions were audio-recorded, transcribed and translated into English.

Data analysis

Thematic analysis (TA) was used to analyse and identify themes in the transcribed FG data, providing a method to organise and interpret data expressed by participants.13 This process relies upon coding to identify ideas expressed within the FG text, and developing this through analysis to higher-level and overarching themes. Initial coding was undertaken by a single researcher (one of the authors, SM) looking to identify patterns and meaning in the expressed FG data.13 High-level codes making up the candidate themes and overarching themes were then reviewed by the study group.

Phase 2: Community-based testing for CVH

Several testing sites in the local area were developed to improve patient access. Ex-Gurkha veterans were identified as community leaders to engage community members, direct activity and to act as peer advocates. The study sites were selected after consultation with stakeholders with particular focus towards venues close to Nepali population clusters and familiar to the local community. Five sites were chosen in Hampshire, Surrey and Berkshire. Dried blood-spot (DBS) testing was chosen to perform HBV and HCV testing, with the aim of providing practical and acceptable method of testing in non-healthcare settings.

Recruitment

Given the uncertainties of CVH rates in this community group and uptake of testing, an initial recruitment target of 500 individuals was set. Open invitations were sent, principally ‘through word of mouth’, to all groups in the Nepali community including advertising through Nepali TV and radio stations, and approaching adults (>18 years) at appropriate sites frequented by members of the community.

DBS testing process

A multidisciplinary team was used to organise and deliver testing at each event, made up of a public health local council representative, a clinician, research nurses, student nurses, and dedicated Nepali members of the research and development team at Frimley Health NHS Trust, Surrey, working with community leaders to inform, consent and test participants.

Patient information sheets (PISs) were distributed on arrival, with Nepali moderators available at each site to assist participants. All study materials were available in Nepali and English and detailed the study aims, as well as basic information on CVH, and the implications of positive diagnoses.

Data collection

Demographic information and potential risk factors for CVH, LD were collected. Geographic information on individual/family origin sites and educational status was collected, with number of years of schooling used as a surrogate measure of socioeconomic status.14

Data analysis

All data collected were categorical, with analysis conducted on Microsoft Excel, and R Studio.

A multiple logistic regression model with the dichotomy of HBcAB status (positive or negative) as the response variable with a total of 15 continuous covariates and categorical factors were performed to identify the prognostic factors of positive HBcAB status. Variable selection was performed using backward elimination technique with significance levels of 0.3 and 0.35 were used as thresholds of entering and staying variables in the model.

The model was designed to predict the probability of positive HBcAB using several predictors. A total of four variables satisfies the stay threshold of 0.3, namely sex, number of adults at home, years spent at school and if drugs are taken or not. Two of these variables report significant p-values namely sex (p-value of 0.0009) and years spent at school (p-value of 0.0128). The odds of HBcAB status in male is 2.4 times higher than that of female (OR of 2.382 and 95% CI between 1.429–3.969). The odds of positive HBcAB status is reduced by 11% for every one additional year at school (OR of 0.892 with 95% CI between 0.815–0.976).

VIF of all continuous variables were computed and none of them exceeds 10, suggesting there is no excessive multicollinearity among variables.

No evidence of lack of fit of the model was found based on the p value of 0.7310 from the Hoshmer and Lemeshow goodness of fit test.

Multiple logistic regression was performed using PROC Logistic procedure in SAS® version 9.4 software.

Further statistical significance testing was explored through Chi-square testing and Fisher's Exact test, with particular application of the Fisher's Exact test given the low absolute numbers of hepatitis B surface antigen and HCV antibody cases detected during the testing study.

Test results dissemination

All patients were asked to return to the same venue a week later to pick up their results, and for any positive cases to be discussed with individuals by the clinician, with support from community leaders. Provision was made for those with active disease to have onward referral to the hepatology team for clinical assessment and formal blood test confirmation.

Results

Phase 1: Focus groups

Thirty-two individuals, all Nepali born, attended the FG sessions. Groups were divided according to sex and age: men and women above or below 30 years of age. The average number of FG attendees was eight (range 6–11), and each session lasted 30–60 minutes.

A detailed summary of the key themes identified (including understanding LD, modern vs traditional medicine, negligence and loss of tradition, stigma, advocacy and self-efficacy) suggested that participants experience a negative outlook and futility when given a diagnosis of LD. These themes are powerful impactful descriptions of first-hand experiences from the community focus groups (Fig 1).

Phase 2: Mass testing for CVH

Testing activity was conducted at five sites over 22 months, with 1,005 individuals and 17 sessions. Ten subjects attended twice for testing over different sessions, despite early questionnaire modification (with ethics approval) to minimise duplicate attendance. A further participant (age 16 years) was inadvertently entered into testing (with reporting to the ethics board and sponsor), and subsequently removed from final data analysis, leaving a total of 994 individuals considered in final analysis (Fig 2).

Hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) was seen in three individuals on DBS testing (0.3%), with HCV antibody detected in four people (0.4%). Hepatitis B core antibody (HBcAB) was detected in 93 individuals, including those who were surface antigen positive (9.4%).

All identified participants were offered outpatient clinic follow-up, with formal correspondence sent to their GP for onward secondary care referral. Both written and verbal consent for this was obtained.

All HBsAg-positive cases were men aged 60–70 years and were linked to secondary care. All were in low replicative state and did not require treatment. HBcAB was identified in 90 (9.3%) participants, with HBsAg positivity in a further three (as above). The median age was 68 years (range 22–84 years, IQR 64–72 years; mean 67 years), with 58 (64%) male.

The four individuals identified as HCV antibody positive had reflex HCV RNA testing via their DBS samples and all tested negative for HCV RNA. These individuals were aged 60–70 years and had an equal sex distribution. No cases of dual HBsAg and HCV antibody were identified, although there was some cross-over in chronic HBV exposure, with one individual with HCV antibody also demonstrating HBcAB.

Clinic invitations were issued to all patients requiring follow up via their GP. All three active HBV cases attended for secondary care review. However, only two of the four HCV antibody positive patients attended for assessment, with no evidence of advanced LD from bloods and ultrasound assessment on these individuals.

The multiple regression model was designed to predict the probability of positive HBcAB. A total of four variables satisfies the stay threshold of 0.3, namely sex, number of adults at home, years spent at school and drugs. Two of these variables report significant p-values namely sex (p-value of 0.0009) and years spent at school (p-value of 0.0128). The odds of HBcAB status in male is 2.4 times higher than that of female (OR of 2.382 and 95% CI between 1.429–3.969). The odds of positive HBcAB status is reduced by 11% for every one additional year at school (OR of 0.892 with 95% CI between 0.815–0.976). Variance inflation factor (VIF) of all continuous variables were computed and none of them exceeds 10, suggesting there is no excessive multicolinearity among variables. No evidence of lack of fit of the model was found based on the p value of 0.3974 from the Hoshmer and Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test.

Results delivery

Attendance at results collection sessions was generally high, but with many attending to pick up results for friends or relatives, often having come together for testing at the session before. Around 10–20% who did not attend to collect results were notified by post to the supplied address. Few positive results were detected in our study, and all individuals with suggested active infection attended at results collection sessions. For those individuals with evidence of previous exposure to HBV, telephone conversation was conducted using our Nepali study team to inform participants if there was no representation at results sessions.

Discussion

We successfully engaged and recruited Nepali migrants to FGs exploring awareness of LD and mass testing for CVH in community venues. We found knowledge of LD to be low, although, there were some personal anecdotes of jaundice. These values of lived over learned experiences were high and so the anecdotes carried weight. While there was conflict between traditional practices, beliefs and modern medicine, the community were keen to engage with medical service and were trusting of the services. Some of the concerns over loss of traditional practices related to fear of losing cultural identity. The community were trusting of doctors’ judgement, and receptive to learn more about health protection/accessing healthcare. This is consistent with our experience with other groups of educational interventions with other groups.4

Stigma was also associated with LD, particularly suspicion of lack of self-control and blame over personal behaviours. This did not impact on the community's belief that there should be help and support for seeking treatments with advocacy being particularly prominent in females. We noted the importance individuals place on the health and wellbeing and the high level of motivation to empower healthcare access.4

Absolute rates of active CVH (HBsAg (0.3%); HCV antibody (0.4%)) were low with no advanced LD detected. Based on these results, rates of CVH in UK Nepali migrants would not meet the threshold (2% prevalence) for a higher risk group as defined by the WHO where targeted case-finding in CVH is recommended in such groups to drive their target of elimination of CVH. However, more work is needed to confirm these findings. Rates of prior HBV exposure were relatively high at 9.25%, raising the concern for additional cases of active HBV in those who did not take part in our testing study and consideration of immunisation in the community. There was a link between CVH and male gender and time spent in formal education, possibly reflecting exposure during military service. Interestingly, we noted nearly all participants were exposed to presumed non-sterile piercings at a young age, with high proportions also exposed to surgical and dental procedures, nonetheless, without associations to CVH, and low absolute levels of active disease.

‘Word of mouth’ spread of information was crucial to the success of the study, as seen in other migrant groups,4 suggesting the community receives some of its information from informal networks. We noted low levels of functional literacy among some participants; therefore, traditional options of sending educational material and appointments in print would be less likely to generate a response.

Overall, this study has several strengths. The greatest strength was our success in engaging with a migrant community where healthcare needs are uncertain who were previously under-represented in the literature. We were able to use FGs to develop key insights into this community, explore ideas around LD and actively engage healthcare programmes and research. We recruited relatively large numbers to our testing study with a wide age range, likely to be representative of the wider population. Notably, our follow-up of active infections was higher than is traditionally seen in studies of this nature.2 The support of key community members within the Nepali population allowed us to reach individuals across the socio-economic spectrum, thus enabling engagement particularly in those with low literacy levels. Furthermore, the ability to engage with and recruit large numbers of Nepali migrants to health promotion and clinical studies with healthcare benefits (ie CVH testing and linkage to care) translates to important lessons in our approach for other disease groups, in addition to developing accessible services for other under-served population groups.9 This approach may be used to develop screening and educational/public health programmes for other chronic asymptomatic conditions, such as cancer, tuberculosis or fatty liver; the use of mobile vans to provide screening methodologies such as community hepatic elastography could be considered.

While recognising the limitations in this work, the power of ‘word of mouth’ in recruitment and engagement cannot be under-estimated, and proved to be a useful distinct means of obtaining valuable information on awareness and perception of LD. Recruitment to our mass DBS testing through advertising, including radio adverts, may not have established a denominator but it proved to be most useful in increasing the number of participants tested. The recruitment of participants may have been biased with those eager for healthcare provision more likely to come forward. We were not able to capture the opinions of those who knew of the study but chose not to participate.

In migrant health, FGs have been well-utilised in exploring knowledge and perceptions towards health and disease states, as well as health-seeking behaviour.15, 16, 17, 18 This approach is beneficial in further improving links with the Nepali community, and addressing research questions on knowledge/perception of LD. The lack of knowledge on LD in Nepali migrants was in keeping with other study results, particularly confusion with jaundice.15, 16 While the Nepali population itself has not been widely studied, there are data confirming barriers to accessing services for this population that relate to communication,19 including language, wider issues and cultural awareness. Several challenges faced in engagement with social minority communities – eg barriers relating to language, cultural awareness and trust of healthcare staff – may be overcome with our approach.9

Our relatively lower reports of alcohol use is contrary to other data. This observation relates to the manner in which we obtained that information (through community leaders), recognising related stigma around this and a concern in South Asian communities over disclosure of personal information.9, 19

Our mixed-methods approach in the Nepali population enabled better understanding of the healthcare needs around LD. Whereas the incidence demonstrated at the mass testing events did not meet thresholds for consideration as a higher-risk group for CVH, the lessons learned have key clinical applications. The willingness to engage with services, particularly primary care emphasises the importance of developing linguistically and culturally tailored approaches to new migrant communities. The majority of the study cohort were unable to read and write in English, with many of the older participants also demonstrating a functional illiteracy in Nepalese. Language remained a barrier through the study process, although good levels of attention, interest and engagement towards healthcare staff were shown during presentations and discussions, which were translated in real time. The involvement of trusted high-profile community members was crucial to addressing these potential barriers and we recommend this approach in the design and implementation of new services. With technological advancement, we suggest that social media/app-based options can be explored to bridge language gaps.

Conclusion

Our experience shows a practical and effective way to meet the WHO target of eliminating CVH as a public health problem by 2030. We describe effective engagement approaches to communicate with and learn of the community's knowledge, attitudes of liver disease and uptake of screening programme. ‘Word of mouth’ proved to be a useful practical way of ‘spreading the message’. The role of peer researchers and peer advocates proved to be most critical and must not be underestimated.

Summary

What is known?

There is national and international stimulus to case-find in ‘higher-risk’ groups for chronic viral hepatitis (CVH), with migrants from countries of high prevalence being one such group. Migrants face inequalities in healthcare access and uncertain healthcare needs, especially in new migrant populations such as the UK Nepali population. It is not known whether they represent a higher risk group for CVH that justifies case finding strategies within this population.

What is the question?

The study aimed to determine knowledge of LD and recruit participants to hepatitis B (HBV) and C (HCV) testing to characterise rates of infection and risk factors for it.

What was found?

This study is the first to explore rates of CVH in the UK Nepali migrant population and engage them in a large programme for healthcare access. The UK Nepali population were eager to engage with community based healthcare. Understanding their baseline knowledge allowed development of a tailored program to facilitate recruitment. FG analysis revealed lack of knowledge of LD. One thousand and five participants were recruited to HBV and HCV testing by finger prick dry blood spot testing. Rates of HBV and HCV were low. Past exposure to HBV was higher and associated with (i) male sex (ii) lower number of years’ schooling.

What is the implication for practice now?

This study was successful in creating a model for engagement in a new migrant group with healthcare. The key feature to its success was using community partnership models with support from community leaders. Development of peer support networks and advisory groups to disseminate information were key to effective recruitment and linkage to care. This model could be rolled out for other disease-specific areas and other migrant populations.
