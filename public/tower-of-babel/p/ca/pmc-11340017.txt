Microphase Separation and Gelation through Polymerization-Induced Self-Assembly Using Star Polyethylene Glycols
Authors: Riku Yamanaka, Ayae Sugawara-Narutaki, Rintaro Takahashi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Polymerization-induced self-assembly (PISA) during the synthesis of diblock copolymers has garnered considerable interest; however, architectures beyond diblock copolymers have scarcely been explored. Here, we studied PISA using 4- and 8-arm star polyethylene glycol (PEG), as well as 2-arm (linear) PEG, wherein each terminus of PEG was functionalized with a chain-transfer agent, holding a constant molar mass for each arm. Styrene was polymerized from each PEG terminus through reversible addition–fragmentation chain-transfer (RAFT) polymerization in an ionic liquid (1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium hexafluorophosphate, [BMIM][PF6]), with a total solute concentration of 40 wt %. While the styrene monomer is soluble in [BMIM][PF6], polystyrene is not; thus, self-assembly and cross-linking (gelation) occur. Structural analysis by small-angle X-ray scattering revealed that a relatively ordered microphase-separated structure for PISA was observed. Two-arm PEG-PS formed hexagonally packed cylinders, whereas 4- and 8-arm PEG-PS exhibited hexagonal close-packed spheres and disordered spheres. The dynamics, studied by oscillatory rheology, were also influenced by the number of arms; the 4-arm star block copolymers showed the highest plateau modulus. This study demonstrates that the topology is an important factor in controlling the microphase-separated structure and mechanical properties when preparing gels through PISA.

Self-assembly is a cornerstone in the realm of materials science, where the functionality of materials often emerges through self-assembly. In this context, polymerization-induced self-assembly (PISA) has drawn significant attention owing to its ability to create well-defined nanostructures of controllable dimensions with relative ease.1−5 Most PISA studies have concentrated on the synthesis of diblock copolymers in dilute or semidilute solutions; that is, from one terminal of solvophilic polymer A, monomer B is polymerized to form an AB block copolymer in solution, followed by the self-assembly of the AB block copolymer as block B becomes insoluble. Micelles or vesicles are formed as polymerization proceeds. Nevertheless, fewer studies have been conducted on PISA of other architecture such as triblock copolymers than that of diblock copolymers.6−14 Recently, Zeng et al. reported that a highly tough gel could be prepared via PISA of triblock copolymers;13 that is, from both terminals of polymer A, monomer B is polymerized to make a BAB triblock copolymer, where block B self-assembles with the polymerization. Furthermore, we previously studied the kinetics of the self-assembly and gelation process during the synthesis of BAB triblock copolymers.14

In the present study, we focus on PISA of star block copolymers. Polymerization of styrene was performed from each terminal of 4- and 8-arm star polyethylene glycols (PEGs) as well as linear (2-arm) PEG in an ionic liquid (1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium hexafluorophosphate; [BMIM][PF6]) (Figure 1a and b). All the PEG terminals are functionalized by a charge-transfer agent (CTA), and all the PEG arms have a nearly identical molar mass (5000) with a narrow dispersity index (Đ ≤ 1.05). That is, we employed reversible addition–fragmentation chain-transfer (RAFT) polymerization using these macromolecular CTAs (macro-CTAs). As the polymerization proceeds, the PS blocks become insoluble, leading to self-assembly and subsequent cross-linking. It should be noted that PEG-PS block copolymers have emerged as the prime candidates for matrix polymers within solid (or gel) polymer electrolytes,15,16 although nearly all samples in those studies were fabricated via solution casting methods (ex situ polymerization). While solution casting methods require the removal of organic solvent over a few days in a vacuum, PISA synthesis circumvents this requirement.

Numerous studies have been performed on elastomers (without any solvent contained) formed from star block copolymers, with samples being prepared via solution casting or ex situ polymerization. Normally, star block copolymers exhibit superior mechanical properties compared to linear triblock copolymers.17−19 In particular, Liffland and Hillmyer demonstrated that an elastomer composed of a 4-arm star block copolymer exhibited higher toughness and stretchability than those of 2- and 6-arm star copolymers.20 In these cases,19,20 highly ordered microphase-separated structures were not formed, and the structures remained substantially invariant with the change in the number of arms. Within gel systems, which are comparatively rare, Hwang et al. reported that a 6-arm star block copolymer enhanced mechanical properties relative to a linear (2-arm) copolymer.21 In those studies, the self-assembled structure was not often studied in depth, and the relationship among the number of arms, self-assembled structure, and properties of gels of star block copolymers has not been fully understood, although an optimal number of arms may appear in numerous aspects. This study also contributes to this issue via PISA. Particularly, the effects of the number of arms on the structure and dynamics of the resultant gels have been probed by small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) and oscillatory rheology.

The macro-CTAs were synthesized from commercially available star PEGs with hydroxy terminals and a CTA bearing a carboxy group through Steglich esterification (Scheme S1), and the synthesis and purification procedures were well established.14,22,23 The full functionalization of PEG by CTA was confirmed using 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy (Figure S1).

In the PISA synthesis, the target degree of polymerization for each PS block was 40 in this study. This decision was informed by our preliminary experiments, wherein a higher degree of polymerization resulted in brittle materials, while a lower degree of polymerization led to fluent materials. The polymerization process could be well controlled under the condition shown in Figure 1b, as indicated by the monomer conversion exceeding 99% and the Đ values remaining below 1.15 for all the samples. The self-assembled structures were studied by SAXS. The SAXS profile of two-arm PEG-PS shows multiple peaks (Figure 2a). The position of each peak aligns with the hexagonally packed cylinders (HEX; Figure 2b); that is, the positions of the peaks match qn/q1 = 1, √3, √4, √7, √9, ..., where q denotes the momentum transfer, and qn is the position of the n-th order peak. The intercylinder domain spacing was estimated to be 17.2 nm. The attenuation of the third-order peak (q3 = √4q1) can be attributed to the coexistence of a certain fraction of spheres, the structure factor of which compensates for the third-order peak of HEX. The cylindrical morphology was further corroborated by transmission electron microscopy (TEM), wherein the samples were swollen with methanol and rubbed against a copper grid to prepare a thin gel layer on the grid for TEM measurements (Figure 3a and b). The white parts in the TEM images reflect the domains of lower electron density, viz., the PS domains. Although the hexagonal packing was disrupted owing to the fabrication of the thin gel layer, the cylindrical or thread-like morphology of the PS domains was observed in 2-arm PEG-PS.

In the cases of 4- and 8-arm PEG-PS, oscillations or relatively broad peaks were observed in the SAXS profiles (Figure 2). The positions of the local maxima do not match the mirror indices of face-centered-cubic (FCC) and body-centered-cubic (BCC) structures, which are typically observed in microphase-separated structures of block copolymers. Additionally, in the TEM images (Figure 3c and d), spherical objects were discerned, and cylindrical objects were not detected in 4- and 8-arm PEG-PS. Thus, our initial postulation was that the spherical PS domains were not highly ordered but dispersed in a disordered fashion, prompting us to fit these SAXS profiles by a form factor of sphere combined with a structure factor of hard sphere potential (see the Supporting Information for the equations).24 However, this model failed to accurately represent the shape of the first-order peak and the position of the secondary local maximum (broken black curves in Figure 2a). We inferred that the discrepancy from the disordered sphere model could be attributed to the partial (HCP) structure (Figure 2b). In the case of 8-arm PEG-PS, considering a shoulder peak at q = 0.38 nm as the first-order peak, the higher-order peaks match the HCP structure (i.e., qn/q1 = 1, √(36/32), √(41/32), √(68/32), √(96/32), ...),25 although the peaks are indistinct. Hence, HCP and disordered spheres coexist, or the spheres exhibit a degree of ordering into HCP. In the case of 4-arm PEG-PS, the structure should be similar to that of 8-arms, but it exhibits a lower degree of ordering, as indicated by more indistinct peaks.

It is noteworthy that creating highly ordered microphase-separated structures through PISA is challenging even for diblock copolymers. When PISA is executed at higher concentrations, highly ordered structures with distinct diffraction peaks have rarely been observed.13,14,26−31 In a rare instance, Rymaruk et al.32 reported the ordered structures in a system where benzyl methacrylate was polymerized from the terminal of hydrogenated polybutadiene in n-dodecane to synthesize diblock copolymers. A transition from body-centered cubic to HEX was observed with the polymerization, and the HCP structure was also present. The HEX structure may be a kinetically trapped state, as discussed with temperature-variable SAXS measurements. In our current study, similarly to the case of Rymaruk et al., HEX and HCP structures were observed. The HCP structures in 4- and 8-arm PEG-PS are presumed to be kinetically trapped and could not transform into HEX due to the covalent cross-linking architecture of the single chains; the packing frustration39 to form HCP structure in linear polymers is relieved by star architecture.33

To address the question of whether the observed structures are in a state of thermodynamic equilibrium or are kinetically constrained, the temperature-variable SAXS measurements were not beneficial in this study. This is because the glass transition temperature (Tg) of PS was unfortunately high—ca. 95 °C for all the number of arms (Figure S4)—and prevented any observable change in the SAXS profiles, even on heating to 100 °C (Figure S5). Alternatively, we performed PISA synthesis at a higher reaction rate by increasing the temperature to 60 °C during the PISA synthesis (see the Supporting Information).34,35 This higher reaction rate resulted in the formation of disordered spheres in all arm numbers, indicated by broader diffraction peaks in the SAXS profiles (Figure S7). In addition, self-consistent field theory for star block copolymers predicted that the formation of close-packed spheres is feasible within a narrowly defined parameter space.33 These aspects suggest that the HCP structure is in a state of kinetic arrest. However, we cannot exclude the possibility that it is in a stable state, as star polymers can form close-packed structures due to their architecture.33

Four- and 8-arm PEG-PS did not form highly ordered structures in contrast to 2-arm PEG-PS. In other words, the star topology appears to interfere with the ordering of the PS domains. Additionally, the domain spacing between the nearest spheres in HCP of 4- and 8-arm PEG-PS was 16.5 nm (Figure 2b), which is shorter than the intercylinder domain spacing (17.2 nm) in HEX of 2-arm PEG-PS (Table 1). These results should stem from the the topological effect; the packing frustration is relieved by star architecture, leading to closer domain spacing in the 4- or 8-arm PEG-PS. Therefore, the kinetics and topological effect are presumed to cause the difference in the structure depending on the number of arms.

Next, the dynamics were studied by oscillatory linear rheology. The master curves of the storage and loss moduli (G′ and G″) as a function of the angular frequency (ω) could be constructed through time–temperature superposition (Figure 4a–c). No structural transitions occurred on heating to 100 °C (Figure S5), and PS domains are rigid with the higher Tg (Figure S4), which may lead to the applicability of the time–temperature superposition in spite of the phase-separated systems. Figure 4a–c also present the rheological data for the samples before polymerization (i.e., mixtures of styrene monomer, PEG, and [BMIM][PF6]), demonstrating a significant enhancement in the elastic moduli and a delay in the relaxation time as a consequence of the polymerization process.

The shift factor (aT) is known to be correlated with the association energy (Ea) as expressed by aT ∼ exp(Ea/kBT), where kBT denotes the product of the Boltzmann constant and temperature.36,37 The plot of ln(aT) against 1/T is shown in Figure 4d; the aT values are invariant with respect to the number of arms, and all data points align on a straight line. This indicates that the relaxation behavior is governed by the association–dissociation dynamics of the PS blocks, all of which have nearly identical molar mass. The slope of this plot gives the Ea values of 168 ± 2 kJ/mol, the magnitude of which lies intermediate between that of covalent bonds and hydrogen bonding.36,37 We note that the contribution of the solvent, calculated by G″ = ωηsolv(38) with ηsolv denoting the viscosity of the solvent, was negligibly small.

Despite the independence of the relaxation time and association energy, the plateau moduli (Ge; G′ at the local minima of G″) depended on the number of arms. Specifically, Ge was 0.24, 0.44, and 0.31 MPa in 2-, 4-, and 8-arm PEG-PS, respectively. Ge reflects the number density of the network strand (ν) as given by Ge = vkBT.38 In this study, the size of the PS domains (physical cross-links) is not negligibly small, and the v are apparent values; that is, they incorporate contributions from the PS chains. The v values for 2-, 4-, and 8-arm PEG-PS were estimated to be 0.065, 0.12, and 0.084 nm–3, respectively, indicating that the 4-arm PEG-PS had the highest cross-link density. This means that the formation of loops in the PEG chains, instead of the bridging, is less likely to occur in 4-arm PEG-PS. This result aligns with the study on the elastomers comprising star block copolymers without any solvent by Liffland and Hillmyer, who reported that the 4-arm block copolymer exhibited higher tensile strength than the 2- and 6-arm block copolymers.20

An additional noteworthy observation pertains to the difference in the low-ω region; 2-arm PEG-PS exhibits a steep slope of G″ within the range of 10–9 < ω < 10–7 rad/s in comparison to its 4- and 8-arm PEG-PS. This phenomenon can be attributed to a specific topological effect. A single molecule of 2-arm PEG-PS bridges two PS domains. Consequently, when one PS block departs from the PS domain, this PEG-PS molecule is no longer a bridge chain. On the other hand, a single molecule of 4- or 8-arm PEG-PS can bridge three or more PS domains. Hence, even in the event of a PS block withdrawing from a PS domain, the PEG-PS molecule retains the potential to be the bridge chain by its remaining arms. We believe that this topological effect led to the difference in the rheological behavior in the low-ω regime.

For purposes of comparison between PISA and a conventional preparation method, we employed the solution casting process to prepare the samples, as described below. The gel samples were dissolved into ethyl acetate, in which PEG, PS, and [BMIM][PF6] are soluble. Subsequently, the ethyl acetate was evaporated off at 50 °C under vacuum conditions for a duration of 3 days ([BMIM][PF6] is a nonvolatile liquid). As a result, there were no significant differences between the master curves of dynamic moduli for the samples prepared by PISA and those prepared by solution casting (Figure S8).

In summary, we performed PISA using star PEGs to prepare gels composed of star block copolymers. Consequently, a microphase-separated structure of a relatively high order (HEX) for PISA was formed in 2-arm PEG-PS. Moreover, the structure depended on the number of arms; spherical domains situated in the HCP structure or disordered positions were formed in 4- and 8-arm PEG-PS. The dynamics were also influenced by the number of arms; although the relaxation behavior was predominantly governed by the association–dissociation of PS blocks, 4-arm PEG-PS showed a significantly higher plateau modulus than 2- and 8-arm PEG-PS. That is, the 4-arm configuration is optimal among the 2-, 4-, and 8-arm PEG-PS in terms of cross-linking instead of loop formation. This study presents a straightforward strategy for fabricating star block copolymer gels through PISA and demonstrates that the topology is a crucial factor in this context.
