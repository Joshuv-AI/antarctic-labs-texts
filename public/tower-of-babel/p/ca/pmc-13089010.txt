Spontaneous degradation of bisphenol contaminants driven by an enhanced electric field in a multicomponent coordinated cation−π catalyst
Authors: Renzhi Zhuang, Yumeng Wang, Shaojie Wang, Weixiang Liao, Peng Zhang, Chun Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBisphenol A (BPA) and its analogues (BPs), including bisphenol F (BPF), bisphenol S (BPS), bisphenol B (BPB), bisphenol AF (BPAF), and bisphenol AP (BPAP), are pervasive endocrine-disrupting contaminants with escalating environmental prevalence. However, current remediation strategies face critical challenges due to the interference from dissolved organic carbon (DOC) in real water matrices and high energy consumption. Herein, we engineered Fe, Zn complex graphene-like carbon (FeZn-GLC) with Fe−O−C, C−O−Zn, Fe−N−C, Zn−N−C, and Fe−C, five distinct chemical coordination bonds to strengthen cation−π interactions and generate a robust intrinsic electric field. It enabled efficient destruction of BPs in water with high and stable catalytic efficiency under ambient conditions. The high active state combination of Fe3C and GLC, with the strongest tensile strain from multicomponent coordination on FeZn-GLC, delivered a strong electric field that triggered BPs’ spontaneous degradation and O2 reduction. The system achieved >97.5% degradation efficiency and 70%–90% mineralization of BPA/BPs under ambient conditions, with further performance enhancement in municipal wastewater. This resulted in organic radicals (R•), O2•-, and •OH, which were shown to charge the Fe species and GLC system, thereby recovering FeZn-GLC for complete BP destruction. The results addressed the inhibitory effect of DOC in real waters on BPs removal. Our findings highlight the potential of high active state and electric energy from the catalyst surface reconstruction through multicomponent coordination for remediating micropollutant contamination in water.Keywords: Bisphenol analogues, Coordination, Cation−π interaction, Self-purification, Heterogeneous catalyst

Introduction

Numerous studies have confirmed that bisphenol A (BPA) and its analogues (BPs), e.g., bisphenol F (BPF), bisphenol S (BPS), bisphenol B (BPB), bisphenol AF (BPAF), and bisphenol AP (BPAP), exhibit endocrine-disrupting effects [1,2], posing significant threats to human health and aquatic ecosystems. Notably, some BPs demonstrate even greater toxicity than BPA [3]. Despite regulatory restrictions on BPA in certain consumer products, its discharge into aquatic environments remains inevitable. Moreover, with the widespread application of BPA alternatives, increasingly higher concentrations of BPs are being detected in natural waters. For instance, Wang et al. [4] reported BPS concentrations reaching 24.8 ng/L in surface waters of the Pearl River in South China, second only to BPA (34.9 ng/L). Other studies have shown that in some water bodies, the concentrations of BPS and BPF already exceed those of BPA [1,5,6]. Furthermore, the U.S. state of California has listed BPS under Proposition 65 [7], highlighting the escalating environmental challenge posed by BPs. Alarmingly, BPs are not currently regulated in any drinking water quality standards [8], and research on their removal remains limited, underscoring the urgent need to develop effective BP elimination technologies. Conventional BPA removal approaches include sonocatalysis [9], electrochemical oxidation [10], ozonation, plasma-based oxidation [11], UV-driven oxidation [12], and various combined oxidation technologies [[13], [14], [15]]. Although effective, these methods require substantial energy input due to their dependence on chemical reagents or external energy sources, which limits their large-scale application in natural water bodies and industrial water systems, highlighting the urgent need for energy-efficient alternatives. Meanwhile, the ubiquitous presence of dissolved organic carbon (DOC) in aquatic systems significantly interferes with complete BPs elimination [16], presenting formidable challenges for achieving total pollutant mineralization. The development of cost-effective technologies for complete BPs mineralization in real water matrices remains an outstanding scientific challenge.

The cation−π interaction between cations and delocalized polarized π-electrons in graphene systems can exhibit strength potentially surpassing hydrogen bonds and even electrostatic interactions [17]. When ionic aqueous solutions flow across graphene surfaces, this cation−π interaction induces the redistribution of both cations and π-electrons, thereby facilitating the generation of interfacial electric fields [18] and enabling electricity production. Recently, we observed that the electric field generated by DOC-PFAS-H2O-O2 synergistic coordination on Fe(II)/Fe(III)-GL@Fe0 could drive surface electrochemical-like reactions, achieving complete PFAS mineralization and O2 reduction [19]. Wang et al. [20,21] constructed a strong surface electric field via Fe−π interactions in Fe-based catalysts, which was further amplified through DOC-antibiotics and BPA-H2O synergistic coordination on the catalyst surfaces, consequently boosting spontaneous pollutant mineralization. These results suggest that constructing cation−π systems is crucial for generating powerful interfacial electric fields, which could provide strong adsorption capacity to facilitate the co-adsorption and synergistic coordination of multiple components on the surface, thereby inducing stronger electrostatic forces at the interface. This process continues until the adsorbed pollutants are fully mineralized. However, the mechanism of spontaneous BPA, antibiotics, and PFAS degradation in multicomponent coordination catalytic systems still lacks direct experimental evidence, and resolving these issues is pivotal for developing efficient and economically viable catalytic systems for BPs removal from real waters.

Iron is cheap and has demonstrated excellent electrical conductivity due to its multiple valence states in previous studies [[19], [20], [21]]. Additionally, an appropriate level of zinc doping helps enhance the magnetic moment of multivalent Fe, reduces electrical conductivity, and contributes to the formation of stable complexes [22]. Considering the economic feasibility for practical applications, a fortified cation−π system was successfully fabricated in the present study, consisting of FeZn complex graphene-like carbon (FeZn-GLC) with five distinct chemical coordination bonds (Fe−O−C, C−O−Zn, Fe−N−C, Zn−N−C, and Fe−C). Through comprehensive microscopic characterization and theoretical calculations, we demonstrated that enhanced cation−π interactions generate a substantial intrinsic electric field, accompanied by a surface voltage of 157 mV, promoting electron transfer between pollutants and active sites. Rapid degradation of bisphenol pollutants occurred in the air-exposure system without requiring additional energy input, achieving exceptional degradation efficiencies (>97.5%) for both BPA and representative BPs, with remarkable mineralization rates of 70%−90%. Notably, electrochemical characterization in DOC-containing real water matrices confirms that the synergistic coordination of BPA-DOC-H2O-O2 on the catalyst surface generates enhanced potential differences, significantly boosting catalytic performance in complex aqueous environments. During this process, the continuously generated organic radicals (R•) serve as sustainable energy donors that recharge the catalyst and maintain its redox activity. These findings provide both an efficient catalytic material and fundamental mechanistic insights for low-energy consumption removal of bisphenol pollutants from authentic water systems.

Results and discussion

Structural characterization of FeZn-GLC

The SEM and TEM images of FeZn-GLC (Fig. 1a and b) reveal an interconnected bamboo-like morphology with elongated segments. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) images (Fig. 1c) revealed the carbon (002) facet with spacings of 0.348 nm verified the typical layered structure of graphitic carbon, and Fe0 (110), Fe2O3 (113), Fe3O4 (400) with spacings of 0.204, 0.219 and 0.210 nm were closely connected together by the common Fe species and collectively embedded within the graphitic carbon. Elemental mapping (Fig. 1d) confirmed the coexistence of Fe, Zn, C, N, and O, with metallic elements predominantly localized at the segment joints or termini while being encapsulated by carbonaceous matrices. The XRD pattern (Fig. 1e) of FeZn-GLC indicated that iron species existed as Fe0, Fe3C, Fe2O3, and Fe3O4 [21]. For Fe-GLC, in addition to Fe0 and Fe3O4, no Fe2O3 was detected. In Zn-GLC, no Zn species peak was observed. Notably, the (002) peak representing graphitic carbon (26.4°) in FeZn-GLC exhibited a slight shift compared to Fe-GLC, Zn-GLC, and GLC, suggesting metal-induced electron redistribution to graphene-like structure [23]. Based on Scherrer equation analysis, the crystallite thickness of (002) diffraction peak in FeZn-GLC increased along the c-axis direction, demonstrating that Zn incorporation promoted the formation of more ordered graphitic microstructures with higher stacking layers and enhanced graphitization [24,25]. This is because ZnCl2 introduction expanded the spatial separation of Fe species and GLC precursors, effectively suppressing their aggregation [26], forming more graphitic carbon around the metal.

The appearance of D-band (1336–1347 cm−1) and G-band (1562–1572 cm−1) in Raman spectra confirmed the formation of a graphene-like structure in different samples (Fig. 1f). With metal doping, these bands underwent shifts and exhibited significantly enhanced peak intensities. Compared to various control samples, FeZn-GLC showed the strongest intensities in D-band, G-band, and the 2D peak, which were split into four peaks at 2444, 2678, 2916, and 3216 cm−1 (Fig. 1g), exhibiting a notable blue shift. These results indicated the greatest tensile strain of GLC in FeZn-GLC [27].

The room-temperature Mössbauer spectrum of FeZn-GLC revealed five distinct iron species (Fig. 1h and Table S1). The sextet with an isomer shift (IS) of −0.08 mm/s and the singlet at 0 mm/s correspond to characteristic peaks of α-Fe and β-Fe, respectively [28]. The sextets with IS values of 0.2 and 0.7 mm/s are assigned to Fe3C and Fe2O3 [28,29]. For Fe3O4, the spectrum exhibits two sextets: the outer sextet with a higher magnetic field originates from high-spin Fe(III) [30], while the inner sextet with a lower field arises from both high-spin Fe(II) and Fe(III) [31,32]. The presence of Fe3C confirms the formation of Fe−C bonds between Fe atoms and GLC carbon atoms in FeZn-GLC.

The Fe 2p XPS spectra of FeZn-GLC, and Fe-GLC confirmed the coexistence of iron species as Fe0, Fe(II), and Fe(III) (Fig. 2a and Table S2) with the Fe atomic percentage of 1.1% and 0.49%, respectively, indicating that Zn introduction increased the Fe species surface amount. However, no Zn 2p3/2 and Zn 2p1/2 peaks were detected in FeZn-GLC containing Zn 5.75 wt% (Fig. 2b, Tables S3 and S4), whereas distinct Zn peaks with a surface atomic percentage of 2.42% were observed in Zn-GLC containing Zn 5.17 wt% (Tables S3 and S4). The results indicated that in FeZn-GLC, Zn was nearly encapsulated by the graphene-like carbon (Fig. 1d), verifying that Zn induced graphene formation, consequently increasing surface Fe distribution.

Additionally, metal incorporation induced peak shifts of 0.4, 1.1, 0.9, and 0.3 eV for C−N, C=O, O−C=O, and π−π∗ transitions in the C 1s XPS spectrum, respectively (Fig. S2a). This was accompanied by increased binding energies for pyridinic N, pyrrolic N, graphitic N, and pyridinic N-oxide species (Fig. S2b), indicating formation of Fe/Zn−N−C bond. Notably, the O 1s XPS spectrum exhibited a Fe/Zn−O coordination at 530.5 eV and a C−O−Fe/Zn at 531.3 eV (Fig. S2c) [33,34].

The Fe K-edge EXAFS spectrum of FeZn-GLC exhibited a major peak at 1.63 Å, longer than the Fe–O peaks at 1.47 and 1.50 Å for Fe2O3 and Fe3O4 (Fig. 2c), respectively, suggesting a mixture of Fe–O and Fe–X bonding. The appearance of a different Fe−Fe coordination environment compared to the Fe foil confirmed the existence of Fe0 species. EXAFS R-space and K-space data have a good fit within the selected fit range (Figs. 2d and S3a). Based on the best-fitting analyses (Table S5), in the first shell, FeZn-GLC showed the Fe–O and Fe–N shells with bond distances of 1.49 and 1.40 Å and coordination numbers (CN) of 2.7 and 5.8, respectively. In addition, the appearance of a Fe−C shell with a long bond distance (1.84 Å) suggested the back scattering from C to Fe species, verifying the generation of the Fe−O−C and Fe−N−C bonding structure on FeZn-GLC. Moreover, the XRD and Mössbauer analyses revealed the presence of Fe3C in the catalyst. Thus, the Fe–C shell detected in EXAFS is also attributed to the direct bonding between composite iron species and C atoms in GLC.

In the Zn K-edge EXAFS spectra in R space, except the Zn–Zn shell, FeZn-GLC exhibits a distinct shift compared to the Zn–O peaks in ZnO standard sample, indicating a mixture of Zn–O and Zn–X bonding (Fig. 2e). The best-fitting analyses showed that both Zn–N (2.08 Å, CN = 2.5) and Zn–O (2.14 Å, CN = 3.2) shells were detected in FeZn-GLC (Figs. 2F, S3b and Table S6). The long bond distance (2.08 Å) of Zn–N indicates that N in GLC was coordinated with Zn in the second coordination layer, confirming the formation of Zn–N–C bonds. Moreover, the bond distance of the Zn–O shell (2.14 Å) in FeZn-GLC was larger than that (1.96 Å) of ZnO [35], and no ZnO was detected in FeZn-GLC according to XRD analysis (Fig. 1e). These results confirmed that the Zn–O bond on the FeZn-GLC surface originated from the coordination of Zn atom with the phenolic hydroxyl group in GLC, rather than from the lattice O of ZnO. Thus, based on the above results, Fe2+, Fe3+, and Zn2+ on the FeZn-GLC surface were predominantly complexed by N or O atoms of GLC, forming Fe/Zn–O–C and Fe/Zn–N–C chemical bonds. This coordination structure resulted in robust Fe/Zn–π interactions between the Fe/Zn species and the delocalized π orbitals of the aromatic rings in GLC.

Two-dimensional valence electron density (VED) calculations via density functional theory (DFT) (Fig. 2g) revealed pronounced charge polarization, with positive charge accumulating at the bridging C atoms in Fe/Zn–N–C and Fe/Zn–O–C motifs while electrons migrate toward Fe/Zn centers through these coordinate bond linkages, establishing an asymmetric electronic distribution. Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy confirmed the presence of unpaired electrons in FeZn-GLC (Fig. S4). The faint EPR signals observed for GLC and Zn-GLC arose from π-delocalized electrons within graphene-like aromatic systems. Remarkably, Fe doping induced substantial EPR signal enhancement, with Fe/Zn co-doped catalysts exhibiting the most intense signals and larger spin Hamiltonian parameters (A-values), demonstrating superior charge delocalization within Fe/Zn–N and Fe/Zn–O–C coordination architectures. Electrochemical tests further revealed that FeZn-GLC exhibited the lowest charge-transfer resistance and highest current density compared with the control samples (Fig. S5), confirming its superior electron transport properties [[36], [37], [38]]. The results confirmed that the greatest tensile strain in graphene-like structures enhanced the electron transport capability of FeZn-GLC.

According to atomic force microscopy (AFM) images (Fig. S6a and b), FeZn-GLC possesses a bamboo-like nanostructure formed by the coordination of metal clusters to GLC. Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) further revealed that the metal clusters and the GLC matrix exhibit higher and lower electric potentials, respectively, collectively creating a surface electric field composed of electron-rich metal areas and electron-poor GLC areas, with a field strength of about 157 mV (Fig. S6c and d).

BPs spontaneous destruction in air-exposure FeZn-GLC suspension

BPA and several typical bisphenol analogues were selected as the target BPs to evaluate the catalytic performance of FeZn-GLC, and their structures are shown in Fig. 3a. Zn-GLC and GC exhibited negligible catalytic activity, with BPA removal primarily governed by adsorption (Fig. 3b). In contrast, Fe-GLC achieved 96.3% BPA removal within 30 min, while FeZn-GLC demonstrated complete BPA removal under identical conditions. FeZn-GLC enabled complete BPA mineralization across three tested dosages (Fig. S7). Balancing catalytic efficiency and cost-effectiveness, a dosage of 0.4 mg/L was selected for subsequent experiments on bisphenol analogues. The performance of FeZn-GLC was further evaluated against representative bisphenols (BPF, BPS, BPB, BPAF, and BPAP) (Fig. 3c). About 87.6% BPAF was removed, while more than 94.2% removal efficiency was achieved for all other bisphenol analogues within 30 min. Upon extending the reaction time to 120 min, 90.8% of BPAF was eliminated, with BPA and BPS removal rates surpassing 97%, and BPF, BPB, and BPAP removal reaching 99.8%. Notably, within 30 min, the total organic carbon (TOC) removal efficiencies for BPAF, BPS, BPA, BPF, BPB and BPAP were 71.9%, 72.2%, 79.2%, 84.1%, 89.4% and 87.8%, respectively (Fig. 3d). After 120 min, these values respectively increased to 79.1%, 84.7%, 87.9%, 88.1%, 91.2% and 90.8%, confirming high mineralization efficiency. These results confirmed the exceptional catalytic purification performance of FeZn-GLC toward diverse bisphenol contaminants.

The laboratory-synthesized, non-biodegradable BPA wastewater was treated using an up-flow reactor packed with FeZn-GLC filler (Fig. S8) without external energy input, with a hydraulic retention time of 120 min. During 24 days of continuous operation, the BPA removal efficiency remained consistently above 96% (Fig. 3e), and the Fe and Zn leaching concentrations were maintained at only 0 and 4 ng/L, respectively, confirming the excellent stability of FeZn-GLC for water purification (Fig. S9). Remarkably, even at 100 mg/L (Fig. 3f), the catalyst maintained >90% BPA removal within 240 min, demonstrating exceptional resilience against concentration fluctuations. In addition, the catalyst also exhibited high efficiency in treating raw hospital wastewater with high salinity (106 mM HCO3−) (Table S7), achieving a 76.8% removal of TOC within 120 min (Fig. 3g).

The practical applicability was evaluated in real water matrices (Fig. 3h). Faster BPA degradation kinetics were observed in municipal wastewater and raw drinking water than in ultrapure water. These results suggested preferential BPA adsorption/oxidation in complex aqueous environments. Furthermore, with the addition of R• quencher TEMPO, BPA degradation was inhibited to some extent, while O2•- quencher p-benzoquinone (PBQ), and •OH quencher isopropanol (IPA) had little effect on the reaction (Fig. 3i). Under nitrogen-exposure condition, no sustained oxidation was observed after 60 min (Fig. 3j). These results indicated that O2 served as an electron acceptor to maintain continuous degradation of organic pollutants in the reaction system, while R• was essential for the catalyst-charging process [19].

Surface reaction mechanism

In the FTIR spectra, the aromatic C=C bonds of BPA-H2O-adsorbed catalyst exhibited significant shifts compared with FeZn-GLC, along with enhanced aromatic C−H intensity in π-systems (Fig. 4a), indicating that BPA interacts with FeZn-GLC via π−π stacking. Notably, the -OH peak for H2O-adsorbed catalyst showed a marked displacement, indicating that the coordinated-BPA strengthens interactions between FeZn-GLC and H2O.

Compared with FeZn-GLC, the C 1s XPS spectrum of BPA-H2O-adsorbed catalyst displayed a 0.1 eV negative shift of π−π∗ peak (Fig. 4b). Correspondingly, characteristic peaks of C−N, C=O, graphitic N, Fe−O, C−O−Fe/Zn, -OH, and H2O in C 1s, N 1s, and O 1s XPS spectra all shifted toward lower binding energies (Fig. 4b–d), and the Fe−N peak emerged, confirming the formation of π-conjugated coordination complexes between BPA and FeZn-GLC. Moreover, the total surface proportion of Fe(II) and Fe(III) increased from 0.701 to 0.739 (Fig. 4e). According to Mössbauer spectra analysis (Fig. 4f), no significant Fe2O3 and Fe3O4 signals appeared in this situation, and the α-Fe content decreased from 0.635 to 0.31, while the total Fe3C content surged from 17.5% to 61.5% (Tables S1 and S8). The results indicated that α-Fe, Fe2O3, and Fe3O4 were reconstructed into Fe3C through the BPA-H2O synergistic coordination on GLC and the Fe species area of FeZn-GLC.

Compared with fresh catalyst, the G band and 2D peaks of BPA-H2O-adsorbed FeZn-GLC Raman spectrum exhibited greater shifts of 11 and 4 cm−1, respectively, indicating greater tensile strain and higher electron concentration in the highly delocalized π-electron system of GLC (Fig. 4g and h). Moreover, the highest G and 2D peak intensities in BPA-DOCMW (DOC in municipal wastewater)-H2O-adsorbed FeZn-GLC Raman spectrum indicated much greater tensile strain and electron concentration in GLC, correlating with the highest BPA degradation efficiency. The results indicated that the Fe-centered coordination environment underwent a complete transformation through multicomponent coordination on the dual-electron center of the catalyst, which effectively amplifies the overlap of d–d orbitals, facilitating electron transfer between metal centers and strengthening d–d orbital interactions [39]. Concurrently, the charge-transfer resistance of the adsorbed-state catalysts decreased with the increasing number of adsorbed components (Fig. 4i).

The open-circuit voltage exhibited a continuous increase across the adsorbed-state catalysts in the order of H2O, BPA-H2O, DOCMW-H2O, and BPA-DOCMW-H2O (Fig. S10a). This was attributed to surface multicomponent synergistic coordination, inducing stronger charge rearrangement of the dual-electron center. With increasing component numbers, the voltage of the adsorbed-state catalysts progressively rose to 507−530 mV, and the highest current density was observed in the BPA-DOCMW-H2O adsorbed-state catalyst (Fig. S10b). The results confirmed that the high active state combination of Fe3C and GLC on multicomponent coordinated FeZn-GLC delivers high open circuit voltage. Moreover, it has been reported that a heterojunction between Fe3C and N-doped graphene carbon boosts the oxygen reduction reaction since Fe3C could provide rapid electron transfer [40]. Therefore, under air exposure, both adsorbed BPA/DOC cleavage and O2 reduction could occur on the BPA-H2O-adsorbed and BPA-DOCMW-H2O-adsorbed catalyst surface.

The voltage changes were tracked by an in-situ open-circuit of FeZn-GLC electrode under varying reaction conditions (Fig. 4j). The voltage increased and stabilized with the successive additions of MW and BPA in N2 atmosphere, then dropped sharply upon air exposure before reaching equilibrium. Subsequent N2 exposure caused another significant voltage increase, while reexposure to air led to further decrease. These results demonstrate that surface BPA-DOCMW-H2O coordination generated a stronger electric field under N2 atmosphere, which was consumed upon air exposure. This high active state on the coordinated catalyst triggered the cleavage of BPA and DOC and O2 reduction.

As expected, the generation of both •OH and O2•- increased (Fig. S11) with the addition of BPA. Meanwhile, TEMPO–C9H12O and TEMPO–C8H10O radical trapping adducts were determined by LC-Q-TOF-MS/MS, confirming the formation of •C9H12O and •C8H10O (Fig. S12). The BPA cleavage process resembled the previous description [22]. The resulting R• further charged the catalyst and simultaneously hydrolyzed to yield adsorbed hydroxylated products (Fig. S13e), in succession, further cleaved into •OH and another R• through surface coordination. This surface reaction cyclically repeated until complete mineralization of BPA was achieved.

LC-Q-TOF-MS/MS analysis identified intermediate products of BPA degradation, including P1 (C3H6O3), P2 (C2H4O2), P3 (C8H8O2), P4 (C9H10O), P5 (C15H16O3), P6 (C9H10O2), P7 (C8H10O), P8 (C2H6O2), P9 (C3H8O3), P10 (C4H6O4), P11 (C4H8O3), and P12 (C7H6O2) (Fig. S13 and Table S9). The fathead minnow LC50 (96 h), daphnia magna LC50 (48h), developmental toxicity, and mutagenicity of BPA and its intermediates (Fig. S14) were evaluated using Toxicity Estimation Software. The toxicity of BPA was indicated by its LC50-96 h (3.24 mg/L) and Daphnia magna LC50-48 h (1.58 mg/L), indicating significant toxicity. Notably, except P5, the toxicity indices of other degradation products were all higher than those of BPA (Fig. S14a and b), suggesting reduced toxicity in the intermediates, with some even approaching non-toxic levels. All intermediates exhibited lower developmental toxicity than BPA, with some even classified as developmental NON-toxicant (Fig. S14c). Additionally, mutagenicity tests yielded negative results for all intermediates (Fig. S14d). Furthermore, FeZn-GLC achieved a TOC removal efficiency of 87.9% for BPA within 2 h (Fig. 3d), with most intermediates being mineralized into CO2 and H2O, confirming its environmental friendliness.

The resulting radicals’ charging role for FeZn-GLC stability

FeZn-GLC exhibited long-term durability as demonstrated by 24-day operation for BPA degradation in ultrapure water. Astonishingly, the same Mössbauer spectra were observed in the used catalyst as in the fresh one, with all Fe species returned to their original states (Fig. 5a and Table S10). Consistent with this, XRD patterns (Fig. 5b) and Raman G and 2D bands (Fig. 5c and d) remained unchanged. The results indicated that opposite-direction electron transfer occurred between Fe species and GLC π system, respectively, in the processes of multi-component adsorption with N2 exposure and surface reaction with air exposure. Since the high active state at dual-electron centers of BPA-H2O-adsorbed catalyst was consumed with BPA cleavage and O2 reduction, the catalyst should be inactivated with increasing time. Therefore, the aforementioned results indicated that some reaction could recover the catalyst during BPA degradation in an air atmosphere. R• has been widely used in rechargeable batteries due to its high reactivity and high energy storage capacity (209.2–397.5 kJ/mol) [41]. Additionally, the resulting O2•- and •OH may also play a crucial role in catalyst stability, although they have little effect on BPA degradation (Fig. 3i).

To test this hypothesis, TEMPO, PBQ, and IPA were added to the system to quench R•, O2•-, and •OH, respectively. The catalyst with R• quenching nearly deactivated after the fourth cycle, and the one with O2•- quenching showed similar deactivation after the fifth run, whereas that one with •OH quenching still maintained about 50% degradation efficiency at this stage (Fig. 5e–g). Correspondingly, almost all iron oxides disappeared in the used catalyst with R• quenching, while the peak intensities for iron oxides were decreased with O2•- and •OH quenching (Fig. 5h and i). Moreover, their G and 2D bands showed great shifts along with markedly decreased peak intensities compared with the fresh catalyst (Fig. S15). The results confirmed that the inactivated catalyst had weakened tensile strain and diminished conductivity, while its performance could be recovered through R•, O2•-, and •OH, releasing high energy to the catalyst by redox reaction, sustaining the catalyst’s long-term stability.

Conclusions

In this study, we developed a catalytic reaction system based on FeZn-GLC, with fortified cation−π interaction, which enabled efficient mineralization of bisphenol pollutants in water with high and stable catalytic efficiency under ambient atmospheric conditions. It did not require the addition of light, electricity, or oxidizing agents. The high active state combination of Fe3C and GLC from BPA-DOCMW-H2O, multicomponent coordinated on FeZn-GLC delivers high open circuit voltage, triggering both the cleavage of BPA and DOC and O2 reduction, an electrochemical-like reaction. The resulting R•, O2•-, and •OH were shown to charge the Fe species and GLC system, recovering FeZn-GLC for complete BP destruction. The results addressed the inhibitory effect of DOC in real waters for BPs removal. Our findings highlight the potential of high active state and electric energy from the catalyst surface reconstruction through multicomponent coordination for remediating micropollutant contamination of water.
