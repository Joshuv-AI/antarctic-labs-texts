Causes and consequences of disordered hyperuniformity in global drylands
Authors: Wensi Hu, Lijuan Cui, Manuel Delgado-Baquerizo, Ricard Solé, Sonia Kéfi, Miguel Berdugo, Nuo Xu, Bo Wang, Quan-Xing Liu, Chi Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceDisordered hyperuniformity is a newly defined form of self-organized spatial patterning characterized by the emergence of order on a large scale and a lack of apparent order on a local scale. This study demonstrates its prevalence in dryland ecosystems worldwide. We show that disordered hyperuniformity in plant spatial distributions associates with an enhanced water retention use and expanded range of aridity conditions under which the system can maintain itself. However, this enhanced functionality comes with a trade-off of slower recovery of vegetation structure from disturbances. By uncovering disordered hyperuniformity as a hidden structural mechanism in drylands, this work offers insights into the resilience of some of Earth’s most extensive and fragile ecosystems, with implications for ecological management and sustainability.

Drylands cover almost half of the planet (1). A conspicuous signature of dryland ecosystems is that water scarcity often results in sparse vegetation surrounded by open spaces, creating all sorts of spatial patterns. Such vegetation patterns are formed through a process known as “spatial self-organization” (2–6). Spatial self-organization may involve various agents including plants (2, 4), soil fauna (7–10), microbes (11, 12), and sediments (13, 14). The biotic and abiotic interactions between these agents can improve environmental harshness locally (e.g., higher soil moisture and nutrient availability) (15–18). Importantly, it has been suggested that self-organized vegetation patterns can enhance the functioning and stability of dryland ecosystems (19–23). Understanding how and why dryland ecosystems self-organize is therefore critical for their conservation, which affects the well-being of millions of people, especially in developing countries (24, 25).

Self-organized dryland vegetation patterns exhibit a rich variety of regularities (as gathered from remote sensing techniques), from spots (26) and stripes (27) to fairy circles (7, 8, 28), as well as disordered ones (29, 30). While regular vegetation structures are often apparent and easily identified through visual checking, some patchy spatial patterns require a proper statistical characterization. This has been the case for the identification of the presence of power laws in patch-size distributions in some drylands (e.g., refs. 20, 29, and 30). Although the generic mechanisms of spatial self-organization are well identified for certain spatial organizations, the detailed mechanisms driving the diversity of vegetation structures are not well understood.

“Disordered hyperuniformity” refers to a recently discovered state of matter characterized by an isotropic, seemingly disordered arrangement of particles at short scales, yet exhibiting long-range uniformity (low-density variations) akin to crystalline order. Such hyperuniformity cannot be identified by visually checking, thus considered a hidden order (see SI Appendix, Table S1 Glossary and Fig. 1 for examples of disordered hyperuniformity vs. nonhyperuniformity) (31). Importantly, disordered hyperuniformity leads to surprising emergent properties. For instance, electrons flow easily through disordered hyperuniform silica, making it effectively a metal (32). In recent years, disordered hyperuniformity has been found in a wide range of systems, starting to invoke much interest across multiple disciplinary fields for studying both non-life and life systems (33–38). Interestingly, disordered hyperuniformity can also arise as a result of self-organization in microscopic biological systems such as avian photoreceptor cells (34), microbial populations (37), and leaf vein networks (38), fulfilling important biological functions.

In dryland ecosystems, regular vegetation patterns driven by scale-dependent feedbacks—often referred to as “Turing patterns”—can appear superficially uniform in high-resolution aerial imagery. However, it is uncertain whether such patterns inherently exhibit disordered hyperuniformity: While local-scale uniformity in patch size and interpatch spacing is visually discernible, this feature does not satisfy the criterion for hyperuniformity, which requires significantly lower patch density variations across larger spatial scales. Recent theoretical work proposes that disordered hyperuniformity could emerge in modeled Turing vegetation patterns under arid conditions (39). However, both empirical and theoretical links between dryland vegetation patterning and disordered hyperuniformity remain largely unclear. First, empirical evidence of disordered hyperuniform vegetation patterns is lacking, making it difficult to tell whether and to what extent disordered hyperuniform patterns exist in real-world dryland ecosystems. Second, spatial self-organization can arise from various mechanisms essentially underlain by multiple principles beyond the classic Turing principle (40), but it remains unknown whether disordered hyperuniformity is restricted to Turing patterns or can arise from other self-organization mechanisms.

Here, we randomly selected high-resolution remote sensing images of 425 dryland sites with spot-like vegetation patterns across the globe to investigate the contribution of disordered hyperuniformity in explaining global-scale patterns in dryland vegetation. We demonstrate that dryland vegetation follows predictable disordered hyperuniform patterns spanning across hundreds of meters. This hidden order is not distinguishable by the eyes, but can be quantified from the density distribution at large spatial scales. We stress that the meaning of “uniformity” in “disordered hyperuniformity” differs from the well-documented vegetation patterning feature of “overdispersion” (SI Appendix, Table S1) (41). Overdispersion patterns are usually identified through distance-based point pattern statistics [such as Ripley’s K, L, or pair correlation function g(r)], conventionally used in vegetation pattern analyses (8, 28, 42). In subsequent analyses, we show that disordered hyperuniformity cannot be identified by such distance-based statistics, but rather can be detected through density-based statistics (so-called “density fluctuations”) or power spectrum analysis (SI Appendix, Table S1, see also Materials and Methods). We further propose three types of specific spatial self-organization mechanisms potentially underlying the disordered hyperuniformity of dryland vegetation. We then use mathematical models to investigate whether these mechanisms indeed generate similar patterns in models. These models we consider allow us to systematically explore the impact of disordered hyperuniformity on the functioning and resilience of ecosystems. This knowledge is critical to understanding the distribution and functioning of the largest and most vulnerable ecosystems on our planet.

Results and Discussion

Our remote-sensing scanning showed that disordered hyperuniform vegetation patterns are a global-scale and common feature in (spot-like) drylands with about 10% of all analyzed drylands showing such patterns. Disordered hyperuniform vegetation was present across contrasting continents, ranging from the Americas and Africa to Asia and Australia, and was especially predominant under arid and hyperarid conditions (Fig. 1). Such prevalence suggests that disordered hyperuniformity can emerge as a common self-organization vegetation structure in water-limited environments, hinting at the possibility that this distinctive spatial vegetation structure can help ecosystems to cope with stress. This idea is supported by our modeling analyses showing that disordered hyperuniform patterns with different underlying mechanisms can indeed result in enhanced efficiency of soil moisture acquisition, in agreement with a previous study using surface water use efficiency as the indicator of ecosystem functioning (39). Also, our models suggest that disordered hyperuniformity can enhance ecological resilience to increasing water stress, but this advantage comes at the cost of lowered engineering resilience to local disturbances (e.g. herbivory). Our results, therefore, pave the way for further exploration of the origins and consequences of disordered hyperuniform vegetation patterns.

Disordered Hyperuniformity Characterizes Vegetation Patterns in Global Drylands.

We conducted conventional distance-based point pattern analyses (using Ripley’s L and pair correlation g(r) statistics), where Poisson point distributions are taken as the null model [often referred to as “complete spatial randomness,” CSR, in ecology (41)]. The 425 studied dryland sites typically present “overdispersed” vegetation patches at small scales (i.e., statistically larger between-patch distances than CSR); but at large scales, their between-patch distances follow CSR (Fig. 2 A and D and SI Appendix, Figs. S1 and S2; see Materials and Methods and SI Appendix). Such a scale-dependent patterning feature has been repeatedly documented in a wide range of dryland ecosystems, and the small-scale overdispersion feature has often been interpreted as a sign of strong plant–plant competition for soil water (2, 4).

However, the conventional distance-based point pattern analyses are incapable of identifying disordered hyperuniform vegetation patterns. Instead, disordered hyperuniformity can be detected through a “density fluctuations” approach, which essentially examines the scaling relation between the variance of vegetation density σρ2L and spatial scale L (σρ2L∼L-λ). The exponent λ of the scaling relation is key: Poisson point processes in 2D space can generate spatial structures with λ =2 (which is the spatial dimension value), therefore, an exponent value of 2 is normally taken as a key reference for Poisson patterns (31). Apparent deviations of λ from 2 toward larger values are seen as a clear indication of long-range hyperuniformity. More specifically, the situation where λ falls between 2 and 3 is referred to as “Class III” disordered hyperuniformity (31). An intuitive interpretation is that the decay of density fluctuations is faster than the increase of observational scale, indicating the suppression of long-range density fluctuations (43). See SI Appendix, Table S1 and Materials and Methods for details of density fluctuations. Remarkably, our density fluctuation analyses revealed that about 90% of the sites have a scaling exponent λ of density fluctuations ≤2.0 (mean = 1.51; s.d. = 0.41) at observational scales >10 m (Fig. 2B). In contrast, the remaining 10% (58 sites) present values of λ >2.0 (mean = 2.29; s.d. = 0.15) at scales around 20-150 m, indicating the existence of disordered hyperuniformity (Fig. 2E and SI Appendix, Fig. S3).

An equivalent way to test for disordered hyperuniformity is to look at the power spectrum characterized by the structure factor Sk and spectral density function Ψ∼k (see SI Appendix, Table S1 and Materials and Methods for details, ref. 43): At large observational spatial scales, the wave number variable k (k=k) approaches 0, disordered hyperuniformity would present a scaling relation Sk∼kα, where the exponent α has a positive value. Simply put, one can identify disordered hyperuniformity by checking whether the scaling exponent α >0. See SI Appendix, Table S1, Materials and Methods, and refs. 31, 43 for details. Indeed, our results show that this criterion is commonly met, reinforcing the existence of disordered hyperuniformity in these study sites (Fig. 2F and SI Appendix, Fig. S4).

Our results thus clearly demonstrate that disordered hyperuniformity as a distinguishable state exists in dryland vegetation: ~10% of the randomly selected landscapes from spot-like drylands across the globe display this kind of pattern (Fig. 1 and SI Appendix, Table S2). One may speculate that such long-range spatial patterning could be underpinned by some common ecological processes, raising the essential question: What are the generalized mechanisms, if any, underlying disordered hyperuniformity of dryland vegetation?

Ecological legacy effect.

Recent work in physics has shown that critical absorbing states of stochastic many-particle systems can give rise to disordered hyperuniformity (47, 48). Briefly put, a system consisting of many movable particles can present a phase transition between an “active phase” characterized by persistent particle motion and an “absorbing phase” in which the particles cease to move. Disordered hyperuniformity can arise at the critical (density) point of this phase transition with gradually increasing particle density (this point is referred to as critical absorbing states) (47).

At first glance, it seems unlikely that vegetation patterns pertain to movement-driven critical absorbing states, as sessile plants cannot move like active matter. However, on a slower time scale, vegetation systems do exhibit spatial turnover similar to particle movement through the mortality-regeneration process: The death of an individual allows a new one to establish in a different location, reorganizing the system spatially. Small random displacements near critical absorbing states can lead to robust disordered hyperuniformity under disturbances such as noise or periodic perturbations (49). In dryland vegetation, this mechanism may arise from ecological legacy effects, where new plants tend to establish near dead ones. These effects include enhanced soil water and nutrient conditions—such facilitation [termed as the “nurse plant effect” between living plants (15–18)] can persist after the benefactor’s death, with soils maintaining higher water infiltration rates for years, supporting seedling regeneration and survival (50, 51).

Indeed, this legacy effect can be supported by the vegetation dynamics captured by time-series high-resolution images in particular dryland sites (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). We used individual-based modeling to test whether this ecological legacy effect may give rise to disordered hyperuniformity. We first looked at a competition model, in which competition between neighboring plants is the only non-neutral process (The competition model in Materials and Methods). In this model, our simulation results showed that at large scales the exponent λ of the density fluctuations scaling relation falls around 2.0 (Fig. 4D) and the exponent α of the structural factor scaling relation falls around 0 (Fig. 4E), indicating nonhyperuniformity.

On this basis, we then tested the impact of including the ecological legacy effect assuming that newborns emerge in the proximity of recently deceased individuals (Fig. 3B, The ecological legacy model in Materials and Methods). Our results revealed that the ecological legacy model can generate long-range hyperuniformity characterized by a scaling relation of density fluctuations σρ2L∼L-2.45 and a structure factor Sk∼k0.45(k→0). In line with previous theoretical studies of critical absorbing state behavior (47, 48), our simulation results show that this disordered hyperuniform pattern arises around the phase transition point separating the active phase and the absorbing phase (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). While such phase transition has been well illustrated for stochastic many-particle systems (47), it remains hypothetical in the context of dryland ecosystems. In our model, the absorbing phase may be understood as the system below certain maximum plant density at given carrying capacity of water resource, where all individuals can still persist given their interactions; whereas the active phase represents the fact that above the maximum plant density, any addition of new individuals into the system would invoke excessive crowding thus competition-induced mortality happens, inevitably leading to system structural change.

Notably, stochasticity (environmental harshness that affects background mortality), as the essential driver of the spatial reorganization, can substantially enhance the robustness of disordered hyperuniformity, consistently with other many-particle physical systems (49): In the absence of environmental stochasticity, disordered hyperuniformity only occurs in a very narrow range of density around the critical absorbing state, whereas its presence substantially expands that density range accommodating disordered hyperuniformity (SI Appendix, Figs. S7 and S8). Since stochastic death-rebirth dynamics are a necessary ingredient giving rise to disordered hyperuniformity, no attractor exists in this model; however, the disordered hyperuniformity feature, once emerged, can persist and is robust to environmental stochasticity. Therefore, it is reasonable to speculate that critical absorbing states may generally underlie disordered hyperuniformity across a range of systems due to its simplicity and robustness.

Scale-dependent feedback.

Extensive studies have shown that scale-dependent feedback (i.e., short-range positive feedback and long-range negative feedback) following Turing’s activation-inhibition principle is widespread, and often plays a key role in shaping patchy vegetation patterns in drylands (2, 4, 52, 53). A common class of scale-dependent feedback hinges on plant–plant interactions: Established plants can provide crucial facilitation to their closely coexisting neighbors (for instance, the aforementioned nurse plant effect), while interfering with the plants at longer distances through soil water competition [for instance, realized by lateral roots that extend far beyond the canopy extent (4)].

We built upon the aforementioned competition model by adding the facilitation effect to mimic the scale-dependent feedback driven by plant–plant interactions (The scale-dependent feedback model in Materials and Methods). Therefore, the only difference between the scale-dependent feedback model and the ecological legacy model is that the facilitation is produced by alive vs. dead plants. Our parameterized scale-dependent feedback model can give rise to spot-like “Turing patterns.” Importantly, we found that these modeled vegetation patterns clearly present long-range hyperuniformity (Fig. 5 A–F), as indicated by the density fluctuations scaling relation σρ2L∼L-2.5 and structure factor Sk∼k0.4(k→0).

Also, in the scale-dependent feedback model, it can be formed in a relatively wide range of environmental harshness conditions (corresponding to background mortality ~10 to 40%, Fig. 5E), implying its robustness in nature. Density-dependent aggregation.

Recent work highlights that “density-dependent aggregation” following the so-called “phase separation principle” in physics can potentially act as a generalized mechanism giving rise to patchy patterns across a range of ecological systems including dryland vegetation (40). Density-dependent aggregation does not require feedbacks at different spatial scales. Instead, it entails the aggregation of a mobile species or substance that remains (nearly) conserved within the system (54, 55), with its influx and outflux negligible compared to local redistribution processes (40). Also, the local rate of change depends on local density, with the species or substance dissipating when the density falls below a certain threshold and aggregating when it exceeds that threshold (see ref. 40 for details). Despite the fundamental difference between the scale-dependent feedback and density-dependent aggregation mechanisms, their resulting spatial patterns are similar in terms of the emergence of (quasi) spatial periodicity at particular stages of system evolution. Density-dependent aggregation is thought to act as a widespread mechanism shaping dryland vegetation patterns (40, 56).

It has been speculated that the (quasi) periodic patterns resulting from density-dependent aggregation could involve disordered hyperuniformity, but rigorous tests remain lacking. Here, we analyzed a plant–sediment interaction model by Siteur et al. (40) (The density-dependent aggregation model in Materials and Methods). This density-dependent aggregation model can also give rise to disordered hyperuniformity, as indicated by the density fluctuations scaling relation σρ2L∼L-3 and spectral density function Ψ∼k∼k4(k→0) (Fig. 5 K and L). Moreover, the resulting disordered hyperuniformity is not restricted to spot-like patterns but also extends to patterns mimicking labyrinths or gaps (Fig. 5 H and I). Our results thus suggest that disordered hyperuniformity can come into being as a transient state during the phase separation processes. Despite the transient dynamics, the disordered hyperuniform spatial pattern can remain robust over time, as the system undergoes a “coarsening” (i.e., large patches getting larger over time) following a power-law scaling with a coarsening exponent of approximately 1/3 (55, 57). This implies that at a later stage, the change of spatial pattern will become extremely slow, allowing disordered hyperuniformity to persist over ecologically relevant timescales, presenting a quasi-stable feature (SI Appendix, Fig. S9C and refs. 40, 55).

Other potential mechanisms.

While the plant–plant and plant–sediment interactions considered here may be prevalent in dryland ecosystems, other mechanisms can plausibly drive the formation of disordered hyperuniformity. In various systems, candidate mechanisms that are worth considering may include plant–soil–animal interactions (7–9, 19), plant-soil microbiota feedbacks (11, 12), geoevolutionary feedbacks (14), hydrological processes (58), among others. We are unable to address all these potential mechanisms in this work; instead, we focus on termite-vegetation systems to exemplify how more complex ecological interactions may give rise to disordered hyperuniformity.

We checked the study sites reported in previous work (5, 8) and analyzed the high-resolution remote sensing images of termite-mound and fairy-circle landscapes in African and South American drylands in these studies. Our results show that four representative examples of these systems follow disordered hyperuniformity (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 B–E). We also analyzed the spot patterns of termite mounds generated by a previously developed model representing positive termite–vegetation interactions (59). The modeled patterns present clear disordered hyperuniformity under a range of colony densities (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). These additional analyses reinforce that disordered hyperuniformity may pertain to a wide range of dryland ecosystems, underscoring the need for more detailed and realistic ecological models for elucidating the mechanisms underlying disordered hyperuniformity.

Disordered Hyperuniformity May Enhance Ecosystem Functioning.

We showed that disordered hyperuniformity is a relatively common feature in drylands, but the ecological consequences of such a pattern remained virtually unknown. There is an increasing suggestion that spatial self-organization can enhance key aspects of ecosystem functioning (2, 19–21, 23). In line with this idea, a previous model study showed that hyperuniformity generated by the scale-dependent feedback mechanism leads to higher water use efficiency of vegetation (39). However, this measure pertains to surface water use, which only possibly happens at relatively high levels of precipitation. This is unrealistic for our studied dryland sites where mean annual precipitation typically falls below 300 mm (SI Appendix, Table S2). Here, we quantified soil moisture acquisition of plants as the ecosystem functioning indicator under the low-precipitation condition, where plants mostly consume precipitation water infiltrated into the soil (without accumulation of surface water). We applied a simple moisture process model on the model-generated vegetation landscapes (SI Appendix) to assess whether and to what extent disordered hyperuniformity and nonhyperuniformity patterns differ in the acquisition of soil moisture, which is arguably among the most critical aspects for dryland ecosystems (3, 60, 61).

We compare four types of spatial patterns with equal plant densities: 1) ordered hyperuniform pattern represented by a hexagonal lattice (characterized by a density fluctuation scaling relation σρ2L∼L-3.0), 2) disordered hyperuniform pattern generated by the ecological legacy model (characterized by σρ2L∼L-2.45), 3) nonhyperuniform pattern with small-scale overdispersion generated by the competition model (characterized by σρ2L∼L-2.0), and 4) nonhyperuniform pattern with complete spatial randomness (CSR, characterized by σρ2L∼L-2.0). Note that here, we focus on the functioning (in terms of soil moisture acquisition) of these “static” patterns (Fig. 6A). Thus, the patterns are held unchanged without considering pattern formation mechanisms. In addition, we did not include the scale-dependent feedback model as it generates plant clusters, making the resulting pattern incomparable with other patterns.

We use the performance of the hexagonal lattice pattern as the reference, which presents the highest function of soil moisture acquisition (Fig. 6 B–E). The disordered hyperuniform pattern of the ecological legacy model shows a slightly lower function level than the hexagonal lattice pattern, but can substantially outperform the nonhyperuniform patterns of the competition and CSR models. Such advantage of disordered hyperuniformity tends to be more pronounced at higher evaporation rates (Fig. 6B) and lower plant uptake rates (Fig. 6C). This finding aligns with the intuition that if the resource is homogeneously distributed in space, more uniform distributions of plant individuals can lead to more uniform partitioning of the space, in turn, faster system-wide uptake, leaving less moisture evaporated. Indeed, it has been shown that disordered hyperuniformity may pertain to “jammed particle packing” in the sense that disordered hyperuniformity tends to present denser packing (62). Therefore, an obvious functional implication of the denser packing is that hyperuniform patterns can support higher densities than the nonhyperuniform counterparts at equal water resource levels.

Resilience of Disordered Hyperuniformity.

Another speculation is that disordered hyperuniformity may enhance the resilience of dryland vegetation, as positive links between ecosystem resilience and self-organized ecosystem patterns have been documented in stressful environments (63, 64). We examined both engineering resilience (return rate of the system after a perturbation) and ecological resilience (range of increasing stress the system can experience before it collapses) (65) of the modeled ecosystems to test this speculation.

We first used perturbation experiments, in which plant individuals are randomly selected to be removed, to quantify recovery rate as a metric of engineering resilience. We compare the hyperuniform patterns generated by the ecological legacy model to the nonhyperuniform patterns generated by the competition model, to probe how they could respond to local disturbances (such as herbivory-induced mortalities). We do not include the hexagonal lattice pattern and the CSR pattern, considering that these patterns lack clear pattern formation mechanisms as a necessary ingredient of recovery.

Interestingly, our results demonstrate that after a perturbation, the nonhyperuniform patterns recover faster than the disordered hyperuniform counterparts (Fig. 7A). A close scrutiny of the models reveals that in stochastic mortality-regeneration processes, it takes longer for the system to get settled in the disordered hyperuniform states representing a more “packed” spatial configuration. On the other hand, when the system is purely driven by local competition, the convergence to equilibrium is faster. Therefore, if stochastic reorganization of the systems is a key element for pattern formation, disordered hyperuniformity may come at the cost of slower recovery of system spatial structure upon perturbations. Such spatial aspect of resilience could be essentially different from the previously studied resilience upon spatially homogeneous perturbation without invoking spatial structure reorganization (11, 22, 64, 66).

Second, we assessed ecological resilience by quantifying the extent to which the patterned vegetation can persist as environmental harshness gradually increases (may also be understood as “persistence” in this context) (67). As the ecological legacy model and the competition model do not present alternative attractors, we followed the approach of Bastiaansen et al. (67) by assuming that plants increase effective water acquisition radius (interaction range, Rc) in response to increasing water stress. As a result, both the hyperuniform (generated from the ecological legacy model) and nonhyperuniform (generated from the competition model) systems lose vegetation patches in a stepwise way (similar to ref. 67), but a key difference is that the loss is slower in the disordered hyperuniform system (Fig. 7B). This result thus indicates that the disordered hyperuniform system can better sustain itself under deteriorating conditions with enhanced efficiency of soil water utilization, thus presenting higher ecological resilience.

Taken together, disordered hyperuniform vegetation patterns present contrasting engineering vs. ecological resilience: these patterns, compared with their nonhyperuniform counterparts, can support higher plant densities under a certain level of environmental stress, corresponding with higher ecological resilience; however, they take longer time to recover once their spatial structure is disrupted, presenting lower engineering resilience. Assessing the resilience of these patterns driven by specific formation mechanisms is an intriguing topic, but requires in-depth theoretical and empirical studies.

Outlook and Summary.

Our global investigation provides evidence that disordered hyperuniformity shapes vegetation structure of drylands worldwide. This hidden order of spatial self-organization is increasingly documented in a wide range of microscopic physical, chemical, and biological systems (33, 34, 37, 38). Our work contributes to expanding this domain to include natural landscape ecological systems that are largely underexplored. More importantly, we have used models to identify three mechanisms that can lead to the emergence of disordered hyperuniform patterns, i.e., ecological legacy effect, scale-dependent feedback, and density-dependent aggregation. These mechanisms differ in their temporal stability and sensitivity to stochasticity, but they all allow for persistence of disordered hyperuniform patterns over ecologically meaningful timescales. Our findings invite future studies to systematically scan vegetation patterns and empirically test pattern formation mechanisms through the lens of disordered hyperuniformity. Our work also advances our understanding of the links between disordered hyperuniformity and ecosystem functioning and resilience, thereby contributing to addressing how ecosystems may cope with stressful environments and climate change through spatial self-organization.

While there is still a long way to go toward unraveling the mechanisms underlying disordered hyperuniform vegetation states, previous studies on microscopic systems have gained valuable insights (37). The known mechanisms are of various forms, but many pertain to long-range interactions. For instance, algae with circular swimming behavior may generate particular fluid flow that results in effective repulsions between the algae individuals in the long range. Such long-range hydrodynamic interactions can suppress density fluctuations and drive the algae to self-organize into disordered hyperuniformity (37). However, long-range interactions are not a necessary condition—critical absorbing states purely driven by local interactions can also give rise to disordered hyperuniformity (47, 48). Indeed, it has been shown in our models that both local (ecological legacy effect and density-dependent aggregation) and nonlocal (scale-dependent feedback) interactions are possible mechanisms for dryland vegetation systems. Nonetheless, these theoretical predictions need to be tested and refined with detailed field observations and thoughtful experiments. For example, it is essential to understand under what conditions disordered hyperuniformity can arise in systems governed by Turing or phase separation principles. The underlying mechanisms could commonly occur in real-world ecosystems, implying that disordered hyperuniform patterns may be frequently found in nature, as supported by our nonsystematic scanning of Google Earth images (Fig. 1 and SI Appendix, Table S2). Apart from pursuing a general understanding, it is interesting to explore specific mechanisms in particular systems such as the termite-shaped landscapes (8, 59). It is also interesting to understand why different mechanisms converge to result in similar spatial patterning in terms of disordered hyperuniformity – it may represent an emergent optimal configuration under water-limited or otherwise resource- and space-constrained conditions.

Our modeling analyses suggest that the existence of disordered hyperuniformity could be sensitive to a range of factors such as environmental conditions, biotic/abiotic interactions (in terms of type, intensity, and operating scale), and the ontogeny of pattern formation. Moreover, environmental heterogeneity (such as soil patchiness and topographic heterogeneity) may easily hinder the emergence of disordered hyperuniformity, as spatial aggregation near resource-rich zones can represent a more optimal configuration under such conditions. Our results suggest that the formation of disordered hyperuniformity may take a long time, as reflected by their relatively low engineering (structural) resilience. Therefore, these patterns are likely sensitive to frequent environmental perturbations that disrupt their formation. Taken together, we need both in-depth theoretical analyses and broad-scale empirical searching (68, 69) for disordered hyperuniformity to fully understand the genesis of this distinctive ecosystem state.

Materials and Methods

Data Collection and Image Analyses.

We scanned the global drylands using the global aridity index dataset version 3 [Fig. 1, areas falling into the categories of “hyperarid,” “arid,” and “semiarid” defined by Zomer et al. (70)] in search for (seemingly) homogeneous, regular vegetation patterns. We are particularly interested in spot-like patterns as this type pertains to the widest range of documented disordered hyperuniformity (31). We focus on the areas where high-resolution (0.3 m) remotely sensed images are available from Google Earth. We avoided areas with apparent landscape heterogeneity with respect to topographic features and human land use. The search was conducted through visually scanning Google Earth randomly. We finally included 425 sites for our analyses (Fig. 1, see SI Appendix for details). Within each site, we set up a plot size of ~500 × 500 m2. For each plot, we georegistered the Google Earth image and then extracted the vegetation patches using a supervised classification approach with a maximum likelihood method. We converted the vegetation patches into polygons and extracted their centroids for subsequent analyses. The image analyses were conducted in QGIS 3.18 and MATLAB 2021a.

Spatial Point Pattern Analyses, Density Fluctuations, and Structure Factor.

We first conducted conventional spatial point pattern analyses based on the patch centroids. These analyses, based on Ripley’s L function and pair correlation function gr, quantify distributions of between-point distances (41). For instance, the gr function, as a summary statistic, is a measure of the probability of finding two individuals at a given distance (r, i.e., spatial scale) from each other. If gr derived from the focal vegetation pattern is significantly lower than that derived from the null model characterizing random distributions (normally Poisson point process, also termed as complete spatial randomness, CSR), the vegetation pattern is interpreted as “overdispersion” (SI Appendix).

The density fluctuations method seems similar with the aforementioned spatial point pattern analysis, but there is an important difference, too. It essentially quantifies how the density of system components varies across spatial scales. For 2D systems, density (ρ) fluctuations are calculated as the variance of density within sampling windows with a fixed size L randomly laid across the system:[1]σρ2L≡ρ2L-ρ2.

Varying window size L, the systems following Poisson distributions can yield a scaling relation σρ2L∼L-2. When density fluctuations decay faster than Poisson distributions, the scaling relation yields σρ2L∼L-λ with 2<λ≤3. This indicates that component distribution presents lower variations (or in other words, more “uniform”) than “random distributions,” an indicator of the presence of hyperuniformity (31). Note that a key feature of disordered hyperuniformity is that density fluctuations of the system components are suppressed at large scales, presenting lower variations than expected by chance, as typically found in crystal lattice structures.

We quantified point density fluctuations for the outputs of the competition model, the ecological legacy model, and the scale-dependent feedback model, as these models generate points in space as the outputs (see the Simple models section below). However, plant individuals are not distinguishable from the Google Earth images. To take into account for vegetation patch size, we quantified density fluctuations of biomass (SI Appendix) for the spatial patterns derived from the remote sensing images, as well as those from the outputs of the density-dependent aggregation model that give relatively continuous (patchy) vegetation distributions.

The structure factor S(k) describes the spatial configurations of points in systems (the spatial correlations of points in systems) over a range of wavelengths Λ, corresponding to different values of the wavevector k:[2]Sk=1N∑j=1ne-ik·rj2,

where N is the total number of points, r(j) is the particle spatial position, and k=2π/Λ. This method is closely related to the characterization of power spectral density. Details of the density fluctuations and structural factor methods can be found in ref. 31 and SI Appendix.

Simple Models.

To test whether disordered hyperuniformity may arise from commonly found interactions in dryland ecosystems with particular respect to facilitation and competition, we analyzed the emergent spatial patterns and their signatures in four simple ecological models to explore the role of different interaction mechanisms.

The competition model.

The competition model adapted from Xu et al. (45) is a simple individual-based model used to account for the effect of plant–plant competition on vegetation patterning. This model assumes that strong water competition acts as a dominant biotic interaction between plants. In this model, each plant individual is represented by a point in a continuous 2D space. Simulations start from randomly generating n points; if the distance between two plant individuals is smaller than a predefined threshold Rc, reflecting the competition interaction range, one of the individuals (selected randomly) dies (this is the “hardcore” scenario). Competition is thereby assumed to be short-ranged. Following the mortality of m deceased individuals, m new individuals are generated randomly, the total individual number is thus maintained at n (n is not emergent but a parameter of the model). Such a mortality-regeneration process is repeated until no individual dies. We also consider a “softcore” scenario, where the between-plant competition is modeled by a Hill function as in ref. 46. Both the hardcore and softcore versions result in consistent patterns (SI Appendix, Fig. S10). See SI Appendix, Table S3 for model parameters.

The ecological legacy model.

The ecological legacy effect model is built by modifying the regeneration process in the aforementioned competition model. Instead of generating new individuals randomly, new individuals are now generated in the proximity of the dead individuals to mimic the facilitative effect of the previously existing plants. More specifically, the m new individuals introduced are distributed randomly within the interaction range Rc of the individuals that died at the previous time step (one newborn is generated around each dead individual). When the system is stabilized (no individual dies, see SI Appendix, Fig. S9A), 20% of individuals are randomly selected and die (representing background mortality in harsh environments) followed by regeneration (thus the same density is maintained). We model this process because it allows the system to spatially reorganize in a way that mimics the random movement of many-particle systems. Recent work shows that such random movement can give rise to disordered hyperuniformity close to the so-called critical absorbing states (see SI Appendix, Figs. S7 and S8, and ref. 49 for details). The background mortality value of 20% is set arbitrarily (an insensitive parameter). Note that this background mortality effect is not considered in the competition model as it does not have any influence on the model results.

The scale-dependent feedback model.

We built a minimal scale-dependent feedback model by adapting the individual-based model of ref. 46. In this model, individuals are randomly generated followed by mortality determined by the effects of short-range facilitation and long-range competition (see SI Appendix for details).

The density-dependent aggregation model.

We adopted the sediment trapping model by Siteur et al. (40) to account for the density-dependent aggregation process. This model mimics the essential plant–sediment feedbacks underlying nebkhas-like biomorphic systems with hummocked structures typically found across the global drylands (71–73): Established plants can enhance sediment deposition rates and/or decrease erosion rates of aeolian sands, while the trapped sediments facilitate plant growth through increasing soil water and nutrient availability, and helping plants escape adverse conditions due to erosion and elevated soil salinity (72, 73). Within this system, sediments are in a deposited or mobile state (due to wind erosion). Established vegetation increases the deposition rate and reduces the erosion rate (i.e., sediment trapping), and the sediments trapped by plants can benefit plant growth, creating a positive feedback. Unlike the previous three individual-based models, this model is continuous as the sediment redistribution process occurs continuously across space. Details of the aforementioned models can be found in SI Appendix.

Model Output Analyses.

We first conducted spatial point-pattern analysis on model outputs as a conventional approach for quantifying vegetation patterns (41, 74). We calculated the spatial statistics of the pair correlation function gr (Figs. 4C, 5D, and 5J) based on vegetation patch centroids. We then conducted density fluctuation analyses: Since the competition model, the ecological legacy, and the scale-dependent feedback model are individual-based models, the density fluctuation analyses were performed using the spatial points (equally sized) from the model outputs.

Soil Moisture Acquisition and Resilience Assessment.

We conducted two experiments with the modeled vegetation patterns to examine the effects of the disordered hyperuniform vegetation patterns on ecosystem functioning and resilience.

First, we applied a simple moisture function to the outputs of the different patterns to assess the efficiency of soil moisture acquisition by vegetation and how this efficiency depends on the vegetation spatial pattern. Specifically, we assume that soil moisture comes from a rainfall event with a fixed amount and spatially homogeneous distribution; subsequently the soil moisture is reduced through evaporation and in the meantime depleted by plant uptake, until all available soil moisture is exhausted. The soil moisture available for each plant individual comes from the spatial domain identified by the Voronoi algorithm (the landscape is partitioned into polygons, each of which represents the root domain of an individual plant). We assess the soil moisture acquisition function as the fraction of plant uptake relative to loss through soil evaporation. This function was applied to compare two types of nonhyperuniform patterns with two types of hyperuniform patterns, respectively: 1) a CSR pattern (representing a nonhyperuniform pattern that can be easily distinguished by naked eyes), 2) an output of the competition model (representing a nonhyperuniform pattern that is difficult to distinguish from the disordered hyperuniform counterpart with naked eyes), 3) an output of the ecological legacy model (representing a disordered hyperuniform pattern), and 4) a hexagonal lattice pattern (representing an ordered hyperuniform pattern). Point density was controlled at the same level (12,000 points within the modeled space) for these patterns.

Second, we assessed the recovery rate upon perturbations as an engineering resilience indicator to compare between disordered hyperuniform patterns and nonhyperuniform patterns. We conducted a perturbation experiment by randomly eliminating 20% of plant individuals (points) from the spatial patterns generated by the competition model and the ecological legacy model using landscapes with the same plant densities. We calculated the number of time steps the systems need to recover to the original plant densities. It should be noted that we did not take into account density-dependent aggregation as this model does not represent typical equilibria, making it difficult to compare recovery rate. See SI Appendix for details.

To quantify ecological resilience, we assessed the capacity of the vegetation to maintain individual plants under deteriorating environmental conditions. Following Bastiaansen et al. (67), in our models, we assume that at worsening conditions, individual plants have to increase their effective water acquisition radius (i.e., competition radius) to ensure survival. This spatial radius expansion intensifies competition, potentially leading to the emergence of competitive interactions between previously noncompeting individuals, thereby driving the system toward spatial reorganization. To infer ecological resilience, we compared how deteriorating environmental conditions induce changes in plant density supported by disordered hyperuniform vs. nonhyperuniform patterns.
