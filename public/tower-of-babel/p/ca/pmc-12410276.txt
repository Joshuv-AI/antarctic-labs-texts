The Prevalence of Myopia in Finnish Conscripts Has not Increased in Recent Decades and Is Lower Than in Many East and Southeast Asian Countries
Authors: Olavi Pärssinen, Marja-Liisa Franssila, Klaus Nordhausen, Mika Mattinen, Roope Sovelius, Markku Kauppinen, Jukka Moilanen, Niko Setälä, Ian Morgan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to determine the prevalence of myopia among Finnish conscripts and to identify the factors associated with its occurrence in Finland.MethodsA total of 1694 conscripts (mean age: 19.3 ± 1.55 years) participated in the study. Refraction was performed on 1522 participants under tropicamide-induced cycloplegia. The final analyses included 1286 male conscripts who had completed the Finnish schooling system. A structured questionnaire collected data about education, time spent doing near work, smartphone use, and outdoor time. Univariate analyses and multivariate logistic regression models were used to determine the factors associated with myopia.ResultsThe overall prevalence of myopia was 23.5%, of which 0.4% had high myopia (SE ≤ −6 D). The main factors associated with myopia included (adjusted odds ratio; 95% confidence interval) near work time (1.184; 1.113–1.259), time spent outdoors (0.823; 0.754–0.895), and basic schooling versus higher education (1.256; 0.796–1.988). Among the higher-educated group, increased smartphone use was associated with a higher prevalence of myopia, whereas in the other educational groups, no association could be established.ConclusionsThe prevalence of myopia among Finnish conscripts was relatively low, and high myopia was rare compared to the rates reported in many East and Southeast Asian countries. Higher education, prolonged near work, reduced time outdoors, and increased smartphone use (in the higher-educated group) were factors associated with myopia prevalence. The reduced educational pressure may be attributed to the lower prevalence of myopia among Finnish conscripts compared to many East and Southeast Asian countries, where myopia is much more prevalent.

Myopia (shortsightedness) is a condition where distance vision is impaired. Axial elongation of the eye is the structural basis of myopia. The most significant clinical challenge of myopia is associated with its complications, especially in high myopia. High myopia is often defined as a refractive power of the eye that is −6 diopters (D) or more myopic. A systematic review and meta-analyses have shown that the risks of myopia-related complications, such as myopic macular degeneration, retinal detachment, cataracts, glaucoma, visual impairment, and blindness, all increase with increased severity of myopia. While these risks are substantial in high myopia, there is no safe threshold of myopia for any of its known ocular complications.1

Over the past few decades, the prevalence of myopia has increased three- to fourfold in East and Southeast Asian countries, where the prevalence of myopia in young adults is now around 80% and the prevalence of high myopia in young adults ranges from 10% to 20%.2 According to a World Health Organization (WHO) report, myopia was estimated to affect 27% (1.8 billion) of the world population by 2010.3 The same report predicted that by 2050, myopia prevalence would rise to 4.9 billion people (52% of the world population), of whom 925 million people (10% of the world population) would have high myopia (≤−5 D) and potentially pathological myopia.3 Uncorrected refractive errors have been reported to be the major cause of vision impairment in the world, and undercorrected myopia is the most common cause of vision impairment when judged by poor visual acuity.4,5 Pathologic myopia is estimated to affect approximately 1% of individuals of European ancestry and 1% to 3% of those of East Asian descent, and myopia-derived vision impairment and blindness are predicted to be more common in East Asians (0.2%–1.4%) than in individuals of European ancestry (0.1%–0.5%).6

To study myopia prevalence, conscripts represent a suitable population at the population level, as military service provides access to a large, relatively representative cohort of young adult males gathered in a single location. Until now, the highest reported prevalence of myopia among 19-year-old male conscripts was observed in 2010 in South Korea (96.5%).7 Similarly, high prevalences were reported in Taiwan among male conscripts aged 18 to 24 years in 2010 to 2011 (86.1%)8 and in China among 16- to 26-year-old university students in 2016 (83.2%).9 In contrast, the prevalence of myopia among Finnish army recruits was significantly lower two decades ago, at 22.2%.10 Genome-wide association meta-analysis has shown a strong genetic correlation between Europeans’ and East Asians’ refractive traits (R > 0.78).11 Therefore, these differences in the prevalence of myopia across countries are unlikely to be explained by genetic factors.

The main aims of this study were to determine whether the prevalence of myopia among Finnish army recruits has increased, identify the environmental exposures associated with myopia, and explore potential explanations for the substantial differences in myopia prevalence between Finland and countries experiencing a myopia epidemic.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Participants

According to the Constitution of Finland, all male citizens aged 18 to 29 years are required to participate in national defense. Upon reaching the age of 18, Finnish males must attend military call-up.12 After this, they are typically assigned to service within 1 to 2 years. However, individuals may postpone their service for personal reasons, such as academic pursuits, until the end of the year in which they turn 28. Women may enroll voluntarily. A person liable for military service can attend the armed or unarmed military service or the civil service. The physical and mental fitness of recruits is evaluated in an annual call-up. Recruits can be exempted from military service for severe health reasons. In the context of ocular health, exemptions due to high refractive error are granted to individuals with hyperopia with a spherical equivalent (SE) greater than +9.00 D or myopia with an SE less than −10.00 D. Of the 60,694 recruits accessed between 2020 and 2021, only 4 individuals with hyperopia and 20 with myopia (0.0004%) met the criteria for exemption.12

In the summer of 2022, a total of 1862 conscripts commenced military service at the Vekarajärvi garrisons. Of these, 1694 individuals (mean age: 19.3 ± 1.55 years) participated voluntarily in this study at the onset of their service. During the initial days, the participants completed a structured questionnaire, and data collection, including autorefraction, was conducted at this point. Study participation was voluntary. The study design is illustrated in Figure 1. Of the 1862 conscripts, 168 (9%) recruits declined participation, while 172 (9.8%) abstained from cycloplegia (Fig. 1). Additionally, 133 recruits reported a home language other than Finnish, representing 32 different languages without forming significant ethnic groups. As one of the primary objectives of this study was to examine the association between education and myopia prevalence, and given the lack of detailed information on their educational backgrounds, these individuals were excluded from the multivariate analyses. The prevalence of myopia in this subgroup was 34.6%, and when included in the total male recruit population, the overall prevalence was 24.2%. Furthermore, 80 participants were females, with a myopia prevalence of 25%. To ensure comparability with previous studies, only male conscripts who had undergone education within the Finnish schooling system were included in the final analyses (n = 1286).

Flowchart of study participants.

Cycloplegia was induced using tropicamide eye drops (Oftan Tropicamid 5 mg/mL; Santen, Japan), with one drop administered to each eye. Approximately 30 minutes postadministration, refractive measurements were taken using an autorefractometer (AR-1s; NIDEK, Tokyo, Japan). Cyclopentolate, which provides more complete cycloplegia, was not utilized due to its prolonged effect, which would have been disruptive during the initial days of military service. Pupil dilation was subjectively assessed before refraction measurement, but precise control of cycloplegia was not possible. The prevalence of myopia among the participants who declined cycloplegia was 28.3%. These individuals were excluded from the analyses.

The SE was calculated using the following formula: SE = sphere + ½ * cylinder. No statistically significant difference was observed in mean SE between the right and left eyes (SE right: −0.13 ± 1.50 D and left: −0.13 ± 1.56 D; r = 0.926, P < 0.001). Consequently, only the values from the right eye were used in the analysis. Myopia was defined as SE ≤ −0.50 D, high myopia as either SE ≤ −6.0 D or SE ≤ −5.0 D (used in the WHO predictions for high myopia), emmetropia as SE > −0.5 D and ≤ +0.5 D, and hyperopia as SE > +0.5 D.

In the first days after entering service, the conscripts responded to the structured questionnaire, which collected demographic and behavioral data, including age, sex, home language, and estimated daily duration (in hours) of near work, time outdoors, and smartphone usage. It was stated in the questionnaire to exclude smartphone use time from the time spent on near work to confirm that these two variables did not overlap. These durations were recorded with an accuracy of 1 hour (categories: ≤1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, or ≥8 hours). Educational attainment was classified into four categories based on the highest educational level achieved: obligatory basic school (starting at the age of 7 and lasting for 9 years), vocational school and upper secondary school (both lasting for 3 years after basic school), and higher education, comprising those individuals who had continued their education either at a university (n = 135) or at a university of applied sciences (n = 50). No statistically significant difference in myopia prevalence was observed between the university students (31.1%) and the university of applied sciences students (28.0%) (Fisher's exact test, two-sided, P = 0.722). Therefore, they were analyzed as a single group (n = 185). Nine individuals had undergone refractive surgery in the past. Preoperative refraction values were obtained from the clinics where the surgeries were performed. The participants were asked to provide signed informed consent at the end of the questionnaire.

Statistical Analysis

The statistical analyses were performed in R (R Project for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) using the packages MASS, statmod, and pROC. The binary response, myopia versus no myopia, was modeled using logistic regression. Univariate models are reported using unadjusted odds ratios (ORs). Using the Akaike information criterion (AIC), a multiple logistic regression model was chosen using backward selection, where a smaller AIC indicated a better model. The initial model for the backward selection used predictors including age, highest level of education, near work, time spent outdoors, and smartphone usage, as well as all first-order interaction terms. The final model was evaluated using randomized quantile residuals. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve criterion in the final model is presented together with the ORs of the predictors and their P values. The significance of differences between the categorical variables was tested by cross-tabulation, and the χ2 test was used for discrete variables.

Discussion

Key Findings

The primary results of this study indicate that the prevalence of myopia among young Finnish male conscripts has not increased in the past two decades as compared to findings from a study conducted in Finland in 2003.10 The prevalence of myopia was 23.5%, with 0.5% classified as high myopia (SE ≤ −6.0 D).10 In Finland, compulsory military service applies to all males, while deferrals are permitted for further education, and exceptions are only possible due to severe health reasons. Exemptions due to high ocular refraction are very rare (20/60,694 recruits in 2020–2021). Thus, the sample corresponds well to a population sample of young Finnish adult males. In this study, females who entered voluntary service were slightly more likely to be myopic (25.0%) than males; thus, the prevalence of myopia in Finns of this age in general, including both sexes, would probably be somewhat higher. In this study, the most significant factors associated with the prevalence of myopia were higher education, increased time spent on near work, and reduced time outdoors, as well as increased smartphone use among individuals with higher education levels.

Comparisons of Myopia Prevalence With Other Studies

The prevalence of myopia increased in Finland throughout the 20th century. Among adults born in the early 20th century (the first three decades), myopia prevalence was below 10%, whereas among those born during the latter half of the century, the prevalence increased to 21% to 30%.12 Currently, the prevalence of myopia among young adults in Finland and in Scandinavian countries, including Sweden,13–15 Denmark,16 and Norway,17 remains at a similar level. This prevalence is notably low compared to many East and Southeast Asian countries (Table 6).

In this study, the prevalence of high myopia was 0.5%, consistent with findings from Norway.18 In contrast, studies from South Korea7 and Taiwan8 have reported significantly higher rates, with high myopia levels reaching 21%. The WHO has projected that the global prevalence of myopia was 27% by 2010 and is expected to rise to 52% by 2050, with high myopia (SE ≤ −5.0 D) increasing from 4% to 10% over the same period.3 However, the findings from our study suggest that the prevalence of myopia in Finland is not increasing at a similar rate.

Factors Associated With Myopia

Education

Educational attainment was a key determinant of myopia prevalence in this study. Over the past few decades, levels of education have increased across Europe,24 including in Finland. Increased levels of education have been associated with slight increases in myopia prevalence during the past few decades.13 An Australian study compared the proportion of myopic progressors among those working or studying in a higher academic environment (16.2% vs. only 2.1% among those in nonacademic environments).25 A meta-analysis of population-based, cross-sectional studies from the European Eye Epidemiology Consortium compared the prevalence of myopia in Europe with educational levels among those finishing primary, secondary, and higher education. The age-standardized prevalences were 25.4%, 29.1%, and 36.6%, respectively.24

The connection between education and myopia is also evident in populations with higher prevalences of myopia. A study of 19-year-old male conscripts in Seoul reported a myopia prevalence of 96.5% and high myopia affecting 21.6%.7 Among the conscripts who had 4 to 6 years of university education, the OR for myopia was 1.69 (P < 0.001) compared to those not having graduated from high school. Thus, a similar association between myopia and education is consistent across populations with both low and high baseline prevalence rates.

Near Work, Time Spent Outdoors, and Smartphone Use

The relationships between myopia and near work or time spent outdoors have been known for a long time.2,26 A Finnish study compared the relationship between time spent on near work and time spent outdoors. If the ratio of near work time to outdoors time was either ≤0.5 or >1.5, the prevalence of myopia was 1.4% versus 5.6% (7-year-olds), 6.3% versus 24.7% (11-year-olds), and 15.9% versus 36.9% (15-year-olds), respectively.26

In the present study, increased near work time was associated with higher myopia prevalence across all educational levels, whereas higher levels of time spent outdoors were linked to a lower prevalence of myopia. These results align with the findings from a Taiwanese conscript study, which identified similar risk factors for myopia: parental myopia, higher education level, more near work, less outdoor activity, and higher urbanization level.8 While our study did not assess parental myopia or urbanization, its results were otherwise consistent, suggesting that the relationship between near work, outdoor time, and education with myopia seems to be independent of how high the prevalence of myopia is in the population.

Smartphone use has become increasingly prevalent in recent years among children, even among toddlers. The use of a smartphone is commonly considered to be near work and, thus, a risk factor for myopia. A recent systematic review found that each additional hour of daily digital screen time was associated with a 21% increase in myopia prevalence.27 Another meta-analysis investigated smartphone and tablet use, either alone or combined with computer screen time, and found a significant association with myopia. Specifically, smartphone use alone was linked to an increase in the likelihood of myopia (OR, 1.26; 95% CI, 1.00–1.60), while combined with computer use, the odds increased to OR 1.77 (95% CI, 1.28–2.45).28 However, there was considerable heterogeneity among the studies included in the meta-analysis (e.g., age-related differences in digital device usage patterns).28

The present study found that smartphone use was associated with an increased prevalence of myopia only among more highly educated individuals. However, smartphone usage was measured solely in terms of daily hours of use, without distinguishing between different purposes (e.g., reading, gaming, listening to audio). It is possible that highly educated individuals use their smartphones for activities such as reading, which may have a greater impact on eye growth compared to the usage patterns of those with a vocational education. Further research is needed to better understand how smartphone usage differs across educational groups and how these differences impact myopia risk. Additionally, self-reported smartphone use may not fully capture variations in usage patterns over time. Future studies should aim to specify the purpose of smartphone use to more accurately assess its impact on myopia and other vision-related outcomes.

Factors Associated With Regional Differences in the Prevalence of Myopia

No genetic basis has been identified to explain the regional differences in myopia prevalence.11 The key factors associated with myopia in this study were level of education, near work, and time spent outdoors. Similar findings have been observed in other conscript studies conducted in Taiwan8 and South Korea, where up to 80% to 90% of 15-year-olds have myopia and nearly 20% develop high myopia by the age of 18.7 These findings strongly suggest that regional differences in myopia prevalence are primarily related to differences in the amount of near work and outdoor activities. Key contributing factors include earlier school entry, more intense academic pressure, and greater use of private supplementary tutoring.

In contrast, Finland emphasizes play-based preschool, limited homework in the early years of school, frequent outdoor breaks, and lower educational intensity in early school years. Prolonged near work and reduced outdoor time further contribute to myopia development. In countries with a high prevalence of myopia, efforts have been made to increase the amount of outdoor activities among schoolchildren. In Taiwan, a prospective nationwide cohort study in 2010 recommended 2 hours of outdoor activity per day and a 10-minute break after every 30 minutes of near work. Over the course of the study, the increasing trend of poor, uncorrected distant vision (myopia) significantly reversed.38 In another study conducted among 6-year-old children in China, their parents were encouraged to go outdoors after school, on weekends, and on holidays, and 40 minutes of outdoor activities were added to their school days. During the 3-year follow-up, the incidence of myopia was significantly less in the intervention group than in the control group.39 Therefore, interventions promoting outdoor activity in countries such as Taiwan and China have shown promising results in reducing myopia incidence and progression.

Limitations

To determine more precise refractive errors and myopia prevalence, cycloplegic refraction using cyclopentolate would have been required. However, we were not able to use cyclopentolate due to its prolonged effect and the potential disturbance to military duties during the first days of service. The prevalence of myopia among those who declined cycloplegia was approximately 6% higher compared to those to whom tropicamide was administered (28.3% vs. 23.5%, respectively). This suggests that the accommodation was largely paralyzed and that the use of cyclopentolate would probably have led to a slightly lower prevalence of myopia. Nevertheless, in our opinion, this does not change our overall conclusion that the prevalence of myopia has not increased in Finland in recent decades.

A small number of the conscripts were female or spoke a language other than Finnish at home. Our primary objective was to compare the findings with a previous study on Finnish male conscripts and to investigate the association between Finnish education and myopia; thus, the above individuals were excluded from the multivariate analyses. Including them in the analyses would have somewhat increased the overall prevalence of myopia. However, we have reported the prevalence rates for these excluded groups separately in the Results section.

Self-reported questionnaire data are subject to several potential sources of bias, as estimations of time spent are subjective. When inquiring about the use of smartphones, we did not specify the purpose of use or the typical viewing distance. This may explain why the association between smartphone use and myopia in this study was different between the highly educated and those with a vocational education. This finding emphasizes the importance of considering both the purpose and viewing distance when investigating the relationship between smartphone usage and myopia.

Additionally, it should be noted that the data were collected during the very first days of military service, reflecting the time before military service, but do not take into account potential changes from childhood.

Despite these limitations, our findings align with previous studies examining similar associations, further reinforcing the established links between myopia, near work, and outdoor activities.

Concluding Remarks

The prevalence of myopia and high myopia among Finnish conscripts was significantly lower compared to many East and Southeast Asian countries. In this study, factors associated with myopia included a higher level of education, increased time spent on near work, reduced time spent outdoors, and increased smartphone use, particularly among the more highly educated. These same associations have been identified in studies from East and Southeast Asian countries, where the prevalence of myopia is substantially higher. In many East and Southeast Asian countries, where an epidemic of myopia is prevalent, formal education starts at an earlier age, is much more intense, and has fewer opportunities for outdoor activities compared to the Finnish educational system. From a public health perspective, efforts to ease excessive near work and promote outdoor activities, particularly during early school years, may be beneficial in reducing the risk of myopia.
