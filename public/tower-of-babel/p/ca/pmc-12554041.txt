Isolated subcoronal epispadias managed with dorsal Thiersch-Duplay urethroplasty: A case-based insight into technique selection and outcomes
Authors: Fiona Yosephine, Jupiter Sibarani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Epispadias is a rare congenital anomaly with an abnormal dorsal urethral opening. Isolated subcoronal cases are uncommon and present specific surgical challenges. We report a 15-year-old male with isolated subcoronal epispadias presenting with an upward urinary stream since birth. He underwent dorsal Thiersch-Duplay urethroplasty without intraoperative complications. Postoperatively, vital signs remained stable, wound healing was satisfactory, and urinary stream improved. No leakage, infection, or incontinence was noted during follow-up. This case highlights dorsal Thiersch-Duplay urethroplasty as a safe and effective option for isolated subcoronal epispadias, though long-term outcomes require continued evaluation.

Introduction

Epispadias is a rare congenital anomaly characterized by the urethral opening located on the dorsal side of the penis. It is typically accompanied by a deficiency of dorsal penile skin and an excess of preputial skin on the ventral side. Most cases also present with varying degrees of dorsal penile curvature. The severity of epispadias depends on the extent of urethral plate tubularization failure, which can range from the glans to the bladder neck, influencing both the severity of the anomaly and the surgical approach required. Epispadias is commonly associated with bladder exstrophy, forming the bladder exstrophy-epispadias complex (BEEC) in over 90 % of cases. The isolated form of epispadias is much rarer, occurring in approximately 1 in 112,000 live male births. Female epispadias is even less common, with a male-to-female ratio of 2.3:1. Based on the location of the urethral meatus, epispadias is classified into three forms: glandular, penile, and penopubic. The penopubic form is the most common and severe, often associated with urinary incontinence due to sphincter mechanism disruption caused by an extensive urethral defect reaching the bladder neck.1,2 In addition to urinary incontinence, patients with penopubic epispadias may exhibit splaying of the cavernosal bodies in the proximal penile region. This results in a broad, short, and flat penile appearance, which can significantly affect psychosexual and psychosocial development, further impacting the patient's quality of life.3

The diagnosis of epispadias is primarily clinical, though additional investigations may be necessary due to associated comorbidities such as urinary incontinence, reflux, renal anomalies, and altered genital appearance. Diagnostic workup may include imaging techniques like ultrasound, voiding cystourethrogram (VCUG), pelvic MRI, renal scintigraphy, cystoscopy, urodynamic studies, and laboratory tests to evaluate renal function and urinary tract integrity.4

Successful postoperative outcomes in epispadias depend on several factors, including the severity of anatomical abnormalities, the degree of voiding dysfunction, and the surgeon's experience. Long-term follow-up is crucial to address potential psychosexual concerns, monitor renal and bladder function, and detect complications such as fistulae. Multiple surgeries may be required to achieve urinary continence and satisfactory cosmetic results. Lifelong monitoring, along with patient and family education, forms a critical part of comprehensive care for individuals with epispadias.4

In 1869, Professor C. Thiersch reported performing urethral plate tubularization in 1857 and 1858 to create a urethral canal in a child with epispadias. Thiersch's article detailed the flap design and asymmetric lateral incisions, ensuring that the urethral suture lines remained unopposed for optimal healing.5

Case presentation

A 15-year-old male presented with a primary complaint of an upward-directed urinary stream noted since birth. He denied experiencing lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) and reported no other congenital anomalies. Upon clinical examination, the patient was diagnosed with subcoronal epispadias, where only the distal portion of the urethra required surgical release with a glans width of 5.4 cm, stretch penile length of 8 cm, and urethral plate 10 mm. Discussion

A thorough pre-operative physical examination is essential in the management of male epispadias, as it facilitates accurate classification into glanular (GE), penile (PE), or penopubic (PPE) types and helps identify associated anatomical anomalies. Common extragenital abnormalities frequently observed in conjunction with epispadias include vesicoureteral reflux (VUR), bladder neck (BN) anomalies, and pubic diastasis (PD).6

Advancements in surgical techniques and postoperative care have significantly improved outcomes in epispadias repair. The primary goals of penile reconstruction are the restoration of urinary continence and sexual function, alongside achieving a cosmetically acceptable appearance. Favorable functional and aesthetic outcomes are generally attainable in mild-to-moderate cases; however, severe forms continue to present greater variability in results.2,3 A range of surgical approaches has been developed for epispadias correction.7 though complications arising after penile growth may necessitate revision procedures during adulthood. Long-term follow-up remains critical to address both functional and psychological concerns. Among these approaches, the complete penile disassembly technique has demonstrated particularly promising outcomes, with recent studies placing greater emphasis on its long-term efficacy.2,8,9

Surgical repair of isolated male epispadias aims to restore urethral continuity, correct dorsal curvature, and achieve an acceptable cosmetic outcome while preserving urinary and sexual function. Several approaches have been described, including the modified Cantwell–Ransley (MCR) technique, partial or complete penile disassembly, and Thiersch–Duplay–based tubularization of the urethral plate.10, 11, 12 For distal variants with a well-developed urethral plate, tubularization (Thiersch–Duplay or tubularized incised plate [TIP]) can be effective and less invasive. In more proximal cases, techniques such as MCR or partial penile disassembly provide anatomic realignment and adequate ventralization while maintaining neurovascular integrity.10, 11, 12 Reported complication rates for isolated distal repairs are low, with urethrocutaneous fistula and residual curvature being the most common but generally correctable. Our patient underwent dorsal Thiersch–Duplay urethroplasty, which is appropriate for distal subcoronal epispadias with adequate urethral plate length and no significant chordee, offering good cosmetic and functional outcomes.10, 11, 12

One of the major challenges in epispadias repair involves preserving lower urinary tract function while avoiding upper tract deterioration. Urethroplasty combined with bladder neck reconstruction remains technically demanding, with long-term complications such as fistula formation, urethral strictures, and persistent incontinence being relatively common. The length and width of the urethral plate are pivotal factors influencing the choice of reconstruction strategy. In cases where the urethral plate is inadequate, an initial hypospadias meatus may be created, with subsequent reconstruction performed using flap or graft techniques. Staged urethroplasty, particularly those utilizing buccal mucosal grafts and local skin flaps has proven to be both safe and effective, offering low complication rates and satisfactory long-term outcomes.2,13

Hypospadias surgery often uses the Thiersch–Duplay technique, which tubularizes the native urethral plate and reinforces it with a vascularized dartos flap, yielding low complication rates (2.1 %).5,14 The Snodgrass tubularized incised plate (TIP) modification expanded indications to distal and some midshaft cases with good functional and cosmetic outcomes (complications 1–10 %).15 Comparative data show alternatives like the Koff procedure may reduce urethrocutaneous fistula and meatal stenosis.

Although TIP urethroplasty was initially described for hypospadias, it has also been applied to selected cases of distal epispadias with an adequate and supple urethral plate, offering good cosmetic and functional outcomes when anatomical conditions are favorable.

Conclusion

Isolated subcoronal epispadias is a rare congenital anomaly that requires careful surgical planning to achieve both functional and cosmetic success. This case demonstrates that dorsal Thiersch-Duplay urethroplasty is a safe and effective technique, offering satisfactory early outcomes with stable wound healing, restoration of urinary stream, and absence of complications. The use of well-vascularized tissue and minimal dissection likely contributed to favorable healing. Nevertheless, long-term follow-up is essential to evaluate durability, functional results, and psychosocial impact as the patient progresses into adulthood.
