Clinical characteristics and prognostic features of patients with blastoid and pleomorphic variant mantle cell lymphoma: a real-world study in China
Authors: Ying Huang, Xiang Zhang, Yunfei Lv, Juying Wei, Xingnong Ye, Xuewu Zhang, Chunmei Yang, Yanan Zhu, Jinhan Wang, Gaixiang Xu, Min Yang, Haitao Meng, Wanzhuo Xie, Jie Jin, Hongyan Tong, Wenjuan Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Blastoid and pleomorphic mantle cell lymphoma (B/P-MCL) represents a high-risk subtype of hematological malignancy with an unfavorable prognosis. We conducted a retrospective analysis of 76 patients with classic MCL and 81 patients with B/P-MCL between January 2010 and May 2024 to investigate clinical characteristics, outcomes, and prognostic factors. Ki-67 ≥ 30%, high MCL International Prognostic Index (MIPI) scores, and frequent CD5 negativity were more prevalent in B/P-MCL than in classic MCL. In B/P-MCL, TP53 mutations (46.2%) were most common, followed by ATM (42.3%), CCND1 (23.1%), NOTCH1 (23.1%), KMT2D (23.1%), SMARCA4 (15.4%), and BIRC3 (15.4%). Multivariate analysis revealed that Ki−67% ≥90% and bone marrow involvement were independent prognostic factors for overall survival (OS); Ki−67% ≥90% and splenic involvement were independent prognostic factors for progression-free survival (PFS). The blastoid and pleomorphic variants of MCL display distinct morphological characteristics and exhibit certain clinical differences; however, no statistically significant differences in PFS or OS have been demonstrated between these two subtypes. B/P-MCL is characterized by highly aggressive features and a dismal clinical course. Even with the adoption of intensified treatment protocols, the long-term prognosis for these patients remains unsatisfactory. Strengthened efforts to explore novel therapeutic strategies are crucial and may ultimately translate into improved clinical outcomes for this patient population.

Introduction

Mantle cell lymphoma (MCL) is a mature B-cell lymphoma characterized by a t(11;14)(q13;q32), leading to Cyclin D1 overexpression. MCL combines features of aggressive and indolent lymphomas, accounting for 6–8% of non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) [1]. The blastoid and pleomorphic variants, representing 10–15% of all MCL cases, are associated with increased tumor aggressiveness and an unfavorable clinical prognosis [2]. The blastoid variant cells are medium-sized and homogenous, resembling lymphoblasts with round nuclei, minimal cytoplasm or absent cytoplasm, fine chromatin, and inconspicuous nucleoli. Pleomorphic variant cells are medium to large, with oval or irregular nuclei, prominent nucleoli, and pale cytoplasm, resembling diffuse large B-cell lymphoma in some aspects. In addition to morphologic features, blastoid MCL (B-MCL) and pleomorphic MCL (P-MCL) show an atypical immunophenotypic profile, with possible CD23 and CD10 positivity [3], more often CD5 negativity and a high Ki-67 proliferation index [4, 5].

The optimal treatment regimen for blastoid and pleomorphic MCL (B/P-MCL) patients remains controversial. Historically, the majority of patients with the blastoid/ pleomorphic variant were treated similarly to those with non-blastoid/pleomorphic MCL. B/P-MCL has poor chemotherapy outcomes and unfavorable long-term prognosis despite intensive treatment [6]. This study retrospectively analyzed clinical data from 76 classic MCL patients and 81 B/P-MCL patients diagnosed between January 2010 and May 2024, focusing on clinical manifestations, pathological features, prognostic outcomes, and treatment responses.

Materials and methods

Patients

76 patients with classic MCL and 81 with B/P-MCL, admitted to the Department of Hematology at the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University and from January 2010 to May 2024, were retrospectively analyzed. The inclusion criterion for patients with classic MCL is the availability of next-generation sequencing (NGS). Pathological diagnosis followed the 2008 World Health Organization (WHO) classification of lymphoid neoplasms [7], confirmed by two experienced pathologists via histological sections and immunohistochemical staining. Clinical staging was performed according to the Ann Arbor staging system, and performance status was evaluated using the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) criteria. Pre-chemotherapy staging assessments included color Doppler ultrasound, positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) or CT, bone marrow aspiration biopsy, and flow cytometry analysis. Prognostic was evaluated using the Mantle Cell Lymphoma International Prognostic Index (MIPI) scoring system.

Next-generation sequencing(NGS)

We used formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue slices (FFPE) or bone marrow samples to extract genomic DNA at diagnosis. Samples were sequenced on the Illumina platform using hybrid capture technology, achieving an average depth of 16,066x and > 90% coverage. The gene panel included 103 lymphoma-related genes (exons, fusion-associated intronic regions, variable cleavage regions) covering approximately 366,802 sites. Sequencing was performed on 26 B/P-MCL and 76 classic MCL cases.

Follow-up

All patients were followed up via telephone interviews and medical record reviews until January 31, 2025. Overall survival (OS) was measured from diagnosis to death or last follow-up. Progression-free survival (PFS) was calculated from diagnosis to disease progression, recurrence, death, or last follow-up. Duration of response (DOR) was defined as the time from initial response to disease progression or death.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 27.0. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test or Pearson’s chi-square test as appropriate. Continuous variables were evaluated using the Mann-Whitney test to evaluate differences between data sets. OS and PFS were estimated through the Kaplan-Meier method. The association of several variables with OS and PFS was assessed using the COX proportional risk regression model. Variables that lacked statistical significance (P > 0.05) in the univariate analysis were excluded. The remaining variables were retained for further evaluation in the multivariate model. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical characteristics of patients with B/P-MCL and classic MCL

The baseline characteristics of 76 classic MCL patients and 81 B/P-MCL patients are summarized in Table 1. The median age of the B/P-MCL cohort was 67 years (range, 36–87 years). The cohort consisted of 65 males and 16 females, with a male-to-female ratio of 4.1:1. The predominant clinical manifestations included enlargement of lymph nodes, fever, abdominal pain and distension, and pharyngeal discomfort. Compared to classic MCL patients, B/P-MCL patients showed significantly elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) level (P = 0.001), poorer physical condition (P = 0.043), and more frequent B symptoms (P = 0.002). Furthermore, B/P-MCL patients were more likely to have a high-risk MIPI score (P = 0.038). Extranodal involvement at diagnosis occurred in 68/71 cases (95.8%), most commonly in the bone marrow (67.6%), spleen (52.1%), Waldeyer’s ring (36.6%), and digestive tract (26.8%). More B/P-MCL patients exhibited extranodal involvement in ≥ 4 sites (P = 0.028). Consistent with previous studies, B/P-MCL patients exhibited higher Ki-67 expression levels (median 70% vs. 30% in classic MCL, P = 0.001). All patients with intermediate- or high-risk MIPI had a Ki-67 index of ≥ 30%. CD5 negativity was observed in 25.3% of patients, higher than in classic MCL (P = 0.009).

Classic MCL shows high mutation frequencies: ATM (38.2%), KMT2D (26.3%), TP53 (17.1%), CCND1 (17.1%), and NOTCH2, BIRC3, MEF2B (11.8%). In B/P-MCL, the most common mutations are TP53 (46.2%), followed by ATM (42.3%), CCND1 (23.1%), NOTCH1 (23.1%), KMT2D (23.1%), SMARCA4 (15.4%), and BIRC3 (15.4%). Compared to classic MCL, B/P-MCL has a significantly higher prevalence of TP53 mutations (P = 0.004; Table 1).

Survival and prognostic factors in patients with B/P-MCL

The median follow-up after the diagnosis of B/P-MCL was 19.8 months (range, 1.0-126.9 months). The median survival was 33.2 months (range, 1.0-126.9 months), with a 3-year OS rate of 49.3%. The median PFS was 15.0 months (range, 0.3-103.4 months), with a 3-year PFS rate of 32.7% (Fig. 1A, B). Univariate analysis identified factors associated with poorer OS included stage III/IV disease, elevated LDH, intermediate and high-risk MIPI score, WBC ≥ 10 × 109/L, HB ≤ 10 g/dL, platelet count ≤ 100 × 109/L, Ki-67 ≥ 90%, bone marrow involvement, splenic involvement (all P < 0.05; Table 2). Using multivariate analysis, we then showed that patients with Ki-67% ≥90% and bone marrow involvement as independent prognostic factors for OS. Univariate analysis identified factors associated with poorer PFS included poor physical condition, B symptoms, stage III/IV disease, intermediate and high-risk MIPI score, WBC ≥ 10 × 109/L, HB ≤ 10 g/dL, platelet count ≤ 100 × 109/L, Ki-67 ≥ 90%, bone marrow involvement, splenic involvement. In multivariate analysis, Ki-67 index ≥ 90% and splenic involvement were identified as independent prognostic factors for PFS (Table 2).

Clinical features and survival outcomes in patients with B-MCL and P-MCL

Table 3 summarizes the clinical features of patients with B-MCL (n = 57) and P-MCL (n = 24). Compared to P-MCL patients, B-MCL patients exhibited significantly higher LDH expression (P = 0.005), elevated β2-microglobulin levels (P = 0.026), worse ECOG performance status (P = 0.040), a higher prevalence of high-risk MIPI cases (P = 0.001), more frequent bone marrow involvement (P = 0.034), and a greater proportion of Ki-67 ≥ 85% (P = 0.045). Median survival was 32.9 months for B-MCL and 70.3 months for P-MCL. No significant differences were observed in PFS or OS between the groups (P = 0.297, P = 0.417) (Fig. 1C, D). There was no statistically significant difference in somatic mutations between B-MCL and P-MCL groups (Fig. 2).

11 (13.6%) classic MCL patients undergo transformation during the disease process, including 7 cases converted to B-MCL and 4 to P-MCL. Among the 11 patients who transformed from classic MCL, the median time to transformation was 11.2 months (range, 2.8–65.0 months), and 8 (72.3%) of these patients subsequently died. The median follow-up time post-transformation was 7.8 months.

The therapeutic response of patients with B/P-MCL

The most common first-line treatment is immunochemotherapy, with regimens varying among patients (Fig. 3). To avoid confounding, 11 patients with transformed B/P-MCL were excluded. The ORR to initial chemotherapy was 64.3%, including a CR rate of 40.0%. The median time to achieve the best response was 5.2 months (range, 2.9–10.8 months), and the median DOR was 13.0 months (range, 0.7–99.5 months). Maintenance therapy may improve the prognosis of these patients. Patients who achieved CR or PR following induction therapy were included. Using landmark analysis, the fifth week following effective treatment was defined as the time zero point. Only patients alive without disease progression at this time point were included. Patients who subsequently initiated maintenance therapy were assigned to the maintenance group; others constituted the non-maintenance group. Of the patients who responded to treatment, 23 received maintenance therapy (13 with BTK inhibitor, 9 with rituximab, and 1 with bortezomib). Maintenance therapy was associated with longer OS and PFS in this cohort (P = 0.010, P = 0.017, respectively) (Fig. 4A, B). However, no significant differences were observed between the various maintenance regimens. But this is a retrospective cohort study with confounding, which influences the observed survival differences.

Nine patients experienced central nervous system (CNS) involvement at relapse or progression; three patients received R-HCVAD (rituximab, hyperfractionated cyclophosphamide, vincristine, doxorubicin, and dexamethasone), and one patient received RBAC (rituximab, bendamustine, and cytarabine) as first-line therapy. Despite intensive chemotherapy, CNS relapses could not be prevented. Notably, P53 and ATM mutations were significant risk factors for CNS relapse (P = 0.018 and P = 0.025, respectively).

Six patients with relapsed/refractory disease received Chimeric Antigen Receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy, with a median follow-up time of 9.0 months (range, 4.1–72.0 months) post-treatment. Among these, four cases (66.7%) achieved CR. Two patients experienced treatment failure and succumbed within a short time, while one patient died due to non-relapse-related causes. Three patients remain alive, with DOR of 11.7 months, 50.6 months, and 70.2 months, respectively.

Discussion

We report the clinical characteristics and outcomes of 81 patients with blastoid and pleomorphic variants of MCL. To our knowledge, this represents the largest published cohort of patients with these MCL variants treated in China during the rituximab era. However, the classic MCL cohort was selected based on the availability of NGS data, a criterion that may introduce selection bias and compromise the representativeness of this cohort relative to the broader population of classic MCL. Consequently, differences between classic MCL and B/P-MCL are presented descriptively. As previously demonstrated [8–10], survival for these high-risk patients was significantly lower compared to those with non-blastoid/pleomorphic MCL, with a median PFS of only 19.8 months and median OS of 33.2 months. These findings are associated with elevated Ki-67 levels and higher-risk MIPI scores, indicating aggressive tumor characteristics [11]. No diagnostically significant genetic alterations that are unique to B/P-MCL but absent in classic MCL have been identified. Molecular features commonly observed in B/P-MCL can also occur in classic MCL. ATM, TP53, NOTCH1, and NOTCH2 are enriched in B/P-MCL patients [12, 13]. In our study, TP53 mutations were detected in 46.2% of patients, with two cases harboring distinct double mutations. Most mutation occurred in the DNA-binding domain. TP53 inactivation leads to genomic instability, accelerates blastoid transformation, and reduces apoptosis induced by BTK inhibitor (BTKi), which may explain the reduced efficacy of BTKi in B/P-MCL. High-frequency mutations in patients with B/P-MCL also include DNA damage repair (ATM), epigenetic regulation (KMT2D, SMARCA4, CREBBP, NSD2), and cell homeostasis and growth (CCND1, BIRC3, CARD11, TRAF2, NOTCH1/2). However, incomplete data from NGS analysis limits a comprehensive evaluation of the prognostic implications of these mutations.

The common characteristics of blastoid and pleomorphic variants include a high proliferation index and poor prognosis. Currently, the definitions of both subtypes are solely based on morphological features. It remains unclear which specific characteristics might be uniquely associated with a particular subtype. The discrepancies in survival and prognosis between B-MCL and P-MCL remain controversial [14–16]. In our study, as in prior studies [14], no significant differences were observed in gender, age, or disease stage. However, B-MCL exhibited markedly elevated levels of LDH, Serum β2-microglobulin, and ECOG scores, as well as more frequent Bone marrow involvement and significantly higher MIPI scores in comparison to P-MCL. Additionally, Ki-67 ≥ 85% was more prevalent in B-MCL. Despite these biological differences, no disparities were noted in OS, PFS, first-line treatment response, or relapse patterns. Thus, while this study identified certain biological disparities between B-MCL and P-MCL, these differences did not translate into clinically meaningful prognostic implications. The prognostic significance of cytological subtypes in MCL has diminished. Nonetheless, evaluating these subtypes remains crucial for identifying patients at high risk of recurrence and progression in pathological reports and clinical trials.

The standard first-line treatment for B/P-MCL remains to be fully unified. There is a general consensus on the use of rituximab in combination with chemotherapy. For younger or transplant-eligible patients, a regimen consisting of rituximab combined with high-dose cytarabine induction, followed by autologous stem cell transplant (ASCT) consolidation, is commonly adopted. In contrast, elderly or transplant-ineligible patients are often treated with induction regimens such as R-CHOP or RB. However, conventional treatments rarely result in long-term remission, and the duration of remission is typically brief, especially in high-risk patients with high MIPI scores or elevated Ki-67 indices [6]. BTKi have demonstrated significant efficacy in the treatment of relapsed/refractory (R/R) MCL and have become a key focus in the management of B/P-MCL [17–19], particularly for elderly patients who cannot tolerate intensive chemotherapy [20–22]. Substantial changes in treatment practices occurred between 2010 and 2024. In our study, B/P-MCL patients initiated treatment immediately following diagnosis without a watch and wait approach. The majority of B/P-MCL patients were treated with R-CHOP-based regimens. Cytarabine was seldom employed due to the predominance of elderly patients who exhibited poor tolerance to the drug. A small subset of patients received BTKi therapy, and the clinical efficacy requires further evaluation in additional studies.

For patients diagnosed with B/P-MCL who are not suitable candidates for transplant consolidation, maintenance therapy is advised. Consistent with prior studies [15], maintenance therapy was associated with longer OS and PFS in this cohort. Rituximab has been shown to improve survival outcomes in patients with MCL [23, 24]. However, there remains no consensus regarding the optimal maintenance therapy for patients with B/P-MCL, and further prospective clinical trials are required to establish the most effective treatment strategy.

In summary, we described a large cohort of B/P-MCL patients treated in the rituximab era and identified their poor prognosis. The management of B/P-MCL patients poses significant clinical challenges, highlighting an urgent need for further research into novel therapeutic agents and innovative treatment strategies.
