A Novel Mouse Model of Mixed Dementia Using Chronic Cerebral Hypoperfusion Induced by Bilateral Carotid Artery Stenosis
Authors: Jang Hun Kim, Ho-Young Kang, Sung Young Park, Yoo Mi Yang, Sang-Kyung Jo, Dongho Geum, Jong-Hoon Kim, Dong-Hyuk Park
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlzheimer’s disease (AD) and vascular dementia (VaD) have distinct pathognomonic features, but they frequently co-occur as mixed dementia (MD) in elderly adults. This study aimed to develop a novel MD mouse model using bilateral carotid artery stenosis (BCAS) in 5 times familial Alzheimer’s disease (5xFAD) transgenic mice and characterize its behavioral and histological features.MethodsThirteen C57BL/6 and sixteen 5xFAD transgenic mice were prepared. Six C57BL/6 and seven 5xFAD transgenic mice underwent BCAS surgery, and all mice were housed for 3 months. Mice were divided into wild-type (n = 7), VaD (n = 6), AD (n = 9), and MD (n = 7) groups. Neurobehavioral tests, including the Y-maze test (YMT) and passive avoidance test (PAT), along with immunohistochemical analysis of amyloid β plaque (6E10) and myelin basic protein, were performed.ResultsMD mice exhibited similar deficits in YMT as AD and VaD groups but had significantly worse performance in PAT compared to all other groups. Histologically, MD mice showed amyloid-β accumulation in the cortex/hippocampus and axonal degeneration in the corpus callosum.ConclusionThis novel MD model demonstrates key features of both AD and VaD, providing a valuable tool for studying the pathophysiology of mixed dementia and testing potential therapeutic interventions.

INTRODUCTION

Dementia is an increasingly common diagnosis in the older population, and its incidence is expected to increase exponentially in the future.1 Alzheimer’s disease (AD) currently affects 4.5 million people in the United States, while millions of people are affected by vascular dementia (VaD), Lewy body disease, and frontotemporal dementia. According to the Central Dementia Center of the Ministry of Health and Welfare in Korea, as of March 2024, the population aged 65 and older has exceeded 10 million, among which 1,052,977 individuals are reported to have dementia (https://www.nid.or.kr/main/main.aspx?ver=PC). Additionally, data from the Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service show that the number of patients receiving medical care for dementia increased by 22.3% over the past four years, from 551,845 in 2019 to 674,963 in 2023. In addition, the incidence of VaD is also gradually increasing such that the disease is definitively regarded as the second most prevalent type of dementia in subsequent Korean epidemiologic studies.2

AD and VaD are the 2 most common types of dementia in the older population3; however, their pathophysiological backgrounds differ. Specifically, AD is associated with amyloid-β and tau proteins accumulated in the brain cortex or hippocampus (Fig. 1A), while VaD is a broad term encompassing dementia associated with vasculopathy, which manifests in diverse clinical presentations: Over time, chronic vascular pathologies commonly lead to subcortical ischemic changes, including degenerative white matter lesions (Fig. 1B) and multiple small infarctions resulting from terminal vasculopathy.45

Ironically, clinical presentations can be similar between the 2 types, contributing to neurological deficits and neurobehavioral symptoms, such as gait disturbance, apathy, abulia, aspontaneity, agitation, disinhibition, frontal executive dysfunctions, and memory impairment, even though the 2 have distinguishable pathognomonic findings in different affected locations.367 In clinical perspectives, multiple subtypes of dementia frequently coexist as a mixed (combinate) form or mixed dementia (MD), particularly in the older population.3 Among MD types, VaD in combination with AD is the most common form, accounting for approximately 50% of all patients with dementia.89 Community-based autopsy studies involving patients with AD consistently report high proportions of both VaD and MD, diagnosed clinically as AD. A total of 42 of the 94 (44.7%) patients who met the accepted neuropathological criteria for AD also exhibited significant cerebrovascular pathology (VaD).10

Although MD was recognized in 1962 (and has been highlighted recently), the prevailing clinical and research perspectives continue to consider AD and VaD as independent disorders.46 Less attention has been paid to the co-occurrence of AD and VaD for several reasons.111213 Representative MD animal model has not been fully established.41415 In particular, when the 2 subtypes are combined, the disease spectrum or continuum is broad, and their clinical and histological findings are difficult to justify. Furthermore, the 2 subtypes have completely different pathological backgrounds, and the experimental design of drugs treating both diseases is difficult. Historically, conventional drugs effective in animal studies were focused on a single disease.1617 Maybe they are associated with the failure in level III clinical trials.1819

Due to the clinical importance of and emerging changes in MD, a representative animal model that accompanies AD and VaD is necessary for future dementia research; however, little effort has been made to develop MD models.111213 The neuropsychological phenotypes of AD and VaD, along with the clinical/pathological continuum or spectrum, should be demonstrated clearly. We aimed to develop a novel MD mouse model using bilateral carotid artery stenosis (BCAS) vascular surgery in 5 times familial Alzheimer’s disease (5xFAD) transgenic mice. We compared MD mice with independent AD/VaD and sham mice to establish the behavioral and histological spectra.

METHODS

Animals

Animal care and handling were performed in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the National Institutes of Health and reported according to the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines. All surgeries were performed under isoflurane inhalant anesthesia, and efforts were made to minimize suffering within the animal facility during the animal’s light cycle. All mice were housed in a climate-controlled room with a 14-hour/10-hour light/dark cycle and ad libitum access to food and water.

We prepared 13 3-month-old C57BL/6 mice (Orient Bio., Seongnam, Korea), 6 of whom underwent BCAS vascular operations. Postoperatively, all mice were housed for 3 more months as we required sufficient time for prolonged cerebral hypoperfusion, which is associated with VaD. All 13 6-month-old mice were divided into wild-type (WT) and VaD groups. In addition, we prepared 16 3-month-old 5xFAD transgenic mice (Jackson ImmunoResearch Inc., West Grove, PA, USA); among them, seven underwent BCAS vascular surgery. All mice were housed for 3 months postoperatively. The surgical group, which had the 5xFAD transgenic trait (AD) and progressive cerebral ischemia (VaD), was defined as the MD group. They were classified into AD and MD groups. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the mice were classified into 4 groups: WT (n = 7), VaD (n = 6), AD (n = 9), and MD (n = 7).

BCAS operation, postsurgical monitoring, and euthanasia methods

Three-month-old male C5BL/6 mice (VaD mice) or 5xFAD transgenic mice (MD mice) were anesthetized with 5% isoflurane for induction, followed by maintenance under 1.5% isoflurane in a mixture of 70% N2O and 30% O2 to induce prolonged cerebral hypoperfusion and progressive ischemic attack.20 The total duration of surgery was approximately 30 minutes, with a maximum of 1 hour in some cases. Sufficient anesthesia depth was ensured throughout the procedure to manage pain and prevent any discomfort.

As illustrated in Fig. 3, a midline incision was made to expose the common carotid arteries (CCAs). Two 4–0 silk sutures were placed under the proximal and distal portions of the left CCA and used to gently lift the artery for placement of the micro-coil (0.18-mm diameter; Waken B Technology Company Limited, Kyoto, Japan) by rotating it around the artery just below the carotid bifurcation. Subsequently, a second micro-coil was placed on the right CCA. After securing both micro-coils, the incision was closed, and the animals were housed for 3 months for observation and experimental analysis.2122

Schematic illustration of BCAS operation.

BCAS = bilateral carotid artery stenosis, CCA = common carotid artery.

Post-operatively, mice were placed in a warmed recovery cage immediately after the surgery. They were monitored until fully conscious, mobile, and showing interest in food and water. Once fully recovered, the animals were returned to their home cages in the animal facility. For 2 weeks following surgery, the mice were closely monitored daily for signs of sickness or discomfort, including infection at the incision site, lethargy, disinterest in food and water, weight loss, and seizures. Particular attention was paid to ensure proper pain management, and any abnormal signs were addressed promptly.

Neurobehavioral tests

Y-maze test (YMT)

Spatial working memory and cognition were assessed using spontaneous alteration behaviors in YMT on postoperative day 90.2324 The test was conducted in the same manner as previously described.25 The maze consisted of three 120° white plastic arms (40 cm long, 10 cm high, and 88.5 cm wide). The floor was cleaned using 10% ethanol to eliminate olfactory cues. Each mouse was positioned at the end of the start arm and allowed to move freely through the maze during an 8 minutes session. The movements of the mice were recorded using a video camera, and the number of arms entered was automatically counted. An arm entry was considered complete when the hind paws of the mice were within the arm. The percentage of alterations was calculated as the ratio of actual alterations (entries into all three arms on consecutive occasions) to total alterations as follows: (Total Number of Arm Entries − 2) × 100.

Passive avoidance test (PAT)

PAT was performed 1 week after YMT to evaluate intermediate memory and learning ability. Additionally, PAT was conducted in the same manner as previously described (Table 1).26 The test typically began by placing the mouse in a lit chamber for 10 seconds and then opening the door between chambers. The mice naturally entered the dark chamber because they were highly exploratory and preferred the dark chamber to the lit chamber. A single electric shock (0.8 mA, 2 seconds) was delivered through the grid floor with the door closed. The mice remained in the dark chamber for an additional 10 seconds, a period designed to strengthen the association between the properties of the chamber and the foot shock. The mice were then returned to their cages. After 24 hours, the mice were placed in a lit chamber with an open door between the chambers. The time taken by the mice to enter the dark chamber was also recorded. Normal mice were slow to enter the dark chamber and, often, would not enter at all, up to a 300 seconds cutoff latency, presumably as they remembered that a shock had been delivered in the dark chamber the previous day.

Tissue preparation and immunohistochemistry (IHC)

After completing the behavioral test, the mice were anesthetized with intraperitoneal injections of ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg), perfused transcardially with 0.1 mol/L phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and following 4% paraformaldehyde. The brain was extracted without any damage and fixed in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution overnight at 4°C, and they were placed in a 30% sucrose solution for 3 days for cryoprotection. Cryoprotected brains were embedded using an optimal cutting temperature compound and frozen in dry ice-cooled isopentane. Frozen brains were sliced into 20-µm-thick coronal sections using a cryostat vibratome (CM3050S; Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). Sliced sections were mounted with a small drop of VECTASHIELD® Antifade Mounting Media (Vector Laboratories, Inc., Newark, CA, USA) following the slide-mounted method.27 Finally, these sections were stored at −80°C until further processing.

For detecting amyloid plaque, amyloid β Recombinant monoclonal antibodies (6E10; BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) were used at dilutions of 1:500. Sections were washed and incubated with biotinylated secondary antibody and streptavidin/horseradish peroxidase reagent (Dako LSAB® + HRP kit; Agilent Technologies Australia, Mulgrave, Australia), followed by chromogen for color development (Dako DAB + Chromogen kit; Agilent Technologies Australia). Slides were counterstained in Harris’s Hematoxylin (Australian Biostain, Traralgon, Australia) before mounting in distyrene-plasticizer-xylene media (DPX new; Merck Millipore, Bayswater, Australia). Before each run, a sample whereby the primary antibody was removed served as a negative control. The plaque area was measured by manually outlining the total plaque and, subsequently, the core and measuring the area in Image J (https://imagej.nih.gov). Only plaques > 20 µm in diameter were analyzed to avoid the possibility of noise/debris being included.28

Monoclonal anti-myelin basic protein (anti-MBP; 1:650; Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) was used to detect demyelination.29 The tissue sections were treated with 2 N HCl for 30 minutes at 37°C, then neutralization with immersion in 0.1 mol/L of the borate buffer (pH 8.5) for 10 minutes at room temperature. Nonspecific binding was blocked by incubating the sections with 10% horse serum in PBS. The sections were incubated overnight with primary antibodies at 4°C.173031 The following secondary antibodies were used: Alexa Fluor 488 anti-mouse immunoglobulin G (IgG) (1:400; Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA), Alexa Fluor 594 anti-goat IgG (1:800; Invitrogen), Alexa Fluor 488 anti-rabbit IgG (1:400; Invitrogen), and Rhodamine Red X-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (1:100; Jackson ImmunoResearch Inc., West Grove, PA, USA). Next, sections were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (Thermo Fisher Scientic, Waltham, MA, USA), and staining was visualized using a uorescence microscope (Axio Scan.Z1; Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). Immunofluorescent images were acquired using a Zeiss LSM 700 confocal laser microscope (Zeiss, Carlsbad, CA, USA).

Humane endpoints during the study

We used moribund conditions as humane endpoints during the study. The moribund condition was defined as irreversible conditions leading inevitably to death. Signs of moribundity include: 1) lack of responsiveness to manual stimulation, 2) immobility, and/or 3) an inability to eat or drink. In such conditions, animals were euthanized by carbon dioxide asphyxiation. The study was designed safely, and the level of mortality was low enough.

It was not expected as having a higher risk of death (or severe suffering) associated with an experiment and evaluation of the current study design. The surgical procedure were performed by the expert neurosurgeon with cautions and efforts for minimizing the mortality and suffering of mice. Only one mouse was euthanized due to surgical complication (1/14, 7.14% of mortality).

Quantification and statistics

For the quantifications of IHC results, 6 sections were obtained every 280 µm, beginning with a section 1.2 mm rostral to the bregma. In amyloid plaque quantification, the area of amyloid plaques was calculated in four images (cortex and hippocampus, bilatearlly) in every section.28 Regarding myelin degeneration, MBP-positive density was evaluated in images captured from bilateral corpus callosum using MetaMorph imaging software (version 7.8.1; Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA) and averaged two density values from each animal. Each area was standardized to 800 × 800 µm2 per regions of interest (ROIs) in the cerebral cortex/hippocampus and 150 × 150 µm2 in the corpus callosum. Totally 24 (4 ROIs × 6 sections, 6E10) and 12 (2 ROIs × 6 sections, MBP) data in a mouse were averaged and used for analysis, respectively.

All data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean. Parametric data were analyzed using the one-way analysis of variance, and non-parametric data were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test on ranks, followed by the Mann–Whitney U test. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Ethics statement

All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Korea University (KOREA-2021-0088). Animal care and handling were performed in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the National Institutes of Health and reported according to the ARRIVE guidelines.

RESULTS

Neurobehavioral functions of MD mice

Spatial working memory and cognition were assessed using YMT, while intermediate memory and learning abilities were evaluated using PAT. In YMT, MD mice demonstrated similar physical activity (number of arms entered) as other types of dementia and presented the lowest alteration percentages, which differed significantly from those of WT mice (WT vs. MD: 54.84 ± 3.68 vs. 37.98 ± 3.62, P = 0.001). However, VaD and AD mice also had few alterations, which could be interpreted as spatial memory and cognitive deficits not characteristic of MD mice (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Table 1). Regarding the PAT, MD mice exhibited a significant reduction in escape latency (49.80 ± 49.09) relative to WT (244.56 ± 45.42, P = 0.003), VaD (184.2 ± 60.72, P = 0.008), and AD (189.96 ± 96.79, P = 0.043) mice (Fig. 4B, Supplementary Table 1). The marked decrease in escape latency in MD mice suggests severe impairments in intermediate memory and learning ability. Given that the PAT involves both memory retention and the avoidance of aversive stimuli, the observed deficits in MD mice likely reflect an intrinsic lack of the capacity to form and retain spatial memory, as well as reduced spatial awareness. These findings further imply that the MD group exhibits more profound cognitive deficits compared to VaD and AD mice, particularly in terms of their inability to integrate learned experiences with spatial perception, which are key components in navigating and avoiding harmful stimuli.

In IHC analyses, MD mice were successfully established, and pathognomonic immunohistochemical findings of VaD and AD were presented simultaneously. DISCUSSION

The current study aimed to develop a novel MD animal model by conducting BCAS surgery in 5xFAD transgenic mice. We successfully developed a novel animal model with pathognomonic findings of AD and VaD, i.e., amyloid-β protein accumulation in the cerebral cortex/hippocampus and demyelination of the corpus callosum, respectively. Neurobehavioral spectra of MD mice also demonstrated decreased spatial/short-term memory and cognition, similar to mice with other types of dementia, in YMT, and characteristic deficits in intermediate-term memory and learning ability in PAT.

The number of patients with dementia is gradually increasing,1 and experiments to develop new drugs for dementia are actively being conducted. Experimentally, several drugs have been verified to be effective and safe for each type of dementia; however, they have often failed in level III clinical trials.1819 The failure may be attributed to the differences between experimental animals and humans. From a clinical perspective, an accurate subtype diagnosis of dementia is difficult, particularly in the early and late stages.632 Physicians usually diagnose patients based on clinical symptoms and radiological findings. Clinical symptoms of dementia are occasionally subjective, varying from mild cognitive impairment to severe neurological disorders.33 In addition, in the early or late stage of dementia, typical radiological findings for the subtype (cortical and hippocampal atrophy, white matter signal changes, narrowing of substantia nigra, etc.) are usually difficult to be distinguished.3435

We developed a novel animal model of MD to narrow the gap between experimental animals and humans. As dementia is classified as a neurodegenerative disease and is significantly associated with age, most outpatients are old. In the aged population, at least 2 types of dementia frequently coexist in the form of MD. Furthermore, completely understanding the degree of each dementia is challenging.3 In retrospective studies, VaD combined with AD was the most common form of MD and suspected to account for half of the affected population,89 which is significantly larger than we anticipated. Therefore, we believe that developing a novel MD model and establishing its neurobehavioral and histological characteristics are necessary for future dementia research. We hypothesized that popular Alzheimer’s transgenic animal models undergoing BCAS surgery would have both AD and VaD traits that can be regarded as a distinct spectrum or continuum of the disease.46

Little effort has been made to combinate the two types of dementia in animal experiments.111213 Previous animal studies have been aimed at elucidating that cerebral hypoperfusion may lead to Alzheimer-related findings36 or that amyloid-β or tau protein could cause myelin degeneration (and vice versa).12 Several mouse models of mixed pathology have been developed, contributing significantly to our understanding of the interplay between AD and vascular cognitive impairment, as well as to therapeutic approaches.3738 For example, one study utilized 5xFAD mice to model AD pathology and induced stroke using an hyperhomocysteinemia diet, while another employed C21 administration to induce stroke-related VaD. These models highlight important advances in mixed dementia research. However, it is worth noting that these stroke models typically demonstrate stepwise cognitive decline due to acute ischemic events, which differ from the chronic global brain ischemia observed in small vessel disease, white matter leukoaraiosis, and axonal damage, as increasingly recognized in recent studies. Furthermore, in clinical practice, stroke-induced cognitive decline is often attributed to stroke sequelae rather than classified as vascular dementia.

In contrast, our model employs surgical intervention to induce chronic global brain ischemia, providing a unique platform to study the synergistic effects of AD pathology and sustained ischemic conditions. This distinction represents a significant strength of our approach. We successfully developed a new MD animal model because the newly developed mice had the pathognomonic histological findings of both amyloid-β protein and demyelination (Figs. 5 and 6). We selected a novel and familiar BCAS surgery to add poor vascular perfusion to the entire brain hemisphere. In the present study, the surgical mortality was low, with only one mouse dying (1/15, 6.7%); current MD animals have the advantage of being easily accessible. Regarding neurobehavioral functions, we predicted MD mice would have poor YMT and PAT results because of the cerebral cortex/hippocampus pathologies and the white matter (corpus callosum). The YMT results in MD mice were similar to those in AD and VaD mice. As YMT helps evaluate the spatial and short-term memory and cognition of mice, spatial memory deficit or cognitive impairment is not the characteristic spectrum of MD mice. However, the MD mice demonstrated significantly poor PAT results. As PAT measures intermediate-term memory and learning ability, intermediate-term memory deficits and impairment of learning ability may be distinct traits in MD mice. Considering the traits, patients with MD may experience forgetfulness and impaired intellectual and adaptive functions.

Symptoms of dementia can vary from mild neurobehavioral deficits, such as memory impairment, to severe neuronal dysfunction, altered mentality, and motor difficulties.367 They can be associated with the location of the affected area and the duration/severity of the disease pathology. Learning is the most important ability among neurobehavioral characteristics, implying an intact high cortical function. One should recognize all information (including circumstances, emotions, spatial data, and reactions), which should be fully memorized to learn new information. Therefore, a decline in learning ability is the best predictor of dementia type and severity.39 MD mice experience more neuronal degeneration than other dementia models, and a recognizable decrease in PAT results can be identified.

Interestingly, we can observe much poorer results in MD mice than AD mice, although MD mice have the same degree (age) of amyloid beta cumulation as AD mice. They have histologically higher plaque area quantification and more severe PAT results behaviorally. It means that BCAS surgery induced the faster aging process of AD presentation; therefore, our novel MD model represents the most severe subtype of dementia. However, this study design does not fully support this hypothesis and further study enhancing the various ages of AD model comparison is needed.

This study is limited by the use of rodent models, where advanced imaging techniques like magnetic resonance imaging are not feasible due to the small size of the animals. Additionally, axonal degeneration observed in the corpus callosum may not be entirely specific to vascular dementia, as similar changes are also seen in other neurodegenerative conditions, such as Parkinsonism, which may influence the interpretation of the results. Furthermore, although the histological confirmation of both dementia traits has been definitive, behavioral characteristics of MD mice have not been fully established. We used 6-month-old 5xFAD transgenic mice with a 3-month progression of bilateral carotid vasculopathy. MD mice may present with various spectra according to the age, degree, disease duration, and combination of each type of dementia. Additionally, we only observed axonal degeneration in the corpus callosum, which bridges the bilateral hemispheres, and a broad white matter lesion of the corona radiata or internal capsule was not included. As leukoaraiosis of the white matter is regarded as a pathognomonic finding of VaD in the human brain, efforts to evaluate the corpus callosum and the white matter are necessary in the current experiment with mice.

In this study, we successfully developed a novel MD animal model by combining elements of both VaD and AD. This model not only replicates key pathological features but also demonstrates significant behavioral deficits that mimic human mixed dementia conditions. The observed reduction in intermediate memory and learning ability, as demonstrated by the PAT results, provides crucial insight into the cognitive impairments associated with mixed dementia. Specifically, MD mice exhibited pronounced deficits in escape latency, indicating a severe compromise in their ability to process and retain information, a core characteristic of intermediate-term memory dysfunction.

The broader implications of this animal model are substantial. It serves as a valuable tool for exploring the complex interactions between vascular and neurodegenerative elements in dementia. As mixed dementia represents a significant portion of dementia cases in aging populations, this model is essential for understanding the underlying mechanisms and evaluating the efficacy of therapeutic interventions. By replicating both the vascular and neurodegenerative components of dementia, this model provides a comprehensive platform for preclinical testing of new treatments aimed at ameliorating cognitive decline across multiple dementia subtypes.
