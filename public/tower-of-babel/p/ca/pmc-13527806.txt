Factors Influencing the Choice of Surgery as a Future Career among Medical Students: A Cross-Sectional Multi-Institutional Study in Edo State, Nigeria
Authors: Esteem Tagar, James Kpolugbo, Afokeoghene Gift Tagar, William Akerele, Lucky Aroboinosen Ehiagwina, Clement Osime, Ifeanyichukwu Michael Chukwu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:There are concerns in some countries regarding a decline in medical students' interest in pursuing surgery as a career. Our study aimed to assess interest in a surgical career and the factors influencing medical students' career choices.Methodology:We conducted a cross-sectional study involving medical students in the three clinical years at three medical schools in Edo State, southern Nigeria. Participants completed an online questionnaire on their sociodemographic characteristics, interest in surgery as a career, and factors influencing their career choices. Statistical analyses included chi-square tests and binary logistic regression. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.Results:A total of 828 out of 1091 eligible students participated in the study, with a response rate of 75.90%. There were 390 (47.10%) males and 438 (52.90%) females, with a mean age of 23.25 ± 3.02 years. Among the participants, 413 (49.90%) were in year 4, 208 (25.10%) in year 5, and 207 (25.00%) in year 6. Overall, 47.50% of students expressed interest in pursuing a surgical career, with general surgery (15.50%) and cardiothoracic and vascular surgery (15.30%) being popular choices. Reasons for considering surgery included the application of practical skills (94.70%) and intellectual stimulation (93.10%). Concerns about surgeons' busy lifestyle (78.20%) and lengthy training duration (63.00%) were major deterrents. Males showed a higher inclination towards surgery than females (54.70% vs. 45.30%).Conclusion:Many medical students are interested in pursuing a career in surgery, with factors like hands-on work and academic interest influencing their choices. Addressing concerns about surgeons' lifestyle and training duration could help attract more students to the field. Tailored mentorship programmes, enhanced training opportunities, and improved compensation for surgeons could potentially strengthen the surgical workforce in Nigeria. Limitations of the study include its cross-sectional design and focus on medical students in Edo State.

Introduction

The journey of medical students begins in medical schools and progresses to specialisation and subspecialisation. Choosing an area of specialisation after obtaining an undergraduate degree is a critical milestone that shapes their professional identity and career trajectory. [1] Exposure to various areas of the profession during the early stages of medical education influences students' preferences for specific specialties. [2,3] The decision-making process regarding future career paths can be challenging for many medical students due to the increasing number of available specialties, leading to potential changes in career preference from entrance into medical school to graduation. [4] Clinical specialties tend to attract students more, with those in the clinical phase finding it easier to decide on a future specialty compared to their preclinical counterparts. [5]

Understanding the specialty preferences of medical students and young medical graduates is vital for predicting the future distribution of specialists in the healthcare workforce. [6] This knowledge is essential for effective workforce planning, healthcare policy formulation, and overall healthcare system improvement, particularly in low-resource countries like Nigeria [1] where the physician-to-patient ratio is far below the World Health Organisation's recommended standard. [7] Nigeria produces a lower number of surgeons annually compared to developed countries [8], resulting in a specialist surgical workforce density and surgical volume that fall well below international recommendations. [9]

Embarking on a career in surgery is often perceived as a prestigious and intellectually stimulating path in the medical field. Surgery is esteemed for its technical intricacies, collaborative aspects, and the quality of care it provides, resulting in surgeons being highly respected and well compensated. [10] Nonetheless, there is a growing concern about the declining interest of medical students in pursuing a career in surgery. [11] Studies from South Africa, [12] Australia, [13] Ireland, [14] USA, [15] and Canada. [16] have all shown a decreasing popularity of general surgery and other surgical specialties. If this trend is not addressed, it could lead to a severe shortage in the surgical workforce, especially as the population ages. [16]

Factors contributing to the declining interest in surgery among medical students include negative perceptions of surgeon personality and lifestyle, a lack of mentorship, and a hostile working environment. The lengthy duration of surgical training programmes can also discourage students seeking a faster career path. [17] Concerns about work-life balance, demanding work hours, and time constraints influence students to opt for less demanding specialties. [18] Women, in particular, encounter additional barriers, with many deciding against pursuing specialisation in surgery even before entering medical school due to societal expectations regarding domestic responsibilities. [19]

Despite a global decline in medical students' interest in surgical specialties, a shortage of surgical trainees, and an increasing demand for more surgeons in the workforce, there is a paucity of research assessing the factors that influence Nigerian medical students' choice to pursue a career in surgery. This study sought to investigate medical students' perspectives on surgery as a potential career option and the factors that impact their decision to pursue a surgical career.

Methodology

Study Design and Setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted among undergraduate medical students at the Colleges of Medicine, University of Benin (UNIBEN) in Benin City, Edo South; Ambrose Alli University (AAU) in Ekpoma, Edo Central; and Edo State University, Iyamho (ESUI) in Iyamho, Edo North, from July to December 2024. These three universities are situated in the three senatorial zones of the state. They were selected for their diverse urban and semi-urban/rural settings, ethnoreligious diversity, and proximity to the researchers. The Bachelor of Medicine, Bachelor of Surgery (MBBS) degree programme at these universities spans six years of full-time academic and clinical training. The first three years focus on basic medical sciences, while the final three years emphasise clinical teaching in various medical and surgical disciplines. Surgery is mainly taught in years four to six.

Sample size calculation

The study's sample size was determined using the Qualtrics sample size calculator. Based on a 95% confidence interval, 2% margin of error, and a population size of 1091, the minimum required sample size was found to be 751 participants.

Population and Sampling

The study population included all medical students in the three clinical years (years four, five and six), who were currently enrolled in the UNIBEN, AAU, and ESUI undergraduate MBBS programme. A total population sampling technique was used to recruit consenting participants. Non-responders were excluded from the study. Participants with incomplete or missing responses were also excluded. Multiple responses were eliminated by comparing demographic factors such as age, gender, marital status, medical school, and level of study.

Study Instrument

The survey used a structured online questionnaire that was developed from previous research. [20-23] The survey items' content accuracy and internal validity were finalised with input from all researchers. To reduce bias, minimise flaws, and improve understanding, the questionnaire was pretested on 25 medical students in the three clinical years without any format changes. The data from the pretesting responses were not included in the final analysis. The survey was conducted using an online platform (Google Forms). Each author was responsible for sharing the survey with their respective medical students' class WhatsApp groups through the class representatives. Students were encouraged to distribute the survey within their class groups to ensure equal participation opportunities for all students. The first page of the online questionnaire included a summary of the study's goal and an informed consent form. The questionnaire focused on two areas. The first part included questions on the sociodemographic characteristics of the students, such as age, gender, marital status, year of study, surgical postings, and parents' profession. The second part assessed the popularity of surgery as a career choice and included Likert items with statements to gauge the participants' opinions on factors that encourage or discourage medical students from pursuing a career in surgery. These factors included social standing/perception of surgeons, remuneration, low mortality rates, longer duration of surgical training, longer working hours, male dominance in the field, attitudes of senior surgeons, lack of competent surgical training programmes, mentor/mentee influence, and family preference or influence. The participants rated the influence of these factors on their career choice of surgery. A factor was considered influential if it was rated 3 or above on a 5-point Likert scale (1-No influence, 2-Slight influence, 3-Moderate influence, 4-Strong influence, and 5-Very strong influence). [24] Responses of 1 or 2 were considered not influential. The Cronbach's alpha (α) for the Likert scales assessing reasons for wanting and not wanting to specialise in surgery in the questionnaire was 0.61 and 0.65, respectively.

Data Analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 25 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA) statistical software. The results were presented as frequencies, means ± standard deviations, and percentages as appropriate. Categorical variables were analysed using Pearson's chi-square test. The internal consistency of the Likert scales in the questionnaire was determined using Cronbach's alpha. Binary logistic regression was carried out, adjusting for age, gender, marital status, medical school, level of study, and having a surgeon as a first-degree relation to identify independent predictors of interest in pursuing a career in surgery. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Ethics Approval and Consent to Participate

This study was approved by the Health Research Ethics Committee of the Ambrose Alli University, Ekpoma, Nigeria (NHREC/12/06/2013; with reference number 69/24). The study protocol was conducted in accordance with the principles described in the Declaration of Helsinki. Participation was voluntary and anonymous. All participants were fully informed and consented to their participation in the study. The collaborators adhered to the ethical requirements of their institution.

Results

The average age of participants was 23.25 ± 3.02 years, with the majority (95.90%) being single. Females constituted 52.90% of the cohort. A significant number of students were in their 4th year of study. A small percentage had a parent in the medical field, and 136 (16.40%) had a close relative who was a surgeon (Table 1).

Specialty of choice for postgraduate specialisation

Figure 2 displays the preferred surgical subspecialties among the students. General surgery was the top choice at 15.50%, followed closely by cardiothoracic and vascular surgery at 15.30%. Other popular choices included orthopaedics and traumatology (13.20%) and neurosurgery (12.20%). Plastic and reconstructive surgery, urology, and otorhinolaryngology were less preferred at 1.30% each, with 34.60% of students still undecided. Gender was a significant factor in the selection of surgical specialties (p < 0.001), with males dominating most subspecialties except for paediatric surgery and otorhinolaryngology. The main reasons given by the students who aspire to be surgeons for choosing the specialty were the practical application of skills (94.70%), intellectual stimulation (93.10%), immediate improvement in the patients' condition after surgical intervention (77.10%), and salary returns (59.30%). Others included accessible role models and mentors (57.50%), social status and prestige (51.90%), lifestyle (49.60%), family preference or influence (23.10%), and having a surgeon as a relation (15.50%). When comparing genders, it was observed that male medical students were more likely to choose the surgical specialty due to the perceived social status and prestige of surgeons (p = 0.004). Practical application of skills/hands-on work (p = 0.052) and accessible role models/mentors (p = 0.094) were noted to be approaching significance (Table 4).

Discussion

Understanding the specialty preferences of medical students is crucial for predicting the future healthcare workforce in a country. Surgery was the top choice for postgraduate medical training among the students in our study, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies in Nigeria. [25-27] However, studies from South Africa, [12] Australia, [13] Ireland, [14] USA, [15], and Canada [16] show varying levels of interest. A plausible explanation for the difference in our results may be the improved profile of the discipline of surgery in Nigeria over time. This could also be attributed to the presence of role models and the level of exposure students receive during surgical rotations in different institutions and countries.

The gender and level of study of the participants were identified as significant demographic factors that shape their career aspirations, which aligns with the findings of a previous study conducted in Saudi Arabia. [20] Our study showed that a greater percentage of male students were more inclined towards pursuing a career in surgery (p = 0.002), which is consistent with the literature highlighting gender disparities in career preferences. [26,28] This trend may be due to women's concerns about balancing work and family responsibilities to avoid potential conflicts between their professional and personal lives, limited flexibility in career structures, and a shortage of female role models in the surgical field. Another possible reason, as suggested in a previous study, is the presence of gender discrimination and bias in the surgical specialty. [20] Conversely, a study in Sudan revealed a different pattern, underscoring the variability of specialty choices among medical students across various countries. [29]

The study found that participants' interest was significantly influenced by their year of study (p = 0.001). Compared to those in their fourth year, students in their fifth and sixth years were less inclined to pursue surgery. This could be due to the limited exposure of fourth-year students to other specialties, which might influence their career decisions at that stage. It could also be that fourth-year students still have a more recent memory of anatomy dissections from their preclinical classes. Students in the later years of study are likely to have broader exposure to various medical specialties, and their evolving training experiences could impact their future career choices. Studies from different countries have equally noted a gradual decline in medical students' interest in pursuing surgical careers as they advance through medical school. [17,21,30] However, Grasreiner et al. reported no significant change in interest with academic year advancement in their study. [31] Notably, having a parent in the medical field or a close relative who is a surgeon did not influence their interest in surgery in this study, which contrasts with the findings of a study among pre-registration interns in Ibadan, Nigeria. [32]

The level of interest in the surgical specialties of general surgery, cardiothoracic and vascular surgery, orthopaedics and traumatology, and neurosurgery in this study is comparable to that observed in Sudan, where they are the most popular subspecialties. [29] Similar to the results in the Sudanese study, this study also shows a lack of interest in paediatric surgery, plastic and reconstructive surgery, and urology compared to the strong preference for general surgery and cardiothoracic and vascular surgery. General surgery was chosen by 23.70% of respondents who selected a surgical subspecialty, contrasting with the global trend of declining interest in general surgery as a career choice. [12-16,32] The increased interest in general surgery in this study may be attributed to the significant presence of general surgery in the curriculum and the

availability of general surgeons in the clinical faculty of the universities who also serve as mentors and role models for students. The geographic location of the institutions may also influence the students' decision to embrace general surgery as their prospective career, as seen in studies on Saudi medical students in Abha and Riyadh, where general surgery was the top choice for their career aspirations. [33,34] Despite a decreasing gender gap in surgery subspecialties worldwide, [28] this study still shows a significant gender disparity, with interest by males dominating most subspecialties except for paediatric surgery and otorhinolaryngology.

When the students who chose to specialise in surgery were asked the reason for their decision, they identified practical application of skills (94.70%), intellectual stimulation (93.10%), and immediate improvement in the patients' condition after surgical intervention (77.10%) as the top reasons. These sentiments were also shared by medical students in other studies, suggesting that personal fulfilment from hands-on work and immediate results of interventions are key motivators for pursuing a career in surgery. [20-23] This highlights the appeal of surgery as a dynamic specialty that offers direct patient care and appreciable outcomes. It was observed in the study that male medical students were significantly more likely to choose surgery as a career because of social status and prestige (p = 0.004).

Several factors have been identified in the literature that could deter medical students from selecting surgery as their specialty. These factors include the demanding lifestyles associated with surgical careers, [35] rigorous on-call schedules, an overwhelming workload in surgical specialties, [18] and the extended duration of residency training in surgery. [23,36] In our study, both male and female students identified the lifestyle of surgeons/working hours (78.20%) and the length of training (63.00%) as the most common barriers to pursuing a career in surgery. These findings are consistent with studies conducted within Africa and other parts of the world. [14,22,23] Many developing countries, such as Nigeria, are grappling with shortages of medical professionals, especially at the skilled level. These unfilled positions further strain an already overburdened medical community, leading to longer working hours, heightened stress levels, and higher rates of burnout among surgeons.

There is a global disparity between the number of male and female surgeons, possibly due to the presence of deterrents and/or the lack of motivation for women to enter the surgical profession. [19] Our study found that a significant proportion of female students (30.80%) cited gender discrimination as a barrier to pursuing a surgical career (p < 0.001), giving credence to the perception that surgery may not be inclusive for women. A study by Cochran et al. demonstrated that women have high self-efficacy in surgery but are discouraged by anticipated gender bias. [37] This highlights the gender-related obstacles women encounter in pursuing a career in surgery and the importance of addressing gender disparities in the surgical profession. [23]

Limitations of the study

While this study provided valuable insights, it is important to acknowledge some limitations. Firstly, relying on self-reported data could result in response bias, as participants may either exaggerate or downplay their motivators and deterrents. The cross-sectional design used in this study may also limit the ability to establish causality between the identified factors and students' decision to pursue a career in surgery. Furthermore, focusing solely on medical students in Edo State may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other regions in the country with diverse religious, cultural backgrounds and educational structures. Lastly, the structured questionnaire format may limit the depth of responses, potentially overlooking nuanced factors influencing career decisions. Future studies utilizing longitudinal designs and qualitative approaches may offer a more comprehensive understanding of the factors that influence medical students' career aspirations in surgery.

Conclusion

The findings of our study reflect a strong interest among undergraduate medical students in pursuing a career in surgery, with a higher level of intention seen among male students. We observed that students' career decisions may be influenced by various factors, and their specialty preferences may change as they progress through medical school. Factors that resonated with medical students who chose a surgical career included the practical application of skills, academic interest, and the immediate impact on patients' health following surgical interventions. However, concerns about the lifestyle of surgeons, such as attitudes, working hours and training duration, deterred some students from choosing a surgical career. Gender bias also played a significant role in dissuading some female medical students from pursuing a career in surgery. By identifying both the motivators and obstacles that shape career aspirations, this study contributes to a better understanding of the dynamics within surgical specialties. Addressing the identified barriers and providing opportunities for exposure through tailored mentorship programmes and surgical career workshops holds promise in building a diverse and resilient workforce capable of meeting changing healthcare needs. Furthermore, to encourage greater participation of women in surgery, it is essential to address and eliminate gender bias by ensuring equal opportunities for females and promoting more female role models.
