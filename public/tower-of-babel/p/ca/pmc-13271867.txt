Burn Wound Infections With Staphylococcus aureus : Clinical Characteristics and Risk Factors for Methicillin‐Resistant Strains
Authors: Xiaolan Hong, Shaobo Zhou, Qinghua Cai, Yuanyuan Cai, Zhaohong Chen, Wanting Zhao, Yongjian Ye, Xubo Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study aimed to systematically delineate the clinical characteristics and identify the key risk factors associated with methicillin‐resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) infections in burn patients, thereby informing targeted preventive measures and therapeutic strategies. This retrospective study included 270 burn patients with Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus ) infections at a Chinese centre (2019–2022), comprising 127 MRSA and 143 methicillin‐susceptible S. aureus (MSSA) cases. Clinical data were analysed to assess infection profiles, resistance patterns and MRSA risk factors. Amongst the infections, 68.1% (184/270) were caused by multi‐drug resistant S. aureus , specifically 47.0% (127/270) by MRSA and 21.1% (57/270) by MSSA. The predominant resistance pattern (penicillin, oxacillin, gentamicin, clindamycin, erythromycin, ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, tetracycline) accounted for 23.9% (44/184) of multidrug‐resistant cases. The overall MRSA detection rate was 47.0% (127/270). Univariate analysis identified multiple factors significantly associated with MRSA infection (p < 0.05). Multivariate analysis identified the use of ≥ 3 types of antibiotics as an independent risk factor for MRSA infection in burn wounds. The detection rate of multi‐drug resistant S. aureus (including MRSA) infections in burn wounds is relatively high. A number of variables are the influencing factors for MRSA infections. Medical personnel should adopt infection control measures to block the transmission of multi‐drug resistant bacteria (including MRSA).Keywords: burn wound infection, infection control, methicillin‐resistant S. aureus , multidrug‐resistant, risk factors

Introduction

As the first line of defence, the skin prevents microbial entry. However, burn injuries trigger a series of pathophysiological changes, including sustained endocrine dysfunction, a hyperinflammatory state, hypermetabolism, hypovolaemia and immunosuppression, which collectively predispose patients to infections. Burn wound infection (BWI) affects up to 60% of patients globally. It represents a critical aetiology for sepsis and subsequent septic shock in individuals with severe burns [1]. As the most common pathogen in BWI, S. aureus poses a serious threat [2]. Poor blood circulation in burn wounds undermines local anti‐infective efficacy and pathogen clearance, thereby facilitating massive pathogen proliferation and the onset of bacteraemia. The mortality rate from bacteraemia in burn patients is reported to be as high as 65%, exceeding that in trauma and other critical conditions. MRSA infections, in particular, lead to prolonged hospital stays, substantial financial burdens and increased mortality [3]. Hence, elucidating the clinical characteristics, antimicrobial resistance patterns of S. aureus in BWI and the risk factors for MRSA is essential for developing effective nosocomial infection prevention strategies and guiding clinical therapy.

Materials and Methods

Sample

We performed a retrospective analysis of 270 cases of burn wound infection from a tertiary burn centre between 2018 and 2022. Eligible patients were required to meet all the following criteria: (1) no pre‐existing infection, (2) onset of infection > 48 h post‐admission, (3) S. aureus isolated from a burn wound culture and (4) availability of complete clinical data. Conversely, cases were excluded for: (1) discharge within 48 h, (2) colonisation without active infection (positive culture without clinical symptoms), (3) absence of S. aureus in culture, (4) insufficient clinical documentation.

Clinical Data Collection

This study collected clinical data upon patient admission, including demographics, underlying diseases and burn‐specific details (cause, area, season). Throughout hospitalisation, we documented all invasive procedures—such as endotracheal intubation, mechanical ventilation, suctioning, catheterization, gastric intubation and surgeries—along with the types of antimicrobial agents used and the total length of stay. Burn severity grading (BSG) was performed based on the judgement criteria of the 1970 China Burn Conference [4].

Definition

The following key variables were defined for this study:

Total body surface area (TBSA) was determined as the total area of second‐ and third‐degree burns. For adults, TBSA was estimated using the Chinese Rule of Nine [4]; for paediatric patients, an age‐adjusted Rule of Nine was used to account for different body proportions. The palm method (1% TBSA per patient's palm) was adopted for scattered small burns and first‐degree burns were excluded. All evaluations were conducted independently by two trained burn surgeons, with discrepancies adjudicated by a senior consultant [4].

Burn severity grading (BSG) was graded according to the 1970 Shanghai National Burn Conference criteria, with separate standards for adults and children [4]. For adults: mild, < 10% total body surface area (TBSA) second‐degree burn (2°); moderate, 11%–30% TBSA 2° or ≤ 9% third‐degree burn (3°); severe, 31%–50% TBSA 2° or 10%–20% TBSA 3°; extra‐severe, > 50% TBSA total burn or > 20% TBSA 3°. For children: mild, < 5% TBSA 2°; moderate, 5%–15% TBSA 2° or < 5% TBSA 3°; severe, 15%–25% TBSA 2° or 5%–10% TBSA 3°; extra‐severe, > 25% TBSA total burn or > 10% TBSA 3°.

Underlying diseases were defined as chronic conditions diagnosed prior to the current burn injury admission. Documented comorbidities included hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary heart disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, chronic kidney disease, cerebrovascular disease (including sequelae of cerebral infarction and intracerebral haemorrhage), malignant tumours and psychiatric or neurological disorders. Based on the presence of any of these conditions, patients were categorised into an underlying disease group or a non‐underlying disease group.

The use of gastric acid‐suppressive medications was defined as any pharmacological intervention administered during hospitalisation for the purpose of stress ulcer prophylaxis or treatment of gastrointestinal conditions, regardless of route, dosage or duration; based on previous literature, documented agents were classified into the following categories: proton pump inhibitors (PPIs, e.g., omeprazole, pantoprazole), H2 receptor antagonists (H2RAs, e.g., famotidine, ranitidine) and gastric mucosal protectants (e.g., sucralfate, hydrotalcite) [5].

Antimicrobial use was defined as the administration of any systemic antimicrobial agent (including antibacterials and antifungals) during hospitalisation, regardless of indication or duration; based on previous literature, documented agents were classified into the following classes: penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems, aminoglycosides, quinolones, macrolides, glycopeptides, oxazolidinones, sulfonamides, tetracyclines and antifungals [6].

Patient outcomes were evaluated based on wound healing status. Cure was defined as > 95% wound healing, whereas improvement was defined as a clean, non‐infected wound with emerging epithelium. Death was defined as in‐hospital mortality from any cause. Abandoning therapy was defined as discontinuation of treatment before completion due to patient or family decision. These outcome classifications were adapted from previous studies on wound healing assessment [7].

Invasive procedures were defined as the following seven interventions performed during hospitalisation: central venous catheterization, nasogastric tube placement, endotracheal intubation, suctioning, surgery, urinary catheterization and mechanical ventilation. The presence or absence of each procedure was recorded for all patients.

Age was first analysed as a continuous variable in the multivariable logistic regression model. Then, the optimal age cutoff (32 years) was determined by ROC curve analysis (Youden index: 31.5 years) and the chi‐squared method (32 years). Age was then dichotomized into < 32 years and ≥ 32 years for secondary analysis.

Bacterial Identification and Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing

Bacterial identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing was conducted with the VITEK IICompact automated system (bioMérieux, France). According to the CLSI M100 document, Performance Standards for Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing, the isolates were categorised as Susceptible (S), Intermediate (I) or Resistant (R) [8]. A S. aureus isolate was defined as multidrug‐resistant (MDR) if it exhibited non‐susceptibility to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial categories, or if it was identified as MRSA. We employed the following strains for quality control: ATCC 51299, ATCC BAA‐977, ATCC BAA‐976, ATCC BAA‐1026, ATCC 29213 and ATCC 25923.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were employed using SPSS software (version 26.0). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, and a p‐value of less than 0.01 was considered highly significant. Normally distributed continuous data are presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using Student's t‐test or paired t‐test. Non‐normally distributed data are reported as median (interquartile range) and compared with the Mann–Whitney U test or Wilcoxon signed‐rank test. In the univariate analysis, categorical variables were analysed using the χ 2 test. Fisher's exact test was applied when the assumptions of the χ 2 test were not met. Multi‐variable logistic regression was performed to identify independent risk factors. Graphical presentations were generated using GraphPad Prism version 8.0.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA, www.graphpad.com).

Results

Characteristics of Patients With S. aureus Burn Wound Infections

Patients with S. aureus burn wound infections were predominantly male (71.9%, 194/270) and aged ≥ 32 years (64.8%, 175/270). Of the 270 patients with S. aureus burn wound infections, 127 (47.0%) had MRSA and 143 (53.0%) had MSSA. The two groups differed significantly in age, burn severity, TBSA, length of stay, gastric tube, suctioning, tracheotomy, surgery, urinary catheterization, invasive procedures (≥ 3), gastric acid suppression, inhalation injury, corticosteroid therapy, types of antibiotics used (≥ 3), hypoproteinemia, anaemia, burn etiologies (mainly flame burns) and burn location (head and neck, trunk and multiple sites) (all p < 0.05). Risk Factors for MRSA Infection in Burn Patients

Univariate logistic regression analysis identified risk factors for MRSA infection in burn patients. The results showed that the following variables were significantly associated with MRSA infection (all p < 0.05): Types of antibiotics used, Anaemia, Urinary catheterization, TBSA, Burn location, Hypoproteinemia, Invasive procedures, Corticosteroid therapy, Suctioning, Inhalation injury, Surgery, Tracheotomy, Age, Gastric tube, Gastric acid suppression, Burn severity and Length of stay. When age was analysed as a continuous variable in the multivariable model, it was not significantly associated with the outcome (odds ratio [OR] = 1.010, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.999–1.022, p = 0.067). All statistically significant variables from the univariate analysis were then entered into a multivariable logistic regression model to adjust for confounding factors. After adjustment, use of three or more types of antibiotics was an independent risk factor for MRSA infection (OR = 2.212, CI = 1.130–4.329, p = 0.021), whilst no other variables retained statistical significance in the multivariable model (all p > 0.05). Clinical Characteristics of S. aureus Burn Wound Infections Across Different Burn Etiologies

Amongst the cases of S. aureus burn wound infections in this study, flame burns were the most common aetiology (112/270, 41.5%), followed by hot liquid burns (87/270, 32.2%) and contact burns from hot metal (27/270, 10.0%). Other causes included hot crush injury (13/270, 4.8%), chemical burns (12/270, 4.4%), electrical burns (7/270, 2.6%), other causes (7/270, 2.6%) and steam burns (5/270, 1.9%). The mean age of individuals sustaining flame burns and contact burns from hot metal was significantly higher than that of those with hot liquid burns. Flame burns were associated with a significantly larger burn surface area than those caused by hot liquid burns, metal contact burns, electrical burns, hot crush injuries and other burn types. Patients with flame burns had a significantly longer hospital stay than those with hot liquid or metal contact burns; patients with chemical burns also stayed in hospital longer than those with hot liquid burns. A significantly wider range of antibiotics was observed for flame burns compared to hot liquid or metal contact burns. Discussion

Burns are a global public health problem. There are 120 000 deaths from burns worldwide each year, particularly in low‐ and middle‐income countries [9]. China has a high incidence of burns. Research shows that in 2019, there were 1.079 million burn patients in mainland China, accounting for 12% of the global incidence rate, ranking first amongst countries worldwide. Amongst these, the number of burn deaths was 11 000, second only to India (26 000), ranking second globally [10]. The destruction of the skin barrier and subsequent immunosuppression render burn patients exceptionally vulnerable to infections. Devitalized tissue on the wound surface provides a nutrient‐rich milieu that promotes robust microbial proliferation, making invasive wound infection nearly inevitable [11, 12].

Burn wound infection (BWI) remains one of the leading causes of mortality in this patient population [13]. S. aureus is the most prevalent pathogen in burn‐wound infections [1, 14] and can significantly delay healing [15, 16]. The organism expresses a diverse arsenal of virulence factors—including exotoxins, surface adhesins, toxic‐shock‐syndrome toxin‐1, α‐toxin and Panton‐Valentine leukocidin—enabling it to progress from a localised wound to a systemic threat [17]. Hypervirulent methicillin‐resistant S. aureus (MRSA) is of particular concern, as it can trigger burn‐wound sepsis, deepen invasive infections, cause bloodstream dissemination, precipitate multi‐organ failure and increase mortality [18]. At the wound site, S. aureus assembles into a tenacious biofilm, a microenvironment that fosters multidrug resistance and thereby transforms acute wounds into chronic bacterial reservoirs. Embedded within this matrix, the pathogen can tolerate antibiotic levels up to 1000 times greater than those required to eliminate free‐living cells [19]. Thus, the prevention, containment and eradication of these resilient S. aureus infections pose a formidable daily challenge for burn centres globally.

In our cohort, males developed burn‐wound infection (BWI) at more than twice the frequency of females (71.9% vs. 28.1%). This pronounced gender disparity is consistent with earlier studies [3] and is reinforced by the predominance of males in high‐risk occupations and their correspondingly higher incidence of industrial accidents [20]. Equally notable, methicillin‐resistant S. aureus (MRSA) was isolated from 47% of burn wounds—a rate substantially exceeding the 30% reported by the China Antimicrobial Surveillance Network (www.chinets.com) and approaching the 51% documented in an East China burn centre [1]. This elevated local prevalence highlights a persistent, centre‐specific challenge that necessitates intensified infection control vigilance.

In the present study, univariate analysis identified burn severity, total burn surface area as significantly associated with MRSA infection in burn patients, which is consistent with previous findings [14, 21]. Deeper and more extensive burns, together with longer hospitalisation, prolong wound exposure to the external environment. When compounded by an immunocompromised state, these conditions become highly favourable for MRSA colonisation and subsequent infection [13, 22]. In the multivariable logistic regression model adjusting for potential confounders, however, only use of three or more types of antibiotics remained an independent risk factor for MRSA infection (OR = 2.212, CI = 1.130–4.329, p = 0.021). In contrast, other variables—including burn severity, total burn surface area and number of invasive procedures—no longer retained statistical significance. The risk of MRSA colonisation in burn wounds increased significantly with the number of prior antibiotic classes administered. Liberal antibiotic use not only selects for resistant organisms and impairs host immunity but also promotes biofilm formation [11], thereby creating a local wound environment conducive to MRSA proliferation [23]. Therefore, antimicrobial stewardship is crucial for the prevention and control of MRSA infections in burn patients.

The International Society for Burn Injuries (ISBI) practise guidelines recommend withholding prophylactic antibiotics during the initial 5–10 days post‐burn, an approach shown to reduce burn wound infection rates without affecting sepsis incidence [24]. Corroborating this, early surgical intervention within 24–48 h after injury has been shown to significantly lower the risk of burn wound infection, thereby reducing antibiotic reliance [1, 25]. In patients with established infection, prompt debridement should be performed alongside bacterial culture and susceptibility testing to guide targeted antibiotic therapy. Notably, MRSA exhibits a capacity to colonise various medical devices—including catheters, surgical instruments, respiratory equipment and wound dressings—whilst invasive procedures may facilitate barrier breach, promoting bacterial colonisation and translocation [26, 27]. Thus, strict disinfection of medical equipment in burn units is essential to control MRSA transmission.

In this study, multidrug‐resistant S. aureus accounted for 68.1% (184/270) of isolates, a prevalence notably higher than the 38.7% (142/367) reported by Özkaçmaz et al. [13], underscoring a serious and growing concern. Studies have demonstrated that prolonged hospitalisation in burn patients elevates the risk of multidrug‐resistant bacterial infections [3, 28]. The spread of these pathogens is fuelled by the evolution of dominant resistant clones combined with selective pressure from inappropriate antibiotic use [29]. Once established, multidrug‐resistant bacteria compromise antimicrobial efficacy, extend hospital stays and increase both treatment costs and mortality rates. A systematic review by Girerd‐Genessay et al. analysing 29 outbreaks of multidrug‐resistant bacteria in burn units identified MRSA as one of the most common causative agents [30]. Our findings further revealed that the predominant MRSA resistance pattern involved co‐resistance to eight antibiotics: penicillin, oxacillin, gentamicin, clindamycin, erythromycin, ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin and tetracycline. Consequently, these agents should be administered with caution in the management of MRSA‐infected burn wounds.

The escalating crisis of antibiotic resistance has intensified the search for alternative therapeutic strategies against multidrug‐resistant infections. Liu et al. demonstrated both in vitro and in vivo synergistic bactericidal activity between gentamicin and antimicrobial photodynamic therapy (aPDT), offering a promising combinatorial approach to combat resistant pathogens [31]. Similarly, Adeniji et al. highlighted the potential of nanomaterials as carriers for antimicrobial agents, showing that such combinations can reduce antibiotic dosage whilst enhancing efficacy and minimising toxicity in treating multidrug‐resistant infections [32]. Furthermore, multiple studies confirm that nanotechnology can address the challenge of multidrug‐resistant burn wound infections through targeted drug delivery, enhanced antimicrobial activity and improved wound healing [33, 34, 35]. Together, these non‐antibiotic therapies provide new avenues for managing multidrug‐resistant infections in burn patients and mitigating the burden of resistance.

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective design precluded comprehensive molecular characterisation of all isolates. Second, the single‐centre design and relatively small sample size after excluding incomplete records may limit generalizability and introduce selection bias. Third, as shown in Table 1, the study population showed heterogeneity in several baseline patient demographics. We adjusted for these using multi‐variable logistic regression; however, residual heterogeneity may limit the generalizability of our findings. Fourth, our cohort was predominantly of a single ethnicity, further limiting generalizability. Fifth, burn depth data were unavailable due to incomplete records, although this factor has been reported to influence MRSA susceptibility. Sixth, the total number of burn admissions was unobtainable due to hospital data access policies, precluding calculation of the overall incidence of S. aureus infections. Future multicentre prospective studies with larger, more diverse populations should record this denominator to validate our findings and more accurately estimate infection burden.

Conclusion

In summary, the use of three or more types of antibiotics was identified as an independent risk factor for MRSA infection in burn wounds. Increased awareness of this risk factor may help reduce the incidence of such infections. Meanwhile, the high detection rate of multidrug‐resistant organisms in burn wounds underscores the need for healthcare workers to implement robust infection control measures. These should include hand hygiene, antimicrobial wound care, procedural optimization, judicious use of medical devices and environmental controls to prevent transmission and outbreaks amongst high‐risk patients.
