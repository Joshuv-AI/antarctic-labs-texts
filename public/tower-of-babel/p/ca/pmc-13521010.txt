Safety and adverse event profiles in neuromodulation therapies for drug-resistant epilepsy
Authors: Yuta Tanoue, Masaki Izumi, Samuel S Ahn, Dawn S Eliashiv, Jerome Engel, Ausaf A Bari, Itzhak Fried, Aria Fallah, Hiroki Nariai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDrug-resistant epilepsy (DRE) is associated with clinical and psychosocial burdens, and non-pharmacological therapies play an important role when seizure control cannot be achieved with antiseizure medications alone. Neuromodulation therapies, including vagus nerve stimulation (VNS), deep brain stimulation (DBS), and responsive neurostimulation (RNS), have become important options for patients who are not suitable candidates for resective or disconnective surgery. Although these modalities have demonstrated efficacy for seizure reduction, they differ in surgical invasiveness, stimulation targets, device structures, programming strategies, and long-term management requirements. These differences result in distinct adverse event (AE) profiles for treatment selection. In this review, we summarize AEs and long-term management considerations associated with VNS, DBS, and RNS and classify them into four categories: procedure-related AEs, stimulation-related AEs including neuropsychiatric and cognitive issues, device-related AEs, and long-term management considerations. VNS avoids intracranial surgery but is associated with stimulation-related laryngopharyngeal symptoms and generator and wire management. DBS allows direct modulation of deep brain networks but carries risks of intracranial lead implantation, targeting accuracy, and psychiatric or cognitive effects. RNS provides closed-loop stimulation and chronic intracranial electroencephalographic recording, but requires implantation of intracranial leads and a cranially implanted neurostimulator, localization of the epileptogenic network, and specialized long-term data review, programming, and device management. Across all three modalities, device-related infection, battery depletion or replacement, and ongoing device management are common safety concerns. Understanding these modality-specific AE profiles may help clinicians select candidates, counsel patients and families, and develop safe and sustainable long-term treatment strategies for DRE.Keywords: VNS, DBS, RNS, Complications, Side effects

Introduction

Epilepsy is one of the most common neurological disorders, affecting approximately 1% of the population, with an annual incidence of 50.4–81.7 per 100,000 people [[1], [2], [3]], and remains a major neurological disorder associated with a substantial disease burden [2]. Among patients with epilepsy, approximately 30% have drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE), defined as failure to achieve sustained seizure freedom despite adequate trials of two or more appropriately selected antiseizure medications [4]. Patients with DRE experience a broad range of clinical and psychosocial difficulties, including seizure-related brain dysfunction, developmental impairment in pediatric patients, increased risk of injury due to falls or accidents, employment limitations, social isolation and an increased risk of sudden unexpected death in epilepsy [5,6], highlighting the need for timely treatment and non-pharmacological treatments [5].

Non-pharmacological treatment options include dietary therapies, such as the ketogenic diet [7], and surgical interventions, which are a major component of treatment for DRE. Epilepsy surgery is generally classified into resective or disconnective procedures aimed at seizure freedom and palliative procedures aimed at reducing seizure frequency or severity. [8,9]. Patients with a clearly defined epileptogenic focus, such as typical mesial temporal lobe epilepsy [10], focal cortical dysplasia, or tumor-related epilepsy, may be appropriate candidates for curative surgery aimed at seizure freedom [8,9]. However, curative resection is often limited in patients whose epileptogenic lesion involves eloquent cortex, or in those with multifocal, bilateral, or poorly localized DRE [11,12]. In such cases, neuromodulation therapy represents an important palliative treatment option.

In recent years, epilepsy has increasingly been understood not only as a disorder of a single seizure onset zone (SOZ), but also as a network disorder involving multiple brain regions [[13], [14], [15]]. Based on this concept, neuromodulation therapy can be considered an approach to modulate seizure-related networks, particularly in patients who are not suitable candidates for resective surgery. At present, vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) [16], deep brain stimulation (DBS) [17], and responsive neurostimulation (RNS) [18] are used clinically and have been shown to be effective treatment options for DRE [19,20]. However, their relative efficacy for seizure control has not been established in head-to-head comparative trials, and differences in patient populations, indications, stimulation targets, and study designs make direct cross-study comparisons difficult. These therapies do not require resection of brain tissue and are generally considered less invasive than resective procedures. However, they differ in implantation site, surgical technique, stimulation method, and device structure.

Each neuromodulation modality has a distinct profile of adverse events (AEs) [[21], [22], [23], [24], [25]]. Although AEs have been reported in clinical trials and long-term follow-up studies, they are often described as safety outcomes rather than as the main focus of investigation. Consequently, relatively few studies have directly compared the AE profiles of these neuromodulation therapies or discussed their clinical implications across modalities.

Given the expanding use of neuromodulation for DRE, AE profiles should be considered not only as safety outcomes, but also as practical factors that shape treatment selection, preoperative counseling, and long-term care. VNS, DBS, and RNS share some common concerns, including infection, device malfunction, battery depletion, and the need for ongoing follow-up, but they differ substantially in surgical invasiveness, stimulation targets, reversibility, neuropsychiatric and cognitive considerations, MRI-related limitations, and programming complexity. This review therefore summarizes and compares procedure-related, stimulation-related, device-related, and long-term management considerations across these three modalities, with the goal of providing a safety-focused framework to support individualized treatment decisions for patients with DRE.

Literature search strategy

This article was designed as a narrative review. We performed a focused PubMed search, supplemented by manual screening of references from relevant articles. Searches were conducted separately for VNS, DBS, and RNS using combinations of modality-specific terms, including “vagus nerve stimulation”, “deep brain stimulation”, and “responsive neurostimulation”, with epilepsy- and safety-related terms such as “adverse events”, “complications”, “infection”, “hemorrhage”, “hardware”, “lead”, “battery”, “replacement”, “stimulation adverse effects”, “neuropsychiatric”, “cognitive”, and “MRI”. Additional searches were performed for clinically relevant rare complications and device-specific issues.

Priority was given to pivotal clinical trials, long-term follow-up studies, registry studies, and large cohort studies. Systematic reviews were also consulted to identify relevant literature or summarize broader trends. Clinically important case reports were included when they described rare but clinically relevant adverse events that were not well captured in larger studies. Manufacturer safety information, product information, and MRI guidelines were consulted for device-specific characteristics, MRI eligibility, and hardware-related considerations. Based on published reports and practical relevance, adverse events and long-term management issues were organized into procedure-related, stimulation-related, device-related, and long-term management categories.

Because this was a narrative review rather than a systematic review or meta-analysis, the search was not intended to be exhaustive, and no formal risk-of-bias assessment or quantitative synthesis was performed. The available literature was interpreted through multidisciplinary discussion among adult and pediatric epileptologists and epilepsy neurosurgeons. Therefore, the completeness of rare adverse event capture and the precision of frequency estimates should be interpreted with caution.

Overview of neuromodulation modalities: efficacy, indications and adverse events (AEs)

Vagus nerve stimulation (VNS)

Efficacy and indications

VNS has the longest history among neuromodulation therapies for DRE. After the first implantation in humans in 1988 [26], VNS was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 1997 as an adjunctive therapy for adults and patients aged 12 years or older with drug-resistant partial-onset seizures. In VNS, stimulation electrodes are wrapped around the cervical vagus nerve, and intermittent electrical stimulation is delivered from an implantable pulse generator (IPG) placed in the anterior chest wall.

Although the mechanism of action is not fully understood, VNS is thought to exert antiseizure effects through vagal afferent pathways. These pathways project brainstem structures, including the nucleus tractus solitarius, and influence broad neural networks involving the thalamus, limbic system, and cerebral cortex [27]. VNS is widely used in both pediatric and adult patients as an adjunctive therapy for DRE when resective surgery is not indicated [16,28]. Previous clinical studies and meta-analyses have shown that approximately half of patients achieve a reduction in seizure frequency of 50% or more, whereas complete seizure freedom is achieved only in a minority of patients [29]. Therefore, VNS is generally regarded as a palliative rather than curative therapy, with the main goal of reducing seizure frequency and severity.

Adverse events in VNS

Procedure-related AEs

Procedure-related AEs in VNS include events that occur during surgery or in the early postoperative period. Surgical procedures related to VNS include initial implantation, generator replacement, lead revision, and device removal. In previous surgical series, overall surgery-related complication rates ranged from approximately 8 to 17%, although these rates may vary according to whether events are analyzed per patient or per procedure, follow-up duration, and the definition of complications used in each study [[30], [31], [32], [33]].

Infection is one of the most clinically relevant complications in any device implantation surgery. Reported infection rates generally range from approximately 2-7% after VNS surgery [25,[30], [31], [32], [33]]. Although management depends on the severity and location of infection and whether the device is involved, some cases require removal of the generator or lead. Careful sterile technique, appropriate perioperative prophylactic antibiotics, and postoperative wound management are important [31].

Bleeding is relatively uncommon, with reported rates of approximately 2%. However, VNS implantation requires dissection around the carotid sheath and nearby venous structures; major vessels may be exposed during the procedure. In rare cases, cervical hematoma may cause airway compression or tracheal deviation, requiring urgent management [33].

Vocal cord paralysis or recurrent laryngeal nerve injury has been reported in approximately 1–6% of patients and may present with hoarseness, weak voice, cough, aspiration risk, or dyspnea [30,31,33,34]. Bradycardia or cardiac arrest during intraoperative lead testing is very rare but has been reported as a serious complication, requiring intraoperative monitoring and close cooperation with the anesthesia team [30,[35], [36], [37], [38]]. Other symptoms, such as pain, sensory disturbance, facial weakness, and local discomfort, have also been described, but they are usually transient and manageable with conservative treatment [33].

Stimulation-related AEs

Stimulation-related AEs in VNS are typically intermittent symptoms that occur during active stimulation and often vary according to stimulation parameters, such as output current, frequency, pulse width, and duty cycle. Common symptoms include hoarseness, voice alteration, cough, throat discomfort, dysphagia, dyspnea, and sleep-disordered breathing. Hoarseness and voice alteration are among the most characteristic stimulation-related symptoms of VNS. These symptoms are thought to result from activation of vagal efferent fibers, recurrent laryngeal nerve branches, the superior laryngeal nerve, or neuromuscular structures involved in upper-airway function. Dysphagia, dyspnea, and sleep-disordered breathing may involve more complex mechanisms related to laryngeal and pharyngeal function, as well as upper-airway respiratory dynamics [39]. In several reports, voice-related symptoms are described as transient and manageable. However, especially in pediatric patients, voice, swallowing, respiratory, and sleep-related symptoms may be more frequent than is usually recognized during routine outpatient follow-up [34,40,41]. Most stimulation-related symptoms in VNS can be improved by adjusting stimulation parameters or may decrease over time. Persistent symptoms or symptoms affecting daily activities should prompt reassessment of stimulation settings.

Unlike DBS or RNS, VNS has not been consistently associated with adverse effects on psychiatric symptoms or cognitive function. VNS acts on broad neural networks involving the brainstem, thalamus, limbic system, and cerebral cortex through vagal afferent pathways [27]. Based on these network effects, the influence of VNS on mood, cognition, and quality of life has been investigated in addition to its antiseizure effects [19]. VNS has also been used or investigated for conditions other than epilepsy, including treatment-resistant depression [42], and improvement in mood and quality of life has been reported in patients with epilepsy [43]. Thus, in VNS, neuropsychiatric and cognitive issues may be better regarded as long-term clinical outcomes to be monitored rather than as clearly defined complications. Because many patients with DRE have pre-existing psychiatric symptoms or a history of suicidality, long-term assessment, including evaluation of psychiatric status before surgery, is desirable. This is particularly important in pediatric patients and in patients with developmental disabilities, for whom information from family members and caregivers may be essential.

Device-related AEs

Device-related AEs in VNS include lead fracture, high impedance, lead disconnection, generator malfunction, spontaneous device activation or deactivation, and generator migration. Rare but clinically important device-related events, such as trauma-related device failure [44], lead traction associated with growth [45], and generator rotation or manipulation resulting in lead coiling, known as twiddler's syndrome [46], have also been reported. In long-term follow-up studies, hardware-related problems have been reported in approximately 3–17% of patients, although the reported rates vary depending on the follow-up duration and event definitions [30,31,33].

Lead-related problems, including lead fracture, disconnection, and high impedance, are among the most common device-related complications, with reported rates of approximately 3–12% [[30], [31], [32], [33], [34]]. Generator malfunction and spontaneous device activation or deactivation are rare, but they may present as unexpected stimulation-related symptoms, such as painful stimulation, hoarseness, or dyspnea [30,34,47].

Long-term management considerations

Long-term management is an important component of VNS therapy. Because VNS is often continued for many years, preoperative counseling should address both the risks of initial implantation and the need for long-term device maintenance, including generator replacement due to battery depletion.

Additional surgery may be required during long-term follow-up because of infection, lead or IPG problems, or the need for surgery for other chest or neck conditions. Several long-term follow-up studies have reported that the time to generator replacement is approximately 5 years [25,31,32]. In a larger long-term surgical follow-up study, approximately half of patients who underwent initial VNS implantation required at least one battery replacement, reoperation, or device removal [32]. Cervical lead management is another important long-term consideration. The helical electrode coils are wrapped around the vagus nerve and may become embedded in postoperative scar tissue, making lead revision or removal more difficult than generator replacement. Although complete lead and coil removal has been reported to be feasible, it requires careful dissection around the vagus nerve and carotid sheath and may carry risks such as vocal cord paresis or vascular injury. In some cases, residual lead or coils may be left in place to avoid nerve injury; however, retained hardware may limit future MRI access and therefore requires careful long-term planning [48].

Battery replacement timing varies depending on stimulation settings and generator model. Because most currently used VNS systems are non-rechargeable, long-term treatment requires continued follow-up and device management beyond the initial implantation. These points should be explained to patients and families before surgery and should be incorporated into long-term care planning.

Deep brain stimulation (DBS)

Efficacy and indications

DBS was originally established as a treatment for movement disorders, such as Parkinson's disease and essential tremor [49,50], and was later applied as the treatment for DRE [17]. In DBS, electrodes are implanted into specific deep brain structures, and continuous or intermittent electrical stimulation is delivered from an IPG placed in the anterior chest wall or another subcutaneous site. For DRE, the anterior nucleus of the thalamus (ANT) is an established DBS target, particularly for drug-resistant focal epilepsy. In the SANTÉ trial and long-term follow-up studies, ANT-DBS demonstrated progressive and sustained seizure reduction, with median seizure reductions of 41% at 1 year, 69% at 5 years, and 75% at 7 years, and responder rates of 43%, 68%, and 74%, respectively [17,21,23]. The ANT is closely connected with limbic networks, including the hippocampus, cingulate gyrus, and mammillothalamic tract, and is considered an important therapeutic target for focal epilepsy, especially seizure involving temporal lobe and limbic networks [51].

Other thalamic targets have also been investigated. The centromedian nucleus of the thalamus (CM), which is connected with widespread cortical, basal ganglia, and brainstem networks, has been studied as a potential target for generalized epilepsy and developmental epileptic encephalopathies, including Lennox-Gastaut syndrome [52]. Additional targets, such as the subthalamic nucleus and pulvinar, have also been reported [53,54], but the current evidence for these targets remains more limited than for ANT-DBS. Recent sensing-enabled DBS systems can also record local field potentials from implanted leads, which may provide biomarkers of seizure-related network activity and support future adaptive stimulation approaches, although this remains investigational in epilepsy [55]. Unlike VNS, DBS requires intracranial electrode implantation and is therefore more invasive. However, it can directly modulate brain networks involved in epilepsy and remains an important therapeutic option for selected patients.

Adverse events in DBS

Procedure-related AEs

Procedure-related AEs in DBS include events that occur during surgery or in the early postoperative period. DBS-related procedures include lead implantation, placement of the IPG, connection of extension wires, lead revision, and device removal. Because DBS requires stereotactic intracranial electrode implantation, it carries risks that are not typically associated with VNS, such as intracranial hemorrhage, malposition, and lead implantation failure. Other procedure-related events include wound or implant-site infection, perioperative pain, and headache [17,21,56].

The reported frequency of procedure-related AEs in epilepsy DBS varies across studies because of differences in study design, follow-up duration, event definitions, and whether events are analyzed per patient, per procedure, or per device [17,21,57,58]. In representative ANT-DBS studies, procedure- or device-related perioperative events have been reported in a substantial minority of patients, whereas serious complications are less frequent [21,58]. Lead malposition, including leads not located within the intended target, and technical difficulties during lead placement are characteristic DBS-related complications. Because lead location directly affects clinical outcomes and therapeutic efficacy, these problems may require lead revision or replacement [17,57,58]. In the MORE registry, revision rates were not negligible [58], and surgical analysis from this registry also showed that successful contact placement within the ANT target differed according to the surgical trajectory, suggesting that anatomical and technical factors may influence lead placement accuracy [59]. However, DBS targeting methods have evolved over time, including a shift from indirect atlas- or coordinate-based approaches toward direct MRI-guided anatomical targeting based on patient-specific imaging [60]. Therefore, reported rates of lead malposition or revision should be interpreted in relation to targeting methodology, stereotactic approach, including frame-based of frameless techniques, trajectory selection, and surgical era rather than being regarded as fixed risks of contemporary DBS practice.

Infection is another important complication of DBS implantation. Management may require antibiotics, wound care, partial device removal, device replacement, or, in some cases, complete system removal and reimplantation [21,22,57]. The reported frequency of infection varies across studies. In representative ANT-DBS studies, implant- or wound-related infections have ranged from a few percent to approximately 10%, whereas hardware removal or reimplantation due to infection appears to occur less frequently, in approximately 3–8% of patients in some series [21,22,57,58]. These differences may also reflect variations in follow-up duration, staged procedures, generator replacement, and local wound conditions [58,61].

Intracranial hemorrhage is also an important complication of DBS. Although uncommon, it can lead to serious neurological consequences. When radiographic and asymptomatic hemorrhage are included, intracranial hemorrhage has been reported in approximately 4–5% of patients undergoing ANT-DBS or DBS in general. Symptomatic or clinically serious hemorrhage is less frequent, with reported rates of approximately 0–3% [17,21,22,56]. Data from DBS for movement disorders are also informative, with symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage generally reported in approximately 1–3% of patients [56]. However, these data should be applied cautiously to epilepsy DBS because some cohorts include procedures performed with microelectrode recording, which may increase the number of intracranial passes and influence hemorrhage risk. Other early postoperative events include surgical site pain, headache, and other procedure-related physical symptoms. These symptoms are usually temporary and manageable with conservative treatment, and persistent long-term symptoms are uncommon [17,21,58].

Stimulation-related AEs

Stimulation-related AEs in DBS can occur when electrical stimulation affects the target nucleus or adjacent neural structures. Their occurrence may depend on the stimulation target, active contact, stimulation amplitude, pulse width, frequency, and cycling parameters. Many of these symptoms are intermittent and stimulation-dependent and can often be improved by adjusting the simulation intensity, changing the active contact, or modifying stimulation parameters [17,21,62].

In ANT-DBS, reported stimulation-related or device-related symptoms include paresthesia, sensory disturbance, dizziness, headache, worsening or changes in seizure frequency or seizure pattern, mood changes, and memory complaints [17,21,57,58]. In the SANTÉ trial, paresthesia was reported as a device-related AEs in 18.2% of patients. During the blinded phase, depression, memory impairment, anxiety, and paresthesia were reported more frequently in the stimulation group than in the control group [17]. In the MORE registry, relatively common AEs included changes in seizure frequency, severity, or type, memory impairment, depressive mood, headache, irritability, anxiety, and cognitive impairment [56,57]. These events should be interpreted cautiously because they may reflect stimulation effects, seizure variability, epilepsy severity, medication changes, or disease-related fluctuations.

Stimulation-related AEs may also differ by target. CM-DBS may cause dysarthria, ataxia, or other motor symptoms, possibly due to spread of stimulation to nearby motor or cerebellothalamic networks [52,63,64]. In a report of CM-DBS in 22 pediatric patients, AEs included paresthesia in 15 patients, agitation/irritability in 7, aggression in 3, reduced alertness in 3, involuntary facial movements in 2, and ptosis, dizziness, and vomiting in 1 patient each [65]. Available safety data for non-ANT targets, including CM and pulvinar-DBS, remain limited compared with ANT DBS. In particular, pulvinar-DBS has not yet accumulated sufficient safety data to define a stable target-specific AE profile [66].

Neuropsychiatric, cognitive, and behavioral effects, including depression, anxiety, irritability, mood changes, cognitive impairment, and memory impairment, have been reported in patients treated with DBS, particularly ANT-DBS, and require careful attention [57,58,67,68]. Because the ANT is closely related to the Papez circuit and limbic networks, stimulation of this target may plausibly affect memory and mood regulation [69]. This possible relationship is also suggested by case reports in which depressive or anxiety symptoms after ANT-DBS improved after changes in stimulation programming [68]. Regarding cognition, subjective memory impairment has been reported; however, objective neuropsychological testing has not consistently shown long-term cognitive decline [67,70]. Some studies have suggested possible beneficial effects of ANT-DBS on cognitive function [70]. Changes in artistic expression, including alterations in color tone and visuospatial organization, have also been reported after DBS [71].

However, compared with procedure-related complications or clearly stimulation-dependent symptoms, the causal relationship between DBS and these symptoms is often difficult to determine. Many patients with DRE have pre-existing depression, mood disorders, or cognitive impairment before treatment. In addition, postoperative psychiatric or cognitive symptoms may be influenced by antiseizure medications, seizure frequency, seizure severity, and disease-related fluctuations [17,21,67]. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted in the context of baseline psychiatric and cognitive status, seizure course, and medication changes, with preoperative assessment and postoperative monitoring considered in patients with pre-existing mood, psychiatric, or memory problems [57,58,67,68].

Overall, stimulation-related AEs in DBS are often adjustable. When new symptoms occur after DBS, clinicians should consider whether they are related to stimulation, natural fluctuation of epilepsy, antiseizure medications, or psychiatric and cognitive factors. If the symptoms are considered stimulation-related, adjustment of active contacts and stimulation parameters should be considered.

Device-related AEs

Device-related AEs in DBS are complications related to the implanted components of the system, including leads, extension wires, the IPG, and connectors. They include lead migration or dislocation, lead or extension wire damage, impedance abnormalities, stimulator malfunction, skin erosion, battery-related problems, and device removal or reimplantation due to infection [21,22,61]. These issues are not specific to DBS for epilepsy but are common considerations in DBS therapy in general [61].

In several major reports of DBS for epilepsy, the term “device-related” is often used broadly and is not restricted to hardware malfunction. In the 5-year follow-up of the SANTE trial, serious device-related AEs were reported in 34% of patients, but the most frequent events were implant-site infection and leads not located within the target [21]. According to the classification used in this review, these events are more appropriately considered procedure-related infection or targeting problems, rather than device-related AEs. Similar findings were reported in the 10-year follow-up, in which most device-related complications occurred during the operative phase [23]. Long-term device-related problems have also been reported in real-world cohorts. In the MORE registry, 34.7% of patients underwent at least one device revision, and 11.2% underwent lead revision [58]. As noted in the procedure-related AEs section, these revision rates should be interpreted in the context of the targeting methodology, stereotactic technique, trajectory selection, and surgical era represented in the registry. Although the timing and indication for revision vary among patients, regular device interrogation and appropriate evaluation are necessary during follow-up. Thus, device-related AEs in DBS require attention during both the early postoperative period and long-term treatment. Regular follow-up may help detect device-related problems early and reduce the risk of unplanned reoperation.

Long-term management considerations

Long-term management considerations are common to all neuromodulation therapies, and DBS also requires careful attention to device-related problems over time. Patients and families should be informed before treatment about the possible need for battery replacement, IPG replacement, lead revision, device removal or reimplantation due to infection, MRI-related device management, and device removal because of insufficient efficacy or intolerable AEs [21,23,58].

Battery depletion and IPG replacement are particularly important in DBS. In the SANTE follow-up study, battery longevity was directly influenced by stimulation settings, and approximately half of the patients with a non-rechargeable IPG required battery replacement after about 3 years [21]. In the MORE registry, which used the non-rechargeable Activa PC device, battery depletion was observed in 20.7% of patients, and replacement was generally required after approximately 4 years [58]. Thus, in non-rechargeable DBS systems, IPG replacement may be required within several years depending on stimulation settings and device type. These estimates should be interpreted in the context of the device generation used in each study.

Rechargeable IPGs may reduce the number of replacement surgeries, but they require regular charging by the patient or caregiver. Device selection should therefore consider battery longevity, cognitive function, manual dexterity, living situation, caregiver support, and the patient's ability to manage the device over the long time. IPG replacement should not be regarded simply as routine maintenance because it may be associated with wound complications, infection, treatment interruption, and healthcare costs. For this reason, battery depletion should be included as an important topic in long-term adverse-event counseling.

Responsive neurostimulation (RNS)

Efficacy and indications

RNS is a closed-loop neuromodulation therapy that records brain activity using intracranial cortical strip or depth electrodes and provides stimulation to patient-specific seizure foci or seizure-related network nodes in response to epileptiform electrographic events [18]. In contrast to VNS and DBS, which are generally used as open-loop stimulation therapies based on preset stimulation parameters, RNS provides closed-loop stimulation tailored to each patient's electrophysiological patterns.

RNS was approved by FDA in 2013 as an adjunctive therapy for adult patients with drug-resistant focal epilepsy arising from one or two identified SOZs. It is considered particularly useful when the SOZ is located in eloquent cortex or when resection carries a high risk of neurological deficits [72]. RNS has also been applied to selected patients with spatially extensive or regional SOZs, in whom stimulation may target a broader epileptogenic network rather than a single discrete focus [73]. Clinical trials and long-term prospective follow-up studies have demonstrated sustained seizure reduction in adults with drug-resistant focal-onset seizures treated with RNS, with a median seizure reduction of 75% and a responder rate of 73% at 9 years [24]. More recently, RNS has also been used in selected off-label settings, including spatially extensive, bilateral, multifocal, or network-based epilepsies, with cortical, mesial temporal, or thalamic targets selected according to the presumed epileptogenic network [[73], [74], [75]]. Candidate selection requires sufficient localization of SOZ or seizure-related network to guide electrode placement, but invasive monitoring is not universally required [76]. In selected patients, such as those with bilateral mesial temporal epilepsy or well-characterized generalized or multifocal epilepsies, targets may be selected based on convergent clinical, scalp EEG, imaging, and prior diagnostic data.

Although pediatric use of RNS remains off-label, accumulating pediatric and young adult experience suggests that RNS may be feasible in carefully selected patients with DRE, particularly when epileptogenic regions are bilateral, multifocal, deep, or involve eloquent cortex and resection is not appropriate. Early multicenter and single-center series, including cortical and thalamic targets such as the centromedian nucleus, have reported seizure reduction with acceptable short-term safety [74,75,[77], [78], [79]].

In addition, because RNS allows chronic intracranial electroencephalogram (EEG) recording, it may contribute not only to treatment but also to evaluation of seizure lateralization and localization, understanding of seizure networks, and assessment of long-term changes in seizure patterns and disease activity [80]. However, RNS requires implantation of intracranial leads and a cranially implanted neurostimulator, and its indication depends on the location of SOZs. It also requires specialized programming and long-term device management.

Adverse events in RNS

Procedure-related AEs

Procedure-related AEs in RNS include events related to lead implantation and placement of the cranial neurostimulator. RNS implantation typically involves placement of depth leads or subdural strip leads, as well as implantation of the neurostimulator within the skull. Therefore, procedure-related complications include events associated with intracranial or cranial surgery, such as intracranial hemorrhage, delayed wound healing, cranial implant-site infection, and skin erosion. As with other implanted neuromodulation devices, lead revision, device-related problems, device removal, and reimplantation should also be considered [18,24,[81], [82], [83]].

Although lead-related technical problems in RNS differ from lead malposition in DBS targeting deep brain structures, procedural issues related to lead placement and trajectory can still occur. Depth leads are stereotactically placed in structures such as the hippocampus, amygdala, SOZs, or thalamus, whereas subdural strip leads are placed near the cortical SOZs. Therefore, lead trajectory, relationship to SOZ or seizure-related network, lead fixation, and connection to the neurostimulator may affect treatment efficacy and device stability [84,85]. Lead misplacement or revision appears to be uncommon, but lead-related complications have been reported. In the RNS pivotal trial, lead revision to improve lead location was performed in 4 of 191 patients, and damaged leads were reported in 5 of 191 patients [81]. In a real-world multicenter series, lead revision was required in 4 of 150 patients (2.7%) [86]. Because RNS leads are used for both stimulation and chronic intracranial EEG (iEEG) detection, lead-related problems may affect detection quality and long-term programming, in addition to hardware issues [84]. In addition, the clinical meaning of lead position in RNS may differ from that in DBS, because RNS may target patient-specific seizure foci or seizure-related network nodes rather than a single anatomical target. Supporting this network-based interpretation, a study in temporal lobe epilepsy reported that patient-specific structural connectivity of the stimulated tissue, rather than the local volume of stimulated tissue alone, informed seizure reduction after RNS [87].

Infection is an important complication in RNS. Because the neurostimulator is implanted within the skull, cranial implant-site infection, delayed wound healing, and skin erosion are characteristic concerns [83]. In the 9-year prospective follow-up study, serious device-related implantation site infection was reported in 12.1% of participants, indicating a non-negligible cumulative infection burden during long-term follow-up [24]. However, the risk appears lower when expressed per procedure, with a reported infection rate of 3.7% per neurostimulator procedure in an analysis of RNS clinical trial data [83]. Real-world and single-center series have reported variable rates, likely reflecting differences in study design, follow-up duration, and reporting definitions. As with other implanted devices, some infections may require device removal or reimplantation. Although most reported infections appear to be superficial, deep-site infections, subdural infections and abscesses have been described in a small number of cases [77,88].

Intracranial hemorrhage is another important complication to consider. Intracranial hemorrhage, subdural hemorrhage, and epidural hemorrhage, including asymptomatic events, have been reported in association with lead implantation. Although reported rates vary across studies, intracranial hemorrhage is generally reported in approximately 2–5% of patients, whereas clinically serious hemorrhage or permanent neurological deficits appear to be less frequent [18,24,86].

Other early postoperative AEs in RNS include wound pain, headache, local discomfort, and wound swelling. These events are usually manageable with conservative treatment and are unlikely to affect long-term continuation of therapy, but they remain relevant in routine clinical postoperative care.

Stimulation-related AEs

Stimulation-related AEs in RNS can occur when electrical stimulation from depth leads or subdural strip leads placed near the SOZ spreads to adjacent cortex, white matter fibers, cranial nerves, or nearby structures. Because RNS delivers stimulation in response to detected seizure-related activity, rather than continuously or cyclically as in open-loop systems such as DBS, the frequency and characteristics of stimulation-related AEs may differ from those of DBS.

In pivotal and long-term studies, serious AEs directly attributed to stimulation have not been prominent, and stimulation has generally been well tolerated [18,24,81,82]. However, stimulation-triggered signs or symptoms have been described as RNS-specific stimulation-related symptoms. In a single-center report, these symptoms were observed in approximately 10% of patients [89]. Reported symptoms include paresthesia, pain, muscle contraction, photopsia, dizziness, and facial pain [89]. In patients with subdural strip leads, trigeminal neuralgia-like symptoms have also been reported in association with lead location or lead migration [90]. Among patients with depth leads implanted in the bilateral CM, stimulation-triggered symptoms were observed in 8 of 16 pediatric patients (50%). Four patients experienced sensory symptoms (painful shocks or paresthesia), three experienced motor symptoms (dystonic posturing, extremity jerking, or nystagmus), and one experienced both sensory and motor symptoms [74]. These symptoms can often be reduced or eliminated by adjusting stimulation parameters and are usually managed as stimulation- or parameter-dependent reversible problems.

Neuropsychiatric, cognitive, and behavioral issues in RNS should be interpreted in the context of its patient-specific stimulation strategy. Unlike ANT-DBS, in which stimulation is applied to a limbic network target involved in memory and emotion, RNS delivers stimulation through depth or subdural electrodes placed according to each patient's SOZ or seizure-related network. In pivotal and long-term studies of RNS, clear adverse effects on cognition or mood have not been prominent at the group level [81]. Some studies have reported improvement in specific cognitive domains, particularly memory outcomes in patients with mesial temporal seizure onset [91], and favorable findings have also been reported for mood and quality of life [92]. These findings suggest that RNS may be a useful option when preservation of cognitive or psychiatric function is an important consideration.

However, it would be difficult to conclude that RNS has no effect, or a uniformly beneficial effect, on neuropsychiatric, cognitive, or behavioral outcomes. Establishing causality is challenging because many patients who are candidates for RNS have pre-existing depression, suicidality, or other psychiatric comorbidities. In the long-term RNS study, patients with a prior history of depression or suicidality were more likely to experience depression- or suicidality-related adverse events during follow-up [24]. In addition, although some studies have reported no major overall worsening in neuropsychiatric or psychosocial outcomes, worsening in behavioral outcomes has been observed in a small number of patients [93]. Therefore, psychiatric symptoms, cognitive function, and quality of life should be monitored longitudinally after RNS implantation.

Overall, stimulation-related AEs in RNS appear to be less prominent in pivotal and long-term studies than those observed with VNS or DBS. Symptoms should be carefully assessed when leads are located near eloquent cortex or cranial nerves, when lead migration is suspected, or after changes in stimulation settings. In RNS, the timing of symptoms can also be compared with stimulation logs, which may help determine whether symptoms are stimulation-related or are due to seizures or other causes.

Device-related AEs

Device-related AEs in RNS include problems related to the implanted components of the system. These include lead damage, lead migration, high impedance, and stimulator malfunction. RNS-specific technical issues may also include problems with data upload or telemetry, as well as device removal or reimplantation due to infection or skin erosion [24,82,83,86,88].

Because RNS leads are used for both stimulation and chronic iEEG detection, device-related problems may affect treatment delivery, detection quality, EEG data review, and long-term programming. Overall, device-related AEs in RNS have been considered acceptable in long-term follow-up studies. Lead revision or lead damage has been reported in a small proportion of patients, generally in the range of a few percent, and may require reoperation or adjustment of stimulation settings [84,86,88].

Serious hardware malfunction appears to be uncommon in RNS. However, because the device is responsible for both stimulation and chronic recording, device-related problems may affect treatment continuity, therapy quality, and long-term device-based management.

Long-term management considerations

Long-term management of RNS involve device maintenance together with optimization of detection and stimulation settings based on chronic iEEG data. Because treatment effects are not judged immediately after implantation, ongoing outpatient follow-up and stepwise programming are required. In representative long-term studies, seizure reduction has been shown to improve over time, supporting the need for continued follow-up and device management [24,82].

A distinctive feature of RNS is the availability of chronic intracranial EEG data during long-term therapy. These recordings may help refine detection settings, adjust stimulation parameters, and assess changes in electrographic seizure activity over time. Thus, RNS can support individualized treatment beyond conventional open-loop stimulation, although regular data upload, review of stored recordings, and programming expertise are needed to use this information effectively.

Battery depletion and neurostimulator replacement are also important long-term management considerations. Battery longevity has improved substantially with newer models. In the 9-year prospective follow-up study, the earlier model had a median time to replacement of approximately 3.5 years [24], whereas the currently available RNS-320 model has a reported median battery longevity of approximately 11 years according to manufacturer information. However, actual longevity may vary depending on stimulation intensity, frequency, impedance, and other device settings. Because replacement of a cranial neurostimulator involves different procedural considerations from replacement of a chest IPG, including scalp incision, cranial hardware exposure, and wound healing, replacement timing, infection risk, and patient-specific circumstances should be considered during long-term planning [24,82].

Cross-modality comparison and clinical implications for treatment selection

Although VNS, DBS, and RNS are all categorized as neuromodulation therapies, they differ in target population, expected therapeutic effect, surgical procedure, stimulation target, device implant location, and invasiveness. Therefore, direct comparison of AEs profiles alone may be misleading when judging the relative advantages or disadvantages of each modality. VNS does not require intracranial surgery and is relatively easy to introduce, but it does not directly stimulate the SOZ or seizure-related brain networks. DBS can directly modulate deep brain networks, particularly thalamic circuits, but requires stereotactic intracranial electrode implantation. RNS enables chronic iEEG recording and closed-loop stimulation of patient-specific seizure foci or seizure-related network nodes, thereby expanding treatment options for selected patients when sufficient localization of the epileptogenic region or network is available. Importantly, the relative ease and lower invasiveness of neuromodulation therapies, particularly VNS, should not lead to their premature selection over potentially resective surgery. In patients for whom seizure freedom may be achievable through definitive surgical treatment, neuromodulation should be considered within a comprehensive epilepsy surgery evaluation and positioned as complementary to, rather than a substitute for, curative surgical options.

Several considerations are shared across modalities. Infection remains an important complication because all three therapies involve implantation of artificial devices. Patient factors, including immune status, activity level, and comorbidities, should be considered. Device damage, battery depletion, component replacement, and long-term follow-up are also common issues. Intracranial hemorrhage is one key differentiating risk, which is not expected to occur with VNS but remains a low-frequency, potentially serious complication of DBS and RNS.

Psychological and social aspects of device implantation should also be considered. After implantation, patients usually live with the device for many years and may require regular outpatient adjustments, device replacement, or reoperation. Device implantation may also affect MRI access and management of other medical conditions. MRI eligibility and practical imaging limitations differ by device type, generation, and system configuration, as summarized in Table 1. Some patients may perceive the implanted device as a psychological burden or a source of stigma. Therefore, treatment selection should consider medical risks and benefits together with patient values, preferences, lifestyle, psychosocial support, and the feasibility of long-term follow-up.

Neuromodulation is a promising treatment option for DRE; however, patients and families should be counseled that treatment requires long-term device management, may involve repeated procedures, and may impose psychological or practical burdens. As clinical experience expands and device technologies continue to evolve, additional long-term safety considerations may become clearer over time. Understanding AE profiles helps clinicians avoid preventable complications and select the most appropriate neuromodulation therapy for each patient.

Clinical challenges and future direction

Neuromodulation therapy plays an important role in the treatment of DRE. VNS, DBS, and RNS have been reported to reduce seizure frequency with relatively acceptable safety profiles. Neuromodulation may also reduce epilepsy-related mortality, including SUDEP, mainly through reduction of seizure burden. Large VNS cohort data and long-term ANT-DBS and RNS studies have reported SUDEP rates in treated populations, suggesting the possibility that neuromodulation may help lower SUDEP risk [94,23,24]. However, the relative efficacy of these modalities for seizure reduction as well as other benefits including SUDEP reduction have not been established in head-to-head comparative trials. Therefore, treatment selection should not rely on expected efficacy alone, but should also consider modality-specific safety profiles, including the AEs summarized in this review.

Future development is expected to move toward less invasive and more individualized. For VNS, non-invasive approaches such as transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation (taVNS) have been investigated as alternatives to conventional implantable devices. Although taVNS may reduce procedure-related risks by avoiding surgical implantation, its efficacy, long-term safety, and optimal stimulation parameters remain under investigation [95,96].

For DBS and RNS, selection of stimulation targets and improved understanding of epileptic networks are important challenges. Based on the concept of epilepsy as a disorder of epileptogenic networks, thalamocortical networks, particularly those involving thalamic nuclei, have attracted attention both for understanding seizure mechanisms and for identifying therapeutic targets [[97], [98], [99], [100], [101]]. In the future, stimulation targets such as the ANT, CM and pulvinar may need to be selected not uniformly, but according to patient-specific seizure networks, thalamocortical connectivity, and seizure propagation patterns [102]. Because safety data for less-established thalamic targets remain limited, target- and trajectory-specific procedural risks, as well as stimulation-related AEs, should be carefully evaluated as thalamic network-guided neuromodulation becomes more widely adopted.

The use of neurophysiological data is another important area for future development. Integration of long-term iEEG data, particularly from RNS, with clinical information may contribute to seizure prediction, treatment-response prediction, and optimization of stimulation settings [14,102]. This data may also be well suited for artificial intelligence and machine learning approaches to identify new biomarkers for seizure detection or prediction. However, these approaches remain exploratory and require further validation.

Neuromodulation is also strongly influenced by device technology. Newer sensing-enabled DBS systems allow chronic recording of local field potentials from implanted leads and may support more individualized DBS programming or future adaptive stimulation strategies [103]. While adaptive DBS has entered clinical use in Parkinson's disease [103], its role in epilepsy remains investigational [104]. Advances in device design may improve implantation safety, target selection, stimulation methods, and long-term management. Device miniaturization, longer battery life, improved durability, and MRI compatibility may contribute to both therapeutic efficacy and reduction of AEs.

At present, understanding the AE profile of each modality is essential for selecting appropriate treatment for each patient. AEs should not be viewed only as complications to be avoided, but also as clinical information that can guide future improvements. Further development of neuromodulation is expected to move toward less invasive, more individualized, and safer treatment for DRE.

Conclusion

In this review, we summarized the AEs associated with currently available neuromodulation therapies for DRE. Neuromodulation therapy is an effective treatment option and is expected to continue to evolve. Understanding AE profiles is essential for selecting the most appropriate neuromodulation therapy for each patient, avoiding preventable complications, and developing a safe and sustainable long-term treatment plan.
