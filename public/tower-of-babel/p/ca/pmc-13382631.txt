Impact of built environment on elderly walking in community life circles: age-friendly perspective, Wuchang District, Wuhan
Authors: Jie Shen, Xi Xu, Tao Chun, Fangxia Ye, Junhang Fan, Yuanbo Fei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryRapid population aging underscores the need for walkable environments tailored to older adults. As community life circles serve as primary daily settings for elders, their built environment critically influences walking activity, yet few studies compare multiple scales across different community types. Based on survey data from 1,965 older adults in Wuchang District, Wuhan, and using street-view image semantic segmentation, deep learning, and geographic information system (GIS) spatial analysis, this study employs ordinal logistic regression models to examine how built environment characteristics in 5-, 10-, and 15-min community life circles affect elderly walking in old versus new communities. The findings reveal significant scale- and community-specific effects. In old communities, building density and land use mix are positively associated with walking in the 5-min circle but negatively associated in the 10-min circle. In new communities, enclosure inhibits walking in the 5-min circle, while the proportion of traffic safety facilities facilitates walking across all circles. These results highlight the need for precise, circle-specific governance of age-friendly walking environments within community life circles.

Introduction

Since the 1970s, declining global fertility rates and rising life expectancy have made population aging a worldwide phenomenon. The proportion of people aged 65 and over is projected to increase from 7% in 2000 to 16% by 2050, reaching an estimated 22% by 2100,1 indicating an increasingly severe global aging trend. In response to this trend, the World Health Organization (WHO) launched the “Age-friendly Cities” initiative in 2005 across 33 cities worldwide, introducing the concept of the “age-friendly city” for the first time.2 A community life circle refers to an urban living area defined by walking time. It serves as the basic unit of daily life for city residents and is the core space for older adults’ social interactions and daily activities. Promoting age-friendliness at the level of the community life circle has become a key spatial strategy for countries aiming to realize the age-friendly city vision and meet the diverse needs of the elderly.

Compared with high-intensity physical activities, walking—due to its moderate intensity and ease of integration into daily routines—has become one of the most common and health-promoting forms of physical activity among older adults.3 Studies have confirmed that walking can effectively increase energy expenditure and reduce or delay the onset and mortality rates of non-communicable diseases.4 With age, the physical function of the elderly declines, which requires a more supportive environment for walking activities. In the context of urban renewal and community life circle planning, exploring the interaction mechanism between the walking activities of the elderly and the built environment is an effective way to promote the construction of an age-friendly society as well as an important basis for healthy aging.

Life Circle Theory is a concept in geography and urban planning. This theory emphasizes meeting residents’ daily activity needs by rationally allocating public service facilities, thereby achieving efficient utilization of spatial resources and matching supply with demand. It provides a foundational framework for defining the spatial scope of this study.5 Community life circles constitute the basic units within the life circle spatial system. They are defined based on residents’ walking time and represent the daily activity range necessary to meet the most fundamental needs. These activities typically occur with high frequency and short duration and are concentrated around the residential community and its surrounding area.5 Design standards such as the “Shanghai 15-Minute Community Life Circles Planning Guidelines”6 and the “Urban Residential Area Planning and Design Standards”7 have been issued in China, establishing a spatial structure for community life circles based on walking distance. In this study, the application of the life circle theory is primarily reflected in the delineation of walking-based scales, where walking time serves as the core criterion for categorizing communities into different hierarchical levels of community life circles.

Time geography explains individual behavior from the perspective of spatiotemporal constraints, positing that individual activities are collectively constrained by physiological capacity, time budgets, and spatial structure. This theoretical framework provides an important basis for identifying the constraint boundaries of older adults' walking activity.8 Due to physiological decline, older adults exhibit clear physical thresholds in their walking activity range, with an average walking speed of approximately 0.8–1.0 m/s. The acceptable walking duration is generally 5–15 min, corresponding to a distance of roughly 240–800 m.9,10,11,12 In this study, the application of time geography is primarily reflected in two aspects: walking capacity constraints and the delineation of accessible range. Drawing upon older adults’ walking speed and acceptable walking duration, their daily activity space is confined within a walking scale of 5–15 min, and a multi-scale research scope is constructed accordingly, thereby ensuring that the spatial analysis more closely reflects actual behavioral characteristics. Therefore, the study designates 5, 10, and 15 min as the daily walking durations for older adults, corresponding to research buffer zones of 300 m, 500 m, and 800 m, respectively.

In 1943, the American psychologist Maslow proposed Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs Theory, which categorizes human needs into five levels: physiological, safety, love/belonging, esteem, and self-actualization.13 Physiological needs constitute the most fundamental conditions for sustaining survival.13 In the context of older adults' walking, this need is manifested as the accessibility and connectivity of the community walking environment, including whether essential service facilities such as daily shopping venues, medical service points, and bus stops can be conveniently reached within walking distance, as well as the completeness and directness of the pedestrian network.14,15 Once physiological needs are largely satisfied, safety needs become increasingly prominent, manifested as slip-resistant pavement conditions, adequate lighting, pedestrian walkways separated from vehicular traffic, and intersection designs that minimize traffic conflicts.16,17 Physiological needs and safety needs represent the most fundamental requirements for walking activity and can be collectively referred to as “instrumental demand.” The transportation network and services that comprise these needs constitute the basic conditions for enabling walking activity.

Once physiological and safety needs are reasonably well satisfied, individuals develop needs for belongingness (also termed emotional needs) and esteem.18,19With the increasing improvement of transportation infrastructure, walking is no longer solely undertaken to achieve positional movement from origin to destination; rather, its affective and social (or symbolic) benefits have gradually attracted scholarly attention. Research indicates that promoting walking activity requires optimizing the “non-instrumental” elements experienced during the walking process.20 This enables walking activity to transcend its instrumental function. A favorable walking experience is associated with elements such as aesthetics, comfortability, and diversity.21,22 Walking, in itself, can be an enjoyable activity.23 During walking, individuals need to make flexible route choices and respond to unexpected scenarios; these “non-instrumental” elements of walking can enhance the sense of freedom and excitement. Showcasing local landscapes and distinctive features, strengthening cultural identity, and creating pleasant streetscapes and artistic experiences help satisfy people’s spiritual needs.24,25 At the same time, the experience of the urban built environment during walking relies not only on large-scale landscape and spatial form design but also on proximate spatial environments and atmospheres that are perceivable, stay-worthy, and experientially engaging, thereby satisfying older adults' higher-level needs for belongingness and esteem.

Social Ecological Theory is a comprehensive framework for studying social systems. It posits that factors influencing behavior extend beyond the individual, encompassing interpersonal relationships, community types, policy orientations, and other multi-level determinants.26 This theory provides an overarching framework for integrating diverse influencing factors. In this study, the application of social ecological theory is primarily manifested in two respects. First, individual characteristics and built environment variables are incorporated into a unified analytical framework. Second, distinctions between different types of communities in terms of spatial structure and resource allocation are considered, enabling an analysis of their differential impacts on older adults' walking behavior.

This study constructs a theoretical research model (Figure 1) based on the context of population aging. First, drawing on Community Life Circle Theory and Time Geography Theory, the spatial extent of Community Life Circles is delineated according to older adults’ walking capabilities. Second, guided by Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs Theory, built environment indicators within Community Life Circle Theory are selected based on physiological, safety, love/belonging, esteem, and self-actualization needs. The relationship between the built environment and elderly walking activity has become a central focus in the study of age-friendly societies and spatial adaptations for aging populations. Early studies primarily adopted the “5D” framework—density, diversity, design, destination accessibility, and distance to transit—to examine how objective aspects of the built environment influence walking behavior.27,28,29 Research has shown that higher building density and greater land use mix reduce the distance to service facilities, enabling older adults to access a variety of services more easily and thereby increasing the frequency of walking activity.30 Design features such as road network density and intersection density improve walking convenience by optimizing route options.31 Well-designed road networks enhance the layout of pedestrian pathways, significantly improving walking safety and comfort. For older adults, convenient and safe routes align more closely with their travel preferences and needs. A cross-sectional study in Belgium confirmed that older adults walk more frequently in neighborhoods with high building density, street connectivity, and land use diversity.32 Additionally, destination accessibility is regarded as a key determinant of elderly walking activity, with the density of facilities such as convenience stores and parks significantly influencing the frequency of their daily outings.33

Compared with the objective attributes of the built environment, greater attention should be paid to human subjective perception, as it may directly or indirectly influence residents’ behavioral patterns.30 Traditional measurements of the Subjective Perceived Environment, such as field interviews and questionnaire surveys,34,35 help to understand older adults’ overall evaluations of the built environment but are limited in analyzing the micro-level relationships between subjective visual perception and environmental elements. With the rapid advancement of big data and computer vision technologies, an increasing number of researchers have adopted approaches that integrate street-view imagery with machine learning.23,24 Subjective visual perception refers to the process of processing environmental information through the visual system—it is a primary means by which people perceive and interpret their surroundings, typically triggered by specific objective environmental features that influence one’s decisions and behaviors.36 Street-view images can reflect the visual street environment from the perspective of older adults and serve as an emerging data source for research on subjective visual perception.28 Leveraging computer vision and deep learning techniques allows the extraction and quantification of Subjective Perceived Environment features from street-view images.37 Therefore, this study employs this approach to derive the visually derived perceptual proxies. Although the subjective feelings of older adults cannot be measured directly, these quantifiable physical features can serve as proxy variables that evoke corresponding perceptions, overcoming the limitations of traditional methods and enabling large-scale, fine-grained analyses of environmental perception within the built environment.

Studies have found that areas assessed as safer through street view analysis can significantly reduce traffic accident rates among older pedestrians, while increasing their walking frequency and travel distance.28,29 For older adults with heightened safety needs, perceiving the environment as safe is a prerequisite for walking with confidence. As an indicator of environmental aesthetics, Greenery Visibility effectively alleviates mental fatigue through exposure to nature, enhances walking enjoyment, and consequently strengthens the walking persistence of older adults.38,39 The association between the subjective perception of comfort and the physical environment can be traced back to the 19th century.40 Early studies primarily focused on indoor environmental factors such as lighting, temperature, and air quality.41 With the rise of human-centered urban design concepts,42 the concept of environmental comfortability has gradually expanded to encompass individuals' subjective perceptions of their surrounding environment. Existing studies have extensively examined the influence of visual and auditory attributes on perceived comfortability, with vision—accounting for over 80% of human perception—playing a central role.43 Visual attributes such as street openness, naturalness, skyline proportion, and transparency have been shown to be closely related to perceived comfortability.44,45 A comfortable environment reduces feelings of oppression, promotes social interaction, and fosters higher levels of self-identity and self-fulfillment, thereby enhancing walking activity among older adults.24,25 The ratio of buildings to signage has been used to assess street imageability.46,47 Clear visual cues can significantly reduce older adults' anxiety about getting lost and difficulties with spatial orientation caused by cognitive decline.48 Enclosure measures the degree to which the pedestrian environment is visually confined by vertical elements such as trees, buildings, and streetlights. Excessive enclosure may create a sense of psychological oppression, indirectly reducing older adults' willingness to travel.49

Although a large number of empirical studies have shown that the built environment influences walking activity among older adults, the conclusions are not consistent. For example, Thornton et al.50 found a positive correlation between land use mix and elderly walking activity in the United States, concluding that shorter distances to services can promote walking. In contrast, Zhang et al.51 reported that in China, high land use mix combined with an increase in road intersections led to traffic congestion and a deterioration of the walking environment, which negatively affected elderly walking activity. The reason for this difference may be that in low-density North American cities, land-use mix promotes walking by enhancing convenience, whereas in high-density Chinese cities, the same level of mix may have negative effects due to traffic conflicts and environmental overload. Zhou et al.,52 using a residential neighborhood in Guangzhou, China, as a case study, confirmed that green visibility effectively encourages older adults to engage in physical activity by offering more exercise opportunities and extending activity duration. However, Zhang et al.53 found that green visibility had no significant effect on walking activity among older adults in a community in Hong Kong, China. In Guangzhou, neighborhood vegetation provides shading for older adults, creating aesthetically pleasing and comfortable walking environments that promote physical activity; however, in Hong Kong’s high-density urban environment, the scarcity of vegetation weakens these positive effects. The aforementioned studies indicate that even when focusing on older adults, variations in geographic context and the selection of buffer zone extents can still lead to substantial differences in results.

To better investigate the impact of the built environment on walking activity, a small number of studies have begun to examine how built environment characteristics at different spatial buffer scales influence residents' walking behavior. Clark et al.54 analyzed the effects of spatial scale on the relationship between the built environment and residents’ active travel, finding that changes in buffer size alter the correlation coefficients of all environmental variables. This occurs because modifications in the unit of analysis can influence the statistical distribution of environmental variables and their association with behavioral variables, resulting in significant differences in findings across different spatial scales. Houston and Douglas55 explored the relationship between the effects of the built environment and residents’ physical activity at moderate levels of intensity based on three buffers of 50 m, 250 m, and 500 m. It was confirmed that land use mixing was positively correlated with physical activity at the 50 m range and was not significant in the 250 m and 500 m buffers. This may be because smaller buffers are more sensitive to the environment, thus exerting a more pronounced impact on residents' physical activity, whereas larger buffers may include destinations and facilities unrelated to walking behavior, resulting in nonsignificant correlations. Wang et al.’s56 study of the Netherlands noted that the 600 m buffer land mix was significantly correlated with recreational walking within the 600-meter buffer, but the correlation was weaker in the 300- and 1000-meter buffers. Within a 600-meter range, a rich environment can provide sufficient attraction and recreational experience, stimulating residents to walk; in contrast, a 300-meter range is too limited with minimal environmental variation, while a 1000-meter range increases the walking burden, reducing the motivation for recreational walking. Although such studies have revealed the differential impacts of the built environment at varying buffer-zone scales on walking activities, they predominantly focus on the general population and pay little attention to older adults who have specific needs in terms of physical functioning, travel modes, and spatial perception.

In summary, research on the relationship between the built environment and older adults' walking activities has yielded substantial findings, evolving from early studies that focused on objective built environment elements such as density, diversity, and accessibility, to more recent attention on visually derived perceptual proxiesal factors, including safety, comfortability, and aesthetics. However, the relationship between the built environment and walking activity has been inconsistent and may be influenced by spatial scale, demographic characteristics, and environmental type. To further clarify the underlying mechanisms, some scholars have begun examining how built environment features at different spatial scales influence walking activity. Nevertheless, most studies focus on the general population, often overlooking vulnerable groups such as the elderly, children, persons with disabilities, and low-income populations. Due to age-related physical decline, reduced mobility, and distinct psychological needs, older adults place higher demands on the built environment of their communities. As global aging intensifies, prioritizing research on older populations is increasingly important. Promoting walking activity is essential for preserving physical function and delaying functional decline in later life.

Based on this, the present study adopts an age-friendly perspective and comprehensively considers multi-scale community built environment elements to examine the differences in how the built environment affects older adults' walking activities within 5-, 10-, and 15-min Community Life Circles in both newly built and older communities. The aim is to provide insights for the development of age-friendly Community Life Circles and to promote the high-quality development of age-friendly cities.

Results

Descriptive statistics

A total of 1,965 respondents were included in the final sample, of whom 51.2% were female. The majority were aged 70–79 years (46.7%), had a junior high school education (38.5%), and reported a monthly income of 1,000–2,999 CNY (38.2%). Additionally, 53.5% of the older adult respondents reported having caregiving responsibilities for grandchildren. 61.4% of the older adults did not own a car, and 53.0% had chronic conditions. Significant differences in the distribution of walking duration were observed across individual characteristics. Age had the most pronounced effect (p < 0.001): The proportion of high-duration walking decreased markedly from 45.3% among those aged 60–69 to 26.8% among those aged 80 and above, showing a clear declining trend with advancing age. Health status also exerted a significant influence (p < 0.001): The proportion of high-level walking among older adults without chronic conditions (43.0%) was notably higher than that among those with chronic conditions (32.5%). Furthermore, caregiving responsibilities for grandchildren significantly reduced walking activity levels (p < 0.001). Gender differences were relatively weaker but still statistically significant (p = 0.041), with males reporting slightly longer overall walking duration than females. In summary, older adults’ walking activity is jointly constrained by physical capacity and family roles (Figure 2).Figure 2Distribution of walking duration among older adults by individual characteristics

Figure 3 illustrates the differences in weekly walking duration among older adults in older and newly built communities across various life circle scales. On average, older adults in newly built communities reported a slightly longer walking duration (2.64) than those in older communities (2.62). Within the 5-min life circle, older adults in older communities had a longer walking duration (2.73) compared to those in newly built communities (2.68). However, within the 10-min and 15-min life circles, elderly residents in newly built communities showed higher walking durations (10-min = 2.59; 15-min = 2.67) than those in older communities (10-min = 2.58; 15-min = 2.56).Figure 3Average number of hours of walking activity

Regression analysis

The study found that built environment factors within different scales of community life circles had different effects on older adults’ walking activity in older communities versus newly built communities. In the 5-min community life circle of older communities, regarding social attributes, being male, aged 60–69 and 70–79, having a monthly income below 1,000 CNY or between 1,000 and 2,999 CNY, and rating satisfaction with the community environment as “very good” were positively associated with walking activity, whereas caregiving responsibilities for grandchildren, car ownership, and the presence of chronic conditions were negatively associated. In terms of the built environment, building density, land-use mix, road network, accessibility of services, percentage of traffic safety facilities, greenery visibility, sky openness, and building and sign proportions positively influenced older adults’ walking activity. In the 10-min community life circle, regarding social attributes, being aged 60–69 and 70–79 and caregiving responsibilities for grandchildren were positively associated, while car ownership and the presence of chronic conditions were negatively associated. In terms of the built environment, land-use mix, road network, percentage of traffic safety facilities, greenery visibility, sky openness, and building and sign proportions were all positively associated with older adults’ walking activity, whereas building density and accessibility of services were negatively associated. In the 15-min community life circle, regarding social attributes, being aged 60–69 was positively associated, being aged 70–79 showed a marginally positive association, while being male and the presence of chronic conditions were negatively associated. In terms of the built environment, building density, land-use mix, accessibility of services, percentage of traffic safety facilities, greenery visibility, sky openness, and building and sign proportions were positively associated with older adults’ walking activity. Discussion

The impact of building density in older communities on older adults’ walking activity exhibits spatial heterogeneity. This study found that within the 5- and 15-min Community Life Circles, building density was positively correlated with walking activity, whereas within the 10-min circle, a negative correlation was observed. According to Maslow’s hierarchy of needs, physiological needs represent the most fundamental human requirements.57 Within the 5-min community life circle, the relatively high building density typically originated from the compact spatial layout of the planned economy era, where functional configurations were oriented toward meeting basic daily needs. High building density clusters small commercial establishments such as convenience stores, community clinics, and fresh markets, creating frequent opportunities for fulfilling daily needs.58,59 This arrangement facilitates older adults’ access to shopping and medical services, effectively promoting their walking activity.60 Studies by Wu61 and Timmermans62 also show that high-density built environments enhance the likelihood of walking activities by increasing the agglomeration and spatial accessibility of service facilities, thereby reducing residents’ daily travel costs. Furthermore, time geography posits that individuals develop habitual paths within their spatiotemporal environments.63 Due to declining physical function, older adults tend to prefer familiar and fixed walking routes. The stable environment provided by high-density older communities reduces the uncertainty of travel for older adults, further reinforcing and increasing their walking activity.60 At a deeper level, the open-style management mode of older communities and the stable social networks formed through long-term residence have transformed gray spaces within the high-density environment—such as street corners and storefronts—into venues for older adults’ spontaneous social interaction and light activity, thereby further promoting their spontaneous walking.64,65

In the 10-min community life circle, however, excessive building density exerted negative effects.22Specifically, narrow sidewalks, encroachment by electric vehicles and market stalls, and the lag in older community renewal policies are closely intertwined. Early urban renewal policies prioritized demolition-and-reconstruction or façade renovation while neglecting age-friendly details; as a result, amenities such as resting benches, sunshades, and barrier-free ramps have long been absent.66 In terms of community management mode, older communities lack professional property management, and the unauthorized occupation of public space by vendors or residents is difficult to fundamentally eradicate.67,68 From the perspective of older adults' daily habits, the 10-min community life circle represents an ambiguous distance that extends beyond physiological needs yet has not reached high-value services. As their safety needs remain unfulfilled69 and their physical function declines, older adults tend to shorten their walking distance.70,71 It is recommended that priority be given to alleviating overly dense areas within the 10-min community life circle, demolishing illegal structures, and widening or regularizing sidewalks, with a minimum passage width of no less than 1.5 m.72 Meanwhile, resting benches spaced at intervals of no more than 50 m should be added, along with sunshades and lighting facilities, to meet older adults’ safety and rest needs.73,74 Second, the community management mode should be improved. For older communities lacking professional property management, a co-governance mechanism combining “property management + self-governance” can be introduced, along with the designation of fixed vending zones, strict regulation of electric vehicle parking, and the installation of physical barrier posts.68 Finally, based on older adults' daily habits, community canteens and activity rooms for the elderly should be supplemented within the 10-min community life circle. Community canteens should be sited with a service radius of 500 m, reachable within a 5–10 min walk, and preferentially located in areas with a high concentration of older adults. Activity rooms for the elderly should be allocated at 100–150 square meters per 1,000 residents, with a service radius of 300–500 m.

At the 15-min community life circle scale, moderate building density exerted a positive facilitating effect on older adults’ walking activity. This benefit stemmed from the open spaces and greater availability of service facilities—such as post offices and banks—afforded by the moderate density.75,76 It was also associated with the policy orientation of older community renewal, as the recent addition of pocket parks and community service centers has enhanced the walkability of the 15-min community life circle.77,78 Meanwhile, in terms of older adults’ daily habits, the 15-min community life circle corresponds to planned long-distance trips—such as collecting pensions or attending specialist outpatient appointments—which promote older adults’ walking activity.

Road network density is positively correlated with older adults’ walking activity within 5- and 10-min community life circles, but shows no significant correlation in the 15-min circle. Within the 5- and 10-min circles, high road network density creates a “small blocks, dense network” pattern, offering multiple route options and shortening linear distances. Older adults can quickly reach destinations via backstreets and alleys, avoiding detours along main roads, directly supporting walking activity.79,80 However, at the 15-min circle scale, a high road network density may have adverse effects. A dense road network implies an increased number of intersections, requiring older adults to make route choices and navigate directions more frequently during walking. At the same time, an excessive number of intersections increases safety risks, especially for older adults with reduced reaction abilities. Therefore, despite the dense road network, older adults may still prefer using public transportation for medium-to long-distance trips due to increased fatigue and decreased sense of safety.81,82 Research by Adhikari et al.83 confirms that high road network density within the 15-min Community Life Circle has no significant effect on older adults’ walking activity. Even when the road network density is high, the lack of pedestrian-friendly design increases the burden of route choice, thereby failing to enhance older adults’ willingness to walk. It is recommended to use anti-slip and even pavement materials within the 15-min Community Life Circle to reduce bumps and uneven surfaces, thereby improving walking comfort and safety84 Install signage or guiding lines to help older adults better discern directions and road boundaries,85,86 Additionally, add adequately spaced seating and small green rest areas at key points,87 improve accessibility to bus stops to substitute for excessively long walks,88 and mitigate the negative effects of dense road networks on medium-to long-distance walking.

Service facility accessibility was positively associated with elderly walking activity in the 5- and 15-min life circles, but showed a negative association in the 10-min circle. Within the 5-min Community Life Circle, the accessibility of service facilities significantly promotes older adults’ walking activity. Due to physical function decline, older adults are highly dependent on daily service facilities within short distances. When frequently used facilities such as convenience stores and community clinics are densely arranged and easily accessible, older adults can meet basic daily needs with minimal physical effort, thereby significantly increasing their willingness to walk.89,90 Research by GUO et al. also suggests that service facility accessibility is positively correlated with older adults’ walking activity; the closer the facilities, the more they reduce travel costs and stimulate willingness to walk.91,92,93

However, this study shows a negative association within the 10-min community life circle. Because many cities allocate community service centers based on population without differentiating the functional roles of the 5-min and 10-min circles. This has resulted in a high degree of duplication between facilities in the 10-min circle and those in the 5-min circle—for instance, multiple convenience stores or barbershops—while medium-sized facilities that genuinely meet the needs of this distance range, such as community canteens and activity rooms for the elderly, remain lacking, ultimately reducing older adults’ willingness to travel.94,95 In recent years, the advancement of “15-min community life circle” planning policies has promoted high accessibility of service facilities such as large hospitals and cultural centers. In newly built communities, building density is negatively correlated with older adults' walking activity. Unlike the low-rise layout of older communities, newly built areas are dominated by high-rise buildings, forming a high-density yet relatively enclosed spatial pattern. This environment insufficiently accounts for older adults' reduced adaptive capacity due to physical and cognitive decline, potentially increasing psychological stress and anxiety, thereby inhibiting their willingness to walk [107]. Additionally, environments composed of highly repetitive buildings, indistinct public spaces, and complex underground garage exits pose risks and uncertainties of getting lost during travel, reducing non-essential exploratory walking and confining walking activity to the most familiar routes.100 Newly built communities generally adopt commercial property management, with safety control and order maintenance as the primary objectives. Unlike the open-style management of older communities, newly built communities rarely feature spontaneously formed “gray spaces” such as street-corner shops or seating areas at stairway entrances. Although the physical population density is high, the density of social interaction remains low, making it difficult for older adults to derive a sense of safety and social belonging from such environments.72 Therefore, future urban renewal should incorporate gray space design—such as elevated ground floors, street-corner pocket parks, and resting areas in front of residential units—as well as clear and easily identifiable spatial signage, in order to alleviate environmental stress and create an inclusive community environment that supports older adults' walking activity and promotes their physical and mental well-being.

Land use mix had a positive effect on elderly walking activity in the 5-min life circle but showed negative associations in the 10- and 15-min circles. In the 5-min life circle, high land use mix reflects the compact distribution of commercial, medical, cultural, and recreational facilities, allowing older adults to quickly and conveniently reach desired destinations. This reduces walking distance and time, significantly enhancing walking frequency and willingness.101,102 The concentration of facilities improves accessibility while offering diverse social and activity options, further encouraging walking participation among older adults.103 Brown et al.104 also suggest that land use mix is beneficial to older adults' walking activity; a functionally diverse land use mix can shorten travel distances, provide rich visual stimulation and resting nodes along the way, and increase the purposefulness and enjoyment of walking.104,105,106

In contrast, in the 10- and 15-min circles, land use mix was negatively associated with walking. Studies suggest that excessive land use mixing may actually reduce walking. This may be because excessive mixing often results in large-scale integrated commercial-residential complexes, where service resources are driven by economic incentives to cater more to younger populations, leading to insufficient age-friendly services and reduced walking motivation among older adults.101 Furthermore, time geography theory posits that individuals’ daily activities are constrained by their physiological conditions, available tools, and time.8 In newly built communities, the 10- and 15-min community life circles have relatively few facilities catering to older adults. To meet medical, social, and other needs, older adults must travel to more distant locations. Moreover, the area has a relatively well-developed public transportation system, offering a more efficient and less physically demanding option for medium-to long-distance travel. Consequently, older adults are more likely to substitute some walking trips with public transportation, reducing their reliance on walking for medium-to long-distance travel.107 It is recommended that elderly friendly facilities—such as community elder care stations and accessible parks—be added within the 10–15-min life circles. Flexible, time-sharing uses of space, such as converting daytime activity centers into public squares for evening group dancing, can enhance resource adaptability and responsiveness to elderly needs.96,108

Greenery visibility, sky openness, and the proportion of buildings and signage are positively associated with older adults’ walking activity in the 10- and 15-min Community Life Circles, but show no correlation within the 5-min circle. Walking within the 5-min circle is typically purpose-driven, such as for daily necessities, along familiar routes with simple decision-making, primarily influenced by infrastructure accessibility and density.109 In such brief and goal-specific trips, older adults experience lower cognitive load and minimal navigation demands, resulting in limited reliance on visually derived perceptual proxies in the environment, and hence, these elements exert no significant moderating effect.80,110

In contrast, the 10- to 15-min Community Life Circles encompass more distant destinations such as parks, hospitals, and banks. Walking in these circles combines both purposeful and recreational elements, requiring greater physical and psychological endurance. At this scale, “supportive elements” in the walking environment become crucial.30 Higher greenery visibility helps alleviate psychological fatigue among older adults through exposure to nature, enhancing walking enjoyment and persistence111,112; Open sky visibility reduces the sense of confinement, improving spatial orientation and comfort113; The proportion of clearly distinguishable buildings and signage serves as key spatial cues, significantly reducing uncertainty during long-distance walking and boosting older adults' confidence in mobility.18,19 Therefore, in the 10- and 15-min circles, environmental design should enhance visual comfort and spatial orientation. Street trees and vertical greening should be increased to achieve a street-level green coverage rate of 25%–30%.114 The street height-to-width ratio and building setbacks should be optimized, and easily recognizable landmarks—such as distinctive signage, color-coded zones, and directional signs—should be installed. Proactively creating a walking environment that is highly supportive, low-stress, and rich in environmental identity can effectively foster older adults’ willingness to engage in long-distance walking.85,115

Enclosure is negatively associated with older adults’ walking activity within the 5-min Community Life Circle, but shows no correlation in the 10- and 15-min circles. Wang et al.116,117 also argue that a higher degree of enclosure is not necessarily better for the environment, and that excessively high enclosure may have negative psychological effects on older adults. This study finds that within the 5-min community life circle, older adults’ walking is primarily aimed at fulfilling frequent and essential daily needs. High enclosure is typically characterized by narrow alleys, streets with disproportionate height-to-width ratios, and limited visual fields.118 Such spatial characteristics hinder walking and can induce feelings of crowding and anxiety, thereby suppressing spontaneous short-distance walking among older adults.119 In the 10- and 15-min circles, older adults' walking activities are typically driven by medium-to long-distance composite needs, such as medical visits, social interactions, or leisure activities.120 The multi-purpose nature of these trips reduces sensitivity to individual environmental elements. Studies indicate that when walking routes cover multiple functional nodes, such as hospitals, parks, and community centers, older adults prioritize facility accessibility and path continuity over the morphological characteristics of enclosure. Even if highly enclosed alleys may evoke a sense of confinement, as long as they connect key facilities and provide continuous paths, older adults will still prefer them, thereby weakening the independent effect of enclosure.121,122 It is recommended that alleyways be slightly widened through measures such as demolishing illegal structures, relocating encroaching sundries, and partially recessing building boundary walls. Bai et al.123 investigated the recreational walking route choices of 133 older adults with cognitive decline aged over 60 in Shanghai and found that segment width significantly influenced walking route selection, exhibiting complex nonlinear threshold effects. Specifically, segments with a width of 5–8 m were preferred, whereas the usage rate of segments 8–10 m wide declined due to increased traffic volume. This finding supports the importance of moderately widening walking spaces. Pocket parks or micro-plazas should be added at entrances, exits, and street corners to create a spatial rhythm, with the street height-to-width ratio controlled within a comfortable range of 1:1 to 2:1. This study focuses on an age-friendly perspective and thoroughly investigates the effects of the built environment within multi-scale Community Life Circles on older adults’ walking activities in Older Communities and Newly built Communities in Wuchang District, Wuhan. By comprehensively analyzing both objective built-environment and visually derived perceptual proxies, it reveals the differential impacts of the built environment within 5-, 10-, and 15-min Community Life Circles on older adults’ walking activities in Older Communities versus Newly built Communities.

Specifically, the study found that built-environment elements within Community Life Circles of different scales have differing effects on older adults’ walking activities in Older Communities versus Newly built Communities. Within the 5-min life circle, factors such as building density, land use mix, road network density, service facility accessibility, proportion of traffic safety facilities, green visibility, sky openness, and the ratio of buildings to signage all positively influenced elderly walking participation. In the 10-min circle, land use mix, road network density, traffic safety facility proportion, green visibility, sky openness, and signage-to-building ratio were positively associated with walking activity, while building density and service facility accessibility were negatively associated. In the 15-min circle, building density, land use mix, service facility accessibility, traffic safety facility proportion, green visibility, sky openness, and signage-to-building ratio showed positive associations, whereas road network density was negatively associated with walking activity. In newly built communities, within the 5-min circle, land use mix and traffic safety facility proportion were positively associated with walking activity, while building density and enclosure had negative associations. In the 10-min circle, traffic safety facility proportion, green visibility, sky openness, and signage-to-building ratio were positively associated with walking activity, while building density and land use mix were negatively associated. In the 15-min circle, traffic safety facility proportion, green visibility, sky openness, and signage-to-building ratio continued to positively influence walking activity, whereas building density and land use mix were negatively associated.

Compared with previous studies that relied primarily on questionnaires to assess environmental perceptions,124 this study offers significant methodological advantages. By using semantic segmentation of street view imagery to capture subjective perceptions of the environment, it effectively avoids self-reporting bias. It also integrates both objective built environment data and subjective perceptions, employing field surveys, geographic information systems (GISs), and machine learning techniques. This approach enables the integration of big and small data across multiple spatial scales. Furthermore, the study overcomes the limitations of prior research that focused on a single type of community or a single spatial scale. By distinguishing between older and newly built communities and examining their respective 5-, 10-, and 15-min life circles, this study clarifies how built environment influences on elderly walking activity differ across community types and spatial scales.

The findings of this study can assist policymakers in better understanding the mechanisms through which built environments across different community life circles influence walking activity among older adults. It offers methodological guidance for the refined design of community life circles, enabling planners to better meet the specific activity needs and preferences of the older adults. Moreover, it provides practical insights for urban planning and development departments aiming to improve age-friendly community environments and promote active lifestyles among older adults.

Limitations of the study

The study was conducted in Wuchang District, Wuhan, China. While the findings may offer reference for urban areas with similar geographic and socioeconomic contexts, expanding the study area and increasing the sample size could yield more generalizable conclusions. Furthermore, the measurement of the visually derived perceptual built environment relied on street-view images, yet the scope of image collection was limited. Additionally, this study only considered the effects of the built environment and social attributes on older adults’ physical activity. However, the determinants of physical activity are complex, and factors such as social participation, policy, exercise attitudes, self-efficacy, and activity-related perceptions were not included. Finally, the study relied on self-reported data, making it uncertain whether respondents accurately recorded their walking duration.

In future research, the study area will be expanded to all seven central urban districts of Wuhan, with an increased number of sampled communities, and stratified random sampling will be adopted to reduce sampling bias, thereby enabling an in-depth analysis of how the built environment across different areas affects older adults' walking activity. Furthermore, when measuring the visually derived perceptual built environment, street-view images can be combined with questionnaire surveys, in-depth interviews, or field observation methods. Meanwhile, the street-view image collection strategy will be optimized—for instance, by expanding spatial coverage and employing multi-temporal sampling—and human verification will be integrated with more advanced semantic segmentation models to improve recognition accuracy. Additional factors influencing walking activity, including social and psychosocial factors such as social participation, policies, exercise attitudes, self-efficacy, and activity-related perceptions, will also be considered. Finally, future research could adopt more objective measures of walking activity, such as using the Global Positioning System (GPS) for real-time recording of older adults' walking paths and durations, thereby reducing subjective measurement bias and providing more accurate data to inform the construction of community life circle built environments.

Study settings and participants

Wuhan is a central city in central China, a nationally recognized historical and cultural city, and an international comprehensive transportation hub. It holds a significant position among cities across the country. According to the United Nations, a society is classified as an aging society when individuals aged 60 and above comprise more than 10% of the total population.125 The 2023 Wuhan Statistical Yearbook reports that the city’s population aged 60 and above has reached 2.0919 million, accounting for 22.15% of the total population, indicating that Wuhan has entered a stage of deep population aging. Wuchang District, one of the seven main urban districts of Wuhan, is located on the southern bank of the Yangtze River. With a favorable geographic location, it serves as a major cultural and political center in the region. As of 2023, Wuchang District has 248,900 residents aged 60 and above, accounting for 28.3% of its total population—the highest proportion of older adults among Wuhan’s seven main districts. As the first pilot area of the “quarter-hour convenient living circle” in Wuhan, Wuchang District focuses on promoting all-age-friendly community services,126 making it a typical representative of population aging in Wuhan.

This study selected 12 community samples, encompassing older communities built before 2000 that had not undergone systematic renovation by the time of data collection, as well as newly built communities constructed after 2000. Most older communities are located in the urban core, with architectural structures that generally no longer meet modern living needs and stable neighborhood relationships formed through long-term residence. According to the Sixth Population Census, about 52% of urban housing in China was built in the 1980s and 1990s, compared with 55% in Hubei Province. Wuhan’s Changlun neighborhood and other old neighborhoods are typical examples of communities now dominated by older adults and facing problems such as aging infrastructure and insufficient public space, with population aging and increased residential mobility as their distinguishing features. Newly built communities are primarily composed of gated high-rise residential complexes with uniform architectural styles, well-developed internal road networks, and complete supporting facilities. These communities have formed a scale effect within Wuchang District. Representative examples such as Kaile Garden District and Rongqiao City reflect the early characteristics of market-oriented urban residential development. Location of the study area and spatial distribution of aging risk

The study was conducted from October to November 2024. Cross-sectional data were collected from 12 representative communities using standardized sampling procedures through questionnaire surveys and in-depth interviews. The study participants were older adults aged 60 and above who had independent walking ability and had lived in the sampled communities for at least one year.Race and ethnicity data were not collected in this study. The primary research objectives focused on the associations between built environment characteristics at different life-circle scales and walking duration among older adults, without any hypothesis involving racial or ethnic differences. Moreover, the study was conducted in a single city in central China where the population is overwhelmingly of Han Chinese ethnicity, resulting in minimal racial/ethnic variation. Therefore, race/ethnicity was not considered a relevant variable for this analysis. Consequently, our findings should be generalized to other populations with different racial/ethnic compositions with caution. A total of 2,103 questionnaires were distributed. Two types of samples were excluded: (1) respondents with missing covariate data, and (2) those whose reported walking destinations were located more than 800 m from the community center, corresponding to the radius of a 15-min Community Life Circle. Ultimately, 1,965 valid questionnaires were obtained, yielding a response rate of 93.43%. The questionnaire design was adapted from the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), focusing on the walking activity characteristics of the older adults.Detailed demographic information is summarized in Table 1.

This study employed Cronbach’s α coefficient to assess the internal consistency reliability of the questionnaire. The reliability test results showed that the overall Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.87, indicating good internal consistency. Regarding validity, exploratory factor analysis was first conducted to examine the construct validity of the questionnaire. The KMO statistic was 0.82, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity reached a significant level (p < 0.001), indicating that the data were suitable for factor analysis. Principal component analysis was used to extract common factors, with factor loadings of all items exceeding 0.6, and a single factor explained more than 50% of the total variance, demonstrating satisfactory construct validity. In addition, the criterion-related validity test results also provided supporting evidence, indicating that the questionnaire possessed acceptable criterion-related validity. In summary, the questionnaire used in this study exhibited good reliability and validity, and can serve as an effective instrument for assessing older adults’ satisfaction with the community walking environment.

Building upon this, to gain deeper insight into older adults’ walking motivations, preferences, and subjective perceptions of the built environment, the research team conducted semi-structured in-depth interviews with 108 older adults, who were stratified and selected by community type, gender, and age from the questionnaire respondents. The interviews centered on topics such as daily walking routes and destinations, subjective willingness to walk, and reasons for changes in walking behavior. Each interview lasted approximately 30–45 min and was conducted in the respondents’ homes or community activity rooms. With the participants' consent, the interviews were audio-recorded and subsequently transcribed into interview records. The qualitative data were used to aid the interpretation of the regression analysis results, providing a contextualized understanding of the statistical findings, particularly in the analysis of the influencing mechanisms operating at different life-circle scales.

All participants were informed of the purpose of the study and their right to refuse participation or withdraw from the study at any time, and informed consent was obtained. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. All experimental protocols were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Wuhan University of Science and Technology (approval number: 2024154).

In this study, the dependent variable was the total weekly walking duration per person, measured in minutes. Following the World Health Organization’s recommendation that older adults engage in at least 150 min of physical activity per week,127 and drawing on previous studies on walking duration and health benefits, weekly walking duration was categorized into four ordinal levels: ≤150 min, 150–300 min, 300–600 min, and >600 min.128,129

Method details

Study design

First, this study combines questionnaire surveys and in-depth interviews to collect data on elderly walking behavior and employs ArcGIS spatial analysis along with semantic segmentation of street-view images to extract both objective built environment characteristics and visually derived perceptual proxy factors within the 5-, 10-, and 15-min Community Life Circles of older and new communities. Second, elderly walking duration is set as the dependent variable, built environment characteristics as independent variables, and individual attributes as control variables to construct an ordinal logistic regression model. Finally, a quantitative analysis is conducted to examine how objective built environment elements and visually derived perceptual proxy factors differentially influence older adults’ walking activities across various scales of Community Life Circles and across different types of communities.

Independent variable: Built environment data

Objective built environment variables were measured based on the “5D” framework proposed by Ewing et al.130 five dimensions: Density, Connectivity, Design, Diversity, and Destination Accessibility (Table 1).1.Density, connectivity and design

Density, connectivity and design

Density, Connectivity, and Design were measured by building density, total road network length, and the number of intersections, respectively. 3.Destination accessibility

Destination accessibility

Destination accessibility was measured by the accessibility of service facilities, with a focus on public services that meet the daily needs of older adults. These include daily living services (e.g., convenience stores, supermarkets, banks, shopping malls), recreational and leisure facilities (e.g., chess rooms, nursing homes, tea houses), medical services (e.g., general hospitals, specialized hospitals, clinics, community health centers), and transportation infrastructure (e.g., subway and bus stations). The greater the number of facilities and the closer their proximity to the community center, the higher the level of accessibility. This study employed the cumulative opportunity method to evaluate service facility accessibility.132 The cumulative opportunity method combines the strengths of both ratio-based and distance-based approaches by accounting for the number of facilities within a defined walking range and the distance from the community centroid to each facility. Accessibility for each type of service facility was calculated using the following formula: The formula is shown in Equation 2:(Equation 2)AP=Pi(D)[D−∑j=1Pi(D)dij/Pi(D)]where, Ap represents the accessibility of service facilities; Pi (D) is the total number of facilities reachable within a radius D from community center i; D corresponds to a walking threshold of 300 m, 500 m, or 800 m; and dij denotes the straight-line distance between community center i and facility j, where dij ≤ D.

Based on existing studies, the visually derived perceptual proxies are categorized into five dimensions: safety,28,133 comfortability,48,58 aesthetics,71 imageability,51 and enclosure.107 These are respectively represented by the proportion of traffic safety facilities, green view index, sky openness, the ratio of buildings and signage, and enclosure degree.134 The visually derived perceptual proxies are objectively calculated physical features based on street view images and semantic segmentation models, rather than being collected from residents’ self-reported questionnaire data. Therefore, they constitute a set of “visually derived perceptual proxies” designed to simulate and quantify, from an objective perspective, the environmental quality as it may be perceived by individuals, methodologically distinguishing them from traditional subjective perception measurements. First, based on the vector road network of the study area, sampling points were selected at 50-meter intervals, with coordinates and street orientations along the main road axis recorded. A Python script was developed to define the image width, height, location coordinates, horizontal and vertical viewing angles, and to call the Baidu Street View API. At each sampling point, street view images were retrieved from four angles—[0, 90°), [90, 180°), [180, 270°), and [270, 360°). In total, 39,074 street view images were collected in 2024. The resulting image dataset was processed using a semantic segmentation model. The constructed street view dataset is processed using a semantic segmentation model. Semantic segmentation is a deep learning–based, pixel-level image classification technique that classifies each pixel in an image to identify its corresponding object category (e.g., sky, trees, buildings), thereby enabling fine-grained scene analysis. This study adopted the ADE20K dataset for training, which includes street view imagery from multiple cities across various countries.135 The dataset contains annotations for 151 feature categories, covering a wide range of visual objects such as vegetation, sky, vehicles, and buildings, with more than 20,000 labeled images. The image semantic recognition algorithm used in this study was DeepLab-V3+ (Figure 7), a state-of-the-art neural network model developed by Google using TensorFlow and convolutional neural networks (CNN), recognized as one of the most accurate semantic segmentation models.136Figure 7DeepLab-V3+ model

DeepLab-V3+ model

To verify the reliability of the model, this study evaluated the performance of DeepLab-V3+ on the validation set. The results showed that the model achieved a mean Intersection over Union (mIoU) of 0.78 and a Pixel Accuracy of 0.92. Regarding the recognition of key categories, the IoU for sky was 0.91, for vegetation was 0.85, for buildings was 0.88, and for roads was 0.86. These results indicate that the model exhibits high accuracy in identifying core environmental elements, thereby providing a reliable basis for calculating indicators such as green visibility, sky openness, and enclosure degree.

Regarding indicator calculation and scale transformation, at the individual sampling point level, the pixel proportions of various elements were first calculated based on the semantic segmentation results, and the image results from the four directions at the same point were averaged to obtain point-level visual perception indicators. Subsequently, based on road network accessibility analysis, the spatial extents of the 5-min (approximately 400 m), 10-min (approximately 800 m), and 15-min (approximately 1200 m) community life circles were delineated. After matching the sampling points to their corresponding life circles, the point-level indicators within each spatial scale were spatially aggregated. Specifically, the arithmetic mean method was employed to calculate the life-circle-level indicator values, and the upper and lower 5% extreme values were trimmed to reduce the interference of outliers.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Prior to conducting the ordinal logistic regression analysis, we performed Pearson correlation analysis in SPSS. The results showed that all correlation coefficients among the independent variables were below 0.8, indicating the absence of serious correlations interference among the variables. Meanwhile, multicollinearity diagnostics were performed on the independent variables. For the older community model, the collinearity diagnostics results showed that the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values for all variables ranged from 1.16 to 2.93, and the Tolerance values ranged from 0.341 to 0.870; the maximum observed VIF was 2.93 (for the variable “Education (elementary school and below)” in the 10-min life circle). For the newly built community model, the collinearity diagnostics results showed that the VIF values for all variables ranged from 1.16 to 2.82, and the Tolerance values ranged from 0.355 to 0.862; the maximum observed VIF was 2.82 (for the variable “Education (elementary school and below)” in the 15-min life circle). All tolerance values exceeded 0.1, and all VIF values were below 10. Consequently, neither model exhibited serious multicollinearity problems, and all independent variables could be simultaneously included in the regression model for analysis (Tables S1 and S2).

We conducted a global Moran’s I test on the residuals of the regression model to assess potential spatial autocorrelation. The results showed that the Moran’s I index was −0.788950, the expected index was −0.090909, the variance was 0.567145, the Z score was −0.926903, and the p-value was 0.353977. Although the Moran’s I value was close to −1, the p-value exceeded 0.05. The analysis did not detect any statistically significant spatial clustering effect, indicating that the model residuals were randomly distributed in space. This suggests that the initial regression model adequately captured the spatial structure of the data structure, and there was no need to further introduce a spatial econometric model for adjustment (Table S3).

Given that the dependent variable, walking activity duration, is an ordinal categorical variable, this study employed an ordinal logistic regression model. First, this model fully utilizes the inherent order information among variable categories, providing higher statistical efficiency and clearer conclusions compared with multinomial logistic regression models that ignore this ordering. Second, the ordinal model requires estimating only a single set of coefficients, resulting in a simpler model structure and avoiding overparameterization issues associated with multinomial logistic regression. Pre-analysis tests confirmed the suitability of this approach. This model effectively assesses the impact of built environment elements within older and newly built Community Life Circles on older adults’ walking activity duration, providing estimates of significance levels, model fit indices, and effect coefficients.
