Decentralized example of a randomized clinical trial: OM-85 use in older patients at risk of respiratory tract infections
Authors: Angela M Abbatecola, Silvia Travaglini, Claudio Pedone, Alba Malara, Andrea Berti, Maria Teresa Vigliotta, Rosanna Pullia, Daniele Elmi, Valerio Alex Ippolito, Raffaele Antonelli Incalzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRandomized clinical drug trials (RCT) in older adults remain limited due to procedural complexity related to multimorbidity, frailty and disabilities.AimsOur study investigates the safety and efficacy of OM-85 in Nursing Home (NH) residents aged 75 years or older at an increased risk of respiratory tract infections (RTIs). We employ a decentralized, randomized, double-blind, and placebo-controlled Phase IV clinical trial.Methods13 NHs that previously participated in the GeroCovid Vax initiatives are involved. The protocol plans to recruit up to 360 NH residents with a documented history of at least two RTIs in 12 months preceding enrollment. A 6-month treatment phase with OM-85 is ongoing. Participants, allocated according to 1:1 randomization, receive either OM-85 (7 mg) or placebo treatment (one capsule) daily for the first 10 days of 6 consecutive months. Comprehensive geriatric assessment is performed at baseline and every 3 months for 12 months (6 months of treatment, 6 months of observation).Results272 participants are currently enrolled (30.9% men, 69.1% women) with a mean age of 84.8 ± 7.1 years, a high burden of multimorbidity (4.8 ± 2.2), polypharmacy (6.9 ± 3.4) and disability (ADL = 1.4 ± 1.5). Enrollment is still active.DiscussionThis RCT, the first study in NH residents aged 75 years with an increased risk of RTIs, will provide additional evidence for the use of OM-85 in a growing frail population that is underrepresented in clinical research.ConclusionsThis trial highlights key methodological challenges and opportunities associated with conducting RCT drug trials in long-term care facilities, offering valuable insights for the design and implementation of future trials in this complex setting.Keywords: Respiratory tract infections, OM-85, Older persons, Nursing home care, Randomized clinical drug trial

Introduction

Respiratory tract infections (RTIs) are highly associated with adverse health outcomes, especially in older adults, who are often frail and affected by multimorbidity [1]. Regular seasonal vaccinations remain the cornerstone of the strategy in preventing RTI, and at least four vaccines are currently recommended in western countries for older adults over 65 years of age [2, 3]. The use of immunomodulator therapies such as OM-85 (lyophilized bacterial lysate, commercially known as Broncho-Vaxom) may also contribute to preventing RTI-related outcomes in this vulnerable population. There is convincing evidence of the efficacy of OM-85 on preventing RTI in children [4] and reducing COPD exacerbations in adults [5] with only minimal side effects [6]. A previous trial testing one 3-month period of OM-85 use in institutionalized patients over 65 years of age with chronic bronchitis found a significant reduction in the number of episodes of acute bronchitis during the three months following the last OM-85 administration [7]. However, this study was short and limited to older adults with chronic bronchitis.

The nursing home (NH) environment has been identified as the “perfect storm” for the spread of transmissional infections, like RTIs [8]. The extremely high mortality rates that were linked to COVID-19 in older adults living in NH caused a severe worldwide crisis in NH organization and functioning [8]. The immediate implementation of protective measures and anti-COVID-19 vaccines in older adults in NHs was associated with a significant decrease in the incidence of SARS-CoV-2 infections and related deaths [9, 10]. The Gerocovid Vax study proved the efficacy and safety of the anti-COVID-19 vaccination in a large multi-center Italian NH study [11], as well as superior humoral immunity in NH residents with previous SARS-CoV-2 infection [12]. This study highlighted the feasibility of conducting high-quality research in NHs and contributed to the establishment of a research network capable of supporting ongoing and future investigations in these settings [13].

Conducting randomized clinical trials (RCTs) in older people, particularly in NH settings, presents substantial ethical, logistical, and methodological challenges. Residents often have multiple comorbidities, functional impairment, cognitive decline, and polypharmacy, which complicate eligibility criteria, informed consent procedures, and outcome assessment. High staff turnover, limited research infrastructure, and competing priorities within long-term care facilities can affect protocol adherence, data quality, and continuity of follow-up. In addition, acute intercurrent illnesses, hospitalizations, and high mortality rates increase attrition and introduce competing risks that may dilute treatment effects. Regulatory requirements and ethical safeguards, while essential, may further increase study complexity and timelines. Together, these factors contribute to the underrepresentation of frail older adults in clinical research and underscore the need for pragmatic, flexible, and decentralized trial designs [14]. To our knowledge, a RCT in frail older adults (≥ 75 years of age) at risk of RTIs living in NHs has never been performed. Our aim is to investigate the effectiveness of add-on immunomodulatory therapy with bacterial lysates in reducing RTIs and adverse related health outcomes in older adults (≥ 75 years of age) living in NHs. A decentralized approach for research in elderly persons in NHs offers a significant advantage in aligning study procedures into real-world patient care.

In this paper, we will describe the design, rationale and preliminary data of the first decentralized RCT testing OM-85 use in older adults at risk of RTIs living in multiple NHs across Italy. We will also highlight procedural problems affecting RCTs in NHs.

Methods

This ongoing study involves 13 NHs across Italy that had previously participated in the GeroCovid and GeroCovid Vax initiatives [11, 15]. These NH facilities consented to participate in this present investigator-initiated, decentralized, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled Phase IV clinical trial.

Fondazione Policlinico Universitario Campus Bio-Medico (Rome, Italy) is the coordinating center for this study. All participating NHs serve as clinical trial centers for study recruitment, enrollment, OM-85 administration, safety control, and follow-up assessments. The study protocol procedure used a decentralized approach between the coordinating center and each NH with specific responsibilities reported in signed contracts between each NH and the coordinating center. The coordinating center is responsible for the randomization, labeling and shipping of the drug/placebo to each NH. The coordinating center is also responsible for the online training of all investigator staff at NHs for the correct selection and registration of participants (informed consent), drug administration protocol, clinical data collection, clinical monitoring, use of data collection forms for baseline and at each follow up, and web-based data registration. Each NH is responsible for the research activities which are object of the training by the coordinating center. Finally, the coordinating center will provide continuous quality control and will manage data and scientific production, but NH researchers will be activley involved in the scientific production.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Territoriale Lazio Area 2 on September 16th, 2024. Clinical Universal Trial Number (UTN) is U1111-1306-8882. Study timelines include: (i) 15-month enrollment period, (ii) 6-month treatment period, (iii) 6-month follow up period. Assessments are conducted at baseline and at 3-month intervals for 12 months.

The clinical trial is being conducted in accordance with the protocol, Regulation [EU] 536/2014 and the principles of good clinical practice. Study protocol began in December 2024 and is following both European and Italian clinical guidelines of the decentralized approach in NHs [16].

Sample size

In alignment with the primary objective, sample size calculation was based on findings reported by Orcel et al. [7] in a study involving 300 individuals at high risk for RTIs. The study demonstrated that OM-85 administration was associated with a 28% reduction in the incidence of RTIs and an approximately 30% reduction in concomitant drug use. Accordingly, we hypothesize a 30% reduction in RTI rate in the active treatment group compared to an expected incidence of 2 RTIs per person per year in the control group. Given that the assumption of event independence may not hold for recurrent RTIs, we modeled the primary outcome using a negative binomial distribution to account for overdispersion (shape parameter = 1.5). Based on these assumptions, a sample size of 149 participants was calculated to provide 80% power to detect the effect size, using a two-sided test with type I error rate (α) of 5%, as described by Zhu [18]. Given the characteristics of this trial target population, an attrition rate of approximately 20% was considered in the calculation. To compensate for potential loss to follow-up and missing data, a maximum number of 360 participants was planned for enrollment.

This sample size will also be adequate for analysis of the secondary outcome – rate of hospital and emergency room (ER) admissions. Under the assumption of an event rate of 0.3 hospital/ER admissions per person per year in the control group (a conservative estimate reflecting only RTI-related admissions, as reported by Moyo et al. [19], a sample of this size will provide 80% power to detect a 25% reduction in the event rate, with a type I error rate of 5%.

Intervention

As previously described in the literature, the 6-month treatment phase in our study was implemented as two sequential 3-month treatment cycles [20, 21]. Participants undergoing protocol have been allocated according to 1:1 randomization performed with computer-generated scheme stratified by center to either OM-85 (7 mg) or placebo treatment. Both groups took one capsule every morning on an empty stomach for the first 10 days of each month for 6 months.

Study procedure

The advantage of the decentralized design of this study is that elderly patient recruitment can be conducted directly in the NH, enhancing feasibility and representativeness of the population.

At baseline, trained physicians at each NH collected sociodemographic information, details on cohabitation conditions within the facility, medical history (including chronic health conditions, current medications, respiratory infections in the past year, vaccination history, and any drug intolerances), as well as performed Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA). Follow-up assessments are performed every three months over the 12-month study period (6 months of treatment and 6 months of post-treatment observation). At each visit, CGA is performed, and medical history data are collected, including incidence of RTIs, rates of hospitalizations and emergency room admissions, antibiotic use, and incidence of adverse events related to investigational medicinal products (placebo or OM-85).

The diagnosis of upper and lower RTIs is established based according to McGeer criteria [22], specifically recognized for use in Long-term care facilities that includes the presence of clinical symptoms – cough or sputum production- with or without associated features such as sore throat, fever, dyspnea, shortness of breath, or chest discomfort/pain, as well as, radiological evidence from chest X-ray or high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT).

Data quality

On-site assessments are regularly performed at each NH to ensure completeness of data collection. External oversight is provided by an external clinical trial monitor. The Coordinating Center tracks performance of NH sites using the REDCap platform, which is specifically designed for data collection in medical research (software compliant with GDPR and data protection standards). All investigators involved in this protocol have undergone standardized training for proficient use of the REDCap platform data capture system. The Italian Society of Gerontology and Geriatrics (SIGG) is the supervisor and guarantor of study data management conducted through REDCap platform software [23]. REDCap SIGG is hosted on a dual server: the first server is accessible via web, and it contains a mask that allows users to upload data. In particular, the server that handles data entry by duly authenticated investigators is separate from the server where the data are stored in encrypted SQL format. This type of electronic based system allows for real-time data entry and dynamic data reports related to study enrollment.

Safety monitoring

Safety is evaluated based on the incidence of treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs). Any adverse drug reaction will be systematically assessed at each clinical visit and registered to the medical review board. All sites monitor and report adverse events to the coordinating center (Fondazione Policlinico Universitario Campus Bio-Medico). A serious adverse event (SAE) is defined as any event that is fatal or life-threatening, results in significant or persistent disability, necessitates hospitalization, or constitutes a medically important event as judged by the investigator that poses a significant risk of harm to the participant and may require medical intervention.

Discussion

This is the first pharmacological and decentralized RCT testing OM-85 use in older adults (75 years and older) living in NHs. RCTs drug trials in older patients remain scarce, despite the disproportionate health burden and continuous demographic rise of this population [24]. Recent data regarding NH residency in the US, clearly demonstrates a steadily rising increase from 1,098,305 in 2021 to 1,204,260 in 2023 [25]. Recruiting older adults, especially those living in NHs, remains a significant challenge for RCTs. The decentralized approach of this protocol allows for NH onsite patient participation in a RCT, onsite assessments with specialized researchers, real world data collection and supports personal patient care in the care setting of mostly frail elderly.

NH residents typically exhibit higher levels of physical disability, greater prevalence of cognitive impairment, increased multimorbidity and frailty compared to community-dwelling older adults. These characteristics contribute to their heightened vulnerability to adverse health outcomes, especially RTI-related outcomes, and functional decline. RTIs can represent a serious health threat for older persons, especially for those living in a long-term care facility, such as the NH. Consequently, identifying effective preventive interventions to enhance immune responses against infections in this population is of critical importance. The COVID-19 pandemic clearly evidenced the consequences of immunodeficiency in this vulnerable population. The GeroCovid Vax initiative network underlined that NHs should be the object of special interest for medical research due to their constantly adapting role in elderly care. The GeroCovid Vax project reached a high level of precision care in NH care by testing the efficacy and safety of COVID-19 vaccination in older NH residents. The study followed precise operational research standards (trained personnel for study involvement, including expertise in data management and quality control) and assessed environmental safety and immune response to vaccination [12]. In addition, as a collateral finding, the use of OM-85 was found to be associated with a 66.7% decrease in RTIs over 2 years of observation in a small sample of home dwelling subjects [26]. Thus, the GerCovid Vax initiative demonstrated that a high-quality research on RTI is feasible in NH, paving the way for this ongoing trial.

Available vaccines protect against selected agents of RTI (Influenza, S. pneumoniae, RSV, Covid-19), while there is evidence that a variety of bacteria and viruses is responsible for an important fraction of RTIs [27]. Bacterial lysates, such as OM-85 contain conserved pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) of 21 bacterial strains from 8 bacterial species (Streptococcus pneumoniae - Streptococcus pyogenes - Streptococcus viridans - Staphylococcus aureus - Klebsiella pneumoniae - Klebsiella ozaenae - Moraxella catarrhalis - Haemophilus influenza). There is strong evidence that OM-85 is correlated with an immunopotentiating effect on both cellular and humoral responses [28, 29]. Thus, the use of OM-85 could complement recommended vaccines by broadening the range of pathogens covered, particularly in the microbiological spectrum. For example, it could provide further aid against Streptococcus pneumoniae by promoting a different immunologic response and contrasting strains not targeted by available vaccines. As previously mentioned, the clinical data regarding immune response in older frail adults remains scarce and those based on a RCT limited to older institutionalized adults (> 65 years of age) with chronic bronchitis [7].

Our main objective is to expand evidence supporting the use of OM-85 in this older population at risk of RTIs and related negative outcomes (including hospitalization, mortality, functional decline).

In addition, acute care admission for negative health issues in frail compared to robust older adults poses an important burden on health care costs [30]. Our study aims to investigate whether OM85 use can prevent RTI-related acute care admissions already plagued with many health risks for older frail adults. Indeed, delirium, infections, falls in older frail adults may jeopardize the safety of these patients in acute care wards.

Currently, the OM-85 study is completing enrollment and the preliminary safety of the intervention of the currently enrolled participants is well-established. Our team of investigators is focused on participant adherence to drug study protocol, monitoring safety, and ensuring high quality data collection. The study protocol has important challenges for the coordination center (IMP randomization and distribution) and each NH study site (recruitment, adherence to protocol, drop out, etc.). However, this study paves the way to a shift in clinical research on NH residents by providing information regarding the problems inherent to a trial in this setting and thus, easing the planning of future research. Collaterally, this study provides interesting information about the NHs population: based on preliminary data, women are significantly older than men, have lower cognitive scores, take more daily medications, and have a higher prevalence of diabetes and heart failure.

Conclusions and implications

Our study has notable strengths. It represents the first decentralized randomized controlled drug trial conducted in adults aged ≥ 75 years residing in NHs and at risk for RTIs. The trial is made possible thanks to the network of the Gerocovid and Gerovax initiatives [10–12]. Furthermore, continuous protocol oversight and systematic data collection procedures will ensure high-quality data for robust outcome assessment (Box 2). Nevertheless, as the first RCT in NHs, this study might face unprecedented procedural and methodological problems. However, such intrinsic limitation may also be considered a strength because the derived information will expand our knowledge of RCTs in NHs.
