Metabolomic Profiling of Tumor Tissues Unveils Metabolic Shifts in Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer Patients with Concurrent Diabetes Mellitus
Authors: Xiaohong Lyu, Yujue Wang, Yuan Xu, Zhewei Zhao, Hongsheng Liu, Zeping Hu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
A comprehensive understanding of the exact influence of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) on the metabolic status of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is still lacking. This study explores metabolic alterations in tumor tissues among patients with coexisting NSCLC and T2DM in comparison with NSCLC patients. A combined approach of clinical analysis and metabolomics was employed, including 20 NSCLC patients and 20 NSCLC+T2DM patients. Targeted metabolomics analysis was performed on tumor tissues using the liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS) approach. A clear segregation was observed between NSCLC+T2DM and matched NSCLC tissue samples in Orthogonal Partial Least Squares Discrimination Analysis (OPLS-DA). Furthermore, the levels of 7 metabolites are found to be significantly different between diabetes/nondiabetes tumor tissue samples. The related pathways included arginine biosynthesis, glutathione metabolism, arginine and proline metabolism, purine metabolism, biotin metabolism, and histidine metabolism. 3-Phenyllactic acid, carnitine-C5, carnitine-C12, and serotonin showed a positive linear correlation with fasting blood glucose levels in NSCLC patients. Uridine, pipecolic acid, cytosine, and fasting blood glucose levels were found to have a negative correlation. Our results suggest that NSCLC patients with concurrent T2DM exhibit distinct metabolic shifts in tumor tissues compared to those of solely NSCLC patients.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) and cancer represent two prominent global health challenges. Individuals with DM have a 20–25% higher risk of developing cancer than those without diabetes.1 According to the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) Diabetes Atlas, the global prevalence of diabetes among people aged 20 to 79 years was 10.5% (about 536.6 million people) in 2021 and is projected to rise to 12.2% (about 783.2 million people) by 2045.2 Among its manifold impacts, type 2 DM (T2DM) is a known risk factor for various types of cancer, including non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma, breast, colorectal,3 liver, pancreatic,4 and bladder cancer.1

Lung cancer stands as the foremost cause of cancer-related deaths globally, with the incidence of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) projected to increase, especially among individuals with chronic diseases like T2DM.5,6 Approximately 2.5–15.7% of NSCLC patients are estimated to have coexisting T2DM,7,8 potentially influencing the clinical outcomes of NSCLC patients.9−14 Therefore, it is crucial to thoroughly investigate the connections and underlying molecular mechanisms between T2DM and NSCLC to effectively address this potential healthcare challenge.

Metabolomics has emerged as a promising tool for investigating the metabolic alterations associated with various cancers, including NSCLC.15,16 While some studies have highlighted variations in serum metabolomics between NSCLC patients and those with NSCLC and T2DM, these findings do not accurately reflect the genuine metabolic alterations within lung cancer tumor cells in a hyperglycemic microenvironment.17 Although previous studies have used metabolomics to analyze tumor tissue metabolic changes in hepatocellular carcinoma patients with concurrent diabetes, to the best of our knowledge, no studies have employed surgically resected lung cancer tumor tissue to conduct metabolomic analyses aimed at elucidating the influence of diabetes on the tumor metabolism of non-small cell lung cancer patients.

The objective of this study was to investigate the metabolic alterations associated with NSCLC in patients with T2DM by integrating clinical data with metabolomics analyses. We hypothesized that NSCLC patients with T2DM would exhibit distinct alterations in specific metabolic pathways, such as glucose metabolism, lipid metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and other pathways, compared to nondiabetic NSCLC patients. To test our hypotheses, we conducted comprehensive clinical data and metabolomics analyses on NSCLC patients with and without T2DM and subsequently compared their respective metabolic profiles.

Methods

Subjects

The study population comprised 40 NSCLC patients who required surgical treatment at the Department of Thoracic Surgery in the Peking Union Medical College Hospital between August 2022 and February 2023. The study included two groups (Figure 1): NSCLC patients with T2DM (NSCLC+T2DM, n = 20) and NSCLC patients without T2DM (only NSCLC, n = 20). The two groups were matched in age and gender. The main inclusion criteria are: T2DM in accordance with the 1999 WHO diagnostic criteria; aged between 18 and 80 years; 18 ≤ body mass index (BMI) ≤ 30 kg/m2; all patients were histopathologically confirmed as adenocarcinoma. Exclusion criteria included: Patients with a past history of other cancers, pneumonia, and any other site’s acute inflammation, hypertension, acute heart infarction, severe hepatic lesion, renal dysfunction, severe anemia, and other serious situations. All patients provided informed consent for tissue sample collection, and the Institutional Review Board of Peking Union Medical College Hospital approved the study (I-22PJ325).

Experimental scheme of the study. NSCLC, non-small cell lung cancer; T2DM, type 2 diabetes mellitus.

Tumor Tissue Sample Collection and Preparation

Primary NSCLC tumor tissue samples were collected from patients undergoing surgery at Peking Union Medical College Hospital. Tumor tissue was taken from the lung cancer tumor lesion and determined intraoperatively, and the largest diameter of the removed tumor tissue for analysis was about 5 mm. The remaining tissues were all confirmed by the pathology department that the tumor tissue was NSCLC. Samples were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until analysis. Metabolite Extraction

The metabolite extraction method was described previously.18 In brief, approximately 50 mg of tumor tissue sample was weighed and homogenized in 1 mL of ice-cold 80% methanol. After homogenization, 100 μL of homogeneous solution was mixed with 900 μL of ice-cold 80% methanol. The mixture was then vortexed and centrifuged at 15,000g for 15 min at 4 °C. 900 μL of supernatant was then transferred to a new tube. To achieve a better recovery, the leftover pellet was resuspended with 500 μL of ice-cold 80% methanol, centrifuged at 15,000g for 15 min at 4 °C, and combined with the previous 900 μL supernatant. 700 μL of the combined supernatant was transferred to a new tube, and all samples were dried together in a speed vacuum concentrator set up at room temperature for up to 4 h. The dried metabolite pellets were kept at −80 °C until LC-MS analysis. Quality control (QC) samples were prepared by pooling 10 μL of homogenate of each tissue sample. Pretreatment of QC samples was paralleled and the same as that of the study samples.

Targeted Metabolomics

The metabolite extraction method was described previously.18 In brief, all 40 samples were analyzed in the same batch. QC samples (pooled from all 40 samples) were evenly inserted between every 10 samples to monitor the instrumentation stability and subsequently used to correct intrabatch variations. Four of the QC samples were run at the beginning of the sequence to ensure the equilibrium had been reached. Dried metabolites were reconstituted in 100 μL of 0.03% formic acid in analytical-grade water, vortex-mixed, and centrifuged at 15,000g for 15 min at 4 °C to remove debris. Thereafter, the supernatant was transferred to a high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) vial for the metabolomics study. Targeted metabolite profiling was performed using a liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry/mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) approach.19−21 Chromatographic separation was achieved on an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 × 150 mm, 1.8 μm) using an ACQUITY UPLC I-Class system (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA). The mobile phases employed were 0.03% formic acid in water (A) and 0.03% formic acid in acetonitrile (B), and the gradient program was as follows: 0–3 min, 1% B; 3–15 min, 1–99% B; 15–17 min, 99% B; 17–17.1 min, 99–1% B; 17.1–20 min, 1% B. The flow rate was set as 0.25 mL/min. The temperatures of the column and autosampler were set to 35 and 4 °C, respectively. Mass data acquisition was performed using an AB QTRAP 6500+ triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (SCIEX, Framingham, MA,USA) in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode for the detection of 258 unique endogenous water-soluble metabolites as previously described,18 with the retention time of each metabolite calibrated using a standard mixture. Data were acquired in both positive and negative ion modes. Chromatogram review and peak area integration were performed using MultiQuant 3.0.2 (SCIEX, Framingham, MA). The coefficient of variation (CV%) of QC samples is an important indicator of the data quality. In this study, the CV% of each metabolite in QC samples was below 30% (median: 5.6%), demonstrating the reliability of the data.

Data Analysis and Preprocessing

QC samples were inserted in an interval of ten test samples to monitor the stability of the instrument and normalize the variations during the run. Therefore, it can serve as an additional QC measure of analytical performance and a reference for normalizing raw metabolomics data across the samples. The detailed process was performed as previously described.18 Briefly, the mean peak area of each metabolite from all QC samples in all given batches (Mean Area), as well as the mean peak area of each metabolite from the QC samples that were the most adjacent to a given group of test samples (Adj Area), was first calculated. The ratio between these two mean peak areas for each metabolite was computed by dividing the same Mean Area by each Adj Area and was used as the normalization factor for each given group of test samples. The peak area of each metabolite from each test sample was normalized by multiplying the corresponding normalization ratio to obtain the normalized peak areas to remove potential instrumental variations.

Statistical Analysis

The quantification and statistical analysis methods for metabolomic analysis and modeling evaluation were described in the Results, figure legends, and corresponding Method subsections. For Metabolic differential analysis, all 40 samples were analyzed by Wilcoxon rank-sum test (FDR < 0.05 and fold change >1.25 or <0.8). Pathway analysis was performed using MetaboAnalyst 5.0.22 For Figure 3, we used metabolites which have P < 0.05 in the Wilcoxon rank-sum test to find related pathways. For Figure 4, FDR < 0.05 in the Wilcoxon rank-sum test is considered statistically significant. For metabolite correlation (Figure 5), the association between tumor tissue metabolites and blood tumor markers (CEA, SCCAg, NSE) was analyzed using the “lm” function in R to perform a t test in Linear Regression. P < 0.05 is considered statistically significant. For the Orthogonal Partial Least Squares Discrimination Analysis (OPLS-DA), we used R package “mixOmics” for the analysis. In brief, a cross-validation method with 100 permutations was applied to assess the model’s predictive accuracy. The OPLS-DA model’s parameters, including R2 and Q2 values, were reported to indicate the model’s explanatory and predictive capabilities, respectively. Box plots were annotated with mean values and standard deviations to provide a clear understanding of the data distribution. GraphPad Prism (v.9.0), R (v.3.6.0) software (https://www.r-project.org/), and Python (v.3.7.4) were used to conduct tests.

Results

General Characteristics of the Study Population

The basic clinical characteristics of the study subjects were presented in Table 1. This study included 22 males and 18 females. The median size of resected lung tumors in the NSCLC+T2DM group was slightly smaller than that in the NSCLC group (1.9 IQR 1.5–2.1 cm vs 2.5 IQR 1.6–3.0 cm, P = 0.048). The median fasting glucose level in the NSCLC+T2DM group was 6.9 mmol/L (IQR 6.3–8.4), which was significantly higher than the NSCLC group (5.6 mmol/L, IQR 5.2–6.2) (P < 0.001). All the patients were T1N0M0 stage.

Metabolic Profiling of Human NSCLC with/without T2DM Tumor Tissues

To investigate the potential metabolic differences in tumors from NSCLC patients with and without T2DM, we analyzed 40 human NSCLC samples: 20 with NSCLC+T2DM and 20 with NSCLC only. Approximately 161 biochemicals of known identity were ascertained for each sample. Our investigation employed Orthogonal Partial Least Squares Discrimination Analysis (OPLS-DA) to visualize the metabolic distinctions. As depicted in Figure 2A, this approach effectively separated NSCLC+T2DM from NSCLC-only tissue samples (R2 = 0.96, Q2 = 0.75). Furthermore, we identified 7 metabolites that displayed differential levels between diabetes/nondiabetes tumor tissue samples (Figure 2B).

Dysregulated Metabolites in Diabetic/Nondiabetic NSCLC Tumor Tissues

In our study, we identified differential metabolites between diabetes and nondiabetes NSCLC tumor tissue samples. The levels of six metabolites were found to be elevated in diabetes NSCLC tumors compared to nondiabetes NSCLC tumors, including putrescine, nicotinate, carnitine-C12, 5-hydroxylysine, biotin, and 2,3-dihydroxybenzoic acid, as shown in Figure 4. Conversely, we observed that cytosine, among others, was the metabolite most significantly decreased in NSCLC tumor tissues in diabetic patients (Figure 4). Discussion

Metabolomics has emerged as a promising tool for investigating metabolic alterations associated with pancreatic cancer and diabetes in order to facilitate an early diagnosis. In our study, we aimed to explore the metabolic profiles of NSCLC patients with diabetes in comparison to those with NSCLC alone, utilizing liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry-based tumor tissue metabolomics analysis. Our findings may provide valuable insights into the metabolic mechanisms underlying the association between diabetes and NSCLC. These insights may hold significant implications for the development of novel therapeutic strategies for both conditions.

Our study takes a pioneering step by delving into the analysis of tumor metabolomics in NSCLC patients, with a focus on the presence of diabetes as a comorbidity. While previous studies have examined metabolomic differences in tumor tissue for hepatocellular carcinoma with or without comorbid T2DM,24 metabolomics has also proven instrumental in identifying pancreatic cancer and T2DM, encompassing the exploration of various biomarkers.25−28 Concerning lung cancer, earlier research has utilized plasma to initiate metabolomics investigations into the impact of comorbid diabetes on lung cancer.17 However, to the best of our knowledge, no preceding studies have employed surgically resected tumor tissue to carry out metabolomic analyses aimed at unraveling the influence of diabetes on lung cancer tumors.

In our study, we identified distinct metabolic profiles between NSCLC patients with and without comorbid diabetes. Moreover, we observed noteworthy correlations between specific metabolites and glucose levels in NSCLC patients. These connections shed light on hyperglycemia’s potential role in influencing tumor characteristics. Previous research has indicated that elevated level of glucose accumulation within cancer cells is linked to higher tumor grades, increased metastatic potential, and poorer survival rates.29 Supporting evidence has emerged from pathology30 and immunohistochemistry.31,32 A Japanese study demonstrated distinct clinical and pathological features in tumor tissue of the NSCLC+DM group compared to the sole NSCLC group.30 Notably, the DM group exhibited a higher number of deaths from other causes and significantly elevated postoperative hemoglobin A1c levels.30 An immunohistochemistry study highlighted that, in both histological types, the NSCLC+DM group displayed a significantly higher level of five markers (E-cadherin, HtrA1, TGF-β, IGF-1R, and vimentin) compared to the sole NSCLC.31 Another immunohistochemistry study indicated heightened level of IGF-1 receptor (IGF-1R) and insulin receptor substrate 2 (IRS-2) proteins in NSCLC patients with pre-existing DM, linked to early NSCLC.23 Cytological studies also provided insights into the potential influence of diabetes, suggesting its impact on tumor cell phenotypes, including epithelial-mesenchymal transition and alterations in the cytoskeleton.29,33 Additionally, chronic hyperglycemia’s association with the formation of advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) hints at its possible contribution to carcinogenesis in specific cases.34,35 Within the scope of metabolic exploration, our study’s insights introduce a new layer to the understanding of diabetes’s effect on NSCLC. This hints at its potential influence on tumor characteristics, opening avenues for further investigations.

Remarkably, the four metabolites identified in our study, showing positive associations with fasting glucose levels in all of the NSCLC patients, have previously emerged in cancer-related investigations. For instance, while 3-phenyllactic acid has been integrated into a composite diagnostic model for prostate cancer diagnosis,36 to the best of our knowledge, its role in lung cancer has not been reported, although the metabolite may be associated with the cancer itself and not with the source of the cancer. In contrast, carnitine has emerged as a potential biomarker combination for distinguishing NSCLC diagnosis from healthy controls.37−39 Recent studies presented an impressive area under the curve (AUC) value of 0.99, sensitivities (SEs) of 0.98, and specificities (SPs) of 0.99 for six biomarkers, including carnitine.37 Notably, levels of metabolites such as carnitine exhibited significant reductions in NSCLC patients following treatment with EGFR-TKI or PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors40 and chemotherapeutic agents.41 Although our study presents fatty acylcarnitines (including carnitine-C5 and carnitine-C12) and not just carnitine, previous findings may still somewhat suggest an association between carnitine-related metabolomics and NSCLC. Additionally, serotonin has been recognized as a tumor cell growth factor, with its receptors potentially serving as therapeutic targets.42 The 5-HT7 receptor, in particular, has been implicated in promoting proliferation, migration, and invasion in NSCLC cells.42 In addition, we also calculated the nicotinate-to-glutamate ratio of the two groups in the supplementary file. As expected, the nicotinate-to-glutamate ratio was higher in the NSCLC+T2DM group than in the only NSCLC group. To some extent, the ratio may also serve as a diagnostic marker as a reference. Our findings suggest that 3-phenyllactic acid, carnitine-C5, carnitine-C12, and serotonin may serve as potential metabolic markers, warranting further investigation into their roles in linking diabetes to NSCLC occurrence, development, and metastasis.

The correlation analysis between glucose and representative metabolic profiles separately for each study group were performed (Tables S2–S4). The results of the correlation analysis were different for the two groups of patients. This phenomenon may reflect the influence of the hyperglycemic environment on the metabolic profile of NSCLC tumors. Hyperglycemia can promote tumor development and progression through various mechanisms, such as inducing insulin resistance, promoting inflammatory responses, and increasing oxidative stress.43−46 These changes may lead to metabolic reprogramming in tumor cells to adapt to the hyperglycemic microenvironment. Studies have shown that hyperglycemia can upregulate the expression of glucose transporters and glycolytic enzymes in tumor cells, enhancing their glycolytic metabolism.47 Additionally, hyperinsulinemia can promote tumor cell proliferation and survival by activating the insulin/IGF-1 signaling pathway.48 Therefore, the observation of more metabolites correlating with glucose levels in the NSCLC+T2DM group may be attributed to the combined effects of hyperglycemia and hyperinsulinemia driving alterations in tumor metabolism. In contrast, the NSCLC group with normal glucose levels may experience less severe metabolic dysregulation, and their tumor metabolism may be less influenced by the glucose levels.

However, we acknowledge that the sample size in our study is relatively small, which is a common challenge in studies requiring highly specific patient cohorts. The rarity of cases with concurrent NSCLC and T2DM limits the availability of suitable samples. Despite this, the samples used in our research were of high quality and directly relevant to the disease pathology. Future studies with larger sample sizes are needed to confirm our findings and provide more comprehensive insights. Furthermore, in the current study, all included patients were classified with T1N0M0 stage tumors, which are defined as early stage lesions with a maximum diameter of less than 3 cm. This criterion was intentionally chosen to reduce heterogeneity and to focus on the impact of diabetes mellitus on the NSCLC prognosis at an early stage. However, we recognize that tumor size is an important prognostic factor, and our selection criteria may limit the generalizability of the results. To address the potential confounding effect of tumor size, future studies aim to include a more diverse range of tumor stages.

Conclusion

Our study serves as a pioneering exploration of tumor metabolomics in NSCLC patients, comparing those with and without comorbid diabetes. The results imply that T2DM could potentially contribute to an altered metabolic landscape in NSCLC tumor tissues, thereby influencing the trajectory of tumor development. It is crucial to recognize that further investigations are imperative to validate these findings and uncover the precise mechanisms underlying these metabolic disparities. These insights may lay the foundation for innovative approaches to address the intricate interplay between T2DM and NSCLC, potentially opening avenues for novel therapeutic strategies in the future.
