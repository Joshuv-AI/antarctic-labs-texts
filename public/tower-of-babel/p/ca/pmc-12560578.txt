Self-reported mental distress in the United States: a Bayesian analysis of the spatial structure over the COVID-19 pandemic across age groups
Authors: Carles Comas, Albert Martínez, Angel Blanch
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe COVID-19 had an outstanding impact on well-being and mental health, which might have elicited geographical variations over time. This study examines the eventual impact of COVID-19 on self-reported mental distress in the mainland USA.AimsThere were two main aims. First, to evaluate the pre-pandemic (2019; ) and post-pandemic (2021; ) mental distress spatial distribution. Second, to contrast spatial data across three age groups, young (18–44 years), middle-aged (45–65 years), and old (older than 65 years).MethodWe considered a the Bayesian modified Besag–York–Molliè (BYM2) model, which is a Bayesian hierarchical model. Introduction

The COVID-19 global outbreak combines a low predictability with a considerable impact on important areas of human activity such as work, education, and family [20], and including unprecedented deleterious effects on the population general well-being and mental health. Most affected mental conditions and symptoms contemplate post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), anxiety, and depression. Moreover, the COVID-19 appears as being more harmful to the most vulnerable people, while bearing notable variations depending on geographical areas and/or countries, and individual factors such as age [13]. Since the COVID-19 inception, several topics have been explored regarding its geographical and spatial distribution. These include spread and transmission mechanisms [2, 10], hospitalised cases and incidence issues [9], or personal and social values [12].

Whether the COVID-19 itself has had a meaningful impact on the spatial distribution of mental health, however, has been rather understudied. A recent study highlights that depression and suicidal behaviour increased especially in rural areas within North Carolina during the outbreak [19]. From this perspective, the COVID-19 can be viewed as a natural global experiment whereby to analyse eventual changes in the geographical patterns of mental health after a major epidemic event. In this study, we examined the geographical patterns of mental distress as indicator of mental health across the United States before and after the COVID-19. We analyse data extracted from the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) in 2019 and 2021, a survey that evaluates self-reported mental health in the United States, among several other factors. Moreover, we consider a general value of mental distress (a global value of this construct for the whole population), and three age groups, young (from 18 to 44 years old), middle-aged (from 45 to 65 years old), and old (above 65 years old).

The main objective of this study is to examine the spatial distribution of mental distress across states and assess whether it is influenced by age group, time period (pre- and post-COVID-19 pandemic), and the interaction between these two covariates. Specifically, we aim to quantify the effect of age group on the spatial variation of the mental distress, distinguishing between general, young, middle-aged, and older adults. We also address the temporal changes in the spatial distribution of mental distress by comparing the years 2019 (pre-pandemic) and 2021 (post-pandemic). The interaction effects between age group and year are also investigated to determine whether the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on mental distress varied between different age groups. Finally, we incorporate spatial dependence explicitly through a Bayesian Gaussian spatial model, allowing for the evaluation of spatially structured and unstructured effects across states.

The present study aims to provide novel insights into the use of advanced Bayesian spatial models to examine psychological variables. Specifically, we apply the Besag–York–Mollié (BYM2) model [4, 16] to assess variability in mental distress across years and age groups, with the goal of characterizing its national-level distribution, spatial dependence, and temporal trends.

Methods

Dataset

The data were derived from the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS), a periodic survey conducted by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), which adheres to scientific integrity policies and quality standards to ensure robust sampling methods. Two time points were considered, 2019 prior to the COVID-19, and 2021 after the COVID-19 outbreak. The data was summarized within a.csv file from the America’s Health Rankings® webpage (https://www.americashealthrankings.org), a partnership between the United Health Foundation and the American Public Health Association.

The BRFSS is a comprehensive telephone survey conducted on a yearly basis since 1984 with US residents aged 18 years or older. The BRFSS collects information about health-related risk behaviours, chronic health conditions, access to health care, and use of preventive health services in the 50 states, District of Columbia, Guam, and Puerto Rico. For the current study, we excluded the states of Alaska, Guam, Hawaii, and Puerto Rico (n = 51).

The 2019 survey includes landline (n = 149,047) and cell phone (n = 263,550) interviews, with a median response rate of 49.4% ranging between 37.3% and 73.1%. The 2021 survey includes landline (n = 117,793) and cell phone (n = 322,282) interviews, with a median response rate of 44% ranging between 23.5% and 60.5%. Data of BRFSS is collected on a monthly basis until July. The response rates, cooperation rates, and refusal rates for BRFSS are calculated using standards set by the American Association for Public Opinion Research (AAPOR). They can be found at state level and with further procedure details at the Annual Survey Data of the BRFSS website (https://www.cdc.gov/brfss/).

Mental health condition

The current study focused on the mental distress as mental health condition. It is reported as the percentage of people showing stress, depression and problems with emotions during fourteen or more days in the past 30 days in the 49 mainland states. This indicator was considered for the general population (general mental distress), and for three age groups, young (from 18 to 44 years old), middle-aged (from 45 to 65 years old), and old (above 65 years old). Mental health was tapped with the responses to the single question:

Now thinking about your mental health, which includes stress, depression, and problems with emotions, for how many days during the past 30 days was your mental health not good?

Answer could be from 1 to 30 (indicating the number of days), none, don’t know, or refuse to answer.

Statistical analysis

We analyzed the spatial structure of the mental distress construct and the impact of COVID-19 across different age groups. To this end, we considered the Bayesian modified Besag–York–Molliè (BYM2) model [4, 16], a Bayesian hierarchical model [3]. This approach permits to analyze the variability in the mental distress response variable as a function of a number of covariates, age group and year (before and after the COVID-19), as well as random effects to model residual variation unexplained by the covariates. In particular, this model can identify spatially structured and unstructured effects modeled by assuming a mixing parameter, which provides the proportion of the marginal variance explained by the spatially structured component and an unstructured component, respectively.

Results

Spatio-temporal patterns of mental distress

Figure 1 shows the spatial distribution of the general measure of mental distress in 2019 and 2021. Visually, there is a noticeable increase in the percentage of people reporting mental distress following the pandemic COVID-19. Moreover, in general population, spatial structures of mental distress were similar in both years. Neighbouring states shared similar values suggesting a positive dependent spatial structure. For instance, East-Central states had larger values than the rest of states, especially in 2021.

Spatial distributions of general mental health under the years 2019 (pre-pandemic) and 2021 (post-pandemic)

Figure 2 shows that for the three age groups and the two years under analysis, neighbouring states shared similar mental distress values, underlining a positive spatial dependence. This distribution of mental distress highlights a dependent spatial structure between neighbouring states regardless of age group and year (2019, 2021). Hence, states with either large or small values in mental distress, were more prone to be surrounded by states with large and small values, respectively. Note that the specific spatial configuration of this dependent spatial structure, states conforming a cluster, varied with age group and year. A general trend or pattern, however, remains uncertain. For the three age groups and the two years, Central-North and Central-East states showed smaller and larger values compared with the other states, respectively. An increase in the age of the respondents also implies a decrease in the percentages related to mental distress, independently of the year of analysis.

Results from the Gaussian spatial model

We modeled the mental distress construct using a Bayesian hierarchical model with spatial random effects. The model included year (2019 vs 2021), age group (general mental distress, young, middle-aged and old), and the year by age group interaction. The reference categories were the year 2019 and the general mental distress. All estimates are reported on the log-scale.

Discussion

In this study, we analyzed the impact of COVID-19 on the mental health of young, middle-aged, and older adults across the 49 mainland U.S. states, using mental distress as an indicator. The main objective was to examine the spatial distribution of mental distress across states and assess whether it was influenced by age group, time period (pre- and post-COVID-19 pandemic), and the interaction between these two covariates. Accordingly, we have studied the spatial dependence of mental distress across states and age groups before and after the pandemic, by building a Bayesian modified Besag-York-Molliè (BYM2) model whose parameters have been estimated via INLA method.

Main outcomes

The main outcomes indicate that the reported severity of mental distress symptoms increased after the COVID-19. In this regard, age groups showed noticeable differences, where older individuals reported less mental distress issues while younger individuals exhibited higher levels both before and after the COVID-19 pandemic. This is consistent with previous results where older adults reported lower levels of depression, anxiety and stress than younger adults even after controlling for adaptability and healthiness [23].

Moreover, younger adults experienced the greatest worsening in mental distress as a result of the pandemic, a finding that European countries have also reported and underscores that young adulthood is a high-risk life stage in which individuals suffered the largest increase in mental distress due to COVID-19 [8]. It has been suggested that younger adults experienced greater lifestyle changes during an already fragile and unstable life period. Examples include uncertainty in their personal and professional lives and life plans, social isolation, and, for those still in education, a sense of being deprived of their university experience [22, 23]. Furthermore, positive feedback mechanisms could differentially amplify pre-existing mental health distress [22]. In contrast, older adults are often retired and report more stable habits and lifestyles even if they deal with physical suffering or difficulties with the daily routine [23].

In this regard, a protective factor that should be taken into account is adaptability, which during the COVID-19 pandemic has been shown to be effective in maintaining well-being regardless of age, but being more determinant in younger adults [23]. Other factors such as personal and social focus values (PF-SF) evaluated at the state level are actually positively associated with the mitigation of COVID-19 severity [12].

At the spatial level, neighbouring states shared similar values of self-reported mental distress regardless of the age group and the year of analysis. Apparently, the spatial distribution of mental distress remains similar after the COVID-19, while the impact of the pandemic implied a general worsening of mental distress for most states. This outcome suggests that the spatial structure observed between neighbouring states may reflect mental health policies that transcend administrative boundaries while still being shaped by geographical influences. In fact, mental health policies in the United States are multifaceted and often involve various levels of government, as well as public and private initiatives. Such a complex framework can result in common strategies being implemented across multiple states. Because most states underwent an increase in mental distress after COVID-19, the spatial structure of this difference results in a very similar configuration of spatial values across most states. This could explain the lack of differences in spatial dependence when looking at the three age groups. Mental distress worsening seems to be rather widespread for the three age groups resulting in a general worsening of mental distress for most of the states regardless of their spatial location.

For instance, the general mental distress in East-Central states displayed larger values than the rest of states over time, 2019 and 2021. In this regard, evidence from North Carolina indicates an increased risk of depression and suicide due to COVID-19. North Carolina is predominantly rural, faces unique transportation challenges, has a relatively older population, and continues to struggle with an ongoing opioid epidemic [19]. The first two issues may also be present in neighboring states with similar orographic and climatic characteristics.

Practical implications

Several important lessons and policy implications can be drawn from the current study. First, we should reconsider the perception of older adults, who have shown the lowest levels of mental distress. Rather than viewing them as vulnerable, we can leverage their knowledge and life experience to strengthen social networks and provide guidance on coping with mental distress. Second, given that critical age stages, particularly younger adults experience more pronounced worsening of mental health, preventive campaigns should account for social determinants that differentially affect age groups. In the context of social isolation–more pronounced in predominantly rural states–developing community-based mental health services and fostering partnerships between community organizations and government initiatives, such as social prescribing, could complement individual-level care and strengthen population-level mental health [8]. Third, by capitalizing on spatial similarities in mental distress patterns, cross-state initiatives and knowledge exchange should be encouraged, as they may help build robust social networks and shared strategies for mental health promotion. Finally, improving the quality of mental health services and making them more user-friendly, with interventions adapted to each life stage, is essential [15].

Study limitations and further research

These outcomes are based on the BRFSS yearly survey, which is particularly useful to evaluate combined spatial and temporal dimensions addressing disease surveillance problems [17]. There are, however, some questions that should be borne in mind concerning the interpretation of the current findings.

The evaluation of the spatial dependence was conducted here with BYM2. It revealed substantial unstructured spatial effects which might be interpreted as we omitted important covariables affecting the distribution.

Although this analysis has considered the spatial distribution of mental distress before and after the COVID-19 pandemic over two years, 2019 and 2021, it might be useful to encompass additional years before and after the COVID-19 pandemic. Analyzing the space-time dynamics of COVID-19 over multiple years might provide a more comprehensive understanding of pandemic long term impact on mental health. Moreover, in this study we focus solely on age as a predictor to propose a parsimonious model, omitting several other significant variables that have been widely examined, such as income, social vulnerability, and the quality of health system performance [15].

The current dataset provides mental health data categorized within USA, which offers a broad overview of regional trends. A more detailed comprehension of the problem, however, could be gained from adapting the dataset to include information based on individual postal codes. This finer level of geographical description would allow for a more precise analysis, enabling researchers to pinpoint localized disparities and more specific nuances in mental health outcomes. By incorporating postal code information, analysts would be better able to identify specific areas within states where mental health resources may be lacking or where interventions are most urgently needed [19].

Another related issue involves the measurement of mental health with the BRFSS data. This indicator implied just the percentage of people who reported stress, depression and problems with emotions during fourteen or more days in the past 30 days of completing the survey. Whereas, the unique circumstances implied by the COVID-19 pandemic, have stirred the adaption of psychological assessment methods of mental health [1]. However, mental health is a complex multidimensional construct that implies a variety of individual problems (i.e., stress, depression, anxiety), and that can be activated or inhibited by contextual factors, for instance depending on counties [6] or even neighbourhoods [7].

Conclusions

The COVID-19 pandemic had an unprecedented impact on people’s lives, particularly on mental health. This study evaluated whether this unique event altered the spatial structure of self-reported mental distress in USA in general population and across different age groups. The findings indicate that, after the pandemic, the spatial structure of mental distress remained largely unchanged, with neighbouring states continuing to show similar levels of mental distress. Overall, the COVID-19 led to a general mental health deterioration. This increase in mental distress was especially marked among individuals aged 18–44, who showed both higher baseline levels and the greatest rise during the study period.
