Association Between Obstructive Sleep Apnea and Atopic Dermatitis in the Korean Adult Population: Results From the 2019–2022 Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey
Authors: Jung Min Lee, Wanhyung Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo examine the association between the risk of obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) and atopic dermatitis (AD), in addition to the dose-response relationship between the risk of OSA and the prevalence of AD.MethodsThis study analyzed data from 15,095 participants aged ≥ 40 years who were part of the 2019–2022 Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES). AD was assessed using self-reported questionnaires, and the risk of OSA was determined using the STOP-BANG score. Demographic distributions were analyzed by using χ2 tests, whereas adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using multivariable logistic regression analysis. The weighted prevalence of AD and 95% CIs were calculated to account for the complex sampling design of KNHANES.ResultsMultivariable logistic regression revealed a significant association between OSA risk and AD, with an OR of 2.159 (95% CI, 1.456–3.202). The weighted prevalence of AD increased with higher OSA risk, from 1.43% in the low-risk group to 1.74% in the intermediate-risk group, and 2.17% in the high-risk group (P value for trend = 0.013).ConclusionsOSA risk was significantly associated with AD prevalence, having a dose-response relationship. Given the observed associations, incorporating OSA management into clinical strategies may warrant further investigation as a potential avenue for mitigating AD-related risk.

INTRODUCTION

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a chronic recurrent, and multifactorial inflammatory skin disease characterized by pruritus, with clinical heterogeneity depending on onset age, lesion morphology, lesion distribution, disease severity, and long-term persistence.12 In AD, impaired epidermal barrier function could lead to increased transepidermal water loss and greater susceptibility to microbes and allergens.3456 Notably, impaired antimicrobial defense mechanisms, particularly against pathogens such as Staphylococcus aureus, are a hallmark of AD.7 A key feature of AD is the upregulation of Th2 cytokines and chemokines, which drive tissue inflammation and suppress the expression of proteins critical for epidermal differentiation and lipid synthesis.8 Several factors aggravate AD, including age, environmental conditions, lifestyles, cosmetic use, dietary habits, stresses, and particularly sleep disturbances.9101112

Sleep disturbances are a common and impactful consequence of AD, exacerbating disease severity and impairing quality of life. These disruptions are bidirectionally linked to the disease, with nocturnal pruritus and cytokine-driven inflammation disrupting sleep architecture, and poor sleep quality exacerbating the systemic inflammatory burden.1314 This interplay extends beyond dermatological outcomes, affecting broader immunological and neuroendocrine homeostasis.1516 Notably, the potential influence of obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), a prevalent sleep disorder characterized by intermittent hypoxia and fragmented sleep, on AD remains underexplored. OSA triggers systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and sympathetic activation, all of which may contribute to AD immunopathogenesis.1718

Although previous studies have highlighted the association between sleep disorders and immune dysfunction, few have systematically examined the relationship between OSA and AD, particularly in adults. Moreover, the STOP-BANG questionnaire, a widely validated tool for accessing OSA risk, offers an opportunity to explore this relationship in a large-scale population-based context. This 8-item tool (yes/no questions) includes 4 self-reported factors (STOP: snoring, tiredness, observed apnea, and high blood pressure) and 4 demographic parameters (BANG: body mass index [BMI], age, neck circumference, and gender).19

Our study addresses notable gaps in the literature. While many studies on AD focus on children, we investigated adults aged ≥ 40 years, addressing the lack of data on adult patients with AD. Additionally, we evaluated OSA risk using the STOP-BANG questionnaire, emphasizing its association with AD in adults. Moreover, by utilizing nationally representative survey data, our study provides a comprehensive analysis of various risk factors contributing to this relationship.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data collection and study participants

This study utilized data from the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES) conducted between 2019 and 2022. The KNHANES employs a carefully designed sampling method and size calculation to ensure that its findings accurately reflect the entire Korean population, making the results serve as a representative of Korea’s overall population for each survey year.20 The survey was conducted over 4 years with participant numbers distributed as follows: 8,110 in 2019, 7,359 in 2020, 7,090 in 2021, and 6,265 in 2022. Individuals aged below 40 years, those without STOP-BANG scores, and those with missing data were excluded from the analysis. The study focused on individuals aged ≥ 40 years, incorporating a comprehensive range of variables, including sex, age, educational level, household income, current smoking status, high-risk drinking behavior, BMI, occupation, aerobic physical activity, risk of OSA, and history of AD (Fig. 1).

Flow chart of the study design.

OSA risk and STOP-BANG questionnaire

The risk of OSA was assessed using the STOP-BANG questionnaire, a validated tool for identifying individuals at high-risk for OSA. The questionnaire consists of 8 items: snoring, tiredness or daytime sleepiness, observed apneas or choking, hypertension, obesity (BMI > 35 kg/m2), age (> 50 years), neck circumference (> 40 cm), and sex (male). Participants were categorized into 2 groups: low-risk for OSA (“No”) and high-risk for OSA (“Yes”). Individuals were classified as high-risk (“Yes”) for OSA if they met at least one of the following criteria: (a) answered “yes” to 5 or more questions, or (b) answered “yes” to 2 or more of the “STOP” questions and met at least one of the following conditions: BMI > 35 kg/m2, neck circumference > 40 cm, or male sex.212223 Those who did not meet these criteria were classified as low-risk (“No”) for OSA.

Assessment of AD

The presence or absence of AD was determined using a single question from the health interview: “Have you ever been diagnosed with AD by a physician?” Participants who responded “yes” were categorized into the AD group. However, the assessment of AD using self-reported data has several limitations. It relies on the memory of the participants, which introduces recall bias, particularly for mild or past episodes of AD. Additionally, the diagnosis is subjective, as individuals may interpret symptoms and severity differently. The lack of clinical validation by healthcare professionals for self-reported AD further increases the risk of misclassification. Despite these limitations, self-reported AD has demonstrated acceptable validity in epidemiological studies. It strongly correlates with clinical diagnoses and is linked to outcomes such as asthma, allergic rhinitis, and mental health disorders, making it a valuable tool for understanding the prevalence and impact of AD.2425

Covariates

The analysis considered a range of covariates, including age, categorized into 3 groups (40–49, 50–59, and ≥ 60 years); sex (male or female); educational attainment grouped as elementary school or lower, middle school, high school, or college and above; household income divided into quartiles (1st, 2nd, 3rd, and 4th quartiles); current smoking status classified as yes or no; high-risk drinking behavior,defined as consuming an average of ≥ 7 drinks per occasion for men and ≥ 5 drinks for women, at least twice per week26; occupational status categorized as none or working; BMI categorized based on the Asian population-specific cutoff values into < 18.5 kg/m2, 18.5–25 kg/m2, and ≥ 25 kg/m2 based on the cutoff values recommended for the Asian population27; and aerobic physical activity, defined as performing at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week, at least 75 minutes of vigorous-intensity aerobic activity per week, or a combination of both intensities (where 1 minute of vigorous activity equals 2 minutes of moderate activity) with equal time allocated to each activity.28

Statistical analysis

A χ2 test was performed to examine the distribution of demographic characteristics among participants with AD. The association between the risk for OSA and AD was assessed using multivariate logistic regression analysis, which yielded adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The analysis accounted for covariates including sex, age, educational level, household income, smoking status, high-risk drinking behavior, BMI, occupation, and aerobic physical activity. We conducted further analyses using weighted prevalence to explore the association between OSA risk and AD, incorporating a dose-response relationship. OSA risk was categorized into low, intermediate, or high groups, according to the total STOP-BANG score, to reflect varying levels of OSA severity. The weighted prevalence and 95% CIs of AD were calculated using a sampling clustering weight that accounted for the complex sampling design of the KNHANES. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). A 2-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

The KNHANES 2019–2022 dataset included 28,824 participants, of whom 17,872 were individuals aged ≥ 40 years with available STOP-BANG scores. After excluding cases with missing data, a total of 15,333 individuals were included in the final analysis. Table 1 summarizes the general characteristics of participants with AD. The overall prevalence of AD in the study population aged ≥ 40 years was 1.55%. Prevalence varied by age group, with 2.11% in those under 49 years, 1.42% in individuals aged 50–59 years, and 1.36% in those aged ≥ 60 years. Current smoking was significantly associated with a higher prevalence of AD, as was the risk for OSA. However, no significant associations were found between AD prevalence and factors such as sex, educational level, household income, high-risk drinking behavior, BMI, occupation, and aerobic physical activity.

DISCUSSION

This study identified a significant association between OSA risk and AD, demonstrating a dose-dependent relationship between OSA risk level and the weighted prevalence of AD. Clinically, individuals at high-risk for OSA are more likely to exhibit an increased prevalence of AD. These findings provide epidemiological insights into how OSA may influence the risk of AD.

The mechanisms underlying the association between OSA and AD are complex. However, various plausible explanations have been proposed. Obesity, a well-known determinant of OSA, has been implicated as a significant risk factor for AD.29 Obesity can contribute to OSA pathogenesis by exerting mechanical stress on the airway and by disrupting upper airway neuromuscular control through systemic inflammation.30 Moreover, recent population-based studies have shown that it is significantly related with higher prevalence and severity of AD in children and adolescents.313233 In obesity, reduced levels of adiponectin and PPAR-γ contribute to the overproduction of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, activating immune cells and driving systemic inflammation.34 This chronic inflammatory state, further amplified by leptin-mediated signaling, is closely linked to the immunopathological features of AD. Specifically, it promotes the upregulation of Th2 cytokines and chemokines, hallmark features of AD, while suppressing regulatory T-cell responses.35 Additionally, obesity impairs innate antimicrobial defense mechanisms and increases susceptibility to pathogens such as S. aureus, thereby heightening the risk of AD.36

Hypertension, another factor contributing to OSA, has been shown to have a significant linear relationship with elevated interleukin (IL)-6 levels, which can induce inflammation.37 In a mouse model of AD, S. aureus-induced severe barrier dysfunction led to the sustained expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor-α, exacerbating skin inflammation.38 Therefore, an increase in pro-inflammatory cytokines may lead to hypertension, which acts as a key component of OSA, thereby influencing the risk of OSA and contributing to AD inflammation. These observations suggest that pro-inflammatory cytokines may mediate the complex interplay among hypertension, OSA, and AD.

Additionally, OSA induces hypoxemia, sleep fragmentation, and sympathetic nervous system activation, which can contribute to daytime fatigue, cognitive impairment, and psychological stress, including depression and anxiety.3940 Stress is known to aggravate AD.1241 Acute stress induces a rapid release of cortisol and adrenaline (or noradrenaline), which stimulates the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, promoting cellular immune responses and inflammation.4243 Simultaneously, stress impairs the epidermal barrier function and shifts immune balance toward a Th2-dominant response, thereby potentially exacerbating the pathophysiology of AD.4445 Therefore, addressing sleep disorders, such as OSA, and reducing the associated stress may help alleviate AD symptoms.46

The observed association between OSA and AD aligns with previous studies.4748 In our analysis of adults aged ≥ 40 years, the prevalence of AD was found to decrease significantly with age, while current smokers had a significantly higher prevalence of AD than nonsmokers. Although high-risk drinking behavior, elevated BMI, employment, and optimal aerobic physical activity were linked to an increased prevalence of AD, these associations were not statistically significant. No significant correlations were found between AD and sex, educational level, or household income.

This study highlights important clinical implications for healthcare professionals, particularly dermatologists and sleep specialists, who encounter patients with AD. Recognizing OSA as a potential comorbidity and contributing factor to AD is particularly crucial for patients with persistent or severe symptoms or those experiencing sleep disturbances. Addressing the underlying OSA, in addition to skin-directed therapies, could be an essential approach. Effective management of OSA in patients with AD could improve treatment outcomes, enhance overall well-being, and potentially reduce the reliance on topical corticosteroids and other AD-specific medications. Integrating these clinical implications into practice will enable healthcare professionals to optimize care for patients with AD and improve treatment outcomes.

This study has notable strengths that contribute to the robustness and reliability of its findings. The use of data from the KNHANES, a nationally representative survey, provided a large sample of Korean adults, enhancing the statistical power of the analysis and improving the generalizability of the results to the broader Korean population. The population-based design of the KNHANES minimizes selection bias and ensures an accurate representation of the overall Korean adult population, enhancing the external validity of the findings. Moreover, the inclusion of objective OSA measures, such as apnea-hypopnea index scores derived from polysomnography, provided a more precise and reliable assessment of OSA risk level compared to self-reported measures. To strengthen the validity of the analysis, the study adjusted for various potential confounding factors, including age, sex, BMI, smoking status, and alcohol consumption, thereby reducing the likelihood of spurious associations. Additionally, the observed dose-response relationship between OSA risk level and AD prevalence provides compelling evidence supporting the potential causal relationship between these conditions.

Despite its strength, the study has some limitations. First, OSA risk and AD diagnoses were based on self-reported questionnaires and STOP-BANG scores, which, although useful, cannot fully substitute clinical diagnostic methods and may present limitations regarding diagnostic accuracy. While the STOP-BANG score is not the gold standard for diagnosing OSA, it has demonstrated high sensitivity, making it a valuable screening tool for assessing OSA risk in population-based studies.2349 A STOP-BANG score of 3 or higher showed sensitivities of 84%, 93%, and 100% for identifying individuals at risk for mild, moderate, and severe OSA, respectively.50 Secondly, this study did not assess disease progression, severity, or patient outcomes, limiting the ability to evaluate the relationship between the severity of AD and OSA risk and to derive more detailed findings. Thirdly, the cross-sectional design precludes establishing a causal relationship between OSA and AD, making it unclear whether OSA is a risk factor for AD or vice versa. Finally, the study population was limited to individuals aged ≥ 40 years, which may not adequately reflect the association between OSA risk and AD in younger populations, where AD prevalence is typically higher. This could lead to an underestimation of the association. Despite these limitations, this study remains significant for its systematic analysis of the relationship between AD and OSA risk utilizing a nationally representative, large-scale dataset. Further research is warranted to elucidate the underlying mechanisms and long-term relationships among these conditions.

In conclusion, this study provides robust evidence of a significant association between OSA risk and AD in Korean adults, emphasizing the importance of considering OSA in the comprehensive management of AD. The clear dose-response relationship observed between OSA risk level and AD prevalence suggests that the need for further investigation into the potential impact of OSA on AD.

These findings, with substantial clinical implications, highlight the need for increased awareness among healthcare professionals, early detection, and management of OSA in patients with AD, and a holistic approach to AD care that addresses both skin-related issues and underlying comorbidities. Further research is required to elucidate the mechanisms linking OSA and AD and to evaluate the long-term impact of OSA treatment on AD outcomes. Such investigations could focus on exploring the roles of obesity, psychological stress, inflammatory mediators, oxidative stress, and sleep disruption in the interplay between these conditions. Additionally, prospective randomized controlled trials are essential to assess the effectiveness of various interventions, such as CPAP therapy and lifestyle modifications, in improving AD outcomes in patients with OSA. By integrating these findings into clinical practice and conducting further research, healthcare professionals can contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of AD and improve the quality of life for individuals affected by this chronic condition.
