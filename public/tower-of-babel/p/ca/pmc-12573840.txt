Linarin alleviates enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli-induced small intestinal mucosal barrier damage in a piglet model by modulating the gut microbiota
Authors: Mingyang Wang, Xiaodan Liu, Xu Ji, Aiyou Wen, Hongyu Wang, Erhui Jin, Feng Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to investigate the preventive effects of dietary linarin supplementation on Enterotoxigenic coli (ETEC) induced small intestinal barrier dysfunction in weaned piglets via gut microbiota modulation.ResultsTwenty-four weaned piglets were randomly assigned to four experimental groups. The four treatments were as follows: BD + NB (basal diet and orally administered nutrient broth), LN + NB (basal diet supplemented with 150 mg/kg linarin, and orally administered nutrient broth), BD + ETEC (basal diet and orally administered ETEC), LN + ETEC (basal diet supplemented with 150 mg/kg linarin, and orally administered ETEC). The results showed that linarin lowers the serum levels of diamine oxidase (DAO), endotoxin, and D-lactate (P < 0.05). Linarin significantly increased villus height and villus/crypt ratio (P < 0.01), while decreasing crypt depth in the duodenum. jejunum, and ileum (P < 0.05). Additionally, Linarin increased the number of goblet cells within villus-crypt units in the duodenum. jejunum, and ileum (P < 0.05). Linarin significantly enhanced intestinal barrier function, upregulated detoxification pathways, reduced epithelial apoptosis, and improved nutrient transporter expression in the small intestine." Linarin decreased the relative abundances of Actinobacillus, Romboutsia, Enterococcus, and Terrisporobacter, consequently modulating key metabolic pathways, including arginine and proline metabolism, steroid biosynthesis, and cysteine and methionine metabolism.ConclusionsDietary linarin supplementation mitigates ETEC-induced intestinal mucosal barrier dysfunction and enhances nutrient assimilation via targeted modulation of microbial communities and associated metabolic networks, thereby providing a new strategy for the prevention and treatment of ETEC diarrhea.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s42523-025-00446-4.

Background

In intensive swine production, early weaning during 21–28 days enhances production efficiency [1]. However, this transition imposes significant physiological and psychological stress, characterized by high morbidity and mortality rates, rendering it one of the most critical phases in a piglet’s life [2]. Post-weaning diarrhea (PWD), triggered by abrupt dietary and environmental transitions, detrimentally affects intestinal morphology, compromises digestive function, and impairs nutrient absorption, resulting in substantial economic losses in the swine industry [3].

Enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli (ETEC), particularly the K88 serotype expressing F4 fimbriae, represents a major enteric pathogen that secretes heat-labile (LT) and heat-stable (ST) enterotoxins, leading to intestinal disorders and diarrhea in both humans and animals [4, 5]. In piglets, ETEC infection compromises intestinal barrier integrity, perturbs microbial homeostasis, and impairs post-weaning growth performance [6, 7]. For decades, the swine industry has heavily depended on antibiotics as a primary strategy to control ETEC-induced diarrhea [8, 9]. However, the extensive and prolonged use of antibiotics has contributed to the emergence of antimicrobial resistance, posing significant challenges to animal health and food safety. The pressing demand for effective antibiotic alternatives underscores the imperative for implementing sustainable strategies in contemporary swine production.

The “Medicine-Food Homology” (MFH) concept describes organisms exhibiting dual functionality as dietary and therapeutic agents in traditional practices [10, 11]. Initially proposed in Huangdi Neijing (The Yellow Emperor’s Inner Canon), the earliest comprehensive treatise on traditional Chinese medicine, MFH embodies the ancient Chinese principle of food-medicine unity [12]. This integration originates from the shared foundations of traditional Chinese medicine and dietary resources, whose synergistic utilization confers diverse health benefits. MFH plants are abundant in bioactive compounds—including alkaloids, flavonoids, polysaccharides, saponins, polyphenols, and essential oils, which have been extensively investigated for their antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer properties [13, 14]. Beyond their therapeutic properties, MFH-derived materials are widely utilized for disease prevention, largely owing to their seamless integration into daily diets [15]. Compared with pharmaceutical agents, MFH-derived dietary components generally exhibit milder side effects, rendering them safer alternatives for long-term application.

Linarin, a flavanol glycoside abundantly present in wild chrysanthemum (Chrysanthemum indicum), has garnered substantial scientific attention due to its medicinal potential [16]. As a representative MFH herb, wild chrysanthemum is widely used in food and traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) for its therapeutic and nutritional benefits [17]. Wild chrysanthemum, abundant in polysaccharides, flavonoids, and volatile oils, demonstrates potent anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, hypolipidemic, and antioxidant properties [18, 19]. Linarin also alleviates inflammation, oxidative stress, and apoptosis while maintaining intestinal barrier integrity, underscoring its significance in gastrointestinal health [17, 20–22]. Experimental studies provide substantial evidence supporting linarin’s therapeutic potential in intestinal disorders. For instance, linarin ameliorates colitis by enhancing intestinal barrier integrity, suppressing pro-inflammatory cascades, and restoring gut microbial homeostasis [23]. It also improves mucosal barrier function in ulcerative colitis (UC) mice by reducing intestinal permeability. Nevertheless, its specific effects on the small intestinal barrier and gut microbiota in weaned piglets, along with their intricate interplay, remain largely unexplored. We hypothesize that linarin alleviates intestinal mucosal barrier injury by selectively modulating the gut microbiota. To test this hypothesis, an ETEC-challenged piglet model was established to evaluate the impact of linarin on intestinal barrier function and gut microbiota composition. Our findings provide a critical foundation for applying linarin to improve gut health and counteract pathogen-induced intestinal dysfunction in weaned piglets, thereby offering a potential novel therapeutic strategy for the management of ETEC-induced diarrhea.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics

The animal experimental protocol was reviewed and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Anhui Science and Technology University (Approval No: AHSTU2023006). All experimental procedures involving animals strictly complied with institutional regulations and the national Guidelines for the Ethical Review of Laboratory Animal Welfare.

Animal experimental design

Twenty-four healthy crossbred piglets (Duroc × Landrace × Yorkshire, aged 24–26 days) were obtained from a local commercial farm for this study. The piglets were weaned at 21 days of age and individually housed in separate pens under standardized environmental conditions, following previously described protocols. All piglets had libitum access to feed and water and were fed a uniform basal diet. Twenty-four weaned piglets were randomly assigned to four groups. BD + NB (basal diet with 10 mL sterile nutrient broth), LN + NB (basal diet supplemented with 150 mg/kg linarin, with 10 mL sterile nutrient broth), BD + ETEC (basal diet with 10 mL Escherichia coli K88 [ETEC] suspension at 1 × 10⁹ CFU/mL), LN + ETEC (basal diet supplemented with 150 mg/kg linarin, with 10 mL ETEC suspension at 1 × 10⁹ CFU/mL). The dosage of linarin was determined based on prior murine colitis studies, converted for piglet administration using body surface area normalization [23]. The ETEC challenge dose and duration was established according to our previous research [24]. All piglets received either sterile nutrient broth or ETEC suspension via oral gavage for three consecutive days (On days 8 and 18). During the experimental phase, piglets were provided with unrestricted access to feed and water, and their growth performance and diarrhea incidence were assessed daily (Fig. 1). Upon completion of the study, intestinal tissues (duodenum, jejunum, and ileum) and digest were aseptically harvested and immediately stored in liquid nitrogen or an RNA stabilization solution for subsequent analyses. The composition and nutritional profiles of the experimental diets are provided in Table S1. Linarin (CAS No. 480-36-4; purity ≥ 90%, HPLC) was purchased from Chengdu Herbpurify Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (China). The Escherichia coli K88 strain was obtained from the CVCC (China Veterinary Culture Collection Center; strain ID: CVCC1500). The purchased ETEC strain was streaked onto MacConkey agar and subjected to two successive 48h subcultures. Single colonies were then inoculated into nutrient broth (NB) and cultured aerobically at 37 °C for 16 h with shaking (200 rpm). Determination of serum DAO, D-lactate, and endotoxin levels

Serum concentrations of diamine oxidase (DAO), D-lactate, and endotoxin were determined using porcine-specific Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) kits (Beijing DKM Biological Technology Co., Ltd., China). The absorbance values were measured using a fully automated microplate reader (Thermo Scientific™ Multiskan™ GO) at the assay-specific wavelengths.

Intestinal morphology analysis

Segments of duodenal, jejunal, and ileal tissue were immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h, followed by dehydration, clearing, and paraffin embedding for histopathological examination. The embedded tissues were sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm using a rotary microtome (Leica RM2235, Germany) and mounted on glass slides. Morphological evaluation was performed under an optical microscope (Olympus CX31, Tokyo, Japan). Ten well-preserved and properly oriented villus–crypt units were measured in each tissue section.

Goblet cells

The distribution of goblet cells in the piglet small intestine was quantitatively analyzed using Alcian blue–periodic acid-Schiff (AB-PAS) staining. Paraffin-embedded sections of the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum were stained with AB-PAS to visualize and quantify goblet cells. For each intestinal segment, at least 10 morphologically intact villus-crypt units were selected for goblet cell counting. The data were normalized and presented as the mean number of goblet cells per 10 villus-crypt units across all analyzed sections.

Real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from small intestine tissues using the RNA Easy Fast Extraction Kit (DP451, Tiangen Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. The extracted RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the HiScript III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (+ gDNA wiper) (R312, Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed based on the protocol described in our previous study using three technical replicates per biological sample. Gene-specific primers were designed using Primer 5.0 software based on porcine genomic sequences and synthesized by Shanghai Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) [24]. Primer sequences are detailed in Table S2. Relative mRNA expression levels of target genes were quantified using the 2−ΔΔCt method and normalized to the reference gene GAPDH (glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase).

Intestinal microbiota and metabolomic analysis

Ileal chyme samples were submitted to Shanghai Ling'en Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) for 16S rRNA gene sequencing and metabolomics analysis. Detailed materials and methods are provided in Table S1.

Statistical analyses

After conducting normality tests and making the required data adjustments, the independent-samples t-tests and two-way ANOVA were utilized, where the statistical model treated the treatment as a fixed effect and the animal as the unit of experimentation Where ANOVA revealed significant interactions (P < 0.05), post hoc comparisons were conducted using Tukey's HSD test with multiplicity adjustment. The correlation between the microbiota structure and the marker genes of intestinal barrier function was analyzed using GraphPad Prism. The Mantel statistic (r) represents the Pearson correlation coefficient between matrix elements, with significance threshold set at P < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics (21.0), and graphical illustrations were generated with GraphPad Prism (10.1.2).

Intestinal chemical barrier

Compared with the BD + NB group, the LN + NB group showed a significant increase of the mRNA expression levels of MUC2 and P-glycoprotein in the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum (P < 0.01), CYP3A4 in the duodenum, and ileum (P < 0.01). Metabolomics analysis

Untargeted metabolomic analysis of ileal digesta was conducted to assess the metabolic effects of linarin (Fig. 7). The combined metabolite abundance data from the BD + NB and LN + NB groups were analyzed using Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) and Non-Metric Multidimensional Scaling (NMDS) (Fig. 7A–C). A clear separation was observed between the BD + NB and LN + NB groups, indicating that the metabolic profile of the LN + NB group significantly differed from that of the BD + NB group. Differential metabolites were identified (Fig. 7D), with 13 metabolites upregulated and 47 downregulated. Discussion

Weaned piglets characterized by immature immune systems and unstable gut microbiota, are highly susceptible to enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli (ETEC) infections, which often result in diarrhea [25]. Previous studies have demonstrated that ETEC disrupts gut microbiota homeostasis and epithelial barrier function through the release of enterotoxins, thereby increasing intestinal permeability and inducing inflammation, oxidative stress, and diarrhea [24, 26]. In our previous study, berberine, a flavonoid compound, was shown to alleviate ETEC-induced intestinal damage by modulating the gut microbiota. However, it remains unclear whether linarin, another flavonoid with known anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-apoptotic properties, can similarly mitigate ETEC-induced intestinal injury in piglets [24]. This study aims to investigate whether dietary linarin supplementation mitigates ETEC-induced mucosal barrier damage in the small intestine of piglets through modulation of the gut microbial community.

Studies have demonstrated that intestinal integrity is critical for maintaining gut homeostasis, with intestinal permeability serving as a key indicator of both nutrient absorption efficiency and overall intestinal health [27, 28]. Serum biomarkers such as DAO, D-lactate, and endotoxin are commonly employed to evaluate the degree of intestinal mucosal barrier disruption and subsequent repair [29, 30]. Elevated serum D-lactate levels are typically associated with impaired intestinal barrier function, while elevated endotoxin levels, which originate from Escherichia coli, have been shown to exacerbate intestinal permeability and barrier dysfunction [31]. In the present study, dietary linarin decreased the level of D-lactate in the serum of piglets. Furthermore, in response to ETEC challenge, the LN + ETEC group exhibited decreased serum levels of DAO, D-lactate, and endotoxin. These findings align with the expression patterns of the aforementioned permeability markers, suggesting that dietary linarin mitigates the increase in intestinal permeability caused by ETEC infection.

The small intestine, as the primary site for nutrient digestion and absorption in the gastrointestinal tract, is evaluated using morphological parameters such as villus height, crypt depth, and the villus/crypt ratio, which collectively reflect absorptive efficiency and surface area [25, 32]. The intestinal tract of healthy pigs has higher villus height, ratio of velvet to crypt and shallower crypt [33]. In this study, dietary linarin significantly increased villus height in the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum, accompanied by an increased villus/crypt ratio and decreased crypt depth in the jejunum. Following ETEC challenge, the LN + ETEC group increased villus height and villus/crypt ratio, and decreased crypt depth, across all intestinal segments. These findings align with the observed improvements in villus/crypt ratio in healthy piglets, demonstrating that dietary supplementation with linarin significantly enhances small intestinal morphology in weaned piglets, particularly following ETEC challenge.

Goblet cells play a vital role in intestinal homeostasis by secreting mucus to form a physical barrier, regulating the gut microbiota, maintaining immune defenses, attenuating inflammatory responses, and facilitating post-injury mucosal repair [34–36]. The current research demonstrates that the supplementation of linarin in the diet led to a notable rise in the quantity of goblet cells located in the villi and crypts of the small intestinal. Likewise, after ETEC infection, the dietary addition of linarin resulted in an increase in goblet cell numbers within the same regions. These results align with earlier studies that showed linarin's effectiveness in reducing mucosal damage caused by DSS and alleviating the depletion of goblet cells [23]. These findings suggest that dietary linarin counteracts ETEC-induced goblet cell depletion in weaned piglets.

The chemical barrier, a critical component of the intestinal barrier system, is primarily composed of mucus and mucins secreted by goblet cells, which constitute the first line of defense against ETEC adherence to the intestinal epithelium [37–40]. Previous studies in piglets have shown that ETEC infection reduces goblet cell numbers and suppresses MUC2 expression [41]. In the present study, dietary linarin increased MUC2 expression in the small intestine. Similarly, following the ETEC challenge, dietary linarin exhibited significantly elevated MUC2 expression levels. These results suggest that linarin may inhibit ETEC invasion by promoting MUC2-mediated mucin secretion. Beyond MUC2, P-glycoprotein and CYP3A4 also contribute to the intestinal chemical barrier [42]. P-glycoprotein, a transmembrane protein of the ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter family, actively transports xenobiotics (e.g., toxins and drugs) from the intracellular to the extracellular space through ATP-dependent efflux, thereby limiting their absorption or promoting excretion [43, 44]. CYP3A4, a key enzyme in the cytochrome P450 system, catalyzes the oxidative metabolism of lipophilic compounds in the liver and small intestine, facilitating their renal or intestinal elimination [45]. In this study, dietary linarin increased the mRNA expression levels of P-glycoprotein and CYP3A4 in the duodenum and ileum, and increased CYP3A4 expression in the jejunum. Following the ETEC challenge, dietary linarin increased the expression of P-glycoprotein and CYP3A4 in the small intestine. Previous studies have indicated that decreased expression of P-glycoprotein and CYP3A4 in the small intestinal of piglets may lead to impairment of the small intestinal barrier during ETEC infection [24]. These findings demonstrate that dietary supplementation with linarin alleviates ETEC-induced barrier damage.

Intestinal epithelial cell apoptosis refers to the programmed cell death of gut epithelial cells under physiological or pathological conditions [46]. These cells are critical components of the intestinal barrier, and maintaining barrier integrity depends on tightly regulated processes of cellular renewal and repair [47]. When ETEC breaches the intestinal chemical barrier and directly interacts with epithelial cells, its enterotoxins rapidly induce apoptosis, thereby facilitating bacterial colonization. BCL2 and BAK, members of the BCL2 protein family, play opposing roles: BCL2 (anti-apoptotic) inhibits BAK and other pro-apoptotic proteins to promote cell survival, whereas BAK (pro-apoptotic) forms pores in the mitochondrial membrane, increasing its permeability, triggering cytochrome c release, and initiating apoptosis [48]. CASP9 mediates the mitochondrial apoptosis pathway by activating CASP3 (the executioner caspase) through cytochrome c-dependent mechanisms, ultimately cleaving intracellular substrates to execute cell death [49]. In this study, dietary linarin decreased BAK and increased BCL2 expression in the duodenum and ileum, concurrently decreased CASP3 and CASP9 expression in the duodenum and jejunum. Following the ETEC challenge, dietary linarin increased BCL2 and decreased CASP3 and CASP9 expression in the small intestine. Consistent with the functional roles of these genes, these findings indicate that ETEC infection compromises anti-apoptotic signaling capacity in weaned piglets, thereby promoting epithelial cell apoptosis. Dietary linarin effectively mitigated ETEC-induced apoptosis by restoring the balance between pro- and anti-apoptotic pathways.

Based on our findings, dietary supplementation of linarin alleviates ETEC-induced disruptions, including increased intestinal permeability, structural damage to the small intestin, reduction in goblet cell numbers, impaired chemical barrier function, and elevated epithelial cell apoptosis in weaned piglets. These results prompted further investigation into the effects of linarin on the integrity of the intestinal physical barrier in weaned piglets. Intestinal epithelial cells form a cohesive monolayer that establishes a physical barrier through tight junctions, adherens junctions, and desmosomes, sealing intercellular spaces and regulating gatekeeping and fence functions [50, 51]. Dynamic alterations in tight junction proteins (ZO-1, ZO-2, Occludin, Claudin-1), cytoskeletal components (β-catenin) and adhesion proteins (E-cadherin) are key indicators of physical barrier disruption caused by ETEC infection in weaned piglet models [24]. Degradation of these proteins allows bacterial toxins and antigens to translocate across the intestinal lumen, compromising barrier integrity and triggering diarrhea [52, 53]. In this study, dietary linarin increased the mRNA expression levels of Occludin, Claudin-1, E-cadherin, and β-catenin in the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum. Following the ETEC challenge, Linarin increased the mRNA expression of ZO-1, ZO-2, β-catenin and Occludin across all intestinal segments, as well as increased E-cadherin in the duodenum and ileum and increased Claudin-1 in the duodenum. These results demonstrate that linarin supplementation not only mitigates ETEC-induced intestinal damage but also enhances intestinal barrier integrity by promoting the expression of tight junction and adhesion junction proteins in weaned piglets.

Improvements in intestinal villus morphology are often associated with enhanced intestinal function. Longer villi increase the absorptive surface area, thereby enhancing nutrient uptake [54]. Previous studies have shown that dietary linarin improves villus architecture, which prompted further investigation into its potential effects on nutrient absorption in weaned piglets. Nutrient absorption in piglets is primarily mediated by specialized transporters localized to the intestinal epithelium, including amino acid transporters, fatty acid transporters, and glucose transporters [55, 56]. SLC1A1 and SLC7A7 encode amino acid transporters essential for the transmembrane transport of amino acids [57]. FABP4, a member of the fatty acid-binding protein family, is pivotal in lipid metabolism and the regulation of inflammation, while FATP1 is an evolutionarily conserved transmembrane protein facilitating fatty acid uptake [58, 59]. Both FABP4 and FATP1 are essential for intestinal lipid absorption, particularly in the metabolism of long-chain fatty acids and their intracellular trafficking [60]. SLC5A1 mediates active glucose absorption in the intestine and kidneys, whereas SLC2A2 facilitates passive glucose diffusion into the bloodstream and regulates insulin secretion. In this study, dietary linarin increased SLC1A1, FABP4, and SLC2A2 expression in the jejunum and ileum, and additionally increased FATP1 and SLC5A1 expression in the jejunum, as well as SLC7A7 expression in the ileum. Following the ETEC challenge, dietary linarin exhibited significantly increased expression of SLC5A1, SLC2A2, FABP4, and FATP1 in the jejunum, and SLC1A1, SLC7A7, and FABP4 in the ileum. Although the upregulation of nutrient transporter genes (e.g., SLC1A1, SLC5A1, FABP4) suggests a potential improvement in the intestinal absorptive capacity of weaned piglets, this conclusion is based solely on mRNA expression data. Functional assays—such as glucose or amino acid uptake studies—are needed to determine whether these transcriptional changes translate into enhanced transporter activity and nutrient absorption. Therefore, while the observed gene expression patterns are consistent with the known functional roles of these transporters and imply that dietary linarin may promote the absorption of amino acids, fatty acids, and glucose, these findings should be interpreted with caution pending further experimental validation.

The gut microbiota is critical for intestinal health in piglets, with key functions in digestion, immune modulation, pathogen resistance, and growth promotion [61, 62]. The occurrence of diarrhea in weaned piglets is often accompanied by gut microbial dysbiosis [63]. Dietary supplementation with linarin (LN + NB group) significantly increased alpha diversity, as reflected by increases in Observed species, ACE richness, and Shannon diversity indices. Beta diversity analysis revealed distinct separation between the BD + NB and LN + NB groups, indicating that linarin substantially altered the gut microbial composition in piglets. To further elucidate the impact of dietary linarin on the intestinal microbial community in weaned piglets, we conducted a genus-level taxonomic analysis of microbial abundance. Terrisporobacter, Sarcina, and Pasteurella are considered harmful genera associated with intestinal disorders and diarrhea in piglets. Increased relative abundances of these taxa have been correlated with gut inflammation and infections [64]. While the role of Sarcina remains poorly characterized, limited evidence suggests its involvement in porcine gastrointestinal pathologies. Pasteurella may exacerbate localized infections or inflammation, particularly in immunocompromised piglets. The notably increased abundances of these genera in linarin-supplemented groups (LN + NB and LN + ETEC) could be attributed to two potential mechanisms: linarin may exhibit broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity with taxon-specific efficacy, thereby potentially favoring the proliferation of Terrisporobacter, Sarcina, and Pasteurella due to their intrinsic tolerance. Alternatively, linarin may alter intestinal conditions and thereby create ecological niches that preferentially support the growth of these genera. In contrast, beneficial genera such as Intestinibacter, Howardella, and Catenibacterium exhibited increased relative abundances. Intestinibacter produces short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) via carbohydrate fermentation, potentially mitigating inflammation and maintaining microbial equilibrium [64]. Howardella contributes to amino acid metabolism, supporting nutrient utilization and growth [65]. Catenibacterium enhances intestinal barrier function through butyrate production and suppresses pathogens via competitive exclusion [66]. Paradoxically, despite linarin’s favorable modulation of these taxa, its ability to fully resolve microbial dysbiosis appeared to be limited. This may be attributed to weaning stress-induced gut dysregulation, wherein rapid digesta transit in the small intestine physically impedes linarin’s colonization-promoting effects on beneficial bacteria. Concurrently, mucosal injury during weaning may impair microbiota-host collaborative repair mechanisms, thereby limiting linarin’s efficacy in restoring microbial balance.

Gut microbial metabolites are essential for preserving barrier integrity, modulating immune responses, and sustaining microbial balance [79]. In this study, integrated multi-omics analyses (microbiome and metabolome) provided comprehensive evidence that dietary linarin elicits significant alterations in microbial communities and metabolite profiles in weaned piglets exposed to stress conditions. Furthermore, we conducted an in-depth investigation of correlations between microbial and metabolomic profiles, as well as interactions between microbiota, metabolites, and key metabolic pathways. The findings of this study demonstrate that dietary supplementation with linarin significantly upregulated the relative abundances of Terrisporobacter, Intestinibacter, and Epulopiscium. Additionally, four differential metabolites—citrulline, ornithine, glutamic acid, and L-cystine—were identified and analyzed in relation to the aforementioned microbial genera. Notably, three key metabolic pathways—arginine biosynthesis, glutathione metabolism, and butanoate metabolism—were closely associated with the linarin-mediated modulation of gut microbiota and its protective effects, collectively underscoring their crucial roles in maintaining intestinal health. Arginine biosynthesis plays multifaceted physiological roles in mammals, contributing to protein synthesis, nitric oxide (NO) production, immune regulation, and cellular proliferation [80]. NO, a critical signaling molecule, facilitates intestinal vasodilation, enhances mucosal blood flow, and supports barrier integrity [81]. Arginine promotes intestinal epithelial cell proliferation and tissue repair, thereby preserving epithelial integrity and preventing hyperpermeability. It also modulates the function of gut-associated immune cells, including macrophages and T cells, to maintain immune homeostasis [82]. Through NO-dependent mechanisms, arginine exerts anti-inflammatory effects, which may help ameliorate inflammatory bowel diseases. Glutathione metabolism, a ubiquitous process in cellular systems and particularly enriched in the liver, gut, and immune cells, plays essential roles in antioxidant defense, detoxification, and immune regulation [83]. Enhanced glutathione (GSH) activity promotes the expression of ZO-1, MUC2, and secretory IgA, while attenuating inflammation and modulating gut microbial composition, thereby reinforcing the intestinal barrier [84]. In this study, the relative abundance of Terrisporobacter showed significant correlations with duodenal E-cadherin, MUC2, and CYP3A4; jejunal P-glycoprotein, Occludin, Claudin-1, E-cadherin and β-catenin; and ileal β-catenin, P-glycoprotein, CYP3A4, and MUC2. Additionally, both Terrisporobacter and Intestinibacter were strongly associated with the amino acid transporters SLC1A1 and SLC7A7. These findings suggest that Terrisporobacter may modulate glutathione metabolism via ornithine, contributing to the enhancement of intestinal physical and chemical barrier functions in weaned piglets. Butanoate metabolism acts as a central node linking gut microbiota to host physiological homeostasis, with its synthetic and signaling pathways playing crucial roles in immune regulation and energy balance [85]. Previous studies have shown that butyrate activates G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) and inhibits histone deacetylases (HDACs), thereby upregulating the expression of tight junction proteins such as Occludin and Claudins [86]. Sodium butyrate has also been demonstrated to enhance epithelial barrier integrity in human colonic cells by increasing MUC2 production [87]. Furthermore, butyrate reduces intestinal permeability and inhibits the translocation of pathogens and toxins by strengthening tight junctions and downregulating the NF-κB signaling pathway, which in turn suppresses the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-8 [88]. Collectively, these data suggest that Epulopiscium may modulate butanoate metabolism through glutamic acid, thereby decreasing intestinal permeability and reinforcing tight junction integrity in weaned piglets. However, the underlying mechanisms remain to be elucidated.

Correlation analysis between microbiome and metabolome data revealed that dietary supplementation with linarin significantly downregulated the relative abundances of Actinobacillus, Terrisporobacter, and Enterococcus in ETEC-infected piglets. Additionally, seven differential metabolites, including prostaglandin E2, S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), and calcitriol, were identified, and their associations with the aforementioned microbial genera were characterized. Three key metabolic pathways were found to be linked to the linarin-mediated modulation of gut microbiota and its protective effects: cysteine and methionine metabolism, arginine and proline metabolism, and steroid biosynthesis. Cysteine and methionine metabolism play pivotal roles in the physiological metabolism of piglets, significantly impacting intestinal health [89]. Cysteine, a precursor of glutathione (GSH), is crucial for antioxidant defense, protecting intestinal cells from oxidative stress and maintaining barrier integrity [90]. Cysteine metabolism generates hydrogen sulfide (H₂S), which exhibits dual roles in the gut: at low concentrations, H₂S promotes mucosal repair and regulates blood flow, whereas high concentrations may induce cellular damage [91]. Methionine is converted to S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) via methylation, a process essential for DNA, RNA, and protein methylation, thereby ensuring normal intestinal cell function and damage repair [92]. Additionally, methionine indirectly supports GSH synthesis by serving as a precursor for cysteine, enhancing intestinal barrier function, reducing systemic translocation of harmful substances, and mitigating inflammation and infection risks. Previous studies have demonstrated that supplementation with cysteine and methionine effectively scavenges oxidants, preventing oxidative damage to intestinal integrity, restoring epithelial permeability, and maintaining normal villus architecture [93]. These findings are consistent with the results of the current study, further validating the critical role of these metabolic pathways in gut health. Steroid biosynthesis encompasses the biological synthesis of steroid hormones, including cholesterol, bile acids, vitamin D, and sex hormones. Cholesterol-derived primary bile acids, synthesized in the liver, are transported to the intestine via the enterohepatic circulation system [94]. Previous studies have demonstrated that bile acids and their receptors enhance intestinal barrier integrity by improving tight junction (TJ) functionality [95]. For instance, bile acid treatment significantly upregulated the expression of TJ-associated proteins, including ZO-1, JAM, Occludin, and Claudin-4 [95]. Consistent with these findings, our study revealed a significant negative correlation between the relative abundance of Actinobacillus and the expression of physical barrier genes (ZO-1, E-cadherin). This suggests that Actinobacillus may modulate steroid biosynthesis by influencing metabolites such as cholecalciferol (vitamin D3) and calcitriol (active vitamin D), thereby improving intestinal TJ function.

Conclusions

This study elucidated the regulatory mechanisms of linarin in alleviating intestinal mucosal barrier injury induced by ETEC infection in a weaned piglet model. Dietary linarin supplementation can effectively alleviate ETEC-induced intestinal mucosal injury through multiple mechanisms, including the improvement of intestinal morphology, upregulation of nutrient transporter gene expression, increased goblet cell numbers, enhancement of chemical barrier function, upregulation of tight junction proteins to reinforce physical barrier integrity, and inhibition of epithelial cell apoptosis. Mechanistically, linarin's efficacy was associated with microbiota-mediated activation of key metabolic pathways: Cysteine and methionine metabolism, Arginine and proline metabolism, and Steroid biosynthesis. In conclusion, linarin enhance both the physical and chemical barrier functions of the small intestine, as well as its digestive and absorptive capacities, by modulating the intestinal microbiota, thereby mitigating ETEC-induced mucosal injury.
