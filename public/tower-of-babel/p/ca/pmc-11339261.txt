Colorimetric detection of serum creatinine on a miniaturized platform using hue-saturation-value space analysis
Authors: E Alperay Tarim, H Cumhur Tekin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic kidney disease (CKD) is a widespread condition with considerable health and economic impacts globally. However, existing methodologies for serum creatinine assessment often involve prolonged wait times and sophisticated equipment, such as spectrometers, hindering real-time diagnosis and care. Innovative solutions like point-of-care (POC) devices are emerging to address these challenges. In this context, there is a recognized need for remote, regular, automated, and low-cost analysis of serum creatinine levels, given its role as a critical parameter for CKD diagnosis and management. This study introduces a miniaturized system with integrated heater elements designed for precise serum creatinine measurement. The system operates based on the Jaffe method and accurate serum creatinine measurement within a microreservoir chip. Smartphone-based image processing using the hue-saturation-value (HSV) color space was applied to captured images of microreservoirs. The creatinine analyses were conducted in serum with a limit of detection of ~ 0.4 mg/dL and limit of quantification of ~ 1.3 mg/dL. Smartphone-based image processing employing the HSV color space outperformed spectrometric analysis for creatinine measurement conducted in serum. This pioneering technology and smartphone-based processing offer the potential for decentralized renal function testing, which could significantly contribute to improved patient care. The miniaturized system offers a low-cost alternative ($87 per device), potentially reducing healthcare expenditures (~ $0.5 per test) associated with CKD diagnosis and management. This innovation could greatly improve access to diagnosis and monitoring of CKD, especially in regions where access to sophisticated laboratory equipment is limited.Keywords: Creatinine, Colorimetric detection, Jaffe method, Microreservoir, HSV, Point-of-care detection, Chronic kidney diseaseSubject terms: Diagnostic markers, Chronic kidney disease, Biomedical engineering

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a condition characterized by long-term changes in kidney function, lasting for more than 3 months, due to various abnormalities in kidney homeostasis1. The causality of this disease, which has multiple causes apart from common factors like diabetes, glomerulonephritis, and cystic kidney diseases, is not yet fully understood2. CKD is a disease that leads to kidney damage, reduced quality of life, and a prolonged and costly treatment process. It is one of the diseases that significantly impact patient health and impose a burden on healthcare institutions globally, especially in developed countries3. CKD affects over 10% of the world’s population, and this percentage rises to over 15% in developed countries4. Additionally, CKD is among the top 12 causes of death worldwide, underscoring the importance of monitoring and diagnosing CKD before it poses a high risk to the patient’s life5. Since CKD cannot be cured, the disease can progress rapidly, leading patients to become reliant on dialysis and requiring kidney transplantation due to kidney failure6. Early diagnosis of CKD is critical because early diagnosis can prevent progression to more serious stages, allowing timely intervention to preserve kidney function and prevent complications7. Not only the early diagnosis but also regular monitoring facilitates lifestyle changes and therapeutic interventions that can significantly slow disease progression and improve patient outcomes. This proactive approach is supported by guidelines that emphasize the importance of early detection of CKD to manage risk factors and initiate preventive treatments8,9. Therefore, early-stage CKD progression can be slowed down or halted through regular patient monitoring and appropriate medication10.

Glomerular filtration rate (GFR) is a key parameter for detecting and managing CKD, and health institutions and clinics employ the estimated GFR (eGFR) to monitor kidney patients11. There are various calculation methods for the determination of eGFR, but the most commonly used approach in clinical practice is the detection of serum creatinine levels12. eGFR can be calculated based on the serum creatinine level and together with the patient’s age, gender, and race13. Creatinine, a byproduct of muscle metabolism, should be filtered by the kidneys and maintained within a constant range in the blood, varying depending on individual metabolic characteristics14. In CKD, the amount of creatinine in the blood rises due to impaired kidney filtering functions. For instance, while a healthy individual typically has a serum creatinine level between 0.5 and 1.2 mg/dL (44–107 μM), this value can increase up to 11.3 mg/dL (1000 μM) in advanced kidney dysfunctions15,16. Kidney damage can be identified by assessing eGFR correlated with serum creatinine level, where a value lower than 90 mL/min per 1.73 m2 indicates compromised kidney function17.

While various techniques exist for determining serum creatinine levels, the Jaffe Method is the widely recognized and employed method in clinical practice18. The Jaffe Method operates on the principle that when creatine reacts with picric acid in an alkaline solution, it produces an orange-yellow color. The intensity of this color is used to quantify the amount of creatinine present in the sample19. Jaffe reaction measurement depends on the concentration of picric acid and creatinine, pH and reaction temperature20. Although glucose, some proteins, aceto-acetate, and cephalosporin can affect the Jaffe reaction, this technique is still one of the most used method for the calculation of eGFR21. Other methods and techniques for the creatinine are enzyme-based assays 22, chromatographic23, nanomaterial-based24, chemiluminescence-based25, amperometric26, potentiometric27,28, impedimetric29, conductometric30, and capacitive31 methods. However, the detection of serum creatinine in clinics still requires over 24 h and the utilization of costly equipment32,33.

The point-of-care (POC) devices were also developed for portable, low-cost, and rapid analysis. For instance, a microfluidic chip was developed with an embedded solid-state sensor for detecting glucose, urea, and creatinine in human serum34. The device utilized magnetic enzyme-carrying alginate microbeads immobilized on an electrolyte-insulator-semiconductor (EIS) sensor surface, allowing the detection reaction to occur when the sample is injected into the microchannel. The resulting potential change on the EIS sensor was used to detect the serum creatinine down to 0.018 mg/dL. Furthermore, a paper-based electrochemical device was also developed to detect creatinine without using enzymes35. The device employed a CuO/IL/ERGO electrode fabricated through a dispenser, suitable for large-scale production. The optimized device showed a linear response range of 0.18–36 mg/dL and a limit of detection (LOD) of 0.0396 mg/dL. However, these types of electrode-based detection systems can require high-cost and complex production techniques that can hinder their usage. The colorimetric detection systems can also be used to rapidly detect creatinine using imaging devices. For instance, the electromechanical lab-on-a-chip platform was designed to detect creatinine enzymatically22. The serum creatinine level was measured in serum samples using a smartphone camera. The platform enables the detection of creatinine at concentrations as low as 2 mg/dL in fetal bovine serum (FBS). On a self-powered microfluidic cartridge (Creacard) with integrated signal processing and a simple colorimetric measurement, serum creatine levels were also measured in a range of 0.76–20 mg/dL with 0.68 mg/dL LOD36. Furthermore, polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS)-based microfluidic chip was utilized for the detection of serum creatinine using the Jaffe method37. This chip could detect the creatinine in a range of 0–4 mg/dL with 0.33 mg/dL LOD using a fiber optic spectrometer at 510 nm for measurements. Moreover, a paper-based microfluidic chip integrated with a portable detection system was introduced for the detection of creatinine from whole blood38. The chip was heated at 37 °C for 5 min to maintain the environment for the Jaffe reaction and could detect serum creatinine between the range of 0.19 to 7.64 mg/dL. Another paper-based microfluidic system integrated with a smartphone-based color picker application for detection was developed for the detection of creatinine in a range of 0.33–5.65 mg/dL with LOD of 0.25 mg/dL using a modified Jaffe reaction39. Moreover, the paper-based microfluidic chip was developed to detect creatinine in whole blood using the Jaffe method with plasma separation by calcium salt-functionalization40. This technique required a preprocessing of the blood to separate plasma and aggregation of red blood cells before creatinine detection. Colorimetric analysis was performed on images captured with a smartphone camera using an application that analyzes the mean intensity of grayscale images. The LOD and the limit of quantification (LOQ) of the developed device were 0.679 and 2.056 mg/dL, respectively. While these colorimetric methods reduce the unit test costs of serum creatinine by eliminating complex chip fabrication processes, the dependence on expensive imaging devices and the limited automation have hindered the widespread adoption of these methods. Therefore, there is a need for remote, regular, automated, and low-cost analysis of serum creatinine levels to assess renal functions using eGFR.

In this study, we have developed a miniaturized system containing a disposable microreservoir chip, a LED, and heater elements for automated and robust measurement of serum creatinine levels using the colorimetric Jaffe method. A key feature of our system is the use of readily available smartphone technology for image acquisition. Microreservoirs containing serum samples are imaged using a common smartphone camera, and image processing algorithms are applied in the Hue, Saturation, and Value (HSV) color space to determine serum creatinine concentration. By utilizing a smartphone for imaging, the developed platform offers convenience, efficiency, and cost-effectiveness for monitoring creatinine levels. Importantly, our system liberates users from the need for expensive external devices for imaging and heating. While other systems using the Jaffe method for creatinine detection38,40 often lack precise temperature control or require expensive detection components, our presented platform allows detection using a smartphone with integrated temperature control elements on the platform, which provide low-cost measurements and tolerates temperature variation for repeatable analysis. Moreover, this study not only introduces an innovative application of image processing algorithms in the HSV color space but also pushes the boundaries by detecting serum creatinine concentrations as low as ~ 0.4 mg/dL, outperforming conventional high-cost spectrometric measurements. With the proposed system, healthcare professionals and patients could easily and automatically monitor serum creatinine levels with high sensitivity at POC settings. This development has the potential to facilitate the widespread dissemination of renal function tests, thereby enhancing patient care.

Materials and methods

Design and fabrication of the Jaffe-based colorimetric platform

The colorimetric detection platform was developed as a specialized device for precise detection of creatinine levels using the Jaffe method (Fig. 1). The platform's framework was designed in a three-dimensional (3D) format and manufactured using the Ultimaker 2 Connect + 3D printer, employing black polylactic acid (PLA) filament from Ultimaker (Utrecht, Netherlands). Its structure was designed to preserve constant temperature for Jaffe reaction and enable easy inspection with a smartphone camera. The platform dimensions were set at 11.47 × 3.4 × 7.6 cm3, aiming to create a miniaturized platform with minimum dimensions.

The Jaffe-based colorimetric creatinine detection. (a) The miniaturized platform consisting of Peltier elements, a microreservoir chip, and LED for the detection of creatinine using a smartphone camera. (b) The Jaffe protocol conducted on microreservoirs. Creatinine reacts with picric acid in an alkaline environment on 15-min incubation at 37 °C, which results in an orange color in the microreservoirs.

The closed platform prevented environmental light from interfering with the colorimetric measurements. The use of black PLA material on the platform minimizes the effect of environmental light and reflections on the analysis. Furthermore, integrated 3 mm white light-emitting diode (LED) with an intensity of 20000 mcd (12383, Robotistan, Türkiye), which operates with 2 V, was positioned beneath the 8 mm thick transparent white Poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) sheet (SBB2001, Depodan Malzeme, Türkiye) to provide constant illumination for measurements. The microreservoir chip was placed on top of the thick PMMA sheet allowing homogenous illumination.

Peltier elements were mounted on the two sides of the platform to maintain a constant temperature (37 ± 1 °C) on the microreservoirs during the reaction (TEC1-04905, Thermonamic, China). Temperature control was effectively managed through an Arduino microprocessor (Arduino Mega 2560 R3, Italy), which utilized a thermocouple (WRN-02B-NiCr-Ni Thermocouple, ISISO, Türkiye) with MAX6675 amplifier for temperature monitoring (Figure S1 and Figure S2). The Arduino microprocessor modulated the power supplied to the Peltier elements in real-time to achieve a constant temperature in the range of 36.5–37.5 °C.

Microreservoir chip

The microreservoir chip, where the Jaffe reaction took place, was fabricated using PMMA with a thickness of 3 mm (Ata Pleksi, Türkiye), a glass slide (76 × 26 mm) (Marienfeld, Germany), and double-sided adhesive (DSA) (OCA8146-3, Thorlabs, USA). To fabricate the chip, the PMMA and double-sided adhesive materials were precisely cut using a laser cutter (Makeblock LaserBox, China). For each chip, one glass slide and two PMMA pieces were thoroughly cleaned with 70% ethanol and then bonded to each other using DSAs (Figure S3). Through this process, a chip containing five microreservoirs each having a diameter of 7.5 mm and a height of 6.15 mm. The presence of these five microreservoirs on the chip enabled the analysis of different samples at the same time.

Jaffe protocol

On the developed platform, the Jaffe protocol was implemented using a working solution containing picric acid (35 M) (Interlab, Türkiye) in an alkaline solution (0.32 M NaOH) (Interlab, Türkiye) prepared in distilled water. In this protocol, creatinine (ab143309, Abcam Inc., UK) molecules react with picrate ions in an alkaline medium, resulting in a yellow-orange coloration of the medium. The intensity of the color was directly proportional to the concentration of creatinine in the reaction. The reaction was conducted with a volume of 25 µL of the working solution and 25 µL of the creatinine sample in the microreservoir. Samples containing different concentrations of creatine (0–20 mg/dL) were prepared in either phosphate buffered saline (PBS) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) or FBS (F0392, Sigma Aldrich, USA). The reaction was carried out either in an incubator of the spectrophotometer (Multiscan Go, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) or on the developed platform at 37 °C.

When the device starts operating, the Peltier elements are heated for approximately 3 min, with the reaction environment expected to reach 37 ± 1 °C. Once the environment achieves the desired temperature, Jaffe working solutions are placed in the relevant reservoir, followed by the addition of the sample solution. The total assay time for a chip with five reservoirs containing five different samples is ~ 20 min, which involve ~ 3 min for heating, ~ 2 min for solution addition, and ~ 15 min for detection including reaction and analysis.

Colorimetric detection

The color change in the microreservoirs as a result of the Jaffe reaction was monitored using a smartphone camera (Oppo RX17 Neo, Guangdong Oppo Mobile Telecommunications Corp., Ltd., China). All images were taken on the same device using fixed settings, such as International Organization for Standardization (ISO 200), default color balance, resolution (3456 × 4608), aspect ratio (4:3) and fixed focus mode for the same capture height. Reaction solutions prepared within microreservoirs were positioned between two Peltier elements in the device, and each reservoir was placed directly above the circular area containing a light source, then captured by the mobile phone (Video S1). During transitions from one microreservoir to another, a glass slide was shifted, and the new microreservoir was similarly captured by the mobile-phone camera. The captured images were then analyzed for hue, saturation, and brightness (HSV) values. Regarding HSV color space detection for the color change from yellow to orangish color for the Jaffe reaction, each image was processed to mask its hue (ranging between 0.098 and 0.145), saturation (ranging between 0.794 and 1.0), and brightness (ranging between 0.491 and 1.0) parameters. The attributes of the HSV color space helped decrease the impact of the camera, ambient light conditions, and image resolution on the analysis, maintaining their effect at a minimal level41,42. Afterwards, the mean saturation value was calculated precisely. All of these image processing steps were conducted using a house-developed MATLAB program to assess the color intensity of the Jaffe reaction in the reservoirs (Figure S4). Additionally, for comparison purposes, the color change was also evaluated using spectrometric absorbance measurements (Multiscan Go, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at 492 nm, which offers maximal sensitivity43,44.

Statistical analysis

The data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) based on at least 3 experimental replicates. Statistical analysis involved conducting an unpaired Student's t-test on the data with a significance threshold set at 0.05 (p < 0.05). The figures included the coefficient of determination (R2) values from linear regression models. The linear regression lines were forced to go through the 0 mg/dL creatinine value with respected absorbance and HSV saturation values. The LOD was calculated as the intersection of linear regression lines with the LOD signal, which is mean plus three times the SD of reference detection signal for 0 mg/dL creatinine45. On the other hand, the LOQ was calculated as the intersection of linear regression lines with the LOQ signal, which is mean plus ten times the SD of reference detection signal for 0 mg/dL creatinine46. All analyses were carried out utilizing GraphPad Prism software (Prism 8.0.2 (263) version for Windows, GraphPad Software Inc., https://www.graphpad.com/, USA).

Results

Optimization of the Jaffe method

It is crucial to determine the optimal reaction time at which the absorbance differences can be clearly observed for different concentrations of creatinine. To address this, the Jaffe reaction was carried out by preparing 25 µL samples with different concentrations of creatinine diluted in PBS, and the reactions were conducted at 37 °C in an incubator.

Spectrometric absorbance values were continuously measured at different time points throughout the reaction. Absorbance results for different reaction times (2–30 min) were presented in (Fig. 2). Notably, there was a highly significant difference observed between all concentrations of creatinine (≥ 1 mg/dL) and the control group, where no creatinine was presented. Furthermore, the mean absorbance values for the 15 min reaction were significantly higher compared to the 2 min reaction for the creatine concentrations larger than 5 mg/dL (Figure S5). There was no statistically difference in absorbance measurements for 15 and 30 min reactions. Hence, a reaction time of 15 min was selected as the optimal condition for conducting creatine detection experiments.

Reaction time-dependent absorbance values of different creatinine concentrations. The graph displays the absorbance results obtained at various time intervals with 25 µL working solution and 25 µL creatinine samples prepared in PBS. Statistical analysis was conducted by comparing the values with the control groups (0 mg/dL creatinine) using an unpaired t-test for each time set. The symbol (***) indicates a statistically significant difference at p < 0.001 level.

Detection of creatinine spiked in PBS

Absorbance and HSV measurements were conducted on the developed platform for different creatinine concentrations spiked in PBS (Table S1). 15 min reaction at 37 °C was performed on the platform for the detection. HSV measurements were conducted on the processed photographs (Fig. 3). For absorbance measurements, we found LOD and LOQ values of 0.11 mg/dL and 0.39 mg/dL, respectively (Fig. 3a). Measurements also showed good linearity up to 20 mg/dL creatinine concentration. Our measurements demonstrated a strong linear correlation up to a creatinine concentration of 20 mg/dL. In contrast, HSV measurements yielded LOD and LOQ values of 0.01 mg/dL and 0.04 mg/dL, respectively, which aligned with the results obtained from the absorbance measurements. However, the linear range for HSV measurements extended up to 5 mg/dL of creatinine concentration. Our findings suggest that the HSV saturation values obtained through photographs captured by a basic smartphone camera, using the developed image processing algorithm, exhibited results comparable to spectrometric absorbance measurements. Thus, the results indicated that the HSV saturation value could be effectively employed to determine creatinine concentration in PBS.

Detection of creatinine spiked in FBS

To simulate the creatinine detection in a patient's serum, the experiments were conducted in FBS (Table S1). Spectrometric absorbance and HSV measurements were performed on-chip with varying concentrations of spiked creatinine in FBS for a reaction time of 15 min at 37 °C. In the absorbance analysis, a LOD of 2.11 mg/dL and a LOQ of 7.05 mg/dL were achieved (Fig. 4a). The linearity of the assay was maintained up to a creatinine concentration of 20 mg/mL. In contrast, HSV measurements yielded a LOD of 0.38 mg/dL and a LOQ of 1.27 mg/dL, demonstrating superior performance compared to absorbance measurements (Fig. 4b). Notably, a strong linear correlation was observed up to a creatinine concentration of 10 mg/dL in the HSV analysis. These results indicate that HSV analysis in serum could effectively cover a broad range of creatinine concentrations, encompassing those in both healthy individuals (0.5–1.2 mg/dL) and advanced CKD patients (< 11.3 mg/dL)47. Although white spots appeared on the captured reservoir images due to central illumination, this does not significantly affect serum creatinine detection within the clinical range. It is essential to indicate that absorbance measurements may be influenced by the interaction of Jaffe solution with other serum proteins and potential interference from serum color48. Conversely, these issues were effectively mitigated in HSV measurements through the implementation of image processing techniques designed to isolate regions of interest in a specific HSV color space. This approach substantially reduced interference, particularly when assessing serum samples with a creatinine concentration of 0 mg/dL, thus enhancing the detection performance of HSV measurements.

(a) Absorbance measurements obtained from microreservoirs alongside their corresponding photographic representations. (b) HSV measurements conducted on processed photographs of microreservoirs. Red, Green, and Blue (RGB) color space measurements were performed on the photographs of microreservoirs with varying creatinine concentrations in PBS and FBS (Figure S6). In PBS, the RGB measurements yielded low R2 values, with LOD and LOQ values falling outside the measured range. In FBS, a satisfactory R2 value was achieved only for the green channel, though the LOD and LOQ values were poorer than those obtained from HSV measurements. Overall, RGB measurements produced inconsistent results across different media, with their performance being significantly inferior to HSV measurements.

To observe the difference between the developed miniaturized platform and a standard incubator for the Jaffe reaction at 37 °C, the detection of 2 mg/dL creatinine in FBS was conducted for both setups. Statistical analysis revealed that no significant difference in measurements was observed for these setups (Fig. 5). Hence, this study demonstrates the feasibility of conducting serum creatinine analysis on a compact and cost-effective platform (Table S2). As a result, it is now possible to perform portable creatinine analysis, with each test costing as little as $0.532, utilizing a device that is not larger than 4 × 8 × 12 cm3 and costs under $87.00, not including pipetting and personnel expenses. This achievement underscores the potential for accessible and affordable POC creatinine testing.

Comparison of the developed platform against the state-of-the-art POC methods

The POC devices for creatinine detection were summarised in (Table 1). Our platform, in addition to offering a wide linear range that covers the clinical spectrum, achieves one of the low LOD values when compared to other devices and gold standard spectrometric measurements for serum creatinine analysis. While concentrations below 0.5 mg/dL may not directly indicate CKD, the capability to detect such low levels enhances the overall sensitivity and utility of the diagnostic tools. The platform enables detection in ∼15 min using a minute sample volume of 25 µL. Furthermore, our system ensures robust measurements, unaffected by environmental conditions, through the integration of Peltier elements for temperature control and LED illumination for consistent lighting. Leveraging the Jaffe reaction as the detection principle, our platform requires nothing more than a simple microreservoir chip and a simple smartphone camera for analysis, facilitating low-cost creatinine measurements that can be performed today for ~ $10–45 per test in clinical settings and in POC devices costing ~ $35049. Moreover, the incorporation of automated image processing algorithms enables seamless creatinine detection from captured images. For our model, the average percentage error50 for serum creatinine detection was calculated as 8.96% within the detection range, which is suitable for eGFR measurements51. When testing the repeatability of the platform, the pooled relative standard deviation52 was found to be 29.2%, indicating that our platform allows for repeatable measurements53. It is expected that the pooled standard deviation could be decreased further with larger sample tests54. As a result, our platform offers a clear advantage over alternative methods and devices, enabling precise yet cost-effective creatinine measurements at the POC.

Conclusions and future works

CKD is a rapidly progressing condition that often necessitates dialysis, kidney transplants, or may even result in death if left untreated. Thus, regular monitoring and timely intervention at every stage of the disease are crucial. While it is possible to measure serum creatinine, a vital biomarker for CKD, in clinical settings, obtaining results within 24 hours is not feasible. The absence of affordable POC platforms prevents patients from monitoring their kidney function regularly. Therefore, there is a pressing need for remote, location-independent, and cost-effective monitoring solutions for serum creatinine levels. Here, we have engineered a portable and low-cost platform capable of detecting serum creatinine levels within microreservoir chips through colorimetric measurements in the HSV color space using a smartphone camera. Our innovation introduced a highly sensitive detection method that can automatically assess creatinine levels in 15 min, surpassing the performance of standard spectrometric and RGB measurements. The developed platform eliminates the requirement for pumps, valves, spectrometers, or integrated detection sensors, which can reduce the cost and complexity of detection systems.

Our platform can be functioned reliably during our testing process. However, regular maintenance and calibration could be conducted for the components of the platform to allow long-time and non-stop operations. Moreover, the presented platform can be used repeatedly for different measurements by simply changing the chip on the platform for each test. Since our platform is intended for use in POC settings, we will further test it with real patient samples under varying temperature, humidity, and pressure conditions in the future.

Although the platform's current design features compact dimensions suitable for benchtop use, it could be further miniaturized to handheld dimensions for enhanced portability. Currently, the system has been validated for detecting creatinine concentrations in serum, but it requires pre-treatment of whole blood samples. Future improvements could include integrating a filtration mechanism to directly process whole blood samples. Additionally, the platform's versatility extends to potential applications in urine creatinine detection and various colorimetric assays for biomarkers and molecules related to different diseases, highlighting its promise as a multifunctional diagnostic tool.

Mobile applications were used for automated analysis of colorimetric assays22. Currently, in our method, images are taken after the reaction concludes and are then analyzed automatically using an in-house developed program. However, in the near future, a mobile application can be developed for our method to enable fully automated analysis of serum creatinine levels. This application would direct the user for creatinine analysis and automate the assay process after sample introduction on the platform and use image analysis to provide measurement results. This approach can offer user-friendly and precise analysis with plug-and-play operations and a well-defined assay time, reducing user errors. To calibrate color and image settings for various mobile devices, we will analyze and adjust camera color variations using standard metrics in the HSV color space, ensuring efficient white balance calibration and minimizing color errors55. This will deliver more consistent shots by preventing color and image inconsistencies between devices.

Hence, the presented platform could significantly aid in the monitoring of CKD patients, particularly in small clinics and home settings, offering an affordable solution for CKD management. Since it provides analysis via mobile phone, it can be integrated easily with telemedicine systems and used for remote monitoring. In this way, healthcare professionals can provide rapid healthcare services by remotely accessing patients' data and gain the ability to quickly intervene in CKD patients. This approach has the potential to revolutionize CKD monitoring and management, leveraging the distinctive features of the platform.
