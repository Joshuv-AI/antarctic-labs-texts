Multi-omic insight into the molecular networks of macrophage polarization in the pathogenesis of atrial fibrillation
Authors: Rui Wang, Aoli Chen, Zongjun Liu, Wei Yang, Kai Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe intimate association between macrophage polarization and atrial fibrillation (AF) has garnered substantial research interest, yet the epigenetic, transcriptional, and proteomic regulation of immune-mediated atrial remodeling remain elusive. This study aims to elucidate the causal relationship between macrophage polarization and AF by integrating multi-omics datasets.MethodsThis study conducted an analysis utilizing genes pertinent to macrophage polarization from the GeneCards database, alongside QTL summary statistics for gene expression (eQTL), DNA methylation (mQTL), and protein expression (pQTL). Through summary-data-based Mendelian randomization (SMR), we explored associations between macrophage polarization-related genes and AF. Colocalization analyses were performed to identify common causal variants.ResultsWe discovered 52 mQTLs, 8 eQTLs, and 2 pQTLs linked to AF risk. Specifically, the genes DNMT3A (cg17742416, cg08485187, cg13558695, and cg08493294) and PRKCA (cg22127848) were associated with AF risk both in terms of gene expression (OR < 1) and methylation levels (OR > 1), with this association supported by colocalization. Further integrated analysis suggests that DNA methylation occurring at CpG sites in DNMT3A and PRKCA directly regulates gene expression (OR < 1) via cis-regulatory mechanisms, thereby influencing individual susceptibility to AF. Tissue-specific SMR analysis further supported the potential relevance of PRKCA in atrial appendages, where its expression levels strongly correlated with AF.ConclusionsEmploying a SMR approach, we have identified DNMT3A and PRKCA as candidate genes potentially implicated in the pathogenesis of AF. These findings pave the way for novel insights into early prevention and therapeutic strategies for AF.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13019-026-04413-1.

Background

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common persistent arrhythmia worldwide, affecting more than 33.5 million people worldwide and posing a significant burden on the public health system [1]. With the trend of aging of the global population, the incidence and prevalence of AF is increasing, becoming an increasingly serious public health problem [2]. AF not only has a serious impact on the health of individuals, but also imposes a significant burden on the economy, with annual medical costs of AF estimated to reach approximately $6 billion in the United States [3]. Thus, it is necessary to explore the causative factors of AF.

During the inflammatory response triggered by oxidative stress or hemodynamic overload within the atrial microenvironment, macrophages can be polarized towards two different phenotypes, broadly categorized as pro-inflammatory (classically activated) or anti-inflammatory (alternatively activated) [4]. Although traditionally categorized into binary M1/M2 phenotypes, contemporary understanding recognizes that macrophages exist along a dynamic phenotypic spectrum, with intermediate states that can shift in response to microenvironmental cues. For clarity, we herein refer to broadly pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory polarization states. Pro-inflammatory macrophages promote inflammation by releasing multiple cytokines, such as interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and are involved in the occurrence and development of AF [5]. For example, in patients with rheumatic mitral stenosis with AF, a large number of pro-inflammatory macrophage infiltrates are seen in the left atrial appendage, which is strongly associated with thrombosis. At the same time, NOD-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome was activated, further amplifying the inflammatory response and leading to apoptosis of cardiomyocytes [6]. In contrast, anti-inflammatory macrophages, while possessing inflammation-resolving properties, can paradoxically contribute to adverse structural remodeling in AF. Upon persistent activation, these cells secrete pro-fibrotic mediators, most notably transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), which promotes fibroblast activation, myofibroblast differentiation, and interstitial collagen deposition, thereby facilitating atrial fibrosis and perpetuating the arrhythmogenic substrate [7, 8]. Recent studies have shown that long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) NRON can promote the polarization of anti-inflammatory macrophages by inhibiting the expression of miR-23a, thereby alleviating atrial fibrosis in AF [9].

Summary-data Mendelian randomization (SMR) is an advanced approach that integrates multiple quantitative trait loci (QTL) datasets to investigate causal relationships between genetic variants and traits, offering a comprehensive understanding of genetic and epigenetic influences compared to traditional association studies that often do not account for pleiotropy or linkage disequilibrium (LD) [10, 11]. This innovative approach not only enhances the statistical strength of causal inference but also offers a comprehensive perspective by exploring the interactions between QTLs and AF across various molecular levels. This exploration can lay a theoretical basis for the development of disease prediction, prevention, and personalized treatment strategies.

In this study, we leverage SMR to merge multi-omics datasets, including gene expression, DNA methylation, and protein expression data, to pinpoint and validate genetic variants correlated with AF.

Materials and methods

Study design

In this study, we extracted instrumental variables for genes associated with macrophage polarization at the levels of DNA methylation, gene expression, and protein abundance. Subsequently, independent MR analyses were conducted for AF at each biological level. Utilizing a primary discovery dataset from the Nature Genetics repository, which comprised 60,620 cases and 970,216 controls, we performed MR analyses [12]. Validation was sought through additional datasets such as FinnGen, enhancing the strength of causal inference. Further insights were gained through colocalization analyses and verification of tissue-specific expression. By synthesizing the MR results across these three distinct biological strata, we pinpointed candidate genes with causal implications. The study design, along with the workflow for the selection of genetic variants and analytical methods, is encapsulated in Fig. 1.

Data resources

To investigate the genetic underpinnings of macrophage polarization, we queried the GeneCards database using the keyword “Macrophage Polarization”, and restricted our search to genes categorized as “Protein Coding”, yielding a total of 731 protein-coding genes for downstream analysis. No additional filtering based on GeneCards relevance scores or literature curation was applied, in order to ensure comprehensive coverage of all annotated macrophage polarization-related protein-coding genes. The primary discovery dataset consisted of GWAS data for AF from Nature Genetics (accession GCST006414), encompassing 60,620 cases and 970,216 controls, with a comprehensive coverage of 33,394,913 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) [12]. To validate our findings, we utilized the AF GWAS data (I9_AF) from the FinnGen R10 release and the AF GWAS dataset (GCST90043977) published in Nature Genetics for replication analyses [13]. Detailed information is provided in Table S1. Summary statistics for blood eQTLs were obtained from the eQTLGen consortium, including genetic effects on gene expression in blood from 31,684 individuals [14]. Summary statistics for blood mQTLs were derived from a meta-analysis of two European cohorts: the Brisbane Systems Genetics Study and the Lothian Birth Cohorts, comprising 614 and 1,366 individuals, respectively [15]. Summary statistics for blood pQTLs were sourced from the UKB-PPP cohort as reported by Benjamin et al., encompassing data from 54,219 donors [16].

To validate findings from blood-based QTL analyses in a cardiac tissue context, we performed SMR analysis using eQTL data from the Heart Atrial Appendage tissue. These eQTL summary statistics were obtained from GTEx v8, comprising 429 donors. Cis-QTLs were selected within a ± 1,000 kb window around each probe using a significance threshold of P < 5.0 × 10⁻⁸, with linkage disequilibrium estimated from the 1000 Genomes Project European reference panel. SMR parameters were identical to those used for blood eQTL analyses. This tissue-specific analysis was performed to assess whether macrophage polarization-related gene expression signals identified in blood are also detectable in a cardiac tissue directly relevant to AF pathophysiology.

SMR

The SMR and HEIDI tests were performed using the SMR software package version 1.3.1. SMR was employed to evaluate the association between methylation, expression, and protein abundance of macrophage polarization-related genes and AF. When exposure and outcome are derived from two large, independent cohorts, SMR based on top-ranked cis-QTLs yields greater statistical power than traditional MR analyses. Top-ranked cis-QTLs were selected within a window centered ± 1,000 kb around the corresponding probe, using a p-value threshold of 5.0 × 10− 8 [17]. SNPs with allelic frequency differences exceeding the specified threshold (set at 0.2 in this study) between any pairwise datasets were excluded. For mQTL, eQTL, and pQTL, the maximum proportion of SNPs with allelic frequency differences greater than 0.2 allowed was set to 0.05. Beyond investigating the causal links between QTL and AF, we delved into the causal effects of mQTL on eQTL, and eQTL on pQTL. The intersection resulting from the SMR analysis of mQTL and eQTL with GWAS was considered indicative of significant associations between these two QTL types. Based on SMR analysis, we employed a multi-SNP SMR methodology (smr-multi) [11]. This approach considers all SNPs within the QTL probe window region (default 500 kb) that exhibit P-values below the default threshold of 5.0 × 10− 8 and linkage disequilibrium r2 values below the default threshold of 0.9 with the most strongly associated SNP. In this study, significant outcomes derived from this approach will be comprehensively evaluated. Subsequently, results unaffected by pleiotropy were selected using an HEIDI test with a P-value threshold greater than 0.01 [18]. In the case of P-SMR < 0.05, results satisfying P-SMR multi < 0.05 and P-HEIDI > 0.01 were used for subsequent colocalization and integration analyses.

Colocalization analysis

Colocalization analyses were conducted using the R package “coloc” to detect shared causal variants between AF and the identified macrophage polarization-related mQTLs, eQTLs, or pQTLs. Within the colocalization analysis, 5 distinct posterior probabilities (PP) were reported, each corresponding to one of five exclusive hypotheses: H0: There is no genetic association between the trait and the SNP within this region; H1: Only Trait 1 exhibits a genetic association with the SNP; H2: Only Trait 2 shows a genetic linkage with the SNP; H3: Both traits are associated with the SNP but involve distinct causal variants; H4: Both traits are linked to the SNP and share a common causal variant. Based on the published literature [19–21], for the colocalization analysis between mQTL-GWAS, eQTL-GWAS, and pQTL-GWAS, the colocalization region windows were set at ± 500 kb, ± 1,000 kb, and ± 1,000 kb, respectively. To accommodate weaker QTL signals that might colocalize with GWAS signals, a two-pronged criterion was applied: colocalization success was determined when (1) the PP.H4 exceeded 0.5 for QTLs with a p12 value of 5 × 10− 5, and (2) the PP.H3 was less than 0.5 for QTLs with a p12 value of 1 × 10− 5 [22].

All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.0. For graphical representation, Manhattan plots were generated utilizing the R packages “ggplot2” and “ggrepel”, while forest plots were constructed with the “forestplot” package. The scripts for generating SMRLocusPlot and SMREffectPlot were sourced from a study conducted by Zhu et al., published in Nature Genetics in 2016 [11].

Results

Macrophage polarization-related gene expression and AF

A total of 34 macrophage polarization-related genes were identified as being associated with the risk of AF (P-SMR multi < 0.05, P-SMR < 0.05, P-HEIDI > 0.01, Fig. 2). All SMR analysis results of macrophage polarization-related eQTLs-GWAS presented in Table S2. We determined that the expression levels of 16 genes were positively correlated with the risk of AF, whereas those of the remaining 18 genes were negatively correlated. Among these, six genes (ALOX15, HNRNPF, JMJD1C, PCCB, PRKD3, and SIK3) were supported by both colocalization evidence and passed FDR validation, while two genes (DROSHA and VDR) were supported solely by colocalization evidence (Fig. 2 PP.H4 > 0.5 and PP.H3 < 0.5, FDR < 0.05).

The colocalization analysis revealed that 52 of the identified signals (associated with 30 genes) had strong evidence for colocalization within the regional window of the corresponding SNP (Fig. 3). 12 of these genes correspond to 27 methylation sites, where both methylation levels and gene expression are associated with risk of AF. Two of these genes (HNRNPF and PRKD3) received colocalization evidence support at both levels. The comprehensive analysis results can be found in Table S5. Among the key CpG sites, CHI3L1 (cg02097014) and DNMT3A (cg17742416, cg08485187, cg13558695, cg08493294) were significantly validated in both the FinnGen R10 AF cohort and the GCST90043977 AF cohort (Table 2, Tables S6-S7).

Integration of multi-omics evidence

Integrated multi-omics SMR and colocalization analyses revealed five genes (DNMT3A, PRKD3, HNRNPF, CTSC, and PRKCA) associated with AF risk at both methylation and gene expression levels. Further integration of methylation (mQTL) and gene expression (eQTL) data for SMR analysis revealed causal associations between methylation sites and their corresponding genes DNMT3A (cg17742416, cg08485187, cg13558695, cg08493294) and PRKCA (cg22127848). Consequently, DNMT3A and PRKCA are identified as genes with the strongest evidence linking them to AF risk. Detailed results are presented in Table 4. The chromosomal distribution of the critical CpG sites, genes, and encoded proteins is illustrated in Figure S1. Specifically, elevated methylation levels at four specific CpG sites (cg17742416, cg08485187, cg13558695, cg08493294) of DNMT3A were positively correlated with an increased risk of AF. Concurrently, the methylation at these sites inhibited the expression of the DNMT3A gene, yet higher expression levels of DNMT3A were associated with a decreased risk of AF (Figures S2 and S3A-S3D). For the cg26106370 site within the PRKD3 gene, lower methylation levels were linked to a higher risk of atrial fibrillation, with methylation levels inversely proportional to gene expression (Figures S4, S3E and S3F). Similarly, increased methylation at the cg22127848 site of the PRKCA gene was associated with a heightened AF risk, and this methylation suppressed gene expression, while higher expression of PRKCA correlated with a diminished AF risk (Figures S5 and S3G).

Discussion

The results of this study showed that the focused genes DNMT3A, PRKD3, and PRKCA showed specific associations with the risk of AF in the multiomics Mendelian randomization analysis. These findings provide important clues for further exploring the role of macrophage polarization in the pathogenesis of AF.

DNMT3A is a key de novo DNA methyltransferase that contributes to epigenetic regulation of gene expression, including processes relevant to cardiac remodeling and fibrosis. Experimental studies have shown that DNMT3A can influence fibrotic pathways through epigenetic silencing of RASSF1A and downstream modulation of ERK1/2 signaling, linking DNMT3A-mediated methylation to structural changes in the heart [23, 24]. Human genetic evidence further indicates that loss-of-function mutations or CHIP-associated DNMT3A variants are associated with enhanced pro‑inflammatory responses and increased cardiovascular risk, including arrhythmias, underscoring the relevance of DNMT3A functional integrity in cardiovascular health [25–27]. In the present study, our SMR analysis revealed that genetically predicted higher DNMT3A expression in blood is associated with reduced AF risk (OR < 1), suggesting a protective effect. Integrated methylation-expression analysis further indicated that methylation at specific DNMT3A CpG sites is inversely correlated with gene expression, consistent with epigenetic repression, such that lower methylation at these sites may permit higher DNMT3A expression. DNA methyltransferases, including DNMT3A, have been implicated in the regulation of macrophage polarization and inflammatory gene expression, processes that critically influence systemic tissue remodeling and immune responses in cardiovascular disease contexts [28].

Given the central role of DNMT3A in regulating macrophage polarization, these methylation-dependent expression changes may influence the atrial immune microenvironment by altering the macrophage secretome, thereby indirectly affecting atrial remodeling. In this context, higher circulating DNMT3A expression may promote a macrophage phenotype that is relatively anti‑inflammatory and anti‑fibrotic, thereby reducing maladaptive atrial remodeling and contributing to the observed protective association with AF risk. These findings refine previous observations by distinguishing the effects of DNMT3A expression level from those of DNMT3A loss‑of‑function mutations. Although DNMT3A is not currently a direct therapeutic target, emerging evidence implicates DNMT3A mutations in clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) as a contributor to elevated cardiovascular risk and AF [25–27]. Consequently, DNMT3A methylation or expression levels could serve as a potential predictive biomarker for AF risk stratification, adding clinical value to the multi-omics integration presented here.

Although the current study has not directly revealed the association between PRKD3 gene and AF, there is evidence that PRKD3 may play a role in the occurrence and development of AF through its effect on the polarization of macrophages. Previous studies have shown that PRKD3 plays an important role in regulating the polarization of macrophages, especially during liver fibrosis, where loss of PRKD3 causes macrophages to skew towards the pro-fibrotic phenotype, thus exacerbating liver damage [29]. Additionally, another study has shown that PFKFB3 is a key enzyme in glucose metabolism, and it can act as a target gene of c-MYC, affecting the polarization and glycolytic metabolism of macrophages [30]. Given the role of macrophages in AF and their pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic properties in a variety of heart diseases, we can infer that PRKD3 affects atrial tissue through a similar mechanism and may in turn be associated with the occurrence and development of AF. Furthermore, the role of PFKFB3-mediated glycolysis in myocardial fibrosis has been demonstrated, and intra-atrial fibrosis can change the structure of heart tissue, cause abnormalities in electrical pathways, and promote the occurrence and development of AF [31]. These findings, which are consistent with our results obtained through multi-omics Mendelian randomization analysis, provide a new perspective on the molecular mechanism of AF and point the way for future research.

Among the pQTL findings, the inverse association between genetically predicted circulating IL6R levels and AF risk merits specific discussion. This result does not contradict the well-established pro-inflammatory role of IL-6 signaling in AF; rather, it reflects the genetic distinction between soluble and membrane-bound IL-6 receptor forms. The pQTL instruments for IL6R, most notably the missense variant rs2228145, promote proteolytic cleavage of the membrane-bound receptor (mIL6R) into its soluble form (sIL6R) [32]. Consequently, elevated circulating sIL6R levels are an indicator of reduced mIL6R on the cell surface, which attenuates IL-6 trans‑signaling. This mechanism mirrors the pharmacological effect of IL-6 receptor blockers such as tocilizumab. This interpretation is consistent with the protective effect observed for genetically proxied IL6R blockade in our SMR analysis and reinforces the notion that inhibition of IL-6‑driven pro‑inflammatory macrophage polarization may confer protection against AF [33].

PRKCA emerged as a key gene exhibiting robust multi-omics associations with AF; however, its effects were notably tissue dependent. In blood-based analyses, genetically predicted higher PRKCA expression and protein abundance were associated with a reduced risk of AF, consistent with a protective role at the systemic immune level. PRKCA is known to regulate inflammatory signaling and mitochondrial quality control, and its activation has been shown to promote mitophagy and suppress excessive inflammatory responses in sepsis-induced organ injury [34]. These functions are highly relevant to AF, a condition characterized by chronic inflammation and oxidative stress, suggesting that PRKCA may mitigate AF risk by maintaining immune and mitochondrial homeostasis in circulating cells. In contrast, tissue-specific SMR analysis using atrial appendage eQTLs revealed that increased PRKCA expression within atrial tissue was associated with a higher risk of AF. This apparent directionality discrepancy between blood and cardiac tissue is not unexpected and likely reflects context-dependent and compartment-specific roles of PRKCA. Although PRKCA is also known to function in atrial myocytes, its effects on inflammation-driven electrical remodeling may be secondary to its primary role in shaping the atrial immune microenvironment through macrophage polarization [35]. Additionally, angiotensin II–induced atrial remodeling has been associated with altered PRKCA signaling and reduced sodium current, further promoting arrhythmogenic substrates [36]. While the role of PRKCA in myocardial remodeling and arrhythmogenesis is well established, the present study extends this knowledge by providing integrated multi-omics evidence that PRKCA expression is under upstream epigenetic control by DNA methylation at the cg22127848 CpG site. This cis-regulatory mechanism, linking methylation to gene expression and subsequently to AF risk, reveals a previously unrecognized layer of regulation at the macrophage polarization-AF interface and pinpoints a specific epigenetic locus that could inform future mechanistic and therapeutic investigations.

Taken together, these findings suggest a dual role for PRKCA in AF pathogenesis: systemically, higher PRKCA activity may be protective by dampening inflammation and preserving mitochondrial function, whereas locally within atrial tissue, PRKCA upregulation may reflect a maladaptive or compensatory response that contributes to electrical remodeling and AF maintenance. This tissue-specific divergence underscores the importance of considering cellular context when interpreting genetically proxied gene expression effects and highlights PRKCA as a regulator with pleiotropic, compartment-dependent functions in AF.

Our integrated multi-omics analysis not only encompassed traditional gene expression analysis but also extended to the epigenetic and proteomic levels, providing richer insights into the complex pathophysiological mechanisms of AF. Despite the strengths of our methodology, several limitations warrant acknowledgment. Primarily, our analysis was based on summary-level data, necessitating validation through replication in independent cohorts to ensure the robustness and generalizability of our findings. Additionally, the inherent complexity of epigenetic regulation implies that our results might capture only a portion of the broader regulatory network at play. Moreover, while our multi-omics approach provided a comprehensive overview, the observational nature of the study precludes definitive causal inference. To substantiate our findings, targeted functional validation studies are required. Furthermore, the eQTL, mQTL, and pQTL summary statistics used in this study were all derived from whole blood samples, which represent a composite of multiple immune cell populations rather than purified macrophages. Consequently, the identified expression and methylation signatures may reflect a systemic immune state that involves, but is not restricted to, macrophage polarization. Future studies incorporating single-cell sequencing data or cell-type-specific QTLs from purified macrophages will be essential to validate the cell-autonomous nature of these associations and to further delineate the precise immune cell populations driving AF susceptibility. We also acknowledge that the sample sizes of the QTL datasets differ markedly across omics layers. The mQTL dataset (n ≈ 2,000) is substantially smaller than the eQTL (n = 31,684) and pQTL (n = 54,219) datasets, which likely results in lower statistical power to detect associations between DNA methylation and phenotypic traits. Consequently, our findings may underestimate the contribution of DNA methylation, and some weaker signals might have been missed. Nevertheless, associations that survived our stringent SMR and colocalization thresholds are expected to be robust. Finally, it is important to note that the SMR and colocalization analyses identify genetic associations that are compatible with a causal relationship; however, they do not directly establish DNMT3A or PRKCA as biological drivers of AF. The term “candidate” is used throughout to reflect this level of evidence, and definitive mechanistic validation will require experimental studies.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study underscores the significance of macrophage polarization-related genes in the development of AF. These findings set the stage for future research, including mechanistic studies aimed at elucidating the precise roles of these genes and considerations of genetic diversity in shaping study outcomes. Future investigations building on these findings will be instrumental in unraveling the complex biological mechanisms of AF and developing effective preventive and therapeutic strategies.
