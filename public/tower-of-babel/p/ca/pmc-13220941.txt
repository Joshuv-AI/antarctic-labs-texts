Evaluating an interprofessional team coaching program to promote a human-centered ICU environment
Authors: Jami L Simpson, John McCartney, Jacqueline M Kruser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTechnology-driven ICU environments can be dehumanizing for critically ill patients, their families, and team members who care for them. Prior educational efforts to humanize the ICU have focused on training clinicians to provide human-centered care for patients and families, with relatively little attention on promoting human-centered environments for team members.ObjectiveWe used the framework of team coaching to develop an interprofessional educational program focused on humanizing the ICU. We sought to evaluate whether this program improves participants’ perceived capability to promote a human-centered environment for the ICU team.MethodsWe conducted a 3-year, mixed-methods evaluation of a novel interprofessional ICU team coaching program in a medical/surgical/trauma ICU within an academic medical center. The 3-session program is led by a certified coach, and annual cohorts are a subset of the ICU team. Session topics are: role identification, end-of-life care, and practices of dignity and respect. We conducted pre- and post-coaching participant surveys and qualitative content analysis of coaching reflections, open-ended survey responses, and an audio-recorded debriefing session.ResultsOver 3 years, 43 ICU team members representing 7 professional roles (advanced practice providers, attending physicians, chaplains, fellow physicians, nurses, respiratory therapists, social workers) participated. The overall session attendance rate was 70%. Before participation, most reported a need for training to promote a human-centered environment for patients (78.1%), families (81.3%), and other members of the team (81.3%). After coaching, this unmet need decreased to 28.6% for patients (P < .01), 42.9% for families (P < .01), and 33.3% for team members (P < .01). Participants’ self-reported ability to provide optimal human-centered care was similar pre-(84.4%) and post-coaching (81.8%; P = .80). Participants’ scores on the interprofessional version of the Consultation and Relational Empathy (CARE) measure improved from 37.1 (95% CI 34.3-39.9) pre-coaching to 40.7 post-coaching (95% CI 38.8-42.6; P = .047). Qualitative analysis revealed commitment to growth in interprofessional relationships among participants and a desire to expand the program’s reach.ConclusionOur findings confirm an unmet need among ICU team members for training in how to promote human-centered ICU environments and suggest that interprofessional team coaching is a promising tool to help meet this need.

Introduction

ICUs are designed around the technology used to extend the lives of patients facing life-threatening illness and injury. Within this highly technical, fast-paced environment, the humanity of critically ill patients, their families, and the professionals who care for them is often overlooked.1 Many within the critical care community—clinicians, patients, families, and researchers—have been highlighting the negative impact of this dehumanizing ­environment and calling for change.2–5

Existing efforts to humanize the ICU environment have, with good reason, focused on the experience of patients and their families. Yet, members of the interprofessional ICU team are also affected by the dehumanizing features of this environment, which is likely reflected in their substantial levels of professional distress and dissatisfaction.6–8 This professional strain is exacerbated by the interpersonal conflict common within ICU teams.9 Interpersonal conflict and the social boundaries within ICU teams generated by clinical hierarchy and role-related responsibilities further perpetuate dehumanization and poor quality care.10,11 Thus, an important next step in humanizing the ICU is promoting a human-centered environment for interprofessional team members.

Team coaching is a promising, human-centered approach used in other industries to improve relationships and collaboration within teams.12-14 At its core, coaching is a strategy used to help people improve performance. Individual professional coaching has recently become widespread in healthcare as a method of professional development.15-19 Team coaching, which is still an emerging approach in healthcare, builds from the individual coaching model to help teams of people work better, together.20,21 While there are multiple team coaching models and frameworks, newer approaches intentionally address intra-team dynamics and conflict by seeking to build interpersonal relationships as fundamental to team performance.22,23

We theorized that this relational concept of team coaching could help humanize the ICU environment for its interprofessional team members. Thus, we developed an annual, interprofessional, human-centered team coaching program for ICU ­professionals. In this study, we sought to evaluate whether this coaching program improves participants’ perceived capability to promote a human-centered environment for the ICU team.

Methods

We conducted a 3-year, mixed-methods evaluation of an interprofessional ICU team coaching program. The coaching program is repeated annually in a combined medical, surgical, and trauma ICU within an academic medical center in the Midwest. Team coaching is one component of a larger educational initiative within the medical center’s Pulmonary and Critical Care Medicine (PCCM) fellowship program, known as Human-centered Education, Action, and Research in critical care Training (HEART). The HEART curriculum is primarily for PCCM fellows and was launched in 2022 to teach fellows the necessary skills to promote human-centered ICU environments for critically ill patients, their families, and the professionals who care for them. Other components of HEART include didactic lectures, an interprofessional shadowing program, and moderated reflection events. This program evaluation study was reviewed and determined to be exempt by the Minimal Risk Research branch of the Institutional Review Board at the University of Wisconsin-Madison (Study 2023-0812). Participants in any component of the HEART educational program have the option to opt out of research activities (ie, surveys and debriefing sessions).

Team coaching program description

The annual team coaching program started in 2022 and is attended by a subset of the interprofessional team staffing the study ICU. Each year, the subset “team” (herein cohort) is comprised of first-year critical care fellows (n = 4-6 per year; PCCM, anesthesia, and surgical ICU), attending critical care physicians (n = 2), and voluntary participants from each of the following roles from the ICU: advanced practice provider, chaplain, nurse, respiratory therapist, and social worker. ICU pharmacists and case managers are also invited but have been unable to attend to date. Because our goal is to build a sustained program that eventually reaches most members of the entire interprofessional team, each year’s cohort is comprised of new participants except for ICU roles fulfilled by only 1 or 2 individuals, who are invited annually. Prior to starting the program, we sought the support and endorsement of the relevant ICU staff managers and fellowship directors, who assist in protecting time and recruiting volunteer participants for the program each year.

The team coaching program is a series of 3 in-person sessions, with detailed session information and objectives in Appendix S1. Each session is 90 min, includes lunch, and takes place in a hospital conference room. The first session focuses on interprofessional role identification, aiming to increase understanding and appreciation of all the members of the team and the important role they each play in ICU care. Building from this understanding, the second session focuses on end-of-life care and its impact across the ICU team. Finally, the third session focuses on practices of dignity and respect in the ICU and aims to increase awareness of the pragmatic, everyday actions that can promote dignity for patients, families, and other ICU team members. Participants also receive a coaching reflection journal and time at the beginning and end of each session to reflect on new insights, how they plan to use these insights in their daily work, and how their behavior has changed since the previous coaching session(s).

The coaching sessions are led by an associate-level professional coach certified through the International Federation of Coaching (ICF) with additional certification in trauma-informed coaching. She is also the Pulmonary and Critical Care Fellowship Program Manager, which provides unique insight into the ICU clinical environment while remaining outside the clinical hierarchy. We also sought consultation on program development and expansion from an experienced healthcare team coach, with 8 years of team coaching experience and 39 years of experience as a nurse. This consultant contributed to session development and co-facilitated sessions in the second year of the program.

One key feature of our team coaching approach is setting explicit ground rules that promote safety and dissolve power dynamics and hierarchy (eg, between attending physicians and nurses or trainees). The coach also uses “powerful questioning,” which is a technique of asking brief (5-7 word limit), open-ended questions (starting with “what” or “how”) that intentionally target assumptions or fixed beliefs.24 For example, if a nurse says, “Physicians don’t seem to understand what it’s like to do invasive things to a patient near the end of life,” a team coach might respond with the following powerful question: “What makes doctors different?” The intent of powerful questioning is to increase awareness and build shared understanding of a multifaceted scenario. Program evaluation

We evaluated whether this team coaching program improves participants’ perceived capability to promote a human-centered environment for the ICU team using pre- and post-coaching self-assessment surveys. The survey included participants’ self-reported evaluation of their own: (1) need for additional training to promote a human-centered ICU environment; (2) current performance in promoting an optimal human-centered ICU environment; (3) interprofessional empathy, as measured by the interprofessional version of the Consultation and Relational Empathy (CARE) measure.25,26 The CARE measure has a maximum score of 50, with higher scores representing higher self-rated empathy for other interprofessional team members. We also asked participants the extent to which they agree the critical care experience in the study ICU is human-centered for (1) patients, (2) families, and (3) ICU team members. The full survey instrument is available in Appendix S2. We used descriptive statistics, t-tests, and tests of proportions to evaluate differences between pre- and post-coaching survey responses, using a P-value of <.05 to define statistical significance.

We also used an inductive approach to qualitative content analysis27 to analyze the open-ended text data from coaching reflections, open-ended survey responses, and a 30-min, audio-recorded program debriefing group session conducted after the third year of the program. We pursued 2 analytic objectives with the qualitative analyses: (1) to characterize how team coaching impacted the participants; and (2) to characterize participants’ views on the program, including areas to improve. One investigator (J.L.S.) initially reviewed all text data to inductively identify preliminary codes, which were iteratively reviewed and revised during regular meetings between J.L.S. and J.M.K. After reaching agreement on the coding taxonomy and code definitions, J.L.S. re-reviewed all text data, applying codes to relevant sections. J.L.S. and J.M.K. reviewed and discussed coded text together to generate higher-level themes.

Results

Over 3 years, 43 interprofessional team members participated in the annual coaching program out of the approximate 166-member unit staff (Table 2). The annual cohort size ranged from 8 to 19 participants, and the number of distinct professional roles represented in each cohort ranged from 5 to 7. The overall program attendance rate across the 9 sessions was 70%, with ≥5 distinct roles participating in every session. We received 32 pre-coaching survey responses (74.4% response rate) and 22 post-coaching survey responses (51.2% response rate). The mean interprofessional CARE score (maximum score of 50) among participants improved from 37.1 [95% CI 34.3-39.9] pre-coaching to 40.7 post-coaching [95% CI 38.8-42.6]; P = .047. Before coaching, most participants reported having an unmet need for training in how to promote a human-centered environment for ICU patients (78.1%), for families in the ICU (81.3%), and for other members of the ICU team (81.3%). After coaching, the proportion of participants reporting these unmet needs decreased to 28.6% for ICU patients (P < .01); to 42.9% for families (P < .01); and to 33.3% for ICU team members (P < .01; Figure 1). Similarly, the proportion of participants who reported receiving adequate professional training related to promoting an overall human-centered ICU environment increased from 53.1% pre-coaching to 86.4% post-coaching (P = .01).

Interprofessional ICU clinicians’ perspectives on their own perceived capability to promote a human-centered ICU environment before and after participation in a team coaching program.

Despite these self-identified training gaps prior to team coaching, most participants agreed or strongly agreed that they currently promote and provide optimal human-centered care in both pre- (84.4%) and post-coaching surveys (81.8%; P = .80). When asked to rate the ICU environment where they work in the pre-coaching survey, 47.9% of participants agreed or strongly agreed that the environment was human-centered for ICU team members. This increased to 68.2% post-coaching (P = .17). More participants agreed or strongly agreed the ICU environment was human-centered for patients (70.8% pre-coaching; 77.3% post-coaching [P = .62]) and for families (60.9% pre-coaching; 68.2% post-coaching [P = .61]).

Relational growth was characterized by choosing behaviors that would build upon human connections already present ­within the ICU team. Participants aimed to improve these connections through a better understanding of others’ experiences and ­viewpoints and through “giving grace” to others. Coaching participants also committed to several areas of personal growth that would also improve their interprofessional relationships, including increasing their patience and building self-awareness.

Notably, these growth reflections were typically signaled by the word “more,” suggesting that participants felt they already knew of or possessed the desired skills. The team coaching process served to prompt and encourage continued and intentional development in how ICU team members relate to each other and themselves. For example, 1 participant reflected:Something I have already seen in myself and I know it will continue in the future, is feeling like I am a lot more patient. Even after our first session, understanding all our different roles, and different hats we need to work and backgrounds. It has helped me be more thoughtful with how I communicate and the goals I have with my communication.

Participants’ perspectives on the team coaching program

When asked to reflect on the coaching program and how to improve it, participants focused on ways to increase and expand the program. These suggestions included making coaching sessions longer (“an hour and a half is pretty short and I don’t want to cut it off. I think this is worth a 4-hour chunk of time”) or continuing the group interactions beyond the 3 planned sessions (“what if we had a cohort bubble that continued […] as a better web of foundations for our intersecting relationships”). Several participants suggested incorporating this interprofessional team coaching process into the orientation process for all new ICU staff, which, “would set a standard and culture for the care that we want to incorporate for our patients, families, and team members.”

Discussion

In this program evaluation, we found that an interprofessional team coaching program within a mixed medical-surgical ICU increases participants’ perceived capability to promote a human-centered environment for ICU team members. We identified an unmet need for training in how to promote human-centeredness for other ICU team members and demonstrated that our annual, 3-session interprofessional coaching program helps fulfill this need. The feasibility of this program, even within a large, busy, interprofessional ICU team, is demonstrated by our sustained, 3-year program with satisfactory session attendance rates (70%) and by participants’ desire to extend and expand the reach of the program. Moreso, participants’ reflections demonstrate the value of this type of program to encourage professional, relational growth.

Our findings underscore the problem of dehumanizing ICU environments, especially for members of the ICU team. Before participating in coaching, almost all participants reported a need for additional training in how to promote human-centeredness among team members, and less than half felt that their current work environment was human-centered for ICU team members. These findings are consistent with prior work demonstrating the dehumanizing nature of the ICU environment for patients and families2-5 and advance our understanding of this problem to highlight the impact on interprofessional team members.

Interprofessional educational efforts are increasingly recognized as a crucial component of critical care delivery and its ­improvement.28 In this study, we contribute to this growing ­recognition by demonstrating the potential of team coaching as a specific tool to accomplish interprofessional education. In healthcare, the dominant focus of coaching interventions has been on individual performance and professional development,15-18 and our study builds from this prior work to demonstrate the promise of team coaching. While this study’s primary focus was on the topic of human-centered ICU care, our findings suggest that team coaching may be valuable for additional educational priorities. In our own institution, based on the success of this program, ­other clinical teams have requested our assistance in developing interprofessional coaching programs with different goals (eg, improving collaboration across subspecialty inpatient and outpatient settings). One potential explanation for the impact of team coaching is that its core techniques (eg, ground rules and powerful questioning) are specifically designed to attend to hierarchy and role-related power dynamics, which are prevalent problems within healthcare teams9,10,29 and barriers for a successful interprofessional intervention.

This program evaluation has limitations to consider when interpreting the results. In the first 3 years of this program, we only reached approximately 26% of the ∼166-member interprofessional staff who work in the study unit. Furthermore, we focused on measuring change in individuals’ perceived capability to promote a human-centered ICU environment and not on overall unit-level culture or on the impact for non-participants. Thus, it remains unknown whether team coaching changes the entire ICU environment and culture over time. Nevertheless, we have been able to sustain and grow this program for 3 years, which suggests that team coaching could offer a durable approach that will ultimately, with the addition of each annual cohort, reach almost all members of the large ICU team. While we were able to successfully build this program within an academic medical center and a single ICU, it also remains unknown whether this type of program can be successfully scaled and disseminated to other institutions and environments. For example, the coach leading our program is a uniquely cross-trained fellowship Program Manager and Professional Coach. Whether other ICUs would be able to identify a similarly skilled coach with knowledge of the clinical environment without being part of the clinical hierarchy is unknown. Future work should focus on testing and disseminating team coaching in other ICU settings. Additionally, we had a lower post-coaching survey response rate (51.2%), and it is unknown whether our results could have been affected by survey non-response bias. PCCM fellows were also exposed to other elements of the HEART curriculum, which could have influenced their post-participation survey results. Given this smaller sample size (n = 22) of post-coaching evaluations, we may not have had the statistical power to detect statistically significant differences. Relatedly, the use of self-reported survey measures is susceptible to self-report bias and a ceiling effect, which could overestimate and/or limit our ability to detect the impact of team coaching.

Conclusions

In this program evaluation, we found that team coaching can increase interprofessional ICU team members’ perceived capability to promote and foster human-centered connection within an interprofessional ICU team. Our findings confirm an unmet need among ICU team members for training in how to promote a human-centered ICU environment and suggest that team coaching is a promising tool to help meet this need.
