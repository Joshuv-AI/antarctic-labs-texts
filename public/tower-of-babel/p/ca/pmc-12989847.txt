Selenium-catalyzed organic transformations
Authors: Avinash Chaurasia, Priya, Sanjana Srivastav, Abhishek Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySelenium-catalyzed organic transformations have recently emerged as a promising frontier in modern organic synthesis. The unique combination of high polarizability, redox tunability, and σ-hole-driven Lewis acidity enables selenium to act as powerful non-covalent activators in both covalent and non-covalent catalytic cycles. These features allow organoselenium catalysts to stabilize transition states, facilitate bond activation, and deliver selectivity unattainable with traditional organocatalysts. Despite remarkable advances, the field remains in its infancy, hindered by mechanistic ambiguities, limited enantioselective control, and scalability challenges. This article summarizes recent progress in selenium-based organocatalysis with a special emphasis on chalcogen bonding, applications in synthesis, and evaluates the current limitations that impede large scale industrial adaptation by discussing existing challenges with perspectives for future industrial translation.Subject areas: Chemistry, Organic chemistry, Organic synthesis, Applied sciences

Introduction

Catalysts accelerate chemical reactions without being consumed or undergoing permanent chemical changes, making catalysis an environmentally friendly and economically advantageous process.1 In fact, as of 2022, approximately 95% of all chemical products by volume were synthesized using catalytic methods.2 Catalysts are broadly categorized based on their phase relative to the reactants: homogeneous catalysts, which exist in the same phase and offer high reactivity and selectivity, and heterogeneous catalysts, which exist in a different phase and are prized for their ease of separation and reuse.3,4 Particularly in selenium chemistry immobilized catalysts have inherent advantages over the homogenous catalyst as illustrated in Table 1. Additionally, catalysts are classified according to their mode of interaction with reactants as either covalent or non-covalent. Covalent catalysts temporarily form covalent bonds with reactants, altering the reaction pathway, while non-covalent catalysts rely on weaker interactions such as hydrogen bonding or van der Waals forces, providing high selectivity without forming covalent bonds.5

In chemical synthesis, covalent catalysts are more common and have their own merits and demerits. To overcome some of the limitations of covalent catalysts, researchers have employed non-covalent catalysis as an alternative strategy.9,10 Non-covalent catalysts are less explored than their covalent counterparts. Among the non-covalent catalysts chalcogen incorporated organocatalysts have been well established in the past few decades, and it is important to know the basics of non-covalent interactions (Ch-Ch, Ch-X, Ch-N, etc.) bonds for a better understanding of non-covalent catalysis. A schematic representation of chalcogen mediated reaction of covalent and non-covalent interactions is shown in Figure 1.11,12

From covalent Lewis-base catalysis to non-covalent chalcogen-X bonding catalysis

Chalcogen bonding is a specialized type of non-covalent interaction in which an electrophilic chalcogen atom engages with nucleophilic sites such as another chalcogen (Ch···Ch), pnictogen (Ch···N, Ch···P), or halogen (Ch···X) as shown in Figure 2A. In catalysis, a Lewis-acidic chalcogen center within the catalyst can interact with substrates or intermediates through such directional σ-hole interactions, thereby stabilizing transition states and enhancing reaction rates.13 These non-covalent interactions provide a complementary mode of catalyst-substrate recognition and activation that is distinct from traditional hydrogen bonding or classical Lewis acid catalysis, enabling new opportunities to modulate reaction selectivity, stereocontrol, and mechanistic pathways. In particular, the exploitation of selenium-mediated chalcogen bonding represents a versatile strategy for the rational design of asymmetric transformations and the development of next-generation organocatalysts. The seminal contributions demonstrated that simple organic molecules could efficiently catalyze a wide range of transformations through non-metal-mediated mechanisms.14,15 Since, organocatalysis has developed into a powerful and versatile branch of catalysis, offering sustainable, metal-free alternatives to traditional transition-metal catalysis and becoming a well-established classification within catalytic chemistry.15,16

The high enantioselectivity observed in selenium-catalyzed transformations arises from strict catalyst-controlled enantiocontrol during electrophilic alkene activation. Enantioselective formation of a chiral seleniranium intermediate ensures effective facial discrimination of the prochiral alkene, which is translated into pronounced asymmetric induction in the subsequent cyclization step. Cooperative noncovalent catalyst-substrate interactions further reinforce this enantiocontrol. Control experiments, comprehensive substrate-scope studies, and consistent trends in enantiomeric excess across structurally diverse substrates collectively demonstrate that the observed enantioselectivity is governed predominantly by catalyst architecture rather than inherent substrate bias, resulting in uniformly high enantioselectivities under mild reaction conditions.16,17

Organocatalysis, when extended to encompass chalcogen-bonding activation, represents an emerging frontier in non-metal catalysis wherein electrophilic chalcogen centers act as Lewis acids or non-covalent σ-hole donors to modulate substrate electronics, stabilize transition states, or facilitate halide abstraction. Among these, selenium-based organocatalysis has gained particular prominence due to selenium’s optimal combination of polarizability, moderate bond strength, and tunable redox chemistry providing sufficient electrophilicity to participate in chalcogen bonding while maintaining chemical stability under catalytic conditions. Recent advances demonstrate that selenium-containing catalysts, or those featuring selenium donor sites, can achieve significant rate enhancements, stereocontrol, and access to novel reactivity modes through precise exploitation of chalcogen bonding interactions. Covalent bonding polarizes the atom, resulting in a σ-hole—a region of positive electrostatic potential opposite the bond axis—due to electron withdrawal into the R–Ch σ∗ orbital, allowing electrostatic attraction to negative potentials (lone pairs/π) (Figure 2B). This is the origin of the anisotropic charge distribution around the chalcogen. This is surrounded by negative potential from lone pairs, which enforces strong directionality (R–Ch···D angle ∼180°). The σ-hole is enhanced by electron-withdrawing substituents, and strength increases along the group (Se/Te > S > O).18,19,20

Chalcogen bonding is a directional non-covalent interaction and this interaction arises due to the presence of a σ-hole opposite to the covalent bond on the chalcogen atom, allowing the formation of highly oriented donor-acceptor complexes, a brief timeline of the chalcogen bonding evolution mediated catalysis is shown in Figure 3.4,7,10,11 The strength and directionality of chalcogen bonding can be modulated by steric and electronic effects, where increased steric congestion around selenium can partially shield the σ-hole and reduce catalytic efficiency. However, recent developments have demonstrated that chalcogen bonding itself can directly contribute to substrate activation and transition-state stabilization, particularly in sulfur and selenium-based Lewis base catalysis.21 An early example of chalcogen bonding in catalysis was observed in the complexation of elemental iodine with diphenyl diselenide, showcasing the amphiphilic behavior of halogens and chalcogens. This interaction demonstrated their ability to stabilize reactive intermediates through dual electrophilic and nucleophilic character. By tuning the steric and electronic features of selenium frameworks, substrate recognition, activation, and selectivity can be modulated via combined covalent and non-covalent pathways.

The unique combination of moderate bonding strength, reversible Se(II)/Se(IV) redox chemistry, and the ability to generate directional σ-hole interactions positions selenium as a particularly versatile catalyst in modern organic synthesis. Selenium compounds can engage in redox catalytic cycles while simultaneously forming transient chalcogen-bonding interactions with substrates, providing dual activation modes. This dual behavior has enabled selenium catalysts to participate in a broad range of transformations, including oxidative couplings, halogenations, lactonizations, C–X/C–C bond formations, and asymmetric reactions. Recent studies have clearly shown that selenium-based catalytic systems can operate not only through classical covalent activation but also by exploiting non-covalent chalcogen bonding as a driving force for catalysis.22,23

The first explicit use of selenium-centered chalcogen-bond donor catalysis was reported by Huber and co-workers, who demonstrated σ-hole-mediated halide abstraction in the solvolysis of benzhydryl bromide using a designed selenium-based chalcogen bondingdonor catalyst. Since then, organoselenium catalysis has rapidly evolved from a specialized domain to a widely applicable strategy in synthetic methodology. The high polarizability of selenium, its ability to form hypervalent intermediates, and its compatibility with mild, metal-free conditions have made it an attractive alternative to traditional transition metal catalysis, especially in the context of sustainable synthesis.12,24

Over the past decade, selenium-based catalysts have been successfully applied to diverse transformations such as alkene and alkyne functionalization, C–H activation, intramolecular cyclizations, and radical-mediated processes, often achieving excellent regio and stereocontrol. Compared to conventional metal catalysts, selenium catalysts offer several advantages, including operational simplicity, tolerance to moisture and air, and compatibility with green oxidants like molecular oxygen. More recently, the concept of selenium π-acid catalysis has gained prominence, where electrophilic selenium species interact with π-systems such as alkenes and alkynes, lowering their LUMO energy and enabling nucleophilic attack under very mild conditions. These catalytic systems often proceed via selenonium ion intermediates or trimeric selenium species, which can undergo subsequent oxidative functionalization or cyclization with high selectivity.25,26

Selenium-based catalysts have emerged as environmentally benign and efficient alternatives to conventional metal catalysts for industrial applications. In methane pyrolysis, selenium-promoted molten metal systems such as NiBi and CuBi exhibit enhanced catalytic performance through controlled selenium incorporation, which lowers methane C–H activation barriers, enriches exposed Ni active sites, and reduces surface tension, thereby outperforming traditional metal catalysts. Beyond molten systems, selenium immobilized on metal and non-metal oxide supports has gained increasing attention as both an active catalytic center and a promoter.27 Compared with conventional metal catalysts, supported selenium systems demonstrate higher catalytic efficiency, improved thermal and operational stability, and reduced metal loading, arising from selenium’s redox flexibility and strong metal-support interactions. Their low cost, durability, and scalability collectively underscore the feasibility of selenium-based catalysts for practical industrial utilization as shown in Table 2.

The term “selenium π-acid catalysis” encompasses a growing class of reactions where electrophilic selenium intermediates activate π-bonds to promote allylic and vinylic functionalizations, oxidative difunctionalizations, lactonizations, and asymmetric variants. Recent mechanistic investigations have shed light on the formation and reactivity of hypervalent selenium intermediates, the role of auxiliary oxidants and photoredox partners, and strategies to control regio- and enantio-selectivity. In many cases, selenium π-activation is combined with chalcogen bonding or redox cycling, resulting in catalytic manifolds that integrate noncovalent activation with controlled covalent reactivity.30,31,32

Importantly, the nature and strength of the σ-hole are governed by the polarizability of the central atom and the electronic properties of the substituents bonded to it, providing a rational means to tune these interactions. Interestingly, owing to their divalent nature, chalcogen atoms exhibit two σ-holes, and up to three in chalcogenium species, in contrast to the single σ-hole typically observed for halogen atoms. As a result, multiple chalcogen-bonding interactions can be established simultaneously. Moreover, chalcogen atoms display ambivalent behavior, acting as Lewis acids through their σ-holes while simultaneously serving as Lewis bases via their lone pairs. Particularly for selenium, its high polarizability allows fine modulation of σ-hole depth and directionality, enabling simultaneous Lewis acidic and Lewis basic behavior that is crucial for cooperative substrate activation and transition-state stabilization.20,24

In this perspective, we aim to provide a comprehensive overview of recent advances in selenium centered organocatalysis, including covalent Lewis base catalysis, non-covalent chalcogen bond-driven activation, selenium π-acid catalysis and redox-mediated transformations and emerging dual-activation strategies.33,34 The library of important selenium-based catalysts is shown in Figure 4. Furthermore, the limitations associated with catalyst stability, recyclability, and large-scale implementation will be critically analyzed, followed by future perspectives on integrating selenium-based catalysis into sustainable and multifunctional reaction platforms.

Important selenium-based catalysts used in various organic transformation

Lewis base-mediated catalysis

General overview of catalytic activity

Although counterintuitive at first glance, chalcogen bonding operates in Lewis base catalysis, where a perpendicular interaction to the R–Ch–R′ axis plays a decisive role in activation. Yeung and co-workers first showcased this concept in 2013 in the chloro-amidation of alkenes, demonstrating that diphenyl selenide could efficiently promote halo-amidation via a noncovalent Se-based interaction rather than classical nucleophilic activation. This finding broadened the functional scope of chalcogen bonding beyond electrophilic catalysis.35

Concurrently, sulfur-based chiral nucleophilic catalysts established themselves as versatile organocatalysts with diverse activation modes, setting the stage for analogous advances in selenium catalysis. In selenium systems, Se···π interactions can trigger ring-opening pathways, which proceed even in the presence of additional chalcogen-bond donors such as Se···O complexes, underscoring their flexible mechanistic profile.36

Chiral organoselenium catalysts, as well as achiral selenium species combined with chiral co-catalysts, now enable a range of enantioselective transformations including electrophilic additions, cyclizations, oxidative difunctionalizations, and sigmatropic rearrangements. These reactions proceed under mild conditions, with stereocontrol shaped by substrate and catalyst design, although background racemic processes often compete.37,38,39,40,41

Selenium catalyzed reaction of various Indole

Selenium catalysis has also been largely shaped by the development of chalcogen···π and chalcogen···O bonding as effective non-covalent activation modes. Vinylindole-based Diels-Alder benchmark studies demonstrated that Se···π interactions enhance activation of π-rich substrates, with dual interaction models enabling stereocontrol as in Scheme 1 (P2).11 In 2025, Zeng and co-workers provided theoretical insight using SeF2 and TeF2 as divalent chalcogen-bond donors, clarifying the mechanistic features of π-activation and providing guidelines for rational catalyst design.43

These advances collectively establish selenium-based chalcogen bonding as a versatile and sustainable catalytic platform, enabling selective activation of π-systems, carbonyls, haloamides, and now nitroalkenes. The transition from classical redox-dependent covalent activation to precisely oriented non-covalent chalcogen bonding activation marks a defining evolution in organoselenium catalysis, opening new design space for supramolecular catalysis driven by directional σ-hole interactions.

Diaryl selenides have attracted considerable attention due to their reversible representative example is the vicinal trifluoromethyl thiolation-hydroxylation of alkenes developed by Zhao and co-workers,44 in which N–CF3S saccharin served as the trifluoromethylthiol source. Selenium catalyzed reactions of various olefins

In chloroamidation reactions, several Lewis basic chalcogen-containing catalysts were evaluated for promoting halogen transfer. Triphenylphosphine oxide was inactive, while its sulfur and selenium analogs exhibited poor efficiency and stability, decomposing under reaction conditions. To overcome these limitations, a diphenyl selenide system was developed, which, in the presence of N-chlorosuccinimide (NCS) and acetonitrile, demonstrated enhanced activity and stability, providing an effective Lewis base-mediated approach for chloroamidation (Scheme 2 P5).45

Subsequent investigations explored thiocarbamate and selenocarbamate catalysts with selenocarbamates showing higher activity but limited stability due to selenium decomposition. Sterically hindered dialkyl selenide catalysts derived from mannitol frameworks were later introduced, offering improved stability and catalytic efficiency. In haloaminocyclization, a cyclic selenium catalyst and a bifunctional amino-thiocarbamate catalyst were applied to 1,1-disubstituted olefinic amides. Both catalysts afforded good yields and enantioselectivities, but the cyclic selenium catalyst outperformed in reaction rate and efficiency, reflecting its stronger Lewis basic activation. Among halogenating reagents, N-bromophthalimide (NBP) delivered the highest enantioselectivity compared to NBS, NCS, or NIS. Mechanistically, the selenium catalyst activates NBP via a Se···Br interaction, generating an electrophilic selenium-bromonium species that undergoes intramolecular cyclization to yield the product (Scheme 2 P6).46

Selenium-based Se···π bonding catalysis has also emerged as a versatile strategy for alkene activation, enabling transformations such as intermolecular enyne cyclizations and selective cross-coupling of alkynes and alkenes. Transitioning from traditional Rh catalysis to Se···π interactions is challenging, particularly in lowering reaction temperatures from 80°C to ambient conditions while maintaining comparable or faster rates. Under Se···π activation, alkene dimerization can occur readily, prompting investigation into whether a competitive π-electron donor, such as an alkyne, can suppress undesired dimerization and selectively promote enyne cyclization (Scheme 2 P7, P8). These studies highlight the potential of Se···π bonding as a flexible platform for π-system activation, addressing long-standing challenges in the functionalization of alkenes, alkynes, and related π-rich substrates.47

Electrophilic halogenation remains a fundamental transformation in organic synthesis due to its broad utility in constructing halogenated molecules. Among halogenating agents, N-haloamides are particularly attractive for their stability and commercial accessibility. Activation of N-haloamides can be efficiently achieved using Lewis basic chalcogen catalysts (Scheme 2 P9), which polarize the amide functionality and facilitate halogen transfer. However, trisubstituted chalcogenium-halogen intermediates are often highly sensitive to moisture and nucleophiles, limiting substrate scope and operational robustness.48 Recent studies have developed efficient catalytic halogenation protocols using phenyl selenide as a Lewis base catalyst. Mechanistic investigations reveal the in situ formation of a neutral, tetracoordinated hypervalent selenium-halogen species responsible for the high reactivity. These intermediate displayed excellent moisture stability and provided a versatile catalytic system, applicable to a broad range of electrophilic functionalization reactions.

Halogenation and cyclization of aryls

Electrophilic aromatic substitution (SeAr) is a cornerstone transformation in synthesis due to the high utility of haloarenes as precursors in cross-coupling and late-stage functionalization. In this direction, Gustafson and co-workers49 developed a second-generation Lewis basic selenoether catalyst that promotes highly ortho-selective chlorination of phenols. This catalyst maintains the selectivity of the earlier system but operates at significantly lower loading, indicating superior catalytic efficiency (Scheme 3 P10).50 Notably, unprotected anilines, which usually undergo para-halogenation due to electronic bias, also furnished high ortho-selectivity under this catalytic system. The enhanced performance arises from integrating a selenoether unit with an additional Lewis basic site, preventing intramolecular deactivation and extending catalytic lifetime. This design delivered a robust selenium-based Lewis base catalyst capable of regulating regioselective aromatic halogenation with selectivity comparable to classical thiourea scaffolds. These findings underline the growing potential of chalcogen-centered Lewis base catalysis as a complementary platform for controlled halogenation and late-stage diversification of bioactive scaffolds. Se-catalyzed cyclization of 2-vinylanilines with isonitriles granted access to 2-aminoquinolines, while an SeO2-mediated intramolecular variant delivered indoles with catalyst recyclability and broad functional group tolerance (Scheme 3 P11).51

Haloarenes play a crucial role in medicinal chemistry, contributing to improved stability and pharmacokinetic profiles. Within Lewis base chalcogen catalysis, electron-rich chalcogen motifs, particularly selenourea derivatives, display strong catalytic activity. In selenourea systems, electron delocalization from nitrogen enhances selenium Lewis basicity through n→σ∗ interaction, generating a resonantly stabilized active form that engages in Se···X (halogen) interactions. Under NBS conditions, the Lewis basic selenourea undergoes initial oxidation to form a stable, non-hygroscopic hypervalent imidazolium selenium dibromide species, which can serve as an active Se-based C–H hydrogen-bond catalyst via non-classical hydrogen bonding. These mechanistic insights provide a solid foundation for advancing electrophilic halogenation strategies using Lewis basic chalcogens. (Scheme 3 P12).52

Further expanding the scope of selenium catalysis, a novel metal-free C(sp2)–H amination protocol has been reported by Ortgies and coworkers via electrophilic activation of simple alkenes. Both ortho-aminostyrenes and vinylated aminopyridines performed efficiently, granting access to a broad library of indole and azaindole derivatives in good to excellent yields (Scheme 3 P13). The transformation tolerates a wide range of functional groups, including halides, ethers, nitriles, and esters, offering a practical and selective approach to densely functionalized indole frameworks. Ongoing efforts aim to extend this strategy toward the synthesis of other heterocyclic scaffolds under chalcogen-mediated electrophilic pathways.53

Miscellaneous type reactions

Beyond amide bond formation, selenium- and sulfur-based catalysts have found wide application in photochemical C–H functionalization reactions. For example, sulfur ylides have been employed as hydrogen atom transfer (HAT) catalysts in photoinduced radical C–H alkylation. The development of enantioselective electrophilic α-selenation has further expanded the scope of organoselenium catalysis, enabling access to chiral selenium-containing amino acid derivatives under mild, metal-free conditions. In 2013, Veselý and co-workers57 demonstrated that Cinchona alkaloid-derived organocatalysts efficiently promoted the α-selenylation of isovaleraldehyde, producing enantioenriched α-selenated amino acid building blocks (Scheme 4 P16).58 Subsequent studies by Waser and co-workers59 validated the use of simple Cinchona alkaloids as bifunctional organobase catalysts for α-selenation using phthalimide-derived selenium transfer reagents. These investigations optimized reaction conditions, evaluated the stability of the α-selenated intermediates, and confirmed their compatibility with downstream transformations, retaining enantiopurity. Collectively, these findings establish a practical, metal-free strategy for synthesizing enantioenriched organoselenium motifs suitable for asymmetric synthesis and peptide functionalization.

Lewis acid-mediated catalysis

General overview of catalytic activity

One of the main topics in organic synthesis is π-bond activation. Conventional techniques employ strong Lewis acids or transition metal π-acid catalysts, such as gold or platinum. Cost, toxicity, sustainability, and compatibility with functional groups make non-metallic substitutes appealing. Selenium has been shown to work as a π-acid catalyst in a variety of oxidation states and electrophilic forms, encouraging the activation of alkenes and alkynes toward nucleophilic addition (also known as oxidative functionalization).60

There remain, however, important challenges regarding selectivity (especially in internal π-bonds), catalyst design for asymmetric induction, and expanding substrate scope toward more complex molecules.61 In addition to occasionally taking part in redox cycles or being eliminated after activation (deselenation), these catalytic systems frequently take advantage of selenium’s capacity to stabilize a partial positive charge on the π-system.

Certain selenium centers, especially in selenium or electrophilic selenide species, can function as Lewis acids in catalytic cycles due to their electron deficiency. Furthermore, due to its polarizability, which enhances its capacity to withstand temporary negative charges, selenium is very efficient in reactions involving charged or polarizable intermediates.

Stoichiometric method

The earliest type of electrophilic selenium chemistry is stoichiometric selenium π-acid catalysis, in which preformed Se(II) or Se(0) reagents, like PhSeCl, PhSeBr, or SeCl4, directly activate π-systems by forming a seleniranium ion intermediate. In the 1991, Tiecco, M. et al. describe the ways Se(II) or Se species directly activate π-systems to form seleniranium ions, which are essential intermediates in selenium π-acid catalysis and result in β-functionalized or halogenated selenide products,62 while catalyst regeneration is not attainable initially indicating that PhSeCl possibly added as an electrophile onto alkenes to produce β-chloro selenides through selenium intermediates used PhSeBr to expand this idea to amino-selenation of alkenes.63

Using SeCl4 as a catalyst (Scheme 5A P17) biomimetic halofunctionalization process for the bromination of aromatic and heteroaromatic chemicals has been carried. Bromination is successfully encouraged by SeCl4, but chlorination was not catalyzed by it.64 Se NMR confirms that SeCl4 hydrolyzes during the process to create H2SeO3. Higher SeCl4 loadings were therefore required to provide satisfactory bromination yields.65

In this context, a new catalytic platform developed by Ke et al., based on 1,2-oxaselenolium salts was introduced, where these cationic selenium species function as strong chalcogen bond donors and alkenes act as chalcogen bond acceptors (Scheme 5B P18).66 This strategy demonstrated that purely noncovalent Se···π activation can successfully polarize alkenes enabling nucleophilic additions under exceptionally mild, metal-free conditions.

Photoinduced multicatalysis

Selenium π-acid catalysis and photoredox catalysis have been recently combined to form photoinduced multicatalytic systems, which combine light-driven redox processes with selenium’s π-activation capability. Milder, more durable conditions, enhanced functional group tolerance, and access to special reactivities including aerobic oxidations, radical-polar crossovers, and selective cyclizations all of which are challenging to accomplish with traditional selenium catalysis are made possible by these dual catalytic techniques.

Study on dual selenium-π-acid/photoredox catalysis for aerobic dehydrogenative lactonization of alkenoic acids is a well-known example. The photoredox catalyst in this system is driven by visible light, the terminal oxidant is molecular oxygen, and the selenium π-acid is diphenyl diselenide (Scheme 7 P21). Mechanistic investigations show that the photoredox cycle and the selenium catalyst work together to transform alkenoic acids into five and six-membered lactones with high yields (up to ∼96%) and in ambient condition of air and light.33 The photo-aerobic Aza-Wacker cyclization of N-alkenyl sulfonamides is another recent study. As represented in Scheme 7 (P22), in this study, diphenyl diselenide with a pyrylium photoredox catalyst under aerobic conditions and visible light to promote selenium π-acid activation and intramolecular cyclization to yield 3-pyrroline products. It has been shown that adding more sulfur co-catalysts increases yields, and the combination of catalysts and reaction conditions (solvent and light) modulates regioselectivity.70

Various chemical syntheses carried out by diphenyl diselenide and photoredox dual catalysis

Using selenium π-acid and photoredox dual catalysis in an O2 atmosphere, as illustrated in Scheme 7 (P23) demonstrate that the reaction produces allylic phosphates in a single step by aerobically phosphating alkenes. The method provides a gentle and long-lasting C–O bond-forming transition through the production of selenium ions, nucleophilic attack by a phosphate, and oxidative elimination.71

A photoredox catalyst excited by visible light initiates single electron transfer (SET) reactions that produce or modify oxidative species, including oxidized selenium intermediates, which activate selenium π-acids. A photoredox-catalyzed 1,2-carbon shift utilizing selenohydrins is shown in Scheme 7 (P24), in which SET is excited by visible light to produce Se(III) species. The way light-driven redox events regulate selenium activation is demonstrated by the cooperation of hydrogen bonding and photoredox processes in modulating selenium electrophilicity.72

A recent 2024 study (Scheme 7 P25) presented a novel catalytic approach by a dual photo-aerobic selenium-π-acid catalytic system to create C–N bonds between alkenes and azole-type nitrogen heterocycles. It offers a straightforward and effective method for the mild synthesis of N-allylated azoles and related compounds.73

In 2023, a ternary selenium-sulfur multicatalytic system that permits Aza-Wacker cyclization and asymmetric photoaerobic lactonization of unactivated alkenes in the presence of air and visible light (Scheme 8A P26) was reported. While the sulfur cocatalyst improves reactivity and enantioselectivity by promoting electron transfer and speeding up β-elimination, the chiral selenium catalyst activates the alkene through the production of selenium ions. Without the need for guiding groups on the substrate, this cooperative system produces excellent enantiocontrol (avg. er 92:8) and high yields. The method’s synthetic value was demonstrated by using it to a brief synthesis of a 3,4-dihydroxyproline derivative.38

Electrochemically enabled selenium catalytic synthesis

Through the anodic oxidation of diselenides or selenides, electrochemical selenium catalysis produces active RSe+ species, providing an environmentally friendly and oxidant-free method. Effective C–Se and C–C bond forms with π-nucleophiles are made possible by these electrophilic selenium intermediates, which makes the process sustainable and adaptable in contemporary catalysis. Simalrily in Scheme 9 (P28) used (PhSe)2 as a catalyst in an undivided cell (Pt anode/C cathode, CH3CN, Et4NPF6) to create 2,1-benzoxazoles by electrochemically cyclizing o-nitrophenylacetylenes with selenium. This benign and useful route to benzoxazoles is made possible by the method’s broad functional group tolerance, scalability to gram scale, and lack of exogenous oxidants.75

Conclusion and perspectives

In conclusion, this perspective provides important insights into chalcogen bonding catalyst which shows wide potential in the catalytic transformation of various substrates. Notably, Se···π bonding catalysis is dependent on the significant weak interactions. These Se···π bonding catalysis approach provides a new avenue to solve synthetic problems particularly with regards to alkenes, alkynes and other π-systems. Recent advancements in chalcogen-bonding catalysis have demonstrated its effectiveness in diverse reactions such as transfer hydrogenation, bromination, bromo-lactonization, dimerization of 1,1-diphenylethylene, and nitro-Michael addition. Although reported methods allow for regio- and stereoselective functionalization, the requirement for stoichiometric or excess amounts of selenium reagents leads to poor atom economy. These drawbacks have driven the development of more sustainable strategies, including redox-catalytic, electrochemical, and photo-induced selenium π-acid catalysis, which aim to minimize reagent consumption while retaining high selectivity. Chalcogen bonding has evolved from a structural curiosity to a purposeful tool in catalysis. Early observations of diphenyl diselenide interacting with iodine revealed selenium’s amphiphilic character. Selenium catalysis has the potential to establish itself as a distinct activation mode rather than just an analog of sulfur or halogen catalysis.
