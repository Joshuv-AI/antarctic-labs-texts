Second-knee transfusion burden persists under a high-dose intravenous iron protocol without tranexamic acid in one-week staged bilateral total knee arthroplasty: a paired cohort study
Authors: Jaemin Lee, Jaeho Lee, Doo Sup Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe 1-week staged interval for bilateral total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is favored for cost-efficiency, but the second knee operates on an incompletely recovered hematologic reserve. Whether high-dose perioperative intravenous iron without tranexamic acid (TXA) eliminates the inter-knee transfusion gap is unknown.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 210 consecutive patients undergoing 1-week staged bilateral primary TKA under a high-dose ferric carboxymaltose protocol (1,500 mg cumulative) without TXA; the transfusion trigger was hemoglobin ≤ 8.0 g/dL or symptomatic anemia. Three hypotheses were pre-specified: that the within-patient second-knee transfusion rate exceeds the first-knee rate; that the lowest tertile of inter-knee hemoglobin recovery has a higher second-knee rate; and that the immediate postoperative hemoglobin after the second operation discriminates second-knee transfusion.ResultsThe second-knee transfusion rate was nearly twice the first-knee rate within the same patient (59.5% vs. 31.0%); inter-knee hemoglobin recovery did not predict second-knee transfusion. The immediate postoperative hemoglobin after the second operation discriminated transfusion (area under the curve 0.718), comparably to the preoperative value. The exploratory Youden-optimal cutoff of 8.8 g/dL had a prevalence-dependent positive predictive value and a sensitivity of only 0.62, missing 38% of transfused patients. Preoperative hemoglobin and body mass index were independently associated with second-knee transfusion, and no surgical site infection or thromboembolic complication occurred within 30 days.ConclusionsHigh-dose intravenous iron did not eliminate the second-knee transfusion burden, and inter-knee hemoglobin recovery did not reliably identify high-risk patients. Preoperative hemoglobin was at least as discriminating and is the more practical screen; the 8.8 g/dL cutoff is exploratory — not a decision threshold for use outside this cohort — and requires external validation. Because no TXA was used, the absolute transfusion rates exceed contemporary TXA-based practice and are not directly generalizable; adding TXA is the priority next step.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12891-026-10222-3.

Background

Bilateral knee osteoarthritis is a common indication for total knee arthroplasty (TKA), and the choice between staged and simultaneous procedures carries measurable mortality and hematologic consequences. A 2025 meta-analysis of 567,915 cases reported lower mortality with staged than simultaneous TKA [1], and a network meta-analysis identified inter-stage intervals of six weeks or longer as safest for mortality [2]. The hematologic cost nonetheless remains substantial, with blood loss, transfusion, and anemia among the most frequently reported complications [3]; historically, staged patients waited three or more months between operations, allowing hemoglobin to normalize before the second knee [4], and a large single-surgeon Korean series found the staged approach retained the lowest transfusion rate with equivalent long-term outcomes [5]. The 1-week interval, favored for cost-efficiency and patient convenience, is therefore the most hematologically demanding, as the second knee operates on a barely recovered reserve. High-dose intravenous (IV) iron (≥ 500 mg ferric carboxymaltose [FCM]) reduces perioperative transfusion in elective arthroplasty [6], and tranexamic acid (TXA) with restrictive triggers has driven transfusion close to zero in selected bilateral cohorts [7].

Three gaps limit the evidence for the 1-week interval. First, dose–response data for IV iron are heterogeneous across iron sucrose, iron isomaltoside, and FCM regimens, with inconsistent transfusion benefit [8–13], and no cohort has reported a paired, within-patient comparison under a high-dose protocol (≥ 1,500 mg cumulative FCM) without TXA, leaving the residual second-knee burden unquantified. Second, recently proposed perioperative-day-zero hemoglobin cutoffs — for example 9.0 g/dL in a predominantly unilateral cohort [14] — have not been validated at the second-knee timepoint, where the value is drawn after the first-knee insult and may need to be lower, and a meta-analysis of 36 arthroplasty transfusion-prediction models identified no bilateral-specific threshold [15]. Third, no paired cohort has tested whether slow inter-knee hemoglobin recovery marks elevated second-knee risk, a phenotype that, were it real, would offer a simple screening tool.

We therefore studied a consecutive single-center cohort of 1-week staged bilateral primary TKAs managed with a high-dose perioperative FCM protocol (1,500 mg cumulative) and no TXA, testing three pre-specified hypotheses: that the second-knee transfusion rate exceeds the first-knee rate within the same patient (H1); that patients in the lowest tertile of inter-knee hemoglobin recovery rate have a higher second-knee transfusion rate (H2); and that the immediate postoperative hemoglobin on the day of the second operation — postoperative day (POD) 0 relative to the second operation (Op2 POD0; the first operation is Op1) — discriminates second-knee transfusion, yielding a candidate cutoff identifiable by Youden’s J statistic (H3).

Methods

Reporting follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guideline for cohort studies.

Study design and setting

This single-center retrospective cohort study included consecutive patients who underwent 1-week staged bilateral primary total knee arthroplasty (TKA) between January 2019 and December 2024. All were treated under a uniform protocol that combined a high-dose perioperative intravenous (IV) iron regimen — ferric carboxymaltose (FCM) — with a hemoglobin-based transfusion trigger and no tranexamic acid (TXA). TXA had not been adopted institutionally over concern about thromboembolic risk in a predominantly older female population. Hematologic outcomes were ascertained from admission for the first operation (Op1) through discharge, and the four prefixed complications through 30 days after the second operation (Op2). The Institutional Review Board of Wonju Severance Christian Hospital approved the protocol (CR326057; 19 May 2026) and waived informed consent for this retrospective analysis of de-identified data, conducted per the Declaration of Helsinki. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Participants

Eligible patients were adults undergoing staged bilateral primary TKA for end-stage osteoarthritis with a 7-to-9-day inter-surgical interval and complete perioperative hemoglobin (Hb) data at the 10 protocol-defined timepoints. We excluded a priori the six 14-day-interval cases (whose longer recovery window would introduce heterogeneity into the paired analysis), revision or unicompartmental procedures, non-osteoarthritic indications, and any documented inherited bleeding disorder or active malignancy. Of 216 screened cases, 210 formed the main analytic cohort; a 7-day-interval sensitivity cohort (n = 199) was pre-specified (Fig. 1).

Surgical technique and perioperative management

A single senior surgeon performed every operation through a midvastus approach. Limb alignment followed a conventional mechanical strategy; individualized morphotype-based planning — for example, by Coronal Plane Alignment of the Knee (CPAK) morphotype — was not used [16]. Bone resections were guided by the implant system’s conventional manual instrumentation (Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN, USA) — an intramedullary femoral and an extramedullary tibial alignment guide — without computer navigation or robotic assistance. Every knee received a cemented posterior-stabilized Persona total knee system (Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN, USA), fixed with polymethylmethacrylate bone cement; patellar resurfacing was performed selectively for advanced patellofemoral degeneration rather than routinely. A non-pneumatic elastic ring tourniquet was applied to every knee, and mean tourniquet time did not differ between the first and second knee (Table 1). Because the protocol contained no antifibrinolytic agent, a closed-suction drain was placed routinely — the institutional standard during the study period — to evacuate the postoperative hematoma and allow blood-loss monitoring in the absence of TXA. Apart from a single mid-cohort change in the drain-retention policy (3-day to 2-day; addressed in the sensitivity analysis), no component of the surgical or blood-management protocol changed during 2019–2024.

Intervention and hemoglobin assessment

FCM 500 mg was infused approximately 7 days before Op1 and 1,000 mg on the day of Op1, for a cumulative 1,500 mg per patient — three times the 500 mg threshold defined as high-dose by Park et al. [6]. A Level I systematic review found that perioperative IV, but not oral, iron accelerates hemoglobin recovery [17], so we term the regimen a high-dose perioperative IV iron protocol. No patient received IV, intra-articular, or topical TXA. Allogeneic leukoreduced packed red blood cell (PRBC) transfusion followed a restrictive trigger — Hb ≤ 8.0 g/dL on any postoperative measurement, or symptomatic anemia (dizziness, dyspnea on minimal exertion, severe fatigue, or pallor) — applied identically to the first- and second-knee periods. Venous Hb was measured at 10 pre-specified timepoints: preoperative (1 day before Op1); Op1 POD0, POD1, POD2, and POD5; Op2 POD0; Op2 POD1, POD2, and POD5; and discharge (POD14 from Op1). Op2 POD0 was the immediate postoperative measurement on the second-operation day — routinely an evening draw after surgery (7 days after Op1 in 199 patients, 8 days in 9, and 9 days in 2). No routine pre-incision hemoglobin was obtained that day, so Op2 POD0 served as the candidate postoperative marker for H3.

Outcomes and variables

The pre-specified primary outcome was paired transfusion status — after the first knee (Op1 through Op1 POD5) versus after the second knee (Op2 POD0 through discharge) — analyzed within patient. Pre-specified secondary outcomes were PRBC units per knee, the Hb trajectory across the 10 timepoints, multivariable predictors of second-knee transfusion, the discrimination of Op2 POD0 Hb by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, and complications. The four prefixed complications (hematoma, surgical site infection, deep vein thrombosis, and pulmonary embolism) were ascertained from inpatient and outpatient records at POD7, POD14, and 30 days after Op2 using pre-specified case definitions, with thrombotic events requiring imaging confirmation. Reoperation, by contrast, was captured over the full institutional follow-up so that late events such as periprosthetic joint infection could be detected. A single trained reviewer extracted demographic, surgical, hematologic (the 10 Hb values), transfusion, and outcome variables on a standardized case-report form. Covariates included body mass index (BMI), American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status, comorbidities, antiplatelet or anticoagulant use, operative and tourniquet time, and drain output. Preoperative anemia followed World Health Organization sex-specific criteria (Hb < 12 g/dL for women, < 13 g/dL for men). No patient was lost to follow-up through discharge or the 30-day window.

Bias

Selection bias was minimized by enrolling consecutive eligible cases under a priori criteria. Transfusions and Hb were ascertained from objective sources — the institutional blood-bank ledger and the central laboratory — rather than chart impressions. All hypotheses and analytic procedures were recorded in the analysis plan before any inferential analysis, and the principal analyst was not involved in surgical or transfusion decisions.

Study size

We performed no formal a priori sample-size calculation and included all consecutive eligible cases over the 6-year period. Rather than a post-hoc power computed from the realized effect, which is circular and uninformative, we characterized precision by the minimum detectable effect size. At 80% power (α = 0.05, two-sided), the H2 lowest-versus-highest recovery-tertile contrast could detect a between-tertile difference of approximately 21% points (odds ratio ≈ 3.0). The McNemar test retained at least 80% power for the pre-specified inter-knee difference of ≥ 15% points (Supplementary Methods). The precision of the covariate-adjusted H2 estimate, not a design-based power figure, should guide interpretation of the null result.

Statistical analysis

Three hypotheses were pre-specified before any inferential statistic. H1: the second-knee transfusion rate exceeds the first-knee rate within patient. H2: patients in the lowest tertile of inter-knee Hb recovery rate have a higher second-knee transfusion rate, where the recovery rate is [Op2 POD0 Hb − Op1 POD5 Hb] / N days and N is 2, 3, or 4 for a 7-, 8-, or 9-day interval. H3: Op2 POD0 Hb discriminates second-knee transfusion at a cutoff identifiable by Youden’s J statistic, expected a priori at 10.5–11 g/dL but allowed to fall outside this range. Because Op2 POD0 is immediately postoperative, the H2 recovery rate indexes net inter-stage hemoglobin change rather than pure erythropoietic recovery. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median [interquartile range] and categorical variables as count (percentage), with Wilson 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for proportions. H1 used McNemar’s test with Yates continuity correction (paired binary outcome), the Wilcoxon signed-rank test (units per knee), and the Spearman coefficient (within-patient unit correlation); the rate difference and its 95% CI came from the discordant-pair counts (Supplementary Methods). H2 used univariable then multivariable logistic regression, adjusted a priori for preoperative Hb, BMI, age, sex, ASA status, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, and chronic kidney disease. With 125 transfused and 85 non-transfused patients across ten covariates, the events-per-variable ratio was adequate by the standard count (12.5) but not by the conservative minority-class count (8.5); all adjusted estimates, especially for low-prevalence comorbidities, are therefore provisional. H3 quantified discrimination by the area under the ROC curve (AUC) with DeLong 95% CIs and selected the optimal cutoff by Youden’s J statistic, recently used to derive preoperative Hb thresholds in revision TKA [18]. Additional cutoffs were evaluated at 0.5 g/dL intervals from 8.5 to 10.5 g/dL. A multivariable model then added preoperative Hb, age, and sex to Op2 POD0 Hb — preoperative Hb being the highest-importance transfusion predictor in machine-learning models of primary TKA [19] — and the three AUCs were compared pairwise by the DeLong test. Because this nested model was evaluated in-sample, its DeLong p-values are anti-conservative and the incremental discrimination requires external validation; we therefore report bootstrap optimism-corrected AUCs, repeated five-fold cross-validated AUCs, and bootstrap stability of the Youden-optimal cutoff (Supplementary Methods). Pre-specified secondary analyses (Supplementary Methods) comprised a linear mixed-effects Hb-trajectory model, a 7-day-interval sensitivity analysis for H1, a drain-subgroup analysis with a drain × first-knee-transfusion interaction, and a five-test complications panel by Fisher’s exact test with Holm–Bonferroni correction. Post hoc robustness analyses (same file) comprised objective Hb ≤ 8.0 g/dL and stricter ≤ 7.0 g/dL trigger proxies, a slow-responder analysis restricted to patients without first-knee transfusion, a test of whether the absolute Op2 POD0 hemoglobin suppresses the recovery-rate association, calendar-period halves, likelihood ratios at the exploratory 8.8 g/dL cutoff, and H3 collinearity and calibration. A preoperative-anemia × first-knee-transfusion interaction was added during peer review. All tests were two-sided at α = 0.05; H1 was the primary confirmatory hypothesis and H2 and H3 were pre-specified secondary analyses interpreted descriptively, so no multiplicity correction was applied across the three hypotheses, while the five complication tests were Holm–Bonferroni-corrected. The complete-Hb inclusion criterion yielded zero Hb missingness, so no imputation was required. Analyses used Python 3.12 (Python Software Foundation, Beaverton, OR, USA), with the pandas (2.x), statsmodels (0.14), and scikit-learn (1.x) libraries.

Primary outcome: paired transfusion burden (H1)

The first-knee transfusion rate was 31.0% (65/210) and the second-knee rate 59.5% (125/210), a difference of + 28.6% points (95% CI 20.5–36.6; McNemar p < 0.001). Of 92 discordant pairs, 76 were transfused only after the second knee, 16 only after the first. Mean PRBC units per knee were 0.50 (first) and 0.98 (second) (Wilcoxon p < 0.001), with correlated within-patient counts (Table 2).

Secondary outcome: slow-responder hypothesis (H2)

Second-knee transfusion rates were 57.5%, 59.4%, and 61.8% across slow, middle, and fast tertiles (Table 3A), and the adjusted odds ratio (OR) for the slow-responder indicator was 0.81 (95% CI 0.42–1.55, p = 0.52; Table 4B). The recovery rate, derived from the same Op2 POD0 hemoglobin used in H3, correlated positively with that value (r = 0.49); the slow tertile started highest at Op1 POD5 (9.80 g/dL) yet ended lowest at Op2 POD0 (8.44 g/dL). The crude recovery-rate effect was null (OR 0.94 per + 1 g/dL/day, p = 0.85). Conditioning on the shared Op2 POD0 term produced a suppression-like sign change (adjusted OR 3.10, 95% CI 1.42–6.77, p = 0.005), while the absolute level dominated (OR 0.34 per + 1 g/dL, p < 0.001). This conditional estimate is thus a mathematical consequence of the shared term, not an independent recovery-rate effect: the static hemoglobin level, not its rate of change, is the operative quantity. Preoperative hemoglobin (adjusted OR 0.53 per + 1 g/dL, p < 0.001) and body mass index (adjusted OR 0.89 per + 1 kg/m², p = 0.012) were independently associated with second-knee transfusion.

Secondary outcome: Op2 POD0 hemoglobin cutoff (H3)

Op2 POD0 hemoglobin discriminated second-knee transfusion with an AUC of 0.718 (95% CI 0.649–0.787; Fig. 3), comparable to the preoperative value (DeLong p = 0.95); adding preoperative hemoglobin, age, and sex reached an apparent in-sample AUC of 0.774 (Table 5A), a model in which both hemoglobin values remained independently associated with second-knee transfusion after adjustment for age and sex (Table 6B). The exploratory Youden-optimal cutoff was 8.8 g/dL (sensitivity 0.62, specificity 0.74; Table 7C), at which 100 of 210 patients were classified high-risk and 78 (78%) received second-knee transfusion. Internal validation indicated limited optimism: the optimism-corrected and repeated cross-validated multivariable AUCs (0.759 and 0.755) were essentially unchanged, the single-marker AUCs similarly stable, and the Youden-optimal cutoff stable on bootstrap resampling (Table 5).

Sensitivity and subgroup analyses

The second-knee excess persisted across every subgroup (all McNemar p < 0.05; Table S2): the pre-specified 7-day sensitivity cohort (n = 199), both drain subgroups, and the preoperative-anemia and non-anemic strata. In a post hoc objective-trigger analysis, restricting transfusion to an objective hemoglobin ≤ 8.0 g/dL proxy preserved the asymmetry (14.3% vs. 49.0%; 83 versus 10 discordant pairs; McNemar p < 0.001), so the discretionary symptomatic-anemia trigger did not generate the excess. A stricter ≤ 7.0 g/dL proxy lowered the modeled rates (first knee 1.4%, second knee 4.8%); the institutional ≤ 8.0 g/dL trigger, more liberal than the 7.0 g/dL in some comparators, drives the high absolute rates. Under this strict proxy the inter-knee gradient remained directional, though no longer statistically significant at these low counts. Among the 125 s-knee transfusions, the lowest recorded hemoglobin was below 7.0 g/dL in 6.4%, between 7.0 and 8.0 g/dL in 48.8%, and strictly above 8.0 g/dL in 44.8%. Because the per-order indication was not coded, these are lowest-recorded-hemoglobin proxy bands; only the strictly-above-8.0 g/dL group lies outside the objective trigger zone, reflecting symptomatic anemia or clinical judgment. The excess also held in both calendar-period halves despite an overall transfusion decline.

Complications

No hematoma, surgical site infection, deep vein thrombosis, or pulmonary embolism occurred within the 30-day surveillance window (each 0/210; 95% upper confidence bound approximately 1.8%). Four patients (1.9%) underwent reoperation over the available follow-up: two for arthrofibrosis requiring manipulation, one for periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) within 90 days, and one for aseptic loosening later. None was related to bleeding or hematoma; the manipulations reflected insufficient rehabilitation, and the infection-related reoperation was a deep PJI after the 30-day surgical-site-infection window. After Holm–Bonferroni correction, no event differed between patients who did versus did not receive second-knee transfusion (all adjusted p = 1.00; Table S3).

Discussion

Principal findings

In 210 patients undergoing 1-week staged bilateral primary TKA under a high-dose ferric carboxymaltose protocol (1,500 mg cumulative) without tranexamic acid, three findings emerged. First, the second-knee transfusion rate was nearly twice the first-knee rate within the same patient (59.5% vs. 31.0%; McNemar p < 0.001; a 4.75-fold discordant-pair asymmetry). Second, slow inter-knee hemoglobin recovery did not identify high-risk patients (adjusted OR 0.81, 95% CI 0.42–1.55). Third, the immediate postoperative hemoglobin after the second operation (Op2 POD0) discriminated transfusion (AUC 0.718) with an exploratory Youden-optimal cutoff of 8.8 g/dL, well below the a priori 10.5–11 g/dL. The excess tracked cumulative reserve depletion rather than a larger second insult. Hemoglobin fell 3.63 g/dL over the first-knee period but only a further 0.35 g/dL from the immediate postoperative value to the nadir after the second knee. Thus a reserve already barely above the 8.0 g/dL trigger crossed it far more often despite the smaller additional loss. This decomposition is observational and lacks direct per-knee blood-loss measurement.

Comparison with the literature

Our magnitude aligns with the limited within-patient literature: 85.4% of transfusions followed the second knee in a 72-hour staggered trial [20], 1-week versus delayed staging carried four-fold higher transfusion odds even with routine TXA [21], and a sub-one-year interval raised second-stage transfusion from 1.5% to 6.9% in staged hip and knee arthroplasty [22]. Iron-treated Korean cohorts reported rates below our 59.5%: Park HS et al. [10] reported 21.8% overall with postoperative ferric carboxymaltose 1 g and, unlike our marked asymmetry, no second-knee excess (13.0% vs. 15.6% after the second versus first knee), and Lee SS et al. [9] a combined two-knee rate of 58.6% with iron isomaltoside. Both gave intraoperative TXA to 70–93% of patients whereas ours received none, so the across-cohort gap cannot be attributed to iron dose alone and is consistent with a TXA contribution, though the cohorts also differed in iron agent, trigger, and case-mix. Our 8.8 g/dL cutoff lay close to the 9.0 g/dL perioperative-day-zero cutoff of Haider et al. [14] but served a different purpose — rule-in in a high-prevalence staged-bilateral setting rather than rule-out in a low-prevalence unilateral one. Bundles combining TXA, restrictive triggers, and no drain reported near-zero transfusion in selected bilateral patients [7], and a simultaneous cohort adding intra-articular TXA to iron with a hemoglobin < 7 g/dL trigger reported 1.3% [23].

Clinical implications

High-dose IV iron alone did not eliminate the residual second-knee burden, so an additional intervention is needed. The cross-cohort pattern — near-zero transfusion when TXA is added to modest IV iron versus 59.5% under 1,500 mg cumulative ferric carboxymaltose without TXA — is compatible with a limited marginal effect of escalating IV iron beyond roughly 1 g and a larger potential TXA contribution where bilateral blood loss is doubled. Institutional adoption lowered transfusion from 18.2% to 2.3% in the most directly comparable 1-week staged cohort [21], and a staggered cohort adding intraoperative TXA to postoperative IV iron recorded a 13.0% second-knee rate [10]. Because these comparators differ from ours in procedure, agent, trigger, and case-mix, they point to a substantial but unquantifiable potential reduction that only a randomized TXA-addition trial can establish. Our center had withheld TXA out of historical concern about thromboembolic risk in an older, predominantly female population; because contemporary TXA-using cohorts report low thromboembolic event rates without a clear safety signal [7, 23, 24], these findings represent a conservative TXA-free benchmark, not an endorsement of omitting TXA. The ceiling is not zero, however: double-dose intravenous TXA with restrictive triggers still required transfusion in approximately 22% of simultaneous bilateral cases [24], with no incremental benefit from a second dose over one [25]. Where TXA-plus-iron bundles fall short, preoperative erythropoiesis stimulation [26, 27] and patient-blood-management bundles built on preoperative hemoglobin optimization [28] offer complementary gains.

For individual risk assessment, a single static hemoglobin value — not its inter-knee rate of change — is the operative quantity. Preoperative hemoglobin is the more practical screen, discriminating second-knee transfusion as well as the Op2 POD0 value (DeLong p = 0.95) yet available days earlier. Prior work corroborates this primacy: preoperative hemoglobin was the leading independent transfusion predictor [19, 29], and untreated preoperative anemia carries elevated transfusion, infection, and mortality risks [30]. Pre-incision optimization for the second knee — additional iron, erythropoietin, or TXA — must therefore be guided by the preoperative hemoglobin, because the Op2 POD0 value is measured only after the second operation; its role is limited to flagging patients for closer postoperative hemoglobin surveillance. The Op2 POD0 ≤ 8.8 g/dL cutoff is exploratory: it rules transfusion in rather than out (positive predictive value 78%, 95% CI 69–85%; negative predictive value 57%), and its sensitivity was only moderate (0.62, missing 38% of eventually transfused patients). It should not be applied as a clinical decision threshold outside this specific cohort pending external validation.

Methodological considerations

Several data features constrain interpretation. First, the inter-knee recovery rate is structurally confounded. A faster apparent recovery may reflect a steeper post-Op1 nadir rather than superior hematopoiesis; first-knee transfusion before the second operation alters its input values; and the rate is not independent of the Op2 POD0 hemoglobin from which it derives. Conditioning on that shared term reversed the sign of the effect — a mathematical consequence rather than evidence of an independent recovery-rate effect (Results; Supplementary Methods). The wrong-direction null persisted among the 145 patients without first-knee transfusion, whose inter-knee values are uncontaminated by intervening transfusion (second-knee transfusion 45.3% in the slow tertile versus 57.8% and 55.3% in the middle and fast tertiles; adjusted OR 0.70, 95% CI 0.32–1.52). Faster apparent recovery may also reflect the iron-sequestration phenotype seen in roughly one-fifth of anemic arthroplasty candidates [31], untestable here without iron-status biomarkers. Second, the cross-cohort iron-versus-TXA comparison is confounded: the cohorts differed in procedure, iron agent, transfusion trigger, drain practice, and case-mix, so these contrasts are hypothesis-generating rather than causal. Third, the 8.8 g/dL cutoff was derived in-sample, so its positive predictive value is inflated by the 59.5% transfusion prevalence and would fall in a lower-prevalence setting; the prevalence-independent likelihood ratios were modest (positive 2.4, negative 0.51). Adding preoperative hemoglobin, age, and sex modestly raised discrimination, a gain that largely survived bootstrap optimism correction and cross-validation but still requires external confirmation.

Limitations

This study has several design and methodological limitations. It is retrospective and single-center, with all operations by one surgeon in a predominantly female, older adult Korean population, so generalizability is not assured, although baseline characteristics resembled comparable Korean cohorts [9, 10]. The 1-week interval falls within the higher-risk window the Chang network meta-analysis favored against for mortality [2]. The transfusion trigger (hemoglobin ≤ 8.0 g/dL or symptomatic anemia) is more liberal than the 7.0 g/dL threshold of some comparators [23], inflating absolute transfusion rates and complicating cross-study comparison; sensitivity analyses quantifying this effect are reported in the Results (Table S2). Iron-status biomarkers were unavailable, limiting mechanistic inference about the H2 null, and the H2 model operated at a conservative 8.5 events per variable, so confidence intervals for low-prevalence comorbidities are wide. The drain-retention policy changed mid-cohort; its effect on the inter-knee asymmetry is addressed in the Results (Table S2). With no internal no-iron, lower-dose, or TXA-treated comparator, the within-patient design quantifies the residual second-stage burden but cannot attribute the absolute rate to the iron dose versus the absence of TXA; accordingly, these data do not endorse omitting TXA, which remains standard care. Because the transfusion trigger is hemoglobin-based, the association between a low Op2 POD0 hemoglobin and subsequent transfusion is partly mechanical. The paired comparison is robust to pre-incision bias: all second-knee transfusions occurred after the second operation, and because only patients who completed both stages were analyzed, any deferral of sicker patients would render the estimate conservative. Finally, prefixed complications were ascertained only through 30 days; reoperations were captured over longer follow-up, but late complications were not systematically surveilled, so dedicated long-term follow-up remains necessary. The study is correspondingly underpowered to exclude clinically important thromboembolic or infectious complications, with event rates and confidence bounds reported in the Results (Table S3).

Future directions

Two prospective steps are warranted. First, a randomized comparison should add TXA — intravenous or topical, for the second knee or for both — to the high-dose IV iron protocol, testing whether its marginal benefit matches the across-cohort contrast and whether a second inter-stage IV iron dose accelerates recovery without affecting the acute nadir [32]. Second, the 8.8 g/dL Op2 POD0 cutoff should undergo prospective external validation in an independent staged bilateral cohort, ideally with iron-status biomarkers. Studies of longer (3- to 6-week) intervals would further clarify whether the residual burden narrows when the second knee operates on a more fully recovered reserve.

Conclusions

In 1-week staged bilateral primary TKA managed with a high-dose perioperative ferric carboxymaltose protocol and no tranexamic acid, high-dose IV iron alone did not eliminate the residual hematologic burden of the second knee, whose transfusion rate was nearly twice the first-knee rate within the same patient. Inter-knee hemoglobin recovery rate showed no association with second-knee transfusion in the hypothesized direction (adjusted OR 0.81, 95% CI 0.42–1.55) and does not support recovery-rate monitoring as a screening tool, although the confidence interval cannot exclude a moderate effect. Preoperative hemoglobin discriminated as well as the second-operation value (DeLong p = 0.95) and is available days earlier, making it the more practical screen. The immediate postoperative second-operation hemoglobin ≤ 8.8 g/dL cutoff (positive predictive value 78%, prevalence-dependent; sensitivity 0.62, missing 38% of transfused patients) is exploratory — hypothesis-generating, not a decision threshold for use outside this cohort — and requires external validation. Because no tranexamic acid was used, the absolute transfusion rates exceed contemporary TXA-based practice and are not directly generalizable; prospectively adding tranexamic acid to the high-dose IV iron protocol is the priority next step.
