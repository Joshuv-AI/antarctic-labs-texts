Quantification of the Spatial Variation and Source Contributions to Ambient Particle-Bound PAHs Using Aerosol Mass Spectrometry
Authors: Oladayo Oladeji, Albert A Presto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons (PAHs), a subset of air toxics, pose severe health risks even at low concentrations due to their carcinogenic and reproductive effects. Despite their significance, spatial distribution studies of PAHs remain limited, largely due to the absence of real-time, chemically specific methods. This study applies high-resolution aerosol mass spectrometry (AMS) for quantification and spatial analysis of particle-phase PAHs. Laboratory experiments were conducted with four PAH standards (anthracene, pyrene, 9-methyl anthracene, and acenaphthylene) to determine their mass spectra and ionization efficiency. AMS mass spectra of the PAH standards were highly correlated with NIST reference spectra (R2 > 0.95), though the aerosol mass spectra exhibited greater fragmentation than the reference spectra. Subsequently, field sampling was carried out in the city of Pittsburgh. A mobile laboratory was driven over defined routes in areas of varying source activities (e.g., the downtown core, residential areas, and industrial areas). These measurements revealed significant spatial variability. The median neighborhood-scale total particle-phase PAH concentrations ranged from 70 ng/m3 to 150 ng/m3 indicating a 2.1-fold difference, with the downtown neighborhood exhibiting the highest intra-neighborhood variability (Q3/Q1 = 3.4). Compared to Black Carbon (BC), particle-phase PAHs showed greater spatial variability. Most of the detected particle-phase PAHs had low background concentrations and the time series of concentration was punctuated by high concentration spikes. These spikes occurred most frequently in high-traffic zones such as Downtown and on major highways, suggesting contributions from vehicle traffic. Additional spikes in industrial areas suggest contributions from industrial sources. Ratio-ratio plots (e.g., PAH/BC) and comparison of particle-phase PAH concentrations with the concentrations of hydrocarbon-like organic aerosol (HOA), an indicator of vehicle emissions, further indicate the importance of vehicle emissions on ambient particle-phase PAH concentrations. However, vehicle emissions cannot fully explain particle-phase PAH concentrations in the mobile sampling data. Introduction

Air Toxics, also known as Hazardous Air Pollutants (HAPs), are a category of pollutants recognized for causing serious and often irreversible health effects. These effects can include cancer, birth defects, reproductive harm, and other significant adverse impacts on environmental and ecological systems (US Environmental Protection Agency 2020). Among the various HAPs are Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons (PAHs) (US Environmental Protection Agency 2024). PAHs are a group of semi-volatile organic compounds generated from the incomplete combustion of organic compounds (Wang et al. 2014).

The sources of PAHs are diverse. Natural sources include phenomena such as volcanic eruptions and wildfires, which release PAHs into the atmosphere as a byproduct of natural combustion processes. However, the majority of PAH emissions stem from anthropogenic sources. These include industrial activities like asphalt production, which involve high-temperature processes that generate PAHs as a byproduct (Song et al. 2020). Mobile sources, such as exhaust emissions from vehicles, are also significant contributors, as are agricultural practices like biomass burning, which usually occur during land clearing or waste disposal processes. This is also an important global source of PAHs (Patel et al. 2020), but not a major contributor in Pittsburgh, where this study was conducted (Cabada et al. 2004).

Despite the recognized dangers posed by PAHs and other HAPs, there is a notable lack of comprehensive data on the spatial distribution of these pollutants, particularly in urban environments. While some monitoring efforts exist (Noth et al. 2016), such as those conducted by the National Air Toxics Trends Station (NATTS) network in the United States, they tend to be limited in scope. The NATTS network, established to support the long-term surveillance of HAPs, operates only 27 monitoring sites nationwide across the United States (US Environmental Protection Agency 2019). This limited coverage means that many regions, particularly within urban areas, lack detailed data on the presence and concentration of PAHs and other air toxics. Furthermore, minimal research has been conducted to measure spatial variations in air toxic concentrations within cities, leaving significant gaps in our understanding of how these pollutants vary across different urban landscapes (Noth et al. 2016).

The spatial resolution of air toxic measurements is important for understanding PAH exposure patterns in urban environments. High spatial resolution measurements can reveal localized pollution hotspots, which are often masked in broader, less spatially detailed monitoring efforts (Shah et al. 2018). Previous research on pollutants like fine particulate matter (PM2.5) has demonstrated that certain neighborhoods in urban areas can experience disproportionately higher pollutant levels due to sources like transportation hubs and industrial zones, with subsequent impacts on population exposures (Apte et al. 2017; Li et al. 2016). Studies with spatially resolved data have shown that populations residing near these hot spots are more vulnerable to pollutant exposure, often coinciding with socioeconomically disadvantaged groups (Hajat et al. 2015; Shah et al. 2020). Therefore, gathering high-resolution data on PAHs could reveal spatial disparities in urban pollutant distributions, providing insights for public health interventions and urban planning aimed at reducing pollution exposure in high-risk areas.

Mobile sampling using chemically specific instrumentation, including mass spectrometers (Shah et al. 2018; Oladeji et al. 2023), can yield insights into specific pollutants and their sources. A few studies have used aerosol mass spectrometry (AMS) for particle-phase PAH identification. For example, Dzepina et al. (2007) measured particle-phase PAHs in Mexico City using an AMS for the first time during stationary sampling. The AMS particle-phase PAH measurements were compared with Gas Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (GC–MS) analysis of filter samples, in which several groups of PAHs showed agreement with both instruments. Lin et al. (2021) used a Time-of-Flight (ToF) AMS to evaluate the impact of cooking emissions on particle-phase PAHs in China. Stationary sampling was conducted on the rooftop of a building in which cooking emissions were found to be the major source of ambient particle-phase PAHs. Herring et al. (2015) identified and quantified 53 particle-phase PAHs with a time-of-flight AMS. Our study builds upon these prior investigations and couples AMS measurements with mobile sampling to gain new insight on particle-phase PAH spatial distributions in a city.

Quantifying the spatial distribution of PAHs and identifying their primary sources support targeted efforts to reduce exposure and associated health impacts (Wang et al. 2022). This research aims to contribute to this body of knowledge by applying advanced analytical techniques to quantify and map the spatial distribution of particle-phase PAHs in urban environments.

Methods

Aerosol Mass Spectrometer

Central to this study was the deployment of the Aerodyne High-Resolution Time-of-Flight Aerosol Mass Spectrometer (HR-ToF-AMS hereafter referred to as the AMS). The instrument provides real-time, high-resolution data on the composition of non-refractory aerosol species (DeCarlo et al. 2006). The AMS operates by drawing air through a critical orifice into an aerodynamic lens assembly, in which the particles are focused into a narrow beam. Upon entering the detection chamber, the particles are directed to a resistively heated tungsten surface maintained at 600 °C where they undergo rapid vaporization. The resultant vapor is then ionized by electron ionization (EI), a process that generates a spectrum of ions detectable by the time-of-flight mass spectrometer (DeCarlo et al. 2006). This ionization process generally leads to significant fragmentation of organic molecules and a lack of molecular ion peaks. However, it is particularly suited for particle-phase PAH analysis due to the resilience of particle-phase PAHs to fragmentation. The mass spectrum of particle-phase PAHs typically exhibits strong molecular ion peaks, facilitating their identification and quantification in complex aerosol mixtures (Dzepina et al. 2007). While particle-phase PAHs are more resilient than many organic molecules, the methodological power in this study lies in establishing a precise fragmentation factor and Relative Ionization Efficiency to correct for this non-negligible fragmentation.

Laboratory Validation Experiments

Laboratory experiments were conducted to examine the fragmentation behavior of particle-phase PAHs within the AMS. Understanding this fragmentation helps quantify particle-phase PAHs in ambient samples, as it directly impacts the interpretation of the mass spectra generated by the AMS. The increased internal energy associated with the vaporization process in the AMS leads to more extensive fragmentation of organic species compared to the standard EI spectra (Dzepina et al. 2007). This means that most species are not detected as the parent ion and instead create fragmentation patterns that are often interpreted with factor analysis. PAHs, however, produce a parent ion, allowing us to leverage this characteristic in our quantification approach.

To validate the PAH quantification process, test standards for individual PAHs, including anthracene, pyrene and 9-methylanthracene, were purchased from Millipore Sigma (St. Louis, MO, USA), while acenaphthylene was purchased from Fisher Scientific (Agawam, MA, USA). The standards were then prepared for analysis. Anthracene and pyrene were dissolved in isopropanol, while 9-methyl anthracene and acenaphthylene were dissolved in acetonitrile, before being atomized using a TSI 307601 atomizer. The resulting particles were subsequently measured by an AMS and a scanning mobility particle sizer (SMPS).

The data from these standard experiments were then analyzed in two ways. First, we compared the AMS mass spectrum of the particle-phase PAHs to the mass spectrum from the NIST library spectrum (NIST Office of Data and Informatics 2023). Second, we size-selected 300-nm particles and conducted an ionization efficiency experiment, following the standard methods used for determining RIE (relative ionization efficiency) in the AMS with ammonium nitrate and ammonium sulfate particles (Jimenez et al. 2003). From these experiments, we determined the fragmentation factor for the various PAHs, defined as the ratio of the parent ion signal to the total signals across all mass-to-charge (m/z) ratios. The fragmentation factor and RIE together allowed us to quantify the concentration of both individual particle-phase PAHs and the sum of particle-phase PAHs from the ambient data.

Mobile Sampling

Mobile sampling was conducted in the city of Pittsburgh. The study employed a comprehensive mobile air monitoring campaign using the Carnegie Mellon University (CMU) mobile air quality laboratory (Oladeji et al. 2023). This laboratory is a specially modified 2013 Nissan cargo van, equipped with a set of advanced instruments designed for real-time air quality assessment. The van’s instrumentation setup and operation have been extensively documented in previous studies (Li et al. 2016; Oladeji et al. 2023). The mobile lab’s instruments are powered by an onboard 110 V, 60 Hz electrical system, which is integrated with the van’s engine to ensure continuous power during extended field operations.

The sampling inlet system comprises a pair of 0.5-inch outside diameter stainless steel tubes mounted on the roof of the van directly above the driver’s seat. This positioning minimizes potential sampling interferences (e.g., from the mobile laboratory’s own exhaust) and ensures the collection of representative air samples as the van traverses various urban environments. Field tests and previous deployments of this mobile lab over the past decade have shown that self-sampling does not present any significant issues (Shah et al. 2018; Oladeji et al. 2023).

The study was conducted in Pittsburgh, a city characterized by a diverse array of emission sources and varying traffic densities. Four distinct regions were selected to capture this diversity as seen in Fig. 1. These regions include Downtown Pittsburgh (a high-traffic urban center), Shadyside (a residential area with moderate traffic), Neville Island (an industrial zone with significant point source emissions and high diesel truck traffic), and Oakland (an area with mixed land use of both residential and commercial activities). The selection of these regions was guided by a strategic consideration of their contrasting land use and potential emission sources, allowing for a comprehensive assessment of particle-phase PAH concentrations across different urban contexts.

Mapping Route showing the high traffic and industrial areas (Red and Yellow colors) alongside the low traffic and non-industrial areas (Green and Blue colors). The inset map shows the location of Pittsburgh in the continental United States

Sampling was carried out over an eight-day period in winter, with the mobile lab operating on a daily schedule from 9:00 AM to 5:00 PM. This timeframe was chosen to capture the daytime variations in traffic patterns and industrial activities, which are key determinants of PAH emissions (Narváez-Valderrama et al. 2024). To enhance the representativeness of the data, the daily driving routes were deliberately shuffled following similar approaches that we have used in past studies (Shah et al. 2018). This approach ensured that the mobile lab encountered a wide spectrum of traffic conditions and emission scenarios, helping to understand the city’s air quality in general.

The mobile lab was driven at a controlled speed of 20–25 mph. The AMS was operated with 20-s resolution in V-mode mass spectrum mode (no particle time of flight data were collected). Thus, the spatial resolution of the data are approximately 220 m. This spatial resolution is sufficient to capture neighborhood-scale and sub-neighborhood-scale variations in PAH concentrations (Wang et al. 2013). Black Carbon (BC) data was also measured by a Magee Scientific AE33 Aethalometer at a frequency of 1 Hz.

AMS Data Analysis

AMS data was analyzed in IGOR Pro version 8.0.4.2 with Squirrel version 1.65 c and Pika version 1.25 c (TOF-AMS Analysis Software. 2023). Particle-phase PAHs previously identified in Herring et al. (2015) were scanned for and identified in the dataset. The complete list of identified particle-phase PAHs is provided in Table S1 in the Supplementary Information. We determined the minimum detection limit (MDL) for each of the particle-phase PAHs as seen in Supplementary Figure S1 and Table S1. To establish this limit, the AMS was operated with a High-Efficiency Particulate Air (HEPA) filter, which effectively removes particulate matter from the sampled air, for a continuous period of 30 min. The data collected during this blank period was then analyzed to calculate the MDL, which we define as three times the standard deviation of measurements with the HEPA filter. MDLs for individual particle-phase PAHs are shown in Table S1.

Positive Matrix Factorization (PMF) analysis was also conducted on the high-resolution organic aerosol mass spectra. Details of the PMF analysis are presented in the supplementary information and supplementary Figures S7–S12.

Spatial Analysis

Spatial analysis was conducted using ArcGIS Pro, a geographic information system (GIS) software widely used for spatial data analysis and mapping (Pucha-Cofrep et al. 2025). Integrating geospatial data with pollutant concentration measurements allows for a detailed examination of distribution patterns of air pollutants in relation to various urban features. The data obtained from the GPS logger, which tracked the mobile lab's location in real time, was synchronized with the concentration data processed in IGOR Pro using a common time step.

We divided the entire sampling domain into 200 m × 200 m grid cells. Following the approach of Shah et al. (2018), we computed the daily median concentration in each grid cell and then the mean of daily medians. This grid cell size was chosen for multiple reasons such as the 200-m grids being similar in size to the spatial resolution of AMS data collection (~ 220 m), and these grids are fine enough to reflect the micro-scale variations characteristic of urban landscapes (Wang et al. 2013).

Results and Discussion

Laboratory Validation Experiments

The results from atomizing PAHs and comparing their AMS mass spectrum to the NIST mass spectrum (NIST Office of Data and Informatics 2023) demonstrated a strong correlation between both mass spectra, as shown in Fig. 2. The comparison of the mass spectra for pyrene and anthracene indicated significant correlation between the NIST and AMS mass spectra, with R2 values of 0.95 and 0.96, respectively.

Mass spectra for pyrene and anthracene between AMS and NIST reference spectra. The upper panels show the overlaid unit mass resolution mass spectra for pyrene (A) and anthracene (B). The lower panels show scatter plots. The NIST and AMS mass spectra are highly correlated, though there is a higher degree of fragmentation in the AMS mass spectrum

While the NIST and AMS mass spectra are highly correlated, there is a greater amount of fragmentation in the AMS. This is evident in the mass spectra plotted in Fig. 2, as the abundance of the parent ion peak is smaller in the AMS than in the NIST spectra. As shown in Fig. 2, the relative abundance of the parent ion peak in the AMS is approximately 78% and 66% of the NIST relative abundance for pyrene and anthracene, respectively.

Figure 3 compares the fractional abundance of the parent ion peak in our AMS measurements and the NIST mass spectra for four PAHs. In all cases, the parent ion is less abundant in the AMS spectra, indicative of greater fragmentation. The ratio of parent peak ion abundance in the AMS to the NIST spectrum ranges from 0.61—0.75 for the PAHs shown in Fig. 3.

Comparison of parent ion contributions for selected PAH compounds in AMS and NIST spectra. The AMS spectra consistently exhibit a lower abundance of the parent ion due to greater fragmentation

We performed ionization efficiency (IE) calibrations with both atomized ammonium nitrate and several PAH standards. The IE of ammonium nitrate was determined to be 8 × 10–8 ions/molecule. In comparison, the IE values for pyrene and anthracene were calculated to be 9 × 10–8 ions/molecule and 1 × 10⁻7 ions/molecule respectively, as shown in Supplementary Figure S3. This results in an average relative ionization efficiency (RIE) of 1.2 when scaled relative to ammonium nitrate.

The laboratory results were used to quantify particle-phase PAH concentrations from the ambient mobile sampling data. For the list of particle-phase PAHs identified in Table S1, we first quantified the abundance of the parent ion peak. We then scaled this value by the fragmentation factor and used an RIE of 1.2 to convert signal to concentration (Takegawa et al. 2005).

Spatial differences in BC and particle-phase PAH concentrations are due to a combination of shifts in the baseline concentration as well as high concentration spikes. Neighborhood-level baselines vary due to differences in long-term source contributions and atmospheric conditions. Mean total particle-phase PAH concentrations for the sampling day shown in Fig. 4 were highest in high traffic and industrial neighborhoods such as Downtown (84.17 ± 96.68 ng/m3) and Neville Island (71.39 ± 31.94 ng/m3). Lower concentrations were observed in residential neighborhoods like Shadyside (55.59 ± 53.02 ng/m3) and Oakland (45.38 ± 14.60 ng/m3). The 10th percentile values followed a similar trend. Neville Island (41.17 ng/m3) and Downtown (34.78 ng/m3) had the highest background levels, while Oakland (26.88 ng/m3) and Shadyside (12.95 ng/m3) were lowest. These findings suggest that Downtown and Neville Island experience both elevated PAH background concentrations and more frequent episodic emissions.

For Black Carbon, the highest mean concentrations were recorded in Shadyside (943.49 ± 1220.84 ng/m3) and Neville Island (905.18 ± 1547.59 ng/m3), followed by Downtown (605.40 ± 595.49 ng/m3) and Oakland (560.55 ± 396.21 ng/m3). Although Shadyside is largely residential, it contains several high-traffic corridors and commercial zones that may have skewed the concentration distribution in this neighborhood and contributed to elevated mean BC concentrations during sampling. Consistent with this interpretation, the spatial BC map for the entire campaign (Supplementary Figure S6) shows that some of the higher BC grid cells in Shadyside are concentrated near areas with greater activity including two commercial corridors rather than within the low-traffic residential areas of the neighborhood.

Interestingly, the 10th percentile values for BC did not follow the same pattern as the means. Oakland (152.60 ng/m3) and Shadyside (129.20 ng/m3) exhibited the highest background levels, while Neville Island (98.20 ng/m3) and Downtown (85.00 ng/m3) were lower. These likely reflect differences in the temporal structure of BC sources across neighborhoods. In high traffic areas such as Downtown and along major highways, BC concentrations are dominated by short-duration high traffic spikes that have limited influence on the background concentrations. In contrast, residential neighborhoods with local commercial activities tend to experience more spatially uniform and persistent BC contributions, leading to elevated background levels. Consistent with this interpretation, the spatial BC map for the entire campaign (Supplementary Figure S6) shows that elevated BC grid cells in Shadyside are concentrated near areas with greater commercial activity.

BC and particle-phase PAH spikes were more frequent in high traffic areas, including when sampling on highways in transit between neighborhoods (white shaded areas in Fig. 4), than in low-traffic areas. Tang et al. (2023) previously showed that many BC spikes observed with mobile sampling are the result of emissions from high-emission vehicles. We seem to observe a similar impact of high emitting vehicles on BC and particle-phase PAHs in this work. The concentrations along highways and in other high-traffic areas were not only elevated but also exhibited considerable variability, reflecting the dynamic nature of emissions in these environments. Here, we define variability using the coefficient of variation (CV), calculated as the ratio of the standard deviation to the mean concentration (CV = σ / μ). Downtown Pittsburgh exhibited a mean total particle-phase PAH concentration of 84.17 ng/m3 with a standard deviation of 96.68 ng/m3, corresponding to a CV of 1.14, while Neville Island had a lower CV of 0.45 (σ = 31.94 ng/m3, μ = 71.39 ng/m3). This suggests that traffic-dominated Downtown is more influenced by short-duration spikes, whereas the industrial areas on Neville Island experience more stable, sustained emissions.

In addition to the overall increase in particle-phase PAH concentrations along busy roads, the data also revealed occasional, coincident spikes in the concentrations of BC, C14H10, and the total PAHs collected on a given day, as shown in Fig. 4 indicated by the circled regions. These spikes suggest that certain emission events, such as the presence of high-emitting vehicles, contribute to simultaneous increases in both particle-phase PAHs and BC. The correlation between these pollutants underscores the close relationship between PAHs and BC as co-products of combustion processes, particularly those associated with vehicular emissions, though other combustion activities may contribute. However, not all spikes are correlated. There are spikes of BC that do not have coincident spikes in particle-phase PAHs, and particle-phase PAH spikes that are not accompanied by a BC spike. This indicates that some of the sources creating concentration spikes are high emitters for both BC and particle-phase PAHs, whereas others are solely high emitters for BC or particle-phase PAHs.

High-concentration hotspots were identified as locations where pollutant levels exceeded the 95th percentile of the overall total particle-phase PAHs concentrations. This percentile-based threshold approach is consistent with prior work in environmental exposure assessment, where the 95th percentile has been used to define spikes in pollutant concentrations and assess short-term exposure risks (New York State Department of Health Bureau of Toxic Substance Assessment 2013). Figure 5 presents the spatial distribution of particle-phase PAH concentration spikes, defined as 20-s resolution values exceeding the 95th percentile of all particle-phase PAH measurements across the full dataset. These spikes are not uniformly distributed across the study area. Instead, they are predominantly concentrated in areas associated with high vehicular activity and industrial presence.

Spatial distribution of Total PAH spikes (defined as values ≥ 95th percentile) across Pittsburgh. Spikes are most frequent in high-traffic areas such as Downtown Pittsburgh and along major roadways. These areas experience dense commuter flow and frequent heavy-duty vehicle activity. Spikes are also observed in Neville Island, an industrial neighborhood, suggesting contributions from industrial sources. In contrast, spikes are less frequent in lower-traffic, primarily residential neighborhoods such as Oakland and Shadyside

Quantitatively, nearly half of all spikes (46.4%) occurred in locations categorized as “Others,” which primarily include major highways and arterial roads. Downtown Pittsburgh, a known high-traffic urban center, accounted for 24.7% of the observed spikes. Neville Island, an industrial zone with several stationary sources, contributed 12.4% of the total. In contrast, residential neighborhoods such as Shadyside and Oakland exhibited significantly fewer spikes, comprising only 11.3% and 5.2% of the total, respectively.

These spatial trends are consistent with expected source patterns for PAHs, which are primarily associated with combustion processes, especially vehicular emissions (Marr et al. 2006). The elevated frequency of spikes along highways and downtown areas supports the hypothesis that on-road traffic is an important source of episodic particle-phase PAH emissions. Additionally, the relatively high contributions from industrial regions such as Neville Island suggest a combined influence of mobile and industrial point source emissions.

While the spatial distribution of spikes clearly aligns with areas of high traffic and industrial activity, attribution of individual spikes to specific sources tends to be qualitative due to the absence of wind resolved plume data and incomplete knowledge of all specific emissions locations for industrial sources.

Spatial Analysis of PAHs

Figure 6 shows a map of the total particle-phase PAH concentrations identified in Pittsburgh over the entire sampling campaign. The data in Fig. 6 show the campaign-wide data aggregated to 200-m grid cells and averaged across the campaign as described in the methods section. Previous studies have shown that short-term mobile monitoring campaigns (6 -10 days) can effectively capture spatial patterns in long-term air pollutant concentrations, particularly when sampling is designed to maximize spatial and temporal coverage (Hankey and Marshall 2015; Riley et al. 2014). Following this precedent, the particle-phase PAH concentration map in Fig. 6 is interpreted as representative of spatial patterns in long-term particle-bound PAH concentrations across Pittsburgh in winter.

Grid-cell average concentrations of Total PAHs for the entire sampling campaign. The colors show quintiles of concentration

As illustrated in Fig. 6, the highest concentrations of particle-phase PAHs were observed in downtown areas and on roads with dense traffic and high vehicle activity. In these zones, particle-phase PAH concentrations measured with the AMS often exceeded 131.3 ng/m3, as seen in the grid-cell averaged concentrations, highlighting the considerable contribution of vehicular emissions to local air pollution. This is consistent with prior studies linking highways and high-traffic urban centers to elevated PAH concentrations (Kim et al. 2019).

In contrast, residential areas such as Shadyside and Oakland exhibited significantly lower particle-phase PAH concentrations, frequently falling below 101.7 ng/m3. This difference highlights the impact of localized emission sources, with residential zones benefiting from fewer direct sources of particle-phase PAHs, such as heavy traffic or industrial operations.

Concentrations are also elevated on Neville Island, where the mean total particle-phase PAH concentration was 180 ng/m3. Neville Island has both industrial point sources and high diesel truck traffic from vehicles that service the industries on the island. Both sources could contribute to the elevated concentrations in that area.

Figure 6 also shows evidence of intra-neighborhood concentration gradients. For example, within downtown, there are grid cells in both the upper and lower quintiles of the concentration distribution. This demonstrates that there can be significant spatial variability in pollutant concentrations, even in neighborhoods where all the grid cells would be classified as high traffic locations. The particle-phase PAH spatial patterns in downtown broadly mirror NO2 spatial patterns measured by Li et al. (2019), suggesting that traffic contributes to the observed spatial variation.

The spatial distribution of Black Carbon (BC), as depicted in Supplementary Figure S6, exhibited similar trends as particle-phase PAHs, further supporting the conclusion that the high particle-phase PAH regions are heavily influenced by combustion processes, whether from industrial operations or vehicular traffic.

Figure 7 summarizes the BC and particle-phase PAH measurements in each neighborhood as box plots. The particle-phase PAH data shown in Fig. 7a are the sum of all particle-phase PAHs measured with the AMS. There are both inter-neighborhood and intra-neighborhood spatial variations in particle-phase PAH concentrations. The median particle-phase PAH concentration is highest in Downtown (150 ng/m3) and lowest in Oakland (70 ng/m3), indicating a 2.1-fold difference between the most and least polluted neighborhoods.

Neighborhood-scale spatial variability of total PAHs (A) and BC (B) based on the entire dataset. The boxes show the interquartile range, the line in the center of the box shows the median, the star shows the mean and the whiskers show the range of the data, excluding outliers

Downtown also has the largest intra-neighborhood spatial variation, with the third quartile (Q3 = 240 ng/m3) nearly 3.4 times larger than the first quartile (Q1 = 70 ng/m3), likely due to transient emissions from traffic. Oakland has the smallest interquartile range (IQR = 80 ng/m3, Q3/Q1 ratio = 2.6), suggesting less spatial variability, likely due to less traffic in this neighborhood. Neville Island has a low vehicle traffic but is home to several industrial point sources and therefore has both a similar median particle-phase PAH concentration (140 ng/m3) as Downtown, and a large interquartile range (IQR = 120 ng/m3, Q3/Q1 ratio = 2.4).

Figure 7b shows the intra-neighborhood variation in BC concentrations. The broad spatial patterns are similar to those for particle-phase PAHs. Oakland has the lowest median BC concentration (640 ng/m3) and the narrowest IQR (710 ng/m3, Q3/Q1 = 3.5). However, unlike particle-phase PAHs, the highest median BC concentrations are observed in Neville Island (880 ng/m3) and Shadyside (860 ng/m3), rather than in Downtown (740 ng/m3).

While particle-phase PAHs and BC share similar spatial patterns, they differ in the magnitude of their variation across neighborhoods. For particle-phase PAHs, the median concentration in Downtown (150 ng/m3) is more than 2.1 times higher than in Oakland (70 ng/m3). For BC, using the same pair of neighborhoods, the difference is smaller. Downtown (740 ng/m3) has only a 1.16-fold higher median than Oakland (640 ng/m3). This smaller difference for BC indicates that particle-phase PAH concentrations vary more strongly between neighborhoods, suggesting greater sensitivity to localized sources.

Elevated particle-phase PAH concentrations in Downtown Pittsburgh might also be a result of the street canyon effect in which pollutants accumulate and remain confined within the canyon walls, which can limit the dispersion of pollutants in urban areas. (Mead 2008).

To further compare the spatial variability of total particle-phase PAHs and Black Carbon across neighborhoods, Fig. 8 presents cumulative distribution functions (CDFs) of their concentrations. The particle-phase PAH CDF clearly shows more inter-neighborhood heterogeneity than for BC. The maximum vertical spread between neighborhood curves, i.e., the largest difference in cumulative frequency at any given concentration is notably greater for particle-phase PAHs than for BC. For particle-phase PAHs, the largest spread occurs at a concentration of approximately 100 ng/m3, where the cumulative frequency ranges from ~ 31% in Downtown to ~ 70% in Oakland, a 39-percentage point difference. In contrast, the maximum spread for Black Carbon occurs near 900 ng/m3, but the difference in cumulative frequency between neighborhoods is only about 15 percentage points (e.g., ~ 52% in Shadyside vs. ~ 67% in Oakland).

Source Identification

Ratio to Ratio Plots

Ratio-to-ratio plots offer a framework for source attribution of PAHs by allowing comparison across multiple source profiles within a source class (Robinson et al. 2006). In these plots, compounds of interest are normalized to a third, chemically inert species, enabling consistent comparison of source profiles on a single plot. Each source profile appears as a point in ratio-ratio space, and the plots can illustrate mixing between sources as well as the effects of atmospheric oxidation on PAHs (Robinson et al. 2006). This technique is particularly useful for distinguishing emissions when multiple profiles exist within a single source class.

In this study, ambient particle-phase PAH data were plotted against established source profiles (May et al. 2014; Hu et al. 2013; Zielinska et al. 2004; Mu et al. 2013; Khare and Baruah 2011; Chang et al. 2012; Schauer et al. 2002), derived from literature. These profiles encompass a range of emission sources, including diesel exhaust, metallurgical coke production, and gasoline combustion. By overlaying the measured particle-phase PAH data onto these source profiles, we were able to visually assess the alignment of the ambient data with known source profiles. Additionally, the linear mixing lines connecting these source profiles facilitates the interpretation of the data by indicating the likely contributions from source mixtures. Figure 9 shows the ratio-ratio plot for C12H8 and C14H10, both normalized by BC. Each panel of Fig. 9 shows the measurements for a specific neighborhood. The box-and-whisker plots near the x- and y-axes show the distribution of data in each neighborhood. For all neighborhoods, the data are generally bounded by source profiles for gasoline vehicles, diesel vehicles, and coke production.

Examining the source contributions between neighborhoods. (A) Downtown, (B) Neville Island, (C) Oakland, and (D) Shadyside.

Source profiles from the literature are shown for diesel vehicles (asterisks), gasoline vehicles (triangles), and coke production (square). The arrow towards the upper right of the figure points to the approximate location of a non-combustion asphalt source that emits PAHs but not BC

Diesel vehicle and coke oven emissions appear in the lower-left region of the ratio-ratio plots, characterized by relatively low PAH/BC ratios. These sources are associated with high-temperature, often non-premixed combustion processes. Older diesel sources that lack modern emissions controls including diesel particle filters (DPF) have a similar source profile as coke emissions, whereas newer diesel vehicles equipped with DPF have significantly lower C12H8/BC and C14H10/BC.

Gasoline vehicle profiles appear toward the upper-right quadrant of each panel, corresponding to high PAH/BC ratios. However, there is a substantial difference observed in the source profiles between older gasoline vehicles lacking catalytic converters and those equipped with them. Specifically, non-catalytic vehicles (represented by all triangles except the yellow triangle) exhibit much higher PAH/BC ratios compared to newer, catalyst-equipped vehicles. Only one of the four available profiles for gasoline vehicles is from vehicles with a catalytic converter, and we expect most vehicles to have catalytic converters. This may suggest that we need more gasoline vehicle profiles, but it also could suggest that we are missing a source with high ratios of both C14H10/BC and C12H8/BC.

One possible source with a high PAH/BC ratio is asphalt paving. Asphalt paving is a known source of PAHs. Asphalt has high emissions of PAHs and other organic vapors during the high temperatures associated with paving, and asphalt can serve as a long-term source of semi volatile organic compounds long after paving is complete (Humes et al. 2023; Khare et al. 2020). Asphalt is also a non-combustion source, so does not emit BC (though BC can be co-emitted by diesel paving equipment when roads are paved), leading to extremely high PAH/BC ratios. The upper right portion of data in the ratio-ratio plot could therefore be the result of a non-combustion source such as emissions from asphalt pavements.

In general, there is a high overlap between the measurements in all four neighborhoods. For example, Downtown has a higher mean C12H8/BC ratio than other neighborhoods, but the mean C12H8/BC in Downtown is within the interquartile range for the other neighborhoods. Since the data from different neighborhoods tends to overlap, Fig. 9 suggests that the same sources impact PAH concentrations across the sampled neighborhoods. It further suggests that the spatial variations in particle-phase PAH concentrations (shown in Fig. 7 and 8) are the result of different source strengths, such as higher traffic volumes in some neighborhoods, rather than a different source mix. Even Neville Island, which has several industrial sources, seems to have a similar source mix as the urban neighborhoods, suggesting that the industrial sources in that area might not have major contributions to the observed particle-phase PAH concentrations or that those industrial sources have similar source signatures as vehicles. Hence, a common set of sources can still result in variable concentrations due to the proximity of the neighborhood to the emission source.

To support the visual interpretation of source similarity observed in the ratio-ratio plots, we applied a statistical test to quantify the multivariate similarity between neighborhoods. While the overlap in ambient data distributions suggests that neighborhoods may be influenced by a similar mixture of sources, a formal comparison helps assess whether differences in PAH/BC profiles are statistically distinguishable. For this purpose, we used the Hotelling’s T2 statistic test.

Hotelling's T2 statistics compares the multivariate similarity among the four neighborhoods. It is used to assess the differences between two groups across multiple correlated variables (Johnson and Wichern 2002). In this study, the T2 statistics quantify the multivariate distance between neighborhood means for the ratios of C14H10/BC and C12H8/BC, accounting for pooled covariance. The results are shown in Table S2 of the Supplemental Information. Lower T2 values indicate greater similarity, while higher values suggest more pronounced differences between neighborhoods. A T2 value of zero represents identical group means, indicating perfect similarity, while larger values reflect increasing dissimilarity with no theoretical upper limit.

Oakland showed moderate similarity to both Shadyside (22.862) and Neville Island (26.693), while Downtown exhibited the highest T2 values in most pairwise comparisons, particularly with Oakland (T2 = 115.96), indicating that Downtown has the most distinct PAH/BC profile. This likely reflects Downtown’s higher traffic density and more frequent transient emissions, which can elevate PAH/BC ratios. In contrast, the other neighborhoods showed more moderate differences. Overall, the T2 analysis indicates that while most neighborhoods share broadly similar PAH/BC characteristics, Downtown stands out due to stronger or more localized emission sources.

Prior studies on PAH source apportionment in Pittsburgh highlighted the significant influence of coke oven emissions on regional air quality. For example, Robinson et al. noted the substantial contribution of coke production, which complicated the apportionment of PAHs to vehicle emissions (Robinson et al. 2006). Similarly, Logue et al. (2009) demonstrated the widespread impact of coke emissions on various locations within the region. These previous findings suggest that coke emissions are likely to contribute to the observed similarities across neighborhoods in our study, reinforcing the need to account for this source in our analysis. However, it remains difficult to separate the impacts of coke oven emissions and diesel vehicle emissions on observed particle-phase PAH concentrations. These two sources have similar source profiles, and both diesel and coke oven emissions lie at the bottom left of the ambient data in Fig. 9.

Comparing our measurements with prior urban PAH studies, we observed that our total particle-phase PAH concentration are slightly higher than those reported in some filter-based literature. Morville et al. (2011) reported a mean total PAH concentration of 127 ng/m3 in an urban neighborhood using HPLC analysis of filters and XAD—2resin. Tsapakis and Stephanou (2005) reported mean total PAH concentrations ranging from 44 ng/m3 to 129 ng/m3 from GCMS analysis of glass fiber filters and polyurethane foam plug. In Pittsburgh, Tan et al. (2014) reported average surface-bound particulate PAH concentrations measured with a photoelectric aerosol sensor across low and high traffic sites, ranging from an approximate median value of 13 ng/m3 to 28 ng/m3. These differences likely result from a combination of the instrument differences as well as our mobile sampling strategy particularly near high traffic and industrial regions.

Correlation Between PAHs and Traffic emissions

Several of the analyses above point to the importance of traffic emissions on particle-phase PAH concentrations observed during mobile sampling. This evidence includes the abundance of high concentration spikes in high traffic areas as well as the ratio-ratio plots where much of the data is bounded by vehicle emissions profiles. In this section, we compare particle-phase PAH concentrations to a separate measure of vehicle emissions.

AMS data is commonly analyzed with PMF (Positive Matrix Factorization) to perform source apportionment (Ulbrich et al. 2009). PMF source apportionment of AMS organic aerosol data typically resolves several factors associated with primary emissions from cooking (COA), biomass burning (BBOA), and traffic emissions (HOA), along with factors associated with secondary organic aerosol.

We performed PMF analysis of the AMS data collected in this study as shown in Supplementary Figures S7 and S8. We identified a three-factor solution that includes HOA, COA, and a single factor for oxygenated organic aerosol (OOA) that represents secondary organic aerosol. We then compared the concentration of HOA to several of the PAHs of interest.

Figure 10 shows the R2 between three particle-phase PAHs (C13H12, C14H10 and C16H14) and the HOA factor, separated by sampling day. Overall, the data shows a moderate correlation between PAHs and the HOA factor. With the exception of one sampling day with R2 ~ 0.95, the R2 were typically in the range of 0.15—0.4.

R2 values between measured concentrations of three PAHs and HOA. The three PAHs includes C13H12, C14H10, and C16H14, and HOA based on the 20-s AMS measurements for each sampling day

Our analysis demonstrates that vehicle emissions are a significant source of particle-phase PAHs, as evidenced by the temporal trends in the time series data and the alignment of ambient data with vehicle source profiles in the ratio-to-ratio plots. However, vehicles are not the sole contributor to particle-phase PAH levels in the region. The low R2 in the HOA comparison suggests that an additional source, likely with minimal or no black carbon (BC) emissions, contributes to the observed particle-phase PAHs. This is consistent with the possibility of emissions from sources like asphalt, which is known to release PAHs both during high-temperature processes and can serve as a long-term PAH source (Humes et al. 2023; Jandová et al. 2025). Exploring the relationship between PAH/BC ratios and temperature could provide further insight, though this analysis falls beyond the scope of the current study.

Furthermore, our findings align with prior studies, such as those by Robinson et al. (2006), which identified significant contributions from coke oven emissions in Pittsburgh. The influence of this source is particularly relevant given its established impact on regional air quality and its potential role in the similarities observed across neighborhoods.

Limitations

The study is based on mobile sampling conducted between 9 am and 5 pm during the winter period. These constraints might bias the observed spatial patterns because PAHs exhibit strong diurnal variability (linked to rush-hour traffic and boundary-layer height). Hence, the spatial patterns are best interpreted as winter daytime conditions only. The extent to which these patterns might be applicable to other seasons or nighttime hours is limited. In addition, seasonal variability and temperature driven gas-particle partitioning of PAHs may influence the representation of these findings. As a result, the spatial patterns observed during this winter campaign may not be a direct representation of the annual-average conditions. Furthermore, while the 220 m spatial resolution is sufficient to capture neighborhood-scale trends, this averaging scale may smooth over intense, hyper-local PAH concentration hotspots.

Also, the AMS measures only the particulate-phase fraction of PAHs. Hence, the ratio-ratio plot reflects the composition of PAHs present in the particle phase rather than the total (particle + gas phase). Some of the PAHs identified in this study are semi-volatile and known to be largely in the gas phase under typical urban conditions. As a result, their particle phase concentration and ratios to BC are influenced by variables such as temperature, volatility, molecular weight, atmospheric lifetime alongside other variables (Gbeddy et al. 2020). Hence, the ratio-ratio plot should be interpreted as reflecting the particle associated subset of the PAHs and not the total.

Several sources of uncertainties tend to influence the concentration of the AMS particle-phase PAH concentrations. Firstly, particle-phase PAH quantification depends on the fragmentation factor in which based on our NIST—AMS comparisons, the fitted slopes for pyrene(0.78) and anthracene(0.66) deviate from the ideal one to one agreement by 22% and 34% respectively, suggesting a 22%—34% uncertainty associated with fragmentation factor variability. Secondly, the assumed relative ionization efficiency (RIE = 1.2) also contributes some uncertainty. Published AMS studies such as Bahreini et al. (2009) and Middlebrook et al. (2011) report that uncertainties in the AMS relative ionization efficiency (RIE) are typically around 20% with an overall uncertainty in the AMS quantification in the range of 20%—30%. Additional uncertainty may also arise from potential matrix effects in ambient aerosols. Furthermore, applying fragmentation factors and RIE values derived from a limited number of PAH standards to a broader set of PAH species may introduce additional uncertainty in the quantification. Overall, while these factors affect the absolute magnitude of reported PAH concentrations, the uncertainties in both the fragmentation factor and RIE act as linear scaling factors. As a result, these uncertainties uniformly shift all concentrations by the same proportion and do not affect the interpretation of spatial patterns, which rely on relative differences between neighborhoods rather than the absolute concentration levels.

Lastly, the study did not account for daily background particle-phase PAH levels due to the absence of background measurements across days. Hence, the analysis focuses on the spatial pattern across the campaign rather than the day-to-day differences in absolute particle-phase PAH levels. In addition, while ratio-to-ratio plots provide a useful framework for source attribution of PAHs by enabling comparison across multiple source profiles within a source class, they may not effectively distinguish industrial signatures from traffic emissions, as these combustion source profiles tend to overlap.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the successful application of high-resolution aerosol mass spectrometry (HR-AMS) in enhancing the quantification and spatial analysis of particle-phase PAHs in urban environments. Laboratory validation experiments confirmed the AMS's capability to replicate standard NIST PAH mass spectra (R2 > 0.95) with minimal fragmentation, supporting accurate quantification. Establishing the relative ionization efficiency of particle-phase PAHs, along with a fragmentation factor, improved the accuracy of the method, enabling reliable identification of particle-phase PAH compounds within complex urban aerosols.

Mobile monitoring across Pittsburgh revealed significant spatial variability in particle-phase PAH concentrations, both between and within neighborhoods. For instance, the median total particle-phase PAH concentration in Downtown (150 ng/m3) was more than twice that in Oakland (70 ng/m3), reflecting a 2.1-fold inter-neighborhood difference. Within neighborhoods, interquartile variation was also substantial. Downtown exhibited a high interquartile range (Q3/Q1 ratio = 3.4), while Neville Island showed similarly large variation, likely driven by industrial sources.

When compared to other aerosol components, particle-phase PAHs exhibited greater spatial variability than Black Carbon (BC). The difference in median BC concentrations between the most and least polluted neighborhoods (Neville Island vs. Oakland) was only 1.16-fold, substantially lower than for particle-phase PAHs. Cumulative distribution functions also showed a maximum vertical spread of 39 percentage points for particle-phase PAHs, compared to only 15 for BC, underscoring the enhanced spatial variability of particle-phase PAHs.

A substantial fraction of particle-phase PAH concentration spikes (defined as values exceeding the 95th percentile) occurred in high-activity zones such as Downtown Pittsburgh (24.7%) and major highways (46.4%). This suggests the potential for short-term, high-intensity exposure in areas characterized by dense traffic or industrial operations. These episodic increase in particle-phase PAH levels may not be well captured by stationary monitors, highlighting the added value of mobile high-resolution monitoring in exposure assessment.

These findings offer insights into urban air quality management, particularly for addressing vehicular emissions and industrial activities that contribute disproportionately to PAH pollution. This research not only advances the methodological framework for PAH quantification but also provides actionable insights into the spatial distribution and emission sources of these harmful pollutants. As cities continue to grow and industrial activities expand, the need for precise and real-time monitoring of air toxics like PAHs will become increasingly crucial for protecting public health and informing effective environmental policies.
