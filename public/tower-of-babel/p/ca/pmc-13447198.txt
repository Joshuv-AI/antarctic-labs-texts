Expert Consensus and Clinical Pathway Recommendations for Transthyretin Amyloid Cardiomyopathy in Malaysia
Authors: Mohd Rahal Yusoff, Raja Ezman Farid Raja Shariff, Choon Keong Tee, Kee Wei Lee, Nor Hanim Mohd Amin, Lean Seng Chen, Thurston Erng, Kavita Arumugam, Siti Nooraein Yaser, Jerome Tsen Chuen Tan, Suhashini Ganapaty, Roslina Omar, Thuhairah Abdul Rahman, Lock Hock Ngu, Soon Chai Low, Nur Shafini Che Rahim, Yen Yee Oon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy (ATTR-CM) is a progressive, life-threatening condition that remains under-recognised in Malaysia. Although disease-modifying therapies are available, their high cost and limited accessibility contribute to delays in diagnosis, compounded by gaps in clinical awareness, differences in diagnostic tool availability and disparities in healthcare resources. While international guidelines offer a comprehensive framework for diagnosing and managing ATTR-CM, local adaptation is essential to ensure their relevance and feasibility within the Malaysian healthcare system. This expert consensus provides practical, locally relevant recommendations to assist Malaysian clinicians in the timely identification and management of ATTR-CM. A multidisciplinary panel of clinicians from cardiology, haematology, nuclear medicine and other related specialties convened to develop tailored recommendations for national practice, drawing on international guidance, current evidence and local clinical practices. The guidance outlines key clinical features to raise early suspicion, proposes a diagnostic algorithm adaptable to different levels of care and offers recommendations on appropriate use of diagnostic tests—including imaging, laboratory investigations, tissue biopsy, and genetic testing when indicated—while emphasising the distinction between ATTR-CM and amyloid light chain amyloidosis, optimising non-invasive diagnostic approaches and fostering multidisciplinary collaboration across the care continuum. These consensus recommendations aim to enhance diagnostic accuracy, reduce variability in clinical practice and support earlier identification of ATTR-CM. By bridging global standards with local healthcare practices, this guidance equips clinicians to deliver timely, effective care for an underdiagnosed and often overlooked condition.Keywords: Cardiac failure, Cardiomyopathies, Amyloidosis, Malaysia, Diagnosis, Rare disease

INTRODUCTION

Amyloidosis refers to a group of diseases characterised by the misfolding and aggregation of various proteins, leading to the extracellular deposition of amyloid fibrils in different tissues and organs.1,2) In cardiac amyloidosis, these fibrils progressively infiltrate and accumulate in the myocardium, eventually resulting in restrictive cardiomyopathy.3) While more than 30 proteins can form amyloid fibrils in the heart, cardiac amyloidosis typically occurs due to misfolded transthyretin, a protein mainly produced in the liver, or monoclonal immunoglobulin light chains aggregation produced by the bone marrow.4,5,6) In the case of transthyretin amyloidosis (ATTR), it can present as hereditary or wild-type, depending on the presence or absence of genetic mutations.5,6)

Estimating the true regional prevalence of transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy (ATTR-CM) is challenging, particularly due to its frequent under-recognition among patients presenting and living with heart failure.2,4) In Malaysia, the prevalence and incidence of ATTR-CM are largely unknown, despite it being registered as a rare cardiac disease.7) Available data suggests that, within the multi-ethnic Malaysian population, the majority of ATTR-CM patients are of Chinese ethnicity, with p.Ala117Ser identified as the most common mutation.8) A retrospective, single-centre, observational study conducted in the state of Sarawak further reported the heterogeneous clinical characteristics of ATTR-CM, and the tendency for delayed diagnosis, resulting in poor outcomes.9) Consistent with global observations, diagnosis delays are often substantial, with the time from symptom onset to diagnosis averaging 6.1 years (median 3.4 years) for wild-type ATTR-CM and 5.7 years (median 2.6 years) for hereditary ATTR-CM.10) These delays are multifactorial, driven in part by symptom overlap with more common conditions, such as heart failure with preserved ejection fraction and aortic stenosis, as well as the prior unavailability of effective therapies.10)

Healthcare system factors may further contribute to delayed recognition and diagnosis of ATTR-CM in Malaysia. The country operates on a dual referral system in which public primary care clinics function as gatekeepers, directing patients to specialists through structured, needs-based referral pathways. In the private healthcare sector, patients may obtain specialist access through general practitioners who are not bound by Ministry referral policies, self-referrals via emergency departments, or direct fee-for-service specialist consultations.11) This fragmentation can further complicate coordinated care and timely diagnosis. These barriers are further reflected by a recent survey among Malaysian cardiologists, which revealed substantial gaps in knowledge and experience in diagnosing and managing ATTR-CM.12) While many clinicians are familiar with the ‘textbook red flags,’ essential diagnostic services, including technetium-99m pyrophosphate (PYP) scintigraphy and specialised pathology for cardiac amyloidosis biopsy interpretation, are often not readily available.12) These findings underscore the importance of enhancing clinician knowledge on diagnosing ATTR-CM and providing clear guidance on referral pathways for diagnostic testing.12)

Multiple guidelines have underscored the importance of timely ATTR-CM diagnosis, advancements in imaging techniques and the potential for achieving non-invasive diagnosis. For example, the American Heart Association (AHA) and the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) Working Group on Myocardial and Pericardial Diseases emphasise early identification of ATTR-CM to reduce diagnostic delay and improve therapeutic outcomes.6,13) Similarly, the Japanese Circulation Society has introduced its own guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of cardiac amyloidosis, while Taiwan’s Society of Cardiology and the Society of Nuclear Medicine emphasise the important role PYP scintigraphy plays in ATTR-CM diagnosis.14,15) In 2022, Lin et al.16) published clinical recommendations for diagnosing and monitoring ATTR-CM in Asia, a valuable regional document that accounts for limited healthcare access, variable test availability, and expertise in image interpretation. However, these recommendations noted that patient pathways may vary across health systems in Asia, including Malaysia, where heterogeneous healthcare infrastructure and uneven distribution of advanced imaging necessitate a tailored diagnostic algorithm for equitable and feasible implementation.

Thus, a multidisciplinary panel of 18 local experts representing cardiology, nuclear medicine, haematology, pathology, genetics and neurology from key Ministry of Health and Ministry of Education hospitals convened in October 2024 to discuss and refine diagnostic approaches for ATTR-CM and locally available screening tests. Recognising the limited resources for diagnosing and managing ATTR-CM in Malaysia, the expert committee curated a diagnostic algorithm tailored to the local context, along with practical recommendations to overcome implementation barriers (e.g., appointing cardiologists as case managers).

RAISING THE INDEX OF SUSPICION FOR ATTR-CM

Variations in the presentation of ATTR subtypes

Asian populations frequently exhibit different ATTR subtypes. Patients with wild-type ATTR often present with cardiomyopathy, carpal tunnel syndrome and spinal canal stenosis.17,18) Wild-type ATTR predominantly affects older patients (i.e., above 70 years of age). Although the age of onset has not been shown to differ significantly between genders, women are often older than men when symptoms suggestive of cardiac manifestation first appear.19) In contrast, hereditary ATTR is characterised by a higher prevalence of gait instability, gastrointestinal symptoms, urinary incontinence, and neuropathic pain.17,18) Although p.Ala117Ser is the most commonly reported ATTR mutation in Asia and is often linked to cardiomyopathies and polyneuropathic syndromes, the genetic landscape of ATTR varies across countries and ethnic groups.8,20,21) For example, p.Val50Met is the predominant mutation pattern in Japan, whereas p.Asp38Ala is more commonly observed in the Republic of Korea.22,23) These regional differences in predominant mutations likely contribute to the observed variation in age of onset for hereditary ATTR across Asia, with patients typically diagnosed around age 60 in Taiwan, around 50 years in the Republic of Korea and as early as 40 years in Japan.16,20,22,23,24,25)

Although early cardiac ‘red flags’ for ATTR may appear to be similar across Asian, American, and European populations, variation in its presentation has been seen in other regions.6,13) For instance, concomitant ATTR amyloidosis and severe aortic stenosis are relatively common among the elderly Indian population, emphasising the role of regional genetic variations and demographic factors in informing diagnosis and treatment strategies.26)

Cardiovascular imaging modalities

Following a thorough physical examination and cardiac biomarkers assessment, the preferred, initial imaging modality often used in the diagnosis of cardiac amyloidosis is transthoracic echocardiogram (Figure 2) as it is widely available, cost-effective and relatively quick to perform.16,33) Combining findings of ‘red flags’ on physical examination and low-voltage ECG with increased left ventricular (LV) septal thickness of more than 12 mm on echocardiogram is particularly helpful in supporting the diagnosis of cardiac amyloidosis (Figure 3). Aside from increased LV wall thickness, other echocardiographic findings, such as a ‘cherry-on-top’ appearance on speckle tracking echocardiography of the left ventricle (Figure 4), suggestive of cardiac amyloidosis are listed in Table 2. When echocardiographic findings are equivocal for cardiac amyloidosis or of substandard quality, CMR imaging can be performed to supplement diagnostic efforts. CMR is useful as part of a comprehensive screening strategy to identify features suggestive of infiltrative heart disease, such as in cases of ATTR-CM, but it should be highlighted that it is not sufficient to confirm a diagnosis of the condition. Nevertheless, CMR has its own utility in the diagnostic pathway of cardiac amyloidosis and should always be considered if services are accessible, as agreed upon in our expert panel discussion. Tissue characterisation by CMR may also be advantageous in diagnosing cardiac amyloidosis and excluding other cardiac conditions that manifest as thick-walled cardiomyopathies, such as hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, Fabry disease and cardiac sarcoidosis, as well as conditions that commonly mimic heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, including constrictive pericarditis or myocarditis.3,33) Key CMR characterisation parameters, including native T1 mapping, extracellular volume (ECV), and late gadolinium enhancement (LGE), provide quantitative and qualitative insights into myocardial composition. Monoclonal protein assessment

A key step in the comprehensive assessment of cardiac amyloidosis and subsequent diagnosis of ATTR-CM involves excluding amyloid light chain (AL) amyloidosis.36) Serum free light chain (FLC) assays, alongside both serum and urine electrophoresis with immunofixation, allow for screening of possible plasma cell dyscrasia.28) Guidelines have established a range of expected values for the FLC kappa/lambda light chain ratio, stratified based on baseline renal function.6,33) Abnormal FLC ratio values and/or the presence of monoclonal protein on immunofixation electrophoresis strongly suggest a diagnosis of plasma cell disorder and warrant further investigation for AL amyloidosis, which must be excluded with a tissue biopsy.13) Our expert panel believes that, whenever feasible and accessible, all healthcare professionals should promptly request the relevant tests for monoclonal protein assessment at the first point of contact following clinical suspicion in their respective centres. This should then be followed by a timely referral to a tertiary cardiac centre with the expertise to manage suspected amyloidosis. Cardiac scintigraphy and single photon emission computed tomography (SPECT)

Cardiac scintigraphy scans utilising bone tracers, such as the aforementioned PYP, technetium-99m 3,3-diphosphono-1,2-propanodicarboxylic acid (DPD) and technetium-99m hydroxymethylene diphosphonate (HMDP), have emerged as useful imaging modalities in diagnosing ATTR-CM and in some circumstances, circumvent the need for tissue biopsies.28) Once monoclonal protein assessment is performed and results from the test are not suggestive of plasma cell dyscrasias, performing immediate PYP scintigraphy (which, at present, is the most readily accessible form of cardiac scintigraphy in Malaysia) is advocated. In fact, in the absence of AL amyloidosis, PYP scintigraphy offers a non-invasive method with nearly 100% specificity for diagnosing ATTR-CM and is undoubtedly preferred over invasive biopsies.27)

Interpretation of PYP scintigraphy (Figure 6), in the context of cardiac amyloidosis, can be divided into 2 major components: i) a quantitative evaluation of the heart-to-contralateral lung (H/CL) uptake ratio and ii) a semi-quantitative visual comparison of myocardial uptake relative to rib bone activity, known as the Perugini visual grading system (grade 0–3).37) According to the American Society of Nuclear Cardiology guidance, the standard protocol is the 1 hour H/CL ratio, where a value of ≥1.5 is considered positive. The 3-hour H/CL ratio (≥1.3) is optional and mainly applied when there is persistent blood pool activity at 1 hour.36) In the Perugini grading system, grades 2–3 are strongly suggestive of ATTR-CM, while grade 1 is considered equivocal and grade 0 is not suggestive of the disease. Accurate interpretation of both planar and SPECT images (Figure 7) remains crucial to confirm diffuse myocardial tracer uptake and to distinguish from blood pool activity, myocardial infarction, or overlapping bone uptake, all of which can potentially confound scan interpretation.36)

Tissue biopsy sampling

There has been increasing interest in diagnostic approaches for ATTR-CM, with international guidelines proposing alternative, non-invasive methodologies using cardiac scintigraphy as opposed to invasive endomyocardial biopsies.6,13,16,27) However, endomyocardial biopsy remains essential in a handful of cases, such as patients with positive monoclonal protein assessment or those with negative or equivocal PYP scintigraphy results (Perugini grade 0 or 1), but with persistent high clinical suspicion of cardiac ATTR-CM.28)

It is often believed that endomyocardial biopsies are rarely needed, because biopsies of surrogate sites are far more accessible, such as those from the abdominal fat pad and rectal tissue. However, the approach to tissue biopsy sampling differs substantially depending on the suspected amyloidosis subtype and clinical context. A recent scientific statement by the Heart Failure Society of America recommends close collaboration between cardiologists and haematologists in determining the most appropriate method for obtaining tissue samples, noting that endomyocardial biopsies may still be warranted if the results could potentially alter clinical management.38)

In cases where AL amyloidosis is suspected (Figure 8) or cannot be excluded based on monoclonal protein screening, 2 approaches to tissue biopsy are recommended: i) direct biopsy of the affected organs, or ii) initial sampling via bone marrow biopsy and/or abdominal fat pad aspirate, followed by direct biopsy of the affected organs if the initial results are negative.38) This sequential approach is particularly important where differentiation between AL amyloidosis and ATTR-CM is required or when urgent diagnosis of AL amyloidosis is needed to initiate timely treatment. By contrast, in patients with suspected ATTR-CM, non-invasive evaluation using nuclear scintigraphy combined with genetic testing can potentially eliminate the need for endomyocardial biopsy.38) When endomyocardial tissue is required for the diagnosis of cardiac amyloidosis, it is recommended to obtain at least four adequate tissue fragments per sample, each with less than 50% fibrous tissue, thrombus or artefacts.39)

Molecular genetic testing

The AHA guidelines and the ESC position statement recommend genetic sequencing of the TTR gene to differentiate between hereditary ATTR and wild-type ATTR.6,13) The varying prevalence of hereditary ATTR and wild-type ATTR in Asia highlights the importance of genetic testing in diagnosing ATTR-CM.16) Molecular genetic testing approaches include single gene testing of TTR, multigene panels that incorporate TTR and other genes of interest, and comprehensive genomic testing (i.e., whole exome sequencing and whole genome sequencing) for individuals presenting with atypical phenotypic features. In addition, pre-test genetic counselling by an accredited genetic counsellor should be a prerequisite to ensure that patients and their families fully understand the potential risks, benefits and implications of genetic testing, as well as the possible outcomes and available treatment options.40) Following the confirmation of hereditary ATTR-CM, post-test genetic counselling should be provided to guide family members regarding the implications of the diagnosis and the necessary steps for ongoing disease monitoring. Despite its high costs (Supplementary Figure 3), genetic testing should be conducted whenever feasible, following appropriate counselling on the potential psychosocial and economic implications, and with the patient’s informed consent.16)

FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Multidisciplinary network approach

In cases of diagnostic uncertainty, a multidisciplinary discussion is required to guide subtype-specific management and optimise the use of limited resources. Given that patients with amyloidosis are typically managed by multiple specialists simultaneously, it is imperative to have a designated primary specialist to coordinate care and ensure effective disease management.28) The expert panel recommends that cardiologists lead ATTR-CM management (with haematologists for AL amyloidosis), sharing responsibility with neurology where nervous system involvement predominates, based on clinical phenotype. Nonetheless, active engagement with other subspecialties and the formation of a therapeutic alliance among the various disciplines are advocated.

A coordinated network of healthcare providers across the country is vital for maintaining continuity of care, particularly when the attending physician relocates or is transferred to another state or region. Enhanced communication among specialists allows for comprehensive management, ultimately improving patient outcomes. Specifically, the expert panel endorses appointing cardiologists as case managers, supported by peripheral centres tasked with screening for ‘red flag’ signs and referring appropriate cases. This approach streamlines screening, reduces unnecessary investigations, and eases the burden on laboratories. Additionally, the panel supports training programmes for disease education, ensuring all specialities recognise red flags and adhere to the consensus diagnostic pathway. We are optimistic that the expert panel convened for this consensus statement will provide a strong foundation for establishing a coordinated network moving forward.

Early identification and timely referrals

With improved knowledge on the ‘red flags’ and the proposed diagnostic pathway for ATTR-CM, it is anticipated to enhance awareness among primary healthcare providers, refining timely referrals to tertiary centres for further diagnostic evaluation. In managing patients with high clinical suspicion of cardiac amyloidosis, healthcare providers in primary care, district or community settings should ideally be equipped to initiate key investigations, particularly monoclonal protein assessments, to help ensure timely diagnosis of AL amyloidosis. Patients with AL amyloidosis generally have a poorer prognosis than those with ATTR-CM, and timely treatment with chemotherapy or allogeneic stem cell transplantation can be lifesaving for the former. Early transthoracic echocardiography, where feasible, may help expedite referrals to tertiary cardiac centres equipped with the appropriate diagnostic tools, given that patients may face long waiting periods before obtaining cardiology appointments. Point-of-care ultrasonography in community settings offers a promising avenue to support earlier identification of cardiac amyloidosis; however, further evidence is needed to validate its routine use in Malaysia.

Considering that logistical challenges in rural healthcare settings may hinder timely care, clinicians in rural hospitals or district healthcare facilities should expeditiously consult cardiology services within their respective states or regions. This will facilitate detailed discussion of diagnostic suspicions and enable timely initiation of necessary investigations based on specialist input, thereby reducing delays in patient care. This proactive approach is preferred over deferring management to case managers at tertiary cardiac centres. However, the expert panel anticipates that cardiology services are inevitably expected to manage most of the referrals for suspected ATTR-CM, with further diagnostic testing including cardiac scintigraphy, conducted at tertiary centres once the patient has been transferred for continued follow-up. This strategy is expected to alleviate the heavy workload of primary healthcare providers within the public health system.

Addressing treatment availability and access

The therapeutic landscape for ATTR-CM has improved significantly in recent years, offering a more hopeful outlook for patient care. In the past, the absence of effective treatments contributed to a lack of motivation among clinicians in diagnosing the condition. The advent of contemporary disease-modifying therapies including TTR stabilisers and gene silencers, which halt disease progression, represents a significant step forward.1,31) These therapeutic advances not only reshape the treatment paradigm but also serve as a driving force for the early recognition and diagnosis of ATTR-CM. There is currently one disease-modifying therapy for ATTR-CM available in Malaysia.

With room for improvement in treatment access and availability, ongoing public and private sector efforts are focused on improving funding and access to care. Disease-modifying therapies, while promising, are often costly and may pose financial barriers to widespread use.28,31) Hence, expanding patient access to clinical trials through a well-coordinated network of healthcare providers offers an important avenue to enhance treatment options and patient outcomes, particularly in resource-limited settings.

Establishing local cardiac amyloidosis registry

Effective governance is the cornerstone of rare disease management, enabling the implementation of a comprehensive national strategy. Patient registries play a pivotal role in supporting this effort by enabling more systematic disease monitoring.52) Rare diseases, such as ATTR-CM, affect approximately 400 million people worldwide.53) Despite the identification of about 10,000 rare diseases, 90% lack Food and Drug Administration-approved treatments.53,54) Moreover, research efforts face substantial hurdles, including limited funding, knowledge gaps, disease heterogeneity and small, dispersed patient populations.54)

Since the listing of ATTR-CM in the Malaysian Rare Disease in 2023, establishing a local patient registry for cardiac amyloidosis (including ATTR-CM and other subtypes) is anticipated to foster collaboration among researchers, clinicians, and patients.55,56,57) This registry will serve as a standardised system that collects data for documentation of disease history, evaluation of treatment efficacy, monitoring of medication safety and improvement in quality of patient care.57) While a patient registry for ATTR-CM has been developed in Singapore, there remains a pressing need for similar initiatives across other Asian countries.58) The introduction of such registries would facilitate the sharing of patient data, enabling more comprehensive research on ATTR-CM across the diverse and expansive Asian population.

CONCLUSION

The diagnosis of ATTR-CM is often delayed or overlooked due to the knowledge and awareness gaps among clinicians, lack of coordinated multidisciplinary care and limitations in diagnostic resources. By recognising the ‘red flags’ associated with ATTR-CM, physicians can initiate cardiac investigations in a timely manner and refer patients with suspected ATTR-CM to tertiary centres for further confirmatory evaluations. Early diagnosis of ATTR-CM has now become achievable with the advancement of non-invasive imaging modalities, coupled with monoclonal protein tests, biopsy assessments and genetic testing. Collaboration between various healthcare disciplines is crucial to expedite the diagnosis of ATTR-CM and consequently enhance the management of these patients, potentially improving their survival rates and quality of life. Cardiologists as case managers for patients with ATTR-CM play a major role in coordinating care among multidisciplinary specialists. The establishment of a cardiac amyloidosis registry in Malaysia would promote collaboration through data-sharing with other Asian counterparts and facilitate more research initiatives that, in turn, would contribute to more timely diagnosis to improve patient outcomes.
