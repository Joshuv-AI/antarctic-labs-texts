Relationship between size-specific dose estimates and image quality in coronary computed tomography angiography depending on tube voltage and scan field of view
Authors: Yihan Fan, Qingting Sun, Jing Wang, Tian Qin, Baohui Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdvances in computed tomography (CT) technology have driven rapid development in cardiac applications, and achieving a balance between image quality and radiation dose has become a current research focus. This study aimed to quantitatively evaluate the effects of tube voltage and scan field of view (SFOV) selection on image quality and radiation dose in coronary CT angiography (CCTA), and analyze gender differences, thereby facilitating the development of personalized scanning protocols for patients.MethodsA total of 120 patients who underwent CCTA examinations on a 256-row CT scanner were retrospectively included and divided into eight subgroups based on tube voltage, SFOV, and gender. Patients’ axial images were collected, with volume CT dose index (CTDIvol) values extracted from dose reports. Axial size-specific dose estimation (SSDEz) of the patients was calculated based on axial CT images or CT localizer radiographs, and the region of interest (ROI) was placed on the target sections of vessels to determine objective evaluation of image quality. First, we analyzed the differences in radiation dose metrics (CTDIvol, SSDEz) between genders under varying tube voltages (120 and 100 kV) and SFOV (large SFOV and small SFOV) settings. Second, we explored the impact of these parameter settings on objective image quality. Finally, we used linear regression fitting to evaluate the correlation between radiation dose metrics and objective image quality indicators.ResultsUnder different tube voltage and SFOV settings, both CTDIvol and SSDEz were higher in female patients than in males. The difference in the latter metric was statistically significant, with P values of 0.031 and 0.047, respectively. SSDEz in different tube voltage groups and SFOV groups were 29% and 49% higher than CTDIvol. A parameter choice of 100 kV and small SFOV generally showed better image quality, and the correlation between SSDEz and signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) was stronger.ConclusionsDue to differences in tissue structure, female patients receive a higher radiation dose during CCTA. The CTDIvol underestimates the patient dose, and it is recommended to use the SSDEz assessment. On the premise of ensuring no omission of lesions, it is advisable to use parameter scanning with 100 kV and small SFOV for superior image quality.

Introduction

Coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) is recognized as a noninvasive diagnostic tool for suspected or known coronary artery disease (CAD). High-end computed tomography (CT) scanners combined with prospective electrocardiogram-gating (ECG-gating) enable coronary imaging in patients of virtually all ages (1,2). During CT scanning, the adjustment of scanning parameters significantly influences both the radiation dose delivered to the patient and the quality of diagnostic images. Guided by the as low as reasonably achievable (ALARA) principle, minimizing radiation dose while ensuring diagnostic reliability has become a focal point of contemporary research. Since the radiation dose is proportional to the nth power of the tube voltage, reducing the tube voltage stands out as the most effective strategy for radiation dose optimization. In a prospective study of 240 CCTA patients, Lin et al. (3) assessed how different tube voltages (70, 80, 100, and 120 kV) affected image quality, radiation dose, and DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) in lymphocytes. It was found that 100 kV tube voltage may be optimal for CCTA when weighed against the above three factors. Furthermore, in a retrospective analysis of 53 patients scanned on a 256-slice CT scanner, Muenzel et al. (4) found a positive correlation between the scan field of view (SFOV) and radiation dose. Their results demonstrate that precise SFOV adjustment can effectively reduce radiation exposure.

Current assessments of radiation dose to CT patients predominantly rely on displayed volume CT dose index (CTDIvol); however, this metric does not account for differences in patient size and tissue attenuation, and reflects the radiation output of the CT scanner at a reference phantom. To address this limitation, American Association of Physicists in Medicine (AAPM) Task Groups 204 and 220 (5,6) proposed the concept of size-specific dose estimation (SSDE), which is the assessment of patient dose by multiplying the CTDIvol by a conversion factor (f). The report specifies that the calculation of the f requires the water-equivalent diameter (Dw), which can be derived from either axial CT images or CT localizer radiographs. The former method provides a cross-sectional map of tissue attenuation, making it a highly accurate method for Dw estimation. However, Dw can also be measured from CT localizer radiographs (7,8). This approach is useful when axial images are incompletely reconstructed or when patient attenuation needs to be analyzed before the scan.

The objective of this study was to quantify and compare the relationships between objective image quality and radiation dose under different tube voltages and SFOV settings—to establish individualized CT scanning protocols. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2025-2065/rc).

Methods

Study design and population

A total of 120 patients who underwent CCTA at The First Affiliated Hospital of Bengbu Medical University between August 2024 and January 2025 were retrospectively enrolled in this study. The inclusion criteria were as follows: sinus rhythm with heart rates ≤75 bpm (9) (to eliminate the impact of high heart rate and arrhythmia on image quality and radiation dose), ability to cooperate with respiratory training, and absence of severe cardiac, hepatic, or renal dysfunction or a history of iodine allergy. To quantitatively assess the impact of tube voltage on objective image quality and dose, tube voltage was stratified into four subgroups (20 patients per group) under a large SFOV: 120 kV [male (M)/female (F) groups], 100 kV (M/F groups); to evaluate the effect of SFOV, additional subgroups were defined at 100 kV: small SFOV (M/F groups), large SFOV (M/F groups). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by institutional ethics board of Bengbu Medical University (No. 2025-508) and informed consent was taken from all the patients.

CCTA scanning protocol

CCTA was performed in end-inspiratory breath-hold with heart rate monitoring by a prospective ECG-gating sequence on the 256-row CT scanner (Revolution CT, GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA). The gantry rotation time was 0.28 s, the X-ray beam collimation was 160 mm, and the kV Assist and SmartmA technologies were enabled to automatically select the tube voltage and tube current. Patients were placed in the supine position with the foot advanced and the hands raised above their head. The scanning range was from the tracheal bifurcation to below the diaphragm.

The contrast agent injection protocol was performed in 3 sequential phases via the cubital vein. First, a 20-mL saline injection was administered at a flow rate of 5.2 mL/s. This was immediately followed by the injection of iodixanol (350 mgI/mL) at a rate of 5.2–5.4 mL/s. The volume of contrast medium was weight-adjusted: a fixed dose of 60 mL was used for patients weighing ≤60 kg. For patients >60 kg, the dose was calculated as 60 mL plus 1.0 mL for each additional kilogram of body weight. Finally, a 40-mL saline injection was administered at the equivalent rate to complete the protocol. Automated bolus triggering was performed to determine the delay time of the arterial phase. The region of interest (ROI) was placed in the ascending aortic with a trigger threshold of 120 Hounsfield units (HU). At the end of the scan, the optimal phase was automatically reconstructed using SmartPhase technology (https://bio.tools/smartphase).

Quantitative image quality analysis

Objective image quality parameters such as signal intensity, image noise (N), signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), and contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) of four vessels [aortic root (AR), right coronary artery (RCA), left anterior descending (LAD), and left circumflex (LCX)] were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). For each patient, the mean attenuation (representing signal intensity) and standard deviation (representing N) were determined by plotting the largest ROI (area of 13.8–14.5 mm2) by AR at the level of the left main coronary artery (10). On axial images, two circular ROIs were randomly placed along each target vessel (RCA, LAD, and LCX) for quantitative image quality assessment. The area of each ROI was limited to 2.3–4.6 mm2. Care was taken to avoid regions with calcifications, plaques, vessel walls, or other artifacts. Finally, ROIs were placed in adjacent perivascular tissue to measure vascular contrast. SNR and CNR were measured as follows: SNR = CTinternal vessel/N, CNR = (CTinternal vessel − CTperivascular tissue)/N, for each target section, the average of two measurements was taken as the final result.

Estimation of radiation dose

CTDIvol from the dose report and the axial SSDE (SSDEz) that takes into account the patient’s body size were used. For scans using the “Cardiac Large” (large SFOV) protocol, the display FOV (DFOV) was set to its maximum (50 cm) during image reconstruction. This ensured that the entire patient cross section, including all body tissues, was presented in the final image. With the “Cardiac Small” (small SFOV) protocol, the DFOV is limited to a maximum of 32 cm. Consequently, it is not possible to reconstruct a section image that includes the entire thorax. In this case, Dw should be calculated through the localizer radiograph.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 27.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). All quantitative variables related to image quality were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. First, it was determined whether the above-mentioned quantitative parameters were normally distributed and variances were equal. Then, independent sample t-tests were performed on the data to compare the variation across scanning protocols. P values less than 0.05 were considered indicative of a significant difference. Last, the Pearson correlation test was used to analyze the correlation between objective image quality indicators and radiation dose metrics, and linear fitting was performed to determine the correlation coefficient.

Discussion

Continued advances in multi-detector row CT have facilitated rapid developments in cardiac applications, including coronary artery calcium score and CCTA (12). Despite these advances, the radiation dose involved in CCTA remains a significant public health concern (13,14). As a CT dose indicator that considers the differences in body size and tissue attenuation, SSDE has a greater value in patient dose evaluation. SSDE is the product of CTDIvol and f, and the measurement of f requires the Dw, provided that the complete section image is reconstructed. This study investigated the radiation dose in patients undergoing CCTA with a small SFOV. The limited size of the DFOV precludes reconstruction of the entire thoracic cross section. Consequently, current work involved the calculation of Dw using the CT localizer radiographs. In the study of Anam et al. (15), a body CTDI phantom was used to establish a relationship between the pixel value on CT localizer radiographs and Tw across two CT scanners. They then validated the results using an anthropomorphic phantom and 30 patients. It was found that the Dw errors calculated from the CT localizer radiograph and axial image were only 5.4%±4.2% and 2.3%±3.2%, thus confirming the feasibility of this method.

After obtaining the transformation relationship between the two methods (Figure 3), the differences in radiation dose indicators (CTDIvol, SSDEz) between genders at different tube voltage and SFOV were compared, and the results are shown in Figure 4. The SSDEz of the F group was higher than that of the M group, and the difference was statistically significant. In addition, the CTDIvol indicators underestimated the patient dose to varying degrees. Kidoh et al. (16) investigated the validity of CTDIvol for patient dose assessment in a study of 37 CCTA patients. They measured skin dose using a metal oxide semiconductor field effect transistor (MOSFET) system placed on the anterior chest and analyzed its correlation with both CTDIvol and SSDE. Their key findings were as follows: (I) SSDE correlated more strongly with skin dose; and (II) the skin dose to SSDE ratio was independent of patient body mass index (BMI). This suggests that the indicator excludes the estimation errors caused by BMI and serves as a potentially better estimator of the organ dose. In contrast, CTDIvol underestimated the actual radiation dose by 34%. In our study, the SSDEz in different tube voltage and SFOV groups were 29% and 49% higher than CTDIvol, respectively, confirming the inadequacy of the CTDIvol in patient dose characterization. Although the breast is considered a non-target organ during CCTA scanning, it is usually included within the z-axis range of scanning. The automated tube voltage and tube current selection technology employed in this study is determined based on the patients’ attenuation from the localizer radiographs. In this study, the Dw values at the center slice for females and males at different tube voltages were 28.48 and 27.15, respectively. At different SFOV settings, the Dw values for females and males were 25.35 and 24.16, respectively. Thus, to achieve comparable image quality at the same tube voltage, a higher tube current setting is required, resulting in higher radiation doses received by female examinees. Several studies (17,18) have also indicated that patients with larger body sizes tend to receive higher radiation doses during CT scans. Moreover, the resting heart rate of the female examinees is higher than that of males, and in prospective axial scanning mode, there is a positive correlation between heart rate and radiation dose (19,20).

In CT contrast studies, image quality primarily relies on the contrast between enhanced and nonenhanced regions. This can be achieved by either sufficient amounts of contrast agent or the enhanced photoelectric phenomenon at low tube voltages, which increases the difference in X-ray attenuation between tissues and contrast agents. Specifically, the X-ray output energy approaches the iodine k-edge of 33 keV, resulting in a significant increase in X-ray absorption by iodine atoms (21). Low-energy photons are typically associated with elevated image noise, attributable to attenuated photon penetration and reduced photon numbers reaching the detector. Nevertheless, as indicated in Table 1, target blood vessels at 100 kV achieved superior SNR and CT values due to the corresponding increase in tube current for compensation. Table 2 demonstrates that small SFOV generally provides better objective image quality. Since pixel size is determined by the ratio of FOV to matrix, the smaller pixels in the case of a reduced FOV lead to higher image sharpness and spatial resolution. Small SFOV scanning protocols mainly consider the heart as the organ of interest, whereas the lungs, pleura, and mediastinum are not entirely included. For patients who need to examine the heart and the entire chest, a choice of large SFOV (300–500 mm) is available for simultaneous evaluation of the coronary arteries, pulmonary arteries, and thoracic aorta in chest pain protocols (22). A recent study (23) demonstrated that up to 24.4% of individuals undergoing CCTA exhibit extracardiac findings, of whom 63.1% needed further observation. The proportion of imaging pulmonary parenchyma to total lung volume varies with the SFOV, and pulmonary nodule detection in CCTA also remains a particular interest. Evaluation of lung nodules (24) may reduce the number of follow-up CT scans required for these patients, without the risk of missing malignant tumors during screening. In addition, it is of great significance for screening non-cardiac causes of chest pain. Therefore, a large SFOV exhibits advantages in the comprehensive evaluation of thoracic lesions; on the premise of ensuring no missed lesions, it is recommended to use small SFOV to improve the overall clarity of images.

Figures 5,6 demonstrate a proportional relationship between CT image quality and radiation dose across different scanning protocols, which is in agreement with the findings reported by Waaijer et al. (25). Through combined phantom experiments, they prospectively evaluated the impact of lower tube voltage and various effective tube currents on image quality for computed tomography angiography (CTA) of the circle of Willis. In the phantom, SNR2 was extremely proportional to effective tube currents and CTDIvol. With identical CTDIvol, lower tube voltages yielded superior SNR. It is concluded that CTA of the circle of Willis is recommended for imaging at 90 kV combined with appropriate tube current adjustment. Further, Kawashima et al. (26) conducted phantom studies [using GE’s LightSpeed VCT scanner and Siemens’ SOMATOM Definition Flash CT scanner (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany)] and found that the relationship between image quality and radiation dose is highly dependent on patient size, which is consistent with our results. Specifically, SSDEz and CTDIvol were respectively correlated with SNR and CNR using Pearson correlation analysis in our study. Tables 3,4 confirm significantly stronger correlations of SSDEz with both SNR and CNR compared to CTDIvol. This discrepancy is attributed to the reality that CTDIvol merely reflects the scanner output dose for a standardized CTDI phantom and is highly sensitive to scanning parameter variations. The actual patient dose not only depends on scanner output (CTDIvol), but is also related to patient dimension and tissue composition at the scanned region (27). The Dw-based SSDE overcomes these limitations and is recommended as a more reliable metric for radiation dose evaluation. Given their high correlation, clinical protocols should adjust CT scanning parameters according to patient body sizes to achieve comparable image quality.

Our study has some limitations. First, our image quality analysis was limited to objective metrics. It did not include a comprehensive evaluation incorporating subjective parameters, which is an area for future refinement; second, the Dw calculated using axial CT images or CT localizer radiographs cannot be completely consistent, resulting in certain error in the SSDEz evaluation; last, as a single-center study, the sample size for each subgroup was not sufficient, and all results were obtained using the GE Revolution CT scanner. Further verification is needed to confirm the applicability of these conclusions to other scanner types. Future research will focus on: (I) the comprehensive evaluation combining subjective image quality; (II) implementation of unified Dw measurement methods to improve consistency; and (III) multicenter, multi-device studies with larger sample sizes.

Conclusions

In prospective ECG-gating CCTA using GE Revolution CT scanner, AR and the main coronary artery branches generally exhibit superior image quality at 100 kV and small SFOV. Given the linear relationship and strong correlation between SSDE and objective image quality indicators, it is recommended that scanning parameters should be set in combination with the body size in clinical practice.
