Effect of different 10-Methacryloyloxydecyl Dihydrogen Phosphate concentrations on the shear bond strength between zirconia and composite resin
Authors: Kankasit Tiyaprijaya, Sasipin Lauvahutanon, Yosnarong Sirimethawong, Patcharanun Chaiamornsup, Pornpot Jiangkongkho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundZirconia ceramics are widely used in restorative dentistry for their mechanical strength, but their lack of a glassy phase makes them resistant to conventional acid etching. While 10-methacryloyloxydecyl dihydrogen phosphate (10-MDP) is utilized to facilitate chemical bonding, the influence of monomer concentration on molecular coordination at the interface remains unclear. This study evaluated the effect of varying 10-MDP concentrations on shear bond strength (SBS) and investigated the underlying chemical bonding mechanisms.MethodsSpecimens were randomly divided into nine groups (n = 6): seven experimental groups treated with varying concentrations of experimental MDP solutions (2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 14% v/v in ethanol), a commercial MDP primer positive control (CMP; Z-Prime™ Plus), and a non-MDP primer-treated negative control (NMP). Zirconia surfaces were treated with these MDP solutions prior to bonding with flowable composite resin. Shear bond strength was measured using a universal testing machine. Chemical coordination modes were analyzed using 31P Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) spectroscopy and Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) to correlate molecular configurations with SBS data.ResultsShear bond strength was significantly influenced by MDP concentration, increasing from 2% (7.1 ± 0.4 MPa) to a peak at 10% (11.3 ± 0.8 MPa), followed by a significant decline at 12% (7.6 ± 0.7 MPa) and 14% (7.7 ± 0.8 MPa).31P NMR analysis revealed that these variations correspond to five distinct bonding configurations (S1–S5). The superior performance of the 10% concentration was associated with the highest proportion of the S2 configuration, representing ionically bonded bidentate complexes. In contrast, the reduction in SBS at higher concentrations coincided with a decrease in S2 intensity and the emergence of S3 (bridging) and S4/S5 (phosphate oligomers).ConclusionsBond strength is dictated by the distribution of MDP coordination modes rather than total presence. A 10% concentration is the ideal threshold for maximizing strong S2 ionic bonding. Exceeding this limit promotes non-productive phosphate multilayers that compromise interface stability.

Background

The evolution of dental materials has undergone a significant transformation from metals to advanced ceramics. For over three decades, zirconia has been established as a viable material for dental prostheses, offering excellent mechanical properties, high biocompatibility [1], and an aesthetic appearance. Its compatibility with computer-aided design and computer-aided manufacturing (CAD/CAM) technology further ensures efficiency within the digital workflow [2]. Due to zirconia’s dense polycrystalline structure, lack of a glassy phase [1], and inert nature, surface preparation techniques typically involve a combination of mechanical and chemical treatments [3]. The most commonly used mechanical surface pretreatment is air abrasion with alumina particles (Aluminum oxide, Al₂O₃), also known as grit-blasting [3, 4]. This process increases surface roughness and creates micro-mechanical retention, which is essential for establishing reliable adhesion and maximizing the resulting bond strength [3, 5]. Minimum acceptable clinical shear bond strength is typically established between 10 and 13 MPa [6]. Achieving these values necessitates specialized surface conditioning and the use of chemical adhesion promoters [3, 6].

Establishing durable adhesion to the zirconia surface through stable chemical interactions remains a significant challenge. Traditional acid-etching techniques are ineffective for surface preparation before adhesive cementation. As a result, establishing a strong chemical bond between zirconia and dental cement during permanent cementation is difficult. One effective approach involves the use of primers containing 10-MDP [3, 6], which has shown promising results in enhancing the adhesive strength between zirconia and resin cement [7].

The 10-MDP (10-methacryloyloxydecyl dihydrogen phosphate) molecule is an amphiphilic functional monomer characterized by three distinct structural components that dictate its adhesive capabilities. At one terminus, the polymerizable methacrylate group enables the monomer to co-polymerize with the resin matrix, ensuring secure integration into the restorative composite or cement [3]. This group is connected to a central hydrophobic spacer, a 10-carbon (decyl) aliphatic chain, which is the structural backbone of the molecule [8]. Finally, at the opposite terminus lies the hydrophilic dihydrogen phosphate group, which serves as the primary reactive site responsible for direct chemical adhesion to the inorganic substrate [3, 8, 9]. The dihydrogen phosphate group forms strong, stable bonds with metal oxides, such as the zirconium in zirconia or the calcium in teeth [9, 10]. This reaction creates water-insoluble salts that act as a chemical anchor, giving 10-MDP superior and long-lasting bond strength [11]. This rapid salt formation also improves biocompatibility by preventing the monomer from leaking into surrounding tissues [10]. Kim EC et al. [12] reported on the cytotoxicity of 10-MDP to dental pulp, noting that it has the potential to induce inflammation or interfere with the natural repair and mineralization processes of dental pulp cells when applied in close proximity to the vital inner tissues of the tooth.

Nagaoka et al. [9] reported that 10-MDP can chemically interact with the zirconia surface, forming hydrogen and ionic bonds. This interaction enhances the bond strength at the zirconia–resin interface. Therefore, the use of a primer containing 10-MDP prior to the application of the adhesive resin improves the bond strength between zirconia and resin cement [6]. This improvement is attributed to the chemical bonding at the interface, which results in significantly higher bond strength compared to using the adhesive resin alone [2, 6, 13].

Establishing a durable resin-zirconia bond requires physical modification, such as silica-coating or selective infiltration etching (SIE), while SIE provides a highly retentive surface that significantly boosts initial bond strengths, the resulting interface remains vulnerable to hydrolytic degradation during long-term water storage [1, 14, 15]. These physical treatments are typically followed by acid-induced chemical hydroxylation to create a reactive surface where specialized chemical primers and silane blends subsequently bridge the zirconia-resin interface by forming stable siloxane networks [16]. Clinically, combining organophosphate primers with silanes yields superior bond strengths (> 25 MPa), whereas methacrylate-only systems fail to meet acceptable standards [6]. However, in this study, the authors focused on grit-blasting followed by the application of a 10-MDP primer and adhesive resin as the primary surface pretreatment protocol.

There are various techniques used to investigate chemical bonding. Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) spectroscopy provides information on the chemical environment of atoms and detects chemical shifts and splitting patterns, which are useful for investigating the type and nature of chemical bonding [17, 18].

The NMR spectroscopy was used to investigate and confirm the chemical interaction mechanism of MDP with zirconia. Significant chemical shifts observed in the ³¹P NMR spectra indicated that the MDP monomer interacts with the hydrated surface layer of zirconia particles. The identified chemical interactions included hydrogen bonding, formed between the P–OH groups and either zirconium dioxide or another phosphate group, and ionic bonding between the deprotonated P–O⁻ anions and the positively charged Zr⁴⁺ ions [9].

Another factor that affects bond strength is the concentration of MDP in the primer agent [19]. The higher the number of free phosphate groups in the MDP solution, the greater the potential for chemical interaction. Therefore, the concentration of MDP is directly related to the bond strength achieved [20]. Several studies have investigated the effect of varying MDP concentrations to determine the optimal level for maximizing the bond strength between zirconia and resin cement. Previous study reported that a primer containing 4.0 wt% MDP resulted in the highest tensile bond strength compared to other concentrations tested (0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 3.0, and 5.0 wt%) as well as a commercial ceramic primer containing both MDP and a silane coupling agent [21]. Another study found that the shear bond strength between zirconia and methacrylate resin showed no significant differences among experimental primer groups containing 10, 15, 20, and 30 wt% MDP. However, all of these groups demonstrated significantly higher shear bond strength compared to the group with 5 wt% MDP [22].

MDP primer appears to be a crucial factor for the chemical bonding interaction between zirconia and resin cement [23]. However, the optimal concentration of MDP in primer for achieving the highest bond strength remains unclear. Although previous research has established the general efficacy of 10-MDP for zirconia bonding, most studies have focused on clinical outcomes without detailing the underlying molecular shifts across a wide concentration gradient. This study advances current knowledge by utilizing 31P NMR analysis to correlate bond strength with chemical coordination and aims to enhance the understanding of the optimal MDP concentration that influences the shear bond strength between zirconia and composite resin. The null hypothesis (H0) was that there would be no significant difference on the various concentration of MDP on the shear bond strength between zirconia and the composite resin.

Preparation of zirconia specimens

The specimen template was designed using Microsoft 3D Builder (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA). Zirconia specimens—cylindrical in shape with a 10.0 mm diameter and 3.0 mm thickness—were fabricated from zirconia discs (KATANA™ Zirconia UTML, Kuraray Noritake Dental, Okayama, Japan) using a dry milling machine (DGSHAPE DWX-52D Plus, Roland DG Corporation, Irvine, CA, USA). Following the milling process, the specimens were sintered in a high-temperature furnace (VITA ZYRCOMAT® 6100 MS, VITA Zahnfabrik, Yorba Linda, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s recommended protocol.

All zirconia specimens were placed into cylindrical polyvinylchloride molds (20.0 mm diameter and 15.0 mm height) and embedded in auto-polymerizing acrylic resin (Instant Tray Mix, Lang Dental Manufacturing Company, Wheeling, IL, USA). The specimens were then grit-blasted with 110 μm alumina particles at 2 bar pressure for 10 s, maintained at a distance of 10.0 mm and an angle of 90 degrees [24]. Subsequently, the specimens were cleaned in an ultrasonic cleaner (SONOREX SUPER 10 P DK 255 P, BANDELIN electronic GmbH & Co. KG, Berlin, Germany) for 5 min.

Surface roughness (Sa) was characterized using an atomic force microscope (Nanosurf C3000). To ensure a comprehensive analysis, a scan area of 50 μm x 50 μm was utilized with a resolution of 256 pixels per line and a scan rate of 1.282 Hz. To ensure a uniform baseline, Sa values were compared across all groups using a one-way ANOVA. No significant differences were found (p > 0.27), confirming that the surface topography was consistent across all specimens prior to bonding.

The bonding area was defined at the center of each specimen as a circle with a 5.0 mm diameter. To isolate the surface pretreatment area, double-sided adhesive tape (Scotch® Professional Grade Vinyl Electrical Tape Super 88, 3 M ESPE, MN, USA) with a pre-drilled 5.0 mm diameter hole was applied to the specimens. The area exposed through the hole served as the designated surface for treatment and subsequent bonding.

Sample size and grouping

The sample size was determined using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.2; Franz Faul, Universität Kiel, Germany) based on a preliminary pilot study conducted by the authors. With an effect size f of 0.89, a power (1 - β error probability) of 0.95 and an α error probability of 0.05, the calculation indicated a minimum sample size of 5; however, to ensure statistical power and reliability, a sample size of 6 specimens per group was selected for this study.

The zirconia specimens were randomly divided into nine groups (n = 6 per group). Seven experimental groups were established based on varying MDP concentrations (2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 14% v/v). A commercial MDP primer (CMP) treated group served as the positive control, while a non-MDP primer (NMP) treated group served as the negative control.

Preparation of experimental MDP

Various concentrations of experimental MDP were prepared by diluting MDP monomer (PCM Products GmbH, Krefeld, Germany) to 2%, 4%, 6%, 8%, 10%, 12%, and 14%v/v in ethanol 99.9%v/v (ETH; RCI Labscan, RCI Labscan Ltd., Bangkok, Thailand) [25]. The dilutions were performed using a micropipette (PIPETMAN®, Gilson Inc., Middleton, WI, USA) and homogenized using a vortex mixer (VX100, GIBTHAI Co., Ltd., Bangkok, Thailand) for 10 min. Surface pretreatment

The zirconia specimens were rinsed with deionized water for 15 s and air-dried using a triple syringe.

For the experimental MDP groups, the 25 µL of each MDP concentration was applied to the bonding area using a micropipette (Pipetman® Classic, Gilson Inc., Middleton, WI, USA) and actively agitated with a micro-brush. This was followed by air-drying for 30 s until the surface was completely dry at room temperature.

For the CMP group, a commercial zirconia primer (Z-Prime™ Plus, Bisco, Schaumburg, IL, USA) was applied to the specimen surfaces following the manufacturer’s instructions. According to the manufacturer’s Safety Data Sheet (SDS), this primer contains a dual-monomer system consisting of 10-MDP (1–5 wt%) and BPDM in an ethanol solvent.

The NMP group did not apply the MDP primer.

Adhesive resin

The adhesive resin (Adper™ Single Bond 2, 3 M ESPE, St. Paul, MN, USA) was applied to the bonding area of the zirconia specimens and air-dried to create a thin, uniform film. The adhesive layer was then polymerized using an LED light-curing unit (Elipar™ S10, 3 M ESPE; light intensity: 1,200 mW/cm²) for 10 s, in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Composite resin

The flowable composite resin (Filtek™ Supreme Ultra Flowable Restorative, 3 M ESPE, St. Paul, MN, USA) was injected into a soft transparent polyvinylchloride mold (5.0 mm in diameter and 3.0 mm in height) positioned over the treated bonding area. The mold was attached to another sticky side of the adhesive tape. The specimen was irradiated for 20 s from the top surface and 20 s from each of the four peripheral sides. During the curing process, the light-guide tip was maintained at a minimal distance (< 0.5 mm) from the mold surface to maximize radiant exposure while avoiding physical contact that might cause accidental displacement of the specimen.

Thermocycling condition

All zirconia specimens were stored in distilled water at 37 °C for 24 h prior to thermocycling. Aging was performed in deionized water baths at 5 °C and 55 °C with a dwell time of 30 s for 5,000 cycles, using the thermocycling machine (Thermocycler THE 1200, SD Mechatronik GmbH, Bavaria, Germany) [25, 26].

Shear bond strength test

The shear bond strength (SBS) test was performed using a universal testing machine (ElectroPlus™ E1000, Instron®, Norwood, MA, USA) at a crosshead speed of 0.5 mm/min. The specimens were positioned in a specialized shear bond jig to align a chisel-shaped rod from the load cell parallel to the bonding interface. Part II: Fracture surface topography by field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) and elemental analysis

Surface characterization

Following the shear bond strength test, the fractured surfaces of the zirconia specimens were coated with a thin layer of gold under vacuum using a sputter coater (SPI Sputter Coater, SPI Supplies, West Chester, PA, USA). The surface morphology was examined using a field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM; Apreo S, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) operated at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV. SEM micrographs were obtained at 500× magnification to evaluate the topographical features of the fracture surfaces.

Elemental analysis

Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS; ULTIM MAX 65, Oxford Instruments, Abingdon, Oxfordshire, UK) was performed in conjunction with FESEM analysis to verify the presence of MDP on the treated surfaces. The analysis served to identify the elemental composition of the specimen surfaces. The primary electron beam energy was operated at 20 kV for each specimen. Three distinct areas (each measuring 0.25 mm × 0.18 mm) at the center of each specimen were examined. The NMP surface was analyzed as a reference.

EDS analysis was employed to identify zirconium (Zr), oxygen (O), and phosphorus (P) on the MDP-treated zirconia surfaces. While zirconium and oxygen were detected on both the NMP and MDP-treated surfaces, phosphorus was detected exclusively on the MDP-treated surfaces. This indicates the presence of phosphate groups from the experimental MDP primer, confirming successful surface pretreatment prior to bonding with the flowable composite resin.

Part III: Molecular analysis

NMR spectroscopy

The chemical bonding mechanism between MDP and zirconia was analyzed using solid-state NMR. The sample consisted of a mixture of zirconium dioxide (ZrO2) powder and an experimental MDP solution. The representative of each subgroup which exhibited statistically significant differences in bond strength were selected for characterization via NMR spectroscopy.

The ZrO2 powder was prepared using a milling machine (DGSHAPE DWX-52D Plus, Roland DG Corporation, Irvine, CA, USA) and subsequently filtered through a 190 μm mesh filter (Gerson Elite™ Paint Strainers, The Gerson Companies, Olathe, KS, USA). The particle size distribution and surface area of the powder were determined using a laser particle size distribution analyzer (Partica LA-960V2, HORIBA Advanced Techno Co., Ltd., Fukuoka, Japan) and an automated gas sorption analyzer (BET method, Anton Paar Ltd., Bangkok, Thailand), respectively. The optimal volume of the MDP solution applied to the bonding area (circular shape; mean diameter = 4.79 mm; mean area = 18.01 mm2) was 1.4 µL, resulting in a ratio of 0.077 µL/mm2 of MDP solution to surface area.

Based on the laser particle size distribution and automated gas sorption analyses, the zirconium dioxide powder exhibited a specific surface area of 132.2 m2/kg (0.132 m2/g) and a mean particle diameter of approximately 322.8 nm. For a 0.5 g sample of ZrO2 powder, the optimal volume of the experimental MDP solution was calculated to be 5.082 mL.

A 0.5 g portion of ZrO2 powder was mixed into 5.0 mL of the 2, 4, 10, and 14% v/v MDP solutions. Each mixture was subjected to ultrasonic agitation for 5 min, followed by static incubation for 20 min to allow for the complete evaporation of the ethanol solvent. This process yielded dry ZrO2 powder coated with different MDP concentrations. Untreated ZrO2 powder was prepared separately to serve as a reference.

NMR experiments were conducted using a 400 MHz Fourier Transform Nuclear Magnetic Resonance Spectrometer (FT-NMR; AVANCE III HD/Ascend 400 WB, Bruker Scientific LLC, Billerica, MA, USA). The ZrO2 powder and MDP mixtures were examined via solid-state NMR spectroscopy using a 4.0 mm zirconia rotor with a spinning frequency of 10 kHz.

Direct polarization 31P NMR spectra were acquired at 162.2 MHz with a pulse length of 3.3 µs (pulse angle of π/2) and a recycle delay of 5 s. Approximately 1,760 pulses were accumulated, using phosphoric acid (H3PO4) as an external reference. High-power decoupling was applied during 31P acquisition.

1H NMR spectra were acquired at 400.6 MHz with a pulse length of 3.0 µs (pulse angle of π/4) and a recycle delay of 3 s. Approximately four pulses were accumulated, using adamantane (C10H16) as an external reference. For comparison, the 1H NMR spectrum of the untreated ZrO2 powder and the 31P NMR spectra of all MDP-treated samples were also recorded.

Deconvolution was applied to the 31P NMR spectra to resolve overlapping peaks and ensure accurate chemical shift assignments. Phosphorus nuclei often reside in multiple chemical environments, resulting in closely spaced signals that manifest as broad or merged peaks. Such peaks can be difficult to interpret directly; however, deconvolution allows these signals to be resolved into individual components. This mathematical approach facilitates the identification of distinct phosphorus species, enables reliable quantitative analysis, and provides a more precise determination of chemical shifts and line shapes.

The molecular structure of the MDP solution was analyzed using liquid-state NMR spectroscopy. 31P NMR spectra were acquired using a 600 MHz Fourier Transform Nuclear Magnetic Resonance Spectrometer (AVANCE NEO Cryoprobe, Bruker Scientific LLC, Billerica, MA, USA) operating at 25 °C. For the 31P measurements, the resonance frequency was 242.93 MHz, with the scan and repetition times set to 10 s. All chemical shifts were referenced to an external phosphoric acid (H3PO4) solution. The 10% MDP solution was prepared following the same methodology described in Part I.

Statistical analysis

The mean shear bond strength across the different experimental MDP concentrations was analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test (SPSS version 20, IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Modes of failure were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test. The significance level was set at α = 0.05.

Discussion

Based on the results of this study, all groups treated with the MDP primer showed significantly higher shear bond strength compared to the NMP group (p < 0.001). Therefore, the null hypothesis which had no significant difference on the difference concentrations of MDP on shear bond strength between zirconia and composite resin was rejected. These findings suggest that MDP plays a crucial role in enhancing the bond between zirconia and composite resin, likely through chemical interactions at the zirconia interface that strengthens the bonding mechanism.

In this study, a flowable composite resin was utilized as the substrate and evaluated the chemical interaction at the zirconia-primer interface. Since both flowable resins and resin cements are methacrylate-based, the chemical bonding mechanism remains consistent. However, the higher rigidity of the flowable composite prevents material deformation during shear testing. This ensures that the applied force is directed specifically at the adhesive interface rather than causing measurement artifacts, providing a more reliable and standardized evaluation of the primer’s efficacy.

In the experimental MDP groups, the mean shear bond strength increased starting from the 2% MDP group, peaked at the 10% MDP group, and subsequently decreased in the 12% and 14% MDP groups. These results indicate that the shear bond strength between zirconia and composite resin does not have a direct linear correlation with the concentration of the MDP primer. Specifically, concentrations exceeding 10% MDP did not yield higher bond strengths. Therefore, a 10% MDP concentration was found to be the optimal concentration for use as a chemical surface pretreatment agent for zirconia in this study.

Surface characterization of the fractured specimens in the NMP group revealed an adhesive failure mode (Fig. 2B). In the absence of an MDP primer or adhesive resin, the composite resin failed to bond to the zirconia; consequently, only the grit-blasted zirconia surface was visible, with no resin remnants remaining.

In contrast, the mixed failure mode was observed in all MDP-treated groups (Fig. 2C). The percentage of composite resin remnants varied across these groups, with the lowest coverage found in the CMP group (16.36%) and the highest in the 10% MDP group (73.23%). This percentage subsequently decreased in the 12% and 14% MDP groups to 35.22% and 33.98%, respectively. The trend in composite resin remnants correlated with the mean shear bond strength results, as higher bonding efficacy was directly associated with a greater amount of resin remaining firmly attached to the zirconia surface.

Following the grit-blasting treatment, FESEM imaging of the zirconia surface (Fig. 6A) revealed a rough, irregular topography characterized by micropits, grooves, and depressions. This increased surface roughness enhances micromechanical interlocking and provides a larger surface area for bonding with resin cement or primers [28].

The SEM image of the zirconia surface treated with the MDP primer (Fig. 6B) shows a thin, continuous chemisorbed layer partially filling surface irregularities and adsorbing onto the grit-blasted surface. Furthermore, the SEM image of the adhesive layer (Fig. 6C) demonstrates a continuous film that covers and infiltrates the primer layer, producing a smoother and denser bonding interface.

Grit-blasting zirconia with 110 μm alumina achieves an optimal Sa which enhancing bond strength [24, 29]. The Sa of the specimens in this study ranged from 400 to 600 nm, with no statistically significant differences observed among the groups. However, excessive pressure or duration can induce microcracks, weakening the material. Clinically, over-blasting predisposes restorations to chipping or fracture under masticatory loads.

Based on the elemental analysis, the molecular structure of MDP contains C, H, O, and P, while the zirconia substrate primarily consists of Zr and O (Elemental mapping was restricted to elements involved in chemical bonding; minor elements (e.g., Y and C) were omitted for clarity). Because oxygen is found in both materials, it cannot be used to track their interaction. Therefore, phosphorus (P) was used as a marker to confirm that the MDP primer reached the bonding interface. However, the presence of phosphorus only confirms that MDP is present; it does not verify whether it has chemically bonded to the zirconia or is merely physically adsorbed on the surface.

The trend observed in the EDS analysis closely mirrored the shear bond strength patterns, indicating a strong correlation between the presence of phosphorus on the zirconia surface and the mean bond strength. Because these patterns are consistent, the phosphorus content measured by EDS may be associated with the quantity of chemical bonds formed between the phosphate functional groups (P–OH) of the MDP and the hydroxyl groups on the zirconia surface. Such chemical interactions play a critical role in enhancing interfacial adhesion between the zirconia and the composite resin. To verify whether a true chemical reaction occurred and to clarify the molecular configuration, NMR spectroscopy was employed.

A fundamental limitation of NMR spectroscopy is that it is restricted to isotopes with a non-zero nuclear spin (I). Nuclei with an even number of both protons and neutrons, such as 12C and 16O, have a spin of zero and are thus NMR-inactive. In this investigation, 31P NMR was utilized for its high sensitivity and favorable spin properties, allowing for a high-precision evaluation of the 10-MDP phosphate group’s chemical environment during its interaction with the zirconia substrate.

The 1H MAS NMR spectrum of untreated ZrO2 powder, along with the 31P MAS NMR spectrum of MDP in ethanol (ETH) solution, are shown in Fig. 5. The spectrum of untreated ZrO2 powder exhibits two major peaks: a prominent, broad peak at 4.81 ppm and a smaller peak at 1.16 ppm. Both correspond to the surface hydroxyl groups on the zirconium dioxide particles, labeled Zr1 [9].

The 31P MAS NMR spectrum of MDP in ETH solution also displays two peaks at 0.59 ppm and − 10.82 ppm, labeled P1 and P2, which represent the monomeric and dimeric forms of MDP, respectively [30]. This spectrum confirms the presence of phosphate groups in the MDP solution, these groups are chemically active and capable of forming bonds with metal oxides such as zirconia. These findings suggest that MDP has the potential to chemically interact with the zirconia surface through its phosphate group, thereby enhancing bond strength and improving the stability of the interface.

The 31P NMR spectrum of the mixture containing zirconia powder and 10% MDP solution (labeled as Z10 in Fig. 7) captures the primary chemical shifts identified in this study through five distinct peaks, labeled S1 to S5. The S1 peak, located at − 1.28 ppm, corresponds to MDP monomers adsorbed onto the zirconia surface via hydrogen bonding. The S2 peak at − 7.03 ppm is attributed to monodentate phosphate–zirconia complexes adsorbed through ionic bonding; these are further stabilized by inter-complex hydrogen bonding to form a bidentate configuration. Meanwhile, the S3 peak at − 12.89 ppm is assigned to a phosphate group bridging two zirconium atoms through a combination of hydrogen and ionic bonding. Finally, the S4 and S5 peaks, found at − 19.43 and − 21.58 ppm, respectively, are likely representative of oligomers formed via covalent interactions between multiple phosphate groups [9, 31].

This progression highlights how the different coordination modes of the phosphate group contribute to the overall stability and strength of the zirconia–resin bond.

The 2%, 4%, 10% and 14% were selected for NMR analysis as a representative statistically equivalent groups to provide a rationale for the observed SBS variations. Lower concentrations were used to observe the start of the chemical bonding process. Higher concentrations were included to check if an excess of 10-MDP would create a weak layer on the surface. This approach allowed us to see the chemical changes at every stage of the bonding process.

Figure 7 presents the 31P NMR spectra of zirconia powder treated with 2%, 4%, 10%, and 14% MDP solutions— subgroups identified as Z2, Z4, Z10, and Z14, respectively. In addition to the primary spectra, the figure includes the deconvoluted peaks for each subgroup, a comparative spectrum labeled Zcomp, and the baseline 31P NMR spectrum of the 10% MDP solution for reference. As observed, the chemical shift peaks varied among the groups according to the MDP concentration, indicating that the nature of the phosphate–zirconia interactions is concentration-dependent.

Peak deconvolution was utilized to separate overlapping signals and reveal hidden individual components [16]. The deconvoluted peaks are displayed as colored lines beneath the black aggregate spectrum: blue for S1, red for S2, green for S3, pink for S4, and yellow for S5. While the spectral patterns of the Z2, Z10, and Z14 groups aligned well with their respective deconvoluted peaks, this was not observed for the Z4 group.

In the Z4 spectrum, the primary signal appeared as a broad, intense peak where the S1 and S2 components were positioned in very close proximity. Due to this overlap, the apparent intensity of S1 and S2 in the aggregate spectrum could not be considered reliable. Instead, the two signals merged into a single broad peak with shoulder points at the S1 and S2 positions. Consequently, the true intensities of the S1 and S2 peaks in the Z4 group should be evaluated based on the deconvoluted components rather than the raw aggregate spectrum.

Figure 7 (Zcomp) compares the 31P NMR spectra of the Z2, Z4, Z10, and Z14 groups. Peaks S1 and S2 were detected across all groups, although only the S1 peak and a low-intensity S2 peak were observed in the Z2 group. In the Z4, Z10, and Z14 groups, the intensity of the S1 peak was relatively consistent and notably higher than that of the Z2 group.

In contrast, the intensity of the S2 peak varied significantly: it was highest in the Z10 group, followed by Z4 and Z14, and lowest in Z2. The S3 peak followed an opposite trend, exhibiting its highest intensity in Z14, followed by Z10, and its lowest in Z4; it was entirely absent in the Z2 group. Finally, the S4 and S5 peaks were detected only in the Z10 and Z14 groups, with no significant differences in intensity observed between them.

Regarding the relationship between the chemical shifts and bond strength, the highest bond strength was achieved in the Z10 group, followed by Z14, Z4, and finally Z2. This trend corresponded to the number of peaks observed in each group— specifically, a greater variety of chemical shifts correlated with higher bond strength. The Z2 group, where only the S1 peak and a low-intensity S2 peak were detected, exhibited the lowest bond strength. This suggests that the predominant presence of the S1 peak, with only a minor contribution from S2, is insufficient to achieve high and stable bond strength, likely due to the low concentration of the MDP solution.

The S1 peak represents MDP monomers adsorbed via hydrogen bonding, whereas the S2 peak corresponds to two MDP monomers bridged through hydrogen bonding and interacting with zirconia via ionic bonding. Based on these observations, it is likely that the initial interaction between MDP and zirconia is characterized by hydrogen bonding (S1), which then facilitates the formation of ionic bonds (S2) as concentration increases.

The Z4 group exhibited increased bond strength, characterized by the presence of peaks S1, S2, and S3. Compared to the Z2 group, Z4 showed higher intensities for both the S1 and S2 peaks. The higher concentration of the MDP solution allowed a greater number of phosphate groups to interact with the zirconia surface; this resulted not only in the increased intensities of S1 and S2 but also in the emergence of the S3 peak. These additional phosphate groups likely bonded to zirconium atoms through combined hydrogen and ionic interactions, directly corresponding to the enhanced intensity of the S3 signal.

While the 31P NMR spectra of both the Z10 and Z14 groups contained all identified chemical shifts, the highest bond strength was observed in the Z10 group. A comparison of the two groups reveals that while the intensity of the S1 peak was similar, the S2 peak in Z10 was approximately twice as intense as in Z14. Meanwhile, the intensities of the S3, S4, and S5 peaks remained nearly identical between the two groups.

Based on these spectral profiles and the corresponding shear bond strengths, it appears that the intensities of S3, S4, and S5 are not directly correlated with bond stability or strength. In contrast, the superior bond strength of the Z10 group corresponds directly to its significantly higher S2 peak intensity. From this observation, it can be concluded that the S2 peak—attributed to ionic bonding—represents a bonding configuration that plays a dominant role in enhancing adhesion. This configuration provides a greater contribution to bond strength than the hydrogen bonding associated with the S1 and S3 peaks.

Based on the chemical structures of S1, S2, and S3, the S1 configuration is defined as a monodentate phosphate–zirconia complex bonded via hydrogen bonding, characterized as a “one-by-one” configuration. In contrast, the S2 configuration consists of two monodentate complexes where each phosphate group bonds to a separate zirconium atom through ionic bonding, further stabilized by inter-molecular hydrogen bonding in a “double one-by-one” configuration; this arrangement facilitates a robust bond both to the zirconia surface and between adjacent MDP molecules. The S3 configuration involves a single phosphate group bridging two zirconium atoms through one ionic and one hydrogen bond, creating a “one-to-two” configuration [8]. These bonding modes are distinct, and as phosphate groups populate the zirconia surface, their molecular tails may cause crowding or obstructing adjacent groups. Consequently, additional phosphate groups may be prevented from approaching the surface closely enough to form direct chemical bonds. Instead, these non-bonding groups likely overlap in subsequent layers to form phosphate oligomers, which are represented by the S4 and S5 peaks.

Due to the crowding of their extended molecular tails and close proximity, these phosphate groups were unable to bond to the zirconia surface or position themselves efficiently. Instead, they were forced to shift away from existing groups or rotate into alternative orientations to fit within the available space. Consequently, bonding area became limited, and the zirconia surface was not fully utilized. This phenomenon likely stemmed from an unbalanced ratio of S1, S2, and S3 configurations. An excessive number of S1 and S3 configurations may occupy disproportionate surface area and obstruct the formation of the S2 configuration, leading to a decrease in S2 intensity even as S1 and S3 intensities increase.

While ionic bonds are typically characterized by their sensitivity to moisture, the S2 and S3 coordination states within the 10-MDP-zirconia interface demonstrate significant resistance to hydrolytic degradation [11]. This stability is attributed to the unique molecular architecture of 10-MDP, specifically the high hydrophobicity of its 10-carbon decyl chains. These chains facilitate the formation of a dense, protective barrier which often described as hydrophobic shielding that isolates the zirconia-phosphate interface from the surrounding aqueous environment [32]. By preventing residual moisture from reaching the ionic bond sites, this molecular arrangement maintains the integrity of the chemical coordination and preserves the durability of the S2 state.

The distribution of coordination modes appears to be directly influenced by the concentration of the 10-MDP solution. At an optimal concentration, the number of phosphate groups produces a balanced ratio of S1, S2, and S3 configurations with the highest possible proportion of S2, thereby achieving the maximum bond strength between the MDP and the zirconia surface.

Increasing the 10-MDP concentration beyond the point of surface saturation leads to an accumulation of monomers and phosphate oligomers, facilitating the formation of unstable multilayers. While the optimal concentration range produces a balanced ratio of S1, S2, and S3 configurations that efficiently occupy bonding sites, exceeding this threshold does not enhance surface interaction. Instead, the decline in bond strength at 12% and 14% concentrations results from a synergy of physical and chemical interference. Elevated viscosity promotes the formation of a dense surface film that acts as an obstructive barrier, trapping residual solvent within a viscous complex that resists removal by standard air-drying. This entrapment, combined with an excess of acidic phosphate groups, neutralizes the tertiary amine co-initiators essential for resin polymerization. This acid-base interaction inhibits free-radical generation, leading to a poorly cross-linked, soft interfacial layer characterized by microscopic defects rather than a stable chemical bond. Ultimately, these factors transform the adhesive interface into a structural weak point, explaining the significant reduction in shear bond strength compared to the optimal 6%-10% range. Overall, a 10% MDP concentration appears to provide the most balanced and suitable coordination profile before saturation or molecular overcrowding effects become significant in this study.

The chemical shifts observed in the 31P NMR spectra of zirconia mixed with various concentrations of MDP provide clear evidence of chemical interactions at the interface. These results offer valuable insight into the specific concentrations that promote optimal bonding. The interactions, including hydrogen and ionic bonding, result in distinct peaks that represent unique coordination configurations. This NMR evidence, supported by EDS analysis, confirms the presence of chemical bonding between MDP and zirconia, which likely drives the improved bond strength between the zirconia and the resin composite [33].

The clinical implication of this study, the optimum concentration of 10-MDP primer can created a chemical bond on the zirconia surface and represent the highest mean shear bond strength that can predict the significance increase of the clinical success rate of zirconia-based restorations and ensured the long-term stability in the oral environment.

Limitations

The limitations of this study were considered. This in vitro study was conducted under controlled laboratory conditions; it could not fully replicate the complex oral environment. Consequently, 5,000 thermal cycles were performed to simulate thermal changes in oral conditions. To better understand the thermal stress at the zirconia-composite resin interface, the long-term clinical durability of the bonding mechanism should be further investigation. However, the shear bond strength test was the non-uniform stress distribution and high stress concentrations at the load site, therefore the failure mode was shown in cohesive failure within the composite resin rather than the adhesive failure.

Future perspectives

The future study should be evaluated the immediate and long-term hydrolytic stability of various 10-MDP concentration. Furthermore, the different zirconia and resin cement should be investigated under the dynamic fatigue test to represent the clinical outcome and longevity.

Conclusions

Within the limitations of this in vitro study, it was concluded that 10-MDP adsorbed onto the zirconia surface through chemical bonds, including hydrogen bonding (S1), bidentate complexes (S2), and bridging configurations (S3). A 10% 10-MDP concentration was identified as the optimal threshold, which may be attributed to the highest proportion of S2 configurations. The ionic bonding appeared to contribute significantly to the enhancement of adhesion, providing greater stability than hydrogen bonding alone under the experimental conditions tested.
