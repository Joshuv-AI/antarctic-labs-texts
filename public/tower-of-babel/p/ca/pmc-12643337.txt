Gypsum Gardens: Self‐Assembled Tubular Structures of Calcium Sulfate with Relevance for the Detection of Extraterrestrial Life
Authors: Selina Reigl, Elisabeth Wagner, Carlos Pimentel, Werner Kunz, Alexander E S Van Driessche, Matthias Kellermeier
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractForm is a common and intuitive criterion to distinguish between the realm of living species and the inanimate nature. However, there are in fact no strict boundaries in terms of morphology, as exemplified by so‐called chemical gardens, which form by self‐assembly in purely inorganic systems and yet closely mimic the appearance of trees and other plants. While such structures have been reported for a broad range of compositions–most notably silicates of various types of metal cations as well as prominent (bio)minerals like calcium carbonate or phosphate–one important material has been missing in the comprehensive list of these chemobrionic systems: calcium sulfate. In the present work, we succeeded in preparing well‐developed CaSO4‐based chemical gardens by addition of a concentrated solution of sodium sulfate to solid crystals of calcium chloride. The formed structures were characterized in detail with respect to their growth behavior, mineralogy, and texture. We find hollow tubular architectures consisting of oriented gypsum crystals and delineating smooth curvatures with multiple branching sites. Beyond the sheer beauty of these self‐assembled mineral structures, the results of our study bear deep implications for the detection and interpretation of potential past life on Mars, where abundant deposits of calcium sulfate exist. In addition, the current picture of geochemical environments on the early red planet is fully consistent with the experimental conditions used in the present work, rendering the formation and presence of chemical gardens on Mars plausible.Keywords: Calcium sulfate, Chemical gardens, Chemobrionics, Gypsum, Life on Mars

Chemical gardens are self‐assembled abiotic structures with remarkable morphological resemblance to living systems.[ 1 ] After their first discovery in the 17th century,[ 2 ] the formation of the characteristic tubular architectures with tree‐like appearance was long regarded as a lab curiosity with didactic value for chemistry classes.[ 3 , 4 ] Over the past 20 years, research in the field has intensified substantially with focus on: i) unraveling the mechanisms that drive spontaneous self‐assembly,[ 5 , 6 , 7 ] ii) identifying potential relevance of related processes in established applications,[ 8 , 9 , 10 ] iii) utilizing the underlying concepts for the design of new functional materials,[ 11 , 12 , 13 ] iv) understanding the proposed role in prebiotic chemistry and the origin of life,[ 14 , 15 , 16 ] and/or v) exploring the space of elemental compositions accessible via the chemical garden route.[ 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 ] In its most classical form, a chemical garden can be grown simply by pouring an alkaline solution of sodium silicate onto crystals of water‐soluble salts of di‐ or trivalent metal cations such as iron chlorides or sulfates.[ 23 ] The newly generated solid/liquid interface becomes instantly covered with a “colloidal membrane” due to local supersaturation with respect to metal (oxy)hydroxides and silica(tes). Subsequent transport of water and ionic species across the formed membrane results in the build‐up of osmotic pressures and critical interfacial instabilities, which eventually lead to the ejection of concentrated metal salt solution and rapid solidification of characteristic filamentous tubules due to drastically different chemical conditions in the surrounding silica‐rich reservoir.[ 1 , 5 , 6 , 7 ] Similar phenomena, though sometimes of inherently higher complexity,[ 24 , 25 , 26 ] have been reported for diverse combinations of (metal) cations and anions (other than silicate), as summarized comprehensively by the so‐called “chemobrionics database.”[ 27 ] One of the most extensively investigated cation in the context of chemical garden formation is calcium–arguably due to its omnipresence and importance in both natural and engineered environments. Apart from calcium silicate,[ 8 , 9 , 28 , 29 ] self‐assembled tubular structures of prominent biominerals like calcium carbonate,[ 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 ] calcium phosphate,[ 30 , 34 , 35 , 36 ] or calcium oxalate[ 37 ] were successfully prepared and characterized in detail. A mineral system obviously missing in this list is calcium sulfate, despite its abundance in geological settings and broad relevance for different industrial applications.[ 38 ] The only experimental evidence for the existence of CaSO4‐based chemical gardens dates back to 1929, when Copisarow reported “mineral tree formations” for a range of scarcely water‐soluble salts, including carbonates and sulfates of alkaline‐earth metals.[ 39 ] While tubular structures of SrSO4 and BaSO4 were obtained upon immersion of crystalline SrCl2 and BaCl2 powders into concentrated solutions of Na2SO4 in full analogy with the corresponding silica gardens, the reaction of calcium and sulfate ions at the interface of overlaid solutions of CaCl2 and Na2SO4 gave “a slightly fluffy gelatinous membrane”, from which ill‐defined “proliferations” developed with time. This documented failure to grow calcium sulfate gardens can be understood based on the higher solubility of the mineral compared to its alkaline‐earth homologues SrSO4 and BaSO4, as also noted by Copisarow[ 39 ]: upon contact of the two solutions, the local supersaturation at the interface was not sufficient for a stable and closed membrane to form, so that effective compartmentalization and the osmotic pressures required for morphogenesis could not be achieved. In the present work, we have revisited these early observations and developed methods to reproducibly grow tubular structures of calcium sulfate dihydrate (gypsum) in highly saline media. Our findings not only add another important class of material to the family of chemobrionic systems, but also bear significant implications for the detection of past life in extraterrestrial environments, particularly on early Mars where calcium sulfate deposits are linked to past habitability.[ 40 , 41 , 42 ]

In order to grow CaSO4‐based chemical gardens, local conditions need to be established at the interface of calcium‐ and sulfate‐containing media that ensure sufficiently high levels of supersaturation for rapid mineralization. To achieve this, we used solid powder of anhydrous calcium chloride and slowly added a 3.24 mol kg−1 solution of sodium sulfate in water (46 g Na2SO4 in 100 g H2O) at 35 °C (see the Supporting Information for detailed experimental procedures). Raising the temperature positively influenced interfacial crystallization in two ways: on the one hand, the rate of gypsum growth increases with temperature[ 43 ]; on the other hand, higher amounts of dissolved sulfate ions can be offered as the solubility of Na2SO4 rises steeply between 20 °C and 30 °C.[ 44 ] Under these optimized conditions, the first successful growth of calcium sulfate gardens was observed, as shown in Figure 1. The observed biomimetic morphological evolution closely resembles the behavior reported for other types of chemical gardens prepared with solid salts of the cation and concentrated solutions of the anion[ 1 ]: soon after initial wetting of the crystals and superficial membrane formation, filamentous structures develop and ascend more or less vertically into the liquid phase–arguably as a consequence of buoyant motion of a lighter CaCl2‐rich solution inside the mineralized interfaces into a surrounding Na2SO4 reservoir of higher density.[ 5 ] About 15 min after Na2SO4 addition, the growth process is essentially completed in the given setup, as numerous tubular protrusions with typical thicknesses in the range of 100 µm have emanated and reached lengths of up to 3 cm near the upper surface of the liquid medium.

Interestingly, CaSO4‐based chemical gardens can also be obtained at room temperature (23 °C) after cooling of the sodium sulfate solution, which then is supersaturated but metastable. The growth behavior under these conditions was found to be very similar in terms of kinetics and morphological evolution (see Figure S1). One peculiarity observed at both temperatures is the presence of a “snow‐like” cloud of dispersed visible particles in the solution above the growing tubes, especially during the early stages of growth. Closer inspection of the time‐dependent behavior of the systems (see Movies S1 and S2) suggests that the particles are actually formed at the bottom near the solid structures and then become transported to the upper part of the vial by dynamic flow patterns. In principle, such fluid flow could be initiated by gradients in concentration (i.e., diffusiophoresis[ 45 ]) or temperature (e.g., Rayleigh–Bénard convection[ 46 ]). Indeed, visual streaks support the existence of significant concentration gradients, while temperature measurements (see Figure S2) confirm an increase of about 3.5 °C in the upper liquid medium during the time of active growth (likely caused by exothermic dissolution of calcium chloride). However, in the recorded movies it seems as if the observed flow patterns originate from the growth of the chemical gardens itself: as the pressure in the compartments enclosed by the initially formed membrane becomes large enough to cause local rupture, jets of CaCl2‐rich solution are released and strive upward due to buoyancy. Since the solubility of calcium sulfate is relatively high, many of these early jets do not solidify immediately (in contrast to other types of chemical gardens), but remain liquid on their way to the top and produce the observed flow patterns. The supersaturation achieved across the liquid/liquid interfaces at this stage allows for the formation of individual calcium sulfate particles, which sprinkle from the top of the liquid jets like in fountains and thus become dispersed in the upper part of the system. With time, as the dissolution of the CaCl2 crystals has proceeded, jets carrying higher concentrations of calcium ions are released and successfully mineralized, while the previously dispersed particles settle to the bottom. In this sense, the presence of “snow‐like” clouds at the beginning can be understood as the result of early liquid jets that failed to transform into solid tubular structures due to insufficient local supersaturation.

In a subsequent set of analogous experiments at room temperature, the concentration of the sodium sulfate solution was progressively reduced from 3.35 mol kg−1 (3.00 M at 25 °C) to 0.51 mol kg−1 (0.50 M at 25 °C) (see Figure S3). Although similar morphological evolution took place at all conditions, the process of self‐assembly was slowed down and fewer tubes were formed in media containing less Na2SO4. The lower limit in sulfate concentration appears to be 0.5 M, where single tubular structures could occasionally, but not consistently, be observed. This suggests that under more dilute conditions, the process is controlled by subtle variations in local concentration gradients. In any case, it should be noted that levels of dissolved sulfate equal to or greater than those required for the growth of chemical gardens can be found in various geological settings, such as closed‐basin lakes in (semi)arid regions,[ 47 ] acidic lakes in volcanic craters,[ 48 ] and sites of acid or rock drainage.[ 49 ]

The biomimetic character displayed by the obtained self‐assembling mineral systems becomes even more apparent from a closer look at the morphologies of individual tubular structures, as illustrated in Figure 2: in fact, many of the tubes show multiple remnants of bifurcation events that occurred at different stages of their development. Per se, tube branching is not uncommon in chemobrionic systems and has been reported for various types of chemical gardens.[ 1 , 50 ] However, it is interesting to note that in most of the cases observed in the present work, only one of the resulting branches grew further while the other was sealed. Moreover, such “dead ends” were usually located on the same side of the bifurcated tube. While the mechanisms driving these complex ramification patterns still need to be elucidated, the morphological similarity to actual living trees is striking.

The formed tubular structures show multiple branching (indicated by red arrows). b) Optical micrographs of characteristic branching sites.

To determine the mineralogical composition of the as‐formed chemical gardens in their native environment, we performed in‐situ Raman spectroscopy along one mature tube grown at room temperature and 3.24 mol kg−1 Na2SO4 in its mother liquor by imaging at different spots ranging from the base of the structure to its upper end. The obtained local spectra (Figure 3) show characteristic bands at 981 and 1008 cm−1, which can be ascribed to the ν 1 stretching modes of sulfate ions in solution and in crystalline gypsum, respectively.[ 51 ] Apart from changes in (relative) peak intensities (e.g., caused by thickness variations), no significant differences could be discerned in the spectra acquired from distinct positions, and there was likewise no evidence for the co‐existence of other crystalline phases such as anhydrite (CaSO4) or bassanite (CaSO4·0.5H2O). Similar observations were made for tubes after prolonged ageing (see Figure S4) as well as in attempts to trace the composition of the chemical gardens by Raman spectroscopy during their early development. The Raman data therefore suggests that the formed structures consist of pure gypsum at all (detectable) stages, even for growth at ambient conditions where the liquid phase is supersaturated with respect to sodium sulfate. This notion is corroborated by complementary ex‐situ characterization performed with CaSO4 tubes in dry state after isolation and careful washing. While both infrared (IR) spectroscopy (see Figure S5) and X‐ray diffraction (XRD; see Figure S6) confirm gypsum to be the only (crystalline) phase present, the absence of any noticeable amounts of sodium (and chloride) is demonstrated by energy‐dispersive X‐ray (EDX) analysis (see Figure S7).

In order to gain further insights into the microstructure of calcium sulfate‐based chemical gardens, we performed scanning electron microscopy (SEM) studies on tubular structures isolated after different times of growth. Figure 4 shows a compilation of representative images taken from samples prepared at room temperature and 3.24 mol kg−1 Na2SO4. As expected for chemical gardens, the observed tubes are hollow at all stages of development. After 5 min of ripening, the tube walls consist of needle‐shaped crystals characteristic of gypsum, which appear to show some degree of preferential orientation along the direction of tube growth (from left to right in Figure 4a).

With proceeding maturation, the outer surfaces become smoother and more homogeneous (Figure 4b–d). In turn, the bulk volume of the tube wall is composed of fine gypsum needles, which point inward (i.e., perpendicular to the direction of tube growth) –and exhibit high levels of co‐orientation along their crystallographic c‐axis (Figure 4e). This suggests that growth of the crystals occurred preferentially along the concentration gradient established during the early formation of the membrane separating the inner calcium‐rich solution from the outer sulfate‐rich reservoir. Individual particles measure ca. 5–10 µm in length and 0.5–1 µm in width (Figure 4f) and display the typical rod‐like habit of gypsum single crystals. Similar morphologies are also observed on the outer surface of the tube wall (Figure 4g). However, the orientation of the crystallites appears more or less random on microscopic scales and the rods are accompanied by certain fractions of plate‐like forms, usually adopted by (‐101) contact twins of gypsum.[ 52 ] Interestingly, optical micrographs acquired from mature tubes prepared under the same conditions do indicate preferential orientation of the gypsum crystals along the direction of tube growth (see Figure S8), much like what was observed in SEM images taken from premature structures (cf. Figure 4a). Whether or not such crystallographic alignment on the outer surface of the tubes indeed occurs only during the early stages of development, or if it depends on subtle local effects and varies from specimen to specimen in a given batch, is subject of ongoing work. In any case, analogous analyses performed on calcium sulfate‐based chemical gardens grown at higher temperature gave very similar results with no significant differences in terms of size, morphology, and microstructure of the formed tubes (see Figure S9).

In a final set of experiments, attempts were made to grow chemical gardens from a pressed pellet of calcium chloride instead of a powder as described above. The motivation behind this approach relies on previous studies with other combinations of anions and cations (e.g., calcium silicate, iron silicate, or calcium carbonate),[ 6 , 9 , 23 , 31 , 53 ] for which the use of disk‐shaped pellets allowed well‐defined single macroscopic tubes to be obtained instead multiple smaller ones with irregular and hardly reproducible growth behavior. Although characteristic tubular structures developed from the CaCl2 pellet over similar periods of time as in the case of powder seeds (see Figure S10) and showed the same type of microstructure (see Figure S11), it was not possible to generate and raise a stable membrane around the perimeter of the pellet. Arguably, the reason for this behavior is again linked to insufficient levels of local supersaturation, which cannot provide enough stability during the early formation of the tube wall and thus prevent the formation of macroscopic tubular structures in the calcium sulfate‐based chemical garden system.

The results presented above demonstrate that chemical gardens of calcium sulfate can be grown in the laboratory, but require fairly concentrated solutions for the mechanisms underlying spontaneous self‐assembly to become active. This may explain the very limited information reported on these systems in the literature thus far. The high concentrations of calcium and sulfate ions needed to reach sufficient local levels of supersaturation, as well as the transient nature of flow patterns without successful mineralization during the early stages of development, call a direct relevance of the observed phenomena for most practical applications of CaSO4 materials into serious question. For example, when used as hydraulic binder for construction purposes, calcium sulfate is commonly employed in solid form either as soluble anhydrite or bassanite and reacts to gypsum by dissolution and subsequent re‐precipitation.[ 38 , 54 ] During this process, the free concentrations of the relevant ions remain relatively low (< 0.1 M) and prevent chemical garden‐like self‐assembly from being initiated. Accordingly, to our knowledge there is no evidence for tubular structures of calcium sulfate in such material systems–in contrast to calcium silicate (hydrate) due to its lower solubility and thus higher propensity toward colloidal membrane formation.[ 8 , 9 ] More plausible scenarios for the growth of CaSO4 gardens can be found in the geological record, exemplified most prominently by anhydrite‐rich hydrothermal chimneys, so‐called black smokers.[ 55 ] These structures are ubiquitous in past and present high‐temperature hydrothermal systems on seafloors, such as the mid‐ocean ridge in the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans or the back‐arc basins in the Pacific Ocean.[ 56 , 57 ] In these settings, calcium sulfate forms as a result of turbulent mixing between seawater and hydrothermal fluid (rather than solely from the rapid heating of seawater) and constitutes the walls of the chimneys.

In contrast to Earth's surface, where calcium‐based minerals occur predominantly as carbonates or silicates, there are abundant amounts of calcium (and magnesium) sulfate on Mars.[ 40 , 41 , 58 ] These deposits, along with other hydrous minerals, such as phyllosilicates or hydrated silica, serve as evidence of past aqueous episodes on the red planet and establish valuable constraints on the geochemical settings in which they formed. Calcium sulfate formations are mainly associated with the later stages of water‐rich eras on Mars during the transition from the wetter early Noachian to the drier Noachian/Hesperian period.[ 58 ] Consequently, they can be considered as likely mineral host structures, in which remnants of potential biotic activity may have been preserved.[ 42 ] Recent evidence of wet‐dry cycling in sulfate‐bearing strata during this period further highlights their importance in understanding the habitable history of Mars.[ 59 ]

Given the inherent limitations in sample collection and subsequent in‐depth (bio)geochemical analysis, morphology has emerged as a primary intuitive criterion for detecting traces of extinct life during the wet period on Mars.[ 60 , 61 ] This has spurred the search for signs of biotic activity in sulfate deposits based on morphological, compositional, and mineralogical characterization of analogue terrestrial environments.[ 62 , 63 , 64 , 65 , 66 , 67 ] However, so far there is no generally accepted approach to assess the possible biogenicity of textures found in calcium sulfate mineral formations.[ 38 ] The ambiguity in the use of any such “biogenicity criteria” has been demonstrated for so‐called “dubiofossils”,[ 68 ] which highlight the fact that morphology may not always be a reliable indicator to discriminate forms of biotic origin from abiotically mineralized structures with biomimetic shapes.[ 69 ] For example, this type of controversy has arisen after the release of photographs taken by the Curiosity rover from Gale crater on Mars,[ 70 ] which show intriguing coral‐like structures consisting of multiple tubular protrusions with morphologies resembling those reported in the present work (see Figure S12). In this context, our results appear highly relevant, as we demonstrate that calcium sulfate architectures with “life‐like” morphologies can readily be generated via self‐assembly of inorganic salts under completely abiotic conditions. This finding inevitably raises the question whether the formation of CaSO4‐based chemical gardens may have occurred during the (intermittent) wet periods on Mars.

Although knowledge about the depositional environments on early Mars is still limited, a number of related physicochemical aspects have been established with a reasonable degree of certainty and can be scrutinized with respect to their compatibility with chemical garden formation. Evidence from in‐situ and orbital identification of saline minerals suggests that sulfate‐rich brines with high ionic strength and sulfate concentrations well above the limit for chemical garden growth (i.e., ≥ 0.5 M) were abundant on the surface of the red planet throughout a significant part of its early geologic history.[ 71 ] The presence of jarosite in many of the sulfate deposits found on Mars further indicates that the pH at the time of deposition was acidic (2–4, as defined by the stability region of this mineral phase).[ 72 , 73 ] The resulting acid‐sulfate brine media should be fully compatible with the conditions required for the growth of calcium sulfate gardens, as confirmed by additional experiments performed at lower pH (see Figure S13). Several hypotheses have been put forward to explain the formation of CaSO4 phases on Mars.[ 74 , 75 , 76 , 77 ] The two main scenarios discussed rely on i) direct precipitation from sulfate‐rich water sources upon evaporation, and ii) dissolution of host rocks into acid‐sulfate brine media with subsequent calcium sulfate mineralization. Indeed, the latter processes could meet the criteria described in the present work for the development of CaSO4 chemical gardens–in particular the dissolution of calcium‐bearing deposits into an (acidic) sulfate brine. While various potential solid sources of calcium have been identified on the surface of Mars,[ 75 , 77 ] care must be taken in drawing definitive conclusions due to the lack of sufficient in‐situ data. One final aspect to consider is the atmosphere of early Mars, which presumably was rich in carbon dioxide.[ 78 ] To simulate these conditions, CaSO4‐based chemical gardens were grown in a chamber filled with CO2. The presence of high amounts of CO2 in the gas phase did not impact the growth behavior noticeably, that is, similar tubular structures were formed across a broad range of sodium sulfate concentrations (see Figure S14)–arguably due to the negligible concentration of (bi)carbonate ions in solution at pH values below 6.

All in all, it thus seems plausible that the physicochemical requirements for the formation of calcium sulfate chemical gardens were met on the surface of early Mars, at least on the basis of our current understanding of its geochemistry. This means that there is an entirely inorganic route to mineralized structures with life‐like morphologies–a notion that must be carefully considered in the exploration of samples from Mars in order to avoid false‐positive results in terms of biogenicity.[ 79 ] On the other hand, chemical gardens can in principle serve as self‐fueled microreactors and may thus have played an active role in the prebiotic chemistry of (meanwhile extinct) life on Mars.[ 1 , 4 , 6 , 15 , 16 , 31 ] Apart from that, the detection of structures characteristic of chemical gardens in samples from Mars would provide valuable information on the physicochemical properties of the waters from which they grew (e.g., composition, pH, and salinity) and constrain the original source of the fluids and solids that contributed to their formation. Understanding the chemistry, geological context and timing of chemical garden growth could hence provide important clues to help elucidate the climatic history of Mars as well as its hydrological and sulfur cycles.
