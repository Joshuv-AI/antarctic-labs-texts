The Role of Microbiota Homeostasis in the Progression and Treatment of Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease
Authors: Tianye He, Zhuoma Cairang, Yanxin Xu, Yan Shangguan, Bin Wang, Yun Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a heterogeneous chronic respiratory disorder characterized by persistent airflow obstruction. Its high morbidity and mortality have posed a substantial public health burden, with current symptomatic treatments exhibiting inadequate control and potential adverse effects. With advances in microecological research techniques, the critical role of microbial homeostasis in the oral cavity, lungs, and gut in respiratory health has become increasingly prominent, and microbial dysbiosis is closely associated with progression and therapeutic outcomes of COPD. This review summarizes the compositional alterations of oral, lung, and gut microbiota in COPD patients, analyzes the interactions of the oral-lung axis and gut-lung axis, and delineates three mechanisms through which microbial dysbiosis promotes COPD progression: pathogenic bacterial migration, abnormal metabolite production and immune dysregulation. Additionally, this review summarizes Western and traditional Chinese medicine interventions targeting microbiota homeostasis, including antibiotics, microecological preparations, and herbal medicines, which have shown potential in improving COPD clinical outcomes. This review aims to provide a theoretical reference for the clinical diagnosis and management of COPD.Keywords: COPD, microbial dysbiosis, oral-lung axis, gut-lung axis, treatment strategies

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a heterogeneous lung disease characterized by chronic respiratory symptoms (dyspnea, cough, sputum production, and acute exacerbation) caused by bronchitis, bronchiolitis, or emphysema, ultimately leading to persistent and progressive airflow obstruction.1 Smoking is its primary risk factor; abnormal pulmonary inflammatory responses induced by harmful gases or particles repeatedly stimulate small airways and lung parenchyma, resulting in progressive decline in lung function.2 COPD is the chronic respiratory disease with the highest mortality worldwide, with over 3 million annual deaths.1,3 In China, the prevalence rate is 13.7% among people aged 40 years and above, and the mortality rate ranks first globally.4,5 It is estimated that the number of annual deaths will increase to 4.5 million by 2030, making it an urgent public health problem to address.6 The current treatment (glucocorticoids + bronchodilators) can alleviate symptoms but cannot effectively reverse or control disease progression, accompanied by certain drug adverse effects.

With the advancement of metagenomics, high-throughput sequencing and other technologies, the importance of the human microecosystem (especially the oral, lung, and gut microbiota) for respiratory health has gradually become prominent. Imbalance of microbiota homeostasis can participate in the pathological process of COPD through multiple pathways.1,7 Therefore, exploring the mechanisms of microbiota dysbiosis and restoration is of great significance for controlling or slowing down COPD progression. This review aims to characterize the compositional alterations of the oral, lung and gut microbiota in COPD patients; to analyze the mechanisms by which the oral-lung and gut-lung axes contribute to disease progression; and to summarize Western and traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) intervention strategies that target microbiota homeostasis to improve clinical outcomes, providing a new perspective for the adjuvant treatment of COPD.

Microbiota Characteristics of the Oral Cavity, Lungs, and Gut in COPD Patients

Patients with COPD exhibit significant microbiota dysbiosis in the oral cavity, lungs and gut, characterized by progressive decline in microbial diversity that inversely correlates with disease severity.8–11 Although a recent meta-analysis noted inter-study heterogeneity and failed to confirm significant alpha diversity reductions across all comparisons,12 the preponderance of evidence supports an association between microbiota dysbiosis and COPD progression.

Microbiota Characteristics of the Oral Cavity in COPD Patients

Up to 760 colonizing microorganisms have been detected in oral tissues such as the human gingival sulcus, teeth, cheeks, tongue, and soft palate.13 The oral microbiota of healthy individuals shows significant overlap with the lung microbiota, but the lung microbiota has a lower concentration, fewer members, and distinct composition.14

The oral microecology of COPD patients is susceptible to the disease itself and treatment factors: on the one hand, COPD can alter the oral microecological environment and facilitate the colonization of pathogenic bacteria. Compared with healthy individuals, patients with COPD exhibit a significant increase in the abundance of oral pathogenic bacteria, along with distinct differences in oral bacterial community and functional characterization.15 Wang et al compared the oral flora of COPD patients complicated with diabetes and patients with diabetes alone, and found that the detection rates of periodontitis-related bacteria such as Porphyromonas gingivalis, Streptococcus, and Actinomycetes were higher in the former,16 while the detection rates of Neisseria and Veillonella were lower16 Yu et al found that the detection rate of Porphyromonas gingivalis (rag-1 type) in saliva of patients with COPD and periodontitis was significantly higher than that in patients with chronic periodontitis alone17 On the other hand, patients with repeated coughing and sputum production, dry oral mucosa, and low immunity often use antibiotics and glucocorticoids, which can easily lead to oral microbiota dysbiosis, abnormal proliferation of pathogens, and even oral fungal infections.18

Microbiota Characteristics of the Gut in COPD Patients

Approximately 40 trillion microorganisms colonize the human gut, with a total number of genes about 150 times that of human genes, known as the “second genome” of the human body.19 As an “invisible organ”, it plays an important regulatory role in body health and life activities.20 The normal gut microbiota is a complex dynamically balanced ecosystem, mainly composed of bacteria, a small amount of fungi, viruses, etc, forming a mutualistic symbiotic relationship with the host. In addition to assisting digestion, the gut microbiota can exert systemic effects through immune and metabolic regulation.19,21

Studies have confirmed that there are significant differences in the intestinal microbiota between COPD patients and healthy individuals, mainly manifested as imbalanced microbiota composition, abnormal abundance of specific genera, and reduced overall diversity.22,23 Studies such as 16SrRNA gene sequencing and fecal microbiota transplantation experiments have shown that the proportion of Bacteroidetes in the intestines of COPD patients is reduced, while the relative abundance of Lachnoclostridium, Streptococcus, Prevotella and other genera is increased. This imbalance not only impairs microbiota diversity but also damages intestinal metabolic function and barrier integrity.9,10,24 Furthermore, some scholars have found that the α-diversity of the gut microbiota is reduced in patients with acute exacerbation of COPD (AECOPD), and the increased levels of Lachnoclostridium and Prevotella may indicate acute exacerbation of COPD, serving as biomarkers for AECOPD.10,25 In addition, some scholars have explored the relationship between gut microbiota and COPD severity, and found that the incidence of gut microbiota dysbiosis in patients with stable COPD stage C and D is higher than that in stage A and B, and the state of gut microbiota is closely related to inflammation levels and lung function, suggesting that gut microbiota dysbiosis is not a simple accompanying phenomenon but has an inherent connection with the pathological evolution of COPD.26,27

Microbiota Characteristics of the Lung in COPD Patients

It was previously believed that the lungs of healthy individuals were in a sterile state, but recent studies have confirmed the presence of different microbial communities in the upper and lower airways and lung parenchyma.28 In a healthy state, the lung microbiota is characterized by low abundance and high diversity, mainly including commensal bacteria such as Streptococcus, Prevotella, and Veillonella, which maintain lung health by inhibiting the adhesion of pathogenic bacteria and regulating local immunity.29,30

In COPD patients, the lung microbiota structure undergoes significant changes: ①the synergistic effect of long-term chronic airway inflammation, oxidative stress, and host immune dysfunction leads to changes in the species composition of the lung microbiota and reduced diversity; ②the colonization rate of pathogenic bacteria such as Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa is significantly increased.31 These pathogenic bacteria can activate pulmonary inflammatory signaling pathways, aggravating inflammatory damage to the airways and lung tissue.15,30,32 The imbalance of airway microbiota caused by the increased abundance of Staphylococcus aureus accelerates the decrease of FEV1 in COPD patients.33 Furthermore, multiple studies have compared the respiratory microbiota between COPD patients in acute exacerbation and stable phases, and found that the diversity of the airway microecology in AECOPD patients is significantly reduced, with differences in the microbiota structure at multiple levels (phylum, class, order, family, genus).10,25,30,34–37 And the abundance of Haemophilus, Lactobacillus and Pseudomonas genera exhibited an increase, whereas Actinomyces and Porphyromonas showed a decrease, indicating that the airway microecological dysbiosis is more pronounced in AECOPD patients.15 Pragman et al found that in patients with frequent exacerbation of COPD, the α-diversity of the sputum microbiome decreased while the β-diversity increased, and α-diversity was closely associated with pulmonary inflammation38,39 Consistently, among patients with moderate to severe COPD (GOLD 2–4 grades), those with frequent exacerbations exhibit lower lung microbiome diversity compared with infrequent exacerbators, and γ-proteobacteria (including Haemophilus and Moraxella) are enriched in the frequent exacerbator group.40

However, not all studies have observed consistent associations between lung microbiota and COPD. Tangedal et al reported that lower airway microbiota richness was comparable between COPD patients and healthy controls, with no correlation between microbiota composition and disease severity or inhaled corticosteroid use.41 Similarly, an earlier study by Erb-Downward et al found extensive overlap in bacterial communities among COPD patients, healthy smokers, and non-smokers, with no distinct “COPD-specific” microbial signature, and reported no significant correlation between reduced diversity and disease severity.42

Interactions Between Oral Cavity, Lungs and Gut in COPD

Oral-Lung Axis Interactions in COPD

The anatomical connection between the oral cavity and the upper respiratory tract provides a physiological basis for the migration of microorganisms to the lungs, and increasing evidence indicates a close association between the oral microecology and COPD.14,43 Studies have detected oral pathogenic bacteria in the sputum and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid of COPD patients, suggesting a linkage between the two microecologies.8,44 Some scholars have further found that the entry of oral microbiota into the lungs can lead to lung microbiota dysbiosis, induce lung injury, thereby affecting lung function and promoting the development and acute exacerbation of COPD.45 And the higher the proportion of oral microbiota in the lungs, the more significant the increase in pulmonary inflammatory factor levels and the decrease in lung function.46 In addition, good oral hygiene and periodontal status can reduce the risk of COPD, and the severity of periodontal disease is positively correlated with lung function impairment.47 Improving oral hygiene in COPD patients with periodontal disease can significantly reduce the frequency of acute COPD exacerbations and improve lung function.48

At the same time, the oral microbiota of COPD patients is prone to changes, manifested as a decrease in beneficial bacteria and an increase in pathogenic bacteria. By damaging immune function, disrupting microbiota homeostasis, and exacerbating the deterioration of oral hygiene, it promotes the progression of oral diseases such as periodontitis, dental caries, and oral candidiasis.49 However, there are no clear literature reports on the reverse regulatory mechanism of the lung microbiota on the oral microbiota in COPD.

Gut-Lung Axis Interactions in COPD

From an embryonic development perspective, the lungs, trachea, and intestines have a homologous origin, and the alveoli, glands, and mucosal epithelium all differentiate from intestinal endothelium. Based on this biological basis, modern medicine has proposed the concept of the “gut-lung axis”, which constructs a bidirectional regulatory axis centered on the immune system and the colonizing microbial communities of the lung and gut.50,51 Functionally, the lungs and intestines form an interaction network through lymphatic circulation, blood circulation, and the mucosal immune system.52,53 The balance state of the microbiota in both sides can regulate local and systemic immune responses, achieving bidirectional regulation. Relevant studies based on the TCM theory of “the lung and large intestine being interior-exteriorly related” have also confirmed that lung diseases and intestinal diseases can mutually affect microbiota balance.54

Alterations in gut microbiota can directly modulate pulmonary inflammation and COPD progression. Notably, the composition of the gut bacterial community exhibits significant disparities among patients with AECOPD, stable COPD, and healthy control subjects.10 At the phylum level, the abundance of Firmicutes was significantly increased in COPD patients compared with healthy controls, whereas that of Bacteroidetes was markedly decreased. At the genus level, the abundances of Lachnoclostridium, Alistipes, Streptococcus and Prevotella were all significantly elevated in the intestinal tract of COPD patients.10 In patients with AECOPD, the abundances of beneficial flora-including Blautia, Faecalibacterium, and Anaerostipes, which are capable of modulating forced vital capacity-were significantly reduced in fecal samples, while the increased levels of Lachnoclostridium and Prevotella may serve as potential biomarkers for AECOPD.10,55 Animal experiments have shown that mice receiving fecal microbiota transplantation from COPD patients exhibited enhanced pulmonary inflammation, accelerated decline in lung function and exacerbation of emphysema, confirming that changes in gut microbiota are closely related to COPD progression.9 The acidophilic bacterium Parabacteroides goldsteinii isolated from the feces of COPD model mice can alleviate the inflammatory response in the gut and lung, and has a significant improvement effect on COPD, suggesting that beneficial bacterial strains and their derivative can be developed as drugs for clinical practice of COPD.56 Gut microbiota exhibits significant heterogeneity across different stages of COPD. The relative abundances of Fusobacterium and Aerococcus were significantly elevated in patients with COPD stages 3 to 4; in comparison, the relative abundances of the Ruminococcaceae and Lachnoclostridium were markedly decreased in those with COPD stages 2 to 4, whereas the relative abundances of Tyzzerella and Dialister were reduced in stage 1.11

Similarly, the pathological changes of the lungs in COPD can also affect the gut microbiota and status: a retrospective analysis of 1228 COPD patients showed that most COPD patients have at least one gastrointestinal symptom, among which inflammatory bowel disease and irritable bowel syndrome are the most common.57–61 COPD patients have gut microbiota dysbiosis, intestinal epithelial cell damage, increased intestinal permeability, and changes in gastrointestinal function, which are related to the continuous activation of lymphocytes and inflammatory cell infiltration caused by lung microbiota dysbiosis, leading to intestinal dysfunction.62–64 Meanwhile, smoking, as the main risk factor for COPD, also has adverse effects on the health of the gastrointestinal mucosa. A study using cigarette smoke to induce COPD modeling in mice showed that both the lung microbiota and gut microbiota were disrupted, which was significantly correlated with the decrease in lung function.65 The specific mechanism may be that cigarette smoke causes oxidative stress and hypoxia of airway epithelial cells, and gas exchange disorders lead to intestinal hypoxia and intestinal cell damage, thereby increasing intestinal permeability, disrupting immune function, and causing microbiota dysbiosis.62,66,67 However, little is known about the impact of the lung microbiota on the gut microbiota, which needs to be further explored.

Mechanisms by Which Microbiota Homeostasis Imbalance in the Oral-Lung/Gut-Lung Axis Promotes COPD Progression

The imbalance of microbiota homeostasis in the oral-lung axis and gut-lung axis is an important driving factor for the occurrence and development of COPD. It promotes the disease process through multiple mechanisms such as pathogenic bacterial migration, abnormal metabolite production, and immune regulation disorders, and various links interact to form a vicious circle (Figure 1).49 The specific interaction mechanisms mainly include the following pathways:

Pathogenic bacteria from both the oral cavity and the gut can migrate to the lungs through the oral–lung axis and gut–lung axis, inducing pulmonary infection and altering the pulmonary microbial environment. Gut microbiota dysbiosis is characterized by decreased production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which weakens anti-inflammatory effects and impairs the integrity of the gut mucosal barrier. In addition, elevated homocysteine levels accelerate the decline of lung function. Meanwhile, bacterial components such as endotoxins, cytokines, proteins, and microbial fragments may enter the systemic circulation via the mesenteric lymphatic system, stimulating macrophages to release pro-inflammatory cytokines including TNF-α and IL-1β, thereby promoting immune dysregulation. Pathogenic Bacteria Migration

When the immune system is weakened, the diversity of oral microbiota decreases and pathogenic bacteria multiply in large quantities, which can invade the respiratory tract and cause infections, leading to acute exacerbation of COPD. Researches have shown that oral pathogenic bacteria can be detected in the sputum and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid of COPD patients.68–73 Oral microbiota dysbiosis can increase the risk of periodontal pathogenic bacteria such as Porphyromonas gingivalis and Fusobacterium nucleatum, as well as respiratory pathogenic bacteria such as Streptococcus pneumoniae and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, invading the lower respiratory tract.74 In addition, dental plaque, periodontal pockets and saliva all contain major respiratory pathogens, suggesting that the oral microecology may be a potential source of some respiratory infections and disease progression.70,75–78

There have also been reports of similar mechanisms directly affecting the progression of COPD between the gut and lungs. Multiple studies have confirmed that gut microbiota dysbiosis can lead to damage to the gut mucosal barrier and increased permeability, and conditional pathogenic bacteria in the intestines can easily enter the blood circulation to trigger systemic inflammatory responses, or directly migrate to the lungs to induce infections.79–82 In summary, the migration of pathogenic bacteria caused by the dysbiosis of oral and gut microbiota can induce pulmonary bacterial infections and inflammatory responses, promoting the occurrence and development of COPD.

Abnormal Metabolite Production

The gut-lung axis can mutually regulate through metabolites, exacerbating the pathological process of COPD. The disorder of the gut microbiota structure in COPD patients leads to abnormal metabolite profiles, among which changes in short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) are particularly critical.83 SCFAs (acetic acid, propionic acid and butyric acid) are produced by the gut microbiota such as Firmicutes on the fermentation and degradation of carbohydrates such as dietary fiber. SCFAs, especially butyric acid, participate in inflammation and immune regulation after being absorbed into the blood through the intestines, and also play an important role in maintaining the integrity of the gut barrier and tissue repair.84–86 The levels of SCFAs in the blood of COPD patients are significantly lower than those in healthy individuals, and overall lower levels of SCFAs were determined in Stage III–IV COPD compared to Stage I–II COPD.84 The deficiency of SCFAs not only weakens the inhibitory effect on pulmonary inflammatory cells but also damages the integrity of the gut mucosal barrier, exacerbating inflammatory responses.87 Animal experiments have confirmed that butyric acid has anti-inflammatory effects, which can inhibit the production of neutrophils stimulated by LPS and reduce the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and nitric oxide; Kotlyarov et al found that SCFAs have anti-inflammatory effects in models of airway hyperresponsiveness and airway inflammation88,89 When gut microbiota is disrupted, the level of SCFAs decreases, and the anti-inflammatory and immune regulatory capabilities are weakened, thereby affecting systemic and pulmonary inflammatory responses.

In addition, elevated levels of the metabolite homocysteine (Hcy) can also exacerbate the pathological progression of COPD. A study reported that respiratory dysbiosis is associated with the accelerated decline in lung function, with the specific mechanism being that Staphylococcus aureus in the airway accelerates the reduction in lung function parameters such as FEV1 via the homocysteine-AKT1-S100A8/A9 axis.33

Immune Regulation Disorders

Risk factors for COPD (such as smoking) can alter mucosal immunity and the gut and lung microbiota, interacting through immune crosstalk.90 Microbiota dysbiosis can cause immune regulation disorders through multiple links, forming a cross-organ inflammatory amplification effect. The lungs, oral cavity, and intestines achieve immune mutual regulation through pro-inflammatory factors stimulated by pathogenic bacteria. The “gut-lung axis” constructs a cross-organ immune interaction network through immune signaling pathways.91 The gut microbiota is a key factor in establishing the host pulmonary immune homeostasis, affecting pulmonary immune function and barrier integrity by regulating innate immunity and adaptive immunity, thereby changing the prognosis of chronic respiratory diseases. Studies have shown that immune imbalance mediated by Th17/Treg is closely related to the occurrence and progression of COPD, and gut microbiota dysbiosis may be an important cause of this imbalance.91,92 The immune transmission of gut-derived factors through mesenteric lymph enhances the development of systemic inflammation and damage to distant organs (lungs). Samuelson et al proposed that bacteria, cell debris, and bacterial proteins in the submucosa of the intestines or mesenteric lymph nodes can enter the systemic circulation along with cytokines such as IL-10 and IL-6 and chemokines through the mesenteric lymphatic system, activating dendritic cells, macrophages, and T cells, and aggravating pulmonary infections93 Another study showed that when gut microbiota is dysregulated, the invasion of opportunistic pathogens can activate local immune cells in mesenteric lymph nodes, affecting pulmonary immune regulation through lymphatic and systemic circulation.94,95

In addition, the release of endotoxins can also cause immune regulation disorders, leading to lung inflammation. Endotoxin is derived from Gram-negative bacteria (such as Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, etc), with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) as the core component. When the intestinal mucosa is damaged, endotoxins released by intestinal pathogenic bacteria reach the lungs through the blood circulation. On the one hand, endotoxins directly damage the lungs, manifested as an increase in mean pulmonary artery pressure, vascular resistance as well as an increase in capillary permeability; on the other hand, endotoxins stimulate macrophages to produce inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α and IL-1β, which can further aggravate pulmonary inflammation.96,97 The above mechanisms provide an important theoretical basis for the pathogenesis and prevention and treatment measures of chronic respiratory diseases such as COPD.

COPD Treatment Strategies and Homeostasis of Oral, Lung, and Intestinal Microbiota

Western Medicine Intervention

Multiple studies have confirmed that some Western medicines can improve respiratory symptoms by regulating the gut, lung, or oral microbiota homeostasis in COPD patients, providing a reference for clinical treatment.

Antibiotics

Antibiotics can improve symptoms and reduce acute exacerbations in COPD patients by regulating the airway and intestinal microecology and inhibiting inflammatory responses. A multi-center randomized controlled trial showed that long-term use of erythromycin can reduce the frequency of acute exacerbations in patients with moderate to severe stable COPD, regulate the airway and intestinal microecology; Similar intervention on mice with emphysema mice showed that erythromycin can restore gut microbiota dysbiosis caused by tobacco smoke exposure (reduce pathogenic bacteria and increase beneficial bacteria), partially reverse metabolic disorders of lung tissue, and reduce L-nicotine content in lung tissue, suggesting that it may exert anti-inflammatory effects by regulating the gut microbiota.98 A clinical trial by Wu et al found that the addition of azithromycin to conventional treatment can reduce the frequency of acute exacerbations, improve clinical symptoms, airway inflammation levels and macrophage polarization ratio, and simultaneously regulate the airway microecology (increase beneficial bacteria, reduce pathogenic bacteria, and improve microbiota diversity)99 And the increase in airway microbiota diversity may be correlated with improved exercise tolerance and reduced pulmonary CO2 retention.

Glucocorticoids

There are differences in the effects of glucocorticoids on airway microbiota and clinical symptoms in COPD patients. A study on elderly COPD patients showed that patients treated with combined inhalation of budesonide had significantly lower respiratory microbiota diversity than those treated with inhaled bronchodilators alone, and had higher rates of acute exacerbations, hospitalizations, and pneumonia. It is speculated that inhaled glucocorticoids may aggravate the disease by altering the respiratory microbiota and immunosuppression.100 Qi Yujing et al found that nebulized inhalation of glucocorticoids had little effect on the diversity of the airway microecology in AECOPD patients; the effect of short-term (5-day) oral glucocorticoids on airway microbiota diversity in AECOPD patients is significantly lower than that of long-term treatment101 Chen et al also confirmed that systemic glucocorticoid administration has a more significant interference on the airway microbiota of AECOPD patients, which is prone to a decrease in beneficial bacteria and the proliferation of opportunistic pathogens, while inhaled administration has a milder impact, providing a basis for the rational selection of steroid administration methods in clinical practice to reduce microecological disorders.102

Microecological Preparations

Microecological preparations (including probiotics, prebiotics, and synbiotics) play an important role in the prevention, treatment, and complication management of COPD by regulating gut microbiota homeostasis, repairing the mucosal barrier, and inhibiting inflammatory responses. Clinical trials of Bifidobacterium-Lactobacillus triple viable bacteria preparations have confirmed that combined with conventional treatment or dietary fiber intervention, they can significantly improve the clinical effective rate of AECOPD and stable COPD patients, improve the gut microbiota (increase beneficial bacteria such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus, and reduce pathogenic bacteria such as Enterobacterium and Enterococcus), alleviate gastrointestinal mucosal barrier damage (reduce D-lactate acid and diamine oxidase levels), downregulate inflammatory indicators such as CRP, TNF-α and IL-6, and simultaneously improve lung function and quality of life scores.25,103–105 Besides, they can also reduce diarrhea, lower the rate of gut microbiota dysbiosis and gastrointestinal dysfunction scores.106–109

Other microbial preparations have also shown positive effects: Saccharomyces boulardii can improve gastrointestinal function and reduce microbiota dysbiosis in AECOPD patients.110 Bifidobacterium quadruple viable tablets have better effects in regulating gut microbiota, improving nutrition and immune function in COPD patients with respiratory failure than single Bifidobacterium preparations.111,112 After 4 weeks of treatment with Bacillus subtilis and Enterococcus faecium granules in COPD patients with non-small cell lung cancer, the gut microbiota composition and immune function were significantly improved, while the incidence of gastrointestinal reactions was notably reduced.113 Live Lactobacillus milk beverage Yakult can reduce the incidence of enteral nutrition-related diarrhea and microbiota dysbiosis.114 Lactocare probiotic (containing 7 types of probiotics such as Lactobacillus acidophilus, Lactobacillus bulgaricus, etc.) can improve chronic pulmonary symptoms related to sulfur mustard exposure; Vivomix 112 billion probiotic (Streptococcus thermophilus, Bifidobacteria, Lactobacilli) can reduce intestinal permeability and inflammation levels, and improve muscle strength in elderly COPD patients.115,116 Lactobacillus reuteri can effectively prevent antibiotic-related diarrhea in hospitalized COPD patients.117 In addition, the intake of prebiotics or yogurt may also play a beneficial role in preventing COPD progression.118 The above research results suggested that microecological preparations, as adjuvant therapy for COPD, exhibited a favorable safety profile, which can correct microbiota dysbiosis, reduce inflammatory responses, and improve respiratory symptoms.

Animal experiments have further revealed the effects and mechanism of microecological preparations on COPD: Probiotics containing Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus can reduce the serum IL-6 level and alleviate systemic inflammation in COPD model mice through the metabolite butyric acid.119–121 Lactobacillus rhamnosus can improve pulmonary inflammation and airway remodeling in cigarette smoke-induced COPD mice, regulate gut microbiota balance, and improve microbiota dysbiosis, airway inflammation, and hyperresponsiveness in mice with asthma-COPD overlap syndrome.122,123 Probiotic preparations containing Streptococcus salivarius, Lactobacillus, Lactobacillus casei, etc, can correct intestinal microbiota dysbiosis in COPD model rats, improve lung function and bone mineral density, and alleviate lung tissue fibrosis and inflammatory responses.124,125 Pediococcus pentosaceus can increase SCFA production through the “gut-lung axis” and reduce pulmonary oxidative stress in COPD mice.126 A Lactobacillus combination containing Limosilactobacillus reuteri and Lacticaseibacillus paracasei can improve pulmonary emphysema and microbiota diversity in COPD mice.127 Yeast β-glucan can regulate the composition of the gut microbiota in COPD rats, improve lung function through the “gut-lung axis” and alleviate inflammation and oxidative stress.128

It is worth noting, however, that not all studies have reported uniformly positive outcomes. Aghamohammadi et al found that three‑month administration of the oral probiotic Familact significantly improved dyspnea as measured by mMRC and Borg scales in COPD patients, but did not significantly change FEV1 values compared with placebo, suggesting that symptomatic benefits may occur independently of improvements in spirometric lung function.129 Similarly, Wang et al reported that treatment with Bifidobacterium triple viable capsules for two weeks significantly improved CD4+/CD8+ ratio and NK cell activity in COPD patients with pulmonary infection, yet no significant change was observed in CD3+ T cell percentage.130 These findings suggest that the efficacy of microecological preparations may be strain‑specific, outcome‑dependent, or influenced by patient characteristics such as disease stage and baseline immune status, and highlight the need for further research to identify which patients and outcomes are most likely to benefit.

Other Drugs

Zhang et al divided 113 COPD patients complicated with bronchiectasis into azithromycin alone group and azithromycin combined with bacterial lysate group131 After 3 months’ treatment, it was found that the combination group showed a lower detection rate of pathogens such as Haemophilus influenzae, Klebsiella pneumoniae and Streptococcus pyogenes in sputum, a significant decrease in peripheral blood inflammation indicators, an increase in immunoglobulin (IgA, IgG, IgM) levels, a significant improvement in lung function, more significant relief of cough and sputum symptoms, and better improvement in mMRC and CAT scores. A randomized controlled study on AECOPD patients showed that the study group treated with negative pressure suction toothbrush combined with 2.5% sodium bicarbonate solution irrigation was superior to the control group treated with 2.5% sodium bicarbonate solution wiping alone in improving oral function, reducing bad breath and regulating pH value. And the detection rate of normal oral microbiota was higher, the detection rate of pathogenic bacteria and fungi was lower, which significantly reduced the incidence of oral fungal infections, hospital-acquired pneumonia, and overall complications, and improved the quality of life of patients.132

Traditional Chinese Medicine Intervention

Chinese Medicine Monomers

Based on the theories of “the lung and large intestine being interior-exterior related” and “gut-lung axis”, TCM monomers have shown significant potential in the treatment of COPD by regulating gut microbiota homeostasis, inhibiting inflammatory responses and repairing mucosal barrier.

Clinical studies have confirmed that Plantago polysaccharide capsules combined with conventional Western medicine treatment can improve the total effective rate of stable COPD patients, improve respiratory symptoms and inflammatory indicators, significantly increase the level of intestinal Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus, and reduce the level of Escherichia coli (no significant change in gut microbiota in the control group).133 Another study showed that after oral administration of Plantago polysaccharide capsules for 2 months in stable COPD patients, there was a significant increase in intestinal Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron coupled with a reduction in Eubacterium rectale counts, decreased serum inflammatory factor levels and improved lung function indicators, along with a lower incidence of acute exacerbations, suggesting that it may exert anti-inflammatory effects and improve lung function by regulating the gut microbiota.134

Animal experiments further revealed the effects and mechanism of TCM monomers on COPD: Research by Ye et al confirmed that Scutellaria baicalensis polysaccharide can reduce the release of inflammatory factors in smoke-induced COPD model rats, alleviate pathological damage to lung, airways and intestines, inhibit airway remodeling, improve lung function, as well as regulate intestinal microbiota (such as increasing Treponema, Lactobacillus, Muribaculaceae and Romboutsia abundance) and related metabolism, which may be related to the gut microbiota-metabolite axis and lung epithelial integrity135,136 For the COPD rat model induced by a mixture of cigarette and wood dust smoke and the in vitro anaerobic culture system of intestinal microbiota, the combined intervention of Platycodon grandiflorum polysaccharides and Platycodon saponins can significantly improve the inflammatory injury of lung tissue and gut mucosal barrier function in rats compared with single administration. The mechanism may be related to the bidirectional regulation of lung-gut mucosal co-immune factors (IL-22, IL-17, etc.) and the inhibition of TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway activation induced by gut-derived LPS. Moreover, Platycodon grandiflorum polysaccharides can regulate the gut microbiota and promote the biotransformation and metabolite production of Platycodon saponins dose-dependently.137

Multiple studies on Polygonatum Sibiricum Polysaccharides have verified its lung and intestinal protective effects: to improve colon damage in COPD model mice caused by regulating colonic microbiota and bile acid metabolism; to regulate the ratio of Th17/Treg cells and related cytokines, enhance mucosal barrier function, and improve inflammation levels and lung function by regulating microbiota and metabolites.138,139 A study on the expectorant mechanism of Tussilago farfara showed that its expectorant effect depends on the intestinal microbiota, and dihydrocaffeic acid and quinic acid are key active compounds, which can regulate the arginine biosynthesis pathway and exert expectorant effects by increasing the abundance of active phospholipids in the lungs.140 In addition, various Chinese medicine monomers such as Bletilla striata polysaccharide, Ficus pumila L. polysaccharides, quercetin, Cordycepin and Cordyceps Polysaccharide, Ephedra polysaccharides have been confirmed in animal experiments to have the effects of improving lung function, reducing inflammation, and regulating gut microbiota.141–145

Chinese Medicine Compound Preparations

A large number of TCM compound preparations have also been reported to significantly improve the clinical symptoms of COPD via regulating gut microbiota.

Clinical studies have confirmed that Xuanbai Chengqi decoction combined with conventional treatment in AECOPD patients significantly improves the total effective rate, increases the number of beneficial intestinal bacteria (Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium), reduces pathogenic bacteria (Enterococci and Enteric Bacilli), improves gastrointestinal mucosal function and inflammation levels, and has a lower incidence of adverse reactions.146,147 Yupingfeng granules/powder combined with conventional Western medicine in COPD treatment can significantly improve the total effective rate, improve lung function, reduce inflammation levels, regulate the gut microbiota, and improve the rate of gut microbiota dysbiosis and intestinal mucosal barrier function.148–150 The combination of Suzi Jiangqi decoction and conventional Western medicine of COPD treatment has a higher total effective rate than the control group, lower levels of inflammatory factors, better lung function indicators, and significantly increased gut microbiota diversity.151 Spleen lung dual supplement mixture combined with bacterial lysate capsules in the treatment of stable COPD has a significantly higher total effective rate (93.33%) than the single treatment group. While improving TCM syndromes, lung function, and quality of life, it can regulate T lymphocyte subsets, reduce inflammation factor levels, and optimize the intestinal microbiota structure.152 In addition, various TCM compound preparations such as Tanreqing Injection, miao medicine mixture, and Qingre Huatan formula have shown the advantage of regulating gut microbiota while treating COPD based on the theory of “the lung and large intestine being interior-exterior related”.153–168

Animal experiments have further revealed the effects and mechanism of TCM compound preparations in COPD treatment through the “gut-lung axis”: Yifei Sanjie formula can improve the lung function of Wistar rat models, downregulate inflammatory factors through the NLRP3/caspase-1/IL-1β signaling pathway, reverse lung pathological damage, reshape the microbiota of the lungs (increasing beneficial genua Lactobacillus and Bacteroides, as well as downregulating pathogenic genua Ralstonia and Mycoplasma), simultaneously regulate the gut microbiota, reduce airway inflammation and remodeling, and enhance lung and intestinal mucosal immunity.169,170 Yupingfeng powder or granules can improve the gut microbiota composition of COPD rat models, regulate inflammation levels, airway microbiota composition and abundance, improve lung function and lung tissue pathological damage, and maintain Th1/Th2 balance.171–173 Modified Shengxian decoction, Xuanbai Chengqi decoction, and Qingyuan Huatan granules can all alleviate pulmonary inflammation and pathological damage by regulating the Th17/Treg balance and gut microbiota metabolism in COPD model rats/mice.174–178 Bufei Jianpi formula improves mucosal immune function by reshaping the intestinal microbiota of COPD rats and inhibiting the SCFAs/GPR43/NLRP3 pathway.179 In addition, various TCM compound preparations such as Tanreqing injection, Qibai Pingfei capsules, Cordyceps militaris, Sijunzi decoction and Bufei Huoxue capsules have also verified their effects of regulating intestinal microbiota, improving COPD pulmonary inflammation, and lung function via animal experiments.180–193

Acupuncture

Acupuncture or electroacupuncture treatment can also regulate the intestinal microbiota homeostasis, improve pulmonary inflammation and pulmonary function based on the “gut-lung axis”, providing a non-pharmaceutical intervention idea for COPD treatment. A randomized controlled study on 100 AECOPD patients showed that the total effective rate of treatment with Chinese medicine atomization combined with acupuncture at “Feishu” and “Zusanli” points (for 4 weeks) was higher than that of conventional Western medicine treatment. The levels of serum inflammatory markers such as CRP and IL-6 were significantly reduced, lung function indicators such as FEV1 and FEV1/FVC were significantly improved, the diversity of sputum microbiota was improved, beneficial microbiota increased, pathogenic bacteria decreased, and there was no significant difference in the incidence of adverse reactions.194 Two other animal experiments confirmed that 2 weeks of electroacupuncture intervention at “Feishu” and “Zusanli” points in SD rats after COPD modeling improved the damage to lung and colon tissues, significantly reduced pulmonary and systemic inflammation levels, increased lung function, and improved intestinal microbiota imbalance and abundance.195,196

Summary and Outlook

As summarized in Table 1, this review demonstrates that COPD patients exhibit reduced microbial diversity and distinct compositional shifts in the oral cavity, lungs, and gut. And dysbiosis of these sites may contribute to COPD progression through at least three interrelated mechanisms: pathogenic bacterial migration, abnormal metabolite production, and immune regulation disorders. These findings indicate that the oral-lung axis and gut-lung axis serve as crucial pathological links in COPD. From clinical and animal data, restoring microbiota homeostasis through antibiotics, probiotics, or traditional Chinese medicine represents a feasible adjunctive strategy to slow disease progression, while monitoring microbial changes may help identify patients at risk of rapid COPD progression. However, the in vivo microbiota is highly complex and dynamic, and the specific molecular mechanisms by which the imbalance of the oral-lung axis and gut-lung axis microbiota affects COPD progression have not been fully elucidated. The comparison of the effects and long-term safety of different treatment methods on microbiota homeostasis still needs further verification. In the future, technologies such as microbiomics and genomics should be combined to explore the molecular mechanisms by which microbiota homeostasis imbalance affects the progression of COPD, clarify key mediator proteins and pathways, develop personalized microbiota regulation schemes, and provide a theoretical basis and practical guidance for the precise treatment of COPD.
