Minimally Invasive Approach to Multiligament Knee Reconstruction: Arthroscopic PCL and PLC With Percutaneous LCL Reconstruction
Authors: Corentin Philippe, Ali Alayane, Stefano Jacotti, Victor Sonnery-Cottet, Dany Mouarbes, Regis Pailhe, Etienne Cavaignac
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Multiligament knee injuries involving the posterior cruciate ligament (PCL), posterolateral corner (PLC), and lateral collateral ligament (LCL) are rare but complex pathologies. Historically managed with open surgery, these injuries are increasingly being addressed using minimally invasive techniques, which provide anatomical reconstruction and less surgical morbidity.Indications:We report the case of a 24-year-old male farmer who sustained a high-energy multiligament knee injury after a motorcycle accident. Six months after the accident, he presented with persistent daily instability affecting his work and recreational activities. Clinical and radiological evaluation confirmed chronic injuries to the PCL, PLC, and LCL.Technique Description:A 1-stage, all-arthroscopic and percutaneous reconstruction was performed. The PCL was reconstructed using a pedicled semitendinosus autograft with tibial tunnel placement via a trans-septal portal for direct visualization. The PLC was addressed arthroscopically by placing a knotless anchor for femoral fixation of the reconstructed popliteus tendon, using an extensor hallucis longus allograft. The LCL was reconstructed using a gracilis autograft via a percutaneous technique, minimizing soft-tissue dissection and avoiding tunnel convergence. This minimally invasive strategy enabled anatomical graft placement with reduced risk of neurovascular injury and greater potential for accelerated recovery.Results:Postoperatively, the patient wore a hinged PCL dynamic brace for 3 months with no weightbearing for the first 6 weeks. Physical therapy was initiated immediately after surgery with a focus on a controlled range of motion and progressive strengthening. The aim was to allow him to safely return to manual labor and sports activities 6 to 9 months after the surgery.Discussion/Conclusion:This case highlights the feasibility and effectiveness of a 1-stage, all-arthroscopic, percutaneous reconstruction of chronic PCL, PLC, and LCL injuries. This technique provides reliable anatomical restoration with minimal surgical morbidity and promising early clinical outcomes, particularly in physically demanding patients.Patient Consent Disclosure Statement:The author(s) attests that consent has been obtained from any patient(s) appearing in this publication. If the individual may be identifiable, the author(s) has included a statement of release or other written form of approval from the patient(s) with this submission for publication.

Background

This video demonstrates a minimally invasive technique for multiligament knee reconstruction, including arthroscopic reconstruction of the posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) and the posterolateral corner (PLC), combined with percutaneous reconstruction of the lateral collateral ligament (LCL).

Multiligament knee injuries involving the PCL, PLC, and LCL are uncommon but challenging conditions. While traditionally treated with open surgery,2,5,7 current trends favor minimally invasive techniques that enable anatomical reconstruction with reduced surgical morbidity.1,4,8

Indications

A 24-year-old male farmer sustained a high-energy knee injury in a motorcycle accident. Despite initial treatment with immobilization and physical therapy, he presented 6 months later with chronic daily knee instability.

Physical examination confirms chronic posterior, posterolateral, and varus knee instability.

Radiographs of the knee (anteroposterior and lateral views) are unremarkable, showing no fractures or bony avulsions. Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrates complete ruptures of the PCL, PLC, and LCL, while the anterior ligament reconstruction (ACL) remains intact, with no evidence of meniscal or cartilage injury.

Many open surgical techniques have been described in the literature to restore knee joint stability, including PCL, LCL, and PLC reconstructions.

However, recently, a few articles have discussed minimally invasive techniques for PCL, PLC, and LCL reconstructions.

Technique Description

We decided to perform a 1-stage reconstruction of chronic PCL, PLC, and LCL ruptures. Arthroscopic reconstruction of the PCL and PLC was performed via a trans-septal approach, 6 using a pedicled semitendinosus autograft for the PCL and an extensor hallucis longus allograft for the PLC. In contrast, LCL reconstruction was performed percutaneously using a gracilis tendon autograft. 3

This technique is indicated for young active patients or physical work and chronic daily instability. Arthroscopic and percutaneous procedures are chosen for their lower invasiveness. Contraindications are symptomatic osteoarthritic cartilage lesions, active infection, or preoperative arthrogenic muscle inhibition.

The preoperative examination under anesthesia confirmed posterior, posterolateral, and varus knee instability.

Arthroscopic exploration of the knee confirms posterior laxity, while the lateral opening of the femorotibial compartment confirms injury to the LCL and the posterolateral corner, with no associated meniscal injury.

The PCL femoral tunnel is created by placing a guidewire 10 mm posterior to the medial femoral condyle cartilage, directing it proximally and slightly posterior to the lateral epicondyle, then overdrilling inside-out with a 9-mm drill to 20 mm depth, and positioning a loop suture.

Using the trans-notch approach, the posteromedial portal is established under transillumination. Under arthroscopic visualization, the posterior septum is debrided with a shaver introduced through the posteromedial portal, keeping the cutting surface oriented toward the bone at all times to avoid injury to the popliteal vessels.

The scope is inserted through the posteromedial portal and advanced through the septal opening, reaching the posterolateral knee compartment to create the posterolateral portal under direct visualization using skin transillumination.

With the scope inserted through the posteromedial portal and the shaver through the posterolateral portal, the septum is carefully released from the capsule to expose the PCL tibial footprint. Under direct visualization of the arthroscope through the posteromedial portal, the hook of the PCL guide is introduced through the anteromedial portal and positioned at the PCL tibial footprint at 10 mm downward from the articular surface.

A guidewire is then inserted through the medial Anteromedial tibial cortex at the level of the hamstring's insertion to the posterolateral tibial footprint and is overdrilled with a 9-mm drill bit. Then, a fiber-stick suture is inserted into the tibial tunnel and retrieved from the anteromedial portal.

The arthroscope is placed in the lateral gutter. Under arthroscopic guidance, a 21-gauge needle is used to accurately locate the lateral epicondylar portal. The PLC femoral footprint, located anterior to the lateral epicondyle, is identified for anchor placement at the popliteus femoral footprint.

A 1.8-mm knotless FiberTak soft anchor is then inserted into the lateral femoral cortex through a predrilled 2.6 × 35 mm hole at the popliteus femoral footprint, and traction is applied to verify secure fixation.

A shaver is introduced through the posterolateral portal to carefully dissect the lateral posterior capsule from the posterior horn of the lateral meniscus, which allows exposure of the PLC footprint at the popliteal sulcus.

The PCL tibial guide is reintroduced through the posterolateral portal and positioned at the popliteus sulcus footprint under direct arthroscopic visualization from the posteromedial portal. A guidewire is then inserted from the anteromedial tibial cortex and overdrilled with a 7-mm reamer. Then, a loop suture is passed through the PLC tibial tunnel and retrieved from the lateral epicondylar portal.

A pedicled tripled semitendinosus PCL autograft is passed through the tibial tunnel. The EndoButton is then pulled into the femoral tunnel under direct arthroscopic visualization and tightened on the medial femoral condyle. The graft is gradually pulled into the femoral tunnel.

A double fixation of the PCL graft into the tibial tunnel is performed using 2 interference screws while maintaining the knee in a reduced position at 90° of flexion.

The PLC graft is pulled through the tibial tunnel using a loop suture under direct arthroscopic visualization via the posteromedial portal, and its looped end is retrieved through the lateral epicondylar portal. The graft is then fixed to the popliteus femoral footprint using a FiberTak Soft Anchor. Then the PLC graft is fixed in the tibial tunnel with a 7-mm bioabsorbable screw, with the knee held in internal rotation.

A doubled gracilis autograft is prepared for percutaneous LCL reconstruction. A guidewire is inserted just posterior and proximal to the lateral epicondyle, and its extra-articular position is confirmed arthroscopically from the lateral gutter. The guidewire is then overdrilled with a 6-mm drill bit to create a 20-mm tunnel, where the graft is secured using a 6-mm bioabsorbable interference screw.

A 1-cm longitudinal skin incision is made over the midpoint of the fibular head. A freehand pin is then drilled horizontally, parallel to the tibiofemoral joint line, crossing the fibular head and tibia to exit through the medial tibial cortex. This pin is subsequently overdrilled with a 6-mm drill bit.

The LCL graft is passed with a relay suture from the femoral to the fibular incision beneath the fascia lata, then guided through the fibular and tibial tunnels. It is secured with an EndoButton on the medial femoral cortex, with the knee positioned at 30° of flexion and slight valgus stress.

Results

Postoperative Instructions/Outcomes

Postoperative radiographs are performed to confirm accurate implant and tunnel positioning.

The postoperative rehabilitation protocol consists of knee immobilization with a hinged brace for 12 weeks, nonweightbearing for the first 6 weeks, and early full range-of-motion exercises focused on vastus medialis activation. Gradual return to sports 6 months after surgery depends on the results of isokinetic muscle testing and the sports retraining program. Expected outcomes are easy walking in daily life at 3 months, light physical activities at 6 months, and heavy physical activities at 12 months.

Key Points

The absence of arthrogenic muscle inhibition perioperatively is important to achieve a good functional outcome after rehabilitation. Keeping the PCL graft pedicled enhances graft healing, and the trans-septal approach allows for anatomical positioning of the tibial tunnels. During tibial tunnel drilling, the guide wires must be securely held to minimize the risk of neurovascular injury. The LCL guidewire should be inserted at the center of the fibular head and oriented parallel to the joint line to avoid peroneal nerve damage. The PCL, PLC, and LCL grafts are then tensioned sequentially with the knee in the reduced position.

Conclusion

This surgical technique provides an anatomical and minimally invasive multiligament knee reconstruction, avoiding extensive surgical approaches and common peroneal nerve dissection, while preserving cancellous bone and reducing the risk of tunnel-related complications.
