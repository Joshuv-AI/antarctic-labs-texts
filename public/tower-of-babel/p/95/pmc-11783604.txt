Complete Surgical Resection of a C3 Neuroenteric Cyst With Concurrent Cervical Fusion Deformity Through Posterior Midline Approach in the Lateral Position: Case Report
Authors: Jin Huang, Yuning Chen, Kefei Chen, Wei Ji, Junfei Shao, Jian Guan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND AND IMPORTANCE:Enterogenic cysts often occur along the spinal axis and are frequently associated with congenital spinal malformations. The special location of the lesion can limit the surgical resection to achieve complete removal. In this report, the authors describe a successful case of complete resection of a ventral enterogenic cyst in the cervical spinal cord using a posterior midline approach in the lateral position.CLINICAL PRESENTATION:This report discusses a case of a 36-year-old young female patient who presented with relatively short-term discomfort and pain in the left shoulder and neck area. MRI examination revealed a cystic mass located ventrally in the C3 cervical spinal cord. We performed a posterior surgical approach and, under the lateral position, the lesion was completely resected. The surgical treatment alleviated the neurological dysfunction. During the follow-up periods of 3 and 6 months, the patient recovered well, and magnetic resonance imaging scans showed no residual lesion.CONCLUSION:Complete surgical resection of the cyst wall is the only effective treatment for enterogenic cysts because partial resection or subtotal resection may lead to recurrence. For patients with concomitant spinal malformations, a posterior midline approach in the lateral position is a viable surgical option.

Neuroenteric cysts often occur concomitantly with malformations in corresponding vertebral regions, such as skull depression, craniocervical malformations, vertebral fusion, spina bifida, hemivertebra, meningocele, and scoliosis. This poses controversy regarding the surgical resection method for cervical medullary neuroenteric cysts. Here, we report a case of a C3 neuroenteric cyst resected through a posterior midline approach combined with lower resection under a microscope in the lateral position and discuss the management approach used. The patient consented to the publication of her image.

CLINICAL PRESENTATION

A 36-year-old female patient presented with a 4-week history of progressive left neck pain. Initial MRI scans on admission revealed a cystic mass located extramedullary and subdural at the C3 level, approximately 12 × 15 × 17 mm in size. This mass was situated on the ventral side of the spinal cord, causing compression and displacement. Further radiological evaluation with computed tomography scans demonstrated fusion deformity of the C4-6 vertebral bodies, along with the disappearance of the intervertebral space. Based on the imaging findings and clinical presentation, a diagnosis of neuroenteric (or enterogenous) cyst was suggested. However, clinical examination did not reveal any positive signs, and the patient had no significant medical history or related diagnostic and treatment processes (Figure 1).

Preoperative imaging data of the patient. A, Preoperative MRI of the spine shows an anteriorly placed central extramedullary cyst at the C3 level. B, The spinal cord was compressed by this ventrally located cyst. C, Computed tomography scans showed fusion deformity of C4-6.

We adopted a posterior surgical approach in the lateral position, resected the C2-3 laminae, and cut the dentate ligament. With opening the cyst, we gently tractioned the capsule and the lesion was completely resected (Video). Intraoperative electrophysiological neuromonitoring was used to maintain the integrity of neurological functions during surgery (Figure 2).

B, After resecting the dentate ligament, the cyst was gradually exposed. C, Without opening the cyst wall, the cyst was completely resected under microscopic.

The postoperative course was uneventful, and postoperative MRI confirmed complete removal of the cyst. The patient was discharged without neurological dysfunction. Follow-up at 6 months showed that the patient had resumed normal life, and MRI showed no residual lesions (Figure 3).

DISCUSSION

Enterogenous cysts in the central nervous system primarily occur within the spinal canal, accounting for 0.7% to 1.3% of spinal canal tumors.1,2 They are most commonly found in the cervical and upper thoracic segments, preferentially located on the ventral side of the spinal cord beneath the dura mater. However, enterogenous cysts often coexist with other congenital malformations, with vertebral deformities at the corresponding site being the most common, such as skull depression, craniovertebral junction malformation, vertebral fusion, spina bifida, hemivertebra, meningocele, and scoliosis.3 Moreover, enterogenous cysts are often closely adherent to the spinal cord, making complete resection challenging. Even if the cyst contents are completely released intraoperatively or most of the cyst wall is resected, residual cyst wall tissue with secretory function can easily lead to recurrence postoperatively. The posterior median approach in the prone position offers limited visualization of the cyst, often resulting in postoperative recurrence due to residual tissue.4,5 The specific challenge in approaching this anteriorly lies in the need for complete resection of the cyst membrane, particularly at the midline where the spinal cord is most vulnerable. While an anterior cervical corpectomy and fusion approach may provide better direct visualization for complete membrane resection and lower recurrence rates in patients without spinal fusion deformities, it would be technically challenging and procedurally complex in this patient because of the presence of concomitant fusion deformities. Performing the surgery in a prone position through a posterior midline approach would significantly hinder the ability to achieve complete resection of the cyst wall.

For cases with normal vertebral structures anterior to the cyst, anterior cervical corpectomy and fusion can be used to resect the cyst under direct vision, minimizing the risk of postoperative recurrence. In this case, the cyst was located ventrally at C3, slightly to the left side. By using a right lateral position, we were able to achieve better exposure of the lesion by leveraging the release of the dentate ligament and the assistance of gravity. This positioning allowed us to gently retract the cyst membrane while minimizing traction on the spinal cord, thus reducing potential iatrogenic injuries. By adjusting the surgical positioning and using skilled microsurgical techniques, we were able to fully exploit the natural intervertebral spaces and achieve complete resection of the cyst.

In this case, with vertebral fusion, a lateral position and skilled microsurgical techniques can be used to achieve complete resection of the cyst, leveraging the cyst's lateral deviation. This is a treatment approach worthy of promotion.

CONCLUSION

Surgical resection of the cyst is a precise and effective treatment approach. Complete removal of the cyst wall during the operation while avoiding damage to the spinal cord and neural structures can lead to a satisfactory clinical outcome for the patient. For patients with concomitant spinal deformities, individualized imaging features and intraoperative position adjustments can maximize the exposure of ventral cysts. However, skilled microsurgical techniques are crucial for achieving complete resection of the cyst.
