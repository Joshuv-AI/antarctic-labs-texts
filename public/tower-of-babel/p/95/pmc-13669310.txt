Comparative organ-wide analysis of age-related N-glycan alterations in mice reveals a link to lysosomal glycosidases
Authors: Keiko Akasaka-Manya, Hisatoshi Hanamatsu, Ikuko Yokota, Kazue Okada, Akihiro Fujita, Jun-ichi Furukawa, Tamao Endo, Hiroshi Manya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Glycosylation is an important post-translational modification, and glycan alterations reflect physiological and pathological states. However, whether common age-associated glycan changes occur across organs remains unclear. Here, we analyzed protein-bound N-glycans in serum, brain, lung, heart, liver, skeletal muscle, and kidney to identify aging-related alterations shared across tissues. Although glycan profiles were strongly organ-specific, age-dependent changes were observed in each organ. The proportion of paucimannose glycans increased in multiple organs and showed a significant positive correlation with age. The lysosomal glycosidases Hexa/Hexb, which are potentially involved in paucimannose glycan generation, were upregulated in the lung during aging. In cultured cells, elevated expression of these enzymes and increased paucimannose glycans were observed in senescent cells. These findings suggest that paucimannose glycans are closely associated with aging, although a causal role of HEXA/HEXB upregulation in their formation was not directly tested; they may also serve as indicators of cellular and organ aging.

Introduction

Aging affects a wide range of biological processes at the molecular, cellular, and tissue levels. Among various post-translational modifications, glycosylation plays a crucial role in maintaining cellular homeostasis and intercellular communication, both of which are often compromised during aging. Glycosylation is mediated by various glycosyltransferases, glycosidases, and sugar nucleotide donors 1,2, and influences protein structure and function, thereby regulating various biological processes such as signal transduction, adhesion, endocytosis, infection, immune responses, and cell migration3. Accordingly, alterations in protein-linked glycans may cause functional changes in cells, tissues, and organs, and affect pathophysiological states throughout the body. Protein glycosylation is defined by the diversity of glycans attached to specific protein sites, and these glycans are subject to changes in response to environmental factors2. Consequently, alterations in the cellular milieu associated with aging are likely to affect the glycan structure of glycoproteins and may render them sensitive indicators of age-related changes in cellular function. Specifically, the agalactosylated glycans of immunoglobulin G (IgG) increase, whereas the digalactosylated forms decrease with age4. As changes in IgG glycans alter the immune response, they may substantially contribute to the process of biological aging by regulating inflammation5. In addition to IgG, alterations in other immune-related glycans have been observed. For example, the branching of N-glycans on T cell surfaces increases with aging6, which suppresses T cell receptor clustering and signaling, thereby maintaining immune balance and preventing excessive activation7. Additionally, N-glycan alterations occur in various tissues, such as the brain, skin, and heart, with aging. An increase in oligomannose glycan levels has been observed in the brains of aged mice8 and rats9. Analyses using mouse epidermal stem cells have also reported age-related glycan changes, demonstrating that the oligomannose type decreases and is replaced by the complex glycans bearing α2,3- and α2,6-sialic acids with aging10. In the aging mouse heart, there is a decrease in complex glycans and an increase in the proportion of oligomannose glycans11. However, these reports only described changes in specific organs, whereas changes common to multiple organs have not been sufficiently elucidated.

The lungs play a critical role in gas exchange and immune regulation, contributing to the ability of organs to respond to pathogens and inflammatory stimuli while increasing its susceptibility to systemic aging12,13. Consequently, age-related changes in lung glycans may substantially affect immune responses and chronic inflammation, suggesting that the lung is a suitable model for studying age-associated diseases. Recent studies using asthma mouse models have reported alterations in glycan levels. Exposure to immunogens increased the selectin ligands, Fuc-α1,2-Gal and Fuc-α1,3-GlcNAc motifs, in airway epithelial cells. These glycan changes are suggested to be a direct response to immunogen exposure within the bronchi rather than a consequence of immune infiltration, and are likely to induce leukocyte infiltration and tissue damage14. However, the investigation of age-related glycan changes in the pulmonary tissue is still in its early stages. Therefore, studying glycosylation in the lungs could provide insight into how aging affects both organ function and immune regulation. To address this, we used the α-Klotho mouse to investigate aging-related changes in pulmonary glycosylation. This mouse model exhibits aging-related phenotypes similar to those observed in human senile lungs due to α-Klotho protein deficiency 15,16. The lungs of α-Klotho mice exhibit a homogeneous alveolar expansion pattern16 without accumulation of inflammatory cells, such as macrophages and lymphocytes17. In addition to the histopathological resemblance, biochemical alterations associated with aging, including µ-calpain activation, have also been reported in our previous report18. These features suggest that α-Klotho mice are a suitable model for examining age-related tissue remodeling. Therefore, although this study examined glycosylation changes across multiple organs, we placed particular emphasis on the lung as a representative tissue in which age-related structural and molecular alterations are clearly manifested.

This study aimed to identify common age-associated changes in protein-bound N-glycosylation across multiple organs. We analyzed N-glycans in serum, brain, lung, heart, liver, skeletal muscle, and kidney using mass spectrometry and examined glycan-related gene expression by RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). In addition, cultured cells were analyzed to gain mechanistic insights into these glycan alterations. Through these approaches, we sought to determine whether shared N-glycan alterations accompany aging across different organs and to interpret these changes within a biological context.

Results

N-glycosylation status of mice by age

Membrane proteins were extracted from the serum, brain, lung, heart, liver, skeletal muscle, and kidney of 6-, 12-, 20-, and 24-month-old mice. Protein-bound N-glycans were enzymatically released and analyzed using MALDI-TOF MS, yielding 264 quantified peaks. The proposed structures and their m/z values are listed in Supplementary Table 1. For convenience, each peak (tentatively named G1–G264) was assigned in ascending order of m/z values, with minor deviations from the strict numerical order. The probable structures of the N-glycans were estimated based on the m/z values.

The N-glycans in each age group were classified into the following nine groups: short-glycans (Short), oligomannose glycans (OM), neutral hybrid glycans (Hyb_n), neutral complex glycans (Com_n), sialylated hybrid glycans (Hyb_a), sialylated complex glycans (Com_a), sialylated complex or hybrid glycans (Com_a/Hyb_a), sialylated complex or neutral hybrid glycans (Com_a/Hyb_n), and neutral complex or sialylated complex glycans (Com_n/Com_a). N-glycans that could not be classified as oligomannose, hybrid, or complex types were categorized as short-glycans (Short) for convenience in analysis. For a number of peaks, the m/z values indicated the presence of two or more structural possibilities. Consequently, Com_a/Hyb_a, Com_a/Hyb_n, and Com_n/Com_a were not assigned to a single group (Supplementary Table 1). The Com_a/Hyb_a group was detected in each organ at levels ranging from 0.5 to 15%, whereas Com_a/Hyb_n and Com_n/Com_a were present only at very low or undetectable levels (0%) (Fig. 1). Each organ exhibited a distinct compositional pattern in terms of the relative abundance of N-glycan groups (Fig. 1). OM is the most abundant glycan type in organs, whereas Com_a is predominant in serum. Although age-related changes were observed in each organ, the overall organ-specific compositional patterns were largely preserved. Notably, the total N-glycan content also remained largely unchanged, except in the lung and kidney (ρ = −0.605 and −0.853, respectively). Age-related changes in N-glycan composition were evaluated using effect sizes (η²) derived from Wilks’ lambda in multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA; Supplementary Table 2). Although the organ-specific N-glycan patterns are generally stable, certain glycan groups exhibit significant age-related changes. Among the organs examined, the majority exhibited significant age-related changes in glycan composition, with an effect size (η²) of approximately 0.4. In contrast, the kidney demonstrated a comparatively modest change (η² = 0.25). These findings suggest that, although the overall N-glycan patterns remain stable during the aging process, individual glycan groups can undergo substantial alterations within specific organs. In summary, this analysis revealed that the brain, liver, and serum exhibit the most pronounced age-related changes, whereas the kidneys exhibit the least variation.

Correlation between relative N-glycan abundance and age

To investigate age-related changes, Spearman’s correlation coefficients were calculated between age and the relative abundance of each of the nine N-glycan groups (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 3). In the brain, skeletal muscle, and lung, short-glycans exhibited the strongest positive correlations with age, with relative glycan abundance significantly increased (ρ = 0.923, 0.830, and 0.791, respectively). Although not statistically significant, short-glycans also showed a moderate positive correlation with age in the heart and serum (ρ = 0.436 and 0.481, respectively). In the liver, an evident increase was observed between 20 and 24 months of age, although the short-glycan group did not show any correlation with age.

As previously stated, the short-glycan category includes multiple short-chain structures, such as G15, whose origins are not clearly defined and are not necessarily a homogeneous category. Consequently, we conducted a separate analysis of paucimannose glycans (PM) to determine whether the increase in short-glycans was driven specifically by PM or other short -glycan species (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 3). This analysis revealed that the increase in the short-glycan group associated with aging was largely explained by PM, and that the short-glycan group and PM exhibited nearly identical patterns of increase. This finding indicates that PM glycans represent a distinct glycan class that demonstrates an age-related response, exhibiting the strongest positive correlation with aging in the brain, skeletal muscle, and lungs, and a moderate positive correlation in the kidney and serum. Since the short-glycan group comprises structurally heterogeneous species, subsequent biological interpretations were restricted to the PM group.

In the brain, short-glycans and PM were the only glycan groups that showed a correlation with age, whereas in the liver, only the Com_a/Hyb_a ratio showed an age-dependent correlation. In the skeletal muscle, the relative abundance of OM and Com_a glycans significantly decreased with age (ρ = −0.923 and −0.683, respectively). In the lung, short-glycans, PM, and OM glycans increased with age (ρ = 0.791, 0.752, and 0.644, respectively), whereas sialylated glycans, including Hyb_a, Com_a, and the Com_a/Hyb_a ratio, decreased and showed strong negative correlations (ρ = −0.683, −0.675, and −0.613, respectively), suggesting that the abundance of shorter glycans increases, while glycans with more complex sialylated structures decrease with age.

Overall, age-dependent changes in glycan proportion were most prominent for short-glycans or PM, which showed positive correlations with age in multiple organs, suggesting that the relative abundance of these glycan groups may serve as a potential indicator of aging status in certain tissues.

Relative abundance of individual N-glycans at each age

Changes in the proportion of individual glycans in each group were analyzed, and the results are presented in Supplementary Table 4. Regarding the glycans that showed statistically significant changes, shorter glycans tended to increase with age, whereas more highly branched complex glycans tended to decrease.

In the PM, glycans containing one to three mannose residues correlated well with aging in several organs (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 4). In the heart and serum, none of the individual PM glycans showed a statistically significant increase, although the total PM group showed a moderate correlation with age. Conversely, in the liver, where the total PM group did not show a correlation with age, G5 and G7 glycans exhibited a statistically significant increase. In the skeletal muscle, shorter G2 glycans were observed most frequently within the PM group compared to other organs. The relative abundance of N-glycans in the lungs of 6-week-old wild-type and α-Klotho mice was also analyzed (Fig. 3b). The N-glycan profile in α-Klotho mice was largely comparable to that in aged mice (Fig. 1), with the oligomannose glycans having the highest proportion. Additionally, the significantly elevated proportion of paucimannose glycans observed in α-Klotho mice, relative to that in wild-type mice was comparable to that observed in aging mice (Fig. 3c). However, some differences were observed between the two groups. Neutral complex glycans were found in higher proportions in wild-type and α-Klotho mice than in aged mice (Figs. 1 and 3b). These differences may partly reflect the younger age of the wild-type and α-Klotho mice (6 weeks old). Moreover, the relative abundance of Com_a and Hyb_a declined during aging (Fig. 1); however, α-Klotho mice exhibited relatively higher proportions of these glycans (Fig. 3b). Overall, these variations are likely attributable to differences in genetic background as the wild-type and α-Klotho mice were of mixed C3H/J and C57BL/6J background, whereas the mice used for the aging study were C57BL/6NCr.

Paucimannose glycan levels increased with both aging and α-Klotho deficiency. Therefore, the relative abundances of the individual paucimannose glycans were compared in detail (Fig. 3c). The findings revealed that G5(PM2F1) was the most abundant in all lung samples, and its content was markedly high in aged and α-Klotho mice. In summary, α-Klotho deficiency may induce lung N-glycan alterations similar to those observed in aging.

Factors contributing to the increase in paucimannose glycans during lung aging

The mechanism underlying the age-dependent increase in paucimannose glycans was further investigated by analyzing the expression of the genes involved in their biosynthesis. The biosynthetic pathway of paucimannose glycans remains poorly understood; however, previous studies have implicated the dimeric N-acetyl-β-hexosaminidase complexes A (Hex A) and B (Hex B) as key enzymes in paucimannose generation22. Hex enzymes have been proposed to influence the balance between complex- and paucimannose-type N-glycans during glycan processing, prompting us to examine their expression levels in the context of age-related glycan changes. Because Hex A and Hex B differ in their subunit composition, which potentially affects their activity and regulation, we examined the expression of both enzymes using RNA-seq and cultured cell analyses. In addition, α-mannosidase 2B1 (MAN2B1), another lysosomal enzyme implicated in paucimannose formation, was included in the analysis. The genes encoding the α and β subunits of β-hexosaminidase (Hexa and Hexb) and Man2b1 exhibited positive correlations with age (ρ = 0.389, 0.626, and 0.626, respectively), although these correlations did not reach statistical significance after False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction (Fig. 4). Additionally, the expression of several lysosomal enzymes, such as Glb1 and Man2b1, increased with age, consistent with the age-associated expansion of lysosomal content. Many paucimannose-modified proteins are lysosomal or lysosome-related; therefore, the increased lysosomal activity may further contribute to elevated levels of paucimannose glycans in aged lungs. This finding indicates that the increase in PM glycans in the lungs is partially attributable to age-related changes in the expression of specific glycosidases. Furthermore, to examine broader transcriptional changes, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) using RNA-seq data revealed that genes associated with the immune system were among the most differentially expressed (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Increased expression of HEXA and HEXB in senescent cells

To assess whether the increased abundance of paucimannose glycans and related enzymes results from cellular senescence, aging experiments were conducted using cultured cells. Human small airway epithelial cells (SAECs) derived from the lung parenchyma and normal human lung fibroblasts (NHLFs) derived from the lung interstitium were induced to undergo senescence using the DNA damage-inducing agents, doxycycline (Dox) and etoposide (Eto), or a reactive oxygen species (ROS)-inducing agent, D-galactose (D-gal).

Increased GLB1 expression was observed in the SAECs, indicating the onset of cellular senescence. As shown in Fig. 5a, these cells exhibited significantly elevated expression of HEXA and HEXB compared with that in cells that had not been treated (i.e., no treat cells). GAPDH expression remained constant across all experimental groups. Notably, an increased proportion of paucimannose glycans was confirmed in DNA-damaged cells but not in ROS-inducing cells (Fig. 5b). In NHLFs, the expression of both HEXA and HEXB increased, similar to that observed in SAECs (Fig. 5c, d). However, in contrast to SAECs, NHLFs exhibited a more pronounced increase in HEXA than HEXB. The proportion of paucimannose glycans increased in DNA-damaged cells, as observed in SAECs, although their paucimannose glycan patterns differed, with G7 being the most prevalent. In summary, aging may directly induce changes in the expression of HEXA, HEXB, and paucimannose glycans. However, an increase in HEXA/B levels was not associated with increased PM levels in ROS-induced aging, suggesting that glycan changes may be pathway dependent.

Discussion

In this study, we identified a common age-associated increase in paucimannose glycans in serum and multiple organs, such as skeletal muscle, brain, and lung. Paucimannose glycan levels in these tissues showed strong positive correlations with age. Notably, the lungs of α-Klotho mice, an established model of accelerated aging, also exhibited increased levels of paucimannose glycans, supporting the association between paucimannose glycan elevation and biological aging. Furthermore, experiments using cultured cells have demonstrated that the expression of enzymes implicated in paucimannose glycan formation, namely HEXA and HEXB, were upregulated during cellular senescence. The upregulation of HEXA and HEXB suggests enhanced lysosomal activity in aged tissues, consistent with previous reports of lysosomal expansion during aging23, suggesting that organ-specific aging dynamics may influence the timing and magnitude of glycan alterations.

Although statistically significant increases in paucimannose glycans were not observed in the heart, kidneys, or liver, all three organs exhibited a trend toward elevation. This organ-specific variation may reflect differences in the rate of biological aging, as proteomic analyses have suggested that aging progresses at different speeds across organs12, with the heart reported to undergo slower aging relative to chronological age or blood24.

In addition to paucimannose glycans, age-related changes in fucosylation were also observed, although these varied across organs. For example, FUT8 expression increased in aged and α-Klotho mouse livers, consistent with elevated serum levels of core-fucosylated N-glycans21. Consistent with these findings, we observed increased levels of total fucosylated glycans in the liver and serum in the present study, whereas comparable increases were not detected in other organs.

Notably, previous studies have reported increased levels of paucimannose glycans in the aging brain8. In particular, synapsin-1 carries higher levels of paucimannose residues in aged mice without changes in total protein expression, indicating that glycosylation can be altered independently of protein abundance. These findings are consistent with our observations and collectively suggest that while certain glycan alterations represent common aging-associated trends, their magnitude and organ specificity vary according to the organ-dependent aging progression and tissue context.

Accumulating evidence suggests that paucimannose glycan levels increase under conditions of altered tissue physiology. Elevated levels of paucimannose glycans, particularly core-fucosylated species, have been observed in lung cancer 25,26. Moreover, lung cancer tissues show significant increases in paucimannose and oligomannose N-glycans, accompanied by reductions in sialylated complex-type structures 27,28. Notably, similar alterations have been detected in the bronchoalveolar space of long-term smokers, even in the absence of overt malignancy29. In colorectal cancer, elevated paucimannosylation has also been reported, especially in advanced stages30.

In line with these observations, we found increased paucimannose glycans in the lungs of α-Klotho mice, a model of systemic premature aging characterized by tissue atrophy, emphysematous lung changes, and metabolic dysregulation. This elevation may reflect age- or stress-related tissue deterioration and impaired homeostatic regulation, supporting a link between increased paucimannose glycans and compromised tissue integrity during aging.

Beyond serving as indicators of tissue integrity, paucimannose glycans may also play functional roles. Complement C3 has been reported to carry paucimannose structures, and such glycans have been implicated in inflammation and cell proliferation31. Elastase in neutrophil azurophilic granules contains paucimannose glycans and may modulate immune responses during infection and inflammatory processes32. In the developing mouse brain, paucimannose glycans are transiently detected in the subventricular zone, and antibodies against these glycans inhibit neural progenitor cell proliferation33. These observations suggest that paucimannose glycans participate in immune regulation and cellular growth. Whether similar mechanisms contribute to tissue remodeling or functional decline during aging warrants further investigation.

Mechanistically, paucimannose glycans are thought to arise primarily through trimming reactions mediated by β-hexosaminidases. Hex B has been implicated in paucimannose formation in colorectal cancer cells30, and suppression of HEX activity has been shown to reduce paucimannose levels in certain contexts. Our observation that HEXB expression increases with age in the lung supports the involvement of lysosomal β-hexosaminidases in age-associated glycan remodeling. However, other pathways, including mannosidase-mediated trimming, have also been proposed to contribute to paucimannose generation under specific conditions, and the relative contributions of these mechanisms in aging tissues remain unclear.

The activities of lysosomal glycosidases, including N-acetyl-β-hexosaminidase, β-galactosidase, mannosidase, fucosidase, and β-glucuronidase, have been reported to increase in senescent cells34. Aging and cellular senescence are also accompanied by lysosomal expansion, altered autophagic flux, and changes in degradative capacity. In this context, increased HEXA and HEXB expression may reflect enhanced lysosomal remodeling in aged tissues. Such upregulation of β-hexosaminidase could promote increased trimming of N-glycans, thereby contributing to the accumulation of paucimannose structures.

Lysosomal enzymes, including β-hexosaminidase, are components of the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), which contributes to chronic inflammation and tissue remodeling35. Consistent with our findings, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed enrichment of immune- and inflammation-related pathways in aged lung tissue (Supplementary Fig. 4). These findings raise the possibility that increased lysosomal activity during senescence may both reflect degradative remodeling and drive inflammation-associated glycan alterations in aging tissues. Supporting this idea, previous studies have reported positive associations between paucimannose glycans and inflammatory mediators 36–38. Therefore, enhanced HEX expression and lysosomal activity during senescence and inflammation may contribute to the accumulation of paucimannose glycans in aging tissues.

Several limitations should be considered. First, analyses were performed using whole organs, and the specific cell types and glycoprotein substrates responsible for the observed increases in paucimannose glycans remain unknown. Because the carrier proteins of paucimannose structures were not identified, we cannot exclude the possibility that increased levels reflect selective upregulation of specific glycoproteins rather than widespread alterations in N-glycan processing. In addition, as the present study is based on relative compositional glycan abundances rather than absolute quantification, the apparent increase in paucimannose glycans may partly reflect reciprocal changes in other glycan species. Second, although HEX expression and paucimannose glycans increased in parallel, direct causality has not been demonstrated. Functional experiments manipulating HEX activity will be required to determine whether elevated HEX expression is sufficient to drive paucimannose accumulation in aging tissues. Third, because MS/MS analysis was not performed, structural isomers could not be resolved, and precise monosaccharide linkages could not be determined; thus, glycan changes were evaluated at the compositional level. Fourth, this study primarily utilized the C57BL/6NCr strain, and validation in additional genetic backgrounds is necessary to determine the generalizability of these findings. Notably, although α-Klotho mice (C3H/J × C57BL/6J background) exhibited differences in overall glycan composition compared with C57BL/6NCr mice, a similar increase in paucimannose glycans was observed, supporting the robustness of this specific alteration.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that age-related increases in paucimannose glycans across multiple organs are associated with elevated HEX expression, lysosomal remodeling, and inflammatory changes. These results support the concept that paucimannose glycans may serve as indicators of tissue deterioration and altered homeostasis during aging, and may represent a glycomic signature of biological aging.

Methods

Ethical approval

All experiments using laboratory animals were approved by the Animal Experiment Committee of the Tokyo Metropolitan Institute for Geriatrics and Gerontology (permit number: 21011) and conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals39. This was in accordance with the Animal Research Reporting In Vivo Experiments guidelines 40,41. Animals were assigned to experimental groups according to age and genotype; therefore, randomization was not applicable. Blinding was not performed during sample collection or data analysis.

Mice organ preparation

C57BL/6NCr mice were housed at the animal facilities of the Tokyo Metropolitan Institute for Geriatrics and Gerontology (Tokyo, Japan) in a specific pathogen-free environment under controlled conditions (22 °C, with a 12-h light-dark cycle, relative humidity, 55 ± 10%, with ad libitum access to food and water). Prior to tissue collection, mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of a mixed anesthetic cocktail consisting of medetomidine (0.75 mg/kg), midazolam (4 mg/kg), and butorphanol (5 mg/kg). Adequate anesthesia was confirmed by the absence of a response to tail pinch. Under deep anesthesia, mice were transcardially perfused with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and euthanized during the perfusion procedure. The serum, lungs, liver, brain, kidney, heart, and skeletal muscle (gastrocnemius muscle) were harvested from male C57BL/6NCr mice at 6, 12, 20, and 24 months (n = 5 per group). For RNA-seq, 6-, 12-, 20-, and 30-month-old (n = 3 per group) were used. Additionally, the lungs were collected from 6-week-old male α-Klotho and wild-type mice (n = 4). The organs were frozen immediately in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. Some lungs were dehydrated using a series of ethanol solutions, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at a thickness of 8 µm. The sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin for histological analysis. All animals collected for this study were subjected to the planned analyses. No animals, samples, or data points were excluded from the study.

Cell culture

SAECs were cultured in Small Airway Epithelial Cell Growth Medium (SAGM; Lonza Bioscience, Walkersville, MD, USA), and NHLFs were cultured in Fibroblast Growth Medium-2 (FGM-2; LONZA) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Cells were seeded at 3 × 105 cells per 10-cm dish. Cellular aging was induced by adding doxorubicin, etoposide, or D-gal at concentrations of 100 nM, 3 µM, and 5 mg/mL, respectively, 24 to 48 h after seeding. The medium was substituted after a 72-h period, and cells were cultivated for an additional 3 days. The cells were harvested and homogenized in a solution of 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 1 mM EDTA, 250 mM sucrose, and a protease inhibitor (Nacalai Tesque, Inc., Kyoto, Japan). After centrifugation at 900×g for 10 min, the supernatant was used as the lysate. The protein concentration was determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Tissue homogenization and protein extraction

Mouse tissues were individually placed in 3 mL of frozen-crushing tubes (ST-0320PCF, YASUI KIKAI, Osaka, Japan) containing 2 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The tubes were stored at −80 °C until use. After freezing, a metal cone was added to each tube, and tissues were disrupted twice using a Multi Beads Shocker MB3200 (YASUI KIKAI) at 3000 rpm for 30 s. Lungs were homogenized in PBS using a bead crusher (TAITEC, Saitama, Japan).

The homogenized suspension (200 µL) was transferred to a 1.5-mL microtube, and 800 µL of cold ethanol was added for precipitation. Samples were centrifuged at 14,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C to separate protein and lipid fractions. Protein pellets were collected and resuspended in H2O. Protein concentration was determined using Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kits (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For liver and skeletal muscle samples, 1 mg of total protein was washed with 10% trichloroacetic acid to remove glycogen contamination.

Cell homogenization and protein extraction

The cultured NHLFs and SAECs were suspended in 200 µL of PBS and disrupted using an ultrasonic homogenizer (TAITEC, Saitama, Japan). To extract the proteins, a fourfold volume of cold ethanol was added. After centrifugation, the resulting pellet was dissolved in H2O, and the protein concentration was measured using Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kits.

Preparation of N-glycans by glycoblotting combined with aminolysis-SALSA

Protein suspensions (corresponding to 100 µg proteins) derived from tissues, cultured cells, and sera were treated with 500 mM TCEP and 100 mM 2-iodoacetamide in ammonium bicarbonate buffer containing 0.1% Triton X-100. After reductive alkylation, the proteins were digested with 10 mg/mL of trypsin at 37 °C for 3 h. To inactivate the trypsin, the mixture was heated at 90 °C for 15 min. Deglycosylation was performed by adding 2 U of peptide N-glycosidase F (PNGase F; Roche, Mannheim, Germany). After incubating at 37 °C for 16 h, N-glycans were subjected to glycoblotting combined with the aminolysis-SALSA method42–44. The glycan sample, which contained the internal standard disialyloctasaccharide (A2GN1; designated as H2N2(6S)2 + H3N1 in this study; see Supplementary Table 1), was transferred to the wells of a MultiScreen Solvinert filter plate (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) containing Blot Glyco beads (5 mg). After capturing the glycans, the beads were washed with 2 M guanidine hydrochloride, H2O, and 1% triethylamine in methanol. Excess hydrazide groups on the beads were inactivated by acetylation using 10% acetic anhydride in methanol (MeOH). The beads were subsequently washed with 10 mM HCl, MeOH, and dimethyl sulfoxide, followed by the addition of SialoCapper-ID Kit Reagents A and B (Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan). Plates were shaken using a microplate mixer (TOMY, Tokyo, Japan). After removing the excess amidation solution, the beads were washed with MeOH, SialoCapper-ID Kit Reagent C, MeOH, and H2O. Finally, the glycans were released from the beads and labeled with aoWR through an imine exchange reaction. Any excess aoWR reagent was removed using an HILIC elution plate before matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF-MS) analysis.

MALDI-TOF MS analysis

Purified glycan solution (excluding the lung samples from α-Klotho mice) was mixed with a solution of 2,5-dihydroxybenzoic acid (10 mg/mL in 30% acetonitrile)42 and analyzed using a Bruker UltrafleXtreme MALDI-TOF MS equipped with a reflector and controlled using the FlexControl 3.4 software (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany). The spectra were collected in the reflectron mode, with an acceleration voltage of 20 kV, a reflector voltage of 20.8 kV, and a pulsed-ion extraction time of 320 ns in the positive-ion mode. Mass data were annotated using the FlexAnalysis 3.4 software.

Lung samples from α-Klotho mice were analyzed using a Bruker Ultraflex II TOF/TOF mass spectrometer equipped with a reflector and controlled using FlexControl 3.0 software (Bruker Daltonics). The spectra were collected in the reflectron mode, with an acceleration voltage of 25 kV, a reflector voltage of 26.3 kV, and a pulsed-ion extraction time of 160 ns in the positive-ion mode. Mass data were annotated using the FlexAnalysis 3.0 software.

For absolute quantitation, the areas of the MS signals from each N-glycan were compared to the area of the signal from a known amount of internal standard (A2GN1; H2N2(6S)2 + H3N1).

Fucosylation was evaluated at the compositional level by summing the relative abundances of all glycan compositions assigned as potentially containing at least one fucose residue (Supplementary Table 1).

RNA-seq and gene set enrichment analysis

Total RNA was extracted from mice (n = 3) aged 6-, 12-, 20-, and 30-months using ISOGEN II (Nippon Gene, Tokyo, Japan), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The purity and concentration of the extracted RNA were assessed using electrophoresis with a Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Inc., CA, USA). mRNA was purified from the total RNA sample using the poly-A selection method. First- and second-strand cDNA were synthesized and purified. After the ends of the double-stranded cDNA were blunted and phosphorylated, and the 3′-dA protruding ends were treated, a sequencing adapter containing dUTP was attached. Selective cleavage of dUTP in the adapter and second-strand cDNA using the USER enzyme (New England Biolabs Ltd., MA, USA) yielded a template library (strand-specific library) synthesized from strand-specific DNA with directional information. Sequencing was performed using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencing system (Illumina, Inc., CA, USA). The quality of raw paired-end sequence reads was evaluated using FastQC (version 0.11.7). Low-quality bases and adapter sequences were removed using Trimmomatic software (version 0.38). The trimmed reads were aligned to the reference genome using the RNA-seq aligner, HISAT2 (version 2.1.0). The frequency of the uniquely mapped reads was estimated using feature counts (version 1.6.3). GSEA was performed using the gene sets provided by MsigDB (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org) to determine the statistical significance of molecular pathways in 30- and 6-month-old mice.

Immunoblot analysis

Samples with an equal protein concentration were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis using 10% TGX gels (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., CA, USA). Subsequently, proteins were transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. Membranes were blocked in TPBS containing 5% skim milk for 30 min at 25 °C. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated with a primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. After three washes with TPBS, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-linked secondary antibodies (Cytiva, MA, USA) for 1 h at 25 °C. The membranes were then washed again. Proteins bound to the antibody were visualized using the Clarity Max kit (Bio-Rad). The expression level of the target molecule was determined by quantifying the bands in the western blots. GAPDH was used as an internal loading control, and the signal intensity of each target protein was normalized to that of the corresponding GAPDH band.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.12.12) at Google Colaboratory. All statistical tests were two-sided. Exact p values are reported unless otherwise specified. All measurements were obtained from independent biological samples. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used to evaluate the correlation (ρ) between glycan abundance and age using SciPy (version 1.16.3). The same method was used to analyze the correlation between gene expression and age.

Glycan abundance was analyzed by setting the total glycan level for each individual to 100% and quantifying the proportion of glycans. This study evaluated the correlation between age and the relative proportions of individual glycans, as well as between age and the relative proportions of seven structurally defined glycan groups. In both analyses, p values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg method implemented in Statsmodels (version 0.14.6), and FDR-adjusted p values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Age-related changes in N-glycan abundance were analyzed separately for each organ using MANOVA implemented in Statsmodels (version 0.14.6). The nine glycan groups were treated as dependent variables, and age was treated as a categorical independent variable. Wilks’ lambda (λ) was used as the test statistic. The F values, numerator and denominator degrees of freedom (df1 and df2, respectively), and p values were calculated using the multivariate approximation implemented in the Statsmodels library. Effect size (η²) was estimated from Wilks’ λ.

For RNA-seq analysis, given the limited sample size (n = 12, n = 3 per age group at 6, 12, 20, and 30 months), multiple testing corrections were not applied. Raw p values are reported, acknowledging the potential for false positives.

Welch’s t-test was performed using SciPy for comparisons between α-Klotho and wild-type mice, as well as between treated and untreated cells. P values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg method, and adjusted p values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Data manipulation and numerical computations were performed using Pandas (version 2.2.2) and NumPy (version 2.0.2). Linear regression analysis and R² calculations were performed using Scikit-learn (version 1.6.1). Figures were generated using Matplotlib (version 3.10.0) and Seaborn (version 0.13.2) software.
