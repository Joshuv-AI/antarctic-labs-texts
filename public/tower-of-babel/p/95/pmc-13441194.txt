Refracting identity: Scaffolding professional identity formation across the continuum
Authors: Ardi Findyartini, Nadia Greviana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Refracting identity: Integrating scaffolding of #identity across the continuum highlights the importance of integrating the roles of physicians and other health professionals #MedEd

Professional identity formation is a critical component for professional development, where medical students learn to “think, feel and act like a physician”. 1 Cruess et al. described socialisation as the process by which an individual interacts with environmental factors including role models, clinical experiences and exposure to the healthcare system and its personnel. 1 , 2 While professional identity formation traditionally encompassed a uniprofessional identity, Zarezadeh et al. extended this discourse by arguing that interprofessional identity should also be reflected. 3 Given the increased importance of interprofessional collaborative practice in healthcare, interprofessional identity—the internalisation of professional and interprofessional values required for collaborative practice—should be further elaborated as an inseparable part of professional development. 4

In this issue, Miller et al. provide a compelling conceptualisation of how medical students navigate the complex landscape of professional identity formation through interactions with other health professionals (OHPs). 5 By framing these interactions as both a mirror (reflecting present readiness) and a prism (refining future visions), the authors illuminate a path towards a more collaborative and interdependent physician identity. The findings show how interactions with OHPs are transformative, often more accessible, and provide a safe harbour for students to contribute. 5 However, such interactions may also risk the necessary uniprofessional modelling required to internalise specific responsibilities of physicians.

Uniprofessional interaction provides the skeleton of a professional role, while interprofessional interaction serves as the flesh that collaboratively makes the skeleton functional. Contact theory, which posits that meaningful social interactions can facilitate the achievement of common goals, helps explain how professional silos can be dissolved through interactions with OHPs. 6 However, it is important that we do not lose sight of core expectations of physician responsibilities. 7

Socialisation views professional identity formation as a continuous process that occurs within a community, emphasising the importance of professional interactions in providing access to role models and mentors in the workplace. 1 , 2 However, interactions with OHPs may also represent a critical component of workplace socialisation. Therefore, balancing both uniprofessional and interprofessional exposures may be an integral mechanism in the development of a well‐rounded professional identity.

Socialisation also explicitly mentions the role of reflection in professional identity formation. This is in line with the mirror metaphor where reflection becomes the essential mechanism in the interaction process among OHPs. For medical students to look in the OHP mirror and elaborate their readiness to contribute, they must recognise their: personal identities, which refer to their innate values and sense of self (personal); professional identity, which refers to the evolving sense of being a physician (professional); and interprofessional identity, which refers to understanding their role as a member of a multidisciplinary team (relational/interprofessional). 3 At the heart of the triad identity reflection during interactions with other OHPs lies intellectual humility, 8 which enables students to identify their meaningful contributions while maintaining an openness to learning from OHPs without feelings of superiority or inadequacy. During these interactions, students with strong intellectual humility are able to balance professional assertiveness with receptiveness to feedback. They proactively engage in patient care while being aware of their limitations and need for supervision. 9

The participation of medical students in the workplace, that is, in patient care settings, is a critical process of becoming medical doctors. Learning in the workplace requires preparedness of the students, support from the environment and facilitation of supervisors. 10 The role of OHPs in medical students' professional identity formation and competence attainment is invaluable, particularly in the different stages of cognitive apprenticeship: modelling, coaching, scaffolding and articulation. 11 Further reflection and exploration can be facilitated not only by consultant medical doctors but also by other OHPs, ensuring learning is comprehensive and impactful with respect to role understanding and their ability to envision future required learning experiences. In other words, while OHPs play a substantial role in medical students' workplace‐based learning and professional identity formation, 5 these processes require comprehensive support and consistent facilitation from both OHPs and medical professionals.

Miller et al.'s 5 findings elaborate how learning in the workplace with OHPs provides opportunities for rich experiences and further socialisation of medical students. The ability to engage in the refractive process through a prism requires readiness and being well equipped toward collaboration and interdependence. An intentional scaffolding approach should be explicitly designed to support students who may not be as self‐reflective. 12 Without this support, the complexity of interprofessional interdependence may feel like a threat to their developing professional identity rather than an invitation to collaborate.

Finally, we note that these findings move away from the false dichotomy that places professional and interprofessional identities in opposition. The metaphorical use of mirror and prism demonstrates that interprofessional interactions are not merely social encounters but are fundamental to how students construct their professional self. However, without intentional scaffolding, grounded in developmental readiness, self‐determination and self‐reflection, as well as ensuring that the workplace supports both uniprofessional and interprofessional exposure as integral mechanisms, these interactions remain at the mercy of the hidden curriculum. We appreciate the authors' 5 move towards a constructivist understanding of professional identity formation and agree that future research should explore how to integrate professional and interprofessional identities into a unified professional identity where collaboration is an intrinsic part of what it means to be a physician. We also call for deeper investigation into the mechanisms of guided reflection and scaffolding that allow students with varying developmental baselines to internalise professional humility and interdependence, especially in designing the interactions with OHPs in various learning contexts.
