Accelerometer-derived moderate-to-vigorous physical activity patterns and incident dry eye disease: a prospective UK Biobank cohort study
Authors: Aixia Jin, Rong Zhang, Xinran Fan, Furong Li, Yanting Feng, Li Zeng, Jian Wang, Daoli Liu, Wei Chi, Ning Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo explore the associations between three accelerometer-derived moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) patterns and the risk of dry eye disease (DED).MethodsThis prospective cohort study included UK Biobank participants who provided a full week of accelerometer-derived MVPA data collected between June 8, 2013, and December 30, 2015. Participants were categorized according to weekly MVPA volume using WHO guidelines (≥ 150 min/week): inactive (< 150 min), active weekend warrior (active WW, ≥ 150 min with ≥ 50% of weekly MVPA accumulated within 1–2 days), and active regular (≥ 150 min but not meeting active WW criterion). The association between MVPA patterns and the risk of DED was assessed using multifactorial Cox models and restricted cubic spline models.ResultsA total of 84,065 participants (55.82 ± 7.84 years; 54.13% women) without DED at baseline were included. During the follow-up period, 726 individuals developed DED. In multivariable-adjusted models, higher weekly MVPA was significantly associated with a lower risk of incident DED (P < 0.0001). The association was non-linear (P < 0.0001), with the steepest decline in risk observed between 0 and approximately 150 min/week, beyond which the risk plateaued. Compared to the inactive group, both active WW (hazard ratio [HR] 0.83, 95% CI 0.70–0.99) and active regular (HR 0.76, 95% CI 0.61–0.94) patterns were associated with a significantly lower risk of DED, with no evidence of a difference in protection between the two active patterns (P for difference = 0.894). Importantly, this trend persisted across multiple definitions of the WW pattern in the sensitivity analyses. Specially, subgroup analysis revealed a significant interaction by sex (P-interaction = 0.002), with women showing a marked protective association (active WW: HR 0.76 [0.62–0.94]; active regular: HR 0.57 [0.43–0.75]), while no significant association was observed in men (active WW: HR 1.14 [0.79–1.63]; active regular: HR 1.36 [0.93–2.01]).ConclusionsAchieving guideline-recommended MVPA levels is associated with a lower risk of DED. The benefit is similar for WW and active regular patterns, and is more pronounced in women. Future studies are warranted to explore the underlying biological mechanisms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-26795-6.

Introduction

Dry eye disease (DED) is a prevalent, multifactorial ocular surface disease characterized by tear film instability and ocular inflammation, resulting in chronic subjective discomfort and visual disturbances [1–3]. Global DED prevalence varies from 5% to 50%, with a higher incidence in females and older adults [4, 5]. Due to population aging, increased video display terminal use, and air pollution, the global prevalence of DED has risen sharply, affecting approximately 750 million people in 2022, and is projected to reach 800 million by 2027. Consequently, the global economic burden of DED is substantial, estimated to reach United States Dollar (USD) 6 billion annually in 2025, and expected to rise to USD 7 billion by 2027 [6]. Furthermore, DED significantly impacts the productivity of digital device users [7]. However, current treatments (e.g., artificial tears) primarily relieve symptoms and lack curative efficacy [8, 9]. Hence, the need to identify modifiable risk factors and interventions that may combat DED development is increasingly urgent.

Recent studies indicate DED is a lifestyle-related disease, suggesting interventions like physical activity (PA), diet, and sleep may be effective for prevention or treatment [10–14]. PA is regarded as favorable for health and is consistently associated with lower risks of death and various chronic diseases [15–17]. Keen interest exists in whether PA may affect chronic ophthalmic conditions such as DED. Several studies have demonstrated that short bouts of PA can acutely and transiently alleviate subjective dry eye symptoms by increasing tear secretion and improving tear film stability [18–20]. Biological evidence supports a plausible link between PA and DED. Exercise-induced myokines such as irisin exert anti-oxidative and anti-inflammatory effects via Akt, ERK1/2, integrin–AMPK, and NLRP3 pathways [21], while PA also influences metabolic function and neurotrophic signaling [22], which are relevant to the pathogenesis of DED [23]. However, the long-term effects of PA on DED incidence and the optimal PA frequency and volume remain unclear.

In addition, DED occurs more frequently in women than in men [24], which is largely attributed to the regulatory effects of sex steroids, other hormones, and genetic factors on the ocular surface. Sex further influences multiple dimensions of DED, including its clinical presentation, immune responses, pain sensitivity, care-seeking behaviors, and other aspects of eye health [23, 25]. Therefore, sex-stratified analyses are essential to fully clarify these associations.

The World Health Organization recommends at least 150 minutes of moderate to vigorous physical activity (MVPA) per week but does not specify an optimal MVPA pattern [26, 27]. Whereas the UK National Health Service recommends MVPA spread “evenly over 4–5 days per week, or every day” [28]. With the current fast-paced lifestyle, sustaining regular PA can be challenging because of time constraints, consequently giving rise to the “weekend warrior” (WW) pattern (1–2 days per week of concentrated PA) [15, 29, 30]. Prior studies have found that the WW pattern offers similar benefits to the active regular pattern in reducing the incidence of diabetes [31, 32], brain disorders [16, 30], cardiovascular disease [15, 33], and mortality [34]. However, no research has been conducted to determine which pattern of PA is more effective in lowering the risk of DED.

To address this gap, we used accelerometer-derived MVPA data from the UK Biobank prospective cohort study to categorize participants into active and inactive groups based on the distribution of exercise time, with the active group further divided into an active regular group and an active WW group. The primary objective of this study was to investigate the associations between different MVPA patterns and incident DED, with subgroup analyses by factors such as sex.

Methods

Study population

This research has been conducted using the UK Biobank resource (project number 627915), a large-scale prospective cohort comprising over 500,000 individuals aged 37–73 years at recruitment (2006–2010) [35]. The overall study design and protocols have been described previously [36]. The study strictly adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and received ethical approval from the North West Multi-Centre Research Ethics Committee (reference number: 21/NW/0157). All participants gave written informed consent.

Initially, 501,902 participants were available after accounting for withdrawals of consent and data curation. We excluded 398,025 individuals missing accelerometer-derived MVPA data. Next, we excluded 4,773 individuals with prevalent DED at baseline (674 from self-reported diagnoses, 686 from general practitioner (GP) prescription records, and 3,413 from GP clinical records; definitions of DED are detailed in Supplementary Table 1), leaving 99,104 participants at risk. After further exclusions for missing covariates, 84,065 participants were included in the final analysis. Participants were followed from the date of baseline assessment until the first diagnosis of DED, death, loss to follow-up, or the administrative end of follow-up (September 30, 2019), whichever occurred first. During the follow-up period, 726 participants developed DED, yielding a cumulative incidence of 0.86%. (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Ascertainment of DED

DED was defined by any of the following criteria: (1) GP clinical records: Read v3 codes (F4F14, X75gS, X77Mq, 1B88, XE16o, XM1F5, Xa9BV, F4A32) (Field 42040). (2) Medication records: repeated (≥ 2) GP prescriptions of tear substitutes (Field 42039), identified based on drug name keywords (Supplementary Table 1). (3) Self-reported diagnoses: self-reported DED (Field 20002). Incident cases were defined as the first occurrence of any of the above after baseline assessment. Details of the definitions and field identifications (IDs) are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Accelerometer-derived MVPA assessment

Participants were required to wear a wrist-mounted triaxial accelerometer (Axivity AX3, Newcastle upon Tyne, UK) on the dominant hand continuously for 7 consecutive days (24 h/day, including both weekdays and weekends), recording raw acceleration data at 100 Hz within a ± 8 g dynamic range. MVPA refers to all physical behavior at ≥ 3 metabolic equivalent of task [37] and was classified using a published machine-learning method developed to categorize a broad range of activities (including walking, jogging, stationary cycling, elliptical training, and others), which has been validated in a UK-based sample [38]. The UK Biobank considers non-wearing time, defined as a period when the sensor does not collect exercise data for 60 min or more. The non-wearing time is excluded from the computational model, and only the daily wearing time is used in computing the participant’s average moderate-to-high intensity exercise.

Participants were categorized according to weekly MVPA volume using WHO guidelines (≥ 150 min/week). Those below this threshold were classified as inactive, while those meeting the threshold were classified as active. Within the active group, participants were further stratified as: active WW (≥ 50% of weekly MVPA accumulated within 1–2 days) and active regular (achieved ≥ 150 min/week of MVPA, but did not meet the active WW criterion) [15]. Given the ongoing debate about optimal device-based MVPA cutoffs and to test the robustness of our findings, we conducted prespecified sensitivity analyses using alternative, data-informed thresholds (≥ 25th and ≥ 50th percentiles of our cohort’s MVPA distribution).

Covariates

Covariate definitions are provided in Supplementary Table 2. Demographics included age, sex, and ethnicity (categorized as White, Black, Asian [including Bangladeshi, Indian, Pakistani, and Chinese], and Other [including mixed or other]). Educational attainment was categorized as Higher (college/university degree), Secondary (General Certificate of Education Advanced Level or General Certificate of Education Ordinary Level/General Certificate of Secondary Education), Vocational (National Vocational Qualification, Higher National Diploma, Higher National Certificate, or other professional qualifications), and Other (none of the above). Household income was grouped into <£18,000, £18,000–30,999, £31,000–51,999, £52,000–100,000, and >£100,000. Lifestyle factors included smoking (never, former, and current), alcohol consumption (never, former, and current), and sleep duration (derived from reported hours of sleep per day and categorized as < 6 h, 6–8 h, and > 8 h). For categorical variables with “prefer not to answer/do not know” responses, we treated these as missing data and excluded the corresponding participants from the analysis.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics of participants, categorized by MVPA patterns according to the WHO guideline threshold (≥ 150 min/week), were summarized as mean (standard deviation, SD) for continuous variables and as number (percentage) for categorical variables. Cox proportional hazards (PH) regression models with restricted cubic splines (RCS) were applied to assess the potential non-linear dose–response association between MVPA and incident DED. Weekly MVPA was truncated at the 99th percentile to reduce the influence of extreme values and modeled using RCS with 4 knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles. Hazard ratios (HRs) were estimated relative to 150 min/week.

For MVPA pattern analyses, we used multivariable Cox regression models to estimate HRs and 95% CIs, adjusting for age, sex, ethnicity, education level, household income, smoking, alcohol consumption, and sleep duration. The PH assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals in the primary model and in sex-stratified models. No material violations were detected for the MVPA pattern effect.

Sensitivity analyses included: (i) alternative MVPA thresholds (25th and 50th percentiles); (ii) alternative WW definitions (≥ 75% on 1–2 days; ≥50% on 1–2 consecutive days; ≥50% on weekend days); (iii) an expanded-adjustment model further adjusting for additional comorbidities and related factors available in UK Biobank (Supplementary Table 6); (iv) tiered outcome definitions of incident DED using a strict definition based on GP diagnostic codes only, an intermediate definition based on GP codes or repeated tear substitute prescriptions (≥ 2), and the primary broad definition including GP codes, repeated prescriptions, or self-report; and (v) lag analyses excluding incident DED within the first 12 and 24 months of follow-up. All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.5.1) using two-sided tests with P < 0.05. We also conducted subgroup analyses to assess potential effect modification by key demographic and lifestyle factors.

Results

Study participants and population characteristics

Our study included 84,065 participants (Supplementary Fig. 1) with a mean (SD) age of 55.82 (7.84) years, 45.87% (n = 38,559) of whom were male. Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics of participants by MVPA patterns stratified at ≥ 150 min/week. When stratified at the threshold of 150 min or more of MVPA per week, a total of 35,999 were in the active WW group (42.82%), 21,613 were in the active regular group (25.71%), and 26,453 were in the inactive group (31.47%). Compared with inactive individuals, participants in the active groups were slightly younger, more likely to be male, had higher education and household income, and were less likely to be current smokers. Participants in the active regular and WW groups showed broadly similar sociodemographic profiles. Lifestyle factors such as alcohol consumption and sleep duration also differed significantly across activity patterns (all P < 0.001). These baseline differences were considered in subsequent multivariable Cox models.

Independent association between total MVPA and risk of DED

Total weekly MVPA was highest among participants in the active regular group (median: 418 min, quartile 1: 302 min, quartile 3: 605 min), followed by the active WW group (median: 288 min, quartile 1: 216 min, quartile 3: 432 min) and the inactive group (median: 86 min, quartile 1: 43 min, quartile 3: 115 min; Fig. 1A). Participants classified as active WW accumulated most of their MVPA on 1–2 days, with markedly lower activity on the remaining 5 days, whereas activity was more evenly distributed across the week in the active regular group (Fig. 1B–C).

Associations between MVPA patterns and risk of DED at different thresholds

In multivariable Cox models, both active patterns were associated with lower risks of incident DED at the 25th percentile (~ 115 min/week) and guideline thresholds (≥ 150 min/week), but not at the median (~ 245 min/week) (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Table 3). At the 25th percentile threshold, HRs were 0.79 (95% CI, 0.65–0.97) for active regular and 0.81 (95% CI, 0.67–0.98) for active WW versus inactive. At the guideline threshold, HRs were 0.76 (95% CI, 0.61–0.94) for active regular and 0.83 (95% CI, 0.70–0.99) for active WW. At the median threshold, estimates were directionally similar but not statistically significant (HR 0.80 [95% CI, 0.65–1.00] and HR 0.84 [95% CI, 0.70–1.03], respectively).

Subgroup analyses

By age (Supplementary Table 4), in adults ≥ 60 years, both active regular and active WW were significantly associated with lower risk of incident DED compared with inactive participants (HR 0.66 [95% CI 0.48–0.89]; HR 0.72 [95% CI 0.56–0.91]), corresponding to ~ 34% and 28% risk reductions, respectively. In adults < 60 years, point estimates were also < 1 (active regular HR 0.71 [95% CI 0.53–0.96]; WW HR 0.82 [95% CI 0.63–1.07]). However, the interaction between age and MVPA patterns was not significant (P for interaction = 0.694).

By sex (Supplementary Table 4), among women, both activity patterns were significantly protective (active regular HR 0.57 [95% CI 0.43–0.75]; WW HR 0.76 [95% CI 0.62–0.94]), corresponding to ~ 43% and 24% reductions. In contrast, among men, both active regular and WW showed no significant association (active regular HR 1.36 [95% CI 0.93–2.01]; WW HR 1.14 [95% CI 0.79–1.63]). We observed evidence of effect modification by sex (P for interaction = 0.002). Sex-stratified estimates, including numbers of participants and events by MVPA pattern, are shown in Supplementary Table 10. Given the smaller number of events in men, the male-specific estimates are less precise and should be interpreted cautiously.

By sleep duration (Supplementary Table 4), participants sleeping ≥ 7 h showed modest risk reductions (active regular HR 0.84 [95% CI 0.66–1.07]; WW HR 0.88 [95% CI 0.72–1.08]), while those with < 7 h sleep also showed protective trends (active regular HR 0.53 [95% CI 0.33–0.86]; WW HR 0.70 [95% CI 0.49–1.01]). However, none of the interactions were statistically significant (P for interaction = 0.112). Additional subgroup analyses stratified by Sjögren’s syndrome, rheumatoid arthritis, prior refractive surgery, and antihistamine use are presented in Supplementary Table 5; no evidence of effect modification was observed (all P for interaction > 0.05).

Sensitivity analyses

Further adjustment for major medical comorbidities and screen-time variables yielded consistent estimates (Supplementary Table 6). We further conducted tiered outcome sensitivity analyses using (i) a strict definition based on GP diagnostic codes only and (ii) an intermediate definition based on GP codes or tear substitute prescriptions. Effect estimates were broadly consistent with the primary (broad) definition (Supplementary Tables 7a–7b). To address potential reverse causation, we performed lag analyses excluding incident DED occurring within the first 12 and 24 months of follow-up; results were directionally consistent with the primary analysis (Supplementary Table 8).

Discussion

Using accelerometer-derived MVPA in 84,065 UK Biobank participants, we found that active MVPA was associated with lower incident DED risk. Notably, across multiple definitions of the WW pattern, the WW pattern was associated with a similarly lower risk of DED as the active regular pattern.

Several prior studies have demonstrated that short bouts of PA can acutely and transiently enhance tear secretion and improve tear film stability in healthy and DED patients. However, these investigations are characterized by small sample sizes (n = 11–90), short-term follow-up (30 min to 6 months), or reliance on self-reported PA data [18–20, 39]. Crucially, our large-scale accelerometer-based study revealed a non-linear, dose-dependent protective association between MVPA and incident DED. The risk reduction appears largest when MVPA increases from 0 to approximately 150 min/week, with diminishing returns thereafter. Accordingly, participants meeting the guideline-based threshold (≥ 150 min/week) exhibited 17%-24% lower HR compared to the inactive group. This overall pattern was robust in sensitivity analyses using alternative thresholds. Although statistical significance was lost at the ≥ 50th percentile, this likely reflects reduced statistical power due to fewer participants achieving this level. The dose-response relationship was further corroborated by our RCS analysis. This non-linear pattern aligns with the inverted J-shaped dose-response relationship reported in previous studies, where excessive PA can cause adverse effects such as muscular fatigue, cardiovascular dysfunction, and cognitive decline [40–43]. The underlying mechanisms have been reported to encompass mechanical forces, inflammatory factors, neuroendocrine dysregulation, and synaptic energy crisis [42, 44, 45]. However, whether and how excessive PA introduces such counteractive risks for DED remains unclear and warrants future investigation. Collectively, these results reinforce the importance of adhering to a balanced PA regimen to optimize health benefits and minimize potential risks.

Our study extends prior evidence establishing comparable health benefits between WW and active regular patterns, now demonstrating this equivalence for DED prevention. Building on previous reports showing similar protective effects against mortality, brain impairment, and cardiometabolic disorders [15–17], we observed that WW was associated with a similarly lower risk of DED as the active regular pattern, with both groups showing 19% and 21% lower risks at the 25th percentile threshold (115 min/week) and 17% and 24% lower risks at the WHO guideline threshold (150 min/week) relative to inactive group. Importantly, this trend persisted across multiple definitions of the WW pattern in our sensitivity analyses, confirming the reliability of our findings. Collectively, these findings hold significant implications for understanding how the allocation of time to MVPA can be used in preventing DED. Future guidelines could place emphasis on making people aware that they could potentially experience important benefits from MVPA. Considering that both active MVPA patterns have a similar effect in reducing the risk of DED, individuals can choose either option based on their personal schedules and preferences.

Although the biological mechanisms of MVPA on DED remain unclear, it is plausible that PA may reduce chronic ocular surface inflammation [46, 47], oxidative stress [48], and mucin depletion [49]. These reductions potentially enhance tear film stability and increase tear secretion [13, 19, 20, 47]. Conversely, physical inactivity was significantly associated with shorter tear breakup time and higher keratoconjunctival staining scores [50]. Nevertheless, these mechanisms require further validation in future studies.

We observed evidence of heterogeneity by sex (P for interaction < 0.001); women showed a 24%–43% lower risk, whereas no clear benefit was observed in men. Several factors may explain this difference. Women are known to report more severe or frequent DED symptoms than men, possibly reflecting higher ocular surface sensitivity or reporting tendency [51]. Moreover, sex-related differences in inflammatory and oxidative responses to exercise may contribute to the heterogeneity observed [52]. However, these findings should be considered exploratory. Given the smaller number of events in men and the multiple subgroup analyses conducted, we cannot rule out residual confounding, differential outcome ascertainment/healthcare-seeking, selection mechanisms, or chance as contributors to the observed pattern. Further studies considering hormonal status and ocular surface physiology are warranted to clarify these sex-specific associations.

Limitations

Our study has several potential limitations. First, MVPA assessment in the UK Biobank was based on a single 7-day accelerometer measurement. While there is currently limited evidence on whether 7 days adequately represents habitual activity, it is worth noting that one previous study reported a strong correlation between a much shorter 2-day measurement and habitual PA levels over a mean follow-up of 3.7 years [53]. Second, GP prescription records and GP clinical records in the UK Biobank primarily focus on outpatient episodes and procedures Because many individuals with mild-to-moderate DED self-manage with over-the-counter lubricants without seeking medical care, case ascertainment based on GP records may not capture all incident DED cases. Third, our study has limitations related to the UK Biobank’s volunteer-based design. Participants are generally healthier and more health-conscious than the general population, which may affect the representativeness of our estimates. Moreover, as the UK Biobank population primarily consists of individuals of European ancestry, the generalizability of our findings to other ethnic groups may be limited. Accelerometer participants differed from the full UK Biobank cohort (Supplementary Table 9), which may limit generalizability and could introduce selection (collider) bias. Fourth, despite extensive adjustment, residual confounding may persist due to unmeasured or incompletely measured factors such as occupation type, contact lens use, specific medication classes, thyroid disorders, time spent outdoors, and ambient exposures (e.g., air pollution, wind, ultraviolet radiation, and allergens). Notably, factors like time spent outdoors and environmental exposures may vary between men and women, which could influence the sex-stratified risk estimates. Fifth, a potential detection bias exists, as physically active individuals may be more health-conscious and utilize healthcare services more frequently, potentially leading to higher DED detection rates compared to inactive individuals. Therefore, further studies are needed to externally validate these findings in more diverse populations, employing longitudinal designs across varied geographic regions.

Conclusions

This large prospective cohort study demonstrated that achieving guideline-recommended levels of MVPA, whether concentrated in a WW pattern or distributed more regularly, was associated with a similarly lower risk of incident DED, with a more pronounced effect observed in women. Future studies are warranted to explore the underlying biological mechanisms (e.g., tear biomarkers and inflammatory processes), employ longitudinal designs with repeated activity measures, explore sex-specific and hormonal factors, and validate findings in diverse populations.
