Limited Clinical Benefit of the Subacromial Balloon Spacer in Massive Irreparable Rotator Cuff Tears
Authors: Waleed Albishi, Mishari Alanezi, Noura Alnasser, Khalid Murrad, Emad Elansary, Abdulrahman Alaseem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
»Subacromial balloon spacer (SBS) implantation has been proposed as a minimally invasive treatment for massive irreparable rotator cuff tears (MIRCTs), particularly in elderly patients with preserved glenohumeral articulation and limited functional demands.»Across observational studies, SBS has been associated with short to midterm improvements in pain and shoulder function, reflected by increases in ASES and Constant-Murley scores.»Evidence from randomized controlled trials is mixed; the SPACE trial showed SBS is noninferior to partial repair, while the START:REACTS trial found it less effective than debridement alone.»Complications are uncommon and primarily include balloon migration, synovitis, and rare infections, supporting SBS's safety profile when patient selection is appropriate.»Based on current high-level evidence, SBS is not recommended for routine treatment of MIRCT. Its use should be restricted to carefully selected elderly patients with chronic, nonarthritic MIRCT who are poor candidates for other reconstructive procedures.

Massive rotator cuff tears account for approximately 10% to 40% of all rotator cuff injuries and are frequently deemed irreparable due to a combination of host-related, tissue, and biomechanical factors1. The underlying causes are multifactorial, including intrinsic degeneration, extrinsic impingement, and acute traumatic injury. Cofield et al.2 defined a massive tear as a one with a diameter of ≥5 cm, whereas Zumstein et al.3 described it as a complete detachment involving 2 or more tendons. Patients typically present with shoulder pain and restricted range of motion, sometimes following trauma or dislocation, and diagnosis is based on history and examination supported by radiography, ultrasound, and arthrography magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)4.

Management of massive irreparable rotator cuff tears (MIRCTs) includes a wide spectrum of options, making individualized treatment selection challenging5. Surgical interventions range from arthroscopic debridement and bursectomy to partial tendon repair, patch-augmented reconstruction, tendon transfer, superior capsular reconstruction (SCR), subacromial balloon spacer (SBS) implantation, and, in advanced cases, shoulder arthroplasty6.

Savarese and Romeo first introduced the SBS for the treatment of MIRCTs in 20127. Since then, the technique has gained substantial adoption across Europe and was granted De Novo classification by the US Food and Drug Administration on July 12, 2021 (InSpace Balloon Implant; DEN200039). Once implanted arthroscopically into the subacromial space, the device is inflated with sterile saline to maintain the acromiohumeral distance (AHD) and to reduce friction between the humeral head and the acromion. The spacer degrades and is resorbed within approximately 12 months7.

Given the growing interest in minimally invasive surgical solutions for MIRCTs, the SBS has emerged as a promising alternative that may provide early pain relief and functional improvement without the technical complexity of tendon transfer or arthroplasty. However, despite its expanding clinical adoption, questions remain regarding its long-term efficacy, durability, and cost-effectiveness, particularly considering recent high-quality studies reporting mixed outcomes. Therefore, this review aims to critically evaluate the current clinical, functional, and economic evidence regarding the use of SBS in the management of MIRCTs.

Materials and Methods

Databases and Search Terms

Two reviewers independently searched PubMed/MEDLINE, Google Scholar, and the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews on October 8, 2025. The following MeSH words were searched: “subacromial balloon,” “massive rotator cuff tear,” “irreparable rotator cuff tear,” and “shoulder.”

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Eligible studies included adult and elderly patients diagnosed with MIRCTs who underwent surgical management using an SBS. We included randomized controlled trials (RCTs), observational studies, case series/reports, and systematic reviews/meta-analyses published between 2012 and the final search date. Exclusion criteria comprised nonclinical studies (cadaveric/animal), letters, and non-English publications. Two reviewers independently screened titles/abstracts and then full texts against the predefined eligibility criteria. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus with a third reviewer.

Results

A total of 673 records were initially identified through electronic database searches. After removing 90 duplicates, 583 unique records remained for screening. Of these, 493 were excluded at title/abstract level. The full texts of 90 potentially eligible articles were retrieved and assessed for inclusion. Following full-text assessment, 30 articles were excluded (e.g., ineligible design/population or nonrelevant outcomes). Ultimately, 60 studies were included in the final review (Fig. 1). These comprised 7 case reports (Table I), 7 clinical trials (Appendix Table 1), 28 real-world observational studies (Appendix Table 2), and 18 systematic reviews and/or meta-analyses (Appendix Table 3).

Discussion

Defining Irreparability and Clinically Relevant MIRCT Phenotypes

Interpretation of the SBS literature depends on how “irreparable” tears are defined. “Irreparability” is variably operationalized across studies using combinations of tendon involvement/size, retraction, fatty infiltration, acromiohumeral interval (AHI), and intraoperative mobilizability8. This inconsistency reduces between-study comparability and complicates the synthesis of outcomes. Key clinical factors that may modify both expected outcomes and appropriate comparators include tear chronicity (acute vs. chronic vs. acute-on-chronic), patient age/activity demand, and the degree of cuff tear arthropathy. Arthropathy severity is commonly described using radiographic classifications such as the Hamada classification and may influence the suitability of joint-preserving strategies9. These modifiers are inconsistently reported in SBS studies and should be standardized in future comparative research.

Mechanism of Action and Biomechanical Rationale

The fundamental premise of the SBS is the restoration of glenohumeral kinematics through temporary mechanical support. By occupying the subacromial space, the inflatable device is designed to depress the humeral head, thereby restoring the AHI10. This mechanical depression is theorized to re-establish an anatomical fulcrum for the deltoid, improving its line of action and mechanical advantage during arm elevation. Consistent with this rationale, forward elevation has been reported to improve after SBS compared with partial repair (PR) in some comparative studies11.

The restoration of the subacromial space is also intended to mitigate the painful effects of superior humeral head migration and subacromial impingement12. Reductions in pain scores observed across multiple prospective trials are consistent with this effect11-13. However, a critical point of debate is the durability of this effect, as the spacer is a biodegradable implant designed to deflate and fully resorb within approximately 1 year of implantation14. This raises the question of how benefit may persist after resorption. Proposed explanations include (1) formation of an organized scar tissue layer that maintains spacing and (2) a rehabilitation “window” created by early pain relief, enabling durable neuromuscular adaptation of the deltoid and periscapular musculature1. However, the long-term durability of these proposed secondary effects is questionable. To date, literature lacks definitive advanced imaging evidence to confirm a specific long-term biological mechanism of action after device resorption, representing a significant gap in the understanding of the device.

Clinical Efficacy and Patient-Reported Outcomes

Clinical and Functional Outcomes

The clinical efficacy of the SBS for MIRCTs presents a mixed evidence base. A substantial body of evidence, primarily from Level III and IV studies, suggests that the procedure is associated with significant improvements in pain, function, and range of motion (ROM). However, higher-level evidence from RCTs and comparative meta-analyses has challenged these findings, highlighting uncertainty regarding its comparative benefit.

Prospective and retrospective case series generally report favorable outcomes following SBS implantation, with sustained improvements in Constant-Murley Score (CMS), pain, activities of daily living (ADL), and ROM observed up to 3 years in a first-in-human pilot study13. Mid-term to long-term studies also demonstrate significant gains across patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), that is, the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) score, Oxford Shoulder Score (OSS), and Subjective Shoulder Value (SSV), including one of the longest follow-ups (84 months) showing high proportions of patients achieving minimally clinically important difference (MCID) for CMS, ADL, and ROM, and another study at 5.8 years confirming improvements in ASES, pain, and forward elevation15-18. These results are reinforced by systematic reviews of largely noncomparative evidence, consistently finding statistically significant improvements in PROMs and ROM at short-term to mid-term follow-up19-22. A meta-analysis by Berk et al.19 reported mean improvements of 40.38 points in ASES and 33.53 points in CMS, both exceeding MCID thresholds.

Despite this volume of positive data, evidence from Level I RCTs shows some conflicting findings. As shown below, the START:REACTS trial reported inferior outcomes for debridement plus balloon vs. debridement alone at 12 months (with a similar trend at 24 months), raising concern regarding the routine use of the device in this setting. By contrast, the SPACE trial demonstrated noninferiority of the balloon spacer compared with PR, with shorter operative time and favorable forward-elevation findings, suggesting potential procedural and functional advantages in appropriately selected patients.

The START:REACTS trial (n = 117; debridement only, n = 61; debridement plus SBS, n = 56), a multicenter, double-blind RCT, found that patients treated with arthroscopic debridement plus SBS had significantly worse outcomes at 12 months, as measured by the OSS, compared with those who received debridement alone. This negative effect persisted as a trend at 24 months, with the debridement-only group also showing superior outcomes on the Western Ontario Rotator Cuff Index and Patient Global Impression of Change19,23,24. The study concluded that the device is unlikely to be of benefit, could be harmful (especially in females, who had significantly worse outcomes), and is not recommended for MIRCTs. This conclusion is supported by a meta-analysis by Sandler et al.25, which directly compared SBS with debridement and found that debridement alone was associated with significantly greater improvements in VAS pain and CMS.

The other major Level I RCT, the SPACE trial (n = 184; InSpace group, n = 93; PR group, n = 91), compared the InSpace balloon with arthroscopic PR for irreparable posterosuperior MIRCTs. The trial demonstrated comparable improvements in patient-reported outcomes between groups at 12 and 24 months, supporting noninferiority of the balloon spacer to PR for the primary effectiveness end point. Notably, operative time was significantly shorter in the InSpace group, and forward elevation was significantly greater at multiple follow-up time points, indicating potential procedural and functional advantages in appropriately selected patients11.

A subsequent cost-utility analysis of this trial found that while the SBS group achieved a slightly higher quality-adjusted life year (QALY) gain in 2 years, its higher cost rendered it an intermediate-value option26. Across meta-analyses, pooled comparative effects are generally neutral, with no consistent differences between SBS and controls for ASES/CMS, pain, or ROM; network meta-analyses variably rank SBS relative to other options, reflecting heterogeneity in comparators and evidence quality27-29.

The temporal pattern of outcomes also warrants discussion. Some studies suggest the benefit of the SBS is front-loaded and may not be durable. A prospective pilot study observed that initial improvements in OSS and SSV at 6 weeks declined significantly by 6 months, with the OSS falling below baseline30, and a “balloon dip” (task-specific functional impairment after the initial postoperative phase) has been described at a mean follow-up of 40 weeks31. Davies et al.32 noted in a systematic review that outcomes peaked at 2 years but showed the most prominent subsequent decline vs. other procedures, although this contrasts with reports of sustained long-term benefit13,15,17,32. A proposed explanation for improvements persisting beyond balloon resorption at 12 months is that SBS serves as a “rehabilitation accelerator,” enabling more effective physical therapy through early pain relief7,18.

The role of SBS as an adjunct to other procedures is also debated. Comparative data on SBS as an adjunct (e.g., combined with tendon transfer, PR, or repair augmentation) are inconsistent, with some studies suggesting benefit and others showing no added effect on function or retear rates33-36. Furthermore, these findings suggest that indication and comparator (debridement vs. PR vs. augmentation) materially influence the observed relative efficacy of SBS.

Radiographic and Imaging Outcomes

The primary biomechanical rationale for the SBS is its function as a mechanical spacer that depresses the humeral head, thereby restoring the AHD and improving deltoid mechanics. Cadaveric studies suggest that SBS implantation significantly increases the AHD by resisting superior humeral head migration, with an average inferior translation of 4 to 5 mm37. However, clinical radiographic findings have been far less consistent. Some studies report a short-term postoperative increase in AHD (e.g., larger gains vs. controls in comparative cohorts)33. However, other cohorts show no change or a decline over time. A systematic review noted such inconsistent results across the literature20. Longitudinal data have reported reductions in AHD/AHI at 12 months and beyond, and comparative analyses suggest that any decline may be only marginally attenuated vs. debridement17,25,38. This discrepancy between cadaveric potential and in vivo results suggests that factors such as balloon deflation/resorption and progression of cuff tear arthropathy may negate any initial spacing effect.

Imaging has also provided insights into the fate of the balloon itself. Although designed to resorb over 12 months, early imaging suggests loss of structural integrity can occur substantially sooner. One imaging substudy found evidence of disintegration in 50% of patients by 6 weeks, without acute rupture symptoms39. This finding further question the durability of any mechanical spacing effect. Furthermore, long-term radiographic follow-up has raised concerns about progression of degenerative changes following SBS implantation. Multiple studies have documented worsening cuff tear arthropathy, measured by the Hamada classification or osteoarthritis grading.

Reported changes include Hamada progression (including ≥1-stage increases), worsening mean Hamada stage at 12 months, and long-term progression in osteoarthritis grade and critical shoulder angle17,38,40. While it is difficult to separate the natural history of the MIRCT from the effect of the implant, these findings suggest that the SBS does not halt or reliably slow the progression of arthropathy.

Complication Profile and Device-Related Safety Concerns

While often described as a low-risk procedure, SBS implantation is associated with a distinct profile of complications and adverse events. Meta-analyses have reported overall device-related complication rates ranging from 3.6% to 6.7%19,41. However, higher rates (13%-17%) have been reported in some individual cohorts38,42. The most frequently cited complications include balloon migration, synovitis, foreign body reactions, infection, and issues related to balloon rupture or degradation.

Balloon Migration and Rupture

Postoperative migration of the spacer is a well-documented concern and has been reported as a cause for revision surgery, with reoperation rates of 2.5% to 8.3%42-44. One study reported that 2 of 11 patients (18%) required reoperation for balloon migration45. While sometimes asymptomatic and detected only on imaging46, migration can cause pain and mechanical symptoms necessitating removal. Early, symptomatic balloon rupture has also been reported. A case report detailed a failure at 3.5 weeks postoperative due to a tear at the balloon's seam, requiring conversion to reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (RTSA)47.

Inflammatory and Foreign Body Reactions

The biodegradable nature of the poly(L-lactide-co-ε-caprolactone) material can provoke an inflammatory response. Clinically significant synovitis requiring device removal has been reported in approximately 4% of patients43, and suspected nonclinically significant synovitis has been observed on MRI13. Rare severe inflammatory/foreign body reactions have been described (including histopathology-confirmed giant cell reaction and chronic inflammatory responses requiring debridement or removal), highlighting potential host responses to the implant or its degradation products40,46,48.

Infection

While the overall reported infection rate is low, with one meta-analysis citing a rate of 0.4%27, deep infections have occurred. One study reported a 13% complication rate that included one deep infection with a fibrinopurulent reaction positive for Staphylococcus epidermidis, which required reintervention38. Other studies have reported superficial infections or suspected site infections that resolved with antibiotics33,49.

Other Complications

A range of other adverse events have been noted, including transient nerve injury42, axillary seroma33, and frozen shoulder36. Importantly, the START:REACTS trial, despite finding no significant difference in the overall number of adverse events, did report a slightly higher number of events in the device group (20%) compared with the control group (15%) at 12 months24. This, combined with the worse clinical outcomes, led to the conclusion that the device could be harmful.

Reoperations and Conversion to Arthroplasty

Reoperation and conversion to arthroplasty (most commonly RTSA) are key end points for any joint-preserving approach to MIRCTs, and SBS studies report substantial heterogeneity driven by selection criteria, technique, and follow-up duration. Pooled estimates from systematic reviews suggest overall reoperation rates of 3% to 7% and conversion-to-RTSA rates ranging from 1.7% to >5%19,20,32, but individual series span from no reoperations46,50 to markedly higher failure rates, including 36% reoperation45, 33% conversion to RTSA21, and a 16.7% revision rate at long-term follow-up (predominantly conversion to RTSA or latissimus dorsi tendon transfer)17. Other cohorts report intermediate revision/conversion rates of 6% to 12%49,51,52, reinforcing that pooled figures can understate the upper range observed in less favorable populations.

Timing data indicate that many failures occur early, often within the first 2 years postimplantation: in a large series all 7 revisions occurred within 2 years and the revision-free survival then plateaued at 83%17, while another study reported a mean time to RTSA of 17 months49. Of particular concern, a meta-analysis comparing SBS with debridement alone found similar overall reoperation rates (5.1% vs. 4.8%) but a substantially shorter mean time to RTSA conversion in the SBS group (11.0 vs. 25.4 months)25, suggesting no clear advantage over debridement in that analysis and potentially earlier progression to arthroplasty when SBS fails. Reoperations are typically prompted by persistent pain, unsatisfactory function, or complications such as migration or infection17,38,44, and although SBS may delay arthroplasty for some patients15, the durability and magnitude of this delay remain inconsistent across the evidence base.

Patient Satisfaction

Patient satisfaction after SBS implantation is an important but subjective end point and is highly variable, likely shaped by expectations, baseline characteristics, and long-term functional outcomes. Several systematic reviews and case series report high satisfaction (often ≥80%) and note that many patients would undergo the procedure again16,20,35,51; for example, a study reported lasting benefit in 65% at a mean 43 months with 58% willing to repeat the procedure49, while another found 73% overall satisfaction with higher satisfaction after a mini-open vs. fully arthroscopic approach53. Conversely, other data sets show mediocre-to-poor satisfaction despite objective improvements: in a long-term follow-up, 42.9% were dissatisfied at final review and even in the revision-free cohort 31.4% remained dissatisfied17, with additional studies reporting satisfaction rates of 62.5%38 and as low as 46%42. Limited evidence suggests that better baseline ROM may predict higher satisfaction42, whereas elderly patients with significant arthropathy/degeneration may have unsatisfactory outcomes due to suboptimal selection45; notably, the high dissatisfaction reported by Kishan et al. despite good early results implies satisfaction may decline over time as functional limitations persist or degenerative changes progress17.

Cost-Effectiveness Analysis

Economic evaluations indicate that the cost-effectiveness of SBS is highly comparator-dependent. Using SPACE trial data, Wang et al.26, classified SBS as an “intermediate-value” innovation vs. PR: although SBS delivered a slightly higher 2-year QALY gain, the higher cost ($9,701 vs. $6,315) produced an incremental cost utility ratio of $106,851/QALY, implying cost-effectiveness only at higher willingness-to-pay thresholds. In comparison with more complex options, Oeding et al.54 modelled SBS as the lowest-cost strategy but found SCR to be the most cost-effective overall, delivering greater value despite higher up-front costs.

High-level clinical evidence further undermines the economic case for SBS in some settings. The Level I START:REACTS trial23,24 reported significantly worse outcomes when SBS was added to arthroscopic debridement, leading to the conclusion that the device is unlikely to be cost-effective and may be harmful, a conclusion supported by Sandler et al.25, who found debridement alone superior and observed faster progression to RTSA in the SBS group (11.0 vs. 25.4 months). Overall, the evidence does not support routine SBS use on economic grounds: it appears poor value vs. debridement, expensive vs. PR, and a lower-cost but less cost-effective alternative to SCR, with implant cost a key barrier55; accordingly, shared decision-making should explicitly address mixed clinical evidence, uncertain economic value, and available alternatives, reserving SBS for selective scenarios where other options are less suitable.

Clinical Implications and Future Directions

The synthesized evidence represents a heterogeneous and, at times, contradictory picture of SBS for MIRCTs. Many noncomparative series report short-term improvements in pain and function, but these findings are difficult to generalize because of variable patient selection, inconsistent definitions of irreparability, and limited control for confounding. Comparative evidence, including randomized trials, has produced mixed results, and differences in study context and comparator procedures limit universal conclusions23-25.

Consequently, the primary clinical implication is that SBS should not be considered a routine intervention for all MIRCTs. If used, it should be positioned as a potentially selective, joint-preserving option within shared decision-making, most plausibly for patients in whom joint integrity is preserved and arthropathy is limited, and where the relevant alternative pathway is also joint-preserving rather than arthroplasty6,40. Clear preoperative counselling should address uncertainty regarding durability and comparative benefit, and the availability of established alternatives (e.g., debridement with biceps management, PR, SCR, tendon transfer, and RTSA depending on phenotype).

Variability in reported SBS outcomes is likely influenced by differences in tear chronicity, patient age/activity demand, baseline functional status, and the degree of cuff tear arthropathy, as well as inconsistent definitions of “irreparable” tear across studies. Differences in the comparator intervention (e.g., debridement-based care vs. reconstructive strategies) further complicate interpretation, as relative effectiveness can vary by both patient phenotype and the alternative treatment pathway. Standardized eligibility criteria and consistent reporting of chronicity, arthropathy grade, and intraoperative reparability assessment would improve interpretability and facilitate phenotype-specific comparative evaluation in future trials and registries.

Looking forward, several research avenues are critical: (1) There is an urgent need for more Level I evidence with long-term follow-up (>5 years). Future trials must move beyond simple debridement or PR as comparators and evaluate SBS against other modern techniques such as SCR and tendon transfers, which are often used in a similar patient population. (2) Studies should focus on identifying predictive factors for success and failure. Subgroup analyses from future trials could help delineate which patients (e.g., based on tear pattern, fatty infiltration grade, acromial morphology, or even gender) benefit most, and which should avoid the procedure. (3) The long-term consequences of SBS implantation on the glenohumeral joint and the ease and outcomes of subsequent RTSA are unknown. Prospective studies should follow patients who convert to RTSA to determine if prior SBS placement leads to issues such as bone loss, scarring, or altered kinematics that could compromise the arthroplasty outcome. (4) The documented cases of adverse inflammatory reactions and unpredictable degradation profiles highlight a need for improved biomaterials. Future models of the device could focus on more biocompatible materials with more reliable resorption kinetics to minimize the risk of foreign body reactions. (5) Economic analyses must be updated as implant costs evolve and should compare SBS not just to PR or debridement, but to the full range of treatment options, including SCR and RTSA, within specific healthcare systems to provide a true reflection of its economic value.

Conclusions

The role of the SBS in treating MIRCTs remains controversial and is best defined as highly selective. Although many uncontrolled studies report short-term symptomatic and functional improvements, heterogeneity in patient selection and definitions of irreparability limits generalizability. High-level evidence from a major RCT suggests that it is clinically inferior and less cost-effective than arthroscopic debridement alone, and other comparative data show that it is not superior to PR. SBS may function as a temporizing procedure in selected patients, potentially delaying the need for a more invasive definitive arthroplasty option such as RTSA in those who later progress or remain symptomatic. However, surgeons and patients must be aware of the variable satisfaction rates, the potential for early failure, and the uncertain long-term durability of its effect, with some evidence suggesting a decline in clinical benefit after 2 years. The existing evidence does not justify its use, and further high-quality, long-term research is essential to definitively establish its place in the complex treatment algorithm for MIRCTs.
