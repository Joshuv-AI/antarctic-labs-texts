High-throughput screening identifies NT-1 that synergizes with MRTX1133 against acquired resistant KRASG12D colorectal cancer
Authors: Natalie Thielen, Ning Wei, Emiko Nagai, Michele Houston, Leonard H Augenlicht, Alice E Shin, Joel T Gabre, Anil K Rustgi, Grace Ha, Lindsay M LaFave, Edward Chu, Seiya Kitamura, Chaoyuan Kuang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC) remains a significant clinical challenge, with a 5-year survival rate of 10%. Approximately 40% of CRCs harbor mutations in the KRAS gene, leading to poor response to standard therapy. This underscores the crucial need for novel therapeutics targeting KRAS and overcoming the growing barrier of resistance. To address these critical challenges, we conducted a high-throughput screen to identify small molecules that synergize with the KRASG12D inhibitor MRTX1133 against CRC. Through screening a 2,652-kinase inhibitor library, we discovered that Osimertinib, an EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor, and its analogs strongly synergize with MRTX1133 against both parental and MRTX1133-resistant cells. The top compound from the screen, NT-1, is a chemical analog of Osimertinib. NT-1 strongly synergized with MRTX1133 to suppress EGFR/MAPK signaling and induce apoptosis in an MRTX1133-resistant patient-derived organoid model of CRC. We present novel small molecule combinations with the potential to overcome the limitations of KRAS inhibitors with direct clinical translational applications.Subject terms: Cancer, Drug discovery, Oncology

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third deadliest cancer globally1. CRC mortality is attributable largely to metastatic CRC (mCRC), especially to the liver and lungs2. The standard treatment options for mCRC are combination cytotoxic chemotherapies and biologic agents, such as EGFR- and VEGF-inhibiting antibodies3. However, the vast majority of mCRC patients ultimately progress through all approved treatments and eventually die from their disease. Thus, novel therapies for mCRC are desperately needed. KRAS-mutant CRC represents approximately 50% of the mCRC population. RAS oncogenes drive the activation of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway and are a principal cancer driver pathway in KRAS-mutant CRCs4. These oncogenes also represent viable treatment targets, as demonstrated by numerous recent advances in KRAS-directed therapies5–8. The KRASG12D mutation is the most common KRAS mutation found in CRC patients, making this particular allele a high-priority therapeutic target5,9.

Previously, the KRAS protein was considered undruggable until the FDA approved Sotorasib, which targets KRASG12C mutant tumors10. This was a landmark event but also highlighted drawbacks to KRAS allele-specific inhibitors. KRAS-mutant CRCs have many adaptive and long-term resistance mechanisms, making single-agent treatment with KRAS inhibitors ineffective for long-term disease control11,12. Another drawback with G12C specific inhibitors is that the patients in clinical studies experienced toxicity and acquired resistance to the point-mutation specific inhibitors. The CodeBreaK100 (NCT03600883) trial assessed Sotorasib in advanced KRASG12C mutant colorectal cancer (CRC), involving 62 patients. The study showed a 9.7% objective response rate, all of which were partial responses13. Despite not meeting the benchmark response rate, once daily Sotorasib demonstrated modest anti-tumor activity with manageable safety. Many patients eventually develop progressive disease (PD) due to mechanisms of resistance, and the clinical landscape has evolved to address this by using combination therapy14–19. Over the last few years, KRAS inhibitor, Adagrasib, combined with the EGFR-directed antibody, Cetuximab, demonstrated promising clinical activity, resulting in accelerated FDA approval for progressive KRASG12C mutant tumors20. Similarly, the combination of Sotorasib with Panitumumab, another anti-EGFR monoclonal antibody, was evaluated in the Phase 3 CodeBreaK 300 trial (NCT05198934) for chemorefractory KRASG12C mutated metastatic CRC. This dual blockade significantly improved outcomes, with the 960 mg Sotorasib plus Panitumumab arm achieving a median progression-free survival (PFS) of 5.6 months and an overall response rate (ORR) of 26%, superior to standard-of-care chemotherapy (3.9 months and 0%, respectively)21,22. However, KRASG12C mutations are relatively infrequent in colorectal cancers, representing only 11% of KRAS mutations in these malignancies, and are only present in 3% of colorectal cancers overall. In contrast, KRASG12D mutation occurs in approximately 20% of CRC patients.

More recently, the KRASG12D inhibitor MRTX1133 was described23. This compound selectively binds in the switch II pocket of the G12D-mutant form of KRAS, inhibiting its activity, thereby disrupting the downstream signaling pathways that drive tumor growth and survival. Despite MRTX1133 not progressing clinically, likely due to poor oral bioavailability, it serves as a useful tool compound in our study. The early clinical experience with KRAS inhibitors has revealed several challenges. There is a diversity of on-target and off-target mechanisms that can confer resistance to KRAS inhibitors, supporting the need for development of additional KRAS-targeting therapeutic strategies. Many such mechanisms, including alternative KRAS mutations and mutations in other MAPK proteins, were detected in response to KRASG12C treatment in patients24. Similarly, in preclinical models of pancreatic cancer, MRTX1133 treatment led to diverse resistance mechanisms including epithelial-mesenchymal transition, PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling, and amplification of Kras, Yap1, Myc, Cdk6, and Abcb1a/b25. Combination therapy is a promising approach to overcome these limitations and induce deeper, more durable responses in patients15. Herein, through unbiased screening of over 2,600 kinase inhibitors against both parental and resistant colorectal cancer cells, we identified novel small molecule combinations that potentiate the efficacy of MRTX1133 and overcome acquired drug resistance. Our top hit, NT-1, represents a potential best-in-class small molecule synergizer with KRAS inhibitors.

Results

MRTX1133 resistant KRAS G12D mutant CRC cell line generation

We first validated that MRTX1133 is selectively effective against CRC cells harboring KRASG12D mutations. Three CRC cell lines, LS174T cells (KRASG12D), HCT116 CRC cells (KRASG13D), and RKO cells (KRASWildtype)- were treated with MRTX1133 in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 1A). As expected, MRTX1133 selectively inhibits the growth of LS174T cells that have a KRASG12D mutation while being ineffective against the other two cell lines that do not have a KRASG12D mutation. This is consistent with the previously reported biochemical inhibitory potency of MRTX1133 as well as previous cellular data of this compound23.

To screen for drug combinations to overcome resistance, we established LS174T cells resistant to MRTX1133. After passaging the cells eight times in the presence of MRTX1133 at 1 µM, we obtained cells that are resistant (R) to MRTX1133, with more than 100-fold decrease of potency compared to the parent cell line (P) (Fig. 1B). Resistant cell proliferation is visualized (Fig. S1A), illustrating that cellular proliferation persists in the presence of up to 1 µM MRTX1133, even after 72 h of treatment. Visual assessment of the new resistant cell line showed that it retained certain characteristics of the parental cells, such as a flat morphology when grown on tissue culture-treated surfaces (Fig. S1B). To understand the mechanisms of resistance, we used reverse-phase protein arrays (RPPAs) and calculated the difference of protein expression between the resistant and parental cell line (Fig. 1C). The antibody repertoire covers key oncogenic pathways such as PI3K/AKT, RAS/MAPK, SRC/FAK, TGF-β/SMAD, JAK/ STAT, DNA damage repair, Hippo, cell cycle, apoptosis, histone modification, and immune oncology. Analysis of the RPPA data revealed significant upregulation of protein expression levels, notably PLK1 and p-SRC in the resistant LS174T cell line. Conversely, there were a number of proteins, including tumor suppressors, that were down-regulated in the resistance cell line, indicating multiple potential mechanisms of resistance at the protein level (Fig. S1C). For upregulated proteins identified in RPPA, we performed an immunoblotting analysis to confirm the increased protein expression levels in the resistant cells compared to the parental cells (Fig. 1D). In addition to proteins identified from RPPA analysis, the resistant cell line exhibited upregulation of p-EGFR (p-Y1068) expression, aligning with previous studies that confirmed the EGFR feedback mechanism upon treatment with MRTX113326. Furthermore, genomic mutational profiling identified a KRASE62D mutation in the resistant LS174T cells but has not been functionally validated in this model (Fig. 1E). Its emergence may be consistent with a potential role in resistance and signaling rewiring11, but direct experimental testing will be necessary to establish its contribution for MRTX1133-resistance. These results indicate that prolonged treatment with MRTX1133 can lead to diverse genomic and proteomic resistance mechanisms in CRC. Our pair of parental and resistant cell lines serves as useful tools for discovery and validation of therapies to overcome resistance.

High-throughput small molecule screening and triage

We next conducted a high-throughput screen to identify compounds that (i) potentiate MRTX1133 efficacy in colorectal cancer cells, and (ii) overcome resistance (Fig. 2A). We hypothesized that the inhibition of kinases in the cancer cell signaling pathway potentiates the efficacy of the KRASG12D inhibitor and helps overcome resistant mechanisms. A kinase inhibitor library consisting of 2,652 compounds was selected for our screening campaign. The library contains inhibitors targeting various protein kinases and lipid kinases. To identify compounds that are effective against resistant cells and those that synergize with MRTX1133, screening was performed under four distinct biological conditions; parental vs resistant LS174T cells in the presence or absence of MRTX1133 (Fig. S2A). The compound library was dispensed at a final concentration of 10 nM onto cells seeded in 384-well plates and cultured for 72 h (Figure S2B). The concentration of MRTX1133 in the screening was carefully selected based on its IC50 values in both parental and resistant cell lines (Fig. 1B), ensuring that its effect remained below the inhibition cutoff in the primary screen while still allowing the identification of potential synergistic compounds.

Among the 2,652 compounds tested, 187 compounds met the primary hit cutoff of 50% inhibition in at least one condition (Table S1), resulting in an overall primary hit rate of 7%. The primary screening scatter plot is shown in Fig. 2B. The screening assay performed well, with an overall Z’ factor exceeding 0.6. Each group yielded distinct hit compounds, with the monotherapy-treated groups showing approximately a 1% hit rate (Fig. S2C). Details of the hit categories from the overall screen are provided in Table S2. To identify synergizers of MRTX1133 and to avoid general cytotoxic compounds, secondary hit cutoff criteria were applied: compounds were selected if they exhibited at least a 30% increase in inhibition in the MRTX1133 combination groups (conditions II and IV) compared to their corresponding monotherapy groups (conditions I and III). A breakdown of the number of compounds in each pathway per four groups is provided in Fig. S2D. Among the 187 compounds meeting our primary hit cutoff, we noted that compounds targeting the RTK, JAK/STAT, and MAPK/ERK pathways appeared to be uniquely effective against the resistant cells when combined with MRTX1133 (Fig. 2C). Among the 18 compounds that remained after applying our secondary synergy cut-off, there were several compounds targeting Raf, p38, EGFR, and ser/thr kinases, which confirmed that synergistic combinations can arise through both on-target and off-target mechanisms. After manually inspecting the hit molecules to exclude pan-assay interference and non-drug-like compounds and selecting based on mechanistic insights and clinical relevance, six compounds were chosen for further biological evaluation of their synergistic activity.

Validation of small molecule combinations for overcoming MRTX1133 resistance

Combination treatment of MRTX1133 and small molecule inhibitors showed marked differences between the parental and resistant cell line. The chemical structures of the top four hit compounds are shown in Fig. 3A, with highly synergistic compounds in both parental and resistant highlighted in red. Dose-response matrices were created for all six selected compounds to validate their synergistic activity when combined with MRTX1133 (Fig. S3A). Based on the cell viability data in a matrix format, we calculated the ZIP synergy score to quantitatively assess synergy between each compound and MRTX113327. Both parental and resistant cell lines revealed robust synergy between the irreversible EGFR inhibitors and MRTX1133, which was further supported by the 3D synergy matrices. Notably, two compounds—Mutant EGFRi and Limertinib—demonstrated synergy scores above 10, signifying strong synergistic potential, highlighted in red (Fig. 3B, C). Mutant EGFRi, an experimental inhibitor with no formal name28 will be referred to as “NT-1” throughout the study to ensure clarity. Three compounds showed strong synergism with MRTX1133 against both parental and resistant cells. It is interesting to note that all four validated molecules target the MAPK pathway, including mutant EGFR inhibitor, Limertinib (EGFR inhibitor), and p38α inhibitor. The ERKi showed synergy in the resistant cell line at higher concentrations, but less synergy in the parental cell line.

To ensure the combinations have broad synergistic effect, we tested the top six inhibitors on two additional KRASG12D cell lines (LS-513 and GP2D). The ZIP score analysis showed that NT-1 is synergistic across all cell lines tested, as opposed to the other compounds (Fig. 3D). A major discrepancy in synergy was found within the GP2D cell line, with several screen hits demonstrating lack of synergy with MRTX1133 specifically in GP2D cells. This may be due to GP2D being relatively sensitive to MRTX1133 monotherapy, compared with the other CRC cell lines (Fig. 3E). Additionally, while the p38α and ERK inhibitor combinations had positive ZIP synergy scores, they did not achieve scores in the synergistic range of greater than 10. These inhibitors’ potential therapeutic value remains a topic of ongoing investigation. The transcriptional inhibitors CDK12-IN-E9 and CDK8-IN-4 did not show promising synergy in parental or resistant cell lines during dose-response validation; however, their synergistic peaks were around the 10 nM range in the dose-response matrix (Fig. S3B), potentially explaining their presence as primary screening hits. This was further confirmed when tested at lower concentrations (Fig. S3C).

Based on these findings, we prioritized the EGFR inhibitors for subsequent biological validation and therapeutic development, as they offer the most promising synergy in the context of overcoming resistance to MRTX1133. NT-1 exhibited potent synergistic effects in both the parental and resistant CRC models, positioning it as a promising candidate for further exploration.

Comparison of EGFR inhibitors as MRTX1133 synergizers in vitro

Given previous literature, it is unsurprising that the top hits that emerged were small molecule EGFR inhibitors. Inhibition of mutant BRAFV600E with BRAF kinase inhibitors, and inhibition of KRASG12C or G12D with allele specific inhibitors, reliably triggers the adaptive mechanism of EGFR upregulation and subsequent increase in mitogenic signaling29. This has led to the expectation that co-inhibition of EGFR and KRAS would lead to improved responses. Our initial findings provided strong evidence for synergy, yet when we look at the complete list of EGFR inhibitors found in the kinase library (Fig. S4A), there is a wide variety of inhibition, leading to some inhibitors working much better than others, suggesting a more complex interaction than initially anticipated. Interestingly, unlike in non-small cell lung cancer, CRC has an incredibly low rate of EGFR mutation ( < 0.5%)30. In both the LS174T-P and -R models used in our screen; EGFR mutations were not detected (Fig. 1E). To investigate the hypothesis that co-inhibition of EGFR is the primary mechanism of synergy, we assessed the interaction between MRTX1133 and a panel of well-characterized EGFR inhibitors.

The top hit, NT-1, was found to be structurally similar to Osimertinib (Fig. 4A), with the minor chemical differences highlighted in red. Despite its structural similarity, Osimertinib did not emerge as a hit in our screen, likely due to the screening concentration and hit cutoff used. To further characterize NT-1 within the EGFR inhibitor (EGFRi) landscape, we selected a panel of EGFRi’s to test alongside NT-1. We first compared inhibition values in our primary screen (Fig. 4B). This analysis revealed that NT-1 is uniquely effective among EGFRi’s, particularly in combination with MRTX1133. Based on previous literature, the cellular monotherapy potency of NT-131 was compared with other well-established EGFRi’s: Osimertinib32, Afatinib32, Limertinib33, Gefitinib32, and Erlotinib32 (Fig. 4C). This comparative analysis suggested that NT-1 is a more potent suppressor of cancer cell proliferation, regardless of tumor type or EGFR mutation status. We also determined direct kinase inhibition in a biochemical assay (Fig. 4D) that demonstrated NT-1 exhibited greater potency toward EGFRwildtype and EGFRT790M compared to Osimertinib and Afatinib. Afatinib was the second most potent wild-type EGFR inhibitor, but there was still a 3-fold difference in potency between Afatinib and NT-1. PLK-1 and SRC, which appeared as potential resistance mechanisms due to their upregulation in our RPPA data, appeared to be unlikely direct targets of NT-1 (Fig. 1C).

We next tested NT-1 across four different cell lines in comparison with other EGFRi’s to determine the relative potency and synergy of NT-1 with MRTX1133. Comparing the different combination treatments, we observed greater decrease in the fold change of cell viability for the NT-1 combination groups compared to the other EGFRi’s in the resistant cell line (Fig. 4E), as well as in the other three cell lines (Fig. S4C). Interestingly, Afatinib showed significant synergy in our testing which confirms its potency in the screen. Since Afatinib is a dual inhibitor of EGFR and HER2, we tested other HER2 inhibitors, Lapatinib and Neratinib, in combination with MRTX1133 (Fig. S4B). These HER2 inhibitors demonstrated no synergy when combined with MRTX1133. Although Afatinib exhibited strong synergy, NT-1 induced the most potent synergy out of all the EGFRi’s (Fig. 4E). We further validated the potency and synergy of NT-1 and MRTX1133 in a dose-response experiment (Fig. 4F, G). NT-1 was the sole EGFRi that achieved the synergistic ZIP score threshold of 10 across all four cell lines. In general, NT-1 surpassed the other small molecule inhibitors as a combination therapy and could create synergy in various KRASG12D solid tumor cell lines tested (Fig. S4D, E). To explore this combination strategy using clinically relevant KRAS inhibitors, we evaluated NT-1 in combination with RMC inhibitors (RMC-7977, -9805, and -6236). The two pan-Ras inhibitors (RMC-6236 and -7977) showed excellent synergy with high ZIP scores against the MRTX1133-resistant model (Fig. 4H, Fig. S4F). Our findings indicate that NT-1 is a distinctly synergistic molecule when paired with KRAS inhibitors in general. These investigations underscore the potential of NT-1 plus KRAS inhibitor combinations as a promising approach for improving therapeutic outcomes in KRAS G12D-mutated colorectal cancer.

Combination Therapy Blocks Downstream MAPK Pathway in MRTX1133 Resistant Cells

We next investigated the synergistic effects of NT-1 (Fig. 5A) compared to Cetuximab (Fig. 5B), a clinically relevant EGFR antibody that was recently approved for combination treatment with Adagrasib (KRASG12C inhibitor) for colorectal cancer34. We compared the synergistic effects of NT-1 versus Cetuximab in combination with MRTX1133 (Fig. 5C, D, Fig. S5A). Additionally, we applied the Chou-Talalay median effect method35 to calculate the combination index (CI) responses of NT-1 (average CI = 0.6) and Cetuximab (average CI = 0.9) when combined with MRTX1133. In the LS174T-R cell line, NT-1 demonstrated better synergy with MRTX1133 as compared to Cetuximab (Fig. S5B). To further validate our findings, we assessed the effects of equimolar Cetuximab versus NT-1 across four different cell lines, initiating treatment at a concentration of 100 nM for 72 h, and measured the relative fold change from day 0 (Fig. 5E). NT-1 plus MRTX1133 combination treatment led to significantly (p < 0.0001) decreased cell viability in three cell lines when compared to Cetuximab out of the 4 CRC cell lines tested.

To investigate the mechanistic effects of NT-1, we treated LS174T-R cells with varying concentrations of NT-1 monotherapy for 24 h, as illustrated in Fig. S5C. Notable suppression of EGFR expression and a concomitant increase in apoptosis were observed at a concentration around 60 nM, leading to the subsequent combination treatment using 60 nM of NT-1. The EGFR family comprises four distinct membrane-bound tyrosine kinase receptors: EGFR/ErbB-1, HER2/ErbB-2, HER3/ErbB-3, and HER4/ErbB-4, which are activated upon ligand binding to their extracellular domains26,36,37. Treatment with MRTX1133 has been shown to induce feedback activation of EGFR family receptor expression, leading to reactivation of KRAS signaling, limiting the sensitivity to KRAS inhibitor single-agent drug treatments. The effects of MRTX1133 monotherapy, as well as the combined treatment of NT-1 and MRTX1133 in the resistant cells, are shown in Fig. 5F. The combination treatment resulted in complete suppression of p-EGFR, along with downstream suppression of p-ERK and an increase in cleaved Caspase 3, indicating that the cells were undergoing apoptosis in response to this therapeutic regimen. Given the pronounced synergy observed between Afatinib and MRTX1133, we sought to further rule out the possibility of HER2 as a mechanistic driver of the observed synergy. For this aim, we investigated the effects of HER2-targeting monoclonal antibodies, Trastuzumab and Pertuzumab, in conjunction with MRTX1133 to determine whether HER2 suppression contributes to the observed synergy of NT-1. However, we observed no synergy from the HER2-targeting combination therapies (Fig. S5D), suggesting that the synergistic effect of our hits is not due to HER2 inhibition.

Immunoblotting was employed to investigate the mechanisms of action for Cetuximab (100 µg/mL), Osimertinib (1 µM), and NT-1 (60 nM) as monotherapy and in combination treatment with MRTX1133 (1 µM) (Fig. 5G). The results indicated that while MRTX1133 alone resulted in the upregulation of phosphorylated EGFR (p-EGFR), the addition of NT-1 effectively inhibited EGFR activation, thereby demonstrating its capability to block the feedback loop associated with EGFR signaling. Combination therapies involving Cetuximab and Osimertinib led to a reduction in p-EGFR levels and an increase in the cleavage of PARP and Caspase 3, indicating enhanced apoptotic activity; however, these combinations were less effective compared to NT-1. Notably, the inhibition of p-EGFR was significantly reduced when NT-1 was administered either as a monotherapy or in conjunction with MRTX1133 compared to MRTX1133 alone (p < 0.0001). A similar reduction was observed with Cetuximab, whereas Osimertinib did not exhibit the same effect (Fig. 5H). This finding supports previous observations regarding Osimertinib’s efficacy, which is primarily indicated for treating tumors with mutant EGFR rather than wild-type EGFR38.

The pharmacological inhibition of EGFR by NT-1 enhances the sensitivity of cells to the KRASG12D inhibitor, effectively disrupting the signaling feedback loop and leading to a more comprehensive suppression of MAPK signaling pathways. Examination of the downstream marker, p-ERK, indicated that NT-1 and its combination with MRTX1133 led to a significant reduction in p-ERK (p < 0.001) compared to combinations with Cetuximab or Osimertinib (p < 0.01) (Fig. 5I). Treatment with Cetuximab in combination MRTX1133 did not seem to lower p-ERK levels, which could be due to the different modes of action between antibody therapies and small molecule inhibitors. Lastly, the activity of active Caspase-3 was evaluated, showing an increase in apoptotic activity across all treatment combinations over a 24 h duration (Fig. 5J). These findings can be further depicted in Fig. 5E, where p-EGFR is completely inhibited in the NT-1 combination group, in contrast to Cetuximab and Osimertinib, which did not accomplish total suppression, although all combinations promote CC-3, demonstrating the synergistic effect of all compounds. In summary, NT-1 stands out as the most effective inhibitor of wild-type EGFR signaling, and when paired with MRTX1133, it further suppresses downstream MAPK pathways, potentially mitigating resistance mechanisms.

NT-1 synergizes and overcomes MRTX1133 resistance in Patient Derived Organoids (PDO) models

To further validate the potent synergy between NT-1 and MRTX1133 in overcoming resistance, we tested NT-1 in combination with MRTX1133 in multiple different patient derived organoid (PDO) models. These PDOs closely resemble the tumor epithelium both phenotypically and genetically, thus providing a more translational model for study39. In one of our KRASG12D organoids from the NCI (PDO-CRC-1), we identified oncogenic alterations, including mutations in SOS1, ATM, and APC40. Genomic sequencing of cancer driver genes in the resistant organoids revealed that KRASG12D mutation was retained, with no additional new mutations detected (Fig. S6A). Similar to the LS174T parental protocol for the KRASG12D cell line, the NCI organoid model was treated with eight repeated cycles of MRTX1133 until the partial loss of organoid integrity was observed. The organoids that survived these eight cycles were designated as PDO-CRC-1R (MRTX1133 resistant) (Fig. 6A). These resistant organoids were then maintained in media containing 1 µM of MRTX1133, with their growth rate and size of the resistant organoids exceeding those of the parental organoids (Fig. S6B). Once both the parental and resistant organoids were established, we tested the combination of NT-1 and MRTX1133 in vitro to determine if the heterogeneity of CRC PDO models could be effectively addressed by the synergistic compounds such as NT-1 (Fig. 6B). Based on dose-response curves and synergy assessment, we observed that the combination therapy works at low concentrations ( ~ 10 nM) with an average CI value of 0.5 (Fig. 6C). To ensure the broad applicability of the NT-1 and MRTX1133 combination, we evaluated cell viability and fold change in both the parental and resistant PDO-CRC-1 models, an additional KRASG12D organoid model (PDO-CRC-2), as well as a lung KRASG12D PDO model (PDO-LU-1) (Fig. 6D, E). There were significant differences in cell viability in both monotherapy and combination treatments compared to MRTX1133 alone. Notably, NT-1 exhibited a strong effect in monotherapy, which is particularly significant given that CRC is not driven by EGFR mutations. This effect was further characterized when comparing NT-1 alone and in combination across the parental and resistant PDO models (Fig. 6D). Both monotherapy and combination therapy resulted in significantly decreased cell viability over 5 days compared to MRTX1133 alone, especially in the G12D-PDO-1R model (p < 0.0001).

We also miniaturized the PDO assay to test the 3D synergy matrix in both KRASG12D and KRAS wild-type PDO models (Fig. S6C). The KRASG12D organoid achieved a ZIP score over 10, indicating a synergistic effect, while the wild-type organoid’s ZIP score was -4.6, indicating an antagonistic effect. The synergistic effect observed in the KRASG12D organoid was further supported by immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis of p-EGFR suppression and other downstream pathways (Fig. 6F). A microscopic view of the organoids undergoing treatment (Fig. S6D, E) showed noticeable apoptosis and was further corroborated in the immunoblot after just 72 hours of treatment (Fig. 6G), where the organoids exhibited a rapid synergistic inhibitory effect after treatment with low concentrations. In the G12D-PDO-1R model, combination treatment resulted in a significant decrease in p-EGFR (Fig. 6H, p < 0.0001) and its downstream target p-ERK (Fig. 6I, p < 0.001), along with an increase in c-PARP (Fig. 6J, p < 0.0001). This led to downstream suppression and increased apoptosis, sensitizing the KRASG12D organoid to treatment after it had become resistant to MRTX1133 alone. In preparation for in vivo testing, we confirmed that NT-1 had no adverse reactions when administered orally to NOD/SCID mice over 7 days, with no signs of malnourishment or weight loss observed at doses up to 20 mg/kg (Fig. S6F). Collectively, these findings strongly indicate that the combination of NT-1 and MRTX1133 has the potential to overcome acquired resistance mechanisms in the treatment of KRASG12D mutant colorectal cancer (CRC) and may represent a promising clinical candidate for combination therapy.

Discussion

Our study provides significant insights into combination therapies with MRTX1133 to improve CRC outcomes. Recognizing that synergy in molecular combinations is rare and varies across tissues and molecular backgrounds41, our approach underscores the importance of evaluating both combination potency and efficacy. We identified compounds that synergize with the KRASG12D inhibitor MRTX1133 in both parental and resistant CRC cell lines through high-throughput screening. By utilizing a screening concentration at 10 nM with one of the largest kinase inhibitor library screens in cancer41–43, we aimed to identify combinations that enable dose reduction, improved efficacy, or both, relative to single-agent treatments. Importantly, combinations that were not cytotoxic in the monotherapy conditions were prioritized to avoid off-target and non-specific cytotoxicity. We further characterized the 187 primary hits to identify promising kinase inhibitors targeting key proteins such as Akt, Raf, and EGFR. Notably, MRTX1133 was included in our library and demonstrated additive, rather than synergistic, effects in our screening process, serving as an internal control. A comparative analysis of our primary hits revealed convergence with findings from a recent CRISPR-Cas9 synthetic lethal screen, which identified EGFR and PLK1 as critical targets for co-treatment with MRTX1133 in LS174T cells. This alignment underscores the efficacy of EGFR inhibitors in synergy with MRTX1133, reinforcing the relevance of our screening results26.

Our primary hits were categorized into 16 groups based on the expected outcomes of the four screening groups (Fig. S2A, Table S2). Categories 2 and 5 targeted general KRAS synergizers, while categories 3, 6, and 7 targeted resistant-selective KRAS synergizers and compounds against resistant cancer cells. Focusing on differences between parental and resistant cell lines, we plotted the number of hit compounds from each group against various biological pathways (Fig. S2D). Noteworthily, the number of compounds in the MAPK/ERK and JAK/STAT pathways increased in resistant versus parental cell lines, indicating a potential reliance on these pathways for survival (Fig. 2C). To ensure selective cytotoxicity in the presence of MRTX1133, our secondary synergizer hit cutoff required compounds to demonstrate at least a 30% difference in inhibition between monotherapy and the MRTX1133 combination. Using this stringent threshold, 18 compounds were identified that demonstrate synergy with MRTX1133 in both parental and resistant cell lines. The key targets for driving synergy included EGFR, Serine/Threonine kinases, p38, and Raf which exhibited above 50% synergy between monotherapy and combination treatments. Interestingly, the hit molecules fell into two main categories: vertical inhibitors of the RTK-RAS-RAF-MEK pathway and cell cycle inhibitors.

Building on these findings, particularly the strong synergy observed with EGFR and related kinase inhibitors, we explored combinations of MRTX1133 with multiple promising kinase inhibitors to address resistance and enhance therapeutic efficacy. EGFR inhibitors, NT-1, and its analogs exhibited the most striking synergy, evident across both parental and resistant models, highlighting their potential in overcoming MRTX1133 resistance. The combination effectively downregulated the MAPK pathway, a critical signaling cascade in CRC progression. These findings are similar to results in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) models, where MRTX1133 demonstrated specificity and effectiveness in vitro44,45. The overexpression of phosphorylated EGFR following MRTX1133 treatment suggests that combining MRTX1133 with inhibitors targeting these receptors can enhance anticancer activity. Interestingly, the low nanomolar potency of NT-1 outperformed Cetuximab in vitro, showing significantly greater synergy in combination treatments. Cetuximab combined with MRTX1133 in multiple CRC cell lines showed very broad synergy effects with numbers ranging from 15-30, similar to other groups26,46, whereas NT-1 displayed very similar values, but significantly higher potency (Fig. 5E), paving the way for its exploration as a less toxic alternative to other wild-type EGFR treatments. The overexpression of phosphorylated EGFR observed following MRTX1133 treatment indicates the presence of a compensatory feedback loop. However, it is important to note that additional signaling pathways, including c-Met and Raf, also appear to play a role in the cellular response. Similarly, studies involving Osimertinib, Gefitinib, and other EGFR inhibitors have shown promising synergy with KRAS-targeted therapies14,30,33,36,47. In our study, NT-1 exhibited superior potency, outperforming other inhibitors like Afatinib, Osimertinib, Limertinib, and Gefitinib (Fig. 4E). Following treatment, NT-1 demonstrated a more pronounced reduction in p-ERK signaling and a greater induction of apoptosis (Fig. 5F–J) compared to the analog Osimertinib, which was administered at a concentration 16-fold higher than NT-1, and Cetuximab, which was administered at an 11-fold higher concentration in the cell lines over a 24 h period. These results support the potential of NT-1 as a leading candidate for further investigation.

Our study highlights the challenge of acquired resistance to KRAS inhibitors. MRTX1133 is a relatively new inhibitor with limited studies on resistance mechanisms25,37,46. We identified a new KRAS mutation, KRASE62D, which may weaken MRTX1133’s potency. While approximately 90% of KRAS mutations occur within codons 12 and 13, there is minimal data on mutations like KRASE62D. Based on the design of MRTX1133 and the expected interaction between the pyrrolizidine moiety and the E62 carboxylate group, it is possible that a KRASE62D mutation leads to reduced binding affinity of MRTX1133 to KRAS protein12. As further support for this theory, a prior study by Choi et al that used deep mutational scanning analysis indicated that KRASE62D is associated with resistance against MRTX113348. This mutation may represent a novel resistance mechanism specific to KRASG12D inhibitors, warranting further investigation. Additionally, resistance-associated pathways, such as SRC and PLK1, emerged from our analysis, suggesting alternative mechanisms beyond direct KRAS mutations. Strikingly, our detection of SRC aligns with a recent study that showed the SRC-JUN pathway, activated via RAF1-MEK-ERK and potentially JNK, drives G12C inhibitor resistance, raising the possibility that analogous MAPK-dependent mechanisms underlies G12D inhibitor resistance49. Understanding these resistance mechanisms will be crucial for designing effective combination strategies. Our screening process identified numerous targets and pathways relevant to KRASG12D inhibition. The hit compounds were categorized based on their effects in parental and resistant models, with significant enrichment observed in the MAPK/ERK and JAK/STAT pathways. The compounds that demonstrated synergy with MRTX1133 were mapped to their respective targets and pathways, reinforcing the importance of dual-targeting strategies. Given the observed reliance on MAPK signaling in resistant models, our study underscores the potential of targeting multiple nodes within this pathway to enhance therapeutic outcomes. We identified and confirmed EGFR inhibition with compounds like Afatinib, Osimertinib, Gefitinib, and MRTX1133 are synergistic as well, but the synergistic potential of NT-1 is greater. Thus, NT-1 warrants further study to validate its therapeutic potential as a part of combination treatments. Our study highlights the value of exploring synergistic drug combinations to overcome resistance mechanisms in CRC. With appropriate consideration of potential toxicity and molecular profiling of patient tumors, these combinations could offer more effective and personalized therapeutic options for patients with KRASG12D CRC.

It is important to acknowledge the limitations of our study. While our screening process has identified numerous synergistic targets and pathways, many of these remain unvalidated within the context of this single study. This presents an opportunity for future research to further explore and validate these findings. Although we utilized two distinct models that are resistant to MRTX1133, a more comprehensive characterization of the transcriptional profiles across a broader range of models is essential. Such initiatives will enhance our comprehension of the mechanisms that drive resistance. It is also important to recognize that MRTX1133 is unlikely to progress in clinical development due to its low oral bioavailability50 and has not progressed beyond phase I/II clinical trials. Consequently, we explored other KRAS inhibitors, particularly Revolution Medicines inhibitors (RMCi), to uncover more clinical relevance, and we discovered that NT-1 exhibits strong synergies with these inhibitors as well. Determining synergistic potential of NT-1 with a wide array of KRAS inhibitors is the subject of future investigations. Moreover, further research is necessary to evaluate the effectiveness of these combinations in murine models, as our findings indicate that combined inhibition not only amplifies the initial response but could also prolong the sustainability of that response. Compiling a comprehensive list of factors that promote resistance will be essential for creating actionable predictive biomarkers, which can aid in the customization of KRAS inhibitor combinations in forthcoming clinical trials. We have also observed early signs of genetic resistance mechanisms to MRTX1133, underscoring the need for further exploration into their prevalence and the formulation of strategies to restore treatment effectiveness. Grasping the development of clinical resistance to future pan-KRAS inhibitors will be crucial for guiding upcoming therapeutic approaches. Finally, while NT-1 appears to be a promising alternative to Osimertinib, ongoing investigations into pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, bioavailability, and in vivo toxicity are necessary to confirm its potential.

In conclusion, our study presents an unbiased screening of a diverse kinase library, leading to the identification and discovery of NT-1, an Osimertinib analog that effectively synergizes with MRTX1133 against KRASG12D mutant colorectal cancer. This combination outperforms other EGFR inhibitor + MRTX1133 combinations and has the potential to overcome resistance to KRAS inhibitor monotherapy. Utilizing patient-derived organoid models, we have confirmed the synergy between the NT-1 and MRTX1133, providing a strong rationale for clinical testing of this combination in patients with KRASG12D mutant colorectal cancer. Moving forward, further investigation into the in vivo efficacy of NT-1 will be essential for optimizing treatment strategies and improving patient outcomes.

Methods

Cell Viability Assay

Cell viability was assessed using the CellTiterGlo 2.0 Luminescent Cell Viability Assay (Promega). Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 3,000 cells per well or 384-well plates at a density of 1,000 cells per well and incubated overnight at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere to allow for adherence. Post-treatment with experimental compounds for 72 h, 20–100 µL of CellTiter-Glo reagent was added to each well, followed by gentle mixing on an orbital shaker for 2 min. After a 10 min incubation at room temperature, luminescence was measured using Cytation 5 reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT). Relative luminescence units (RLUs) from treated wells were normalized to control wells to determine cell viability. IC50 values were calculated by GraphPad Prism version 10.3.1 (RRID: SCR_002798) using a 3-parameter dose-response model.

Cell Lines and Cell Culture

LS-513 (RRID: CVCL_1386), LS174T (RRID: CVCL_1384), SNU-601(RRID:CVCL_0101), HCT116 (RRID: CVCL_0291), RKO (RRID: CVCL_0504), and HT29 (RRID: CVCL_0320) human colon cancer cell lines were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). GP2D (RRID:CVCL_2450) was obtained from Millipore (95090714-1VL). LS-513, SNU-601, HCT116, RKO, and HT29 cells were cultured in RPMI1640 (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% P/S. LS174T and GP2D cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle's medium (DMEM; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% P/S. All cell lines were incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Cell lines were authenticated every 6 months while in use. Cell lines were monitored every 6 months for Mycoplasma infection and treated when necessary.

Establishment of MRTX1133 Resistant Cells

MRTX1133-resistant cells were established by exposure of LS174T cells to increasing concentrations of MRTX1133. The initial concentration of MRTX1133 was 10 nM. When the cells adapted to the drug, the concentration of MRTX1133 was gradually increased by 1.5–2 times every week to a final concentration of 1 μM.

Cell Seeding and Screening

Freshly trypsinized colorectal cell lines were seeded into black CELLSTAR® uCLEAR® 384 well plates (Greiner, Monroe, NC, USA) with 1,000 cells per well. After cell seeding, the cells were incubated overnight to adhere to the bottom of the plate before drugs were added. The kinase inhibitor library was purchased from a vendor (MedChemExpress, RRID: SCR_025062). All kinase inhibitors were tested at 10 nM, and, for selected hit compounds, a concentration range of 1–1,000 nM was tested. Cells were incubated for 72 h with small molecules in media at 37 °C, 5% CO2 before viability assay read out. Small molecules were obtained from a commercial vendor as a powder, dissolved in DMSO at a concentration of 10 mM, and diluted to the indicated final concentrations for each experiment, with a maximum final DMSO concentration of 0.1%. All viability measurements were compared to viability of cells treated with 0.1% DMSO (vehicle only). Small molecules purchased from MedChemExpress: NT-1 (Mutant EGFR Inhibitor, HY-13984), ASN007 (HY-136579), p38α inhibitor 2 (HY-131335), Limertinib (HY-138751), CDK8-IN-4 (HY-111465), CDK12-IN-E9 (HY-117203A), Osimertinib (HY-15772), Afatinib (HY-10261), Gefitinib (HY-50895), Erlotinib (HY-50896), Neratinib (HY-32721), Lapatinib (HY-50898A). Small molecules purchased from Chemietek: MRTX1133 (CT-MRTX1133), RMC-9805 (CT-RMC9805), RMC-6236 (CT-RMC6236), RMC-7977 (CT-RMC7977). Monoclonal antibodies were obtained from commercial sources and reconstituted in water or saline as per manufacturer instructions, prior to diluting to final treatment concentrations: Cetuximab (Erbitux, Eli Lilly), Trastuzumab (trastuzumab-qyyp, Pfizer), Pertuzumab (Perjeta, Genentech).

Reverse-phase protein array (RPPA)

LS174T-P and LS174T-R cells were treated with MRTX1133 (1,000 nM) for 24 h, the cells were harvested, and protein lysates were prepared. RPPA was performed by the Functional Proteomics RPPA Core Facility at the MD Anderson Cancer Center (RRID: SCR_016649) for analysis.

Synergy determination

CRC cell lines were seeded at a density of 0.05 × 106 cells/well in 384-well black clear plates (Greiner) and were further treated as described above. Synergy scoring was determined using the “inhibition readout” (calculated as “100 - Cell Viability” on the online SynergyFinder software, RRID: SCR_019318) and implementing the ZIP calculation method. The matrix started with 1 µM in the hit compounds and 300 nM or 3,000 nM in the MRTX1133 starting concentration with a 3-fold decrease in the parental and resistant, respectively. The cell viability results were input into Synergyfinder3.0 to determine synergistic potential. The ZIP synergy score14 was used to evaluate the synergisms between compound and MRTX1133. Scores above 10 are considered synergistic, between -10 and 10 are considered additive, and below −10 are considered antagonistic.

Western Immunoblot

Cell lysates were harvested using a lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Proteins from each sample (10–50 µg) were separated on 4–20% mini-PROTEAN TGX gels and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). After blocking in 5% milk with Tris-buffered saline with Tween (TBST) buffer, the membranes were probed with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The membranes were washed three times in TBST and incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Image acquisition and band intensity quantification were performed using an Odyssey infrared imaging system (LI-COR Biosciences, LincoIn, USA) and Image J software (NIH, Bethesda, USA, RRID: SCR_003070), respectively. The following primary antibodies were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology and used at a dilution of 1:1,000: anti-EGFR (#4267, RRID: AB_2895042), anti-p-EGFRY1068 (#3777, RRID: AB_2096270), anti-ERK1/2 (#9102, RRID: AB_330744), anti-p-ERK1/2 (#4370, RRID: AB_2315112), and anti-PARP (#9542, RRID: AB_2160739).

Patient-derived organoids

All human subjects research was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. In brief, all frozen aliquots of PDO tissue were obtained, thawed, and re-established as PDOs in our lab for this study. Organoids were grown and treated in their respective media at 37 °C and 5% CO2. All PDOs used were passaged at least twice and growing with a doubling time of 3–10 days, prior to use in experiments. Brightfield micro-photographs were obtained using the Echo Revolve 4 (RRID: SCR_026523).

PDO-CRC-1 was obtained from the NCI Patient Derived Models Repository (ID: 362531-283-R-V1) and cultured as described by PDMR SOPs.

PDO-CRC-2 was previously established at Columbia University (ID: CRC27)52 and cultured as previously described, using tumor tissues obtained from patients undergoing elective surgery at NewYork-Presbyterian/Columbia University Irving Medical Center with written informed consent under a protocol approved by the Columbia University Institutional Review Board (IRB; protocol AAAT8778).

PDO-LU-1 was established in the Montefiore Einstein Comprehensive Cancer Center Biobank from a KRASG12D mutant lung adenocarcinoma specimen obtained after standard of care resection (ID: LU-25-012), after written informed consent under protocols approved by the Albert Einstein College of Medicine Institutional Review Board (#2021-13730, 2025-16811). Briefly, tumor tissue was minced and embedded in Matrigel (Corning) domes on tissue culture treated plates. Solidified domes were immersed in complete lung organoid media. Treatment was also carried out in complete lung organoid media. Lung organoid media composition: DMEM/F12 supplemented with B27 (1x), HEPES (10 mM), and GlutaMAX (1x), nicotinamide (10 mM), N-acetylcysteine (1.25 mM), R-spondin (500 ng/mL), Rh-Noggin (100 ng/mL), P/S (0.5%), Primocin (50 µg/mL), FGF-10 (50 ng/mL), FGF-7 (12.5 ng/mL), A83-01 (500 nM), SB202190 (1 µM), and Y-27632 (5 µM).

Genomic sequencing

Cancer-focused mutation analysis was performed using the Ion Torrent AmpliSeq Colon Cancer Panel (AmpliSeq Library 96LV Kit 2.0, ThermoFisher). Library preparation, quality control, sequencing, and data analysis were performed as per manufacturer instructions.

Immunohistochemistry

PDOs were grown and treated, then isolated from Matrigel domes using Cell Recovery Solution (Corning #354253). PDOs were fixed with 4% PFA, embedded in 1% agarose, then processed into FFPE blocks. 5 µm thick sections were subjected to standard IHC. The following primary antibodies were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology and used at a dilution of 1:1,000: anti-p-EGFRY1068 (#3777, RRID: AB_2096270), anti-ERK1/2 (#9101, RRID: AB_330744), anti-cleaved caspase-3 (#9661, RRID: AB_2341188), and anti-Ki-67(#9027, RRID: AB_2687447). Visualization was with ImmPACT DAB Substrate Kit (Vector Laboratories #SK-4105). Brightfield micro-photographs were obtained using the Echo Revolve 4 (RRID: SCR_026523).

Kinase Assay

The “HotSpot” assay platform was performed by Reaction Biology Corporation (Malvern, PA) using NT-1 across a select panel of recombinant kinases51. NT-1 was tested at 10 different concentrations, starting at 1 μM and decreasing by 3-fold dilutions. ATP concentration of 10 µM was used for the assay. Recombinant proteins and assay concentrations: EGFR (Invitrogen, Cat# PR7295B, 4 nM), EGFR T790M (Invitrogen, Cat# PR8052A, 50 nM), IGF1R (Invitrogen, Cat# PV3250, 1 nM), c-SRC (Invitrogen, Cat# PR4336E, 0.6 nM), and PLK1 (BPS, Cat# 40033, 12 nM). Briefly, specific kinase/substrate pairs along with required cofactors were prepared in reaction buffer: 20 mM Hepes pH 7.5, 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EGTA, 0.02% Brij35, 0.02 mg/mL BSA, 0.1 mM Na3VO4, 2 mM DTT, and 1% DMSO. Compounds were delivered into the reaction at the indicated final concentrations, followed ~ 20 min later by addition of a mixture of ATP (Sigma, St. Louis, MO) and 33P ATP (Perkin Elmer, Waltham, MA) to a final concentration of 10 μM. Reactions were carried out at room temperature for 120 min, followed by spotting of the reactions onto P81 ion exchange filter paper (Whatman Inc., Piscataway, NJ). Unbound phosphate was removed by extensive washing of filters in 0.75% phosphoric acid. After subtraction of background derived from control reactions containing inactive enzyme, kinase activity data was expressed as the percent remaining kinase activity in test samples compared to vehicle (DMSO) reactions. IC50 determination and curve fits were performed where the enzyme activities at the highest concentration of compounds were less than 65% using GraphPad Prism Software (RRID: SCR_002798). The assays for Osimertinib and Afatinib were conducted under the same conditions.

In vivo Toxicity

Nude mice (Jackson Labs) were obtained at 6-8 weeks of age. NT-1 was dissolved in 10% DMSO, 20% PEG400, 60% Solutol HS15, 10% water (% volume) and dosed via oral gavage, daily at the indicated concentrations for seven days.
