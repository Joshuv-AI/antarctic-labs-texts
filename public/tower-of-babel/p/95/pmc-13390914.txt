Lung ultrasound interpretation using deep learning for the detection of B-lines in dogs
Authors: Jessica L Ward, Blake VanBerlo, Benjamin Huggard, Delaney Smith, Robert Arntfield
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDeep learning (DL) shows promise for interpretation of lung ultrasound (LUS) images in humans, but its performance in animals remains underexplored.Hypothesis/ObjectivesAssess performance of a B-line detection algorithm (BLDA) trained on LUS images from humans when applied to images from dogs.AnimalsA total of 1,950 clips collected from 201 LUS examinations in 90 dogs across 4 studies.MethodsUltrasound clips were collated and labeled with A-line or B-line profiles by an expert reviewer. A DL model previously trained on LUS images from humans was applied to detect presence of B-lines at a framewise level. A clip classification algorithm was calibrated to maximize clip-level performance of the BLDA using a calibration data set. Performance of the DL model and BLDA was assessed on a held-out test set of LUS images. A heatmap-based explainability method was used to visualize regions most utilized by the model for predictions.ResultsWhen applied to the test set, the BLDA showed an overall accuracy of 87%, with sensitivity of 73% and specificity of 93%. The algorithm performed best when identifying images with strong (versus weak) B-line profiles. Assessing raw model predictions, zero-shot performance for identification of B-lines was excellent (area under the curve, 0.96). Heatmaps suggested that the DL model utilized image areas that were plausibly relevant for LUS interpretation.Conclusions and clinical importanceA LUS model trained on images from humans maintained strong performance when applied to dogs, supporting cross-species generalization to accelerate veterinary diagnostic innovations.

Introduction

Point-of-care lung ultrasound (LUS) has become an invaluable diagnostic tool in small animal emergency and critical care medicine.1,2 Features such as portability, lack of ionizing radiation, and rapid bedside applicability make LUS particularly well-suited for evaluation of dogs and cats in respiratory distress. Identification of B-lines (vertical, hyperechoic, narrow-based artifacts extending from the pleural line to the far field) provide strong diagnostic value for detecting alveolar-interstitial syndromes.3–7 Numerous multifocal B-lines increase the index of suspicion for interstitial or alveolar lung disease, such as congestive heart failure (CHF), as the cause of respiratory distress, whereas their absence can support alternative causes such as airway disease.3,5,7 Consequently, LUS has become a standard component of cardiopulmonary assessment in veterinary emergency settings. Despite these advantages, widespread dissemination of LUS remains limited by operator dependence and variable expertise in image interpretation.

Deep learning (DL), a branch of artificial intelligence (AI), has emerged as a promising tool for automating medical image interpretation. Models using DL can automatically recognize diagnostic image patterns by training on large labeled datasets, thereby enabling consistent interpretation by non-experts and facilitating broader access to advanced imaging modalities. In human medicine, DL has achieved impressive performance across numerous diagnostic applications,8,9 including analysis of thoracic radiographs,10,11 thoracic computed tomography,12,13 and echocardiographic images.14,15

In veterinary medicine, the use of AI for imaging interpretation is an emerging field with encouraging early results. Recent studies have demonstrated its feasibility for detecting cardiac enlargement on thoracic radiographs,16–18 pulmonary edema on thoracic radiographs,19 cardiac murmur grade from phonocardiograms,20 left ventricular size from echocardiographic images,21 and cavitary effusions and pneumothorax from point-of-care ultrasound images.22 Collectively, these studies underscore the promise of AI to augment veterinary diagnostic imaging by improving consistency and efficiency of interpretation while decreasing reliance on operator expertise.

In human healthcare, DL models and algorithms have been developed to automate recognition of B-lines on LUS and to distinguish between normal (A-line) and abnormal (B-line) lung patterns with high accuracy.23,24 Such models also have been extended to differentiate B-line morphology among various causes of pulmonary disease, such as cardiogenic edema and acute respiratory distress syndrome,25 and to identify pleural effusions.26 The application of AI to veterinary LUS remains sparsely investigated. One recent study utilized a proprietary automated B-line detection tool originally developed for LUS images from humans and reported good agreement with human observers for B-line quantification.27 Further investigation of AI in veterinary LUS is needed, including efforts to calibrate and validate AI models trained on images from humans for accurate interpretation of veterinary LUS images.

Our purpose was to determine whether a DL model originally developed for LUS images from humans could be successfully calibrated and applied to LUS images from dogs for automated identification of B-lines. We predicted that the calibrated model would accurately identify B-lines in dogs, supporting the potential for AI-assisted LUS interpretation to enhance diagnostic consistency and accessibility in veterinary medicine. Our study builds on emerging work in this area by comprehensively describing the calibration and validation of an AI model for automated interpretation of LUS in dogs.

Materials and methods

Our diagnostic accuracy study evaluated the performance of a previously developed DL B-line detection algorithm (BLDA) trained on images from humans for identifying B-lines in archived LUS examinations from dogs. Detailed descriptions of image acquisition, labeling, algorithm development, calibration, and statistical analyses are provided in the Appendix.

Lung ultrasound cineloops were obtained from image archives of 4 previously completed prospective studies performed at the Iowa State University Lloyd Veterinary Medical Center, each of which had Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee approval. Additional details about image acquisition in each study are provided in Table 1. Examinations were performed by cardiologists or cardiology residents using a standardized 8-site LUS protocol for dogs.3,28 After quality assessment by an expert observer masked to dog identity, sampling timepoint, and LUS site, clips were classified as A-line profile, weak B-line profile, strong B-line profile,3,29,30 or non-A/non-B profile. Burnt-in graphical elements were removed using an automated image-cleaning tool before analysis. Table 2 provides summary information for the numbers of clips, frames, and clip classifications for the 4 studies used in this project. To preserve dataset independence, all clips from a given study were assigned exclusively to either a validation dataset (Studies A and B) or a held-out test dataset (Studies C and D).

The BLDA consisted of a previously trained convolutional neural network (CNN) that generated frame-level predictions of B-line presence and a clip classification algorithm (CCA) that combined frame-level outputs into a single clip-level prediction.31 The CNN was not retrained or fine-tuned using data from dogs. Instead, CCA parameters were calibrated25,26 using the validation dataset to optimize performance on LUS clips from dogs before evaluation on the independent test dataset.

The primary outcomes were the sensitivity and specificity of the calibrated BLDA for detecting the presence versus absence of B-lines on the held-out test dataset. Secondary analyses, including subgroup performance by LUS site, B-line characteristics, framewise CNN performance, and model explainability using Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM)25,32 are described in the Appendix.

Sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value were calculated with 95% confidence intervals (CI) estimated using Wilson’s method. Framewise CNN discrimination was assessed using area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), with patient-level nonparametric bootstrap resampling (10 000 replicates) used to estimate 95% CIs. Complete details of algorithm calibration, subgroup analyses, and statistical procedures are provided in the Appendix. A graphical overview of the study methods is provided in Figure 1.

A graphical overview of the methods in this investigation. A convolutional neural network (CNN) model trained on human data was used to obtain B-line prediction confidences for each frame in each clip of the validation set. Model calibration was performed to determine an assignment for clip classification algorithm (CCA) parameters that struck a balance between clip-wise sensitivity and specificity on the validation set. The CNN model and the finalized CCA were used to obtain clip-wise predictions for all clips in the test set. Test set metrics were computed using the ground truth labels and clip-wise prediction for the test set. The weights of the original CNN trained on human data were not modified.

Results

Algorithm calibration

Frame-wise confidences were computed for all clips in the validation dataset (studies A and B). In selecting the optimal values of CCA parameters w, τ, and t, the following parameter ranges were examined: w in {1, 2, 3, 4, 5} frames, τ in {1, 2, 3, 4, 5} frames, and t in {0.05, 0.10, 0.15, 0.20, 0.25, 0.30, 0.35, 0.40, 0.45, 0.50}. Clips were standardized to 29 frames per second, such that 1 frame corresponds to approximately 34 milliseconds. Clip-wise predictions then were produced for all assignments of (w, τ, t) and optimal parameters were selected via grid search.

Table 1 links each parameter assignment to validation set classification metrics. The Pareto plot in Figure 2 shows the sensitivity and specificity of the BLDA on the validation set under each parameter assignment. Figure 3 provides violin plots that show the distributions of sensitivity and specificity under different values of each parameter. As is frequently observed in binary classification, lowering the classification threshold (t) or contiguity threshold (τ) increased sensitivity at the expense of specificity, whereas lower values of the smoothing window size (w) resulted in slightly higher sensitivity with minimal change in specificity. Parameter assignments that achieved a balance between sensitivity and specificity on the validation set were prioritized. The following parameters were selected: t = 0.4, τ = 1 frame, and w = 1 frame, which yielded an overall sensitivity of 85.7% and specificity of 85.8% on the validation set.

Algorithm evaluation

After having selected CCA parameters that achieved a balance between clip-wise sensitivity and specificity, we evaluated the entire BLDA on the test set, which consisted of all 865 clips in Study C and Study D that met the quality criteria. Predictions were produced for each clip in the test set and were compared to their ground truth labels. Figure 4 shows 2 examples of the CCA applied to a true negative and a true positive clip.

Table 3 provides classification metrics summarizing the BLDA’s performance on the full test set, as well as for each of its constituent studies. Confusion matrices for the test set and constituent studies C and D are provided in Figure 5. Overall test set sensitivity was 73.0% (CI: 67.5%-77.8%) and specificity was 93.4% (CI: 91.1%-95.2%). Although this result represents a similar accuracy to the validation set, the balance between sensitivity and specificity observed on the validation set was not observed in the test set, with the algorithm exhibiting higher specificity on the test set at the expense of sensitivity. There was also a marked difference between the classification metrics computed for the clips originating from Study C compared with Study D. Study C showed reasonably high and balanced sensitivity (87.3%, CI: 81.7%-91.4%) and specificity (75.0%, CI: 59.8%-85.8%). In contrast, for Study D, the model had high specificity (94.8%, CI: 92.6%-96.3%) but poor sensitivity (47.0%, CI: 37.5%-56.7%), with a large number of false negative clips.

Quantitative framewise model evaluation

In addition to clip-level performance using the BLDA, we also evaluated the zero-shot frame-wise performance of the DL model on the test set. This analysis was limited to the homogeneous clips in the test set (clips classified as A-lines or homogeneous B-lines, excluding heterogeneous B-line clips), because B-line labels apply to the entire clip instead of individual frames. Figure 7 presents receiver operating characteristic curves summarizing performance across the overall test set and the Study C and D subsets.

The AUC was computed to assess the model’s ability to distinguish the positive and negative classes (frames with B-lines present vs. absent). Point estimates and CIs for the overall test set, along with stratified results for study and LUS site, are presented in Table 6. Overall, the model trained on data from humans demonstrated 0.96 AUC on homogenous clips in the test set. Generally, the model demonstrated higher AUC on frames from Study C than Study D, and the AUC for clips of the left-sided LUS sites was often higher than those on the corresponding right-sided LUS sites. For Study D, the observed difference between the left and right sides was particularly large for the cranial and middle LUS sites.

Qualitative framewise model evaluation

The model’s framewise predictions were evaluated qualitatively using Grad-CAM heatmaps32 to visualize the spatial area of each frame that contributed most to the model’s prediction. Figure 8 displays Grad-CAM heatmaps for an example of true positive, true negative, false positive, and false negative predictions for B-lines. For each frame featured in Figure 8, videos displaying the heatmaps and prediction confidences across the corresponding clip can be found in Supplementary Videos 1-8. In general, heatmaps for correctly classified negative class (A-line) clips suggested that the model focused on a horizontal area at the level of the pleural line, whereas heat maps for correctly classified B-line clips suggested that the model focused on vertical artifacts in the mid-field of the LUS image (see examples in Figure 8 and Supplementary Videos 1-8).

In each quadrant, the left image shows the raw frame of the video, and the right image shows the heatmap overlaid onto the frame, indicating the areas of the image used by the model to make its prediction in that frame. The source videos for all frames are provided in the supplementary materials. Examples demonstrate true positives (A, B), true negatives (C, D), false positives (E, F), and false negatives (G, H). Frames (A, C, E, G) originate from dogs in Study C; frames (B, D, F, H) originate from dogs in study D. Supplementary videos 1-8 correspond to frames A-H, respectively.

Subjectively, false negatives tended to occur in cases in which the pleural line was disrupted (eg, subpleural consolidation) or in heterogeneous clips with only a single transient B-line. False positives tended to occur in images that were relatively lower quality (eg, images still meeting quality threshold but subjectively relative over- or under-gained), or in clips in which the pleural line was relatively deep within the image (eg, very large or obese dogs). Although most heatmaps suggested patterned activity centered on a particular area of the image, heatmaps for some clips (both correctly and incorrectly classified) were seemingly random, and some included activity outside of the ultrasound field (see examples in Figure 8 and Supplementary Videos 1-8).

Discussion

We describe a novel approach to the calibration and validation of an automated LUS image classification system for dogs using an AI algorithm trained on data from humans. Because the DL model applied in our study was trained on LUS frames from humans, its application to images from dogs provides an evaluation of cross-species generalization, reflecting the model’s initial zero-shot performance on a distinct target population without species-specific fine-tuning. These results provide proof of concept and suggest strong potential for clinical applications of AI for LUS interpretation in veterinary medicine, as well as the potential for enhanced accuracy with model training or fine-tuning on species-specific images.

One prior study evaluated automated B-line quantification in dogs utilizing a proprietary B-line detection tool developed for LUS in humans (the Butterfly Auto B-line Counter), reporting good agreement with human observers for B-line count and identification of strong positive sites.27 That study utilized a relatively small and clinically homogeneous sample (20 healthy dogs and 20 dogs with CHF) and provided limited information regarding algorithm design, performance characteristics, and sources of error. Our study expands this emerging body of work by providing more methodological transparency in model calibration and validation and by evaluating a more clinically diverse sample of dogs with variable causes of B-lines. Collectively, these findings help lay the groundwork for development of veterinary-specific AI models for LUS interpretation.

In our study, a previously developed BLDA was successfully adapted to detect B-lines in LUS clips from dogs. Its CCA was calibrated using data from 2 previous LUS studies in dogs to optimize parameters for clip-level classification of presence or absence of B-lines. This calibration struck a high and balanced performance on the validation set (sensitivity of 85.7% and specificity of 85.8%). The previously optimized CCA parameters in humans have similar values for the contiguity threshold and moving average window, but a higher classification threshold. We speculate that this difference could be related to decreased confidence in B-line predictions on data from dogs, which is out-of-distribution for the previously trained DL model. In future work, B-line confidence distributions could be compared before and after fine-tuning the DL model on data from dogs, along with comparing optimal CCA parameter assignments after fine-tuning.

When the calibrated BLDA was applied to a test set of LUS images from 2 additional LUS studies of dogs (Studies C and D), the model showed an overall accuracy of 87% for clip-level classification, with a sensitivity of 73% and specificity of 93%. These results support our prediction that a calibrated AI model trained using data from humans could accurately identify B-lines in dogs. The model’s zero-shot performance at identifying B-lines was also excellent, with an AUC of 0.96. For comparison, the BLDA has exhibited 91% sensitivity and 84% specificity on a large test set of 1005 videos from humans with international representation from sites geographically distinct from those used for training.31 In prior work, an earlier version of the BLDA showed 90% sensitivity and 92% specificity on a test set consisting of clips from the same institution from which the training data originated; the same model demonstrated 83% sensitivity and 82% on an external test set from a separate institution.23 On individual frames, the neural network exhibited a mean AUC of 0.96 during cross-validation on local data, while achieving 0.92 AUC on the external test set.23 The decrease in sensitivity, specificity, and AUC when evaluating external data from humans was analogous to the decrease observed when evaluating the BLDA model on LUS studies from dogs, because both scenarios are examples of domain shift. The modest decrease in performance observed on data from dogs compared to data from humans suggests that LUS in dogs may be as dissimilar to LUS in humans as the dissimilarity of LUS in humans across healthcare institutions. Further analysis would be required to compare cross-species and cross-institution LUS examinations.

Interestingly, the algorithm performed quite differently between the 2 studies contributing images to the test set. In Study C, the model performed well overall with an accuracy of 85%, sensitivity of 87%, and specificity of 75%, relatively similar to the calibration set. In Study D, although specificity was high (95%), sensitivity was poor (47%) with many false negative results. We suspect that this finding reflects differences in dog characteristics and distribution of the different clip-level classifications between the 2 studies. Study C consisted of dogs with respiratory distress secondary to severe pulmonary hypertension, a patient sample relatively similar to the calibration sets (dogs with respiratory distress secondary to CHF). The ratio of B-line to A-line labeled clips in this study was approximately 5:1. In contrast, Study D involved healthy dogs before and after general anesthesia, and the ratio of B-line to A-line labeled clips was approximately 1:5. Furthermore, a post hoc review of other LUS characteristics determined that approximately 10% of clips in Study D had subpleural consolidations or pleural line irregularities, whereas subpleural abnormalities were only present in 4% of clips in Study C. Given our heatmap findings suggesting that the area of the pleural line was important for the algorithm’s classification of an A-line profile, and the area deep to the pleural line was particularly important for the algorithm’s classification of a B-line profile, it is possible that subpleural consolidations might be particularly challenging for the algorithm to correctly classify. The effect of subpleural consolidation on the BLDA’s performance has not yet been assessed in LUS in humans.

Qualitative examination of Grad-CAM heat maps allowed the investigators to visualize which areas of LUS images were being predominately used by the model to make its classifications. These results suggested that in many cases, the model seemed to be focusing on image areas that were plausibly relevant for LUS interpretation. For images correctly classified as A-line profiles, heat maps often were focused on the pleural line, an area where a human sonographer would look to confirm lung sliding and examine for any disruption suggesting the origin of a B-line. For images correctly classified as B-line profiles, heat maps often were focused deep to the pleural line in the mid-field, in a vertical orientation overlapping with the location of visible B-lines. Although subjective, these results are encouraging and suggest that the algorithm is basing its classification on true sonographic characteristics rather than association with a spurious variable. Importantly, text and other visuals (eg, patient information, timestamps) were removed from images before analysis. Doing so helps prevent reliance on image features corresponding to non-sonographic confounding variables that could be correlated with the incidence of B-lines (eg, time of day, amount of patient information entered, respiratory rate). In humans, a qualitative analysis conducted for prior work distinguishing LUS images containing B-lines from those containing A-lines found that salient regions for B-line and A-line predictions corresponded to vertical and horizontal echoic artifacts, respectively.23 This problem differs slightly from the BLDA evaluated in our study, because the present BLDA was trained to distinguish LUS images containing B-lines from those that do not. The subtle difference lies in the definition of the negative class. In the present BLDA, clips containing neither A-lines nor B-lines were included in the negative class. It is not surprising, then, that heatmaps for A-line profiles did not necessarily fixate on A-lines alone. We posit that the original model learned to detect whether vertical artifacts originate from the pleural line, instead of prioritizing the existence of A-lines. This heuristic could be related to the observation that false negatives in the study tended to occur in clips containing subpleural consolidation, because B-lines originate from subpleural consolidations in such cases instead of from the pleural line.

Based on Grad-CAM observations, false negative classifications tended to occur when the pleural line was disrupted (eg, subpleural consolidation), and false positive classifications tended to occur with subjectively relatively lower-quality images or those where the pleural line was deeper in the image than typical. These patterns of misclassification are also plausible within the assumption that the model is particularly focused on the area of the image around and deep to the pleural line when making A-line vs. B-line classifications. For the LUS studies included in our project, image depth was standardized at 4-6 cm such that for very large dogs, the pleural line appeared deeper in the LUS image than the model might have expected, which could have affected classification of images in these dogs. This finding emphasizes the importance of image optimization, particularly location and visualization of the pleural line, when utilizing AI for analysis of LUS images.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, the LUS images were sourced from 4 previous studies, each with distinct patient samples and inclusion criteria. Although technical aspects were standardized within each individual study, they were not harmonized across studies, and the number of sonographers varied in the studies. The 2 studies used for model calibration included dogs with respiratory distress, many of which were diagnosed with CHF, whereas the 2 studies used for testing included dogs with pulmonary hypertension and healthy dogs undergoing anesthesia. If sonographic characteristics of B-lines differ by underlying cause, this heterogeneity may have decreased the comparability between the calibration and testing datasets. Additionally, each LUS clip was analyzed independently for BLDA calibration and testing. However, clips were not truly independent because each represented a single site within an 8-site LUS examination, and most dogs underwent examinations at multiple time points. Regardless, we ensured that the validation and test sets were independent of each other by confining all clips from a single study to one of these datasets. Our ability to perform valid statistical testing of subgroups (eg, to compare model performance among LUS sites) was limited because of small sample size and lack of independence among sites. Although we provided 95% CIs to characterize the precision of metrics reported, subgroup comparisons should be interpreted with caution because of the limited number of independent patients. Only clips meeting predefined quality thresholds were included; model performance might differ if lower-quality images were incorporated. Additionally, the “ground truth” classification of A-line versus B-line profiles was determined by a single expert observer labeling images at one timepoint, and thus did not assess intra- or interobserver variability of the labeler. Although interobserver agreement in LUS interpretation has been reported to be high,3 model performance could be different if the labeler had repeated multiple observations, or if multiple observers classified clips and reached consensus. Any study of AI model performance that uses a human observer as “ground truth” involves the critical assumption that the reference observations made by the human observer are completely reliable, which is not necessarily the case. Lastly, framewise model performance was quantified using AUC; because framewise labels were only available for homogenous B-line clips, the AUC metric does not account for frames in heterogeneous clips, which are more likely to contain difficult examples because of recurring disappearance and reappearance of B-lines.

In conclusion, our study provides promising proof of concept for automated interpretation of LUS in dogs using a DL algorithm trained on data from humans. Although the calibrated model showed high overall accuracy, performance varied across studies in the test set, and certain image characteristics such as subpleural consolidations appeared to challenge the model’s classification. Nevertheless, generalization of a model trained on data from humans to images from dogs showed strong performance overall. These findings suggest that leveraging large, well-annotated ultrasound datasets from humans may offer an efficient pathway to accelerate development of veterinary diagnostic models. Further training on LUS images from dogs could improve accuracy and reliability. Our study provides important preliminary information for future research focused on model refinement, validation across diverse clinical scenarios, and exploration of AI-assisted point-of-care imaging in veterinary medicine.
