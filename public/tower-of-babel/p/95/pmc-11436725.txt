Utilizing environment-friendly eugenol as a diluent with trioctylamine for the reactive extraction of aqueous levulinic acid
Authors: Mohammed Saad, Behnaz Asadzadeh, Petri Uusi-Kyyny, Ville Alopaeus
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This paper focuses on the reactive extraction of levulinic acid (LA) from aqueous solution by reactive extraction. This goal is achieved using eugenol, a green alternative in the industry, as a solvent in the liquid-liquid equilibrium (LLE) measurements for the ternary system of LA + Eugenol + H2O and quaternary systems of LA + Eugenol/ Methanol (MeOH) + H2O + Tri-n-octylamine (TOA) at T = 293.15 K. Additionally, the distribution coefficients (KD) were calculated for LA using the two diluents. Also, the ability of different diluents with TOA, in the extraction of LA were compared. The distribution coefficient of eugenol with TOA (KD = 9.44) is compared with other organic diluents which indicated that eugenol is a suitable option. MeOH, being the shortest chain alcohol, also turned out to be a diluent that could be utilized for extraction of LA with TOA. Furthermore, the Non-Random Two-Liquid (NRTL) excess Gibbs energy model was applied to correlate the measured phase equilibria. The obtained parameters were further validated using a decanter model.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-72881-3.

Introduction

Levulinic acid (LA), a carboxylic acid, is one of the top 12 promising bio-based building blocks according to the United States Department of Energy for the synthesis of fuels and chemicals1. LA represents an important platform chemical for the synthesis of several raw materials for applications in fragrances, fuel additives, pharmaceuticals, plasticizers, and solvents2,3. Reactive extraction is a convenient technique for the separation of major carboxylic acids such as LA because the combined reaction-separation results in a higher apparent solute distribution coefficient4. Reactive extraction has the advantage of combining chemical extraction with physical extraction, resulting in exceptionally higher recovery efficiencies compared to the non-reactive separations5. During the last 30 years, reactive extraction has been used in the recovery of alcohols, phenols, and carboxylic acids from complex media6–9. Carboxylic acid separation by reactive extraction is a well-known process10–15; however, there is a growing need for refinements to minimize costs and environmental impacts prior to their implementation in industrial settings. Increasing usage of toxic solvents in chemical processes has a negative impact on the global environment, necessitating the need to identify and utilize efficient, economical, and most critically, eco-friendly solvents5,15–20.

Selection of the organic phase components, the extractant and the diluent are important steps in reactive extraction. The extractant plays an important role in reactive extraction while the diluents control the physical properties (viscosity, density, surface tension, etc.) of the solvent phase. Diluents also affect the stability of the complex formed between the solute and the extractant21. Substituting diluents in reactive extraction is significantly easier relative to replacing extractants due to the limited availability of the latter for selection. Efficient extractants often fall in the category of high molecular weight aliphatic amines and organophosphorus compounds22,23 while alternatives have not been reported so far. Consequently, majority of the studies focus on alternative diluents or solvents used in the organic phase (extractant + diluent). The previous two decades have seen studies carried out in the use of vegetable oils in reactive extraction of carboxylic acids from aqueous media as an environment-friendly and cost-effective option16,18,20,24,25. Their eco-friendliness, non-volatility and immiscibility in aqueous solutions are predominant and fascinating properties for their usage as organic phase diluents. Solvents such as dichloromethane26, 1-octanol14,27, toluene26,28, methyl-iso-butylketone (MIBK)26,29, 1-hexanol28, chloroform29, xylene29, and ethyl propionate30 are some of the toxic diluents that have been used in the organic phases of the mentioned systems. In comparison, only one work had studied eco-friendly organic phase diluents for LA extraction. Sunflower oil and groundnut oil was tested as the diluent in the aqueous system of LA without an extractant31. Eugenol, a major constituent of clove oil and a phenolic aromatic compound is a promising candidate for a wide range of applications. The multiple functional groups which are part of the chemical structure of eugenol play an active role in the extraction of LA. It is considered as an extractant and diluent in this work. A recent study also indicated that the recovery of LA from aqueous solutions increased with shorter chain alcohols32. This increases our interest with regards to methanol (MeOH) since it is the shortest chain alcohol. Previous works have shown that a mixture of physical diluent with an extractant tends to enhance the extraction efficiency compared to the use of only an extractant33,34. A new study carried out recently by Marti et al. indicated that extraction yields with tri-n-octylamine (TOA) using vegetable oils were similar to that of using 1-octanol as a diluent5.

Considering these circumstances, this study set out to fill a missing link in the literature by delivering new experimental data on the liquid-liquid equilibria of the ternary system of LA + eugenol + H2O and quaternary systems of LA + eugenol/MeOH + H2O + TOA at T = 293.15 K. For this purpose, the gas chromatography and cloud point methods were utilized to determine tie line and binodal composition values. The distribution coefficients (KD) values were also calculated from the experimental results. Finally, the measured data is modeled with NRTL35 and used as the activity coefficient model for the liquid phase. The data is modeled with available literature data on LLE36,37 followed by regression of the missing parameters. No prior works are available in literature which have considered the specific systems mentioned above.

Apparatus and method

Determination of tie lines

The analytical LLE data measurements were carried out at T = 293.15 K and atmospheric pressure using the glass cell apparatus described by Männistö et al.48. The experimental equipment used in the current study is the same as the one described in detail in our previous work49. The tie lines are determined by generating a biphasic mixture in the glass cells at a fixed temperature, vigorously stirring them for a minimum of 4 h and placing them in a constant temperature water bath for at least 72 h to achieve equilibrium. Equilibrium is assumed to be reached, when the difference in weight fractions between the two repeats is less than 0.0001. The temperature was controlled by a thermostat (Model: Lauda E200, Germany). The temperature of water at the input and output were measured using calibrated Pt-100 probes and an ASL CTR-2000-24 thermometer with an uncertainty of u(T) = 0.2 K. This uncertainty is caused by the cell design and the temperature within the cell. The uncertainty of the thermometer itself is much better at u(T) = 0.03 K. After separating into two clear phases with a Hamilton sample-lock syringe, samples from both phases were carefully extracted to evaluate LA, TOA, and the diluent concentrations. The samples in the syringe were injected into the vials where the solvent (2-propanol) was placed prior to sampling. For each solution, after reaching equilibrium the sampling and analysis was replicated at least three times. Gas chromatography (GC) with an Agilent 7890B was used in the determination of the concentration of each component in the top and bottom phases. The Agilent 7890B GC parameters are the same as presented in our previous work49. The known masses of 2-propanol with levulinic acid, TOA, eugenol, MeOH and water were prepared for the calibration of GC. Thermodynamic modeling

The modeling of the tie lines was carried out in the Aspen Plus software V12.1.

Results and discussion

Experimental tie line data

The tie line compositions for the quaternary aqueous systems including LA + eugenol/MeOH + H2O + TOA, were measured at T = 293.15 K and atmospheric pressure. The results are presented in Tables 2, 3 and 4, expressing the concentrations in weight fractions. The correlation tie line data obtained by the NRTL method for all the systems are provided in Tables S1–S3 in the Supporting Information. The measured values indicated in Table 2 for the ternary system of LA + eugenol + H2O, show that as the concentration of LA increases, the KD increases.

In the ternary system containing LA + Eugenol + H2O, the extraction is based on physical interaction (solubility). However, in the quaternary systems of LA + Eugenol/MeOH + H2O + TOA, eugenol is utilized as a diluent wherein the extraction of LA is taking place according to the chemical interaction between solute (LA) and extractant (TOA). According to the data in Table 3, eugenol improved the extraction of LA due to its functional group (–OH group) which improves solvation of the TOA-LA complex22,50,51. Accordingly, a comparison of the extraction power of the different diluents were carried out49 as shown in Fig. 1. Figure 1 shows that the extraction power of eugenol is comparable to isoamyl alcohol49. It also further supports the possibility of considering it as a green and eco-friendly alternative. Similar results were obtained for vegetable oils as a diluent by Marti et al. for pyruvic acid5. Overall, both methanol, as a short chain alcohol, and eugenol in combination with TOA can satisfactorily extract LA from aqueous solution.

The interaction parameters for LA + H2O, TOA + H2O, LA + TOA, and MeOH + H2O were taken from the earlier studies36,37,49. All the parameters were tested using the decanter unit in the simulation environment of Aspen. The starting points from the experiment were inputted as the weight fraction of the feed in the decanter. The composition of the exiting streams was contrasted with the experimental data and plotted to discern the agreement between the experimental and calculated results.

The ternary system of Eugenol + LA + H2O was also fitted with the NRTL model. The binary interaction parameters between eugenol + LA and eugenol + H2O were obtained by regression in this work. At dilute amounts of LA, the estimated tie lines are in good agreement with the experimental data. As the concentration of LA increases, the slope of the modeled tie lines slowly starts deviating from the experimental tie lines. The quaternary system of LA + Eugenol + H2O + TOA were also correlated with the NRTL model. The phase behavior described by the binary interaction parameters obtained from the ternary system experimental data described the quaternary system as well. The remaining pair of parameters in the system was of TOA + Eugenol which was obtained by regression from the quaternary data. The estimated tie lines were in close agreement with the experimental results for the ternary and quaternary systems as indicated in Figs. 2 and 3 respectively.

Conclusion

The purpose of the current study was to determine the extraction ability of TOA with eugenol and methanol in the LLE of LA from aqueous solution. The LLE data were experimentally measured using analytical method for the systems of LA + MeOH/Eugenol + H2O + TOA at T = 293.15 K and atmospheric pressure. The extraction ability of the diluents with TOA to extract LA were determined by calculating the KD using experimental values. The obtained results concluded that both methanol and eugenol with TOA can be successfully used as diluents in the reactive extraction of LA from aqueous solution. Additionally, a comparison of different organic diluents showed that eugenol could be a viable option due to its non-toxicity and as a substitute for other organic diluents based on its extractability of LA with TOA. Additionally, the experimental tie lines were fitted with the NRTL model, and it can be deduced that there is a good agreement between calculated results and experimental data.
