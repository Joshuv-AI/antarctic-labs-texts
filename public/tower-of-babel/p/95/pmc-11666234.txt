Organizational on-boarding practices and shifts in alcohol misuse among at-risk college recruits
Authors: Peter A. Bamberger, Mo Wang, Samuel B. Bacharach, Mary Larimer, Inbal Nahum-Shani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
With a significant proportion of college students in many countries engaging in risky drinking behavior, this study examines the tendency of such young adults to ‘mature out’ of such behavior in their first year of employment after graduating, and the degree to which three mainstream organizational on-boarding experiences may expedite such ‘maturing out’. Focusing on newcomers’ experiences with alcohol-oriented job orientation, job empowerment, and organizational efforts to facilitate the development of supportive peer relationships, we test hypotheses regarding the direct effects of time on the change in alcohol misuse among those reporting misuse in college, as well as the degree to which individual on-boarding experiences account for the variance in young adults’ maturing out trajectories over the course of their first year of employment. Findings generated from data collected from over 400 young adults over multiple waves offer important theoretical and practical implications regarding how and why particular onboarding tactics may be more or less effective in influencing newcomers’ health-related behaviors.

Introduction

With work demanding ever higher levels of intellectual skill, enterprises continue to rely on college recruiting to fill many entry-level positions (Collegiate Employment Research Institute, 2017; Fuller et al., 2022). Over 15% of entry-level professional positions in the USA are filled by young adults recruited out of college (National Association of Colleges & Employers, 2022), with that level reaching nearly 100% in certain types of organizations, such as professional service firms (Rivera, 2015). Fueling this trend is an assumption on the part of many employers that, having already been screened by college admissions officers and then socialized to meet challenging demands in their classes, these individuals are more mature and better positioned than their non-college peers to meet the responsibilities of contemporary employment (Arnett, 2000; Rivera, 2015).However, evidence suggests that this assumption may be questionable. For example, Payscale (2016) reports that over 50% of managers surveyed reported their newly hired graduates to be lacking ‘soft skills’ including critical thinking, attention to detail, communication and interpersonal/teamwork skills. Moreover, while managers may assume that associated with college graduation is a ‘maturing out’ out of the kind of college-typical behaviors that can be problematic for employers, studies suggest that this may not be the case. In the current study, we focus on alcohol misuse—the use of alcohol in a manner not consistent with legal or medical guidelines (WHO, 2017)—as one such behavior, doing so for several reasons. First, among the college-typical behaviors that can be problematic if carried over into employment (e.g. cheating, chronic tardiness, sensation-seeking, inappropriate cellphone use), alcohol misuse is among the most prevalent. Indeed, the ‘Monitoring the Future’ (MTF) study found 37% of American college students (versus 28% on their non-college peers) engaging in heavy drinking, with 44% (versus 29% for their non-college peers) reporting having been drunk in the prior 30 days (Johnston et al., 2004). Moreover, data suggest a positive association between educational attainment and alcohol misuse in many countries (e.g. UK, Huerta & Borgonovi, 2010). Second, to the extent that these young adults maintain such patterns as they transition from college to work, the implications can be significant, heightening the risk of workplace accidents and injury, undermining task and contextual performance, and raising the odds of counter-productive work behavior (for reviews, see Frone, 2019; Frone & Bamberger, 2023). Finally, consistent with the findings of the Institute of Medicine (1998, pp. 110–111) indicating that it is precisely in this age group that ‘developmental trajectories become established and increasingly difficult to alter’, several studies (e.g. Sher et al., 2001) suggest that while the protective effect of college with respect to problem drinking increases over time, significant maturing out only begins to manifest after age 25; well after most students have entered the workforce. In fact, Hingson et al. (2009) report that in the United States, it is precisely among young adults ages 21–24 that alcohol morbidity is at its highest, and that since 1999, the age at which alcohol morbidity peaks has consistently increased. Similar findings, particularly for men, are reported for Sweden (Andersson et al., 2007), Spain (Moure-Rodríguez et al., 2016), and Eastern Europe (Zadarko-Domaradzka et al., 2018), with young adults between the ages of 18–29 accounting for 1/3 of those engaging in risky drinking in Sweden (Bendtsen et al., 2011).

These trends, along with employers’ growing reliance upon college grads as a key talent source, suggest a need to better understand the variability in young adults’ maturing out of such college-typical patterns of drinking, as well as the onboarding experiences potentially underlying such variance. Such an understanding may have significant practical value by providing employers with insight into which onboarding tactics may be effective in addressing such behavior among students transitioning from college to career employment. Unfortunately however, the extant literature on onboarding, focusing largely on newcomer task mastery and social integration (Batistič, 2018; Klein & Polin, 2012), offers little systematic evidence regarding the impact of conventional onboarding tactics on newcomer health and health-related behaviors (Ellis et al., 2015). Accordingly, the current study aims to address this research gap by integrating motivational theories of alcohol misuse with the literatures on newcomer socialization and employer onboarding tactics.

Based on our review of both literatures, we identify three onboarding tactics, namely alcohol-oriented orientation training, job empowerment and the facilitation of supportive relationships that may be effective in mitigating any tendency among those recruited out of college to maintain alcohol misuse over the first year of employment. More specifically, given that newcomers may maintain or even exacerbate college-based patterns of alcohol misuse in order to cope with the strain associated with onboarding (Ellis et al., 2015) or to expedite their entry into work-based social networks (Liu et al., 2015), as shown in Figure 1, we posit that experience with onboarding tactics aimed at clarifying organizational expectations regarding drinking, empowering newcomers with the control needed to tackle workplace demands and stressors, and facilitating social support at work, will be efficacious in helping young adult newcomers with a history of alcohol misuse in college to more quickly mature out of such behavior.

Following convention (Frone, 2013), we operationalize alcohol misuse along two dimensions, namely the frequency of a problematic drinking behavior (i.e. heavy episodic drinking), and the potentially adverse behavioral and physiological consequences of all forms of drinking behavior (including heavy modal, as opposed to strictly episodic, consumption) more generally (i.e. problematic consequences of drinking). Indeed, Devos-Comby and Lange (2008) suggest that in assessing young adult misuse, there may be an advantage of using both types of indicators in that: (a) even frequent acute intoxication may not necessarily result in relationship or work-related problems, and (b) while some young adults may downplay (and hence under-report) adverse consequences, their reporting of heavy episodic drinking may be less subject to social desirability. Furthermore, from a practical perspective, both misuse outcomes have relevance to employers, with HED having been consistently identified as a predictor of employee absenteeism and job impairment (e.g. Bacharach et al., 2010; Thørrisen et al., 2019), and ARP having been documented as spilling over into a wide range of work-related issues including workplace injury, aggression and presenteeism (Mangione et al., 1999; McFarlin et al., 2001; Severeijns et al., 2024; Webb et al., 1994). Our analyses then test the degree to which each of these three onboarding tactics amplifies any inverse effect of time on the severity of alcohol misuse, or in other words, expedites any natural ‘maturing out’ process.

Our findings offer several important contributions to research on newcomer socialization. First, in the context of increased interest in the link between socialization and newcomer well-being (Ellis et al., 2015), our findings suggest that while many of those misusing alcohol in college disengage from such behavior in their initial year of post-college employment, such ‘maturing out’ is limited, slow and inconsistent across newcomers, with many of those entering career employment with a history of misuse unlikely to change that pattern simply as a function of transitioning from college to work. Second, our findings offer some of the first empirical insights into the efficacy of onboarding experiences in mitigating newcomer tendencies to maintain such risky behavior.

Theory and hypotheses

Transitioning from college to work and the motivation to maintain patterns of misuse

Aside from the risks posed by alcohol misuse to individuals (i.e. heightened risk of liver disease, pancreatitis, dementia, and various types of cancer; Zhao et al., 2023), several decades of research indicate that employee alcohol misuse can impose significant costs on employers. For example, alcohol misuse has been linked to employee absenteeism (Bacharach et al., 2010), reduced productivity (Sacks et al., 2015), and a heightened risk of interpersonal conflict and harassment (Bacharach et al., 2007; Frone, 2013) as well as work accidents and injuries (Frone, 2013). And because such behaviors and outcomes heighten the risk of early termination (Bauer et al., 2007; Cable et al., 2013; French et al., 2011), alcohol misuse among newcomers has the potential to threaten the return on investment made in recruitment and training (Batistič, 2018). Indeed, in a sample of newly hired sales and service staff in China, Liu et al. (2020) found work-based misuse to increase the odds of turnover within 6 months from hire by nearly three times.

Prevention-oriented onboarding practices can play a key role in addressing these employment-related costs by motivating behavioral change among transitioning newcomers that engaged in such problematic behaviors while in college. Such practices tend to focus on interventions targeting the two primary mechanisms underlying employee alcohol misuse, namely coping motives and social motives (Cooper, 1994). Coping motives refer to ‘drinking to reduce or regulate negative emotions’ (Cooper, 1994, p. 118); with the implicit logic being that one may use alcohol to achieve tension reduction and deal with unpleasant affective states (Cooper et al.,1995). Social motives refer to ‘drinking to obtain positive social rewards’ (Cooper, 1994, p.118), with misuse driven by an interest in solidifying social relations and/or ‘fitting in’ (Ames et al., 2000). In the same way that normative and environmentally demanding conditions in college may provide the motivational basis for student alcohol misuse (Perkins, 2002), similar conditions in the college-to-work transition may provide the motivational basis for the maintenance or exacerbation of such behavior.

Tension-reduction motivation

Tension reduction (Conger, 1956) and self-medication (Frone, 2013) theories suggest that young adult newcomers transitioning into career employment may drink to cope with negative emotional states precipitated or exacerbated by transition-related conditions. Although many newcomers experience a type of ‘reality shock’ when starting work with a new employer (Louis, 1980), according to Bauer & Erdogon (2012, p. 105) this shock ‘can be especially pronounced for recent graduates embarking upon new careers’. Building on this idea, Ellis et al. (2015, p. 206) identified uncertainty relating to tasks, roles and social relationships as both widely prevalent and ‘innately stressful’ for newcomers.

However, to the extent that newcomers positively frame such stressors and demands as challenges (Ashford & Black, 1996) and proactively leverage resources to gain clarity and build supportive networks, theory (Bakker et al., 2010; Hobfoll, 1989) and empirical evidence (Ellis et al., 2015) suggest that they may be less likely to experience resource drain and strain and more positively adjust. In contrast, newcomers having fewer resources at their disposal may tend to appraise these same demands and stressors as hindrances, disengage from the adjustment process, and myopically adopt coping patterns that focus on resource conservation and withdrawal (Ellis et al., 2015). Such patterns, while perhaps lessening resource drain in the short-run, have the potential to result in an increasingly rapid and self-reinforcing pattern of resource depletion (i.e. loss spirals), and, ultimately, the exacerbation of personal resource impoverishment and adverse psychological and physiological outcomes (Halbesleben et al., 2014).

In sum, an ecological perspective (Hobfoll, 1989) on newcomer stress suggests that to the extent that young adult newcomers’ alcohol misuse may be based on transition-related demands and resources, experience with onboarding tactics emphasizing the development and enhancement of those personal (e.g. self-efficacy) and social (e.g. supportive network) resources potentially leveraged to address such demands may be among those most effective in mitigating the maintenance or exacerbation of patterns of alcohol misuse.

Social motivation

Models of social behavior emphasize the importance of peer attitudes/behaviors as social and normative influences that provide vicarious learning experiences regarding the precipitation and maintenance of behavior (Bandura, 1977; Maisto et al., 1999). More specifically, alcohol misuse may be influenced by whether and under what conditions social norms signal the acceptability or unacceptability of alcohol use. The social acceptability of alcohol use is a function of descriptive (perceived use of substances by one’s social network members) and injunctive norms (inferred approval or disapproval of substance use by the same; Borsari & Carey, 2003), with both found to have a direct impact on student (Lewis et al., 2011) and employee (Bacharach et al., 2002; Frone, 2013) drinking.

Newcomers often experience a lack of clarity as to what constitutes appropriate drinking in the context of one’s work role, with the result being that they often look to others for insight (Liu et al., 2015). The newcomer socialization literature consistently demonstrates that it is largely through observing, seeking information from, and receiving feedback from work-related others that newcomers strive to attain often uncertain and potentially conflicting goals (e.g. Ashford & Black, 1996; Zhou et al., 2022). But with respect to certain behaviors such as drinking in and around work, the signals inferred from one’s supervisors, peers and even clients may be inconsistent, with even intra-person signals (such as those inferred from a supervisor) being difficult to interpret (Liu et al., 2015).

While a variety of factors (e.g. the salience of those identified with the perceived norm) are likely to influence which of these informal signals newcomers more strongly weighted (Biron & Bamberger, 2012), research on newcomer socialization suggests that such informal socialization forces are likely to play a greater role in shaping newcomer work behaviors when formal, institutional socialization is lacking or transmits unclear or mixed messages. Indeed, Liu et al. (2015) note, ‘when the most appropriate behavior is unclear, descriptive norms may provide insight into the most effective, justifiable, and least risky action for the situation’. In contrast, when formal socialization is targeted to communicate a particular message and clarify how uncertainties are resolved, there is good reason to expect newcomers to behave accordingly. Hence, from a social motivational perspective, onboarding tactics that provide clarity for the newcomer as to the what the organization expects from them regarding potentially risky behavior such as alcohol use may mitigate pressures to use drinking as a means by which to ‘fit in’, and thus facilitate adjustment.

Drinking motives and the potential for maturing out over time

Ashforth (2012, p. 161) writes that, ‘the role of time must be explicitly incorporated into a theory… if a theory is to provide an ontologically accurate description of a phenomenon. This is particularly true of socialization…’ Despite the diminished effect of employment on college graduates’ maturing out of alcohol misuse and the finding that such maturing out is increasingly delayed (Hingson et al., 2009), we explicitly incorporate time into the current model, positing that we are still likely to observe some degree of time-based, ‘maturing out’. Indeed, according to both the tension reduction and social motivation perspectives noted above, some reduction in alcohol misuse behavior (i.e. maturing out) may be expected already over the course of newcomers’ initial year on the job.

From a tension-reduction perspective, maturing out may be expected in that with time, newcomers are likely to adjust to their new position, face less uncertainty, and have more resources to leverage in response to the uncertainties and demands that they encounter (Ashforth, 2012; Wang et al., 2015). Additionally, from a social motivation perspective, greater clarity regarding expectations of relational behavior, along with the natural development of relationships with others in the organization resulting from task-related exchanges, are likely to ameliorate early pressures for social integration. Thus, whereas upon entry, young adult newcomers may rely upon college-practiced methods of using drinking to facilitate entry into new social networks, over time newcomers may become less dependent upon such approaches and more willing to experiment with alternative approaches for entering, building and embedding themselves in new social networks (Liu, 2015).

Taken together, these two general trends suggest:

Hypothesis 1: Newcomers manifest a downward trajectory in alcohol misuse over the course of their onboarding experience.

Time, onboarding tactics and the acceleration of ‘maturing out’

But even assuming such a general downward trend, the question remains, what tactics may employers adopt with respect to onboarding to accelerate this downward slope of newcomer maturing out? Saks and Gruman (2012, p. 29) conceptualize onboarding tactics as ‘organization-initiated activities, programs, events, and experiences that are specifically designed to facilitate newcomers’ learning, adjustment, and socialization into a job, role, work group, and organization so that they can become effective members of the organization’.

Scholars have proposed a variety of frameworks for classifying the types of tactics typically used by organizations to onboard newcomers, implicitly linking these tactics to the tension reduction and social integration motivations noted above. Van Maanen and Schein (1979) were among the first to offer such a framework, differentiating between six different tactic dimensions such as collective/individual and formal/informal. More recently, Saks and Gruman (2012) proposed socialization resource theory to extend our understanding of the types of onboarding organizations may use to facilitate newcomer adjustment. This framework, grounded on same resource-based perspective as Ellis et al. (2015) analysis of newcomer stress, suggests that onboarding tactics can be conceptualized as revolving around three main types of resources required for effective newcomer adjustment. These are: informational resources (such as those provided in formal, collective orientation programs), social resources (such as those provided in the context of programs aimed at facilitating the development of supportive social networks), and psycho-emotional resources (e.g. confidence and self-efficacy such as those facilitated on the basis of newcomer empowerment efforts).

Taken together, these frameworks suggest that in examining tactics addressing young adult newcomers’ motivations for continued misuse, it is important to examine newcomers’ experiences with at least three main onboarding tactics, namely: (a) formal, structured orientation programs oriented towards providing newcomers with information regarding organizational policies regarding such behaviors, (b) programs and efforts aimed at building newcomers’ supportive social relations within the organization, and (c) empowerment-oriented job design efforts (such as those promoting autonomy) aimed at boosting newcomer self-efficacy and sense of control.

Formal, structured orientation programs

Cable and Parsons (2001, p. 4) describe formal, structured orientation programs as programs ‘designed by organizations to ensure that newcomers receive a common message about the organization’s values and how they should interpret and respond to situations’. Bauer & Erdogon (2012, p. 55) suggest that such programs reduce employee uncertainty regarding the culture of the organization they are about to join, and nature of the role they are about to enter, by ‘providing them with information for being successful in their new jobs’, and that ‘employees tend to experience more positive job attitudes, higher levels of fit, and lower levels of turnover after they go through institutionalized socialization tactics compared with those who undergo individualized tactics’. Widely prevalent in organizations (Klein et al., 2015), research suggests that these programs can facilitate newcomer adjustment along a wide range of dimensions (Bauer & Erdogon, 2012), particularly to the extent that they are grounded on content targeted towards achieving specific outcomes (Saks & Gruman, 2012). For example, a field experiment evaluating the efficacy of an orientation program specifically aimed at reducing newcomer stress found that this program to indeed be associated with lower stress and enhanced adjustment months later (Fan & Wanous, 2008).

Incorporating alcohol-related content into formal orientation programs may accelerate newcomer maturing out on the basis of both tension-reduction and social-normative motivations for drinking. With respect to tension reduction, research suggests that by clarifying and aligning expectations (in this case regarding modes of acceptable behavior), participation in onboarding training is inversely associated with newcomer anxiety and stress (Taormina & Law 2000). Relating to such findings, Ashforth et al. (2007, p. 452) conclude that ‘orientating newcomers about the job, role, group, and organization reduces uncertainty and anxiety’.

Similarly, such a tactic may be effective in facilitating maturing out by addressing social/normative motivations to drink. First, as noted by Feldman (1989, p. 399), targeted orientation training can facilitate sense making, and serve as ‘the setting where norms about social behavior are developed’. Kim and Moon (2021, p. 1319) similarly note that, such efforts ‘teach newcomers definitions and reactions to situations to establish a common organization-wide value system’. Thus, to the extent that newcomers are put through orientation training experiences aimed at clarifying the organization’s policies regarding substance misuse, normative uncertainty regarding the conditions under which alcohol consumption is deemed acceptable may be reduced, and newcomers may have less of a need to infer normative expectations based on observations of others’ behavior. Second, by clarifying the organization’s policies regarding alcohol misuse as well the sanctions potentially enforced for the violation of these policies, alcohol-focused orientation programs may offer a counterforce to newcomer positive vicarious experiences with respect to drinking. That is, explicitly cautioning newcomers about the physical and employment-related risks associated with alcohol misuse may reduce the subjective expected utility newcomers associate with drinking as a means by which to socially integrate and build social relations within the organization. Accordingly we propose that:

Hypothesis 2: The greater newcomers’ exposure to information regarding their organization’s alcohol-related policies as part of a formal orientation program, the more accelerated their pattern of declining alcohol misuse (i.e. ‘maturing out’).

Efforts boosting newcomers’ supportive social relations

Numerous studies have found that informal support from socialization agents such as peers and senior colleagues plays an important role in newcomer adjustment (Ellis et al., 2015; Ou et al., 2018; Saks & Gruman, 2012). For example, support from peers and veteran coworkers was identified by Louis et al. (1983) as the most important onboarding practice. Korte (2009) found such supportive relations to serve as a key mechanism for successful socialization. Finally, using Chinese data, Ou et al. (2018), found that among newcomers receiving greater peer support, supervisory support was let influential in driving newcomer adjustment.

Building on the two motivational logics noted earlier, proactive efforts on the part of management to encourage and facilitate the development of such supportive relations with workplace peers may be expected to accelerate a downward slope in newcomers’ alcohol misuse (i.e. maturing out) for two main reasons. First, in terms of tension-reduction motivations, social support is identified as one of the most critical resources that individuals may use to manage stressors at work (Hobfoll, 1989), with studies finding that along with job control, social support serves as a key resource attenuating the adverse effect of demands on employee wellbeing (Karasek & Theorell, 1990), and meta-analytic findings indicating that social support significantly attenuates the stressor-strain relationship (Viswesvaran et al., 1999).

Several mechanisms may underlie the mitigating or buffering effect supportive relations may have on newcomers motivation to use alcohol as a mode of self-medication or escapist coping. First, supportive peers may, by providing advice, information, social capital and other forms of help, reduce the newcomers’ work demands, and hence any strain resulting from them (Nelson & Quick, 1991). For example, Kim and Moon (2021) found that the more that Korean newcomers were partnered with veteran peers as a socialization tactic, the greater that information acquisition in the early stages of onboarding which in turn reduced their sense of uncertainty regarding their future in the organization. Second, even in the absence of objectively reducing the work demands experienced by newcomers, supportive peers may help newcomers reappraise such stressors such that they are perceived more as challenges than hindrances. There is growing evidence that such cognitive reappraisal has robust effects on felt strain (Dawson et al., 2016). Finally, supportive peers at work may help newcomers find alternative modes of coping with strain or facilitate the kind of help-seeking necessary to reduce any dependence upon drinking as a mode of coping with stress (Bacharach et al., 2001).

Supportive peer relations may also have beneficial, maturing out implications with respect to social motivations to drink. More specifically, to the extent that such individuals often serve as important sources of information and contacts for newcomers (Kim & Moon, 2021), more supportive social relations may provide newcomers an alternative to drinking as means by which to forge new relations and build social capital. No less importantly, to the degree that the newcomer already has a network of supportive relations at work, they may feel less of a need to use drinking as a basis for social integration either at work or outside of it (Thoits, 2011). Indeed, Kammeyer-Mueller et al. (2013: 1106) note that beyond providing valuable information, supportive peers also provide a sense of social validation. With more senior colleagues potentially symbolizing the social setting into which a newcomer is attempting to fit, ‘signs of social support can be interpreted as evidence that the environment for the newcomer will be positive and accepting’, thus perhaps reducing the motivation to use drinking as a means by which to enhance one’s position in an existing social network or enter an alternative one. Accordingly, we posit that:

Hypothesis 3: The greater newcomers’ sense that they have a supportive set of colleagues at work, the more accelerated their pattern of declining alcohol misuse (i.e. ‘maturing out’).

Designing newcomer jobs to facilitate empowerment

Although relatively few studies have examined the impact of newcomers’ job characteristics on newcomer adjustment, according to Saks and Gruman (2012), those that have suggest that empowerment-related job characteristics such as task significance and meaningfulness, as well as self-determination and autonomy play an important role in socialization success. For example, research suggests a positive association between the level of autonomy, meaning and significance afforded by newcomer jobs, and newcomers’ satisfaction, adjustment and commitment at the end of their onboarding (i.e. approximately one year after entry) (Ashforth et al., 1998). Although largely untested, the literature suggests that empowerment-oriented job design facilitates such beneficial socialization outcomes by boosting newcomer task proficiency and enhancing newcomers’ sense of self-efficacy (Saks & Gruman, 2012). More generally, Bauer & Erdogon (2012, p. 57) note that ‘self-efficacy and similar concepts, such as task mastery, are highly related to socialization outcomes, such as organizational commitment, satisfaction, and turnover’.

In the same way that empowerment-oriented job design may boost newcomer adjustment and facilitate more successful socialization, it may also mitigate the tension-reduction motivation to drink, thus accelerating maturing out. With its focus on self-determination and autonomy, empowerment-oriented job design is likely to offer newcomers a greater sense of job control, a key parameter in resource-based models of occupational stress such as the Job Demands-Control/Support (JD-C/S) model (Karasek & Theorell, 1990) and Job Demands-Resources (JD-R) Model (Bakker et al., 2010)). The sense of mastery accompanying such control may facilitate newcomers’ framing of job- and role-related demands as challenges rather than hindrances, thus reducing felt strain (Ellis et al., 2015). Indeed, research on French healthcare workers found an inverse relationship between the characterization of a job as more empowering and negative emotional states (e.g. depressive symptoms) (Gillet et al., 2023). Furthermore, consistent with the JD-C/S and JD-R models, positions affording newcomers greater autonomy and self-determination are likely to buffer the incumbent from the adverse effects of job demands, and thus mitigate any motivation to engage in drinking to cope. Hence, we posit that:

Hypothesis 4: The greater newcomers’ characterization of the job as empowering, the more accelerated their pattern of declining alcohol misuse (i.e. ‘maturing out’).

Methods

Participants and procedures

Data were collected as part of a larger study entitled, The College-to-Work Transition & Alcohol Misuse: An Etiologic Study”, approved by Cornell University’s Office of Research Integrity and Assurance (IRB Protocol ID # 1408004876). Names and contact information for nearly 16,000 seniors in their final semester were received from the registrars of four large, research universities (3 public, 1 private) located in the Northeast, Midwest, West and Southern United States. Contacting them via email, we screened these students for graduation status and plans to begin working upon graduation, and then followed up with those deemed eligible to participate at least three more times over a period extending up to 20 additional months. Such a longitudinal approach is consistent with the multi-wave assessment strategies suggested by others (e.g. Ashforth, 2012; Kammeyer-Mueller et al., 2013).

5318 of these individuals responded to this screening survey (a coverage rate of 33%). Participation was proportionate to each school’s representation in the graduating cohort such that 14%, 28%, 27%, and 31% were from schools in the Pacific Northwest, Midwest, Southeast, and Northeast, respectively. Among these 5401 students (responding to the initial screening survey), 2250 indicated that they were not graduating or were graduating but not entering the U.S. labor market (e.g. continuing their studies, traveling), and thus were excluded from further participation. The remainder, indicating that they were graduating and planning to begin working upon graduation, were eligible to participate in the study.

Among them, 1330 responded to the screening survey after their school-specific sample size targets (determined by a priori power analysis for detecting small effects) in the main study had already been reached. Accordingly, these students were excluded from further participation. Another 83 students refused consent when invited to further participate in the study. The remaining 1738 students were immediately directed to a second pre-graduation online survey, with most participants completing it 2–3 months prior to their expected graduation. Across both of these pre-graduation surveys, participants provided information on assessing their demographics (e.g. gender, race), individual differences (e.g. personality traits, GPA), alcohol use, and academic/vocation-related variables. Among these students, 1730 completed both of the pre-graduation surveys, and received a $15 e-gift certificate. Those who completed both surveys were then asked to complete another survey one month after graduation, receiving an additional $25 e-gift certificate. 1649 participants (95.3% of those completing the pre-graduation surveys) completed this post-graduation survey.

As we were interested in the effects of onboarding on the maintenance or maturing out of alcohol misuse over the course of the first year of employment, we included in our analysis only those participants who: (a) reported being full time employed either one month after graduation (n = 605) or in a supplementary follow-up survey four months later (i.e. graduation plus 5 months; n = 509), and (b) reported still being full-time employed one year subsequent to reporting such initial full-time employment (n = 498 of those reporting employment one month after graduation and 425 of those reporting employment only five months following graduation) (total n = 923). We included in our analysis only those remaining employed over the course of that year with the same employer (n = 737). Additionally, because our study focused on maturing out behavior among those engaging in risky drinking behavior in college, we excluded those individuals not meeting criteria for such behavior (i.e. at least one binge—defined as more than 4 (women) or 5 (men) standard drinks in a single day—in the previous three months, or a score on the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test [AUDIT; Saunders et al., 1993) of greater than 5), thus leaving 467 full-time employed participants meeting all study criteria (i.e. at-risk drinkers in college, employed within five months of graduating, completed a 12 month period of on-boarding). Of these, 55% were women, 75% were Caucasian, 14% were Asian, 2% were African American, and 8% were multiracial. Mean age at graduation was 22 (SD = 0.76).

Measures

Alcohol misuse, ‘a pattern of drinking that results in harm to one’s health, interpersonal relationships or ability to work’ (CDC, 2024), can be operationalized in terms of a behavioral pattern of consumption that is objectively associated with acute intoxication (i.e. harm to one’s health), as well as in terms of harm manifestations (i.e. harmful interpersonal or work-related consequences)’ (Boden et al., 2012), with several widely used measures (e.g. AUDIT; Saunders et al., 1993) incorporating both (Devos-Comby & Lange, 2008). Accordingly, we assessed misuse based on two criteria, namely the frequency of heavy episodic (binge) drinking (HED) and problematic consequences of drinking, with both constructs assessed in all three waves of the study. We used the Single Alcohol Screening Question instrument (SASQ; Canagasaby & Vinson, 2005) to assess HED. Using the same definition of a standard drink noted above, participants were presented with a 0–30 day digital dial and asked, ‘How often in the past month did you drink more than 4 (if you are a woman) or 5 (if you are a man) standard drinks in a single day?’ We assessed problematic consequences of drinking based on the short-form Rutgers Alcohol Problem Index (RAPI; White & Labouvie, 1989) was used. The short RAPI includes 18 multiple-choice items assessing alcohol-related consequences occurring from 0 (never) to 4 (more than 10 times). Because change in RAPI is the dependent variable of interest in our study, the pre-graduation and post-graduation measures needed to contain identical items to appropriately model such change. Accordingly, we removed the item related to homework which would not have been applicable to participants in the post-college survey. In both surveys, the instructions asked participants to report: ‘How many times did the following things happen to you while you were drinking or because of your alcohol use during the past month?’ (e.g. ‘neglected your responsibilities’). The RAPI scale score was computed by summing across items.

Onboarding tactics

Klein et al. (2015) recommend assessing onboarding tactics on the basis of newcomer experience rather than managerial accounts. To ensure that such reports were in close proximity to the onboarding experience itself, we asked study participants to report on such experiences in the second survey, the same one in which they reported being employed (i.e. one month after graduation or five months after graduation if employment was not immediate). Orientation Program Tactics were assessed using a 2-item measure supplemented to and framed around Cable and Parson’s (2001) socialization experiences measure. Using the same format as the regular socialization experiences items, participants were asked to indicate their agreement (1= strongly disagree to 7 strongly agree) with the following two statements: ‘I have been through a set of training experiences that are specifically designed to give newcomers a thorough understanding of this organization’s policy with regard to employee alcohol and substance misuse;’ ‘This organization makes its alcohol and substance misuse policies very clear to newcomers’. Cronbach’s α was .86. Supportive Social Relationship Tactics were assessed using two items drawn from the short version of Cable and Parson’s (2001) socialization experiences measure. We assessed this shortened version of the instrument in two separate student samples prior to incorporating it into the main survey instrument, with Cronbach alpha and concurrent validity being 0.823 and 0.939, respectively in the first pilot study, and 0.752 and 0.912 in the second. Using the same agree/disagree question format noted above, the two items were, ‘almost all of my colleagues have been supportive of me personally’, and ‘my colleagues have gone out of their way to help me adjust to this organization’. Finally, we assessed Empowerment Tactics using an eight-item version of Spreitzer’s (1995) empowerment measure. We pre-tested this shortened version using a separate student sample prior to including it in the main survey instrument finding concurrent validity to be .977. Cronbach’s α was .81.

Time (fixed effect) was assessed in terms of months since the in-college baseline assessment (i.e. two months prior to graduation or t = 0), with subsequent times being 1 month post-graduation (t = 3), five months post-graduation (only for those reporting no employment at one month post-graduation, i.e. t = 7), and 12 months following the reporting of employment (i.e. either t = 15 or t = 19).

Control variables

We controlled for gender (coded as 0 = Male and 1 = Female) because of evidence that women misuse alcohol less than males (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, Office of Applied Studie). Additionally, we controlled for two alcohol-related, individual characteristics that could confound any underlying maturing out process, namely age at first intoxication and family history of alcohol abuse because there is some evidence that these factors may underlie young adult alcohol misuse (Hingson et al., 2006; LaBrie et al., 2010).

Analytical strategy

Following Aguinis et al. (2013) we estimated cross-level interaction effects on the basis of a series of fixed and random slopes models. Initial models were hierarchically structured, testing first random intercept, and then sequentially specifying parameters for: (a) control variables, (b) the main effects of the orientation tactics, and (c) the fixed effect of time (i.e. specifying the average effect of time). Subsequently, we tested: (a) a model specifying the random intercept and random slope of time and the direct effect of orientation tactics on participants’ misuse-related slopes (this, in part, to assess the variance around the average slope), and (b) the degree to which orientation tactics moderate these effects, accounting for that variance. We assessed model fit improvement on the basis of the change in −2LL and each model’s relative explanatory power on the basis of change in a Pseudo R2.

As our dependent variables were count in nature and manifested over-dispersion relative to a Poisson distribution, we estimated these models using a generalized Poisson distribution model. The GLIMMIX procedure (SAS version 9.4) was used to fit each model by maximum likelihood. The marginal likelihood of the data was approximated by adaptive quadrature (METHOD = QUAD option of GLIMMIX).

Results

Table 1 presents the means, standard deviations, and correlations. Notable is the decline in both the frequency of heavy episodic drinking (HED) and problematic drinking consequences (PDC) from baseline (i.e. in college) to initial post-graduation (i.e. 3 and 7 months following; T3 and T7) (from 3.58 to 2.73 and 2.79 for HED; from 3.83 to 2.68 and 2.16 for PDC). Empowerment tactics are inversely associated with the frequency of post-employment HED (r is −0.14, −0.12, and −0.17 at T7, T15 and T19; p < 0.05), while both orientation and supportive relations tactics are associated with lower PDC in the first month on the job (T3; −0.13, p < 0.05 for orientation; −0.19, p < 0.01 for supportive relations).

Results of the multivariate tests are displayed in Table 2 (for the frequency of HED) and Table 3 (for the intensity of PDC). For both, Model 1 tests the null model, indicating significant variability in mean values of HED and PDC (i.e. how much variability there is in the intercept) relative to the total variability (estimate = 1.45 and 0.71 for HED and PDC, respectively, p < 0.01 in both cases). Model 2 incorporates the three control variables into this same model, indicating that both (female) gender (estimate = −0.27 and −0.25 for HDE and PDC, respectively; p < 0.01) and age at first intoxication (‒0.03 and −0.04 for HDE and PDC, respectively; p < 0.01) are inversely related with misuse and explain a significant portion of the variability in the intercepts for both misuse variables. Model 3, in which we incorporate the main effects of the three onboarding tactics, indicates that while empowerment tactics are inversely related to both misuse variables (estimate = −0.16 [p < 0.01] and −0.12 [p < 0.05] for HDE and PDC, respectively), formal orientation has no significant association with either. Interestingly, relational socialization tactics are positively associated with the frequency of HED (estimate = 0.09; p < 0.05).

Model 4 presents the results of a test of Hypothesis 1. Consistent with this hypothesis, the estimates for the fixed effect of time (i.e. average effect of time scaled in months) was −0.16 and −0.22 (p < 0.01 for both) for the frequency of HED and intensity of PDC, respectively. However, while Model 5, which takes the variance in time slope into account shows improved model fit (change in ‒2LL = 5.35 and 6.25 for HED and PDC, respectively, [p < 0.05 for both]), it also indicates a significant level of variance in the slopes of time on both misuse outcomes (0.083 [p < 0.05] and 0.196 [p < 0.01] for HED and PDC). The latter indicates that there is merit in assessing the cross-level moderating effects of the three onboarding tactics hypothesized to accelerate the maturing out process (i.e. steepen the rate of decline in misuse over time).

We tested the accelerating cross-level effects hypotheses for orientation (Hypothesis 2), supportive relations (Hypothesis 3) and empowerment (Hypothesis 4) tactics in Model 6 of Tables 2 (for HED) and 3 (for PDC), with the results showing only partial support for these hypotheses. Contrary to Hypothesis 2, orientation tactic experience had no significant moderation effect on the variance in the slopes of time on either HED (Table 2) or PDC (Table 3), or in other words, is not associated with any acceleration in maturing out.

Consistent with Hypothesis 3, supportive relations onboarding tactics significantly moderated the effect of time on the frequency of HED (estimate = −0.08; p < 0.05). However, it had no significant effect on participants’ time-PDC slopes. As shown in Figure 2, the significant effect regarding time-HED slopes was largely consistent with Hypothesis 3 in that the downward slope for those reporting exposure to a high level of supportive relations tactics (estimate −0.212, p < 0.01) is steeper than that for those reporting exposure to only a low level of supportive relations tactics estimate = −0.05; n.s.), indicating that such tactics significantly accelerate the maturing out process. Interestingly, however, the intercept for those reporting exposure to a high level of supportive relations tactics was also substantially greater than that reported for those experiencing only a low level of supportive relations onboarding tactics.

Finally, consistent with Hypothesis 4, the level of newcomers’ exposure to empowerment tactics significantly moderated the effect of time on the severity of PDC (estimate = −0.15; p < 0.01). However, it had no significant effect on participants’ time-HED slopes. As shown in Figure 3, the significant effect regarding time-PDC slopes was largely consistent with Hypothesis 4 in that the downward slope for those reporting exposure to a high level of empowerment tactics (estimate −0.41, p < 0.01) is steeper than that for those reporting exposure to only a low level of empowerment tactics (estimate = −0.136; n.s.), indicating that empowerment tactics significantly accelerate newcomer maturing out.

Discussion

We examined the degree to which young adults identified as risky drinkers in college mature out of alcohol misuse over the course of their first year on the job, and the potential of three onboarding experiences in accelerating this process. Our findings indicate that while some of those misusing alcohol in college do disengage from such behavior in their initial year of post-college employment, such ‘maturing out’ is limited, slow and inconsistent across newcomers, with many such individuals largely maintaining the pattern exhibited in college despite their transition from college to work. For HR scholars and practitioners, this finding is significant in that it counters the assumption that problematic behaviors exhibited by young adults while in college are mitigated as a function their transition into career employment (Hettich, 2010; Vondracek & Reitzle, 1998). Regarding the role of onboarding experiences, contrary to our hypothesis, the findings indicated that the most prevalent of such tactics, namely alcohol-related orientation training, had no significant effect on the slope of either the frequency of episodic drinking (HED) nor on the slope of problem drinking consequences (PDC). This suggests that interventions orienting newcomers to employer alcohol policies may do little to accelerate the rate by which college graduates with a history of misuse while in college mature out of such behavior once transitioning into career employment. In contrast, we found that tactics aimed at empowering newcomers and boosting their supportive relations each had significant, accelerating effects, albeit each on a different indicator of alcohol misuse. More specifically, while empowerment amplified the downward slope of time on newcomer’s heavy episodic drinking, supportive relations amplified the downward slope of time on problem drinking consequences.

These results raise several questions. First, why was alcohol-targeted orientation not significantly associated with the rate of misuse decline over time? One possibility is that, consistent with findings from a study of socialization tactics in Japanese organizations (Takeuchi & Takeuchi, 2009), content-focused training is poorly tailored to shift newcomers’ sense of identification with the organization and its goals. Another possibility is that such institutionalized training-based efforts are predicated on the assumption that a better understanding of the potential costs to the newcomer of maintaining behaviors deemed risky by the organization will be sufficient to elicit and maintain behavioral change. However, Cable et al. (2013) argue that such institutionalized tactics often lack efficacy because they are aimed at getting the newcomer to adopt behaviors that, while perhaps consistent with the norms and values of the organization, are inconsistent with the employee’s sense of self. They suggest that sustainable behavioral change is likely only to the extent that the desired behavior is aligned with the employee’s authentic self. As they note (p. 3), ‘socialization practices that succeed in causing newcomers to behave inauthentically might not be sustainable.’

Second, why do the two remaining tactics—experience with supportive peer relations and job empowerment—have unique misuse outcomes, with the former accelerating maturing out from heavy episodic drinking (HED), and the latter accelerating out from problematic drinking consequences (PDC)? It may be that each of these outcomes taps a different aspect of alcohol misuse, with the two only moderately correlated (from 0.31 to 0.61) over the time-period studied. Indeed, there is evidence that the relationship between the frequency of HED and PDC may not be monotonic, with increasing but low levels of HED frequency (i.e. less than twice a week) not necessarily resulting in significantly higher levels of PDC, particularly when the quantity of consumption is at sub-threshold levels for categorization as ‘heavy’ (Carlfjord & Johansson, 2012). Similarly, PDC need not stem only from more frequent HED. Rather, more frequent modal alcohol consumption at quantities below the cut-off for heavy consumption can also result in problematic consequences (Werch et al., 1987). In this context, it may be that newcomers experiencing greater supportive relations at work find it less necessary to engage in HED to establish and maintain social relations outside of work. Similarly, newcomers employed in more empowering job contexts may take precautions to ensure that, regardless of the precise nature or timing of their drinking, such behavior does not generate consequences potentially affecting their ability to meet the responsibilities of their job.

Implications for theory and practice

While we can do little more than speculate on the answers to the questions raised above, our findings do offer some important implications for the link between newcomer socialization and well-being; a key aspect of socialization deemed by Ellis et al. (2015) as remaining poorly understood. Indeed, in their review of socialization outcomes, Bauer and Erdogan (2012) focus on only one well-being outcome, namely stress, highlighting how the few studies examining the predictors of stress in newcomer socialization have primarily focused on the role of individual factors such as task knowledge (Ostroff & Kozlowski, 1992) and self-efficacy (Saks & Ashforth, 2000). Our model and findings offer an important theoretical contribution in this regard in that they extend the range of impact of socialization to dimensions of psychological adjustment beyond stress to include behavioral health as well. Additionally, they suggest that both social and job resources can facilitate young adults’ behavioral health when these individuals confront the stressors and social pressures often accompanying the transition from college to career employment. Moreover, by incorporating both temporal and social factors, our model offers a theory-grounded framework that scholars may build upon to further clarify: (a) how health-related socialization outcomes, ‘unfold over time’, and (b) ‘what socialization practices—either individual or organizational tactics—contribute to enhanced resources critical for new employee wellbeing’ (Ellis et al., 2015, pp. 226–227).

Additionally, our findings contribute to the literature on organizational socialization and newcomer on-boarding by focusing attention on the specific case of young adults transitioning into career employment for the first time. Although scholars (e.g. Fouad & Bynner, 2008; Ng & Feldman, 2007) have suggested that organizational socialization processes and outcomes for young adults have unique features and elements distinct from socialization as it has been examined in the mainstream socialization and onboarding literatures, there are few models of how HR policies and practices may impact young adult entrants’ onboarding outcomes, and empirical research on the topic is even more limited. Indeed, while Wanberg’s (2012) Handbook on Organizational Socialization has several chapters on specialized groups such as expatriates, none of the chapters make any reference to the specific socialization challenges face by young adults or those transitioning into career employment for the first time. Building on the notion that the post-college years represent a period of emerging adulthood exemplified by ‘unbridled experimentation’ (Fouad & Bynner, 2008, p. 243) and an often challenging shift from a student to work role identity (Ng & Feldman, 2007), the current study highlights why it is important to examine the college-to-work transition as a unique socialization experience and offers one of the first models of how onboarding outcomes may be enhanced for this large and important group of organizational newcomers.

A third way in which our study contributes to newcomer socialization theory is by arguing and demonstrating that, counter to conventional wisdom, not all newcomers recruited out of college automatically and rather instantaneously ‘mature up’, disengaging from problematic behaviors that they may have experimented with while in while in college. Rather, for many of them, socialization into the work role involves a rather slow process of ‘maturing out’ of these behavioral patterns over time. Importantly, however, we argue and demonstrate that particular characteristics of the workplace and work role may help explain why some of those transitioning may mature out faster than others. More specifically, consistent with research on career entrants (Kammeyer-Mueller & Wanberg, 2003; Wendlandt & Rochlen, 2008), our findings suggest that interventions facilitating interactions and supportive relations with coworkers, perhaps by allowing for an easier cultural transition or simply by addressing feelings of isolation and anomie (Holton, 1998), may enhance adjustment as manifested by a steeper decline in alcohol misuse. Similarly, consistent with research highlighting the discrepancies between the expectations of graduating college students, particularly regarding autonomy and job challenge, and the reality they subsequently experience at work, (Gardner & Lambert, 1993), our findings demonstrate that the rate of maturing out of college-type patterns of alcohol misuse is significantly greater among those reporting higher levels of empowerment. In this regard our model and findings offer a significant contribution to theories of onboarding by enhancing our understanding of why the maturing-out process may be faster for some than others, and identifying organizational factors instrumental in explaining this variance.

Finally, our findings offer an important contribution to theories of work-site substance misuse (Frone, 2013), suggesting that two factors may be critical in understanding how and when on-boarding tactics may affect the variance in such rates of maturing out. The first factor has to do with the degree to which the tactic addresses the motivation to engage in such behavior in the first place. We posited and found that tactics addressing the two main motivations for alcohol misuse (i.e. tension reduction and social integration) had the potential to steepen the maturing out curve. However, we also found that even when the tactic addresses the motivation to maintain a particular behavioral pattern, behavioral change may not occur if, as in the case of alcohol-directed orientation training, such a tactic focuses strictly on the extrinsic utility of change (e.g. promotion, avoidance of sanctions). Accordingly, the second factor has to do with the degree to which the tactic generates a shift in the newcomer’s sense of self, and a more intrinsic interest in moving away from the potentially problematic pattern of behavior manifested in college. In contrast to orientation training tactics, tactics oriented toward enhancing job empowerment and facilitating the development of supportive relationships at work, while both potentially addressing newcomer stress and social integration motives to misuse alcohol, do so in a more active and authentic manner. That is, consistent with the approach suggested by the findings of Cable et al. (2013), these tactics create contexts facilitating a shift in individual identity (i.e. from ‘party animal’ to responsible colleague), thus driving newcomers to proactively disengage from behaviors that may be inconsistent with this new sense of self.

In sum, our findings suggest that to facilitate young adult newcomers’ ‘maturing out’ of problematic college-typical behaviors, onboarding tactics need to not only address the work-based motivational factors potentially driving such behavior, but in doing so, also facilitate a shift in self-identity more consistent with the demands of career employment and the responsibilities that come with it. In this way, our model and findings offer important insights into how young newcomers experience and manage the uncertainties of transitioning from college to work from a behavioral health perspective, as well as preliminary, theory-grounded insights into the health-related aspects of newcomer socialization that, as Ellis et al. (2015, p. 228) note, ‘has rarely been explicitly addressed’.

Our findings offer several practical implications as well. First, they suggest that employers can strategically design their onboarding programs to address the kinds of risky behaviors that young adults recruited out of college may bring with them if they have a reasonable basis for understanding the motives driving such behavior in the first place. Recognizing that such individuals may have become habituated in college to turn to alcohol misuse as a means of tension reduction, this suggests that employers might want to focus efforts on identifying and addressing key stressors for such newcomers, as well as providing mentors that can help these individuals learn alternative means of stress-coping. Second, they suggest that in designing such programs, employers rely less on passive, informative modes of onboarding (such as orientation training), and instead, place an emphasis on tactics aimed at driving a more autonomous motivation for behavioral change (Gagne et al., 2018) such as facilitating entry into supportive peer networks modeling alternative behavior (e.g. Google’s Blue Dot program and CSX Transportation’s Operation RedBlock; McHugh-Johnson, 2020; Bacharach et al., 2001), and designing more empowered work roles providing a sense of control and mastery key to mitigating stress and driving a shift in self-identity.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations that present opportunities for further research. First, given our interest in retaining our participants over multiple survey waves, we were forced to restrict the length of our on-line survey, and thus adopted rather narrow measures of the on-boarding tactics that we examined. While we pre-tested these abbreviated measures to ensure concurrent validity, the narrowness of some of our onboarding measures may have led to diminished sensitivity, and hence an inability to fully capture the true extent to which these tactics affect maturing out processes.

Second, given our interest in the role of organizational on-boarding in facilitating maturing out, we restricted our sample to those reporting alcohol misuse while in college. However, at least some of those not misusing alcohol in college may nevertheless begin misusing once employed. As our sample failed to capture these individuals, we are unable to report on the prevalence of such an upward shift, or the degree to which onboarding tactics may mitigate such tendencies. External validity may also be limited by a focus on students attending major research universities as they may secure employment distinctively different from graduates of less prestigious colleges, with the result being that their onboarding experiences may lack broader generalizability.

We encourage replication research addressing both limitations, as well as studies examining additional onboarding tactics such as mentoring and specific supervisory socialization behaviors as studies have found such behaviors to impact a wide range of post-entry, newcomer behaviors ((Kammeyer-Mueller & Wanberg, 2003; Saks & Gruman, 2012).

Conclusion

With a large proportion of college students engaging in risky drinking behavior, a substantial proportion of those graduating and beginning career employment may bring such risky behaviors with them to their new job. While some degree of maturing out among such individuals appears to occur following employment, the level of such tempering is both limited and variable. Moreover, this variance is sensitive to newcomers’ experience with supportive relations and job empowerment, two widely prevalent on-boarding strategies.
