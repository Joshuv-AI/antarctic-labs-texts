Influence of grain types and graphene nanopowder characteristics on insecticidal efficacy against common grain insects
Authors: Evagelia Lampiri, Pei Lay Yap, Christos G. Athanassiou, Dusan Losic
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The increasing resistance of insects to chemical-based pesticides is a critical challenge in crop production, demanding the urgent development of sustainable and effective pest control alternatives. In response, this study presents the insecticidal potential of graphene materials in the form of nanopowders as new chemical and resistance free grain protectants. The influence of the grain types such as rice, maize, and wheat and graphene nanopowder characteristics on insectidicial efficacy against common grain insects was evaluated against three most destructive grain insects including: the rice weevil, Sitophilus oryzae (L.) (Coleoptera; Curculionidae), the maize weevil, Sitophilus zeamais Motschulsky (Coleoptera; Curculionidae), and the red flour beetle, Tribolium castaneum Herbst (Coleoptera; Tenebrionidae). Three industrially produced graphene nanopowders with distinct physicochemical properties (particle size, surface chemistry, hydrophobicity) were used at two dosage rates (500 and 1000 ppm). Mortality of insects was assessed after 7, 14, and 21 days of exposure, and progeny production was evaluated after 65 days. The results indicated that S. oryzae exhibited the highest susceptibility among the tested species, with rice grains experiencing the most significant insect mortality across all graphene concentrations (500 and 1000 ppm). Significant reductions in progeny with minor produced insects were observed, especially in maize, highlighting the long-term protective effects of graphene nanopowders. The insectidicial mode of action is attributed to a physical mechanism involving the adhesion of graphene particles to insect bodies, obstructing respiration and disrupting the cuticle. These findings suggest that graphene nanopowders, due to their unique structural, chemical and interfacial properties, have a strong potential to be used as new grain protectants, providing unique physical mode of action.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11671-025-04359-8.

Introduction

Insect resistance in agriculture has emerged as a significant global challenge, threatening food security, economic stability, and sustainable farming practices [1]. Over the past several decades, the widespread use of toxic pesticides such as organochlorines, carbamates, organophosphates, and pyrethroids has played a critical role in controlling agricultural pests, ensuring higher crop yields, and reducing post-harvest losses [1–3]. However, this success has come at a cost, causing the evolution of pest populations that are increasingly resistant to conventional pesticides, causing environmental contamination and health concerns. Insect resistance to chemically based pesticides is driven by a range of metabolic, physiological, and genetic factors, including mutations that are often triggered by the overuse and misapplication of pesticides. Practices such as improper dosing, the use of uncalibrated spraying equipment, and other forms of mishandling frequently expose insect populations to sublethal concentrations. This suboptimal exposure facilitates detoxification processes within the insect body and accelerates the selection of resistant individuals, ultimately diminishing the efficacy of pesticide treatments. Insect resistance to chemical treatments during the post-harvest stages is a major concern in many countries, as it is closely associated with grain infestation and can lead to significant losses in grain production, sometimes exceeding 50% [3–6].

Several new approaches have been developed in recent years in response to the pest-resistance, health, and environmental concerns of conventional chemical insecticides [4]. Among them, one approach from ancient times is “re-invented” as a very attractive way to treat insects with dust particles that have specific insecticidal properties, without involving chemicals [4–7]. Currently, inert mineral dust particles, including diatomaceous earth (DE), bentonite clays, kaolin, zeolites and sands have been explored and practically used, showing strong insecticidal properties capable of exterminating insects through contact and physical mechanisms (abrasion, desiccation, wax and moisture adsorption, etc.). This pest protection approach is offering a sustainable, non-chemical and non-biochemical mode of action that circumvents the development of resistance [7–10]. Among them, DE, known as diatomite or kieselgur from fossilized diatoms, composed of whole diatoms and its fractured silica particles has proven to be the most effective contact dust pesticide and currently implemented in pest management against stored grain insects [9, 10].

Traditional management strategies for pest control of stored grain, including based on synthetic chemical insecticides, have shown several limitations, such as inconsistent efficacy, environmental persistence, toxicity to non-target organisms, and, most critically, the development of resistance in insect populations. The overreliance on conventional insecticides has contributed to resistance build-up and ecological imbalances, reducing their long-term sustainability. In this context, the rapid advancement of nanotechnology offers promising alternatives through chemical-free and resistance-mitigating strategies. Numerous nanoscale materials have demonstrated potent insecticidal activity and can be formulated into nanopesticides, representing a new generation of pest control agents with improved efficacy, target specificity, and environmental safety [11, 12]. Nanopesticides showed ability to surpass traditional chemical pesticides due to their diminutive size, not exceeding 100 nm, which enhances the efficacy by improving the stability of active ingredients, decreasing the dosage, and exhibiting their own unique insecticidal activity [13–15]. Their application in the post-harvest protection of stored agricultural products and pest management systems is still at an early stage that needs further development. Key remaining aspects that require further investigation include a deeper understanding of the mode of action compared with conventional insecticides, identification of critical structural and interfacial parameters that optimize their insecticidal performance across different insect species and evaluation of their effectiveness under varying storage and environmental conditions.

Among the emerging nanomaterials, graphene and its derivatives, have shown significant promise as graphene-based nanopesticides. Owing to their unique physicochemical properties, they offer an innovative, environmentally friendly alternative to conventional pesticides addressing critical challenges such as pest resistance, environmental pollution, and diminishing effectiveness of traditional chemical agents. Their numerous outstanding properties, such as ultra-high surface area (2630 m2 g−1), two-dimensional (2D) structure, high aspect ratio, tunable chemistry, and interfacial properties, make them ideal for addressing many problems in agriculture and creating a new generation of advanced and sustainable agents and carriers for pest control [16, 17]. The first study on applications of graphene for pest control reported the application of graphene and graphene oxide (GO) as carriers for synthetic pesticides or biopesticides, such as pyrethroid insecticides like cyhalothrin, bifenthrin, and fenpropathrin cysteine protease, showing their improved performance compared with their conventional applications [18, 19]. Our group performed pioneering work using graphene powders as nanopesticides, and in our comprehensive study, we used a broad range of graphene materials, demonstrating for the first time their potent insecticidal characteristics and their dependency on particle size and surface chemistry [20, 21]. These studies revealed that graphene composed of nano-sized particles is the most effective compared with micro-sized graphene particles and GO that have considerably lower insecticidal properties. These industrially produced graphene powders with nano dimensions were demonstrated to have promising insecticidal properties against three common stored grain insects including the rice weevil, Sitophilus oryzae (L.) (Coleoptera: Curculionidae), the lesser grain borer, Rhyzopertha dominica (F.) (Coleoptera: Bostrichidae) and the larger grain borer, Prostephanus truncatus Horn (Coleoptera: Bostrichidae) and potential applications as new contact nanopesticides for grain protection [21]. However, the insecticidal performance of graphene nanopowders can be influenced by many parameters, such as dosage, formulation, temperature, humidity, different types of grains, and insect species revealed in our most recent studies [22–24]. The insecticidal efficacy of graphene may also be influenced by the physicochemical properties of both the grain commodity and the insect species. Variations in surface roughness, chemical composition, and surface charge may affect the adhesion and interaction of graphene particles and these aspects are not explored. This assumption is based on extensive studies of graphene’s antibacterial properties, which have demonstrated a strong dependence on the specific characteristics of graphene particles such as particle size, defects, charge, hydrophobicity, crystallinity, chemical composition and etc [25]. Therefore, the influence of different types of graphene powders on insecticidal performance against grain insects across various grain commodities is a critical area of research that warrants thorough investigation to prove this hypothesis.

In this study, to address these research questions and hypothesis gaps, we evaluate the insecticidal activity of three types of industrially produced graphene materials with nano-size particles, on three different grain commodities, namely rice, maize, and soft wheat, against three common grain insects, including S. oryzae, the maize weevil, Sitophilus zeamais Motschulsky (Coleoptera: Curculionidae), and the red flour beetle, Tribolium castaneum Herbst (Coleoptera: Tenebrionidae). These insects, which are frequently employed as model organisms for ethological and food safety research, are acknowledged to be the most destructive worldwide pests of stored items. They attack stored grain and other food products, such as flour, cereals, pasta, biscuits, beans, and nuts, resulting in significant losses and damage. Graphene powder formulations and dosages (500 and 1000 ppm) were selected as common concentrations used for inert dust chemical-free pesticides with bioassays determining these insects’ mortality counted after 7, 14, and 21 days of exposure, while progeny production after 65 days. The used graphene materials were thoroughly characterized using a wide range of techniques to reveal their structural, chemical, and interfacial properties. This comprehensive characterization enabled the correlation of these properties with their insecticidal activities and observed insect mortality rates, providing insights into the influence of three different types of graphene nanopowders, three different types of grains on selected insect species. To investigate the hypothesized physical mode of action, underlying the insecticidal activity of graphene powders, we conducted scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis on both graphene-treated grain surfaces and exposed insects. This provided additional evidence of graphene–insect interactions and offered visual insight into the material’s insecticidal effects that is demonstrated for the first time.

Experimental section

Materials

Graphene powders

Graphene powders with nano size (ca. 100 nm) were obtained from Australian graphene manufacturer (Graphene Manufacturing Group, GMG ltd., Brisbane) and labeled as Gr-1, Gr-2, and Gr-3. They have nanosized lateral dimensions, but with slight differences in other properties such as level of oxygen and sp2 carbon, structural defects, hydrophobicity, and number of layers.

Grain commodities

Three different uninfected and clean grain commodities were used in the experiments, namely puddy rice, maize, and soft wheat. The paddy rice was sourced from a local producer in Serres, Greece, while the maize and soft wheat were obtained from commercial suppliers in Thessaly, Greece. The samples were accommodated to lab environment for at least 48 h before testing. The measurements of the moisture using Multitest meter, (GODE Co, France) were 15.1%, 11.6%, and 10.7% for puddy rice, maize, and soft wheat kernels, respectively.

Materials characterizations

A scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM, Quanta 450 FEG, FEI) and a transmission electron microscope at 120 kV (TEM, FEI Tecnai G2 Spirit, FEI, USA; Philips CM200, Japan at 200 kV) were used to determine the lateral size and morphology of supplied graphene materials.

Raman spectrometer (LabRAM HR Evolution, Horiba Jvon Yvon Technology, Japan) with a 532 nm laser (mpc 3000) was used to determine the vibrational characteristics and distinguish the structural defects in graphene materials. Samples for SEM and RAMAN were prepared by drop casting of GR2M dispersion on Si wafer.

X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS, AXIS Ultra DLD, Kratos, UK) equipped with a monochromatic Al Kα radiation source (hv = 1486.7 eV) was applied to identify the chemical composition of the graphene materials.

X-ray powder diffraction analysis (XRD) connected with a Cu X-ray tube (600 Miniflex, Rigaku, Japan) was conducted at 40 kV and 15 mA with 10 min−1 scan speed in the range of 2θ = 5–80 to investigate the crystallinity and the interlayer spacing of graphene materials.

Chemical functional groups present in the samples were studied using FTIR (Nicolet 6700, Thermo Fisher) in the range of 500–4000 cm−1 using pressed pellets from GR2Ms. Particle size distribution (PSD) measurements were performed by a nano sizer (Malvern) instrument using prepared dispersions of GR2MS in this study.

Contact angle (CA) measurements were performed using a CA meter (ATA instrument) on pressed graphene films made from graphene materials powders. CA on different grain samples was also performed to determine their interfacial properties and ability for adherence of graphene powders.

Bioassays

Insects

Mixed-sex adults, < 1 month-old adults of S. oryzae, S. zeamais and T. castaneum were utilized in the tests. S. oryzae was reared on whole soft wheat kernels, S. zeamais on whole maize kernels, while T. castaneum on whole wheat flour, according to the laboratory’s rearing protocols. All insect rearing were maintained in the Laboratory of Entomology and Agricultural Zoology, Department of Agriculture, Crop Production and Rural Environment, University of Thessaly, at 25 °C and 65% relative humidity (r.h.) and continuous darkness for more than 20 years.

Bioassay protocol

For the bioassays, lots of 500 g of paddy rice or maize or soft wheat were separately added in glass jars of 1 L capacity (15 cm diameter, 35 cm high, Bormioli Rocco, Italy) and treated with three powder graphene formulations, namely Gr-1, Gr-2 and Gr-3 at two dosage rates: 500 and 1000 ppm (mg graphene/kg grain), which was measured by the balance. A separate series of glass jars containing each untreated commodity were served as controls. Then, the jars were tightly sealed with lids and thoroughly shaken by hand for 5 min to achieve a uniform distribution of the graphene formulations in the entire grain mass. The experimental units for the tests were cylindrical plastic vials (3 cm in diameter, 8 cm high, Rotilabo Sample tins Snap-on lid, Carl Roth, Germany), which previously have been covered with Fluon (polytetrafluoroethylene; Northern Products, Woonsocket, USA) in the upper one-quarter, to avoid insects escaping. In each vial, 20 g of graphene-dusted grains from each jar (paddy rice or maize or soft wheat) were placed and subsequently, 20 adults of each insect species were introduced into each vial, with different series of vials per species. For each insect species-dose-grain commodity combination there were six replicates. All vials were maintained in an incubator set at 25 °C and 56% r.h. Dead adults were counted after 7, 14, and 21 days of exposure. At the end of this interval, all adults, dead or alive were removed from the vials and the vials remained at the same experimental conditions for an additional period of 65 days, where the vials were opened again, and the production of the offspring was counted.

Statistical analysis

Repeated measures multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA-Fit with Wilk’s Lambda) was performed for both mortality data and progeny production counts with commodity, graphene formulation, and dose as main effects by using the JPM 7 software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, North Carolina, USA). To detect the differences among the different commodity types, one-way ANOVA was carried out for each graphene formulation, dosage, exposure interval, and insect species. Means were separated by the Tukey–Kramer (HSD) test at P = 0.05. One-way ANOVA was also prepared for progeny production at each insect species with graphene formulation as the main effect, and the means were separated by the Tukey–Kramer (HSD) test at P = 0.05. In addition, for each commodity type, the mean comparison between control and graphene treatment was performed by using the independent sample t-test at P = 0.05 (Sokal and Rohlf, 1995), using SPSS software version 17.0 (IBM Corp, 2007).

Results and discussion

Structural and chemical characteristics of graphene materials

Graphene powder materials used in this study were extensively characterized using a broad range of characterization methods to reveal their structural, interfacial, and chemical properties including particle size, number of layers, level of structural defects, oxygen level, crystallinity, surface charge, and hydrophobicity/hydrophilicity with results summarized in Fig. 1 and Table 1. The visual observation of these graphene samples showed their very soft, light, and fluffy powdery texture (Fig. 1a–c). Their bulk density values were measured in a range from 30 to 40 mg/cm3, indicating their very light, high surface area and lower aggregation (stacking) of graphene nanosheets in powder material, which are favorable properties that are expected to have a positive impact on their insecticidal activity. Characterization results by SEM (Fig. 1d–f) confirmed that all graphene powder materials have nano flakes structure with average particle size (length × width) of ca. 78 × 63 nm for Gr-1, 119 × 86 nm for Gr-2, and 110 × 81 nm for Gr-3 ( Table 1). High-resolution TEM results (Fig. 1g–i) and their number of layer analyses (data not shown) indicate they have a large variation of the number of layers with a minority of particles with few and < 10 layers and the majority between 10 and 20 layers.

The Raman spectroscopy results of investigated graphene materials presented in Fig. 2a, compared with control (single layer graphene) showed typical Raman graphs for graphene with two characteristic bands D and G at ~ 1345 cm−1 and ~ 1600 cm−1 and a 2D band located at 2719 cm−1. These results revealed the degree of defects based on the peak intensity ratio of the D to G bands (ID/IG) in order − 0.5 to 1.0, which indicates a significantly higher level of disorder for all nG, as compared to mG (0.2–0.5) and control (< 0.2). The level of defects indicated by the ID/IG ratio is found to be larger for Gr-2 compared to Gr-1 and Gr-3 graphene samples. The XRD results presented in Fig. 2b verified the structural properties of these graphene materials, showing a peak at 2θ = 26.24°, indicating that they have a graphitic structure with a calculated interlayer distance of 0.344 nm. The level of oxygen and carbon is quantified with XPS with details presented in Table 1; Fig. 2c. These results showed some difference in sp2 carbon and oxygen levels in graphene samples where sample Gr-2 showed lower sp2 C level (64.39%) compared to Gr-1 and Gr-3 (76.02% and 75.50%) and higher oxygen level (C–O) and double lower C/O ratio of 22.7 versus 50.5. These results indicate that Gr-3 is more hydrophobic and has a higher level of aromatic sp2 carbon structure compared with Gr-2, which has more defects, sp3 carbon, and oxygen groups. The hydrophobicity/hydrophilicity of graphene samples measured from their films by CA confirmed previous results, showing they have highly hydrophobic properties with an average CA of 143 ± 5.0° for Gr-1, 107 ± 3.0° for Gr-2, and 130 ± 4.0° for Gr-3 [21]. Their hydrophobic properties could impact their better adherence to the hydrophobic surface of the insect’s body and the disruption of hydrophobic protective layers on the insect’s cuticle surface. The hydrophobic properties are considered favorable for their insecticidal activity, resulting in increased adherence of graphene particles on the insects’ exoskeleton and stronger interaction with its protective hydrophobic layers on the insect’s skin surface that can be more easily disrupted in a similar way as graphene sheets can penetrate through cell membranes [21].

Mortality of Sitophilus oryzae (Rice weevil)

The mortality (% ± SE) of S. oryzae adults exposed to three grain commodities (rice, maize, and wheat) treated with three different graphene formulations (Gr-1, Gr-2, Gr-3, and control) with two dosages (500 and 1000 ppm) are presented in Fig. 3. Results showed that mortality in all three grain commodities was higher than in controls in all graphene formulations tested for both dosages. On Day 7 of exposure at 500 ppm, the mortality rates of S. oryzae were low and did not exceed 44%, while the lowest rates were recorded in the Gr-3 graphene formulation with 9.1%, 21.6%, and 10.8% in rice, maize and wheat, respectively (Fig. 3c). Over time, an increase in mortality levels was observed in all graphene formulations with rice and maize recording significantly higher numbers of dead adults compared to wheat in both Gr-2 and Gr-3 formulations, while the Gr-1 formulation showed similar efficacy in all grain types (Fig. 3a–c). At 1000 ppm, the Gr-2 graphene formulation was more effective on maize compared to wheat throughout the exposure time, reaching up to 97% mortality on the last day of observation (Fig. 3f). In contrast, the Gr-3 formulation showed higher efficacy rates in wheat than in maize on Day 7 and rice on Day 14 (Fig. 3g). However, in the same formulation on the last day of exposure, the effectiveness of graphene was equal in all commodities (Fig. 3g). In Gr-1 formulation, apart from the first day of observation (Day 7), where maize accumulated a significantly higher percentage of dead individuals of S. oryzae than rice, on the 14th and 21 st days of exposure, no significant differences between the three grains were recorded (Fig. 3e).

Mortality of Sitophilus zeamais (Maize weevil)

The mortality (% ± SE) of S. zeamais adults exposed to three grain commodities (rice, maize, and wheat) treated with three different graphene formulations (Gr-1, Gr-2, Gr-3 and control) with two dosages (500 and 1000 ppm) is presented in Fig. 4. Contrary to S. oryzae, in the Gr-2 graphene formulation at 500 ppm maize showed significantly lower efficiency rates compared to rice and wheat, reaching only 16.6% on the Day 21 of exposure, while as in the case of S. oryzae, the formulation Gr-3 showed higher percentages of mortality in rice than in the other two grains (Fig. 4a–d). The Gr-1 graphene formulation recorded overall the highest percentages of effectiveness for all three types of commodities, exceeding 82% in rice on the last day of exposure (Fig. 4a).

Increasing the concentration to 1000 ppm, the mortality of S. zeamais fluctuated at similar levels at all exposure intervals in both Gr-2 and Gr-1 graphene formulations (Fig. 4e–h). The effectiveness of these two formulations was significantly higher in rice, reaching 98.3% mortality on Day 21 of exposure, and lower in wheat, showing 43.3 and 59.1% mortality at Gr-2 and Gr-1, respectively (Fig. 4e–g). Regarding Gr-3, maize accumulated a significantly lower number of dead individuals of S. zeamais in the first 2 days of observation, while on the last day (Day 21), the efficacy of the formulation varied at similar levels in all three grain types (Fig. 4g).

Mortality of Tribolium castaneum (Red flour beetle)

The mortality (% ± SE) of T. castaneum adults exposed to three grain commodities (rice, maize, and wheat) treated with three different graphene formulations (Gr-1, Gr-2, Gr-3 and control) with two dosages (500 and 1000 ppm) is presented in Fig. 5. Regarding T. castaneum, using concentration at 500 ppm from the first day of exposure (Day 7) the effectiveness of the Gr-2 graphene formulation was higher on maize compared to the other two grains tested. At the end of the exposure period, the mortality of T. castaneum in this formulation exceeded 85% in maize, while in rice and wheat, it ranged to 51.6 and 41.6%, respectively (Fig. 5b). In both Gr-1 and Gr-3 graphene formulations, rice recorded the highest mortality rates on Day 14 of exposure compared to maize and wheat. However, higher effectiveness in all three grains was recorded by the Gr-1 formulation with 95, 52.3, and 71.6% mortality in rice, maize, and wheat, respectively (Fig. 5a).

At 1000 ppm, mortality rates were particularly high even from Day 7 of exposure for the majority of grains examined (Fig. 5e–h). Very good results were presented by the Gr-2 formulation in rice and maize, exceeding 89% and 86% mortality on the first day of observation (Day 7), a percentage that differed significantly from the corresponding one in wheat, which did not exceed 8% (Fig. 5f). Reduced mortality of T. castaneum was also recorded on Day 7 of exposure to the Gr-3 formulation when it was applied to wheat, which was significantly different from rice and maize (Fig. 5g). However, from Day 14 onwards the Gr-3 formulation was equally effective on all grains examined, as was the Gr-1 formulation on all exposure intervals (Fig. 5e–h).

Evaluation of graphene particles insecticidal activities on progeny production in three grain insect species

The progeny results on three grain insects exposed to three grain commodities (rice, maize, and wheat) treated with three different graphene formulations (Gr-1, Gr-2, Gr-3, and control) with two dosages (500 and 1000 ppm) are presented in Fig. 6; Table 3. The commodity type, along with its interaction with graphene formulation, affected the progeny production of S. oryzae (Table 3). For S. zeamais and T. castaneum, all the main effects and their interactions were significant, apart from Commodity type* Dose interaction for T. castaneum (Table 3). For S. oryzae and S. zeamais the highest progeny counts were observed in wheat in comparison with the other two grain commodities tested. However, in the same commodity, no significant differences were recorded among graphene formulations and control for any of the two doses applied in the case of S. oryzae adults. This was also evident in the case of paddy rice, with the sole exception of S. zeamais at 1000 ppm, where Gr-2 and Gr-3 had significantly lower adult emergence rates compared to Gr-1 and control vials (Fig. 6). In general, progeny counts in paddy rice ranged at low levels and did not exceed 20, 13.1 and 0.3 individuals per vial at 500 ppm for S. oryzae, S. zeamais and T. castaneum, respectively. Maize was the only grain commodity, in which all graphene formulations significantly reduced the number of offspring in relation to the control vials for all the doses and insect species tested, while this was also presented for T. castaneum in wheat (Fig. 6). Significant differences between the two applied doses were observed in only few combinations of graphene formulation and grain type, most of which occurred in rice (Fig. 6).

The evaluation of an insecticidal agent for its effectiveness, especially when that agent is applied to the product in dust form, should not be limited to the parental mortality it can cause, but the possibility of suppressing the production of offspring should also be assessed in terms of long post-harvest grain protection [26]. In this context, the results on progeny production showed that there was no offspring suppression on wheat for both of the Sitophilus species tested, which may be justified by the low adult mortality rates during the graphene exposure period. In contrast, although rice accumulated the highest number of dead individuals in the first 21 days of exposure to graphene, no differences in progeny production were noted between the graphene formulations and the control for any of the insect species and doses examined. This can be due to the rice husk being covered in the paddy rice, which acts as a protector for the immature life stages of the primary colonizers, as in our case, the Sitophilus species, which developed and fed inside the kernel. Our findings are consistent with those of Agrafioti et al. [27], where parental mortality of a broad spectrum of stored product insects was particularly high in wheat applied with three different diatomaceous earth formulations, while progeny production was greatly increased regardless of the dose applied [27]. On the other hand, the greatest suppression of progeny production was observed in maize for all graphene formulations, insect species, and doses evaluated. This interesting finding contrasts with the results obtained for diatomaceous earth powders and its effect on maize, where low parental mortality led to high offspring production, showing that in our case, the physicochemical characteristics of graphene influenced the maize in a manner different from that of diatomaceous earth [28, 29].

Mechanistic insights into graphene’s insecticidal activity

The results presented in the previous sections demonstrate that the investigated graphene materials investigated exhibit significant insecticidal activity against three common insect species across all tested grain commodities. The results indicate that insecticidal activity is influenced by a combination of factors, including insect species, grain type, graphene concentration, and the physicochemical properties of the graphene material. However, the individual contribution and interplay of these factors are yet to be systematically investigated. In our previous study, we investigated the influence of different graphene materials, their type, properties, and concentrations [21]. Results revealed that the graphene materials with nanoscale lateral particle size, which are used in this study were significantly more effective than micro-sized few-layer graphene or graphene oxide, achieving 100% insect mortality within 7 days at concentrations of 500–1000 ppm [21]. To further elucidate the impact of grain commodity type and insect species on the insecticidal activity of nanoscale graphene materials and to gain deeper insights into the observed mortality and progeny reduction, we performed comprehensive SEM imaging of grain surfaces and insect bodies throughout this study.

Our bioassay results, presented in Figs. 2, 3, 4 and 5, indicate that the type of grain commodity has a less significant influence on insecticidal activity compared to the dependency on insect species and graphene formulation (concentration and type). At a higher graphene concentration of 1000 ppm, after 21 days of exposure, no significant differences in mortality rates were observed for S. oryzae and T. castaneum adults across all grain types treated with the Gr-1 and Gr-3 graphene formulations. In contrast, for the same insect species and dosage, the Gr-2 graphene formulation demonstrated higher mortality levels on paddy rice and maize compared to wheat. This trend was also evident for S. zeamais, which exhibited lower mortality rates for most graphene formulations tested. The differences in efficacy among the graphene formulations became more pronounced at the lower application dosage of 500 ppm, where the mortality of insects followed the trend: paddy rice > maize > soft wheat. These findings suggest that the physical and compositional characteristics of both the graphene formulations and the grain types may contribute to the observed insecticidal efficacy. These factors could also influence the developmental and reproductive rates of specific insect species, underscoring the complexity of the interactions at play.

Adherence of graphene particles on grain surface

The primary interaction points between graphene and insects infesting stored grain originate at the grain surface, where graphene particles adhere, following the application of graphene powder as a protective treatment. As insects crawl between or feed on grain kernels, they are directly exposed to graphene particles adhered to the grain surface. This level of interaction is expected to play a critical role on insecticidal activity of graphene formulations, which exhibits strong time and concentration dependent effects. Understanding the actual concentration of graphene particles adsorbed on the grain surface after treatment is essential, as it can vary significantly depending on several factors beyond the initial application dosage. These factors include the grain surface’s roughness and topography, surface chemistry, hydrophobicity or hydrophilicity, surface charge, and the properties of the graphene powders, such as defect density, particle size, and interfacial surface chemistry, which can promote or hinder adherence via electrostatic forces or physical trapping mechanisms. These characteristics can either promote or hinder graphene adherence through mechanisms such as electrostatic interactions or physical entrapment. SEM imaging of the surface topography of rice, maize, and wheat grains before and after treatment with graphene powders, as shown in Fig. 7, reveals significant differences in surface roughness and topography among these grains. These variations may explain the observed differences in the insecticidal performance of graphene formulations across different grain types.

The surface of rice kernels is highly rough, characterized by a distinctive topography with linearly organized arrays of hemispherical protrusions with sharp ends (Fig. 7a). These protrusions, approximately 50 microns in diameter, are separated by deep valleys, making them ideal for trapping graphene particles. After treatment with graphene powders, SEM imaging revealed a high concentration of graphene particles for Gr-1 and Gr-3 compared to Gr-2. The graphene particles were predominantly located in the valleys between the protrusions, with noticeably large aggregates of graphene particles observed for all formulations, particularly in Gr-1, as shown in Fig. S1a. In contrast, the surface of maize kernels, depicted in Fig. 7b, displayed a less rough topography with long, parallel grooves. The concentration of graphene particles in maize was significantly lower than in rice, with no substantial differences among the graphene formulations. Maize kernels, being much larger in size and exhibiting a smooth, shiny, gold-colored surface, naturally displayed less adhesion of graphene particles. However, spots of large graphene aggregates were observed (Fig. S1b), suggesting that the graphene particles, unable to strongly adhere to the maize surface, agglomerated while remaining on the grain surface and thus available for transfer to insect bodies. The soft wheat surface exhibited a moderately rough texture with irregular, fiber-like structures. Following graphene treatment, graphene particles did not adsorb individually but instead formed characteristic globular aggregates of approximately 10 microns in size, randomly dispersed across the surface. The highest concentration of these aggregates was observed for Gr-1 compared to Gr-2 and Gr-3. Figure S1c provides detailed views of these aggregates, showing individual graphene particle clustered together. We propose that these aggregates were formed via a “snowball” mechanism, wherein individual graphene particles were combined with other dust particles present on the grain surface.

Comparative analysis of SEM images across the three grain types revealed the highest concentration of graphene particles on rice surfaces, followed by wheat, with maize showing the lowest concentration. These differences in graphene particles’ adherence on grain surface are influenced not only by the surface roughness but also influenced by other factors, such as hardness and chemical composition, which affect hydrophobicity, hydrophilicity, and surface charge. Additionally, the type and properties of the graphene powders significantly influenced adsorption. For instance, Gr-1 exhibited the highest concentration on rice and wheat surfaces compared to Gr-2 and Gr-3, highlighting the importance of considering the properties of graphene material for insecticidal applications.

Interfacial properties of grain commodities

The interfacial properties of the grain surfaces for the three grain commodities were determined through water contact angle (CA) measurements, as shown in Fig. 8. The results indicate that maize has the most hydrophobic surface, with a CA of 105.6 ± 7°, followed by rice with a CA of 96.8 ± 2°, and wheat, which is hydrophilic, with a CA of 81.0 ± 2°. Grains with hydrophobic surfaces tend to attract hydrophobic graphene powders due to favorable electrostatic interactions, whereas grains with hydrophilic surfaces are more likely to attract graphene materials with oxygen or other functional groups. Gr-1 and Gr-3, identified as more hydrophobic (CA 143º and 130º), demonstrated higher distribution on hydrophobic grain surfaces such as rice and maize compared to Gr-2. However, as mentioned previously, hydrophobicity is only one of many factors influencing graphene adsorption on grain surfaces. Other surface properties, such as roughness, surface charge, and surface chemistry, also play critical roles in this process. While the current findings suggest hydrophobicity significantly contributes to graphene distribution, further studies are required to investigate these additional characteristics. A deeper understanding of these factors will help optimize strategies for the protective treatment of specific grain commodities.

Building on the understanding of graphene particle adherence to different grain surfaces and the key influencing factors discussed in previous sections, critical next question emerges including How do these adhered graphene particles interact with insect bodies to induce mortality, and how does this mortality vary across insect species? This was one of the primary objectives of this study. Previous studies have established that the insecticidal efficacy of inert dust-based insecticides largely depends on their degree of adherence to the grain kernel [28–31]. This principle provides a basis for interpreting the observed mortality results and insecticidal activity of graphene materials in our study. For instance, Kavallieratos et al. [28] reported that rice exhibited the highest diatomaceous earth retention levels (>87%) among seven grain commodities tested, which aligns with the high mortality rates recorded on rice in our experiment [28]. However, their study also showed that soft wheat had a high retention level (>70%), yet this did not directly correspond to insecticidal efficacy. This discrepancy underscores the complexity of the relationship between dust adherence and mortality, suggesting that factors beyond retention levels also play a significant role. Notably, all previous studies using inert dusts, including diatomaceous earth, primarily focused on counting insect mortality without examining the adherence of particles to grain and insect surfaces. This absence limits the ability to correlate adherence levels with observed mortality that can be compared with graphene materials. In our study, graphene treatments at a dosage of 1000 ppm revealed varying adherence levels across different grain types, with higher adherence on rice and wheat compared to maize. Adherence also varied by graphene formulation, with Gr-1 and Gr-3 exhibiting higher adherence than Gr-2. Mortality results for the tested insect species (S. oryzae, S. zeamais, and T. castaneum), presented in Figs. 3, 4 and 5, generally followed the pattern of graphene adherence across grain types, with some exceptions. For example, maize treated with Gr-2 showed higher mortality for all insects compared to wheat, which contrasts with the adherence results presented in Fig. 7, where wheat demonstrated higher graphene particle retention. These findings suggest that while adherence plays a significant role in insect mortality, other factors, such as insect behavior, physiology, and the mode of graphene interaction with insect bodies, may also contribute to the observed results. Further studies are needed to fully unravel these complex interactions and optimize graphene formulations for specific grain-insect combinations.

Interactions of graphene particles with insect cuticle structures

To understand the transfer of graphene particles from grain surfaces to insects’ bodies and their role in causing mortality, we conducted a limited SEM examination of deceased insects after 21 days of exposure. SEM images presented in Fig. 9a revealed the accumulation of graphene particles on the abdominal regions of T. castaneum as a typical example, particularly around the mechanoreceptors and spiracles at pleural sides, which are key openings on the exoskeleton responsible for tracheal respiration and vital breathing functions. The presence of graphene particles obstructing these openings suggests a possible critical mode of action for their insecticidal activity. Given graphene’s known gas barrier properties, the mechanism of blocking the gas respiration points on insect’s body could contribute to its efficacy. These results indicate that graphene particles transfer from grain surfaces to insects’ bodies, causing mortality likely through physical modes of action. The primary mode of action for inert dusts involves damaging the protective wax coating on the insect cuticle, leading to water loss and eventual desiccation, a well-documented cause of insect death [9, 10, 21–34]. For graphene nanoparticles, some of these physical mechanisms, such as cuticle and wax protective layer interruption, are likely applicable and summarized in Fig. 9d. However, graphene can also provide a unique mode of action through its gas barrier properties, that could interrupt the respiration system which is not observed in traditional inert dust-based grain protectant. Insects breathe through openings in the thorax and abdomen called spiracles and graphene particles could close the tracheal tubes in these spirales used to deliver oxygen directly into the insects’ tissues and exchange carbon dioxide with significant impact causing its death. These multiple physical actions highlight the novel and unique insecticidal capabilities of graphene materials not observed before and their promise as effective, chemical-free alternatives for grain protection.

Influence of insect and grain type on insectidicial efficacy of graphene nanopowders

Considering the differences in body size, anatomy, and the chemical composition of the protective layers on the cuticle of insects, it is reasonable to expect variations in their susceptibility to graphene treatment. Mortality results, summarized in Fig. 9e–f show that S. oryzae and T. castaneum are more susceptible to graphene treatment compared to S. zeamais.

Previous studies have reported similar observations using diatomaceous earth attributed to the insects’ characteristic exoskeleton and anatomical features [7, 10, 28, 35–37]. As shown in Fig. 9a, T. castaneum, with its hairy-like structure, can collect more graphene particles from grain surfaces, leading to higher exposure and increased insecticidal activity. The increased mortality of S. oryzae can be explained by its smaller body size, which makes it more sensitive to graphene treatment, a phenomenon also observed in studies using inert dusts [7, 10, 28, 35–37]. Regarding the influence of grain commodities on insect mortality, the results show that mortality increases from wheat to maize, with rice showing the highest mortality rates. These findings can be attributed to the greater surface roughness of rice grains, which facilitates higher adherence of graphene particles, which is one of the critical factors in enhancing insecticidal activity.

Conclusion

This concludes the investigation into the influence of grain types and graphene material properties on their insectidicial efficacy of graphene against common stored grain insects. The comprehensive parametric study employs three graphene formulations, three common stored grain insects species, and three types of grains reveal the insecticidal effectiveness of graphene over 21 days and progeny for 65 days. The obtained results indicate that insectidicial performance of three industrially produced graphene nanopowders against grain insects is influenced by both the insects species and the types of grain commodities. These findings are consistent with previous studies on inert dust protectants, which have also shown substrate- and species-dependent efficacy. The observed differences in insects’ mortality across various grain types are primarily attributed to variations in the grain surface roughness and potentially surface hydrophobicity, both of which can enhance the adhesion of graphene particles to the grain surface and facilitate their transfer to the insect body. The study also highlights notable differences in mortality between insect species which are influenced by the type of the grain commodity used. These findings suggest that in addition to graphene dosage and concentration, factors such as grain surface properties and graphene characteristics, including particle size, hydrophobicity, and oxygen content, should be carefully considered to further optimize the insecticidal performance of graphene formulations. The proposed mechanism underlying the insecticidal activity of graphene accounts for the observed influence of the physicochemical properties of graphene as well as the variations grains, and insect types on its overall effectiveness. The results suggest that the graphene insecticidal activity is primarily driven by a physical mode of action, similar to that of dust protectants, and it is further enhanced by its gas barrier properties, which may contribute to respiratory or desiccation effects in insects. However, further research is required to fully elucidate the underlying mechanisms of these interactions and to validate their consistency across different conditions and species. It is important to note that the experiments were conducted under controlled laboratory conditions, and thus, real-world environmental variables may influence the practical performance of graphene powders under commercial storage settings. Moreover, this study did not assess the potential impact of graphene treatments on grain quality or food safety, including whether any residues remain on the treated commodities. These aspects represent important limitations of the current work and should be addressed in the future research to fully evaluate the field applicability and safety of graphene-based pest protectants.
