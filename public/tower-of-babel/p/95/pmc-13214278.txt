Primary liver cancer at tertiary healthcare facility in Guinea: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Djenabou Diallo, Kadiatou Diallo, Mamadou Sarifou Diallo, Ahmed Tidiane Diallo, Ousmane Sow, Kadio Jean-Jacques Olivier Kadio, Thierno Mamadou Barry, Ibrahima Sory Sylla, Djibril Sylla, Pascal Pineau

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGuinea has the highest incidence of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) in Sub-Saharan Africa (> 21 cases/100,000 habitants/year). This high incidence is largely attributed to the combination of a high prevalence of chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection and significant exposure to aflatoxin B1 (AFB1), a known carcinogen. However, the clinical presentation of HCC in Guinea has not been well-documented to date.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of 216 HCC cases from patients who received care between 2015 and 2023 at the Donka University Hospital in Conakry, the main tertiary care institution of the country. Demographical, clinical, and biological data were extracted from medical records and analyzed using both descriptive and inferential statistics.ResultsThe median age of patients was relatively young, at 43.0 years (IQR = 34.5–55.0) with a male-to-female ratio of 3.1 (162/54). Half of the male patients were younger than 41 years. Chronic HBV infection was the predominant risk factor accounting for 75.0% of cases. Almost all patients have been exposed to HBV at some moment of their lives (total anti-HBc seropositivity: 92.2%). Chronic HBV infection was rarely replicative with only 7.8% of patients tested positive for HBeAg. Hepatitis C virus Infection was observed in a small subset of cases (7.9%). Toxic risk factors such as alcohol (12.5%) or tobacco (19.4%) were also rather infrequent. HBV-infected patients were significantly younger than non-HBV patients (median age: 40.0 vs. 50.5 years, p = 0.0109). Susu ethnicity, a Mande-speaking group primarily residing in Guinea’s coastal plain was associated with an earlier onset of HCC than other ethnic groups (median age: 37.0 vs. 46.0 years, p = 0.0007).ConclusionsHBV infection is the major risk factor of HCC in Guinea with an earlier onset of the disease by nearly a decade compared to other risk factors. A notable ethnic variation was observed with Susu patients displaying a predisposition to early HCC. Further molecular research is needed to precisely assess the potential role of AFB1 in liver carcinogenesis of Guinean populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-026-04881-8.Keywords: Hepatocellular Carcinoma, Hepatitis B virus, Hepatitis C virus, Aflatoxin, Ethnicity

Background

Primary liver cancer (PLC), almost exclusively corresponding to hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), still represents an alarming plague in West Africa [1, 2]. This region of the world is characterized by a significant prevalence of some of the major risk factors (RF) of PLC including chronic infection with hepatitis B virus (HBV) and a frequent exposure to the mutagenic aflatoxin B1 (AFB1) [3, 4]. In the region, the impact of these deleterious RF starts early in lifespan through a perinatal or early childhood infection by HBV and uterine or breastfeeding-associated intoxication by AFB1 [5, 6]. These circumstances plausibly explain why PLC onset tends to occur earlier in Sub-Saharan Africa than on other continents [7]. In sub-Saharan Africa, the age-standardized rate of PLC is 6.3 cases/100.000 in both sexes while it reaches 7.9 cases/100.000 in Western Africa [8].

Guinea is a lower-middle income (ranking 153rd out of 179 countries for GDP per inhabitants) West African coastal country peopled with more than 13 million habitants (https://www.worldometers.info/gdp/gdp-per-capita/). According to the African Health Organization, communicable diseases still represent the major plagues affecting Guinea population (https://aho.org/countries/guinea/). Among the 10 causes of mortality registered in the country, six are communicable (lower respiratory tract, malaria, diarrheal diseases, tuberculosis, HIV/AIDS, meningitis) (https://www.healthdata.org/research-analysis/health-by-location/profiles/guinea). Guinea is also affected since a decade by recurring epidemic of Ebola virus disease of different magnitudes [9]. Chronic malnutrition of children is still highly prevalent in Guinea and contributes to weaken the health status of grew-up generation [10]. Finally, chronic intoxication of the population by AFB1 is known for decade to represent a major threat for Guinean populations [11].

This overall epidemiological context tightly connected with infectious agent circulation affects also liver health explaining why Guinea occupies a critical position in that domain by comparison with its six borderer countries (Bissau Guinea, Côte d’Ivoire, Liberia, Mali, Senegal, and Sierra Leone). While non-communicable RF of chronic liver diseases (alcohol, obesity) are not major issues in Guinea, the prevalence of chronic infection with hepatitis B or C viruses is higher here than in neighboring countries and, therefore, Guinea presents the highest PLC incidence of Sub-Saharan Africa (21.3 cases/100,000/year ASR) [8, 12–14] (https://www.globalhep.org/).

However, so far, no publication came to describe the clinical and epidemiological presentation of PLC in Guinea. Therefore, we have no clues at disposal to explain the worrying situation of HCC in the country. In the current manuscript, we describe the presentation and risk factors in 216 cases of PLC diagnosed in Donka University Hospital, the largest tertiary healthcare facility of the country.

Methods

Site, design, population, and period study

The design of the conducted study was retrospective cross-sectional and observational on patients suspected to be affected from primary liver cancer (PLC) in the Department of Internal Medicine, Donka University Hospital of Conakry, Guinea. The period of records retrieval extended from December 2015 to July 2023. The aim was to describe the clinical presentation as well as the risk factors of the disease. The sample size was determined by the availability of medical records. All of them were a priori considered. No power calculation was performed.

Inclusion/exclusion criteria

The criteria of inclusion for HCC were the presence of a liver mass above 1 cm of diameter at ultrasound or computed tomography with an arterial enhancement and a wash-out in venous phase (88.9% of cases, n = 188/216), and/or a serum alpha-foetoprotein (AFP) concentration above 10ng/mL in a clinical and serological context of protracted chronic liver disease. When imaging was unavailable, diagnosis was always supported by clinical, and serological data known as defining the local presentation of HCC. Such circumstances are frequently observed in resource-limited countries. It clearly deviates from the diagnostic recommendations generally used in high-income countries and represents obviously one of the limitations of the present study. Criteria of exclusion were the lack of sufficient imaging, clinical, or biological data and the concomitant development of an extra-hepatic primary tumor as reported in the medical records.

Data collection

The diagnosis of PLC was inherently multimodal and primarily based on the presence of a single or several liver masses at imaging (General Electric Logiq 9 for ultrasound, Toshiba Alexion for CT scan) as well as on an evocative clinical context, characterized by a context of chronic liver disease including liver cirrhosis and its numerous accompanying signs, on the presence of serological markers signing chronic hepatitis infections, and on the measurement of AFP levels (physiological level < 10 ng/mL, diagnostic level > 400 ng/mL).

Data about socio-demographic status, clinical symptoms, clinical biochemistry, blood cell count, serological data concerning hepatitis B and C viruses, lifestyle-related risk factors (alcohol intake or tobacco consumption), metabolic risk factors as type 2 diabetes and arterial hypertension, risk factors of infections, were recovered from medical files and stored in a database. Due to the lack of resources that affects the Guinean health system, the cost of laboratory tests is generally borne by the patients. Therefore, some data were missing because a significant proportion of the patients cannot afford the necessary explorations. Consequently, a sizeable quantity of laboratory tests was lacking from most medical records.

This study was designed and reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines to ensure transparency and completeness in reporting observational research.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Prism 10.0 statistical package (GraphPad Software, Inc, La Jolla, CA, USA). Continuous numerical variables were summarized by their median values and interquartile range. They were compared using Mann-Whitney U or Kruskal-Wallis tests depending on the number of groups. Mann-Whitney U test was employed when data distribution was not respecting a normal distribution. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies that were compared either by Fisher exact or Chi-square tests. Small cell counts were handled by Fisher exact test. All tests were univariate and two-sided. Level of significance was set at p < 0.05. The lack of multiple comparison adjustments represents a limit of our study.

Study limitations and biases

The current study is limited by various factors, primarily the conditions of public hospital medical practice in Guinea. Notably, the serological data were incomplete, particularly regarding hepatitis delta markers. Similarly, some liver function tests, such as gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase and alkaline phosphatase, as well as variables linked to lipid metabolism, were lacking in more than 90.0% of cases. Consequently, they were not considered for further analyses and a risk factor such as infection with hepatitis Delta virus, a major determinant of HCC in sub-Saharan Africa, is not assessed in our study. Likewise, the role and importance of metabolic dysfunctions in the occurrence of HCC in Guinea was not assess either. Future studies should incorporate these features to improve our understanding of the pathophysiology of liver tumor disease in Guinea.

Because laboratory tests and imaging examinations should be afforded directly by the patients in Guinea, it is likely that a certain proportion of HCC cases affecting the poorest were excluded from the study due to the inability to confirm a diagnosis of HCC. This situation obviously introduces a selection bias in the current analysis.

Results

Cases description

A collection of 279 medical records corresponding to HCC cases were retrieved from the 3465 clinical records of patients receiving care at the Internal Medicine and Hepato-Gastroenterology department of Donka Hospital between 2015 and 2023. After careful examination, six files (2.1%) were found to be duplicated while 57 (20.4%) additional records were rejected due to a lack of diagnostic evidence (essentially in imaging and laboratory features) susceptible to confirm a bona fide PLC (Fig. 1). We retained a set of 216 PLC cases for further analyses.

Concerning risk factors, the epidemiology of PLC in Guinea-Conakry is marked by the predominance of chronic infection by HBV as illustrated by HBsAg prevalence (74.8%) in patients. However, almost all the patients tested have been in contact with HBV at some point in their lives, as revealed by their seropositivity for total anti-HBc (92.2%). The infection was rarely in the highly replicative phase as only 7.8% of HBsAg (+) patients were still carrying HBeAg. The role of infection with HCV was marginal in this series (7.9%). No serological test aiming to detect hepatitis delta virus (HDV) is currently routinely implemented in Guinea, the current work is thus deprived from this crucial information. Toxic risk factors of liver diseases such as alcohol intake (12.5%) or tobacco consumption (19.4%) were found in minor subsets of the cohort. Metabolic risk factor as type 2 diabetes (T2D) was rarely diagnosed and therefore of marginal concern (2.8%, Fig. 2B).

A history of infection risk was not found in a majority of cases (54.4% of cases), a situation rather expected in a country where HBV transmission occurs mostly through perinatal or early childhood contacts with infected persons [5]. The most frequent potential infection routes were either history of dental care (22.8%) or traditional cultural practices such as circumcision/excision (27.4%). However, neither HBV nor HCV infections were associated with a higher prevalence of one of these RF (e.g. circumcision/excision, 34.5% vs. 15%, p = 0.079, ns) suggesting that neither dental care nor circumcision/excision are primary responsible for these viral infections in Guinea.

Concerning the clinical presentation, liver cirrhosis was present in 92.5% of cases. The three major symptoms of an advanced liver disease were in a decreasing order, pain in the upper right abdominal quadrant (97.1%), hepatomegaly (88.5%), and presence of ascites (81.0%). Most patients received medication aiming at reducing ascites or the consequences of liver cirrhosis (aldactone, furosemide, propranolol, lactulose) at the time of PLC diagnosis (53.2%). An even larger proportion was already treated by analgesic drugs (92.0%). In line with this later observation, the disease had already progressed to an advanced stage by the time of diagnosis, with 57.0% of patients having an ECOG performance status of 3 or higher.

The tumor was secreting the oncofetal biomarker AFP above the diagnostic level (400ng/mL) in 50.0% of cases but was in a physiological range (< 10ng/mL) in 20.0% of cases. Tumor dimension was known in only 30 cases. It was large as the median diameter of the main nodule was 80 mm (IQR = 37–145).

Clinical and biological correlations

We looked thereafter for correlations enabling us to delineate more accurately disease presentation or evolution in Guinea. When stratified for median age, we observed that patients from the younger median (< 42 years) were significantly more often carriers of HBsAg (81.9% vs. 67.0%, OR = 2.2, 95%CI: 1.06–4.73, p = 0.0264, Fig. 3A). In addition, younger patients displayed a significantly higher proportion of exposition to an infectious risk than elder patients (53.7% vs. 37.6%, OR = 1.9, 95%CI: 1.08–3.44, p = 0.0201, Fig. 3B), although no category of infectious risk was salient. As expected, clinical signs (lower limb edema), worsened biological features (creatinine) and increased fibrosis scores (API, ABIC) assessing liver health were in general less severe in younger median than in the elder ones. The only exception was concerning ALT aminotransferase which was more increased in younger than older patients (median = 57 IU/mL vs. 33 IU/mL, p = 0.008, Fig. 3C), as a reflection of the better liver physiological reserve that characterizes young individuals [15]. The distribution of ethnicities was different in the two age subsets with a significantly higher prevalence of the Susu ethnicity in the younger median for age (38.0% vs. 21.1%, p = 0.0447, Fig. 3D).

We looked thereafter for the differences between men and women. The age of male patients tended to be younger than that of female patient (44.0 years vs. 48.9 years) albeit without reaching the significance level (p = 0.0689, ns). Ethanol consumption was significantly more frequent in males (16.0% vs. 1.8%, OR = 10.0, 95%CI: 1.5-422.3, p = 0.0039, Fig. 4A) just as the risk of infection associated with dental care (27.3% vs. 9.2%, OR = 3.6, 95%CI: 1.3–12.5, p = 0.0050, Fig. 4B). Two liver fibrosis scores, Bonacini score (6.0 vs. 4.0, P = 0.0297) and Fibro-Alpha index (8.2 vs. 1.5, p = 0.0005) were also significantly higher in males when compared to females suggesting that PLC develop in males on a tissue more severely altered (Fig. 4C-D). The distribution of patient origins was different in males and females with an overrepresentation of male patients from Middle Guinea (10.5% vs. 1.8%, p = 0.0277, Fig. 4E). The only specificity of HCC patients exposed to liver toxicants (alcohol and tobacco) was the fact that they were almost exclusively males (96.0% vs. 68.7%, OR = 10.8, 95%CI: 2.6–95.7, p = 2.5 E-05, Fig. 4F).

We examined the differences of HCC presentation induced by risk factors and foremost by HBV infection. HBV surface antigen (HBsAg) carriers were ten years younger than other patients (median = 40.0 years vs. 50.5 years, p = 0.0109, Fig. 5A). The presence of HBsAg tended to exclude anti-HCV seropositivity as HBV-infected patients were less often seropositive for HCV than other patients (1.9% vs. 20.0%, OR = 0.08, 95%CI = 0.001–0.806, p = 0.0132, Fig. 5B). The carriage of HBsAg was associated with a larger proportion of patients without clear RF of infection (35.0% vs. 17.3%, OR = 2.54, 95%CI: 1.06–6.84, p = 0.0262) confirming indirectly that in Guinea, chronic infection with HBV frequently finds its origin in perinatal or early childhood horizontal transmission routes (Fig. 5C). Regarding biological features, aminotransferases (AST, ALT) were significantly increased in case of chronic hepatitis B by comparison with non-HBV cases (e.g. AST median = 50.5 IU/mL vs. 31.0 IU/mL, p = 0.0052, Fig. 5D). By contrast, the Age-to-Platelets index (API), was significantly lower in HBsAg (+) patients (3.6 vs. 4.9, p = 0.0146) due to the lower age of the seropositive patients (Fig. 5E). The number of symptoms at HCC diagnosis (Diagnosis Score) was significantly lower in HBsAg carriers (3.0 vs. 4.0, p = 0.0053) suggesting that the tumor process is faster HBV-infected patients and does not strictly parallel an inexorable degradation of health status (Fig. 5F).

Finally, we wondered whether Guinean ethnicities might be a feature modulating HCC presentation in Guinea. The Susu ethnicity, a Mande-speaking group, living primarily on the coastal plain of Guinea was clearly outlying when compared to other groups. Susu underwent a much earlier onset of HCC (median: 37 years vs. 46 years, p = 0.0007, Fig. 6A). They were also more frequently smokers than patients from other ethnicities (28.6% vs. 15.7%, OR = 2.14, 95%CI: 0.99–4.55, p = 0.0347, ns, Fig. 6B). The APP (albumin-platelets product), a recently proposed metric aiming to assess liver fibrosis stage in patients with chronic liver disease independently of age and hepatitis activity, was significantly higher in HCC patients of Susu ethnicity (7.5 vs. 4.5, p = 0.0105, Fig. 6C). The reverse was true for API (Age-to-Plaletets index, 2.0 vs. 4.0, p = 0.0032) due to the younger age of Susu at disease onset (Fig. 6D).

Discussion

The age of Guinean patients with HCC was remarkably low as most cases were diagnosed in the early fifth decade of lifespan (mean = 45.4 years, median:43 years). The age at diagnosis was thus somewhat earlier than in recent series reported from the neighboring countries such as Côte d’Ivoire (series collected between 2007 and 2014, mean and median at 49.0) or in Mali (2006–2015, 51–52 years), Senegal (2012–2017, mean = 47.2 years) or even the Gambia (an older series, 1997–2001, 49.0 years) [16–20]. No relevant data concerning the clinical presentation of HCC was found for Guinea Bissau, Sierra Leone and Liberia. The issue of age at presentation is particularly worrying when considering patients with a Susu ethnic background who developed HCC in the 4th decade of lifespan (median = 37.0 years, IQR = 28-48.5). In absence of a proper aflatoxin B1 exposure assessment, it is difficult to propose a solid explanation to this situation such as a synergistic carcinogenic activity of AFB1 and viruses [21, 22]. Obviously, further epidemiological and molecular studies are now warranted to identify additional RF responsible for the early presentation of HCC in populations living in the wet coastal plains of Guinea around Conakry.

For HCC, the M: F sex ratio is considered to range largely above 1.0 worldwide. This situation is tightly linked to the fact that HBV was and still remains the principal RF of HCC worldwide [23]. Mechanisms installing this male predominance are presumably cumulative and include the increased susceptibility of males to chronic infection, noticed very early by B. Blumberg, and the higher risk of chronic disease progression observed in the same sex [24, 25]. Other important RF of HCC, such as alcohol intake or tobacco consumption are also more prevalent among male patients than in women [26]. In our experience, this situation is particularly obvious, albeit to various extent, in Africa including in the present study (alcohol consumption, 16.0% vs. 1.8%) [16, 27, 28]. The issue of sex ratio is of interest in Sub-Saharan Africa as its value tended to decrease in the recent decades in some countries plausibly due to the introduction of HCV infection in the population [16, 27, 29]. The observed sex ratio in the current large Guinean series (M: F = 3.0) is grossly in keeping with that observed in neighboring countries with smaller sex ratios are occasionally observed as in Côte d’Ivoire (2.6) or Niger (2.2) while much higher values are still reported north of Guinea i.e. in Senegal (6.6) or in the Gambia (3.9) [16, 20, 29–31]. In Mali, recent sex ratios of incident HCC cases are like those observed in Guinea ranging from 2.9 to 4.0 [17, 18, 32].

Chronic infection with HBV was the dominant RF of HCC in this series from Guinea (75.0%). Overall, this value is higher than those observed recently in HCC patients from the Gambia (60.0%), Côte d’Ivoire (65.1%), Senegal (69.4%), Ghana (55.8%), or Mali (33.3%-49.1%) [16–18, 20, 33]. In neighboring countries, the only higher value in recent reports comes from a small series (n = 61) from Gao, in Eastern Mali (90.1%) [32]. The situation is a matter of concern as the median age of HCC onset in HBsAg(+)patients in Guinea is particularly early (40.0 years), ten years before the age of onset with other etiologies, a situation also observed in neighboring or more distant countries and that could be considered as a general pattern in Sub-Saharan Africa [7, 16, 27]. In Central Africa it was observed that early onset was also associated with a hepatitis Delta virus coinfection [34]. Unfortunately, biomarkers of hepatitis delta are not tested routinely in Guinea. Another issue concerns HBV genotypes involved in liver tumorigenesis of Guinean HCC patients. It was shown on a small series of HCC patients in Central Africa a clear heterogeneity in the age of onset of patients infected with HBV quasi-subtype A3 (mean = 31.2 ± 12.6 years, n = 7) and those infected with genotype E (mean = 49.1 ± 15.1 years, n = 35) [35]. HBV subtypes found at different stages of chronic liver diseases in Guinea remain to be defined.

As abovementioned, one of the most intriguing features of the present paper concerns the younger age of patients of Susu ethnicity at HCC onset (13 years earlier than other ethnicities). Susus are occupying Guinea Maritime, a vast marshy region between Conakry, the national capital on the Atlantic Ocean and the city of Kindia in the inner country. A logical hypothesis to explain this situation could be found in the issue of food contamination by aflatoxin B1 (AFB1). Early studies conducted in habitants living around Kindia have shown that AFB1-albumin adduct could be found in 90% of sera [36]. AFB1 contamination was coming from groundnuts that represent a staple around Kindia [37]. More recently, surveys investigating healthy children living around Kindia reported a prevalence of aflatoxin in 85–90% of urine or blood samples [38, 39]. Thus, although molecular evidence from HCC patients, such as TP53 R249S mutants are lacking, it seems highly probable that the worrying presentation of HCC in Susus is due to the early and deleterious combination of chronic HBV infection and AFB1 exposure [40].

We acknowledge the fact that our work suffers from various shortcomings such as the incomplete serological (including hepatitis delta markers) and biological data. However, it also represents the first attempt conducted in Guinea to provide a comprehensive landscape of HCC. Our investigations confirm that HBV represents in Guinea, maybe more than in neighboring countries a hyper-dominant RF of HCC. We also observed that a significant geographical or anthropological heterogeneity might affect HCC presentation in Guinea. We consider, however, that similar situations are observable in most West African countries from Senegal to Nigeria where HBsAg carriage is known to be almost uniformly high.

Conclusions

Our data substantiate the urgent need to conduct further studies exploring for the presence of hitherto neglected risk factors such as infection with hepatitis Delta virus or AFB1 exposure, that might explain the early onset and the worrying high incidence of HCC in Guinea. However, due to the particularly high incidence of HCC in Guinea in comparison with all other sub-Saharan country we cannot rule out the possibility that another, hitherto not identified, risk factor contribute to the carcinogenic process in the liver. We consider that an improvement of the epidemiological situation of HCC in Guinea (around 1600 new cases of liver cancer per year) will necessitate the implementation of significant efforts at different levels from the local public health stakeholders [8]. The first step concerns vaccination against hepatitis B. Unfortunately, anti-hepatitis B immunization coverage of children is still deficient (around 40.0% are immunized) meaning that younger Guinean generations remain largely unprotected from the virus [41]. The second step concerns secondary prevention and focuses on the chronic carriers of HBV estimated to represent 10.0% of the general population while it appears less than 1.0% of them is either diagnosed or properly treated by antivirals [42]. A third step of improvement will consist in implementing a proper surveillance of HCC among patients living with HBV [43]. Finally, a last effort will have to be conducted to merely improve the fate of patients affected by HCC. In a resource-limited country like Guinea, it seems reasonable to advocate the implementation of percutaneous ablative therapies to treat HCC patients [44].
