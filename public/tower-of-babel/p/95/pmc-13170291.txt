Standard Therapy versus Plasmalyte in Children with Dengue Shock Syndrome (SPLID trial): a study protocol for an open-labeled randomized control trial
Authors: Vidushi Mahajan, Neel Sandeep Shah, Tanya Vasudeva, Seema Gupta, Vishal Guglani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntravenous fluid resuscitation is the first-line therapy in the management of dengue shock syndrome (DSS), a severe complication of dengue fever characterized by vascular leakage and organ dysfunction. However, the choice of intravenous fluids for resuscitation in DSS remains contentious. Evidence is lacking with regard to standard therapy, like normal saline (NS) and Ringer lactate (RL), versus newer balanced crystalloids like Plasmalyte (PL) for resuscitation of DSS.MethodsThis study is a three-arm, prospective, open-label, parallel-group randomized clinical trial (RCT) to be conducted at a tertiary care hospital in north India over 36 months. We will recruit 135 children aged 2 months to 18 years, admitted with DSS who fulfill the eligibility criteria as per the WHO classification. The participants will be randomized to receive NS, or RL or PL in a 1:1:1 assignment. The primary outcome is a ≥5 mmol/L increase in serum chloride within 48 h post-randomization. The secondary outcomes include changes in acid-base status, hospital stay, need for colloid bolus, new-onset acute kidney injury (AKI), organ dysfunction, and mortality. AKI and organ dysfunction will be defined as per KDIGO criteria and Pediatric Logistic Organ Dysfunction 2 score (PELOD-2), respectively. Any serious adverse event occurring during the course of the trial will be reported to the ethics committee and the monitoring committee within 24 h. Both intention-to-treat and per-protocol analyses will be conducted.DiscussionDSS initially requires prompt fluid resuscitation with crystalloids. NS in large volumes may lead to hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis. RL, containing lactate as a buffer, is contraindicated in children with compromised liver function due to their reduced ability to metabolize lactate. PL, a balanced crystalloid, is isotonic with a chloride concentration similar to that of plasma. Thus, the RCT aims to determine the change in serum chloride between standard treatment vs. PL within 48 h of fluid resuscitation and to compare the change in acid-base status, hospital stay, new-onset AKI, organ dysfunction, and mortality in children with DSS.Trial registrationThe SPLID trial has been registered prospectively with the Clinical Trial Registry of India (CTRI) registry no. Introduction

Background and rationale {6a}

Dengue is a febrile illness with clinical manifestations ranging from asymptomatic infection to severe illness with multiorgan involvement. The incidence of dengue has increased dramatically, with almost half of the world’s population at risk. The number of dengue cases has increased eightfold, from 505,430 cases in 2000 to over 5.2 million in 2019 [1]. Dengue has been hyper-endemic for decades in south-east Asia, and the region has the highest incidence of dengue in the world. According to the National Vector Borne Disease Control Program (NVBDCP), from 1 January 2022 to 31 October 2022, 110,473 cases of dengue fever and 86 deaths were reported in India [2]. Dengue shock syndrome (DSS) is the most serious manifestation of dengue fever [3] and is mostly observed in children and adolescents. DSS, an incompletely understood vasculopathy, manifests as severe vascular leakage, bleeding, thrombocytopenia, and disordered hemostasis, progressing to death in 20% of cases [1, 4].

The mainstay therapy for DSS is intravenous fluids. As per the WHO, replacement of plasma losses in DSS is initially done with crystalloid fluids [2, 4]. Standard therapy in DSS consists of fluid therapy with isotonic crystalloids like normal saline (NS) or Ringer lactate (RL). In small volumes, NS is a suitable option for initial fluid resuscitation. However, in dengue shock syndrome (DSS), where prolonged fluid resuscitation is often needed, the use of large volumes of NS may lead to hyperchloremic acidosis. Hyperchloremia has been associated with life-threatening complications like acute kidney injury (AKI), coagulopathy, gastrointestinal dysfunction, and even an increased risk of mortality in critically ill adults and children [5, 6]. In adult surgical postoperative patients, infusion of intravenous fluids with high chloride content resulted in metabolic acidosis due to reduced strong ion difference, i.e., (Na+ + K+ + Ca2+ + Mg 2+) minus (Cl− + lactate) in plasma [7]. Also, chloride-rich fluid has been shown to have a detrimental effect on renal perfusion in both animal models and adult studies [8]. Ringer lactate (RL), conversely, has lower sodium and chloride content than NS, and an osmolality of 273 mOsm/L. RL contains lactate as a buffer and is contraindicated in children with compromised liver function, as they have a reduced ability to metabolize lactate [9]. Hypotonic fluids like 0.45% saline or 0.2% saline are no longer used due to the risk of iatrogenic hyponatremia [10]. Considering these caveats in the existing fluids, the focus has recently shifted from unbalanced to balanced fluids in the management of hemodynamically unstable children. Balanced crystalloids are sodium-based solutions with chloride content less than 154 mmol/L, with added buffers for better approximation of plasma composition [9]. Compared with NS, PL contains gluconate and acetate, which act as buffering agents and increase bicarbonate levels more quickly. In vitro studies showed that blood products washed with PL had decreased hemolysis and improved platelet function as compared with those washed with NS [9].

The physiological advantages of Plasmalyte are also reflected in clinical outcomes in several studies in hemodynamically unstable adults and pediatric patients. According to a recent meta-analysis in adults, 12 RCTs out of which eight were performed in the perioperative setting, two in the intensive care unit, one in the emergency department, and one in healthy volunteers that compared PL with other balanced crystalloids (including Hartmann’s solution, RL, Sterofundin, Ringerfundin) demonstrated that PL use was associated with lower postinfusion serum chloride concentration, lactate levels, along with higher postinfusion base excess [11]. PL was found to be more beneficial in acute diarrhea, diabetic ketoacidosis, and transplant recipients, in whom the blood bicarbonate levels normalized more rapidly [12–15]. Balanced fluids like PL offer a theoretical advantage in resuscitation scenarios, given that their pH, osmolality, and electrolyte composition closely resemble that of plasma, and they contain a physiologic buffer [9]. The drawback of PL is that it gets precipitated with some critical care drugs like midazolam and ketamine if given at the same cannulation site [16]. A randomized trial (PRoMPT BOLUS) in children with sepsis, comparing balanced fluid to NS, is currently underway [17].

However, to date, no study has been conducted to compare PL vs. NS or RL in DSS patients. Given that the burden of dengue fever and consequently DSS is increasing, it is of importance to study the benefits and adverse effects of these fluids. Hence, we planned this trial to examine whether Plasmalyte (PL) causes less chloride rise and better preservation of renal function than does standard therapy (i.e., NS or RL) in children with DSS.

Objectives {7}

The primary objective is to determine the change in serum chloride from baseline to 48 h of fluid resuscitation in dengue shock syndrome, by comparing two standard treatment arms, i.e., normal saline (NS) or Ringer lactate (RL), with the intervention arm, i.e., Plasmalyte (PL). The secondary objectives include comparing changes in acid-base status from baseline to 48 h after intravenous fluid resuscitation, the duration of hospital stay, the incidence of new-onset acute kidney injury, multiorgan dysfunction, and the risk of mortality between standard vs. PL therapy in these children.

Trial design {8}

This RCT is an investigator-initiated, three-arm parallel-group, open-label, randomized control trial and will be conducted in the emergency ward and pediatric intensive care unit of a tertiary care hospital in north India. The study duration is 36 months, from 15.02.2024 to 14.02.2027.

Methods: participants, interventions, and outcomes

Study setting {9}

This study will be conducted at a tertiary care hospital in Chandigarh, India.

Eligibility criteria {10}

We will include children aged 2 months to 18 years admitted to our center with dengue shock syndrome (DSS) defined according to WHO classification [1, 4]. Those who have already received fluids from outside before enrollment in the last 6 h, children with cardiogenic shock [18], known patients with chronic kidney disease as per KDIGO [19], severe malnutrition defined as per WHO [20], and whose parents deny consent will be excluded. Consecutive children fulfilling the above-mentioned eligibility criteria for DSS shall be prospectively enrolled after obtaining written and informed consent from either parent.

Who will take informed consent? {26a}

The research staff will obtain informed consent ± assent from participants and their legal guardians. For detailed information regarding the collection and use of participant data, please refer to the attached informed consent (Appendix 1).

Additional consent provisions for collection and use of participant data and biological specimens {26b}

Not applicable, no sample collected.

Interventions

Explanation for the choice of comparators {6b}

Standard therapy in DSS constitutes use of NS or RL for fluid resuscitation. Large volumes of NS may cause hyperchloremic acidosis. RL contains lactate as a buffer, so it is contraindicated in children with compromised liver function as they have a reduced ability to metabolize lactate. Balanced fluids like PL which has pH, osmolality, and electrolytes similar to plasma in addition to a buffer may offer a therapeutic advantage in DSS. The trial aims to evaluate whether a more physiologically balanced solution offers better clinical outcomes versus NS or RL as comparators, reflecting real-world practice.

Intervention description {11a}

The participants will be randomized in a 1:1:1 ratio to receive one of three crystalloid solutions—normal saline (NS), Ringer lactate (RL), or Plasmalyte (PL) as part of fluid resuscitation therapy. The random sequence will be computer-generated using stratified and blocked randomization. Stratification will be performed by weight (<20 kg vs. ≥20 kg). We will use randomly varying blocks of sizes 3, 6, and 9. Allocation concealment will be ensured through the use of serially numbered, opaque brown sealed envelopes.

Baseline assessment

The children will be assessed for clinical status, hematological parameters (hematocrit, blood counts), biochemical parameters (serum electrolytes, renal function tests, liver function tests), and microbiological parameters (fever workup including blood cultures) at the baseline.

After enrollment, the children will be attached to the multichannel monitors, and their vital signs will be continuously monitored and periodically recorded in the case record form. Children in the three intervention groups will start with 10–20 mL/kg bolus of study fluid in the initial 30 min and will be followed for therapeutic end points, i.e., stabilization of vital signs and normalization of hematocrit [2, 4]. If the hematocrit falls, pulse rate and blood pressure stabilize, and urine output is between 0.5 and 1.0 mL/kg/h, the fluid infusion rate shall be reduced from 10 to 7 mL/kg/h for 2–4 h, 5 to 3 mL/kg/h for next 2–4 h, and from 3 to 1.5 mL/kg/h for next 2–4 h. However, if the hematocrit falls and there is no improvement in shock status or if the hematocrit rises, a second bolus of study fluid 10–20 mL/kg will be administered over 15–30 min. If vitals stabilize and hematocrit normalizes [2, 4], this will be taken as the end point of resuscitation, usually achieved by 48–72 h. If clinical worsening occurs and hematocrit shows rise, a third bolus may be given as described in Fig. 1. The key features of SPLID trial are described in Table 1. Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions {11b}

If resuscitation is not complete by 48 h and clinicians provide another fluid (e.g., colloid) in case of worsening clinical status within 48 h post-randomization, the event will be recorded. Any protocol deviation will be recorded. The study children will be followed up until discharge from the hospital.

Strategies to improve adherence to interventions {11c}

The training of research staff will be done prior to the start of the trial and then at regular intervals using printed teaching material prepared for project execution. Clear effective communication and continuous bedside monitoring of the patient’s vital signs, urine output, and hematocrit levels shall be maintained. Investigators and research staff will follow a strict administration schedule based on protocol-defined criteria. Data collection will be facilitated by standardized case record forms.

Relevant concomitant care permitted or prohibited during the trial {11d}

Relevant concomitant care will be permitted on the basis of clinical need and will be recorded.

Provisions for post-trial care {30}

Children will be followed up until discharge from the hospital. Any adverse events will be managed as per the protocol. The protocol ensures no compromise in participant care regardless of trial involvement.

Outcomes {12}

Primary outcome: Our primary outcome will be an increase in the serum chloride level ≥ 5 mmol/L from the baseline, by 48 h post-randomization (Table 2) [21, 22]. Baseline levels will be taken immediately upon clinical presentation, preferably before initiation of fluid therapy. However, in severe shock where intravenous access is difficult, it shall be sent within 1 h of starting fluids. An alternate route of access, such as the intraosseous route, shall be employed in such cases where IV access is not achieved within 90 s, as per the PALS guidelines [23]. Repeat sample for outcome assessment shall be taken at 48 h (46–50 h) after initiation of fluid therapy. In the event of a hemolyzed sample, the sample would be repeated at the earliest opportunity. The blood samples will be processed by the autoanalyzer (Siemens Advia Centaur XPT) in the biochemistry laboratory. The secondary outcomes will be hospital stay duration, need for colloid bolus, new-onset AKI, change in acid-base status and electrolytes, serum lactate levels using the ABG analyzer (ABL 800 FLEX, Radiometer, Denmark), renal replacement therapy, and organ dysfunction [24]. Organ dysfunction will be defined by Pediatric Logistic Organ Dysfunction 2 score (PELOD-2) (Appendix 2). AKI will be defined as per KDIGO criteria (Appendix 3). Patient characteristics

Any adverse event occurring during the course of the trial will be reported to the IEC and the DSMB and documented within 24 h and final reporting within 14 days.

Participant timeline {13}

The SPLID trial is expected to be completed over 36 months. The participant timeline includes screening and enrollment. All patients presenting with DSS will be screened for eligibility criteria. If the patient fulfills eligibility criteria, she/he will be enrolled after obtaining written and informed parental consent. Baseline tests will be done immediately after admission by drawing 6 mL venous blood sample under aseptic conditions to check hematocrit levels, serum electrolytes including serum chloride, renal function tests, and blood gas analysis. All these investigations will be repeated after 48 h. The children will be continuously monitored for vital signs, which will be periodically recorded. Their urine output will be recorded every 6 h, or earlier, for the initial 48 h. Laboratory monitoring, which includes blood gas analysis, serum electrolytes, and renal function tests, will be monitored every 12–24 h. The hematocrit level and urine output will be used to decide the rate of intravenous fluids and to ascertain therapeutic end points, along with improvement. Any adverse events or protocol deviations will be recorded. At the end of participation (after 48 h or upon resolution of shock), participants will undergo study-related monitoring, although medical care will continue as per hospital protocol. Figure 2 shows the schedule of enrollment, intervention, and assessments in accordance with the SPIRIT reporting guidelines [22].

Sample size {14}

The sample size was calculated to detect a mean difference of 5 meq/dL in serum chloride level [21] between the three intervention arms: Plasmalyte (PL), normal saline (NS), and Ringer lactate (RL). With a standard deviation of 7 meq/dL, a type I error rate of 0.05, and a power of 90%, we will need to study 41 children in each group. To account for missing laboratory data (an attrition of 10%), we shall recruit 135 subjects (45 each in PL, RL, and NS groups).

Recruitment {15}

Recruitment is prospective and will be conducted at a tertiary care hospital in north India. Children meeting the criteria for DSS will be consecutively assessed for eligibility and enrolled after obtaining informed written consent from a parent or guardian. The recruitment process will continue until the required sample size is achieved. Our protocol is patient-centric and project staff will be available to clarify patient doubts. We recognize that dengue is a seasonal illness in India, so we plan to collaborate with state functionaries of nearby states of Punjab and Haryana to achieve adequate participant enrollment to reach the target sample size.

Assignment of interventions: allocation

Sequence generation {16a}

The randomization sequence will be generated via web-based software (www.randomizer.org). We will perform stratified and blocked randomization. The children will be enrolled in two strata according to the weight, i.e., <20 kg vs. >20 kg. Each stratum will have permuted randomly varying block sizes of 3, 6, or 9.

Concealment mechanism {16b}

Allocation concealment would be achieved by use of serially numbered opaque brown sealed envelopes, which shall be opened by the study investigator just prior to the administration of the study fluid.

Implementation {16c}

The randomization sequence will be generated by an investigator not involved in recruitment or management of study patients. Each stratum (<20 kg and >20 kg) will have its own random sequence. Allocation will be ensured by the use of serially numbered opaque envelopes. Participants will be randomized in a 1:1:1 ratio into the three intervention arms: PL, NS, or RL. Participants will be enrolled by staff nurses at the study site.

Assignment of interventions: blinding

Who will be blinded {17a}

This is an open-label trial; hence, blinding of participants and investigators is not applicable. However, the person analyzing the data will be blinded to the intervention and patient characteristics.

Procedure for unblinding if needed {17b}

Not applicable as it is an open-label trial.

Data collection and management

Plans for assessment and collection of outcomes {18a}

The assessment will include clinical and laboratory measures. Vital signs, rate and duration of fluid administration, urine output, and other clinical parameters will be recorded periodically in a case record form. Baseline and follow-up (at 48 h) blood tests (hematocrit levels, serum electrolytes, renal function test, and blood gas analysis) will be conducted under aseptic conditions (Tables 2 and 3).

The primary outcome is ≥5 mmol/L increase in serum chloride level within 48 h post-randomization. Secondary outcomes include duration of hospital stay, need for colloid bolus, new-onset AKI, change in acid-base status from baseline, renal replacement therapy, incidence of organ dysfunction, and risk of mortality [22].

Plans to promote participant retention and complete follow-up {18b}

The children will be continuously monitored for vital signs, which will be periodically recorded to ensure compliance until discharge. Any protocol deviations or dropouts will be recorded and analyzed via intention-to-treat analysis.

Data management {19}

Data will be recorded in structured case record forms and will be entered directly into Microsoft Excel. All entries will be anonymized by a unique participant identification number. Periodic data backups will be performed, and results will be verified for completeness and accuracy. All study data will be password-protected and coded with a study number to ensure confidentiality. We have added a dedicated appendix on data management detailing procedures for data entry, coding, security, storage, and data quality assurance (Appendix 4).

Confidentiality {27}

Participant confidentiality and privacy will be strictly maintained by the principal investigator and their research staff. This protection extends to all data collected during the study. All study-related activities will be carried out in a setting that ensures maximum privacy.

Participants’ contact information will be securely stored and used only for internal study purposes. Upon completion of the study, all records will be securely retained as specified by ICMR. Research data collected from study participants for statistical analysis and scientific reporting will be stored in a secured, locked location. This data will not include any personal contact details or identifying information. Instead, each participant and their corresponding data will be linked only through a unique study identification number. The data entry and management systems used by the research staff will be password-protected and secured. Upon study completion, all databases will be deidentified and archived accordingly.

Plans for collection, laboratory evaluation, and storage of biological specimens for genetic or molecular analysis in this trial/future use {33}

Not applicable. No biological samples will be stored or used for genetic or molecular analysis.

Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {20a}

Descriptive statistics will be used to define demographic and clinical variables. The baseline variables will not be compared and will be presented for the description of intervention groups. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) or Kruskal–Wallis H test will be used to analyze continuous variables wherever indicated. If the ANOVA test demonstrates a statistically significant difference among groups (p < 0.05), pairwise comparisons between study arms will be conducted using Tukey post hoc test for all pairwise comparisons. The change in serum chloride levels after 48 h of intravenous fluid resuscitation between the two groups will be compared by repeated measures analysis of variance or mixed linear models after adjusting for patient weight (<20 kg vs. >20 kg), wherever applicable. The longitudinal data in each group will be checked for normality. If all the assumptions for parametric tests are fulfilled, we will use repeated measures analysis of variance (RM-ANOVA) to compare the change of numerical variables from the baseline to post-intervention. If the assumptions of ANOVA are not met, we will use mixed linear models. The covariates will be selected a priori to check the confounding effect of other variables on change of chloride pre and post sample. All p values will be 2-tailed; p < 0.05 shall be considered statistically significant. All statistical analysis would be done using both intention-to-treat and per-protocol analyses in SPSS v. 29.0.

Interim analyses {21b}

One formal interim analysis is planned after approximately 50% of the target sample size has completed the 48-h primary outcome assessment. The interim analysis will be performed by an independent statistician who is not otherwise involved in trial conduct, and the results will be reviewed by the DSMB. The interim analysis will evaluate both safety outcomes and efficacy with respect to the primary endpoint (increase in serum chloride ≥5 mmol/L at 48 h). Early stopping for efficacy or harm will be considered using a group-sequential design with O’Brien–Fleming alpha-spending boundaries, thereby preserving the overall two-sided type I error rate of 0.05. The specific nominal significance levels for the interim and final analyses are provided in Appendix 5. Recommendations regarding trial continuation, modification, or termination will be made by the DSMB based on the interim data. These recommendations will be communicated to the sponsor/funding agency (ICMR), which will implement the final decision in accordance with DSMB guidance. No other formal interim efficacy analyses are planned.

Methods for additional analyses (e.g., subgroup analyses) {20b}

The change in serum chloride levels from the baseline, after 48 h of intravenous fluid resuscitation between the three groups will be compared by repeated measures analysis of variance or mixed linear models after adjusting for patient weight (<20 kg vs. >20 kg), wherever applicable. Subgroup analyses are planned to explore whether the treatment effect differs across predefined strata such as weight-based or age category-based stratification. A per-protocol (PP) analysis will be performed as a sensitivity analysis to assess the robustness of the findings. This will include only participants who fully adhered to the intervention protocol without protocol deviations. Results from the per-protocol analysis will be compared with the primary intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis to evaluate consistency. All p values will be 2-tailed; p < 0.05 shall be considered statistically significant.

Methods in analysis to handle protocol non-adherence and any statistical methods to handle missing data {20c}

We shall do per-protocol analysis in addition to the ITT. All protocol deviations will be documented. These deviations will be assessed to determine their potential impact on the validity and integrity of the trial outcomes. Major deviations such as failure to meet inclusion or exclusion criteria, administration of non-permitted interventions, or deviation from the prescribed fluid regimen may lead to exclusion of the affected participant from the per-protocol analysis set. Efforts will be made to ensure complete data collection. The primary analysis will be conducted using repeated-measures linear mixed-effects models as these models effectively handle missing data. Multiple imputation will be considered as a sensitivity analysis if the extent of missing data is substantial (e.g., approximately 15% or greater) or if patterns of missingness suggest that the missing at random assumption may be questionable. Imputation will not be applied when missing data are minimal and unlikely to materially influence the results (Appendix 5).

Plans to give access to the full protocol, participant-level data, and statistical code {31c}

This is a single-center study. An independent three-member DSMB comprising a senior clinician, a member of the institute’s research committee/IEC, and a statistician will oversee trial conduct and review data annually or after enrollment of 1/3 of patients, whichever is earlier.

Composition of the data monitoring committee, its role and reporting structure {21a}

The trial will be overseen by an independent 3-member committee, DSMB, which will constitute a senior clinician, a member of the institute’s research/ethics committee, and a statistician. Their role would be to monitor and supervise the progress of the trial and review the trial data at regular intervals. It will make recommendations regarding trial continuation or termination based on cumulative safety and efficacy data. All DSMB meetings will be documented, with records shared with the IEC and ICMR.

Routine protocol reviews and quarterly evaluations of enrollment status, protocol compliance, and adverse event trends will be conducted. The DSMB will evaluate final efficacy and safety data to inform end-of-study recommendations. Ad hoc meetings may also be convened if the ICMR identifies urgent concerns related to safety, data quality, or protocol adherence. The DSMB functions independently of the ICMR and has no conflicts of interest.

Adverse event reporting and harms {22}

Any adverse event occurring during the course of the trial would be reported to the DSMB and IEC and documented within 24 h and a final report sent by 14 days. Expected complications related to DSS such as bleeding will be recorded though not reported as an adverse event.

While the study does not involve a device, any unexpected reactions, medical errors, or safety concerns related to fluid administration will be handled with immediate corrective measures and documented as part of trial safety oversight.

Frequency and plans for auditing trial conduct {23}

Audits may be conducted at any point during the study by authorized representatives of the ICMR, DSMB, or the IEC as per Standard Operating Procedures. If a regulatory authority requests an inspection, the principal investigator will inform the ICMR. The goal of audits is to independently evaluate trial conduct and ensure that the integrity, validity, and reliability of the data are maintained throughout the course of the study.

Any significant protocol amendment defined as a change that could impact participant safety, ethical considerations, or the scientific validity of the study will be thoroughly reviewed and approved before implementation. Study intervention

Discussion

The SPLID trial is a randomized, open-label trial assessing whether PL offers a clinical advantage over NS or RL in managing DSS in children, with primary outcome being a ≥5 mmol/L increase in serum chloride level from baseline within 48 h post-randomization. As fluid therapy in DSS is the cornerstone of management, we hope to determine the optimal fluid for resuscitation in children presenting with DSS. The RCT would help clinicians to make a more informed decision on whether balanced crystalloids like PL are advantageous to use over the standard therapy (i.e., NS or RL) in terms of hyperchloremia, metabolic acidosis, and organ dysfunction in children with DSS. If the SPLID trial results support our research hypothesis, i.e., balanced crystalloids viz. PL is advantageous over standard therapy, which could be translated into clinical practice.

The study’s randomized design ensures robust and unbiased comparisons between intervention groups. Stratified randomization ensures that treatment groups in a study are balanced on predefined weight cut-off, an important variable that is known to influence the outcome. Blocking is introduced to ensure balance in treatment assignments within each stratum. Comprehensive outcome measures, including biochemical, clinical, and functional parameters, ensure a holistic evaluation of interventions.

The open-label design may introduce performance bias, as clinicians are aware of the allocated intervention. However, the use of objective outcome measures such as serum chloride levels mitigates this risk. This trial represents an essential step in evaluating the efficacy and safety of balanced crystalloids in pediatric DSS. Future research should explore the long-term outcomes of fluid resuscitation strategies and cost-effectiveness analyses to inform resource allocation in low- and middle-income countries.

Appendix 5

Interim analysis and handling of missing data

Interim analysis

One formal interim analysis is planned after approximately 50% of the target sample size has completed the 48-h primary outcome assessment. The interim analysis will be performed by an independent statistician who is not otherwise involved in trial conduct, and the results will be reviewed exclusively by the DSMB.

The interim analysis will evaluate both safety outcomes and efficacy with respect to the primary endpoint (increase in serum chloride ≥5 mmol/L at 48 h). Early stopping for efficacy or harm will be considered using a group-sequential design with O’Brien–Fleming alpha-spending boundaries, thereby preserving the overall two-sided type I error rate of 0.05. Recommendations regarding trial continuation, modification, or termination will be made by the DSMB based on the interim data. These recommendations will be communicated to the sponsor/funding agency (ICMR), which will implement the final decision in accordance with DSMB guidance. No other formal interim efficacy analyses are planned.

Assessment and extent of missing data

As specified in the protocol, missing laboratory and outcome data are anticipated to be minimal, and the sample size has already accounted for an anticipated attrition of approximately 10% due to missing laboratory measurements.

Prior to final analysis, the extent, pattern, and reasons for missing data will be evaluated and summarized overall and by treatment group. Missingness will be explored to assess whether data are missing at random or not, where feasible and clinically justifiable.

Handling of missing data

The primary analysis will be conducted using repeated-measures linear mixed-effects models as these models effectively handle missing data.

Multiple imputation will be considered as a sensitivity analysis if the extent of missing data is 15% or greater or if patterns of missingness suggest that the missing at random assumption may be questionable. Imputation will not be applied when missing data are minimal and unlikely to materially influence the results. Imputation models will incorporate treatment allocation, baseline covariates, and available longitudinal outcome data using appropriate model diagnostics to assess convergence and plausibility (3).

Use of linear mixed-effects models

As prespecified in the protocol’s statistical analysis section, repeated measures analyses for primary and secondary outcomes will be conducted using linear mixed-effects models (4).
