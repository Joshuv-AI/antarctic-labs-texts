Pulmonary coexistence of immunoglobulin light chain amyloidosis and light chain deposition disease: A case report
Authors: Yasushi Murakami, Makoto Goshima, Mitsuki Tanimoto, Naoto Kobayashi, Atsushi Sumida, Reo Kondo, Shoichi Mori, Ayami Kiriyama, Hideki Murakami, Kai Miyazaki, Yukako Shintani-Domoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe report the case of a 73-year-old woman with diffuse pulmonary cysts and multiple nodules incidentally detected during the follow-up for chronic liver disease. High-resolution computed tomography revealed numerous thin-walled cysts, predominantly in the lower lobes, with randomly distributed nodules. Pathological evaluation of the surgical lung biopsy specimens revealed nodular deposits of eosinophilic, amorphous material with peribronchiolar lymphoplasmacytic inflammation and identified pulmonary mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue lymphoma. Congo red staining revealed coexisting positive and negative foci within a single nodule, whereas immunohistochemistry showed kappa (κ) light chain positivity in both. Laser microdissection with liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry identified a κ light chain dominant signature with amyloid signature proteins in the Congo red-positive region, consistent with immunoglobulin light chain amyloidosis (AL) κ-type, and abundant κ light chain peptides without amyloid signature proteins in the Congo red-negative region, consistent with non-amyloid κ light chain deposition. Systemic evaluation revealed no extrapulmonary amyloid or light-chain deposition, whereas Sjögren disease was confirmed by serological testing and minor salivary gland histology. The patient was managed conservatively with surveillance and remained clinically and radiographically stable without disease progression for approximately 1 year. Pulmonary AL and light-chain deposition are rare lung disorders, and pathologically proven pulmonary coexistence is rare. This case may help delineate the clinicopathological features of pulmonary monoclonal light chain deposition disorders.Keywords: Immunoglobulin light chain amyloidosis, Light chain deposition disease, Diffuse cystic lung disease, Sjögren disease, Mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue lymphoma, Laser microdissection followed by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry

Introduction

Pulmonary immunoglobulin light chain amyloidosis (AL) and light chain deposition disease (LCDD) are rare lung disorders caused by the deposition of monoclonal immunoglobulin light chains [[1], [2], [3]]. Given that these disorders are frequently associated with hematological malignancies and may rarely progress to respiratory failure, early recognition and timely management decision-making is essential [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6]]. In their systemic forms, which typically arise from a plasma cell dyscrasia, AL (characterized by fibrillar structure) and LCDD (defined by granular structure) have been shown to represent distinct disease processes with different patterns of organ involvement [[1], [2], [3]]. However, clinically significant pulmonary involvement is uncommon in both systemic diseases, and pulmonary AL and LCDD are often present as incidental, lung-limited processes [[1], [2], [3]]. Consequently, the evidence of pulmonary diseases is limited, and their epidemiology and overall disease characteristics are not yet well established.

Until recently, AL and LCDD have been primarily studied as separate entities. Nevertheless, rare cases in which amyloid and nonamyloid light-chain deposits coexist within the same organ have been reported [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12]]. In a multicenter cohort of 37 renal cases with coexisting AL and LCDD, their co-occurrence was associated with clinicopathological features that were potentially distinct from those of either condition alone [12]. Accordingly, to advance the understanding of pulmonary immunoglobulin light chain deposition disorders, studies on coexisting pulmonary AL and LCDD are important; however, published cases remain exceedingly few. Herein, we report the case of a patient with diffuse pulmonary cysts and multiple nodules, in whom surgical lung biopsy and proteomic analysis confirmed the coexistence of nodular pulmonary AL and LCDD associated with Sjögren disease and mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) lymphoma.

Case presentation

A 73-year-old woman was referred to our respiratory department for evaluation of incidentally detected bilateral pulmonary abnormalities. One year earlier, evaluation for elevated liver enzymes with positive antinuclear and antimitochondrial antibodies raised suspicion of autoimmune hepatitis-primary biliary cholangitis overlap syndrome, and she was followed up with ursodeoxycholic acid. She had hypertension, never smoked, and had no occupational dust exposure. The patient was asymptomatic at presentation and exhibited no respiratory or sicca symptoms. Her body temperature was 36.1°C, and oxygen saturation was 98% in ambient air. Physical examination results were unremarkable, except for chronic periodontitis.

Laboratory tests showed a white blood cell count of 5100/mm3 and a C-reactive protein level of 0.108 mg/dL. Liver enzymes were consistent with biochemical remission of chronic liver disease: aspartate aminotransferase, 19 U/L; alanine aminotransferase, 10 U/L; alkaline phosphatase, 141 U/L; γ-glutamyltransferase, 23 U/L; and total bilirubin, 0.6 mg/dL. High-resolution computed tomography (CT) performed at the first visit revealed numerous thin-walled cysts and multiple pulmonary nodules in both lungs (Fig. 1). The cysts were numerous, predominantly in the bilateral lower lobes, measuring 3–26 mm in diameter; 20 nodules were identified (2.6–18.1 mm in diameter) with a random distribution, 6 of which were mural nodules along cyst walls. Several vessels crossing the cyst wall were observed. No calcified nodules, mediastinal/hilar lymphadenopathy, or pleural effusion was present. Pulmonary function testing results suggested mild small-airway obstruction: forced vital capacity (FVC), 2.21L [106.3% predicted]; forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), 1.69 L (104.3% predicted); FEV1/FVC ratio, 76.5%; V50/V25 (ratio of maximal expiratory flows at 50% and 25% of vital capacity), 5.47; and diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide, 113.8% predicted.

Considering metastatic disease and chronic pulmonary infection in the differential diagnosis, we performed an extensive work-up, including upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy and bronchoscopy; however, no diagnosis was established. Several months after the initial visit, a video-assisted thoracoscopic lung biopsy targeting the subpleural nodules and adjacent cystic changes in the left S5 and S8 segments was performed. Gross examination of the resected specimens revealed multiple yellow-white firm nodules. Histologically, hematoxylin and eosin staining showed nodular deposits of eosinophilic, amorphous material, in part with ossification, and foreign body-type giant cells surrounded by aggregates of lymphocytes and plasma cells. Multiple cystically dilated airspaces were observed around the deposits (Fig. 2A). Marked peribronchiolar infiltration by lymphocytes and plasma cells, consistent with lymphocytic bronchiolitis, was associated with luminal narrowing of the bronchioles (Fig. 2B). Similar inflammatory changes were observed in the bronchioles contiguous with the cysts. No findings indicating chronic interstitial pneumonia were found. A lesion composed of small monotonous lymphocytes was noted in an area adjacent to the deposits. Immunohistochemical staining of this region revealed infiltration of CD20-positive B lymphocytes lacking CD3, CD5, and CD10 expression, with lymphoepithelial lesions involving the pan-cytokeratin (AE1/AE3)-positive bronchial epithelium. Additionally, ultrasensitive RNA in situ hybridization (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland) demonstrated kappa (κ) light chain restriction, leading to the diagnosis of pulmonary MALT lymphoma (Fig. 2C–F).

Congo red staining of nodular deposits revealed coexisting positive and negative areas within the same nodule (Fig. 3A). Congo red-positive areas exhibited apple-green birefringence under polarized light, which is consistent with amyloids (Fig. 3B). Immunohistochemistry for immunoglobulin κ light chain was positive in both the Congo red-positive and -negative deposits (Fig. 3C), whereas lambda (λ) (Fig. 3D), serum amyloid A, and transthyretin were negative. To characterize the protein composition of the deposits, we performed laser microdissection followed by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LMD-LC-MS/MS) on κ-positive areas, which were subdivided into Congo red -positive and -negative regions (Table 1). In the Congo red-positive region, κ light chain-dominant signature and amyloid signature proteins (i.e., apolipoprotein E, apolipoprotein A-IV, and serum amyloid P component) was detected, consistent with AL κ-type. In contrast, the Congo red-negative regions showed more abundant deposition of immunoglobulin κ light chain-derived peptides without amyloid signature proteins, findings consistent with nonamyloid κ light chain deposition. Based on these pathological results including proteomic analysis, we established a diagnosis of coexisting AL and LCDD.

Following pathological diagnosis, systemic evaluation was performed for underlying plasma cell dyscrasia, autoimmune disease, and extrapulmonary disease involvement. Serological testing showed polyclonal hypergammaglobulinemia (immunoglobulin [Ig] G, 2217 mg/dL; IgA, 440 mg/dL, and IgM, 555 mg/dL) and slightly elevated serum free light-chain κ/λ ratio (1.96). No monoclonal proteins were detected on serum or urine electrophoresis. Transthoracic echocardiography, urinalysis, and biopsies of the abdominal fat, labial salivary glands, and duodenal mucosa revealed no evidence of extrapulmonary amyloid or light-chain deposition. Whole-body CT showed no findings suggestive of an extrapulmonary lymphoma. Sjögren disease was diagnosed based on anti-Sjögren's syndrome type A (anti-SSA) antibody positivity and the characteristic histopathology of the labial salivary glands. She received no disease-specific treatment and was monitored for approximately 1 year, with serial imaging studies showing no enlargement of the pulmonary nodules or cysts.

Discussion

The key finding of this case was that in a patient presenting with diffuse pulmonary cysts and multiple nodules, the histopathology of surgically biopsied lung specimens with proteomic typing demonstrated the coexistence of pulmonary AL and LCDD within the same nodule. Additionally, pulmonary MALT lymphoma and Sjögren disease were diagnosed, and the cystic component was histopathologically assessed. Pathologically proven pulmonary coexistence of AL and LCDD is uncommon. This case may help delineate the clinicopathological spectrum of pulmonary monoclonal light chain deposition disorders.

Nodular pulmonary amyloidosis (NPA) typically presents as localized AL; however, rare cases of systemic AL, amyloid A, and localized transthyretin amyloidosis have been reported [4,5]. Autoimmune diseases are reported in approximately 20–35% [13,14] and MALT lymphoma in approximately 4–33% [4,5] of cases, with a particularly strong emphasis on the association with Sjögren disease. While a monoclonal protein is detected in the serum or urine in approximately 20% of cases [5], clonal plasma cells have been demonstrated locally in >50% of lesions, and the disease is considered to arise largely from the local production of clonal immunoglobulins in association with a lymphoplasmacytic neoplasm [14]. Chest CT displayed this entity characterized by the coexistence of multiple pulmonary nodules and numerous cysts. Calcification within nodules (reported in approximately 60–80% of cases) and mural nodules along the cyst walls are additional characteristic findings [15,16]. Diffuse cyst formation is a salient feature; therefore, NPA should be included in the differential diagnosis of diffuse cystic lung disease [17]. Although the mechanism of cyst formation remains incompletely understood, a plausible hypothesis is that amyloid deposition and associated inflammation cause bronchiolar obstruction, leading to a ball-valve effect with dilatation of distal airspaces [16]; in this case, we observed expansion of alveolar spaces and lymphocytic inflammation around contiguous bronchioles. Even in the absence of active treatment, a watchful waiting strategy is frequently adopted because disease progression is often slow [5]. When intervention is required, surgical resection and systemic therapy with corticosteroids or chemotherapy may be used, depending on the underlying condition [5,18,19]. Consistent with these previous reports on NPA, our patient showed a characteristic CT pattern and an indolent clinical course.

Compared with pulmonary amyloidosis, pulmonary LCDD remains poorly defined, with most data coming from small case series and single case reports [6,[20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25]]. Previous studies have broadly categorized LCDD into nodular [[22], [23], [24], [25]] and diffuse pulmonary LCDD [6]. Across multiple case series, nodular pulmonary LCDD (NPLCDD) apparently shares many clinical features with NPA, most notably, a chest CT pattern of multiple nodules accompanied by numerous cysts, and frequent associations with underlying autoimmune diseases or MALT lymphoma [[22], [23], [24], [25]]. In NPLCDD, however, calcification of the nodules is uncommon, whereas vessels traversing the cysts have been repeatedly emphasized [21,22]. Consistent with these observations, our patient showed no calcification of the nodules and multiple cyst-traversing vessels. Although an overall indolent clinical course is often described, Bhargava et al. emphasized the need to distinguish NPLCDD from NPA because NPLCDD may carry a higher incidence of underlying plasma cell disorders and renal failure than NPA [20]. The clinical features of the diffuse pulmonary LCDD have been reported in a recent multicenter retrospective cohort of 32 patients [6]. The diffuse type is defined pathologically by light-chain deposition along the basement membranes of the alveoli, bronchi, and vascular walls. On CT imaging, diffuse cystic changes were present in all cases, with bronchiectasis in 77% and small nodules in 42% of them. In this cohort, from which systemic LCDD was excluded, Sjögren disease was uncommon, and most patients had an abnormal serum κ/λ free light chain ratio. A progressive decline in pulmonary function was characteristic; approximately 48% of the patients underwent bilateral lung transplantation at a median of 5 years after diagnosis, and the authors concluded that diffuse pulmonary LCDD had a poor prognosis. To summarize, clinicians should avoid assuming a favorable prognosis for pulmonary LCDD with multiple nodules and cysts. Closer follow-up than that for NPA is warranted, with an ongoing assessment for progressive respiratory impairment and potential plasma cell neoplasms or renal failure.

With respect to the pathological diagnosis, AL is diagnosed by demonstrating Congo red-positive deposits that exhibit apple-green birefringence under polarized light, followed by amyloid typing to confirm an immunoglobulin light chain origin. Immunohistochemistry is the first-line approach for amyloid typing in routine practice because of its wide availability [3]. When feasible, LMD-LC-MS/MS provides near-perfect diagnostic accuracy and is therefore highly useful for amyloid typing [26]. In contrast, LCDD presents as Congo red-negative, monotypic immunoglobulin light chain deposits confirmed by κ/λ restriction on immunofluorescence. Electron microscopy typically reveals granular, nonfibrillar deposits that provide diagnostic information [2]. Recently, LMD-LC-MS/MS has also been applied to LCDD for definitive protein identification in multiple published reports [11,12,21,23,25]. In practice, limited familiarity with the disease entity among clinicians and pathologists, together with restricted access to confirmatory testing, likely contributes to the underdiagnosis of LCDD [3,11,23]. In this case, an isolated NPA process was favored at the initial pathological review, but subsequent expert consultation using LMD-LC-MS/MS also identified a concomitant LCDD.

Previous studies have occasionally documented same-organ coexistence of amyloid and nonamyloid light chain deposits, supporting the recognition of the potential concurrence of AL and LCDD [7]. More recently, an increasing number of reports employing LMD-LC-MS/MS have provided a more detailed characterization of same-organ coexistence [11,12,21]. Said et al. reported a multicenter cohort of 37 patients in whom renal AL and LCDD coexisted [12]. Compared to patients with AL or LCDD alone, those with coexistence more frequently had concomitant multiple myeloma and had a higher risk of renal failure and death. In the same study, proteomic analysis suggested that both LCDD and amyloid deposits could originate from the same clone-derived single light-chain variable domain. These observations suggested that coexisting AL and LCDD have distinct clinicopathological features. Pulmonary coexistence of AL and LCDD is exceptionally rare; to our knowledge, only seven cases have been reported [[9], [10], [11],21]. All reported case of pulmonary coexistence presented with nodular disease, and three were associated with Sjögren disease or pulmonary MALT lymphoma. These observations are consistent with those of our case and align with the clinicoradiological features commonly described for NPA and NPLCDD. Charles et al. suggested that the apparent rarity of coexistence may reflect underdiagnosis and recommended heightened attention to Congo red-negative deposits suspicious for amyloid, consideration of LCDD, and confirmation by LMD-LC-MS/MS [11]. Some reported cases classified as NPA or NPLCDD possibly have an unrecognized simultaneous coexistence. Clinicians and pathologists should consider this possibility when evaluating patients with suspected pulmonary amyloidosis.

Conclusion

We report a case of an incidentally discovered diffuse pulmonary cysts with multiple nodules. Extensive work-up, including surgical lung biopsy with LMD-LC-MS/MS, established coexisting nodular pulmonary AL and LCDD associated with Sjögren disease and pulmonary MALT lymphoma. Clinicians should recognize these rare disorders in the differential diagnosis of diffuse cystic lung disease and consider careful pathological evaluation. Further accumulation of cases of coexisting nodular pulmonary AL and LCDD is warranted.
