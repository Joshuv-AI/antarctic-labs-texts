Dynamic illusory waves generated by relative motion in periodic displays
Authors: Ian M Thornton, Sunčica Zdravković, Anna Riga, Dejan Todorović
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe describe a new display involving what we are calling ‘dynamic illusory waves’. The display brings together a number of previously documented illusory effects and perceptual mechanisms to generate non-rigidly deforming phantom surfaces that take even experienced observers by surprise. There are only two physically drawn components to the display: a shape with a sinewave-modulated contour and a regular ‘pinstripe’ background grating. When the shape is superimposed on the grating, and relative motion is added, a third element immediately emerges. This is a dynamic illusory wave (diwa): a coherent, deforming illusory surface that often appears as a transparent layer partially occluding the two physically drawn components. The nature of the illusory wave is primarily a function of the spatial frequency ratio between the periodic patterning of the physical shape and the grating, but changing the relative speed, amplitude, or contrast of the drawn components also modulates the nature of the perceived surface. We discuss and make available an online interactive tool so that readers can further create and explore their own examples of such dynamic illusory waves.Keywords: illusory waves, visual illusions, subjective contours, modal completion, motion

The purpose of this brief report is to introduce and make available an online interactive tool where readers can create and manipulate what we are calling ‘dynamic illusory waves’. As illustrated in Movie 1, the appeal of the display is the manner in which a very salient and compelling illusory surface can be generated from the combination of two relatively simple physical components: a shape with a sinewave-modulated contour and a regular ‘pinstripe’ grating. The exact form taken by the dynamic illusory wave (diwa) is primarily determined by the spatial frequency ratio of the two physical elements. Local phase differences at points of intersection define a subjective contour, which in turn gives rise to an illusory surface. When relative motion is added – either by temporally modulating the borders of the physical shape or by drifting the grating – this appears to amplify the illusion, producing a non-rigidly deforming illusory wave, which typically appears as a transparent occluding surface.

The online interactive demo for creating and manipulating illusory waves can be accessed at https://maltacogsci.org/diwa/. A permanent archive of all resources can also be found on the OSF page associated with this paper at https://osf.io/6p3sc. Within the demo, the control panel makes it possible to adjust all aspects of the display, including the shape, appearance and motion of the illusory wave. As an example, Movie 2 explores the impact of modulating the spatial frequency ratio between the physical shape and the grating. This can be achieved either by scaling the target (as in the still image) or by changing the grating period. Both options are illustrated in the movie. The changing ‘harmonic’ structure of the generated wave is extremely regular and does not depend on absolute size, which can be verified by rescaling the browser window. Changing the border amplitude of the physical shape, the speed of relative motion, or the colour/contrast of the drawn elements, all have systematic effects on the nature of the illusory wave. Note in particular that even in the absence of relative motion, an illusory surface may still be visible. Motion thus appears to amplify the salience of the illusory wave and to supply the non-rigid dynamics, but is not crucial for generating the initial modal surface itself.

It seems likely that the emergence of these illusory waves can be explained in terms of previously documented effects and perceptual mechanisms. Our current thinking is as follows: 1) Points of intersection between the border of the physical shape and the vertical grating give rise to oriented local contrast edges; 2) These edges are spatially integrated to produce a single subjective contour that outlines a global illusory figure, a wave when in motion; 3) That illusory wave spatially overlaps with the physical shape to a large extent; 4) This overlap typically gives rise to a transparency effect, altering the perceived appearance of the physical components; 5) Motion updates the illusory wave, giving it a non-rigid, elastic appearance and also seems to amplify the transparency effect.

The modal surfaces that emerge in the illusory waves display are almost certainly variants of those described by Ehrenstein (1941/1987) and Kanizsa (1955/1987, 1976). The strength of these new variants may be due to several factors. Even in static displays, periodically repeated contrast changes at salient intersections can give rise to compelling illusory global contour effects, as reviewed by Todorović (2021). For example, see his Figure 2c, which uses regular checkerboard patterns. With the current illusory wave displays, the local contrast edges do not have a uniform orientation/position, but are modulated systematically due to the phase differences between the borders of the physical shape and the grating. Kaniza (1955) had noted that variations in the nature of intersections stopping along the same perimeter could enhance illusory effects, and this may be a factor here. For example, such variation could assist contour integration mechanisms in separating an (illusory) figure from the highly regular background elements (e.g., Machilsen & Wagemans, 2011) particularly once motion is added and there would be a strong ‘common fate’ component (Wertheimer, 1923).

The salience of the illusory waves is also enhanced by perceived changes to the surface properties of the display elements. For example, with white grating lines, as in Movies 1 and 2, the illusory wave appears slightly darker grey than the wider background. When the grating lines are black, this same surface appears lighter grey than the background, as illustrated in Movie 3. Such appearance changes are known to occur both for the Ehrenstein illusion (Hamada, 1987; Spillmann et al., 1984) and for Kanizsa figures (Kanizsa, 1955/1987; Zdravković, 2015). The current online demo provides a convenient method to further explore such appearance changes. For example, changing the colour, transparency or stroke weight of the physical display elements will also have consequences for the appearance of the illusory wave.

Finally, we note that the spatial overlap between the illusory wave and the physical shape typically also adds a sense of transparency to the modal surface – almost certainly enhanced by motion (Anstis et al., 2016) – which in turn affects the perceived colour of the physical elements (Hambrouck et al., 1987; Singh & Anderson, 2006). With some color/contrast settings, the illusory wave can give the appearance of being partially behind the physical shape. In these situations, amodal as well as modal completion would seem to be involved.

We are clearly only at the very start of our own exploration of these dynamic illusory waves. However, we wanted to share the online tool immediately in the hope that others will also find these displays intriguing. So far, everyone who has been shown the effect appears to have been genuinely surprised and impressed at the strength of the illusion. We note that while we typically choose to pronounce our acronym ‘diwa’ as DEE-vah – as if describing a standout performer – the Tagalog word ‘diwa’ (DEE-wah) coincidentally refers to the essence or ‘inner spirit’ associated with a concept, which also seems to fit our new displays quite nicely. Regardless of pronunciation, we hope that others will be encouraged to generate and ride their own sets of illusory waves, and we look forward to the future insights and discussions that might follow from this initial report.
