A chromatin-structure-guided framework for predictive and interpretable regulatory genomics
Authors: Bowei Ye, Lin Du, Min Chen, Yang Dai, Ao Ma, Jie Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChromatin organization shapes gene regulation by linking distal elements across megabase scales, yet most predictive genomics models still treat the genome as linear, without incorporating 3D structure. Hi-C provides genome-wide chromatin conformation information, but its contact maps are population-averaged, distance-biased, and noisy, obscuring biologically specific contacts. We present CHROME, a framework built on a self-avoiding polymer ensemble null model that identifies physically specific, nonrandom Hi-C contacts. By integrating these contacts into graph representations, CHROME enables efficient information transfer across spatially connected loci. It integrates sequence, chromatin accessibility, or pretrained embeddings into a graph attention architecture to predict cell-line-specific ChIP-seq profiles, improving performance over matched local encoder baselines. In a held-out cell line, CHROME demonstrates improved performance in selected settings, suggesting potential for cross-cell-type transfer. The resulting graph embeddings also enhance prediction on tissue-specific eQTL and ClinVar variant pathogenicity, compared with local sequence-based embeddings. Beyond predictive performance, CHROME provides interpretability through attention-derived neighbor-to-center contributions that reveal how spatially connected loci influence local regulatory activity over multi-megabase distances. Together, these results highlight the value of incorporating physically validated chromatin interactions for improving regulatory prediction and variant interpretation.

Background

The regulation of gene expression and DNA replication arises from a complex interplay among DNA sequence, chromatin state, and 3D genome architecture [1–4]. Advances in chromosome conformation capture technologies [5–7], particularly Hi-C [8, 9] data from the 4D Nucleome Project [10], have generated extensive maps of 3D genome organization across diverse tissues and cell types.

Over the past decade, deep learning has transformed our ability to predict and interpret epigenomic signals, with models increasingly learning predictive features directly from raw genomic inputs. Early efforts such as DeepSEA [11–13] demonstrated that the convolution neural network (CNN) [14] could predict a wide range of epigenetic profiles from DNA sequence alone. This was extended by variant-effect predictors like ExPecto [15], which estimated the functional impact of mutations. To capture distal regulatory interactions, models including Basenji [16] and the transformer-based Enformer [17] expanded receptive fields to enable long-range sequence modeling. Other sequence-based approaches, such as Sei [18], emphasized interpretability by grouping predictions into regulatory programs. Moving beyond sequence-only inputs, multimodal frameworks such as EPCOT [19] combined sequence with chromatin accessibility to improve predictive power. More recently, large-scale models have pushed generalization further: EVO2 [20] generates universal genomic embeddings, Borzoi [21] and GET [22] predict RNA-seq tracks, and AlphaGenome [23] integrates epigenetic, transcriptomic, and Hi-C data, extending the receptive field to 1 Mb. These models have demonstrated strong performance in capturing long-range regulatory dependencies using sequence-based or multimodal representations. Yet, scaling purely linear sequence-based architectures to multi-megabase contexts remains computationally demanding, which may limit their ability to capture very long-range dependencies in certain settings. This motivates interest in alternative modeling paradigms that can efficiently represent extended genomic contexts.

Despite these advances, most existing approaches still treat the genome as a linear sequence, overlooking the 3D chromatin organization revealed by Hi-C. Structural features such as topologically associating domains (TADs), compartments, and loops, often extending beyond 1 Mb, connect distal enhancers to promoters and align with cell-type-specific epigenetic profiles [24, 25], including ChIP-seq signals. Although Hi-C data capture these structural relationships, extracting biologically meaningful and physically specific, nonrandom contacts remains challenging because interaction frequencies are population-averaged, distance-biased, and affected by experimental noise [26, 27]. In contrast to the protein field, where topology has been extensively studied [28, 29] and graph-based models have proven effective [30–32], analogous graph frameworks for chromatin remain less developed. Existing methods such as GraphReg [33] incorporate Hi-C contacts into graph structures but restrict edges to interactions within 2 Mb, which may limit the extent of long-range dependencies captured under certain settings.

These limitations motivated the development of a deep-learning-driven, chromatin-structure-guided framework that leverages the spatial information from Hi-C to integrate biologically meaningful contacts across multi-megabase scales for diverse predictive tasks. To extract such contacts from Hi-C data, we employed CHROMATIX [34], which identifies physically specific, nonrandom contacts using a self-avoiding polymer ensemble null model. Building on these validated nonrandom contacts, we developed CHROME (CHROMatin-structure–guided graph Embedding framework), which incorporates nonrandom contacts into a unified graph representation that enables efficient information flow across distal regulatory elements. Although broadly applicable to various genomic prediction tasks, we demonstrate CHROME using ChIP-seq signal prediction as a representative case. CHROME introduces four key innovations: (i) Hi-C contact filtration using CHROMATIX [34], retaining only physically specific, nonrandom interactions; (ii) an extended receptive field of up to 4 Mb to capture long-range regulatory influences; (iii) flexible locus-level embeddings accommodating raw sequence, chromatin accessibility, or pretrained representations; and (iv) intrinsic interpretability, with graph attention layers that assign neighbor-to-center contribution weights.

Applied to GM12878, IMR-90, and K562, CHROME consistently improved performance over matched local encoder-only baselines for raw sequence, DNase + sequence, and Evo2 embeddings. In all cases, locus-level representations from baseline encoders were contextualized by graph attention over nonrandom contacts. On an unseen cell line (HepG2, chr9) not included during training, CHROME showed improved performance in a held-out setting, suggesting potential for cross-cell-type transfer under limited conditions. Beyond regulatory prediction, graph embeddings from trained CHROME models improved tissue-specific expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) classification (mapping GM12878\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\rightarrow $\end{document}lymphoblastoid [blood] and IMR-90\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\rightarrow $\end{document}fibroblast [lung] tissues from GTEx [35]) and ClinVar variant pathogenicity prediction [36]. Because CHROME is trained on regulatory profiles, its attention-derived contribution maps provide direct interpretability, revealing how spatially connected, nonrandom loci influence local regulatory activity. These contributions remain detectable across multi-megabase distances, with ablation tests confirming a clear preference for nonrandom over random Hi-C contacts. Together, our findings show that physically validated chromatin contacts are functionally informative for gene regulation, highlighting the value of incorporating 3D genome structure for improving regulatory prediction and variant interpretation.

Material and methods

Overview of the CHROME framework

CHROME is a chromatin-structure-guided graph embedding framework that integrates DNA sequence, chromatin accessibility, or pretrained sequence embeddings with CHROMATIX-validated [34] nonrandom chromatin contacts derived from a self-avoiding polymer ensemble null model (Fig. 1). For each ChIP-seq peak, CHROME constructs a graph representation that captures spatially interacting genomic loci, enabling information flow across both proximal and distal nonrandom contacts. Details of the CHROMATIX procedure used to identify nonrandom contacts, along with additional dataset descriptions, hyperparameters, and training details, are provided in Supplementary Sections S1–S9.

Chromatin-structure-guided graph framework (CHROME): (a) CHROMATIX validates Hi-C contacts by using a self-avoiding polymer ensemble model to generate random three-dimensional chromatin conformations within a confined nuclear volume, forming a physical null distribution of contact probabilities against which measured Hi-C contact frequencies are statistically compared to identify physically specific, nonrandom contacts; (b) signal-centered graphs are constructed by overlaying nonrandom contact maps with 5 kb loci containing ChIP-seq signals, using each signal locus as a graph center and connecting it to direct nonrandom chromatin-contact neighbors, with overlapping subgraphs allowed when nearby signals share neighbors; and (c) CHROME encodes each 5 kb locus using a sequence feature encoder, including a 1D CNN for one-hot DNA sequence or DNA sequence with DNase values and an MLP for Evo2 embeddings, propagates encoded nodes through two graph attention layers for attention-based feature aggregation and embedding update, and predicts 751 cell-line-specific ChIP-seq profiles.

Key experimental settings are summarized as follows. The genome was partitioned into nonoverlapping 5 kb loci, and for each ChIP-seq peak, a signal-centered subgraph was constructed using CHROMATIX-validated nonrandom Hi-C contacts. Locus features were represented using (i) DNA sequence, (ii) DNA sequence with DNase-seq accessibility, or (iii) Evo2 embeddings, and encoded into a shared embedding space using a 1D CNN (sequence-based inputs) or a multilayer perceptron (MLP) (Evo2). Graph propagation over nonrandom contacts was performed using two graph attention (GAT) layers with multi-head attention, residual connections, normalization, and dropout, followed by pooling of neighbor context and concatenation with the center embedding. Model training followed a chromosome-wise split, with chromosomes 1–7, 10–22, and X used for training, chromosome 8 for validation, and chromosome 9 for testing. A two-stage training strategy was used, where locus encoders were first pretrained on center nodes and then fine-tuned jointly with graph attention layers. The model was trained as a multi-label classifier to predict 751 ChIP-seq profiles using a masked binary cross-entropy objective optimized with stochastic gradient-based methods. Full implementation details are provided in Supplementary Sections S2–S5.

Hi-C data and identification of nonrandom chromatin contacts

We used bulk Hi-C data for GM12878 (4DNES3JX38V5), IMR-90 (4DNES1ZEJNRU), K562 (4DNESWST3UBH), and HepG2 (4DNFICSTCJQZ; chromosome 9 only), obtained from the 4D Nucleome Consortium [10, 37].

To identify physically specific chromatin contacts, we adopted a self-avoiding polymer ensemble as a physics-based null model following CHROMATIX [34]. In this framework, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $5\times 10^{5}$\end{document} polymer conformations are simulated under nuclear confinement to estimate contact probabilities arising from random folding. Unlike approaches that derive background expectations directly from Hi-C data, this null model is independent of experimental measurements and captures fundamental biophysical constraints, including polymer connectivity, excluded volume, and spatial confinement. Observed Hi-C interaction frequencies are compared against this null distribution, and interactions passing a false discovery rate threshold of 0.05 are retained as physically specific, nonrandom contacts.

Chromatin is partitioned into nonoverlapping 5 kb loci. At this resolution, modeling chains of length 4 Mb (800 loci) captures the majority of TADs and enhancer–promoter hubs while remaining computationally tractable [38]. We therefore use 4 Mb as an upper bound on the receptive field considered by CHROME (Fig. 1a). Additional summaries are shown in Figure S1.

Graph neural network framework for ChIP-seq prediction

In total, 751 ENCODE assays [39] were used, including 539 profiles for GM12878, 168 for IMR-90, and 44 for K562. Model generalization was evaluated on an unseen cell line, HepG2, using 324 additional profiles from chromosome 9 (Figure S4).

Graph attention propagation over nonrandom contacts

Information is propagated over nonrandom chromatin contacts using two graph attention (GAT) layers [41]. At each layer, neighboring loci contribute to the updated representation of a node with learned attention weights, enabling CHROME to prioritize informative distal interactions. Multi-head attention, residual connections, normalization, and dropout are used to stabilize training. The learned attention coefficients provide an intrinsic measure of the contribution of each neighboring locus to the center node. Detailed layer definitions and mathematical formulations are provided in Supplementary Section S4.

Two-stage training strategy

Training proceeds in two stages. In the first stage, locus encoders are pretrained on center nodes only, without graph context, until validation performance saturates. In the second stage, the pretrained encoders are applied to all nodes in each signal-centered subgraph and fine-tuned jointly with the graph attention layers.

Variant analyses with CHROME embeddings

eQTL prediction

To evaluate whether CHROME embeddings capture regulatory information relevant to eQTL interpretation, we aligned cell lines to GTEx tissues following Borzoi [21]: GM12878 to EBV-transformed lymphocytes (blood) and IMR-90 to cultured fibroblasts (lung). The same eQTL datasets and tissue restrictions as in Borzoi were used [35].

For each candidate eQTL, two loci were defined: a 5 kb TSS-centered locus for the target gene and a 5 kb variant-centerd locus together with its nonrandom chromatin contacts in the corresponding cell line. The TSS locus was encoded using the pretrained locus encoder to obtain a TSS embedding \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{TSS}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$\end{document}, while the variant-centered subgraph was processed by the CHROME graph attention network to yield a variant embedding \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{var}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$\end{document}. The two embeddings were concatenated,

and used as input to an MLP classifier to distinguish eQTLs from matched non-eQTL controls.

ClinVar variant pathogenicity classification

We further evaluated CHROME on ClinVar variant pathogenicity [36], using variants annotated as benign or pathogenic, including both coding and noncoding sites. Because ClinVar does not provide tissue-specific annotations, we constructed a consensus chromatin context by intersecting nonrandom contact maps from GM12878, IMR-90, and K562, and averaging DNase accessibility across these cell lines.

For each variant, a 5 kb variant-centered locus and its nonrandomly interacting neighbors were extracted to form a variant-centered subgraph. Locus-level features were encoded using the corresponding locus encoder and propagated through the CHROME graph attention network to obtain a variant embedding \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{ClinVar}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$\end{document}. This embedding was used for binary classification of benign versus pathogenic variants. Details of classifier training and data splits are provided in Supplementary Section S6.

RESULTS

Accurate prediction of ChiP-Seq profiles

We evaluated CHROME on cell-line-specific transcription factor and histone modification prediction, testing whether physically specific, nonrandom chromatin neighborhoods provide predictive signal beyond local sequence-based encoders.

Across 751 ENCODE profiles in GM12878, K562, and IMR-90, CHROME consistently improved performance over matched local-only baselines that used identical input features but no neighborhood aggregation. For Seq and DNase inputs, CHROME was compared with EPCOT-style CNN baselines, and for Evo2 inputs to a matched MLP baseline. Per-class analyses showed systematic AUPR gains when nonrandom contacts were included (Fig. 2a). Seq and Evo2 achieved comparable performance ranges, consistent with reports that foundation genomic models do not reliably surpass simpler encodings for regulatory prediction [42], whereas DNase-based models achieved substantially higher absolute performance due to the added cell-line-specific accessibility signal [43] (Figure S2).

Contribution landscape of CHROME

Beyond predictive performance, CHROME provides mechanistic insight into how physically specific, nonrandom chromatin contacts influence regulatory activity. Using an attention mechanism [41, 44], CHROME assigns contribution weights that quantify each neighbor’s influence on the center locus, enabling interpretable assessment of 3D regulatory effects (Fig. 3a).

To test whether these contributions preferentially reflect true nonrandom contacts, we extended the ablation analysis from Fig. 2e by combining real nonrandom contacts with degree- and distance-matched random Hi-C contacts, doubling the edge set. Using the trained CHROME (DNase) model, we ranked all neighbors for each center locus on chromosome 9. The resulting contribution distributions were strongly skewed toward real nonrandom contacts, with most centers assigning more than half of their total weight to true contacts (Fig. 3b). Contributions from nonrandom contacts decayed with genomic distance but remained detectable at 1–2 Mb and extended weakly to 3–4 Mb (Fig. 3c), indicating that CHROME captures long-range regulatory influences beyond local proximity.

To relate CHROME contributions to established enhancer–promoter frameworks, we compared neighbor-to-center contribution weights with Activity-by-Contact (ABC) scores [45]. ABC links were lifted from hg19 to hg38 using UCSC liftOver [46] and assigned to 5 kb loci to match CHROME’s resolution. On chromosome 9, CHROME contributions in GM12878 and K562 generally increased across ABC-score deciles (threshold \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\geq $\end{document} 0.015), but notable discrepancies were observed, with some loci receiving high CHROME weight despite modest ABC scores and vice versa (Fig. 3d). While genome-wide correlations were modest, CHROME recapitulated many ABC-supported interactions and highlighted additional neighbor-to-center relationships not captured by activity–contact heuristics alone (Table S1).

Prediction and interpretation of tissue-specific eQTLs

We next assessed whether regulatory representations learned by CHROME from ChIP-seq prediction transfer to tissue-specific regulatory variation, using GTEx eQTLs [35] as an independent benchmark. Following Borzoi [21], we mapped GM12878 to blood and IMR-90 to lung to approximate relevant regulatory contexts. CHROME models trained on ChIP-seq prediction were repurposed as embedding generators and applied to the same curated eQTL datasets, which include matched positive and negative controls.

For each candidate eQTL, a graph embedding was generated for the variant-centered 5 kb locus using CHROME, while a local embedding was generated for the corresponding TSS-centered 5 kb locus. The two embeddings were concatenated and classified using an MLP to predict whether the variant represented a true eQTL. Baseline CNN or MLP models were evaluated using the same framework, ensuring a direct comparison of CHROME-derived embeddings against local-only representations.

Across five-fold cross-validation, CHROME-based embeddings consistently improved performance relative to baseline embeddings in precision–recall space (Fig. 4a). This improvement was robust for DNase- and sequence-based inputs, whereas Evo2 performed less consistently, particularly in lung. Summary F1 scores with 95% confidence intervals confirmed that CHROME (DNase) and CHROME (Seq) achieved the strongest overall performance (Fig. 4b). ROC and AUROC analyses showed concordant trends (Figure S9A–C), and top-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $k$\end{document} precision and calibration analyses further demonstrated improved ranking performance and well-calibrated probabilities for CHROME relative to baselines (Figure S9D–E). Together, these results indicate that nonrandom chromatin context learned during regulatory prediction transfers effectively to gene–variant association, particularly when cell-line-specific features such as DNase are included.

At the locus level, CHROME contribution maps reveal how distal neighbors mediate eQTL effects through their connections to the TSS. Previous work has suggested that many-body chromatin interactions contribute to gene regulation [47–50]. Consistent with this, CHROME (DNase) highlights both local and distal regulatory influences. For PNPLA7 in blood (Fig. 4c), an eQTL variant lies within a nonrandom neighbor that contributes strongly to the TSS and overlaps DNase hypersensitivity and active histone marks. In contrast, for CCZ1B (Fig. 4d), both local and distal eQTLs reside in neighbors receiving strong contribution weights despite minimal overlap with canonical chromatin features. These examples illustrate how CHROME identifies regulatory loci both supported by epigenetic signals and not readily explained by conventional annotations. Finally, we explored whether CHROME provides insight into the directionality of eQTL effects. Predicting whether variants increase or decrease gene expression remains challenging for existing models [15, 17, 51–53], even with personalized genomes [54]. Although CHROME was not trained to predict quantitative expression changes, its contribution framework enables comparison between predicted shifts in expression-associated ChIP-seq profiles and GTEx eQTL slopes. Focusing on four transcription-associated marks (H3K27ac, H3K4me3, POLR2A, H3K36me3), we observed that sign agreement between CHROME (DNase) predictions and eQTL slopes exceeded baseline expectations in blood for H3K4me3 and, when full neighborhoods were included, for POLR2A (Fig. 4e). In lung, overall agreement remained near baseline, but stratified analyses revealed increasing agreement with larger absolute eQTL effect sizes, particularly for H3K27ac and POLR2A (Fig. 4f). These results suggest that incorporating nonrandom chromatin context can provide partial insight into eQTL directionality, especially for variants with stronger regulatory effects.

Variant pathogenicity prediction and non-coding regulatory disruption

We evaluated whether regulatory information learned by CHROME transfers to variant pathogenicity classification using curated ClinVar datasets [36]. Because ClinVar lacks tissue-specific annotations, we constructed a consensus input representation by averaging DNase accessibility across GM12878, K562, and IMR-90 and defining nonrandom Hi-C contacts as the intersection across these cell lines. Each variant was centered within its 5 kb locus, and CHROME embeddings were generated by aggregating information from consensus nonrandom neighbors. Matched CNN or MLP baselines encoded the variant locus in isolation. All embeddings were classified using an identical MLP, directly testing whether chromatin-contextual information improves pathogenicity prediction.

Across five-fold validation, CHROME embeddings consistently improved performance relative to baseline encoders (Fig. 5a–c). Gains were largest for sequence- and Evo2-based inputs, with improved precision–recall performance and better calibration reflected by lower or comparable Brier scores. The violin plots further showed clearer separation between benign and pathogenic variants under CHROME. DNase-based models performed substantially worse overall, likely due to loss of cell line specificity when DNase accessibility was averaged [39, 55]. Nevertheless, incorporating consensus nonrandom contacts improved performance across all feature types, including DNase, demonstrating that nonrandom chromatin structure provides a robust and transferable signal. Discussion

Most deep learning models in regulatory genomics treat the genome as a linear sequence, despite the fundamentally 3D nature of gene regulation. Although Hi-C provides genome-wide contact maps, these data are population-averaged, distance-biased, and noisy, complicating the identification of biologically specific interactions. CHROME addresses this limitation by building on CHROMATIX [34], which identifies physically specific, nonrandom chromatin contacts using a self-avoiding polymer ensemble null model. Recent studies have shown that incorporating such physically constrained interactions improves the mapping of noncoding variants to their regulatory targets [57, 58]. By using these validated contacts as a structural scaffold, CHROME propagates regulatory information along meaningful spatial connections.

CHROME’s graph-based formulation allows regulatory signals to flow across spatially linked loci, extending the effective receptive field to 4 Mb without increasing sequence context or model depth. Across GM12878, K562, and IMR-90, CHROME consistently improved performance over matched local encoder-only baselines across all input types—including raw sequence, DNase accessibility, and Evo2 embeddings. In a held-out HepG2 setting (chr9), CHROME showed modality-dependent transfer performance, with stronger transfer observed for models incorporating cell-line-specific signals. Ablation analyses confirmed that these gains arise from physically specific contact topology: replacing nonrandom contacts with degree- or distance-matched random interactions substantially reduced performance, demonstrating that CHROME leverages biologically meaningful structure rather than graph size alone.

Beyond ChIP-seq prediction, CHROME embeddings improved downstream analyses. In tissue-specific eQTL classification, CHROME improved performance relative to local-only baselines when mapping GM12878 to blood and IMR-90 to lung tissues from GTEx [35], and showed partial concordance with GTEx slope directionality by comparing predicted mutant-versus-wild-type ChIP-seq changes. For ClinVar variant pathogenicity [36], CHROME again exceeded CNN and MLP baselines, with noncoding pathogenic variants enriched both locally and distally among loci with large predicted regulatory perturbations. These results align with evidence that disease variants frequently act through regulatory mechanisms [59, 60] and highlight the amplifying role of nonrandom chromatin contacts in shaping functional outcomes.

A key advantage of CHROME is mechanistic interpretability. Graph attention layers assign neighbor-to-center contribution weights that trace how specific spatial contacts influence regulatory predictions. Contribution landscapes reveal strong local effects alongside detectable long-range influences spanning 1–2 Mb, with weaker signals persisting out to 3–4 Mb, consistent with reports of multi-megabase regulatory domains [61, 62]. By combining physics-based validation of chromatin interactions with attention-based attribution, CHROME directly links 3D genome organization to regulatory function.

Conceptually, CHROME integrates biophysical modeling, graph learning, and large-scale genomics. It complements sequence-centric frameworks such as Enformer [17] and multimodal architectures such as EPCOT [19], which extend receptive fields along linear sequence but do not explicitly encode spatial topology, and augments pretrained sequence models such as Evo2 [20], which capture molecular-scale features but remain blind to higher-order chromatin organization. By explicitly modeling regulatory geometry through physically constrained graph representations, CHROME provides a scalable foundation for structure-aware regulatory modeling, highlighting the value of incorporating physically validated chromatin contacts. Several practical considerations remain: CHROME relies on high-quality, cell-line-specific Hi-C data, which may limit applicability where such data are unavailable; the current graph construction uses only direct nonrandom neighbors, while higher-order interactions may provide additional information; and chromatin contacts represent proxies for potential long-range regulatory interactions rather than definitive regulatory links, suggesting that integrating alternative connections such as co-accessibility or transcription factor co-binding may further improve information propagation and generalizability.

Conclusion

CHROME introduces a chromatin-structure-guided deep learning framework that integrates physically specific, nonrandom Hi-C contacts to enable predictive and interpretable regulatory genomics. By combining CHROMATIX-derived [34] contact validation with graph attention modeling, CHROME captures long-range regulatory dependencies beyond the reach of linear sequence models. This results in improved ChIP-seq prediction across multiple cell lines, input-dependent performance in a held-out cell type (HepG2, chr9), and enhanced performance in downstream eQTL classification and ClinVar pathogenicity prediction.

CHROME’s attention-derived contribution maps provide mechanistic insight into how proximal and distal 3D contacts shape local regulatory activity and mediate the functional impact of noncoding variants. These findings demonstrate that physically validated chromatin interactions encode essential regulatory information rather than acting as passive structural features. As high-quality Hi-C data expand across tissues, developmental stages, and disease contexts [63], CHROME can be extended to incorporate dynamic, cell-state-specific, and single-cell chromatin structure [64, 65]. Overall, CHROME provides a physics-informed, structure-aware framework for predictive and interpretable regulatory genomics.
