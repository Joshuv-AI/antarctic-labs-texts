The Prevalence of Toxoplasmosis Antibodies Among Pregnant Women Attending Antenatal Care Services in Urban and Rural Health Facilities in Three Regions of Burkina Faso: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Achille Sindimbasba Nikièma, Armel Moumouni Sanou, Mamadou Ouattara, Barnabé Relwendé Yaméogo, Ad Bafa Ibrahim Ouattara, Mariam Seynou, Djara Konaté, Nina Geneviève Mathuola Ouattara, Dieudonné Ilboudo, Ina Marie Angèle Traoré, Henri Gauthier Ouédraogo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsCongenital toxoplasmosis can cause severe diseases to the fetus and newborns or abortion, and is associated with significant morbidity and mortality. In Burkina Faso, while seroprevalence data for toxoplasmosis are available for urban areas, such data are scarce for rural areas. This study aims to determine the seroprevalence of toxoplasmosis in pregnant women attending antenatal services in primary health facilities in rural and urban areas.MethodsA cross‐sectional study was conducted between July 2022 and June 2023 in health facilities located in the Cascades (n = 2), Hauts‐Bassins (n = 2), and Sud‐Ouest (n = 2) regions. All pregnant women attending the health facilities for antenatal care who consented to participate in the study were enrolled. Venous blood samples were collected from each pregnant woman, and serum was analyzed for IgG and IgM antibodies using a rapid diagnostic test. Seroprevalences of IgM and IgG were determined. The association between seroprevalence and socio‐demographic characteristics was assessed by bivariate and multivariate analyses.ResultsA total of 2509 pregnant women were enrolled and screened. Their mean age was 25.71 years, and the overall seroprevalence (IgM‐positive and or IgG‐positive) was 29.33% (736) (95% CI: 27.56–31.16). Of the 736 women tested positive for both antibodies, 27 were seropositive for IgM antibody; 703 for IgG antibody and 6 for both antibody (Ig M; Ig G). No statistically differences were observed in the seroprevalence according to age, place of residence (rural, urban), and educational level of the participant (p = 0.002).ConclusionThe results of our study indicate a high seroprevalence of toxoplasmosis among pregnant women, with no statistically difference between rural and urban areas. Some women acquired the infection during pregnancy, underscoring the risk of congenital toxoplasmosis. Health authorities should integrate toxoplasmosis diagnosis into a free antenatal screening program to facilitate the detection of toxoplasmosis during pregnancy and prevent congenital toxoplasmosis.Keywords: congenital toxoplasmosis, pregnancy, primary health facilities, seroprevalence

Background

Toxoplasmosis is an endemic zoonotic disease caused by an intracellular protozoan, Toxoplasma gondii. The parasite is worldwide distributed and can infect humans and animals [1]. Humans and numerous species of mammals and birds are intermediate hosts; cats and other Felidae are the definitive hosts. In other mammals considered as intermediate hosts, the most receptive species in farm animals including sheep, goats, pigs, cattle [2].

Toxoplasmosis is a public health concern. It is estimated that one‐third of human worldwide population is infected by Toxoplasma gondii [3, 4]. The seroprevalence of toxoplasmosis varied between countries throughout the world, and between regions and communities in the same country [1, 3]. It is assumed that the seroprevalence is influenced by some factors like socioeconomic and environmental conditions, and cultural habits [5].

Infection of humans is mainly through three routes; with oocysts shed by cats contained in food, water, or soil, by eating undercooked or raw meat containing bradyzoites and tachyzoites, and vertical transmission from mother to fetus [5, 6]. Materno fetal transmission or congenital transmission, occurs during pregnancy and involves tachyzoites passing from the mother to the fetus via the placenta, which occurs when the infected mother acquires a new infection just before or at the start of pregnancy or by reactivation of latent infection [7, 8, 9]. The risk of congenital toxoplasmosis is lowest when a pregnant woman acquires primary infection in early pregnancy. Conversely, this risk is highest when the infection is acquired during the third trimester [10, 11]. Acute Toxoplasma infection acquired during pregnancy can result in severe disease in the fetus, including intrauterine growth retardation, and in the newborn, including wide‐ranging symptoms or abortion [8, 12]. To prevent congenital toxoplasmosis, accurate diagnoses are needed during pregnancy. This is not always readily available in low‐income countries [7].

The state of maternal and child health in Burkina Faso is a cause for concern. Maternal mortality rates remain elevated, with a ratio of 330 per 100,000 live births. Moreover, the rates of neonatal and infant mortality are also considerable, at 81 per thousand [13]. Infections with microorganisms constitute a significant cause of maternal and fetal mortality. To prevent the transmission of infection caused by virus and parasites that occurs during pregnancy, the Ministry of Health through the direction of family health, has made the diagnosis of infections like Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV), and distribution of Long‐Lasting Insecticide mosquito bed nets to prevent malaria free of charge in public health facilities everywhere in the country to pregnant women. Nevertheless, toxoplasmosis, which is also one of the most significant infectious diseases during pregnancy, is not included in the list of free prenatal diagnoses for pregnant women. Diagnosis of toxoplasmosis was primarily made by serological tool [7]. During pregnancy, that diagnosis is useful for determining whether the infection is present and also for evaluating the risk of congenital toxoplasmosis. The data on toxoplasmosis in Burkina Faso are predominantly derived from urban settings, particularly the cities of Bobo‐Dioulasso and Ouagadougou. To date, few studies have examined the seroprevalence of infection among pregnant women in rural and urban areas using primary health care facilities. Having reliable data allows health authorities to develop strategies to prevent congenital toxoplasmosis.

This present study forms part of a larger investigation into parasitic, bacterial, and viral infections among pregnant women attending primary healthcare facilities for antenatal consultations in three health regions of Burkina Faso. This paper presents the seroprevalence of Toxoplasma gondii antibodies and identifies factors associated with infection in pregnant women seen for antenatal care in six primary healthcare centers.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This cross‐sectional study was conducted between July 2022 and June 2023 at the following six health facilities: Health and Social Promotion Center of Lokosso (a rural area), Medical Center of Gaoua (an urban area) in the Sud‐Ouest region, Medical Center of Bama (a rural area) and Medical Center of Sakaby (an urban area) in Hauts‐Bassins, Medical Center of Banfora (an urban area) and Health and Social Promotion Center of Diarbakoko (a rural area) in the Cascades Region. These primary healthcare facilities serve as the initial point of contact for pregnant women seeking antenatal care.

Sample Size

The sample size was determined using a power of 80%, a confidence level of 80%, a prevalence of HBsAg in pregnant women of 19.5% in Burkina Faso and a tolerated margin of error of 2% according to the following formula N =Z1−α/2p(1−p)d2 where N = required sample size, Z = 95% standard deviation, confidence interval = 1.96, p = prevalence of HBsAg in pregnant women, d = tolerable margin of error, 2% (0.02).

The sample size to be included was estimated to be 2512 pregnant women.

The estimated sample size for the study was 2512 pregnant women. The sample size to be included by health centers and by region was calculated according to the number of women seen in antenatal care in 2021, according to the data available in the “Entrepôt des données sanitaires du Burkina Faso (ENDOS BF).” The distribution of pregnant women by health facility is shown in Table 1.

Data and Blood Sample Collection

The study population consisted of pregnant women who were recruited at their first antenatal care visit at one of the six Health and Social Promotion Canters' facilities participating in the study. Once eligibility had been established and consent obtained, a range of socio‐demographic information was gathered, together with details of gynaeco‐obstetrical history. A structured questionnaire was administered by a healthcare professional trained for the research purpose (health nurses) during an interview with the participants. A blood sample was also taken. For each consenting pregnant woman, 8 mL of whole blood was collected by venipuncture from the elbow using a dry tube and an EDTA tube (anticoagulant). The samples were transported to the laboratory. Samples were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 5 min and the serum/plasma was aliquoted into two well‐labeled tubes. The aliquot was stored at −20°C before undergoing a serological test.

Determining Anti IgM and Anti IgG Toxoplasma Antibodies

A serological diagnosis of toxoplasmosis was made using a commercially available test, namely the CTK Biotech, Inc. The OnSite Toxo IgG/IgM Combo Rapid Test kit (13855 Stowe Drive, Poway, CA 92064, USA) is a diagnostic tool that is capable of simultaneously detecting and differentiating IgG and IgM antibodies to Toxoplasma gondii in humans. Serum obtained from each pregnant woman was used to detect IgG and IgM, in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions.

Statistical Analysis

R version 4.4.0 (2024‐04‐24 ucrt) software was used to perform statistical analysis. We used the Chi‐Square test and Fisher's exact test for comparison of the frequencies of seroprevalence of IgG and IgM anti‐toxoplasma antibodies among different age groups, study sites, and urban and rural areas. The bivariate analyses that did not meet the Chi‐Square assumptions were analyzed using Fisher's test. The significance threshold for the analyses was set at p ≤ 0.05. Logistic regression was used to determine associations between Toxoplasma IgM and IgG antibodies and participants' characteristics. We constructed multivariate and univariate regression models simultaneously from the study's independent variables with a probability of p less than or equal to 0.05. This highlighted a potential factor associated with the occurrence of anti‐Toxoplasma antibodies. We used the “gtsummary” package for making these analyses.

Ethical Considerations

The study was approved by the Institutional Health Research Ethics Committee (number 016‐2022/CEIRES). Participants who aged over 18 years signed a freely and fully informed consent form before enrollment and those who age was under 18 years of age, their assent was obtained and also the consent from their parents/guardians. To ensure data anonymity, a unique identification number was assigned to each participant.

Results

Participant Characteristics

A total of 2512 pregnant women who were attending their first antenatal care appointment were enrolled in the six primary health care facilities. Of these, sera from 2509 pregnant women were tested for IgG and IgM toxoplasmosis antibodies. The remaining three participants lacked sufficient serum for screening for toxoplasmosis. The mean age of the participants was 25.71 years (standard deviation ± 6.45), with an age range of 16–49 years (Table 2). The distribution of participants by study site is presented in Table 2. With regard to the participants' place of residence, 61.25% (1537) were from urban areas, with a significant difference observed (< 0.001). The age group with the highest representation was that of 20–29 years old, comprising 1335 participants (53.20%). The majority of participants were illiterate (61.69%) and housewives (84.97%). The participants were enrolled at all trimesters of pregnancy, with 1033 (41.17%) having initiated antenatal care for the first time during the third trimester.

Seroprevalence of Toxoplasmosis in Participants

Among the total of 2509 sera from pregnant women who tested for the presence of IgG and IgM antibodies anti‐Toxoplasma gondii, 736 were found to be seropositive for IgG and/or IgM, resulting in an overall seroprevalence of 29.33% (95% CI: 27.57–31.16). From these screened pregnant women, 27 (1.08%) were IgM seropositive, 6 (0.24%) were seropositive for IgG and IgM, and 703 (28.02%) were seropositive for IgG only. The description of 27 pregnant women with IgM seropositivity revealed that 12 were in the first trimester of pregnancy, 11 were in the second trimester, and 4 were in the third trimester. There was a statistically significant difference observed in the seroprevalence of toxoplasmosis according to age group (p = 0.01), level of education (p = 0.028), place of residence, and study site among the participants (Table 3). However, no statistically significant differences were observed in seroprevalence according to study region (p = 0.789), occupation (p = 0.304) and age of pregnancy (p = 0.867). Indeed, the seroprevalence was found to be higher in the Sud‐Ouest region than in the other regions, and among those employed in farmer laborer (Table 3).

Discussion

The epidemiological situation of toxoplasmosis in Burkina Faso remains poorly documented, with an even greater dearth of data concerning pregnant women. The overall seroprevalence of toxoplasmosis among pregnant women attending antenatal care in primary health centers in rural and urban areas of the Hauts‐Bassins, Cascades, and Sud‐Ouest regions was found to be high in this study (29.33%) compared to the average global seroprevalence rate worldwide (25.7%) [14]. The seroprevalence found in our study was comparable to that observed by Bamba and colleagues in the city of Bobo‐Dioulasso (31.1%) and those reported in Ouagadougou by Ouermi and colleagues (27.2%) and Simpore and colleagues (31%) [15, 16, 17]. Previous studies identified environmental factors, dietary habits, and the presence of cats in the home as potential contributors to the high seroprevalence [15, 16, 17]. The factors associated with this seroprevalence were not investigated in this study; therefore, further large‐scale studies are needed in urban and rural areas to identify the factors associated with toxoplasmosis in pregnant women. The seroprevalence observed in this study was also lower than those in other countries, including Ethiopia (35.6%), Democratic Republic of Congo (80.3%), and Benin (54.6%) but higher than in Zambia (4.7%) [18, 19, 20, 21].

No statistically difference was found in the seroprevalence of pregnant women in urban and rural (p = 0.02). To the authors' best knowledge, this is the inaugural report on data ascertaining the prevalence of toxoplasmosis antibodies (IgM, IgG) in pregnant women residing in rural areas within primary healthcare facilities in Burkina Faso. The study did not reveal a difference between seroprevalence of toxoplasmosis in rural and urban areas, whilst this seroprevalence was observed to be higher in urban areas (31.6%). Previous studies have already documented variations in seroprevalence according to geographical origin within the same country or between different countries [22, 23]. The present finding of seroprevalence in a rural area cannot be generalized to all rural areas of Burkina Faso. However, the data offer insights into the epidemiology of toxoplasmosis in rural areas and in outlying facilities, which represent the initial point of contact for pregnant women during antenatal consultations in the study regions. The seroprevalence of toxoplasmosis among pregnant women in rural areas, which was 25.8% was lower than that reported in rural areas in Benin (36.1%) [24].

In the context of Burkina Faso, a systematic prescription of the examination of toxoplasmosis is required for pregnant women in urban areas. However, the cost of this examination represents a significant barrier to accessibility for pregnant women. In rural areas, the lack of laboratories capable of performing the toxoplasmosis examination means that this examination is not systematically prescribed. A percentage of 28.02% of pregnant women tested positive for IgG antibodies, indicating a lack of immunity against toxoplasmosis in the remaining pregnant women. The aforementioned seronegative pregnant women are at significant risk of infection during pregnancy, which presents a potential for congenital toxoplasmosis to occur. It is thus recommended that the health authorities implement a program of seronegative surveillance of pregnant women and an awareness campaign during antenatal care, with the objective of educating pregnant women on the means of preventing infection during pregnancy. Additionally, pregnant women were found IgM seropositive during pregnancy. The presence of IgM antibodies during pregnancy indicates that the infection is recent and poses a risk of congenital toxoplasmosis. The risk of congenital toxoplasmosis was increased when primary‐infection occurred during the third trimester of pregnancy [9]. During this screening, four women who found positive to IgM were in the third trimester, which was identified as a high‐risk trimester for the transmission of congenital toxoplasmosis. The results of positivity of IgM were communicated to the head of nurses in the health facilities concerned, who referred the women to medical doctor for management. This finding of positive IgM in pregnant women is different of those of previous studies in Burkina Faso, in Benin, which did not identify any IgM seropositive cases [15, 24]. This seroprevalence of IgM antibody found in our study is lower than that found in Ethiopia [25].

A primary infection acquired during pregnancy can result in congenital toxoplasmosis, which constitutes the most part of the disease burden in humans [4]. Congenital toxoplasmosis remains a significant public health concern, particularly in regions where systematic screening programs are lacking [12]. The diagnosis of acute toxoplasmosis infection during pregnancy can be based on the detection of specific IgM and or IgA. One of the most effective methods of detecting infection during pregnancy in a timely manner to prevent congenital toxoplasmosis is serosurveillance during pregnancy [26]. The IgG avidity test was not performed due to the cross‐sectional design of the study. However, the results indicate the necessity for the development of sero‐surveillance strategies and the inclusion of IgG avidity testing to date infections during pregnancy to reduce the risk of congenital toxoplasmosis in this context.

Furthermore, seroprevalence has been observed to fluctuate according to occupational category, exhibiting a pronounced prevalence among farmer laborer. One of the most at‐risk occupations is that of workers who are engaged in direct contact with soil, undertaking activities such as market gardening and rice growing. A review of the literature reveals that contact with soil is frequently identified as a risk factor for Toxoplasma gondii infection in multiple studies.

Limitations of the Study

The present study was subject to certain limitations. The design did not allow for the collection of data on dietary and environmental factors that have been considered as associated with Toxoplasma infection [15]. Antibody detection was based on rapid diagnostic tests, and confirmation of positives using high‐performance methods such as Enzyme‐linked Immunosorbant assay would have been advantageous.

Conclusion

The prevalence of Toxoplasma gondii antibodies was found to be high among pregnant women in the three regions under investigation. This phenomenon exhibited distinct geographical variations between rural and urban sites. Some of the women tested positive for IgM antibodies anti‐Toxoplasma gondii, indicating recent infection and thus a risk of congenital toxoplasmosis. It is imperative that toxoplasmosis detection be incorporated into a systematic and free‐of‐charge antenatal screening program during gestation, including awareness of toxoplasmosis to prevent infection during pregnancy.
