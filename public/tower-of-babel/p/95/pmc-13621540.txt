Introduction

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a major systemic vasculitis that affects veins and arteries of different sizes. Its chronic, multilayered nature and recurrent, remitting inflammatory course are its defining characteristics. Although the data points to a hereditary propensity that may be triggered by unidentified environmental circumstances, the pathophysiology is still poorly understood [1]. The most well-known genetic link to BD is the major histocompatibility complex antigen HLA-B*51, which carries a six-fold increased risk [1].

Research shows changes in the adaptive immune response, including downregulation of T-regulatory cells and activation of T-helper lymphocytes. Although BD is seen worldwide, it is more common in the Silk Road region of the Middle East [2]. Men are more likely to be diagnosed between the ages of 15 and 45, and their prognosis is also worse [3]. Since there are no definitive diagnostic tests, the International Study Group (ISG) updated the BD classification criteria in 2012 with a focus on clinical characteristics [4].

Oral ulcers are the most sensitive mucosal lesions, while genital ulcers are the most specific and indicative of the illness [5]. Ocular, vascular, cutaneous, neurological and gastrointestinal involvement are additional systemic symptoms.

Neuro-Behçet’s disease (NBD) is a severe form that contributes significantly to morbidity and mortality. Due to different definitions and approaches, reported prevalence varies greatly between research (1%–59%) [3]. Over time, the criteria for both BD and NBD have evolved. Additionally, some studies only include patients who have neurological symptoms following a BD diagnosis, while others include any neurological symptoms, even if they do not fit into a typical clinical syndrome [6].

This report describes a diagnostically complex case of suspected temporal arteritis occurring in a patient with a background of suspected Behçet disease and recurrent thromboembolic events.

Case presentation

A 49-year-old female presented to the emergency department with acute neurological symptoms. She had a significant past medical history of recurrent systemic venous and arterial thrombosis (deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, aortic embolism, femoral artery thrombosis requiring embolectomy, and prior ischemic stroke). She has been diagnosed with Behçet disease (diagnosed clinically by history, without imaging confirmation). She also has a history of seizure disorder, hypothyroidism and depression.

Previous workup for antiphospholipid syndrome and inherited thrombophilia was repeatedly negative. There was no family history of autoimmune disease or thrombophilia. She was a non-smoker and denied alcohol or illicit drug use.

On arrival, the patient reported one month of intermittent holocranial headaches, progressively worsening. Severe, persistent headache over the preceding two days. There was acute onset (one day prior to admission) of right-sided facial, upper limb, and lower limb numbness and weakness along with mild slurring of speech, associated with nausea and fatigue.

On examination the Glasgow Coma Scale was 15/15. She had mild right facial weakness along with decreased sensation on the right side of the face and body. The motor power was 4/5 in right upper and lower limbs and 5/5 on the left limb. She has marked tenderness over the right temporal region with a palpable cord-like structure; milder tenderness on the left temporal region. Vital signs are stable; ambulatory and not in distress. Syphilis serology was included in the infectious workup for stroke in young patients and was negative, helping to exclude neurosyphilis as a differential diagnosis.

On examination, the patient demonstrated marked tenderness over the right temporal region with a palpable cord‑like structure. Although temporal artery ultrasound is an important diagnostic adjunct in suspected giant cell arteritis, MRI vessel‑wall imaging was prioritized and demonstrated clear mural thickening and enhancement of the superficial temporal artery, supporting the diagnosis.

Diagnostic assessment

Laboratory findings

Inflammatory markers were significantly elevated, with ESR persistently raised at 26 mm/hr and CRP at 55 mg/L. These findings supported an active inflammatory process consistent with temporal arteritis. Coagulation parameters show a fluctuating, unstable INR. The Quantiferon TB Gold Plus test is positive, suggesting latent or active tuberculosis infection. Renal function is preserved, and the antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) workup is negative. Factor II and V were negative. The ENA profile, ANA, ANCA profile were negative. The ESR was consistently high.

Imaging

Significant vascular pathology was found by imaging studies. CT angiography of the brain revealed no signs of intracranial bleeding and thrombotic blockage of the right internal carotid artery with intact middle cerebral artery perfusion via collateral circulation. On August 1, 2025, an MRI of the brain (Fig. 1) revealed cortical laminar necrosis and a subacute to chronic infarct in the right posterior temporal lobe. Right internal carotid artery occlusion persisted, and intracranial filling was preserved via the Willis circle. Thickening and enhancement of the superficial temporal artery mural were seen.

Differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis included giant cell arteritis, vascular-type neuro-Behçet disease, a primary hypercoagulable disorder, secondary vasculitis, and embolic cerebrovascular disease.

Therapeutic intervention

Given the high clinical suspicion for temporal arteritis, the rheumatology team initiated treatment with intravenous methylprednisolone 500 mg daily for three days, followed by a transition to oral prednisolone at a dose of 0.5 mg/kg/day. In view of fluctuating INR levels and the known interaction between warfarin and corticosteroids, warfarin was discontinued and therapeutic enoxaparin was started. Supportive management consisted of gastric prophylaxis, bowel care, antiepileptic therapy, and analgesia.

High‑dose intravenous methylprednisolone at 500 mg daily for three days was selected based on the absence of visual loss and the patient’s high thrombotic risk, where ultra‑high‑dose steroids may exacerbate hypercoagulability. The transition to oral prednisolone at 0.5 mg/kg/day was chosen to balance anti‑inflammatory efficacy with safety considerations in a patient with recurrent arterial and venous thrombosis.

Temporal artery biopsy was planned once INR fell below 1.5 but was postponed due to anticoagulation management and scheduling constraints. Evaluation for latent versus active tuberculosis was initiated due to positive Quantiferon testing prior to potential biologic therapy consideration. The patient did not report acute visual loss at presentation. There were no visual obscurations, diplopia, or amaurosis fugax. Ophthalmology evaluation was planned; however, fluorescein angiography was not performed during the acute admission. No clinical evidence of ocular vasculitis was identified.

Discussion

This case illustrates a rare and complex overlap between suspected Behçet disease and probable giant cell arteritis in a patient with extensive thrombotic history. The absence of imaging evidence for active intracranial vasculitis, combined with focal temporal symptoms, elevated inflammatory markers, and steroid responsiveness, strongly supported a diagnosis of temporal arteritis. The coexistence of recurrent arterial and venous thrombosis further complicated diagnostic and therapeutic decision-making, particularly regarding anticoagulation.

Recurrent oral and vaginal ulcers, ocular lesions, and mucocutaneous symptoms are the hallmarks of BD, a chronic vascular inflammatory disease that primarily affects Arab male young adults aged 20 to 40 [7]. In 3–9% of BD cases, NBD, an uncommon consequence of BD, affects the brain’s blood vessels or brain tissue and results in a variety of neurological symptoms [8]. Since there is no particular diagnostic test or set of criteria for NBD, diagnosing it is difficult [8]. Clinical symptoms, imaging results, laboratory testing, and ruling out alternative explanations of neurological diseases are the basis for the diagnosis [9]. Vasculitis (e.g., systemic lupus erythematosus, antiphospholipid syndrome, granulomatosis with polyangiitis), thrombophilia (e.g., factor V Leiden mutation, prothrombin gene mutation), infection (e.g., syphilis, zoster), cancer (e.g., lymphoma), and demyelination (e.g., multiple sclerosis) are among the other causes of stroke in young people that are included in the differential diagnosis of NBD [10]. In our case, we conducted a thorough workup for autoimmune, hypercoagulable, infectious, and neoplastic illnesses in order to rule out these possibilities.

Depending on the kind and location of the lesion, NBD imaging results can vary. The brainstem, basal ganglia, thalamus, internal capsule, and cerebral hemispheres are frequently affected by parenchymal NBD. These lesions are often hyperintense on T2-weighted images (T2WI) and FLAIR sequences, and they exhibit restricted diffusion on diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) [11]. Necrosis and tiny vessel rupture in parenchymal NBD can also result in hemorrhagic lesions. On MRA or traditional angiograms, nonparenchymal NBD usually exhibits arterial occlusion or dural sinus thrombosis. In NBD, vessel wall imaging may reveal vessel wall thickening and augmentation [11].

Given the suspected overlap with Neuro‑Behçet disease, long‑term immunosuppressive therapy is anticipated in this patient. However, the patient’s positive Quantiferon TB Gold Plus test necessitated evaluation for latent versus active tuberculosis before initiating biologic agents such as anti‑TNF therapy. This step is essential to avoid TB reactivation.

This case underscores the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis in patients with multisystem inflammatory disease and highlights the role of therapeutic response when definitive diagnostic testing is delayed or inconclusive.

Patient perspective

The patient reported significant relief from headaches and improved quality of sleep following steroid therapy and expressed understanding of the need for continued evaluation and follow-up.
