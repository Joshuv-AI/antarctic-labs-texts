The dorsal column nuclei encode and transmit the network signatures of mechanical allodynia
Authors: Amandine Virlogeux, Tejapratap Bollu, Xiangyu Ren, Ana M Palma, Martyn D Goulding
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYThe neural circuits that transmit the sense of pain and how pain is encoded by these circuits are still poorly understood.Mechanical allodynia is a prominent form of chronic pain characterized by painful responses to innocuous touch that develops as a consequence of nerve damage and inflammation. Here, we show that alterations to the normal log-normal distribution of neuronal activity and structure of neural correlations between neurons in the dorsal column nuclei (DCN) constitute a signature feature of mechanical allodynia, with the transmission of ‘‘allodynic’’ light touch information to the thalamus by somatostatin-positive projection neurons in the DCN being essential for its expression and development. Our findings are consistent with neural activity in the DCN encoding a reference landscape of tactile percepts that animals use to differentiate innocuous touch from potentially noxious mechanical stimuli.

INTRODUCTION

The nature of pain and how the nervous system encodes its physiological and affective aspects has challenged neuroscientists for more than a century.1 Various theories have been proposed for the mechanism by which pain is encoded.1–3 These include the specificity theory that is consistent with labeled-line transmission of painful stimuli, the intensity theory that argues for the graded coding of innocuous and noxious information, and the pattern theory that is based on the combinatorial coding and discrimination of somatosensory stimuli. The gate theory of pain,4 a variant of the pattern theory, makes specific predictions about the circuit mechanisms that drive chronic pain at the level of the spinal cord. However, it provides little if any insight into the neural mechanisms that encode pain at supraspinal levels.

To date, a number of supraspinal sensory areas have been identified as critical for the affective and discriminative aspects of pain.5–16 While imaging and lesion studies have indicated a prominent role for the thalamus in pain perception,17,18 the specific contributions that thalamic pathways make to pain perception are still not well defined. Nociceptive and innocuous somatosensory information from the spinal cord accesses the thalamus directly via the spinothalamic tract and indirectly via the medial lemniscal pathway, with the spinothalamic tract contributing to the protopathic sense of pain, whereas the medial lemniscal pathway conveys light touch information that is highly discriminative in nature.

The dorsal column nuclei (DCN), comprising the cuneate (CuN) and gracile nuclei (GrN), are integral elements of the medial lemniscal pathway that integrate tactile sensory information as it ascends from the body’s periphery to higher brain regions.19–22 These nuclei play a pivotal role in processing light touch information from cutaneous low-threshold mechanoreceptors, and are essential for the perception and discrimination of tactile stimuli.23–25 A number of studies have implicated the DCN in the development of neuropathic pain as indicated by allodynia-induced changes in neuropeptide expression,26 c-fos expression,27–29 and neural activity30–32 within the GrN. Moreover, pharmacological and chemogenetic inhibition of GrN activity blocks mechanical allodynia,29,33 arguing that neural activity in the DCN contributes to the development of allodynia. What is not known is: (1) how light touch information is encoded within the DCN and how it is transformed following nerve injury and inflammation, and (2) whether neurons in the DCN then convey this transformed information to the thalamus to drive mechanical allodynia.

In this study, we showed that the firing rates of neurons in the GrN are log-normally distributed when a tactile stimulus is delivered to the hindpaw, and that this log-normal distribution is substantially perturbed following inflammation- and injury-induced mechanical allodynia, thereby revealing a network-level signature for mechanical allodynia in the DCN. Moreover, the neural correlations in the GrN are altered in a manner that amplifies signal transmission from the GrN to the thalamus. Strikingly, attenuating neural transmission in the GrN completely reverses the aberrant distributions of neural activity and their correlation structures, and it markedly diminishes mechanical allodynia, as does the selective modulation of excitatory GrNSst neurons and their thalamic projections. Taken together, these findings show that mechanical allodynia alters network-level neural interactions at the level of the DCN, and the salient transmission of this aberrant touch information to the thalamus is necessary to construct the sensation of pain that drives allodynia.

RESULTS

The DCN is a critical node for mechanical allodynia but not thermal allodynia

Previous studies have shown that nerve injury-induced mechanical allodynia is mediated by ascending spinal projections to the DCN.33 To assess whether this pathway is required more generally for other forms of allodynia, we assessed the functional contribution DCN makes to inflammatory (complete Freund’s adjuvant; CFA),34 nerve injury (spared nerve injury; SNI),35 and thermal allodynia by expressing and activating the inhibitory designer receptors exclusively activated by designer drugs (DREADD) hM4D in the GrN compartment of the DCN (Figure 1A). SNI and CFA mice that were injected with an adeno associated virus (AAV) expressing hM4D (AAV-hM4D) and treated with clozapine-N-Oxide (CNO) displayed a marked reduction in dynamic (brush) and static (von Frey) mechanical allodynia during both the developmental and persistent phases of this form of neuropathic pain (Figures 1B, 1C, 1E, and 1F). Strikingly, disrupting GrN neuronal transmission had no effect on thermal (Hargreaves) allodynia over this same time course (Figures 1D and 1G), nor was there any substantial effect on acute pain responses, as measured by the pinprick test, or motor performance when compared to controls (AAV-dsRed-injected mice treated with CNO) (Figure S1). Taken together, these findings demonstrate that neural activity within the GrN is essential for the development of mechanical allodynia in two different models of neuropathic pain but not required for thermal allodynia.

We then used a tightly controlled dynamic stimulus to test whether the firing rates of neurons in the GrN are increased following the induction of inflammatory- and nerve-injury induced-mechanical allodynia (Figures 2A–2D). We reasoned that using two different mouse models of allodynia that have different induction mechanisms should reveal a common signature of neural activity in the GrN that defines the allodynic transmission of touch information. Surprisingly, while the CFA mouse model displayed an increase in the average firing rates of responsive neurons in response to tactile stimulation (Figures S2B and S2D), in SNI mice, the overall average firing rates of responsive neurons were seen to decrease (Figures S2C and S2D). Moreover, there was no difference in the duration of stimulus-dependent neural activity between the naive and either of the allodynic conditions (Figure S2E), thus arguing against a simple intensity model whereby mechanical allodynia is driven by a general increase in neural activity within the GrN.

Mechanical allodynia disrupts the population neural activity of GrN

We then analyzed the population dynamics and coding of GrN neurons using this same series of dynamic stimuli to ask whether there are changes that are better described by the pattern model of pain, which would support a model in which the aberrant combinatorial coding of tactile information drives mechanical allodynia. To do this, we first modeled the neural data from naive mice as the three fat-tailed distributions that are found in the central nervous system—Weibull, gamma, and log-normal.36–39 The structure of the firing rate distribution in the GrN exhibited a log-normal distribution as opposed to Weibull or gamma distributions (Figures 2E–2G).

We then asked whether this log-normal distribution is maintained over a range of different dynamic stimulus intensities, with brush speed serving as a proxy measurement for stimulus intensity. While the overall activity of neurons in the GrN was seen to scale with the intensity of the tactile stimulus, the firing rates of the GrN population as a whole maintained a log-normal distribution (Figures 2E–2G). This finding demonstrates that the neural population activity in the GrN maintains a log-normal structure across a range of stimulus intensities, as expected if it were representing the nature of the tactile stimulus, which is a key function of the DCN.40 By contrast, the population firing rate distribution of the GrN in SNI and CFA mice was pronouncedly different from that of naive control mice. Specifically, the neural activity in the GrN no longer displayed a log-normal distribution in both the CFA and SNI mice (Figures 2H–2J). We propose that deviations from the log-normal neural firing distribution that naive mice use to represent a dynamic touch constitutes a common signature for both CFA and SNI allodynia.

To further test our hypothesis that changes in log normality might underlie the development of mechanical allodynia, we examined the population firing rate distribution in CFA and SNI mice following hM4D-induced silencing of the GrN that abrogates mechanical allodynia (Figures 2H–2J, top). Silencing the neural transmission of GrN neurons in allodynic mice significantly changed the firing rates in ∼30% of neurons (Figures S3F and S3J). Interestingly, the firing rates of individual GrN neurons were both up- and down-modulated (Figures S3F–S3H and S3J–S3L), arguing that the effects of hM4D-induced silencing were not limited to reducing the activity of individual neurons, but instead changed the interactions between neurons and neural activity at the network level. Moreover, the average neural activity was not altered (Figures S3I and S3M), thus allowing us to independently test the pattern model and the intensity model. Most importantly, both models of mechanical allodynia displayed a marked reversion toward a log-normal distribution of firing rates (Figures 2H–2J, bottom), which supports our hypothesis that the loss of a log-normal firing distribution in the GrN is a core feature and potential driver of mechanical allodynia.

Mechanical allodynia alters the correlation structure of the neural activity in the GrN in a manner consistent with increased information transmission

We then set out to determine how the structure of neural correlations in the GrN is altered by injury and inflammation (Figure 3). Previously, it has been shown that the structure of neural correlations within a neural network provides a quantitative readout of the functional interactions between neurons within the network and how the network performs its computations.41 Two distinct forms of correlations can be measured in pairs of simultaneously recorded neurons (Figures 3A–3C and S3A), signal correlations and noise correlations42 (Figure 3D), with the magnitude and the structure of the signal and noise correlations in pairs of neurons making specific theoretical predictions about the information capacity and the fidelity of transmission from the network.41 Our analysis revealed a pronounced increase in the mean signal correlations for GrN neurons in CFA and SNI mice (Figure 3E). This was accompanied by a decrease in mean noise correlations at the highest intensity (Figure 3F). These coordinated changes in signal and noise correlations reshape the structure of the signal-noise slope for the network (Figures 3G and S3B), with a significant enhancement at 0° (uncorrelated) for the allodynic conditions compared to the naive condition.

In view of the prominent role the thalamus plays in processing the sensation of pain,18 we set out to determine whether tactile information that is transmitted from the GrN to thalamic targets is altered by nerve injury and inflammation, and if so, how. Previous studies in the cat and primates have identified a characteristic neural signature that is specific to projection neurons. Specifically, projection neurons show high-frequency bursting, whereas DCN interneurons do not.43,44 Using these criteria to identify putative GrN projection neurons in our recordings (Figure S4) enabled us to analyze the signal and noise correlations for pairs of neurons in which at least one of the neurons was a putative projection neuron. In doing this, we observed a ∼2- to 3-fold increase in signal correlations in the CFA and SNI mice compared to naive mice (Figure 3H). This increase was accompanied by significant reductions in the noise correlations at the 800-mm/s intensity (Figure 3I) and a strong enhancement of pairs with a 0° signal-noise slope (Figures 3J and S3C). Taken together, these analyses demonstrate that putative thalamus-projecting neurons are more strongly correlated with other neurons in the local GrN network in CFA and SNI allodynic mice. The increase in signal correlations of GrN projection neurons is expected to lead to enhanced transmission to the thalamus through coincidence detection.45 Specifically, the increase in correlations between the projection neurons will lead to an increased coincidence of spikes onto the dendrites of downstream neurons in the thalamus. Since dendritic summations are often supralinear,46 the thalamic neurons will then be brought to threshold more consistently, leading to an increased firing rate. Noise correlations typically have a detrimental effect on encoding capacity;47,48 thus, the reduction of noise correlations is expected to increase the encoding capacity of tactile signals. Altogether, these findings are consistent with the increased transmission of corrupted tactile percepts from the GrN to the thalamus.

In keeping with this conclusion, hM4D induced-silencing of neural transmission in GrN resulted in the structure of the signal-noise slope present in allodynic mice reverting toward the naïve-condition. This ‘‘normalization’’ was due to a significant increase in the magnitude of noise correlations between projections neurons and the rest of the network (Figures 4A–4E), with increases in both strong correlation (>0.6) and anti-correlation (<−0.6) (Figures 4G and 4J), and not accompanied by any substantial change in signal correlations (Figures 4F and 4I). The net result is an altered signal-noise slope marked by a reduction in the fraction of pairs at 0° (Figures 4H and 4K). This change in the signal-angle slope has an information-limiting effect by aligning the noise information onto the signal axis.41 This phenomenon, when observed in other brain areas, is associated with low attention and salience.49 Thus, these results are consistent with a reduction in transmission of tactile information and its salience from the GrN to the thalamus.

SstCre neurons in the GrN constitute a major population of medial lemniscal projection neurons

The DCN is known to be composed of multiple excitatory and inhibitory cell types, including projection neurons that innervate multiple brain structures, the most prominent being the thalamus, periaqueductal gray (PAG), cerebellum, inferior olive, and inferior colliculus.50 In a series of expression analyses, we identified multiple molecularly discrete populations within the GrN, including a large population of neurons that express the neuropeptide somatostatin (Sst). Neurons lineage traced with SstCre, hereafter referred to as GrNSst neurons, were found to represent around 40% of the total GrN neuron population (Figures 5A, 5B, S5A, and S5B). The majority of the GrNSst neurons co-express VGluT2 and are excitatory (Figures 5C and 5D). No overlap was seen in VGAT and Sst expression in the GrN in SstCre;VGATFlpO Ai6551 mice, where tdTomato expression is dependent on the co-expression of Cre and FlpO, confirming that the inhibitory VGAT transporter is not expressed in GrNSst neurons (Figure S5C) and that the GrNSst neurons are predominantly excitatory.

A large fraction of the excitatory neurons in the GrN are projection neurons, many of which form the medial lemniscal pathway to the ventral posterolateral nucleus of the thalamus (VPL).52 To assess whether SstCre captures GrN projection neurons that innervate the VPL, AAV DIO-mCherry was injected into the GrN of the SstCre mice, and the axons of these neurons were then traced throughout the brain and spinal cord (Figure 5E). Strikingly, the axonal processes of the GrNSst neurons were almost exclusively restricted to the medial lemniscal pathway and VPL (Figure 5F). To confirm this, an AAV2-hSyn-FlpO virus was injected into the GrN of the SstCre;R26ds-SypGFP mice53 (Figure S5D) to map the synaptic terminals of the GrNSst projections. Synaptic contacts from GrNSst neurons were primarily localized to the VPL, with a small number of puncta also present in the red nucleus (Figure S5E). By contrast, other structures that are known to be innervated by DCN projection neurons, including the cerebellar cortex, PAG, and inferior colliculus, were completely devoid of GrNSst-derived processes, indicating that SstCre primarily captures projection neurons that innervate the VPL (Figures S5E and S5F).

To determine if the GrNSst projection neurons transmit tactile information to the VPL, we undertook a series of opto-tagging experiments where recordings were made from identified GrNSst neurons in SstCre;Ai32 animals (Figure 5G) while analyzing their responses to dynamic tactile stimuli. GrNSst neurons were identified by their short latency and low jitter responses to 473-nm light stimulation (Figures 5H and 5K). As predicted, the majority of the GrNSst neurons (n = 21/24) exhibited burst-like firing patterns with short interspike intervals. These neurons were either active spontaneously or in response to brush (Figures 5I and 5J). The firing rate statistics of the GrNSst neurons were in line with those previously described for projection neurons in the DCN of cats and primates, where they are considered a classifier for this population of projection neurons.43,44 This electrophysiological identification of GrNSst VPL-projecting neurons is consistent with our observation that a large fraction of the SstCre-derived neurons in the GrN innervate the VPL. Of the optotagged GrNSst neurons, 12/24 were modulated by tactile stimulus to the glabrous skin of the hindpaw, thereby demonstrating that they respond to tactile stimulation and are able to relay this information to the VPL. While these GrNSst neurons displayed higher average firing rates compared to the GrN neuron population as a whole over a range of stimulus speeds (Figure 5L), there was no difference in the duration of stimulus-dependent neural activity between them and the GrN neuron population as a whole (Figure 5M).

GrNSst projection neurons are required for the expression of mechanical allodynia

Our observation that excitatory GrNSst neurons are a major population of VPL-projecting neurons encoding responses to tactile stimulation led us to ask if the GrNSst neurons play an essential role in mechanical allodynia. To test this, stereotaxic injections of AAV-DIO-hM4D or a control virus, AAV-DIO-mCherry, were made into the GrN of the SstCre mice (Figure 6A) in order to measure their responses to punctate and dynamic touch following CFA or SNI. CNO silencing of the GrNSst neurons was observed to strongly attenuate both static and dynamic mechanical allodynia in both models (Figures 6B–6E), whereas the three control groups (hM4D, saline; mCherry, saline and mCherry, CNO), displayed no observable difference in the von Frey thresholds or dynamic allodynia scores following brush (Figures 6B–6E).

In contrast to the strong attenuation of mechanical allodynia that followed the silencing of the GrNSst neurons, activating the GrNSst neurons resulted in a marked increase in dynamic allodynia (Figures S6A, S6E, and S6H). Both the SNI- and CFA-treated mice displayed enhanced licking and guarding behaviors compared to their control groups. By contrast, activating the GrNSst neurons had little effect on punctate allodynia scores on both CFA and SNI models (Figures S6D and S6G); however, this is not entirely surprising given that the measured thresholds for allodynia are already very low from day 1 onwards following SNI- and CFA-treatment. It should also be noted that silencing or activating the GrNSst neurons in naive mice did not affect nociceptive response or motor behaviors as assessed by the accelerating rotarod or overall locomotor activity (Figures S6B, S6C, and S6J–S6L). Finally, we did not detect any differences in the Hargreaves test, again arguing that the GrNSst neurons are not involved in thermal allodynia (Figures S6F, S6I, S6M, and S6N). Taken together, our results reveal an essential role for the GrNSst neurons in the induction and maintenance of dynamic mechanical allodynia.

Our observation that many of the GrNSst neurons are VPL-projection neurons led us to test whether inhibiting the transmission of the disorganized neural activity found in the GrN of allodynic mice attenuates mechanical allodynia. To do this, we injected Cre-dependent hM4D AAV bilaterally into the GrN of SstCre mice (Figure 6F). As a control for these experiments, similar analyses were undertaken using SstCre mice injected with Cre-dependent mCherry AAV into the GrN. After performing SNI or CFA, the experimental and control mice were tested for static and dynamic mechanical allodynia, as well as for thermal allodynia, before and after the delivery of CNO by cannula onto the axon terminals of the GrNSst neurons in VPL. Strikingly, static and dynamic allodynia were both alleviated when the axon terminals of the GrNSst VPL-projection neurons were silenced (Figures 6G–6J). By contrast, there was no change in thermal responses in CFA and SNI mice following the silencing of the GrNSst axon terminals in the VPL (Figures S6O–S6Q). In summary, we conclude that the GrNSst projection neurons in the GrN-VPL pathway are essential for the expression of mechanical allodynia.

DISCUSSION

We show that changes in the log-normal firing rates of GrN neurons that encode the haptic features of touch stimuli are a robust signature of mechanical allodynia. We also provide evidence that the altered representation of tactile information, at the level of the DCN, together with its subsequent transmission to the thalamus, is a requisite driver of mechanical allodynia. Specifically, when the neural activity patterns that encode innocuous touch are corrupted by injury or inflammation so they no longer comport with the patterns of activity that the dorsal column nuclei and thalamus normally use to represent innocuous touch, animals sense innocuous stimuli as being painful.

Neural activity in the GrN displays a balanced network structure

Our demonstration that the GrN network in naive mice displays a log-normal distribution of firing rates and irregular Poisson-like firing of individual neurons is consistent with the GrN operating as a balanced excitatory-inhibitory network.54,55 Further support for the GrN and CuN functioning as balanced networks that operate in a fluctuation-driven regime comes from studies showing that neural networks in the CuN are sparsely connected by relatively strong synapses,43,56 a characteristic of exciation-inhibition balanced networks. Balanced networks are well-suited to encoding the temporal features of dynamic touch, as they have the ability to rapidly and accurately track time-dependent input compared to any individual neurons in their network.55 They also have the capacity to generate neural responses that are robust to noise, and they provide a scaffold for high-capacity memory.57,58 Evidence that the DCN possesses the capacity for rapid high-capacity coding comes from studies in the cat showing that CuN is able to encode a broad range of touch precepts, and that single neurons in the CuN and its functional equivalent, the trigeminal nucleus, receive convergent multimodal inputs.40 These findings are consistent with the DCN network encoding learned network states or engrams that form a library of ‘‘normal’’ or ‘‘innocuous’’ tactile percepts, and which animals can then use to rapidly identify potentially harmful tactile stimuli or use to misidentify innocuous stimuli in the case of mechanical allodynia.

Mechanical allodynia perturbs the pattern of network activity in the GrN

Our use of two mechanistically distinct models of mechanical allodynia enabled us to search for common signatures in the patterns of neural activity that characterize this form of neuropathic pain. This led to the discovery that injury- and inflammation-induced mechanical allodynia disrupts three facets of neural processing within the GrN. (1) It changes the log-normal firing rate distributions of neurons in the GrN; (2) it changes the structure of signal and noise correlations between neurons in the GrN; and (3) it leads to pronounced changes in the structure of signal and noise correlations for excitatory GrNSst neurons that project to the thalamus. These changes in neural processing in allodynic mice point to a crucial role of DCN and the medial lemniscal pathway in discriminating innocuous touch stimuli from potentially harmful touch stimuli.

Our findings argue that mechanical allodynia is primarily driven by a change in the coding of touch information by the DCN as opposed to a simple increase in neural activity within the DCN. Whereas prior studies using c-fos expression as a readout of neural activity were interpreted as showing increased activity in the DCN following nerve injury,27,28 our analysis of neural firing rates revealed a decrease (SNI) and an increase (CFA) in the mean maximum firing rates of GrN neurons (Figure S2D). However, while firing rates in the GrN were starkly different for SNI and CFA (Figure 2B), their neural correlation structures were nearly identical (Figures 3E–3G and S3). This strongly argues against a model in which mechanical allodynia is simply due to touch driving an increase in the firing rates of neurons in the dorsal spinal cord and DCN. Moreover, our demonstration that naive mice maintain a log-normal firing distribution at different brush speeds (Figures 2E and 2F) predicts that a simple increase in firing rates without an underlying change in the structure of the neural activity would result in tactile hypersensitivity as opposed to mechanical allodynia.

The changed neural correlations that we see in the GrN following the induction of mechanical allodynia are likely to arise from both changes within the GrN and altered sensory inputs to the DCN. Changes in neural correlations in the spinal cord have been reported following nerve injury,59 along with increases in synchronized and coupled sensory neuron activity in SNI and CFA models of mechanical allodynia.60,61 While the coupled activation of sensory neurons seen in sensory neurons would predict an increase in both the signal and noise correlations in the downstream DCN network, we saw an increase in signal correlations and a decrease in noise correlations in the GrN. The opposing changes in signal and noise correlations indicate that network changes in the GrN of CFA and SNI mice are driven in part by alterations to the GrN network structure rather than just being inherited from sensory inputs. Indeed, our observation that the hM4D-induced silencing of GrN neurons in CFA and SNI mice results in a reversion to lognormality is consistent with the changed firing rate distributions in allodynic mice being partially driven by altered neural computations within the GrN (Figures 2H–2J).

Sst projection neurons that form the medial lemniscal pathway are essential for mechanical allodynia

The stronger signal correlations for putative projection neurons that we found in CFA and SNI mice are consistent with an increase in the coherence of transmission from the GrN to the thalamus in allodynic mice, thus having the ability to drive recipient neurons in the VPL more strongly. It therefore follows that reducing the coherence of information transmitted to the VPL or attenuating the transmission of information from GrN to the thalamus should alleviate mechanical allodynia. Consistent with this prediction, the broad DREADD silencing of the GrN reduced the coherence of the signals being transmitted by GrNSst projection neurons to the thalamus (Figures 4G–4J), and selectively inactivating the GrNSst projection neurons attenuated mechanical allodynia (Figure 6). Moreover, the silencing of GrNSst axon terminals in the VPL supports our hypothesis that mechanical allodynia is mediated by the transmission of corrupted tactile information from the GrN to the VPL. Additional evidence that the GrNSst projection neurons drive this form of neuropathic pain comes from our demonstration that activating the GrNSst neuron population enhances dynamic mechanical allodynia (Figure S6).

The DCN-VPL pathway that we have described operates in concert with other ascending pain pathways to drive mechanical allodynia, the most prominent being affective parabrachial pathways62–64 that connect to the amygdala and medial thalamic nuclei.7,65,66 A large fraction of spinoparabrachial projection neurons express tachykinin receptor 1 (Tacr1),67–69 the loss of which markedly attenuates tactile and thermal hyperalgesia.70,71 It therefore appears that the discriminative DCN-VPL pathway and the affective spinoparabrachial pathway collaborate to orchestrate the multidimensional aversive sensations that give rise to mechanical allodynia. It is notable that hM4D silencing of the GrN had no effect on thermal allodynia (Figures 1D–1G), which is consistent with the DCN-VPL pathway having a dedicated role in encoding epicritic touch information. Thermal pain is instead transmitted via the parabrachial nucleus and protopathic spinothalamic pathways that do not project to the VPL but instead converge on the medial thalamus.72 Some spinothalamic neurons project to the VPL; however, the contribution that they make to mechanical allodynia is not known.

In summary, this study reveals that mechanical allodynia distorts the log-normal coding of tactile information in the GrN with the transfer of this distorted information to the thalamus required for evoking allodynic pain responses. In so doing, this study provides a mechanistic framework for understanding the role that the medial lemniscal pathway and VPL play in mechanical allodynia, namely mechanical allodynia is driven by the aberrant representation of innocuous tactile information by the DCN and its transmission to the brain, where it is perceived as potentially harmful or pain-like. This role for the DCN in driving mechanical allodynia aligns with the specialized function the DCN has in representing and transmitting the epicritic features of light touch, which animals use to distinguish innocuous from potentially harmful touch stimuli.

Limitations of the study

This study has identified changes in the neural firing rates and correlations in the GrN following mechanical allodynia that revert to log-normality upon chemogenetic inhibition of GrNSst neurons; how this affects neural responses in the VPL and somatosensory cortex is yet to be determined. Our findings, while revealing a role for the altered processing of touch information within the DCN following SNI- and CFA-induced mechanical allodynia, did not assess the contribution that the altered representation of touch by the direct and indirect pathways make to these altered responses in the DCN.

STAR★METHODS

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Mice were maintained in adherence with the protocols for animal experiments approved by the IACUC of the Salk Institute for Biological Studies according to NIH guidelines for animal experimentation. 11–19 weeks old mice of both sexes were used. The animals were randomized to experimental groups and no sex differences were discernible.

METHOD DETAILS

Induction of inflammatory and spared nerve injury models of allodynia

Inflammatory allodynia was induced by injecting 10 μL of Complete Freund’s adjuvant (F5881, Sigma) into the left intraplantar surface of the paw.

For the SNI, the surgical transection of the tibial and peroneal nerves was performed under 1–2.5% isoflurane anesthesia. The left hindlimb was immobilized in a lateral position, elevated and the lateral surface of the left thigh was shaved. The skin was cleansed with betadine and alcohol 3 times, and an incision of the skin was made at the mid-thigh level and through the biceps femoris muscle. The common peroneal and tibial nerves were ligated and axotomized before suturing the skin. After recovering, the mice returned to their house cage.

Behavioral testing

Mice littermates were group housed as mixed groups. All experimental groups were tested on the same day, during the light phase and 3–4 weeks after the viral injection. During the habituation period, the experimenter remained in the room while the mice were placed in the room for 30 min and onto the apparatus for another 30 min daily over a span of 3 consecutive days prior to testing. Before each testing day, mice underwent a 30-min acclimatization period in both the test room and on the apparatus.

Mice were excluded from analysis if their performance was calculated to be a significant outlier (Grubb’s test, no outlier). Prior to SNI or CFA, all mice underwent all the behavioral tests (day 1: brush assay, von Frey, Pinprick; day 2: Hargreaves; day 3: accelerating rotarod). After SNI or CFA, all mice were tested using the brush, von Frey and Hargreaves assays at the different time points. For the DREADD manipulation experiments, mice were injected with CNO or saline i.p., then tested 10 min afterward. At the completion of the behavioral testing regime, brain tissues were collected from each experimental animal for anatomical evaluation of the viral injection site.

Brush assay

Mice were placed in a red plexiglass chamber on an elevated platform with a mesh floor. The hind paw of the mice was stimulated with a paintbrush, applied in a heel-to-toe direction. The testing protocol included 5 stimulations, alternating between the left and right paws, with a 3-min interval between each stimulation session. Prior SNI or CFA, the withdrawal reflex responses were assessed based on the percentage of responses elicited out of the total number of brush strokes applied. After SNI or CFA, allodynia score was measured as described previously.75 Briefly, 0 = fast paw withdrawal, 1 = a prolonged paw withdrawal toward the body or single gentle flinching, 2 = one strong lateral lifting above the level of the body or a startle-like jumping and 3 = repeated flicking or flinching of the affected leg or licking of the paw.

von Frey

Mice were placed in the same setup as described in the brush assay section. The plantar side of the hind paw was stimulated with calibrated von Frey filaments (North coast medical, NC12775–99, 0.008–4 g). The paw withdrawal threshold for the von Frey assay was determined by Dixon’s up-down method.76 Post SNI, von Frey testing was confined to the sural nerve innervating region of the paw.

Pinprick

Mice were placed in the same setup as described in the brush assay section. Pain sensation was assessed by applying pressure to the plantar surface of the hind paw using a custom-designed pinprick stimulator. 10 stimulations were applied alternatively on the left and right paws, with a 3-min interval between each stimulation session. Withdrawal reflex responses were observed and recorded during the testing process. The data were expressed as the average of the 10 trials.

Hargreaves

Thermal pain sensation was evaluated through the measurement of paw-withdrawal latency using the Hargreaves apparatus. Mice were confined in the plastic chamber of the apparatus, and the plantar hind paw surface was subjected to a radiant heat beam. Each animal underwent 5 trials, and the latency to paw withdrawal was recorded and averaged, with a 10-min interval between trials. To avoid potential tissue damage, a cutoff time of 30 s was established. After SNI or CFA, only the paw which underwent the injection, or the surgery was tested.

Accelerating rotarod

Motor coordination and balance were assessed with the rotarod test using an accelerating rotarod. Mice were first habituated to the rotarod for 2 days by placing them on the rotating drums three times at a constant speed of 3 rpm for a maximum of 5 min. Mice were trained for 3 days, with each day featuring training sessions at one speed: 10 rpm, 15 rpm, 20 rpm, with each lasting a maximum of 5 min and repeated 3 times. On the day of the test, each mouse underwent 3 trials at speeds that increased from 4 rpm to 40 rpm over 300 s. Each trial was separated by 10 min resting periods. The data were expressed as the average of the 3 trials.

Stereotaxic surgery

Surgical procedures were conducted on adult mice under isoflurane anesthesia (4% induction and 1–2.5% for maintenance using a nose cone [Dräger Vapor 2000, Draeger, Inc.]). After shaving the surgical site, mice were placed on a heating pad, on a stereotaxic frame (Kopf Instruments). Eye lubricant was applied, and the surgical site was cleansed with betadine and alcohol. Local anesthesia was performed by subcutaneous injections of Lidocaine (7 mg/kg).

To target the GrN in behavioral experiments, each mouse’s head was positioned on a stereotaxic frame using custom-made ear bars, which were then adjusted to flex the head ventrally by approximately 30° from the horizontal position. An incision was made in the midline from the top of the skull to the neck. The superficial muscles were retracted to expose the base of the occipital bone. The deeper layers of the muscle were also retracted to expose the cisterna magna and the obex. The coordinates were determined relative to obex, and small holes in the dura were made with a 30G syringe. For thalamic injections and GrN injections for electrophysiological experiments, animals were placed in a stereotaxic frame in a flat skull position. A skin incision was made at the top of the head, coordinates were determined relative to bregma, and the cranium drilled with a high-speed stereotaxic drill (Model 1474, Kopf Instruments).

Viruses were obtained from the Salk Institute Viral Vector Core and administered with a glass pipette (tips broken for an inner diameter of 20μm) connected to a Nanoject III Programmable Nanoliter Injector (Drummond Scientific) at a rate of 2 nL/s for the GrN (from obex site 1: A/P −0.25 mm; M/L ±0.25 mm; D/V −0.20 mm; site 2: A/P 0 mm; M/L ±0.25 mm; DV −0.20 mm; site 3: A/P 0.25 mm; M/L ±0.45 mm; D/V −0.20 mm; 50 nL per site) (from bregma site 1: A/P −7.75 mm; M/L ±0.35 mm; D/V −4 mm; site 2: A/P −8 mm; M/L ±0.15 mm; D/V −4.15 mm; site 3: A/P −8.25; M/L ±0.1 mm; D/V −4.25mm; 50 nL per site) or 10 nL/s for the VPL (site 1: A/P −1.25 mm, M/L ±1.75 mm, D/V −3.2 to −3.4 mm; site 2: A/P −1.50 mm, M/L ±1.8 mm, D/V −3.3 to −3.5 mm; site 3: A/P −1.85 mm, M/L ±1.9 mm, D/V −3.5 to −3.7 mm.; 100 nL per site).

For drug infusion, a bilateral micro-cannula set (P1 Technologies), including guide cannula (C235G, 3.0mm C/C, P1 Technologies), dummy cannula (C235DC, P1 Technologies) and dust cap (303DC/1A, P1 Technologies), was implanted into the VPL. The implants were securely fixed with superglue and dental cement (1334, Ortho-Jet Package) for stabilization.

The skin and the muscle layers were then sutured using 6–0 black braided silk (Ethicon). Buprenorphine ER (1 mg/kg) was then injected subcutaneously, and animals were allowed to recover on a heating pad before being returned to their housing cage. 3 to 4 weeks after injecting the virus, experimental mice were subjected to behavioral testing or terminal perfusion for histological purposes. For experiments involving CNO infusion the mice were first injected with the appropriate virus 3 to 4 weeks prior to cannula implantation. Mice were then allowed to recover for 2 weeks prior to conducting further behavioral tests.

CNO injection

Apart from the experiments involving the silencing of Sst+ VPL-projection neurons, all mice received i.p. injections of CNO (2 mg/kg; C0832, Sigma) or saline (NDC63323–186-10, APP Pharmaceuticals).

For intra-VPL infusion of CNO via a cannula, the dummy cannula was removed, and the internal cannula (C235I, P1 Technologies) was inserted and left in place for 10 min prior to the behavior test. We used a syringe drive (BASi Bee optional 3-syringe bracket) with a controller (Hive Syringe Pump Controller-BASi) for infusing a total volume of 150nL at a rate of 1 μL/min.

In vivo GrN multielectrode array recordings

For electrophysiological experiments, CFA or SNI mice were tested with the brush assay to confirm they had an allodynic response. Head-plate-installation surgery was then performed on these mice. Briefly, the mice were placed on a heating pad in a stereotaxic frame in a flat skull position under isoflurane anesthesia (4% induction and 1–2.5%). Ophthalmic ointment was applied to the eyes, and the surgical site was cleansed with betadine and alcohol 3 times. The scalp between the bregma and the lambda was removed, and the skull was dried with a cotton swab. The head plate was fixed to the skull with C&B Metabond (Parkell). Then, the cisterna magna and the obex were exposed as described previously, and the dura was removed with a micro scissor. The mice received urethane (1.2 mg/kg) and xylazine (20 mg/kg) i.p. and were then moving to the electrophysiological recording rig. The neck was dorsiflexed at 30° with the help of the head plate for the duration of the recording session.

Extracellular recordings were made acutely using sharpened 64-channel silicon probes (ASSY-77 H10, Cambridge Neurotech). The 64-channel voltage signals were amplified, filtered, and digitized (16 bit) on a headstage (Intan Technologies), recorded on a 512-channel Intan RHD2000 recording controller (sampled at 20 kHz), and stored for offline analysis (see electrophysiology analysis). To determine proper placement of the probe in the GrN, the hind paw was gently brushed, and responses checked online with robust responses confirming insertion into the GrN. Coverage of the site with saline solution was maintained throughout the recording session. Recordings were then paused for at least 30 min prior to acquiring the experimental datasets used for our analysis. A servo motor was used to apply the brush stimulus on the hind paw to ensure a high level of reproducibility with respect to the applied stimulus. The speed and the angle of the brush was controlled by a custom-built FPGA control system using the National Instruments sbRIO-9636 FPGA and programmed with LabVIEW. We applied three speeds to the hindpaw (800 mm/s, 400 mm/s and 200 mm/s) while recording from the GrN. For optical tagging experiments, a laser diode light source (LDFLS_450, Doric Life Sciences) attached to an optical patch cord was used to deliver light to an optical cannula on the surface of the GrN (200 μm core diameter; NA = 0.66, Thorlabs). To test for light-evoked spiking, 50 pulses of light (10ms duration, 1ms offset ramp) were delivered at 3 different power intensities (5, 10, and 25mW/mm2 at fiber tip) at the beginning and at the end of the recording.

Electrophysiological analysis

Extracellular voltage traces were first notch-filtered at 60Hz. The voltage traces were then spike-sorted automatically with Kilo-sort2.574 and curated manually with Phy2 to identify putative single neurons (Units). Units with low amplitude (<100μV), variable, or non-physiological waveforms were excluded from further analysis.

Units were considered to be ‘modulated’ by tactile stimulus if, across trials, the number of spikes in the window 500 ms before the stimulus were statistically different from the number of spikes in the window 500 ms after the stimulus (Two sample t test, p < 0.05). To estimate the firing rate of a unit for each stimulus speed, the recording windows corresponding to the specific stimulus were selected and aligned to the stimulus onset. Peristimulus firing-rate histograms were then constructed on these aligned recordings using a 10 ms bin size and a 75 ms Gaussian smoothing kernel. The maximum firing rate of the unit was calculated as the maximum value of the PSTH in the window between the onset of stimulus and 200 ms after the offset of the stimulus. The response duration was calculated as the duration of the PSTH that was significantly different from the mean PSTH. The significance was estimated by converting the PSTH to a Z score, mean and standard deviation for z-scoring the PSTH were calculated on the window from 1000 ms before the stimulus onset to 1000 ms after the onset.

The peak firing rates of the units were modeled as lognormal, weibull or gamma distributions. We used the MATLAB function ‘fitdist’ to generate fits for the firing rate distributions. fitdist uses a maximum likelihood estimation algorithm to fit the parameters of the selected distribution. To calculate the error of the fit, we took the difference between the quantile values of the data and those predicted by the fit. We normalized the error by the quantile value from the data. We used a bootstrap analysis to generate the confidence intervals and the standard deviation for the estimated parameters of the fits. The bootstrap analyses were performed in the following way, for each condition (i.e., naive, CFA, and SNI or while assessing fit type, i.e., lognormal, gamma, and weibull) we resampled the units recorded for that condition. We selected a set of units (randomly, without replacement) of the half the size as our original set. We then estimated a new fit based on the resampled dataset. We performed this resampling 1,000 times to get a distribution for the parameters.

Signal correlation for a pair of simultaneously recorded neurons is the Pearson correlation coefficient of the PSTHs of the two units aligned to stimulus onset. Noise correlation for a pair of simultaneously recorded neurons was estimated as the Pearson correlation of the vectors containing the number of spikes per trial for the two units. Signal-Noise slope was reported as the four quadrant inverse tangent of the ratio (NoiseCorrelation/SignalCorrelation).

To quantify the change in distributions of signal and noise correlations induced by experimental manipulations we used the total variation distance (TVD) metric. TVD is defined as the sum of absolute differences between two probability distributions. To calculate the null distribution for the difference, we shuffled the identities of the correlations and recalculated the TVD. We performed this procedure 10000 times. The observed TVD was considered significant if it was outside the 95th percentile window of the null distribution, and the exact percentile was reported as the p-value.

Histology and immunohistochemistry

Animals were administered anesthesia with 100 mg/kg ketamine and 10 mg/kg xylazine before undergoing transcardial perfusion with cold PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde. The brains were extracted, fixed overnight in 4% PFA, and cryoprotected in 30% (w/v) sucrose in PBS prior to embedding in O.C.T (Tissue-Tek). Coronal sections (30 μm) were obtained using a cryostat and mounted on slices. Following a PBS wash and a 2-h blocking at room temperature in 10% Donkey Serum and 3% Triton X-100 in PBS, the sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies (Chicken ⍺-GFP (1:1000), GFP-1020; RRID: AB_10000240, Aves Labs INC or Rabbit ⍺-dsRed (1:1000), 632396; RRID: AB_10013483, Clontech) in a 1% Donkey Serum and 1% Triton X-100 in PBS. After three washes in PBS-T (1% Triton X-100 in PBS), the sections were incubated at room temperature for 2 h with appropriate fluorescent secondary antibodies in a 1% Donkey Serum. After 2 washes in PBS-T, the sections were briefly incubated in DAPI solution (1:10 000 in PBS, 10236276001, Milipore Sigma) and washed one more time before being mounted on slides using Aqua-Poly/Mount. Immunostainings were acquired with a ×20 air-immersion objective (0.8 NA) using an inverted confocal microscope (LSM 900, Zeiss).

RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization

The tissue was first prepared as described above for immunohistochemistry followed by cryosectioning at 14 μm. Multiple-labeling fluorescence in situ hybridization was performed using the RNAscope Multiplex Fluorescent Reagent Kit v2 Assay (323100, Advanced Cell Diagnostics) following the manufacturer’s recommended protocol. The following probes were used: Mm-Sst-C2 (404631, Advanced Cell Diagnostics), Mm-Slc17a6-C3 (319171, Advanced Cell Diagnostics) were revealed with Opal 520, Opal 650 (PerkinElmer), and DAPI was used to counterstain the nucleus (Advanced Cell Diagnostics). Images were acquired with a 40× oil objective (1.4 N.A) using an inverted confocal microscope (LSM 900, Zeiss). For quantification, four sections were analyzed per animal.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

For the behavior, all graphs represent the mean and SEM. The statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 and are reported in the figure legends. We employed two different statistical methods: two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test and paired two-tailed Student’s test. The two-way ANOVA was used to statistically compare the influence of condition and time on the data. In the graphs where a two-way ANOVA has been used, the comparisons shown were made between the same condition (hM4D(Gi) or hM3D(Gq)) injected with saline or CNO (for further comparisons see the relevant tables). For the test performed in naive mice, we used paired two-tailed Student’s tests to compare the before and after CNO data. Exact statistics are in the accompanying data tables.

For the electrophysiological data, graphs are typically represented as medians and inter-quartile-intervals, except when noted in the figure legends. We employed a Wilcoxon rank-sum test to statistically compare the medians of two distributions. Exact statistics and the statistical tests used are noted in the Supplemental Table (see Table S1).
