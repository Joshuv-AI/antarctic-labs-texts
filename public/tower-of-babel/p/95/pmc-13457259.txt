Oral Manifestations of Sweet Syndrome in a Child: A Case Report
Authors: Muhammad Asyraf Hamzah, Mern Jo Tay, Syed Nabil, Ahmad Shuhud Irfani Zakaria, S Nagarajan MP Sockalingam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSweet syndrome is a relatively rare disease characterized by neutrophilia, tender and erythematous skin lesions with sudden onset of fever. Oral involvement is usually rare. We report a case of a 5-year-old boy who presented with fever, spontaneous erythematous swelling over the right periorbital and suprasternal region, upper left lip swelling, generalized gingival hyperplasia over the upper gingiva, and a single ulcer on the right soft palate region. Blood results was consistent with bicytopenia and leukocytosis, while biopsy of the skin and gingival lesions revealed dense infiltration of mixed inflammatory cells composed predominantly of neutrophils. Sweet syndrome was suggested based on the clinical presentations and histological features. Steroid treatment was initiated according to the suggested protocol; unfortunately, the child's condition started to deteriorate 3 weeks after his initial presentation, and he passed away 2 weeks later due to massive intracranial bleeding. Proper investigation and screening are crucial when any oral lesions with unknown causes are encountered.

Introduction

Sweet syndrome, also known as acute febrile neutrophilic dermatosis, was first described by Dr Robert Douglas Sweet in 1964. The term “acute febrile neutrophilic dermatosis” was based on four cardinal features of the disease: pyrexia, tender and erythematous skin lesions, neutrophilia, and histologically mixed inflammatory cells consisting predominantly of neutrophils.1 This disease is indeed uncommon, especially in pediatric populations, with fewer than 70 cases being reported over the years.2 Generally, no gender predilection is noted in children, but male predominance has been observed in those younger than 3 years.3 Oral involvement in children with Sweet syndrome is relatively rare and usually presents in the form of mucosal ulcer or gingival hyperplasia.4

Case Description

A 5-year-old Iban boy was referred to us for management of oral ulcers and gingival swelling. He initially presented with localized purplish interdental papilla swelling over the upper anterior region for the past 10 days, along with persistent fever, right abdominal pain, and bleeding gums, especially upon tooth brushing. Initial blood investigation results showed anemia, thrombocytopenia, and leukocytosis with predominant neutrophils. A preliminary diagnosis of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) was made, and the lesion was treated as leukemic infiltration. Preventive dental care, which includes oral hygiene and dietary advice along with parent education for possible acute and long-term treatment complications, was given as advocated by the American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry (AAPD).5 However, the gingival swelling gradually increased in size and became generalized over the upper anterior and lower left posterior region (Figs 1B and C). The child then developed ulcers over the soft palate, erythematous blisters, and upper left lip swelling, as well as swelling on the right periorbital and suprasternal region (Fig. 1A), and 5 days later presented with right forearm and left leg swelling. Bone marrow aspiration and trephine were performed, but no evidence of acute leukemia or nonhematopoietic tumor infiltration was noted, while infective screening test results were negative for common possible infections. Skin and gingival lesion biopsies were performed and revealed dense infiltration of mixed inflammatory cells composed predominantly of neutrophils (Fig. 2), a feature suggestive of Sweet syndrome. Topical and intravenous corticosteroids were given as the main drug for his treatment, whereas the oral lesions were treated with high molecular weight hyaluronic acid gel and spray besides regular oral toileting to maintain optimal oral hygiene. Nevertheless, his condition started to deteriorate 3 weeks after initial presentation, and unfortunately, he passed away 2 weeks later due to massive intracranial bleeding.

Discussion

Sweet syndrome is commonly associated with upper respiratory or gastrointestinal tract infections, inflammatory diseases, drugs, and neoplasms.4 Based on the associated conditions, Sweet syndrome can be categorized into three main subtypes: classical (idiopathic), malignancy-associated, and drug-induced.6 To date, the pathogenesis of this disease remains a debate. It was postulated that it could be related to an unusual hypersensitivity reaction toward certain conditions, which is mediated by cytokines, followed by infiltration of neutrophils that are likely activated by interleukin (IL)-1.7

In pediatric population, upper respiratory tract infection (URTI) usually precedes the presentation of dermatosis,8 although URTI was not present in the current case. The skin lesions usually manifest as single or multiple painful, red or purple-red papules, macules, nodules, or plaques. Asymmetrical distribution of lesions is noted and often involves the upper extremities, head, or neck,4 consistent with the presentation in the current case. Extracutaneous involvement of the lungs, eyes, and oral mucosa has been reported, with common symptoms ranging from flu-like symptoms to acute respiratory distress syndrome; conjunctivitis, episcleritis, glaucoma, iritis, peripheral ulcerative keratitis; and oral ulcers or pustules involving the lips, buccal mucosa, and tongue.4 However, mucosal involvement is rarely seen. A retrospective study done on seven children with Sweet syndrome reported that only two cases were found with mucosal lesions, one of whom presented with underlying myelodysplastic syndrome.2

Specific diagnostic criteria of Sweet syndrome7 are shown in Table 1. Diagnosis of this condition is made by exclusion of infectious, inflammatory, and neoplastic disorders, together with evidence of clinical, laboratory, and histopathological findings. Full blood count with differential, inflammatory markers, tissue culture, and sensitivity are useful investigations, and skin or mucosal biopsy often shows dense neutrophilic infiltration.6 For blood investigations, peripheral leukocytosis with neutrophilia along with elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) are commonly expected.6 Age-appropriate cancer screening, tests for pregnancy, inflammatory bowel disease, and autoimmune conditions should be included in the workup process to investigate possible associated conditions. Peripheral blood smear, assessment for paraproteinemia, and bone marrow biopsy for hematological malignancy are useful for further investigations in a child presenting without any obvious cause of lesions.

Systemic corticosteroid is used as the main therapeutic drug for Sweet syndrome; however, other steroid-sparing drugs such as potassium iodide and colchicine are also available as a first line of treatment.4 Subsequent improvement or resolution of the dermatosis lesions is usually seen following initiation of steroid therapy. In some cases, spontaneous resolution of the skin lesions can also occur without any treatment interventions over the course of months.4

The recurrence rate of Sweet syndrome in children is 45%, with a higher incidence in children with malignancy.3 Cardiovascular complications such as sterile pericarditis, dilatation of great vessels, valvular diseases, or myocardial infection may occur in patients with Sweet syndrome and lead to life-threatening event.3 The overall prognosis of the disease closely depends on the severity of the associated systemic illness and the presence of extracutaneous manifestations.3

The present case highlights the importance of prompt investigation and accurate diagnosis in providing holistic care to the patient. Oral lesions may manifest first, preceding other systemic signs and symptoms. Any suspicious and unusual oral lesion, especially nonhealing ulcer, should trigger the clinician, mainly dentist, to investigate further, and referral to the appropriate dental or medical specialty is key in achieving an accurate diagnosis.

Limitations

Despite its clinical significance, the present report is limited by its single-case study design. The findings in the study cannot be generalized to all pediatric patients, particularly given the heterogenous clinical presentation of this rare disease. Furthermore, rapid clinical deterioration and subsequent death of the patient limit the assessment of long-term therapeutic response and resolution of the oral lesions following corticosteroid therapy. Future multicenter case series are warranted to better comprehend and characterise the oral manifestations of Sweet syndrome besides allowing evidence-based recommendations for early diagnosis and multidisciplinary management in children with Sweet Syndrome.

Conclusion

Sweet syndrome is a relatively rare disorder with an unknown etiology. Nevertheless, clinicians should be aware of the possible oral manifestations of the disease. Appropriate and detailed screening or investigations should be done to ensure proper diagnosis and management of the disease.
