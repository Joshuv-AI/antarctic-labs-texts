Novel myostatin inhibition by Acacia farnesiana: A network pharmacology workflow decoding its multi-target mechanism in sarcopenic obesity
Authors: Weixin Zhang, Furqan Akhtar, Ji Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sarcopenic obesity (SO) represents a multifaceted metabolic disorder characterized by the pathological coexistence of skeletal muscle atrophy and dysfunctional adiposity, leading to severe metabolic and functional impairments. Myostatin, a key negative regulator of myogenesis, has emerged as a critical therapeutic target for counteracting muscle atrophy associated with SO. In this study, we deployed an integrative network pharmacology strategy to elucidate the pharmacokinetic profiles and therapeutic efficacy of Acacia farnesiana-derived bioactive agents, specifically palmitic acid and α-terpineol, as putative myostatin antagonists. The chemical constituents of A farnesiana were screened using various cheminformatics databases, followed by an evaluation of their pharmacokinetic and drug-likeness properties through SwissADME and ProTox-II. In silico target prediction, conducted using Swiss Target Prediction, was synergized with gene–disease associations from Gene Cards and Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man. Protein–protein interaction networks were constructed using STRING and visualized via Cytoscape, delineated core molecular targets implicated in myostatin signaling. Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway investigation shows interactions between the identified bioactives and sarcopenia-associated signaling pathways, including myofiber regeneration, lipid metabolism, and oxidative stress response. Molecular docking provided structure-based support for the interaction between α-terpineol and estrogen receptor α, consistent with estrogen receptor 1-related findings from network pharmacology analyses, suggesting potential relevance to SO-related signaling pathways. This investigation shows the potential of A farnesiana-derived compounds in modulating muscle-related pathways and mitigating SO, providing a foundation for future preclinical and clinical investigations.

1. Introduction

Sarcopenia is a progressive musculoskeletal disorder characterized by the accelerated loss of muscle mass and function, culminating in clinically significant sequelae such as falls, functional decline, frailty, and mortality.[1] While traditionally associated with aging, emerging evidence suggests that sarcopenia can also manifest in younger populations due to multifactorial etiologies.[2] Sarcopenic obesity (SO), a pathological condition resulting from the coexistence of adiposity-driven metabolic dysregulation and muscle atrophy, exacerbates musculoskeletal impairment by simultaneously diminishing muscle mass, strength, and functional capacity.[3] This dual burden significantly heightens the risk of metabolic disorders, cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), insulin resistance, and mortality. The increasing prevalence of SO, particularly among the elderly, underscores the urgent need for effective therapeutic strategies targeting both muscle degeneration and metabolic dysfunction.[4] Estrogen plays role especially in skeletal muscle maintenance.[5–9] The knockdown of estrogen receptor 1 (ESR1) in muscle cells has been shown to impair fatty acid oxidation, highlighting the crucial role of ESR1 in skeletal muscle function.[10] Additionally, PRKAA1 has been found to be elevated in patients with sarcopenia.[11] The protein kinase AMP-activated catalytic subunit alpha 2 (PRKAA2) and 5′AMPK pathways have been identified as potential mediators of muscle regeneration, particularly in CDN1163-treated mice.[12] These findings collectively suggest that hormonal regulation and metabolic pathways play integral roles in the pathophysiology of sarcopenia, offering promising targets for therapeutic intervention.

Natural bioactive compounds have attracted significant attention for their potential therapeutic applications in chronic diseases, including metabolic disorders and muscle-wasting conditions.[13] Acacia farnesiana was selected for this investigation based on its long-standing ethnopharmacological use and its emerging relevance in metabolic and inflammatory disorders.[14] Traditionally, A farnesiana has been employed for the management of inflammation, infections, and metabolic dysregulation,[15] conditions that share mechanistic overlap with the pathophysiology of SO. Phytochemical analyses have demonstrated that A farnesiana contains a diverse spectrum of bioactive constituents, including monoterpenes, fatty acids, flavonoids, and phenolic compounds, many of which are implicated in oxidative stress regulation, metabolic diseases, and anti-inflammatory effects.[16–20] Given that SO arises from the interplay between muscle degeneration, chronic inflammation, and metabolic imbalance,[21] A farnesiana represents a rational candidate for network-based investigation aimed at identifying multi-target mechanisms relevant to myostatin regulation and muscle metabolism.

Among various medicinal plants, A farnesiana has gained recognition for its diverse pharmacological activities. Acacia farnesiana, commonly known as sweet acacia, is a perennial shrub widely distributed across tropical and subtropical regions. Traditionally, it has been used in ethnomedicine for its anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, antioxidant, and metabolic-regulating properties.[17] Phytochemical studies have revealed that A farnesiana contains a variety of bioactive compounds, including flavonoids, tannins, terpenoids, and fatty acids, which contribute to its therapeutic potential.[22] Among those, 2 major bioactive constituents, palmitic acid and α-terpineol, have been extensively studied for their biological activities. Palmitic acid, a saturated fatty acid, has role in muscle function and energy homeostasis.[23] Emerging evidence suggests that fatty acids, including palmitic acid, influence muscle protein synthesis and mitochondrial function, which are key factors in the pathophysiology of sarcopenia.[24] α-Terpineol, a monoterpene alcohol, exhibits strong oxygen radical scavenging and anti-inflammatory properties. It has been reported to mitigate oxidative stress-induced muscle damage, regulate lipid metabolism, and support cellular energy balance, making it a promising candidate for muscle-wasting conditions.

The selection of A farnesiana in this study is driven by its rich phytochemical composition and its long-standing use in traditional medicine for managing inflammatory and metabolic disorders. Given that SO involves chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and metabolic dysfunction, bioactive compounds may offer significant therapeutic benefits. Given their potent anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects, the bioactive compounds are present in A farnesiana, particularly palmitic acid and α-terpineol, hold promise in modulating muscle metabolism and mitigating oxidative damage. By integrating network pharmacology and pharmacokinetic assessment, this study aims to explore the multi-target mechanism of A farnesiana-derived bioactives as potential myostatin inhibitors. The findings will offer valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying their therapeutic action, providing a strong foundation for the development of novel pharmacological interventions against SO.[25]

Sarcopenia is influenced by ROS, which contributes to mitochondrial dysfunction and disruptions in lipid metabolism.[26] Recent studies have demonstrated that camphene, a naturally occurring monoterpene, can alleviate muscular dystrophy.[27] Similarly, sabinene, another monoterpene, has shown promising effects in mitigating muscle degeneration in preclinical models.[28] Dietary fat subtypes are increasingly recognized for their potential role in regulating muscle mass and function during aging.[29] Fatty acids have demonstrated potential efficacy in improving sarcopenia in preclinical experiments. However, current clinical studies present conflicting results regarding their actual impact on muscle health, necessitating further investigation into the underlying mechanisms. Consequently, future research should include rigorously designed randomized controlled trials to evaluate the efficacy of fatty acid supplementation in clinical populations.[30] Despite the promising therapeutic potential of plant-derived bioactive compounds, their clinical application requires comprehensive assessments encompassing pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic profiles. Pharmacokinetics involves the study of the absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) of bioactive compounds, which directly influence their bioavailability and therapeutic efficacy. Additionally, potential off-target effects, toxicity profiles, and interactions with other biomolecules must be thoroughly evaluated to ensure their safety and effectiveness as therapeutic agents.[30,31]

Network pharmacology has emerged as a powerful methodological framework for investigating the complex interactions among bioactive compounds, their molecular targets, and associated biological pathways. By integrating systems biology, bioinformatics, and cheminformatics, network pharmacology enables a holistic understanding of drug action and promotes the discovery and identification of novel therapeutic targets. This approach is particularly valuable in the context of multi-target diseases such as SO, where traditional single-compound, single-target strategies are often inadequate to address the multifaceted nature of the underlying pathological mechanisms.[32,33]

In this study, we employ computational tools, including SwissTargetPrediction, ProTox-II, and ADME prediction models, to systematically assess the pharmacokinetic properties and therapeutic potential of A farnesiana-derived bioactive compound as potential myostatin inhibitors. SwissTargetPrediction provides insights into the probable protein targets of those bioactives, offering valuable information on their potential mechanisms of action. ProTox-II is utilized to predict the toxicity profiles of these compounds, focusing on critical parameters such as hepatotoxicity, cardiotoxicity, and mutagenicity, which are critical considerations in drug development. Additionally, ADME modeling is employed to evaluate the absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion of plant derivatives, ensuring their suitability for therapeutic applications.[34]

Furthermore, gene–disease association databases, including GeneCards and Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM), are utilized to systematically identify critical genetic factors associated with SO.[35] GeneCards represents a comprehensive database that integrates information from multiple sources to provide insights into gene functions, interactions, and associated diseases. OMIM provides valuable genetic and clinical data related to inherited disorders and complex diseases. By leveraging these resources, we aim to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying SO and identify potential genetic targets for intervention.[36]

The insertion of network pharmacology and pharmacokinetic assessment constitutes a novel approach to systematically evaluating the therapeutic potential of bioactive compounds. This integrative strategy facilitates the identification of bioactives with optimal efficacy and safety profiles, thereby laying a solid foundation for their potential development as therapeutic candidates for SO. Moreover, by specifically targeting myostatin, we aim to provide a mechanistic foundation for muscle regeneration and functional improvement, addressing a critical aspect of SO management.[36]

2. Methodology

2.1. Screening of chemical constituents of A farnesiana

The bioactive constituents of A farnesiana were identified through comprehensive searches in several chemical and pharmacological databases, including Tropicos, Google Scholar, KNApSAcK, and PubMed. SMILES and other information were obtained by searching the PubChem database, which can be accessed at https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/. The pharmacokinetic characteristics of the entire active chemical constituent were then determined using these canonical SMILES.[37]

3. Bioavailability and drug-likeness

The oral bioavailability (>30%) and drug-likeness (DL ≥ 0.18) thresholds were adopted based on commonly used criteria in network pharmacology studies to prioritize compounds with predicted oral exposure and structural similarity to known drugs. However, these thresholds are heuristic and may exclude biologically active compounds with suboptimal predicted pharmacokinetic properties. Furthermore, absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity predictions represent computational approximations rather than experimentally measured pharmacokinetic parameters. Therefore, these criteria were employed as preliminary filters rather than definitive indicators of therapeutic suitability.[38,39]

4. Absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity profiling

All 20 ligands were assessed for toxicity using the ProTox 3.0 web server (https://tox.charite.de/protox3/#). The compounds’ LD50 values were estimated by integrating the website with the corresponding ligands’ SMILES. The compounds were classified into 6 toxicity categories, with Class 1 denoting substances exhibiting the highest acute toxicity and Class 6 representing nontoxic compounds. Among the 51 chemicals studied, only compounds classified as Class 4 or higher were retained for subsequent analyses, thus ensuring an acceptable safety profile for potential therapeutic applications.[40]

Toxicity prediction using ProTox-II primarily estimates acute toxicity parameters, such as LD50, based on animal models and does not account for chronic exposure, metabolite toxicity, tissue-specific accumulation, or long-term biological effects. Consequently, toxicity classifications derived from this tool should be interpreted with caution. The predicted safety profiles serve as preliminary indicators and do not substitute for comprehensive toxicological evaluation, which is required for translational and clinical relevance.

5. Possible sarcopenia target screening

Swiss target prediction is a computational tool that can be used to predict the protein targets of bioactive compounds, such as small molecules and natural products. It was utilized to identify the potential targets of specific active components by inputting their canonical SMILES strings from PubChem. The species of interest was Homo sapiens, and the anticipated results were obtained. The potential targets were retrieved from GeneCards and DisGeNET using “Sarcopenia” as the search term. The targets were combined from these 2 databases, and duplicate genes were excluded from the analysis. The standard names of the targets were determined using UniProtKB, with Homo sapiens selected as the organism. Finally, a Venn diagram was constructed to visually depict the overlapping targets between compound-predicted targets and sarcopenia-associated genes, thereby identifying common molecular targets for further investigation.[41,42]

6. Gene expression and enrichment analysis

Using the Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (https://davidbioinformatics.nih.gov/), the Gene Ontology function, and pathway enrichment for Sarcopenia targets were analyzed. Pathways significantly associated with sarcopenia were identified via Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis. Only pathways with statistical significance (P ≤ .05) were selected and subjected to detailed examination, ensuring the robustness and relevance of the enrichment results.[41]

7. Formation of protein association network

Protein–protein interaction (PPI) analysis was performed to investigate the interactions among sarcopenia-related gene targets using the online STRING tool (version 11.5). The resulting interaction network was then visualized and assessed using Cytoscape (version 3.9.1).

8. Analysis of PPI network

A comprehensive interaction network comprising bioactive compounds, proteins, and pathways was constructed in Cytoscape (version 3.9.1), and redundant data were eliminated. In this network, bioactive compounds and their target genes are represented as nodes, while interactions between compounds and targets are illustrated by edges. The degree, a topological metric calculated using Cytoscape analysis tool, was utilized to measure the significance of a component, target, or pathway in the network.[38,41]

9. Analysis of statistics

Statistical analysis was conducted to evaluate the significance of our findings. Pathway analysis and the Cytohubba plugin were utilized to determine the importance of the results.[38]

No independent statistical hypothesis testing was conducted in this study. Reported P-values were generated by enrichment analysis tools and represent measures of pathway or functional overrepresentation rather than experimental significance testing. Where applicable, false discovery rate correction was applied to control for multiple comparisons. These values should be interpreted as indicators of computational enrichment rather than effect size or causal inference.

10. Molecular docking studies

10.1. Target protein preparation

Following hub gene identification through network analysis, estrogen receptor α (ERα) was selected as a hub-associated target potentially involved in sarcopenia-related pathways. The 3D crystal structure of ERα (PDB entry ID: 9MDV), corresponding to the ERα ligand-binding domain, was retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org).[43] The protein structure was refined using Swiss-PDB Viewer v4.1.0, where incorrect bonds and side chain anomalies were corrected, and missing residues were added to ensure structural accuracy. The processed file was saved as target.pdb for subsequent docking analysis. Ligand-interaction regions and binding pockets were defined using BIOVIA Discovery Studio Visualizer version 4.0 (Accelrys Software Inc., San Diego), which facilitated the identification of potential ligand interaction sites[43,44] (Fig. 1).

Molecular docking visualization showing the 3D binding pose of the ligand within the protein’s ligand-binding region (left), and a 2D interaction map highlighting key amino acid residues involved in ligand binding (right). Residues such as HIS A:309, MET A:310, and ASN A:477.

10.2. Ligand preparation

The phytochemical α-terpineol was selected as the candidate ligand based on its reported antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties potentially relevant to muscle degeneration.[45] The ligand structure was retrieved from the PubChem database (CID: 17100) and converted into PDB format. Energy minimization was performed using ChemBio3D Ultra, and the ligand was subsequently converted into PDBQT format using AutoDock Tools. The prepared ligand file was saved as ligand. PDBQT for docking studies.[44,46]

10.3. Docking studies

Docking simulations were carried out using AutoDock4 integrated with the AMDock interface. A grid box was defined around the ERα ligand-binding region to explore potential binding interactions. The Lamarckian Genetic Algorithm was employed to generate multiple docking poses, and binding affinities, inhibition constants (Ki), and ligand efficiencies were calculated. Interaction analysis was performed using PyMOL and BIOVIA Discovery Studio Visualizer to identify hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic contacts, and electrostatic interactions between α-terpineol and ERα. These docking studies provided preliminary structural insights into the potential engagement of α-terpineol with ERα-associated ligand interaction regions, thereby providing structural support for ERα interactions underlying ESR1-related signaling hypotheses in the context of sarcopenia.[47]

Molecular docking of α-terpineol against ERα (target 9MDV_ERα_filtered) yielded a top-ranked pose with an affinity of −6.24 kcal/mol (estimated Ki ≈ 26.7 µM; ligand efficiency: −0.57). Multiple poses clustered tightly in the range −6.23 to −5.54 kcal/mol (Ki ≈ 27–87 µM; ligand efficiency: −0.57 to −0.50), indicating consistent engagement of a shallow or overlapping pocket rather than a single, uniquely favorable site. Two lower-affinity orientations (−4.72 to −4.67 kcal/mol; Ki ≈ 0.35–0.38 mM; ligand efficiency: −0.43 to −0.42) likely represent solvent-exposed or nonproductive placements.[47] Overall, the micromolar binding estimates and favorable ligand efficiency for a small monoterpenoid alcohol support α-terpineol as a fragment-like scaffold with moderate ERα engagement, and α-terpineol exhibits moderate, fragment-like binding to ERα with a best estimated affinity of −6.24 kcal/mol (Ki ≈ 26.7 µM), consistent across multiple docking poses, supporting its consideration as a low-potency scaffold for exploratory, structure-level investigation of ERα-associated interactions in the context of sarcopenia and warranting further comparative docking and experimental validation.[48]

11. Results and discussion

11.1. Screening of phytoactive compounds

Bioactive compounds from A farnesiana were initially identified using various scientific databases, including Google Scholar, PubMed, and ScienceDirect. A total of 51 phytochemicals were collected and subsequently evaluated for their pharmacokinetic properties, specifically oral bioavailability and DL, using SwissADME and Molsoft, respectively. After screening, 2 compounds were selected for further analysis (Table 1). Palmitic acid and α-terpineol were screened as the target compounds. According to reference[49], palmitic acid makes up around 20% to 30% of all fatty acids and is the most common saturated fatty acid in humans. Palmitic acid can be produced endogenously by de novo lipogenesis or obtained exogenously from food sources. At the cellular and tissue levels, palmitic acid performs a variety of essential biological tasks, and its endogenous production by de novo lipogenesis, a metabolic pathway that dates to the lower evolutionary scale, ensures its constant concentration. Furthermore, reference[50] emphasized that long-chain fatty acids play essential roles in cellular energy metabolism, membrane structure, and autacoid synthesis, and suggested their potential involvement in sarcopenia pathogenesis. We looked at the relationship between the serum long-chain fatty acid profile and sarcopenia in CVD patients. Green cardamom essential oils include an alcoholic component called α-terpineol, which lowers insulin resistance and inflammation.[51]

However, palmitic acid is an endogenous saturated fatty acid with well-documented context-dependent effects, including associations with lipotoxicity, inflammation, and insulin resistance under conditions of excess. Therefore, palmitic acid should not be interpreted as a therapeutic agent but rather as a metabolic node within sarcopenia-associated signaling networks.[52] In contrast, α-terpineol exhibits a more plausible protective profile due to its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, warranting prioritization for further investigation.[53]

The 200 related targets corresponding to the 2 selected bioactive compounds from A farnesiana were predicted using SwissTarget Prediction. This integrated database search yielded a total of 424 sarcopenia-related genes, providing a substantial dataset for subsequent network pharmacology analyses.

14. PPI network

Although 15 overlapping targets were initially identified between compound-predicted targets and sarcopenia-associated genes, subsequent PPI analysis using STRING excluded nodes lacking known or confident interaction evidence.[55] This filtering resulted in a connected subnetwork comprising 10 genes, which was subsequently used for topological and hub gene analysis. Therefore, hub gene identification was performed exclusively on this connected interaction network.[55,56]

The 15 common genes identified through the previous analysis were further examined using the STRING.EMBL database. This network, illustrating functional interactions among sarcopenia-related molecular targets, was visualized and analyzed using Cytoscape software (Fig. 3). To assess the network’s topological properties, the network analyzer plugin within Cytoscape was employed. The resulting network consists of 2 distinct but closely related clusters, comprising 10 nodes interconnected by 18 edges, with an average node degree of ~4 connections. The network exhibits a diameter of 3 and a radius of 2, signifying the longest and shortest paths between any 2 nodes, respectively. Furthermore, the average characteristic path length of 1.528 demonstrates efficient connectivity within the network. Network heterogeneity (0.425) and network centralization (0.482) collectively indicate moderate variability in node connectivity and a fairly distributed structure without extreme central nodes. The top 10 hub genes within this network were identified using the CytoHubba plugin in Cytoscape (Table 2 and Fig. 4). The protein–drug interaction network identified various sarcopenia-related genes (hub genes): ESR1, ESR2, NCOA3, NCOA6, androgen receptor (AR), POLR2D, POLR2G, PRKAA2, FABP4, and CCDC62.

Lee et al.[57] reported that sarcopenia is characterized by an age-associated decline in skeletal muscle mass and functional capacity. Sarcopenia has a decline in skeletal muscle mass and functional capacity due to age. The study found that FABP3 is upregulated in aged muscles, disrupting homeostasis through lipid remodeling. Overexpression alters membrane lipid composition, leading to impaired protein synthesis and delayed muscle recovery. Knockdown restores youthful lipid composition, alleviates ER stress, and enhances protein synthesis and muscle recovery.

Sarcopenia is characterized by progressive skeletal muscle atrophy, partly resulting from alterations in muscle metabolism. AMP-activated protein kinase serves as a crucial regulator of skeletal muscle metabolic pathways in skeletal muscle, influencing numerous cellular processes that become dysregulated with advancing age.[58] Specifically, PRKAA2, which encodes the catalytic alpha-2 subunit of AMP-activated protein kinase, exhibits AMP-activated kinase activity, ATP-binding activity, and protein-macromolecule adaptor activity. PRKAA2 participated in several key metabolic processes, including cellular responses to glucose, fatty acid homeostasis, and positive regulation or glycolysis. Furthermore, it is located within several cellular components, including apical plasma membrane, dendrites, and neuronal cell bodies, and forms part of the nucleotide-activated protein kinase complex. Human ortholog of this gene, PRKAA2, has been associated with type 2 diabetes mellitus.[59]

AR is a protein-coding gene involved in diverse physiological functions. Diseases associated with AR include androgen insensitivity, partial, and spinal and bulbar muscular atrophy, x-linked 1.[60] Estrogen has a complex effect on muscle physiology. Under physiological settings, estrogen plays a vital role in muscle.[61] By preserving the structural integrity and functional ability of myosin, which is necessary for muscular contraction and force production, estrogen, acting through its receptors, has been proposed to improve muscle quality.[61–64]

Sarcopenia is a multi-cause disease with risk factors including lifestyle, hormonal and inflammatory imbalances, disruption in protein synthesis and degradation, and changes in neuromuscular architecture. Sarcopenia often occurs alongside a range of chronic conditions, including CVDs, various cancers, chronic kidney disease, chronic nonobstructive pulmonary conditions, endocrine disorders, and autoimmune rheumatic diseases. The imbalance between muscle synthesis and degradation is primarily driven by suppressed skeletal muscle cell proliferation pathways and heightened activation of apoptotic signaling, contributing to disease development. Moreover, shared pathological mechanisms, such as inadequate nutrition, sedentary lifestyle, insulin resistance, systemic inflammation, hormonal imbalances, oxidative stress, autophagy, and programmed cell death, link both CVDs and sarcopenia. There is a strong bidirectional relationship between sarcopenia and cardiovascular disorders, where each condition can worsen the outcomes of the other.[65,66] CVDs further intensify the negative effects of sarcopenia, including risks of falling, bone fractures, physical frailty, cachexia, increased hospital admissions, and higher mortality rates. Conversely, individuals with sarcopenia also exhibit a significantly higher incidence of cardiovascular issues such as heart failure, hypertension, atherosclerosis, and coronary heart disease.[67]

15. Myostatin inhibition and pathway modulation by A farnesiana bioactives

According to the previous and recent assumptions, illustrate that the derived compounds of A farnesiana such as palmitic acid and α-terpineol have promising potential as myostatin inhibitor for controlling SO.[36,41] However, the studies of[3,12] also stated that muscle atrophy, obesity, and insulin resistance are the major regulators, which are largely influenced by myostatin. Previous in silico studies predicted that these compounds modulate critical pathways in mechanisms by targeting hub genes such as ESR1, ESR2, PRKAA2, and FABP4, while stating that these genes are involved in muscle metabolism, including adipocytokine signaling and as well prolactin-mediated pathways.[41,42] Furthermore, experimental validity of stated assumption evidenced by the rodent model explains that A farnesiana extracts have the ability to improve glucose uptake, mitochondrial function, and energy expenditure while contrast reducing inflammation and oxidative stress.[17,24] In addition to that, the therapeutic potential of these bioactive compounds is further supported by the fact that myostatin inhibition has the capabilities to enhance increases in muscle mass and strength when amplify in paired with resistance training and essential amino acids.[30] However, clinical translation still requires further investigation into pharmacokinetics, safety, and functional outcomes in SO populations.[40,68]

16. Molecular docking validation

In the current study, AutoDock Vina was used to conduct molecular docking simulations with the objective of bridging the gap between in silico predictions and biological activity. The findings indicate that palmitic acid and α-terpineol exhibit foundational binding affinities toward the crystallographic structures of key hub targets, including PRKAA2, ERα, and myostatin (GDF8). Notably, α-terpineol displayed consistent docking interactions within the myostatin binding region, suggesting a potential structural basis for its involvement in myostatin-associated signaling.[69] Mechanistically, these advantages were linked to reduced blood inflammatory markers such as TNF-α and IL-6, decreased skeletal muscle myostatin protein levels, and enhanced histology indicators of muscle health.[70] The therapeutic potential of A farnesiana bioactive in regulating myostatin signaling and reducing the intricate pathology of SO is supported by the agreement between the physiological improvements and the structure-based computationally predicted multi-target mechanisms.[71]

This study was designed as a computational, hypothesis-generating investigation integrating network pharmacology, target prediction, and molecular docking approaches. All chemical, genetic, and pathway associations were derived from publicly available databases, which provide curated and predictive relationships rather than experimentally demonstrative evidence. Consequently, the findings represent associative biological hypotheses rather than causal or functional validation. While silico frameworks are valuable for prioritizing candidate compounds and molecular targets, experimental validation through in vitro, in vivo, and biophysical assays remains essential to confirm biological relevance and therapeutic potential.

The identification of phytochemical constituents in this study was based on literature mining and curated chemical databases rather than direct experimental extraction or quantitative profiling. As a result, compound abundance, concentration-dependent bioavailability, tissue distribution, and metabolic stability were not experimentally assessed. Additionally, the physiological presence and therapeutic relevance of these compounds in vivo cannot be conclusively determined from database-derived information alone. Future investigations incorporating liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry profiling and pharmacokinetic studies are required to validate compound presence and biological relevance.

17. Conclusion

The study underscores the promising therapeutic potential of bioactive compounds derived from A farnesiana for managing SO. By integrating network pharmacokinetic assessment and gene–disease association databases, the study provides valuable insights into the pathophysiology of SO and identifies specific molecular targets that may facilitate therapeutic intervention. The findings suggest that bioactive compounds, such as monoterpenes and fatty acids, exhibit significant promises as novel therapeutic agents targeting sarcopenia-related molecular pathways. However, further preclinical studies remain essential to comprehensively validate the efficacy, safety, and therapeutic feasibility of these compounds in practical clinical settings.
