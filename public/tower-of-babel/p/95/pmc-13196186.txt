No improvement in parental vaccine hesitancy in the Netherlands in 2023 versus 2022
Authors: Marthe Knijff, Alies van Lier, Marion de Vries, Jeanne-Marie Hament, Minke Holwerda, Ingrid Drijfhout, Hester de Melker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPositive perceptions of the Dutch National Immunisation Programme (NIP) have somewhat declined since the COVID-19 pandemic. To assess parental vaccine hesitancy, we studied whether psychosocial determinants of vaccine uptake (intention, attitudes, trust, deliberation and beliefs) differed between 2022 and 2023.MethodsIn 2022 and 2023, two online cross-sectional surveys were conducted among parents of young (< 3.5 years) and older children (9–14 years) via a panel organisation. Psychosocial determinants were measured on 7-point Likert scales. Chi-square and multivariate logistic regression analyses were used to study differences in ‘negative’ (1–2) and ‘positive’ scores (6–7) of psychosocial determinants among parents of young and of older children between 2022 and 2023.ResultsIn 2022 and 2023, 983 and 1,002 parents of young children and 993 and 1,022 parents of older children participated, respectively (response = 12%, 10%, 21% and 15%, respectively). Most parents were positive about vaccination: e.g. 90.5% of parents of young children and 81.5% of parents of older children indicated their child participates in the NIP in 2023. However, in 2023 compared to 2022, significantly fewer parents of older children indicated their child participates in the NIP (2022 = 85.9%, 2023 = 81.5%), expressed positive attitudes towards the value (2022 = 74.2%, 2023 = 69.4%) and importance (2022 = 74.7%, 2023 = 69.5%) of vaccination and reported high trust towards the NIP vaccinations (2022 = 61.0%, 2023 = 52.8%) and the governments’ recommendations about vaccination (2022 = 54.3%, 2023 = 49.9%). Similar (significant) trends were observed for their beliefs about vaccination. Except for trust in the governments’ vaccination recommendations, these findings were confirmed by the multivariate analysis. Perceptions of parents of young children were similar in 2023 compared to 2022.ConclusionsWhile most parents still held positive views about vaccination in 2023, somewhat fewer parents of older children were positive about vaccination in 2023 compared to 2022. Perceptions of parents of young children did not differ between these years. This suggests parental vaccine hesitancy remained stable or increased between 2022 and 2023. It is important to monitor these vaccine uptake determinants and implement appropriate interventions to sustain high vaccine uptake.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27256-w.Keywords: National Immunisation Programme (NIP), Childhood vaccination, Psychosocial determinants, Vaccine uptake, Vaccine hesitancy, Vaccine confidence, COVID-19 pandemic, Survey

Background

Vaccination is one of the greatest contributors to global health. It has led to the eradication of smallpox in 1980 and near-eradication of polio [1]. According to estimates from the World Health Organisation (WHO), immunisation currently prevents 3.5-5 million deaths every year from diseases like diphtheria, tetanus, pertussis, influenza and measles [2]. Vaccination programmes rely on high and homogeneous vaccine uptake rates in order to reduce the prevalence and incidence of infectious diseases and protect against associated morbidity and mortality [3, 4].

In the Netherlands, children are vaccinated via the Dutch National Immunisation Programme (NIP). At the time of this study, the programme included vaccination against 12 infectious diseases: at three, five and 11 months, children are vaccinated against diphtheria, tetanus, pertussis, polio, hepatitis B, Haemophilus influenzae serotype b (Hib) disease and pneumococcal disease. Vaccination against diphtheria, tetanus, pertussis and polio is repeated at four years of age, and, except for pertussis, again at nine years. Children are vaccinated against measles, mumps and rubella as well as meningococcal ACWY disease at 14 months and again at nine and 14 years respectively. At ten years of age, children also receive vaccination against human papilloma virus (HPV) infection [5] (Fig. 1). After completion of this study, vaccination against rotavirus infection was included in the NIP in 2024 and vaccination against respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) will be implemented from late 2025 onwards. NIP participation is free of charge and voluntary. Children aged < 4 years are vaccinated on an individual basis at well-baby clinics. Subsequent vaccinations (for children aged 9–14 years) are mainly administered by means of group vaccination sessions [5, 6].

The NIP vaccination schedule in 2023

Vaccine uptake rates within the Dutch NIP have always been high. For most childhood vaccinations, coverage is around 90% (with the exception of vaccinations that are offered to older children such as HPV – their uptake tends to be lower). However, in 2023 it was reported that uptake rates within the NIP had slightly declined since the COVID-19 pandemic, while beforehand (in reporting years 2020–2021 - which predominantly covered vaccinations scheduled before the COVID-19 pandemic) they had increased after a prior decrease [7]. In 2024, it was reported that national vaccine uptake in the Netherlands has likely continued to fall among school-aged children, but not among infants and toddlers [8]. In several other European countries, declines in vaccine uptake have also been observed. For 2020, the WHO European Region reported a 1% point decrease, from 95% in 2019 to 94% in 2020, in vaccination coverage of the third dose of diphtheria-tetanus-pertussis-containing vaccine (DTP3) [9]. For 2021–2023, routine immunisation in the WHO European Region averted further COVID-19 backsliding but fell short of full recovery to pre-pandemic levels, with disparities among countries being evident [10–12].

One major challenge to achieving and sustaining high vaccine uptake rates is vaccine hesitancy. The WHO Strategic Advisory Group of Experts (SAGE) Working Group on Vaccine Hesitancy has defined vaccine hesitancy as a “a motivational state of being conflicted about, or opposed to, getting vaccinated; this includes intentions and willingness” [13]. It encompasses a broad spectrum of attitudes and behaviours that are associated with vaccination decision-making, ranging from having some doubts and postponing vaccinations to outright vaccine refusal [14, 15]. Growing evidence suggests that the COVID-19 pandemic has exacerbated vaccine hesitancy [16] or at least led to a potential “spillover” of beliefs associated with COVID-19 vaccination to beliefs about other vaccinations [17]. The European Commission’s State of Vaccine Confidence in the European Union 2022 and UNICEF’s The State of World’s Children 2023 revealed that the public’s perceptions towards the importance, safety and effectiveness of vaccination in general have declined since the pandemic, both across Europe [18, 19] and the globe [19].

In a previous study, we examined how several psychosocial determinants of vaccine uptake had changed in 2022 compared to 2013 among parents of children younger than four years of age eligible for the NIP [20]. The conceptual underpinning of our previous study [20] was based on The Theory of Planned Behaviour [21] and earlier work from Harmsen et al. [22], in which a model of parents’ vaccination intention was developed. The Theory of Planned Behaviour [21] dictates that intention is the strongest predictor of (vaccination) behaviour. Harmsen et al. [22] found that parents’ positive intention to vaccinate their child was best predicted by a positive attitude, having trust in the NIP, high anticipated regret of not vaccinating, low perceived barriers of getting their child vaccinated, not making a deliberate decision, and having positive beliefs about vaccination. In our previous study, we found that proportions of parents with young children with negative perceptions towards vaccination were somewhat greater in 2022 compared to 2013. In 2022, significantly more parents tended to have negative attitudes towards the NIP (3 items: 2013 = 1.4%-3.3%, 2022 = 4.5%-5.9%), a preference of natural infection over vaccination (2013 = 8.3%, 2022 = 23.8%) and lower trust in the NIP (2013 = 5.2%, 2022 = 8.3%). In addition, more parents in 2022 tended to have negative beliefs about the effectiveness and side effects of vaccination. This possibly could have partly explained the decline in NIP vaccine uptake among younger children during the pandemic, in addition to decrease in uptake caused by lack of personnel due to relocation of human resources for the implementation of COVID-19 vaccination (from January 2021 onwards) and social distancing measures delaying group vaccination among older children (spring 2020) as well as limiting attendance of well-baby clinics for parents or children with flu-like symptoms over the course of the pandemic (2020–2022) [20]. Importantly, in 2022 there were indications that among older children (9–14 years) in particular, vaccine uptake had decreased. Therefore, in the 2022 study, an additional group of parents of older children was included to be able to study psychosocial factors regarding vaccine uptake. Parents of older children were found to be largely similar to parents of younger children in terms of vaccine uptake, intention, attitudes, beliefs, trust and deliberation towards the NIP [20].

In the current study, our main objective was to study parental intention, attitudes, trust, deliberation and beliefs towards the current NIP and assess how these are different in 2023 compared to 2022, among parents of young children (aged < 3.5 years) and among parents of older children (aged 9–14 years). Therefore, we incorporated the same vaccine uptake determinants as in the previous studies. As secondary objectives, we assessed parents’ willingness towards adopting potential future expansions of the NIP (i.e. additional vaccinations) and self-reported changes in their opinions towards the NIP, and whether there were differences between 2022 and 2023 in these regards. In order to allow for international comparisons, we also examined the priority questions from the WHO’s framework for Behavioural and Social Drivers (BeSD) of vaccination [23], but only among parents in 2023.

Methods

Study population and design

Two cross-sectional surveys with similar design were conducted in July 2022 and July 2023 via an online survey panel organisation (Flycatcher Internet Research, ISO 20252). The Flycatcher panel is an opt-in panel and consists of more than 10,000 Dutch adults. Panel participants actively registered their consent to participate in surveys when they joined the panel. Flycatcher panel members receive a small reward (points) for each completed questionnaire, which can be redeemed for a gift card. Furthermore, panel members are automatically enrolled in a quarterly lottery, where participation in surveys partly determines the chances of winning [24]. Flycatcher occasionally collaborates with other panel agencies to carry out survey research. In our case, both surveys were conducted using participants from Flycatcher’s partner agencies. The 2023 survey employed the same two partner agencies as were used in 2022 and incorporated an additional third partner agency to increase representativeness of the study population.

In both 2022 and 2023, surveys were conducted among parents with at least one child aged < 3.5 years (parents of young children) and parents with at least one child aged 9–14 years (parents of older children). Parents were predominantly1 invited to participate via email, with a link to an online questionnaire about vaccination. The parents that participated in 2023 were different from the ones in 2022. In 2023, 10,064 parents of young children and 6,926 parents of older children were approached between July 11th and July 21st, 2023, and between July 11th and July 17th, 2023, respectively2. Reminders were sent to several less well-educated parents who had not (fully) completed the questionnaire to enhance representativeness of the samples. Questionnaires were accessible until at least a 1,000 parents per group had completed them. To improve sample representativeness, Flycatcher stratified samples by sex and educational level and weighted data for sex and educational level based on population statistics from Statistics Netherlands (Supplemental Table 1). The procedures for the 2022 survey were similar and are described elsewhere [20].

The Centre for Clinical Expertise at the National Institute for Public Health and the Environment (RIVM) assessed the research proposal and concluded that this research was not subject to the Dutch law for Medical Research Involving Human Subjects (WMO) [25] or with the European Union (EU) Clinical Trial Directive (2001/20/EC). Therefore, our study was exempted from seeking further approval from an ethical research committee or institutional review board.

Questionnaire development

Survey questions addressed parents’ demographic characteristics, self-reported vaccine uptake (of their child) and perceptions towards vaccination. The questionnaire used in the 2023 survey was based on the 2022 survey, and thus almost identical (see Supplemental Table 2 in Knijff et al. [20]). Any differences between 2022 and 2023 regarding survey questions used in the present study are described in Table 1. To be able to make comparisons with other countries, six questions were added to the 2023 survey based on the WHO’s BeSD framework [23] (Table 1). Some of these resembled other survey questions. Questionnaires were designed by researchers from the RIVM in consultation with Flycatcher. In both 2022 and 2023, Flycatcher pre-tested the questionnaires before data collection.

Survey questions

Table 1 provides an overview of the survey questions and corresponding measurements that were used to assess parents’ perceptions towards vaccination in the current study.

Vaccine uptake and its psychosocial determinants

We measured parents’ self-reported vaccine uptake of their child by asking them whether their child participated in the NIP (answer categories: ‘yes’, ‘partially’, ‘no’ and ‘don’t know’) (Table 1). The selected vaccine uptake determinants and corresponding items were identical to the ones used in the 2022 survey, except for the item measuring intention (Table 1). Their selection was based on a previous study conducted by Harmsen et al. [22], in which they developed a model of parents’ vaccination intention. All items were measured on 7-point Likert scales. Parents’ (only those with a young child) intention towards potential future expansions of the NIP with additional vaccinations was also evaluated using 7-point Likert scales (Table 1). To ensure sufficient information was available for respondents about disease specific aspects for infectious diseases that the NIP may incorporate in the future, the questionnaire contained hyperlinks to government websites targeted to the general public.

Self-reported changes in opinion towards the NIP

Self-reported changes in parents’ opinions towards the NIP were assessed using two items, which were measured on 5-point Likert scales (Table 1). Parents were asked to what extent their opinion about vaccinations within the NIP had changed, both since they had (or had not) vaccinated their (oldest) child and because of the COVID-19 pandemic.

WHO’s BeSD framework

According to the WHO’s BeSD framework [23], behavioural and social drivers of vaccination can be grouped and measured into four domains: (1) thinking and feeling about vaccines, (2) social processes that drive or inhibit vaccination, (3) motivation (or hesitancy) to seek vaccination and (4) practical issues involved in seeking and receiving vaccination. In this study, we incorporated the five priority questions from the BeSD childhood vaccination survey for caregivers, which addressed parents’ intention to get their child vaccinated (categorical variable), confidence in vaccine benefits (4-point Likert scale), family norms (dichotomous variable), knowledge about where to get their child vaccinated (dichotomous variable) and perceptions towards the affordability of vaccination (4-point Likert scale) (Table 1). We also incorporated one of the sociodemographic questions, which asked parents about their child’s vaccination status (categorical variable) (Table 1). The WHO’s BeSD questions were translated to Dutch and, in some cases, slightly altered to fit the way the Dutch NIP is organised. They were incorporated into the 2023 survey only.

Statistical analysis

We used descriptive analysis to describe parents’ demographic characteristics, self-reported vaccine uptake and its psychosocial determinants, self-reported changes in parents’ opinion towards the NIP and WHO’s BeSD questions. Comparisons were made between (1) parents of young children in 2022 and 2023 and (2) between parents of older children in 2022 and 2023. Demographic differences were analysed with two-sample t-tests for parents’ age, Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for their children’s age and chi-square tests for all other demographic variables. To assess whether parental perceptions towards vaccination had become more negative and/or positive in 2023 compared to 2022, scores of psychosocial determinants were dichotomised into ‘negative’ (score 1–2; range 1–7) vs. ’other’ (score 3–7) and ‘positive’ (score 6–7) vs. ‘other’ (score 1–5) and analysed with chi-square tests. We used multivariate logistic regression analysis to study differences in ‘negative’ (1–2) and ‘positive’ (6–7) scores of psychosocial determinants as well, while controlling for several demographic variables. Similarly to our previous study [20], we adjusted models for parents’ age, income level and country of birth. In addition, models were adjusted for parents’ total number of children and the age of their oldest child. We took the same approach for vaccine uptake and self-reported changes in parents’ opinion towards the NIP. Hereby, vaccine uptake was dichotomised into ‘negative’ (not vaccinated) vs. ‘other’ (fully vaccinated, partially vaccinated or don’t know) and ‘positive’ (fully vaccinated) vs. ‘other’ (not vaccinated, partially vaccinated or don’t know). Self-reported changes in parents’ opinion towards the NIP were dichotomised into ‘negative’ (score 1–2; range 1–5) vs. ‘other’ (score 3–5) and ‘positive’ (score 4–5) vs. ‘other’ (score 1–3).

For parents in 2023, associations between vaccine uptake (categorical variable) and psychosocial determinants (continuous scale; range 1–7) were analysed with Kruskal-Wallis tests. Spearman’s rank correlations (2-tailed) were calculated between all psychosocial determinants (continuous scale; range 1–7) separately. Results for parents in 2022 are described elsewhere [20]. Associations between WHO’s BeSD indicators and the vaccine uptake determinants derived from the model developed by Harmsen et al. [22] were analysed with chi-square tests (vaccine uptake), Kruskal-Wallis tests (intention) and Spearman’s rank correlations (attitude towards importance of vaccination).

All analyses were performed on weighted data. Items were reverse-coded when applicable. P-values below 0.05 were considered significant. Analyses were conducted in R 4.3.0 and RStudio 2023.3.1.446 using the survey package.

Sensitivity analysis

In a first sensitivity analysis, to establish whether results were robust to using different cut-offs for the psychosocial determinants of vaccine uptake, we dichotomised determinants into ‘negative’ (score 1–3; range 1–7) vs. ‘other’ (score 4–7) and ‘positive’ (score 5–7) vs. ‘other’ (score 1–4). Second, we included age of the youngest child (within the target age group) as a covariate into our models rather than age of the oldest child. Third, we performed analyses for those parents who indicated that their child participates in the NIP only (although on the one hand part of the research question, representativeness and comparability of the data may be affected by differences in terms of vaccine uptake between 2022 and 2023). For the sake of conciseness, sensitivity analyses were restricted to the multivariate analysis only.

Results

Response

In 2023, 1,002 parents of young children (response rate: 10%) and 1,022 parents of older children (response rate: 15%) participated. In 2022, 983 parents of young children (estimated response rate: 12%) and parents of older children (response rate: 21%) participated [20]3.

Demographic characteristics

Parents of young children

Respondents with young children in 2023 were comparable to those in 2022 in terms of their age, sex, educational level, household income level, country of birth and region of residence (Table 2). Although they differed significantly from respondents in 2022 in the sense that they had slightly fewer and slightly younger children, these differences were so small they are probably irrelevant (Table 2).

Parents of older children

In 2023, respondents with older children were significantly younger than those in 2022 (Table 2). In addition, in 2023 more respondents with older children had an income below average or an average income and significantly fewer of them did not know their income level or preferred to withhold from answering compared to 2022. All other demographic characteristics were comparable (or not meaningfully different) (Table 2).

Vaccine uptake, intention, attitudes, trust, deliberation and beliefs towards the current NIP

Significant associations were found between vaccine uptake and all psychosocial determinants among 2023 respondents (Supplemental Table 2). Almost all psychosocial determinants correlated significantly and moderately-to-strongly among 2023 respondents (Supplemental Table 3). This was also the case for respondents in 2022 [20].

Overall, most respondents had positive perceptions towards the NIP in both 2022 and 2023 (Table 3). Most 2023 respondents reported that their child participates in the NIP, they intended to vaccinate their child according to the NIP in the future and they trusted the NIP vaccinations. In addition, they expressed positive attitudes towards vaccination and considered vaccinating their child self-evident in 2023. Finally, most 2023 respondents believed that the NIP is beneficial for the protection of their child, vaccinating their child protects the health of other children and vaccination protects against complications from infectious diseases. This was also the case for 2022 respondents.

Parents of older children

Respondents with older children tended to have somewhat less positive perceptions about vaccination in 2023 compared to 2022 (Tables 3 and 4). Univariate analysis indicated slightly but significantly smaller proportions of respondents with older children reported that their child participates in the NIP, had positive attitudes about the value and importance of vaccination and trusted the NIP vaccinations and the governments’ recommendations about vaccination in 2023 compared to 2022. Likewise, slightly (but significantly) fewer respondents with older children believed that vaccination offers sufficient protection against infectious diseases, is beneficial for the protection of their own child as well as that of other children, protects against complications from infectious diseases, does not contain harmful substances and does not lead to severe side effects in 2023 compared to 2022 (Table 3). Except for trust in the governments’ recommendations about vaccination, all of these findings were confirmed by the multivariate analysis (Table 4).

In contrast to the findings concerning positive perceptions, proportions of respondents with older children with negative perceptions were mostly equal in 2023 and 2022 (Table 3). In both univariate and multivariate analysis, respondents with older children in 2023 only differed significantly from respondents with older children in 2022 in the sense that slightly (but significantly) fewer respondents believed that vaccination offers insufficient protection in 2023 compared to 2022 (Tables 3 and 4).

Intention towards potential future expansions of the NIP

In general, respondents with young children had a somewhat more positive intention towards potential future expansions of the NIP in 2023 compared to 2022 (Tables 3 and 4). Univariate analysis showed that proportions of respondents with a positive vaccination intention towards chickenpox, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) infection, meningococcal B disease and influenza were slightly but significantly greater and that proportions of respondents with a negative vaccination intention towards chickenpox were slightly but significantly smaller in 2023 compared to 2022 (Table 3). Multivariate analysis confirmed all of these findings, except for intention towards RSV vaccination (Table 4).

Self-reported changes in opinion towards the NIP

Overall, most respondents in 2022 and 2023 declared that their opinion about the NIP had not changed, neither since they had (or had not) vaccinated their child nor because of the COVID-19 pandemic (Table 5). In both 2022 and 2023, slightly greater proportions of respondents indicated that their opinion had become more negative rather than positive due to the pandemic (Table 5).

Sensitivity analysis

Psychosocial determinants of vaccine uptake

Except for attitudes towards vaccination among respondents with older children (which tended to lose their statistical significance), sensitivity analyses showed that most results tended to be relatively robust to using different cut-off values for the psychosocial determinants of vaccine uptake (Supplemental Table 4), correcting for the youngest child’s age rather than the oldest child’s age (Supplemental Table 5) and only including data from respondents whose child participates in the NIP (Supplemental Table 6).

Self-reported changes in opinion towards the NIP

Results concerning self-reported changes in parents’ opinion towards the NIP were robust to correcting for the youngest child’s age rather than the oldest child’s age, but among respondents with older children only (Supplemental Table 7). None of the aforementioned findings were robust to only incorporating data from respondents whose child participates in the NIP (Supplemental Table 7).

WHO BeSD framework

Results from the WHO’s BeSD survey framework are presented in Table 6. In 2023, the majority of both respondents with young children and respondents with older children reported that their child had received all applicable NIP vaccinations, intended for their child to receive all NIP vaccinations, considered vaccination moderately or very important, indicated most of their friends and family wanted them to get their child vaccinated, knew where to get their child vaccinated and considered paying for the costs of getting their child vaccinated moderately or very easy (Table 6). These results are consistent with our findings for the vaccine uptake determinants described above. For all assessed variables (vaccine uptake, intention and attitude towards importance of vaccination), significant associations were found between WHO’s BeSD indicators and Harmsen et al.’s vaccine uptake determinants (data not shown).

Discussion

Our study aimed to study parental intention, attitudes, trust, deliberation and beliefs towards the current NIP and assess how these are different in 2023 compared to 2022, among parents of young children (aged < 3.5 years) and among parents of older children (aged 9–14 years). Our results indicate that most parents were positive about vaccination. Parents of young children tended to have similar perceptions about vaccination in 2023 compared to 2022, but they were more positive about potential future expansions of the NIP in 2023. However, somewhat fewer parents of older children had positive perceptions about vaccination in 2023 compared to 2022. These findings suggest that parental vaccine hesitancy has remained stable or increased in 2023.

Parents of older children especially were more likely to indicate that their opinion about the NIP had become more negative rather than positive due to the COVID-19 pandemic. There have been various reports on increased vaccine hesitancy towards routine childhood vaccination following the COVID-19 pandemic [18, 19]. The leading hypothesis is that the COVID-19 pandemic has exacerbated vaccine hesitancy towards non-COVID-19 vaccines. Possible explanations that have been put forward include old and new issues, such as vaccine safety concerns, spread of misinformation, prepandemic mistrust in pharmaceutical companies, governments and health institutions that has exacerbated during the pandemic, spillover between beliefs about COVID-19 vaccines and routine childhood vaccines and backlash following COVID-19 policies, measures and vaccination mandates [16, 26, 27].

In our study, we only found differences in parental vaccine hesitancy between 2022 and 2023 for parents with older children. Similarly, Vashist et al. [28] studied changes in vaccine hesitancy among parents of children belonging to different age groups in the United States between 2019 and 2022. They found that parental vaccine hesitancy increased after the authorization of the COVID-19 vaccine for children aged 5–11 years and declined for children aged 6 months-4 years. However, they also found that parental vaccine hesitancy was lowest among adolescents aged 12–17 years throughout the study period compared to other age groups, and did not change [28]. Likewise, a study by Schelbar et al. [29] found a minimal change in perceptions related to the HPV vaccine due to the COVID-19 pandemic among parents of 13–17 year-old children in a small Kansas county. However, they did observe decreased rates of HPV vaccination during this time [29].

A possible reason for our finding that vaccine hesitancy has slightly increased among parents of older children but not among parents of young children, may be the way in which vaccine administration is organised among different age groups in the Netherlands. Children aged < 4 years are vaccinated on an individual basis at well-baby clinics, during routine health check-ups [5, 6]. Subsequent vaccinations are mainly administered by means of group vaccination sessions, usually once or twice a year in a sports hall or other large venue [6]. At the well-baby clinics, there is more opportunity for conversations between healthcare professionals (HCPs) and parents and context specific dialogue is considered an effective approach for overcoming vaccine hesitancy [30, 31]. In addition, it has been shown that HCPs can have a positive influence on parents’ vaccination decisions [32]. There is a strong correlation between parents’ perceptions of vaccination and those of HCPs [33] and individuals who receive a recommendation from an HCP tend to have a higher vaccine acceptability [34, 35]. This may be a possible explanation for the different results we found among parents of young and of older children.

Our results also indicate that parents of young children tended to have a more positive intention towards potential future expansions of the NIP in 2023 compared to 2022, especially regarding chickenpox, meningococcal B disease and influenza vaccination. While this could be attributed to the fact that parents’ perceptions towards these candidate vaccines allow for “more room for improvement”, it is also plausible that there was increased awareness about their existence among parents in 2023. The COVID-19 pandemic was followed by a rise in the incidence of other infectious diseases, including chickenpox, meningococcal disease, rotavirus infection, RSV infection, hepatitis A and influenza [36, 37]. Furthermore, a recent advisory report by the Health Council of the Netherlands for vaccination against meningococcal B disease in 2022 [38] may have contributed to increased media attention.

To our knowledge, our study is among the first to use the WHO’s BeSD framework [23] in a European setting. To be able to compare findings internationally, we encourage other countries to do the same. Our results indicate that most parents in 2023 took part in the NIP, had positive intention towards vaccination and indicated most of their family and friends were in support of them getting their child vaccinated. In addition, most of them had confidence in vaccine benefits and experienced little practical issues. These results align with our findings for the vaccine uptake determinants that were based on the model developed by Harmsen et al. [22].

The most important limitation of this study, as in the 2022 study [20], is the low response rate, which could have affected generalisability of our findings. Indeed, even though weighted data were representative of the Dutch population in terms of sex and educational level, this was not necessarily the case for parents’ country of birth, household income level and region of residence (https://www.cbs.nl/en-gb/). It is also important to note that weighting does not necessarily guarantee that findings can be generalised [39].

Moreover, Flycatcher uses a non-probability sampling method, which may also decrease generalisability. Those with very strong pro- or anti-vaccine views might have been more likely to respond, resulting in an overrepresentation of either positive or negative views. Furthermore, there might be an overlap between those who are inclined to participate in research and those who are inclined to participate in vaccination programmes, and vice versa. In 2023, significantly fewer parents of older children indicated that their child had been vaccinated. Although on the one hand part of our research question, this may also indicate reduced representativeness and comparability of our data. Sensitivity analyses, nevertheless, revealed that most results were robust to only incorporating data from parents whose child had been vaccinated.

Additionally, comparability of the 2022 and 2023 study may have been affected by the use of an additional partner agency by Flycatcher. In the 2023 sample, parents of older children differed significantly with regards to age and household income level compared to the 2022 sample. As previous research has shown lower vaccine uptake among children of parents not born in the Netherlands, and in regions with higher religious objections to vaccination or lower socio-economic status, this may have affected our findings [40]. However, adjustment for age, income level, country of birth as well as number of children and age of the eldest child in the multivariate analysis did confirm all findings of the univariate analysis, except for governmental trust among parents of older children.

Another limitation to consider is the possibility of residual confounding. Although we corrected for several demographic variables in the comparison between 2022 and 2023, we may have failed to account for other relevant factors. As our data were cross-sectional in nature, this may have affected comparability of our samples. One other limitation is the use of a self-reported measure for vaccine uptake, which might be susceptible to response bias. Finally, we did not formally assess whether the determinants we studied are vaccine uptake determinants. However, this was not the purpose of our study, and we did observe significant associations between vaccine uptake and the studied psychosocial determinants and between all psychosocial determinants separately.

In 2024, it was reported that national vaccine uptake within the Dutch NIP has likely further declined among school-aged children, but not among infants and toddlers [8]. This is in line with our finding that vaccine hesitancy has slightly increased among parents of older children, but not among parents of young children. As they play a significant role in determining vaccine uptake rates, it is crucial to monitor parental perceptions towards vaccination and identify any concerns or misconceptions that may exist. Appropriate interventions should be implemented to address vaccine hesitancy and sustain high vaccine uptake rates. To gain more in-depth insight into this, the RIVM has established the SocioVax Programme: social science research on vaccination [41]. Interventions may include dialogue-based approaches, improved accessibility of vaccination services, education and awareness campaigns, as well as targeted outreach efforts to communities with lower vaccination coverage rates. This, in turn, can help to improve overall public health and prevent the spread of infectious diseases.

Conclusion

In 2023, somewhat fewer parents of older children were positive about vaccination compared to 2022. Perceptions of parents of young children did not differ between 2022 and 2023. Most parents, however, were still positive about vaccination in 2023. These results may explain why vaccine uptake within the NIP has likely declined among school-aged children but not among infants and toddlers, as reported in 2024 [8]. It is important to monitor parental perceptions towards vaccination and implement appropriate interventions to sustain high vaccine uptake.
