Pharmacists’ approach to oncology medicine shortages: results of a cross-sectional survey in Pakistan
Authors: Sundus Shukar, Muhammad Hassaan Shahid, Jinwei Zhang, Shayan Muhammad Din, Faiz Ullah Khan, Yu Fang, Caijun Yang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe study estimated the shortages of oncology medicines in Pakistan, their causes, impacts, mitigation strategies, and possible interventions.DesignCross-sectional survey.SettingOncology pharmacists working at 43 oncology settings (out of 80) from five regions of Pakistan (four provinces (Punjab, Sindh, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and Baluchistan) and one federal territory (Islamabad)) were approached.ParticipantsOncology pharmacists with more than 1 year of experience were selected using stratified random sampling, and data were collected using a questionnaire from September 2021 to January 2022.Primary and secondary outcome measuresTo estimate the prevalence of oncology medicine shortages in Pakistan and establish recommendations to overcome them.ResultsOf 167 responded pharmacists, 87% experienced shortages in their practice. Most respondents (50%) experienced both oncology and support agent shortages. It was a current problem in hospitals (58%) and increased with time, but the situation varied across regions (p=0.007). Mainly shortages occur half-yearly (p=0.001) and last for <3 months. Injectable (56.8%) and branded drugs (44.9%) were short. The most frequently mentioned drugs affected by shortages were etoposide, paclitaxel, dacarbazine, bleomycin and carboplatin. Usually, distributors (51.5%) notify the pharmacists about the shortages, and federal agencies (36%) are responsible for these shortages. Distributors (53.4%) were the main source of supply, and shortages were encountered by them as well. The impacts included delayed care (25%) and suboptimal outcomes (23%) on patients, extra time (32%) for staff, prioritisation issues (31%) for pharmacists, delayed clinical trials (60.5%) and increased drugs prices (52.1%). Some hospitals have reporting systems (39.5%) and recording (29.3%) drug shortages. The shortages were managed using available alternative options (21%) and redistributing (21%) the currently available stock.ConclusionPakistan’s healthcare system is affected by oncology medicine shortages. The government should establish a cancer registry and drug shortage platform, revise drug prescribing/pricing policies and practice penalties for breaching regulations. Oncology medicines must be widely available to avoid the grey market.Keywords: Health policy, ONCOLOGY, Epidemiology, PUBLIC HEALTH

Introduction

Oncology medicine shortages are persistent and severe global concerns.1 2 It challenged global healthcare, exceptionally patient care.3 4 More than 65% of admitted patients with cancer receive chemotherapy in their treatments, and a single shortage of chemotherapeutic drugs would destabilise the regimen, resulting in insufficient drug administration and therapy interruptions. It will become impossible if the complementary medicine options are few.5–8 Moreover, oncology and related supportive medicines are costly9 10 and common in short supply in developed and developing countries,11 putting an enormous burden on patients and healthcare institutions.12–14

Shortages of oncology medicines occur worldwide. A study in Saudi Arabia found shortages of both oncology and supportive medicines,3 whereas shortages of 73 oncology medicines were found in Iran.15 Another study from Moroccan hospitals found that 67.3% of patients were affected by oncology medicine shortages.16 A European Association of Hospital Pharmacists survey found that oncology medicine shortages were increased from 39% in 2018 to 47% in 2019.17 In contrast, the University of Utah Drug Information Services (UUDIS) stated that the number of shortages fluctuated between 174 and 320 in a given quarter in the USA.18 Two surveys in the USA found shortages of oncology medicines by 66%.12

Pakistan has also been affected by oncology medicine shortages.19 20 Cancer has become a significant public health threat in Pakistan due to the predicted 319 395 new cases in both sexes and all ages from 2020 to 2040.21 22 On one hand, cancer cases are increasing; on the other hand, the availability of oncology medicines is facing problems.23 There is no local production, and most oncology medicines are procured from other countries. The Drug Regulatory Authority of Pakistan (DRAP) is responsible for managing the supply of medicines.19 23 24 However, due to the compromised role of DRAP, the regulation for the registration, renewal and import processes is complex, which leads to frequent medicine shortages. Moreover, no national cancer registry, estimates, or data on cancer incidence or prevalence further complicate the issue.23 25

Solving the shortage problem of oncology medicines requires multifaceted effects, and understanding the prevalence and causes are the prerequisites. However, there is no national drug shortage reporting system.26 27 Moreover, to tackle the increasing cancer morbidity and mortality, confronting oncology medicine shortages is important, especially in developing countries. This study will establish how these shortages paralyse healthcare systems and propose necessary recommendations in countries like Pakistan. Therefore, we conducted a national-level survey targeting hospital pharmacists dealing with oncology medicines to determine the prevalence of oncology medicine shortages in Pakistan, its causes, adopted mitigation strategies, and the impact.

Method

Study design

A multicentre cross-sectional study design is adopted to determine the pharmacists’ perspectives about oncology medicine shortages working in oncology hospitals or oncology departments in Pakistan. Pharmacists are chosen as they are the first health professionals from the hospital to solve drug-related issues, negotiate with manufacturers and distributors, and advise oncologists and nurses about drug shortage management.3

The survey questionnaire was designed based on study requirements and literature review to collect information about healthcare professionals’ experiences with oncology medicines’ short supply.3 17 28–30 The content validity test was performed through draft evaluation by three research experts. Pilot tests were performed on 15 hospital pharmacists throughout the country, which were not included in the final study sample. Feedback was received and used to format the final questionnaire (see online supplemental file S1) that contained 30 questions covering five sections: basic information (eight questions), drug shortage information (10), causes (2), impact (6) and mitigation strategies (4). The healthcare system of Pakistan consists of state-funded, semigovernment, trusts and private-sector healthcare facilities. Pharmacists from all these hospitals specialised in oncology and general institutes with oncology departments (referred as cancer hospitals in the following) were included in the survey. The Ministry of Health, Pakistan, has no centralised combined list of oncology setups in the country. Based on the self-observation of the research team, a list of 80 hospitals was prepared. Then 43 hospitals were selected from all five regions (Punjab 14, Sindh 11, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (KPK) 9, Islamabad 6 and Baluchistan 3). Totally, there were 260 pharmacists working in 43 hospitals. The study sample size was estimated at 156 participants using an online sample size calculator (RAOSOFT) at 95% CI and 5% margin error. Considering 15%–20% of rejections, 190 pharmacists with more than 1 year of oncology experience were approached online or face-to-face (figure 1).

The survey was conducted from September 2021 to January 2022. For online data collection, the pharmacists were approached, and study details were shared through email addresses on the hospital websites, WhatsApp and LinkedIn accounts. One to three reminders were sent to the participants after 1 week to motivate them to complete the survey. For the hospitals where online access was impossible, the data collectors visited the facility and received the responses on hard copies. We collected the email addresses of all the participating pharmacists to prevent duplication.

Data analysis

The data were first coded and recorded into MS Excel before being imported to the IBM SPSS V.22 for analysis. Frequencies and percentages were used to perform descriptive statistics for categorical questions. To find out the mean age of men and women, an independent sample t-test was used. Using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, the prevalence of oncology medicine shortages was compared among five regions of Pakistan. An alpha of 0.05 was used to evaluate statistical significance.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the general public were neither involved in planning nor conducting this study.

Results

Characteristics of participants

Out of 190 approached pharmacists, 167 responded with a response rate of 88% and their responses were included in the final analysis, of which 47% (n=78) were men and 53% (n=89) were women and mean age values were different between gender with p value less than 0.05. Most pharmacists (82%) were young, between the ages of 24 and 33. Of all, 48.5% of participants have 2–5 years of experience. There were 40% (n=67) from Punjab, 39% (n=65) from Sindh, 10% (n=17) from KPK, 7% (n=12) participants from Islamabad and 4% (n=6) from Baluchistan. Private hospitals hosted the majority of hospital pharmacists (45%), followed by not-for-profit organisations (34%). The demographic information and hospital information are presented in table 1.

Prevalence of oncology medicine shortages

Eighty-seven per cent of pharmacists reported that they experienced shortages in their practice, and 13% (22) did not over the past 12 months. Moreover, 50% (83) of respondents found both oncology and supportive agents in shortage, whereas 29% (48) of respondents experienced only chemotherapy medicine shortages. Among 167 participants, 58% (97) said that oncology medicine shortages were a current problem in their hospitals, whereas 19% of participants were unsure, and the situation differed across regions (p=0.007).

Most participants from Punjab reported that the oncology medicine shortage occurred half-yearly, while most participants from the other four regions faced the shortage monthly; however, participants from all regions were convinced that the shortage normally lasts for less than 3 months.

Most participants (44.9%) agreed that brand shortages are dominant in Pakistan, and 33% found that generic shortages were more. For the dosage form, the overall results highlighted that the injectable (56.8%) was the most affected dosage form, but in Punjab, the oral dosage was more in shortage. Most participants (33.5%) found that oncology medicine shortages increased compared with the previous year. A significant number of participants (36%) were convinced that the federal agencies have a major responsibility for managing oncology medicine shortages in Pakistan, while in Baluchistan, more participants attributed the problem to the provincial agencies (table 2).

Medicines affected by the shortages

Of 167 participants, 62% (103) reported shortages of 59 drugs (online supplemental file S2, table 1), whereas 38% neither responded nor mentioned any medicine. The survey found that 40% (41) of the participants from Punjab responded, followed by 38% (39) from Sindh. The most frequently mentioned drugs affected by shortages were etoposide, paclitaxel, dacarbazine, bleomycin and carboplatin.

Causes

On the whole, 53.4% of responses mentioned the distributors, and 29.7% mentioned the pharmaceutical companies as their main external supply source. While among these external sources, 46% of respondents stated that they frequently encounter shortage problems from distributors, followed by pharmaceutical companies (37%) (online supplemental file S2, table 2).

Impact

Considering the impacts of oncology medicine shortages on patients, 25% (91) of responses reported delayed care until the medicine supply resumed, and 23% (84) mentioned suboptimal results; the impacts of medicine shortages varied across regions (p<0.05).

To cope with the oncology medicine shortages, 63% (127) of respondents reported that staff has to spend extra time and effort. Nevertheless, among these answers, 64 mentioned it resulted in increased financial pressure, and 63 reported it without increasing it. Of 309 responses, 31% (97) were convinced that pharmacists face ethical issues while prioritising patients for short medicines, and 28% (85) reported needing extra time to educate the public and health professionals. Out of 167 participants, 61% (101) responses found that clinical trials were delayed due to shortages, whereas 24.6% (41) responses were unsure about the impact on clinical trials. Among the 255 responses, 46% mentioned that patients become frustrated but want to consult the pharmacists for a solution after they share the information about the oncology medicine shortage.

Out of 167 participants, 52% (87) reported that the medicines’ shortages led to increasing drug prices by manufacturers. At the same time, significant responses were also found for increased prices due to purchasing from the ‘gray’ or ‘parallel market’ in all regions except Baluchistan. Mitigation strategies

Out of 167 participants, 40% reported that there was a reporting system/platform for oncology medicine shortages in their hospital, and 29% agreed that there was a record of oncology medicine shortages but no reporting system, whereas 23% of participants reported that both were absent (online supplemental file S2, table 3). Most participants (96%) from all regions agreed that a reporting system, programme, or policy, is necessary. The most adopted strategies to tackle oncology medicine shortages were the redistribution of available stock (21%) and the use of complementary medicine after multidisciplinary team discussion (21%). Moreover, 20% of participants agreed to obtain drugs from another supplier. Most of the participants reported the consumption of 1–5 hours to find a solution for a shortage.

Discussion

Oncology medicine shortages are a complex global problem significantly affecting the healthcare systems, including Pakistan.1 We estimated the oncology medicine shortage problem through a national-level survey targeting accessible hospital pharmacists dealing with oncology medicines in Pakistan.

In the survey, most participants experienced shortages during their practice. A similar situation was seen in Saudi Arabia3 and the USA.29 The facts are justified because Pakistan is importing oncology medicines, and this survey was conducted during the pandemic, so oncology medicine shortages are highly expected.19 Most participants faced supportive medicine shortages along with oncology, and similar results were found in another study from Pakistan on oncology medicine shortages.19 Overall, the injectable is the most impacted dosage form in Pakistan, the USA (UUDIS 201831) and the EU (ASHP12 32); However, the oral dosage form was also most affected in Punjab. The reason is unknown. Around half of the participants reported brand shortages. In Pakistan, generics are preferred for oncology medicines, and only some oncology brands/generics are available. Some institutions keep one brand/generic based on oncologists’ requests, whereas others keep more. So a brand shortage in an institution is, in fact, a generic scarcity on a regional level.26 This is also because brand prescribing in Pakistan is a common trend for most drug classes. Furthermore, this trend may prove threatening in the case of cancer as patients are unaware of generics, and they may hold the treatment and wait for the shortage to resume if that specific brand is unavailable.33

In Pakistan, the oncology medicine shortage resulted in delayed treatment or changes in protocol, both leading to suboptimal conditions. Similar results are found in studies from the USA, Egypt, and Botswana, Africa.33 34 A study at a Moroccan hospital found that delayed care was the main effect of the oncology medicine shortage.16 The results are consistent with another study in the USA, where 65% of the participants mentioned delayed treatment. Delayed care in the case of cancer can pose a high risk to the survival rate of patients. A survey conducted in Saudi Arabia reported that professionals prefer using alternative drugs to delayed care,26 and a survey in European countries found that oncology medicine shortages led to the substitution of medicines.35

Oncology medicine shortages also affected clinical trials.36 In Pakistan, most participants found delayed clinical trials, and the results are consistent with a study from the USA.29 However, some participants were unsure about this because some institutions did not conduct such trials as they were costly.37 On shortages, drug prices increase, as seen in studies from the USA.38 In Pakistan, prices increase either from manufacturers or consumers purchasing from a ‘gray’ or ‘parallel market’ that is costly and with a compromised efficacy.

Managing oncology medicine shortages remains a complex challenge that necessitates significant commitment and hard decisions by healthcare professionals. The participants faced ethical issues in prioritising the patients for medicine dispensing, and they needed more time to educate patients and other healthcare professionals about shortages. We also noted that patients/caregivers become frustrated after being informed of shortages, but most of them still asked pharmacists for the solution, and only a few became violent. Studies in Europe and Saudi Arabia highlighted that medicine shortages led to severe professional stress35 as patients lost trust in the institution and became unsatisfied.26 A study at a Moroccan hospital found that the patients became frustrated and anxious about the oncology medicine shortage.16

In Pakistan, there is no reporting system at the national level,26 but some healthcare institutions have their reporting systems and some keep records in inventory. In comparison, in the USA, most institutions have a programme to deal with medicine shortages.30 Moreover, in some institutions, both reporting system and records within the inventory were absent. Most participants highlighted the need for reporting system, programme or policy, and the results are consistent with the study from the USA.30 In Pakistan, pharmacists either redistribute or select alternatives after discussion with a multidisciplinary team to manage shortages, and similar results are found in Saudi Arabia.3 While in the USA, practically all hospitals are subjected to complementary medicine due to a shortage.6 Managing medicine shortages, especially oncology medicine shortages, is extremely time-consuming. In the case of Pakistan, the pharmacists consumed about 1 hours/week to 5 hours/week in shortage management. The results are consistent with studies in Saudi Arabia and the UK.3 On the contrary, 12.8 hours/week was needed to manage shortages in Europe35 and 20 hours/ week in the USA.29

To tackle the advancing issue of oncology medicine shortages in Pakistan, the government should play the role, first, by establishing a national-level cancer registry. This platform will provide precise rates of various cancers and also a more accurate demand forecasting of oncology medicines. Second, the government should establish a robust national drug shortage platform. This platform will gather all stakeholders, from regulators and manufacturers up to the consumers, and provide them information regarding early notification on shortages, available alternate options and their use. It will also act as a communication centre for a smooth information flow and an awareness desk for professionals and the public. Third, the government needs to update policies for implementing a national-level generic prescribing policy. It will give more choices to patients (patients usually will insist on using the same medicine as prescribed) and also can help to lower drug expenditure.

The study has some strengths. Oncology medicine shortages in public, semipublic, non-profit and private hospitals have yet to be evaluated quantitatively in any previously published research. Also, this is the first study to measure the prevalence of oncology medicines nationwide. In order to ensure that cancer treatments are accessible to everyone, this study will enable researchers in Low-Middle-Income Countries (LMICs) to evaluate the accessibility of these necessary drugs and related barriers to optimal cancer therapy in their local settings.

There are a few limitations to this survey. First, we only focused on pharmacists. In the future, additional quantitative research studies could be conducted to investigate the perceptions of other key stakeholders (regulators, suppliers and oncologists) on anticancer medicine shortages. Second, the sample size is small. Considering the small population of oncology pharmacists in Pakistan, the sample included in the study was representative. Third, most participants’ experience was between 2 and 5 years. Moreover, coverage biases might happen as we did not take an equal number of institutions per region but included the number of institutions based on their total populations in selected regions. Furthermore, we selected representative institutions ensuring maximum participation from all regions. Finally, non-responsiveness biases might also be expected, as many hospitals were not ready to share data. To tackle this issue, participants were ensured of their data confidently and their identity would remain anonymous.

Conclusion

This study estimated the oncology medicine shortages from the perspective of hospital pharmacists in Pakistan. The results showed that the anticancer medicine shortages were the current and ongoing issue affecting the national healthcare system. The government should establish a cancer registry and national drug shortage platform, revise policies, including policies for implementing generic prescribing policies and fixing drug pricing and increase the availability of anticancer medicines to protect them from the gray market. Moreover, the penalties for not following the guidelines should be practised.
