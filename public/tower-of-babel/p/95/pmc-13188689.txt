Isorhamnetin-preconditioned MSC-derived exosomes restore ovarian function by inhibiting ferroptosis in chemotherapy-induced POF
Authors: Qiang Zhang, Jinyu Yu, Yan Zheng, Jinlan Jiang, Lianwen Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChemotherapy-induced premature ovarian failure (POF) is a major cause of infertility, with limited treatment options. Mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes (MSC-Exos) have therapeutic potential. This study investigated whether preconditioning MSCs with the antioxidant flavonoid isorhamnetin (ISO) enhances the efficacy of their exosomes (ISO-MSC-Exos) against POF.MethodsA cyclophosphamide-induced POF rat model was established, and the role of the ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1 was evaluated. MSC-Exos and ISO-MSC-Exos were isolated by ultracentrifugation and administered via tail vein injection. Ovarian recovery was assessed by monitoring the oestrous cycle, serum hormone levels, and histological findings. Lipid peroxidation and iron metabolism were evaluated by quantifying malondialdehyde, glutathione, iron deposition, and mitochondrial ultrastructure. Immunohistochemistry was used to assess the expression levels of GPX4, ACSL4, and FTH1. Proteomic analyses were performed to explore the underlying mechanisms.ResultsFerroptosis plays a pivotal role in the cyclophosphamide-induced POF rat model. Both exosome therapies improved ovarian function and suppressed ferroptosis, with ISO-MSC-Exos showing superior efficacy. ISO-MSC-Exos significantly restored hormone levels, ameliorated oestrous cycle disorders, reduced follicular atresia, and enhanced fertility. Furthermore, ISO-MSC-Exos more effectively elevated glutathione levels, reduced malondialdehyde and Fe2⁺ levels, and reversed the abnormal expression of ferroptosis-related proteins GPX4, ACSL4, and FTH1. Proteomic analysis suggested that ISO-MSC-Exos effectively inhibit ferroptosis by downregulating Alox15 and Tf, thereby reducing lipid peroxidation substrates and cellular iron uptake. This finding represents a potential molecular mechanism underlying their superior efficacy compared with that of MSC-Exos.ConclusionsISO-MSC-Exos showed superior efficacy compared with MSC-Exos in restoring ovarian function and inhibiting ferroptosis, suggesting that ISO pretreatment enhances the therapeutic effect of MSC-Exos in the POF model. Proteomic data provided supportive mechanistic insights into this enhanced efficacy, with the key pathways identified requiring subsequent functional validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-026-04989-4.Keywords: Mesenchymal stem cells, Exosomes, Ferroptosis, Premature ovarian failure, Isorhamnetin

Introduction

Premature ovarian failure (POF) refers to the premature decline of ovarian function in women before the age of 40. Clinically, it is characterized by infrequent menstruation, amenorrhea, and reduced fertility. Endocrine manifestations typically include elevated follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) levels, reduced estrogen (E2), and decreased anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH), which are key indicators of ovarian reserve function [1, 2]. The prevalence of POF among women under 40 is approximately 1% [3], although its precise etiology and pathogenesis remain unclear. Contributing factors include genetic predisposition, autoimmune responses, iatrogenic causes, and environmental influences [4]. In recent years, with the trend toward earlier onset of cancer, iatrogenic factors such as chemotherapy and radiotherapy have emerged as major risk factors for POF in young women undergoing cancer treatment [5]. Hormone replacement therapy remains the primary clinical approach; however, long-term use is associated with increased risks of breast and ovarian cancers, as well as thrombosis [6, 7]. Moreover, it does not effectively restore reproductive function. Therefore, there is an urgent need for safer and more effective therapeutic strategies.

In recent years, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have Recent studies have also suggested that ISO acts as an effective ferroptosis inhibitor significant potential as multipotent stem cells for the treatment of tissue damage [8]. MSCs possess strong paracrine effects, immunomodulatory properties, and multipotent differentiation capacity [9]. However, compared with MSCs themselves, exosomes secreted by MSCs exhibit greater stability, superior biocompatibility, and lower immunogenicity in vivo [10]. Consequently, MSC-derived exosomes (MSC-Exos) hold considerable promise as emerging cell-free therapeutic agents. Recent studies have shown that MSC-Exos can improve ovarian function in animal models of POF by reducing oxidative stress, inhibiting apoptosis, and exerting antifibrotic effects [11, 12]. Nevertheless, the therapeutic efficacy of exosome monotherapy remains limited. Functional optimization of exosomes through engineering strategies has therefore become an important research direction to further enhance their therapeutic potential [13]. Specifically, pretreating donor cells with hypoxic agents, drugs, or cytokines can effectively augment the biological activity of MSCs and alter the composition of proteins, nucleic acids, lipids, and other bioactive molecules within their exosomes, thereby achieving synergistic therapeutic effects. For instance, exosomes derived from hypoxia-pretreated hair follicle stem cells strongly promoted cell proliferation and migration [14]. Similarly, pretreatment of MSCs with the natural compound tanshinone produced exosomes (DSS-Exos) that significantly improved peritoneal function, with miRNA profiling revealing markedly upregulated miR-27a-5p expression, suggesting that modifications in exosomal bioactive components underlie their enhanced functionality [15]. These findings indicate that pretreatment strategies represent an efficient and feasible approach for engineering exosomes, significantly enhancing their therapeutic efficacy by modulating their molecular cargo.

The natural flavonoid isorhamnetin (ISO) exhibits multiple pharmacological activities, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-apoptotic, and antifibrotic effects [16], and shows promising potential for improving ovarian function. Its mechanism involves regulating steroid hormone synthesis in ovarian granulosa cells, promoting cell proliferation, inhibiting apoptosis and alleviating oxidative stress. Additionally, ISO supports oocyte development by maintaining cumulus -oocyte communication, and these combined effects play crucial roles in regulating ovarian function [17, 18]. Recent studies have also suggested that ISO acts as an effective ferroptosis inhibitor [19]. Notably, a growing body of evidence indicates a close association between the pathological processes of POF and ferroptosis [20]. Research shown that chemotherapeutic agents such as cisplatin and cyclophosphamide (CTX) induce ferroptotic changes in ovarian granulosa cells, ultimately impairing granulosa cell secretory function and normal follicular development [21, 22]. Moreover, administration of the ferroptosis inhibitor Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) to mice with ovarian dysfunction significantly reduces lipid peroxidation-induced oocyte damage [23]. These findings suggest that ferroptosis is a key mechanism underlying the pathogenesis of POF and that targeting this pathway holds promise as a novel therapeutic strategy.

MSC-Exos have shown promise in ameliorating ovarian damage in premature ovarian failure models, primarily by mitigating oxidative stress and apoptosis. However, it remains unclear whether exosomes can be functionally engineered via donor cell preconditioning to specifically inhibit ferroptosis, a distinct cell death pathway critically implicated in chemotherapy-induced ovarian injury. Here, we propose a novel strategy to precondition MSCs with the natural compound ISO to generate engineered exosomes (ISO-MSC-Exos). We systematicallysuggested that ISO-MSC-Exos are superior to naïve MSC-Exos in restoring ovarian function and, for the first time, revealed that this enhanced effect may stem from the multi-target inhibition of ferroptosis.

Materials and methods

Culture and identification of MSCs

The MSCs used in this study were derived from umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (UC-MSCs). Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) for UC-MSCs, and cultures were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. When the cell density reached 80%, passage the cells. The cells were washed twice with PBS,digested with 0.25% trypsin, collected, and subcultured in α-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (P/S). For adipogenic differentiation, UC-MSCs were cultured in an adipogenic induction medium for approximately 21 days. Lipid droplets within the cytoplasm were visualized microscopically and stained red with Oil Red O staining solution (Solarbio, China). For osteogenic differentiation, UC-MSCs were cultured in osteogenic induction medium for approximately 28 days. Under microscopic observation, mineralized nodules appeared as opaque areas and were stained with Alizarin Red (Solarbio, China), revealing dense red deposits. Phenotype identification of UC-MSCs was performed by flow cytometry using MSC-specific surface marker antibodies (BioLegend, USA). Positive markers (FITC-CD90, PE-CD73, and PE-CD105) and negative markers (FITC-CD11b, FITC-CD19, FITC-CD34, PE-CD45, and PE-HLA-DR) were detected to confirm MSC identity [24, 25].

MSC-Exos and ISO-MSC-Exos extraction and characterization

MSCs were seeded in α-MEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S. After incubation at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere for 48 h, the medium was replaced with FBS-free α-MEM and cultured for an additional 48 h. For the ISO-pretreated group, 5 µmol/L ISO was added to the FBS-free α-MEM medium, and cells were incubated under identical conditions [26]. The collected culture supernatants were sequentially centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min, 2000 × g for 10 min, and 10,000 × g for 60 min at 4 °C to remove floating cells, dead cells, and debris. The resulting supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter and transferred to an ultracentrifuge tube. Ultracentrifugation was performed at 100,000 g for 90 min at 4 °C. Exosomes were ultimately obtained by collecting the pellet after centrifugation at 100,000 × g for 90 min [27, 28]. Each cell produces approximately 200 exosomes.The morphology of MSC-Exos and ISO-MSC-Exos was examined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM; Hitachi HT7800, Japan). Particle size distribution and concentration were determined by nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA; NanoSight LM10, UK). The presence of exosome-specific markers CD9, CD81, and TSG101, and the absence of the negative marker Calnexin, were confirmed by western blot analysis. High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis was performed on ISO-pretreated mesenchymal stem cells (ISO-MSCs) and their extracted extracellular vesicles (ISO-MSC-Exos).

Animal experimental design

Specific-pathogen-free (SPF) female Sprague -Dawley (SD) rats, aged 6–8 weeks and weighing approximately 200 g, were purchased from Speifu (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). Animals were housed under controlled conditions (temperature approximately 25 °C, relative humidity 60%) with ad libitum access to food and water and maintained on a natural light–dark cycle. All animal experiments were conducted at the facilities of the School of Public Health, Jilin University (SYXK(Ji)2021-0003) under the supervision of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and in accordance with the Guidelines for Ethical Welfare Review of Laboratory Animals (GB2018-35892) and GB14925 for housing conditions. All procedures were approved by the Jilin University Animal Experiment Ethics Committee.The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

Randomization was employed. The allocation sequence was generated using an online randomization tool (www.randomizer.org). Based on pre-experimental data and calculations using G*Power 3.1 software, this study requires a minimum of eight rats per group. This ensures robust results and aligns with the typical sample sizes observed in comparable studies within the field [29]. Participants were assigned to groups according to this sequence. Following a one-week acclimatization period, 24 female SD rats were randomly divided into Control, CTX, and CTX + Fer-1 groups (n = 8 per group). To establish the POF rat model, CTX (50 mg/kg, Sigma) was administered via intraperitoneal injection on day 1, followed by daily administration of 8 mg/kg from days 2 to 14 [30, 31]. Control rats received an equivalent volume of PBS. To investigate the role of ferroptosis in POF, rats in the CTX + Fer-1 group received intraperitoneal injections of Fer-1 (1 mg/kg) every other day for 2 weeks, while the remaining groups received equivalent volumes of PBS [21]. To evaluate the therapeutic effects of MSC-Exos and ISO-MSC-Exos, 40 female SD rats were further assigned to Control, CTX, MSC-Exos, and ISO-MSC-Exos groups (n = 10 per group). The POF model was established as described above. Rats in with successfully induced POF were divided into two treatment subgroups and administered 150 µg of MSC-Exos and ISO-MSC-Exos suspended in 100 µL PBS via tail vein injection every 2 days for 2 weeks. This experimental approach utilized the same SD rat model and disease induction method to evaluate exosome therapy [32]. The schematic diagram of the experimental design is shown in Fig. 1. All reagents and consumables used within the same replicate experiment were sourced from the same batch to avoid batch effects. To minimize bias, we implemented blinding in the main phase of the experiment. At the end of the treatment, rats were euthanized by cervical dislocation following deep anesthesia induced by inhalation of isoflurane, and blood samples were collected for serum hormone analysis. Ovaries were excised, weighed, and divided into two portions: one was rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at – 80 °C, and the other half was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for subsequent histological analysis.

MSC-Exos and ISO-MSC-Exos in vivo distribution

To assess the biodistribution of MSC-Exos and ISO-MSC-Exos in rats with POF, exosomes were labeled with the near-infrared fluorescent dye 1,1′-dioctadecyl-3,3,3′,3′-tetramethylindotricarbocyanine iodide (DIR). Experiment according to the instructions for DiR iodide. Briefly, 1 mg of DIR powder was dissolved in 200 μL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a 5 mM stock solution, which was subsequently diluted with PBS to a final concentration of 100 µM. Ten microliters of the working solution were mixed with 100 μL of PBS containing 150 μg of MSC-Exos or ISO-MSC-Exos. The mixture was vortexed for 1 min and then incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min to allow complete dye labeling. Subsequently, Excess dye was removed by ultracentrifugation under identical parameters used during exosome isolation, and the resulting pellet was resuspended in PBS [33, 34]. DIR-labeled exosomes were administered via tail vein injection to CTX-induced POF rats. Twenty-four hours post-injection, the animals were anesthetized [35]. The anaesthetic method selected was inhalation anaesthesia. Rats were placed in an induction chamber, and anaesthesia was induced by administering a mixed gas containing 5% isoflurane. The isoflurane concentration was subsequently reduced to 2% to maintain anaesthesia [36]. Using the small animal in vivo imaging system, select the excitation/emission wavelengths of 745/820 nm for detection in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions (BOLT Bio-Technology Co., Ltd., China).

Estrous cycle monitoring

The estrous cycle of rats in each group was monitored between 8:00 and 9:00 a.m. throughout the experimental period. Vaginal smears were collected by gently inserting a cotton swab moistened with physiological saline into the vaginal canal and rotating it to collect epithelial cells. The collected samples were evenly smeared onto glass slides and stained with 1X Giemsa solution for 45 min according to the instructions. Slides were rinsed gently with distilled water from one side, air-dried, and examined under a light microscope. The stages of the estrous cycle were determined based on the cytological characteristics of vaginal epithelial cells and documented accordingly [37, 38].

Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining

Excised ovarian tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and processed for histological analysis. Following fixation, samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Serial sections of 5-μm-thickness were prepared and mounted on glass slides. Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with H&E staining according to standard protocols. Morphological changes in ovarian tissue were examined under a light microscope. Ovarian follicles at different developmental stages were identified, classified and counted. The total follicle number from five serial sections was averaged to obtain the final follicle count per ovary [35, 39, 40]. Histological assessment (including counting of follicular numbers) was conducted by researchers unaware of the experimental groupings, employing a blinded procedure.

Serum hormone levels determination by ELISA

Blood samples were collected from rats in each experimental group, and serum was separated by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 15 min [21]. The concentrations of E2, FSH, and AMH in serum were quantified using commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Shanghai Enzyme-Linked Bio-Technology Co., Ltd., China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader, and hormone concentrations were calculated based on standard curves generated for each assay.

3′-Diaminobenzidine (DAB) enhanced Prussian blue staining

To assess iron deposition in ovarian tissue, paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and subjected to DAB-enhanced Prussian blue staining. Equal volumes of 2% potassium ferrocyanide and 2% hydrochloric acid were mixed and applied to the tissue sections, which were incubated in the staining solution for 30 min at room temperature. The sections were then rinsed twice with distilled water. Subsequently, DAB chromogen solution was added dropwise and incubated for 5–10 min. After discarding the chromogen, the sections were washed once with PBS and three times with distilled water. Hematoxylin counterstaining was performed for 1 min, followed by rinsing under running water, differentiation with hydrochloric acid solution, and bluing with ammonia solution. Finally, the sections were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted with coverslips. Stained sections were examined under a light microscope, and representative images were captured for analysis [41, 42].

Ovarian tissue ferrous ions (Fe2+) content detection

The concentration of Fe2⁺ in ovarian tissue was quantified using a Ferrous Ion Content Detection Kit (Solarbio, China), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, ovarian tissue was homogenized in extraction buffer at a ratio of 0.1 g tissue per 1 mL buffer and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min to collect the supernatant. Absorbance was measured at 593 nm using a microplate reader, and Fe2⁺ content was calculated from a standard curve.

Ovarian tissue malondialdehyde (MDA) levels detection

MDA levels in ovarian tissue were measured using a lipid peroxidation (MDA) assay kit (Beyotime, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Tissue homogenates were prepared at a ratio of 10% (w/v) in lysis buffer and centrifuged at 10,000 g for 10 min. For each reaction,100 μL of sample or standard was mixed with 200 μL of MDA working solution and heated at 100 °C for 15 min. After cooling to room temperature, samples were centrifuged at 1,000 g for 10 min, and 200 μL of supernatant was transferred to a 96-well plate. Absorbance was measured at 532 nm using a microplate reader, and MDA content was normalized on to protein concentration.

Ovarian tissue glutathione (GSH) levels detection

Reduced GSH levels in ovarian tissue were quantified using a GSH assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng, China). According to the kit instructions, tissues were weighed and homogenized in physiological saline at a 1:9 (w/v) ratio, then centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 10 min. A portion of the supernatant was used for protein quantification. For GSH measurement, 100 μl of supernatant was mixed with 100 μl of Reagent 1 and centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was used for colorimetric analysis. Absorbance was measured at 405 nm using a microplate reader, and GSH levels were calculated based on the sample’s protein concentration.

TEM

Ovarian tissue were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde for at least 24 h at 4 °C, washed with PBS, and post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide at room temperature for 1.5–2 h. Samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol and acetone series, infiltrated with resin, and embedded according to standard protocols. Ultrathin Sections (50–70 nm) were prepared using an ultramicrotome and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate by the drop-in method [43]. The mitochondrial morphology in granulosa cells was examined using TEM (Hitachi HT7800, Japan).

Immunohistochemistry

Paraffin-embedded ovarian tissue sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and subjected to antigen retrieval using ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) buffer. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by incubating sections with 100 µL peroxidase blocker for 10 min at room temperature. After three washes with PBS, sections were blocked and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against the target proteins. Following PBS washes, sections were incubated with the secondary antibody for 30 min at room temperature. Visualization was performed using DAB chromogen solution for 3 min, and nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Finally, sections were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted, and stained tissues were examined under a light microscope [27, 44].

Data-independent acquisition (DIA) quantitative proteomics

Ovarian tissue samples were ground in liquid nitrogen and lysed in SDT buffer (containing sodium dodecyl sulfate [SDS], dithiothreitol [DTT], and Tris–HCl) according to standard filter-aided sample preparation (FASP) protocol requirements. Lysates were boiled for 3 min, ultrasonicated for 2 min, and centrifuged at 16,000 g for 20 min at 4 °C. Supernatants were collected, and protein concentrations were determined using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. Proteins were digested using the FASP method. Briefly, DTT was added to a final concentration of 100 mM, samples were boiled for 5 min, cooled, mixed with urea buffer, and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min. After alkylation with iodoacetamide (IAA) for 30 min in the dark, samples were washed twice with urea buffer and twice with ammonium bicarbonate (NH₄HCO₃). Trypsin digestion was performed at 37 °C for 16–18 h, and peptides were desalted using a C18 cartridge, lyophilized, and resuspended in 0.1% formic acid (FA). Peptides were separated by liquid chromatography on a Vanquish Neo UHPLC system (Thermo Scientific) and analyzed by DIA mass spectrometry using an Orbitrap Astral mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). DIA data were processed using DIA-NN software for protein identification and quantification. Proteins identified in at least 50% of the samples were retained for statistical analysis, and missing values were imputed prior to analysis. Differentially expressed proteins were identified using a t-test with a significance threshold of p < 0.05 and a fold change (FC) ≥ 1.5 or ≤ 1/1.5.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed and visualized using GraphPad Prism version 10.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc., USA). Statistical comparisons were performed using one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's post hoc test for multiple comparisons. If the data does not satisfy normality, then the corresponding nonparametric test is used. All experiments were conducted independently at least three times. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Effects of Fer-1 on ovarian function in POF model rats

After a one-week acclimation period, rat body weight was recorded weekly. On day 28, animals were anesthetized, euthanized, and their ovaries were excised, weighed, and examined for morphological changes. Compared with the Control and Fer-1 intervention groups, the CTX group exhibited a significant reduction in ovarian volume. Body weight in the CTX group was markedly lower than that in the control group, whereas Fer-1 treatment mitigated this weight loss. Analysis of the ovarian index showed a significant decrease in the CTX group relative to the control group, which was substantially increased following Fer-1 intervention (Fig. 2A–C). Vaginal cytology revealed characteristic cellular compositions at different stages of the estrous cycle: proestrus (P) was dominated by nucleated epithelial cells; estrus (E) displayed abundant keratinized epithelial cell aggregates; metestrus (M) contained leukocytes along with keratinized and nucleated epithelial cells; and diestrus (D) was characterized primarily by leukocytes with occasional epithelial cells. Control rats exhibited a regular estrous cycle, whereas the CTX group showed prolonged diestrus, indicative of disrupted cyclicity. Fer-1 treatment partially restored normal estrous cycling (Fig. 2D). Histological assessment of ovarian tissue using H&E staining showed that CTX-treated ovaries exhibited reduced tissue volume, significantly decreased primordial, preantral, and antral follicles, and a higher proportion of atretic follicles. Although Fer-1 treatment did not significantly decrease the number of atretic follicles, it markedly increased primordial, preantral, and antral follicle counts compared with the CTX group (Fig. 2E–F). Furthermore, ELISA assay of serum hormone levels revealed that, relative to controls, the CTX group had significantly reduced serum E2 and AMH levels and elevated FSH levels. Fer-1 intervention reversed these changes, significantly increasing E2 and AMH levels and decreasing FSH levels. These improvements in hormonal profiles suggest that Fer-1 effectively restored ovarian endocrine function (Fig. 2G–I).

Fer-1 inhibits ovarian ferroptosis in a POF rat model

To investigate whether CTX-induced ovarian damage is mediated by ferroptosis, we performed multilevel validation using biochemical assays, histochemical staining, mitochondrial ultrastructure analysis, and ferroptosis-related gene expression profiling. Biochemical measurements revealed that, compared with the control group, CTX treatment significantly increased MDA levels, a marker of lipid peroxidation, elevated Fe2⁺ concentrations, and markedly depleted GSH levels in ovarian tissue. Fer-1 administration significantly reduced MDA and Fe2⁺ levels and restored GSH levels (Fig. 3A–C). Prussian blue staining further revealed extensive iron deposition in the ovarian tissue from the CTX group, whereas Fer-1 treatment markedly reduced iron accumulation, indicating mitigation of CTX-induced ovarian iron overload (Fig. 3D). TEM analysis revealed that granulosa cells in the CTX group exhibited abnormal mitochondrial morphology characterized by disrupted or absent cristae, increased electron density, and compromised mitochondrial membranes. Fer-1 treatment largely restored mitochondrial ultrastructure, rendering it comparable to that observed in the control group (Fig. 3E). Immunohistochemical analysis revealed that CTX exposure significantly upregulated the ferroptosis-promoting enzyme acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4) while reducing the expression of the ferroptosis-protective enzyme glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) and the iron storage protein ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1). Fer-1 intervention effectively reversed these changes, normalizing the expression of ferroptosis-related proteins (Fig. 3F–I). Collectively, these results suggest that ferroptosis may be the primary mechanism underlying CTX-induced ovarian damage and that intervention with Fer-1 effectively suppresses this process, thereby mitigating ovarian injury in the POF rat model.

Characterization of MSCs and exosomes

To assess the multipotent differentiation potential and surface marker profile of MSCs, osteogenic and adipogenic induction assays were performed, and surface markers were analyzed by flow cytometry. Following osteogenic induction, Alizarin Red S staining revealed distinct red mineralized nodules within the cells (Fig. 4A). After adipogenic induction, Oil Red O staining revealed the accumulation of red lipid droplets in the cytoplasm (Fig. 4B), confirming the robust osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation capabilities of MSCs. Flow cytometric analysis further showed that MSC expressed high levels (> 95%) of the mesenchymal surface markers CD90, CD105, and CD73, while expression of hematopoietic and immune markers CD11b, CD19, CD34, CD45, and HLA-DR remained below 2% (Fig. 4C). Exosomes were isolated from conditioned media of MSCs and ISO-pretreated MSCs by differential ultracentrifugation. TEM observations revealed that both MSC-Exos and ISO-MSC-Exos exhibited the characteristic cup-shaped, double-membrane morphology of exosomes (Fig. 4D). Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) showed that the majority of vesicles had a particle size distribution in the range of 100–150 nm (Fig. 4E). Western blot analysis confirmed the presence of canonical exosomal markers CD9, CD81, and TSG101in both MSC-Exos and ISO-MSC-Exos, while the endoplasmic reticulum protein calnexin was nearly undetectable (Fig. 4F). Together, these results suggest that MSC-Exos and ISO-MSC-Exos share similar morphology, particle size distribution, and protein marker expression, indicating that ISO pretreatment does not alter the fundamental characteristics of MSC-derived exosomes. HPLC analysis revealed detectable ISO peaks within ISO-pretreated UC-MSCs, indicating that ISO was taken up and retained by the MSCs. Furthermore, chromatographic peaks for ISO were also detected within exosomes (ISO-MSC-Exos) isolated from ISO-pretreated UC-MSCs. However, these peaks were significantly lower than those observed in the donor MSCs themselves (Fig. 4G-H). The results suggest that ISO is not highly enriched in exosomes, and its primary function may be to exert effects indirectly by regulating donor cells.

ISO-MSC-Exos therapy improves ovarian function in CTX-induced POF rats

To investigate the biodistribution of exosomes in vivo, DiR-labeled MSC-Exos and ISO-MSC-Exos were administered via tail vein injection into CTX-induced POF rats. In vivo fluorescence imaging performed 24 h post-injection revealed distinct signals in the ovarian region for both exosome types, indicates that MSC-Exos and ISO-MSC-Exos are enriched in ovarian tissue (Fig. 5A). However, whether this enrichment is due to passive accumulation or receptor-mediated uptake requires further investigation in subsequent studies. Gross morphological observation showed significant ovarian atrophy in the CTX group compared with the control group, whereas treatment with MSC-Exos or ISO-MSC-Exos ameliorated ovarian shrinkage (Fig. 5C). Additionally, CTX-treated rats exhibited significant weight loss and a markedly reduced ovarian index. Both MSC-Exos and ISO-MSC-Exos treatments alleviated these changes, with the ISO-MSC-Exos producing a more pronounced recovery compared with MSC-Exos (Fig. 5B, D). Vaginal smears cytology revealed regular estrous cycles in control rats, whereas the CTX group exhibited severe disruption, with most rats remaining in the anestrus phase. MSC-Exos treatment partially improved cycle regularity, while ISO-MSC-Exos treatment earlier restored normal estrous cycling (Fig. 5E). H&E staining further revealed severe ovarian tissue damage and atrophy in the CTX group, accompanied by significant reductions in primordial, preantral, and antral follicles, and increased atretic follicles. MSC-Exos treatment partially mitigated these changes, significantly increasing primordial follicle numbers. In contrast, ISO-MSC-Exos treatment not only repaired ovarian tissue damage but also significantly increased primordial, preantral, and antral follicles counts while reducing atretic follicles, thereby preserving ovarian reserve function (Fig. 5F, G).

ISO-MSC-Exos restores endocrine function and improves fertility in POF rats

ELISA was performed to evaluate serum levels of E2, FSH, and AMH in rats from each group. Compared with the control group, E2 and AMH concentrations were significantly reduced in the CTX group. Treatment with MSC-Exos partially increased E2 and AMH levels relative to the CTX group; however, ISO-MSC-Exos group treatment led to a more pronounced elevation in both hormones. In contrast, FSH levels were significantly elevated in the CTX group compared with controls. Both MSC-Exos and ISO-MSC-Exos treatments significantly reduced FSH concentrations, with ISO-MSC-Exos demonstrating superior efficacy. These findings indicate that ISO-MSC-Exos more effectively restored ovarian endocrine function and enhanced ovarian reserve capacity (Fig. 6A–C). To further evaluate fertility following treatment, four female SD rats from each group were randomly selected and housed with male SD rats at a 2:1 ratio. Successful mating was confirmed by the presence of vaginal plugs and microscopic detection of sperm in vaginal smears. Pregnancy outcomes and the number of implanted embryos were then evaluated. Compared with the control group, CTX-treated rats exhibited impaired fertility, with significantly reduced or absent uterine embryos. MSC-Exo treatment increased the number of implanted embryos relative to the CTX group, although this difference did not reach statistical significance. In contrast, ISO-MSC-Exos treatment resulted in a significant increase in embryo implantation compared with both CTX and MSC-Exos groups (Fig. 6D–E). Collectively, these findings suggest that ISO-MSC-Exos effectively restore ovarian endocrine function and markedly enhance fertility in POF rats.

ISO-MSC-Exos alleviate CTX-induced premature ovarian failure by inhibiting ferroptosis

The above results indicate that ferroptosis is a key pathogenic mechanism underlying CTX-induced POF. To further assess the therapeutic effects of MSC-Exos and ISO-MSC-Exos on ovarian ferroptosis, we measured GSH, MDA, and Fe2⁺ levels in ovarian tissues. Compared with the control group, the CTX group exhibited significantly elevated MDA content, markedly reduced GSH concentrations, and substantial Fe2⁺ accumulation, indicating severe oxidative stress and disrupted iron metabolism. Treatment with MSC-Exos partially reduced MDA and Fe2⁺ levels while increasing GSH content, suggesting moderate improvement. ISO-MSC-Exos treatment produced more pronounced effects, with MDA and Fe2⁺ levels significantly lower than those in both the CTX and MSC-Exos groups, and GSH levels significantly higher (Fig. 7A–C). These results indicate that ISO-MSC-Exos more effectively attenuate lipid peroxidation and restore iron homeostasis in ovarian tissue. Prussian blue staining further confirmed extensive iron deposition in the CTX group. Both MSC-Exos and ISO-MSC-Exos treatments alleviated iron accumulation; however, scattered deposits remained detectable after MSC-Exos intervention, indicating incomplete recovery. In contrast, iron deposition after ISO-MSC-Exos treatment closely resembled that of the control group, demonstrating superior efficacy in mitigating CTX-induced iron overload (Fig. 7D). TEM of granulosa cell mitochondria revealed hallmark ferroptotic changes in the CTX group, including increased electron density, reduced mitochondria volume, and diminished or absent cristae. Both MSC-Exos and ISO-MSC-Exos treatments, with ISO-MSC-Exos showing greater restorative effects (Fig. 7E). Immunohistochemical analysis of ferroptosis-related proteins revealed markedly increased ACSL4 expression in the CTX group relative to controls, promoting lipid peroxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) and accelerating ferroptosis. Both treatments reduced ACSL4 expression, with a more substantial decrease in the ISO-MSC-Exos group. Conversely, GPX4 expression was significantly reduced in the CTX group, impairing lipid peroxide scavenging. Both treatments increased GPX4 levels, with ISO-MSC-Exos producing a more pronounced effect, suggesting enhanced antioxidant defense. Furthermore, expression of the iron storage protein FTH1 was reduced in the CTX group, indicating diminished iron sequestration and increased free iron accumulation. Both treatments upregulated FTH1 expression, with ISO-MSC-Exos showing a greater effect, indicating more effective restoration of iron storage and inhibition of ferroptosis (Fig. 7F–I).

Proteomics reveals potential mechanisms of action

Animal experiments revealed that ISO-MSC-Exos significantly improved ovarian function in rats with POF, the underlying repair mechanism remains unclear. To investigate these mechanisms, DIA quantitative proteomics was performed to systematically analyze ovarian tissue proteins and identify differentially expressed proteins following exosome treatment. This approach enabled the screening of proteins specifically regulated by ISO-MSC-Exos and provided preliminary insights into the molecular pathways underlying ovarian repair. Pairwise comparisons identified 596 differentially expressed proteins between the MSC-Exos and CTX groups, including 266 upregulated and 330 downregulated proteins. In the ISO-MSC-Exos versus CTX comparison, 429 differentially expressed proteins were identified, comprising 165 upregulated and 264 downregulated proteins. The distribution of upregulated and downregulated proteins across groups is shown in volcano plots (Fig. 8A, B). Further Gene Ontology (GO) functional annotation of differentially expressed proteins highlighted the top 10 most enriched functional categories, presented as bubble plots (Fig. 8C, D). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis indicated significant enrichment of the ferroptosis pathway in both the MSC-Exos vs. CTX and ISO-MSC-Exos vs. CTX comparisons (Fig. 8E, F). A Venn diagram depicting the intersection of differentially expressed proteins between pairwise comparisons (Fig. 8G) revealed 251 shared proteins associated with ovarian function repair following MSC-Exos and ISO-MSC-Exos treatment. These included ferroptosis-related proteins such as ceruloplasmin (Cp) and Slc39a8 (zinc-iron transporter), suggesting that both exosome types promote ovarian repair by modulating ferroptosis. Notably, analysis of the ISO-MSC-Exos vs. CTX comparison identified two additional ferroptosis-related proteins, Alox15 (arachidonic acid 15-lipoxygenase) and Tf (transferrin), that were significantly differentially expressed. These results suggest that ISO-MSC-Exos, beyond sharing the regulatory effects observed with MSC-Exos, more comprehensively inhibit lipid peroxidation and modulate iron metabolism, thereby suppressing ferroptosis more efficiently. This enhanced regulation may underlie the superior therapeutic efficacy of ISO-MSC-Exos.

Discussion

POF is a serious condition that affects the reproductive health of women of childbearing age, and chemotherapy is one of the primary causes of iatrogenic POF [5]. The conventional view holds that chemotherapy drugs, such as CTX, induce ovarian granulosa cell apoptosis, which is considered the core mechanism leading to follicular depletion [45]. In recent years, ferroptosis—a ferrous-iron-dependent form of regulated cell death characterised by lipid peroxide accumulation—has been shown to participate in diverse pathological processes and has garnered increasing attention in chemotherapy-induced ovarian injury [44, 46]. This study used the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 to validate the pivotal role of ferroptosis in a CTX-induced POF rat model. Fer-1 intervention ameliorated oestrous cycle disruption and hormonal imbalances in rats while mitigating pathological ovarian tissue damage. At the molecular and morphological levels, Fer-1 reversed CTX-induced ovarian tissue hallmarks of ferroptosis, including GSH depletion, elevated MDA levels, Fe2⁺ overload, increased mitochondrial density in granulosa cells, and reduced cristae [47]. Furthermore, we focused on three core regulatory proteins at the molecular level: GPX4, ACSL4, and FTH1. GPX4, a central inhibitor of ferroptosis, maintains membrane stability by reducing lipid peroxides, and its inactivation is a critical trigger for ferroptosis [48]. ACSL4 catalyses the esterification of PUFAs, thereby supplying substrates for lipid peroxidation and acting as a ferroptosis promoter [49]. FTH1, an essential ferritin subunit, limits free iron availability for the Fenton reaction by sequestering it, thereby inhibiting lipid peroxidation [50]. Immunohistochemical results suggested that Fer-1 upregulates the expression of the anti-ferroptotic proteins GPX4 and FTH1 in ovarian tissue while downregulating the pro-ferroptotic protein ACSL4. This indicates that ferroptosis may be a primary factor in the pathology of CTX-induced POF; however, it is not the sole mechanism. These findings corroborate those of other studies in this field, such as those observing ferroptosis hallmarks, including suppressed GPX4 activity in ovarian granulosa cells and accumulation of lipid peroxides in CTX-induced mouse POF models [51]. In cisplatin-induced POF models and ovarian granulosa cells, iron ion accumulation in ovarian tissue and upregulation of ACSL4 expression have also been identified, collectively supporting the involvement of ferroptosis in the decline of ovarian function [52].

Given the role of ferroptosis in the pathology of POF, exploring therapeutic strategies that effectively inhibit this process and restore ovarian function is important. MSC-Exos, a cell-free therapeutic approach with favourable biocompatibility and low immunogenicity, have shown potential in tissue repair and have been investigated for POF treatment [53]. In POF therapy, natural MSC-Exos improve ovarian function by alleviating oxidative stress, inhibiting apoptosis, and modulating the immune microenvironment [54]. However, enhancing therapeutic efficacy remains crucial for clinical translation. Exosome engineering, particularly through drug pretreatment to optimise donor cell status and thereby improve exosome composition, represents an effective strategy for augmenting therapeutic potency [55, 56]. This study innovatively employed the natural flavonoid compound ISO, possessing antioxidant and anti-ferroptotic activities, to pretreat MSCs, yielding functionally enhanced ISO-MSC-Exos [16, 19]. Our findings suggest that ISO-MSC-Exos exhibit significantly superior efficacy compared with that of conventional MSC-Exos in ameliorating CTX-induced POF. Functionally, ISO-MSC-Exos effectively restored serum hormone levels, increased the follicular number, and improved fertility. At the molecular level, they suggested enhanced ferroptosis inhibition, markedly reducing ovarian tissue malondialdehyde and Fe2⁺ levels while elevating glutathione levels. They also effectively reversed the abnormal expression of GPX4, FTH1, and ACSL4. This synergistic effect aligns with research approaches that employ similar pretreatment strategies. For instance, Yang et al. reported that exosomes derived from coenzyme Q10-pretreated MSCs inhibited ferroptosis, enhanced proliferation and migration capability, and significantly promoted diabetic wound healing by enriching anti-ferroptosis-associated molecules [43]. The findings of this study further suggest that drug pretreatment may be a viable strategy. ISO pretreatment enhances the functional properties of exosomes, thereby increasing their therapeutic potential for treating ferroptosis-related diseases.

To elucidate the synergistic mechanism of ISO-MSC-Exos, we performed a DIA quantitative proteomic analysis. The results revealed that MSC-Exos and ISO-MSC-Exos jointly mediated their regulatory effects through 251 differentially expressed proteins, constituting a common molecular basis for the improvement of ovarian function. These included two key ferroptosis-related proteins, Cp and Slc39a8, as identified by KEGG enrichment analysis. However, the unique advantage of ISO-MSC-Exos is their ability to specifically downregulate two key ferroptosis-driving proteins, Alox15 and Tf. Alox15 is a core driver of ferroptosis and specifically catalyses the lipid peroxidation of PUFAs in phospholipids. The resulting lipid peroxides act as ultimate executors, causing cell membrane rupture and triggering ferroptosis [57, 58]. Tf is a primary iron transporter in the bloodstream. By binding to the transferrin receptor on the cell membrane, iron is delivered to cells as a transferrin–iron complex, which serves as a key molecule for maintaining iron homeostasis. Dysfunction in this cycle directly causes intracellular free iron overload, thereby inducing ferroptosis [59, 60]. Therefore, ISO-MSC-Exos may synergistically downregulate Alox15 and Tf, thereby effectively blocking ferroptosis by reducing both lipid peroxidation substrates and cellular iron uptake, which are the two core pathways. This represents a key potential mechanism explaining their superior efficacy compared with conventional MSC-Exos.

Although this study suggested the significant therapeutic potential of ISO-MSC-Exos, certain limitations remain. First, although proteomics revealed potential mechanisms, the specific active components within ISO-MSC-Exos responsible for regulating Alox15 and Tf expression, such as miRNAs or proteins, remain unclear. Moreover, proteomics provides hypothesis-generating evidence and offers important clues for understanding the potential mechanisms of action of ISO-MSC-Exos; however, subsequent functional validation is required in future studies. Second, protein expression quantification in this study primarily relied on immunohistochemistry. Future investigations should employ more precise methods, such as western blotting or qPCR, for validation. Finally, this study primarily focused on the ferroptosis pathway, without ruling out the possibility of synergistic effects through other pathways, such as autophagy and anti-apoptosis. This is an important area requiring further in-depth research. Furthermore, from a clinical translation perspective, although ISO-MSC-Exos show promising application prospects, they face challenges that require further exploration. First, the ISO drug pretreatment strategy employed in this study offers the advantages of simplicity and low cost, facilitating its integration into existing cell culture systems and alignment with scalable production requirements. However, scaling up from laboratory- to large-scale standardised production compliant with Good Manufacturing Practice remains a primary challenge [61–63].Second, although this study established effective doses in animal models, providing a starting point for subsequent preclinical research, translating animal dosages to equivalent human doses requires systematic pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic studies [64–66]. Furthermore, the long-term in vivo safety of engineered modifications requires comprehensive evaluation. Finally, regulatory authorities impose specific requirements for quality control, preclinical studies, and clinical trial design for such active biological products, necessitating early alignment with regulatory standards throughout the research and development process [62, 67–69]. In summary, this study's engineered pretreatment strategy offers a viable approach to enhance exosome efficacy. However, future validation of its safety and efficacy in larger-scale animal models remains essential to establish a robust foundation for eventual clinical application.

Conclusion

In this study, we systematically investigated the therapeutic efficacy and underlying mechanisms of ISO-MSC-Exos in a CTX-induced POF rat model. Our findings suggest that ferroptosis is a critical pathological pathway contributing to CTX-induced ovarian dysfunction. Importantly, engineering MSC-Exos with the natural bioactive compound ISO significantly enhanced their biological activity. ISO-MSC-Exos may potentially reverse ovarian dysfunction by inhibiting ferroptosis through multi-target inhibition, highlighting their substantial therapeutic potential as a novel acellular strategy for POF treatment.
