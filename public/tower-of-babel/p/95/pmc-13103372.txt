Rheumatic immune-related adverse effects of immune checkpoint treatment: a retrospective multicentre study
Authors: Arturo Llobell-Uriel, Íñigo González-Mazón, Jordi Gratacós, Soledad Retamozo, Carmen Secada-Gómez, Adrian Martín-Gutiérrez, Almudena García-Castaño, Luis Fernández-Morales, Enrique Gallardo-Diaz, Antoni Berenguer-Llergo, Jorge del Estal-Jiménez, Javier Delgado-Rodríguez, Antonio Gómez-Centeno, Miguel Angel Segui-Palmer, Ricardo Blanco, Joan Calvet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo describe the frequency, clinical spectrum, and management of rheumatic immune-related adverse events (R-irAEs) in patients receiving immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) at two oncology centers in Spain. This was a retrospective, observational study involving 734 patients treated with ICIs between January 2016 and December 2022 in two Spanish hospitals. Electronic medical records were systematically reviewed to identify R-irAEs, with diagnoses confirmed by rheumatologists. The data collected included demographics, cancer type, ICI regimen, time to R-irAE onset, clinical features, management, and outcomes. Among the 734 patients, 227 (30.9%) developed any irAEs, with 54 (7.35%) presenting R-irAEs, accounting for 23.8% of all irAEs. The most common cancers were lung cancer and melanoma. Most R-irAEs occurred within six months of ICI initiation. The most frequent manifestations were polymyalgia rheumatica (n = 12) and various forms of arthritis/arthralgia (n = 26). Myositis is a particularly severe manifestation that presents at the first or second cycle of treatment. Corticosteroids are the primary treatment, with some patients requiring immunosuppressants, biologics, or intravenous immunoglobulins. Clinical improvement was observed in 88% of the treated patients, although 18 patients required ICI discontinuation due to toxicity. The increasing use of ICIs in cancer treatment is limited by development of irAEs (30.9%) and R-irAEs (7.35%) accounting for 23.8% of irAEs. Prompt recognition and multidisciplinary management, including rheumatology input, are essential to optimize patient outcomes and balance the continuation of cancer treatment. Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have revolutionized cancer treatment in the past decade, offering patients novel therapeutic options and, in many cases, significantly improving clinical outcomes. ICIs, such as antagonists of programmed death receptor 1 (PD-1), programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated antigen 4 (CTLA-4), exert their effects by restoring the immune system’s ability to recognize and eliminate cancer cells1,2. However, modulating the immune system may also lead to complications known as immune-related adverse events (irAEs).

irAEs are immune system-mediated side effects that can affect multiple organs and systems3. These events can range in severity from mild signs or symptoms to potentially life-threatening manifestations and may require specific therapeutic interventions4. Although irAEs have been reported in numerous clinical trials, there is considerable variability in their incidence, and further research is essential to better understand their underlying mechanisms and associated risk factors5.

The broad spectrum of irAEs is thought to result from the heightened activity of T cells, with these toxicities occurring in up to 90% of patients treated with ICIs.

The most commonly involved systems include the gastrointestinal tract, endocrine glands, skin, and liver. Less frequently, irAEs can impact the central nervous, cardiovascular, pulmonary, musculoskeletal, and hematologic systems6.

Previous studies have shown that irAEs can vary depending on the type of ICI and the affected organ7. PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors are associated with a greater incidence of adverse cutaneous, pulmonary, and endocrine events, whereas CTLA-4 inhibitors seem to be more strongly related to adverse gastrointestinal, hepatic, and endocrine events8. Moreover, irAEs might manifest at any time during and after ICI treatment, although some studies suggest that specific adverse events tend to occur at specific stages9.

Among the less common but clinically significant manifestations are rheumatologic irAEs, which affect approximately 5–10% of ICI-treated patients10. These manifestations encompass a diverse array of disorders, including inflammatory arthritis, sicca syndrome, polymyalgia rheumatica, scleroderma, myositis, and vasculitis. Arthralgia and myalgia are the most frequently reported rheumatologic irAEs in clinical trials, with prevalence rates ranging from 1% to 43% and 2% to 21%, respectively11. Effective management of these conditions requires careful assessment and a tailored therapeutic approach. Limited awareness of rheumatic immune-related adverse events (irAEs) poses a significant challenge in their identification and management.

Given the growing use of ICI therapy in oncology, it is crucial to understand and properly manage irAEs. Previous studies have suggested the usefulness of management strategies such as regular monitoring of affected organ function, administration of corticosteroids, and, in severe cases, temporary or permanent suspension of ICI treatment12,13. The use of a systematic approach to detect and manage irAEs is expected to improve patients’ quality of life and optimize the use of ICIs in cancer care. In this sense, the diverse spectrum of potential immune-related adverse events necessitates a multidisciplinary and collaborative approach involving healthcare providers across various clinical specialties, including rheumatologists, to manage its specific manifestations and participate in clinical guidelines.

With respect to rheumatic irAEs (R-irAEs), studies focused specifically on rheumatic irAEs are few in number and have heterogeneous results14,15, in part because of the heterogeneity of the clinical descriptions16. This represented a significant point, as in several surveys conducted in different countries, over 70% of rheumatologists reported a lack of confidence and experience in managing patients with rheumatic irAEs17,18. In this study, our aim was to describe the frequency of rheumatic immune-mediated adverse effects observed in two cohorts from two oncology services in Spain, assessing the specific rheumatologic manifestations that appeared as side effects, the type of cancer treated, the specific immune checkpoint inhibitor used, the time between treatment initiation and the onset of side effects and the type of management performed. This information might help inform physicians about the potential clinical implications of ICI therapy and identify future research areas to improve the safety and efficacy of these treatments in clinical practice.

Methods

This was a retrospective, observational study involving two hospitals in different regions of Spain, Catalunya and Cantabria (Parc Taulí University Hospital in Sabadell and Marqués de Valdecillas University Hospital in Santander). We performed a systematic revision of all the patients receiving ICI treatment according to the Pharmacy registries of the two Spanish care institutions, including a 6-year period, from January 2016 to December 2022.

Following a thorough revision of the electronic health records (EHRs) of these individuals and on the basis of the expert opinion of a rheumatologist, we established a clinical case definition for rheumatic irAEs (R-irAEs). The meticulous examination of the EHRs performed by the rheumatologists who participated in the study ensured that all patients who initiated immunotherapy during the specified period were included and evaluated, although the study focused exclusively on those who developed rheumatologic manifestations after treatment, as documented in the clinical notes and confirmed by the rheumatologist. The data collected included demographic information, type of cancer and age at diagnosis, age at R-irAE manifestation onset, specific immunotherapy details (dose, regimen, number of cycles administered) and personal and family history of autoimmune diseases of patients that developed an R-irAE, when available. Chemotherapy and concurrent treatments received, reasons for treatment discontinuation and biological data, including inflammatory markers and immune factors, were also documented when available as per clinical practice. Finally, we recorded all the data related to R-irAEs. First, the type of Rheumatic manifestations was diagnosed per EHRs and reviewed by the rheumatologist, and then the management interventions registered for R-irAE treatment included glucocorticoids, immunosuppressants, and biologic agents with their respective regimens and doses; the therapeutic response was categorized as ICI discontinuation or resolution of the manifestation; the requirement for rescue therapy; and, finally, mortality (time and cause of death), when available.

A descriptive statistical analysis was conducted to summarize the collected demographic, clinical and treatment data. Categorical variables were described using absolute and relative frequencies, whereas continuous variables were summarized with mean values and standard deviations to capture central tendency and variability. Cross-tabulations and stratified analyses were also performed to explore the relationships between patient characteristics further.

The study protocol adheres to the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the ethics committees of the respective hospitals. Owing to the retrospective study design, the requirement for informed consent was requested from the Research Ethics Committee (CEIm) and subsequently waived.

Results

The study included a total of 734 patients who received immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy, with 227 (30.9%) developing any irAEs, with 54 (7.35%) individuals experiencing R-irAEs according to the expert opinion of a rheumatologist, which represents a 23.8% of the total irAEs. The demographic data of this group included 29 (53.7%) males and 25 (46.3%) females, with a mean age of 64.6 years (SD ± 11.5) at the initiation of ICI treatment (Table 1). Notably, a minority of these patients that developed R-irAEs, only four, had a history of autoimmune disease prior to ICI therapy (1 patient psoriatic arthritis, 1 patient psoriasis, 2 patients spondylarthritis).

The malignancies encompassed a total of seven different types, with lung cancer (n = 24) and melanoma (n = 18) being the most represented forms of cancer (77.7%). We observed several other malignancies, including colorectal, renal cell, urothelial, gastric, hepatocellular, and oral cavity cancers, although they represented less than 25% of our series overall. The majority of these patients, 50 out of 54 (93%), received ICIs as monotherapy regimens. In terms of specific ICI molecules, 48 patients (88.9%) received anti-PD1 ICI treatment, 5 (9.2%) were treated with anti-PDL1, and only 1 (1.9%) anti-CLTA4 ICI treatment was prescribed.

The onset of R-irAEs occurred in the majority of cases within the first six months following the initiation of ICI treatment, except for myositis, which in all cases appeared shortly (within the first 4 weeks after the first dose), and Sicca syndrome, which emerged later (up to 22 months after therapy initiation). The spectrum of R-irAEs was diverse, with the most represented entities, including polymyalgia rheumatica (fulfilling the 2012 classification criteria; n = 12) and various forms of arthralgia (n = 26), including rheumatoid arthritis-like syndrome (polyarthritis presented in similar distribution as RA; n = 10), unspecific arthralgia (joint pain with inflammatory pattern, without clear clinical suspicion of arthritis; n = 9) and unspecified arthritis (joint involvement with clear clinical suspicion of arthritis and, in a minority of cases, imaging confirmation; n = 7), representing 70.5% of the R-irAEs registered. The remaining 16 (29.5%) comprised myositis (n = 5), of which 2 presented with confirmed myasthenia and one with confirmed myocarditis, Sicca syndrome (defined as dryness of eyes and/or mouth, appearing after ICI exposure, with other secondary causes of dryness discarded), psoriatic arthritis, large vessel vasculitis, and spondylarthritis, with myositis and Sicca syndrome being the most frequently reported among the less common entities, each with a frequency of 9.2%.” (Table 2).

Laboratory findings revealed elevated levels of both C-reactive protein (CRP) and ESR in 18 out of 21 and 8 out of 10 patients, respectively, in those with available data. Autoantibody assays were positive in 11 of the 40 patients tested, with antinuclear antibodies (ANAs) being the most commonly identified (n = 5), three of whom exhibited a homogeneous pattern, although none of them developed systemic lupus erythematous or SLE-like syndrome as R-irAEs. Autoantibody assays were positive in 11 of 40 patients tested, with positive antinuclear antibodies (ANAs) in 5, positive anti-thyroglobulin in 2 patients, and positive anti-beta 2-Glicoprotein, anti-SRP, anti-parietal cell, and anti-GAD in 1 patient each. Of those with positive ANA, three had a homogeneous pattern, without clinical features of systemic lupus erythematous.

Regarding the treatments received; a handful of patients were treated with NSAIDs only (n = 2) with good response. These included unspecified arthralgia and spondyloarthropathy. One was treated with intraarticular cortisone and NSAID with good response. Majority of patients with R-iRAEs were managed with corticosteroids (n = 40; 74%) with some patients requiring additional treatments, including synthetic immunosuppressants (n = 3), biologic agents (n = 3), and intravenous immunoglobulins (n = 4). Of those with predominantly joint involvement, only one patient with ICI-PsA required csDMARD (methotrexate) which lead to clinical improvement. The five patients with ICI-myositis were treated most aggressively, with csDMARDs (Mycophenolate), two with bDMARDs (Rituximab) and 4 with IVIG. Despite aggressive treatment, only one had clinical improvement. There were two cases of ICI-LVV manifest with giant cell arteritis syptomatology, treated with corticosteroids and, in one patient, anti-IL6 bDMARD, with good response in the patient that was treated only with corticosteroids. In our cohort, 38 (70.4%) patients subjected to therapeutic interventions achieved clinical improvement and 18 (33.3%) patients required ICI discontinuation due to the severity of the R-irAE (Table 3).

Discussion

The main goal of our study was to establish the prevalence and characteristics of R-irAEs in a cohort of patients treated with ICIs. Our findings indicated that the cumulative six-year prevalence of R-irAEs is approximately 7.35%, aligning with previously reported ranges in the literature (from 2.6% to 10%10,14,19,20. The fact that R-irAEs are not as frequent as other irAEs, such as dermatologic irAEs (incidences ranging from 30 to 60%), or digestive tract irAEs (10–30%)8, could be in part due to underdiagnosis of the most nonspecific or mild R-irAEs, such as Grade I inflammatory arthralgias or sicca syndrome. Lack of rheumatologist confidence and knowledge of these events might contribute to the underdiagnosis. Both these points highlight the necessity of a multidisciplinary approach to R-irAEs identification and management, and the need for deeper research and education of the involved medical specialists. Another important issue that might have contributed to this possible misdiagnosis of R-irAEs is due to the retrospective nature of our study, in which only ESH reported symptomatology information was utilised to identify and classify R-irAEs, with only a minority of patients being formally referred to rheumatology consultations and properly evaluated.

The spectrum of R-irAEs reported in our study was diverse, with the most common being arthritis/arthralgia (including RA-like arthritis and unspecified inflammatory arthritis) and polymyalgia rheumatica-like syndrome. Our data is consistent with previous publications20, but we found a higher incidence of myositis in our series than in previous publications21. Further studies on the risk factors that may trigger this higher incidence in our patients (age, history of muscle damage, ICI type and combinations, tumour type,…) could prove valuable to predict the risk of developing ICI-myositis. Other characteristics of ICI-myositis such as early onset after ICI exposure (during the first 3 months, association with myasthenia and/or myocarditis, is consistent with previously reported data21, making it the most serious rheumatic adverse effect, with a high mortality rate, requiring systemic treatment and ICI discontinuation in most cases, which makes it one of the most irAEs to be quickly identified and treated.

Interestingly, the presence of a pre-existing autoimmune disease (PAD) was lower in our cohorts than previous studies22, which may be due to exclusion criteria to access to some clinical trials involving ICI treatments and oncologists’ preferring other antineoplastic treatments in those patients with PAD to avoid exacerbation of their autoimmune disease. Of those with PAD, they presented mild symptoms compatible with the exacerbation of their autoimmune disease that had a good response to GC treatment, which could suggest that a past history of autoimmunity, although it presents a greater risk of irAEs or autoimmune disease flares, is not a crucial predisposing factor for the development of severe R-irAEs, therefore not limiting those patients when considering ICI treatment. However, this was not the goal of our study and is a complex area that warrants further investigation, particularly considering the potential for underdiagnosis or reporting bias.

Although tumour type (lung cancer and melanoma being the most frequent) and ICI treatment types and distributions (anti-PD-1 therapy being the most commonly used) were consistent with clinical guidelines and protocols23,24, our study centered on the patients that presented R-irAE, which presents a limitation regarding the lack of information on the remaining patients that received ICI treatment (baseline characteristics, tumour type, ICI type received).

Our results support the rapid onset of symptoms within the first 6 months after the initiation of ICI treatment, in most cases (Table 2). The data in the literature concerning this subject are heterogeneous and range from 4 days to more than 14 months25,26. Most likely, the fact that, in our series, the results from a systematic review of medical records by a rheumatologist may explain these differences. A similar point can be made regarding laboratory findings. Elevated CRP and altered ESR are common, supporting the evidence that suggests that an inflammatory substrate of these processes could serve prognostic value in predicting the development of R-irAEs27, although elevated CRP and/or ESR in oncologic patients can be derived from other factors. The prevalence of positive autoantibody tests is similar to that reported in other publications28; however, not all patients were screened for autoantibodies, and there was no pre-treatment autoantibody screening, so these results warrant further analysis. Both these points highlight the need for a multidisciplinary approach involving oncologists and other specialists such as rheumatologists from the early stages of ICI treatment, so that an appropriate clinical and analytical evaluation can be performed.

In our study, treatment approaches varied, with a majority of patients responding to corticosteroids, a finding that is in agreement with established treatment guidelines29,30. Other treatments, such as csDMARDs or bDMARDs, also included in the guidelines, were also used, perhaps in lower proportions than those used in recent publications because of the 6 years of retrospective data used in our study. Regarding ICI treatment discontinuation due to ICI toxicity, R-irAEs present some differences from other irAEs, considering even Grade 3 manifestations such as arthritis, may require only temporary discontinuation of ICI therapy until symptoms resolve, in contrast to manifestations such as irAE colitis or hypophysitis, or cardiovascular events, in which permanent ICI discontinuation is recommended12. However, when considering the rare but severe R-irAEs, mainly myositis and giant cell arteritis, permanent ICI discontinuation may be necessary, which raises concerns about the management of the more complex and severe R-irAEs and the impact these treatments have on the antineoplastic effects of the ICI.

Our study presents several limitations, including its retrospective design, the fact that the information was obtained by reviewing EHR, and that the baseline information of ICI receiving patients that did not develop a R-irAE was not acquired, which limited the possibility to establish standardized diagnosis criteria and indices of rheumatic disease activity and response to treatment, and the lack of a standardized protocol for the diagnosis and management of R-irAEs across the two institutions, which reinforces the call for uniform guidelines, as advocated by the Society for Immunotherapy of Cancer (SITC) and other organizations22, and the need for referral to the Rheumatology Clinic when those rheumatic manifestations are identified. However, the fact that data was obtained after an exhaustive review of all clinical records of patients receiving ICIs by a rheumatologist minimizes the potential biases of inclusion and allows us to establish with greater precision the prevalence and clinical spectrum of the observed manifestations. Moreover, evidence regarding the specific risk of developing R-irAEs depending on the tumour and ICI type could not be accurately determined. With respect to the association between pre-existing rheumatic autoimmune diseases and the development of non-rheumatic irAEs, our study design does not allow us to analyse this, as it is focused on the identification of clinical manifestations compatible with R-irAEs, but it does not follow patients with previous autoimmune diseases that did not present those manifestations.

In summary, our work aims to contribute to the understanding of R-irAEs associated with ICI therapy, highlighting the need for systematization and a multidisciplinary approach for monitoring and managing these adverse events to optimize patient outcomes, with adequate formation of rheumatologists and oncologists on these shared pathologies. Future research focused on prospective data collection may help define predictive biomarkers for R-irAEs, as well as future intervention trials to refine treatment strategies and improve patient outcomes by improving the management of these irAEs and minimizing the detrimental effects of these treatments on ICI therapy.
