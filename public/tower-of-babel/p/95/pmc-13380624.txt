Resolving Abrikosov vortex entry in superconducting nanostring resonators via displacement-noise spectroscopy in cavity optomechanics
Authors: Thomas Luschmann, Tahereh Sadat Parvini, Lukas Niekamp, Achim Marx, Rudolf Gross, Hans Huebl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abrikosov vortices in type-II superconductors influence current flow, dissipation and coherence, thereby limiting superconducting quantum technologies. In mesoscopic circuit elements, the entry and pinning of a single vortex can shift resonant frequencies, redistribute flux and degrade device performance. Here, we use a flux-mediated cavity-optomechanical platform, in which a suspended superconducting aluminum nanostring forms one arm of a SQUID terminating a microwave resonator, to investigate vortex-entry processes through nanomechanical elastic signatures. Operating at T ≈ 85 mK with 0.25 mT in-plane-field resolution, we resolve discrete jumps of the mechanical resonance frequency consistent with the stochastic entry of one or a few singly quantized Abrikosov vortices. Their field positions vary between thermal cycles, indicating disorder-mediated nucleation through a quenched pinning landscape. These events are superimposed on a smooth power-law stiffening background identified with the collective Campbell–Labusch elastic response. Recasting the jumps as equivalent force signatures yields attonewton-scale forces and, within a single-event pinning model, characteristic vortex-pinning energies of 0.09 − 0.44 eV. The smooth background gives a collective Labusch parameter of order 1014 N m−4. Our results establish cavity-optomechanical sensing as an in situ probe of vortex matter and identify vortex entry as a quantitative design constraint for flux-mediated superconducting quantum circuits.

Introduction

Nanomechanical resonators have emerged as powerful sensors for the detection of minuscule forces, accelerations, or masses1–12. In addition, they allow the investigation of the intrinsic properties of the underlying materials, such as Young’s modulus, stress, loss mechanisms, and coupling between elastic and other degrees of freedom in the solid-state13–28. Often, these properties are encoded in the displacement and resonance frequency of the oscillator, which are then measured using optical, electrical, or optomechanical schemes29–31. Cavity optomechanics, also known as nano-electromechanics, combines superconducting (quantum) microwave circuits with mechanical oscillators and has the potential to study mechanical resonators made of superconducting materials.

Type-II superconductors exposed to magnetic fields above the lower critical field Hc1 enter the mixed state, in which magnetic flux penetrates the material in the form of Abrikosov vortices. The pinning and motion of these vortices govern the critical current and generate dissipation in the flux-flow state32. Mesoscopic structures are particularly well suited for studying vortex matter, because their reduced dimensions can reveal discrete vortex nucleation, nontrivial flux configurations, and pinning dynamics33–36. Local magnetic imaging techniques, including scanning SQUID microscopy37, Hall-probe microscopy38, and magnetic force microscopy39, provide direct real-space information on vortex distributions, configurations, and pinning landscapes, in favorable cases with nanometer-scale spatial resolution37. However, these approaches can be experimentally demanding at millikelvin temperatures and elevated magnetic fields and, depending on the modality, may also perturb the vortex state through probe stray fields or tip–vortex interactions39. Electrical transport measurements, by contrast, are non-invasive but inherently yield only the ensemble-averaged response of the vortex population32. These complementary limitations motivate integrated, minimally invasive approaches to mesoscopic vortex detection. Mechanical sensing offers a distinct and complementary route. Vibrating-reed techniques40–44 established that the resonance of a vibrating type-II superconductor encodes vortex pinning elasticity through the Campbell-Labusch stiffness. Nanomechanical resonators have also been used previously to explore mesoscopic vortex physics. For example, Bolle et al.45 investigated micrometer-sized NbSe2 crystals on a silicon micromechanical torsional oscillator and monitored the resonance frequency through the magnetic torque transmitted from the crystal to the resonator. At T/Tc ≈ 0.95 and near Hc1, they observed sharp, discrete jumps in resonance frequency associated with irreversible vortex rearrangements. More recently, superconducting cavity optomechanical platforms have enabled continuous, non-invasive, and highly sensitive readout of nanomechanical elements under magnetic fields31,46–52. Exploiting such a platform, we previously showed that the intrinsic mechanical resonance frequency of a superconducting polycrystalline aluminum nanostring exhibits an offset power-law increase with in-plane magnetic field beyond the Lorentz-force description53, and attributed this phenomenologically to vortex penetration without direct experimental evidence or quantitative microscopic justification. That unresolved question motivates the present work. By improving the magnetic-field resolution by more than an order of magnitude to 0.25 mT and carrying out the measurements at T ≈ 85 mK (T/Tc ≈ 0.07), we resolve discrete, stochastic jumps superimposed on the smooth power-law background, with field positions that vary between thermal cycles, consistent with stochastic vortex nucleation in a quenched disorder landscape. From these signatures, we simultaneously extract both the collective Campbell–Labusch pinning elasticity and single-vortex pinning stiffness and energetics within a single nanoscale, device-integrated experiment. Unlike earlier externally coupled micromechanical studies45, our platform uses the superconducting nanostring itself as both the vortex-hosting element and the mechanical resonator, providing direct, device-level access to vortex-induced elastic stiffening in a superconducting geometry relevant to quantum-circuit applications.

Device concept

We realize a hybrid nano-electromechanical platform in which a superconducting aluminum (Al) nanostring resonator is integrated into the loop of a flux-sensitive dc SQUID. The SQUID terminates the end of a superconducting λ/4 coplanar waveguide (CPW) resonator to ground, thereby realizing a flux-tunable optomechanical interaction46,47,53. The device is fabricated using standard nanofabrication techniques, combining double-layer Al shadow evaporation with reactive-ion etching on a high-resistivity silicon substrate. The suspended nanostring has dimensions (l, w, d) = (30, 0.2, 0.11) μm and an effective mass of meff = 0.6 pg (for details, see ref. 47). Figure 1a depicts the device layout, with an inset highlighting the dc SQUID containing the two Josephson junctions (JJs) and the suspended nanostring, together with the applied in-plane (Bip) and out-of-plane (Boop) magnetic control fields and the nanostring displacement δx. The equivalent circuit is shown in Fig. 1b. The CPW resonator has a resonance frequency of ωc/2π ≈ 7.4 GHz at zero flux bias. The dc SQUID’s flux-tunable inductance enables in situ control of the CPW resonance from 7.4 GHz to 6.6 GHz via Boop53. In addition, out-of-plane motion of the nanostring in the in-plane magnetic field Bip modulates the flux threading the SQUID loop, thereby changing the SQUID’s effective inductance and the microwave-resonator frequency. This implements a flux-mediated optomechanical interaction with single-photon coupling rate g0 = η (∂Φωc) l Bip xzpf, where η, l, and Bip are the mode-shape factor, nanostring length, and in-plane magnetic flux density, respectively46,48,53,54. Experimental and theoretical considerations

Measurements are performed in a dilution refrigerator at T ≈ 85 mK after cooling the sample in zero applied magnetic field (zero-field-cooled, ZFC). The experimental protocol, magnetic-field control, and microwave displacement-noise readout are described in the Methods Section. For mechanical spectroscopy, we use thermal-noise sideband spectroscopy with an ultra-low-power microwave probe tone at frequency ωp (Pprobe < 2 fW), tuned to the flux-tunable cavity resonance, ωp = ωc(Φb), thereby avoiding the optomechanical spring effect31. The optomechanical interaction generates motional sidebands at ωp ± Ωm (Fig. 2a). Complementary vector network analyzer (VNA) measurements are used to extract the flux-tunable mechanical frequency in regimes where the anti-Stokes sideband signal is too weak to resolve. Sweeping Boop over the full accessible SQUID flux period at fixed Bip generates the effective SQUID flux bias Φb and produces a periodic, dome-shaped modulation of the mechanical frequency Ωm(Φb; Bip) (see Fig. 2c). Small residual misalignment between the applied field directions and the SQUID-loop normal, or weak cross-coupling between Bip and the effective flux bias, can shift the Φb axis. This shift is absorbed phenomenologically into the fit parameter ϕoff (see Methods). The dome shape of the flux-dependence of Ωm arises predominantly from flux-dependent circulating currents in the SQUID loop (denoted by J in Fig. 1a). In the presence of the in-plane field, a Lorentz force acts on these currents and, in turn, on the nanostring motion46,53,54. To quantify the observed Ωm(Φb; Bip), we model the nanostring as a damped harmonic oscillator driven by a displacement-dependent Lorentz force46,56. The Lorentz force FL = ηlBipJ arises from the interaction of Bip with the circulating current J and introduces a flux-dependent modification of the mechanical frequency. Vortex entry signatures in the resonance of an aluminum nanostring

To justify a vortex-based interpretation, we first estimate the superconducting parameters of the Al film. The capacity of a superconductor to host vortices is governed by the Ginzburg–Landau parameter κGL = λ/ξ, the ratio of the magnetic penetration depth λ to the coherence length ξ. The history dependence of the resolved entry sequence is consistent with disorder-mediated vortex nucleation. Across the independent field sweeps in Fig. 3, discrete jumps appear in every run, supporting their physical origin, while their precise field positions vary from run to run. This behavior is expected in a mesoscopic superconducting strip with a quenched but disordered pinning landscape. Edge roughness, grain boundaries, and local thickness variations define weak nucleation sites that are fixed by fabrication and remain unchanged by thermal cycling. Vortex entry through these sites is governed by the local Bean–Livingston surface barrier, which is non-uniformly reduced by such defects65,74,75. As a result, several edge sites with comparable threshold fields can compete during a given ZFC ramp. The realized sequence of vortex-entry events, and the spatial arrangement of vortices after pinning, can therefore vary between independent cooldowns and depend on magnetic and thermal history33,35,58,69,76.

Vortex-induced elastic response of the nanostring

Vibrating type-II superconducting resonators exhibit magnetic-field-dependent resonance shifts through several well-established mechanisms42,44. In the mixed state, Bc1 < Bip < Bc2, Abrikosov vortices penetrate the nanostring through geometry-dependent entry pathways that modify the resonance frequency. One such mechanism is magnetoelastic coupling between Abrikosov vortices and the crystal lattice, established within the Ginzburg–Landau theoretical framework77–79. The physical origin of this coupling is the so-called volume effect, that is, the difference between the specific volumes of the normal and superconducting phases. Therefore, the normal cores of flux lines result in local deformations in the surrounding superconducting material, allowing vortices to interact via the resulting elastic field78,79. Since the relative volume change of Al is of the order of 10−880, the expected frequency shift in our tensile-strained nanostrings is very small, ΔΩ0/Ω0 ~ 10−8, and is too small to explain the observed discrete steps of magnitude ΔΩ0/Ω0 ~ 10−5. While the sign of the effect, an increase in Ω0, is consistent with normal-state contraction, the magnitude rules out this mechanism as the dominant contribution.

The dominant mechanism governing these frequency shifts is vortex pinning elasticity. In the small-amplitude Campbell regime, vortices trapped at defects or sample edges oscillate within their pinning wells, acting as distributed springs that stiffen the resonator. Discussion

We have demonstrated that cavity optomechanics provides a highly sensitive probe of vortex matter in mesoscopic superconductors. By embedding a superconducting aluminum nanostring into a SQUID-terminated microwave cavity and monitoring its mechanical resonance frequency, we uncover two distinct magnetic-field-dependent signatures that reveal complementary aspects of vortex physics. First, the resonance frequency exhibits a smooth power-law scaling, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\Omega }_{0}^{2}\propto {{B}_{{{{\rm{ip}}}}}}^{1.81}$$\end{document}Ω02∝Bip1.81, consistent with elastic stiffening in the Campbell regime as vortex density increases. From this collective behavior, we extract a Labusch parameter of αL ~ 1014N m−4, quantifying the ensemble-averaged pinning elasticity characteristic of disorder-mediated collective pinning in polycrystalline thin films. Superimposed on this background, discrete frequency jumps are signatures consistent with the entry of one or a few singly quantized Abrikosov vortices, resolved with attonewton-scale force sensitivity and corresponding, under the single-vortex-event assumption, to event-scale pinning energies of 0.09–0.44 eV. These vortex-entry events occur at stochastically varying field values between thermal cycles, reflecting the disorder landscape that governs nucleation pathways.

These results establish cavity optomechanics as a quantitative tool to investigate vortex physics and test pinning theories in mesoscopic superconducting structures. By operating directly in chip-relevant geometries, this approach enables the study of fundamental superconducting phenomena in architectures akin to those used in superconducting quantum circuits and offers a route to identifying how material disorder and device design affect flux-noise-related decoherence. Frequency precision could be further enhanced through coherent actuation schemes, for example by piezoelectric driving86, particularly in tensile-strained resonator geometries where dissipation dilution narrows the mechanical linewidth and extends the linear dynamic range91 (see Supplementary Note 6). In addition, our results clarify a central design trade-off in flux-mediated cavity-optomechanical architectures, particularly those employing inductive coupling and aiming for the single-photon strong-coupling regime, g0 > κ, Γm. Increasing the in-plane field can enhance g0, but may also promote vortex entry and motion, introducing microwave loss, mechanical dissipation, and flux noise. By quantitatively identifying the vortex-entry threshold and the associated pinning energies, our work makes this trade-off explicit and points to mitigation strategies based on materials choice, device geometry, and operating conditions.

Methods

Experimental protocol

All measurements were performed in a dilution refrigerator at T ≈ 85 mK using a zero-field-cooled (ZFC) protocol. For each independent run, the device was first cooled to base temperature in nominally zero applied magnetic field before the magnetic fields were applied. The in-plane field Bip, oriented along the nanostring, was generated by a superconducting solenoid and varied up to 35 mT. In the high-resolution measurements reported here, the Bip step size was 0.25 mT. The out-of-plane field Boop, used to control the SQUID flux bias, was supplied by a small superconducting coil mounted on the sample enclosure. Magnetic shielding was omitted to avoid screening of Bip, and slow fluctuations of the static flux environment were compensated by a feedback loop acting on the out-of-plane coil. The feedback error signal was obtained from the transmission of a weak off-resonant stabilization tone, kept below the probe power. For each fixed value of Bip, Boop was swept over the accessible SQUID flux period, and the mechanical response was recorded; the in-plane field was then incremented and the measurement repeated.

The mechanical response was acquired primarily by thermomechanical sideband spectroscopy. At each flux bias, a weak microwave probe was tuned to the instantaneous cavity resonance, ωp = ωc(Φb), with an estimated device power below 2 fW. This ultra-low-power, resonant probing condition kept the microwave resonator in the linear-response regime and suppressed dynamical backaction; no coherent drive was applied to the mechanical mode. The thermal displacement fluctuations of the nanostring generated motional sidebands at ωp ± Ωm. The transmitted signal was amplified and downconverted, and the voltage power spectral density was recorded around the anti-Stokes sideband at ωp + Ωm. Each spectrum was fitted with the response of a damped harmonic oscillator to extract Ωm and Γm. At flux-bias points where the thermomechanical sideband signal was insufficient, complementary vector-network-analyzer measurements were used to determine Ωm(Φb; Bip). The intrinsic nanostring frequency Ω0(Bip) was then obtained from these traces using the fitting procedure described below.

Cryogenic setup

All experiments reported in this work were performed at millikelvin temperatures in a commercial dilution refrigerator, requiring microwave wiring to access the device under test (DUT). To suppress thermal noise and minimize spurious reflections, the input microwave line incorporates a total attenuation of 50 dB before reaching the DUT. On the output side, the transmitted signal is amplified by a high-electron-mobility transistor (HEMT) located at the 4K stage. Three microwave circulators are installed between the DUT and the HEMT to isolate the device from amplifier back-action and suppress reflected signals. In addition to the microwave circuitry, a dedicated DC line supplies a superconducting coil mounted outside the DUT package. By varying the current through this coil, the out-of-plane magnetic field Boop can be adjusted, thereby tuning the cavity resonance frequency through changes in the effective Josephson inductance of the dc-SQUID. A large in-plane magnetic field Bip is provided by a commercial three-axis vector magnet, at the center of which the DUT and the auxiliary coil are positioned.
