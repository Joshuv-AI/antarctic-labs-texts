Air-stable Li5FeO4 additive enabled by carbon coating for energy-dense lithium-ion batteries
Authors: Canshang Liu, Hao Zhang, Weiwei Zhou, Xu Tian, Tiantian Zhang, Sicheng Niu, Jianing Li, Minglei Cao, Qin Wang, Fei Lv, Tangping Peng, Lijuan Tao, Xiaodong Rang, Zhicheng Chen, Xin Su
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLi5FeO4 is a promising pre-lithiation additive for the positive electrode in lithium-ion batteries, offering the potential to enhance energy density. However, its susceptibility to air degradation presents a significant challenge for commercialization. In this study, we develop an effective carbon coating strategy utilizing pitch to improve the air stability of Li5FeO4. The coating process results in the formation of a compact carbon layer on the surface of Li5FeO4 particles, enabling the coated Li5FeO4 to retain a high specific capacity of 743.4 mAh g−1 after 72 h of exposure to air with 20% relative humidity. This retention represents 92.3% of its initial capacity and 85.7% of its theoretical maximum capacity. In contrast, uncoated Li5FeO4 undergoes rapid degradation, losing most of its electrochemical activity within just 4 h under identical conditions. Beyond improving air stability, the carbon coating enhances Li5FeO4’s specific capacity, rate capability, and cycling stability. To substantiate the practical application of carbon-coated Li5FeO4, we construct a pouch-type cell, which exhibits a 13.7% increase in energy density compared to the cell without the prelithiation additive. These findings collectively suggest that the carbon-coated Li5FeO4 represents a viable strategy for advancing the commercial deployment of this material in lithium-ion batteries.Subject terms: Batteries, Batteries

Introduction

In the realm of lithium-ion batteries (LIBs), the ongoing mission has been to push the boundaries of energy density1–3. However, a notable impediment in this endeavor is the initial capacity loss that arises from the formation of the solid electrolyte interphase (SEI), leading to the inevitable depletion of up to 15% of active Li+ ions. This issue is particularly acute for new-generation silicon-based negative electrodes, which undergo pronounced volume changes during Li+ ions intake and release processes4,5. These volume changes result in continuous destruction and reformation of the SEI layer, severely limiting the achievable energy density of LIBs6.

To address the lithium loss during the first charging process of LIBs, the industry has widely validated a direct and efficient method: introducing supplementary lithium into the battery, a process known as prelithiation7–10. It should be noted that prelithiation can be applied to different battery components, including positive electrodes11,12, negative electrodes13,14, and separators15. Unlike negative electrode prelithiation techniques, such as chemical16,17, electrochemical18, and additive-assisted methods19,20, which may encounter issues like uneven lithiation, limited environmental compatibility, and safety concerns, positive electrode prelithiation utilizing lithium-rich compounds to replenish lithium in the positive electrode is more economical and simpler to execute8,10. Generally, this method presents a straightforward process, ensures high safety standards, and can be readily integrated into the current LIB industry without significant difficulties. To date, researchers have explored various additives for positive electrode prelithiation, including Li2NiO221, Li2O22, Li2O223, Li2C4O424, Li2S25, Li3N26, Li6CoO427, and Li5FeO4 (LFO)11,28,29, etc. Whereas, incorporating binary compounds like Li2O, Li2O2, Li2S, and Li3N as prelithiation reagents into current battery processing technology faces significant challenges. The primary issues are their incompatibility with existing electrode preparation systems and their high cost. Alternatively, ternary compounds like Li2NiO2, Li2C4O4, Li6CoO4, and LFO demonstrate good safety, stability, and compatibility, suggesting their suitability for real-world applications10.

Among the emerging positive electrode prelithiation additives (as illustrated in Fig. 1a), LFO with an anti-fluorite structure has garnered significant attention. This structure originates from the substitution of Li+ ions with Fe3+ ions and vacancies within a Li2O framework, resulting in a three-dimensional (3D) network where both Li–O and Fe–O bonds form tetrahedral arrangements28. The unique architectural configuration of LFO imparts distinctive characteristics. When functioning as a prelithiation additive, this configuration facilitates the release of a substantial quantity of Li+ ions, granting LFO a high theoretical capacity for LIB applications. Additionally, the charge/discharge voltage window of LFO aligns well with that of commercial positive electrode materials, enabling seamless integration into existing battery systems10,28,30. However, the same unique architectural configuration that confers LFO’s advantageous electrochemical properties also renders it prone to structural degradation. Specifically, its inherent structural instability makes LFO highly reactive with specific gases, particularly H2O and CO2. This characteristic renders it promising for gas chemisorption applications, where its reactivity with these gases can be harnessed for beneficial purposes31–34. Nonetheless, when considered as a prelithiation additive, LFO’s chemical instability poses a significant challenge. It decomposes swiftly upon air exposure, and this vulnerability to air-triggered deterioration significantly undermines its commercial potential. The rapid breakdown not only impairs its performance but also jeopardizes its reliability in real-world applications. Future research must therefore prioritize strategies to enhance LFO’s air stability without sacrificing its electrochemical performance, addressing this critical bottleneck to unlock its full potential in battery technologies.

In response to this challenge, researchers have embarked on studies to improve the air stability of LFO, with Zhang’s group making numerous attempts in this regard. Initially, they coated LFO with Li6CoO435, achieving a specific capacity of 518.7 mAh g−1 after 1 h of air exposure. Then, they explored modified carbon coatings, including F-doped36 and N, S-doped37 carbon, which allowed the LFO to maintain a capacity exceeding 500.0 mAh g−1 even after being exposed to humid air (20% relative humidity, RH) for up to 12 h. Furthermore, they experimented with direct Zr doping38, enabling the LFO to retain a capacity of 577.6 mAh g−1 following an 8 h exposure to humid air (20% RH). More recently, Zhang’s group utilized a polyethylene oxide-polypropylene oxide-polyethylene (P123) copolymer as a carbon coating source29, achieving stable performance in a CO2 atmosphere with a high capacity of 728.5 mAh g−1 after 24 h. However, in this work, they did not investigate the air stability of the P123-coated LFO. In parallel, Li et al. modified LFO through surface phosphating39, enabling it to retain a specific capacity of 637.3 mAh g−1 after 3 h of exposure to air at 25 °C and 57% RH. These attempts represent nearly all of the published efforts to bolster the air stability of LFO. Despite the advancements attained, current methodologies are still inoperative in realizing the commercial application of LFO, as the capacity retention rate of the modified LFO after being exposed to air for 24 h is hardly able to reach 80%. Consequently, the challenge of ensuring long-term air stability for LFO remains an unresolved issue.

In this work, we have devised a highly efficient pitch-enabled carbon encapsulation (PECE) strategy to create stable LFO. This coating strategy allows LFO to maintain a capacity of 743.4 mAh g−1 even after 72 h exposure to air with 20% RH. Importantly, the capacity retained after 72 h amounts to 92.3% of the initial capacity and 85.7% of the theoretical maximum, indicating enhanced air stability relative to earlier studies. This improved air stability is mainly attributed to the uniform and compact carbon coating enabled by the melt-processable nature of pitch. Simulation studies have preliminarily established a correlation between LFO’s susceptibility to air-induced degradation and its reactivity with H2O, thereby underscoring the crucial role of uniform carbon encapsulation. Furthermore, we investigate the impact of integrating LFO into commercial positive electrode materials on the overall battery performance. The efficacy of carbon-coated LFO (LFO@C) is rigorously validated through the pouch-type full cell assembled on a small-scale industrial pilot line. Comprehensively, the failure analysis of the pouch cells is provided, revealing the true role that LFO@C plays in practical applications. Our results demonstrate improved long-term air stability of LFO, with capacity retention increasing from approximately 70% after 8 h to over 98% after 24 h, in comparison with values reported in previous studies. Given that the testing conditions are aligned with industrial standards, these findings may accelerate the practical development of LFO as a prelithiation agent for the positive electrode.

Results

LFO reaction with H2O, CO2: theoretical study

As depicted in Fig. 1a, the major impediment to the commercialization of LFO is its instability upon exposure to air (Supplementary Table 1)15,24,29,40–43, primarily due to its tendency to undergo reactions with atmospheric H2O and CO2 (outlined in Eqs. 1 and 2). It is noted that theoretical investigations into these reactions have yet to be reported. In this study, we initiate by computing the Gibbs free energies (ΔG1 and ΔG2) for these reactions (Fig. 1b). Specifically, the ΔG1 value for the reaction between LFO and H2O is −1.987 eV, which is 3.33 times the ΔG2 value of −0.596 eV for the reaction with CO2. This finding suggests a thermodynamic preference for LFO to react with H2O over CO2.

To further reveal the reactivity of H2O and CO2 with LFO, we conducted calculations of the chemisorption energy of these molecules on the (112) and (222) crystal surface of LFO in an air environment. The rationale for selecting these specific planes stemmed from their distinctive X-ray diffraction (XRD) peak intensities (Li5FeO4-PDF#75-1253), which indicate a high probability of surface exposure for both the (112) and (222) planes. This exposure renders these planes conducive to the chemisorption of H2O and CO2 from the surrounding atmosphere. As illustrated in Fig. 1c and Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2, during the chemisorption of CO2 molecules, the carbon atoms bind to the oxygen active sites on the LFO surface with a chemisorption energy (Eads) of −0.4154 eV and −0.3963 eV, while the oxygen atoms attach to the metal ionic sites through coulomb interactions, exhibiting an Eads of −0.5471 eV and −0.5160 eV. A comparison of these chemisorption energies reveals a tendency for CO2 to primarily form adsorptive bonds with the LFO surface through its carbon atoms. Given the favorable energetic landscape for CO2 binding (i.e., stronger chemisorption via carbon sites), LFO has been leveraged as a promising adsorbent for CO2 capture and storage, particularly under high-temperature conditions, where its robust surface interactions enable efficient molecular retention31–34. Conversely, when H2O molecules adsorb on the LFO surface, the hydrogen atoms form hydrogen bonds with the reactive oxygen species on the surface (Eads = −0.3312 eV and −0.2951 eV), while the oxygen atoms are adsorbed onto the metal ion sites via Coulomb interactions with an Eads of −0.9421 eV and −0.9155 eV. Notably, the oxygen atoms of H2O preferentially bond with the metal ions on the LFO surface. A comparison of the chemisorption energies of LFO with H2O and CO2 demonstrates that LFO exhibits a stronger chemisorption for H2O. Given the higher concentration of H2O (15.7 times that of CO2) at room temperature (25 °C) and 20% RH, combined with thermodynamic and chemisorption energy data, LFO is more likely to react with H2O in air, causing instability upon exposure.

The aforementioned findings underscore the heightened importance of enhancing LFO’s resistance to H2O. Therefore, developing a protective layer on the LFO surface that effectively shields it from air, particularly H2O, emerges as a viable strategy to achieve air stability for LFO (Fig. 1d). This protective layer would act as a barrier, mitigating the detrimental reactions between LFO and H2O, as well as CO2, thus paving the way for the potential commercialization of LFO materials.

Synthesis and characterization of LFO@C

Drawing from the preceding analysis, we have devised a carbon-coating strategy to isolate LFO from the ambient air environment. For the carbon coating to serve as an effective air barrier, it is imperative that it has a dense and uniform structure. This requirement highlights the critical importance of choosing a suitable carbon source. After a thorough evaluation, we identified pitch, phenolic resin (PF), and glucose as potential candidates. Preliminary characterization revealed significant limitations in using PF and glucose for carbon coating. For a detailed account of the challenges and drawbacks associated with these two carbon sources, please refer to the Supplementary Information (Supplementary Discussion 1, Supplementary Fig. 3, and Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). In contrast, pitch showed great promise as a carbon-coating material, capable of forming a dense and uniform carbon layer around LFO particles. We attribute the uniform encapsulation of LFO by pitch-derived carbon to the low softening point of the pitch we employed and our well-designed calcination process (as schematically shown in Fig. 2a). Specifically, after ball-milling and mixing, the pitch is applied to the LFO surface. A low-temperature holding step is then initiated, during which the pitch melts into a liquid state, facilitating uniform coating of the LFO. Subsequently, the temperature is rapidly increased. This is crucial to avoid the liquid pitch being dislodged from the LFO surface. As a result, this approach ensures that LFO is densely encapsulated by pitch-derived pyrolytic carbon. Prior to this, pure LFO was synthesized using nano-Fe2O3 and Li2O as raw materials through a solid-state method in an argon-filled glovebox. The XRD refinement confirms the successful synthesis of pure-phase LFO (Rp = 3.57%, Supplementary Fig. 4).

The scanning electron microscope (SEM) images show that both LFO and LFO@C are composed of single-crystal particles, each approximately 5 μm in size (Fig. 2b, c and Supplementary Fig. 5). Furthermore, the surface of pure LFO displays a coarse appearance, in contrast, the LFO@C demonstrates a smooth surface following the application of carbon coating. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) characterization further confirms the formation of a uniform and dense carbon layer that fully encapsulates the LFO particles (Fig. 2d–f and Supplementary Fig. 6a–d). Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (EDS) analysis reveals a carbon-rich surface with no detectable Fe, aligning with our expectations for the carbon coating (Supplementary Fig. 6e). Upon examination of the magnified edge of the LFO@C particle (Fig. 2f), it becomes evident that the carbon layer possesses a substantial thickness, ranging from approximately 80–100 nm, and exhibits an amorphous structure. A distinct boundary is visible between this layer and the underlying LFO.

To further validate the compactness of the carbon coating, we employ X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements to analyze both LFO@C and LFO. Consistent with our hypothesis, the XPS spectrum of pristine LFO exhibits unambiguous Fe 2p core-level peaks, confirming the presence of iron in its oxide form. In contrast, the LFO@C sample shows no discernible Fe-related signals, indicating complete suppression of Fe signal penetration through the carbon layer (Fig. 2g). Correspondingly, the Li 1s core-level analysis of LFO@C reveals no characteristic Li-derived peaks, while the C 1s region is dominated by prominent features attributable to C–C bonding configurations (Supplementary Fig. 7a, b). Given XPS’s high sensitivity to surface composition, these results strongly suggest the successful formation of a continuous and dense carbon coating on the surface of LFO@C. Moreover, the Raman spectrum of LFO shows its characteristic peaks within the range of 50–700 cm−1, with no additional phases being observed. After carbon coating, distinct peaks attributed to the D and G bonds of carbonaceous materials emerge, featuring an ID/IG ratio of 1.05 (Fig. 2h). This suggests that the carbon layer covering the surface of LFO is primarily amorphous in nature. According to the XRD Rietveld refinement analysis of LFO@C (Rp = 4.63%) (Fig. 2i), the carbon coating does not alter the fundamental structure of LFO nor introduce any significant impurities.

Furthermore, electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) measurement analysis of both LFO and LFO@C show that subsequent to the secondary calcination process aimed at achieving carbon coating, the concentration of free radicals in LFO increases by a factor of 1.94 (from 7.098 × 1012 spins g−1 to 1.376 × 1013 spins g−1) (Fig. 2j). This observation suggests that, although the carbon coating doesn’t change the overall structure of LFO, it does introduce defects within the material. To better support these EPR findings, we performed X-ray Absorption Spectroscopy (XAS) tests on LFO and LFO@C. Upon observing the Fe–K edge absorption peak of LFO and LFO@C (Supplementary Fig. 8a), it is noted that the Fe–K edge in LFO@C shifted to a lower energy position. This observation implies that a partial reduction in the oxidation state of Fe within LFO@C occurred during the carbon coating process. The likely cause of this reduction is the reductive atmosphere (e.g., CO) generated as a result of the pyrolysis of pitch. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 8b, c, wavelet transform extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) analysis reveals that the Fe–O bond peak intensity in LFO@C is lower than that in LFO, suggesting an increase in internal defects within LFO@C. Additionally, the Fe–O bond peak shifts to a low-k direction, indicating an expansion in the interatomic distance between Fe–O atoms. The carbon coating process introduces more oxygen vacancies in LFO, leading to a reduced oxidation state of Fe, a more disordered internal structure, and an enlarged interlayer spacing. These findings collectively indicate that carbon coating can induce more defects within LFO, facilitating the easier extraction of Li ions from the material.

Air stability evaluation of LFO@C vs LFO

To demonstrate the efficiency of pitch-derived carbon encapsulation in enhancing the air stability of LFO, we have conducted comprehensive analyses. Firstly, the experiment was started by placing both LFO@C and LFO directly in air (25 °C, 20% RH). It was observed that pure LFO underwent rapid degradation within a matter of minutes, accompanied by a noticeable macroscopic color change from light yellow to deep yellow, whereas LFO@C retained its black appearance (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 9). Accordingly, in situ Raman characterization of LFO@C and LFO exposed to air (25 °C, 20% RH) produced consistent results (Fig. 3b). To be specific, after just 1 min of exposure, the characteristic peaks of LFO began to diminish rapidly, whereas by approximately 6 min, they had nearly disappeared. This indicates that LFO is highly reactive with air, initiating its degradation process shortly after contact and propagating quickly throughout the entire material. In contrast, after carbon coating, the Raman spectrum of LFO@C under the same conditions remained essentially unchanged (Fig. 3c), implying that the internal structure of LFO@C could remain stable and resistant to air-induced degradation. It should be noted here that the carbon content in the aforementioned LFO@C and the LFO@C to be discussed later has undergone a series of optimization procedures, with the specific value being approximately 2.68 wt%. An insufficient carbon content is inadequate for attaining a uniform and dense coating on the LFO particles. Conversely, while an excessive carbon content can indeed confer good air stability to LFO, it concurrently dilutes the proportion of the active material within the composite. This dilution subsequently results in a diminished specific capacity. Comprehensive details and supplementary data are provided in the Supplementary Information (Supplementary Discussion 2, Supplementary Fig. 10, and Supplementary Table 4).

Because of the limited penetration depth of Raman testing, which primarily restricts signal capture to the powder’s surface, we sought to gain deeper insights into the bulk phase changes occurring in LFO and its interaction with air. To achieve this, we subjected both pure LFO and LFO@C to an environment of 20% RH air, while simultaneously monitoring mass variations and performing in situ XRD characterization. As depicted in Fig. 3d, the pure LFO experienced a swift increase in weight upon exposure to air, indicating its high reactivity with atmospheric conditions and the continuity of this reaction. In contrast, when LFO@C was exposed to air, its mass remained nearly unchanged over 12 h, confirming the effectiveness of the carbon layer in shielding the LFO from external air interactions. Meanwhile, in situ XRD results revealed that when pure LFO was exposed to air, its characteristic peaks quickly diminished. Then, the surface of LFO became coated with degradation products, which acted as a barrier against further decomposition. This process gradually decelerated until, approximately 8 h later, the characteristic peaks of the LFO phase were no longer detectable, indicating the near-complete decomposition of LFO. Corresponding decomposition products were characterized to mainly consist of Li2CO3 and LiOH (Supplementary Figs. 11 and 12). By comparison, the XRD analysis of LFO@C under the same conditions reveals that the characteristic peaks of the LFO phase persist without notable intensity attenuation or the appearance of impurity peaks within a 12 h period. This implies that LFO@C can maintain long-term stability in air. In view of the above characterizations, it is strongly believed that LFO@C features a full carbon coating that ensures its stability and resistance to air exposure.

To solidify the above hypothesis, we have further undertaken a series of rigorous electrochemical tests. It is noted that both LFO and LFO@C electrodes used in these tests underwent the entire preparation process, including mixing, coating, and slicing, in ambient air conditions (25 °C, 20% RH). The results disclosed that LFO@C demonstrated a remarkable specific capacity exceeding 800.0 mAh g−1, whereas pure LFO displayed a significantly lower specific capacity of just 240.9 mAh g−1 (Supplementary Fig. 13). To further highlight the enhanced air stability of LFO@C to LFO, we exposed both materials to a Constant Temperature and Humidity Box (20% RH, 25 °C) and performed electrochemical capacity tests after various durations of exposure. As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 14a–c, LFO experienced rapid degradation upon even brief exposure to air, with its specific capacity dropping from 706.1 mAh g−1 to 354.6 mAh g−1 after just 1 h. After 4 h, the capacity further decreased to 86.1 mAh g−1. Prolonging the exposure time to 8 h led to substantial degradation of LFO, causing the material’s color to completely transform into deep yellow (Supplementary Fig. 15). Moreover, the strongly alkaline nature of the degradation products resulted in severe gelling of the slurry (inset of Fig. 3g), rendering normal electrode preparation impractical. In direct comparison, LFO@C exhibited a striking improvement in air stability under identical testing conditions. As evident from Fig. 3g and Supplementary Figs. 16 and 17, after an 8 h exposure to 20% RH, the LFO@C electrode retained 99.4% of its initial capacity, with negligible signs of degradation. Following a 24 h exposure, the specific capacity of LFO@C remained at 789.8 mAh g−1, which corresponds to a capacity retention of 98.2%. Even after a 48 h exposure, the capacity was maintained at 766.6 mAh g−1, representing 95.2% retention. After 72 h of exposure, the LFO@C powder exhibited no visible changes, and the electrode slurry maintained its good flowability. At this point, the specific capacity was measured at 743.4 mAh g−1, corresponding to a 92.4% retention. These results demonstrate that the carbon encapsulation strategy effectively enhances the electrochemical stability and air-tolerance performance of LFO@C.

Additionally, we conducted placement experiments on LFO@C at 25 °C under high humidity, with the results presented in Supplementary Figs. 18 and 19. The findings show that, after a 24 h exposure in air with 30% RH, LFO@C retained approximately 97.1% of its initial capacity. Furthermore, when subjected to the more challenging condition of 60% RH for 8 h, LFO@C’s specific capacity remained above 750.0 mAh g−1, with a capacity retention rate exceeding 93.7%. It should be noted that previous strategies to improve the stability of LFO in humid air have led to limited suppression of its reactivity with the atmosphere, and challenges remain in achieving comprehensive environmental protection. Consequently, in an environment with 20% RH, LFO struggles to maintain structural stability and a specific capacity above 650.0 mAh g−1 with no more than 80% capacity retention for prolonged periods (exceeding 8 h) (Fig. 3h). In comparison, the carbon-coated LFO@C exhibited improved stability, delivering a specific capacity of over 780.0 mAh g−1 and retaining more than 96% of its initial capacity after 24 h of air exposure.

Enhanced electrochemical performance of LFO@C vs LFO

By applying a carbon coating, not only is the air stability of LFO substantially enhanced, but its overall electrochemical performance is also markedly improved. As shown in Fig. 4a, after the encapsulation of a carbon shell, the specific capacity of LFO increases from 706.1 mAh g−1 to 804.6 mAh g−1, making a notable 14% improvement. A comparison of the galvanostatic initial charge and dQ/dV curves between LFO and LFO@C reveals that the carbon coating significantly reduces the prominence of the two charging platforms. Additionally, the peak values observed in the dQ/dV curves of LFO@C exhibit substantial enhancement compared to those of LFO, suggesting that the release of Li+ ions from LFO@C occurs with greater ease. This reduction in polarization provides LFO@C with a distinct advantage by furnishing a stronger driving force for Li+ ions release. Furthermore, the introduction of the carbon coating enables a clearer comparison, indicating that LFO@C exhibits better rate performance than pristine LFO. Specifically, LFO@C retains 88% of its capacity as the current density rises from 0.05C to 1C, maintaining a specific capacity above 700.0 mAh g−1 at 1C. In contrast, LFO experiences a significant capacity decay, retaining only 42% of its capacity and delivering a specific capacity of only 320.4 mAh g−1 under the same conditions (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 20).

To investigate the kinetics of the chemical reactions involving LFO and LFO@C electrodes, we performed galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) tests. As shown in Fig. 4c, LFO@C exhibits a lower potential for Li+ extraction compared to LFO, and it is capable of releasing more Li+. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 21, we confirmed that a linear relationship exists between the potential and the square root of time (t1/2) during the electrochemical experiment. The ion diffusion coefficients for both LFO and LFO@C were further calculated based on the GITT curves at the designated voltage points (3.70 V and 4.05 V) (Supplementary Table 6). By examining these specific points, it is evident that the ΔEt value for LFO@C is much lower than that for LFO, while the ΔEs value shows a notable increase. Specifically, within the voltage range of 3.70 V to 3.75 V, the diffusion coefficient of LFO@C (D-LFO@C) is 90.04 times that of LFO (D-LFO). And in the range of 4.05 V–4.10 V, D-LFO@C is 5.89 times that of D-LFO (Supplementary Table 6). This noticeable enhancement in the Li+ diffusion rate of LFO@C can be attributed to the formation of numerous oxygen vacancies during the carbon coating process, which effectively expands the migration pathways for Li+ ions within LFO. The EIS spectra of Li||LFO and Li||LFO@C exhibit a semicircular arc in the high-frequency region, followed by a linear sloping line in the low-frequency region (Fig. 4h and Supplementary Table 7). This pattern aligns well with the Randles circuit model (inset in Fig.4h)44. The EIS results further reveal that, prior to charging, the charge transfer resistance (Rct) of LFO was measured at 265.5 Ω. However, upon carbon coating to form LFO@C, the Rct value significantly decreased to 180.7 Ω. This reduction in Rct is accompanied by a marked decrease in the diffusion resistance as well. When the Li+ ions were completely released, the Rct further dropped from 5.786 Ω for LFO to 4.194 Ω for LFO@C. Clearly, both the GITT and EIS results explain well the improved rate capability of LFO@C compared to uncoated LFO.

Furthermore, we conducted cycling tests on both LFO and LFO@C (Fig.4i). The results showed that, following the initial cycle, the Coulombic efficiency of LFO@C remained consistently stable at 100% throughout subsequent cycles. In contrast, the coulombic efficiency of LFO fluctuated between 75% and 80%. This indicates that LFO undergoes side reactions with the electrolyte, whereas these reactions are significantly suppressed when the material is coated with carbon. It is noted that the occurrence of these side reactions may be accompanied by the leaching of element Fe from the LFO. To further investigate this phenomenon, we fabricated cells using both LFO and LFO@C and subjected them to 50 cycles of testing. Following this, the cells were allowed to rest for 15 days to ensure complete dissolution of Fe. We then performed XPS analysis on the surface of the lithium foil serving as the negative electrode (Supplementary Fig. 22). It is obvious that the Li foil corresponding to LFO exhibited a distinct Fe characteristic peak, whereas the Li foil associated with LFO@C showed no significant Fe peaks. Additionally, we conducted Inductively Coupled Plasma (ICP) testing on the Li foils to assess the potential Fe impurity contamination (Supplementary Table 8). The results revealed that the Fe content in the Li foil paired with LFO@C was nearly identical to that in the pure Li foil, registering at 76.74 ppm compared to 78.15 ppm for the pure Li foil. In stark contrast, the Li foil used in conjunction with LFO showed a substantially elevated Fe content of 115.24 ppm, indicating a higher degree of Fe contamination relative to the pure Li foil. To further investigate the Fe dispersion, ICP testing was also carried out on the electrolytes. It was found that the Fe content in the electrolyte of the Li||LFO@C cell was merely one-tenth of that observed in the electrolyte of the Li||LFO cell (Supplementary Table 9). Collectively, these findings strongly suggest that the carbon coating on LFO effectively mitigates the leaching of Fe elements, thereby minimizing Fe-related contamination and potential adverse effects on the electrochemical performance of the lithium-based systems.

Application demonstration of LFO@C as a prelithiation additive for the positive electrode

In practical LIB applications, gas evolution constitutes a critical parameter that directly compromises electrode structural stability, thermal safety margins, and long-term electrochemical durability. Consequently, prior to investigating the lithium-compensating efficacy of LFO@C as an additive for the positive electrode, we conducted a systematic examination of its gassing behavior during the charging process (Fig. 5a). Mechanistically, the delithiation of LFO@C involves the sequential extraction of Li+ ions from the host lattice. Initially, at the initial stage of Li+ extraction from LFO@C, the system’s positive charge drops, prompting some Fe3+ to oxidize to Fe4+, while a fraction of O2− anions mildly oxidize to higher-valence oxygen species (On−, where 0 < n < 2), releasing a small amount of O2 gas due to the moderate reaction conditions. With the continued extraction of Li+ ions, the oxidation reactions gain momentum, with more Fe3+ ions turning into Fe4+ ions and a greater number of O2− anions converting to O2, leading to a significant rise in O2 output. Oxygen vacancies also form in the lattice at this stage, facilitating further oxidation of adjacent oxygen anions. As Li+ extraction progresses toward a higher degree, the system’s potential rises, making the high-oxidation-state Fe4+ ions unstable. These Fe4+ ions readily lose electrons and revert to the more stable Fe3+ state, reducing the system’s positive charge further. This, in turn, drives the oxidation of even more O2− anions to O2, substantially increasing O2 release. The elevated potential also triggers side reactions, with the generated unstable O-related species reacting with the electrolyte to form unstable carbonates that decompose, releasing CO2. As delithiation progresses and the potential keeps climbing, these side reactions accelerate, causing a continuous increase in CO2 emission. It is noted that the phenomena observed during the process of the initial four Li+ ions’ extraction are consistent with previous reports45. After four Li+ ions are extracted from LFO, it turns into LiFeO228,45. In our case, the well-distributed carbon coating probably increases the electrode’s conductivity. This boosts the material’s reactivity, potentially letting LiFeO2 formed after the first four Li+ ions extraction keep delithiating46,47, steadily releasing O2 as it decomposes. With no further reduction in the Fe oxidation state, O2 generation slows. High-potential charging persists, amplifying side reactions like carbonate decomposition from the unstable O-related species and electrolyte interaction, causing ongoing CO2 emission. Further in-depth investigations into the gas-generation problem associated with LFO are presently being conducted.

To evaluate the lithium-ion compensation effect and electrochemical compatibility of LFO@C with positive electrode materials, it was coupled with commercial LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 (NCM811), and electrochemical tests were first carried out using NCM811 as the positive electrode and lithium metal as the negative electrode. The results indicate that adding 4% LFO@C to NCM results in an approximately 16.1 % increase in the initial charge capacity, while the discharge capacity remains nearly unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 23a). Further comparison of the Galvanostatic Charge–Discharge (GCD) and dQ/dV curves of NCM electrodes before and after the addition of LFO@C shows that the incorporation of LFO@C reduces the first charge voltage plateau by roughly 0.13 V (from 3.81 V to 3.68 V) (Supplementary Fig. 23b). This decrement is attributed to the lower charge voltage platform of LFO@C, which subsequently lowers the overall voltage of the positive electrode. Moreover, upon introducing LFO@C, the voltage plateau of NCM near 4 V is significantly prolonged, indicating that LFO@C effectively replenishes Li+ ions when paired with the positive electrode material. It is also observed that the inclusion of LFO@C into the positive electrode not only supplies Li+ ions but also helps to decrease polarization to a certain extent (Supplementary Fig. 23c). This reduction is primarily due to the conductive nature of the carbon coating on LFO@C. Additionally, the rate and cycling performances of the NCM + LFO@C electrode also reveal a slight improvement compared to pure NCM (Supplementary Fig. 24).

We further conducted in situ XRD tests on both NCM + LFO@C and pure NCM electrodes to study in detail the effects of introducing 4% LFO@C on the NCM electrode during the first cycle. As shown in Fig. 5b, c, we monitored the changes in the peaks corresponding to the (003) plane of NCM during charging and discharging. Upon introducing LFO@C, a notable extension was observed in the charging process of NCM, leading to an increase in its charging capacity. Moreover, during the delithiation process of NCM, the interlayer spacing of the (003) plane initially expanded and then contracted, accompanied by a prominent peak splitting phenomenon during the expansion phase (Fig. 5b). However, after incorporating LFO@C, due to the effective reduction of the charging plateau of NCM (Supplementary Fig. 23a), and LFO@C accompanied by NCM to release Li+ ions, the deintercalation process of Li+ becomes smoother (Fig. 5c). Consequently, the increase in the interlayer spacing of the NCM (003) plane was more gradual, and no significant peak splitting was observed. The introduction of LFO@C has no significant effect on the phase transition during the discharge process, which is beneficial to maintain the stability of the NCM structure. Moreover, it is found that the changes in the interlayer spacing of the NCM (101) plane showed similar trends to those of the (003) plane (Supplementary Fig. 25a, b).

To carry out a more comprehensive evaluation of its practical applicability, we assembled a full cell by employing LFO@C as the prelithiation additive for the positive electrode, utilizing commercial NCM as the positive electrode material, and adopting a composite negative electrode consisting of SiOx and graphite. It is noted that the Gr + SiOx negative electrode exhibits a specific capacity of 458.6 mAh g−1, but with a low initial Coulombic efficiency of only 83% (Supplementary Fig. 26). Consequently, when paired with NCM, the substantial lithium loss incurred during the formation of the SEI leads to a decrease in the quantity of reversible Li+ ions available for the NCM positive electrode. As depicted in Fig. 5d, when bare NCM is coupled with the Gr + SiOx negative electrode, the resultant Gr + SiOx||NCM cell demonstrates a specific capacity of merely 185.1 mAh g−1. In contrast, the incorporation of 4% LFO@C into the positive electrode as a prelithiation agent results in an obvious boost of the Gr + SiOx||NCM + LFO@C cell’s specific capacity, which increases to 212.2 mAh g−1. This represents an improvement of approximately 14.5%, approaching the specific capacity of a Li||NCM cell. Consistent with the electrochemical behavior observed in the Li||NCM + LFO@C cell, the introduction of LFO@C significantly lowers the charge voltage plateau in the full cell, yielding a distinct voltage plateau within the range of 3.9–4 V (Supplementary Fig. 27). Furthermore, it has been observed that the incorporation of LFO@C markedly enhances the full cell’s capacity across a range of current densities (Supplementary Fig. 28). This enhancement can be attributed to the increased availability of active Li+ ions and the enhanced electrical conductivity facilitated by the integration of LFO@C (Supplementary Fig. 29). Following the rate test, we conducted an additional 50-cycle test at a rate of 0.33C (1C = 200 mAh g−1) for both full cells (Supplementary Fig. 30). Notably, the introduction of LFO@C led to a 2% increase in the capacity retention of NCM, indicating its capability to mitigate the degradation of NCM. To gain a more in-depth understanding of the impact of LFO@C on the NCM particles before and after the cycling test, we disassembled the full cells and examined the SEM images of the positive electrode electrodes (as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 31). The results indicate that NCM particles without LFO@C exhibited significant cracking, whereas those with LFO@C largely maintained their structural integrity. This is most likely due to the persistent lithium-deficient state of NCM in the absence of a lithium-supplementing additive, leading to the development of internal stress within the positive electrode particles.

To rigorously validate the feasibility of LFO@C as an effective positive electrode prelithiation additive for industrial applications, we assembled corresponding pouch cells on a pilot-scale production line. It is noted that, throughout the entire electrode preparation procedure, encompassing slurry mixing, coating, rolling, and slicing, the RH was meticulously controlled at approximately 20%. Preliminary observations revealed that the introduction of pure LFO into NCM leads to an excessive accumulation of residual alkali due to its moisture affinity. This triggered severe slurry gelation and subsequently caused uneven electrode thickness upon coating. In contrast, when LFO@C is incorporated into NCM, the slurry retains good flowability, thereby yielding a uniformly consistent electrode thickness across the entire surface area (Supplementary Fig. 32). Following the coating process, the subsequent stages of pouch cell preparation were carried out in accordance with the established industrial protocol, utilizing specific parameters detailed in Supplementary Table 10. Upon completion of the chemosynthesis procedure, direct current (DC) impedance tests were conducted on the pouch cell, with the outcomes presented in Supplementary Table 11. Analysis of these results revealed a notable decrease in impedance, from 25.89 mΩ to 18.42 mΩ upon the introduction of LFO@C.

Moreover, with the addition of LFO@C, an obvious enhancement in battery performance was observed. Specifically, the battery capacity increased from 1210.8 mAh to 1375.9 mAh at a current density of 0.5C (1C = 1.5 A), representing a 13.6% improvement. Additionally, the specific energy of the single pouch cell increased from 171.2 Wh kg−1 to 194.6 Wh kg−1, corresponding to a 13.7% enhancement (Fig. 5e and Supplementary Fig. 33). After 100 cycles (from the 1st to 100th cycle) at 0.5C rate, the Gr + SiOx||NCM + LFO@C pouch cell exhibits higher capacity retention (92.4%) compared to the Gr + SiOx||NCM baseline (89.5%). Upon extending cycling to 200 cycles (from the 1st to 200th cycle), the LFO@C-modified pouch cell demonstrates further improved cyclability, retaining 86.7% capacity vs 78.4% for the baseline, thereby highlighting the sustained stability enhancement under prolonged operation (Supplementary Fig. 34). In addition, it is observed that the Gr + SiOx||NCM + LFO@C pouch cell reveals negligible swelling before and after 100 cycles (Supplementary Fig. 35), implying that the LFO@C facilitates thorough Li+ extraction during the initial cycle without inducing gas formation or stimulating adverse reactions in subsequent cycles.

To gain deeper insights into the ramifications of incorporating LFO@C in pouch cells, we conducted a disassembly of the pouch cell and characterized both the negative electrode and positive electrode particles (Supplementary Fig. 36). The ICP analysis performed (Supplementary Table 12) on the disassembled negative electrode particles yielded comparable results to those observed in Li||LFO@C cells. To be specific, no signal indicative of the element Fe was detected in the Gr + SiOx negative electrode, confirming that LFO@C exhibits no Fe leaching during practical applications. For the positive electrodes, SEM observations of the cycled NCM positive electrode showed severe cracking, whereas the cycled NCM + LFO@C exhibited substantially less cracking (Fig. 5f, g and Supplementary Figs. 37–38). Furthermore, we compared the CEI films on the NCM particles in the pouch cells before and after cycling. It is evident that in the Gr + SiOx||NCM + LFO@C pouch cell, the NCM particles developed a uniform CEI film with a thickness of 3–4 nm after the initial charge (as shown in Supplementary Fig. 39a). Upon extended cycling, the CEI film thickened to 5.1 nm but maintained its uniform and compact structure (as depicted in Supplementary Fig. 39b). This indicates that the LFO@C additive effectively mitigated lithium deficiency and suppressed internal stress in the NCM particles, preserving CEI integrity. In contrast, for the Gr + SiOx||NCM pouch cell, it was observed that while the CEI film formed on the NCM particles after the first charge was similar to that in the Gr + SiOx||NCM + LFO@C pouch cell, it exhibited significant thickness non-uniformity after multiple cycles (Supplementary Fig. 39c, d). Consistent with the results from the coin-cell tests, this may also arise from the internal stress generated by lithium deficiency. In addition, XPS analysis of the disassembled positive and negative electrodes from the Gr + SiOx||NCM + LFO@C pouch cell further demonstrated a notable increase in the proportion of LiF components within both the CEI and SEI films (Fig. 5h-i and Supplementary Fig. 40). This may be due to the fact that the introduction of LFO@C significantly reduces the initial charging potential (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 23a), which greatly facilitates the formation of CEI and SEI films with a higher LiF content48–53. In concert, CEI and SEI films rich in LiF components facilitate the homogeneous insertion/extraction of Li+ ions within the CEI/SEI films and ensure the uniform diffusion of Li+ ions across both the positive and negative electrodes. This also helps to reduce internal stress and maintain particle integrity, thereby enhancing the electrochemical performance of the battery54,55.

Discussion

In this study, we utilize the meltability of pitch to construct a uniform and dense carbon layer on the surface of LFO. This carbon coating effectively shields LFO from humid air, allowing it to deliver a substantial capacity of 743.4 mAh g−1 even after 72 h exposure to air with 20% RH. This alters the prevailing scenario where LFO swiftly deteriorates upon contact with air. Besides bolstering air stability, the carbon layer enhances the electrical conductivity of LFO and elevates its specific capacity by 14%. Moreover, the carbon layer inhibits side reactions between LFO decomposition products and the electrolyte, averting potential risks associated with incorporating LFO into the electrode. As a practical demonstration of its application, we incorporate LFO@C into the commercial NCM positive electrode and carry out validation tests on an industrial pilot soft pack production line, complemented by exhaustive failure analyses. Our findings indicate that the integration of LFO@C holistically enhances the performance of the pouch cell while mitigating the degradation of NCM. These advancements hold considerable promise for facilitating the industrial deployment of LFO.

Methods

Materials synthesis

In a typical synthetic procedure, phase-pure LFO was prepared by mixing Fe2O3 (99.5% purity, Macklin) and Li2O (99.9% purity, Macklin) in a molar ratio of Li:Fe = 5.1:1, followed by sintering the precursor mixture at 700 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, the as-prepared LFO was ball-milled at a ball-to-material ratio of 10:1 and 450 rpm for 5 h to crush the pure LFO into small particles. Thereafter, 6 wt% pitch (Jining Carbon Group Co., softening point: 80 °C) was introduced into the ball-milled LFO, and the resulting mixture was further ball-milled for 2 h at 450 rpm to ensure homogenization of the pitch and LFO. The homogenized mixture was then transferred to a tube furnace and sintered under an inert atmosphere: the temperature was first maintained at 150 °C for 2 h to melt the pitch, followed by a rapid temperature ramp to 650 °C (ramping rate: 20 °C min−1), where it was held for 6 h to achieve the formation of LFO@C.

Electrochemical measurements

Li||LFO cells: LFO positive electrodes were prepared by mixing 78 wt% LFO, 14 wt% Super-P (SP, 99.9%, Shenzhen Kejing Star Technology Co., Ltd.), and 8 wt% Polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF, 99.9%, Aladdin) dissolved in N-Methyl-2-pyrrolidinone (NMP, 99.9%, Aladdin). The slurry was homogenized using an automatic deaeration mixer under 25 °C and RH below 20%. The resulting slurry was uniformly applied onto Al foil (14 μm thick, 99.9% purity) using an automatic coating machine (MSK-AFA-II-VC-IR, Shenzhen Kejing Star Technology Co., Ltd.), followed by drying in a vacuum oven at 80 °C for 12 h. The electrodes were cut into disks (~15.0 mm in diameter) and then dried under vacuum at 80 °C overnight before being transferred to an argon-filled glovebox. The film thickness was ~200 μm when wet and ~80–85 μm after drying. CR2032-type coin cells were assembled in an argon-filled glovebox (O2 < 1 ppm, H2O < 1 ppm, 25 °C) using a LFO positive electrode (diameter ~ 15.0 mm, thickness 80–85 μm, mass loading ~ 5.5 mg cm−2), a lithium metal electrode (diameter ~ 15.5 mm, thickness 0.5 mm, purity 99.9%, supplied by China Energy Lithium Co., Ltd.), and a Celgard 2400 polypropylene separator (Celgard, LLC, USA) with a diameter of ~17 mm, thickness of 25 μm, porosity of ~40%, and average pore size of ~0.043 μm. The separators were vacuum-dried at 60 °C for 12 h before being transferred into the glovebox and punched into discs using a stainless steel cutting die. The coin cell configuration was: negative case—spacer—lithium foil—separator—LFO electrode—spacer—spring—positive case. The lithium foils were stored in the glovebox for no more than 30 days before use. The electrolyte was 1 M LiPF6 (≥99.9%) dissolved in a 3:7 v/v mixture of ethylene carbonate (EC) and diethyl carbonate (DEC), with 3 wt% fluoroethylene carbonate (FEC) and 1 wt% vinylene carbonate (VC) additives. All electrolyte components were provided by Guangzhou Tinci High-tech Materials Co., Ltd. and used as received without additional drying. Electrolyte preparation and transfer were conducted in an argon-filled glovebox (O2 < 1 ppm, H2O < 1 ppm, 25 °C), using Teflon-coated magnetic stir bars for mixing and polypropylene pipette tips for transfer. The electrolyte was stored in sealed glass bottles within an argon-filled glovebox (25 °C, H2O < 1 ppm, O2 < 1 ppm) and was used within 30 days of preparation. The Li||LFO cells were tested in the voltage range of 2.8 V–4.3 V at different current densities (1C = 700 mA g−1).

Li||NCM and Li||NCM + LFO@C cells: NCM positive electrodes were prepared by mixing 90 wt% NCM811, 5 wt% SP, and 5 wt% PVDF dissolved in NMP. NCM + LFO positive electrodes were prepared by mixing 86 wt% NCM811, 4 wt% LFO, 5 wt% SP, and 5 wt% PVDF dissolved in NMP. Afterward, the slurry was coated onto Al foil and dried at 80 °C (12 h) in a vacuum box. CR2032-type cells were assembled in the same manner as Li||LFO cells. The Li||NCM and Li||NCM + LFO@C cells were tested in the voltage range of 2.8–4.3 V at different current densities (1C = 200 mA g−1). The cycling test of the coin cell was carried out at C/3 current.

Li||Gr + SiOx cells: Gr + SiOx negative electrodes were prepared by mixing 83.2 wt% commercial graphite, 10.4 wt% SiOx (BSO-1, BTR), 2.4 wt% SP, 1.8 wt% carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC), and 2.2 wt% styrene butadiene rubber (SBR) dissolved in deionized water. Afterward, the slurry was coated onto Cu foil (14 μm thick, 99.9% purity) and dried at 80 °C (12 h) in a vacuum box. CR2032-type cells were assembled in the same manner as Li||LFO cells. The Li||Gr + SiOx cells were tested in the voltage range of 0.005–1.5 V at the current of 0.1C (1C = 200 mA g−1).

Gr + SiOx||NCM and Gr + SiOx||NCM + LFO full cells: CR2032-type full cells were similarly assembled using the same separator and electrolyte as Li||NCM and Li||NCM + LFO cells, with a positive electrode (diameter: ~15.0 mm, mass loading: ~5.5 mg cm−2) and a negative electrode (diameter: ~16.0 mm, mass loading: ~3.0 mg cm−2). For the Gr + SiOx||NCM and Gr + SiOx||NCM + LFO full cells, the ratio of negative to positive electrode capacity (N/P ratio) was 1.06 ± 0.02. The Gr + SiOx||NCM and Gr + SiOx||NCM + LFO full cells were tested in the voltage range of 2.8–4.25 V at different current densities (1C = 200 mA g−1). Corresponding cycling test of the coin cell was carried out at C/3. Pouch-type full cell. The NCM/NCM + LFO@C positive electrode and Gr + SiOx negative electrode were employed to assemble a pouch-type full cell. The mass loadings for NCM/NCM + LFO@C and Gr + SiOx were 26.0 mg cm−2 and 14.2 mg cm−2, respectively. Furthermore, the ratio of N/P was 1.06 ± 0.02 as well. The separator and electrolyte were the same as those applied in Li||LFO cells. The process parameters for the pouch cell were also presented in Supplementary Table 10. Cells were cycled within a voltage range of 2.8–4.25 V, with three initial formation cycles at 0.1C followed by long-term cycling at 0.5C. The DC internal resistance test for the pouch cell was conducted using a battery internal resistance tester (BT3564, Hubei Yino New Energy). All the electrochemical tests were carried out at 25 °C. The specific energy values reported in this work are calculated based on the total mass and capacity of the single pouch cell, using the formula: Specific energy = Energy output of the pouch cell/mass of the pouch cell.

All electrochemical measurements were independently repeated on at least three separate cells, all of which exhibited consistent and comparable results, ensuring the reproducibility and reliability of the data reported.

Coulombic efficiency was calculated as the ratio of discharge capacity to the charge capacity in the preceding charge cycle. The specific capacity values reported in this work are calculated based on the mass of the active material only. All EIS measurements were carried out using a Princeton Applied Research (PAR) electrochemical workstation in potentiostatic mode (i.e., constant potential perturbation), with an AC amplitude of 10 mV, over a frequency range from 100,000 Hz to 0.01 Hz, and 10 data points per frequency decade. The measurements were conducted at open-circuit voltage (OCV) after the cells were allowed to rest for 3 h to reach electrochemical equilibrium. The EIS data were analyzed using ZView software (Scribner Associates), and the equivalent circuit model was used for fitting.

Air stability test

In the air stability placement experiment, the powder sample was placed in open sample bags to ensure unobstructed air exchange, and subsequently positioned within a Constant Temperature and Humidity Chamber (ASR-80EL, Guangdong Aisirui Instrument Technology Co., Ltd.). The chamber features an adaptive environmental control system that independently manages temperature and humidity levels after user-defined setpoints are entered. A circulation air duct ensures highly uniform temperature and humidity distribution throughout the chamber, with a temperature deviation of less than 2 °C and a humidity deviation of less than 2% RH, thereby guaranteeing the stability and consistency of the testing conditions.

Physical characterizations

The crystal structure was monitored by XRD utilizing a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer with Cu–Kα radiation at a scan increment of 0.020°. Subsequently, the acquired data were fitted using Rietveld refinement techniques implemented in Full Prof software. For electrochemical in situ XRD characterization, a slurry was first coated onto a beryllium window (to facilitate X-ray penetration) and then assembled within a specially designed Bruker chamber to conduct the test with an increment of 0.040°. Raman spectra were obtained using a HORIBA XploRA Nano spectrometer, excited by 633 nm radiation. Carbon and sulfur content analyses were performed using an infrared carbon-sulfur analyzer (HCS-140, Dekai Shanghai). The chemical environment of the samples was detected by XPS (Thermo SCIENTIFIC ESCALAB Xi+), with the C 1s peak calibrated at 284.8 eV. The fine structure for the powder was measured post-mortem by EPR (Bruker-EmxPlus). Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM, TESCAN MIRA LMS), TEM (FEI-Talos F200S), and Energy Dispersive Spectrometer (EDS, Oxford X-MaxN 80 T IE250) were employed to characterize the morphology and chemical composition of the prepared samples and electrodes. To determine the Fe element content, the electrode was dissolved in HCl, the solution was diluted, and then analyzed using Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectrometry (ICP-OES) with an Agilent 725-ES ICP-OES instrument. The powder impedance tests were conducted using the ST2742B Electric Powder Resistivity Tester. Fe K-edge analysis was performed with Si (311) crystal monochromators at the BL11B beamline of the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF) (Shanghai, China). Before beamline analysis, the LFO and LFO@C samples were pressed into thin pellets with a diameter of 1 cm and sealed using Kapton tape film. The XAS spectra were collected at room temperature using a 4-channel Silicon Drift Detector (SDD, Bruker 5040). Fe K-edge EXAFS spectra were recorded in transmission mode. All spectra were processed and analyzed using the Athena and Artemis software packages. The delithiation-induced gas evolution was monitored by Differential Electrochemical Mass Spectrometry (DEMS) using a QMG220 mass spectrometer (Shanghai ZhiLu, China). DEMS measurements were conducted during the electrochemical cycling of LFO@C to identify and quantify the gases released (such as O2 and CO2) during the lithium extraction process.

The in situ XRD measurements were performed using a custom-designed electrochemical cell. To ensure consistency and reliability of the electrochemical environment, the electrode composition, electrolyte formulation, lithium counter electrode, and separator used in this cell were identical to those employed in the CR2032-type coin cells described above.

Theoretical methods

First-principles calculations were carried out using density functional theory (DFT) as implemented in VASP56–58. The generalized gradient approximation (GGA) with the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional was employed59. The projector augmented-wave (PAW) method was used to describe the interaction between ions and electrons60–62. A plane-wave cutoff energy of 520 eV was set throughout all calculations. The GGA + U approach was adopted, with the effective U value for Fe set to 5.32 eV. Partial occupancies of the Kohn-Sham orbitals were handled using Gaussian smearing with a width of 0.05 eV. The convergence criteria for electronic and ionic relaxations were 10−5 eV and 0.05 eV Å−1, respectively. A 2 × 2 × 1 Monkhorst-Pack k-point grid was used for Brillouin zone sampling. Adsorption energies (Eads) were calculated as Eads = Ead/sub − Ead − Esub, where Ead/sub, Ead, and Esub represent the total energies of the adsorbate-substrate system, isolated adsorbate, and clean surface, respectively.
