Tea eggs-inspired Pickering emulsion stabilized by cooked egg white protein-EGCG complexes: Astaxanthin loading and 3D printing.
Authors: Xinru Gao, Xu Zhong, Qian Zhang, Yinxu Zhang, Wentao Su, Xiaomei Liu, Mingqian Tan, Hao Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Astaxanthin (AXT) demonstrates significant potential in food applications due to its diverse biological activities. However, its practical implementation is constrained by poor water solubility, environmental instability, and low bioavailability. This study aimed to leverage Pickering emulsions stabilized by heat-denatured egg white protein (EWP)-EGCG complexes as carriers for AXT encapsulation, and exploring the system's feasibility in 3D food printing. The results revealed that EWP and EGCG formed complexes through electrostatic and hydrogen bonding interactions. The emulsions exhibited exceptional stability, favorable viscoelasticity and thixotropic recovery. In vitro digestion experiments showed that the 1% EWP-EGCG complex-stabilized emulsion released 29.61% AXT in gastric fluid and 81.03 % AXT in intestinal fluid, demonstrating pH-dependent controlled-release behavior. Additionally, the emulsion had higher scavenging rates of DPPH, ABTS and hydroxyl radicals, which were 96.05%, 95.05% and 59.02%, respectively. Notably, the system demonstrated excellent 3D printing accuracy and structural support, making it a promising food-grade edible ink.

Introduction

Astaxanthin (AXT) exhibits potent antioxidant activity, effectively scavenging free radicals. As a chain-breaking antioxidant, it can efficiently neutralize reactive oxygen species and nitrogen substances (Liu et al., 2019). It has been reported that cells containing AXT were more tolerant to excess reactive oxygen species, suggesting that AXT exerts protective effect against oxidative stress damage (Focsan et al., 2017). However, due to the highly unsaturated structure and the existence of conjugated double bonds, AXT is extremely sensitive to temperature, light and oxygen, resulting in degradation and loss during processing, and even along with the generation of undesirable flavors. Furthermore, its high lipophilic and hydrophobic properties result in naturally low solubility in the gastrointestinal tract, which also greatly limits its application in water-based dispersion systems prevalent in food products. Therefore, the development of appropriate delivery systems to enhance the stability and solubility of AXT is of practical significance.

At present, the delivery systems of AXT primarily include hydrogels (Yu et al., 2022), oleogels (Zhao et al., 2023), nanoparticles (Chen et al., 2022) and Pickering emulsions. Yu et al. prepared a double-emulsion-filled gellan glue hydrogel by a two-step method, simultaneously encapsulating AXT and phycocyanin, which exhibited intestinal-targeting capability. Chen et al. (2022) designed novel oral sequential-targeting nanoparticles capable of precise AXT delivery to alleviate colitis in mice. Moreover, Pickering emulsions are widely regarded as one of the most suitable methods for encapsulating lipophilic bioactive compounds. The stabilization of Pickering emulsions with corn protein/hyaluronic acid nanoparticles exhibited excellent stability and achieved efficient AXT delivery (Zhang et al., 2023). Pickering emulsions are emulsions that are stabilized using solid particles instead of surfactants (Ribeiro et al., 2021). Compared to conventional surfactant-stabilized ordinary emulsions, Pickering emulsions eliminate surfactants while avoiding toxicity issues and regulatory constraints associated with food systems. These particle-stabilized colloids show enhanced physicochemical stability, biocompatibility, and are expected to be a versatile delivery platform for nutraceuticals and bioactive compounds (Zhou et al., 2024). In addition, Pickering emulsion has good applicability for 3D printing, and the Pickering emulsion stabilized by gelatin-catechin nanoparticles demonstrated superior structural integrity and stability in 3D printing applications (Li et al., 2024). It's worth considering that the application of Pickering emulsions in 3D-printed food systems necessitates the use of edible emulsifiers to ensure biosecurity.

Traditional emulsifiers such as soy protein isolate and whey protein isolate can stabilize Pickering emulsions. However, their acquisition requires separation and purification processes, which increase production costs. Egg white protein (EWP) is a commonly used protein mixture in the food industry that is isolated from fresh eggs, which contains all essential amino acids for humans (Yan et al., 2024). Additionally, EWP demonstrates simple preparation and low production cost, holding significant advantages in food industry applications. However, emulsions stabilized based on a single EWP usually cannot be loaded with too much oil phase, and are greatly limited in 3D printed foods due to their poor stability and weak support capacity (Yu, Wang, Li, et al., 2022). Xiong et al. (2025) prepared Pickering emulsion by compounding EWP with chitin, which exhibited a more uniform droplet size and enhanced stability. Chang et al. (2016) incorporated polysaccharides into EWP microparticles-stabilized Pickering emulsions, the emulsion exhibited higher protein surface loading and viscoelastic modulus. Furthermore, the preparation of Pickering emulsions using EWP and polyphenol complexes for AXT delivery, alongside the evaluation of their 3D printing suitability, has not been reported.

The interaction of proteins with polyphenols have garnered increasing attention, as these complexes enhance polyphenol hydrophobicity within emulsions to improve emulsion stability, while also augmenting free radical scavenging capacity and suppressing lipid oxidation (Yin et al., 2014). Catechins are biologically active polyphenols in tea, including eight species, among which epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) is the most abundant and bioactive, and has a good application prospect in the development of functional foods (Chen et al., 2019). Structurally, EGCG is rich in phenolic hydroxyl groups, which gives it good bioactivity and can bind to amide groups in protein molecules by hydrogen bonding (Cheng et al., 2022). However, previous study revealed that EGCG alone is unable to stabilize a Pickering emulsion. To overcome this limitation, Su et al. (2020) constructed lutein Pickering emulsions using β-lactoglobulin-EGCG composite nanoparticles as emulsifiers. The results demonstrated that EGCG incorporation reduced droplet size, enhanced emulsion stability, and prevented flocculation and sedimentation. Wang et al. also stabilized emulsion gels by incorporating waxy cornstarch with EGCG. The results showed that EGCG incorporation enhanced the viscoelasticity and stability of the emulsions while also significantly improving their free radical scavenging capacity (Wang & Duan, 2023). These studies suggested that EGCG may stabilize Pickering emulsions through interactions with EWP, thereby providing a stronger physical barrier against droplet coalescence and conferring suitable rheological properties. These enhanced characteristics make the emulsion system particularly suited for advanced applications such as 3D printing.

In this study, a “tea-egg" biomimetic complex was ingeniously engineered, where “tea” denotes EGCG and “egg” signifies cooked egg white protein. By fine-tuning gradient concentrations of EGCG and EWP, we constructed high-stability Pickering emulsions with hierarchical structures for efficient AXT encapsulation. The system's feasibility for 3D food printing was systematically validated through multi-dimensional characterizations, encompassing macroscopic-microscopic structural analyses, environmental stress resistance testing, rheological property profiling, and moisture distribution tomography of the emulsions. Furthermore, in vitro simulated gastrointestinal digestion models and antioxidant capacity assays were employed to evaluate the AXT controlled-release kinetics and bioactivity preservation of the complex. As a testament to its practical utility, intricate “Chinese knot” architectures were successfully 3D-printed, demonstrating the system's potential for fabricating functional food structures with aesthetic complexity. This work not only presents an innovative complex design strategy for AXT delivery but also bridges the gap between biomimetic materials and additive manufacturing in the food science domain.

Materials and methods

Materials

Eggs and corn oil were purchased from a local supermarket in Dalian (Dalian, China). EGCG (CAS#: 989-51-5, 95 % purity) and AXT (CAS#: 7542-45-2, 96 % purity) were supplied by Shanghai Maclean's Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The artificial digestion fluid system consisted of sterile artificial saliva (pH 6.0), sterile simulated gastric fluid (SGF, with pepsin, pH 2.0), and sterile simulated intestinal fluid (SIF, with trypsin and phosphate, pH 6.8) were purchased from Beijing Regan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). In addition, sodium hydroxide (NaOH) and hydrochloric acid (HCl) were provided by Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All chemical reagents used in the experiments were analytically pure.

Preparation of cooked EWP-EGCG complexes

The eggs were heated at 100 °C for 20 min, after which the cooked EWP was separated from the yolk. Subsequently, 120 g of cooked EWP was mixed with 300 mL of deionized water, and stirred at a rate of 35,000 rpm/min with a wall breaker for 3 min to prepare a 40 % (w/v) EWP solution. In the meantime, 1 g EGCG was dissolved in 50 mL deionized water, stirred at room temperature for 1 h, and concentrated to a 2 % (w/v) EGCG solution. The 2 % (w/v) EGCG solution was serially diluted to 1.5%, 1%, and 0.5% (w/v) using a stepwise gradient method. Finally, the EWP solution was mixed with the EGCG solutions at a 1:1 (v/v) ratio, and the pH was adjusted to 8 with 0.1 M NaOH solution to prepare the complexes. In these complexes, the concentration of EWP was 20%. While the concentrations of EGCG were 0%, 0.25%, 0.5%, 0.75% and 1% (w/v), respectively. Thus, the complexes were named as EWP-EGCG 0%, EWP-EGCG 0.25%, EWP-EGCG 0.5%, EWP-EGCG 0.75% and EWP-EGCG 1%.

Characterization of EWP-EGCG complexes

The absorbance of the EWP-EGCG complexes solutions were determined using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (UV-5200, Shanghai Yuanxi Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) at 600 nm to determine its turbidity. After diluting the EWP-EGCG complexes solutions 100-fold, the zeta potentials were measured using a nanoparticle size analyzer (Zetasizer 3000HSA, Malvern, London, UK). All samples were freeze-dried, mixed with potassium bromide at a mass ratio of 1:100 (w/w), and the infrared spectra were collected using fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Frontier, Perkin Elmer, Waltham Massachusetts, America) in the range of 4000-500 cm−1. The contact angles (θ) of EWP-EGCG complexes were determined by a droplet shape analysis system (DSA 30R, KrüSS, Hamburg, Germany). The EWP-EGCG complexes were first pressed into thin flakes using a tablet press, thoroughly moistened with corn oil, and then removed and dried. Thereafter, 5 μL of deionized water was added to the flakes, and the droplet morphology was recorded with a high-speed camera.

Preparation of EWP-EGCG complexes-stabilized Pickering Emulsions (EGTs)

EWP-EGCG complexes were mixed with corn oil at 2:3 (v/v), then the mixtures were subjected to high-speed shearing at 12,000 rpm for 2.5 min to obtain EGTs. In order to distinguish EGTs prepared by different complexes, the emulsions prepared with EWP-EGCG 0%, 0.25%, 0.5%, 0.75% and 1%, were named as EGT-0, EGT-1, EGT-2, EGT-3 and EGT-4, respectively.

Stability of EGTs

A comprehensive evaluation was conducted on the stability of EGTs, encompassing thermal stability, storage stability, and centrifugal stability assessments. In the thermal stability test, the viscosity and oil-water separation of the emulsions were evaluated after heating at 90 °C for 0, 30 and 60 min. Microscopic images of the emulsion droplets were captured using a microscope to visually observe the droplet morphology. In the storage stability test, EGTs were stored at 25 °C in the dark for 0, 7 and 14 d, followed by the examination of their macroscopic and microscopic structures. In the centrifugation stability test, 5 mL EGTs were taken in a test tube, centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min, and then 1 mL syringe was used to draw water from the lower layer of the centrifuged EGTs, and the water holding capacity of the EGTs was calculated by the eq. Preparation of AXT-loaded composite Pickering emulsions (EGTs@AXT)

A total of 10 mg AXT was accurately weighed and homogeneously mixed with 10 mL corn oil. The resulting mixture was stirred continuously overnight under dark conditions to achieve complete dissolution, resulting in a 1 mg/mL AXT-loaded oil phase. Subsequently, EWP-EGCG complexes were mixed with AXT-loaded oil phase at 2:3 (v/v), then the mixtures were subjected to high-speed shearing at 12,000 rpm for 2.5 min to obtain EGTs@AXT. The stable Pickering emulsions loaded with AXT, prepared using different EWP-EGCG complexes, were named EGT-0@AXT, EGT-1@AXT, EGT-2@AXT, EGT-3@AXT and EGT-4@AXT, respectively.

Characterization of EGTs@AXT

Firstly, the microstructure and morphology of EGTs@AXT were observed by fluorescence inverted microscopy (Ti-S, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) and cryogenic scanning electron microscope (SU8010, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). And the microscopic images were analyzed using ImageJ image software to measure and statistically evaluate the particle size distribution. Secondly, the transverse relaxation (T2) curve of the EGTs@AXT was determined by low-field nuclear magnetic resonance. Specifically, the samples were placed in the sample vial, and the parameters were set as follows: a 90° pulse duration of 31 μs, a 180° pulse duration of 62 μs, π value was 1500 ms, repetition time was 1600 ms, and echo time was 50 ms.

Finally, the rheological properties of EGTs@AXT were systematically characterized by the rheometer (Discovery HR-2, TA, New Castle, America). The thixotropic recovery performance was evaluated by applying a strain of 0.1 % to 100 % sequentially at a fixed frequency of 1 Hz at 25 °C. At the same time, the apparent viscosity changed at different shear rates from 0.1 to 100 s⁻1 was investigated. All tests were performed at 25 °C with a 1 mm test gap. The rheological behavior of the samples was tested in frequency sweep mode (0.1-10 Hz, constant stress 1 Pa). Furthermore, the temperature scanning test was performed from 20 to 80 °C with a fixed strain of 1.0%, an angular frequency of 10.0 rad·s⁻1, and a heating rate of 5 °C/min.

In vitro simulated digestion experiment

The in vitro simulated digestion experiment was divided into three stages: saliva digestion, SGF digestion and SIF digestion. All steps were performed at 37 °C, and the digestion juice was pre-warmed for 2 h before the experiment. In the saliva digestion stage, an equal volume of EGTs@AXT was added to the artificial simulated saliva, the pH was adjusted to 6.8, and then placed in a shaker for 10 min. In the SGF digestion stage, the above saliva mixture was added to an equal volume of SGF, the pH was adjusted to 2, and the mixture was placed in a shaker for 110 min. During the SIF digestion stage, the SIF was mixed with the digestion juice mixture from the previous stage in equal volumes, adjusted pH to 7, and placed on a shaker for 120 min. To determine the rate of AXT release, 1 mL of the mixture was taken from the digestion system every 30 min. The sample was mixed with 9 mL of a dichloromethane-methanol solution (2:1, v/v), vortexed for 2 min, centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 10 min, measure the absorbance of the lower liquid at 480 nm and calculate the concentration of AXT using a standard curve. 3D printing

3D printing technology was used to shape the EGTs@AXT. During the experiment, the emulsions were extruded through a nozzle with a diameter of 1 mm at a constant rate of 1 mm3·s−1. The entire printing process was conducted at room temperature, and the molded 3D graphic structure was imaged and recorded.

Statistical analysis

At least three independent replicates experiments were used to ensure the reliability of the data, and the mean and standard deviation were calculated. Subsequently, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed on the data using SPSS software, and the significance level was set to p < 0.05 to determine the significance of the difference between groups.

Results and discussion

Interaction of EWP and EGCG

The interaction between proteins and polyphenols has a significant impact on the turbidity and stability of their solution systems (Qu et al., 2025). As shown in Fig. 1 A, the turbidity of EWP-EGCG complexes were positively correlated with EGCG concentrations under weakly alkaline conditions, which indicated that the increase in EGCG concentrations may promote the interactions of EWP and EGCG, leading to the small aggregation of the system and a significant increase in turbidity. A similar phenomenon was observed in the increased turbidity caused by the interaction between procyanidin and lactoferrin (Li et al., 2021). The surface potential serves as a primary determinant in charge-related interaction mechanisms. At low EGCG concentrations (0.25% and 0.5%), the zeta potentials of EWP-EGCG complexes did not show significant changes (Fig. 1B). When the EGCG concentrations were 0.75% and 1%, the zeta potentials for EWP-EGCG complexes were -36.95 mV and -35.66 mV, respectively. The increase in the absolute value of the zeta potential indicated that higher EGCG concentrations can enhance the stability of the EWP-EGCG complexes (Ke & Li, 2024), which confirmed a stronger electrostatic interaction between EWP and EGCG.Fig. 1Characterization of EWP-EGCG complexes. The interaction between EWP and EGCG was further investigated by FTIR spectroscopy. As shown in Fig. 1C, the characteristic peaks of EWP (EWP-EGCG 0%) were observed at 1651.05, 1533.94 cm, and 1232.79 cm⁻1, corresponding to amide I, II, and III bands respectively. This observation was consistent with the previous study by Zhao et al. (2016). Meanwhile, the EGCG had characteristic peaks at 3473.52 and 3357.55 cm−1, which were attributed to the -OH functional group in the molecule. And the characteristic peaks at 1692.63, 1617.42, 1526.09, 1448.20, 1343.44, 1147.35, 1039.91 and 824.60 cm−1 were corresponded to carbonyl stretching, C=C alkenes, C=C aromatic ring, C–H alkanes, C–O alcohols, C-OH alcohols, C–O alcohols and C–H alkenes (Ma et al., 2024). When the EWP-EGCG complexes were construted, the characteristic peaks of EGCG at 3473.52 and 3357.55 cm−1 disappeared, indicating that the -OH group in EGCG was involved in the reaction with EWP (Minnelli et al., 2020). In addition, as the concentrations of EGCG in the complexes increased, the characteristic peaks of the EWP-EGCG complexes in the amide II band shifted to a lower wavenumber, indicating that the addition of EGCG changed the C–N or N–H bond of EWP. While the characteristic peak of the amide I band shifted to a higher wavenumber, suggesting C=O tensile and hydrogen bonding may play crucial roles in the formation of EWP-EGCG complexes. It is worth noting that for the EWP-EGCG 1% complex, the amide I band moved from 1651.05 cm−1 to 1658.09 cm−1, and the amide II band moved from 1533.94 cm−1 to 1516.37 cm−1, and the difference mainly occurred in the amide II band. Combined with the turbidity results in Fig. 1 A, these results suggested that the concentrations of EGCG positively connected with the interaction strength of EWP and EGCG.

The aggregation and localization efficiency of complexes particles at the oil-water interface depend on their wettability, which may affect the physical stability of emulsions (Xu et al., 2024). The wettability of EWP-EGCG complexes was measured by the three-phase contact angle (θ) (Fig. 1D). For EWP-EGCG 0 %, the θ formed by the particle, oil, and aqueous phases was 57.2°, indicating a weak hydrophobicity at this time. When the EGCG concentration was 0.25%, the θ of the particles was 64.0°, indicating the hydrophobicity of the EWP-EGCG complex increased. When the EGCG concentrations were 0.75% and 1%, the θ was up to 71.6°, indicating that the addition of EGCG could increase the surface tension and hydrophobicity of the EWP-EGCG complexes. This phenomenon may be attributed to the interaction between EGCG and EWP, leading to the exposure of more hydrophobic groups in the protein structure (Xu et al., 2024). Previous study has also reported that the wettability of bovine serum albumin can be enhanced through polyphenol modification (Chen et al., 2023). Therefore, the addition of relatively high-concentration EGCG can significantly promote the effective accumulation of particles at the oil-water interface and form a barrier that prevents droplet coalescence.

Stability studies of EGTs

The EWP-EGCG complexes were used to prepare Pickering solutions. In food processing processes, thermal treatment is often used for processing and sterilization, therefore, the thermal stability of the emulsions is critical for their applications in food products. To evaluate the thermal stability of emulsions, based on the previous method (Zhong et al., 2024), macroscopic and microscopic observations and evaluations were carried out. As shown in Fig. 2A, after 30 and 60 min heat treatment in a 90 °C water bath, different degrees of collapse and water separation were observed in the EGTs, and the color changed from milky white to light brown, which was due to the darkening of the EGCG in the EGTs due to the oxidation of the EGCG in the EGTs after heating. After heat treatment, the microstructure of the emulsion was observed under the microscope (Fig. 2B), and it was found that the emulsion droplets became larger to varying degrees after heating, which was positively correlated with the heating time and negatively correlated with the EGCG concentrations. The average particle size of EGT-0 was 11.45 μm, the average particle size of EGT-0 after 30 min and 60 min of heating was 12.79 μm and 13.52 μm, while the average particle size of EGT-4 was 8.42 μm, and the average particle size of EGT-4 was 10.82 μm and 11.51 μm after 30 min and 60 min of heating. It was worth noting that EGTs as a whole exhibited good thermal stability. In particular, EGT-4 had no oil and water precipitation from the macrostructure after heating, and there were fewer bubbles on the surface. The droplet size of EGT-4 after heating was still relatively uniform, which indicated that EWP-EGCG 1% complex could better maintain the stability of Pickering emulsion.Fig. 2Thermal stability of EGTs. The storage stabilities of EGTs were also detected. As shown in Fig. 3A, prolonged storage resulted in thinning of EGT-0, which starting after 7 d of storage and becoming more pronounced after 14 d storage. However, EGT-3 and EGT-4 showed no significant change in appearance after 14 d of storage. In addition, the microstructure showed that the droplet size of EGTs increased with storage time (Fig. 3B). The average particle sizes of EGT-0 after 0, 7, and 14 d of storage were 12.19, 12.30, and 12.51 μm, while the average particle sizes of EGT-4 were 10.58, 10.90, and 11.00 μm. In addition, higher concentrations of EGCG can help to slow down the stratification of emulsions, which had a positive effect on prolonging storage time and improving storage stability. Furthermore, under centrifugal force, the degree of water-oil separation of ETGs is one of the important indicators for evaluating the stability of emulsions. It can be observed that the volume of the aqueous phase separated by EGT-0 after centrifugation was significantly higher than that of EGT-4 (Fig. 3C). The volume of the aqueous phase separated by EGTs after centrifugation can be used as an indicator of the water-holding capacity of the emulsion, reflecting its centrifugal stability. As shown in Fig. 3D, the water-holding capacities of EGTs were increased with EGCG concentrations, which were 62.3%, 68.7%, 72.3%, 75.8%, and 78.2% respectively. The improvement of the water-holding capacities of EGTs can be attributed to the increase of EGCG concentrations in EWP-EGCG complexes, which enhanced the interaction with EWP and further improved the interfacial adsorption performance of the complexes. Morphology and microstructure of EGTs@AXT

The EGTs were further applied to encapsulate AXT. In Fig. 4A, the brown colors of the emulsions were due to AXT loading, and the concentrations of EGCG did not cause any significant changes in samples color. When the turnover experiment was carried out, it can be seen that the fluidity of EGT-0@AXT and EGT-1@AXT was strong and not in a stable state. On the contrary, EGT-2@AXT, EGT-3@AXT, and EGT-4@AXT were not in the flow state, and exhibited good structural enhancements. It can be attributed to the interfacial adsorption of EWP-EGCG complexes, which increased the stabilities of the EGTs@AXT. The encapsulation efficiencies of AXT were also detected. As shown in Fig. 4B, the AXT encapsulation efficiencies of EGTs@AXT were 88.40%, 92.02%, 95.21%, 96.79%, and 98.58%, respectively. It can be seen that EGTs@AXT had higher encapsulation efficiencies, which were increased with the EGCG concentrations. This phenomenon may be attributed to the ability of high concentrations of EGCG to form more stable EWP-EGCG complexes, which can increase the interaction at the oil-water interface. To evaluate the structural role of the EWP-EGCG complex, a control emulsion (WGT-4@AXT) was formulated by substituting EWP with an equivalent amount of whey protein isolate (WPI) under identical conditions. This substitution resulted in a smaller and more uniform particle size (Supplementary Fig. 1A), which might suggest improved dispersion. However, the reversal experiment revealed that WGT-4@AXT exhibited excessive fluidity (Supplementary Fig. 1B), indicating a lack of mechanical integrity for 3D printing applications. Then, the EGTs@AXT was characterized using an optical microscope (Fig. 4C). The microstructures showed that the droplets of the emulsions were more uniform with the EGCG concentration increased. Previous studies have shown that the size of the droplets can be used as an indicator of the emulsifying capacity of an emulsifier, with smaller droplets indicating greater emulsification (Xiong et al., 2025). Combined with the results of droplet size distributions in Fig. 4D, as the EGCG concentration in EWP-EGCG complex increased, the EGTs@AXT droplets also become smaller and the droplet size distributions, were more concentrated, indicating EGCG increased the emulsifying capacity of EWP-EGCG complexes. Cryo-scanning electron microscope images (Fig. 4E) showed a consistent trend in particle size variation with that in Fig. 4C and D. At larger multiples, the droplets of the EGTs@AXT were interconnected with each other to a certain extent, which was most obvious in the EGT-4@AXT. Lateral relaxation low-field magnetic resonance imaging

Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) spectroscopy is a common method for the analysis and characterization of water distribution in emulsions. Pseudo-color images of EGTs@AXT in Fig. 5A, the EGT-0@AXT showed a denser red patch, possibly due to the distribution of more active water molecules in the emulsion system, while there showed weaker red signals as the EGCG concentrations in the EWP-EGCG complexes increased. Specially, pseudo-color images of EGT-3@AXT and EGT-4@AXT showed a uniform yellow-green color, which may be due to the increase of EGCG concentrations leading to the binding of hydrogen bonds between EWP and EGCG, and a part of the free water was converted into fixed water and bound water, which reduced the fluidity of hydrogen protons. Fig. 5B showed the data visualization of the red signal intensity in a false-color image, which demonstrated the downward trend of free water content with the increasing concentrations of EGCG more intuitively. In order to further explore the the state of water in EGTs@AXT, transverse relaxation time (T2) distribution was detected. As shown in Fig. 5C and Supplementary Table 1, the T22 (fixed water) and T23 (free water) of the EGTs@AXT decreased as the EGCG concentrations increased, which indicated the decrease in the freedom of oil and free water (Ben Messaoud et al., 2018). Therefore, higher concentrations of EGCG can restrict the mobility of oil, immobilized water, and free water in the emulsion, thereby enhancing the stability of the emulsion. These results indicated that the increase of EGCG concentration enhanced the EGTs@AXT water-holding capacity, which was consistent with the results of pseudo-color image (Fig. 5 A) and the water-holding capacity (Fig. 3C). And this observation was corresponding to the results of the turnover experiment (Fig. 4A), which showed a significant decrease in the fluidity of the Pickering emulsion as the concentration of EGCG increased. Liu et al. (Liu et al., 2025) also reported the similar phenomena, demonstrating the enhancing effect of EGCG concentration on the stability of the delivery system.Fig. 5Moisture distribution of EGTs@AXT. Rheological properties of EGTs @AXT

The rheological properties of EGTs@AXT were investigated to observe their flow behavior and further understand the internal structure of the emulsions. Generally, good self-supporting properties of emulsions are necessary for their application in 3D printing, which will ensure the precise formation of the target shape. The viscoelasticity of emulsions reflects their self-supporting properties. In general, the higher the G′ value, indicating the better the self-supporting capability (Zhang, Yang, et al., 2024). Therefore, under strain sweep (ranging from 0.01 % to 100 %), the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G") of all samples were measured in Fig. 6A. As the concentration of EGCG increased, the G′ value significantly increased, indicating that the viscoelasticity of the emulsion was enhanced. This illustrated that the ability of the emulsion structure to resist mechanical forces was enhanced, demonstrating strong self-supporting properties. Compared with the G′ of EGT-3@AXT, the G′ of EGT-4@AXT increased rapidly, indicating that the 1 % EGCG affected the viscoelasticity of the Pickering emulsion more effectively than other EGCG contraptions (0%–0.75%). The frequency sweep test was conducted to acquire information about the fluid or solid properties of Pickering emulsions. Fig. 6B showed the relationship between the viscosity of the EGTs@AXT and the shear rate (0–100 s−1). The increase in shear rate reduced the viscosity of the EGTs@AXT, which can be attributed to the shear thinning behavior caused by the increase in shear rate. Previous study has shown that as the shear rate gradually increased, the droplet network of the emulsion was disrupted by the sheer force, resulting in a decrease in viscosity (Xu et al., 2021). And the viscosity of the EGTs@AXT increased with the increase of EGCG concentration, indicating that the stability of the emulsion was improved (Zhang, Li, et al., 2024). The reason may be that the interactions between particles and the interactions between particles and the liquid were enhanced at higher EGCG concentrations, forming a denser network.Fig. 6Rheological properties of stable EGTs@AXT of EWP-EGCG complexes at different EGCG concentrations. Strain sweep experiments (A); Viscosity tests (B); Oscillation frequency sweep experiments (C); Dynamic temperature scan tests (D).Fig. 6

Rheological properties of stable EGTs@AXT of EWP-EGCG complexes at different EGCG concentrations. Strain sweep experiments (A); Viscosity tests (B); Oscillation frequency sweep experiments (C); Dynamic temperature scan tests (D).

As shown in Fig. 6C, when the EGCG added, the G′ and G" of all samples were frequency-dependent, and the G′ of all emulsions was greater than G″ over the entire frequency range from 0.01 to 10 Hz, indicating that the degree of reversible deformation was greater than that of the irreversible deformation of the EGTs@AXT, resulting in an elastic gel structure (Jiang et al., 2022). As the concentration of EGCG in EWP-EGCG complexes increased, the values of G′ and G" also increased accordingly, indicating the gel structure of the emulsion was strengthened. Meanwhile, it was observed that the addition of EGCG led to a reduction in the droplet size of the emulsion (Fig. 3C), which further confirmed the occurrence of structural reorganization.

A dynamic temperature scan test was also performed on the EGTs@AXT (Fig. 6D). It can be seen that the G' and G″ of EGT-3@AXT and EGT-4@AXT were relatively stable when heated from 20 to 80 °C, indicating the gel structure of the EGTs@AXT were not destroyed under heat treatment, and the thermal stability of the EGTs@AXT also increased with the increase of the concentration of EGCG. The excellent thermal stability of EGTs@AXT gives them a unique advantage in food processing (Wang et al., 2021). Overall, based on the above rheological property analysis, EGTs@AXT stabilized by EWP-EGCG complexes had good damage resistance, excellent viscoelasticity, and thermal stability, which provided great potential in food applications.

In vitro simulated digestion

In order to explore the digestion process of EGTs@AXT in the gastrointestinal tract, in vitro simulated digestion experiments were carried out. The microstructures of the EGTs@AXT and samples at different digestion stages were shown in Fig. 7A. In saliva, the droplets of all samples were larger in size and more aggregated than other stages. In response to SGF, the size of the droplets was gradually reduced, allowing the droplets to disperse and be digested (Nuntarat et al., 2020). Finally, under the digestion of SIF, the droplets of the sample were reduced to a minimum. This phenomenon was due to the digestion fluid broke down the lipids in the sample, thus achieving the purpose of digestion. The release of AXT at different stages of digestion was further detected. As shown in Fig. 7B, when the sample was in the SGF stage, the EGT-4@AXT showed a lower release rate of 22.17%, while when it was in the SIF stage, the AXT release rate increased substantially, reaching 79.24%. This pH-dependent release behavior can be attributed to the structural evolution of the EWP-EGCG complex under different digestive conditions. In the acidic environment of SGF, the complex maintained a dense and coherent interfacial network, which acted as an effective barrier against AXT release (Liu et al., 2024). However, upon transition to the neutral-to-alkaline conditions of SIF, the electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonds between EWP and EGCG were weakened, leading to a looser interface structure. Concurrent enzymatic hydrolysis of EWP by pancreatic enzyme and trypsin further disrupts the interfacial film, resulting in the rapid release of AXT. Similar results were found in the EGT-0@AXT, with a release rate of 33.07% for AXT in SGF and 58.90% for AXT in SIF. The AXT release rate of EGT-0@AXT in SIF increased by only 25.83%, which is much lower than the 57.07% of AXT-4@AXT. The low release rate of AXT in EGT-4@AXT during the SGF digestion stage can be attributed to the fact that the EWP-EGCG 1% stable emulsion has a better protective effect on AXT at the oil-water interface, effectively reducing the release efficiency of AXT (Miao et al., 2021). This enhanced protective effect is positively correlated with EGCG concentration, as higher EGCG levels promote the formation of stronger and denser interfacial films through interactions between its phenolic hydroxyl groups and EWP, significantly improving the hydrophobicity and structural integrity of the interface (Liu et al., 2019).Fig. 7In vitro simulated digestion. Antioxidant activity of EGTs@AXT

In order to study the effect of heat treatment on the antioxidant capacity of EGTs@AXT, the scavenging of free radicals was measured at different time points. As shown in Fig. 8A, the DPPH scavenging rate of EGTs@AXT increased with increasing EGCG concentration in EWP-EGCG complexes without heat treatment. The scavenging rate of DPPH by EGT-0@AXT was 85.32%, while the scavenging rate of DPPH by EGT-4@AXT was 96.05%. The enhancement of free radical scavenging ability may be due to two main factors. First, the increase in EGCG concentration led to the decrease in droplet size of EGTs@AXT, which increased the interfacial surface area and improved the solubility of AXT, resulting in an enhancement of DPPH free radical scavenging capacity (Bai et al., 2023). Second, EGCG itself is a potent water-soluble antioxidant that contributes directly to free radical scavenging (Yin et al., 2014). After heat treatment, it was found that the DPPH radical scavenging rate of all EGTs@AXT decreased with the increase of heating time. It is important to note that the scavenging rate of EGT-4@AXT was generally higher than that of EGT-0@AXT under the same conditions. Interestingly, the EGT-4@AXT under all conditions had a high scavenging rate of DPPH radicals, indicating that the increase in EGCG concentration improved the water solubility of the AXT embedded in the EGTs@AXT and enhanced the antioxidant capacity of the EGTs@AXT (Han et al., 2019). At the same time, the dense layer formed at the interface of EGCG and EWP effectively isolated the direct erosion of AXT by light, oxygen, and heat, reducing its degradation loss during heating process. In order to more comprehensively explore the antioxidant capacity of EGTs@AXT, the ABTS free radical scavenging rate (Fig. 8B) and hydroxyl radical scavenging rate (Fig. 8C) were also measured. In the process of heat treatment, the scavenging rates of ABTS and hydroxyl radicals in EGTs@AXT were basically consistent with that of DPPH. With the increase of EGCG concentration, the free radical scavenging rate also increased, and the free radical scavenging rates were negatively correlated with the heating time. This decrease in scavenging can be attributed to the fact that heat treatment disrupts the oil-water interface of the emulsion, resulting in the loss of strong reducing properties due to the exposure of loaded AXT in the oil phase, and thus the scavenging of free radicals. It was worth noting that the scavenging rates of EGT-0@AXT on ABTS free radicals and hydroxyl radicals were the lowest, and the addition of EGCG improved that capacity, indicating that EGCG had great impacts on the antioxidant capacity of EGTs@AXT.

3D printing

3D printing technology is widely used in the food industry due to the innovation of its technology and the uniqueness of its appearance design. Based on the above analysis of the EGTs@AXT, the 3D printing capabilities and potential of the EGTs@AXT stabilized by EWP-EGCGs were evaluated. As shown in Fig. 8D, the “Chinese knot” patterns printed by 3D printing were observed from the front and side of EGTs@AXT, and their stability was evaluated. The results showed that the shape of the “Chinese knot” printed by EGT-0@AXT still had strong fluidity, and the printed pattern was relatively fuzzy and had relatively poor support. As the EGCG concentration increased, the patterns becomed increasingly complete. When the concentration of EGCG was 1 %, the support and integrity of EGT-4@AXT were greatly improved, with a clear contour. Specially, the EGT-4@AXT has the most complete pattern, the holes are clearly visible, the printing path is clear and complete, showing high accuracy, and forming a clear printing structure, with good 3D printing stability and accuracy (Yu, Li, Wang and Yong., 2023). Combined with the above analysis of rheological properties and stability, the concentration of EGCG in EWP-EGCG complexes played key roles in the Pickering emulsions, EGT-4@AXT exhibited the best 3D printing effect.

To quantitatively assess the impact of 3D printing on EGTs@AXT, we evaluated the AXT retention rate and DPPH/ABTS radical scavenging activities before and after 3D printing. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 2A, the AXT retention rate of EGT-0@AXT decreased from 90.47% to 85.17%, while that of EGT-4@AXT was not changed significantly. These results indicated that the EWP-EGCG complex formed at higher EGCG concentrations effectively protects AXT, and the 3D printing process does not significantly affect its retention. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 2B, the DPPH free radicals scavenging rate of EGT-0@AXT decreased from 85.85% to 74.61% before and after 3D printing, whereas that of EGT-4@AXT showed no significant change. A parallel trend was observed in ABTS assays, where EGT-0@AXT decreased from 23.48% to 16.31%, but EGT-4@AXT retained high scavenging activity. These findings clearly demonstrated that incorporating EGCG into EWP-EGCG complex was essential for preserving both AXT integrity and antioxidant efficacy of the Pickering emulsion throughout the 3D printing process, consistent with previous study (Zhang, Xiang, et al., 2024). Therefore, EGT-4@AXT can applied to 3D printing food, and various structures can be designed and produced to meet the needs of consumers in 3D printing, which has a wide range of application potential and value.

Conclusion

This study successfully engineered Pickering emulsions stabilized by egg white protein-EGCG complexes for encapsulating AXT and evaluated their suitability for 3D food printing. Results demonstrated that EGCG concentration significantly influenced the emulsions' stability, moisture distribution, rheological behavior, astaxanthin release profile, and antioxidant performance. In vitro simulated gastrointestinal digestion experiments revealed that the EGTs@AXT exhibited controlled-release characteristics, releasing AXT slowly in gastric juice and rapidly in intestinal fluid, thus optimizing bioavailability under physiological conditions. Notably, EGTs@AXT displayed excellent potential for 3D printing applications, particularly when the EGT-4@AXT formulation was utilized as edible ink, showcasing precise pattern deposition and structural integrity. These findings establish a novel approach for integrating AXT into 3D printed foods, offering practical insights into its encapsulation, retention, and targeted release. The proposed system paves the way for developing functional, customized 3D printed products, expanding the application scope of bioactive compounds in modern food manufacturing.
