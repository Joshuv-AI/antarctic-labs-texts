Macular OCT inner retinal changes reflect CNS involvement in m.3243A>G disease
Authors: Hatem Jouda, Andrew C Browning, Akhunzada M Aftab, Ikhlas Mahmoud, Saima Bibi, Robert McFarland, Sarah J Pickett, Helen Devine
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe m.3243A>G mitochondrial DNA variant is the most common cause of adult mitochondrial disease and is associated with a heterogeneous clinical phenotype. The retina and optic nerve are among the most metabolically active tissues, making them vulnerable to mitochondrial dysfunction. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) studies have demonstrated retinal nerve fibre layer (RNFL) thinning in mitochondrial and other neurodegenerative diseases. We investigated whether temporal RNFL thinning is associated with central nervous system (CNS) involvement in individuals with the m.3243A>G variant.MethodsHigh-resolution OCT was used to assess peripapillary RNFL thickness and perform macular segmentation. Participants were categorised into normal RNFL (n=14) or temporal RNFL thinning (n=15) groups. Demographic data, mean-corrected m.3243A>G heteroplasmy, Newcastle Mitochondrial Disease Adult Scale (NMDAS) scaled scores and NMDAS neurological traits were compared.ResultsTemporal RNFL thinning was significantly associated with neurological features (Fisher’s exact test, p=0.027). In multivariable analysis, RNFL thinning and age were independent predictors of neurological involvement. Macular OCT revealed concomitant thinning of the ganglion cell-inner plexiform (GC-IPL) complex in the RNFL thinning group, with preservation of outer retinal layers, supporting primary retinal ganglion cell vulnerability. No significant associations were found between RNFL thinning and m.3243A>G heteroplasmy or NMDAS scaled scores.ConclusionTemporal RNFL thinning, accompanied by GC-IPL loss, is associated with neurological involvement in m.3243A>G-related mitochondrial disease, supporting its potential as a non-invasive biomarker of CNS dysfunction. Longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether these retinal changes are progressive and predictive of neurological decline.Keywords: MITOCHONDRIAL DISORDERS, NEUROOPHTHALMOLOGY, CLINICAL NEUROLOGY, NEUROMUSCULAR

Introduction

Mitochondrial diseases are a group of clinically heterogeneous disorders caused by dysfunction of the electron transport chain, leading to impaired energy production and cell death.1 These disorders may result from pathogenic variants in either mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), which are typically maternally inherited, or nuclear DNA, which are generally inherited in a Mendelian fashion, though de novo mutations are also well recognised.2 Among these, the m.3243A>G variant in the MTTL1 gene is the most common and exhibits a broad clinical spectrum, from single-organ involvement to multisystem disease.3 Clinically, m.3243A>G-related disease may present as one of several syndromes, such as MELAS (mitochondrial encephalomyopathy, lactic acidosis and stroke-like episodes) or MIDD (maternally inherited diabetes and deafness), although many individuals exhibit overlapping features or remain asymptomatic.4 This clinical variability is influenced, in part, by heteroplasmy; the proportion of mutant to wild-type mtDNA within a cell can vary significantly between individuals and tissues, contributing to heterogenous clinical manifestations that are difficult to predict.5 Furthermore, m.3243A>G-related disease is progressive, and the phenotypic profile can evolve over time. Conversely, some carriers may remain asymptomatic for years, making incidental findings critical for earlier molecular diagnosis, as the variant can later manifest with severe multisystem involvement.6 The Newcastle Mitochondrial Disease Adult Scale (NMDAS) is a validated tool for quantifying phenotypic severity and disease progression in these patients.7

The retina and optic nerve are among the most metabolically active tissues in the body, making them particularly vulnerable to mitochondrial dysfunction. Moreover, as functional extensions of the central nervous system (CNS), they offer a unique and accessible window to evaluate CNS pathology.8 Mitochondrial dysfunction has been linked to a spectrum of ophthalmic manifestations, from subtle pigmentary changes of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) to a more severe chorioretinal atrophy involving the fovea.9 Optical coherence tomography (OCT) has emerged as a non-invasive imaging modality that enables detailed visualisation of the retina and optic nerve head, including quantification of retinal nerve fibre layer (RNFL) thickness.8 Peripapillary RNFL measurement was selected for this study because it is quick, reproducible and widely available in both hospital and community settings. Its ease of use and minimal patient burden make it a practical tool for routine assessment and longitudinal monitoring, as is already established in glaucoma care, and it could serve a similar role in patients with m.3243A>G-related disease.

Thinning of the RNFL, particularly in the temporal quadrant, has been observed in mitochondrial optic neuropathies,10 as well as in neurodegenerative conditions such as Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s disease.11 12 However, De Laat et al reported that the predominant ocular phenotype in m.3243A>G-related disease is a characteristic macular dystrophy, rather than optic neuropathy, and found no correlation between heteroplasmy and retinal dystrophy, although poorer vision did correlate with higher NMDAS scores.9 This highlights the complex relationship between retinal and neurological manifestations in m.3243A>G and underscores the importance of exploring alternative, objective markers such as RNFL thickness.

To date, no studies have directly examined the relationship between RNFL thinning and CNS involvement in patients with m.3243A>G-related mitochondrial disease. Establishing a link between RNFL changes and CNS involvement could support its use as a simple, widely available screening and monitoring tool in this population. This study therefore aimed to assess the association between temporal RNFL thinning and neurological involvement in individuals with the m.3243A>G mutation and no evidence of confounding macular dystrophy, as well as to explore potential correlations with m.3243A>G heteroplasmy and NMDAS scores in a well-characterised mitochondrial disease cohort.

Methods

Patient cohort

From 91 adult carriers of the m.3243A>G mtDNA variant screened from the Mitochondrial Disease Patient Cohort UK, 29 individuals (16 women, 13 men) were eligible for inclusion in this retrospective observational case series. All participants were evaluated at the NHS highly specialised service for rare mitochondrial disorders of adults and children in Newcastle on Tyne and subsequently reviewed at the Mitochondrial Retinal Genetics Clinic at the Royal Victoria Infirmary between August 2019 and October 2024 as part of comprehensive phenotyping. Exclusion criteria were co-existing primary neurodegenerative or ocular disease, a history of retinal surgery, macular dystrophy diagnosed by OCT or fundus autofluorescence (FAF) imaging, refractive error greater than ±3 dioptres or evidence of tilted optic discs on clinical examination. The clinical data are summarised in table 1. Ethical approval was granted by the Newcastle and North Tyneside Research Ethics Committee (13/NE/0326) and written informed consent from patients was obtained prior to study inclusion. All clinical investigations were evaluated according to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Investigations

Ophthalmic examinations

Each participant underwent a comprehensive ophthalmic evaluation including best-corrected visual acuity (VA), intraocular pressure measurement, anterior segment examination and dilated funduscopy, conducted by a retinal specialist (ACB). VA was expressed using LogMAR (Logarithm of the Minimum Angle of Resolution) scale, where a score of 0.0 corresponds to standard 20/20 (6/6) vision, with higher values indicating worse visual function.13

OCT analysis

Spectral-domain OCT (Spectralis, Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany) was used to assess peripapillary RNFL thickness of a ring 3.46 mm in diameter, centred on the middle of the optic disc, as previously described.14 The segmentation of the RNFL layer was performed automatically by the Heidelberg Spectralis device and all scans were verified manually to check for centration and segmentation errors or artefacts. For each eye, we extracted the peripapillary-RNFL thickness values for the temporal sector which were classified into two age-matched groups: normal (green, within the 95th centile) or abnormal (either amber, below the 95th centile or red (below the 99th centile).15 Those with any evidence of macular dystrophy were excluded from analysis.9 To generate a single representative measurement per patient and to enable the patients to be divided into normal and thinned temporal RNFL groups, we used the mean temporal RNFL thickness from both eyes to reflect overall optic nerve involvement and reduce intrasubject variability.

Macular retinal layer thickness

Posterior pole images of each eye were also acquired using SD-OCT, using the protocol described by Invernizzi et al.16 A magnification correction was applied by the instrument to take account of corneal curvature. The automatic segmentation function was used to provide Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) thickness maps of the RNFL, ganglion cell layer (GCL), inner plexiform layer (IPL), inner nuclear layer (INL), outer plexiform layer (OPL), outer nuclear layer (ONL) and the RPE. Each scan was reviewed within the ETDRS area by one of the authors (ACB) to check for segmentation errors before thickness measurements were recorded. Segmented thickness values for each of the nine ETDRS subfields were documented for each eye as previously described.15 For each retinal layer, mean thickness was computed across the four quadrants of the inner and outer ETDRS rings; the central area, represented by a single measurement, was also included in the analysis.

m.3243A>G heteroplasmy

Of the 29 participants, 28 had at least one measurement of blood m.3243A>G heteroplasmy. For the remaining participant, urine m.3243A>G level was used. First, we applied published formulae to age-adjust blood heteroplasmy levels and sex-adjust urine heteroplasmy levels, then calculated a mean of all available measurements for each participant.17

Age-adjusted blood level = (blood heteroplasmy)/0.977(age+12)

Male adjusted urine level=logit-1((logit (urine heteroplasmy)/0.791)–0.625)

Female adjusted urine level=logit-1((logit (urine heteroplasmy)/0.791) + 0.608)

NMDAS data

All participants were assessed using the NMDAS, which evaluates multisystem disease burden through 29 questions scored from 0 to 5.7

Scaled scores were calculated as: (total NMDAS score ÷ number of questions answered) × 29, as previously described.5 All participants underwent OCT imaging within 3 years of their NMDAS assessment, with a mean interval of 5.5 months (range: 0–31 months) between the two assessments.

Neurological features were defined using binary thresholds informed by clinical descriptors, to ensure traits were as objective as possible: hearing loss (≥3), seizures (≥1), stroke-like episodes (≥1), encephalopathy (≥1), pyramidal involvement (≥1), cerebellar ataxia (≥2) and cognitive impairment (≥3), as previously described.16 Migraine was excluded due to its multifactorial nature and high prevalence in the general population. Patients were classified as having neurological involvement if they met the threshold for any of these traits. Cognitive testing scores were not available for eight individuals (four with normal RNFL and four with thinned RNFL).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R V.4.3.1. Continuous variables were assessed for normality and compared between groups using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test. Associations between continuous variables were evaluated with linear regression, and results are reported with the coefficient of determination (R2), regression slope and p value. To identify predictors of neurological involvement, logistic regression with binomial distribution was applied, reporting ORs, 95% CIs and p values. McFadden’s R2 was calculated using the formula (log likelihood(model)/log likelihood(null model).

Associations between macular retinal layer thickness and RNFL group, m.3243A>G heteroplasmy and the presence of neurological involvement were assessed using linear mixed models to account for inclusion of both eyes from individual participants. Participant IDs were included as random effects and fixed effects included both sex and age at OCT measurement to control for potential confounding. Throughout, a p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Boxplots display the median (horizontal line), IQR (25th–75th percentiles; box), with whiskers representing 1.5 times the IQR. For regression analyses, scatter plots display individual data points, with linear regression lines shown alongside shaded 95% CIs.

Results

Cohort characteristics

We reviewed the clinical (NMDAS and LogMAR), imaging (OCT) and m.3243A>G heteroplasmy data of our cohort of confirmed m.3243A>G carriers. 14 patients (4 men, 10 women) were found to have normal temporal RNFL thickness (median 68.8 µm), and 15 (9 men, 6 women) were found to have thinned temporal RNFL thickness (median 49.0 µm; p<0.001; table 1). Age and sex distributions did not differ significantly between groups (p=0.694 and p=0.121, respectively).

Younger participants exhibited higher blood m.3243A>G heteroplasmy, consistent with previous reports of age-related fall in heteroplasmy.5 NMDAS scaled scores increased with age, reflecting the progressive nature of m.3243A>G-related disease. However, neither reached statistical significance and the proportion of variance explained by age was low for both measures (R² =0.071 for heteroplasmy; R² =0.080 for NMDAS) (figure 2A–B). In addition, a positive correlation was observed between m.3243A>G heteroplasmy and NMDAS scaled scores (figure 2C), suggesting that higher heteroplasmy levels are associated with greater disease burden; however, this association also did not reach statistical significance (R² = 0.078; slope=0.143; p=0.078).

(B) NMDAS scaled scores and age (R² = 0.080, slope =0.365, p =0.075). (C) NMDAS scaled scores and m.3243A>G heteroplasmy levels (R² =0.078, slope=0.143, p=0.078). Comparison between normal and thinned RNFL groups

To investigate whether temporal RNFL thinning is associated with neurological involvement in individuals with the m.3243A>G variant, we divided the cohort into two groups based on RNFL thickness measured by OCT (table 1). No participants showed evidence of macular dystrophy (excluded during selection).

We compared molecular and clinical measures between participants with normal RNFL thickness and those with RNFL thinning. While there was no statistically significant difference in m.3243A>G heteroplasmy between the RNFL thinning group (median 100 (IQR 54.36)) and the normal group (median 68.42 (IQR 52.23)) (Mann-Whitney U test, W=62, p=0.061) (figure 3A), a trend towards higher heteroplasmy levels in individuals with RNFL thinning was observed. Similarly, no significant difference was found in median NMDAS scaled scores between the RNFL thinned group (20.4 (24.7)) and the normal group (13.2 (13.4)) (W=76.5, p=0.222) (figure 3B). There was also no significant difference in mean LogMAR VA between groups (median 0.049 (0.188) in the thinned group vs 0.093 (0.200) in the normal group; W=104.5, p=1.000), suggesting RNFL thinning does not impact central VA.

(B) NMDAS scaled scores (Mann-Whitney U test, W=76.5, p=0.222). NMDAS, Newcastle Mitochondrial Disease Adult Scale; RFNL, retinal nerve fibre layer.

Association between temporal RNFL thinning and neurological features (NMDAS)

We next compared the frequency of binary NMDAS neurological traits between RNFL groups to investigate potential associations between RNFL thinning and CNS involvement. Comparisons were performed using Fisher’s exact test. When assessing individual NMDAS traits (hearing loss, seizures, stroke-like episodes, encephalopathy, cerebellar ataxia, pyramidal involvement and cognitive impairment), no statistically significant differences were observed between groups (table 2).

To further explore whether cumulative neurological involvement differed between groups, we next analysed a combined CNS variable. This combined measure included the presence of any of the following: seizures, stroke-like episodes, encephalopathy episodes, cerebellar ataxia, pyramidal involvement, cognitive impairment or sensorineural hearing loss. Using this combined variable, we found that 4 of 14 participants (28.6%) with normal RNFL exhibited at least one neurological feature, compared with 11 of 15 participants (73.3%) with thinned RNFL. This difference was statistically significant (Fisher’s exact test: p=0.027; OR=6.363; 95% CI 1.072 to 47.927), indicating that RNFL thinning is associated with greater neurological involvement in individuals with m.3243A>G-related disease.

Brain MRI data were available for 16 of the 29 patients within the cohort. In the normal RNFL group, 8 patients underwent MRI brain scans, with 2/8 (25%) demonstrating radiological evidence of cerebral atrophy. In the thinned RNFL group, eight patients also had MRI scans, of whom 5/8 (62.5%) exhibited clinical atrophy. Although a higher proportion of patients with RNFL thinning showed evidence of cerebral atrophy, this difference did not reach statistical significance (Fisher’s exact test: p=0.315, OR=0.224, 95% CI 0.013 to 2.447).

Logistic regression analysis: predicting neurological involvement

To evaluate whether RNFL thickness provides additional predictive value for CNS involvement (defined by the presence of one or more NMDAS neurological traits), we performed a multivariable logistic regression including RNFL thickness, age and m.3243A>G heteroplasmy as predictors. Thinner RNFL was associated with higher odds of CNS involvement (OR=0.913, p=0.044), as was increasing age (OR=1.109, p=0.025), while heteroplasmy demonstrated a positive association that approached significance (OR=1.036, p=0.062). Model performance improved with the addition of RNFL thickness, with McFadden’s R² increasing from 0.21 (age and heteroplasmy only) to 0.38 (full model).

Discussion

Neurodegeneration involves the progressive loss of neuronal structure and function, ultimately leading to CNS dysfunction.18 In m.3243A>G-related mitochondrial disease, impaired neuronal energy metabolism and disruption of neural networks are key drivers of pathology.19 As extensions of the CNS, the retina and optic nerve offer a valuable window for evaluating CNS health and enhancing our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying neurodegeneration.8 While MRI remains the gold standard for diagnosing and monitoring CNS disease, its high cost and limited accessibility necessitate alternative approaches. OCT provides a non-invasive, affordable and reproducible means of assessing retinal and optic nerve integrity.20 In mitochondrial optic neuropathy, OCT typically reveals RNFL thinning that is most pronounced temporally and correlates with disease duration and severity, although paradoxical thickening may occur in early stages.21

Summary of key results

Although severe optic atrophy is considered uncommon in m.3243A>G disease,9 in the present study, we sought to investigate whether RNFL thinning is associated with broader neurological involvement in individuals with m.3243A>G-related disease and to explore potential correlations with m.3243A>G heteroplasmy. Given the marked clinical heterogeneity of m.3243A>G-related disease, examining differences within the patient cohort itself provides valuable insights into the relationship between retinal changes and neurological involvement, beyond what cross-sectional comparisons to healthy controls could reveal.

While no significant differences emerged when analysing individual neurological traits between groups, combining traits revealed a significant association between RNFL thinning and neurological involvement (Fisher’s exact test, p=0.027). The neurological features analysed included seizures, stroke-like episodes, encephalopathy, cerebellar ataxia, pyramidal signs, cognitive impairment and sensorineural hearing loss. Migraine was excluded from this composite outcome due to its transient and multifactorial nature. These findings suggest that temporal RNFL thinning may serve as an early structural biomarker reflecting underlying cerebral atrophy and neurodegeneration in m.3243A>G disease. Logistic regression further supported this, showing that age, m.3243A>G heteroplasmy level and temporal RNFL thinning were predictive of neurological features, with age and RNFL thickness being statistically significant. MRI evidence of cerebral atrophy was more prevalent among individuals with RNFL thinning, although MRI data were only available for 55% of the cohort (n=16), limiting statistical power. Expanding MRI imaging to the entire cohort in future studies would allow more definitive correlations between brain atrophy and RNFL thinning to be drawn, particularly as brain atrophy may precede overt neurological symptoms.

Consistent with these associations, macular OCT segmentation demonstrated concomitant thinning of the GCL, IPL and INL across all ETDRS subfields in the RNFL-thinning group, with preservation of the ONL and RPE and OPL differences confined to the central subfield. This pattern supports preferential involvement of the GC-IPL complex rather than outer retinal degeneration. Taken together, these findings suggest that RNFL thinning may represent an early structural biomarker of underlying cerebral atrophy and neurodegeneration in m.3243A>G-related disease.

Regarding other clinical and molecular parameters, although no significant differences were observed between groups or sexes in m.3243A>G heteroplasmy, NMDAS scores or LogMAR VA (Mann-Whitney U test, p>0.05), several important correlations emerged across the cohort. Consistent with prior findings in a broader cohort, mean-corrected m.3243A>G heteroplasmy levels in this subset were positively correlated with NMDAS scaled scores, supporting the established association between mutation load and clinical severity.5 Corrected m.3243A>G heteroplasmy negatively correlated with age at OCT, likely reflecting a later clinical referral of individuals with lower levels, while NMDAS scores increased with age, emphasising the progressive nature of m.3243A>G-related disease.5 While age correction accounts for the well-characterised decline in blood heteroplasmy over time, this model has limitations, particularly at the extremes of age and heteroplasmy, where individual variability and tissue-specific dynamics may reduce its accuracy.5 Nevertheless, variability remained high, highlighting the multifactorial determinants of disease expression. These findings reinforce that low heteroplasmy cannot reliably exclude pathogenic mitochondrial involvement and underscore the likely influence of additional modifiers, including nuclear genetic background, environmental factors and epigenetics.17

Comparison to literature: RNFL thinning in neurodegenerative disease

Our findings align with previous literature demonstrating that RNFL thinning is a common feature across a wide spectrum of neurodegenerative diseases. In Alzheimer’s disease (AD), a meta-analysis demonstrated significant RNFL thinning compared with controls.22 Furthermore, RNFL thinning has been shown to correlate with cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers (eg, tTau) and hippocampal atrophy, even among cognitively unimpaired individuals.23 Similarly, in Parkinson’s disease (PD), studies have consistently shown significant RNFL thinning, with further reductions associated with increasing disease severity.24,26 RNFL thinning has also been observed in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and Huntington’s disease, particularly affecting the temporal quadrant.27 28 In multiple sclerosis, temporal RNFL thinning has emerged as a sensitive marker of axonal degeneration, even without prior optic neuritis.29 30 Collectively, these studies highlight OCT’s potential as a non-invasive biomarker of early or preclinical neurodegeneration. Nonetheless, validation in larger cohorts and through longitudinal studies remains essential to validate its clinical utility.

In the context of m.3243A>G-related disease, RNFL thinning is most plausibly explained by anterograde degeneration due to direct mitochondrial dysfunction in the retina, a mechanism supported by the high metabolic demand of retinal ganglion cells and mirrored in other mitochondrial optic neuropathies such as Leber’s hereditary optic neuropathy.31 32 Nonetheless, widespread CNS neurodegeneration, including cortical and subcortical atrophy, may also contribute via retrograde trans-synaptic degeneration, and the relative contribution of these processes warrants further investigation.33

Ganglion cell complex and inner retinal layer analysis

While previous studies in m.3243A>G carriers have primarily focused on peripapillary RNFL thickness, our work extends this by analysing macular OCT segmentation across all retinal layers. The GCC, comprising the RNFL, GCL and IPL, provides a broader measure of retinal ganglion cell integrity and may be particularly sensitive to neurodegenerative changes.34 In individuals with temporal RNFL thinning, we also observed significant thinning of the GCL, IPL and INL across all ETDRS subfields, with preservation of the ONL and RPE and OPL differences confined to the central subfield. This selective inner retinal involvement, in the absence of significant outer retinal changes, supports primary retinal ganglion cell vulnerability in m.3243A>G-related disease rather than secondary degeneration from photoreceptor or RPE loss.

These findings build on earlier work in a small MELAS cohort (including four m.3243A>G carriers) that reported GCC thinning correlated with disease duration.35 Similar patterns are seen in other neurodegenerative diseases such as AD and PD, where GCC and GC-IPL thinning correlate with cognitive decline and brain atrophy and may outperform RNFL thickness in detecting early changes.36 37 The concomitant RNFL and GC-IPL thinning in our cohort supports their combined use as structural biomarkers in m.3243A>G disease and underscores the potential value of longitudinal GCC analysis to track CNS disease progression.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the rarity of the m.3243A>G mutation, combined with our focus on cases without concomitant macular dystrophy, limited the sample size. Larger, multicentre studies with longer follow-up will be needed to validate these findings. Second, while the NMDAS is a clinically validated tool, it may be weighted towards particular phenotypes and contains subjective elements, particularly when relying on patient or caregiver reports. Furthermore, certain parts of the NMDAS, such as cognition testing, can be time-consuming and are often skipped, reducing the number of data points available for analysis.7 This resulted in missing cognition data for eight individuals within our cohort (four in each group).

Moreover, patients with significant maculopathy were excluded from the primary analysis to avoid potentially abnormal RNFL measurements secondary to outer retinal disruption. However, we recognise that characteristic MIDD maculopathy, typically presenting as parafoveal retinal pigment epithelial atrophy, represents an important and relatively common phenotype in symptomatic m.3243A>G carriers. This subgroup may have distinct structural and functional implications, including potential effects on inner retinal layers, and future studies should incorporate dedicated subgroup analyses to examine associations between macular disease, segmented retinal layer thickness and RNFL integrity.9

Although an age-matched healthy control group was not included, RNFL thickness values were derived using the Heidelberg Spectralis OCT device, which incorporates age-adjusted normative data from a large healthy population cohort.38 However, a formal GCC-specific analysis was not undertaken and should be considered in future work. Finally, although higher blood heteroplasmy levels were associated with RNFL thinning in our cohort, heteroplasmy was not measured in ocular tissues. Given the known variability of mutant load between tissues, the relationship between systemic and retinal heteroplasmy remains to be clarified.

Future directions

Building on these findings, future research should recruit larger, multicentre cohorts for longitudinal follow-up to determine whether RNFL and GC-IPL changes are progressive, how they relate to the onset and evolution of neurological symptoms and to establish their predictive value. Including patients with maculopathy in dedicated subgroup analyses will also be important to capture the full spectrum of retinal involvement in this genotype. Such studies will help refine OCT-derived metrics for integration into clinical utility.

Conclusion

Mitochondrial ophthalmic diseases pose significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges due to their complex, multisystem nature.39 In this study, we report on a subgroup of patients with the pathogenic m.3243A>G variant, stratified by normal versus thinned temporal RNFL not attributable to a macular dystrophy. Temporal RNFL thinning was significantly associated with the presence of neurological features on the NMDAS, supporting a potential link between retinal neurodegeneration and broader CNS involvement. Macular OCT analysis demonstrated concomitant thinning of the GC-IPL complex, consistent with primary retinal ganglion cell vulnerability in m.3243A>G-related disease and mirroring changes reported in other mitochondrial optic neuropathies. These findings support the validation of OCT as a non-invasive biomarker for early neurological involvement in m.3243A>G-related disease. Longitudinal studies are now needed to establish whether these retinal changes are progressive and predictive of further neurological decline. Ultimately, OCT-derived biomarkers could support risk stratification, guide disease monitoring and inform the evaluation of emerging therapeutic interventions.
