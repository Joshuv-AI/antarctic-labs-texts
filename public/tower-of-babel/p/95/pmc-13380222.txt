Affordable thermal control for cell culture chambers: An open-source, PID-based heating module
Authors: Majid Rostami, Sesha Sai Ramineni, Sangyoon J Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCommercial environmental control systems for live cell imaging chambers frequently cost $10,000–20,000. This paper presents an open-source, PID-controlled heating module built entirely from off-the-shelf components for approximately $500. The system employs four adhesively mounted polyimide heater pads driven at 12 VDC through a closed-loop chain: a K-type thermocouple feeds a Delta DTB4848LV controller, whose analog output is converted to PWM and applied to the heaters via an opto-isolated MOSFET switch. The required heating power was determined through analytical thermal sizing and verified by finite-element simulation (ANSYS Mechanical), confirming that the cell culture region reaches 37°C at steady state. Bench testing validated these predictions: the system reached 37°C within approximately 41 min from a 22°C cold start and maintained 37.1±0.4°C over 3 h of continuous operation. The modular, low-voltage design requires no permanent modification to the host instrument and scales to different chamber geometries by adjusting the number of heater pads. Although validated here on a CytoStretcher, a substrate-stretching platform with a compact culture area, the analytical sizing methodology, finite-element thermal model with integrated PID control, and generalization guide provided in the repository enable adaptation to other stage-top microscope cell culture chambers by users with basic heat transfer knowledge. Larger or more complex chambers may require additional insulation or multi-point temperature sensing to ensure spatial uniformity. All design files, simulation models, and the bill of materials are released under an open-source hardware license.Keywords: Open-source hardware, PID controller, Cell culture heating, Polyimide heater, Thermal control, Mechanobiology

Hardware in context

Live cell imaging experiments require precise environmental control, typically maintaining 37°C and 5% CO2 [1]. Mammalian cells cannot tolerate more than approximately 2°C above normal for more than a few hours [1], making avoidance of overheating the primary thermal design constraint. Many scientific instruments used for live cell observation, e.g., cell stretchers, compression bioreactors, and microfluidic perfusion systems, are sold as standalone platforms without integrated environmental control. When manufacturers offer heating and gas enclosures, these proprietary add-ons cost $5000–14,000+, placing a significant burden on laboratories with constrained equipment budgets [2].

Several commercial stage-top incubation systems address this need. The OKO Lab Bold Line 3 provides closed-loop control of temperature (±0.1°C in sample feedback mode), CO2/O2, and humidity through a unified touchscreen interface [3]. The ibidi Silver Line combines continuous-current heating with feedback-controlled humidity regulation [4]. The Tokai Hit INUB series integrates an internal humidifier modeled after conventional CO2 incubators, with models available for multiple microscope platforms [5]. These systems offer excellent environmental control but share three limitations that restrict their adoption in broader research settings. First, they are designed specifically for microscope stages and cannot be readily adapted to non-microscopy platforms such as cell stretchers, compression bioreactors, or microfluidic perfusion systems, which have different form factors and mechanical constraints. Second, their system-level costs typically range from $5000 to $14,000+, placing them out of reach for laboratories with limited equipment budgets. Third, they are proprietary and closed-source, preventing users from modifying the hardware to accommodate non-standard chamber geometries or experimental configurations.

The same thermal control challenge extends beyond cell stretchers such as the Strex STB-140, Flexcell FX-6000, and open-source biaxial stretchers to at least three other instrument categories. Microfluidic perfusion chambers, such as the ibidi μ-Slide series, are optimized for shear stress delivery but lack integrated heating and are geometrically incompatible with standard stage-top incubators. Electrophysiology recording chambers, such as those from Warner Instruments, must remain open for electrode access, precluding the use of enclosed incubation systems. Custom organoid and 3D culture bioreactors are increasingly 3D-printed with non-standard geometries that are unsuitable for any off-the-shelf commercial stage-top solution. In all these cases the thermal problem is identical: a mechanically functional platform with a non-standard geometry that cannot be enclosed in a commercial stage-top incubator. The open-source heating module described in this work, combining analytically sized polyimide heater pads, finite-element thermal simulation, and closed-loop PID control, addresses this gap regardless of the specific platform.

On the open-source side, Duru et al. [6] presented a modular incubator with interchangeable 3D-printed chambers, Peltier-based heating, and CO2/humidity control for approximately $750. Two configurations were demonstrated, i.e., a portable unit for cell transport and a stationary unit for parallel electrophysiological recordings. However, in both cases, the instrument is placed inside the incubation enclosure. Arumugam et al. [7] described the PrintrLab incubator, a portable CO2 incubator built on a Raspberry Pi and 3D printer controller architecture for under $400, validated for bacterial culture using dry ice sublimation for CO2 supply, but mammalian cell culture was not validated. Marchus et al. [8] presented a Proportional-Integral-Derivative (PID)-controlled hypoxia chamber regulating O2 (1%–21%) and CO2 (5%) for $832, designed to fit inside a standard incubator and relying on the host incubator for temperature control. While each system makes a valuable contribution, none addresses the problem of adding thermal control onto an existing scientific platform whose geometry and mechanical function preclude enclosure in a box.

Furthermore, to our knowledge, none of the existing open-source systems documents a transferable engineering design process. They report what was built and demonstrate that it works, but do not provide analytical thermal sizing, finite-element simulation of the temperature distribution, or a systematic procedure for PID tuning, the information a researcher would need to adapt the design to a different chamber geometry.

In our laboratory, the target platform is a CytoStretcher-LV (Curi Bio, Inc.) [9], a microscope-mounted cell stretching instrument that applies programmable uniaxial stretch to cell-seeded polymer substrates while permitting simultaneous live imaging through an optically transparent chamber. A removable lid attaches to the chamber via magnetic connectors and provides access to the culture area. An environmental control accessory is available from the manufacturer, but at substantial additional cost.

The present work fills both gaps. It contributes: (1) a retrofit heating architecture using adhesively mounted polyimide heater pads, an industrial PID controller, and a low-voltage Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistor (MOSFET) power stage that attaches to any cell culture chamber without permanent modification, and (2) a complete, reproducible design methodology proceeding from analytical thermal sizing (Section 2.2) through finite-element simulation (Section 7.1) to coupled PID tuning (Section 7.2), providing a quantitative framework for adapting the design to other chamber geometries. Although developed for a CytoStretcher mechanobiology platform at a total cost of approximately $500, the methodology is directly applicable to any cell culture chamber. An explicit generalization guide is provided in Appendix.

Hardware description

The hardware is a PID-controlled direct current (DC) heating system that maintains the temperature of the cell culture region inside a chamber at a user-defined setpoint, normally 37°C. Unlike commercial stage-top incubators, which use proprietary all-in-one controllers tied to specific microscope platforms, this system is assembled entirely from discrete, individually replaceable off-the-shelf modules. The system is composed of five functional modules (temperature sensing, PID control, voltage-to-PWM conversion, MOSFET power switching, and heater pads), forming a closed-loop control chain (Fig. 1), and can be useful to researchers in the following ways:

The jaw is not directly attached to the heated lid, making this location a representative measurement point for the thermal environment experienced by the cells.

Design rationale for PWM+MOSFET architecture.

The DTB4848LV controller outputs a continuously variable 0–10 V analog voltage proportional to the computed control effort. A DC-rated solid-state relay (e.g., SSR-25DD) is a threshold switch that turns fully on when the input exceeds approximately 3 V and fully off below that level, so it cannot interpret a proportional analog signal. Fed the controller’s 0–10 V output directly, an SSR would remain on nearly all the time, collapsing the control loop to bang–bang behavior. The (Jessinie) voltage converter solves this by translating the analog signal into a 0%–100% duty cycle at 2 kHz, which the opto-isolated MOSFET follows cycle-by-cycle to deliver true proportional power modulation. This high-frequency switching is particularly important for the CytoStretcher, which is an open chamber with a hollow underside accommodating the microscope objective for live imaging. The resulting continuous air exchange produces low thermal mass and rapid response to disturbances. Slow on/off cycling from an SSR (typically seconds) would cause temperature oscillations too large for viable cell culture, whereas the 2 kHz PWM is smoothed by even the modest thermal inertia of the chamber into effectively continuous heating. The MOSFET architecture also offers lower losses: at the 3.4 A operating current, the MOSFET on-state resistance (∼15 mΩ) dissipates only ∼0.17 W, compared to ∼5 W through the forward voltage drop of a typical DC SSR. Finally, the opto-isolated module decouples the low-voltage control electronics from the 12 VDC power stage, improving noise immunity on the thermocouple signal path.

Electrical architecture

The electrical system is organized into two isolated domains:

AC domain (120 VAC mains): Two independent mains connections supply the system (Fig. 1). An IEC-fused rocker switch (0.5 A slow-blow; B1 in Fig. 1) inside the control box powers the Delta DTB4848LV controller (100–240 VAC input; A3 in Fig. 1). The (WANPTEK) bench power supply (A2 in Fig. 1) is connected to a separate 120 VAC outlet.

DC domain (12 VDC regulated): The (WANPTEK) bench power supply provides a regulated 12 V rail with 4 A current limit. A 3-gang toggle switch panel (10 A fuse per channel; B3 in Fig. 1) distributes power to three branches:

•Heater branch (10 A fuse): 12 V to MOSFET VIN+ (A6 in Fig. 1), then to four parallel heaters (A1 in Fig. 1).

System overview

The system comprises three physically separate units connected by detachable cables:

1.Control box (Fig. 1), which houses the PID controller (A3 in Fig. 1), voltage-to-PWM converter (A5), MOSFET switch (A6), toggle switch panel (B3), and bus bar (B4) in a single enclosure (C1). Four panel-mount interfaces provide all external connections: an XT60 female for 12 VDC input (from the (WANPTEK) bench power supply; B10), a DC power jack (5.5 × 2.1 mm) female for 12 VDC heater output (B11), an IEC rocker switch for 120 VAC mains (B1), and a pass-through hole for the thermocouple probe leads (A4). Different connector types (XT60 for input, DC jack for output) prevent accidental cross-connection. The thermocouple wires pass directly through the enclosure without a connector to minimize electrical noise on the sensor signal.

Validation and characterization

The heating system was validated through three complementary approaches: analytical thermal sizing (Section 2.2), finite-element simulation (ANSYS Mechanical), and coupled thermal–PID controller simulation.

Finite-element thermal simulation

A transient thermal analysis coupled with PID control was performed in ANSYS Mechanical to predict the temperature distribution and closed-loop response of the CytoStretcher chamber assembly.

Geometry. The chamber assembly geometry was modeled as a simplified external CAD representation based on physical measurements of the commercial cell stretching platform. The model includes the chamber body and mounting hardware. Internal mechanical details of the commercial instrument were not modeled, as only the thermal envelope is relevant for this analysis (Fig. 5).

Materials. The chamber assembly was modeled as structural steel (Table 6). Convective heat losses were represented through boundary conditions applied to all exterior surfaces.

Mesh. The assembly was meshed using ANSYS Mechanical’s automatic meshing with SOLID291 elements (20-node quadratic thermal brick; Fig. 5). The mesh comprises four bodies with a total of 63,955 elements and 74,428 nodes, distributed as follows: Body 1 (36,057 elements), Body 2 (9466 elements), Body 3 (2165 elements), and Body 4 (495 elements). The element load balance ratio was 1.40.

Boundary conditions. The heater pads were modeled as surface heat sources with a combined installed capacity of 40 W (4 × 10 W measured), modulated by the PID controller. All exterior surfaces of the assembly were assigned natural convection boundary conditions with a uniform heat transfer coefficient h=7.5W/(m2K), representative of low-velocity natural convection in a laboratory environment [10] (Fig. 5). The initial temperature of the entire assembly was set to T0=22°C. This simplified thermal model is intended as a design estimation tool for feasibility assessment and initial PID coefficient selection. Experimental validation of the actual system performance is presented in Section 7.2.

Solver settings. A transient thermal analysis was performed using ANSYS Mechanical (Workbench 2021 R1) with MKS units. The simulation end time was 1500 s, solved in 18 adaptive substeps with time increments of 60–120 s. Heat flow convergence was achieved at every substep (91 cumulative iterations). The simulation was executed on 12 cores with a total CPU time of approximately 170 s.

The simulation confirms that the cell culture region reaches the target temperature of approximately 37°C, while exterior surfaces remain at 22–26°C due to convective losses. The maximum temperature is 37.264°C at the heater pad contact areas. The minimum is 22.834°C at the outer extremities (Fig. 5).

Coupled PID control. To predict the closed-loop transient response, PID control was implemented directly within the ANSYS transient simulation using the PID Controller ACT extension (version 1.0). The extension creates a feedback loop inside the FEA solver: monitor nodes on the stretching jaw surface provide the temperature feedback signal. The jaw was selected as the monitor location because it is the closest solid component to the cell culture area that is not directly attached to the heated lid, making its surface temperature a reasonable proxy for the local air temperature. Modeling the air domain explicitly would require a coupled fluid–structure (FSI) simulation, which is unnecessarily complex for this thermal feasibility study. In the physical system, the thermocouple measures the actual air temperature directly. The feedback temperature is compared to a setpoint that ramps from T0=22°C to 37°C over 400 s. The PID algorithm modulates the heat flux applied to the heater pad surfaces, with a power limit of 40 W.

The simulation used PID coefficients P=30, I=0.1, D=0. The results predict a smooth, monotonic temperature rise with no overshoot, converging to ∼ 37.2°C by t≈2300 s (∼38 min). The settling time is governed primarily by the available heating power (40 W) relative to the thermal mass and convective losses of the chamber assembly, rather than by PID tuning. This confirms that the thermal sizing (Section 2.2) is the dominant design driver. Fig. 5 shows the proportional and integral power contributions: the proportional term dominates during the initial transient, while the integral term gradually accumulates and provides the steady-state maintenance power.

This result underscores a critical generalization principle: thermal sizing must precede controller selection. If the installed heating capacity is insufficient to overcome steady-state convective losses, the controller will saturate at 100% duty cycle and the system will never reach the target temperature, regardless of PID tuning. Conversely, excessive capacity wastes cost and complicates tuning due to a faster, more aggressive plant response. The analytical sizing procedure in Section 2.2 (Eqs. (1), (2)) and the generalization guide in Appendix provide the framework for matching heater capacity to any chamber geometry before selecting or tuning the controller.

These simulation-derived coefficients served as initial feasibility estimates. The actual controller was tuned using the DTB4848LV built-in auto-tune feature (see Section 6).

Experimental validation

The assembled system was tested on the bench to validate the simulation predictions and characterize real-world thermal performance. The DTB4848LV controller was configured using its built-in auto-tune feature (see Section 6). All temperature measurements were read from the controller display via the K-type thermocouple probe positioned in the chamber air space. DC power supply voltage and current were captured via screen recording of the (WANPTEK) supply display at ∼20 s intervals and extracted through optical character recognition to produce time-series data (534 data points over 178 min).

Fig. 6 presents the experimental results. The system reaches the 37°C setpoint in approximately 41 min from a cold start at 22°C, in good agreement with the simulation prediction of ∼38 min (Section 7.1). During warm-up, the controller drives the heaters at full power (∼38.5 W continuous), confirming the power-limited settling behavior predicted by the thermal sizing analysis. The red dashed curve in Fig. 6 overlays the ANSYS coupled PID simulation (P=30, I=0.1, D=0) from Section 7.1. The simulation and experimental transient responses agree closely during warm-up, with the simulation predicting a settling time of ∼38 min compared to ∼41 min measured, a difference of approximately 8%, attributable to simplifications in the thermal model (uniform h, no air domain, structural steel only). Consistent with the simulation prediction, no overshoot was observed experimentally: the temperature approached the setpoint monotonically and settled without exceeding it (Fig. 6). After reaching setpoint, the controller transitions to on–off cycling to maintain the target temperature. The steady-state temperature over the subsequent ∼2.3 h was 37.1±0.4°C (mean ± one standard deviation; range 36.4–37.7 °C). The power trace shows periodic switching between full power (∼38.5 W) and zero as the controller modulates the heater duty cycle. However, this apparent on–off behavior may not reflect true bang–bang control: the DTB4848LV outputs a continuous 0–10 V analog signal, which the (Jessinie) voltage converter translates into a PWM waveform driving the MOSFET. The actual heater modulation occurs at the PWM switching frequency, which is too fast to resolve on the (WANPTEK) supply display (∼20 s sampling). The observed power dropouts likely correspond to periods of low PWM duty cycle during which the time-averaged current falls below the display resolution. Despite this on–off appearance in the recorded data, the chamber thermal mass acts as a low-pass filter, keeping the air temperature within the ±0.4°C band. The close agreement between simulation and experiment (∼8% in settling time) confirms that the simplified modeling approach (using jaw surface nodes as a proxy for local air temperature with convection boundary conditions, rather than explicit air-domain FSI modeling) provides sufficient accuracy for thermal feasibility assessment and initial PID tuning. Importantly, the measured steady-state temperature of 37.1±0.4°C (1σ; range 36.7–37.5 °C) remains well below the ∼ 39°C threshold above which mammalian cell viability degrades rapidly [1]. This thermal performance was confirmed biologically: the cell viability assay presented in Section 7.3 demonstrates that LUVA human mast cells maintained metabolic activity and membrane integrity throughout a 2-hour live imaging session, with sustained fluorescence observed for over 3 h of continuous operation.

Cell health validation

As a functional demonstration, the heating system was used to maintain 37°C during a live cell imaging experiment on the CytoStretcher platform. Human LUVA mast cells (Cat. No. T0843) were cultured in StemPro-34 SFM and labeled with CellTracker Green CMFDA (Thermo Fisher, Cat. No. C7025). This dye freely permeates cell membranes and reacts with intracellular thiols (primarily cysteine residues of glutathione) via glutathione S-transferase, producing a cell-impermeant fluorescent product retained in the cytoplasm. Sustained fluorescence therefore indicates that cells remain metabolically active and membrane-intact.

Cells were imaged on a spinning disk confocal microscope controlled by Micro-Manager, using a 20× objective, 488 nm laser excitation (power level 1), a 525/50 nm bandpass emission filter, and 100 ms exposure time. Images were acquired at 0, 40, 80, and 120 min under continuous heating at 37°C (Fig. 7). Fluorescent signal was retained throughout the 2-hour observation period, confirming that cells remained viable and metabolically active under the thermal conditions provided by the open-source heating module.

Limitations. Several design boundaries should be noted. First, the system provides heating only and does not include active cooling. If the ambient temperature exceeds the setpoint, the module cannot cool below ambient. Second, CO2 regulation is handled by a separate, pre-existing system and is not part of this design. Furthermore, temperature feedback relies on a single K-type thermocouple, so spatial uniformity depends on heater placement and chamber geometry. Finally, the PID coefficients were tuned via simulation for the CytoStretcher geometry. Other chambers may require re-tuning using the controller’s built-in auto-tune feature or manual adjustment.

Conclusion and future directions

This work presented an open-source, PID-controlled heating module that can be retrofitted onto existing cell culture platforms at a total cost of approximately $500 USD. The design follows a systematic methodology that includes analytical thermal sizing, finite-element verification, and closed-loop PID tuning. This approach can be adapted to other stage-top microscope cell culture chambers using the sizing calculator and generalization guide provided in the repository. Larger or more complex chambers may require additional insulation or multi-point temperature sensing to ensure spatial uniformity. Experimental validation on a CytoStretcher platform demonstrated a settling time of approximately 41 min from a 22 °C cold start, with steady-state regulation of 37.1±0.4°C over 2.3 h. The simulation and experimental results agreed within ∼8%, confirming that the simplified thermal model provides sufficient accuracy for feasibility assessment and initial controller tuning.

Several avenues exist for future improvement. Multi-point temperature sensing would enable characterization of spatial uniformity across larger or more complex chamber geometries. Integration of active cooling, such as a Peltier module, would extend the operating envelope to applications requiring sub-ambient temperature control or tighter overshoot suppression. Incorporating CO2 regulation into the module would eliminate the dependency on a separate gas supply system, yielding a fully self-contained environmental control solution. A further direction is to design and manufacture a purpose-built chamber using CNC milling or similar fabrication methods, with heating elements integrated directly into the chamber walls rather than adhesively bonded to the lid. This approach would improve both thermal contact and overall system aesthetics while simplifying assembly. Finally, validation on additional chamber platforms beyond the CytoStretcher would further demonstrate the generality of the design framework.
