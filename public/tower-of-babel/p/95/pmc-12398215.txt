Comprehensive analysis of cancer and non-cancer mortality in patients with bone metastases: A population-based study
Authors: Xia Yan, Hongyu Liu, Xueqi Bai, Jin Xu, Xiaojun Lou, Lai Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•186,404 patients with bone metastases were analyzed, where non-cancer deaths rose from 6.3 % (2010) to 9.5 % (2021)•Cardiovascular disease, COPD, and infections were major non-cancer contributors.•SMRs showed high mortality from septicemia, suicide, and pneumonia.•Preventive strategies should target infection and comorbidity management.

Introduction

Bone metastases represent a significant clinical challenge in advanced malignancies, especially common among patients with breast, prostate, and lung cancers [1,2]. Historically, the presence of bone metastases was considered a marker of uniformly poor prognosis. However, with recent advances in systemic therapies, multidisciplinary supportive care, and improvements in imaging and diagnostic techniques such as PET-CT and MRI, survival outcomes for patients with bone metastasis have significantly improved due to earlier detection and more precise disease stratification [3]. Nonetheless, the presence of bone metastases remains indicative of advanced, incurable disease, accompanied by considerable morbidity, primarily due to skeletal-related events such as pathological fractures, spinal cord compression, and hypercalcemia [4].

Although several studies have characterized overall mortality in metastatic cancer patients, these analyses often included different metastatic sites, potentially obscuring important site-specific insights [5,6]. A notable recent study by Mani et al., utilizing SEER data, reported that approximately 80 % of metastatic cancer patients succumb to their primary malignancy, whereas around 20 % die from competing non-cancer causes such as cardiovascular disease, COPD, stroke, and infections [7]. However, the mortality profile of patients with bone metastases may differ substantially due to unique clinical factors, such as prolonged immobilization, skeletal fragility, and skeletal-related events, which increase their vulnerability to both cancer-specific and non-cancer events [8].

Importantly, as survival improves, patients with bone metastases are increasingly living long enough to develop serious comorbidities or experience late effects of cancer-directed therapy [9,10]. Yet, the specific causes of death in this population have not been comprehensively described. Understanding the balance between cancer-related and competing non-cancer mortality risks is critical for informing clinical care, including personalized monitoring strategies, supportive interventions, and end-of-life decision-making [[11], [12], [13]].

In this study, we aimed to address this knowledge gap by first quantifying and characterizing causes of death among patients with bone metastases, stratified by primary cancer type and calendar year. Our goal is to support more nuanced, data-driven approaches to survivorship planning and clinical decision-making in this growing patient population.

Methods

Study design and data source

This study utilized a three-part analytical framework to investigate causes of death among patients with bone metastases (Supplementary Fig. 1). Patient-level data were extracted from the SEER 17 database, maintained by the National Cancer Institute’s Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) program, encompassing cancer cases diagnosed between 2010 and 2021. The SEER 17 registry captures approximately 28 % of the U.S. population and includes detailed demographic, clinical, and survival data across more than 27 cancer types. This large, population-based dataset enables robust epidemiological investigations and supports clinical and public health decision-making in cancer care.

Only patients with a diagnosis of bone metastasis were included. Individuals diagnosed solely through autopsy or death certificate were excluded to ensure data accuracy. Institutional review board approval was waived due to the publicly available and de-identified nature of the SEER data.

Cause of death classification

The causes of death were grouped into three mutually exclusive categories based on death certificate coding: (1) diagnosed metastatic death, defined as death due to the initially diagnosed primary cancer; (2) subsequent metastatic death, indicating death from a second primary malignancy; and (3) non-cancer death, referring to any other cause not directly attributed to cancer. Non-cancer mortality was further classified into 25 categories using ICD-O-3 codes (Supplementary Table 2). An alternative classification system based on the updated SEER ICD-O-3 2023 + coding was also employed to improve granularity in the categorization of cardiovascular and pulmonary causes (Supplementary Tables 3 and 4). Although conditions such as pneumonia, septicemia, and suicide may be indirectly influenced by cancer or its management, they are not recorded as cancer-related causes of death-on-death certificates. Consistent with established epidemiological methodology, these causes are classified as non-cancer related.

To assess the effect of the COVID-19 pandemic, we included an additional analysis of mortality patterns among patients with active follow-up during 2020, incorporating COVID-19-related deaths (Supplementary Table 1).

Standardized mortality ratios (SMRs)

To quantify the relative risk of non-cancer mortality in bone metastasis patients compared to the general U.S. population, standardized mortality ratios (SMRs) were calculated for each cause of death. SMRs were adjusted for age, sex, and race in accordance with U.S. census data and SEER protocols. The 95 % confidence intervals (CIs) for SMRs were calculated using SEER*Stat software version 8.4.4.

All statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.4.2. Supplementary Methods provide further details regarding model fitting, assumptions, and limitations.

Results

A total of 186,404 patients newly diagnosed with bone metastases between 2010 and 2021 were identified from the SEER database. Among them, 133,393 (71.5 %) died from the initially diagnosed cancer (diagnosed metastatic death), 11,062 (5.9 %) from non-cancer-related conditions, and 929 (0.5 %) from subsequent cancer (subsequent metastatic death). The demographic and clinicopathologic characteristics of the cohort are provided in Table 1. Over the study period, the proportion of deaths attributed to the primary cancer declined slightly (from 92.9 % in 2010 to 90.2 % in 2021) yet remained the leading cause each year (Supplementary Fig. 2). Annual trends in absolute mortality across the ten most common bone-metastatic cancers are presented in Fig. 1, showing a steady increase in cancer-related deaths until a peak in 2016 (12,861), followed by a decline through 2020 (5,854). This pattern was particularly pronounced in lung, hepatobiliary, gastric, colorectal, esophageal, and prostate malignancies. Meanwhile, the number of deaths from noncancer causes increased up to around 2017, consistent with the pattern of deaths for hepatobiliary and lung cancers, but with greater differences for kidney, stomach, and bladder tumors. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 4, relative cancer mortality remained dominant across all years (ranging from 92.91 % to 90.21 %), while non-cancer mortality increased gradually (from 6.31 % to 9.49 %). Across all tumor types, subsequent malignancies accounted for a consistently small proportion of total mortality (0–2.5 %) (yellow lines, Supplementary Fig. 4). Age-stratified data (Supplementary Fig. 5) indicated that cancer-related deaths primarily occurred in those aged ≥50, while non-cancer causes became more prevalent from age 60. Cardiovascular disease was most frequent (3,310 deaths; 29.9 %), followed by COPD (881; 8.0 %) and cerebrovascular events (699; 6.3 %). Notably, in 2020, COVID-19 was responsible for 17.3 % of non-cancer deaths in patients with active follow-up (Supplementary Table 1). Discussion

In this large, population-based cohort study, we evaluated cause-specific mortality in patients diagnosed with bone metastases using data from the SEER registry between 2010 and 2021. While death from the primary cancer remained the dominant outcome, our analysis reveals a non-negligible and increasingly relevant burden of non-cancer mortality in this population. These findings underscore the complex clinical trajectory of patients with skeletal involvement and highlight opportunities for tailored, multidisciplinary care strategies.

As expected, cancer progression remained the primary cause of death, particularly in patients with lung, pancreatic, and gastrointestinal primaries [[14], [15], [16]]. In contrast, among patients with prostate and breast cancer—tumor types typically associated with more indolent characteristics—we observed a markedly higher proportion of non-cancer deaths. This suggests that patients with slower-growing primaries may live long enough to develop competing health risks, underscoring the need for tumor-specific care in survivorship. For instance, prostate cancer patients, who demonstrated among the highest proportions of cardiovascular death, may benefit from closer cardio-oncology integration and management of pre-existing comorbidities. Conversely, the rapid progression seen in lung cancer metastases justifies a focus on early palliative care and streamlined treatment goals, rather than aggressive screening for competing conditions. While the occurrence of deaths due to subsequent primary malignancies may seem unexpected in a population with advanced cancer, such cases likely reflect long-term survivors who lived long enough to develop a second malignancy. Nonetheless, the small proportion of such deaths (0.5 %) suggests this remains a rare but notable phenomenon in the metastatic setting.

Beyond differences by tumor type, our study also uncovered distinct patterns in non-cancer causes of death. Cardiovascular disease, cerebrovascular accidents, COPD, and infections were the leading causes. While these have been previously reported in broader metastatic populations [17], our study isolates their impact in the context of bone metastases, a group often burdened by prolonged immobility, treatment toxicity, and multimorbidity. Among specific causes, septicemia exhibited the highest standardized mortality ratio (SMR: 5.8, 95 % CI: 5.3–6.3), underscoring the heightened vulnerability of these patients to systemic infections. This increased susceptibility likely arises from several intertwined factors, including immunosuppression secondary to anticancer therapies (such as chemotherapy or targeted therapy), prolonged and repeated hospital stays due to treatment-related complications, and reduced mobility associated with skeletal-related events, all of which may collectively elevate the risk of life-threatening infections. Pneumonia/influenza (SMR: 6.04), suicide (SMR: 6.68), and hypertension without cardiac involvement (SMR: 2.4) also demonstrated significantly elevated risks. Among these, the observed excess suicide risk in this cohort is concerning. While prior studies, including Zaorsky et al., have reported elevated suicide rates among patients with metastatic cancer [18], our results highlight this vulnerability specifically in those with bone metastases. This group is often affected by chronic pain, functional decline, and loss of independence—factors that may contribute to psychological distress [19]. Pointing to an urgent need for early identification of psychosocial distress and the integration of mental health services into oncology care. In addition, a notable observation was the substantial impact of COVID-19, accounting for 17.3 % of non-cancer deaths among patients actively followed in 2020. This finding underscores the heightened susceptibility of immunocompromised cancer patients to emerging infectious diseases, highlighting the urgent necessity for targeted preventive interventions. These should include prioritized vaccination programs, stringent infection-control measures, and timely initiation of antiviral treatments, especially for patients with bone metastases who are undergoing immunosuppressive or cytotoxic therapies [20,21].

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective design and use of registry data limit our ability to capture treatment regimens, functional status, and detailed comorbidity profiles—all of which influence outcomes. Second, reliance on death certificate data introduces the possibility of misclassification, particularly in complex clinical scenarios where multiple comorbidities coexist or the proximate cause of death may be difficult to determine. This limitation is particularly relevant for causes such as infections and cardiovascular disease, which may be under- or over-attributed depending on documentation practices. For instance, sepsis or pneumonia occurring in the context of progressive cancer may be variably classified as cancer-related or non-cancer-related, potentially affecting the precision of cause-specific mortality estimates. Third, while SEER provides broad geographic representation within the U.S., findings may not be generalizable to populations managed in different health care systems or under varying treatment standards. Furthermore, despite the well-established association between cancer and venous thromboembolism (VTE)—including deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE)—the SEER registry does not consistently differentiate these events as discrete causes of death. Instead, such fatalities are commonly subsumed under broader cardiovascular or circulatory classifications, thereby constraining our capacity to accurately identify and quantify VTE-specific mortality.

Conclusion

Non-cancer-related mortality is an increasingly important contributor to death in patients with bone metastases, with septicemia showing the highest excess risk. Targeted strategies for infection prevention, cardiovascular health, and supportive care are essential to improve outcomes in this population.
