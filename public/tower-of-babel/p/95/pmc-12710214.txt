Factors associated with utilization of cervical cancer screening services among women at a tertiary hospital in eastern Uganda: A cross sectional study
Authors: Priscillah Kwehayo, Milton W. Musaba, Faith Oguttu, Samuel Olowo, Lydia Ssenyonga, Brendah Nambozo, Solomon Wani, Ritah Nantale, David Mukunya, Julius N Wandabwa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Globally, cervical cancer can be cured if diagnosed at an early stage and treated promptly. However, this is rarely done in low- and middle-income countries, where the highest rates of cervical cancer incidence and mortality are reported. In Uganda, less than 20% of the targeted population utilize cervical cancer screening services despite these being readily available in health facilities across the country at no cost. We aimed to determine the factors associated with utilization of cervical cancer screening services among women of reproductive age at Mbale regional referral hospital in eastern Uganda.Methods:This was a cross-sectional study involving 355 participants, we recruited clients attending the family planning clinic at Mbale regional referral hospital. We conducted multivariable logistic regression to determine the factors associated with utilization of cervical cancer screening services. Data were analyzed in Stata version 14.1.Results:Only 33/355 (9.3 %) women had ever utilized cervical cancer screening services. Majority of the women 23/33(69.7 %) who had ever screened for cervical cancer, did so after recommendation by a health worker. Utilization of cervical cancer screening services was associated with: maternal age of 30 years and above [Adjusted Odds Ratio (AOR) 11.25 Confidence Interval (CI) (3.67–34.55)] and being more knowledgeable about cervical cancer [AOR 5.13 95 % CI (1.75–15.08)].Conclusion:Adequate knowledge about cervical cancer and level of utilization of cervical cancer screening services was low among women at a regional referral hospital in Eastern Uganda. We recommend the following interventions targeted at increasing access to services and health literacy levels in the community. 1. Integrating cervical cancer screening into existing community outreach programs such as immunization. 2. Use of mass media and text messages in local dialects to communicate information about cervical cancer. 3. Community led campaigns and outreaches aimed at educating the public about risk factors and available methods of prevention of cervical cancer. 4. Health workers are also encouraged to routinely recommend and encourage cervical cancer screening services to all women of reproductive age that are under their care.

Background

Cervical cancer is the fourth most common form of cancer among women worldwide despite it being preventable and curable (World Health Organization, 2023). Globally in 2020, there were an estimated 604 127 new cases of cervical cancer and 341 831 deaths (Singh et al., 2023). The burden of cervical cancer is highest in low- and middle-income countries with over 90 % of the global deaths from cervical cancer being registered in these regions (World Health Organization, 2023). Incidence is three times and mortality is six times higher secondary to cervical cancer in low- and middle-income countries as compared to high income countries (Singh et al., 2023). In Uganda, cervical cancer is the leading cause of cancer morbidity and mortality among women with an estimated 6959 new cases and 4607 deaths in 2020 (Beyer et al., 2022). The higher rate of cervical cancer incidence and mortality in low and middle income countries is attributed to the limited access to health care and ineffective implementation of screening and treatment for cervical cancer (World Health Organization, 2023).

Secondary prevention of cervical cancer involves screening, identifying and treating precancerous lesions (Basu, Mittal, Vale, & Kharaji, 2018). Precancerous lesions are caused by oncogenic human papilloma virus strains (Faridi, Zahra, Khan, & Idrees, 2011). Early detection and treatment of lesions has been reported to reduce incidence and mortality due to cervical cancer by 80 % among target populations (Landy, Pesola, Castañón, & Sasieni, 2016). Over 88.5 % of low-grade precancerous lesions regress within 1 year, 11 % persist and 0.7 % progress to high grade cervical lesions which are treatable (Abzouzeid & El-Agwanyei, 2016). However, if not treated 30 % –70 % of pre-malignant lesions progress to invasive cancer (Faruk & Murat, 2014). In Uganda, 80 % of the cases of cervical cancer diagnosed annually show-up at health facilities at advanced stage (Beyer et al., 2022).

The World Health Organization launched the global cervical cancer elimination initiative in 2020 (Gultekin, Ramirez, Broutet, & Hutubessy, 2020). The initiative’s plans include having 70 % of women screened for cervical cancer at least twice between 35 and 45 years by 2030 (World Health Organization, 2023). The ministry of health, Uganda launched the strategic plan for cervical cancer prevention and control in Uganda in 2010 (Ministry of Health, 2010). The strategic plan projected to have over 80 % of eligible women screened and positive ones treated for cervical cancer premalignant lesions by 2015 (Ministry of Health, 2010). Free services for screening for cervical cancer are available in public health facilities however uptake is still very low at 4.8 %–30 % (Campos et al., 2017; Mukama, Ndejjo, Musabyimana, Halage, & Musoke, 2017; Ndejjo, Mukama, Kiguli, & Musoke, 2017; Twinomujuni, Nuwaha, & Babirye, 2015). In addition, continuous surveillance and monitoring for cervical cancer prevention programs is needed to identify gaps and take actions. A good understanding of the social, cultural, societal and structural barriers to the uptake of cervical cancer screening services is crucial to inform the development of context-specific and culturally appropriate service delivery platforms.

Therefore, our study aimed at assessing factors associated with utilization of cervical cancer screening services among women attending a family planning clinic at Mbale regional referral hospital in eastern Uganda. Our study will inform future strategies to prioritize and accelerate progress towards the WHO elimination targets of cervical cancer.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a cross-sectional study among women attending a family planning clinic at Mbale regional referral hospital. Mbale regional referral hospital is a general and teaching hospital found in eastern Uganda. The hospital serves over 4 million people in 14 districts. Mbale regional referral hospital offers both inpatient services in 12 wards and outpatient services including specialized clinics in the; Pediatrics, Surgery, Medicine, Obstetrics and Gynecology departments. The Obstetrics and Gynecology department runs a postnatal and family planning clinic where teaching and cervical cancer screening is done by visual inspection with acetic acid (VIA). The family planning clinic is open from Monday to Friday between 0800 h and 1700 h, except on public holidays. Daily attendance at this clinic is about 50 clients, seeking for various services that are offered through the clinic.

Study population

These were women of reproductive age attending the family planning clinic at Mbale regional referral hospital.

Eligibility criteria

Women of reproductive age attending the family planning clinic were included. Women who declined to give informed consent to participate in the study were excluded.

Sample size estimation

We employed the Kish and Leslie’s formula for sample determination (N = Z2pq/e2) (Omair, 2014). A tolerable margin of error of 5 % (e = 0.05) and normal standard deviation at 95 % level of significance (Z = 1.96) was considered. This study considered a 30 % prevalence of uptake of cervical cancer screening services reported from a previous study done in Uganda (Campos et al., 2017). We considered a non-response rate of 10 %. After substitutions, the final sample size required was 355 participants.

Sampling

The researchers used lottery as a method of simple random sampling technique. The sampling was done at 10am daily on week days. The reason is at this time most of the women have arrived as it’s a routine whereby a general health education is first delivered to all the women about family planning starting 9am. The researcher took advantage of this arrangement to brief the prospective participants about the objectives of the ongoing study. All women that expressed interest into the study were given more information about the study in one-to-one sessions before a written informed consent was obtained. On the other hand, women who did not express any further interest to participate in the study were served by the clinic staff and discharged. On a daily basis, 15–20 participants were interviewed by one of the four well trained research assistants, we deemed this to be an ideal and cost-effective work load.

Variables

The primary outcome variable was having ever screened for cervical cancer. The independent variables included; socio-demographic characteristics, cervical cancer screening awareness and knowledge about cervical cancer screening. Assessment of knowledge and awareness of cervical cancer screening services was done using a standardized questionnaire. The questions were adapted from the African Women Awareness of CANcer (AWACAN) tool (Moodley et al., 2019).

The first section assessing for awareness had 3 questions. The second section assessing for level of knowledge had; 8 questions about signs and symptoms, 6 questions about risks factors, and 5 questions about prevention. Each correct response was scored as 1, while incorrect or “do not know” responses scored as 0. A total score for knowledge was calculated. To obtain a binary outcome of level of knowledge a 50 % correction rate was used to determine women who were less or more knowledgeable about cervical cancer. Women who had scores above 9.5/19 were considered to have adequate knowledge about cervical cancer while those who scored below 9.5/19 were considered to have inadequate knowledge about cervical cancer. (Ahmed, Sabitu, Idris, & Ahmed, 2013; Bansal, Pakhare, Kapoor, Mehrotra, & Kokane, 2015; Liu, Li, Ratcliffe, & Chen, 2017).

Data collection tool and procedures

We adopted and used standardized data collection tools that have been used in similar settings (Aweke, Ayanto, & Ersado, 2017; Mitiku & Tefera, 2016; Moodley et al., 2019; Mukama et al., 2017; Ndejjo et al., 2017). The tool was translated into the commonly spoken local dialects, and piloted at a nearby health center. After the pilot interviews, the tool was found to be acceptable and feasible, no changes were made to it. Data were collected from October 2022 to November 2022 and the questionnaire was administered by trained research assistants. Interviews were conducted in a designated private room at the family planning clinic, and lasted 20 min. Each filled questionnaire was checked by the principal investigator for completeness before the participant exited the clinic.

Data analysis

We summarized continuous variables such as age as mean (standard deviation) and categorical variables as proportions. The uptake of cervical cancer screening services was operationalized as ever (coded 1) and never utilized (coded 0) cervical cancer screening services. We used logistic regression to estimate odds ratios between selected variables and uptake of cervical cancer screening services. A multivariable model that adjusted for confounding was developed. Variables were added in the model basing on a statistical significance of ≤ 0.25 at bivariable analysis (maternal age, husband level of education, type of marriage, parity, knowledge about cervical cancer) and biological plausibility (maternal level of education) (Damiani et al., 2015). Odds ratios and 95 % confidence intervals were used as measures of association.

Results

A total of 355 women participated in this study. Majority of participants were between 20 and 35 years with a mean age of 27.2 ± 5.4 years. Most 234/355 (65.9 %) of the study participants were from rural residence. Majority 239/355 (67.3 %) of the study participants attained at least secondary education and 128/355 (36.1 %) were self-employed. Majority 340/355 (95.8 %) of the study participants were married, more than half were in a monogamous type of family 248/355 (69.9 %). Less than a quarter (33/355) were HIV Positive. Discussion

Understanding factors affecting utilization of cervical cancer screening services is essential in preventing new cases and mortality due to cervical cancer. This study aimed to assess factors associated with utilization of cervical cancer screening services among 355 women attending a family planning clinic at a regional referral hospital in eastern Uganda. In this study, we found out that hardly one of every ten women had ever screened for cancer of the cervix. This low level of utilization of cervical cancer screening services was not surprising because it has been reported by studies conducted in other regions of Uganda such as Central (7 %) (Twinomujuni et al., 2015) and Busoga (4.8 %) (Ndejjo, Mukama, Musabyimana, & Musoke, 2016). Low uptake of cervical cancer screening services in our setting may be attributed to the fact that 326/355(91.8 %) of the participants had inadequate knowledge about cervical cancer screening services and less than a quarter of the women; 75(21.1 %) had attended tertiary education. Level of education can affect level of health literacy which affects one’s ability to obtain, process, and understand basic health information and services needed to make appropriate health decisions (Damiani et al., 2015). Similar findings of a low utilization rate for cervical cancer screening services have been reported in studies done in other African countries such as Ethiopia (9.9 %), Ghana (3 %), Nigeria (1.5 %), Malawi (15 %) and Sudan (2.3 %) (Ampofo, Adumatta, Owusu, & Awuviry-Newton, 2020; Aweke et al., 2017; Kamanga, Zhang, & Stones, 2023; Mohamed, Elamin, Babikir, Rayis, & Development, 2021; Sharma et al., 2021). From a public health perspective, this finding is worrying with regards to the campaigns to reduce cervical cancer related morbidity and mortality deaths through early detection and treatment of cervical lesions in low resource settings where 80 % of the patients are diagnosed late with advanced disease that is incurable (Beyer et al., 2022). On the other hand, this finding is in contrast with reports from studies done in higher income countries, where far more women utilize these cervical cancer screening services (Liu et al., 2017). One possible explanation for this disparity in the utilization of cervical cancer screening services between the high and low income countries, might be due to differences in social economic characteristics of the population, and health system factors (Orach & Garimoi, 2009).

In addition, we found that more than three quarters of the women that utilized the cervical cancer screening services, did so on the recommendation of a health worker. This is also not surprising because it is consistent with findings from various studies conducted in other settings (Ferdous et al., 2018; Vahabi & Lofters, 2016). This is an important finding because it underpins the important role which health care providers play in the promotion and therefore, success of various health interventions in the community. This is because health workers’ are trusted sources of health information because their advice is taken seriously and absence of their recommendation makes the patients to assume that a particular test is not worth performing (Grant et al., 2017). Therefore, as a matter of policy, it is important to involve providers of health services as key stakeholders’ promotion of cervical cancer screening, and other reproductive health related services.

We found out that women aged 30 years and above had 11.25 times the odds of screening for cervical cancer as compared to the younger women. This finding is in agreement/similar with a study done in Ethiopia (Getachew et al., 2019) where women aged 30–39 and those 40–49 years were 2 and 4 times respectively more likely to seek cervical cancer screening services than those aged 21–29 years. Another related study in Tanzania revealed that women in older ages were more likely to be screened for cancer cervix than their counterparts of younger ages (Cunningham et al., 2015). This can be explained by the fact that the older one grows, the more they may perceive themselves at risk of acquiring non-communicable diseases such as cancer. Furthermore, evidence shows that older women are at a significant risk for cancer cervix than younger women (Woldetsadik et al., 2020). However, cervical cancer screening services should also be targeted at all sexually active women.

We also found that women who had adequate knowledge about cervical cancer had 5.13 times the odds of screening for cervical cancer as compared to women who had inadequate knowledge about cervical cancer. This can be explained by the fact that women with lower level of health-literacy have a poor health seeking behavior (Damiani et al., 2015). Women with low level of health literacy also have a poor understanding of instructions about health behavior, this results in low adherence to advice from health care workers on the importance of cancer screening tests for prevention and early detection of cancer (Damiani et al., 2015).This is in agreement with findings from others studies done in similar settings, showing that awareness and adequate knowledge about cervical cancers are good predictors of utilization of cervical cancer screening services (Adoch et al., 2020; Akinlotan et al., 2017; Ashtarian, Mirzabeigi, Mahmoodi, & Khezeli, 2017; Gele, Qureshi, Kour, Kumar, & Diaz, 2017; Mukama et al., 2017) This calls for more effort to not only increase awareness about cervical cancer screening which was high in this study but to also empower women with knowledge about signs, symptoms, screening methods and management of cervical cancer. We recommend that healthcare providers are put at the center of this campaign to promote utilization of these free of charge cervical cancer screening services.

Strengths and limitations

Our study provides timely information, unique to the Elgon region in eastern Uganda where no study has been done to assess utilization and factors affecting uptake of services for cervical cancer screening. This can be used to design context specific interventions to increase uptake of cervical cancer screening services. We used a cross sectional study design, this enabled us generate evidence for the hypothesis that “women who have adequate knowledge about cervical cancer are more likely to utilize cervical cancer screening services”. However, given our study design, we are unable to establish causality and can only report association. Another limitation to our study, is the lack of a community component, since we only interviewed women that had come to the facility for family planning services, this introduces a selection bias. Therefore, this might be an over estimate of the uptake (35 %) of cervical screening services in the Elgon region.

Conclusion

Adequate knowledge about cervical cancer and level of utilization of cervical cancer screening services was low among women at a regional referral hospital in Eastern Uganda. We recommend the following interventions targeted at increasing access to services and health literacy levels in the community. 1. Integrating cervical cancer screening into existing community outreach programs such as immunization. 2. Use of mass media and text messages in local dialects to communicate information about cervical cancer. 3. Community led campaigns and outreaches aimed at educating the public about risk factors and available methods of prevention of cervical cancer. 4. Health workers are also encouraged to routinely recommend and encourage cervical cancer screening services to all women of reproductive age that are under their care.
