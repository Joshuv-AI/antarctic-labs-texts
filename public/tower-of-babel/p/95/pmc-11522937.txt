Sociodemographic Disparities in 1-Year Outcomes of Children With Community-Acquired Acute Kidney Injury
Authors: Omer S. Ashruf, Zaid Ashruf, Valerie Luyckx, David C. Kaelber, Sidharth K. Sethi, Rupesh Raina
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionDo disparities in clinical outcomes persist along sociodemographic lines among children with community-acquired acute kidney injury (CA-AKI)?FindingsIn this cohort study of 17 125 eligible pediatric patients with an episode of CA-AKI from 2003 to 2023, Black and Hispanic children had poorer outcomes compared with White children, whereas Asian children experienced similar outcomes at 1 year. Compared with their older counterparts, children aged 0 to 9 years were at greater risk of experiencing poor outcomes.MeaningThese findings suggest that better understanding of the underlying disparities in outcomes of pediatric CA-AKI is required to develop targeted approaches to improve follow-up and management.

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) carries serious clinical consequences in the pediatric population, increasing the risk of mortality, morbidity, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and kidney failure (KF).1,2,3,4,5 Disparities in kidney care span socioeconomic, age, racial, and ethnic dimensions, often resulting in greater rates of complications, inferior hemodialysis service quality, and poor transplantation access.6,7 Racial and systemic biases exacerbate health care inequities and have been reported for children with CKD and KF, particularly Black and Hispanic children.8 Yet, data have been conflicting on the role of race and mortality among patients with AKI.9,10 Patient age has also been found to be an important variable that may influence AKI incidence and mortality, but findings have been mixed.11,12 Poor awareness and timeliness of diagnosis have been reported as ongoing obstacles to improving outcomes.13,14

The literature on pediatric AKI reports largely on hospital-acquired AKI, defined as AKI developing after 48 hours of hospitalization. Yet, community-acquired AKI (CA-AKI), which develops outside the hospital, has been shown to constitute a greater share of pediatric AKI, with increasing incidence and similar long-term outcomes.15,16,17,18,19 To our knowledge, this study is the first to examine racial disparities in CA-AKI among children in the US. This cohort study evaluated differences in clinical outcomes associated with age, race, and ethnicity among pediatric CA-AKI patients at 1 year of diagnosis.

Methods

Database Description

Data used in this study were accessed through the TriNetX platform,20 which aggregates electronic health records (EHRs) across 61 large health care organizations in the US and includes 21 million pediatric patients (aged <18 years). A retrospective cohort study was conducted from 2003 to 2023 querying data from deidentified patient encounters per the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) criteria—Section §164.514(a) of the HIPAA Privacy Rule. TriNetX pulls International Statistical Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) diagnosis codes, Logical Observation Identifier Names and Codes laboratory codes, Current Procedural Terminology procedure codes, and medical prescription normalized codes from the contributing EHRs. Race and ethnicity data in TriNetX are derived from self-reports in the contributing EHRs. Data were accessed in January 2024. More information is provided in the eMethods in Supplement 1.

Studies using TriNetX in this way have been deemed exempt by the MetroHealth System (Cleveland, Ohio) institutional review board because the patient data analyzed are deidentified aggregated data and, therefore, not considered human participants research from the institutional review board perspective; thus, the study did not require informed consent, in accordance with 45 CFR §46. The TriNetX corporation obtained independent expert attestation that using the TriNetX platform in ways described here meet the HIPAA criteria of deidentified data. The TriNetX platform has been used extensively in high-risk patient populations to gain a greater understanding of population-based epidemiological outcomes.21,22 This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines.23

Participant Selection and Study Design

The study included data from pediatric patients (aged <18 years) with medical encounters who had an ICD-10-CM encounter diagnosis of AKI (76% of patients) or serum creatinine greater than 0.3 mg/dL (to convert to micromoles per liter, multiply by 88.4) above baseline (24% of patients) with race and ethnicity data available (18 152 patients). The race and ethnicity data collected from the TriNetX platform originate from self-reports within clinical EHR systems and are then mapped to standardized categories. Specifically, race is categorized as American Indian or Alaskan Native, Asian, Black or African American, Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander, White, and unknown race. Ethnicity is categorized as Hispanic or Latino, not Hispanic or Latino, and unknown ethnicity. For our analysis, the sample was limited to Asian (including Asian, Native Hawaiian, and Other Pacific Islander), Black, Hispanic, and White.

Patients who were hospitalized at the time of diagnosis were excluded to properly identify cases of hospital-acquired AKI; patients with known prior kidney disease such as CKD, KF, or dialysis dependence were also excluded. Outcomes within 1 year of the diagnosis of CA-AKI among Asian, Black, and Hispanic children were compared with those among White children in 3 separate analyses stratified arbitrarily by age to identify children and adolescents: 0 to 18 years, 0 to 9 years, and 10 to 18 years (Figure 1).

Statistical Analysis

The outcomes assessed were all-cause emergency department (ED) visits, intensive care unit (ICU) admissions, mechanical ventilation, and mortality within 1 year of a CA-AKI diagnosis. Survival probabilities for each outcome at the end of the time window from Kaplan-Meier analysis were also reported. Composite outcomes were defined as a patient’s risk of experiencing any outcome. The relevant codes used to identify clinical outcomes and study design are supplied in eTable 1 in Supplement 1. The study cohort and age subgroups were propensity matched for demographic variables (age and sex), comorbid or etiological conditions ICD-10-CM encounter diagnoses (hypertension and/or hypertensive disease, volume depletion and/or gastrointestinal issues, diabetes, anemia, or urinary system issues), pertinent laboratory values (serum albumin and serum creatinine), and medications (antihypertensives, salicylic compounds, and diuretics). Study outcomes were reported after propensity matching solely for age and sex and again for all aforementioned covariates. The relevant codes used to identify covariates are supplied in eTable 2 in Supplement 1. Each analysis and all groups were independently propensity matched. The data analysis process was conducted on patient outcomes subsequent to propensity score matching, using the TriNetX built-in 1:1 propensity score matching function, which uses a nearest-neighbor, greedy-matching algorithm. Composite outcomes, defined as the risk of experiencing any clinical outcomes, were also calculated and compared.

Measures of association, Cox proportional hazard analyses, and Kaplan-Meier survival curves were performed within the TriNetX Advanced Analytics platform between racial and ethnic groups for each analysis. Measures of association were used to determine risk difference and odds ratios with associated 95% CIs. Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was used to compare time-to-event rates in matched groups. Kaplan-Meier analysis was used to estimate the survival probability of given clinical outcomes. Hazard ratios (HRs) and associated 95% CIs were calculated using the R Survival package version 3.2-3 (R Project for Statistical Computing). Significance tests were 2-sided and paired, and statistical significance was set at P < .05. A standardized mean difference between propensity scores of less than or equal to 0.1 was used by the analytics platform.

Results

The study population included 17 125 eligible pediatric patients with CA-AKI (9424 male [55.3%]; mean [SD] age, 11.2 [5.2] years), including 1169 Asian children (6.5%), 4636 Black children (27.3%), 1786 Hispanic children (10.2%), and 9534 White children (55.9%)). After 1:1 matching to White children, 4499 Black children, 1168 Asian children, and 1776 Hispanic children were included (Table 1, Table 2, and Table 3). After matching for only sex and age, within 1 year of CA-AKI, Asian children experienced greater rates of mechanical intubation or ventilation (HR, 1.94; 95% CI, 1.61-2.34), lower rates of ICU admissions (HR, 0.58; 95% CI, 0.46-0.72), and no statistically significant difference in ED visits (HR, 1.06; 95% CI, 0.91-1.22) or all-cause mortality (HR, 1.02; 95% CI, 0.81-1.28) compared with White children. Black children experienced greater rates of ED visits (HR, 1.63; 95% CI, 1.51-1.76), ICU admissions (HR, 1.29; 95% CI, 1.17-1.42), mechanical intubation or ventilation (HR, 1.36; 95% CI, 1.19-1.55), and all-cause mortality (HR, 1.20; 95% CI, 1.05-1.36) compared with White children. Hispanic children experienced greater rates of ED visits (HR, 1.51; 95% CI, 1.35-1.68), mechanical intubation or ventilation (HR, 1.37; 95% CI, 1.14-1.64), and all-cause mortality (HR, 1.56; 95% CI, 1.33-1.83) compared with White children, but no statistically significant difference was observed for ICU admissions (HR, 0.94; 95% CI, 0.81-1.10).

After matching for all covariates, such as demographic variables, comorbid conditions, and laboratory values, among patients aged 0 to 18 years, compared with White children within 1 year of CA-AKI, Asian children experienced greater rates of mechanical ventilation (HR, 1.69; 95% CI, 1.26-2.27), but no statistically significant difference in ED visits (HR, 0.99; 95% CI, 0.81-1.21), ICU admissions (HR, 0.82; 95% CI, 0.61-1.11), or mortality (HR, 1.27; 95% CI, 0.90-1.78). Kaplan-Meier analysis found that compared with White children, Asian children had a 2.50% higher probability of survival after ICU admission and a 7.03% lower probability of survival after being ventilated; they also had a 1.33% higher survival probability after ED visit and a 0.65% higher survival probability overall, but those differences were not statistically significant. Black children experienced greater rates of ED visits (HR, 1.53; 95% CI, 1.40-1.67), ICU admissions (HR, 1.31; 95% CI, 1.16-1.47), mechanical ventilation (HR, 1.33; 95% CI, 1.13-1.56), and all-cause mortality (HR, 1.27; 95% CI, 1.09-1.48) that White children. Compared with White children, Black children had a survival probability 11.41% lower after an ED visit, 3.34% lower after ICU admission, 1.95% lower after being ventilated, and 2.11% lower overall. Hispanic children experienced greater rates of ED visits (HR, 1.40; 95% CI, 1.21-1.62), mechanical ventilation (HR, 1.41; 95% CI, 1.08-1.85), and mortality (HR, 1.66; 95% CI, 1.31-2.09) compared with White children, but there was no statistically significant difference in ICU admissions (HR, 1.09; 95% CI, 0.88-1.34). Hispanic children had a survival probability 7.14% lower than White children after ED visit and 4.54% lower overall; they also had 0.82% lower survival probability after ICU admission and 0.96% lower survival probability after being ventilated, but those differences were not statistically significant. Black children were at greatest risk of composite outcomes (HR, 1.43; 95% CI, 1.33-1.53), followed by Hispanic children (HR, 1.33; 95% CI, 1.19-1.49) and Asian children (HR, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.85-1.15). Discussion

The findings of this retrospective cohort study suggest that outcomes within 1 year of a diagnosis of CA-AKI were poorer among Black and Hispanic children compared with White children. The risk for all-cause mortality was greatest in Hispanic children, and mechanical ventilation requirements were greatest for Asian children. The 1-year survival probability difference was greatest between Black and Hispanic children compared with White children after the index ED encounter when CA-AKI was diagnosed. Black and Hispanic children aged 0 to 9 years were at greater risk of experiencing composite outcomes by the end of the study period. The observed disparities in outcomes after an episode of CA-AKI across age, race, and ethnicity require further investigation and may reflect disparities in disease burden, progression, clinical care, or social determinants of health. Large gaps remain in pediatric AKI, and large epidemiologic studies are needed to quantify disease burden and understand the impact of social determinants of health and race and ethnicity.24

Limited data exist quantifying sociodemographic discrepancies in CA-AKI, especially in the vulnerable population of children in the US. In a cross-sectional study25 evaluating racial and ethnic disparities in AKI in hospitalized premature infants, AKI diagnosis was more prevalent among Black and Hispanic infants and less prevalent among Asian infants compared with White infants. Furthermore, and similar to the present study, White infants experienced lower mortality compared with the Black population. Similar outcomes were observed in the neonatal ICU setting.26 A secondary analysis of an inpatient database evaluating racial and health insurance disparities in pediatric AKI found that AKI occurred more frequently among patients of lower socioeconomic status.7 They reported a higher AKI risk associated with children of older age and suggested cautious interpretation because of the use of administrative data.

Several hypotheses have been posited to understand the root of such disparities in kidney care. Socioeconomic status has been widely reported as an independent risk factor for poor outcomes in the setting of AKI, with populations who have decreased social and financial capital experiencing worse survival.27,28,29 The intimate relationship between socioeconomic deprivation and sociodemographic disparities, specifically race and ethnicity, may impact the disparities in outcomes observed here. These are, in turn, associated with health literacy, immigration and insurance status, dietary choices, and exposure to environmental pollutants, all of which may impact risk of AKI and access to high-quality kidney care.30

Potential interventions to address disparities in access to care and health outcomes in general revolve around awareness, advocacy, diagnosis, and management.31 Given the poorer access to care among minoritized and more disadvantaged groups, there have also been calls for greater advocacy and reform regarding insurance coverage and access to social programs to ensure equitable access to diagnosis and care across ethnic and socioeconomic groups, which should also reduce barriers to kidney care.30 In the present study, Black and Hispanic children had the lowest survival probabilities after initial ED encounter, a disparity that may be explained by diagnostic delays and improper disease identification experienced in the ED setting. It has also been suggested that certain kidney injury detection methods be made more accessible in the ED setting, specifically, adoption of a renal angina index,32,33 an AKI risk stratification tool, or offering point-of-care serum creatinine and urine dipstick testing, which has been shown to improve AKI identification in low-resource settings.17

The potential interventions to address these disparities revolve around education, advocacy, and reassessment of detection methods. As discussed, a complex and multidimensional relationship exists between sociodemographic variables, educational attainment, health literacy, and health insurance access. Emphasis should be placed on a patient’s kidney disease awareness, a physician’s cultural competency and bedside education, and adequate community outreach to provide opportunities for primary prevention and include family in decision-making.32 There have also been calls for greater advocacy and reform regarding insurance coverage and access to social programs. Such initiatives may alleviate the impact of social deprivation by providing education and health care affordability and, in turn, reduce barriers to kidney care.30

Limitations and Strengths

An inherent limitation of this analysis is its observational, retrospective nature, which can only identify trends and associations, but not infer causality. The study population was identified using encounter diagnosis codes, which are susceptible to inaccurate or incomplete coding. Data from EHRs on race and ethnicity may not adequately report a patient’s diverse background. Another important limitation of this study is that children with an encounter diagnosis of AKI were included. It is, therefore, not possible to assess outcomes in those with missed AKI, and it is difficult to assess whether and how the observed outcomes would have been biased. Accurate AKI diagnoses were reliant on correct EHR documentation. In pediatric CA-AKI in particular, baseline serum creatinine measurements are less available, and cases may be missed altogether.34,35 Other surrogate measures that exist, such as height-dependent formulas or urine output measurements, are difficult to obtain in the ED setting. These impediments to accurate and timely diagnosis and management are further exacerbated by potentially another factor: delayed access to ED care, especially in minoritized populations and in urbanized areas.36 Often, in the low-income, rural, or outpatient settings, creatinine measurements or central laboratory testing facilities often not readily available.

A retrospective study37 of 4 million ED visits in the US observed marked racial and ethnic disparities in the duration of routine visits and ED stays, most pronounced in Black and Asian patients. A cross-sectional study38 assessing ED care and treatment from 2005 to 2016 found that Black patients received lower triage scores and had higher mortality rates. Arguably, as it is known that Black and minoritized children tend to have less access to care, it is possible that those who did access care and had a diagnosis of CA-AKI were more ill than White children who accessed care, which may have contributed to the observed outcome disparities. It is not common for children to have kidney function measurements unless there is a specific suspicion or a child is obviously ill; therefore, milder cases of AKI are likely to have been missed, and the outcomes reflected here likely reflect those in children with more severe overall illness.

A further important limitation of this study is the inability to reliably assess and stratify by severity, timing, or cause of AKI; therefore, the impact of these factors on outcomes and the association with race and ethnicity are unknown. Comprehensive sociodemographic factors, such as insurance and immigration status or education attainment, were not available. Furthermore, although TriNetX derives race and ethnicity data from self-reports in the EHR systems, such data are not validated. In addition, despite rigorous propensity matching, residual confounding cannot be excluded.

Despite these limitations, however, this study has strengths in terms of the large number of pediatric patients included, across a diverse set of health care institutions and population groups across the US, and the availability of follow-up outcomes. Although purely descriptive, this study highlights important discrepancies in outcomes among children with CA-AKI associated with self-reported ethnicity, which require further study and should be remedied. Future research should prioritize studying individual contributions of adverse social determinants of health related to socioeconomic and psychosocial circumstances to better understand their impact better on racial disparities and clinical outcomes.

Conclusions

The findings of this retrospective cohort study of pediatric patients with CA-AKI suggest that important disparities exist in clinical outcomes along sociodemographic lines, in particular, race, ethnicity, and age. Younger Black and Hispanic children experienced poorer clinical outcomes compared with White children. Further studies are required to examine the factors underlying such disparities while emphasizing the value of diagnostic awareness and appropriate management and follow-up after emergency care.
