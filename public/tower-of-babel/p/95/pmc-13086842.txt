Diagnostic utility and discriminative ability of cholesterol-modified prognostic nutritional index and inflammatory indicators in colorectal cancer: a retrospective case-control study
Authors: Berrin Papila, Sinem Durmus, Murad Guliyev, Ozkan Alan, Nebi Serkan Demirci, Remise Gelisgen, Cigdem Papila, Hafize Uzun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMalnutrition and altered biochemical parameters are common in colorectal cancer (CRC) patients. This study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic utility and discriminative ability of nutritional indices, including cholesterol-modified prognostic nutritional index (CPNI), prognostic nutritional index (PNI), nutritional risk index (NRI), and controlling nutritional status (CONUT) score in CRC. This retrospective case-control study included 100 CRC patients and 100 healthy controls. Baseline clinical and laboratory data were compared between the two groups to determine the capacity of these indices to distinguish CRC patients from healthy individuals. CRC patients demonstrated significantly lower nutritional markers (BMI, HDL, total protein, albumin, PNI, and NRI; p < 0.05) and markedly higher inflammatory/tumor-related parameters (CA-19.9, CEA, CRP, and CPNI; p < 0.001) compared to controls. Malnutrition prevalence, defined by CPNI, was significantly higher in the CRC group (54% vs. 10%, p < 0.001). ROC analysis identified CPNI as the most effective diagnostic discriminator (AUC = 0.804), followed by NRI (AUC = 0.756) and PNI (AUC = 0.728). Multivariate analysis revealed that elevated CPNI (OR = 2.339) and PNI (OR = 3.386) were independent indicators of CRC presence. No indices significantly correlated with tumor localization, size, or lymph node involvement, suggesting they reflect systemic status rather than local tumor burden. CPNI, PNI, and NRI demonstrated strong diagnostic performance in distinguishing CRC patients from healthy controls and appear to reflect systemic nutritional and inflammatory status at the time of diagnosis. In contrast, the CONUT score showed limited clinical utility, and none of the indices were associated with tumor size, location, or lymph node metastasis in this cross-sectional cohort. While these markers are effective diagnostic indicators, prospective longitudinal studies are required to validate their actual prognostic value regarding survival and recurrence.Keywords: Cholesterol-modified prognostic nutritional index, Colorectal cancer, Prognostic nutritional index, Nutritional risk index, CONUT scoreSubject terms: Biomarkers, Cancer, Diseases, Gastroenterology, Oncology, Risk factors

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is among the most common and lethal cancers globally, with rising incidence, particularly in developed countries, due to aging populations, poor dietary habits, obesity, and inactivity. Beyond TNM staging and clinicopathological factors, host immune response and tumor microenvironment also play key roles in prognosis, especially in stage 2 and 3 CRC1. Systemic inflammation is a well-established contributor to cancer progression. Markers such as white blood cells, platelets, and liver-derived proteins like albumin reflect this response. While neutrophils, monocytes, and platelets promote tumor growth and metastasis, lymphocytes support anti-tumor immunity. The presence of systemic inflammation is associated with poorer outcomes, as supported by numerous studies in CRC2–7.

Recent studies have highlighted neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), and platelet large cell ratio (P-LCR) as emerging systemic inflammatory markers8,9. Elevated preoperative NLR and PLR levels have been associated with poor prognosis and reduced survival in CRC, as well as in lung and ovarian cancers8–11. Additionally, increased MPV, PLR, and platelet counts have been linked to conditions involving endothelial dysfunction, such as metabolic syndrome, diabetes, coronary artery disease, and malignancies12–14.

Nutritional status is considered a key indicator of overall health, reflecting immune function, protein metabolism, and physical condition15. It plays a critical role in defending against infections. The Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI) is an immunonutritional screening tool based on serum albumin levels and lymphocyte counts16–19. Similarly, the Controlling Nutritional Status (CONUT) score and the Prognostic Nutritional Ratio (PNR) have been developed to assess nutritional and immunological status. These indices have shown strong associations with disease prognosis and reflect the impact of nutritional status on clinical outcomes. The cholesterol-modified prognostic nutritional index (CPNI), initially studied in breast cancer, has shown promise as a simple and effective tool for evaluating nutritional status and predicting survival20,21. Recent evidence also supports the prognostic value of nutritional indices in other gastrointestinal malignancies. For instance, Hou et al.22 demonstrated that prognostic nutritional indices are significantly associated with clinical outcomes in patients with gastric or gastroesophageal junction cancers who receive immune checkpoint inhibitors.

Although the CPNI is a useful prognostic marker in certain cancers, such as breast cancer20,21, its clinical utility in CRC has not yet been evaluated. Additionally, while other immunonutritional indices such as the PNI, CONUT, and PNR have been investigated in various malignancies, comparative data on CRC remain limited. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate and compare the prognostic value of CPNI, PNI, CONUT, and PNR in patients with newly diagnosed, non-metastatic CRC, with a focus on their association with nutritional status, systemic inflammation, and clinical outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study participants

This study was designed as a retrospective case-control study. The study protocol received approval from the Ethics Committee of Istanbul Atlas University Medical Faculty (Approval number: E-22686390-050.99-64879; Date: 28.04.2025). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. An informed consent form was obtained from all the patients. No patient data were accessed, reviewed, or analyzed before the ethical approval date.

A total of 132 consecutive CRC patients admitted to the Istanbul University-Cerrahpasa Medical Faculty, Department of Medical Oncology, between June 2024 and June 2025 were assessed for eligibility. Following the exclusion of 32 patients due to criteria such as non-adenocarcinoma histology, history of secondary malignancies, or missing clinical data, a final cohort of 100 patients was included in the study. The recruitment process is summarized in Fig. 1. As this was a cross-sectional assessment at the time of diagnosis, no long-term follow-up or survival outcomes were recorded. All patients underwent a full colonoscopic examination reaching the cecum. CRC was diagnosed based on the colonoscopic findings and histopathological evaluation of biopsy specimens. Only patients with histologically confirmed adenocarcinoma were included in the analysis. Demographic characteristics, anthropometric data, clinical features, tumor localization, and hematologic parameters were retrieved from medical records. The first blood sample obtained within one week before surgery and before any treatment (surgery, chemotherapy, or radiotherapy) was used for analysis.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves of nutritional indices (PNI, CPNI, NRI, CONUT) for distinguishing colorectal cancer patients from healthy controls.

The control group consisted of 100 healthy individuals attending our outpatient clinic for routine health check-ups. Controls were selected to match the patient group by age and sex distribution. Individuals with chronic diseases, fever, infection, hepatic steatosis, renal dysfunction, gallstones, cholecystitis, or alcohol consumption were excluded. Both groups were composed of individuals of Turkish descent.

Inclusion criteria

(i) Age ≥ 18 years, (ii) Histopathologically confirmed diagnosis of colorectal cancer, (iii) No previous history of other malignancies, (iv) Availability of complete clinical, laboratory, and imaging data, (v) Voluntary participation with informed consent.

Exclusion criteria

(i) Prior history of another cancer, chemotherapy, or radiotherapy, (ii) Presence of renal failure, chronic liver disease, or advanced hepatic steatosis, (iii) Evidence of infection, fever, or inflammatory disease at the time of sampling, (iv) Presence of distant metastasis, (v) Alcohol abuse or use of herbal/alternative therapies, (vi) Severe obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²), (vii) Pregnancy or breastfeeding, (viii) Incomplete clinical or laboratory records. (ix) Patients with secondary diabetes resulting from conditions such as Cushing’s disease, Glucagonoma, Pheochromocytoma, Hyperthyroidism, Somatostatinoma, Hepatogenic diabetes, or drug-induced diabetes.

Patients were stratified into subgroups according to key clinicopathological features.

Lymph node involvement: Patients were classified as node-positive (N+) or node-negative (N) based on pathological examination.

Colon localization: Tumors were categorized as right-sided (cecum, ascending colon, hepatic flexure, and transverse colon) or left-sided (splenic flexure, descending colon, sigmoid colon, and rectum).

Tumor size: Patients were divided into two groups according to maximum tumor diameter: ≤4 cm or > 4 cm.

Demographic characteristics, anthropometric measurements [body mass index (BMI) calculated as weight (kg)/height² (m²)], clinical information, and laboratory data were collected from patient records. Laboratory parameters included hemogram, biochemical markers, lipid profile, inflammatory markers [C-reactive protein (CRP)], and tumor markers [carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) and carbohydrate antigen 19 − 9 (CA 19 − 9)].

Nutritional indices calculated were:

Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI)

10 × serum albumin (g/dL) + 0.005 × total lymphocyte count (/mm³)22.

Nutritional Risk Index (NRI)

1.519 × serum albumin (g/dL) + 41.7 × (current weight / ideal body weight)23.

Ideal body weight (IBW) = [height (m)]2 × 22.

Cholesterol-Modified Prognostic Nutritional Index (CPNI)

4.8 × cholesterol (mmol/L) − 1.5 × albumin (g/L) − 7.7 × total lymphocyte (×10⁹) + 12620,21.

Controlling Nutritional Status (CONUT) score

The CONUT score ranges from 0 to 12, with higher values indicating worse nutritional status. It is calculated based on serum albumin, total lymphocyte count, and total cholesterol, scored as follows.

Serum albumin (g/dL): ≥3.5 = 0 points; 3.49–3.0 = 2 points; 2.99–2.5 = 4 points; <2.5 = 6 points.

Total lymphocyte count (/µL): ≥1600 = 0 points; 1200–1599 = 1 point; 800–1199 = 2 points; <800 = 3 points.

Total cholesterol (mg/dL): ≥180 = 0 points; 140–179 = 1 point; 100–139 = 2 points; <100 = 3 points.

The final CONUT score is obtained by summing the three subscores23. In the present study, the CONUT score was analysed as a continuous variable (range 0–12) and used in its original numerical form in all statistical analyses, including ROC curve and multivariate regression analyses.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using JASP software version 0.19.3 (University of Amsterdam, Amsterdam, The Netherlands). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD and compared using the independent samples t-test, while categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages and compared using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test.

Given the retrospective design of the study, the sample size was determined by the available cohort. A post hoc power analysis was performed using G*Power version 3.1 to evaluate the statistical adequacy of the available sample for group comparisons. Based on a two-tailed t-test comparing means between two independent groups (control group and colorectal cancer patient group), assuming an effect size of d = 0.5 (medium effect), α = 0.05, and power (1-β) = 0.80, the minimum required sample size was estimated to be 196 participants (98 per group). However, this power calculation should be interpreted as descriptive and does not substitute for formal a priori sample size estimation, particularly for multivariable regression analyses.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to evaluate the discriminative (diagnostic) performance of nutritional indices, with area under the curve (AUC), 95% confidence intervals (CI), cut-off values, sensitivity, and specificity reported.Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed including PNI, NRI, CONUT, and CPNI as independent variables. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The baseline demographic and biochemical profiles of the two groups are detailed in Table 1. CRC patients had significantly lower BMI, HDL, total protein, albumin, PNI, and NRI values compared to controls (all p < 0.05). Conversely, serum CA-19.9, CEA, PLT, CRP, total cholesterol, LDL, VLDL, triglycerides, neutrophil count, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), WBC, and CPNI values were significantly higher in CRC patients (all p < 0.001). The prevalence of malnutrition according to CPNI was markedly higher in the CRC group (54%) compared to controls (10%) (p < 0.001). No significant differences were observed for Ideal Body Weight (IBW), fibrinogen, total lymphocyte count, or CONUT score between the groups (p > 0.05).

According to the correlation analysis, a very strong inverse relationship was identified between PNI and CPNI (r = − 0.97, p < 0.001). Furthermore, PNI demonstrated a strong positive correlation with albumin (r = 0.80, p < 0.001), whereas CPNI exhibited a strong negative correlation with albumin (r = − 0.78, p < 0.001). Discussion

The most notable finding of this study is the significant association between nutritional indices, particularly the CPNI, PNI, and NRI, and CRC status. CRC patients exhibited markedly lower BMI, HDL, total protein, albumin, PNI, and NRI values, reflecting a poorer nutritional and immunological status compared to healthy controls, while markers of systemic inflammation and tumor burden, including CA-19.9, CEA, CRP, and various lipid parameters, were significantly elevated. Among the indices evaluated, CPNI demonstrated the highest discriminative ability for identifying CRC patients, outperforming both NRI and PNI, whereas the CONUT score showed no significant predictive value. These findings highlight the consistency of CPNI as a nutritional marker. Importantly, subgroup analyses revealed that nutritional indices did not significantly differ according to lymph node metastasis, tumor location, or tumor size, suggesting that these parameters may primarily reflect systemic nutritional and inflammatory status rather than local tumor characteristics. These results underscore the potential clinical utility of CPNI and PNI as simple, cost-effective tools for early detection and risk stratification in CRC, highlighting the interplay between nutritional status, systemic inflammation, and tumor progression. While previous studies have evaluated individual nutritional indices such as PNI and CONUT in CRC, our study systematically compares multiple indices, including the PNI, NRI, and CONUT, in a single cohort10–13. We demonstrate that CPNI exhibits superior discriminative power for identifying CRC patients compared to other indices. These findings highlight the potential of CPNI as a simple, cost-effective, and clinically accessible tool for early detection, risk stratification, and prognostic assessment, representing a meaningful clinical advancement over existing approaches.

PNI reflects both nutritional and immune status and has been shown to correlate with systemic inflammation in CRC patients. Yamamoto et al.5 conducted a review summarizing inflammation-related biomarkers for CRC prognosis. Inflammation-related biomarkers, such as CRP and NLR, are closely associated with CRC prognosis, suggesting that combining inflammatory and nutritional indices may improve risk stratification. Consistently, our study found that CRC patients had significantly lower PNI values alongside elevated CRP and NLR levels, highlighting the interconnection between nutritional status and systemic inflammation in CRC. Thus, numerous papers have proven that NLR can be used as a significant predictor for the survival of CRC patients, although the characteristics of the patients enrolled and the optimal cutoff values of NLR varied from report to report5.

In line with previous studies, our findings highlight the prognostic and diagnostic significance of nutritional and immunological indices in CRC. CRC patients exhibited significantly lower PNI, NRI, and CPNI values, reflecting impaired nutritional and immune status. Notably, CPNI demonstrated the highest discriminative ability for distinguishing CRC patients from controls, while the CONUT score showed no significant predictive value. The limited discriminatory ability of the CONUT score may be due to its reliance on serum albumin, total cholesterol, and total lymphocyte counts, which may not fully capture the complex nutritional and immunological alterations in CRC patients, as well as the relatively homogeneous patient population and unmeasured factors such as tumor stage and baseline nutritional status. These results are consistent with Keskinkılıç et al.24, who reported that lower PNI values in metastatic CRC were associated with poorer prognosis and advanced disease stage. Separately, Kim et al.25 demonstrated that combining PNI and CONUT scores provided valuable prognostic information for overall survival in stage I–III CRC patients, emphasizing the complementary utility of multiple nutritional indices. Collectively, these findings support the use of PNI and CPNI as simple, cost-effective tools for early detection, risk stratification, and prognostic assessment in CRC.

Tokunaga et al.26 investigated 468 CRC patients who underwent curative surgery and found that PNI was an independent predictor of overall survival (OS) and recurrence-free survival. They also reported that combining PNI with TNM staging provided greater sensitivity than using the modified Glasgow Prognostic Score (mGPS) alone, applying a PNI cutoff value of 45. Similarly, Mohri et al.27 evaluated 365 CRC patients after curative resection and demonstrated that a PNI below 45 was independently associated with poorer OS. In contrast, Tominaga et al.28 determined an optimal PNI cutoff of 42.4 using ROC curve analysis in a cohort of 84 elderly patients (≥ 85 years) and confirmed that lower PNI values were independently linked to both reduced OS and recurrence-free survival (RFS). Liu et al.29 evaluated the prognostic significance of the preoperative CONUT score in CRC patients and demonstrated that it serves as an effective nutritional index for predicting postoperative outcomes. Their study suggests that assessing nutritional status before surgery can provide valuable prognostic information and guide perioperative management. Collectively, these results underscore the clinical significance of evaluating nutritional and immune status in CRC, and our study corroborates these observations by demonstrating significantly lower PNI, NRI, and CPNI levels in CRC patients compared to controls.

In current study, ROC analysis supported the baseline discriminative utility of the evaluated immunonutritional indices for distinguishing CRC patients from healthy controls, with CPNI showing the strongest overall discrimination, and NRI and PNI also demonstrated significant, albeit comparatively lower, performance; in contrast, CONUT did not provide meaningful discrimination in this cohort. This divergence between CPNI and CONUT despite partial overlap in constituent parameters likely reflects structural differences in index construction: CONUT is a threshold-based, ordinal score that can compress variability and produce score clustering in relatively homogeneous samples, whereas CPNI is a continuous, formula-based index that preserves the full dynamic range of laboratory measures and may be more sensitive to subtle cancer-associated nutritional–metabolic alterations. Accordingly, CONUT’s limited performance here should be interpreted as a cohort- and structure-dependent finding rather than definitive evidence of lack of utility, and prospective studies are warranted to confirm these observations and define practical cutoffs. The CPNI in breast cancer patients demonstrated that incorporating cholesterol into the traditional PNI improved its ability to assess nutritional status and predict survival outcomes. Their findings suggest that CPNI provides a more comprehensive evaluation by reflecting both protein-energy malnutrition and lipid metabolism, which are crucial in cancer progression. Furthermore, time-dependent ROC analysis revealed that CPNI has greater predictive capability than the previously mentioned nutritional indicators20. Similarly, our study suggests that CPNI could be a valuable tool for early detection, risk stratification, and prognostic assessment of CRC, as CRC patients exhibited significantly altered CPNI values compared to controls. Chen et al.21 evaluated the CPNI in patients undergoing liver resection for hepatocellular carcinoma and demonstrated that CPNI effectively reflected nutritional status and predicted postoperative survival. Their results highlight that incorporating cholesterol into PNI enhances the index’s ability to capture both protein-energy malnutrition and lipid-related metabolic changes, which are critical for cancer prognosis. Zhu et al.30 investigated the CPNI in patients with primary biliary cholangitis and found that CPNI served as an independent prognostic biomarker, effectively reflecting nutritional status and predicting disease outcomes. Their findings support the broader utility of incorporating cholesterol into traditional PNI to enhance prognostic accuracy across different disease contexts. Consistently, our study suggests that CPNI may also be valuable in colorectal cancer, as CRC patients showed significantly altered CPNI values compared to controls, highlighting its potential role in early detection, risk stratification, and prognostic assessment. Our findings align with recent evidence highlighting the increasing integration of nutritional care within oncology practice. Consistent with this survey, we observe that while nutritional assessment and the involvement of nutrition specialists are improving, gaps remain, particularly regarding standardization and accessibility. Our results reinforce the need for targeted educational initiatives and the development of consensus-based guidelines to optimize nutritional care, enhance multidisciplinary collaboration, and ultimately improve patient outcomes, including survival, treatment tolerance, and quality of life.

Interpretation of nutritional status

It should be emphasized that PNI, NRI, CONUT and CPNI are not direct measures of dietary intake or body composition; rather, they are immunonutritional surrogates derived from routine laboratory parameters that integrate protein-energy reserves (albumin/total protein), immune status (lymphocyte-related components), and in the case of CPNI lipid metabolism (cholesterol). Accordingly, these indices capture a combined nutritional–inflammatory phenotype. In our cohort, the differences were accompanied by significantly lower BMI, albumin and total protein in CRC patients, and malnutrition prevalence defined by CPNI-based criteria was substantially higher in the CRC group (54% vs. 10%), suggesting that the observed values likely reflect a clinically relevant malnutrition-risk state at diagnosis. Nevertheless, albumin and cholesterol are negative acute-phase reactants and lymphocyte-related parameters are influenced by systemic inflammation; therefore, part of the discriminatory signal may reflect inflammatory burden in addition to nutritional depletion. For this reason, we interpret these indices primarily as pragmatic screening tools for identifying patients at risk of nutritional depletion and heightened systemic inflammatory stress, which may warrant comprehensive nutritional assessment and early supportive care.

Strengths & limitations

This study provides preliminary evidence supporting the diagnostic utility of CPNI and other immunonutritional indices in CRC. Notable strengths include the inclusion of both CRC patients and healthy controls, comprehensive clinical and laboratory characterization, histologically confirmed adenocarcinoma, and preoperative blood sampling, which minimized treatment-related bias. Nevertheless, several limitations should be considered. First, the single-center, retrospective case–control design and relatively small sample size may limit external validity and increase the risk of selection bias. Second, because the data were collected cross-sectionally, the indices were evaluated for baseline discrimination rather than prognostic performance, and causal inferences cannot be made. Although age- and sex-matching was applied and major comorbidities were excluded, residual confounding may persist due to incomplete adjustment for key factors such as tumor stage, baseline nutritional status, and lifestyle-related variables (e.g., dietary intake and physical activity). Importantly, comprehensive nutritional assessment tools (e.g., PG-SGA or GLIM-based phenotypic criteria), dietary intake records, and objective body composition measures were not available; therefore, we could not validate these indices against a gold-standard nutritional diagnosis. Accordingly, PNI, NRI, CONUT, and CPNI should be interpreted as surrogate markers reflecting the nutritional–inflammatory phenotype, rather than definitive measures of malnutrition. These constraints may also reduce generalizability, particularly to older or comorbid populations in whom malnutrition and lipid abnormalities are more prevalent. Future multicenter, prospective longitudinal studies incorporating standardized nutritional assessment and comprehensive adjustment for clinical, tumor-related, metabolic, and lifestyle covariates are warranted to validate these findings and clarify their prognostic implications.

In conclusion, this cross-sectional case–control study shows that immunonutritional indices particularly PNI, NRI, and CPNI differ significantly between patients with CRC and healthy controls, with CPNI demonstrating the highest discriminative performance in ROC analysis. It should be emphasized that, in this cross-sectional case–control comparison of CRC patients versus healthy controls, the ROC results primarily reflect within-cohort group differentiation and should not be interpreted as established clinical diagnostic or prognostic utility without prospective validation in representative populations. These indices likely reflect a combined systemic nutritional–inflammatory phenotype at the time of diagnosis rather than local tumor characteristics. While our findings support the potential utility of these readily available measures as adjunctive indicators in the baseline clinical assessment of CRC patients, their roles in risk stratification and prognosis cannot be established from the present design. Therefore, well-designed prospective, multicenter longitudinal studies incorporating standardized nutritional assessment and time-to-event outcomes are required to validate these observations, determine incremental value beyond conventional parameters, and define clinically applicable thresholds.
