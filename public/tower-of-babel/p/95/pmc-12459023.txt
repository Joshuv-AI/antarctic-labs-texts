Mediating roles of family functioning and self-efficacy in the relationship between depression and family resilience among kidney dialysis patients
Authors: Yuru Wang, Yanwei Yang, Qing Zeng, Fangqing Liu, Yiting Wang, Hongchen Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKidney dialysis patients often experience intensified depression after prolonged maintenance dialysis, which may diminish family resilience. Conversely, family functioning and self-efficacy might bolster resilience by serving as crucial internal resources. The mechanisms through which depression impact family resilience via these resources remain unclear.MethodsThe present study used a cross-sectional design and included 211 kidney dialysis patients. The study was conducted in Lanzhou, China. The participants were selected through convenience sampling from September 2023 to January 2024. General Information Questionnaire, Depression subscale of the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), Family Resilience Rating Scale (FRRS), Family APGAR Index (APGAR) and Simplified Coping Style Questionnaire (GSES), were used for data collection. Independent-sample t-tests and one-way ANOVA determined the influential factors using SPSS 27.0. Structural equation modeling, executed with AMOS 24.0 software, validated the mediating effects of family functioning and self-efficacy between depression and family resilience.ResultsFamily resilience levels among kidney dialysis patients differed significantly by education level, marital status and per capita monthly income. Depression were inversely associated with family resilience. Family functioning (β = 0.22, p < 0.01) and self-efficacy (β = 0.04, p < 0.01) mediated the relationship between depression and family resilience, respectively.ConclusionsThis study provides further insights into the psychological mechanism of depression and family resilience in kidney dialysis patients. Caregivers can actively help kidney dialysis patients to reduce depression and improve the family functioning, so as to enhance the family resilience.Keywords: Renal dialysis, Depression, Family functioning, Self-efficacy, Family resilience, Mediating effect

Background

Kidney disease is a significant health problem worldwide, affecting an estimated 10% of the global population [1]. Recent data indicate that the incidence of kidney failure is on the rise in China, with a predicted prevalence of 629.67 per million population in 2025 [2]. Patients with kidney failure depend on kidney replacement therapies such as kidney transplantation and kidney dialysis—the latter being the more common form, encompassing hemodialysis and peritoneal dialysis. In China, due to the inequality of distribution of medical resources and economic constraints, most peritoneal dialysis patients will undergo dialysis treatment in hospitals in order to ensure the safety and efficacy of the treatment. Approximately 3.9 million patients with kidney failure worldwide receive kidney replacement therapy, kidney transplantation, or various forms of dialysis, and the numbers continue to rise [3].

Kidney dialysis patients are highly susceptible to psychological distress due to repeated punctures, fluid and dietary restrictions, and dialysis complications during frequent dialysis treatments, with prevalence rates of depression as high as 50.4% [4]. Studies have shown that kidney dialysis patients are tend to suffer from depression, stress, anxiety, irritability, impatience, aggression, fatigue and inattention [5]. These patients face enormous challenges within their family units, as they have limited self-care capabilities and depend extensively on families for daily living support, including dietary management, medication adherence, and transport to healthcare facilities [6, 7]. Family is the best resource for kidney dialysis patients, and family resilience plays a critical role in coping with these psychological challenges [5, 8].

Poston et al. define family is a group of persons, whether by marriage, blood, adoption or other intimate relationship, the members of which live together and have responsibilities for mutual support and upbringing [9]. Family resilience means that when family members are in a difficult situation, the family as a whole faces and resists the situation together and helps the family members as a whole to detach themselves from the stressful or dangerous situation and to restore them to their normal condition or to maintain the function of the family as a whole [10]. According to Family Resilience Theory, the continued buildup of depression emotions as stressors can overwhelm families [11]. Studies have found that depression is negatively associated with family resilience in kidney dialysis patients [12]. Family resilience and mental health are causally linked and predictive of each other, and depression emotions are not merely detrimental; they can also serve as protective factors by preparing families to face future challenges [13]. Depression in kidney dialysis patients affects family resilience by increasing caregiving stress among family members. One study found that caregivers of kidney dialysis patients with depression experienced anxiety in 41.1% of cases, significantly higher than families of non-depressed patients [14]. This mechanism of emotional transmission may lead to the depletion of psychological resources of core family members, which in turn weakens the family’s ability to cope with crisis.

Family functioning is a multidimensional construct encompassing the efficacy of emotional connections, communication, regulation of family relationships, and collective responses to external events, all posited to influence family resilience [15]. Previous studies have highlighted the high prevalence of depression among kidney dialysis patients [16]. Family members of kidney dialysis patients have a worse quality of life than other caregivers [17]. It is found that good family functioning can help families to cope with stress [18]. Optimal family functioning facilitates emotional support, resource sharing, and problem-solving among members, enhancing cooperation and support during adversities, thus bolstering family resilience [18]. Positive family support promotes patients to take self-care behaviors such as medication adherence and physical activity [19]. Furthermore, it also promotes effective communication, problem-solving, which undoubtedly has a positive effect on family members’ physical and mental health, and improves family resilience [20]. Thus, kidney dialysis patients’ depression may reduce their family resilience levels by lowering family functioning.

Self-efficacy, defined as an individual’s confidence in their capability to achieve goals within a specific domain [21], is crucial in this context. Study have confirmed significant correlation between self-efficacy and family resilience [22]. Research indicates that depression in patients negatively predict self-efficacy, with heightened anxiety and depression correlating with reduced self-efficacy [23]. A protective factor for resilience is self-efficacy, and self-efficacy reduces the adverse effects of illness and increases resilience [24]. Zhang et al. revealed that self-efficacy is a key predictor of family resilience maintenance [25]. Individuals with high self-efficacy are more effective at responding to stressful events through goal setting, resource mobilization, and emotional regulation, thereby increasing family resilience [21, 26]. According to Family Resilience Theory [17], family members with high levels of self-efficacy have more creative brainstorming and resourcefulness to overcome adversity. As a result, the depression may influence Kidney dialysis patients’ family resilience through self-efficacy.

Further, studies suggest that Patients with well-functioning families are better able to draw energy from their families and have greater confidence in their ability to manage themselves [27, 28]. It is found a significant negative correlation between family functioning and self-efficacy, meaning that the healthier a family functioning is, the higher its members’ overall self-efficacy is [27]. From a social learning perspective, Bandura identified the family as a pivotal source of self-efficacy modeling. Individuals are more likely to develop a higher sense of self-efficacy when the family environment is supportive, communicative and emotionally warm [29]. Depression, serving as psychological stimuli, can impair family functioning and significantly weaken self-efficacy, reducing family resilience. However, the evidence linking depression to family resilience in kidney dialysis patients needs further research. Therefore, this study aims to explore the potential mechanisms underlying family resilience by examining the relationships between depression, family functioning and self-efficacy. Although the association between depression and family resilience has been examined, the mechanisms behind the association still remain largely unknown. In particular, it is unclear whether depression affects family functioning and self-efficacy, and whether family functioning and self-efficacy have a mediating role in the relationship between depression and family resilience. Consequently, there remains a need to analyze the mediating effect among kidney dialysis patients. This study will help us to understand the mechanism by which depression leads to less family resilience among kidney dialysis patients, and to develop effective interventions.

The ABC-X model of family stress and adaptation provides a theoretical basis for this study [30], which consists of four basic determinants, namely, A, B, C, and X. Factor A represents the stressor, which refers to any internal or external environmental stimulus event that can cause a stressful reaction; Factor B represents the resources, which include personal, social and family resources; Factor C represents the individual’s subjective cognition of the stressful event; and Factor X represents the consequences caused by the stress. The above four factors interact with each other. In this study, we explored the effect of depression as a stressor (Factor A) on family resilience (Factor X) among kidney dialysis patients according to ABC-X model. We also examined the mediating role of family functioning (Factor B) and self-efficacy (Factor C) in the relationship between family resilience. Methods

Design and sample

This research involved a cross-sectional study conducted using a face-to-face self-administered paper-and-pencil test and followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline [31]. The convenience sampling method was used to select 234 kidney dialysis patients from the nephrology departments of two tertiary-level hospitals in Lanzhou City, China. The study was conducted over a period from September 2023 to January 2024. The inclusion criteria were: (1) patients diagnosed with chronic kidney disease undergoing maintenance hemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis in hospitals; (2) patients aged 18 years and older; (3) patients who provided informed consent and voluntarily participated in the study; (4) patients without serious dysfunction of other organs; (5) patients without mental illness. The exclusion criteria was patients in other clinical trials during this study.

Measures

Demographic data were collected using a twelve-item self-report questionnaire covering gender, age, ethnicity, religious beliefs, education level, profession, marital status, residency status, residence, medical expense payments, post-hospitalization care, per capita monthly income, dialysis age and types of kidney dialysis.

Hospital anxiety and depression scale (HADS)

The degree of depression was assessed using the Depression Subscale (HADS) of the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), adapted by Yeh Wei-Fei [32] from the original scale by Zigmond and Snaith. The subscale consists of seven items (e.g., “I have lost interest in my appearance (grooming myself)”) rated on a 4-point Likert scale from 0 (“not at all”) to 3 (“absolutely the same”). The subscale is scored on a scale ranging from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating more severe symptoms. Scores are categorized as follows: 0–7 indicates no symptoms, 8–10 indicates possible or severe depression, and 11–21 indicates marked depression. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for this scale is 0.803. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the depression subscale in this study was 0.826.

Family resilience rating scale (FRRS)

Family resilience was measured using the Family Resilience Rating Scale (FRRS) developed by Yan Dai [33]. This scale consists of 49 items across two subscales: Family Beliefs and Family Strengths, encompassing ten factors including Dilemma Interpretation (7 items, e.g., “My family believes there’s always a way to deal with adversity and solve problems.”), Positive Foresight (6 items, e.g., “Being alive is more important than everything else.”), Life Excellence (4 items, e.g., “My family likes to be close to nature.”), Problem Solving (6 items, e.g., “My family tries different ways to solve problems”), Intimate Harmony (4 items, e.g., “Family members are close to each other”), Social Support (4 items, e.g., “Being able to discuss our family’s challenges with relatives or friends”), Orderliness (3 items, e.g., “In my family, there’s a clear division of labor for everyone.”), Emotional Sharing (4 items, e.g., “Family members regularly and openly express their feelings for each other”), Clear Communication (5 items, e.g., “Even if you don’t agree, you still have the opportunity to express your opinion in my home.”), and Collaborative Coordination (6 items, e.g., “Each member of the family is involved in major family decisions”). It employs a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (“non-compliant”) to 5 (“compliant”), with total possible scores ranging from 0 to 245. Higher scores indicate greater family resilience. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the total scale was 0.912, and the Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of the subscales are 0.538–0.856. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the total scale is 0.929, and the subscale Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were 0.842–0.899.

Family APGAR index (APGAR)

The Adaptation, Partnership, Growth, Affection, and Resolve (APGAR) scale, revised by Smilkstein [34] and translated into Chinese by Zhang [35], is used to assess patients’ family functioning. This scale encompasses five dimensions: adaptability (e.g., “I can get satisfactory help from my family when I am in trouble”), cooperation (e.g., “I’m happy with the way my family discusses things and shares problems with me”), growth (e.g., “My family is accepting and supportive when I want to engage in new activities or developments”), affection (e.g., “I am satisfied with the way my family shows care and love for my emotions (likes and dislikes)”), and resolve (e.g., “I’m happy with the way my family spends time with me”), each evaluated through one item. It employs a 3-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“almost seldom like this”) to 2 (“often like this”), with total scores varying from 0 to 10. Higher scores indicate better family functioning. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the scale was 0.894, and the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the scale was 0.859 in this study.

Simplified coping style questionnaire (GSES)

The Simplified Coping Style Questionnaire (GSES), localized and revised by Wang [36], measures patients’ self-efficacy when facing challenges. This unidimensional scale includes ten items (e.g., “I’m confident that I can effectively deal with the unexpected.”) scored on a 4-point Likert scale from 1 (“completely incorrect”) to 4 (“completely correct”), and total scores range from 0 to 40. Higher scores reflect greater self-efficacy. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the scale was 0.870, and the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the scale was 0.943 in this study.

Data collection

Data collection was completed face-to-face on-site within two tertiary care hospitals in Lanzhou City, China. Two researchers were trained before the survey, covering the harmonized understanding of scale items, principles of informed consent, and verbal communication skills. Before the investigation, the researcher first explained the purpose, significance of the study in detail to the patients, emphasizing their voluntary participation and right to withdraw from the study at any time. Standardized instructions were used to assist patients in completing the questionnaires. For participants who had questions or those who had difficulty reading, the researchers provided on-site explanations or readings. After completion, the questionnaires were collected on the spot and checked for missing items. If any omissions or duplicates were found, patients were asked to reconsider and complete the questionnaire again. Completed questionnaires were placed into envelopes, sealed and collected. A total of 211 questionnaires were collected between September 2023 and January 2024 with a response rate of 90.2%.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS 27.0 and AMOS 24.0 software. Descriptive statistics (frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation) were used to summarize general information and scores for family functioning, self-efficacy, depression, and family resilience. The effects of different demographic characteristics on family resilience were analyzed using an independent-sample t-test or a one-way ANOVA. The normality test was performed using skewness and kurtosis values. The variable could be regarded as normally distributed with a skewness value ≤ 2 or a kurtosis value ≤ 4 [37]. Pearson correlation analysis was utilized to examine the relationships between depression, family resilience, family functioning, and self-efficacy. Structural equation modeling, executed in AMOS 24.0, assessed the mediating roles of family functioning and self-efficacy. The mediation effects were tested using the Bootstrap method, setting up 5,000 repetitive samples with a 95% confidence interval. The significance level was set at α = 0.05. The following criteria were used to evaluate the model: χ2/df < 5, RMSEA < 0.08, RFI > 0.9, NFI > 0.9, CFI > 0.9, TLI > 0.9 and IFI > 0.9. The significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the College of Nursing at Lanzhou University, with the grant number LZUHLXY20230027. Before the survey, we obtained oral and written informed consent from the patients. All questionnaires were submitted anonymously with an emphasis on confidentiality, and the data collected were kept strictly confidential for research purposes only, accessible only to research team and not disclosed to others. Participants were allowed to withdraw from the study at any time without any consequences.

Discussion

It was observed that family resilience varies significantly among patients in different demographic contexts, consistent with prior research [40]. Initially, the patients’ educational level affects how he or she perceives the meaning, causes, and possible outcomes of family adversity, and different ways of interpreting this can significantly influence the family’s attitudes and approaches to coping with adversity, which in turn affects the family’s level of resilience [41]. Liu et al. found that differences in education levels create cognitive differences that allow families to respond to treatment with less emotional conflict and stronger family resilience [42]. Next, the higher the per capita monthly income of kidney dialysis patients, the higher the level of family resilience. Yang et al. found that low-income families are financially burdened, which can hinder the family’s recovery process and worsen the challenge of family burdens [12]. Family stress theory emphasizes that financial burdens families face over a long period can be a significant source of stress that poses a serious threat to the stability of family functioning, undermining the family’s ability to recover from crisis events [43]. Eventually, a stable marital status was a protective factor for family resilience, and the quality of the spousal relationship plays a vital role in sustaining harmonious family dynamics, as in previous studies [44]. Married patients significantly improved family coping efficacy through spousal emotional support [45].

Several noteworthy phenomena are thus revealed. The study showed that the family resilience of kidney dialysis patients was higher than that reported in previous studies [5], which may be related to the educational level of the participants, with 23.7% of them having a college education or higher. Families with higher educational attainment had stronger problem-solving and decision-making skills, more social and cultural resources, and greater family resilience. Interestingly, the prevalence of depression was lower than previous findings [46], which may relate to the lower proportion of female participants (39.8% versus 51.2% in earlier studies). This difference suggests gender-specific psychological characteristics, with women being more prone to mood fluctuations and health consciousness, whereas men show lower depression risks, likely due to lesser domestic and child-rearing pressures [47].

The study validates that family functioning and self-efficacy mediate the relationship between depression and family resilience in kidney dialysis patients. However, it also reveals that the hypothesis 3 is not statistically significant in structural equation modeling. The development of self-efficacy in these patients depends more on individual adaptation to treatment and professional healthcare guidance rather than on immediate impacts from family functioning.

The results of this study affirm that depression in kidney dialysis patients directly diminish family resilience, corroborating previous findings where higher levels of depression were associated with reduced resilience [48]. Negative cognitive patterns due to depression in kidney dialysis patients significantly reduce family communication. This often leads to poor communication within families, diminished mutual understanding and trust, and reduced social support, all of which contribute to decreased family resilience [49]. Depression in kidney dialysis patients impairs their ability to fulfill family roles, thereby reducing the family resilience in coping with adversity [50].

Hypothesis 1, which states that family functioning mediates the relationship between depression and family resilience, was confirmed in this study. Initially, depression may increase the patients’ family support needs, but at the same time, it may reduce their ability to actively participate in family activities, which weakens family functioning [51]. Second, the development of family functioning is significantly influenced by emotional factors [52], which entails the family’s ability to adapt and provide support amid the patients’ ongoing health challenges. Third, good family functioning with strong cooperation enables members to support one another during hardships, as well as to have more emotional interaction, fostering a closer family environment and enhancing resilience [53]. These findings are consistent with previous research that the mediating effect of family functioning is stronger in family-oriented cultural contexts [54]. Thus, depression in kidney dialysis patients may impair family functioning, resulting in diminished household capacity to maintain stability during crises.

Furthermore, hypothesis 2 suggest that self-efficacy mediates the relationship between depression and family resilience. Emotional states are critical in shaping self-efficacy. Depression can foster negative thoughts and diminish self-efficacy. Specifically, kidney dialysis patients may experience frustration, anxiety, and depression due to the rigorous treatment regimen and declining quality of life, which can lead to doubts about their ability to manage their condition effectively. Kidney dialysis patients are prone to attribute treatment setbacks to their own incapacity, and this can lower their self-confidence, which may adversely affect self-efficacy, leading to diminished energy and social avoidance. Consequently, it weakens the patients’ confidence in contributing to family roles, thereby reducing the overall resilience of the family [26]. Yin et al. found that the self-efficacy of chronic cancer patients has a significant relationship with their family resilience [55]. Zhang et al. found that self-efficacy has a significant effect on the family resilience of chronic stroke patients [26]. Reducing depression in kidney dialysis patients and increasing their self-efficacy can help their families adapt to stress.

Strengths and limitations

This study explored the mechanisms by which depression influenced family resilience among kidney dialysis patients, and we provided evidence that the negative effect of depression on kidney dialysis patients’ family resilience might be reduced by improving family functioning and self-efficacy. Meanwhile, the study has some limitations. First, the results’ generalizability was limited by the convenience sample that was selected from a single province, suggesting the necessity for multicenter, large sample studies in the future. Second, the causal relationships between the study variables could not be confirmed by this study, and longitudinal studies are needed to expand our knowledge about this association. Finally, other factors might also have an impact on the family resilience of kidney dialysis patients; further research incorporating a wider range of variables will be required to thoroughly understand the mechanisms underlying kidney dialysis patients’ family resilience.

Implication

Our findings suggest that depression influences family resilience among kidney dialysis patients through a psychological mechanism. Therefore, interventions can be provided from the following aspects to improve the family resilience. First, healthcare providers can use cognitive behavioral therapy, mindfulness therapy, sensory art therapies and other psychotherapeutic methods to reduce depressive symptoms [56–59]. Second, healthcare providers can help family members build a supportive relationship with the patients, assisting the patients to focus on current life, and expanding health education to cover the entire family system. Third, patients’ self-management programs should be developed with the participation of family members and supported by a systemic approach to enhance their self-management behaviors and self-efficacy. Fourth, incorporate family functioning and self-efficacy assessments into the routine care processes of kidney dialysis patients (e.g., monthly screenings) in order to identify high-risk families early.

Conclusions

This study indicates that family resilience among kidney dialysis patients is moderately low, influenced by factors such as education level, marital status and per capita income. Healthcare professionals should develop positive coping strategies for patients with low education, provide spiritual care and psychological support to divorced and widowed patients [60], offer tailored treatment plans for low-income patients to alleviate financial stress, and enhancing understanding of the disease [61] to mitigate patients’ guilt and emotional burden [62, 63]. Additionally, depression are inversely related to family resilience, with family functioning and self-efficacy serving as partial mediators in this relationship. Caregivers must prioritize interventions that enhance family functioning and self-efficacy to reduce depression and improve family resilience in kidney dialysis patients.
