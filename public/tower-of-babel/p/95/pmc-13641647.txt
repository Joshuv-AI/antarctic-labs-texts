Clinical Validation of AI-Assisted Evaluation of Indeterminate Biliary Strictures in Digital-Single Operator Cholangioscopy: A Transcontinental Multicentric Study

ABSTRACT
INTRODUCTION:Biliary strictures (BS) are a significant challenge, with malignant strictures frequently diagnosed at advanced stages, limiting curative options. Digital single-operator cholangioscopy (D-SOC) enables high-resolution, direct visualization of the bile duct, yet with suboptimal accuracy. Artificial intelligence (AI) has shown promise for detection and differentiation of BS in frame-level analysis and small clinical series. This study aimed to validate a deep learning model for AI-assisted D-SOC image analysis.METHODS:This multicenter study included 135 D-SOC examinations from 129 patients (61 with malignant BS) across 14 centers in the United States, Brazil, Spain, Colombia, Australia, and Saudi Arabia. For each examination, up to 25 clinically relevant frames were selected and uploaded to a web-based platform for AI analysis. The model performed both detection and differentiation of BS: Detection was assessed by comparing AI-generated bounding boxes with expert-defined annotations using intersection-over-union (IoU), while differentiation was benchmarked against histopathology. Performance metrics included accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and positive and negative predictive values.RESULTS:At the patient level, malignant BS were identified with 86.0% accuracy, 84.1% sensitivity, and 85.7% specificity, with an area under the curve of 0.904. The model demonstrated robust detection performance, achieving a mean IoU of 70.3%. Performance was maintained across demographic variables and centers.DISCUSSION:This first multicentric validation study demonstrates real-world performance of AI-assisted D-SOC analysis across multiples continents and devices, with robust accuracy for BS detection and differentiation. These findings support AI as an adjunctive tool in D-SOC, enhancing a more accurate evaluation of patients with indeterminate BS.

INTRODUCTION

Biliary strictures (BSs) are a concerning medical condition because most are malignant and associated with a poor prognosis. Correct diagnosis is crucial because the treatment and prognosis of benign and malignant BSs differ significantly (1,2). Therefore, definitive exclusion of malignancy is crucial in the presence of BSs.

Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) is the classic examination modality for this purpose. However, the presence of malignancy is only evaluated through indirect signs (surface irregularity and stricture length), complemented by tissue sampling. However, the sensitivity of ERCP and its associated conventional ancillary techniques (particularly, brush cytology) is low, frequently resulting in frequent false-negative results for malignancy.

Given the above, the development of digital single-operator cholangioscopy (D-SOC) has enabled high-resolution direct visualization of the bile duct for both diagnostic and therapeutic purposes, with performance of targeted biopsies. Nevertheless, despite the increased diagnostic accuracy of BSs when compared with ERCP, the specificity of visual impression in D-SOC remains suboptimal (89%) (3).

In this context, Smart Cholangio (DigestAID, Porto, Portugal) was developed to provide noninvasive, deep learning-driven assistance in the evaluation of malignancy risk in indeterminate BS by automatically differentiating between benign and malignant findings in D-SOC images. In recent years, several studies have been published demonstrating the potential of artificial intelligence (AI) tools for the assessment of biliary disease, particularly BS (4–6). However, to this date, there is a lack of clinical validation studies assessing the clinical value of these technologies. This study aimed to clinically validate a deep learning model for D-SOC image analysis.

METHODS

Study design

This study adopted a multicenter, retrospective, observational design. Patients older than 18 years of male and female sex who underwent D-SOC for suspected biliary disease were included in the study. The D-SOC examinations were performed between January 2017 and September 2025. Patients with BSs were excluded if there was an absence of ground truth (GT) histopathological analysis.

D-SOC procedure and ground truth definition

D-SOC examinations included in the study were performed with the SpyGlass DS Direct Visualization System (Boston Scientific Corp., Marlborough, MA), SpyGlass DS II system (Boston Scientific), and the EyeMax system (Micro-Tech, Nanjing, China). The procedures were performed by expert gastroenterologists, each with prior experience of more than 1,000 ERCPs and 150 cholangioscopies. The examinations were performed using the Olympus TJF-160V or TFJ-Q180V duodenoscopes (Olympus Medical Systems, Tokyo, Japan) and Fujifilm ED-580XT (Fujifilm Corp., Tokyo, Japan) with biopsy specimens obtained using the SpyBite forceps (Boston Scientific Corp) and the EyeMax biopsy forceps (Micro-Tech, Nanjing, China), under direct visual guidance, ensuring a minimum of 4 biopsies in all the study examinations.

For each case, the GT for the classification module was defined by final histopathological results, obtained either from tissue sampling techniques (D-SOC-guided biopsy, fluoroscopy-guided biopsy, brush cytology, or endoscopic ultrasound [EUS]-guided biopsy) or from surgical specimens. Patients with BS were excluded in the absence of conclusive histopathological analysis. In cases of suspected benign BS, a benign course was confirmed if no histopathological evidence of malignancy was present and clinical follow-up of at least 6 months showed no malignancy. An exception was made for typical postliver transplant anastomotic strictures, where temporal correlation with transplantation and a minimum 6-month follow-up sufficed—provided that liver transplants because of hepatocellular carcinoma or cholangiocarcinoma still required exclusion of histopathological evidence of malignancy.

Deep learning software development

Smart Cholangio model development process has been described elsewhere (7). Briefly, Smart Cholangio is based on a deep neural network with a transformer-based backbone (ResNet model). The training and tuning process for the model was executed using the PyTorch Lightning framework. To enhance the model's robustness and mitigate overfitting, a range of augmentation techniques, including rotation, flipping, scaling, and color adjustment, are applied by chance. The dataset was partitioned into training and validation datasets (80% and 20%, respectively) and an independent final holdout test set.

Clinical validation Study Design

Digital cholangioscopy examinations performed at 14 participating centers were included in this study (Figure 1). The examinations were performed by gastroenterologists with expertise in D-SOC from each center. All procedures follow a harmonized protocol, and all examinations were processed using a consistent data management flow to ensure methodological integrity, in accordance with the latest European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy guidelines (5). All examinations have been previously performed. The histological result served as the GT for the classification module, supported by a granted minimum follow-up period of 6 months. The study was conducted using clinically relevant still frames, as judged by the D-SOC expert performing the procedure at each center. After image collection, the study was conducted in 2 stages. On the first stage, all images selected by the endoscopist are marked with a bounding box, identifying the location corresponding to at least part of the lesion, and are attributed the classification of “malignant” or “benign,” according to the GT classification. This labeling process was conducted in a web platform (Encord, Cord Technologies Inc., London, United Kingdom). The position of this bounding box was validated by a second D-SOC expert, from an independent center, blinded to patient information and histopathology reports; in case of disagreement, a third expert from another independent center was responsible for the final decision.

Smart Cholangio clinical validation study design. D-SOC, digital single operator cholangioscopy.

In a second phase, the anonymized and unannotated D-SOC images were analyzed by Smart Cholangio after upload on a web app platform by clinicians. The Smart Cholangio system generated a report for each D-SOC examination, highlighting the identified finding of BS with a bounding box and their suggestive evaluation (classified as benign or malignant).

For the classification module, performance metrics were established both at per-image and per-examination analyses. In the per-image analysis, Smart Cholangio’s prediction for each frame was compared with the GT classification. For BS, the GT relied on the definitive histopathological diagnosis of a BS. At a per-examination level, the attribution to either of the 2 classes depended on the classification of >50% of submitted frames as belonging to that class.

For the detection module, the intersection-over-union (IoU) between Smart Cholangio’s and expert-defined bounding boxes was calculated and used as a surrogate for the accuracy of spatial localization. This analysis was performed at a frame level.

This study was conducted in a noninterventional fashion, and the patient management followed the standard clinical protocols of each center. Each endoscopy team had no knowledge, at the time of each procedure, that images would be subsequently evaluated by Smart Cholangio. A team with Data Protection Officer certification assured the nontraceability of data according to the general data protection regulation.

Outcomes

Table 1 summarizes the study outcomes. The primary outcome of this study is the diagnostic yield (DY) achieved with AI-assisted D-SOC analysis using Smart Cholangio, defined as the proportion of D-SOC examinations in which Smart Cholangio successfully provides a classification (benign or malignant) that is confirmed by histopathological analysis (cytology, D-SOC-guided biopsy, surgical resection, cytology, or EUS-guided sampling). Analyses were performed both at patient and frame level. Coprimary endpoints included sensitivity and specificity. To further assess the reliability of Smart Cholangio across different clinical scenarios, positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV) were also included as secondary endpoints.

DY—Because no examinations were excluded from the analysis and no indeterminate classifications were observed (all cases were classified as either benign or malignant), the total study population was fully represented by the sum of true positives (TP), true negatives (TN), false positives (FP), and false negatives (FN). Under these conditions, the diagnostic yield, defined as the proportion of examinations in which the Smart Cholangio provided a correct diagnostic classification, becomes mathematically equivalent to overall accuracy (TP + TN)/(TP + TN + FP + FN). Accordingly, DY and accuracy are reported as a single, combined metric in this analysis.

A key secondary endpoint relates to Smart Cholangio’s performance as an object detection system (detection module). As such, as a secondary endpoint, the study will evaluate the accuracy of bounding boxes to evaluate the spatial localization performance of Smart Cholangio, by comparing its AI-generated bounding boxes with those created by physicians in phase 1 using the Encord platform. This will be quantified using IoU as the core metric. The mean IoU and the proportion of cases exceeding a defined threshold (e.g., IoU ≥ 0.5) will be reported as indicators of successful lesion localization.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis in the study was primarily designed to compare the performance of AI analysis of D-SOC images with Smart Cholangio with the GT of histopathological analysis (characterization module) or independent expert-based annotation of stricture location (detection module). AI analysis was performed at both frame and examination levels. Descriptive statistics were reported for all measured variables and derived parameters. Continuous variables were calculated with means and SDs. The categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages.

Considering the characterization module of AI-assisted analysis, all evaluations were performed on a per-patient basis, using histopathology as the reference standard and performance metrics such as DY, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV. For each proportion p, 2-sided 95% CIs were obtained using the standard binomial test. In addition, the detection component of AI-assisted analysis was evaluated with IoU, using a frame-level analysis.

An additional comparative analysis was performed to provide a comparison between the diagnostic performance of Smart Cholangio relative to published data on conventional diagnostic approaches for BS, particularly D-SOC-guided biopsies (2). Smart Cholangio AI performance was evaluated at the patient level using histopathology as the reference standard. For each primary endpoint (DY, sensitivity, specificity), comparative analysis was based on the difference in proportions. RESULTS

From January 2017 to September 2025, 135 D-SOC procedures were performed in 129 patients because of biliary disease in 14 centers in Spain, the United States, Brazil, Australia, Saudi Arabia, and Colombia. Table 2 summarizes baseline patient and examination characteristics. The patients had a mean age of 68.1 years (SD 15.2), and 65 (50.4%) were male. Most of the procedures were performed with SpyGlass DS2 system, followed by the SpyGlass DS and EyeMax cholangioscopies. The total number of findings per patient ranged from 1 to 25, with a mean of 13.1 ± 9.2. 61 patients had a malignant BS (47.3%), with 68 patients (52.7%) having a benign biliary disease.

Patient level analysis

From the 129 patients, Smart Cholangio achieved an overall DY/accuracy of 86.0%, as presented on Table 3. The sensitivity and specificity were, respectively, 84.1% and 87.9%. The PPV was 86.9%, and the NPV was 85.3%. The patient-level receiver operating characteristic analysis demonstrated an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.904 (Figure 2).

Examination level analysis

At the examination level (composed of 135 examinations), the overall DY/accuracy (Table 3) reached was 86.7%. The sensitivity and specificity were, respectively, 84.1% and 88.9%. The NPV attained was 86.5%, and in parallel, a PPV of 86.9% was reached. Receiver Operating Characteristic analysis (Figure 2) showed an AUC of 0.906. Comparing examination-level and patient-level analysis demonstrated similar results, showing no statistically significant difference.

Frame level analysis

At the frame level, 1757 images were analyzed. Across all frames, the model achieved an average accuracy of 76.4% ± 27.1%. The mean IoU was 70.3% ± 15.8%, while the mean average precision was 33.6% ± 26.8%. Model confidence across frames averaged 78.8% ± 16.2%. The frame-level receiver operating characteristic analysis yielded an AUC of 0.779. Figure 3 demonstrates examples of detection of malignant (3a) and benign (3b) BSs.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses demonstrated consistent performance metrics across different demographic and clinical groups, with no significant differences. Stratifying by age group, DY/accuracy ranged between 80.0% for patients older than 80 years and 92.9% for patients younger than 50 years. No gender differences were observed, with DY/accuracy ranging between 84.6% in female patients and 88.6% in male patients. Similarly, no significant differences in diagnostic performance were identified across different cholangioscopy devices or across race or ethnicities.

Performance comparison of Smart Cholangio and standard of care

When benchmarked against pooled standard-of-care performance (2) for D-SOC-guided biopsies, Smart Cholangio showed, as depicted in Figure 4, a comparable diagnostic accuracy for the identification of malignant BS. Sensitivity was higher for Smart Cholangio (84.1%; 95% CI: 73.1–91.1%) than for D-SOC-guided biopsies (75.7%; 95% CI: 69.1–81.2%), corresponding to an absolute difference of +8.4%. This difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.162), and while superiority could not be formally concluded (P = 0.081), inferiority was excluded (P = 0.919).

Smart Cholangio detection and characterization modules outputs and comparison with standard of care.

Specificity was slightly lower for Smart Cholangio (87.9%; 95% CI: 77.9–93.8%) compared with D-SOC-guided biopsy (94.1%; 95% CI: 89.5–96.8%), with a nonsignificant difference of −6.2% (P = 0.105). No evidence of inferiority was observed at the prespecified significance level.

Overall DY/Accuracy was similar between Smart Cholangio (86.0%; 95% CI: 79.0–91.0%) and D-SOC-guided biopsy (84.4%; 95% CI: 80.3–87.8%), with no statistically significant difference (Δ = +1.6%, P = 0.655).

DISCUSSION

Early diagnosis of malignant BS is fundamental to optimize patient outcomes. However, current diagnostic ancillary techniques—namely brush cytology and D-SOC-guided biopsy—exhibit suboptimal performance, frequently resulting in nondiagnostic findings and delays in diagnosis (2,8,9). In a multicentric study including 135 D-SOC examinations from 14 centers across 4 continents, AI-assisted image analysis with Smart Cholangio achieved an overall accuracy of 86.0% and sensitivity of 84.1% for differentiating malignant from benign BS at the patient level. Importantly, Smart Cholangio retained similarly adequate performance metrics for both benign and malignant lesions, thus ensuring robust outputs for both classes. When benchmarked against standard tissue sampling techniques, Smart Cholangio demonstrated both to be noninferior and showed a trend toward a superior sensitivity compared with D-SOC-guided biopsies. Furthermore, the model demonstrated good agreement at the lesion-detection level when compared with expert-defined bounding boxes. This validation study demonstrates the clinical utility of AI-assisted interpretation of previously performed D-SOC procedures as a tool to enhance diagnostic DY/accuracy and potentially improve patient outcomes.

Several studies have evaluated AI-assisted cholangioscopy for detecting and differentiating malignant BS (5,6,10,11). A recent study clinically validated an AI system, achieving 85.0% accuracy in distinguishing benign from malignant BS in 61 patients (12). However, predictions relied on consecutive high-quality frames from a single D-SOC device. By contrast, our dataset was more heterogeneous and representative of real-world practice, using user-uploaded WebApp images from multiple D-SOC platforms. Interoperability is essential for successful AI implementation, and our study prioritized cross-device validation—an important step toward broader clinical applicability (13).

Moreover, demographic bias is a major concern in the development and clinical application of AI technologies. Deep learning models are often trained on imbalanced datasets, resulting in algorithms that may not generalize well to the populations in which they are ultimately deployed. Demographic bias has therefore been frequently cited as a key contributor to prediction imbalance in AI models (14,15). To mitigate this risk, our study incorporated patients from 14 reference centers across different demographic contexts. This approach ensured that model validation included a representative worldwide population, reducing the impact of geographic and demographic differences in the incidence and presentation of biliary disease.

Finally, one of the main challenges of AI integration into clinical practice is the limited transparency surrounding its predictions. This issue is often described as “black-box” medicine, in contrast to more interpretable “white-box” approaches, highlighting the difficulty clinicians may face in understanding how an AI system reaches a decision (16). In line with the need for interpretability, our model integrates bounding boxes as an explainability tool for detecting BS. The IoU analysis demonstrated substantial overlap between AI-generated and expert-generated bounding boxes, confirming a robust detection component and supporting the reliability of the AI system in a clinical setting. Understanding the factors that influence model decision-making is essential for clinician confidence, and the use of bounding boxes offers a concrete way to visualize and validate the AI's reasoning process. The application of these explainability techniques is helpful both on a real-time diagnostic setting, as well as in the context of postprocedural analysis. The latter remains extremely relevant, particularly for multidisciplinary discussion and case reassessment of clinical situations where conventional standard of care techniques did not provide clear answers. In addition, in a real-time setting, the further development of these tools may assist in the detection of specific visual markers which may lead to an overall increase in the DY of guided tissue sampling, thus potentiating standard techniques.

Despite the encouraging results of this multicenter clinical validation study, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the study aimed to compare AI-driven D-SOC image analysis with the current gold standard of histopathological analysis (characterization module) and expert-based annotation of bounding boxes (detection module). Indeed, the study design did not contemplate a comparison between the diagnostic accuracy of AI analysis and the diagnostic accuracy of each individual histopathological sampling method. This choice was driven by the inherent selection bias of including only cases with a definitive histological diagnosis in a retrospective setting, with exclusion of cases where a definite diagnosis was not obtained. Nevertheless, AI-driven characterization demonstrated higher diagnostic accuracies than those historically reported for D-SOC-guided biopsy or brush cytology and a slightly higher sensitivity than the SoC cohort (2,17,18). Future studies will focus on the prospective validation of Smart Cholangio and its impact on the DY of D-SOC-guided biopsies.

Second, the design of the current study focused on the evaluation of the algorithm's capacity to detect and differentiate malignant from benign BS and did not address the subsequent impact of these findings on clinical decision-making, the implications in management or, ultimately, on patient outcomes. Third, although different D-SOC devices were included, there was no statistical power to compare the performances between each device. Subsequent studies should address this question and include a broader range of D-SOC systems because this would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the algorithm's performance across different platforms and potentially reveal any system-specific variations in its diagnostic capabilities. Moreover, although noninferiority relative to D-SOC-guided biopsies was ensured, the demonstration of superiority was limited by the study sample size. Fourth, while the AI model includes bounding boxes to support interpretability. Finally, this study did not evaluate interobserver variability or the impact of Smart Cholangio on endoscopist performance and workflow. Future research should assess how the integration of AI affects clinical interpretation, user confidence, and diagnostic efficiency in real-world settings.

In conclusion, this multicentric validation study demonstrates the real-world performance of an AI-assisted approach for D-SOC analysis across 4 continents and multiple device types, with robust accuracy in the detection and differentiation of BS. The system performed consistently at both patient and frame levels, addressing a context in which visual assessment and biopsy sampling often provide limited DY, showing high reliability in excluding malignancy. These findings support the integration of AI as an adjunctive tool in D-SOC, enhancing diagnostic confidence and contributing to a more informed evaluation of patients with indeterminate BS.
