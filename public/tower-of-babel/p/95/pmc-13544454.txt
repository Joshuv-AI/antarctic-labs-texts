An Inferential‐Based Model of Scrupulosity and Obsessive–Compulsive Disorder Across Cultures: The Roles of Fear of Self and Obsessive Beliefs
Authors: Mujgan Inozu, Nicholas S Myers, Özlem Ergin Ayan, Yasemin Erol, Elif Usta, Elif Uzumcu, Joseph B Friedman, Emily K Juel, Jonathan S Abramowitz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAlthough inferential confusion (IC), fear of self (FOS), and obsessive beliefs have each been linked to obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD), they have rarely been examined together within the inferential‐based approach (IBA) or considered across different cultural contexts. This study examined a cross‐sectional model in which IC was associated with scrupulosity, mental contamination, and OCD symptom dimensions via indirect paths involving FOS and obsessive beliefs in undergraduate samples from the United States and Turkey. Participants were 903 undergraduate students (347 US, 556 Turkish) who completed measures of IC, FOS, obsessive beliefs, scrupulosity, mental contamination, and OCD symptoms. Path analyses provided stronger support for the modified final model in the Turkish sample, whereas the US model showed more limited overall fit and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Within this context, significant indirect associations between IC and scrupulosity, mental contamination, and most OCD dimensions were observed through FOS and/or obsessive beliefs in one or both samples. Indirect pathways through FOS were significant for scrupulosity, mental contamination, and unacceptable thoughts in both samples, whereas other OCD dimensions showed sample‐specific patterns: responsibility for harm and symmetry were significant only in the Turkish sample, and contamination was significant only in the US sample. Indirect pathways through obsessive beliefs were significant for scrupulosity, mental contamination, responsibility for harm, unacceptable thoughts, contamination, and symmetry in both samples. These findings suggest that an IBA‐consistent pattern may be present in both US and Turkish samples, although the role of self‐related concerns and obsessive beliefs may vary across symptom domains and samples. However, given the limited fit of the US model and the nonidentical final models, cross‐sample comparisons should be considered exploratory rather than confirmatory.Keywords: fear of self, inferential confusion, mental contamination, obsessive beliefs, OCD, scrupulosity

Introduction

Obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD) is characterized by recurrent distressing thoughts or images that intrude into consciousness (obsessions) and repetitive behaviors or mental acts performed either in response to an obsession or in accordance with rigidly applied rules (compulsions) (American Psychiatric Association [APA] 2022). These symptoms are thematically heterogeneous and subject to individual variability (Abramowitz et al. 2010; McKay et al. 2004). For instance, one individual may experience obsessive doubts about having unknowingly hit a pedestrian while driving and subsequently feel compelled to repeatedly check the road. Another may experience persistent intrusive sexual images, which lead to avoiding situations that trigger these thoughts and engaging in excessive prayer to counteract or neutralize what they perceive as “sinful” content. This idiosyncratic nature of OCD symptoms complicates the understanding of the disorder. While categorical diagnostic systems including the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM; APA 2022) classify it under a homogeneous framework, research has shown that this approach fails to adequately reflect the clinical heterogeneity of the disorder (Abramowitz et al. 2010).

Research largely supports a dimensional structure of OCD with four core domains (Abramowitz et al. 2010; Mataix‐Cols et al. 2005; Myers et al. 2024). Contamination involves fears of germs, dirt, or moral impurity and associated washing/cleaning rituals; it also encompasses mental contamination, an internally felt dirtiness or moral pollution not contingent on physical contact, which elicits distress and avoidance (Rachman 1994; Elliott and Radomsky 2009; Rachman et al. 2015). Responsibility for harm/mistakes reflects inflated personal responsibility to prevent harm, often driving checking and other preventive compulsions. Symmetry/incompleteness is marked by a need for exactness or “just‐right” sensations, prompting arranging and repetition until subjective completeness is achieved. Unacceptable thoughts comprise intrusive, ego‐dystonic thoughts about taboo themes (e.g., aggression, sexuality, blasphemy) typically managed with mental or behavioral neutralization (e.g., praying, counting, and repeating).

In addition to the core symptom dimensions, scrupulosity represents a culturally influenced phenomenon within the broader OCD spectrum. Research on cultural and individual belief systems in OCD indicates that obsessions concerning religious or moral correctness, fear of sin, and doubts about one's relationship with God (i.e., scrupulosity; Abramowitz 2008; Ciarrocchi 1995) are common among individuals with strong religious commitment and are often accompanied by heightened guilt (Himle et al. 2011; Raj et al. 2024). Scrupulosity is also empirically linked with other OCD symptoms, including contamination fears (Nelson et al. 2006), mental contamination (Inozu et al. 2025), and sexual or aggressive obsessions (Abramowitz et al. 2003), supporting its place within the broader OCD symptom spectrum (Greenberg and Huppert 2010). Consistent with the multifaceted nature of scrupulosity, cognitive models of OCD emphasize mechanisms beyond purely religious influences, particularly dysfunctional appraisals of intrusive thoughts and the meanings attached to them, such as inflated responsibility and thought–action fusion (Inozu, Karanci, et al. 2012; Salkovskis 1985; Shafran et al. 1996). Within this framework, rigid religious or moral rules may represent one pathway for some individuals by imposing exceptionally high moral standards that render minor transgressions spiritually significant. Rather than serving as a standalone cause, these standards likely interact with underlying cognitive vulnerabilities, contributing to experiences such as “Not‐Just‐Right Experiences”—a subjective sense that something is morally or spiritually incomplete or “wrong” (Mancini et al. 2008). In response, individuals may engage in repetitive behaviors such as excessive prayer, ritual cleansing, or avoidance to restore a sense of moral or spiritual “rightness” (Abramowitz et al. 2002).

Traditional appraisal‐based cognitive‐behavioral models of OCD propose that normally occurring intrusive thoughts escalate into clinical obsessions when these thoughts are misinterpreted as significant threats that must be controlled. These misinterpretations are fueled by cognitive biases (i.e., obsessive beliefs) such as an inflated sense of responsibility, exaggerated threat perception, over‐importance attributed to thoughts, and an inability to tolerate uncertainty (Obsessive Compulsive Cognitions Working Group 1997). Obsessive beliefs are considered vulnerability factors linked to the development and maintenance of OCD (Abramowitz et al. 2006) and this conceptual model is supported by a large body of correlational and experimental research (e.g., Clark 2019).

Another conceptual model of OCD that has garnered increasing attention is the inference‐based approach (IBA), which highlights the role of inferential confusion (IC), a faulty reasoning process that gives rise to obsessional doubts (Aardema et al. 2005, 2010). Unlike appraisal‐based models which attribute the primary dysfunction to the interpretation of intrusions, IBA argues that the primary disruption is in reasoning that substitutes imagined possibilities for reality; appraisals and obsessive beliefs are conceptualized as downstream byproducts (Aardema et al. 2005, 2010). For example, after using a kitchen knife, a person may think, “What if I nicked someone?” and treat this hypothetical as credible despite no corroborating evidence, thereby engendering threat appraisals and inflated responsibility. Empirically, IC is reliably associated with OCD symptoms in self‐report studies (Aardema et al. 2018; Yorulmaz et al. 2010; Wu et al. 2009; Pozza et al. 2016) and in experimental manipulations (Baraby et al. 2023), with converging cross‐cultural findings (Aminaee et al. 2024; Khosravi‐Roubiat and Najafi 2023; Tunç and Yorulmaz 2025).

IBA also emphasizes fear of self (FOS)—the feared possibility of a dangerous, immoral, or deviant self that one might become (Aardema and O'Connor 2003, 2007; Aardema et al. 2018). From an inference‐based perspective, IC is viewed as an upstream disturbance in reasoning in which imagination displaces perception, while FOS functions as a thematic vulnerability that organizes the content of obsessional doubt by shaping the imagined possibilities individuals treat as evidence. Within this framework, individuals may rely on hypothetical possibilities (e.g., a feared harmful self) rather than objective evidence, evaluating these imaginings as if they were real (Aardema et al. 2005). For example, a parent who strongly values being a “good mother” may fear the possibility of being a harmful or dangerous parent. Under conditions of IC, this feared identity can become the organizing theme of doubt, leading the individual to reason, “What if I'm actually a bad mother who could suffocate my baby?” and to treat the mere occurrence of this thought as evidence of its plausibility (Aardema and O'Connor 2007; Aardema et al. 2013). Empirically, FOS correlates with greater OCD severity, broader symptom profiles, and stronger obsessive beliefs (Aardema et al. 2013, 2017; Melli et al. 2016; Godwin et al. 2020; W. Yang et al. 2021). Together, FOS and IC are proposed to sustain OCD by blurring imagination with reality, fostering mistrust in perception, and motivating compulsive responses (Aardema et al. 2005; Aminaee et al. 2024).

Numerous studies conducted within the IBA framework have shown that IC, FOS, and obsessive beliefs are significant correlates or predictors of OCD symptoms (Aardema et al. 2013; Khosravi‐Roubiat and Najafi 2023; Myers et al. 2024; Nikodijevic et al. 2015; W. Yang et al. 2021). However, most empirical work within this framework has been conducted in Western samples (Aardema and O'Connor 2007; Myers et al. 2024). Importantly, these studies have rarely examined scrupulosity and mental contamination together with the core OCD dimensions within a single model. These symptom domains were included because they represent morally and self‐referentially loaded manifestations of OCD that may vary across cultural and religious contexts (Abramowitz and Jacoby 2014; Buchholz et al. 2019; Krause and Radomsky 2023), yet they have received relatively limited attention within IBA‐based empirical models. Prior research indicates that scrupulosity is shaped by cultural norms regarding purity and obedience, leading to variations in symptom severity and content between Muslim and Christian samples (Inozu, Clark, et al. 2012; Kaviani et al. 2015). For instance, although scrupulosity is elevated in both groups, cultural values dictate the specific interpretations and beliefs through which these symptoms are expressed (Inozu, Karanci, et al. 2012). Similarly, while mental contamination is a cross‐cultural phenomenon involving “moral taint,” its salience is often tied to identity‐related threats rather than mere physical safety (Aardema 2020). Based on this, we propose that cultural norms provide the “thematic landscape” for the inferential process. By examining scrupulosity and mental contamination alongside traditional OCD dimensions—such as responsibility for harm and unacceptable thoughts—this study tests whether IC, as an upstream reasoning disturbance, generalizes to symptom presentations that are uniquely sensitive to moral and religious meanings.

Moreover, as IBA posits IC as an upstream vulnerability—and direct associations between IC and OCD symptoms—are already well‐documented (Julien et al. 2016), the present study focuses on the roles of two distinct mechanisms in this association: thematic self‐vulnerability (FOS) and obsessive beliefs (downstream formulations) across different cultural contexts. Estimating the model separately for US and Turkish participants allowed us to examine patterns of associations among IC, FOS, and obsessive beliefs within each sample. We hypothesized that IC would be positively related to both FOS and obsessive beliefs, which, in turn, would be associated with higher levels of scrupulosity, mental contamination, and other OCD symptoms, albeit with potential variations in path strength. Methods

Participants

We tested our hypotheses with a nonclinical undergraduate sample for three reasons: (a) the continuous (i.e., dimensional) nature of the cognitive constructs and OC symptoms, (b) the time and financial challenges in recruiting a sufficiently sized clinical sample for meaningful statistical analyses, (c) the sample accessibility. Furthermore, research suggests that many processes related to OC symptoms occur similarly in clinical and nonclinical samples (Abramowitz et al. 2014).

For both US and Turkish samples, participants who voluntarily agreed to participate were initially included in the study, whereas those with incomplete responses, failed attention check items, or identified as outliers were excluded from the analyses. For path analysis, a sample size at least 10 times larger than the number of estimated free parameters is recommended (Kline 2016). Given that our model included 38 free parameters, a sample size of approximately 380 participants per group was considered desirable. The initially recruited samples exceeded this recommended threshold in both countries (N US = 437; N Turkey = 685). However, after exclusions, the final analytic sample consisted of 347 participants in the US sample and 556 participants in the Turkish sample. Thus, the Turkish analytic sample exceeded the recommended threshold, whereas the US analytic sample fell slightly below it. Accordingly, the adequacy of the US sample size should be interpreted with caution.

The initially recruited US sample consisted of 437 undergraduate students enrolled in psychology courses at a major university in the southeastern region of the country. Data from 19 participants who did not complete at least one scale, 45 who failed at least one attention check item, and 26 who were identified as outliers based on univariate analyses (Z‐scores exceeding +/− 3.29) and multivariate normality analyses (Mahalanobis distance with a significance threshold of p < 0.001; Mahalanobis 1936) were excluded from the analyses. The final analytic sample included 347 participants, with an age range of 18–29 years (M = 19.04, SD = 1.32). Of these, 287 participants identified as female (82.7%), 59 as male (17.0%), and one participant chose not to disclose their gender (0.3%). In terms of race, most of the sample identified as White or European (56.5%). Others identified as Asian (15.6%), Black or African American (8.1%), Middle Eastern or North African (1.4%), American Indian or Alaska Native (0.9%), Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander (0.6%), and multiracial (10.1%). Additionally, 5.8% of the sample was of Hispanic or Latino descent, while 1.2% chose not to disclose their race/ethnicity. Participants reported a variety of religious affiliations. The most common religious affiliations were Mainstream Protestant (28.8%) and Roman Catholic (11.8%), followed by Hindu (5.2%), Jewish (2.3%), Muslim (2.0%), Greek Orthodox (1.2%), Buddhist (0.9%), and multiple religions (6.3%). In addition, 18.16% reported believing in something else or nothing in particular, 15.3% stated that they were not affiliated with any religion, 5.5% identified as agnostic, and 2.6% as atheist.

The initially recruited Turkish sample included 685 undergraduate students from 5 universities located in different regions of Turkey, including Eastern Anatolia and the Mediterranean region. Among these participants, 50 who did not complete at least one scale, 40 who failed at least one attention check item, and 39 who were identified as outliers based on univariate analyses (Z‐scores exceeding +/− 3.29) and multivariate normality analyses (Mahalanobis distance with a significance threshold of p < 0.001) were excluded from the analyses. The final analytic sample consisted of 556 participants, of whom 462 identified as female (83.1%) and 94 as male (16.9%). Participants' ages ranged from 17 to 40 years (M = 21.16, SD = 2.53). In terms of the sample's ethnic distribution, 86.9% identified as Turkish, 7.9% as Kurdish, and 0.2% as Laz. The most commonly reported religious affiliation was Muslim (73.7%), followed by Roman Catholic (2%) and Buddhist (2%). In addition, 9.9% reported believing in something else or nothing in particular, 6.7% stated that they were not affiliated with any religion, 8.1% identified as agnostic, and 1.3% as atheist.

Procedure

Ethical approval for research procedures was obtained from IRBs of both universities. The research link, containing self‐report measures and four attention check items, was created on an online data collection platform (Qualtrics). This link was shared with participants in both countries as part of their psychology courses, and all participants received bonus points for their participation. Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained from all participants before completing the scales.

Measures

Demographic Information Form (DIS)

The DIS was developed by researchers based on previous studies (Bilekli and Inozu 2018; Inozu, Karanci, et al. 2012). This form includes questions on sociodemographic information such as gender, age, race or ethnic identity and religious affiliation.

Vancouver Obsessive Compulsive Inventory – Mental Contamination (VOCI‐MC; Radomsky et al. 2014)

The VOCI‐MC assesses the intensity of obsessive‐compulsive symptoms related to mental contamination through 20 items rated on a 5‐point Likert scale. Internal consistency was excellent for both the original form (α = 0.93–0.97) and its Turkish version (α = 0.93; Inozu et al. 2016). In the present study, this self‐report scale showed strong reliability, with Cronbach's α of 0.93 for US and 0.91 for Turkish sample.

Penn Inventory of Scrupulosity (PIOS; Abramowitz et al. 2002; Olatunji et al. 2007)

The PIOS measures religious obsessions and compulsions through 19 items rated on a 5‐point Likert scale and includes 2 subscales as Fear of Sin (FS) and Fear of God (FG). This self‐report scale was first translated into Turkish by Altın (2009). The Turkish version (Inozu et al. 2017) showed excellent internal consistency for total scale, FS and FG subscales (α = 0.95, α = 0.90, and α = 0.95, respectively). Similarly, the original scale demonstrated high reliability, with Cronbach's αs of 0.93 for total scale, 0.90 for FS, and 0.88 for FG subscales. In the present study, the total scale, FS and FG subscales showed high reliability for US (α = 0.95, α = 0.93, and α = 0.88, respectively) and for Turkish samples (α = 0.94, α = 0.90, and α = 0.92, respectively).

Dimensional Obsessive‐Compulsive Scale (DOCS; Abramowitz et al. 2010)

The DOCS evaluates severity of obsessive–compulsive symptoms by measuring distress, duration, inability to resist compulsions, avoidance behavior, and impairment in functionality. This self‐report scale includes 20 items rated on a 5‐point Likert scale and 4 subscales as unacceptable thoughts, responsibility for harm, contamination, and symmetry. Internal consistency for both the original form (α = 0.83–0.96) and its Turkish version (α = 0.87–0.95; Şafak et al. 2018) was good to excellent. In this study, Cronbach's αs were good to excellent for US sample (α = 0.82–0.90) and acceptable to good for Turkish sample (α = 0.69–0.85).

Fear of Self Questionnaire (FOSQ; Aardema et al. 2013; Melli et al. 2016)

The FOSQ is an 8‐item self‐report scale rated on a 6‐point Likert scale assessing misjudgments about the self commonly observed in OCD. Both the original scale (α = 0.93; Melli et al. 2016) and the Turkish version (α = 0.82; Akın et al. 2016) showed good internal consistency. In this study, the scale demonstrated excellent reliability for the US sample (α = 0.91) and good reliability for the Turkish sample (α = 0.84).

Inferential Confusion Questionnaire‐Expanded Version (ICQ‐EV; Aardema et al. 2010)1

The ICQ‐EV evaluates IC through 30 items rated on a 6‐point Likert scale. This self‐report questionnaire demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with Cronbach's αs ranging from 0.96 to 0.97. Due to the unavailability of the Turkish version, this scale was used for US sample, where it showed excellent reliability with a Cronbach's α of 0.97.

IC was assessed in Turkish sample by using the Inferential Confusion Questionnaire (ICQ; Aardema et al. 2005). The ICQ includes 15 items rated on a 5‐point Likert scale. Internal consistency of Turkish version was good, with Cronbach αs ranging from 0.85 to 0.86 across different samples (Yorulmaz et al. 2010). In this study, this self‐report scale demonstrated excellent reliability for Turkish sample (α = 0.92).

Obsessive Beliefs Questionnaire‐20 (OBQ‐20; Moulding et al. 2011)

The OBQ‐20 evaluates the severity of dysfunctional beliefs about obsessions. It comprises four subscales as responsibility, threat perception, intolerance of uncertainty/perfectionism, and control and importance of thoughts. This 20‐item self‐report questionnaire rated on a 7‐point Likert scale was developed by shortening the OBQ‐44 (OCCWG 2001). Internal consistency was acceptable to good for both the original scale (α = 0.77–0.80) and the Turkish version (α = 0.77–0.79; Yorulmaz et al. 2019). In the present study, total scale showed excellent reliability for both samples (α = 0.94 for US and α = 0.90 for Turkish sample) and subscales showed good reliability both for US (α = 0.76‐.80) and Turkish (α = 0.75‐.77) samples.

Statistical Analyses

Data screening and descriptive analyses (means, standard deviations, skewness, kurtosis) were conducted in SPSS 24.0 (IBM Corp 2016). When fewer than 20% of the responses were missing for a given variable, missing values were replaced with the variable mean, a procedure commonly used in psychological research for low levels of missingness (Tabachnick and Fidell 2019). In the present data set, the proportion of missing responses for all variables was below this 20% threshold.

Path analyses were conducted in SPSS AMOS 23.0. Model fit was evaluated using the χ 2 statistic, the comparative fit index (CFI), the Tucker–Lewis index (TLI), and the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA). Because the study tested a theory‐driven structural model, theoretically specified paths were retained in the final models even when they were nonsignificant, in order to preserve the conceptual structure of the hypothesized relations among IC, FOS, obsessive beliefs, and OCD symptom dimensions and to avoid making the final models entirely dependent on sample‐specific statistical significance decisions. Model modifications were informed by modification indices but were made only when they were theoretically justifiable and improved model fit. Accordingly, retained nonsignificant paths were not interpreted as empirically or substantively supported associations; rather, they represent relationships that were theoretically predicted but did not receive empirical support in the current sample. Final models with the most satisfactory fit were retained for both the US and Turkish samples. Path models were estimated separately for the US and Turkish groups to examine whether the proposed relations among IC, FOS, obsessive beliefs, scrupulosity, mental contamination, and OCD dimensions showed a similar pattern across cultures. Estimating the models separately was also appropriate because the two groups completed different versions of the ICQ (ICQ‐EV vs. ICQ‐15), which limits strict comparability of IC scores across samples. Thus, the two models were compared descriptively, in terms of the presence, direction, and relative strength of paths, rather than through formal multigroup invariance tests.

The maximum likelihood estimation method was employed in the path analysis conducted with the observed variables. Indirect effects were tested using bootstrapped confidence intervals based on 5000 resamples. For both direct and indirect effects, the unstandardized coefficient (B), the lower and upper bounds of the 95% confidence interval, the standardized coefficient (β), and the p values were reported.

Path Analyses

The originally hypothesized model tested for both US and Turkish samples is presented in Figure 1. Since the originally hypothesized model did not yield a satisfactory fit for either sample, modification indices were examined to identify theoretically meaningful model respecifications. For the Turkish sample, the indices suggested adding paths from FOSQ to OBQ and from ICQ to DOCS Responsibility. For the US sample, only the path from FOSQ to OBQ was suggested.

FOS reflects the fear of being a morally flawed individual due to the presence of intrusive aggressive, sexual, or otherwise unacceptable thoughts. Accordingly, FOS may increase the personal significance and perceived threat of intrusive thoughts—interpreting them as evidence of the “feared self”—thereby contributing to obsessive beliefs. Based on this theoretical rationale, the path from FOSQ to OBQ was added to the model for both samples. Furthermore, individuals with high levels of IC tend to rely more on imagined possibilities than on sensory evidence, a tendency theoretically linked to heightened responsibility for potential harm and increased doubt. Consequently, the path from ICQ to DOCS Responsibility was added to the model for the Turkish sample (Models 2 and 3; see Table 3). Because these additional paths were based on modification indices and were not part of the originally hypothesized model, Models 2 and 3 should be regarded as modified final models. Moreover, because the final US and Turkish models were not identical, cross‐sample comparisons of these models are interpreted descriptively rather than as a strict confirmatory comparison of equivalent models.

In both samples, theoretically specified nonsignificant paths were retained to preserve the conceptual structure of the hypothesized model; however, these paths were not interpreted as empirically or substantively supported associations. Although the final model for the US sample (Model 2; see Table 3) showed some improvement relative to the initial model, its overall fit remained limited, particularly with respect to RMSEA and TLI values. In contrast, the final model for the Turkish sample (Model 3; see Table 3) demonstrated a satisfactory fit. Therefore, the US model should be interpreted with caution, and the findings should not be taken as indicating comparable model support across the two samples. The results for the direct pathways are presented in Figure S1 and Table S1 for the US sample, and Figure S2 and Table S2 for the Turkish sample.

For the US sample, analysis of indirect pathways revealed that ICQ showed significant indirect associations with PIOS, VOCI MC, DOCS Contamination, and DOCS Unacceptable Thoughts through FOSQ. However, the indirect pathways from ICQ to DOCS Responsibility and DOCS Symmetry through FOSQ were not statistically significant. Additionally, significant serial indirect associations were observed between ICQ and all measured OCD dimensions (PIOS, VOCI MC, and all DOCS subscales) through both FOSQ and OBQ. Furthermore, ICQ was found to predict PIOS, VOCI MC, and all DOCS subscales through the mediating role of OBQ. Given the limited overall fit of the US model, these indirect effects should be interpreted cautiously. The results are presented in Figure S1 and Table S3.

For the Turkish sample, results indicated significant indirect associations between ICQ and PIOS, VOCI MC, DOCS Responsibility, DOCS Unacceptable Thoughts, and DOCS Symmetry through FOSQ. However, the indirect pathway from ICQ to DOCS Contamination through FOSQ was not statistically significant. Additionally, significant serial indirect associations were observed between ICQ and all measured OCD dimensions (PIOS, VOCI MC, and all DOCS subscales) through both FOSQ and OBQ. Furthermore, ICQ significantly predicted all dimensions (PIOS, VOCI MC, and all DOCS subscales) through the mediating role of OBQ. The results are presented in Figure S2 and Table S4.

Discussion

This study examined how IC relates to OCD symptom dimensions, including mental contamination and scrupulosity through FOS and obsessive beliefs in US and Turkish samples. Overall, the findings provided partial and sample‐specific support for the proposed model, with stronger model fit observed in the Turkish sample than in the US sample. Nevertheless, IC showed significant positive associations with all symptom dimensions within each sample. Notably, the mediational pattern was most consistent for scrupulosity, unacceptable thoughts, and mental contamination, suggesting that FOS and obsessive beliefs may be particularly relevant to morally salient OCD presentations. The significant indirect paths to mental contamination observed in the modified models suggest that these concerns may operate partly through a “moral–self” route rather than solely through contact‐based threats. However, given the limited overall fit of the US model and the stronger fit of the Turkish model, these cross‐sample differences should be interpreted cautiously. Moreover, because the final models included modification‐index‐based additions and were not identical across samples, the observed differences should be considered descriptive and exploratory rather than confirmatory evidence of cultural differences.

In the present study, FOS and obsessive beliefs were associated with core, morally laden OCD domains in both samples. These findings are consistent with the view that IC does not constitute a belief itself; rather, it reflects an upstream disturbance whereby an epistemic shift allows imagined possibilities to acquire evidential status. This pattern was reflected in the serial indirect pathways of the modified final models, which were significant across all measured OCD dimensions in both samples, although these findings should be interpreted in light of the sample‐specific model fit and the exploratory nature of the cross‐sample comparisons. Within this framework, FOS may act as an identity‐relevant anchor that shapes the moral meaning of obsessional doubt, while obsessive beliefs may serve as secondary, downstream elaborations that stabilize the constructed doubt (Aardema 2025; Aardema et al. 2005, 2010). Rather than competing, inferential and appraisal mechanisms may be complementary, with intrusive thoughts and beliefs viewed as downstream expressions of a prior inferential shift. This sequential process—where an altered inferential stance may trigger self‐related concerns followed by belief‐based elaborations—is consistent with recent evidence highlighting FOS as a key link between IC and OC symptoms (Aminaee et al. 2024).

For both symmetry and responsibility for harm, IC showed significant indirect associations through obsessive beliefs across the Turkish and US samples. In contrast, for these two dimensions, the indirect pathways through FOS were supported only in the Turkish sample. This pattern may reflect differences in the associations between self‐related vulnerabilities and these symptom dimensions within the two samples, although such observations should be interpreted descriptively and with caution. Regarding the symmetry dimension, the indirect pathway through obsessive beliefs in both samples suggests that this dimension may involve cognitive processes that appeared less sample‐specific in the present data. From an inference‐based perspective, symmetry often reflects sensory‐driven “not‐just‐right” and incompleteness experiences (NJREs), which may be less dependent on culture‐bound moral or religious meanings (Coles and Pietrefesa 2007). These experiences may serve as a proximal sensory anchor for obsessional doubt, prompting individuals across different cultural contexts to prioritize subjective, internal feelings over objective reality (Aardema and O'Connor 2007). However, while these rigid self‐standards for exactness may resonate with a fear of losing control, the nonsignificant role of FOS in the US sample suggests that, in this sample, symmetry‐related concerns may have been more closely associated with nonmoral, sensory domains. Conversely, the significant FOS‐mediated pathway in the Turkish sample raises the possibility that sensory‐driven symptoms may be more closely linked to appraisals of self‐vulnerability in this sample.

A similar divergence appears when looking at the responsibility for harm dimension. In the US sample, the nonsignificant indirect path through FOS is consistent with the view that responsibility‐focused symptoms may sometimes be more oriented toward external consequences rather than toward the immediate defense of a morally acceptable self (Aardema et al. 2018). Additionally, the significant indirect association through obsessive beliefs may partly reflect conceptual overlap between IC and OBQ domains, particularly responsibility and threat appraisal, which might have reduced the incremental explanatory role of FOS in the US sample. However, this overlap is unlikely to fully account for the observed pattern, given that FOS remained a significant mediator in the Turkish sample. This pattern suggests that the role of FOS may vary across symptom dimensions and samples, rather than operating uniformly across OCD presentations. In particular, its significant indirect associations with symmetry and responsibility for harm in the Turkish sample may indicate that self‐related vulnerability was more closely linked to these dimensions in this sample. While rigid self‐standards for exactness in symmetry and heightened accountability in responsibility for harm may be consistent with fear of one's inner impulses in the Turkish sample, this link appeared less salient in the US sample.

Beyond the initial hypothesized paths, model modification indices suggested the inclusion of a direct path from FOS to obsessive beliefs in both samples. This addition does not imply that FOS and obsessive beliefs operate as parallel, co‐equal mechanisms; rather, it suggests that self‐related vulnerability may anchor inferentially generated doubt, with obsessive beliefs potentially serving to articulate and stabilize that doubt. Put differently, higher levels of feared‐self experiences tended to co‐occur with stronger obsessive beliefs, a pattern consistent with the idea that viewing the self as potentially unacceptable may coincide with beliefs concerning heightened responsibility, threat, or the need to control thoughts (Llorens‐Aguilar et al. 2022; Y. H. Yang et al. 2025). In line with this, the serial indirect association from IC to DOCS responsibility through FOS and obsessive beliefs (IC → FOS → obsessive beliefs → responsibility) was significant in both samples, although this pathway should be interpreted more cautiously in the US sample given its limited overall model fit. This finding is noteworthy because responsibility symptoms are conceptually closer to evaluative belief content than to self‐threat alone. It may indicate that self‐related vulnerability and subsequent belief elaborations are relevant to responsibility‐focused presentations, reflecting a prior epistemic shift in which imagined possibilities are granted evidential authority. Thus, within this theoretical framework, obsessive beliefs may be better conceptualized as narrative products of this shift rather than its primary cause. While the cross‐sectional design precludes definitive conclusions about temporal ordering, these results align with recent evidence highlighting FOS as a key link between IC and OC symptoms (Aminaee et al. 2024).

Contamination concerns showed somewhat different patterns of association across the two samples; however, these observations should be interpreted descriptively. In both samples, IC showed significant indirect associations with contamination symptoms through obsessive beliefs, suggesting that dysfunctional belief systems may contribute to contamination concerns through this indirect pathway. In contrast, the FOS pathway reached significance only in the US sample. This finding may indicate that FOS‐related processes were more closely integrated into contamination concerns in this sample, whereas this pathway was not evident in the Turkish sample. However, this pattern should be interpreted cautiously given the comparatively limited overall fit of the US model and the use of different ICQ versions across samples. Taken together, these findings should be interpreted descriptively rather than as evidence of a definitive cross‐sample difference.

Several limitations of the present study warrant consideration. First, the use of nonclinical university student samples may limit the generalizability of the findings. While previous research indicates that the qualitative characteristics of OCD symptoms are similar across clinical and nonclinical populations (Abramovitch et al. 2023; Abramowitz et al. 2014), clinical populations typically exhibit higher symptom severity and greater functional impairment (Macy et al. 2013). Thus, future research should replicate these findings in treatment‐seeking samples to determine the robustness of these cognitive pathways across severity levels. Second, the exclusive reliance on self‐report measures introduces potential biases related to subjective reporting and shared method variance. Third, the cross‐sectional nature of the data restricts causal interpretation of the observed pathways: while the observed indirect effects provide informative patterns of association, they cannot establish temporal ordering. Consequently, these results are consistent with an upstream inferential model but do not constitute a definitive test of it. Another limitation is that the final US and Turkish models were not identical, as they included modification‐index‐based additions that differed across samples. In addition, formal measurement invariance testing was not conducted, which further limits the extent to which the models and path estimates can be directly compared across samples. Accordingly, cross‐sample comparisons should be regarded as descriptive and exploratory rather than as confirmatory comparisons of equivalent models. Future research should test preregistered equivalent models and formally evaluate measurement invariance to allow stricter cross‐cultural comparisons.

Furthermore, potential measurement inequivalence and the use of different ICQ versions across the US and Turkish samples may have influenced the results. Although both versions assess IC and show a single‐factor structure, they differ in scope: the ICQ‐15 emphasizes inverse reasoning, whereas the expanded version includes broader reasoning types (e.g., distrust of senses, irrelevant associations) and excludes threat‐related content to reduce overlap with obsessive beliefs. These variations may limit the comparability of IC‐related results across samples. Future research could address these limitations by employing longitudinal or experimental designs to draw stronger inferences regarding the interplay between IC, FOS, and belief formation. Additionally, given recent findings that specific feared‐self variants—such as the corrupted or contaminated self—are differentially linked to distinct symptom domains (Aminaee et al. 2025), future studies would benefit from employing multidimensional feared‐self measures, such as the FOSQ‐MV.

Despite these limitations, the present findings suggest the potential clinical relevance of interventions targeting IC, FOS, and obsessive beliefs, while recognizing that these constructs reflect distinct theoretical processes. From an inference‐based perspective, IC is conceptualized as a disturbance in one's epistemic stance, whereas FOS and obsessive beliefs function as organizing or elaborative mechanisms that shape responses to this stance within specific symptom domains. Accordingly, interventions that explicitly target reasoning processes and evidential evaluation, such as inference‐based therapy, may be especially relevant for symptom domains characterized by strong moral or self‐referential content, including scrupulosity and unacceptable thoughts. Other evidence‐based interventions, such as Exposure and Response Prevention and Cognitive Behavioral Therapy, may also influence these processes indirectly, they are likely to operate through different theoretical mechanisms. Furthermore, our results point to the potential value of clinically attending to self‐related vulnerabilities such as FOS, particularly in samples or contexts in which such concerns are especially salient. Treatment planning may therefore benefit from a comprehensive approach that targets upstream inferential processes while also addressing downstream belief‐based elaborations.
