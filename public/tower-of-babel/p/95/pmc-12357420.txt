The prevalence of Leishmania RNA virus in cutaneous leishmaniasis: a meta-analysis and systematic review
Authors: Narjes Yektaeian, Majid Dousti, Gholamreza Hatam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile numerous studies have investigated the role and presence of RNA viruses within Leishmania parasites, the existing literature presents a fragmented view of Leishmania RNA virus (LRV) prevalence in parasites associated with cutaneous leishmaniasis (CL). Therefore, the present study aimed to elucidate the prevalence of LRV in parasites obtained from patients with CL.MethodsTo achieve this aim, we conducted a systematic literature search across international databases, specifically Web of Science, PubMed, Embase, and Scopus. We extracted relevant studies reporting the prevalence of LRV in parasites obtained from patients with CL, utilizing predefined keywords and covering the period from December 1988 to August 2024. The extracted data underwent meta-analysis using a random-effects model, with statistical analyses performed in STATA version 14.ResultsOur search yielded 44 studies that fulfilled the eligibility criteria, representing a total sample size of 2,276 participants. LRV detection was performed on biopsied lesion fragments and cultured isolates derived from these lesions. Among the 2,276 CL patients, 647 tested positive for LRV. We observed regional variations in LRV prevalence, with 45.16% in the New World (NW) and 30.00% in the Old World (OW). The majority of included studies examined Leishmania (Viannia) guyanensis, L. (V.) braziliensis, L. (Leishmania) major, and Leishmania (L.) tropica. LRV prevalence was 32.26% in lesion biopsies and 30.96% in cultured isolates. Notably, LRV exhibited a high prevalence in Leishmania species obtained from mucocutaneous leishmaniasis (MCL), suggesting a strong association between LRV1 and this clinical manifestation in the NW.ConclusionThis study demonstrated a substantial prevalence of LRV in CL. LRV was identified in both lesion biopsies and cultured isolates, revealing significant regional variations with higher prevalence in the NW compared to the OW. Furthermore, a strong association was observed between LRV1 and MCL in the NW.Keywords: Leishmania RNA virus, Cutaneous leishmaniasis, Prevalence

Introduction

Leishmaniasis is a complex of disorders caused by different species of the Leishmania genus, transmitted by sandfly [1]. Clinical manifestations of leishmaniasis vary, including cutaneous leishmaniasis (CL), mucocutaneous leishmaniasis (MCL), visceral leishmaniasis (VL), and disseminated forms. CL represents the most prevalent form globally [1].

This disease primarily affects impoverished populations in tropical and subtropical regions of developing countries. According to the WHO, up to one million new cases occur annually, with around 400 million people at risk [2].

CL presents in various clinical forms, though most infected individuals remain asymptomatic. The most common form, localized cutaneous leishmaniasis (LCL), typically causes one or a few facial sores that usually heal spontaneously but may leave permanent scars [3]. Diffuse cutaneous leishmaniasis (DCL) is rare and more severe form, characterized by multiple non-ulcerative nodular lesions that can spread throughout the body [4]. Mucocutaneous leishmaniasis (MCL) is a chronic, disfiguring form that affects the mouth, nose, and pharynx, particularly when diagnosis or treatment is delayed. CL’s clinical presentation depends on the parasite species, host immunity, and sandfly vector. In the New World, L. (V.) braziliensis is a primary cause of chronic CL, which rarely resolves without treatment [5].

Most cases respond well to standard treatments, although a small percentage (5.3% from 2017 to 2022, according to the Pan American Health Organization) may later progress to MCL, requiring longer treatment with higher doses of antimonials or amphotericin B [6].

Since the detection of the RNA virus in Entamoeba histolytica in the 1960 for the first time, several pathogenic parasites, such as Leishmania, have also been shown to carry this virus [7]. Leishmania RNA virus (LRV) is composed of a double-stranded RNA of about 5.3 kb genomes, RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, and a non-enveloped capsid protein, which is a suspected factor in the severity of the disease, which belongs to the Totiviridae family [8]. Based on genetic organization, LRVs are divided into two species: Leishmania RNA Virus 1 (LRV1) and Leishmania RNA Virus 1 (LRV2). However, the older classification system, which identifies 14 subtypes of LRV1 (LRV1-1 to LRV1-14), is now considered obsolete. Recent studies have proposed a more accurate classification based on the virus’s association with its host species. This emerging approach emphasizes the species-specific relationship between LRVs and the Leishmania parasites. A study by Tirera et al. (2017) [9] explores this new classification system, where LRVs are categorized based on the host they infect. Additional insights into the genetic diversity of LRV1 and its species-specific relationships with Leishmania parasites have been provided by recent research [10, 11]. Phylogenetic analysis of LRV1 sequences consistently shows that viral strains tend to cluster according to the species of Leishmania in which they reside [10]. For instance, L. (V.) guyanensis sequences formed distinct groups (A, B, D, E) based on their genetic makeup, while L. (V.) braziliensis strains clustered separately (Group F). Interestingly, sequences from L. (V.) shawi were also analyzed for the first time and were found to group with those of L. (V.) guyanensis in groups A and B, suggesting closer genetic relationships between these two Leishmania species [10]. L. (V.) braziliensis and other species of New World Leishmania (NWL), such as L. (V.) guyanensis, contain LRV1, which is a significant virulence factor in the worsening of sore and metastatic development of MCL; treatment failure via pro-inflammatory cytokines like toll-like receptor 3 (TLR3) activation to produce IFN-β and hyperinflammatory immune reaction resulted in exacerbation of the phenotype of disease and antimonial therapy failure [12].

Leishmania species such as L. (L.) major, L. (L.) tropica, and L. (L.) aethiopica are classified as Old World (OW) species and are associated with LRV2. However, L. (L.) infantum is found in both the Old and New Worlds and can also harbor LRV2 [13].

Similar to LRV1, LRV2 also influences clinical outcomes, notably symptom recurrence and treatment resistance. While LRV2 has been studied less extensively than LRV1, the presence of either LRV1 or LRV2 in Leishmania species has been associated with recurring symptoms, treatment failure, alterations in lesion morphology, immune modulation, and inflammation [13]. Both LRV species are found in both the New World (NW) and the OW, with LRV1 predominating in the NW and LRV2 in the OW. This underscores the importance of accurate species identification in CL cases, as viral presence and strain variation can significantly affect clinical outcomes [14].

Notably, the detection of LRV1 in L. tropica, as reported by Zolfaghari et al. (2023), represents a novel finding in CL epidemiology. This observation supports the established close genetic relationship between L. (L.) tropica and L. (L.) aethiopica, and suggests shared viral dynamics among these species [15]. LRV1 and LRV2 exhibit approximately 40% nucleotide sequence homology, revealing substantial diversity as demonstrated by comprehensive sequence analyses [16].

In both human and animal models of leishmaniasis, LRV significantly exacerbates the inflammatory phenotype and enhances the pathogenesis of CL. For example, in LRV-infected mice, increased macrophage-secreted hyper-inflammatory markers promote parasite replication and pathology [17].

The present study aimed to clarify the prevalence of LVR in Leishmania parasites obtained in CL patients by means of a systematic review and meta-analysis.

Methods

PRISMA or Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis Guidelines checklist was used [18].

Search strategy

A comprehensive search was performed to extract the published studies on the LRV prevalence in Leishmania spp. The keywords used included “Leishmania RNA virus’”, “LRV”, “prevalence’”, “Leishmaniasis”, “diffused cutaneous leishmaniasis”, “DCL”, “cutaneous leishmaniasis,” and “mucocutaneous leishmaniasis”. International databases, including ISI, PubMed, Embase, and Scopus, were searched using various combinations of these keywords and Boolean operators (“OR” and “AND”). To locate studies that were not in the databases above, Google Scholar was also searched. Additionally, potentially relevant papers were found by looking through the references of the extracted studies. The endnote was then imported with all the records. Records that were duplicated were then removed. We also checked the reference list of related articles and searched Google Scholar as grey literature to prevent missing any eligible studies.

Additionally, potentially relevant papers were identified by screening the reference lists of the extracted studies. All records were imported into EndNote (version 20), and duplicate records were removed. To ensure comprehensive coverage, we also conducted a dedicated grey literature search. This included reviewing conference proceedings, institutional reports, preprints, and other non-peer-reviewed sources that traditional databases may not index. Furthermore, we searched relevant databases for grey literature, such as OpenGrey and others, to capture additional sources. Google Scholar was used to identify other relevant studies that may not have been indexed in the primary databases.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were included in our systematic review and meta-analysis based on the following criteria: (1) original articles published in English, and (2) studies examining the prevalence of LRV across various types of CL, including DCL and MCL patients. We excluded (1) review articles, editorial articles, and book chapters; (2) articles that focused on forms of leishmaniasis other than CL or were not based on CL; and (3) studies describing LRV prevalence in in-vitro experiments, case reports, or experimental animal models. The flow diagram (Fig. 1) illustrates the selection process for the studies included in this review.

PRISMA flow diagram illustrating selection of articles

Data extraction

Two different authors extracted data from the selected studies. The related data, including the name of the first author, place, year of publication, sample size, mean age, and study design, were collected. Also, we mentioned the LRV detection method, number of patients, Leishmania spp. type of identified LRV (LRV1/LRV2), number of LRV-positive cases, clinical manifestation, and sample type for LRV detection. Other authors reviewed the data for potential mistakes and then confirmed by all authors.

Risk of bias in individual studies (quality assessment)

The risk of bias in individual studies was evaluated using the Newcastle-Ottawa scale (NOS) [19], with 9 points for case-control and cohort studies, indicating excellent quality and low risk of bias. The studies 1–3, 4–6, and 7–9 were rated as low, moderate, and high quality, respectively.

Risk of bias across studies

The Egger test and Begg’s Funnel plots assessed the publication bias. For heterogeneity, P values under 0.05 were considered.

Statistical analysis

Stata version 15 was used to calculate the effect size and the 95% CI. Moreover, the publication bias was assessed by calculating the P-value with Stata software. We measured the heterogeneity of each group using the inconsistency index (I2). An I2 greater than 50% or P lower than 0.05 is recognized as significant heterogeneity. If the heterogeneity was high, a random-effect model was used to calculate the pooling effect and 95% CI. Otherwise, the fixed effect was used. Subgroup analysis was performed to investigate the overall results based on LRV (LRV 1, LRV 2, LRV LAE, LRV 1–2), Reign (OW or NW), metastatic (Yes or No), and detection method (Electrophoresis, PCR, IFM-Dot plot-PCR-IFA, PCR-Sequencing, Sequencing).

Result

Following a thorough examination, 44 full-text articles deemed suitable for inclusion were incorporated into this study. The selected articles span a publication period from December 1988, marking the year when LRV was first molecularly identified in L.(V) guyanensis [20], to August 2024, covering a total of 35 years and including the latest research on LRV. Notably, over half of the studies (63%) were conducted in the NW region. Various species of Leishmania, responsible for CL and VL, were investigated across the included studies, including L. (V.) panamensis, L. (V.) guyanensis, Leishmania (V.) braziliensis, L. (V.) amazonensis, L. (L.) major, L. (L.) tropica, and L. (L.) aethiopica.

In the studies analyzed, LRV detection was performed in both biopsied fragments from lesions and in isolates derived from these lesions after routine culture. All isolates and lesion biopsies were obtained from CL patients. The majority of the studies performed LRV detection in the cultured parasites (isolates) following appropriate culture procedures, while a smaller subset also conducted direct LRV detection in lesion biopsies. Among the 2,276 CL patients analyzed, 647 tested positive for LRV, resulting in a prevalence rate of 28.43%. All isolates and lesion biopsies were obtained from CL patients, with most undergoing routine culture before LRV detection.

LRV1/LRV2 has been established in various Leishmania species associated with CL. Notably, L. (L.) major, L. (L.) tropica, and L. (L.) aethiopica, harboring LRV2 type, predominated in studies conducted in the OW. Conversely, Leishmania species such as L. (V.) braziliensis, L. (V.) amazonensis, L. (V.) panamensis, and L. (V.) guyanensis were found to contain the LRV1 type, with predominant application in most studies. Different molecular techniques, including Real-time PCR and RT-PCR, were utilized across the reviewed articles for LRV detection. The comprehensive list of included articles is shown in Table 1.

Meta-analysis results

Among all samples, 647 tested positive for LRV, while 1629 tested negative. Given the methodological diversity across studies, random effect models were employed to account for variability. The inverse variance index (I2) was utilized to assess the heterogeneity across the included studies. Results indicated a high level of heterogeneity (I2 = 92.6%, p = 0.000) within the reviewed literature. Utilizing forest plots and employing random effect model analysis, the overall pooled prevalence of LRV was estimated to be 28.43% with a 95% confidence interval (CI) of 21.86–35.01 (Fig. 2).

Subgroup analysis

Geographically, leishmaniasis is categorized into the OW, where LRV2 predominates (encompassing western Asia, the Middle East, Europe except for southern regions, and Africa), and the NW, comprising Latin America. In the NW, LRV1 prevails, while LRV2 is predominant in the Old World, with prevalence rates of 45.16% and 30%, respectively (Fig. 3). Among the 647 samples testing positive for LRV, the highest prevalence of the virus within parasites was observed in L. (V.) guyanensis (41.39%), followed by L. (V.) braziliensis (33.1%), L. (L.) major (38.5%), L. (L.) tropica (20.6%), and other Leishmania species (6.94%), respectively.

Discussion

This study comprehensively analyzes LRV prevalence across various Leishmania species associated with human CL. The reported prevalence of LRV across these species, ranging from 4 to 70%, underscores its significant presence within Leishmania parasites. This substantial prevalence may contribute to atypical clinical manifestations, recurrent infections, and increased disease severity. Our findings confirm the broad distribution of LRV in both NW and OW species, with consistent detection in both lesion biopsies and cultured isolates.

However, beyond merely establishing prevalence, the influence of LRV on disease progression, immune response modulation, and treatment outcomes remains a subject of considerable interest and ongoing scholarly discussion. This emphasizes the necessity for further research to fully elucidate the clinical significance of LRV.

Saberi et al. conducted a meta-analysis in 2019, revealing a higher prevalence of LRV in the NW (39.1%) compared to the OW (8.4%) [58]. This difference could be attributed to the greater number of studies and samples utilized in the NW settings. However, a review by Shita et al. in 2022 presented contrasting results, indicating a higher prevalence of LRV in the OW (52.1%) compared to the NW (38.1%) [59]. Notably, our findings suggest a relatively similar prevalence of LRVs in both the OW (34.2%) and NW (30.9%), highlighting a convergence in LRV prevalence across geographical regions.

In the OW, Leishmania species such as L. (L.) major, L. (L.) aethiopica, and L. (L.) tropica have increased LRV prevalence, attributed to an expanded scope of sample examination and a surge in research endeavors within this domain. Saberi et al. (2020) examined 85 samples of L. (L.) tropica and L. (L.) major isolated from CL patients in Iran, revealing that 59 out of 85 CL samples were positive for LRV2 [34].

The Farrokhi-Karibozorg et al.’s (2022) study in Iran identified four instances of Leishmania major and one instance of L. (L.) turanica among the five rodents sampled. Additionally, LRV2 was detected exclusively in 15.3% of CL cases, which included L. (L.) major and L. (L.) tropica. Notably, there were no reports of LRV2 detection in rodent reservoirs [46].

COVID-19 emerged as a potential contributor to the variance in prevalence rates of LRV between OW and NW regions. Recent studies across various endemic areas of leishmaniasis have underscored the significant influence of the COVID-19 pandemic on the prevalence of CL globally, including such regions as Iran [60]. In some areas, the prevalence of CL has shown a marked increase post-COVID-19, attributed to factors such as reduced access of CL patients to healthcare facilities for diagnosis and treatment owing to the apprehension surrounding COVID-19 exposure. Consequently, rigorous investigations into the heightened prevalence of CL and its potential resistance to treatment have been prioritized, particularly in the wake of the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic 2019. These efforts aim to elucidate the multifaceted reasons behind the observed surge in CL cases, thereby informing targeted interventions and healthcare strategies [61].

In contrast to the numerous reports of LRV1 in the NW, there has been limited research on LRV2 in the OW. The initial isolation of LRV2 from L. (L.) major was conducted by Widmer and Dooley in 1995, followed by subsequent investigations by researchers from Iran, Turkey, and Uzbekistan [62]. In Iran, Hajjaran et al. (2016) demonstrated the presence of LRV2 in one isolate of L. (L.) infantum obtained from a VL patient, as well as in a L. (L.) major isolate from a Rhombomys optimus gerbil [30]. Recently, the highest prevalence of LRV2 was reported in Golestan province of Iran (69.4%), contrasting with the global prevalence estimate of 26.2% for LRV overall [48].

MCL represents one of the most intricate and severe clinical manifestations of leishmaniasis, characterized by its resistance to spontaneous resolution and the necessity of treatment. Unlike CL, MCL does not exhibit self-limiting tendencies. Adaui et al. (2016) provided evidence supporting the involvement of the LRV in the pathogenesis of MCL [13].

In our study, which included various clinical forms of CL, such as ML/MCL and DCL/DL, different prevalence rates of LRV were observed. Specifically, LRV prevalence was 22.3% in ML/MCL cases, 21.8% in DCL/DL cases, and 36.3% in localized CL cases. Notably, a higher prevalence of LRV was detected in L. (V.) braziliensis (n = 570) and L. (V.) guyanensis (n = 505) species known to cause MCL, DCL, or metastatic forms—compared to LCL or non-metastatic forms. DCL is primarily caused by L. (V.) amazonensis or L. (V.) mexicana in the NW, is a rare but distinct clinical form that was not included in this analysis.

These findings are in the same line with previous reports describing the role of LRV in exacerbating CL severity, leading to severe, disfiguring, and metastatic forms of the disease.

Based on the findings from multiple studies, including the systematic review of LRV prevalence by Saberi et al. (2019), it has been observed that within the L. Viannia complexes, such as L. (V.) guyanensis and L. (V.) braziliensis, which are implicated in MCL, LRVs exhibit a higher prevalence [58]. This observation suggests a hypothesis that posits a potential link between the heightened prevalence of RNA viruses within the parasites causing metastatic leishmaniasis and the exacerbation of disease and clinical symptoms, particularly in NW regions.

Saberi et al.’s (2019) [58] findings suggested that the highest prevalence of LRV among leishmaniasis cases in the NW might be associated with metastatic clinical forms of the disease in endemic regions. This conclusion was corroborated by the review article by Shita et al. (2022) [59], further supporting our results.

Another study by Ives et al. (2011) [17] demonstrated that LRV1 significantly burdened metastasizing Leishmania parasites. These parasites are recognized by TLR3 on the host’s immune cells, triggering the secretion of chemokines and proinflammatory cytokines, which can lead to tissue damage and disfigurement. This underscores the notion that the presence of LRV alone may not be the sole factor contributing to the severity of the disease. Contrary to these findings, Valencia et al. (2022) [43] reported that the presence of LRV did not correlate with various clinical forms of Leishmania, treatment failure, or disease deterioration.

Across all studies, based on geographic location, there is a notable variability in the prevalence of LRVs, particularly LRV1 and LRV2. For example, Gonzalez et al. (2023) [52] and Kariyawasam et al. (2024) [57] both found LRV1 in L. (V.) panamensis isolates, with 20% and 23% rates, respectively, in Panama and Latin America. A key similarity in all studies is the lack of a consistent, clear association between the presence of LRVs and the severity of leishmaniasis. Most studies, including Gonzalez et al. (2023) [52], Bonilla et al. (2024) [56], and Kariyawasam (2024) [57], found that although LRVs were detected in certain isolates, there was no significant difference in disease severity between those with and without the virus. This suggests that while LRVs might be present in some Leishmania isolates, they do not necessarily correlate with more severe clinical forms of the disease, such as MCL.

Many studies, including Gonzalez et al. (2023) [52], Kariyawasam et al. (2023) [57], and Bonilla et al. (2024) [56], found LRV1 in L. (V.) panamensis. These studies suggest that LRV1 is relatively common in this species, though the prevalence rates vary (9–23%). This indicates that L. (V.) panamensis might be a common host for LRV1, particularly in Latin American regions. Several studies, such as Rêgo et al. (2023) [54] and Rosales-Chilama et al. (2023) [51] note the importance of having larger sample sizes to understand the role of LRVs in leishmaniasis better. Small sample sizes in many studies limited the ability to draw more definitive conclusions, but the findings still provide valuable insights into the distribution and prevalence of LRVs in different Leishmania species.

A significant contrast between the studies lies in the prevalence of LRV1 and LRV2 across different Leishmania species and geographical regions. Gonzalez et al. (2023) [52], Kariyawasam et al. (2024) [57], and Bonilla et al. (2024) [56] all focused on L. (V.) panamensis and found that LRV1 was present, with rates ranging from 9 to 23%. In contrast, Mazaherifar et al. (2024) [55] and Rêgo et al. (2023) [54] focused on different species and found higher rates of LRV2, particularly in L. (L.) major and L. (L.) infantum, respectively. Mazaherifar et al. (2024) [55] found LRV2 in 27.6% of L. (L.) major isolates from Iran, while Rêgo et al. (2023) [54] detected LRV2 in only 2.8% of L. (L.) infantum isolates in Brazil. This contrast underscores the possibility that LRV1 and LRV2 may be differentially distributed across species and regions. For instance, LRV1 seems more associated with L. (V.) panamensis in the NW, whereas LRV2 is more prevalent in OW species such as L. (L.) major.

Rosales-Chilama et al. (2023) [51] reported LRV1 exclusively in L. (V.) braziliensis strains from the Amazon Basin, with no LRV1 detected in L. (V.) panamensis. This highlights a contrast in the viral distribution between L. (V.) braziliensis and L. (V.) panamensis, a difference not seen in the studies from Panama or Latin America, where LRV1 was found in both species. The studies also differ in interpretation of the clinical relevance of LRV presence. For instance, Gonzalez et al. (2023) [52] and Bonilla et al. (2024) [56] found no significant association between LRV1 presence and lesion severity, supporting the idea that LRVs may not directly contribute to disease progression in L. (V.) panamensis infections. On the other hand, Kariyawasam et al. (2024) [57] and Mazaherifar et al. (2024) [55] obtained similar results in terms of LRV1 presence, but the study by Mazaherifar et al. (2024) [55], focusing on L. (L.) major, revealed higher rates of LRV2 without a clear correlation with clinical severity. This suggests that even though viral presence is detectable in many Leishmania isolates, it may not be a primary driver of disease severity across different species and regions. Our study found a 20% prevalence of LRV2 in 20 clinical Leishmania isolates (10 L. (L.) tropica and 10 L. (L.) major), with positive results in 3 out of 10 L. (L.) tropica and one out of 10 L. (L.) major samples. This prevalence is consistent with regional variations observed in previous studies, such as those in Turkey and Iran. For instance, a study conducted by Nalçacı et al. (2010) [37] in Turkey reported LRV2 in 10 of 29 isolates L. (L.) major and L. (L.) tropica (34%) to be positive. The lower rate of 20% in our study is consistent with the variability of LRV2 prevalence reported across different geographical regions. Notably, the prevalence of LRV2 was lower in the studies conducted in Iran [30], where only 4% of 50 isolates from L. (L.) major, L. (L.) tropica, and L. (L.) infantum tested positive for LRV2.

The studies like that of Gonzalez et al. (2023) [52] note gender-based differences in LRV1 prevalence (with higher rates in females), the impact of these factors on disease outcomes was not explored in depth in other studies. This highlights the variability in how host factors, such as immune responses and hormonal differences, may influence the prevalence and clinical outcomes of LRV infections.

The distribution of LRV1 demonstrates a marked association with L. (V.) guyanensis and L. (V.) braziliensis in the NW. Notably, L. (V.) panamensis appears to be the predominant species associated with LRV1 in Central America. This observation suggests a discernible regional variation in the host-virus relationship, wherein LRV1 exhibits a higher prevalence within specific Leishmania species across distinct geographical regions. Conversely, OW species, such as L. (L.) major, predominantly harbor LRV2.

This observed distinction underscores the importance of conducting region-specific investigations when examining the role of LRVs in leishmaniasis. Across the studies reviewed, a conclusive association between LRV presence and disease severity was not established. This finding implies that these viruses may not directly exert a universal influence on disease progression. However, it is plausible that regional, species-specific, and host-related factors, including migration patterns, may modulate their effects.

While considerable similarities exist in the findings regarding LRV prevalence and its clinical implications, the studies also reveal significant contrasts in viral distribution, the potential impact of migration, and the variability in the relationship between viral presence and disease severity. Consequently, further research, particularly involving larger sample sizes and cross-species comparisons, is warranted to elucidate the complex role of LRVs in leishmaniasis.

Paranaiba et al. (2018) [63] reported that L. (L.) major, which exhibited a 38.5% LRV prevalence in our study, typically induces self-healing lesions, whereas L. (L.) tropica (20.6% prevalence) is more frequently associated with chronic cutaneous leishmaniasis (CCL), a condition characterized by persistent and recurrent lesions.

The increased pathogenicity of NW Leishmania species may be attributed to their capacity to modulate the host immune system through TLR3 and type I interferon (IFN-I) signaling pathways. This observation aligns with our findings, which revealed a significantly higher LRV prevalence in metastatic cases (30.6%) compared with non-metastatic cases (25.34%). Atayde et al. (2019) [14] and Ives et al. (2011) [17] have linked this inflammatory response to treatment resistance, particularly in LRV1-bearing parasites, which exhibit reduced clearance following standard pentavalent antimonial (SbV) therapy.

LRV1 exhibits a strong association with mucosal involvement and disease dissemination [64, 65]. Our study further investigated LRV prevalence across distinct clinical forms of CL, revealing rates of 36.0% in CL, 22.36% in ML/MCL, and 2.88% in DCL/DL. Hartley et al. (2014) [66] have previously documented lower cure rates for LRV1-positive L. (V) guyanensis and L. (V) braziliensis infections treated with SbV therapy, which corroborates our data demonstrating higher LRV prevalence in severe disease forms.

Conclusion

Our findings are consistent with previous research indicating that LRV1 is frequently associated with metastatic and severe forms of CL, potentially contributing to immune modulation and treatment resistance. However, the role of LRV2 in exacerbating CL remains unclear. The variability in findings across studies suggests that the mere presence of LRV may not reliably predict disease severity. Instead, host-related factors, environmental influences, and migration patterns likely contribute to LRV distribution and its impact on clinical outcomes.

Given the observed regional variations and the complex interactions between LRVs, Leishmania species, and host immune responses, further large-scale studies are essential. Future research should prioritize broader geographical coverage, standardized methodologies, and in-depth investigations into the mechanisms underlying LRV-associated pathogenesis. A comprehensive understanding of these factors will be critical for refining disease management strategies and developing targeted therapeutic interventions.
