Deep learning models for the prediction of acute postoperative pain in PACU for video‐assisted thoracoscopic surgery
Authors: Cao Zhang, Jiangqin He, Xingyuan Liang, Qinye Shi, Lijia Peng, Shuai Wang, Jiannan He, Jianhong Xu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative pain is a prevalent symptom experienced by patients undergoing surgical procedures. This study aims to develop deep learning algorithms for predicting acute postoperative pain using both essential patient details and real-time vital sign data during surgery.MethodsThrough a retrospective observational approach, we utilized Graph Attention Networks (GAT) and graph Transformer Networks (GTN) deep learning algorithms to construct the DoseFormer model while incorporating an attention mechanism. This model employed patient information and intraoperative vital signs obtained during Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) surgery to anticipate postoperative pain. By categorizing the static and dynamic data, the DoseFormer model performed binary classification to predict the likelihood of postoperative acute pain.ResultsA total of 1758 patients were initially included, with 1552 patients after data cleaning. These patients were then divided into training set (n = 931) and testing set (n = 621). In the testing set, the DoseFormer model exhibited significantly higher AUROC (0.98) compared to classical machine learning algorithms. Furthermore, the DoseFormer model displayed a significantly higher F1 value (0.85) in comparison to other classical machine learning algorithms. Notably, the attending anesthesiologists' F1 values (attending: 0.49, fellow: 0.43, Resident: 0.16) were significantly lower than those of the DoseFormer model in predicting acute postoperative pain.ConclusionsDeep learning model can predict postoperative acute pain events based on patients' basic information and intraoperative vital signs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12874-024-02357-5.Keywords: Acute postoperative pain, Attention mechanism, Deep learning, Graph attention network, Graph transformer networks, Video‐assisted thoracoscopic surgery

Background

Surgical injury procedures often lead to postoperative pain, which can contribute to various complications such as organ dysfunctions, increased morbidity, extended hospital stays, and even chronic pain. After undergoing Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS), an estimated 12.7–38% of patients report experiencing postoperative pain within a 24-h period [1, 2]. The management of acute postoperative pain after VATS still presents a great potential for quality improvement [3]. Previous research has primarily focused on exploring different pain management methods, providing anesthesiologists with numerous options to enhance postoperative pain control. Nevertheless, effective management of postoperative pain remains a critical challenge encountered by clinical anesthesiologists.

The identification of patients experiencing postoperative pain is crucial for effective pain management. Unfortunately, patients who undergo VATS were often under general anesthesia, rendering them unconscious and making pain evaluation challenging. Numerous scholars have dedicated their efforts to developing objective evaluation methods for intraoperative nociceptive stimulation. These methods aim to assess pain levels in unconscious patients, guide the administration of analgesic drugs, and reduce the incidence of postoperative pain. Currently, there are four main evaluation methods being utilized in clinical practice: Analgesia and Nociception Index (ANI), Intraoperative Volume Index (SPI), Pupil Pain Index (PPI), and Nociception Level Index (NOL) [4]. Although these indicators may aid anesthesiologists in improving the control of analgesic drug administration, their calculation often necessitates the use of additional monitoring equipment, which can make the deployment of these indicators expensive and may limit their widespread adoption in clinical settings.

Deep learning or machine learning is widely used in the field of anesthesia, with applications ranging from assisting ultrasound-guided puncture technology in anesthesia to predicting intraoperative hypotension and Bispectral Index [5–7]. In recent years, with the rapid progress of deep learning methods, many scholars began to try to apply machine learning and deep learning methods to the field of pain prediction. Several studies have explored the application of machine learning in pain prediction, particularly in chronic pain research [8, 9]. Davoudi had used CatBoost machine learning models to predict the binary outcome of low (≤ 4) and high pain (> 4), and The area under the curve was 0.71 [10].

Deep learning models were also performed in pain predict area. Teoh’s team set up a model combined VGG16, GAP, and KNN that gave a maximum of 89.75% accuracy and 0.91 Cohen's kappa scores in predicting Knee Osteoarthritis Pain [11]. And in cancer pain, Bang investigated the clinical relevance of deep learning models to predict the onset of cancer pain exacerbation, demonstrating that a long short-term memory-based model exhibited good performance [12]. In pediatric pain assessment, deep learning-based models have emerged as a promising tool, offering the potential for swift and more impartial determination of pain levels [13].

Yet, in the realm of acute postoperative pain management, there remains a notable absence of scholarly exploration utilizing deep learning techniques. Traditionally, anesthesiologists have relied on basic health metrics—such as a patient's height, weight, blood pressure, and heart rate—during surgery to anticipate and manage pain. These vital signs are critical in guiding the choice and dosage of analgesics to preempt postoperative discomfort. To bridge this gap, we propose a deep learning-based postoperative pain prediction system called DoseFormer to enhance acute postoperative pain management. In our methodology, we've incorporated not only the deep learning techniques but also benchmarked them against traditional machine learning models. This comparative analysis allows us to evaluate and enhance the efficacy of each model. The result is a robust system crafted to empower anesthesiologists with the tools to forecast and adeptly handle postoperative pain with greater precision.

Participants

The patients who underwent VATS at the Fourth Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine from January 2018 to January 2022 were included in this study. Patients younger than 18 years or patients unable to tell NRS pain score were excluded. In order to make better use of existing data, we employ a tenfold cross-validation on the training set and use early stopping to train the model. We stop the training process when the validation accuracy did not improve for a long period (e.g., 20,000 out of 238,000 steps), (Figs. 1 and 2).

Study design and models. In total, 1552 eligible patients were assigned to the training and test data sets. Model endpoint definition

The occurrence of acute postoperative pain was extracted as a binary event (0,1) based upon the Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) scores in post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) obtained from the Docare system. In the PACU, it is essential to assess the NRS score on at least two separate occasions: once when the patient is fully alert and again before they are discharged from the PACU. In the PACU, an NRS score exceeding 3 denotes the presence of significant pain, scored as 1, while an NRS score below or equal to 3 indicates adequate pain control, scored as 0.

Model input features

Many different procedures may be performed in a single operation for each patient. Under the guidance of experts, these surgical methods were divided into 16 categories according to the degree of harmful irritation caused to the patient during the operation. The surgical information was then encoded using 16 binary variables.

The administration of anesthesia and analgesic drugs during surgery represented crucial aspects of pain management conducted by anesthesiologists. Both variables were documented within the DoCare system. Anesthesia methods encompass a qualitative characteristic that indicates the specific approach employed, such as general anesthesia or a combination of general anesthesia and regional nerve block. One-Hot coding is utilized to convert non-numerical features into a numerical format. Multiple types of analgesic drugs were employed in anesthesia, and the dataset analysis revealed the presence of fentanyl, remifentanil, sufentanil, oxycodone, and hydromorphone. Given that remifentanil is classified as an ultra-short-acting analgesic, its omission from this analysis is justified, as we postulate its negligible influence on postoperative pain. Regarding the other analgesic drugs, the conversion of dosage was performed using the opioid equivalent dose conversion formula.

To get a dense representation of each patient’s feature, we encode the characteristics as a numerical vector. In this process, the distinctive patient characteristics were fed as input into the Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) network [14], followed by the initialization of the hidden state and cell state within the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) module [15] of a dynamic encoder. This initialization procedure effectively emulates the prior knowledge of an anesthesiologist when monitoring the dynamic signs exhibited during the surgical procedure. In total, 72 features are extracted from the original data set.

Attention model

According to clinical experience, an anesthetist usually pays more attention to some periods that represent the state change of the patient. Therefore, we want to extract important information from the LSTM outputs across all time steps. In this module, we use the attention method to deal with the information extraction problem, which is mostly used in sequence prediction and classification. We divided the anaesthesia process into five time-intervals, labelled Time0 to Time5. Time0 refers to the preparation time, which is from when the patient enters the operating room until the surgery commences. Development of the model

The DoseFormer model employs a dynamic encoder to initialize static features and extract mixed semantic features for each patient. The process of constructing the model is depicted in Figs. 1 and 2. Firstly, empirical evidence from clinical practice indicates a correlation between the occurrence of postoperative acute pain and the physiological state of patients. Anesthesiologists depend on the physiological condition of patients to evaluate their pain levels. Thus, the patients sharing comparable physiological profiles tend to exhibit analogous postoperative pain manifestations. Secondly, we introduce a dynamic forward process that enhanced the representation of patient data by employing static-dynamic fused embeddings for constructing weighted graphs, which serve as input for the graph neural network. Lastly, we employ Graph Attention Networks (GAT) [16] and graph Transformer Networks (GTN) [17] to build the graph neural network and compute the final patients’ feature for postoperative pain. We employ GTN to further analyze the data, in order to augment the representational capacity of the graph neural network and align it with the dynamic forward process of graph construction. GTN facilitates node classification and predicts the likelihood of postoperative pain based on the patients' present condition.

Dynamic encoder

We design a dynamic encoder based on a 3-layer 1D convolutional neural network (CNN), a LSTM model and an attention module. The dynamic encoder aims to capture the dynamic features during the operation, such as systolic arterial pressure, heart rate, and oxygen saturation, based on the patient’s static features, such as age, sex, and type of operation, and fuse both of them using an attention module. The temporal data is sliced into 15 min windows. Concretely, for each patient, the dynamic features f∈Rn×7×5 are fed into the CNN-LSTM module, which is initialized by the static features s∈Rd, and forms an encoded feature O∈Rn×d, where 7 represents the number of feature types from the various modalities, and n×5 denotes n time slices with a length of 5 for the operation and d is the intermediate feature size.

The attention module then attends to time slices O based on the static features and produces a static-dynamic fused feature hx by Eq. (3).

Graph transformer network

The postoperative pain prediction problem can be treated as a node classification problem in the context of graph neural networks. Therefore, we construct a patient graph by similarity between the patients’ embeddings. Using the features obtained from dynamic encoder, we compute the cosine similarity between the patients using sa,b=ha→·hb→ha→·hb→, where ha→ and hb→ are the patients’ feature embeddings.

Subsequently, we prune the patients’ graph by a threshold θ, which means the patients less than θ similarity do not connect together. For the patients who have links, the similarity sa,b is treated as the weight of the links. Thus we obtain a simple undirected weighted graph G=V,E, V=n1,n2,⋯,nN is the node set and E=e1,e2,⋯,eM is the edge set, where V=N and E=M.

Implementation

We implement our method and baselines with Pytorch 1.13.0 in Python 3.7 environment and train it with 128 GB memory and two GeForce RTX 3090 GPUs.

The hidden-size is set to 160, and the similarity threshold θ is set to cosπ4=22 which is searched in the space 1,cosπ6,cosπ5,cosπ4,cosπ3. We apply Adam optimizer, and the learning rate is set to 1e-4.

The 1D-CNN model comprises three 1 × 3 1D-convolutional layers with 80 channels, window size 3 and stride 1, followed by a global average pooling layer and the LSTM model is bidirectional with 80 hidden size. The graph transformer model uses 2 transformer blocks with a hidden size 160 and initialize the parameters uniformly in [-0.1,0.1]. The classifier uses a single layer linear transformation, projecting the hidden features into a 2-class probability distribution. The activation function in the CNN-LSTM model and the graph transformer model are ReLU and GELU respectively.

Outcome measurement

The primary outcome was the area under the receiver operating characteristic (AUROC) curve for the classification models. The secondary outcomes included the accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and the F1-score. DoseFormer model would compare with powerful classic machine learning algorithms, including logistic regression (LR), support vector machine (SVM), k nearest neighbor (KNN), random forest (RF), extra trees (ET), extreme gradient boosting (XGB).

To enhance the assessment of Doseformer's model performance, we conducted a random selection of 100 patients and shared comprehensive patient information with three varying levels of anesthesiologists: an attending, a fellow, and a resident. Independently, each anesthesiologist utilized their own expertise to determine whether the patients were likely to experience pain in the PACU. Afterwards, we compared the judgments of the model with those of the anesthesiologists at each level.

Discussion

This study shows that a deep learning model can predict acute postoperative pain in PACU after VATS based on automatically extractable and objective intraoperative data. We developed a novel GNT-based deep learning model called DoseFormer for predicting acute post-VATS pain. The F1 Score of DoseFormer was better than classic machine learning algorithms. This conclusion highlights the efficacy of deeply enhanced deep learning methods for forecasting postoperative pain.

Postoperative pain is an uncomfortable post-operative experience that can also interfere with recovery. VATS surgery has one of the highest incidence of postoperative pain. Currently, numerous clinical studies are being conducted to alleviate postoperative pain in VATS, such as developed novel nerve block [18], improved operate produce [19], using patient-control-analgesia [20] and so on [21]. But all these studies were force on the pain that patients were already felt, and what is more important for anesthesiologist is prevent postoperative pain. Therefore predicted pain during general anesthesia was very important. Kumar and his mates [22] used 4 different algorithms to develop predictive models for 24-h fentanyl requirement, 24-h pain scores, and time for first analgesic. The most accurate algorithms in predicting 24-h postoperative fentanyl requirement and pain scores were SVM-Linear; meanwhile, the RF algorithm was the most effective in forecasting the time required for the first analgesic. These findings illustrate the ability of traditional machine learning algorithms to accurately predict pain. In our study, we also applied the RF and SVM algorithms to forecast postoperative pain. When we compared these traditional methods with our in-house developed deep learning model, the DoseFormer, we discovered that our novel model offered a substantially higher level of predictive accuracy. This comparison demonstrates the potential of advanced deep learning techniques to surpass conventional methods in predicting and managing postoperative pain.

One of the most appealing aspects of this model is its ability to deliver a completely automated and extremely dependable approach to calculating a patient's postoperative pain risk following anaesthesia. All data contained in the risk score are easily obtained from the electronic medical record and could be automatically loaded into a model. Importantly, anesthesiologists no longer require additional equipment or monitoring to determine the likelihood of acute pain after surgery. Previous methods for monitoring analgesia, such as Nociception Level Index (NOL[) [23], Analgesia/Nociception Index (ANI) [24], Surgical Pleth Index (SPI) [25], and others, have not been widely adopted in clinical practice due to their limited ability to predict postoperative pain and the need for extra monitoring equipment [23–25]. In other side, these methods primarily rely on the principle that pain can induce changes in the autonomic nervous system, leading to alterations in blood pressure, heart rate, and other vital signs. Therefore, we added dynamic intraoperative vital signs when designing the pain prediction model, utilizing LSTM and attention mechanism, and unsupervised learning can benefit from detection of clinically imperceptible and subtle changes that cannot be recognised by human visualisation, so as to predict postoperative acute pain.

In our comparison between the DoseFormer model and the anesthesiologists, the results revealed that the DoseFormer model outperformed in distinguishing the occurrence of postoperative pain. It demonstrated superior sensitivity, specificity, and other performance indicators. While this underscores the strength of our developed model, it is worth noting that anesthesiologists are generally expected to possess better predictive skills for postoperative pain than our results. This could be attributed to the fact that our model only leverages vital signs information provided every 5 min, whereas anesthesiologists can observe vital sign waveforms and discern trends within shorter intervals (within 5 min) during surgery. This ability allows them to gather more information and enhance the accuracy of postoperative pain prediction. Thus, the five-minute interval for vital sign information we provide does not fully reflect the predictive capabilities of anesthesiologists.

The attention mechanism within the DoseFormer indicates that artificial intelligence could focus on the stages of the initial and final of the operation. Moreover, anaesthesiologists focus more on these two periods. Attention mechanism would allow the DoseFormer model to better focus on the details of important periods of time, and reinforcing its ability to capture critical moments in the surgical process for predicting postoperative pain.

Study limitations

Our study has several limitations. Firstly, it relies on a single data source, which may introduce bias and limit the generalizability of our findings. Additionally, external validation was lacking, further reducing the robustness of our results. Moreover, our selection of VATS patients, who have a higher incidence of postoperative pain, introduces a selection bias that may affect the applicability of our findings to a broader population. To improve the generalizability of our DoseFormer model, future research should consider including a wider range of surgical procedures, such as general, gynaecological, thoracic, and urogenital surgeries. This will contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of the model's effectiveness across different surgical contexts. Another limitation is related to the data collection process. The AIS collects patient's biological data at a low frequency, specifically every 5 min during surgery. This limited sampling frequency may not accurately depict the real waveform observed by the anesthesiologist and may result in the omission of important details. And real-time waveforms like atrial pressure and electrocardiogram may make the DoseFormer model better.

Conclusion

This study establishes a significant advancement by showcasing the novel application of deep learning in predicting postoperative pain using diverse intraoperative patient data. The proposed deep learning model, DoseFormer outperforms traditional machine learning models in terms of predictive accuracy. Ultimately, when compared with anesthesiologists, the DoseFormer model demonstrates superior capability in predicting postoperative pain. Through this research, we have unlocked new possibilities for leveraging deep learning techniques in revolutionizing pain prediction in the postoperative setting.
