CK7-negative and CK20-Positive intestinal- type gastric adenocarcinoma metastasis to the bladder: A case report of an unprecedented phenotype
Authors: Ferhat Çoban, Merve Doğan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The majority of bladder tumors (around 90 %) are urothelial carcinomas, while metastatic secondary tumors are extremely rare, comprising less than 2 % of cases. Gastric adenocarcinoma rarely metastasizes to the bladder, and these metastases typically exhibit a CK7-positive, CK20-negative phenotype. To date, there have been no reports of gastric cancer metastasis to the bladder that displays a CK7-negative and CK20-positive profile. This article presents a rare case of a patient who had previously undergone subtotal gastrectomy for gastric adenocarcinoma and later presented with macroscopic hematuria, where a CK7-negative, CK20-positive secondary adenocarcinoma was detected in the bladder.

Introduction

According to the most recent data, approximately 5.2 % of all new cancer cases in Turkey are diagnosed as bladder cancer.1 It is the fourth most common malignancy in men in Turkey.2 Histopathologically, 90 % of bladder cancers are urothelial carcinomas. Platinum-based combination chemotherapy has been shown to provide significant clinical benefit in patients with advanced-stage disease.3

Metastatic secondary bladder tumors, on the other hand, account for less than 2 % of all bladder tumors. The most common metastatic secondary tumor is malignant melanoma, followed by breast cancer and gastric cancer.4 Secondary adenocarcinomas of the bladder are considered chemotherapy-resistant, and a standard chemotherapy regimen has not been defined.5 Therefore, a differential diagnosis of both primary and secondary adenocarcinomas of the bladder must be made. Although rare, if adenocarcinoma is diagnosed in the bladder, a thorough investigation for gastrointestinal malignancies is necessary.6

Metastatic gastric adenocarcinoma to the bladder is extremely rare and typically presents with a phenotype of Cytokeratin 7-positive and Cytokeratin 20-negative (CK7, CK20). To date, there have been no reported cases in the literature of gastric adenocarcinoma with a CK7-negative and CK20-positive immunohistochemical profile metastasizing to the bladder. This article presents a case of a patient who underwent subtotal gastrectomy two years ago, with no other focal lesions detected on whole-body scans, and was diagnosed with gastric adenocarcinoma. The patient later presented with macroscopic hematuria, and a solid bladder tumor was detected. Transurethral resection (TUR) of the tumor was performed, and a diagnosis of secondary adenocarcinoma of the bladder was established.

Case presentation

A 58-year-old male patient presented to the urology clinic with a complaint of macroscopic hematuria occurring twice in the past 3 months. His medical history revealed that two years ago, he underwent subtotal gastrectomy after a mass was detected in the gastric antrum. The pathology report indicated a 5 cm tumor with positive serosal invasion, diagnosed as a well-differentiated adenocarcinoma. The tumor was identified as an intestinal-type adenocarcinoma located in the antrum, with a CK7-negative and CK20-positive immunophenotype. Following surgery, the patient received 4 cycles of oxaliplatin + capecitabine chemotherapy, after which he was monitored. During follow-up, multiple nodules were found in the mesenteric area, prompting the administration of 6 cycles of fluorouracil + irinotecan chemotherapy. Additionally, the patient had a history of diabetes mellitus and a 15-pack-year smoking history. His family history included breast cancer in his sister.

On physical examination, there were no significant findings except for tenderness on palpation in the suprapubic region. A urinary system ultrasound revealed no abnormalities except for grade 3 right-sided hydronephrosis, leading to a contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) scan. The CT scan revealed a mass measuring 6.5 × 4.5 cm extending from the base of the bladder into the perivesical area (Fig. 1). Cystoscopy performed by our team revealed a mass at the bladder base, with bullous-like structures on the surface and more solid structures deeper in the lesion. The mass was found to invade the right ureteral orifice. A double J catheter was placed in the right ureter, and transurethral resection was performed on the bladder mass (Fig. 2). Due to incomplete resection, as the tumor base could not be accessed during the procedure, only partial resection was achieved. The procedure was concluded after cauterization. The pathology report confirmed the presence of adenocarcinoma with a CK7-negative, CK20-positive, and CDX2-positive immunophenotype. The patient's upper gastrointestinal endoscopy performed two years prior, along with biopsy material from the stomach and subtotal gastrectomy, showed CK7-negative and CK20-positive results. After pathology results were obtained, the patient was referred to oncology. It was decided to initiate paclitaxel therapy. Following the second cycle of paclitaxel, imaging showed regression of the pelvic mass. After resolution of right-sided hydronephrosis, the double J catheter was removed. The patient has been followed up by both the urology and oncology teams for the past 5 months without any complications, and he is clinically stable.

Discussion

Bladder tumors are predominantly of primary urothelial origin, with urothelial carcinoma observed in nearly 90 % of cases. Adenocarcinomas, on the other hand, are rare among bladder tumors, and a significant proportion of them are secondary, meaning they result from metastases of primary malignancies from other organs. Secondary bladder tumors account for less than 2 % of all bladder tumors, but when adenocarcinomas are detected, the possibility of metastatic origin should always be considered.6

Among the primary malignancies that can metastasize to the bladder are breast, lung, prostate cancers, malignant melanoma, and gastric cancer. The metastasis of gastric adenocarcinomas to the bladder is extremely rare. This spread typically occurs in advanced-stage tumors via peritoneal surfaces, hematogenous, or lymphatic routes.7 The cases reported in the literature often involve peritoneal carcinomatosis, previous abdominal surgery, and systemic disease.8

Peritoneal seeding is a frequent route of spread in advanced gastric adenocarcinomas.8 Tumor cells may involve the pelvic peritoneum and invade the bladder or ureteral orifices, leading to secondary masses, hydronephrosis, and hematuria. Involvement of bladder base or trigone is particularly important, as it may impair urinary flow and threaten renal function. Thus, peritoneal dissemination carries not only oncological but also urological implications.

In our case, a patient who had previously undergone subtotal gastrectomy and was diagnosed with well-differentiated gastric adenocarcinoma presented with macroscopic hematuria. Imaging revealed a mass at the bladder base, extending into the perivesical area. Following TUR, histopathological evaluation revealed adenocarcinoma, and immunohistochemical analysis showed a CK7-negative, CK20-positive, and CDX2-positive profile. This phenotype is most commonly seen in colorectal adenocarcinomas, but it has also been reported in gastric adenocarcinomas, particularly of the intestinal type. In a study by Bayrak et al. , 59 gastric adenocarcinomas were examined, and 3 of them (5 %) exhibited a CK7-/CK20+ phenotype.9 This suggests that although rare, a CK7-/CK20+ phenotype can be observed in gastric adenocarcinomas. The similar immunophenotype found in the patient's previous gastric biopsy further supports the gastric origin of the lesion.

There is no standard treatment approach for gastric adenocarcinoma metastasizing to the bladder. In the reported cases, palliative treatments with systemic chemotherapy have been applied, and some cases have shown good clinical responses.10 In our case, after paclitaxel-based systemic therapy, regression of the pelvic mass and resolution of right-sided hydronephrosis were observed. This demonstrates that in suitable patients, systemic therapy can reduce tumor burden and help control symptoms.

Additionally, following the placement of a double-J catheter due to grade 3 hydronephrosis caused by the invasion of the bladder mass into the right ureteral orifice, obstruction was alleviated with systemic therapy, and the catheter was eventually removed. This suggests that complications due to metastatic bladder involvement can also be managed effectively.

Conclusion

The detection of adenocarcinoma in the bladder should be carefully evaluated in terms of secondary metastatic involvement rather than primary bladder tumors. In patients with a history of gastrointestinal malignancies, immunohistochemical analysis plays a crucial role in distinguishing the origin of bladder adenocarcinomas.

In this case, the tumor tissue showed CK7 negativity and positivity for CK20 and CDX2, which was consistent with the previously diagnosed gastric adenocarcinoma of the patient. While the CK7-negative and CK20-positive expression pattern is typically seen in colorectal adenocarcinomas, the literature has also reported the presence of this phenotype in certain subtypes of gastric adenocarcinomas. However, there are no studies specifically regarding the metastasis of this phenotype to the bladder. In this context, CK7 and CK20 staining not only ensures diagnostic accuracy but also plays a critical role in identifying the primary source of the metastasis.

In conclusion, in every patient with adenocarcinoma in the bladder, the combined evaluation of clinical history and immunohistochemical markers such as CK7, CK20, and CDX2 is indispensable for accurate diagnosis and effective treatment planning. This case serves as a reminder that, although rare, gastric adenocarcinoma can metastasize to the bladder and provides valuable insights for managing similar cases.
