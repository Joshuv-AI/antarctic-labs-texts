Onconeural antigen NOVA1 dysregulates RNA alternative splicing in breast cancer
Authors: Daniel F Moakley, Ye Wang, Chaolin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe neuron-specific splicing factor NOVA1 was first discovered as an onconeural antigen ectopically expressed in breast tumors, causing rare autoimmune reactions and paraneoplastic neurological disorders (PNDs). The PND model suggests a plausible role of the tumor antigen expression in tumor suppression, whereas it has emerged that NOVA exhibits oncogenic activity in cell-based models. Whether NOVA mediates alternative splicing (AS) in native cancer remains unknown. Here we examine the AS profiles of breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA) tumor samples and demonstrate that aberrant NOVA1 overexpression led to the activation of neuron-like splicing patterns in many genes, including alternative exons targeted by NOVA in the brain. The splicing dysregulation is especially prevalent in cell periphery and cytoskeleton genes related to cell-cell communication, actin-based movement, and neuronal functions. We find that NOVA1-mediated AS is most prominent in Luminal A tumors and high NOVA1 expression in this subtype is associated with poorer prognosis. Our results suggest that ectopic NOVA1 in tumors has regulatory activity affecting pathways with high relevance to tumor progression.Keywords: alternative splicing, NOVA1, RNA-binding proteins, cancer

INTRODUCTION

Paraneoplastic neurological disorders (PNDs) are rare autoimmune conditions in which patients exhibit neurological symptoms in conjunction with a cancer diagnosis. Tumoral expression of normally immune-privileged, neuron-specific onconeural antigens (ONAs) triggers an adaptive immune response targeting both the neoplasms and, in cases of PNDs, the patient's central nervous system (CNS). While ONA expression confers an immunogenic risk that causes neuronal degeneration, it was reported that the response can effectively curb cancer progression (Darnell and Posner 2003). While ONAs detected so far encompass a range of molecular and cellular functions, several most common ONAs are RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) involved in regulation of neuron-specific RNA processing such as alternative splicing (AS) (Dalmau et al. 1992; Buckanovich et al. 1993). Neuro-oncological ventral antigen 1 (NOVA1) was originally identified as the ONA recognized by the anti-Ri antibody in serum or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of patients with paraneoplastic opsoclonus myoclonus ataxia (POMA) associated with breast, lung, or ovarian tumors (Buckanovich et al. 1993; Buckanovich and Darnell 1997). NOVA1 and its paralog NOVA2 regulate a critical AS program in neurons, with functional implications in neuronal migration, axon outgrowth and guidance, and synaptic formation (Ule et al. 2005, 2006; Licatalosi et al. 2008; Zhang et al. 2010). Intriguingly, recent evidence has implicated NOVA1 as a potential oncogene affecting tumor proliferation, migration, tissue invasion, metastasis, and patient survival in various cancers (Zhang et al. 2014, 2021; Ludlow et al. 2018; Saito et al. 2024), although the opposite has also been suggested (Yoon et al. 2016). These studies in general point to NOVA1's oncogenic roles enacted via gene regulation, including through dysregulated AS and inhibition of microRNA activities, as demonstrated in cell-based models (Ludlow et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2021; Saito et al. 2024). However, whether tumoral NOVA1 expression has AS regulatory activity in native tumors and how such activity is associated with tumor development and prognosis have not been assessed.

RESULTS

A subset of BRCA tumors display high NOVA1 overexpression

To address the question of whether NOVA mediates AS in cancer, we first surveyed NOVA1 and NOVA2 expression using The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) data of tumor and normal samples across 33 cancer types (see Materials and Methods). We found NOVA1 exhibited “runaway” expression in the majority of glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) and lower grade glioma (LGG), which are of brain origin, as well as a subset of breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA) samples, far exceeding that of the corresponding normal tissues (Supplemental Fig. S1A). In contrast, NOVA2 was generally expressed at low levels and was rarely overexpressed in tumors (Supplemental Fig. S1B). This expression pattern, also noted independently in a recent study (Saito et al. 2024), is consistent with the restricted expression of NOVA in the brain and also its known association with POMA in patients with breast cancer (Buckanovich et al. 1993).

NOVA1 mediates splicing dysregulation in BRCA

To assess NOVA1 splicing-regulatory activity in BRCA, we focused on cassette exons and performed differential splicing analysis using 10 samples with the highest and lowest NOVA1 expression levels, respectively (Fig. 1A). We detected 817 differentially spliced exons (DSEs) including 537 upregulated and 280 downregulated exons in NOVA1-high versus NOVA1-low samples using stringent thresholds (changes in percent spliced in [PSI], or |ΔPSI| ≥ 0.2, false discovery rate [FDR] < 0.05; Fig. 1B; Supplemental Table S1).

To evaluate whether the DSEs are bona fide NOVA targets, we identified their orthologs in mice and compared them to a list of high-confidence NOVA target exons in the mouse brain that were previously determined by integrative modeling of NOVA-dependent splicing and direct NOVA-RNA interactions (Zhang et al. 2010). This analysis leveraged the observation that NOVA tends to regulate conserved AS events and many of the genes harboring neuron-specific exons have broad expression including in normal breast and cancer samples. DSEs associated with NOVA1 expression overlapped significantly with the known NOVA mouse brain targets (P = 6.73 × 10−6, Fisher's exact test; Fig. 1C, top panel). Importantly, among the 27 DSE mouse orthologs that overlap with known NOVA targets defined in the mouse brain, 77.8% were concordant in regulatory direction between BRCA samples and the mouse brain, including 15 of 19 NOVA-activated targets and six of eight NOVA-repressed targets (P = 0.0248, Fisher's exact test; Fig. 1C, bottom panel). This overlap is robust when a less stringent splicing change threshold (|ΔPSI|≥0.1) was used to include more NOVA target exons in cancer samples in our analysis (Supplemental Fig. S2). Together, these results suggest that high NOVA1 expression in BRCA tumors engages a conserved mechanism of NOVA-dependent splicing regulation.

NOVA exhibits a stereotypical position-dependent binding pattern around its target exons (a.k.a., RNA-map), promoting exon inclusion when bound downstream from target exons and promoting skipping when bound upstream (Ule et al. 2006; Zhang et al. 2010). We examined evidence of NOVA binding around upregulated and downregulated DSE mouse orthologs and found an enrichment of both NOVA binding motif (YCAY, Y = C/U) clusters (Fig. 1D) and NOVA CLIP tags (Fig. 1E) in the expected locations, suggesting binding position-dependent splicing regulation. In addition, YCAY clusters predicted in the human genome also showed the expected pattern in BRCA NOVA1-high versus -low DSEs without mouse orthologs, indicating that the tumors employ a partially human-specific NOVA-dependent splicing program (Supplemental Fig. S3). Together, these results demonstrate that tumoral expression of NOVA1 elicited AS regulatory activity through direct protein-RNA interactions reminiscent of its activity in neurons.

Dysregulated exons are enriched in neuronal, cell-cell signaling, and cell motility pathways

We next considered the possible biological functions of the dysregulated AS events detected in BRCA samples. DSEs significantly overlapped with CNS-specific alternative exons, consistent with the role of NOVA in establishing neuron-specific AS programs (Supplemental Fig. S4). Gene ontology (GO) analysis of the genes containing DSEs showed an enrichment for GO terms related to the cytoskeleton, cell-cell adhesion, cell motility, and neuronal functions including ensheathment, ionotropic signaling, and action potential regulation (Fig. 1F). For example, among the 21 DSEs that passed a strict inclusion change threshold (|ΔPSI| ≥ 0.2) and matched the NOVA-dependent splicing change directions in the mouse brain, the majority show CNS-specific splicing and reside in a large proportion of cytoskeletal, membrane-bound, and synaptic genes. These include protein 4.1 (EPB41), a critical component of the membrane cytoskeleton that modulates physical properties of the membrane (Kiyomitsu and Cheeseman 2013), and integrin β 4 (ITGB4), a known oncogene that modulates cancer cell organotropism and correlates with tumor size and grade in BRCA (Fig. 1G; Diaz et al. 2005; Hoshino et al. 2015). Also among them is leukemia-associated Rho guanine nucleotide exchange factor 12 (ARHGEF12), a Rho guanine nucleotide exchange factor associated with lung and breast cancers (Fig. 1G; Ong et al. 2009). Others are oncogenes, tumor suppressor genes, and transcription factors, suggesting modulation of different pathways classically associated with cancer (Fig. 1G).

To experimentally validate NOVA1-associated splicing events identified from global RNA-seq analysis, we selected EPB41, ITGB4, and ARHGEF12 for further analysis in HEK293FT cells with NOVA1 overexpression as well as in breast cancer cell lines with high (HCC1500) or low NOVA1 levels (MDA-MB-231) (see Materials and Methods). NOVA1 expression in HEK293FT cells upon transient transfection of the NOVA1 expression plasmid was confirmed by western blot analysis. Similarly, differential NOVA1 expression in HCC1500 and MDA-MB-231 cells as quantified by RNA-seq was also confirmed at the protein level (Fig. 1H). Accordingly, in both cellular contexts, RT-PCR analysis of the selected alternative splicing events showed splice patterns consistent with the RNA-seq results, with EPB41 exon 15 and ITGB4 exon 33 showing NOVA1-dependent exon inclusion and ARHGEF12 exon 4 showing NOVA1-dependent exon skipping (Fig. 1I). These results confirmed a conserved NOVA1-dependent splicing-regulatory mechanism in BRCA tumors and the brain.

NOVA1-associated splicing dysregulation and reduced survival are specific to Luminal A BRCA

BRCA tumors can be grouped into five subtypes based on a panel of 50 cancer marker genes (termed “PAM50”) (Parker et al. 2009): Normal-like, Basal-like, HER2-positive, Luminal A, and Luminal B. These subtypes approximately capture the clinical diagnostic groups that are differentiated based on histology, molecular profile, and the presence of estrogen (ER), progesterone (PR) or HER2 receptors, and correspond to different cellular compositions, mutational profiles, prognoses, and treatment options (The Cancer Genome Atlas 2012). To determine whether NOVA1 splicing activity is specific to particular tumor subtypes, we compared its expression among BRCA samples based on their PAM50 classification. High-NOVA1 samples were enriched in Luminal A and Luminal B tumors, so we selected the highest and lowest expressing samples of those subtypes to repeat the splicing analysis (Supplemental Fig. S5A). High- versus low-NOVA1 Luminal A and Luminal B samples showed splicing differences in 226 and 158 DSEs, respectively (|ΔPSI| ≥ 0.2, FDR < 0.05; Supplemental Table S1). Interestingly, orthologous DSEs in mice showed high concordance with the known NOVA targets for Luminal A but not Luminal B tumors (Fig. 2A; Supplemental Fig. S5B), suggesting that NOVA1-mediated AS dysregulation is most prominent in the Luminal A subtype.

We next asked whether high NOVA1 expression was associated with prognosis in Luminal A and Luminal B BRCA. Survival analysis of patients with Luminal A tumors in the top 10% of NOVA1-expression showed significantly decreased survival compared to the other Luminal A cases (P = 0.017 by log-rank test, Fig. 2B,C). The difference in survival is not significant for Luminal B tumors (P = 0.36), although high NOVA1 expression is also associated with a trend of reduction (Supplemental Fig. S5C). These data implicate NOVA1 as an oncogene active in Luminal A tumors.

BRCA samples are also classified into two pathologic types that differ in their tissue infiltration patterns: invasive lobular carcinomas (ILCs) include mostly Luminal A-type tumors, derive from mammary glandular cells, and display a discohesive, single-file migration pattern that often escapes radiographic detection. Invasive ductal carcinomas (IDCs) form coherent, often calcified masses that are easily detectable on mammograms (Hogan et al. 2015). ILCs mostly fall under the Luminal A subtype (Ciriello et al. 2015) and express NOVA1 significantly higher than IDCs (Supplemental Fig. S6A). NOVA1 expression is also negatively correlated with that of E-cadherin (CDH1, Supplemental Fig. S6B), whose loss is a hallmark of ILC. Further work is warranted to determine whether NOVA1 is involved in ILC-specific pathology.

DISCUSSION

Tumor immunity caused by ONAs was suggested to effectively suppress tumor growth so that the diagnosis of neurodegeneration frequently preceded cancer diagnosis (Darnell and Posner 2003). In small cell lung cancer (SCLC), the detection of high-titer Hu antibody, which recognizes another neuronal RBP called HuD/ELAVL4, was reported to be associated with better prognosis in multiple independent studies (Darnell and DeAngelis 1993; Gozzard et al. 2015; Maddison et al. 2017). A conundrum underlying the current PND model, however, is why tumor cells would ectopically express ONAs such that they can be recognized and attacked by the adaptive immune system. It was recently proposed that ONAs might be intrinsically oncogenic to provide selective advantage to the tumor cells (Saito et al. 2024). This hypothesis is supported by multiple reports suggesting NOVA acts as an oncogenic RNA regulator when tested in cell-based models (Ludlow et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2021). Our work extends these previous studies by providing evidence that tumoral expression of NOVA1 has splicing-regulatory activity in native breast tumors, leading to a large number of DSEs and the production of protein isoforms that are normally unique to the CNS. These exons, dysregulated particularly in Luminal A tumors, lie in genes involved in neuronal morphogenesis, cell-cell communication, and migration and may contribute to tumor migratory or metastatic functions, resulting in poorer prognosis. We note that our focus on high-confidence targets limited our analysis to DSEs with NOVA-regulated orthologs in mouse brain and as such we have not eliminated the possibility that Luminal B DSEs may include human- or breast-tissue-specific NOVA1 targets rather than an absence of NOVA1 regulatory activity in that subtype. However, Luminal A remains the subtype in which the splicing-regulatory activity of NOVA1 is most clearly detectable, as shown by the robust concordance of DSEs in Luminal A samples with canonical NOVA targets in the brain and the subtype-specific survival association as shown in Figure 2A–C.

Whereas NOVA1 also showed high levels in GBM and LGG samples, we focused on BRCA because it represents a nonneuronal tumor context in which NOVA1 expression is clearly ectopic and closely related to its original identification as an onconeural antigen. In contrast, the observed high NOVA1 level in GBM and LGG is complicated by their brain origin and the cellular heterogeneity of bulk tumor samples, which may contain neuronal cells known to have high NOVA1 activity.

Our study provides evidence that ectopic NOVA1 expression drives a neuron-like alternative splicing program in native breast tumors. By identifying hundreds of NOVA1-dependent DSEs in cytoskeletal, cell-adhesion, and cell-motility genes, which converge with known NOVA targets defined in mouse brain, and by linking this splicing program to reduced survival in Luminal A BRCA (Fig. 3), this work establishes NOVA1 as a functionally relevant oncogenic splicing factor in a clinically defined tumor context. Dissecting the contribution of individual NOVA1-regulated exons to tumor progression and anti-tumor immunity identified in PNDs, testing whether similar activity operates in other cancers with NOVA1 overexpression, and evaluating NOVA1-regulated splicing as a biomarker or therapeutic vulnerability in cancer are important directions for future work.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

RNA-seq data processing and analysis

Gene expression matrices estimated from RNA-seq data and accompanying PAM50 classifications and tumor metadata for BRCA samples were downloaded from the TCGA database, generated by the TCGA Research Network (https://www.cancer.gov/tcga). For NOVA1 and NOVA2 RNA expression analysis, a total of 9664 tumors and 5540 normal samples across 33 cancers were examined.

For a subset of BRCA samples selected for splicing analysis (N = 10 low-NOVA1 and N = 10 high-NOVA1 for pooled BRCA analysis, N = 10 low-NOVA1 and N = 10 high-NOVA1 for Luminal A tumor analysis, N = 10 low-NOVA1 and N = 10 high-NOVA1 for Luminal B tumor analysis), raw RNA-seq reads downloaded from TCGA were mapped to the hg19 genome using OLego (Wu et al. 2013; Zhang et al. 2014). Quantification of cassette exon inclusion and differential splicing tests were performed using the Quantas pipeline (https://zhanglab.c2b2.columbia.edu/index.php/Quantas), as previously described (Yan et al. 2015). Exons with FDR < 0.05 and |ΔPSI| ≥ 0.2 were called NOVA1-associated exons, although in some cases, a less stringent threshold on the splicing change |ΔPSI| ≥ 0.1 was used as indicated.

CNS-specific splicing was determined using Gene-Tissue Expression (GTEx) RNA-seq data. In brief, for tissue cassette exon inclusion quantification, splicing junction read counts were downloaded from the GTEx Consortium (The GTEx Consortium 2020). Splicing junctions were matched with the cassette exon annotations used in Quantas for splicing analysis to calculate PSI values. Differential splicing between brain areas and all other tissues was tested using eBayes (Smyth 2004), a moderated t-test of mean PSIs of the two compared groups. Exons with FDR < 0.05 and |ΔPSI| ≥ 0.2 were called CNS-specific exons.

NOVA-binding motif cluster, NOVA CLIP tags, and NOVA targets defined by integrative modeling

Mouse NOVA binding motif (YCAY) clusters used in Figure 1D, NOVA CLIP data used in Figure 1E, and NOVA mouse brain targets defined by the integrative Bayes network modeling used in Figures 1C, 2A, Supplemental Figures 2 and 4 were obtained from our previous study (Zhang et al. 2010, 2013). To predict human NOVA binding motif clusters used in Supplemental Figure S3, we searched for YCAY elements in human genes and calculated their accessibility and conservation scores across 33 mammalian species, which were used as input of the same hidden Markov model (HMM) trained with the mouse data, as described previously (Zhang et al. 2013; Weyn-Vanhentenryck and Zhang 2016).

Gene Ontology (GO) analysis

To annotate gene functions enriched in NOVA-associated exons detected in BRCA samples, we used the R package “topGO” (Rahnenfuhrer 2024) to perform Gene Ontology analysis. The “parent-child” algorithm was used for testing to control for term dependencies.

Survival analysis

The R packages “survival” (Therneau 2024) and “survminer” (Kassambara et al. 2016) were used for survival analysis. Change in survival probability was assessed using the log rank test. Sample numbers were as follows: Luminal A analysis (high NOVA1: 63; low NOVA1: 567); Luminal B analysis (high NOVA1: 23; low NOVA1: 208).

Experimental validation of NOVA1 expression and alternative splicing events

To validate NOVA1-dependent alternative splicing events identified in BRCA samples, we used HEK293FT cells transiently transfected with NOVA1. The HEK293FT cells were maintained in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) and supplemented with 10% FBS and seeded in 12-well plates one day before transfection (∼1.5 × 105 cells per well of a 12-well plate). Transfections were performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's instructions, with 1200 ng V5-tagged NOVA1 expression plasmid DNA per well. Transfected cells were harvested 48 h posttransfection.

In addition, we also examined BRCA cell lines with high and low endogenous NOVA1 expression based on RNA-seq data from DepMap (https://depmap.org/portal/data_page/?tab=allData; Public 25Q3 release). From the list of the top five BRCA cell lines with the highest or lowest NOVA1 expression, we selected HCC1500 (high NOVA1, ATCC CRL-2329) and MDA-MB-231 (low NOVA1, ATCC CRM-HTB-26) for experimental validation, because these two cell lines can be maintained in standard mammalian cell culture conditions. The MDA-MB-231 cell line was cultured in 6-well plates with DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS for 48 h before harvesting. The HCC1500 cell line was cultured in 6-well plates with RPMI-1640 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% FBS for 48 h before harvesting.

For western blot analysis, cells were harvested by removing the culture medium from each well, followed by one wash with ice-cold 1× PBS (Gibco). Cells were lysed on ice in 250 µL RIPA lysis buffer (Pierce RIPA Buffer; Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 1× protease inhibitor cocktail. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 13,000g for 15 min at 4°C, and the supernatants were collected as total protein lysates. Total protein lysates prepared from each cell line were mixed with NuPAGE LDS Sample Buffer (4×; Thermo Fisher Scientific), boiled at 95°C for 5 min, and resolved by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). Proteins were transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes using iBlot 2 NC nitrocellulose transfer stacks (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Membranes were probed with rabbit anti-NOVA1 antibody (E9E6U, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000) overnight at 4°C. Mouse anti-GAPDH antibody (6C5, EMD Millipore, 1:1000) was used as a total protein loading control. After washing, membranes were incubated with IRDye-conjugated anti-rabbit and anti-mouse secondary antibodies (LI-COR Biosciences) at room temperature for 1 h. Membranes were washed and imaged using an Odyssey imaging system (LI-COR Biosciences).

For splicing validation, total RNA was isolated from each sample using the Quick-RNA Microprep Kit (Zymo Research) according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNA was then reverse transcribed into cDNA using the LunaScript RT Master Mix Kit (New England Biolabs). RT-PCR was performed using primers flanking the alternative exons of three representative NOVA1-associated target genes, EPB41 (exon 15), ITGB4 (exon 33), and ARHGEF12 (exon 4) (Fig. 1G). These candidates were selected based on their overlap with NOVA-regulated splicing events previously defined in the mouse brain (Zhang et al. 2010) and known gene functions in cytoskeletal organization, cell-cell signaling, and cell motility pathways that are relevant for tumor cellular phenotypes. PCR products of EPB41 and ARHGEF12 were resolved by 1.5% gel electrophoresis and ITGB4 was resolved by 3% gel electrophoresis to assess isoform patterns. The observed differential splice patterns were compared with direction of splicing regulation based on BRCA RNA-seq analysis and with known NOVA-dependent regulatory patterns defined in the mouse brain.

MEET THE FIRST AUTHORS

Meet the First Author(s) is an editorial feature within RNA, in which the first author(s) of research-based papers in each issue have the opportunity to introduce themselves and their work to readers of RNA and the RNA research community. Daniel Moakley and Ye Wang are co-first authors of this paper, “Onconeural antigen NOVA1 dysregulates RNA alternative splicing in breast cancer.” Dan has recently transitioned into industry as a Computational Biologist at Quiver Bioscience, where he is interested in how an understanding of RNA biology can enhance the applications of the company's high-throughput optopatch electrophysiology platform. Ye is a postdoctoral researcher at Columbia University. Her research focuses on RNA biology, alternative splicing, and the use of computational and experimental approaches to understand how sequence and regulatory factors shape splicing outcomes in health and disease.

What are the major results described in your paper and how do they impact this branch of the field?

In this study, we focused on how the onconeural RNA-binding protein NOVA1 affects RNA alternative splicing in breast cancer. We found that dysregulated NOVA1 expression is associated with widespread changes in alternative splicing, including splicing events in genes relevant to cancer cell behavior and gene-expression regulation. NOVA1 is best known for its role in neurons, but our work shows that it can also affect RNA splicing in breast cancer cells. This helps us understand how cancer cells may use RNA-regulatory proteins that normally function in very different biological contexts.

More broadly, these results highlight the importance of studying tissue-restricted or lineage-associated splicing regulators outside of their canonical biological settings. Our findings suggest that aberrant expression of RNA-binding proteins such as NOVA1 may contribute to cancer phenotypes by reshaping the splicing landscape, adding another layer to how gene regulation is altered in breast cancer. We hope this work encourages further investigation into how cancer cells exploit RNA splicing programs and how these programs might eventually be targeted.

What led you to study RNA or this aspect of RNA science?

DFM: I became interested in RNA biology after a conversation with an experienced lab technician at Massachusetts General Hospital, who talked excitedly about how the continued discoveries of new functions of RNA put it at the center of both biological understanding and therapeutic intervention. I entered graduate school especially interested in how mRNA splicing can “customize” the function of a gene in different cell types.

YW: I was drawn to RNA biology because of its complexity and flexibility. I am especially interested in how we can combine experimental systems with computational analysis to understand splicing regulation in a more quantitative and predictive way. This aspect of RNA science is exciting to me because it connects molecular mechanisms with broader questions in development, cell identity, and disease.

What are some of the landmark moments that provoked your interest in science or your development as a scientist?

DFM: One of the most influential moments that pushed me toward biology actually came from a YouTube video. My introductory biology professor showed us an animation of DNA replication from The Walter and Eliza Hall Institute, and it was the first time I truly appreciated “what we were talking about” as we learned about genes … which were often represented as blobs on a whiteboard. Being a visual learner, seeing a clear representation of what was actually going on in a cell unlocked something for me.

YW: One landmark moment was seeing how small changes in RNA sequences can have major effects on gene expression and cellular function. That made me fascinated by RNA biology and motivated me to study splicing regulation more deeply.

If you were able to give one piece of advice to your younger self, what would that be?

DFM: I would advise myself to worry less about the day-to-day challenges and instead try to visualize best-case scenarios. If you take a wider view to make sure you're headed in a good direction, each step toward your goals has solid footing. Also, don't take yourself or anyone else too seriously!

YW: I would tell my younger self not to be discouraged by the slow and nonlinear parts of science. Experiments fail, analyses need to be repeated, and good ideas often take time to become clear. I would also remind myself to ask questions earlier and more openly.

What were the strongest aspects of your collaboration as co-first authors?

One of the strongest aspects of our collaboration was that we brought complementary expertise to the project. One of us focused more on the computational analysis, including identifying and interpreting NOVA1-associated splicing changes, while the other focused more on the experimental validation needed to test and support those findings. This division of labor allowed the project to move efficiently from transcriptome-wide observations to biologically meaningful and experimentally supported conclusions.
