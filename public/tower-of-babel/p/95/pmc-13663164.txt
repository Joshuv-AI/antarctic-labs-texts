Bayesian brain edge-based connectivity (BBeC): a Bayesian model for brain edge-based connectivity inference
Authors: Zijing Li, Chenhao Zeng, Shufei Ge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBrain connectivity analysis based on magnetic resonance imaging is crucial for understanding neurological mechanisms. However, edge-based connectivity inference faces significant challenges, particularly the curse of dimensionality when estimating high-dimensional covariance matrices. Existing methods often struggle to account for the unknown latent topological structure among brain edges, leading to inaccurate parameter estimation and unstable inference.MethodsTo address these issues, this study proposes a Bayesian hierarchical model based on a finite-dimensional Dirichlet distribution. Unlike non-parametric approaches, our method utilizes a finite-dimensional Dirichlet distribution to model the latent topological structure of brain networks, ensuring constant parameter dimensionality and improving algorithmic stability. We reformulate the covariance matrix structure to guarantee positive definiteness and employ a Metropolis-Hastings algorithm to simultaneously infer network topology and correlation parameters. Furthermore, to alleviate the computational burden of parameter inference in large-scale networks, we optimized the calculation process of the likelihood function to reduce the algorithm’s time complexity. Our implementation is available at https://github.com/mimi6501/BBeC.ResultsSimulations validated the recovery of both network topology and correlation parameters across various settings. Furthermore, we quantitatively compared the proposed framework with the Graphical Lasso and a Dirichlet process-based non-parametric Bayesian model. Experimental results show our model offers flexible parameter tuning while outperforming baselines in estimation accuracy and convergence stability. Sensitivity analysis reveals the diagonal adjustment parameter λ has minimal impact on model accuracy, and parameter sampling order has negligible impact on final inference. When applied to the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative dataset, the model successfully identified structural subnetworks. The identified clusters were not only validated by composite anatomical metrics but also consistent with established findings in the literature, collectively demonstrating the model’s reliability. The estimated covariance matrix also revealed that intragroup connection strength is stronger than intergroup connection strength.ConclusionsThis study introduces a Bayesian framework for inferring brain network topology and high-dimensional covariance structures. The model configuration effectively reduces parameter dimensionality while ensuring the positive definiteness of covariance matrices. As a result, it offers an efficient and reliable tool for investigating intrinsic brain connectivity in large-scale neuroimaging studies.

Introduction

The concept of human brain connectivity has sparked extensive research and witnessed explosive growth since its introduction [1]. Research on brain connectivity is fundamental to advancing our understanding of human brain disorders, with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) emerging as the predominant methodological approach in this field. In this domain, functional MRI (fMRI) is widely used to map whole brain functional architecture at high spatial resolution by inferring functional connectivity from temporally correlated blood oxygen level dependent signals [2]. Complementing this, structural MRI (sMRI) offers a parallel approach to investigate the brain’s physical connectivity and organizational patterns. By modeling the statistical interdependencies of morphological properties, such as cortical thickness or volume, across different brain regions, it is possible to construct structural covariance networks [3]. These networks are thought to reflect long-term, coordinated neurodevelopmental and pathological processes, potentially arising from shared genetic or environmental influences, or long-range axonal connections [4]. This approach allows connectivity data to be systematically represented as network graphs, where anatomically defined brain areas constitute nodes, and the correlations between their structural properties form weighted edges-collectively establishing a biologically grounded topological architecture [5]. Research indicates that alterations in the topological architecture of these structural brain connections are linked to numerous neurological disorders, including Parkinson’s and Alzheimer’s disease [6]. Consequently, the analysis of structural connectivity provides a powerful paradigm for investigating human brain organization, enabling the identification of potential biomarkers for neurodegenerative disorders.

In brain connectivity studies, researchers employ phenotypic measures derived from MRI data to estimate interregional correlations, subsequently applying statistical analyses to identify connectivity patterns that covary with pathological and behavioral phenotypes. For instance, Durante et al. developed a Bayesian framework to detect both global network topology alterations and local nodal changes, thereby revealing between-group differences in structural brain organization [7]. However, these statistical analyses ignore the correlations between connectivity edges, which may lead to inaccurate parameter estimation. Current edge-based analytical strategies primarily adopt three distinct paradigms. First, pairwise edge-level analysis operationalizes connectivity through correlation coefficients computed between regional time series. This approach necessitates rigorous multiple comparison correction, typically employing false discovery rate (FDR) procedures, to account for elevated false positive rates inherent in mass univariate testing [5, 8]. Alternatively, subnetwork-based approaches reconceptualize functional connectivity alterations as modular assemblies of edges. These methods detect group differences by identifying coordinated variations within functionally defined subnetworks, thereby capturing mesoscale organizational changes [9]. Finally, whole-network analyses adopt a comprehensive perspective by explicitly modeling higher-order interactions across the complete edge ensemble. This paradigm enables detection of system-wide reorganization patterns through multivariate characterization of network topology [10]. However, due to the complexity of brain connectivity data and internal brain structures, fitting an appropriate model to determine connectivity characteristics poses a significant challenge.

Generally, modeling edge-based brain connectivity patterns requires estimation of a covariance structure characterizing interdependencies among connection edges. However, reliable estimation of the covariance matrix parameters presents some challenges. The correlation between connected edges is likely influenced by the underlying network topology of brain regions. Since each edge connects two nodes, a group of edges may be constrained by a set of nodes with an inherent topological structure. Due to the large number of nodes, the number of edges also surges, leading to a need to estimate a vast number of parameters in the covariance matrix. The dependency structure between edges is related to the network topology of brain regions, so considering this characteristic can accurately estimate model parameters. Previous parameter structure modeling strategies mainly relied on spatial distance for estimation [11–13]. However, the edge correlations in brain networks are determined by the underlying topological structure and are not necessarily related to spatial distance. Therefore, models based on spatial proximity may not be suitable for accurately analyzing edge correlations. While advanced regularization techniques have been developed for high-dimensional data [14–16], these methods often fail to incorporate the intrinsic topological organization of neural systems. This oversight may lead to biologically implausible connectivity estimates that disregard the brain’s hierarchical network architecture.

Fiecas et al. developed a variance component linear modeling framework to enable statistical inference of functional connectivity networks, with specific application to comparative analysis between neurotypical young adults and those with dyslexia [17]. However, this modeling framework does not account for the topological architecture of brain networks and becomes computationally intractable for edge parameter estimation in high-dimensional scenarios where the number of regions of interest (ROIs) exceeds practical thresholds. Chen et al. developed a non-parametric Bayesian framework for simultaneous estimation of brain network topology and covariance matrix parameters [18]. Their method took the sample covariance matrix of edges as the input and employed a Dirichlet process (DP) to identify the underlying topological structure of brain networks, followed by Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling for posterior inference of model parameters. Modeling brain network topology with a DP enables automatic selection of the cluster number but introduces parameter dimensionality variations during MCMC iterations. The change of parameter dimensionality not only increases the complexity of parameter estimation but may also lead to difficulties in achieving algorithm convergence. To overcome this limitation, in this work, we adopt a finite-dimensional Dirichlet distribution to model the latent topological structure of brain networks, ensuring a constant parameter dimension throughout the MCMC iterations and thereby improving algorithmic stability. At the same time, we reformulate the structure of the covariance matrix to guarantee that it remains positive definite throughout the entire inference process. Furthermore, to alleviate the computational burden of large-scale network inference, this study proposes a computational optimization strategy for the likelihood function. We utilize Cholesky decomposition to reduce the number of floating-point operations and solve systems of linear equations to lower the computional complexity of calculating the trace term.

The remainder of the manuscript is organized as follows. Section "Model" provides a detailed description of the proposed Bayesian model and presents the corresponding mathematical formulations. In "Model inference" section, we explain how the Metropolis-Hastings (MH) algorithm is employed to perform sampling from the posterior distribution of the model parameters. Additionally, this section covers the optimization of the likelihood function calculation. Section "Simulation studies" evaluates the performance and effectiveness of the proposed method through simulation studies, compares our model against competing approaches to demonstrate its advantages, and conducts a sensitivity analysis of the diagonal adjustment parameter in the covariance matrix. In "Application" section, we demonstrate an application of the proposed model on a real-world dataset consisting of 632 subjects and 20 ROIs, aiming at identifying potential brain connectivity patterns. Finally, "Discussion and conclusion" section summarizes the model, covering its advantages, limitations, and potential future extensions.

Model inference

This section describes the posterior inference workflow for the BBeC model, which consists of two core parts. First, we detail the MH algorithm for parameter sampling, covering the implementation of the finite-dimensional Dirichlet distribution, the specific inference steps, and the formulation of the acceptance rate. Second, to mitigate the computational challenges posed in high-dimensional brain networks, we introduce a computational optimization strategy to improve algorithmic efficiency for large-scale networks.

Metropolis hasting algorithm

This section provides a detailed description of the implemented MH sampling procedure for inferring the brain network assignment parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbf {\omega }$$\end{document} and the correlation parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\rho }$$\end{document}. Please note that in our model, the number of brain network categories K is treated as a tuning parameter. In practice, when assigning initial class labels to the V brain regions, we suggest ensuring exactly K distinct categories are included-consistent with the tuning parameter K, and each category contains at least two brain regions. This requirement arises because each category must include at least two edges, and two edges necessitate at least two nodes. Formally, there exists a vector of category sizes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(n_1, n_2, \ldots , n_K)$$\end{document} of length K, satisfying:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} n_k \ge 2, \quad \sum _{k=1}^{K} n_k = V. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This requirement also guides the tuning of the parameter K. In theory, the initial values of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\rho }$$\end{document} can be set to any real numbers; however, in practice, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\rho }$$\end{document} is initialized to be positive to avoid the explosion of Eq. (2).

In this work, we employ a block-wise updating strategy to enhance sampling efficiency. The procedure iterates in the following two alternating phases. Label updating: Using the MH algorithm, we sequentially update the class labels \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ \omega _i $$\end{document} for each brain region.Parameter updating: With class assignments fixed, the MH algorithm is reapplied to update the correlation parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ \boldsymbol{\rho } $$\end{document}.As highlighted by [18], such an alternating scheme not only improves sampling performance but also generally demonstrates favourable convergence behaviour.

Label updating: Using the MH algorithm, we sequentially update the class labels \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ \omega _i $$\end{document} for each brain region.

Label updating: Using the MH algorithm, we sequentially update the class labels \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ \omega _i $$\end{document} for each brain region.

Parameter updating: With class assignments fixed, the MH algorithm is reapplied to update the correlation parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ \boldsymbol{\rho } $$\end{document}.

Parameter updating: With class assignments fixed, the MH algorithm is reapplied to update the correlation parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ \boldsymbol{\rho } $$\end{document}.

In the updating of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\omega _i$$\end{document}, we adopt a proposal distribution identical to its prior, implementing it as a degenerate form of the DP [29]. Specifically, we conceptualize the sampling mechanism as drawing from a DP with the concentration parameter set to zero (α =0). In this setting, when the base measure is discrete, the DP induces a standard finite-dimensional Dirichlet distribution in terms of the probabilities of the underlying categories [30, 31]. And for the update of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\omega _i$$\end{document}, the acceptance probability of new proposal \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\omega _i^*$$\end{document} is computed as follows:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {\alpha }_{\omega _i^*} = \min \left( 1, \frac{p({\textbf{R}} \mid \omega _i^*, \boldsymbol{\omega }_{-i}, \boldsymbol{\rho })}{p({\textbf{R}} \mid \omega _i, \boldsymbol{\omega }_{-i}, \boldsymbol{\rho })}\right) . \end{aligned}$$\end{document}For each parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho _k$$\end{document}, k = 0, … , K, the update is performed by generating a candidate value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho _k^*$$\end{document} from the proposal distribution \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$q(\cdot \mid \rho _k)$$\end{document},\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \rho _k^* \mid \rho _k \sim q(\cdot \mid \rho _k), \quad k = 0, \ldots , K. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The choice of the proposal distribution q(·) is flexible. Typical prior selections include a range of distributions, such as the Normal distribution (often employed in random-walk formulations), as well as Gamma or exponential families, which offer flexibility in modeling different underlying processes. The acceptance probability for the candidate value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho _k^*$$\end{document}, denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha _{\rho _k}$$\end{document}, is given by the generalized MH criterion:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \alpha _{\rho _k^*} = \min \left( 1, \frac{p({\textbf{R}} \mid \rho _k^*, \boldsymbol{\omega }, \boldsymbol{\rho }_{-k}) p(\rho _k^*)}{p({\textbf{R}} \mid \rho _k, \boldsymbol{\omega }, \boldsymbol{\rho }_{-k}) p(\rho _k)} \times \frac{q(\rho _k \mid \rho _k^*)}{q(\rho _k^* \mid \rho _k)}\right) . \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This formula can be simplified under certain conditions. For instance, if the proposal distribution is chosen to be the parameter’s prior, i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$q(\rho _k^* \mid \rho _k) = p(\rho _k^*)$$\end{document}, the acceptance probability reduces to the ratio of the likelihood functions. Alternatively, if the proposal distribution is symmetric, such that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$q(\rho _k \mid \rho _k^*) = q(\rho _k^* \mid \rho _k)$$\end{document}, the acceptance probability becomes the ratio of the posterior distributions.

Computational optimization

As the number of nodes V increases, the number of edges E in the brain network grows quadratically as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(V^2)$$\end{document}. This leads to a significant expansion in the dimensionality (E × E) of the edge-based covariance matrix, imposing a heavy computational burden on the posterior inference process. Therefore, it is essential to optimize the likelihood calculation to improve overall efficiency.

In standard numerical computing, the log-determinant of a matrix is typically computed via eigenvalue decomposition, while the inverse matrix in the trace term is obtained through direct inversion methods such as Gauss-Jordan elimination or LU decomposition. For an E × E matrix, the computational complexity of these general algorithms is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(E^3)$$\end{document}. Given that these high-dimensional operations must be repeated in every iteration of the MH algorithm, conventional methods may result in prohibitive execution times for large-scale networks. Since the edge covariance matrix in our model is guranteed to be symmetric positive definite, we can leverage this property to implement more efficient computational strategies.

The primary bottlenecks in the likelihood calculation are the log-determinant of the high-dimensional covariance matrix and the trace term involving its inverse. Let the edge covariance matrix be \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\textbf{A}} = \boldsymbol{\Lambda } + \lambda {\textbf{I}}$$\end{document}. Since \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\textbf{A}}$$\end{document} is symmetric positive definite, we can perform Cholesky decomposition such that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{A}} = {\mathrm{LL}}^T$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\textbf{L}}$$\end{document} is a lower triangular matrix.

By utilizing the property that the determinant of a triangular matrix equals the product of its diagonal elements, the log-determinant of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\textbf{A}}$$\end{document} can be simplified as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \log (\det ({\textbf{A}})) = \log \left( \det ({\textbf{L}})\det ({\textbf{L}}^T)\right) = 2 \sum _{i=1}^E \log (L_{ii}). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}After obtaining \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\textbf{L}}$$\end{document}, this step only requires summing the logarithms of the diagonal elements. Although the computational complexity of Cholesky decomposition remains \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(E^3)$$\end{document}, its constant factor is much smaller than that of eigenvalue decomposition, leading to a faster computation in practice.

For the second bottleneck, the trace term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{tr}({\textbf{H}}{\textbf{A}}^{-1})$$\end{document}, recall that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H= \frac{1}{S}{\textbf{X}}^T{\textbf{X}}$$\end{document}, where S is the sample size and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\textbf{X}}$$\end{document} is the S × E data matrix. Using the cyclic property of the trace, the expression can be rewritten as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{tr}({\textbf{H}}{\textbf{A}}^{-1}) = \textrm{tr}\left( \frac{1}{S}{\textbf{X}}^T{\textbf{X}}{\textbf{A}}^{-1}\right) = \frac{1}{S}\textrm{tr}({\textbf{X}}{\textbf{A}}^{-1}{\textbf{X}}^T). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}To avoid direct matrix inversion, we define an intermediate matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\textbf{Y}} = {\textbf{A}}^{-1}{\textbf{X}}^T$$\end{document}, which is equivalent to solving the linear system \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\textbf{A}}{\textbf{Y}} = {\textbf{X}}^T$$\end{document}. Substituting the Cholesky decomposition, we solve \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\textbf{L}}{\textbf{L}}^T{\textbf{Y}} = {\textbf{X}}^T$$\end{document} in two steps: first, solving \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\textbf{L}}{\textbf{Z}} = {\textbf{X}}^T$$\end{document} via forward substitution, and then solving \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\textbf{L}}^T{\textbf{Y}} = {\textbf{Z}}$$\end{document} via backward substitution.

Since the sample size S is typically much smaller than the number of edges E, the complexity of this step is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O (E^2S)$$\end{document}, which is significantly more efficient than the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(E^3)$$\end{document} complexity of direct inversion. Finally, the trace calculation is reduced to:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{tr}({\textbf{X}}{\textbf{Y}}) = \sum _{i=1}^S \sum _{j=1}^E X_{ij} Y_{ji}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This element-wise summation avoids the storage of large intermediate matrices, saving memory and further increasing computational speed. These optimized values are then used to calculate the MH acceptance probability.

Evaluation metrics

To evaluate the discrepancy between estimated and true covariance matrices, we employed two key metrics: (1) the normalized Frobenius norm \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$||\mathbf {\Lambda } - \hat{\mathbf {\Lambda }}||_F / E$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbf {\Lambda }$$\end{document} denotes the true connectivity matrix and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\mathbf {\Lambda }}$$\end{document} denotes the estimated counterpart [32]; and (2) the normalized error \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$||\mathbf {\Lambda } - \hat{\mathbf {\Lambda }}||_1/(E\times E)$$\end{document}.

To quantitatively evaluate the node clustering performance, the adjusted rand index (ARI) was adopted [33]. The ARI measures the similarity between the estimated clusters and the ground-truth topological structure, where a higher value indicates better clustering performance. The index reaches its maximum value of 1 when the estimated results perfectly match the true labels.

Furthermore, we evaluated the performance of our method in identifying the underlying brain network topology by assessing its sensitivity and specificity. To this end, we directly compared the presence and absence of non-zero entries in the estimated covariance matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\mathbf {\Lambda }}$$\end{document} against the ground-truth matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbf {\Lambda }$$\end{document}. The true/false positive and negative rates are defined by comparing the non-zero entries of the true covariance matrix and its estimate. Specifically: a true positive (TP) occurs if both \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbf {\Lambda }_{ij}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\mathbf {\Lambda }}_{ij}$$\end{document} are non-zero; a false positive (FP) if \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbf {\Lambda }_{ij}$$\end{document} is zero but \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\mathbf {\Lambda }}_{ij}$$\end{document} is non-zero; a true negative (TN) if both \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbf {\Lambda }_{ij}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\mathbf {\Lambda }}_{ij}$$\end{document} are zero; and a false negative (FN) if \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbf {\Lambda }_{ij}$$\end{document} is non-zero but \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\mathbf {\Lambda }}_{ij}$$\end{document} is zero. Sensitivity is calculated as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ \text {TP}/(\text {TP}+\text {FN}) $$\end{document}, reflecting the model’s ability to detect true brain connections, while specificity is defined as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ \text {TN}/(\text {TN}+\text {FP}) $$\end{document}, representing the capacity to suppress false positives.

Finally, the split-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hat{R}}$$\end{document} statistic [34] was introduced to assess the convergence of the MCMC algorithm. This metric evaluates convergence by comparing the between-chain variance and within-chain variance of the split sequences. Specifically, after discarding a certain proportion of burn-in samples, the remaining sequence of length N is divided into two halves of length n. Let W denote the average within-chain variance and B represent the between-chain variance. The split-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hat{R}}$$\end{document} is calculated as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {\hat{R}} = \sqrt{\frac{\frac{n-1}{n}W + \frac{B}{n}}{W}}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Generally, a split-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hat{R}}$$\end{document} value between 1.0 and 1.1 indicates that the model has reached a satisfactory state of convergence [35].

Tuning K

Since K was treated as a tuning parameter, we evaluated the model’s performance across different values of K using the Watanabe-Akaike information criterion (WAIC) [36], defined as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {WAIC} = -2 \sum _{i=1}^{n} \log {\tilde{p}}(y_i | {\textbf{y}}) + 2\text {Var}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {Var} = \sum _{i=1}^{n} \text {Var}_i$$\end{document} denotes the sum of the posterior variances of the pointwise log-likelihoods, with\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} Var_i = \text {Var}_{\mathbf {\theta }|{\textbf{y}}} (\log p(y_i | \mathbf {\theta })) . \end{aligned}$$\end{document}In this context, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ $$$${\textbf{y}} = (y_1,..., y_n)$$$$ $$\end{document} represents the vector of observed data, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbf {\theta }$$\end{document} denotes the vector of model parameters, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\tilde{p}}(\cdot | {\textbf{y}})$$\end{document} generically denotes the posterior predictive density. A lower WAIC value indicates a better model fit. For the V = 10 case G = (3, 3, 4) (true subnetwork count was 3), we test K = 2, 3, 4 for both S1 and S2. For V = 20 and V = 30 , we test K = 4, 5, 6 and K = 5, 6, 7 , respectively, with both structures sharing the same K range. For V = 40 , due to structural differences between S1 and S2, we test K = 7, 8, 9 for S1 and K = 8, 9, 10 for S2. The WAIC values across K settings were summarized in Tables 4 and 5, guiding the selection of optimal clustering numbers. For each combination of settings, the value of K was determined by identifying the lowest WAIC presented in Tables 4, 5 and we defined \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hat{K}}$$\end{document} to represent the distinct number of labels.

Furthermore, we also conducted related simulation experiments on large-scale networks with V=100, please refer to Appendix 4 for detailed results. The results show that the model can still infer the topological structure and correlation parameters in large-scale networks, and the parameter estimation can be completed within an acceptable timeframe. Additionally, we evaluated the model’s performance under model misspecification by introducing multivariate t-distributed noise and skew-normal distribution noise. Detailed simulation settings and results are provided in Appendix 6. The result indicated that the model maintains stability in model inference and parameter estimation, even when heavy-tailed noise were introduced or when samples perturbed by a certain proportion of asymmetric abnormal samples.

Following the analysis of the model’s performance under various parameter settings, we further conducted quantitative comparisons with existing mainstream methods under identical simulation conditions to evaluate the performance of the proposed model in network inference and parameter estimation. Specifically, we compared the proposed BBeC model, based on a finite-dimensional Dirichlet distribution, with two baseline methods: (1) the Graphical Lasso (GLasso) algorithm [23]; and (2) a Bayesian model based on the Dirichlet Process (CDP) [18].

First, we compared the BBeC model with the GLasso method. While BBeC simultaneously infers network topology and estimates edge-related covariance matrices, GLasso is primarily designed for estimating sparse precision matrices and lacks an inherent node clustering mechanism. Therefore, we implemented a two-step strategy for this baseline: covariance matrices were first estimated using GLasso, followed by K-Means clustering to infer the topological structure.

Specifically, the input data were first standardized to prevent unbalanced penalty weights in the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_1$$\end{document} regularization of GLasso due to differences in feature scales. Subsequently, GLasso with 3-fold cross-validation was employed to estimate the marginal covariance matrix, with the optimal sparsity regularization parameter determined via grid search.

To infer node topology from the estimated covariance matrix, node-wise features were constructed. For each node, the rows corresponding to its connected edges were extracted from the covariance matrix, and their mean vector was calculated as the connectivity feature for that node. Finally, these features were standardized, and K-Means clustering was applied to obtain the final class labels.

In these experiments, to assess the recovery capability of the baseline method under ideal conditions, the value of K in K-Means was set to the true number of clusters (i.e., K=3, 5, 6, 8 for V=10, 20, 30, 40, respectively). By providing the ground truth K, we aimed to test whether this two-step approach could accurately recover the latent topology when the number of classes is known. The quantitative results are summarized in Tables 6, 7, 8, and 9.

As shown in the Tables 6, 7, 8, and 9, the GLasso + K-Means pipeline successfully recovers the correct topology with acceptable estimation errors when provided with the true number of clusters. However, a practical limitation of this baseline is its strict dependence on a known K, which is typically unavailable in real-world scenarios. In contrast, the BBeC model offers a practical advantage by not requiring a prior specification of the exact number of classes; the latent topology can be automatically inferred as long as the initial K is set slightly larger than the expected value. Furthermore, BBeC demonstrates higher precision in covariance matrix estimation, with errors typically one order of magnitude lower than those of GLasso. Next, we compared the BBeC model with the CDP model proposed by Chen et al. [18]. Unlike the finite-dimensional Dirichlet distribution approach adopted in this paper, CDP utilizes a non-parametric Bayesian framework, which theoretically allows the model to infer the node clustering structure from any initialization state.

During the iterative inference process of the CDP model, a node can either be assigned to an existing class or independently form a new class. Specifically, the full conditional posterior probability of node i being assigned to a newly generated class is as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned} p(\omega _i \ne \omega _j \text { for all } j \ne i \mid \boldsymbol{\omega }_{-i}, \boldsymbol{\rho }, {\textbf{R}})&\propto \; \exp \Bigg \{ -\frac{S}{2}\log \left( \det \left( \mathbf {\Lambda } + \lambda {\textbf{I}}\right) \right) \\&\hspace{-3em} -\frac{S}{2}\,\textrm{tr}\left( {\textbf{H}}\left( \mathbf {\Lambda } + \lambda {\textbf{I}}\right) ^{-1}\right) \Bigg \} \times \frac{\alpha }{V-1+\alpha }, \quad i=1,\ldots , V, \end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where α is the concentration parameter used to adjust the probability of generating a new class. In this experiment, α is set to 10.

Given that both BBeC and CDP are built upon the Bayesian framework, it is necessary to evaluate the convergence of the MCMC algorithms. We used the split-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hat{R}}$$\end{document} metric for convergence diagnostics. Since both the topological parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\omega }$$\end{document} and the correlation parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\rho }$$\end{document} are multi-dimensional vectors, reporting only the average split-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hat{R}}$$\end{document} metric might obscure convergence issues in specific parameter components. Therefore, the maximum split-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hat{R}}$$\end{document} values observed among all components of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\omega }$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\rho }$$\end{document} are reported. This approach adopts a stricter criterion: the model is considered to have converged as long as the parameter component with the worst convergence meets the convergence threshold.

In the comparative analysis, all simulated datasets were generated based on the S1 topology. In addition to the previously introduced parameter configurations, the following settings were included: For V=10, the group structure is set to G=(5,5) with intra-group correlation strengths \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\exp (-\boldsymbol{\rho })=[0.2, 0.4]$$\end{document}; For V=20, G=(10, 5, 5) and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\exp (-\boldsymbol{\rho })=[0.2, 0.4, 0.6]$$\end{document}; For V=30, G=(10, 10, 10) and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\exp (-\boldsymbol{\rho })=[0.001, 0.2, 0.4]$$\end{document}; Finally, for V=40, G=(20, 10, 10) and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\exp (-\boldsymbol{\rho })=[0.001, 0.1, 0.2]$$\end{document}.

Furthermore, when the true number of classes increases and the network topology becomes more complex, the clustering accuracy of the CDP model reduced substantially. Meanwhile, in all experiments, the maximum split-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hat{R}}$$\end{document} value for the topological allocation parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\omega }$$\end{document} is shown to be 1.00. In such cases, the ARI serves as a reliable metric for objective clustering evaluation.

In contrast, the BBeC model maintains accuracy in parameter estimation. By setting the initial K value to a larger value, BBeC is able to recover the topology and correlation parameters. In addition, the maximum split-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hat{R}}$$\end{document} values for its correlation parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\rho }$$\end{document} are consistently below 1.03, reflecting better convergence characteristics. These results also verify the applicability of the BBeC model across different scenarios.

Furthermore, we investigated the impact of the regularization parameter λ on the estimation accuracy of the model. In the experimental setup, the initial number of clusters K was set to 6 for the case with V=20 nodes, and K=9 for V=40 nodes. The detailed quantitative results are summarized in Tables 14 and 15. The corresponding trends for the Normalized Error and Frobenius Norm are illustrated in Figs. 3 and 4, respectively.

The results indicate that the BBeC model is generally robust to the choice of the regularization parameter λ . The estimation metrics remain stable across several orders of magnitude. However, a slight increase in error is observed when λ reaches \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^{-2}$$\end{document}. Notably, in the V=40 scenario, setting \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda = 10^{-2}$$\end{document} leads to convergence issues for the correlation parameter ρ , with the maximum split-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hat{R}}$$\end{document} value exceeding 1.1. This instability likely arises because an excessively large λ may exceed the actual minimum connection strength within the network, thereby introducing bias into the parameter inference. Despite these localized convergence challenges, the model maintains high accuracy in node clustering and topology recovery, as evidenced by the ARI values. In conclusion, while the structural identification is relatively insensitive to λ , we recommend selecting a smaller λ value in practical applications to ensure stable and accurate parameter Application

The proposed methodology was applied to a sMRI dataset collected from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) database.1 The data contained n=632 subjects and 10 paired ROIs considered in Szefer et al. (2017), Greenlaw et al. (2017) and Song et al. (2022) on the left and right hemispheres of the brain [37–39]. As described in the literature [37, 39, 40], the response variables in the ADNI sMRI dataset used in this study were already adjusted during the preprocessing stage. Specifically, the data were adjusted not only for confounding factors such as age, gender, education, handedness, baseline total intracranial volume, and potential population stratification, but also for clinical disease status, including control, mild cognitive impairment, and Alzheimer’s disease.

In this study, we focused on modeling the edge-based connectivity among 20 specific ROIs and the justification for selecting the 20 ROIs is provided in Appendix 7. These regions include anatomical landmarks in the cerebral cortex, including the primary sensorimotor cortex, parietal association areas, and frontal regions associated with higher-order cognition and research indicated that these regions constitute structural covariance networks and exhibit certain connectivity patterns [41, 42]. These 20 ROIs included 6 composite metrics, such as the mean cortical thickness of the precentral and postcentral gyri. These composite metrics allowed us to verify whether the model can spontaneously assign anatomical regions to the same category as their corresponding composite means, thereby assessing the accuracy of the clustering. Furthermore, preliminary K-Means clustering performed on the covariance matrix of these 20 ROIs revealed that these regions constitute a subset with a rich internal covariance structure (see Appendix 3). We standardized the 20-dimensional ROI feature vectors for all 632 subjects by subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation for each feature to eliminate scale differences. Then, for each subject, we generated a symmetric matrix by computing the outer product of their 20-dimensional feature vector, then used the flattened upper triangular part (190 elements) as the final feature input for the model. These feature vectors from all 632 subjects were stacked to form the complete input matrix for the proposed model.

For the model specification, we tuned the number of clusters for the brain region topology, i.e., K=5. Furthermore, to evaluate the edge-based correlations across different clinical conditions, we conducted a stratified analysis on the entire cohort, which consists of cognitively normal (CN) individuals, late mild cognitive impairment (LMCI) individuals, and Alzheimer’s disease (AD) patients. The sample sizes are 179, 309, and 144, respectively. For each clinical group, we computed the outer product of the region-of-interest vectors and extracted the upper triangular elements to form the edge data. We then applied our model to each group independently with K set to 5.

For the LMCI and AD groups, the identified clustering structures are identical to those presented in Table 17. The CN group showed a distinct clustering pattern for the left supramarginal gyrus: this region was assigned to Cluster 1, corresponding to the sensorimotor network, rather than to Cluster 3, as observed in the LMCI and AD groups. From a neurobiological perspective, the supramarginal gyrus is an associative area involved in sensory-motor integration. In a healthy aging brain, it maintains structural covariance with primary sensorimotor areas to support intact sensory processing and movement coordination. As the disease pathology progresses, the inferior parietal lobule becomes vulnerable [54]. This pathological burden likely causes the supramarginal gyrus to disconnect from the core sensorimotor network and instead co-atrophy with the broader default mode and parietal networks [41], explaining its shift to Cluster 3 in the impaired groups.

Discussion and conclusion

In this study, we proposed a Bayesian model designed to infer latent topological structures and estimate high-dimensional covariance matrices from MRI data. By applying this model to both simulated datasets and the ADNI cohort, we demonstrated its efficacy in identifying structural subnetworks. Simulations indicated its accuracy in recovering predefined node topology and correlation parameters. Notably, in the ADNI data analysis within selected ROIs, the model identified three major assemblies: the sensorimotor module, a fronto-parietal circuit, and the medial-lateral parietal module. Crucially, the biological validity of these clusters was supported by composite anatomical metrics.

In our clustering results, while Cluster 1 successfully captured the structural link between left and right sensorimotor regions described by Zielinski et al. [51], while Clusters 2 and 3 exhibited a divergence regarding the superior parietal gyrus (SupParietal), where the model assigned the left and right regions to different groups. This finding is consistent with the significant lateralization difference of the superior parietal gyrus reported in [58]. Specifically, the left SupParietal region was grouped in Cluster 2. This aligns with Chen et al., who defined the superior frontal gyrus as a distinct structural core [52], while the inclusion of the parietal node in our model likely reflected the expansion and integration of frontal and parietal structural connections observed in the mature brain [51]. In contrast, the right SupParietal region was assigned to Cluster 3, clustering with the precuneus and inferior parietal gyrus. Spreng and Turner have identified the precuneus and inferior parietal regions as core areas of structural covariance [53]. In the ADNI cohort, this likely reflects that the right superior parietal gyrus shows a similar pattern of atrophy to these core regions due to Alzheimer’s pathology.

One of the main contributions of this work is its effective resolution of the curse of dimensionality inherent in edge-based connectivity analyses by explicitly modeling the latent topological structure. Traditional pairwise approaches often suffer from reduced statistical power due to the massive number of multiple comparisons required for high-resolution atlases [10]. By contrast, our model operates by first grouping brain regions into latent clusters and subsequently estimating the covariance parameters based on these cluster assignments. This efficiency in unsupervised structure learning was rigorously validated in the simulation studies, where the model successfully recovered the ground-truth node topology and connection strengths. This simplified representation facilitates the interpretation of complex connectomes and leads to robust parameter estimation, particularly in datasets with limited sample sizes. Specifically, in high-dimensional settings where the number of potential connections often exceeds the number of subjects, standard sample covariance may be singular. By reducing the effective degrees of freedom to a small set of cluster-specific parameters, our model aggregates statistical information across topologically similar edges to stabilize parameter estimation.

A distinct feature of our model is that, unlike the non-parametric Bayesian approach based on the DP employed by Chen et al. [18], this study utilizes a finite-dimensional Dirichlet distribution to model network node assignments. Although the DP allows for an infinite number of clusters in theory, it introduces variable dimensionality during MCMC iterations. This variation may hinder algorithm convergence and cause label-switching problems. In our simulation experiments, the CDP model only achieved accurate topological inference in a few simple structural scenarios and struggled to cluster effectively in more complex cases. In contrast, our approach stabilizes the parameter space, thereby reducing the risk of the Markov chain being trapped in local optima and improving sampling efficiency. This stability enables the reasonably accurate estimation of both topological structures and correlation parameters.

Furthermore, the proposed model provides direct clustering inference. Comparisons with the GLasso model indicate that our approach holds an advantage in controlling parameter estimation errors. Moreover, the GLasso model does not inherently perform clustering and relies on external algorithms, such as K-Means, to infer the internal topology of the data. However, in practical applications, it is difficult to accurately specify the true number of clusters in advance for K-Means. Our model, by contrast, only requires setting the initial cluster number K to a value slightly larger than the expected true number. Through iterations, it can automatically identify the topological structure, making it more applicable in practice.

The sensitivity analysis of the regularization parameter λ shows that the model is generally insensitive to its selection, and changes the order of magnitude of this parameter generally do not obviously affect the overall estimation accuracy. Although larger λ values introduce a slight increase in estimation error, the errors remain within an acceptable range. But, if the selected λ exceeds the actual minimum connection strength present in the covariance matrix, the Markov chain corresponding to that connection strength may fail to converge. This non-convergence manifests as the parameter estimation diverging after an initial period of fluctuation and stability, leading to local estimation bias. Nevertheless, even in the presence of such local parameter divergence, the model continues to correctly identify the node clustering within the data, demonstrating its robustness in topological inference. In a summary, while structural identification is relatively insensitive to λ , we recommend selecting a smaller value in practical applications to ensure stable and accurate parameter estimation.

In addition, we investigated the impact of the sampling order (i.e., whether to prioritize the sampling of clustering labels ω or correlation parameters ρ ) on the inference. The results indicate that, given an appropriate setting of the initial K, the model yields consistent conclusions regardless the sequence of the sampling series. However, in certain complex scenarios, a slightly larger initial K can help the model achieve better convergence and estimation performance for the reverse sampling order strategy. Detailed quantitative results are provided in Appendix 5.

Regarding the implementation, determining the number of latent brain networks (K) presents a practical consideration. The simulation results indicate that the algorithm’s convergence is highly sensitive to this tuning parameter. Failure to specify a sufficient number of networks (i.e., fewer than the ground truth) can prevent convergence of the MCMC algorithm. In contrast, setting an overly large K is still likely to converge to the true number of clusters, as the superfluous groups will be effectively pruned away during the progression of the Markov chains. This finding suggests a practical strategy for real-world applications: setting K to a moderately more overspecified value than anticipated. This practice strengthens algorithmic robustness without substantially compromising estimation performance. To further validate this strategy, we additionally evaluated the model’s performance using different criteria. In addition to WAIC, we explored the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Although these two criteria had different theoretical foundations and calculation methods, the experimental results showed that the differences between the BIC values across different K settings, as well as the differences between the WAIC values, were negligible. This occurred because, in the BBeC model, superfluous clusters were automatically emptied during the MCMC iterations as long as the initial K was set larger than the true number of clusters. Consequently, the final estimated correlation parameters and topological assignments were nearly identical across all adequate K settings, leading to very little difference in the resulting likelihood values and evaluation metrics. These findings further confirmed that an adequate initial K allowed the model to automatically converge to the correct topological structure, eliminating the need to meticulously adjust K based on minor variations in the evaluation criteria.

Despite these strengths, the current framework relies on specific modeling assumptions that warrant further refinement. First, the model currently assumes that the correlation between groups is positive. However, neuroimaging studies suggest that brain connectivity is complex and may involve negative correlations between competing networks. In scenarios where negative associations exist, the current model specification might fail to accurately capture the true topological structure. Future extensions could incorporate signed stochastic block models [59], which explicitly account for both positive and negative interactions between functional modules. In this context, a limitation of the present study is that we primarily considered positive correlation structures, focusing on the major positive connections for methodological verification and model evaluation. We acknowledge that negative correlations, including weak negative associations, may also be informative in certain downstream clinical or biological analyses. Therefore, future work extending the proposed framework will systematically evaluate the contribution of these negative correlation structures to model performance and interpretability. Second, regarding highly sophisticated hub nodes, from a neurobiological perspective, connection strengths between different nodes within the same functional network typically vary. The current modeling approach may average out these subtle connectivity differences, potentially obscuring local disease-related features. To address this limitation, future research could consider adopting degree-corrected stochastic block models [60] to identify and quantify the distribution of connection strength variations among nodes within the same cluster. Third, regarding isolated nodes within biological networks, the current hard assignment framework still exhibits certain limitations. Real brain networks contain hub nodes that connect to multiple other clusters. Under the current model specification, if a hub node is assigned to a single-node cluster, it forms no internal edges; thus, the covariance matrix lacks corresponding correlation information, making it difficult for the model to identify such singleton clusters. Therefore, future work could explore methods to enable the model to identify these isolated nodes, thereby improving the inference of brain hubs. Fourth, the current model still requires a manual initial setting for the number of clusters K in practical applications, and cannot yet fully rely on the data itself to automatically infer the topological structure. Although the preceding comparative experiments indicate that traditional Dirichlet process-based models face convergence difficulties in complex scenarios, there is still potential to overcome this bottleneck by introducing more flexible non-parametric Bayesian frameworks in the future. For example, more advanced merge-split MCMC algorithms [61] could be considered. Finally, regarding computational inference, while the MH algorithm ensures asymptotic exactness, it can be computationally intensive for large-scale datasets. To address this, future work could adopt variational inference (VI) techniques [62].

In summary, this study introduces a Bayesian framework for inferring brain network topology and high-dimensional covariance structures. The model configuration ensures the positive definiteness of the covariance matrix. Furthermore, by introducing a finite-dimensional Dirichlet distribution to model the latent topological structure of brain regions, the proposed approach effectively reduces the parameter dimensionality, thereby alleviating challenges associated with high-dimensional estimation. The framework thus establishes an efficient and reliable tool for investigating the intrinsic connectivity of the brain in large-scale neuroimaging studies.
