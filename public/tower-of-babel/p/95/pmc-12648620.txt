Self-assembled silk fibroin as an interfacial reinforcer: A synergistic strategy with amorphous calcium phosphate for durable dentin bonding
Authors: Dengwei Hong, Jinghui Lin, Bingjie Zhong, Zhicen Lu, Hao Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe limited longevity of dentin bonding interfaces remains a significant challenge in dentistry, primarily due to the degradation of the hybrid layer. Incomplete penetration of dental adhesives leaves demineralized dentin collagen vulnerable to enzymatic degradation and mechanical stress. While existing approaches offer temporary solutions, dentin bonding still faces incomplete and unsustained protection for the critical interfaces. In this study, we explored an innovative dual-action biomimetic strategy combining silk fibroin (SF) and amorphous calcium phosphate (ACP) to address long-term stabilization of the dentin-adhesive interface. SF rapidly formed self-assembled fibrous network in demineralized dentin collagen. While 0.1 % and 1 % SF maintained high microshear bond strength (μSBS) values after 10,000 thermocycles, performance declined after 30,000 thermocycles (P < 0.05). Nanoleakage patterns progressed from discrete dots (10,000 thermocycles) to continuous lines (30,000 thermocycles). Combinations of 0.1 %SF+1 %ACP and 1 %SF+1 %ACP preserved high μSBS values after 30,000 thermocycles (P > 0.05), minimized nanoleakage and enzymatic activity, and maximized surface microhardness (P < 0.05). X-ray diffraction confirmed enhanced mineralization in these synergistic groups. The synergistic SF/ACP combination establishes a durable bond interface through a dual mechanism: the SF network reinforces the collagen matrix against degradation, while ACP provides a source for interfacial remineralization. This biomimetic strategy represents a promising advance for achieving long-term stability in resin-dentin bonds.

Introduction

The longevity of dentin-adhesive interfaces remains a major challenge in modern dentistry. Although contemporary adhesives achieve high immediate bond strength [[1], [2], [3]], long-term durability remains unsatisfactory [4,5]. Studies report up to 30 % reduction in bond strength within 2 years [6,7], and failure rate of direct restorations can reach 29.7 % over 4.8 years of service [8]. These failures necessitate costly replacements [9], which is estimated at $4–5 billion annually in the United States alone [10,11]. Despite over 10,000 publications on dentin bonding in the Web of Science Core Collection between 2005 and 2025, the problem of interfacial degradation persists.

The primary cause is degradation of the hybrid layer [12]. Current dentin bonding strategies depend on the intentional dissolution of hydroxyapatite (HA) minerals surrounding collagen fibrils [13] to create a micro-mechanical retention surface. However, this process results in structural “sacrifice”, leaving exposed collagen in dentin bonding interface (hybrid layer). Subsequent incomplete infiltration of adhesive leaves demineralized collagen matrix exposed [14], rendering it highly susceptible to enzymatic degradation by endogenous proteases such as matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and cysteine cathepsins, as well as to mechanical fatigue [15]. Over time, these vulnerabilities lead to microleakage, secondary caries, and eventual loss of retention. A fundamental limitation of current approaches lies in their inability to concurrently protect demineralized collagen fibrils and actively promote their long-term remineralization. While current strategies such as protease inhibitors [16,17], collagen crosslinkers [18,19], and extrafibrillar demineralization techniques [20,21] aim to preserve the existing collagen structure and delay its degradation, they do not actively restore the mineral component essential for the dentin matrix's original biomechanical integrity. Consequently, progressive hybrid layer degradation persists, even with protease inhibition [22]. For instance, while MMP inhibitors may preserve bond strength up to 6 months, bond degradation typically recurs within 12 months [23]. Therefore, the development of strategies that combine protective effects with regenerative capacity warrants further exploration.

Remineralization strategy that rebuilding mineral layers around collagen fibrils offers a more sustainable solution. Amorphous calcium phosphate (ACP) serves as a mineral precursor, facilitates the in situ formation of protective hydroxyapatite (HA) deposition around exposed collagen fibrils via non-collagen protein mediation [[24], [25], [26]]. However, the remineralization efficiency in demineralized dentin is limited by the scarcity of nucleation sites. Although higher ACP concentrations (e.g., 40 %) enhance remineralization by increasing particle-nucleation site interactions [27,28], previous research suggests these compromises adhesive properties and decreases bond strength [29]. Thus, this calls for strategies that enable effective remineralization at lower ACP concentrations by increasing nucleation efficiency.

Silk fibroin (SF), a protein derived from Bombyx mori, offers such potential. Its high density of acidic amino acid residues provides abundant sites for metal ion chelation, which can concentrate calcium ions and facilitate the initial nucleation of minerals [30]. Moreover, SF can self-assemble into β-sheet-rich nanofibrous networks [31]. This physical structure is believed to act as a template, guiding the growth of the mineral phase by stabilizing amorphous precursors and promoting their orderly transformation into apatite crystals [32]. This β-sheet structure demonstrates an elastic modulus (∼15 GPa) similar to that of dentin (∼18.6 GPa) [33,34], along with superior ductility, fracture toughness, and compressive strength [35]. Importantly, SF can actively interpenetrate type I collagen (the primary organic component of dentin) via hydrogen bonding [36], suggesting its potential to reinforce the dentin-adhesive interface.

Given these attributes, we hypothesized that SF pretreatment could create a biomimetic fibrous network within demineralized dentin, enhancing both mineral nucleation and mechanical integrity (Fig. 1). However, its effects on both immediate bonding affinity and long-term durability remain inadequately investigated, especially when combined synergistically with ACP-modified adhesives. The tested null hypotheses were: 1) there is no significant difference in dentin bond strength between the negative control and SF-pretreated groups and 2) there is no significant difference in dentin bond strength between the negative control and SF + ACP synergistic treatment groups.

Flowchart of the experimental procedure.

Phase I Dentin bonding performances with SF pretreatment

Preparation of SF solution

Intact human third molars were collected and stored in 0.1 % thyme camphor solution at 4 °C. All teeth were used within one month of extraction [16]. A commercially available SF solution (5 %, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was diluted with deionized water (DW) to obtain gradient concentrations of 0.001 %, 0.01 %, 0.1 %, and 1 % for experimental use. Dentin specimens underwent randomized group allocation via computational methods in Microsoft Excel 2019 (Microsoft, USA). Each specimen was assigned a unique identifier generated by the RAND function, followed by systematic group assignment through the data sorting algorithm. This computer-based randomization protocol was uniformly implemented across all subsequent experimental phases.

Field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM) analysis of SF

Mid-coronal dentin slabs (1 mm thickness) were sectioned under water cooling using a diamond saw (IsoMet High Speed, Buehler, USA). The surface opposite to the pulp chamber was wet-polished with sequential SiC papers (600, 1200, 2000, and 3000 grit), followed by ultrasonic cleaning for 5 min [6]. After etching with 35 % phosphoric acid for 15 s and water-rinsing for 30 s [37], 6 specimens were coated with 5 % SF solution for 60 s while 6 specimens received DW pretreatment for 60 s; all specimens were subsequently air-dried. Three specimens from each group further received adhesive application (Single Bond Universal, SBU, 3M ESPE, USA) via brushing for 20 s, solvent evaporation under moisture-free air for 5 s, and light-curing for 10 s [6]. Composite buildups (Z350XT, 3M ESPE, USA) were subsequently constructed and light-cured for 20 s [38]. Prior to dehydration and gold sputter-coating, specimens were vertically sectioned vertical to the treated surface. Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM; Verios G4, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was employed to examine surface and cross-sectional morphology of DW/SF-pretreated dentin and SF-pretreated adhesive interfacial morphologies.

Transmission electron microscope (TEM) analysis of SF

A 10 μL aliquot of 5 % SF solution was stirred with a brush (60 s), deposited on a 200-mesh carbon-coated Ni grid (T10022N, Tianld Co., China) [39], and imaged via TEM (HT7800, Hitachi, Japan) at 80 kV to assess SF nanostructure.

Attenuated total reflection Fourier transform infrared spectrum (ATR-FTIR) analysis of SF

Two groups of glass slides were treated with 10 μL of 5 % SF [40]. One underwent mechanical brushing for 60 s; the other underwent no mechanical stimulation. In another experiment, dentin slabs (5 mm × 5 mm × 0.5 mm) were demineralized with 10 % phosphoric acid for 5 h [39], rinsed, dried, and brushed with DW and various concentrations of SF (0.001 %, 0.01 %, 0.1 %, 1 %, and 5 %) for 60 s. All samples were subsequently analyzed by ATR-FTIR (Nicolet iS50, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with parameters set to 32 scans over 4000-400 cm−1 at 4.0 cm−1 resolution [41].

Contact angle measurement of adhesive in SF-pretreated dentin

Dentin slices (5 mm × 5 mm × 1 mm) were prepared and polished with 600 grit SiC paper for 1 min to create a standardized smear layer and a clinically relevant surface roughness, a protocol that produces a topography analogous to that created by a clinical diamond bur better [37]. After etching with 35 % phosphoric acid for 15 s and water-rinsing for 30 s, specimens were coated with following solutions for 60 s (n = 6) [42,43]: DW, 0.001 %SF, 0.01 %SF, 0.1 %SF, 1 %SF, and 5 %SF. All specimens were subsequently air-dried. A 5 μL droplet of SBU was deposited onto the dentin surface [16]. Side-view images were captured 3 s after droplet deposition, in accordance with the ISO 19403–2:2024 standard, and the contact angles were calculated automatically.

Specimen preparation and grouping

The sample size calculation was conducted via G∗Power version 3.1.9.2. for Windows (G∗Power, Dusseldorf, Germany). The effect size f, α err prob., power were set to 0.4, 0.05 and 0.8, respectively. This method was uniformly implemented across subsequent experimental phases. Dentin cubes (2 mm × 2 mm × 2 mm) were embedded in acrylic resin with the occlusal surfaces exposed [44]. After polishing and etching as above, specimens were randomly divided into 6 groups (n = 24) based on the following pretreatments for 60 s [45]: DW and SF solutions (0.001 %, 0.01 %, 0.1 %, 1 %, and 5 %). After drying, adhesive was applied as previously described, followed by the construction of composite buildups with a diameter of 1 mm and height of 2 mm [44]. Each group was subdivided into 3 subgroups (n = 8 per subgroup) based on aging: no aging (subgroup N), 10,000 times thermocycles (subgroup T1), or 30,000 thermocycles (subgroup T3) (5–55 °C, with a 30 s dwell time in each bath [46]).

Microshear bond strength (μSBS) test and failure mode analysis

After aging process, samples were fixed on a universal testing machine (AGS-X, Shimadazu, Japan) and tested at a crosshead speed of 1 mm/min. The μSBS was expressed in mega pascals (MPa) [44]. Failure mode analysis was performed using a measuring microscope (Stemi2000-C, Zeiss, Germany). The failure modes were assigned to following types [47]: (1) adhesive failure, (2) cohesive failure in the dentin, (3) cohesive failure in the composite, and (4) mixed failure.

Nanoleakage evaluation

Each dentin bonding sample (subgroups N, T1, and T3) was prepared and allocated following the procedure described as above (n = 3 per subgroup). Samples were sectioned vertically at the bonding interface into 1 mm slices and surfaces were nail-varnished, leaving 1 mm exposed above/below the interface. Immersion in 50 % (w/v) ammoniacal silver nitrate in darkness for 24 h [48] was followed by 8 h-photodeveloping solution treatment. After polishing, cleaning and gold sputter-coating, samples were examined by FE-SEM in backscattered mode [49].

Selection of optimal SF concentrations

Based on μSBS retention and nanoleakage results, the two best-performing SF concentrations (0.1 % and 1 %) were selected for Phase II testing.

Phase II Dentin bonding performance and remineralization efficacy with SF-ACP synergy

Preparation of ACP-modified adhesive

ACP nanoparticles (693871, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) were carefully weighed on a precision balance and added to the SBU adhesive in different concentrations (0 %, 1 wt%, 10 wt%, or 20 wt%) under light-protected conditions, with ultrasonic stirring to ensure uniform dispersion until obtaining a homogeneous solution, with the particles fully incorporated [28].

TEM analysis of ACP-modified adhesive

Aliquots (10 μL) of ACP-modified adhesive were placed on carbon-coated nickel grids and imaged using TEM.

Contact angle measurement of ACP-modified adhesive

Dentin slices (5 mm × 5 mm × 1 mm) were prepared and polished with 600 grit SiC paper for 1 min. A 5 μL droplet of adhesive from each group was deposited onto the dentin surface (n = 6) [16,50]. Contact angles were measured as described in section 2.1.5.

Degree of conversion (DC) of ACP-modified adhesive

Each drop of 20 μL of ACP-modified adhesives was spread on the top plate of ATR-FTIR, and cured for 20 s using a curing light and immediately scanned. The spectra were obtained with 32 scans in the range of 4000-400 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1 (n = 4) [41]. The DC was calculated using the formula (Eq. (1)). The (A1638) indicated the absorbance of aliphatic C=C stretching vibration at 1638 cm−1, and (A1608) indicated the absorbance of aromatic C=C symmetric ring stretching at 1608 cm−1, which was the reference.

μSBS test and failure mode analysis

The μSBS test and failure mode analysis were operated as above.

Nanoleakage evaluation

Samples were grouped, prepared and detected as above (n = 3 per subgroup).

In situ zymography

Dentin bonding slices (thickness of 1 mm) were grouped as above (n = 3 per subgroup) and ground to 500 μm. Each sample received 80 μL fluorescent gelatin mixture was covered with slip [22] and incubated in dark (37 °C, 24 h, humidified) [51]. After thorough washing, gelatinolytic activity was assessed via confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM, FV3000, Olympus, Japan) by observing the green fluorescence (Ex/Em: 488/530 nm) [51].

Surface microhardness (SMH)

Demineralized dentin slices (prepared as above) were grouped as in Table 1 (n = 12). With universal adhesive replaced by DW to prepare remineralization liquid, slices were pretreated for 60 s and immersed in liquid for 7 d [52]. SMH was measured using a hardness tester (VH1102, Buehler, USA). Three random indentations per sample (500 g load, 10 s dwell time) were performed [53], and means were calculated [54].

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis

Dentin samples were prepared and grouped as above (n = 3, except SF-pretreated group). XRD was performed using an X-ray diffractometer (X'Pert3 MRD, Malvern Panalytical) with Cu anode at 45 kV/40 mA. Diffraction intensities were measured from 5° to 70° (2θ) [55] at room temperature.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS 26.0 software (SPSS Inc., USA) and the significance was determined at α = 0.05. The contact angle, DC and SMH results were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by post hoc Tukey's test. Two-way ANOVA and post hoc Tukey's test were performed in μSBS values analysis. Error bars in the figures represent the standard deviation. All experiments were conducted independently at least three times.

Results

Phase I Dentin bonding performances with SF pretreatment

FE-SEM analysis of SF

FE-SEM images (Fig. 3A) revealed that SF pretreatment formed a continuous coating on the demineralized surface, with SF infiltrating dentin tubules without complete occlusion. At the bonding interface, SF self-assembled into an interconnected fibrous network that penetrated the demineralized collagen matrix.

TEM analysis of SF

TEM revealed SF's characteristic dual-scale architecture (Fig. 3B), featuring both macroporous layers and well-defined nanofibrils. Notably, the interconnected pore architecture and distinct interwoven nanofibrillar microstructure are clearly resolved.

ATR-FTIR analysis of SF

ATR-FTIR spectrum (Fig. 3C) after pretreatment revealed characteristic amide absorptions at: 1637 cm−1 (amide I, C=O stretching), 1526 cm−1 (amide II, N-H bending), 1239 cm−1 (amide III, C-N and N-H functionalities) and 3360 cm−1 (amide A, N-H stretching). Critically, amide I (1620-1640 cm−1) and III (1230-1250 cm−1) bands resided within the characteristic β-sheet regions, confirming SF self-assembly. These features were absent in the spectrum before rubbing. Fig. 3D showed concentration-dependent enhancement of amide I, II, III, and A band intensities, with signal strength proportional to SF concentration, while demineralized dentin collagen peaks remained preserved.

Contact angle measurement of adhesive in SF-pretreated dentin

As shown in Fig. 3E, the 5 %SF pretreatment significantly reduced the contact angle of SBU on the dentin surface, indicating improved wettability. No significant changes in wettability were observed with other SF concentrations.

μSBS and failure mode analysis

Two-way ANOVA of μSBS revealed significant effects of pretreatment (F = 48.158, P < 0.001, Partial η2 = 0.656), thermocycling (F = 264.266, P < 0.001, Partial η2 = 0.807), and their interaction (F = 9.922, P < 0.001, Partial η2 = 0.441) (Fig. 4A and Table S1). Mean μSBS values showed no differences among no aging groups (P > 0.05). After 10,000 thermocycles, 0.1 %SF and 1 %SF groups maintained μSBS comparable to no aging levels (P > 0.05) while others decreased significantly (P < 0.05). Following 30,000 thermocycles, both 0.1 %SF and 1 %SF groups showed reduced μSBS but remained significantly higher than all other groups (P < 0.05). Failure mode analysis demonstrated predominant occurrence of mixed failure in all no aging groups and 0.1 %SF and 1 %SF groups after thermocycling (Fig. 4B).

Nanoleakage evaluation

Representative FE-SEM images demonstrated the silver deposition within the highlighted areas of the hybrid layer. (Fig. 4C). In no aging group, negligible silver deposition was detected. Following 10,000 thermocycles, discrete dots of silver deposition persisted in the 0.1 %SF and 1 %SF groups, while all other groups exhibited short linear or bulk deposits. After 30,000 thermocycles, short linear silver deposition appeared in the 0.1 %SF and 1 %SF groups and the remaining groups demonstrated extensive linear deposits that occupied significant portions of the bonding interface within the observed field.

Contact angle measurement of ACP-modified adhesive

Contact angles significantly increased in 10 % and 20 % ACP adhesives compared to 0 %ACP adhesive, with the 20 % group exceeding 10 % group (P < 0.05). The 1 %ACP adhesive showed no difference from the 0 %ACP adhesive (P > 0.05) (Fig. 5B).

DC of ACP-modified adhesive

The DC values revealed that only 20 %ACP adhesive exhibited significantly reduction compared to 0 %ACP adhesive (P < 0.05), while 1 % and 10 % group showed no statistical differences from 0 % group (Fig. 5C). ATR-FTIR analysis was presented in Fig. 5D.

μSBS test and failure mode analysis

Two-way ANOVA tests of μSBS values demonstrated that pretreatment (F = 31.252, P < 0.001, Partial η2 = 0.649) and aging (F = 46.979, P < 0.001, Partial η2 = 0.410) both had significant effects on μSBS with a significant interaction between these factors (F = 5.096, P < 0.001, Partial η2 = 0.377). μSBS results (Fig. 6A and Table S2) revealed that in non-SF-pretreated groups, both DW and 10 %ACP exhibited significant μSBS reduction after thermocycles (P < 0.05), while 1 %ACP maintained immediate-equivalent strength after 10,000 thermocycles (P > 0.05) but degraded significantly after 30,000 thermocycles (P < 0.05). Following SF pretreatment, 0.1 %SF+1 %ACP and 1 %SF+1 %ACP groups preserved μSBS after 30,000 thermocycles (P > 0.05), whereas 0.1 % SF+10 %ACP and 1 %SF+10 %ACP groups still showed significant reduction (P < 0.05). Failure mode analysis (Fig. 6B) revealed that mixed failure modes were predominant in most groups. Specimens incorporating SF and 1 % ACP demonstrated relatively sustained interfacial fracture patterns after thermocycles.

Nanoleakage evaluation

Representative nanoleakage patterns (Fig. 6C) revealed negligible or sporadic leakage in all groups in no aging assessment. After 10,000 thermocycles, 0.1 %SF, 1 %SF, 0.1 %SF+1 %ACP and 1 %SF+1 %ACP groups showed no detectable leakage, whereas others exhibited granular or short-linear patterns. Following 30,000 thermocycles, continuous interfacial leakage emerged in DW and 10 %ACP groups, while 1 %ACP displayed localized linear deposits. Concurrently, 0.1 %SF, 1 %SF, 0.1 %SF+10 %ACP and 1 %SF+10 %ACP groups demonstrated granular leakage. Crucially, 0.1 %SF+1 %ACP and 1 %SF+1 %ACP groups maintained nearly undetectable nanoleakage throughout aging.

In situ zymography

Representative in situ zymography (Fig. 7) revealed minimal enzymatic activity (negligible fluorescence) in all no aging groups. After 10,000 thermocycles, 0.1 %SF+1 %ACP and 1 %SF+1 %ACP groups maintained undetectable fluorescence, while localized interfacial fluorescence in 0.1 %SF and 1 %SF groups indicated regional enzymatic activation. Other groups showed continuous interfacial fluorescence, denoting widespread enzymatic activation. Following 30,000 thermocycles, 1 %SF+1 %ACP group remained fluorescence-free, whereas 0.1 %SF+1 %ACP developed granular fluorescence. Discontinuous linear patterns emerged in 1 %ACP, 0.1 %SF+10 %ACP, and 1 %SF+10 %ACP groups. Crucially, both control and 10 %ACP groups exhibited intense continuous fluorescence, signaling substantially enhanced enzymatic activity.

SMH

The results of SMH values (Fig. 6D and Table S3) showed that treatment (F = 579.876, P < 0.001, η2 = 0.979) significantly affected the SMH. All groups except the 10 %ACP, 0.1 %SF, and 1 %SF groups demonstrated significantly increased SMH of demineralized dentin (P < 0.05) at various degrees. Notably, the 0.1 %SF+1 %ACP and 1 %SF+1 %ACP groups exhibited the most pronounced SMH enhancement among all treatments (P < 0.05).

XRD

XRD patterns (Fig. 6E) showed demineralized dentin exhibiting only a weak peak at 2θ = 42.5°, whereas all other groups displayed characteristic peaks at 28.9°, 39°, 42.5°, 47°, 48°, and 56.8°. Stronger 28.9° peak intensities in 0.1 %SF+1 %ACP and 1 %SF+1 %ACP groups indicated higher mineralized content. Though patterns generally matched HA standards, minor peak shifts suggest mineralized products may contain partially crystalline HA or HA mixed with other phases.

Discussion

Given the critical importance of hybrid layer integrity in long-term dentin bonding performance, this study aimed to stabilize the demineralized dentin collagen, which is inherently weak and highly susceptible to enzymatic degradation and mechanical fatigue [1,15,56]. The results demonstrated that while SF alone (0.1 % and 1 %) enhanced dentin bonding durability, its combination with a low concentration (1 %) of ACP yielded superior outcomes. Thus, both null hypotheses were rejected.

The concentration of SF was limited to 5 % based on reports that solutions below this threshold remain stable against self-assembly under stationary conditions [57], ensuring workability prior to application. For ACP, concentrations of 10 wt % and 20 wt % were chosen based on established efficacy in promoting remineralization without compromising bond strength [29,58,59]. A lower concentration of 1 wt% was also included to assess whether SF could induce effective remineralization even at reduced ACP levels [60]. Although a gradual increase in β-sheet content generally promotes the formation of a fibrous network within SF, excessive induction of β-sheet structures is known to increase material brittleness [61]. This may explain the significant reduction in bond strength observed in the 5 % SF group after thermocycling, despite the wettability improvement afforded by this pretreatment. Furthermore, the combination of SF with 10 % ACP underperformed compared to the combination with 1 % ACP. The SMH results also indicated no substantial improvement in mechanical hardness in the 10 % ACP group. This discrepancy may be due to particle aggregation at higher ACP concentration, which may hinder penetration into intrafibrillar spaces. TEM imaging supported this hypothesis, revealing larger cluster diameters in the 10 % ACP group (∼1.5 μm) compared to the more finely dispersed particles in the 1 % ACP group (∼100 nm). Although 1 % ACP alone moderately recovered hardness, its performance remained inferior to that of the SF+1 % ACP groups. This suggests that 1 % ACP alone may only induce mineralization at limited residual nucleation sites, whereas combination with SF enhances remineralization efficacy through supplemental nucleation sites within the collagen matrix.

ACP is widely recognized as a promising calcium and phosphate source and a key precursor to HA [62,63], the primary mineral phase in dentin. It can infiltrate dentin tubules and promote HA formation, facilitating dentin remineralization [64]. However, the scarcity of nucleation sites in demineralized dentin often limits remineralization efficacy. Although high concentrations of ACP have been employed to overcome this limitation, the inherent instability of ACP, due to its high specific surface area and van der Waals forces, leads to particle aggregation shortly after preparation. Such aggregation impedes adhesive infiltration and hybrid layer formation. Our findings are consistent with previous reports that high ACP concentrations result in particle agglomeration within 15 min, reducing adhesive wettability and degree of conversion. Previous studies have incorporated modified ACP formulations, such as casein phosphopeptide-ACP (CPP-ACP) [65] and polyacrylic acid–stabilized ACP (PAA-ACP) [66], to improve remineralization. However, these approaches have been associated with a bond strength reduction of approximately 36 % after 1.5 years of aging [65]. Moreover, such materials tend to promote extrafibrillar remineralization and are often evaluated for remineralization potential within adhesives, yet their effects on bond durability remains inadequately investigated. Furthermore, their complex synthesis, prolonged application times, and additional handling steps increase both cost and procedural complexity.

An alternative strategy involves using macromolecular agents, such as hyaluronic acid [67,68] and poly (amido amine) (PAMAM) [69,70], to provide nucleation sites and enable the use of lower ACP concentrations. However, these materials are typically applied topically and accumulate predominantly on the dentin surface with limited penetration into the collagen network, which can interfere with resin infiltration and compromise bond strength. For instance, hyaluronic acid treatment resulted in over 65 % reduction in bond strength, both immediately and after six months of aging [68]. In contrast, SF, a self-assembling biomaterial used in this study, exists at the nanoscale prior to assembly and readily infiltrates demineralized dentin. Under mechanical stimulation, it rapidly forms an integrated fibrous network through tribo-responsive self-assembly. ATR-FTIR confirmed the rapid formation of β-sheet structures following 60 s of mechanical stimulation, consistent with this self-assembly behavior. The appearance of amide I, II, III bands within this short interval supports the clinical feasibility of a brief SF application. Importantly, no detectable shift occurred in the amide bands of demineralized dentin collagen after SF pretreatment, indicating preservation of its native triple-helix structure [71]. TEM imaging revealed a dense SF fibrous network. The morphological similarity between this SF nanostructure and the native dentin collagen matrix may facilitate improved mechanical interlocking with the resin adhesive, potentially contributing to the enhanced μSBS results. FE-SEM showed that this network infiltrated the demineralized dentin without occluding tubules. These findings suggest that SF maintains collagen accessibility and resin infiltration pathways while reinforcing the hybrid interface. Notably, SF alone significantly maintained bond strength after 10,000 thermocycles (equivalent to 1 year of aging) [72], which can be attributed to the formation of a stable interpenetrating network that enhances collagen resistance to proteolysis and mechanical degradation. Although 10,000 thermocycles represent a conventional model for accelerated aging, the protocol was further extended to 30,000 cycles. This extended aging protocol better simulates the long-term clinical service of dental restorations and was crucial for investigating the durability of SF's reinforcing effect over time. Although this effect diminished after 30,000 thermocycles (equivalent to 3-year aging) [73], the sustained stability during the first year provides a critical window for collagen protection and remineralization. Moreover, contact angle measurements suggest that improved wettability is unlikely to be the primary mechanism behind SF's efficacy, as bond strength was maintained in effective pretreatment groups without significant changes in wettability.

In comparison, conventional strategies exhibit a 41 % decline in bond strength after the same number of thermocycles, and even novel extrafibrillar demineralization approaches show an 11 % reduction [1]. This contrast not only highlights the importance of protecting collagen with minerals but also underscores the distinct underlying mechanisms of SF modification compared to other bonding approaches. Although extrafibrillar demineralization can reduce collagen vulnerability by preserving intrafibrillar minerals, its capacity to prevent interfacial enzyme activation remains limited. In the present study, the introduction of SF appears to provide both structural reinforcement and a potential template for mineral nucleation. This dual action could mitigate hydrolytic and enzymatic degradation while also promoting subsequent remineralization. Consequently, the provision of nucleation sites and facilitation of remineralization prior to any potential SF degradation is likely a key factor contributing to the long-term interfacial stability. This remineralization can reestablish a mineralized environment that inactivates proteolytic enzymes activated during bonding procedures [16], thereby suppressing enzymatic activity and enhancing mechanical properties. The observed minor peak shifts in the XRD patterns suggest the formation of incompletely crystallized HA, potentially coexisting with other intermediate calcium phosphate phases. While the characteristic broad halo of residual ACP was not distinctly identified, its presence as a transient precursor cannot be entirely ruled out. Importantly, the maintained bond strength observed in our study suggests that SF pretreatment may help stabilize the bonding interface during this dynamic, transitional period of mineralization. This potential stabilizing effect could be clinically valuable, ensuring interface integrity during the early stages of remineralization when the mineral structure is evolving. Additionally, SMH and in situ zymography demonstrated significantly better performance in the 0.1 %SF+1 %ACP and 1 %SF+1 %ACP groups compared to the SF or ACP alone after 30,000 thermocycles, indicating enhanced crystallization and inhibited enzymes activation.

In summary, this study presents a practical strategy that incorporates a low concentration of ACP into the adhesive, which is a straightforward approach that enhances technical feasibility, while using SF to provide additional nucleation sites and a biomimetic scaffold integrated with dentin collagen. This synergistic interaction enhances the stiffness and resilience of the collagen matrix, thereby stabilizing the dentin bonding interface.

From a translational perspective, the SF + ACP strategy can be readily incorporated into universal adhesive systems with minimal modification. While a brief SF pretreatment step followed by the application of an ACP-containing adhesive offers a practical, chairside approach to significantly improve restoration longevity, its seamless integration into the clinical workflow requires careful consideration. Key challenges include ensuring the shelf-life and stability of the aqueous SF solution, potentially via refrigerated storage. To minimize operator variability in forming a uniform SF network, standardized application protocols regarding time and technique must be established. Furthermore, the compatibility of the SF solution with the diverse chemistry of various commercial adhesive systems warrants thorough investigation. Future studies will focus on addressing these practical aspects to improve the robustness and reproducibility of the SF + ACP strategy for clinical use.

Despite these advantages, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the optimal pretreatment time of SF and ideal SF-ACP concentration ratios remain to be determined through additional investigation. Second, the multi-step application protocol presents challenges for clinical implementation. Third, while the biocompatibility of SF and ACP is supported by previous literature, the specific biological response to any residual components within the pulp chamber warrants further investigation to fully assure clinical safety. Nevertheless, this research successfully establishes proof-of-concept for using SF as a bioinspired scaffold that promotes remineralization through ACP delivery. This dual-functional strategy holds considerable promise for enhancing dentin bonding durability and merits further investigation toward simplified clinical applications.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates a synergistic strategy for durable dentin bonding using SF and ACP. While SF pretreatment self-assembled into a reinforcing fibrous network within demineralized dentin, its long-term effectiveness was limited. The key finding is that the combination of SF and ACP significantly enhanced remineralization and sustained bond strength after aging, outperforming either agent alone. By triggering the in situ self-assembly of SF to improve ACP efficacy, this approach establishes a promising strategy for achieving long-lasting dentin bonds.
