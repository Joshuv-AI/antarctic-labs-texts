State Abortion Laws and Moral Distress in a National Sample of Obstetrician-Gynecologists
Authors: Erika L. Sabbath, Taylor P. Brennan, Andrea Chalem, Melissa L. McTernan, Kavita S. Arora, Mara Buchbinder
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cross-sectional study investigates the experience of clinical constraints related to state abortion laws and its association with moral distress among obstetrician-gynecologists.

Introduction

In the years since the 2022 Dobbs v. Jackson Women’s Health Organization decision, which allowed states to impose full or partial bans on abortion provision, multiple studies have documented the association of post-Dobbs abortion restrictions with adverse maternal1,2,3,4,5 and infant5,6,7,8 morbidity and mortality outcomes. These laws overlay state-level abortion restrictions that predated Dobbs, including gestational age limits, mandatory waiting periods, and targeted restrictions on abortion provider (TRAP) laws.

A key difference between pre- and post-Dobbs abortion laws is that the latter emphasize civil and criminal penalties for clinicians, including fines, loss of medical licenses, and prison sentences. Given that many laws provide minimal or unclear exceptions for threats to maternal life or health,9 clinicians in abortion-restrictive states may be forced to choose between their own legal liability and providing patients with the standard of care.4,10,11,12,13 Additionally, several states have prohibited “aiding and abetting” abortion care, which certain institutions have interpreted as barring physicians from referring patients for abortion care or counseling them on abortion.14,15 After Dobbs, obstetrician-gynecologists (OB-GYNs) in restrictive states have reported a range of clinical outcomes, including deviations from the standard of care for pregnancy loss,1,16,17 waiting until patients were critically ill to provide abortion care,4,14 and disruption to patient-physician relationships.18,19

These clinical changes for OB-GYNs may be associated with moral distress, which may arise when a clinician knows the correct course of clinical action but cannot take that action due to external constraints (eg, state law, institutional policy, or resource scarcity).20 Indeed, national surveys of maternal-fetal medicine fellows21 and abortion providers22 have found higher levels of moral distress among clinicians in abortion-restrictive compared with abortion-protective states. Qualitative studies of OB-GYNs in restrictive states have found that OB-GYN residents23,24,25 and attendings4,14,26,27 and non–OB-GYN clinicians28,29,30 report a range of morally distressing clinical constraints. These include waiting to treat patients with potentially life-threatening pregnancy complications until the patient became sicker, limitations on counseling patients or providing referrals for out-of-state abortion care, and being compelled to move forward with care provision in legal gray areas without adequate legal guidance or support from their institutions.

While these studies of state abortion policies and moral distress provide important context, they have limitations. First, national surveys have focused on the 2 groups of OB-GYNs most likely to interface with abortion laws: maternal-fetal medicine subspecialists21 and abortion providers.22 It is unknown whether findings hold for general OB-GYNs, who constitute more than three-quarters of OB-GYNs.31 One report32 describes a national survey of OB-GYNs, but data were collected in early 2023, less than 1 year after Dobbs. Clinician outcomes likely evolve over time, but the direction of that evolution is unknown; under restrictive conditions, clinicians could become inured to legal constraints or moral distress around clinical restraints could intensify. Second, qualitative studies that include general OB-GYNs focus on abortion-restrictive states, without a national comparative perspective. This study sought to evaluate the extent to which OB-GYNs in a national sample experienced clinical constraints related to their state’s abortion laws 3 years after Dobbs, and whether those clinical constraints were associated with moral distress.

Methods

This cross-sectional study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill. All participants consented prior to beginning the online survey. This study follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline for cross-sectional studies and the American Association for Public Opinion Research (AAPOR) reporting guideline for reporting results of survey research.

Sample

In April and May 2025, participants were recruited and managed via email by Medefield, a physician market research firm that surveys physician members of their panel on behalf of clients, with findings from surveys regularly appearing in peer-reviewed literature.33,34,35 Panel members are recruited using contact information from verified physician directories. Thus, we have high confidence that all contacted physicians were OB-GYNs.

This approach, which yields a nonprobability sample of OB-GYNs nationally, offers multiple advantages over convenience-sampling strategies (eg, circulation of an open survey link on social media and recruitment at national meetings) that have dominated this research space to date.21,22,36 The latter strategies increase risk of falsified identities (eg, non-OB-GYNs) by individuals seeking the incentives37 or preferential distribution of surveys within networks with strongly held views on abortion, with implications for selection bias. A nonprobability sampling strategy is justified because there are few, if any, other ways to efficiently survey a sample of verified OB-GYNs without introducing other biases (eg, attendance at certain meetings and conferences). For nonprobability samples, traditional response rates or participation rates are not accurate indicators of the presence of potential bias.38

The online survey (eAppendix in Supplement 1) was designed for completion within 20 minutes; participants were compensated with a monetary incentive. Participants were eligible if they met all inclusion criteria: attending OB-GYNs, practicing in the US, a general OB-GYN or a subspecialist in maternal-fetal medicine or complex family planning, and providing care to pregnant patients.

We created minimum sampling quotas for abortion-restrictive and abortion-protective states to ensure sufficient distribution of respondents across abortion policy environments (defined subsequently). We capped the number of participants from large, abortion-protective states (New York and California) to ensure a range of geographies within each policy stratum.

Measures

Exposure: State Abortion Policy Environment

Participants indicated their primary state of practice on the survey. We then categorized each state into 1 of 7 abortion policy environment levels using the Guttmacher Institute state abortion policy tracker as of May 2025, when the survey closed.39 The tracker uses multiple indicators to categorize states as very restrictive, somewhat restrictive, restrictive, some restrictions or protections, protective, very protective, and most protective. We then dichotomized states as restrictive (very restrictive through some restrictions or protections; 29 states) and protective (protective through most protective; 21 states and the District of Columbia) for primary analyses. Exposure: Clinical Constraints Related to State Abortion Policies

In our prior qualitative studies,14,15 we found distinct clinician outcomes associated with counseling restrictions, overall care restrictions, and restrictions on care during obstetric emergencies. Drawing from these findings, we developed 3 survey questions about the extent to which participants experienced clinical constraints related to their state’s abortion policies. We pretested and then refined draft questions with a small sample of OB-GYNs and with a survey research expert. Questions ask to what extent respondents’ state abortion laws constrained their patient counseling, ability to provide care, and ability to provide care to a patient with a potentially life-threatening pregnancy complication. Response options for all questions were not at all, a little bit, moderately, quite a bit, and extremely. We recategorized responses into not at all, somewhat (a little bit or moderately), and very much (quite a bit and extremely).

Exposure: Attitude Toward State Abortion Laws

On the survey, we asked, “What is your opinion about your state’s abortion laws?” Response options were much too restrictive, a little too restrictive, just right, a little too accessible, and much too accessible. For analysis, we collapsed the latter 2 response options into a single category (too accessible) due to low frequency.

Outcome: Moral Distress

We measured moral distress using the 8-item Moral Distress Appraisal (MD-APPS) scale.40 This scale is designed to measure global moral distress among health care professionals and is not specific to abortion care provision. An example scale item is “I am prevented from carrying out my work in a way that I believe is morally right.” Response options for all items ranged from totally disagree (0) to totally agree (5). The mean of responses to the 8 items was calculated to create an overall moral distress score (range, 0-5), with higher scores indicating greater moral distress.

Covariates

We included covariates, all collected via the survey instrument, that were plausibly associated with the outcome (moral distress) and the exposure (clinical constraints). These included self-reported individual sociodemographic characteristics: age (25-34, 35-44, 45-54, 55-64, and ≥65 years), sex (male, female, missing, another option, and prefer not to answer), race, and Hispanic ethnicity (yes or no). Participants were able to select all that applied from the following prespecified options for race: American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian or Asian American, Black or African American, Middle Eastern or North African, Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander, White, and other, please specify. Responses were reclassified into Asian, Black, White, multiracial (>1 yes response), another race or ethnicity, or unknown or unspecified. We included covariates for subspecialty (general OB-GYN or subspecialist in maternal-fetal medicine or complex family planning), practice setting (private practice, community-based health clinic, hospital, Indian Health Service or Veterans Affairs, or other), rurality (urban, suburban, or rural), and whether the participant worked in a Catholic hospital (yes, no, or do not know).

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were conducted in Stata statistical software version 19 (StataCorp). After stratifying participants by their state’s abortion policy environment (restrictive vs protective), we assessed the distribution of moral distress and of the 3 clinical constraints, overall and by abortion policy environment. Next, we assessed the distribution of clinical constraints by the 4 levels of abortion law opinion (much too restrictive, a little too restrictive, just right, and too accessible). Bivariate associations were assessed using Pearson χ2 or Fisher exact tests for categorical variables and independent-sample t tests or analysis of variance for continuous variables, depending on each variable’s level of measurement and distribution. We used linear mixed-effects models, with states specified as random effects to account for clustering, to assess associations of clinical constraints and abortion law opinion with moral distress, overall and stratified by abortion policy environment (restrictive and protective). In the latter models, we added an interaction term for policy environment × constraint to evaluate whether the nature of the association between each constraint and moral distress varied by policy environment, using a Wald test for significance of the overall interaction. All models were adjusted for age, sex, race, ethnicity, subspecialty, practice setting, rurality, and Catholic hospital status. Statistical significance was established at a 2-tailed P < .05. In sensitivity analyses, we repeated descriptive analyses stratified by the 7 categories of abortion policy environment vs collapsed into 2.

Results

Of 1607 OB-GYNs who opened the survey invitation, 1299 fulfilled initial screening criteria. Of these, 142 were excluded because we had filled their state abortion policy quota (eg, protective states), 293 partially completed the survey and were excluded from analyses, and 864 had complete data and were included in analyses (483 female [56.0%]; 623 aged ≥45 years [72.1%]; 113 Asian [13.1%], 35 Black [4.1%], and 625 White [72.3%]; 54 Hispanic [6.3%]). Among included participants, 566 respondents (65.5%) primarily practiced in restrictive states and 298 respondents (34.5%) primarily practiced in protective states (Table 2), with participants practicing in 49 US states and Washington, District of Columbia (see eTable 1 in Supplement 1 for the state-by-state participant count). The sample included 795 general OB-GYNs (92.0%), 522 respondents in private practice (60.4%), 78 respondents in rural hospitals (9.0%), and 83 respondents in Catholic hospitals (9.6%). There were 154 respondents who self-identified as abortion providers (17.8%). The distribution of sociodemographic and professional characteristics was similar between protective and restrictive states, although respondents in restrictive vs protective states were more likely to be White (434 respondents [76.7%] vs 191 respondents [64.1]%; P < .001), less likely to work in an academic hospital (149 respondents [26.3%] vs 104 respondents [34.9%]; P = .008), younger (eg, 177 respondents [31.3%] vs 63 respondents [21.1%] aged 25-44 years; P = .02), and less likely to self-identify as abortion providers (77 respondents [13.6%] vs 77 respondents [25.8%]; P < .001) (Table 2). No data were missing. P values for race and age are across all categories rather than specific pairwise comparisons.

Across the sample, the mean (SD) overall moral distress score was 2.25 (0.92) (Table 2) on a scale of 0 to 5 (higher scores indicate greater moral distress). Stratified by abortion policy environment, mean (SD) moral distress scores were similar in restrictive states (2.29 [0.93]) and protective states (2.17 [0.88]) (P for difference = .07). Levels were comparable to those in the original scale validation, which found a mean (SD) moral distress score of 2.45 (0.77) in a sample of 406 health care professionals.40

We saw statistically significant differences in the distribution of each clinical constraint by policy environment (Figure, A). For counseling constraints, comparing restrictive with protective states, prevalence (those reporting any experience) was 405 respondents (71.6%) vs 86 respondents (28.9%) (P < .001). For constraints on care provision, prevalence was 430 respondents (76.0%) vs 85 respondents (28.5%) (P < .001). For constraints on care provision in potentially life-threatening circumstances, prevalence was 381 respondents (67.3%) vs 65 respondents (21.8%) (P < .001). Moreover, the proportion reporting each constraint very much (quite a bit or extremely) was higher in restrictive vs protective states: 195 respondents (34.5%) vs 15 respondents (5.0%) for counseling constraints, 186 respondents (32.9%) vs 13 respondents (4.4%) for provision of care constraints, and 153 respondents (27.0%) vs 9 respondents (3.0%) for constraints on care in a life-threatening situation. We additionally evaluated constraint outcomes by 7 levels of state restrictiveness (eTable 2 in Supplement 1).

OB-GYNs’ attitudes toward their state’s abortion laws also varied by policy environment: 429 respondents (75.8%) in restrictive states rated their state’s abortion laws as much too restrictive or a little too restrictive vs 41 respondents (13.8%) in protective states (P < .001) (Table 2). We evaluated how level of reported constraints varied by participants’ attitudes toward their state’s abortion laws (Figure, B). We observed consistent gradients by abortion law opinion in endorsement of each clinical constraint. For example, for the question about abortion laws constraining ability to provide care to a patient with a potentially life-threatening pregnancy complication, we observed high endorsement (“quite a bit” or “extremely”) among 141 of those 309 respondents (45.6%) who felt that their state’s laws were much too restrictive, 12 of 161 respondents (7.5%) among those who felt the that laws were a little too restrictive, 8 of 337 respondents (2.4%) among those who felt thate laws were just right, and 1 of 57 respondents (1.8%) among those who felt thate laws were too accessible (P for difference < .001). These findings indicate that OB-GYNs’ broad opinions about their state laws corresponded with their direct experiences of clinical constraints from these laws.

Using adjusted linear mixed models of associations between clinical constraints and overall moral distress, we observed a consistent pattern: greater endorsement of each constraint was associated with progressively higher moral distress scores, in a dose-response fashion (Table 3). For example, for the extent to which state abortion laws constrained participant ability to provide care, the effect estimate (standard error [SE]) was 0.24 (0.08) for respondents somewhat experiencing this constraint (P = .003) and 0.69 (0.08) for those very much experiencing this constraint (P < .001) compared with those who did not experience this constraint. For the extent to which their state’s abortion laws constrained their ability to provide care for patients with potentially life-threatening pregnancy complications, participants who endorsed this somewhat had an effect estimate (SE) of 0.29 (0.07) for increased moral distress score (P < .001), while those who very much endorsed this had an effect estimate (SE) of 0.78 (0.10) for increased score (P < .001) compared with those who endorsed this not at all.

In analyses stratified by abortion policy environment, the pattern was similar in restrictive and protective states; within each policy stratum, greater exposure to each constraint was associated with progressively higher moral distress scores. We observed an interaction between state policy environment and counseling constraints. Here, counter to our initial hypothesis, we observed that the dose-response pattern was similar in both policy environments but the magnitude of the association between counseling constraints and moral distress was significantly higher in protective states, with an effect estimate (SE) of 0.57 (0.18) (P = .004) for those who endorsed this somewhat and 1.10 (0.19) (P < .001) for those who endorsed this very much, than in restrictive states, with an effect estimate (SE) of 0.20 (0.06) (P = .003) for those who somewhat endorsed this and 0.57 (0.10) (P < .001) for those who very much endorsed this (P value for interaction = .007). Other interaction terms were not statistically significant.

Finally, we evaluated associations between attitudes toward state abortion policies and moral distress (Table 3). Across all states, respondents who felt that their state’s laws were much too restrictive had higher levels of moral distress compared with those who felt that their state’s abortion laws were just right, with an effect estimate (SE) of 0.46 (0.10) (P < .001). Stratified models indicated that associations were mostly driven by restrictive states, with an effect estimate (SE) of 0.44 (0.17) (P = .02) in restrictive states and 0.54 (0.25) (P = .048) in protective states (P for interaction = .04). Moral distress was not significantly elevated among respondents who felt that laws were a little too restrictive or who felt that abortion was too accessible (Table 3).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study, one of the first national surveys of both general and subspecialist OB-GYNs in a post-Dobbs environment, we found that experiencing clinical constraints on care from state abortion laws was associated with generalized moral distress for OB-GYNs nationally. This expands prior research examining such associations qualitatively or in only abortion-restrictive states.14,15,25,41,42

Several studies have found that post-Dobbs state abortion policies were associated with both routine and emergency care of pregnant patients.14,15,27 To date, national quantitative research on OB-GYN moral distress after Dobbs has focused on abortion providers specifically22 or maternal-fetal medicine subspecialists.21,26 Because these subgroups likely interface with state abortion restrictions, their exposure to state abortion laws does not necessarily represent experiences of OB-GYNs more broadly. In our large national sample, of which 82.2% did not self-identify as abortion providers and 92.0% were general OB-GYNs, we found that the clear majority of OB-GYNs in restrictive states reported that their day-to-day clinical practice was nevertheless constrained by their state’s abortion policy environment in specific areas: counseling (71.6%), overall care provision (76.0%), and provision of care to patients with potentially life-threatening pregnancy complications (67.3%). These results affirm prior findings14,15 that state abortion restrictions penetrate everyday work environments of general and subspecialist OB-GYNs in restrictive settings by limiting routine care (eg, miscarriage management and treatment of obstetric complications). We, likewise, found that clinical constraints were not limited to OB-GYNs providing elective abortion care.

While the dose-response pattern between clinical constraints and moral distress is unsurprising, we found (perhaps counterintuitively) that experiencing clinical constraints related to state abortion laws was associated with moral distress in abortion-protective states. Nearly 3 years after Dobbs, OB-GYNs in restrictive states may have come to expect certain clinical constraints and become habituated to navigating associated moral dilemmas, whereas OB-GYNs in protective states may experience distress on learning that their states’ relatively liberal abortion laws may still preclude essential care provision. Other scholars have suggested that moral distress among OB-GYNs in protective states may reflect anticipatory stress about potential future abortion restrictions in their states or nationally or may stem from receiving out-of-state patients seeking abortion care.22 More research is needed to test these hypothesized explanations. It is also possible that OB-GYNs in states that are classified as protective but still have some legal limits on abortion may still experience moral distress related to care provision. Additionally, institutional policies may limit provision of abortion care beyond what is required by state law.43

Strengths of this study include a national, albeit not nationally representative, sample of verified OB-GYNs who were contacted directly, with no possibility of "bot" (nonhuman) completion by entities seeking guaranteed incentives or flooding of responses by ideologically driven groups. Participants worked in 49 states and Washington, District of Columbia; we had large enough subsets of OB-GYNs in restrictive and protective states to conduct adequately powered comparative analyses.

Although our nonprobability sample was not designed to be nationally representative, our final sample was largely reflective of the OB-GYN workforce by race (61% White nationally and 72.3% in our sample), Hispanic ethnicity (7% nationally and 6.3% in our sample), and sex (62% women nationally and 56.0% in our sample).44 Potential overrepresentation of male OB-GYNs would likely bias results toward the null given prior findings that abortion laws may have a more blunted association with well-being of male vs female OB-GYNs.15 In terms of selection bias with respect to abortion provision, 17.8% of our sample identified as abortion providers, below the 24% of OB-GYNs who reported abortion provision in a pre-Dobbs national survey.45 Thus, participants likely did not self-select into the study based on abortion-related ideology or practice, a limitation of the latter national survey.

Moral distress has been associated with negative downstream outcomes for clinicians, including burnout46,47 and attrition.47,48 Thus, addressing moral distress is essential to preserving the integrity of the broad OB-GYN workforce, not only those who provide abortions. While health care institutions cannot change state laws that restrict physician ability to provide certain forms of care, institutions can potentially mitigate adverse outcomes for physicians associated with these laws by implementing policies and practices that allow OB-GYNs and other clinicians to practice ethical medicine, within both abortion-restrictive and abortion-protective policy climates.29,49,50,51 There are projected national shortages of OB-GYNs in all but 6 states by 203552 and high rates of burnout and turnover intention among OB-GYNs that predated the COVID-19 pandemic and Dobbs.53 Given these factors, our findings affirm that a focus on the entire OB-GYN workforce, not just self-identified abortion providers or those in the most restrictive states, is imperative.

Limitations

The study has limitations, many of which relate to the sampling strategy. These tradeoffs were described previously.

Data collection took place in April and May 2025, a period of high stress and uncertainty for physicians in general and OB-GYNs in particular due to projected and enacted federal policy changes around gender-affirming care, Medicaid coverage, and federal research funding. These stressors, which may have been associated with moral distress, transcended abortion policy context and may help explain why we did not see differences in moral distress by policy climate. Additionally, there was heterogeneity of abortion restrictiveness within the restrictive category, ranging from a full ban with prison sentences of up to 99 years for clinicians who violate the law (Texas) to a ban beginning at viability with additional restrictions on previability care (Pennsylvania).39 Classifications in many cases incorporated constraints that predated Dobbs (eg, waiting periods and TRAP laws). To evaluate whether dichotomization oversimplified the policy exposure, we presented descriptive findings stratified by 7 levels of state restrictiveness. We observed progressively greater reports of clinical constraints as state abortion restrictiveness increased, with a sharp cutoff between some restrictions or protections and protective, supporting our decision to dichotomize.

Conclusions

In this cross-sectional study, we found that clinical constraints related to state abortion laws were associated with moral distress among OB-GYNs across the US. Findings from this study suggest that institutions in all states, not just those with the strictest abortion policies, could improve the well-being of the OB-GYN workforce by directly addressing sources of moral distress identified in this study.
