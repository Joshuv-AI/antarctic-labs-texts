Overactivation of PERK-Mediated Endoplasmic Reticulum Stress-Induced Testis Damage in Chronically Stressed Mice
Authors: Lixuan Peng, Xiaohan Tang, Pingping Tang, Cuilan Zhou, Yuyan Xiang, Wenyu Cao, Yichun Li, Cuiying Peng, Yang Xu, Suyun Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction: Although endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS) is known to be associated with spermatogenesis, there is limited information about the role and impact of ERS on chronic stress-induced male infertility. Methods: To determine the alteration in ERS-related proteins after chronic restraint stress (CRS), these male C57BL/6J mice were randomly divided into the control or CRS group. An ERS inhibitor, integrated stress response inhibitor (ISRIB), was used to establish the causal relationship between ERS and CRS-induced testicular damage, for which male C57BL/6J mice were randomly divided into the following three groups: normal + normal saline (NS), CRS + NS, and CRS + ISRIB. The ERS pathway-related proteins, sperm parameters, testicular structure, spermatogenic cell markers, enzymes involved in steroid biosynthesis, serum corticosterone (CORT) and gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) levels, and c-fos expression in the hypothalamic paraventricular nucleus (PVN) were evaluated. Results: CRS primarily activated the pancreatic endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK)-mediated ERS pathway in the testis. ISRIB administration improved histology structure, low semen quality, expression of spermatogenic cell markers, and the level of enzymes related to steroid biosynthesis in CRS mice. ISRIB administration also reduced serum CORT levels and normalized hypothalamic PVN neuronal activation, accompanied by increased serum GnRH levels in CRS mice. Conclusions: CRS increased p-PERK expression in the testis. However, treatment with the PERK blocker ISRIB improved sperm quality, preserved testis structure, and rescued the expression of steroidogenesis genes in CRS mice, which might be due to the restoration of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis and hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal function.

Introduction

Both chronic physiological and psychological stresses are suggested as vital causes of male reproductive dysfunction. Unfortunately, chronic stress widely exists in modern society, which results in decreased sperm amount, viability, motility, and semen volume and abnormal sperm morphology. , Therefore, animals that experienced chronic stress also demonstrated a breakdown of the blood–testis barrier, loss of germ cells, and disorders of hormonal function. , Our recent study also indicated that chronic restraint stress (CRS) causes germ cell apoptosis and a disorder in testosterone secretion in mice; however, the signaling pathway involved in this process remains poorly explored. Therefore, it is necessary to explore further the underlying molecular mechanisms.

The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) is concerned with calcium storage, cellular functions by modulating lipid metabolism, and protein homeostasis. Accumulation of unfolded proteins in the ER induces a condition of ER stress, which is relieved by decreased levels of incoming proteins when global protein synthesis is inhibited. Pancreatic endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK), inositol-requiring kinase 1 (IRE1), and activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6), which stay inactive due to their attachment to glucose-regulated protein 78 (GRP78), are the three transducers in the regulation of endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS). In particular, a recent in vitro study demonstrated that tri­(2-ethylhexyl) phosphate (TEHP)-induced cell injury in testis Sertoli cells possibly depended on ERS; however, there is no evidence from in vivo data. The integrated stress response inhibitor (ISRIB) has been classically used to modulate ERS signals, which might be achieved through the inhibition of PERK and its downstream pathway. In fact, ISRIB restores translation downstream of the kinase phosphorylation of eIF2α by activating the nucleotide exchange factor eIF2B. Although acting downstream of eIF2α phosphorylation, ISRIB was shown by Krukowski et al. to lower p-eIF2α levels in aging brains by disrupting the ATF4-GADD34-eIF2α phosphatase feedback loop, thereby reversing age-associated ISR activation.

In the present study, we demonstrated that ERS modulated testicular injury induced by CRS, as determined by the expression of major marker proteins in the ERS response in a CRS mouse model. We then used ISRIB to investigate whether there might be a causal relationship between ERS and CRS-induced testicular injury. Considering the important etiological role of testosterone synthesis in chronic stress-induced male reproductive toxicity, we further determined the potential effect of ISRIB on the expression of genes associated with testicular testosterone synthesis. Considering that the overactivation of the HPA axis could suppress HPG function that is involved in stress-induced male infertility, we also examined the effect of ISRIB treatment on HPA and HPG function by analyzing hypothalamic paraventricular nucleus (PVN) neuronal activation and serum levels of corticosterone (CORT) and gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH).

Materials and Methods

Experimental Animals

C57BL/6J male mice (aged 8 weeks; weighing 22–25 g), purchased from the Hunan SJA Lab Animal Center of Changsha (Hunan, China), were used in this study. The animals were maintained at 23 ± 2 °C, a relative humidity of 50 ± 10%, with lighting controls (12-h light/12-h dark cycle) and with free access to food and water. The experimental protocol was approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of South China and complied with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Drug Administration and Experimental Design

After 7 days of adaptive feeding, the mice were randomly divided into the control and CRS groups. Mice in the CRS group were bound in an acrylic cylinder (with an inner diameter of 6.5 cm, a length of 20 cm, and a cylinder nose with ventilation holes) from 9:00 am to 15:00 pm; this process lasted for 35 days. On the 35th day, the mice were sacrificed, and their testis was collected for further assay.

After 7 days of adaptive feeding, the mice were randomly divided into control + normal saline (NS), CRS + NS, and CRS + ISRIB groups. Mice in the CRS + NS and CRS + ISRIB groups were restricted in an acrylic cylinder (with an inner diameter of 6.5 cm, a length of 20 cm, and a cylinder nose with ventilation holes) from 9:00 am to 15:00 pm; this process lasted for 35 days. From 21 days onward, mice in the CRS + ISRIB group were daily injected with 0.5 mg/kg ISRIB intraperitoneally (TargetMol, dissolved in 0.9% NaCl) for 14 days, whereas mice in the control + NS and CRS + NS groups were injected with the same amount of NS. At the 35th day, the mice were sacrificed, and the testis and epididymis of the animals were collected for analysis of sperm parameters and further evaluation of molecular mechanisms.

Analysis of Sperm Count and Sperm Deformity Rate

Sperm count analysis was conducted according to a previous study. Briefly, the right epididymis was weighed, and the cauda epididymis was minced in 2 mL of prewarmed NS and incubated for 5 min at 37 °C to release the sperm. After filtering with a 200 nylon net, the sperm suspension was diluted 1:4 in NS, followed by thorough mixing, and then, 15 μL suspensions were placed on the blood cell counting plate. The number of sperm was counted under a light microscope at 20× using the conventional method.

Analysis of sperm deformity rate was conducted according to a previous study. The cauda epididymis was cut up in the centrifuge tube and incubated with prewarmed NS at 37 °C, after which it was placed in a water bath at 37 °C and incubated for 5 min. Next, a 500 μL sperm suspension was transferred into a centrifuge tube, and 50 μL of 2% eosin was added, followed by incubation for 1–2 min. Then, 15 μL of the mixed solution was smeared on a slide, completely dried, and observed under a light microscope at 40× (Zeiss). A total of 200 sperm were counted to obtain the ratio of deformed-to-normal sperm. Sperm deformity rate was calculated as the number of deformed sperm/(number of deformed sperm + number of normal sperm).

Morphological Evaluation by Light Microscopy

The testicular tissues were fixed overnight in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution at 4 °C. On the second day, the testicular tissues were dehydrated in an alcohol gradient, washed in xylene, and then embedded in paraffin blocks. The paraffin-embedded testis tissues were cut into 5 μm thick slices. The slices were unfolded in a film expander at 37 °C and then removed and dried without water. The paraffin sections were dewaxed with xylene and hydrated with alcohol of different concentrations. Then, the sections were subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, dehydrated in an alcohol gradient, dewaxed with xylene, and sealed with neutral resin. An ocular micrometer (Zeiss) was used to observe the histopathological changes in the seminiferous tubules at 40× and obtain images. Johnsen’s mean testicular biopsy score (MTBS) standard, listed in Table , was used to evaluate the histopathological changes in 20 seminiferous tubules of each testicular section. Each group of mice had multiple different cross sections and was divided into 0–10 points based on the maturity of the germinal epithelium of the seminiferous tubules.

RNA Isolation and Real-Time Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR)

After the mice were anesthetized with 10% sodium pentobarbital (45 mg kg–1), their testis was rapidly removed following decapitation. Trizol reagent (CWBIO) was used for RNA extraction according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the RNA purity was determined at an A260/A280 nm absorption ratio. The RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Fermentas, Canada) was used for cDNA synthesis according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The expression levels of genes were determined on an Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 3 real-time PCR system using Taraks Bio (TB) Green Premix Ex TaqII (Takara). All of the primers (listed in Table ) used in this study were designed using Primer 3 software, and the glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) gene expression was used as an internal control. The PCR cycling conditions were 30 s at 95 °C followed by 40 cycles at 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 45 s. The 2–△△Ct method was used for determining the relative gene expression according to our previous study.

Western Blot Analysis

Mice were anesthetized with 10% sodium pentobarbital (45 mg kg–1), and their testes were rapidly removed after decapitation. Frozen tissue was homogenized in a tissue protein extraction plus protease inhibitor (CWBIO). After centrifugation at 12 000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C, the supernatants were collected, and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Assay kit (CWBIO). Then, 20–30 μg of protein was separated on gradient polyacrylamide gels by 10–15% sodium dodecyl sulfate–poly­(acrylamide gel electrophoresis) (SDS-PAGE). Nitrocellulose membranes were blocked in 5% skim milk solution at room temperature for 2 h, after which they were incubated with primary antibodies (Table ) at room temperature for 2 h and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After washing the membranes three times in 0.05% Tween-Tris-buffered solution, they were incubated in horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat antirabbit (1:5000, CWBIO) or goat antimouse (1:1000, CWBIO) secondary antibodies for 2 h. After washing again three times in 0.05% Tween-Tris-buffered solution, the membranes were developed using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) system (CWBIO). The optical density of each band was measured using the NIH program ImageJ (NIH, Bethesda, Maryland), with β-tubulin or GAPDH used as a loading control for normalization.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

Mouse GnRH ELISA kit (ELK Biotechnology, China), CORT ELISA kit (ELK Biotechnology, China), and testosterone ELISA kit (Elabscience, China) were used for determining the levels of serum GnRH, CORT, and testosterone, respectively, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, after anesthetizing mice with 10% sodium pentobarbital (45 mg kg–1), they were sacrificed, and the trunk blood was collected for centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatants were collected and used to measure the levels of GnRH, CORT, and testosterone.

GnRH and CORT ELISA kit: First, 50 μL of standard or sample and 50 μL of biotinylated conjugate were added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1 h. After the wells were washed, 100 μL of HRP-conjugated antibody was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 60 min. After washing, 90 μL of 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) was added to each well and incubated for 20 min at 37 °C. Then, all of the wells were developed using 50 μL of stop reagent in the dark, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm (BioTek Instruments, Inc., EPOCH).

Testosterone ELISA kit: First, 50 μL of standard or sample and 50 μL of biotinylated conjugate were added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 45 min. After the wells were washed, 100 μL of HRP conjugate was added to each well and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. After another washing, 90 μL of TMB was added to each well and incubated for 15 min at 37 °C. Then, all wells were developed using 50 μL of stop reagent in the dark, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm (BioTek Instruments, Inc., EPOCH).

Immunohistochemistry

Mice were sacrificed for an immunohistochemistry assay according to our previous study. After anesthetizing mice with 10% sodium pentobarbital, they were perfused with NS and 4% paraformaldehyde. Then, the brains were removed and placed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C, followed by dehydration in 15 and 30% sucrose solutions at 4 °C. Next, the brain tissues were cut using a cryostat (30 μm thick). To remove endogenous peroxidase, the sections were placed in 3% H2O2 for 25 min, and then the free-floating sections were washed three times (10 min each time) with 0.01 M phosphate buffer solution (PBS). The sections were then blocked in 5% goat serum containing 0.1% Triton X-100 at room temperature for 2 h. Next, the sections were incubated with rabbit anti-c-fos primary antibody (1:1000, CST) and rabbit anti-Corticotropin-Releasing Hormone (CRH) primary antibody (1:500, ABconal) at room temperature for 2 h, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C. On the second day, the sections were incubated at room temperature for 30 min and then washed three times (10 min each time) with 0.01 M PBS. Then, the sections were incubated for 2 h in secondary reagents comprising biotinylated goat antirabbit immunoglobulin (Proteintech). After being rinsed three times (10 min each time) with 0.01 M PBS, the sections were incubated for 2 h at room temperature with the prepared AB solution (1:200, Vector Company). Finally, diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride (DAB) was used as a peroxidase substrate. After mounting on gelatin-coated slides and dehydration in gradient ethanol and xylene, all sections were covered with coverslips. All images were obtained by using an optical microscope (Olympus, Japan) under the same conditions. c-fos and CRH-immunopositive cells were counted by a researcher who was blinded to the treatment.

Statistical Analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted using Graphpad Prism 8.0 software (Graphpad Software, San Diego, California). Significant differences were determined using either Student’s t test for two-group comparisons or analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by post hoc Bonferroni testing for multiple comparisons between more than two groups (one-tailed α level). Values falling outside average ± 2.5 standard deviations were excluded as outliers. , P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To explore the changes in ERS in the testis of CRS mice, we first examined the expression of ERS markers between the control and CRS mice. Effect of the PERK Inhibitor ISRIB on Chronic Stress-Induced Spermatogenesis Dysfunction

After determining that the increased PERK expression mediated the ERS pathway in the testis of CRS mice, we further explored whether ISRIB, a PERK inhibitor, can prevent the CRS-induced testis damage (Figure A). One-way ANOVA revealed a significant difference among the groups in the sperm count (F (2,12) = 5.837, P = 0.0170) and sperm deformity rate (F (2,15) = 19.29, P < 0.0001) (Table ). Bonferroni post-test analysis revealed that compared with that in the control + NS group, the sperm count (t = 3.037, P = 0.031) decreased significantly and the sperm deformity rate (t = 4.007, P = 0.0034) increased significantly in the CRS + NS group. However, ISRIB treatment could reverse this situation (sperm count: t = 2.874, P = 0.0419; sperm deformity rate: t = 6.114, P < 0.0001). As depicted in Figure B, the sperm density decreased significantly in the CRS + NS group compared to that in the control + NS group, which was reversed by ISRIB treatment.

Effect of the PERK Inhibitor ISRIB on CRS-Induced Alterations in Testicular Structure

The histopathology results revealed obvious pathological structural changes in the testis of mice in the CRS + NS group, including loosely arranged spermatogenic cells and significantly reduced diameter of the seminiferous tubules of the testis, which were restored after ISRIB treatment (Figure A). We further confirmed these results using Johnsen’s scores, wherein one-way ANOVA revealed a significant difference in Johnsen’s scores (F (2,177) = 39.08, P < 0.0001) among the groups. As illustrated in Figure B, Bonferroni post-test analysis revealed significantly decreased Johnsen’s scores in the CRS + NS group (t = 8.772, P < 0.0001) compared with those in the control + NS group, which were reversed by ISRIB treatment (t = 5.336, P < 0.0001).

Effect of the PERK Inhibitor ISRIB on CRS-Induced Alterations of Spermatogenic Cells

To further explore the effects of ISRIB on various spermatogenic cells in the testis of CRS mice, we detected the levels of CD29 (a marker of spermatogonial stem cells), androgen receptor (AR) (a marker of Sertoli cells), GATA1 (a marker of Sertoli cells), and INSL3 (a marker of Leydig cells) by PCR (Figure A,D). One-way ANOVA revealed a significant difference in the levels of spermatogenic cell markers (CD29: F (2,14) = 9.150, P = 0.0029; AR: F (2,15) = 5.634, P = 0.0150; GATA1: F (2,15) = 8.377, P = 0.0036; INSL3: F (2,15) = 15.08, P = 0.0003) among the groups. Bonferroni post-test analysis revealed significantly decreased levels of CD29, AR, GATA1, and INSL3 in the CRS + NS group (CD29: t = 3.186, P = 0.0198; AR: t = 2.978, P = 0.0281; GATA1: t = 3.516, P = 0.0093; INSL3: t = 3.337, P = 0.0135) compared with those in the control + NS group, which were reversed by ISRIB treatment (CD29: t = 4.028, P = 0.0037; AR: t = 2.830, P = 0.0380; GATA1: t = 3.573, P = 0.0083; INSL3: t = 5.446, P = 0.0002). We further validated the spermatogenic cell markers, including CD29 and GFRA1 (markers of spermatogonial stem cells) and AR and SOX9 (markers of Sertoli cells), using Western blot assays (Figure E,F). One-way ANOVA revealed a significant difference in the levels of the spermatogenic cell markers (CD29: F (2,15) = 13.02, P = 0.0005; GFRA1: F (2,11) = 10.03, P = 0.0033; AR: F (2,13) = 5.983, P = 0.0144; SOX9: F (2,15) = 5.457, P = 0.0166) among the groups. Bonferroni post-test analysis revealed significantly decreased levels of CD29 (t = 4.929, P = 0.0005), GFRA1 (t = 2.924, P = 0.0415), AR (t = 3.109, P = 0.0249), and SOX9 (t = 2.869, P = 0.0351) in the CRS + NS group compared with those in the control + NS group, which were reversed by ISRIB treatment (GFRA1: t = 4.379, P = 0.0033; AR: t = 2.747, P = 0.0499; SOX9: t = 2.853, P = 0.0363).

ISRIB Treatment Increases the Levels of Enzymes Involved in the Biosynthesis of Steroids in the Testis of CRS Mice

We further explored the effects of ISRIB on reproductive dysfunction in CRS mice based on enzymes involved in the biosynthesis of steroids. One-way ANOVA revealed a significant difference in the levels of enzymes involved in the biosynthesis of steroids (STAR: F (2, 15) = 11.73, P = 0.0009; P450: F (2, 14) = 15.46, P = 0.0003; 3β-HSD: F (2, 15) = 9.020, P = 0.0027; CYP11A1: F (2, 15) = 12.17, P = 0.0007) among the groups. As depicted in Figure A,D, Bonferroni post-test analysis revealed significantly decreased levels of STAR (t = 3.707, P = 0.0063), P450 (t = 4.245, P = 0.0024), 3β-HSD (t = 3.179, P = 0.0187), and CYP11A1 (t = 3.367, P = 0.0127) in the CRS + NS group compared with those in the control + NS group, which were reversed by ISRIB treatment (STAR: t = 4.554, P = 0.0011; P450: t = 5.180, P = 0.0004; 3β-HSD: t = 4.029, P = 0.0033; CYP11A1: t = 4.806, P = 0.0007). Western blotting was also performed to investigate the expression of STAR (F (2, 11) = 12.11, P = 0.0017) in the testis. One-way ANOVA revealed a significant difference in the protein expression of STAR among the groups. Bonferroni post-test analysis revealed significantly decreased STAR levels (t = 4.287, P = 0.0039) in the CRS + NS group compared with those in the control + NS group, which were reversed by ISRIB treatment (STAR: t = 3.897, P = 0.0075) (Figure E,F).

ISRIB Treatment Resets the HPA Axis and HPG Axis in CRS Mice

Immunohistochemistry was performed to examine the neuronal activation markers c-fos and CRH in the PVN, a key brain region in the regulation of the HPA axis, based on the report that an increased number of c-fos-positive cells can be used to determine brain activation. One-way ANOVA revealed a significant difference in the expression of c-fos (F (2, 37) = 4.353, P = 0.0201) and CRH (F (2, 37) = 7.073, P = 0.0025) among the groups. Bonferroni post-test analysis revealed significantly increased c-fos (t = 2.668, P = 0.0338) and CRH (t = 3.648, P = 0.0024) levels in the CRS + NS group compared with those in the control + NS group, which were reversed by ISRIB treatment (c-fos: t = 2.554, P = 0.0448; CRH: t = 2.736, P = 0.0285) (Figure A,D). Moreover, one-way ANOVA revealed a significant difference in the levels of CORT (F (2, 11) = 34.18, P < 0.0001), GnRH (F (2, 12) = 14.11, P = 0.0007), and testosterone (F (2, 11) = 9.083, P = 0.0047) among the groups. Discussion

Chronic stress, leading to testis damage and spermatogenesis dysfunction, is identified as a key environmental factor that causes a dramatic increase in infertility problems. In this study, we found that CRS resulted in increased p-PERK expression in the testis, whereas treatment with ISRIB improved sperm quality, preserved testis structure, and recovered the expression of steroidogenesis genes in CRS mice, which might be attributed to the restoration of the HPA axis and HPG function. Thus, our results provide experimental evidence that ISRIB, a representative PERK inhibitor, preserves testicular structure and steroid hormone biosynthesis underlying chronic stress through normalization of the HPA axis and HPG function.

ERS activates the UPR, primarily including the three signal transducers IRE1, PERK, and ATF6, and the PERK signaling cascade is involved in modulating protein translational initiation and synthesis. Nevertheless, it remains unclear whether chronic stress will activate testicular ERS. Considering our previous study that demonstrated that CRS causes testis damage with its underlying signaling pathway unexplored, we first determined whether CRS would cause an alteration of the ERS pathway. Long-term CRS exposure exerted no impact on the expression of IRE1 and ATF6; however, we observed that testicular phosphorylation of PERK was upregulated by CRS. Despite the use of different animal models, previous studies demonstrated that activation of the PERK signaling pathway caused testicular damage involving blood–testis barrier destruction, spermatocyte apoptosis, and steroidogenesis disruption. − The PERK inhibitor ISRIB was used to establish the relationship between PERK activation and testicular damage. Although no difference was observed in the weight of the testis or epididymis after ISRIB treatment, we found that ISRIB rescued the CRS-induced low sperm concentration and high sperm deformity rate. Histopathological studies further demonstrated the protective effects of the ISRIB. CRS destroyed the normal morphology of spermatogonia, indicated by a lower testicular Johnson’s score than that in the control group; however, ISRIB treatment largely maintained the typical morphology and organization of seminiferous tubules, with improved testicular Johnsen’s scores. Because sperm analysis, including concentration, motility, and morphology, is a reliable parameter to demonstrate male reproductive health, it might be reasonable to consider that the activation of PERK-mediated ERS contributes to the damage effect of CRS-induced male reproductive dysfunction in mice.

Considering the protective effect of ISRIB on sperm parameters and testicular structure in CRS mice, we further investigated which spermatogenic cells might be involved in this phenomenon. Consistent with our previous study, chronic stress results in a downregulation of the markers of spermatogonial stem cells (CD29), Sertoli cells (AR and GATA1), and Leydig cells (INSL3). − Interestingly, ISRIB treatment could restore the CRS-induced low expression of the spermatogenic cell markers, which was consistent with the results of the sperm parameters. Although different markers were used in the Western blot assay, we found that the protein levels of Sertoli cell markers (AR and SOX9) and spermatogonial stem cell markers (CD29 and GFRA1) were also normalized by the ISRIB treatment. Because ISRIB treatment exerted protective effects on spermatogenic cells during the chronic stress, we then determined the expression of STAR, P450, CYP11A1, and 3β-HSD (all known as steroidogenic enzymes) in mouse testes. As anticipated, long-term chronic stress exposure remarkably reduced the testicular mRNA expression of STAR, P450, CYP11A1, and 3β-HSD; however, ISRIB treatment almost restored their expression. STAR can transfer cholesterol into mitochondria in Leydig cells, which plays a vital role in steroid hormone biosynthesis; therefore, we further determined the protein level of testicular STAR. In particular, we found that the CRS-induced downregulation of testicular STAR was also effectively normalized by ISRIB treatment. These findings indicate that PERK-mediated ERS may suppress both transcription and translation of steroidogenic enzymes.

Perturbation of the HPA axis and HPG function is involved in the regulation of reproductive function. Stress-induced CORT, the final product of the activation of the stress axis, causes suppressed HPG function. In fact, chronic stress stimulates corticotropin-releasing hormone neurons from the hypothalamic PVN, resulting in adrenocorticotropic hormone release from the anterior pituitary, which facilitates the production and release of glucocorticoids from the adrenal glands. The decrease in testosterone concentration was also in fact due to the increase in CORT level, as part of the stress-induced activation of the HPA axis, resulting in inhibition of the HPG axis. Previous research also demonstrated that CRS causes an elevation of serum CORT concentrations and a decline of testosterone concentrations. Corroborating with previous studies, we also demonstrated that CRS causes overactivation of PVN, as indicated by the increased number of c-fos-positive cells in the PVN of CRS mice. In particular, we found that the increased expression of PVN c-fos was normalized by ISRIB treatment. Consistent with the normalized HPA axis function, we also observed that the increased serum CORT level in CRS mice was downregulated by ISRIB treatment. Because excess CORT levels caused by chronic stress inhibit male sex hormones, including GnRH, luteinizing hormone, follicular-stimulating hormone, and testosterone, we further determined the serum level of GnRH and found that the reduced GnRH level in CRS mice was rescued by ISRIB treatment. Therefore, we consider that the protective effect of ISRIB on chronic stress-induced male reproductive dysfunction might be due to the restoration of the HPA axis and HPG function, which also requires further investigation. Considering the protective nature of the ISR, adverse effects may occur, which necessitate caution. In fact, we note that ISRIB might cause potential toxicity effects under certain conditions. It has been proposed that pharmacological inhibition of the ISR may be beneficial in the context of neurodegenerative diseases that are linked to the activation of this pathway. However, prolonging the ISR might also have a therapeutic effect in mouse models for protein-misfolding diseases. − We also noted that cell apoptosis was significantly elevated with ISRIB treatment in heat-stressed mouse testis. ISRIB enhances the survival of neurons, but other ISRIB-like eIF2B activators (2BAct and PRXS571) aggravate muscle denervation and motor neuronal death of the amyotrophic lateral sclerosis mice model. Therefore, a potential side effect of ISRIB should also be monitored, as the potential toxicity that ISR inhibition may cause at least a subset of ALS patients or other diseases. Thus, one might speculate that whether the ISRIB and other ISR inhibitors will be beneficial or detrimental may depend on the ability of such compounds to prevent the aberrant activation of the ISR without hindering its protective function. ,

This study has several limitations. Due to limited resources, we could not obtain some data using fMRI or a biopsy of living mice. We could not exclude the other mechanisms underlying stress-induced testicular damage, such as impaired blood–testis barrier and apoptosis. Therefore, more detailed mechanisms underlying the protective impact of ISRIB on stress-induced male infertility should be investigated. Moreover, it is necessary to further explore whether ISRIB treatment would benefit chronic stress-induced testicular damage in middle-aged or aged mice.

Conclusions

Abnormal testicular PERK expression might contribute to spermatogenesis dysfunction, which was related to chronic stress-induced gonadotoxicity. ISRIB could prevent the testicular toxicity of CRS through restoration of the HPA axis and HPG function; these effects were associated with improvements in testicular structure, sperm quality, and hormone levels. Overall, these findings might expand our understanding of the mechanism underlying chronic stress-induced testis damage.
