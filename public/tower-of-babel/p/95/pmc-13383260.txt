Synergistic eradication of NDM-1 Klebsiella pneumoniae biofilm infection by a Meropenem/EDTA Co-delivery system based on gelatin microspheres
Authors: Zhaoyou Chu, Shu Fang, Wanyue Fu, Jun Liang, Yayun Wu, Ling Xu, Hanqing Zhang, Jiyang Wang, Haisheng Qian, Min Shao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNew Delhi metallo-β-lactamase (NDM)-producing drug-resistant bacteria often form stubborn biofilms and cause severe pneumonia, resulting in conventional antibiotic failure. In this study, we developed gelatin-genipin microspheres co-loaded with EDTA and meropenem (GEM) for targeted treatment of NDM pneumonia. EDTA exerted dual functions: it chelated Zn2+ to inactivate NDM enzyme and restore meropenem activity (FIC = 0.046875), while also disrupting bacterial biofilms through chelation. GEM microspheres enable accelerated drug release in the artificial phlegm environment. In a mouse model of NDM pneumonia, GEM microspheres can enhance the recruitment of neutrophils and M1 cells at the early stage (12 h) through bacterial clearance, modulate the secretion of interferon-γ by CD8+ T cells and natural killer cells, and reshape the pulmonary immune microenvironment. Furthermore, GEM treatment reduced pathological pulmonary immune cell infiltration and excessive inflammation at late time points (days 3-7). Taken together, GEM microspheres integrate biofilm disruption, enzyme inhibition, synergistic bactericidal effects, and immunoregulation, representing a promising strategy against NDM drug-resistant bacterial pneumonia.Keywords: NDM-Producing bacteria, Microspheres, Pneumonia, Biofilm

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has become one of the most severe challenges in global public health [1]. In particular, infections caused by Carbapenem-Resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) are associated with high morbidity and mortality due to extremely limited therapeutic options [2,3]. Among these pathogens, Klebsiella pneumoniae strains producing New Delhi Metallo-β-lactamase (NDM) represent a typical subtype of CRKP. NDM enzymes can inactivate the vast majority of β-lactam antibiotics, including carbapenems, by hydrolyzing the β-lactam ring, leading to a situation where almost no effective antibiotics are available [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. Concurrently, these CRKP strains frequently form biofilms-an independent bacterial lifestyle that coexists with enzymatic resistance. Biofilms not only physically block the penetration of antibiotics but also encase bacteria within a self-produced matrix, where cells exhibit heterogeneous metabolic states with reduced activity, thereby further decreasing antibiotic susceptibility. This convergence of enzymatic resistance and biofilm-mediated tolerance may lead to chronic, recalcitrant infections that are ultimately difficult to cure [[8], [9], [10]]. Therefore, the development of novel therapeutic strategies that can effectively overcome NDM-mediated resistance and simultaneously eradicate biofilms has become an urgent need and frontier direction in current infectious disease research [[11], [12], [13]].

In response to the above challenges, current clinical strategies mainly rely on the last-resort antibiotics, namely polymyxins and tigecycline, used alone or in combination. However, the efficacy of these regimens remains unsatisfactory, and issues such as nephrotoxicity and heteroresistance have limited their widespread application. Meropenem (MEM), a broad-spectrum carbapenem antibiotic, is ineffective against NDM-producing strains but still shows potential value when used in combination with other agents [[14], [15], [16], [17]]. In recent years, combination therapeutic strategies based on a “synergistic strategy” have been regarded as a promising approach to address the dilemma of antimicrobial resistance. Studies have demonstrated that metal ion chelators, such as ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), can specifically chelate the zinc ions (Zn2+) essential for the active center of NDM enzymes, leading to their inactivation and directly restoring the antibacterial activity of carbapenems including meropenem [18]. In addition, EDTA can disrupt the biofilm extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) structure stabilized by divalent cations (e.g., Ca2+, Mg2+), enhancing the penetration and killing efficacy of antibiotics against bacteria embedded within biofilms. Nevertheless, the safe and effective delivery of EDTA to infection sites remains a major challenge [19]. Systemic administration hardly achieves effective concentrations in the lungs, while pulmonary local delivery (e.g., aerosol inhalation) of high concentrations of free EDTA may cause severe irritation to lung tissues and systemic toxicity risks (such as hypocalcemia), severely limiting its clinical translation. Therefore, the development of a drug delivery system capable of co-targeting EDTA and antibiotics to pulmonary infectious lesions with controlled release is crucial for maximizing therapeutic efficacy while minimizing toxic side effects.

Microsphere carriers based on biomaterials (e.g., gelatin) exhibit great potential in the field of pulmonary targeted delivery due to their excellent biocompatibility, degradability, and drug-loading flexibility. Accordingly, this study aims to design and construct a drug delivery system based on gelatin microspheres loading MEM and EDTA, to combat drug-resistant bacterial pneumonia induced by Klebsiella pneumoniae expressing NDM enzyme. Gelatin microspheres loaded with MEM and EDTA were prepared via a simple emulsification-crosslinking method, with genipin as the crosslinking agent. Based on the controllable size design of these microspheres (2-5 μm), effective pulmonary delivery of antibiotics is achieved, and the crosslinking structure enables sustained degradation to exert long-term antibacterial effects. The core function of EDTA released upon degradation is to serve as a potent metal ion chelator. It can efficiently bind (chelate) divalent cations in the microenvironment, such as Ca2+, Mg2+, and Fe2+/3+, which are critical for maintaining biofilm integrity. Thus, EDTA disrupts the crosslinking of the EPS structure, leading to disintegration and loosening of the EPS matrix, thereby making the biofilm more penetrable. Furthermore, EDTA increases the permeability of bacterial cell membranes and restores the bactericidal activity of MEM by chelating the Zn2+ ions in the active center of NDM enzyme, which can greatly enhance the killing effect of antibacterial agents against bacteria within biofilms. Therefore, gelatin microspheres co-loaded with MEM and EDTA not only alleviate the direct pulmonary irritation and toxicity caused by high-dose EDTA, but also act as adjuvants or sensitizers for antibacterial agents to jointly combat refractory pulmonary biofilm infections.

Materials and methods

Chemical reagents

The NDM-1 (BNCC358232) was purchased from the official website of BNCC. EDTA, gelatin, genipin, trihydrate meropenem, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), imidazole, phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), isopropyl-β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG), lysozyme and HEPES buffer were purchased from Millipore Biosciences Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Hexane and Span 80 were purchased from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM), Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) medium, penicillin-streptomycin mixture, fetal bovine serum (FBS) and trypsin were purchased from Invitrogen Company. Dulbecco's Phosphate Buffered Saline (PBS) was purchased from Gibco (New York State, Grand Island, USA). All reagents were of analytical purity and were used directly after purchase without further purification.

Preparation of GEM and its physical and chemical characterization

GEM microspheres were prepared by emulsification cross-linking. First, 200 mg gelatin, 20 mg EDTA, and 10 mg MEM were dissolved together in 5 mL deionized water to form Solution A. Then, 50 mL liquid paraffin and 8 mL SP-80 were added to a 100 mL beaker, and the mixture was stirred at 10,000 rpm using a high-speed homogenizer. Solution A was slowly added dropwise into the beaker, and stirring was continued for 20 min. Next, 5 mg genipin was dissolved in DMSO and slowly added dropwise into the beaker, followed by stirring for another 5 min. The beaker was then placed on a stirrer and stirred at 500 rpm for 12 h. Finally, the beaker was removed, unencapsulated drug was washed out with n-hexane, and the product was freeze-dried to obtain GEM microsphere powder.

To characterize the morphology, particle size, Zeta potential, crystal structure and drug loading rate of GEM microspheres, scanning electron microscopy (Carl Zeiss, Germany, GeminiSEM 300) was used to investigate their microsphere morphology, nanoparticle size and Zeta potential analyzer (90 Plus PALS) was used to explore their particle size and Zeta potential, X-ray diffractometer (DX-2700BH) was used to determine whether they have a lattice structure, and ultraviolet-infrared detector was used to investigate their drug absorption peaks and loading rate. In order to study its drug loading rate, 1 mg of GEM microspheres were ultrasonically dissolved in 1 mL of deionized water. The ultraviolet detector was used to measure the UV absorption peak at 297 nm, and the curves of UV absorption peak versus concentration of the drug was compared. The drug loading rate was calculated. To investigate the release of MEM from GEM microspheres under different pH conditions, 1 mL of GEM solution with a concentration of 1 mg mL−1 was added to an 8-14 k dialysis bag. The bag was sealed with a dialysis clamp and placed in a 50 mL centrifuge tube. 20 mL of PBS solution with a pH value of 7.4 or artificial phlegm was added to it. The dialysis bag was taken out at 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, 72, and 168 h, and placed in a new 50 mL centrifuge tube containing 20 mL of deionized water. Then, the ultraviolet absorption peak of the solution in the centrifuge tube at 297 nm was measured to obtain the MEM drug release curve.

In Vitro Antibacterial Assays of GEM microspheres

To investigate the antibacterial performance of GEM microspheres, a series of in vitro antibacterial assays were performed.

In Vitro Antibacterial Assays: NDM-1-producing strains were inoculated into sterile TSB medium and cultured in a constant-temperature shaker at 37 oC with a shaking speed of 200 rpm until the logarithmic growth phase was reached. The bacterial suspension was then diluted with fresh TSB medium to a concentration of 1 × 105 CFU mL−1. A series of MEM and EDTA solutions with concentration gradients (0, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256 μg mL−1) was prepared in 1 mL volume using TSB, MEM, and EDTA. Subsequently, 50 μL of the above bacterial suspension was added to each solution. The mixtures containing bacteria, MEM, and EDTA were transferred into 96-well plates, which were then sealed and incubated at 37 oC for 12h. The absorbance at OD600 was measured using a microplate reader, and the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was confirmed by visual inspection. The minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) was further verified by plate counting on TSB solid medium. Meanwhile, the synergistic coefficient was calculated according to the corresponding formula, and the synergistic curve was plotted.

Inhibition Zone Assay: First, the bacterial suspension with a concentration of 1 × 105 CFU mL−1 was uniformly spread on solid culture dishes. Subsequently, six circular wells of the same size were punched in each culture dish. This experiment was divided into 6 groups: Control group, EDTA group (concentration: 3.6 μg mL−1), MEM group (concentration: 2.5 μg mL−1), 1/4 GEM group (concentration: 20 μg mL−1), 1/2 GEM group (concentration: 40 μg mL−1), and GEM group (concentration: 80 μg mL−1). The corresponding materials were added into the wells separately, and the culture dishes were placed in a bacterial incubator for 12h of incubation. Afterwards, the diameter of each inhibition zone was measured using ImageJ software, and statistical analysis was performed.

Growth Curve Assay: The strain that produces NDM-1 was introduced into sterile TSB broth and cultured at 37 oC in a constant temperature at 200 rpm until the exponential phase was reached. Afterwards, the bacterial suspension was adjusted to a concentration of 1 × 105 CFU mL−1 with fresh TSB medium. This experiment was divided into 6 groups: Control, EDTA (3.6 μg mL−1), MEM (2.5 μg mL−1), 1/4 GEM (20 μg mL−1), 1/2 GEM (40 μg mL−1), and GEM (80 μg mL−1). For each group, 1 mL of the corresponding solution was prepared, and 50 μL of the above bacterial suspension was added. Then, 200 μL of each mixture was transferred into 96-well plates. The plates were sealed and incubated at 37 oC. The absorbance at OD600 was measured using a microplate reader at 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 24h, respectively, and the growth curves were plotted accordingly.

Bacterial SEM Imaging: The strain that produces NDM-1 was introduced into sterile TSB broth and cultured at 37 oC in a constant temperature at 200 rpm until the exponential phase was reached. Afterwards, the bacterial suspension was adjusted to a concentration of 1 × 105 CFU mL−1 with fresh TSB medium. The experiment was divided into 6 groups: Control, EDTA (3.6 μg mL−1), MEM (2.5 μg mL−1), 1/4 GEM (20 μg mL−1), 1/2 GEM (40 μg mL−1), and GEM (80 μg mL−1).For each group, 3 mL of the corresponding solution was prepared, followed by the addition of 150 μL of the above bacterial suspension. Then, 1 mL of the mixture was transferred into 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes. The tubes were sealed and incubated at 37 oC for 12h. After incubation, the medium was removed by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 5 min, and the pellets were washed with PBS 1-2 times to completely remove the residual medium. Subsequently, 500 μL of 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution was added for fixation for 4h. After centrifugation to remove the glutaraldehyde, the samples were dehydrated and washed sequentially with 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% ethanol solutions. Finally, the samples were resuspended in ethanol, dropped onto silicon wafers, and air-dried. Bacterial morphology was then observed and imaged using scanning electron microscopy (SEM).

Bacterial Live/Dead Staining: The strain that produces NDM-1 was introduced into sterile TSB broth and cultured at 37 oC in a constant temperature at 200 rpm until the exponential phase was reached. Afterwards, the bacterial suspension was adjusted to a concentration of 1 × 105 CFU mL−1 with fresh TSB medium. This experiment was divided into 6 groups: Control group, EDTA group (concentration: 3.6 μg mL−1), MEM group (concentration: 2.5 μg mL−1), 1/4 GEM group (concentration: 20 μg mL−1), 1/2 GEM group (concentration: 40 μg mL−1), and GEM group (concentration: 80 μg mL−1). For each group, 3 mL of the corresponding solution was prepared, and 150 μL of the above bacterial suspension was added. Then, 1 mL of the mixture was transferred into 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes, which were sealed and incubated in a 37 oC constant-temperature incubator for 12 h. After incubation, the medium was removed by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 5 min, and the bacterial pellets were washed with PBS 1-2 times to completely remove the residual medium. In accordance with the protocol of the LIVE/DEAD BacLight Bacterial Viability Kit, the test sample was mixed with a combination of DMAO (1.5 μg mL−1) and PI (1.5 μg mL−1). After blending thoroughly, the mixture was kept in darkness for 20 min to achieve complete staining. Afterwards, the bacteria were visualized and photographed under an inverted fluorescence microscope, and the proportion of live to dead cells was determined.

Bacterial Biofilm Crystal Violet Staining Assay: The strain that produces NDM-1 was introduced into sterile TSB broth and cultured at 37 oC in a constant temperature at 200 rpm until the exponential phase was reached. Afterwards, the bacterial suspension was adjusted to a concentration of 1 × 105 CFU mL−1 with fresh TSB medium. A total of 24 mL of TSB medium was taken, to which 1 mL of the above bacterial suspension and 600 μL of 0.1 g mL−1 glucose solution were added and mixed thoroughly. Subsequently, 1 mL of the mixed solution was added to each well of a 24-well plate. After sealing, the plate was statically incubated in a 37 oC constant-temperature incubator. At 0h, 12h, and 24h, corresponding materials were added to each group separately according to the grouping (Control, EDTA (concentration: 3.6 μg mL−1), MEM (concentration: 2.5 μg mL−1), 1/4 GEM (concentration: 20 μg mL−1), 1/2 GEM (concentration: 40 μg mL−1), and GEM (concentration: 80 μg mL−1)). The plate was then sealed again and incubated at 37 oC for 12h. After incubation, the medium was discarded, and each well was washed 1-2 times with PBS. Then, 1 mL of 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution was added to each well for fixation for 30 min. After fixation, the glutaraldehyde solution was aspirated, and each well was washed once with PBS. Subsequently, 0.1% crystal violet solution was added to each well for staining for 15 min. The dye was then aspirated, and each well was washed 2-3 times with deionized water to remove residual dye. Images of the biofilms were captured immediately. Afterwards, ethanol was added to each well to elute the biofilms, and the eluates were transferred to 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes, followed by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was collected, and its absorbance at 570 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

Bacterial Biofilm Live/Dead Staining Assay: The strain that produces NDM-1 was introduced into sterile TSB broth and cultured at 37 oC in a constant temperature at 200 rpm until the exponential phase was reached. Afterwards, the bacterial suspension was adjusted to a concentration of 1 × 105 CFU mL−1 with fresh TSB medium. A total of 24 mL of TSB medium was taken, to which 1 mL of the above bacterial suspension and 600 μL of 0.1 g mL−1 glucose solution were added and mixed thoroughly. Subsequently, 1 mL of the mixed solution was added to each confocal dish. After sealing, the dishes were statically incubated in a 37 oC constant-temperature incubator. At 0h, 12h, and 24h, corresponding materials were added to each group separately according to the grouping (Control, EDTA (concentration: 3.6 μg mL−1), MEM (concentration: 2.5 μg mL−1), 1/4 GEM (concentration: 20 μg mL−1), 1/2 GEM (concentration: 40 μg mL−1), and GEM (concentration: 80 μg mL−1)). The dishes were then sealed again and incubated at 37 oC for 12h. After incubation, the medium was discarded, and each confocal dish was washed 1-2 times with PBS to completely remove the residual medium. In accordance with the protocol of the LIVE/DEAD BacLight Bacterial Viability Kit, the test sample was mixed with a combination of DMAO (1.5 μg mL−1) and PI (1.5 μg mL−1). After blending thoroughly, the mixture was kept in darkness for 20 min to achieve complete staining. Afterwards, the bacteria were visualized and photographed under an inverted fluorescence microscope, and the proportion of live to dead cells was determined.

Extraction Experiment of NDM-1 β-Lactamase: NDM-1-producing strains were inoculated into sterile TSB medium and cultured in a constant-temperature shaker at 37 oC with a shaking speed of 200 rpm until the logarithmic growth phase. Subsequently, 0.2 mM isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) and 0.2 mM zinc sulfate (ZnSO4) were added to induce overexpression of the target protein, followed by overnight incubation at 25 oC. After incubation, the medium was removed by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 5 min, and the bacterial pellets were washed 1-2 times with PBS. The pellets were then resuspended in 10-20 mL lysis buffer (20 mM HEPES, 0.5 M NaCl, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), 0.1 mg mL−1 lysozyme, pH 7.0) and incubated on ice for 30 min. Bacterial membranes were disrupted using an ultrasonic cell disruptor (power: 200-300 W, work time: 3 s, interval time: 5 s, total time: 30-60 min) until the bacterial suspension became clear. The lysate was centrifuged at 10000 rpm for 10 min to remove bacterial debris, and the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm filter membrane. The filtrate was then loaded onto a pre-equilibrated Ni2+-NTA gravity column (equilibrated with Binding Buffer: 20 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, 20 mM imidazole, pH 7.0). After allowing sufficient binding of the protein to the column, the lower end was opened to drain the liquid. Weakly bound impurity proteins were then eluted with Washing Buffer (20 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, 50 mM imidazole, pH 7.0) until the absorbance of the ultraviolet (UV) detector at 280 nm returned to the baseline. Subsequently, the NDM-1 enzyme was repeatedly eluted with 10 mL Elution Buffer (20 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, 250 mM imidazole, pH 7.0) to obtain the stock solution of NDM-1 enzyme, which was then quantified using a BCA protein assay kit.

Zn Displacement Experiment of NDM-1 Enzyme: Ten micrograms of NDM-1 enzyme was taken from the above-prepared NDM-1 enzyme solution into a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube. Subsequently, GEM solutions of different concentrations (0, 5, 10, 20, 40, 80 μg mL−1) were added to the tubes separately. The mixtures were incubated in a constant-temperature shaker at 37 oC with a shaking speed of 200 rpm for 8h. After incubation, the solutions were transferred into 8-14 kDa dialysis bags for dialysis for 12h. Then, the enzyme solution in the dialysis bags was subjected to nitric acid digestion and filtration. The content of Zn ions in the filtrate was measured by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS), and the corresponding curve was plotted.

NDM-1 Enzyme Activity Assay: Ten micrograms of NDM-1 enzyme was taken from the above-prepared NDM-1 enzyme solution into 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes. Subsequently, solutions were prepared according to different groups: Control group, EDTA group (concentration: 3.6 μg mL−1), 1/4 GEM group (concentration: 20 μg mL−1), 1/2 GEM group (concentration: 40 μg mL−1), and GEM group (concentration: 80 μg mL−1). MEM solution was added to each group to make its final concentration 20 μg mL−1. The mixtures were then transferred into a 96-well plate and placed in a microplate reader set at 37 oC. The ultraviolet absorbance at 297 nm was detected every 5 min for a total of 4h. Finally, curves were plotted according to the absorbance values.

Zn Supplementation Experiment of NDM-1 Enzyme:Ten micrograms of NDM-1 enzyme was taken from the above-prepared NDM-1 enzyme solution into 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes. Subsequently, solutions were prepared according to different groups: Control group, EDTA group (concentration: 3.6 μg mL−1), 1/4 GEM group (concentration: 20 μg mL−1), 1/2 GEM group (concentration: 40 μg mL−1), and GEM group (concentration: 80 μg mL−1). The mixtures were incubated in a constant-temperature shaker at 37 oC with a shaking speed of 200 rpm for 4 h. After incubation, MEM solution was added to each group to make its final concentration 20 μg mL−1. The mixtures were then transferred into a 96-well plate and placed in a microplate reader set at 37 oC. The ultraviolet absorbance at 297 nm was detected every 5 min for a total of 4 h, and curves were plotted according to the absorbance values.

Bacterial Transcriptome Sequencing Experiment: The strain that produces NDM-1 was introduced into sterile TSB broth and cultured at 37 oC in a constant temperature at 200 rpm until the exponential phase was reached. Afterwards, the bacterial suspension was adjusted to a concentration of 1 × 105 CFU mL−1 with fresh TSB medium. This experiment was divided into 2 groups: Control group and 1/2 GEM group (concentration: 40 μg mL−1). For each group, 1 mL of the corresponding solution was prepared, and 50 μL of the above bacterial suspension was added. Then, 1 mL of the mixture was transferred into 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes, which were sealed and incubated in a 37 oC constant-temperature incubator for 12h. After incubation, the medium was removed by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 5 min, and the bacterial pellets were washed 2-3 times with PBS. The samples were then stored in dry ice for subsequent transcriptome sequencing.

Bacterial MPC (Mutant Prevention Concentration) Assay: The strain that produces NDM-1 was introduced into sterile TSB broth and cultured at 37 oC in a constant temperature at 200 rpm until the exponential phase was reached. Afterwards, the bacterial suspension was adjusted to a concentration of 1 × 105 CFU mL−1 with fresh TSB medium. This experiment was divided into 7 groups: Control group, MEM groups (concentrations: 64, 128, 256 μg mL−1), and GEM groups (concentrations: 40, 80, 160 μg mL−1). For each group, 1 mL of the corresponding solution was prepared, and 50 μL of the above bacterial suspension was added. Then, 200 μL of each mixture was transferred into 96-well plates, which were sealed and incubated in a 37 oC constant-temperature incubator for 12h. After incubation, the absorbance at OD600 was measured using a microplate reader. The bacterial suspension from the turbid well with the highest MEM concentration was taken for subculture for another 12h, and then inoculated into new plates again. Whenever turbidity appeared in the second highest concentration of MEM or GEM, this concentration was designated as the minimum concentration, and its 2-fold and 4-fold concentrations were used for subsequent plate inoculation. This process was continued for 10 consecutive days. The MIC value of each plate inoculation was recorded, and a graph was plotted according to the fold changes.

In vitro cellular and animal studies of GEM microspheres

Cytotoxicity Assay: Normal dermal fibroblasts in the exponential phase of growth were plated into sterile 96-well plates. Once the cells had attached and the monolayer reached 50% confluency, the spent medium was removed and fresh medium containing group-specific supplements was added: Control group, EDTA group (concentration: 3.6 μg mL−1), 1/4 GEM group (concentration: 20 μg mL−1), 1/2 GEM group (concentration: 40 μg mL−1), and GEM group (concentration: 80 μg mL−1). Each group was set with 3 replicate wells. The 96-well plate was kept at 37 oC in a onstant-temperature incubator under an atmosphere of 5% CO2 for 24 h. After incubation, 10 μL of MTT reagent was added to each well, and incubation was continued for another 4h. Subsequently, the supernatant was discarded, and DMSO was added to each well for dissolution for 15 min. Finally, the absorbance value of each well at 490 nm was measured using a microplate reader, and the cell viability was calculated to evaluate the toxic effect of the materials on normal cells.

Mouse Pneumonia Model Experiment: NDM-1-producing strains were inoculated into sterile TSB medium and cultured in a constant-temperature shaker at 37 oC with a shaking speed of 200 rpm for 12h. After incubation, the bacterial suspension was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min to concentrate to a concentration of 1 × 108 CFU mL−1. This experiment was divided into three groups, with 8 mice in each group: Blank group, Control group, and GEM group. The Blank group was not modeled and served as the healthy control group; the Control group was modeled but not treated; the GEM group was modeled and treated with GEM microspheres. Fifty microliters of the bacterial suspension were injected into the lungs of mice through intubation to establish the pneumonia model. 1 h after modeling, 6 mice were randomly selected and treated by spraying GEM microspheres into their lungs via aerosol, which was regarded as the GEM group. The survival status and body weight changes of the mice were recorded every day for 7 consecutive days, and the corresponding curves were plotted.

Blood Biochemistry and Routine Blood Tests: On days 0.5, 1, 3, and 7 of mouse treatment, mouse blood was collected by eyeball extraction. Blood biochemistry indicators (LDH, AST, CREA, UREA, B/C, TP, ALB, Glo, AST, AST/ALT, ALP) and routine blood indicators (WBC, RBC, HGB, HCT, Neu%, PLT) were measured respectively.

Mouse Lung Bacterial Load Assay: At 12 h after treatment, the lungs of mice in the Blank, Control, and GEM groups were harvested. PBS was added to the harvested lungs for grinding. Serial dilution and plate counting were performed on TSB solid medium to observe the bacterial survival rate in the mouse lungs.

Mouse Lung Pathological Section Assay: At 12 h after treatment, the lungs of mice in the Blank, Control, and GEM groups were harvested and fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution. Subsequently, the lung tissues were subjected to paraffin embedding and sectioning. The sections were then stained with H&E, TNF-α, IL-6, and IFN-γ dyes, respectively. The pathological changes of the mouse lung sections were observed under a microscope.

Flow Cytometry Detection of Mouse Lungs and Spleens: 12 h after treatment, lungs were harvested from mice in the Blank group, Control group, and GEM group. The tissues were homogenized in PBS to prepare single-cell suspensions. Subsequently, the single-cell suspensions were incubated with various fluorescent dye-conjugated antibodies according to the manufacturer's instructions. The antibodies used included: CD45-AF700, Gr-1-FITC, F4/80-BV785, CD86-PE, CD206-BV711, CD4-BUV395, CD8-APC/Cy7, TNF-α-BV605, IFN-γ-BV421, CD3-PE/Cy7, and CD49B-PE/Dazzle 594. After cell fixation and permeabilization, intracellular staining of TNF-α and IFN-γ was performed. Fluorescence data were acquired using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter). Data analysis was conducted using FlowJo software (Tree Star Software, San Carlos, CA, USA).

Statistical Analysis: All results are expressed as the mean ± SD, as indicated. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8. A one-way ANOVA with Tukey's post-hoc test was used to evaluate statistically significant differences in multiple group data (P > 0.05 (n.s.), *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001). Statistical significance between two groups was evaluated using the Student's t-test. (P > 0.05 (n.s.), *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001) (n.s., not significant).

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of GEM

Fig. 1 shows the schematic illustration of gelatin (Gel) crosslinked with genipin (GEN) to fabricate EDTA- and MEM-loaded microspheres (denoted as GEM) via the emulsification-crosslinking method. As shown in Fig. 2A and B, SEM images and the corresponding magnified images reveal that the microspheres have a size of approximately 2-2.5 μm. Meanwhile, Fig. 2C shows that their hydrodynamic diameter is about 2.2 μm, which falls within the optimal aerosol particle size range (2-5 μm) [20]. The zeta potential of GEM microspheres was approximately −16.34 mV, which may be attributed to the crosslinking between the gelatin matrix and genipin. Subsequently, XRD analysis of GEM microspheres indicated no crystalline lattice structure (Fig. 2D). As displayed in Fig. 2E, MEM exhibited a characteristic UV absorption peak at 297 nm, and GEM microspheres also showed an absorption peak at 297 nm, confirming the successful loading of MEM in GEM microspheres. On the basis of the successful construction of GEM microspheres, the loading contents of MEM and EDTA were investigated by UV vis absorption spectroscopy. EDTA can complex with Cu2+ to form a chelate with a characteristic absorption peak at 254 nm. As shown in Fig. S1, the standard calibration curves of MEM and EDTA were established by fitting the concentration versus UV absorption intensity. As listed in Fig. 2F, in 1 mg of GEM microspheres, the average content of MEM was approximately 30.381 μg mg−1 with a loading efficiency of about 3.04%, and the average content of EDTA was about 45.319 μg mg−1 with a loading efficiency of approximately 4.53%. Therefore, the successful co-loading of MEM and EDTA lays a prerequisite for the subsequent exertion of antibacterial activity, and it is of great significance to investigate their drug release behavior. Considering that the focus of respiratory tract infection is often encapsulated by viscous sputum, where the microenvironment markedly differs from normal physiological conditions, this study examined the release behaviors of MEM and EDTA in pH 7.4 PBS and artificial sputum, respectively. As shown in Fig. 2G and H, the release rates of both drugs in artificial sputum were significantly faster than those in PBS. In PBS, the cumulative release of MEM was approximately 50% and that of EDTA was approximately 35% on day 1; whereas in artificial sputum, the cumulative release of both MEM and EDTA reached around 80% on day 1. This phenomenon can probably be ascribed to the enzymatic degradation of the gelatin carrier by proteases present in artificial sputum, which disrupts the genipin-crosslinked gelatin network and leads to a sharp increase in microsphere permeability. Meanwhile, components such as mucins in the sputum may also interact with the drugs or the gelatin matrix, further weakening the drug-carrier binding and thereby accelerating the diffusional release of the drugs [21]. This feature enables GEM microspheres to respond rapidly to the sputum-covered infectious microenvironment, achieving localized burst release of MEM and EDTA, which is beneficial for the timely exertion of synergistic antibacterial effects in drug-resistant respiratory tract infections and improves therapeutic efficacy. To assess cytocompatibility in the context of NDM-1 K. pneumoniae pneumonia, Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVEC) (as a model of vascular endothelium susceptible to infection-induced injury) were exposed to Control, EDTA, MEM, and GEM at 20, 40 and 80 μg mL−1 (the latter being the therapeutic dose) for 12 h. All groups retained >90% viability (Fig. S2), indicating negligible cytotoxicity and supporting the safety of the formulation for potential vascular exposure during acute pneumonia. Next, the synergistic antibacterial effect of EDTA and MEM was evaluated. As shown in Fig. 2I and S3A, the checkerboard assay was employed to verify the synergistic antibacterial activity of EDTA and MEM against the tested bacteria. The results showed that the MIC and MBC of MEM against this bacterium were both higher than 128 μg mL−1. This value is much higher than the MIC breakpoint (8 μg mL−1) for Enterobacterales against meropenem as defined by the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST). After the addition of EDTA, plate counting results demonstrated that bactericidal efficacy could be achieved even when the MEM concentration was reduced to 2 μg mL−1. Therefore, the FIC index for the synergistic bactericidal effect of EDTA and MEM was calculated as follows: FIC = FIC(MEM) + FIC(EDTA) = (2/128) + (4/128) = 0.046875 (<0.5). The corresponding synergistic bactericidal curve is presented in Fig. 2J. As shown in Fig. 2K, the growth curves of NDM-1-producing Klebsiella pneumoniae revealed that treatment with MEM alone, EDTA alone, or GEM microspheres at low concentrations failed to cause effective bacterial killing. In contrast, GEM microspheres at higher concentrations exerted a potent bactericidal effect, completely inhibiting bacterial growth within 24 h. Fig. 2L presents the antibacterial heatmap at 12 h. Meanwhile, aliquots of the culture were withdrawn at 0, 2, 4, 8, and 12 h and spread on agar plates to evaluate the bacteriostatic and bactericidal activities. As displayed in Fig. 2M, the antibacterial activity against the NDM-producing strain increased progressively with increasing GEM microsphere concentration, and effective killing was observed at all tested time points when the concentration reached 80 μg mL−1. Furthermore, the inhibition zone assay was used to assess the antibacterial effect of GEM microspheres against NDM-producing K. pneumoniae. As shown in Fig. 2N and O, neither EDTA nor meropenem alone could effectively inhibit bacterial growth, whereas the GEM microsphere system loaded with both agents exhibited significant antibacterial activity. These findings lay a solid foundation for subsequent in vitro antibacterial experiments and for the construction of an animal pneumonia model to conduct in vivo studies.

Antibacterial effect of GEM

Based on the synergistic antibacterial properties of EDTA/MEM co loaded GEM microspheres, their antibacterial efficacy was further investigated in detail. The schematic diagram of the antibacterial experiments is presented in Fig. 3A. After co incubation of EDTA, MEM, and GEM with NDM-1 producing bacteria, bacterial plate counting images (Fig. 3B and C) revealed that the GEM group could completely inhibit bacterial growth and achieve bactericidal efficacy at a concentration of 80 μg mL−1, whereas neither EDTA alone nor MEM alone showed such an effect. As shown in the confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) images of bacterial live/dead staining (Fig. 3D and E), the GEM group exhibited intense red fluorescence, which stood in stark contrast to the strong green fluorescence of the control group, indicating compromised bacterial membrane integrity. Quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensities showed that the proportion of red fluorescence was approximately 80% in the GEM group, but only about 13% in the control group, verifying the synergistic antibacterial effect of MEM and EDTA. SEM images of bacteria (Fig. 3F) showed that bacteria in the control group displayed an intact rod like morphology, whereas those in the GEM group exhibited severe shrinkage and collapse, reflecting the excellent antibacterial effect of GEM microspheres. Accordingly, the effect of GEM microspheres on bacterial biofilms was explored. As shown in Fig. S3B, GEM was added at 0 h during biofilm formation and co cultured for 12 h. Subsequent crystal violet staining and quantitative analysis demonstrated that the GEM group exerted potent anti-biofilm activity and effectively inhibited biofilm formation. In contrast, the EDTA alone group showed no significant inhibitory effect. This may be attributed to the small molecular size and high-water solubility of free EDTA, which impairs its retention and accumulation in biofilms. Moreover, free EDTA only disrupts biofilms without direct bactericidal activity, thus failing to eradicate biofilms fundamentally. In comparison, GEM microspheres can serve as a drug depot to achieve local retention, sustained controlled release, and deep penetration of both EDTA and MEM. EDTA chelates divalent cations to degrade the extracellular matrix of biofilms, while MEM kills the embedded bacteria. Additionally, GEM can accelerate drug release in response to the weakly acidic microenvironment of biofilms, ultimately enabling efficient biofilm disruption and inhibition. Crystal violet staining (Fig. 3G–H and S3C) qualitatively and quantitatively evaluated biofilm formation at different stages: 0, 12, and 24 h representing the early, middle, and late phases of biofilm development, respectively. The powerful metal chelation capacity of EDTA leads to the degradation of the EPS matrix of biofilms, generating gaps and increasing permeability, which renders bacteria more susceptible to antibiotic killing. Compared with the control group, treatment with MEM alone or EDTA alone at all stages showed almost no change in biofilm biomass, because MEM cannot disrupt biofilms and EDTA lacks potent bactericidal activity. Only GEM microspheres co loaded with MEM and EDTA achieved simultaneous biofilm degradation and bacterial killing, resulting in effective destruction of biofilms at all developmental stages. As shown in Fig. 3I and J, CLSM further confirmed the potent biofilm inhibition efficacy of GEM. The GEM group exhibited extensive red fluorescence throughout the biofilm, with a quantitative proportion of approximately 87.78%, indicating massive bacterial death. In the control group, only a small fraction of red fluorescence (about 9.80%) was observed, while the green fluorescence accounted for approximately 90.20%, suggesting that only a small number of bacteria were inactivated. This synergistic effect confirmed that EDTA disrupts the biofilm matrix, thereby enhancing the penetration and bactericidal activity of MEM inside biofilms. Fig. 3k shows the schematic diagram of EDTA chelating metal ions in biofilms and disrupting bacterial biofilm structures. In summary, GEM microspheres not only retain the outstanding antibacterial activity of MEM but also exert a strong synergistic effect with EDTA in biofilm disruption, laying a solid foundation for the subsequent therapeutic application in pneumonia infection models.

Antibacterial mechanism of GEM

The underlying mechanism by which GEM microspheres exert their inhibitory activity was further explored. The active site of metallo-β-lactamase in NDM-producing Klebsiella pneumoniae relies on Zn2+ ions. Given the strong metal chelation ability of EDTA, we hypothesized that inhibition of NDM-1 enzymatic activity occurs via Zn2+ chelation by EDTA, leading to enzyme inactivation, loss of MEM hydrolytic activity, and restoration of the bactericidal effect of MEM. In this study, β-lactamase extracted from NDM-producing Klebsiella pneumoniae was co-incubated with varying concentrations of GEM microspheres. After dialysis to remove free metal ions, the remaining enzyme was subjected to nitric acid digestion, and the residual Zn2+ content was determined by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) and fitted quantitatively. As shown in Fig. 4A, the Zn2+ concentration in NDM enzyme decreased with increasing GEM concentration [22,23]. At a GEM concentration of 40 μg mL−1, the residual Zn2+ content in NDM enzyme had dropped to approximately 20%. Subsequently, NDM enzyme was co-incubated with EDTA or different concentrations of GEM microspheres for 4 h, followed by the addition of an equal amount of MEM. The enzymatic activity was determined by monitoring the change in UV absorption at 297 nm using a microplate reader. As presented in Fig. 4B, compared with the control group, the enzymatic activity in both the EDTA and GEM groups decreased significantly, retaining only approximately 20% of the control activity. Meanwhile, a Zn2+ supplementation assay was performed by adding an equal amount of Zn2+ ions together with MEM. As shown in Fig. 4C and D, NDM enzyme activity was partially restored after Zn2+ addition, with the EDTA and GEM groups recovering to approximately 30% of the control activity. This indicates that the inactivation of NDM enzyme by EDTA and GEM microspheres via Zn2+ chelation is partially reversible, and the activity of Zn2+-depleted NDM enzyme can be restored by Zn2+ supplementation. Subsequently, the ability of GEM to suppress the evolution of NDM-1-mediated resistance was evaluated. During 20 passages of bacterial culture, treatment with MEM alone failed to eliminate all high-level resistant mutants, even at 32-fold MIC (Fig. 4E). In contrast, the GEM combination treatment significantly suppressed the evolution of high-level drug resistance. The MIC of the treated bacteria increased only 8-fold and remained stable over multiple passages.

Finally, we obtained gene sequencing results by co-incubating GEM microspheres (1/2 MIC: 40 μg mL−1) with a standard strain expressing NDM-1 enzyme, aiming to explore the molecular mechanism underlying its effect on bacterial growth and survival. Analysis revealed that the global gene expression program of bacteria was profoundly reprogrammed after treatment, being forced to switch from a state characterized by rapid growth and normal metabolism to a survival mode with high energy consumption, multiple stresses, and limited growth (Fig. S4). Volcano plot analysis showed that there were a large number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the treatment group compared with the control group, among which the number of upregulated genes was significantly higher than that of downregulated genes (Fig. 4F). Among the most significantly differentially expressed genes, the upregulated genes were mainly involved in extensive stress responses (e.g., ybiW, ybiY), alternative pathways of energy and carbon source metabolism (e.g., hycE, exaC, scrY), sulfur metabolism and detoxification (e.g., dszC, dmoA), as well as active uptake of specific nutrients (e.g., metQ). In contrast, the downregulated genes were concentrated in the general transport of basic nutrients (e.g., gltS, aapM) and key steps of central carbon metabolism (e.g., fbpC). This feature initially revealed that when facing the combined treatment, bacteria actively shut down some basic growth functions, while urgently activating a series of specific defense and adaptation mechanisms.

To further understand this transformation at the systematic level, we performed Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) of Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways. The results, presented in a visualized and quantitative manner, confirmed that therapeutic stress caused fundamental remodeling of the core physiological processes of bacteria. KEGG pathway analysis showed that the Bacterial secretion system pathway (NES = 1.76) exhibited the strongest positive enrichment. This systematically confirmed that bacteria significantly enhanced their protein secretion and membrane remodeling capabilities to repair cell wall/membrane damage caused by meropenem and potentially efflux harmful substances. Meanwhile, the positive enrichment of the Butanoate metabolism pathway (NES = 1.23), consistent with the upregulation of genes such as hycE and exaC, collectively indicated that when aerobic respiration may be impaired, bacteria switch to an inefficient energy production mode dominated by fermentative pathways (e.g., butanoate metabolism) to maintain the most basic energy supply [24].

GO functional analysis further reinforced this scenario (Fig. 4G–I). The significant upregulation of primary active transmembrane transporter activity (NES = 1.62) and ATPase-coupled transmembrane transporter activity (NES = 1.60) functionally defined the substantial energy cost incurred by bacteria to combat EDTA-induced ion imbalance, actively uptake scarce metal ions (e.g., zinc) and key amino acids, and potentially efflux drugs. The enrichment of amino acid transport (NES = 1.22) precisely corresponded to the bacteria's strategy of enhancing specific, high-energy-consuming active transport systems to ensure the supply of core amino acids, while shutting down some general transporters (downregulation of gltS and aapM).

Combined GSEA analysis of GO terms and KEGG pathways demonstrated that GEM microsphere treatment places NDM-1-producing Klebsiella pneumoniae in a severe survival dilemma through a dual destruction-inhibition mechanism. On one hand, meropenem directly disrupts cell wall synthesis, while EDTA chelates Zn2+ to inactivate NDM-1 enzyme. This forces bacteria to initiate a series of high-cost programs, including repair (secretion system), detoxification (sulfur metabolism), and homeostasis maintenance (active transport), resulting in the rapid depletion of their energy reserves. On the other hand, therapeutic stress extensively inhibits the bacterial basic metabolism and biosynthetic networks, cutting off the pathways for efficient energy acquisition and rapid growth. This dual effect collectively traps bacteria in a vicious cycle of energy exhaustion and growth inhibition. This state not only directly explains the observed bactericidal or bacteriostatic effects but also may greatly impair the ability of bacteria to colonize, spread, and cause persistent infections in the host, thereby fundamentally assisting the host immune system in clearing the infection [25].

Results of animal experiments

Based on the excellent antibacterial activity of GEM microspheres against NDM bacteria in previous studies, the therapeutic effect of GEM microspheres on bacterial pneumonia in vivo was investigated (Fig. 5A) (approval number: LLSC20252398). First, a pneumonia model was established by intratracheal injection of NDM bacteria with a CFU of 108. GEM microsphere aerosol treatment was administered 1 h after the model establishment. Subsequent experiments were conducted by recording and sampling at different time points to explore various aspects.

Experimental study on NDM-1 bacteria-induced pneumonia in mice. (A) Time diagram of the mouse pneumonia infection experiment. (B)-(D) Survival curves, weight change curves and weight changes of each mouse in the different groups of this model. (E) Bacterial load in the lungs of mice at 12 h post-treatment. (F) H&E staining and immunohistochemical staining (TNF-α, IL-6, IFN-γ). (G) Some blood routine indicators and blood biochemical indicators (WBC, ALT/AST, Neu%).

In order to investigate the in vivo therapeutic effect of GEM microsphere aerosol, mice were divided into two groups (n = 6). Group C was established by intratracheal injection of NDM-1 bacteria without any treatment and group GEM was treated with GEM microsphere aerosols. The disease condition of bacterial pneumonia was determined by observing the death situation of the mice after modeling. As shown in Fig. 5B, in Group C, 3 out of 6 mice died at different time points within 7 days, and one mouse died within about 12 h, which indicates that the pneumonia caused by NDM bacteria shows a strong killing effect. However, no mouse died in Group GEM, indicating the excellent therapeutic effect of GEM microsphere aerosol. The daily recorded weight data (Fig. 5C–D) show that the weight of the mice in group C continuously decreased in the first four days, reaching the lowest point on the fourth day, and then began to recover until the seventh day, failing to return to the initial weight at the beginning of the experiment. While in the GEM group, the weight started to recover on the second day, and reached the original value on the fifth day. Here, it can be observed that the GEM group shows a faster recovery efficiency. This might be attributed to the effective antibacterial effect of the GEM microspheres. Therefore, we investigated the lung bacterial infection situation in the early stage (12 h) and observed whether the GEM group could effectively control the growth of bacteria. After extracting and grinding the lung tissues from the normal group (Blank group), the Control group, and the GEM group, and conducting plate coating, as shown in Fig. 5E, compared with group C, the GEM group effectively reduced the bacterial load in the lungs, which also proved the effective antibacterial effect of the NDM bacteria mentioned earlier. This also indicates that the antibacterial effect of the GEM microspheres can effectively control the spread of bacterial infection in the early stage.

Next on different days (0.5, 1, 3, 7), HE staining and immunohistochemical staining (for IL-6, TNF-α and IFN-γ) were performed on the lung tissues of the Blank group, the Control group and the GEM group to investigate the lung tissue damage and inflammatory changes after treatment. As shown in Fig. 5F, the lung structure of the Blank group was normal, with no signs of inflammation; the expression levels of IL-6, TNF-α, and IFN-γ were low, indicating no inflammatory response. The Control group showed thickened alveolar septa, obvious inflammatory cell infiltration, and serious tissue damage; the expression of TNF-α, IL-6, and IFN-γ was significantly increased, indicating a strong inflammatory reaction, these features are consistent with lung inflammation caused by Klebsiella pneumoniae infection [26]. However, compared with the model group, the thickness of the alveolar septa was reduced, and the amount of exudate was decreased. Although the alveolar structure was still damaged to some extent and inflammatory cell infiltration remained, the degree was milder. TNF-α was significantly decreased, showing that the treatment effectively inhibited inflammation and reduced tissue damage.IL-6 showed a small decrease, which may be related to the secondary immune response or tissue repair process, suggesting that inflammation still persisted to a certain degree. IFN-γ was almost restored, showing that the immune system gradually regained balance and the inflammatory response was effectively controlled. These results suggest that GEM microspheres have a therapeutic effect on lung inflammation and promote the recovery of lung tissue. However, it is possible that due to the limitations of the drug's sustained-release property or the presence of secondary immune responses, the normal condition of the lungs was not fully restored.

Then, on different days (0.5, 1, 3, 7), blood samples were taken from the Blank group, the Control group, and the GEM group for blood routine and blood biochemical tests to investigate the impact of infection on multiple organs after treatment. As shown in Fig. 5G and Fig. S5, ALT, AST, and LDH in the early stage (0.5 and 1 day) were significantly lower than those in the model group. This result indicates that meropenem can quickly control infection and reduce liver and tissue damage. CREA and UREA were similar to or slightly lower than those in the model group, showing mild renal protection. In the later stage (3 and 7 days), ALT and AST were similar to or slightly higher than those in the model group, and LDH was higher at 7 days. This may be caused by the continuous drug release due to the sustained-release property of microspheres. CREA and UREA were slightly lower, showing certain protection of renal function. Other indicators: Glo, TP, ALB and the B/C ratio were similar to those of the control group, indicating that the treatment had little effect on protein metabolism. These results confirmed that the model group was successfully established with multiple organ injury caused by Klebsiella pneumoniae infection, while the treatment group was able to effectively alleviate liver damage and tissue damage in the early stage of infection.

Flow cytometry and analysis of mouse tissues

To further investigate the cellular immune response at the early phase of treatment (12 h post-infection), we analyzed immune cell populations in lung tissue and spleen by flow cytometry (Fig. 6). At this time point, GEM had already achieved a >99% reduction in bacterial load, but residual bacterial debris and pathogen-associated molecular patterns continued to drive localized, functional immune activation. Fig. S7 (lung) and Fig. S8 (spleen) illustrate the application of the gating strategy for identifying neutrophils (CD11b+Gr-1+), M1 (F4/80+CD86+), M2 (F4/80+CD206+), CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, cytokines (CD8+IFN-γ+ and CD8+TNF-α+), and CD49B+IFN-γ+ and CD49B+TNF-α+ (mainly NK cells).

Flow cytometry analysis and sequencing of lung tissues from NDM-1 mouse pneumonia model. (A)-(D) Flow cytometry analysis and statistics of neutrophils, M1 macrophages, M2 macrophages, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, CD8+ T cells secreting tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), CD8+ T cells secreting interferon-γ (IFN-γ), natural killer T (NKT) cells secreting TNF-α, and NKT cells secreting IFN-γ in the lungs of mice.

Analysis of immune cells in lung tissue showed that drug treatment mainly affected the intensity and quality of the innate immune response. As shown in Fig. 6A–D and S9-10, flow cytometry results indicated that compared with the Blank group, the percentage of neutrophils among CD45+ cells in lung tissue was significantly increased in the Control group. This result suggested that bacterial infection induced strong infiltration of innate immune cells and acute inflammatory response. After drug intervention, the proportion of this cell population instead increased significantly. This indicates that the antibacterial effect of the drug can rapidly reduce the bacterial load, thereby promoting the stimulatory signals that trigger massive neutrophil recruitment and activation.

The analysis of the macrophage phenotype further revealed the immunomodulatory effect of the drug. After modeling, the percentage of pro-inflammatory F4/80+CD86+ (M1) macrophages was significantly increased, which was consistent with the formation of an inflammatory environment. After treatment, the proportion of M1-type macrophages was significantly higher than that of the Control group, indicating that the treatment promoted the polarization of macrophages towards the pro-inflammatory phenotype. This increase does not represent pathological hyperinflammation; rather, it reflects an organized antimicrobial focus. Rapid bacterial killing by GEM releases abundant chemotactic signals that recruit neutrophils and M1 macrophages precisely to the sites of residual infection, enhancing local pathogen elimination without causing diffuse alveolar damage (as confirmed by the histopathological result at day 1 and 3). Meanwhile, the proportion of F4/80+ CD206+ (M2) macrophages was higher in the treatment group compared to the Control group, suggesting that as the infection resolved, the tissue began transitioning toward an anti-inflammatory, repair-oriented state.

From the perspective of T cells and natural killer (NK) cells, our data suggest a delicate immune “rebalancing” in the host. The proportion of CD4+ T cells in the GEM group was comparable to that in the control group, indicating that this treatment mobilized CD4+ helper T cells and promoted adaptive immune responses, which is consistent with the aforementioned trends in neutrophils and M1 macrophages. The overall proportion of CD8+ T cells in the GEM group was slightly lower than that in the control group, and the percentages of interferon-γ (IFN-γ)- and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α)-secreting cells were also lower than those in the control group, yet remained higher than those in the healthy group. These findings indicate that GEM treatment preserved the killing functions of NK cells and T cells while suppressing excessive inflammatory responses, thereby maintaining immune homeostasis. Overall, GEM treatment rapidly killed and cleared bacteria, leading to the release of abundant bacterial debris and pathogen-associated molecular patterns. This subsequently elevated the proportions of neutrophils, M1 macrophages, and CD4+ T cells, facilitating more thorough elimination of intracellular residual pathogens. Meanwhile, the ability of CD8+ T cells and NK cells to secrete IFN-γ and TNF-α was retained, supporting sustained pathogen clearance. These results suggest that this therapeutic strategy can shift the immune response from an indiscriminate, excessive inflammatory attack mode to a precise and controllable adaptive response mode.

As a peripheral immune organ, the cellular composition of the spleen reflects the systemic immune status. As shown in Fig. S11–12, the proportion of immune cells in the spleen changed less among different groups compared with that in the lungs. Consistent with the results in the lungs, M1 macrophages (F4/80+ CD86+) in the spleen were increased after modeling and decreased after treatment, while M2 macrophages (F4/80+ CD206+) showed an increasing trend after treatment. These results indicated that the macrophage phenotypic switch induced by the treatment was not only a local effect at the infection site but also a systemic effect [27]. The proportions of total CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in the spleen remained stable among all groups. However, the percentages of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells capable of producing IFN-γ and TNF-α were higher in both the model group and the treatment group than in the blank group, and the differences between groups were less significant than those observed in the lungs. These results suggest that systemic T cell responses were activated in the early stage of infection, but the therapeutic effect of the drug was more rapid and obvious at the local infection site (the lungs).

In conclusion, nebulized GEM microspheres do not indiscriminately suppress all immune cells but rather orchestrate a temporally regulated response. At 12 h post infection, they enhance the recruitment of neutrophils and M1 macrophages to residual infection sites, representing a functional and spatially confined antimicrobial reaction. Subsequently, as the bacterial load declines, pathological diffuse infiltration resolves and the tissue shifts toward M2-dominant repair. This sequence follows a parsimonious model: rapid bactericidal activity leads to antigen clearance, which in turn drives the natural resolution of inflammation. The precise immunomodulation by GEM, together with its direct antibacterial action, underlies its therapeutic efficacy against multidrug-resistant bacterial pneumonia.

Analysis of mouse lung sequencing results

Finally, we systematically explored the molecular mechanisms by which this therapeutic strategy affects the host lung tissue response and repair through transcriptome sequencing of lung tissues from mice in the meropenem and EDTA-loaded gelatin-genipin microsphere treatment group and the Klebsiella pneumoniae (NDM-1) model group (Fig. 7). The volcano plot shows the differentially expressed genes, with 1747 up-regulated genes and 876 down-regulated genes. It lists the 10 most significantly up-regulated genes (Klhl41, Ckm, Atp2a1, Myl3, Tnnt3, Acta1, Rptn, Tnni2, Mylpt, Myl1) and the 10 most significantly down-regulated genes (Gbp2b, Cxcl9, Gbp5, Cxcl10, Cxcl11, Calhm6, Ubd, Gm4841, Gbp11, Rsad2). Regarding the upregulated genes, they are mainly involved in the structural remodeling and repair of tissues, including the regulation of muscle contraction (Tnnt3, Tnni2), cytoskeleton reconstruction (Acta1), and energy metabolism (Ckm, Atp2a1); regarding the downregulated genes, they are mainly involved in the comprehensive resolution of immune and inflammatory responses, including interferon-stimulated chemokines (Cxcl9, Cxcl10, Cxcl11) and antibacterial effector proteins (Gbp2b, Gbp5, Rsad2). The volcano plot shows that after effective treatment, the gene expression program of lung tissue undergoes a fundamental transformation: from the “anti-infection state” centered on the recruitment of immune cells and inflammatory defense, to the “repair and reconstruction state” characterized by the reorganization of the cytoskeleton and the strengthening of contraction functions.

Subsequently, we further conducted GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis as well as GSEA analysis to systematically reveal the functional state transformation of lung tissue after treatment. In the GO-GSEA analysis, significant negative enrichment of items such as “immune response activation signal transduction” (NES = −2.43), “defense response against bacteria” (NES = −1.64), and “response to interferon-γ" (NES = −2.93) was observed. This was highly consistent with the strong inhibition of the “cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction” pathway in KEGG-GSEA (NES = −2.26). These results indicated that after effectively eliminating pathogens, the treatment systematically shut down the overactive innate and adaptive immune signaling networks driven by core cytokines such as interferon-γ, freeing the tissue from a persistent “immune alert” state and achieving a profound anti-inflammatory effect.

Meanwhile, the repair and reconstruction programs were specifically activated. KEGG-GSEA analysis revealed that the “apoptotic cell engulfment” pathway (NES = 0.72) showed an upward trend, indicating the activation of the programmed clearance mechanism of apoptotic cells, which cleared the microenvironment for subsequent repair; while the strongly positively enriched “cardiac contraction” pathway (NES = 2.12) directly corresponded to the upregulation of a large number of muscle structure genes in the volcano plot. This was not cardiac compensation, but a clear sign of “myofibroblast activation and differentiation” in the lung tissue. These cells perform functions such as maintaining tissue tension, wound contraction, and structural remodeling by enhancing calcium signal transduction (such as Atp2a1) and expressing contractile proteins (such as Acta1, Tnnt3), and are the core cytological events of substantial repair after pneumonia.

The above data collectively present a clear dynamic picture: Under the treatment with meropenem and EDTA microspheres, the molecular state of the lung tissue underwent a complete transformation from “passive defense” to “active reconstruction”. On one hand, the treatment, through a synergistic antibacterial effect, eradicated the source of infection, forcing the high-energy “immune warfare” machinery represented by the “interferon-γ signaling axis” and “cytokine storm” to come to a complete halt; on the other hand, after the inflammation subsided, the tissue immediately initiated an endogenous repair process, clearing the battlefield through “enhanced autophagy”, and instructing “myofibroblast activation”, by expressing a set of contraction devices to carry out structural reconstruction. This “one suppression and one enhancement” coordinated change marks the return of the tissue from a pathological state dominated by pathogens to a regenerative process autonomously regulated by the host.

In summary, based on the sequencing analysis of the host lung tissue transcriptome, we have systematically depicted the molecular panorama of the bacteria's transformation from an active infection and inflammation-inducing state to a passive immune signal attenuation and host-driven active repair state. This result not only confirms the effectiveness of the microspheres loaded with meropenem and EDTA in treating drug-resistant bacterial pneumonia from the multi-omics perspective, but also reveals its unique mechanism beyond simple bactericidal action: that is, on the basis of strong antibacterial action, it simultaneously achieves the resolution of excessive inflammation and the precise initiation of the tissue repair process, laying a molecular foundation for the recovery of lung function.

Conclusions

The global spread of Klebsiella pneumoniae carrying NDM enzymes has exacerbated the antimicrobial resistance (AMR) crisis, creating an urgent need for innovative therapeutic approaches. In this study, we proposed and validated a synergistic therapeutic strategy that encapsulates the metal chelator EDTA and MEM into inhalable GEM for the treatment of pneumonia caused by NDM-producing Klebsiella pneumoniae.

The core findings of this study are that EDTA, as a metal chelator, can inhibit NDM enzyme activity by chelating Zn2+ in the NDM enzyme to restore the efficacy of MEM; additionally, it can disrupt bacterial biofilms through chelation. Transcriptomics analysis showed that GEM triggers toxic responses, abnormal catabolism, and nutrient/energy depletion in NDM-1-positive bacteria, and synergizes with MEM to induce metabolic collapse.

GEM suitable for deep pulmonary deposition with an average size of approximately 2.2 μm were successfully prepared via the emulsification-crosslinking method. GEM exhibited excellent loading efficiency (4.53% for EDTA and 3.04% for MEM) and sputum-responsive release properties: in artificial sputum (simulating the infected airway microenvironment), approximately 80% of the co-loaded MEM and EDTA was rapidly released within the first day. This ensured spatiotemporal synergy and laid a solid foundation for the antibacterial efficacy. Aerosolized GEM exhibited high translational potential in a mouse model of NDM type pneumonia. In lethal NDM 1 pneumonia, it achieved 100% survival (control mortality: 50%), reduced lung bacterial load by more than 99% within 12 h, and alleviated pathological injury.

It is worth noting that GEM microspheres treatment does not indiscriminately reduce all immune cells; rather, it resolves pathological diffuse inflammation at late time points while preserving or even enhancing functional antimicrobial recruitment (neutrophils and M1 macrophages) at the early stage (12 h). Furthermore, GEM microspheres can enhance the recruitment of neutrophils and M1 cells through bacterial clearance, modulate the secretion of interferon-γ by CD8+ T cells and natural killer cells, and reshape the pulmonary immune microenvironment. These observations are consistent with a model wherein efficient microbial killing drives antigen clearance, leading to a natural resolution of inflammation, rather than a non-specific immunosuppression. It can not only alleviate inflammatory damage but also enhance antibacterial ability, effectively treating the infection. Transcriptomics confirmed that inflammatory pathways (such as cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction and inflammatory responses) were inhibited in lung tissue, while tissue repair programs (including vascular smooth muscle contraction and efferocytosis) were activated, thereby rebalancing the immune microenvironment.

In conclusion, this inhalable GEM strategy integrates intelligent delivery, the dual mechanisms of EDTA (NDM-1 inhibition and bacterial biofilm suppression), and immunomodulatory functions. It can eliminate pathogens, regulate inflammation, and promote tissue repair, providing a promising translational solution for carbapenem resistant Gram-negative bacterial pneumonia.
