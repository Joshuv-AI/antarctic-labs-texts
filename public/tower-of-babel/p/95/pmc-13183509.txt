Arterial supplies to the spleen in the rat (Rattus norvegicus), degu (Octodon degus), and red-bellied squirrel (Callosciurus erythraeus)
Authors: Tetsuhito KIGATA, Yuine WATANABE, Mio MATSUURA, Keiko SAKAI
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRodents exhibit diverse morphological and ecological adaptations across species, making them important species for considering the evolutionary background of morphological traits. Although the splenic artery has been described in various rodent species, interspecific comparisons remain difficult due to a lack of uniformity in anatomical descriptions. We therefore traced the ramification patterns of the splenic artery in 15 rats (Rattus norvegicus), 15 degus (Octodon degus), and 10 red-bellied squirrels (Callosciurus erythraeus) using standardized anatomical criteria to enable rigorous interspecific comparisons. In all degus and red-bellied squirrels, the splenic artery was the first independent branch of the celiac artery, while in 73% of rats, the splenic artery emerged as one of the trifurcated branches of the celiac artery. The splenic artery of red-bellied squirrels exhibited the highest average number of divisions before entering the splenic parenchyma compared to the other two species, resulting in the highest average number of splenic branches. Further, the average number of gastric branches, including the short gastric and gastroepiploic arteries, was highest in red-bellied squirrels and lowest in rats. The differences in branching patterns of the splenic artery among three rodent species, as revealed in this study, are expected to provide clues to understanding species differences in ramification patterns of the splenic artery in rodents.Keywords: degu, macroscopic anatomy, rat, red-bellied squirrel, splenic artery

INTRODUCTION

Rodents represent a taxonomic group exhibiting diverse morphological and ecological adaptations [8], making interspecific comparisons useful for understanding the evolutionary background of organ morphology. The spleen exhibits some species-dependent functional diversity, filtering the blood by removing old or damaged red blood cells and pathogens and playing a role in immune function, and in some mammals, serving as a blood reservoir [6, 25]. The functions of the spleen are closely related to blood circulation [2, 6, 24]. The splenic artery, which solely supplies the spleen, repeatedly branches on its way to the spleen and becomes the splenic branch. Each splenic branch is thought to supply a distinct area. Once inside the spleen, the splenic branches become the trabecular arteries and further branch into the arteries supplying the white pulp and red pulp, which are crucial for immune function and filtering and storing red blood cells, respectively. Thus, the branching pattern of the splenic artery, such as the number of splenic branches, may be a key factor in understanding species-specific functional characteristics. Elucidating the detailed branching pattern of the splenic artery across different species may thus be important for understanding splenic function from a morphological perspective.

For the splenic artery, numerous reports on various rodents are available [3, 4, 7, 9, 10, 23, 26,27,28]. Generally, the splenic artery originates from the celiac artery and divides into two major branches [2, 3, 23, 26,27,28]. These two branches, respectively, supply the dorsal and ventral regions of the spleen in the muskrat (Ondatra zibethicus) and American beaver (Castor canadensis) [3, 4] or the dorsal and middle to ventral regions in the degu (Octodon degus) and dormouse (Eliomys quercinus) [26, 27]. In previous studies on branching patterns of the splenic artery, the nomenclature of branches and the methods for describing the distribution areas were not standardized, making comparisons between species difficult. Moreover, most previous studies have dealt with only one animal species, with few studies comparing branching patterns of the splenic artery between species [20].

To enrich the knowledge of species differences in ramification patterns of the splenic artery, this study compared branching patterns of the splenic artery among three rodent species using objective criteria: the rat (Supramyomorpha: Rattus norvegicus), the degu (Hystricomorpha: Octodon degus), and the red-bellied squirrel (Sciuromorpha: Callosciurus erythraeus).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

All experimental procedures were approved by the Research Ethics Committee for Animal Experimentation of the Tokyo University of Agriculture and Technology (approval nos. 27–17, 30–43, R03–15).

We used 15 male Wistar rats (180–300 g, 7 weeks old, purchased from Tokyo Laboratory Animal Science, Tokyo, Japan), 15 male degus (120–250 g, purchased from Tokyo Laboratory Animal Science), and 7 male and 3 female red-bellied squirrels (338–467 g, borrowed from the National Museum of Nature and Science, Tokyo, Japan). All rats and degus had been used in our previous studies [13,14,15,16, 19]. Accurate ages of degus and red-bellied squirrels were unknown, but it was surmised that degus were 6 weeks old or older [18] and red-bellied squirrels were fully-matured adult individuals [29].

Rats were euthanized with sodium pentobarbital (60 mg/kg, intraperitoneal), then perfused with 10% formalin transcardially. Degus were euthanized with sodium pentobarbital (over 75 mg/kg, intraperitoneal), then perfused transcardially with 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer. Red-bellied squirrels had been preserved by freezing and were thawed before being used for dissection. Perfusion fixation was not performed in these specimens.

To improve traceability, a mixture of latex (LACSTAR DFP-800; DIC Corp., Tokyo, Japan) and water in a ratio of 1:1 in conjunction with red acrylic paint coloring (Acryl Gouache; Turner Colour Works, Osaka, Japan) through a catheter inserted into the thoracic aorta (0.3–0.8 mL to degus, 1.0 mL to rats) or femoral artery (1.0 mL to squirrels). All specimens were immersed in 10% formalin before dissection. The splenic artery was traced using a surgical microscope (L-0950SDP; Inami & Co., Tokyo, Japan) and photographed using a digital camera (Nikon D5500; Nikon Imaging Japan, Tokyo, Japan). The brightness, contrast, and resolution of images were adjusted using Adobe Photoshop (Adobe, San Jose, CA, USA), and schematic drawings were prepared using Adobe Illustrator (Adobe).

In this study, to describe the distribution area of the splenic artery, the spleen was divided into three roughly equal parts along the longitudinal axis, designated as the ventral (caudal), middle, and dorsal (cranial) regions. Moreover, this study quantified the number of divisions of the splenic artery before reaching the spleen, the number of splenic branches entering the parenchyma, and the number of branches supplying the stomach as objective comparative metrics (see Supplementary Tables 1 to 3 for details). The obtained data were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Steel–Dwass multiple comparison tests using JMP version 18 software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Values of P<0.05 were considered statistically significant.

The nomenclature of the arteries and related structures was applied according to the Nomina Anatomica Veterinaria [22].

RESULTS

Figure 1 to 3 show representative branching patterns of the splenic artery in the rat, degu, and red-bellied squirrel. In all three species, the spleen was located in the cranial abdominal region, adjacent to the stomach. The shape of the spleen was flat and elongated with no significant individual variation. In all specimens, arterial supply to the spleen was solely from the splenic artery (Figs. 1–3). Although individual variation was detected in the origin and branching patterns of the splenic artery, no clear correlation was found between them. Besides, no clear sex difference was detected in the branching pattern of the splenic artery in red-bellied squirrels.

The celiac artery in rats and squirrels was always an independent branch from the abdominal aorta (Figs. 1 and 3), while the celiac and cranial mesenteric arteries formed a common trunk (the celiacomesenteric trunk) in all degus (Fig. 2). In 11 rats (73%), the splenic artery emerged as one of the trifurcated branches of the celiac artery (Fig. 1), while in 3 rats (20%), the splenic artery arose from the celiac artery as a common trunk with the left gastric artery. In the remaining one rat (7%), 15 degus (100%), and 10 red-bellied squirrels (100%), the splenic artery was the first independent branch of the celiac artery (Figs. 2 and 3).

Figure 4 shows the schematic drawing (Fig. 4A) and box plots (Fig. 4B–D) illustrating the branching pattern of the splenic artery with three measurements: the number of divisions before entering the splenic parenchyma, number of splenic branches, and number of branches to the stomach. The splenic artery bi- or trifurcated multiply before reaching the splenic parenchyma. In each splenic branch, the mean (± standard deviation) number of divisions before reaching the spleen was 3.55 ± 1.25 (range, 1–7) in rats, 2.63 ± 0.71 (range, 1–4) in degus, and 3.67 ± 0.89 (range, 2–6) in red-bellied squirrels (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Table 1). The splenic artery of the degu showed significantly fewer divisions before reaching the splenic parenchyma compared to the other two species (P<0.01) (Fig. 4B). Moreover, the mean number of splenic branches entering the splenic parenchyma was 8.27 ± 1.67 (range, 5–12) in rats, 5.93 ± 1.67 (range, 4–9) in degus, and 10.6 ± 2.17 (range, 8–14) in red-bellied squirrels (Fig. 4C and Supplementary Table 2). Significantly fewer splenic branches were seen in degus compared to the other two species (P<0.01), and red-bellied squirrels had more splenic branches than rats (P<0.05) (Fig. 4C).

Degus and red-bellied squirrels showed a left gastroepiploic artery, while rats uniformly lacked this artery (Figs. 1 to 3). In red-bellied squirrels and degus, the splenic artery gave rise to a number of short gastric arteries, while most rats had a single short gastric artery (Figs. 1 to 3). The mean number of branches distributed to the stomach, including the left gastroepiploic artery and short gastric artery, was 1.13 ± 0.52 (range, 1 or 3) in rats, 2.13 ± 0.74 (range, 1–3) in degus, and 3.80 ± 1.03 (range, 3–6) in red-bellied squirrels (Fig. 4D and Supplementary Table 3). The number of branches to the stomach decreased significantly in the order of red-bellied squirrels to degus to rats (P<0.01 for all comparisons) (Fig. 4D).

DISCUSSION

This study detected characteristics in branching patterns of the splenic artery in three rodent species by focusing on the origin, number of divisions before entering the splenic parenchyma, number of splenic branches, and number of branches to the stomach.

The origin of the splenic artery was the celiac artery in all three species. In rats and red-bellied squirrels, the celiac artery emerged directly from the abdominal aorta, while in degus the celiac artery arose from the celiacomesenteric trunk. The origin of the splenic artery was consistent with previous studies of rats and degus [9, 10, 27]. We could not find any prior research on red-bellied squirrels, but the origin of the splenic artery was similar to that reported for the European ground squirrel (Citellus citellus) [5]. The origin of the splenic artery from the celiac artery is common among rodents, but the incidence of the celiacomesenteric trunk appears higher in Hystricomorpha species, such as degus and guinea pigs, and lower in other subfamilies including rats and red-bellied squirrels.

Regarding the distribution pattern of the two main branches of the splenic artery, the most common pattern detected in rats and degus was to have one branch supplying the dorsal region, and the other supplying the middle-to-ventral region of the spleen. Although this distribution pattern was also detected in red-bellied squirrels, frequent individual variations were apparent, and it was not a typical distribution pattern. In other rodents, the distribution patterns observed in rats and degus have been reported to be the typical pattern [3, 4, 23, 26,27,28]. To standardize descriptions across the three species, the splenic artery in red-bellied squirrels was provisionally regarded as bifurcating into two main branches. However, this species may lack two clearly defined primary branches of the splenic artery. The branching pattern observed here is likely to reflect species-specific differences in the branching pattern of the splenic artery of rodent species, and further investigation will be valuable to confirm these findings.

This study used several anatomical characteristics to compare branching patterns of the splenic artery. Among the three species investigated in the present study, red-bellied squirrels exhibited a more complex branching pattern than the other two species, as supported by the numerous divisions, splenic branches entering the splenic parenchyma, and gastric branches. In contrast, degus exhibited fewer divisions and fewer splenic branches, showing a relatively simple branching pattern. Rats exhibited a similar complexity to squirrels in the ramification pattern of branches distributing to the spleen, but were characterized by fewer branches directed toward the stomach. The anatomical features identified in this study, such as the number of divisions, are closely related to hemodynamics and are considered useful for understanding the function of the spleen from a morphological perspective [11, 12, 17, 21].

One limitation of this study was the difficulty in extracting and directly comparing equivalent parameters from previous descriptions in the literature. While the three species examined in this study do not fully represent the characteristics of their respective subfamilies, the findings suggest that further comparisons across a broader range of species with more diverse phylogenetic relationships would provide a more comprehensive understanding. Moreover, the absence of findings related to sex difference also constitutes a limitation of this study. In humans, the splenic artery is reported to exhibit sex difference [1]. Previous studies in rats and degus have not documented sex differences [9, 10, 27], but considering reports in humans, further investigation into sex differences in rodents might be of value. Nonetheless, the present findings are expected to contribute to a better understanding of comparative anatomy in rodents and may provide morphological insights into splenic function.
