Integrating silver nanomaterials in wound healing: from physicochemical properties to clinical translation
Authors: Haishan Zhang, Kai You, Qifang Liu, Xuewen Xu, Rui Hua, Liming Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Wound healing is a complex process that involves the interplay of infection, inflammation, and tissue regeneration. Nevertheless, the conventional therapies tend to treat these processes individually. Silver nanomaterials have been found to serve as platforms for surface chemistry and controlled ion-release kinetics, conferring a wide array of biological activities beyond their traditional antimicrobial applications and making them promising therapeutic platforms with broad-spectrum antimicrobial, immunomodulatory, and regenerative properties. Their versatile physicochemical properties, such as size and morphology. This review provides a critical, detailed examination of the biological processes that determine the therapeutic effects of silver nanomaterials, as well as their design principles and physicochemical properties. We discuss their multi-target antimicrobial effects against bacterial, fungal, and viral infections, as well as their role in regulating innate and adaptive immunity during wound healing, including in biofilm infections. These materials, along with soft-tissue and internal-organ repair, are discussed in both cutaneous and non-cutaneous applications, as well as in orthopedic and dental applications. Moreover, the most significant translational considerations, such as safety, dose-dependent toxicity, biodistribution, the long-term fate, and regulatory factors, are critically examined. The review highlights the importance of drawing on insights from materials science, microbiology, immunology, and regenerative medicine to demonstrate how rationally designed silver nanomaterials can address unmet clinical needs and identify opportunities for their safe and effective clinical use.Graphical abstract

Introduction

To effectively restore the functional status of damaged or diseased tissues, it is imperative to address infection, inflammation, and structural regeneration in a synergistic manner [1–3]. In other clinical conditions like chronic wounds, surgical site infections, complications due to orthopedic equipment use, and inability to control such processes may negatively impact healing, tissue damage during the inflammatory response, and result in prolonged morbidity or implant failure, necessitating repeated treatment [4–7]. Conventional treatment methods tend to treat them individually, using systemic or anti-inflammatory drugs to modulate the immune response, topical antibiotics to treat infections, and biomaterials or growth factors to promote tissue healing [8–10]. Nevertheless, these weaknesses in the face of antimicrobial resistance, compartmentalized responses, increasingly dysregulated immune responses, and complex wound microenvironments require multifunctional solutions.

Silver has an extensive history of use in medicine as a potent antimicrobial against a wide range of microorganisms [11]. Prior to the discovery of antibiotics, foams, creams, and silver-based treatments, such as silver salts, were widely used clinically in the treatment of burns and wound management [12, 13]. The use of silver as a broad-spectrum antimicrobial has a long history, evolving from bulk and ionic forms to present-day nanostructured systems characterized by controlled delivery and increased bioactivity. The Hippocratic writings later noted silver’s usefulness in wound care. The development of silver nitrate to silver sulfadiazine, and then to nanocrystalline and nanoparticulate silver systems reflects a progressive progression to delivery [11, 14, 15]. The development of nanotechnology has revived interest in antimicrobials due to their ability to precisely regulate physicochemical characteristics, especially those of silver, therapeutic performance, and biological activity. Silver nanomaterials, including nanoparticles, nanowires, nanoclusters, and hybrid composites, not only possess antimicrobial properties but also exhibit dynamic interactions with biological interfaces at the nanoscale, including cells and proteins [16, 17]. This communication enables the transmission of inflammatory signals, repair, and the control of cell behavior in tissues. This is especially effective in healing applications, where infection, inflammation, and regeneration are closely related, and the adaptability of silver nanomaterials is pertinent [18].

Microbial contamination directly sustains inflammatory cascades that inhibit cell migration and tissue destruction while facilitating angiogenesis and extracellular matrix remodeling. On the other hand, the inflammatory process creates conditions favorable to unresolved infection and biofilm formation. Conversely, unresolved to persistent infection and biofilm formation, inflammation creates conditions conducive [19, 20]. The optimal immune homeostasis- and regenerative-healing material should thus have antimicrobial effects and, at the same time, induce signaling [21, 22]. Silver nanomaterials are uniquely positioned when properly designed to address these requirements through interactions among silver ion release, regulation of reactive oxygen species, and nanoscale interactions with biological systems [23]. The clinical application of silver nanomaterials has not been explored despite their potential [24].

The issues of long-term accumulation, cytotoxicity, the need for scientific rigor, and regulatory approval have limited enthusiasm for material design that affects biological effects, and a mechanism-based view [25–27]. Moreover, has focused on individual attributes, with much of the literature to date mainly on antimicrobial efficacy and regenerative issues, without a thorough synthesis that incorporates immunological features to meet clinicians’ needs. A multipurpose silver nanomaterial is urgently needed to develop a rationally designed, safe, and effective healing factor that integrates microbiology, immunology, materials science, and regenerative medicine [28].

A wide variety of reviews on wound healing, or largely summarizing the antimicrobial mechanisms, have investigated the in vitro and in vivo results of silver nanomaterials by synthesis strategy. Recent studies have made valuable contributions to the analysis of nanocrystalline therapeutic applications [29, 30]. The field tends to consider material properties, clinically oriented silver dressings, and biological performance in isolation, and there is little combination of wound-healing assessment and rational design principles into a single biology-comparative material framework. The use of silver nanoparticles in multifunctional nanomaterials for chronic wound applications has been the focus of recent research [31]. Although earlier reviews have reviewed the antimicrobial mechanisms, synthesis methods, or clinical use of silver-based devices, they often focus on material properties and biological responses separately. The physicochemical determinants, wound-phase-specific mechanisms, and the translational constraints are integrated into a single framework in the review. This paper goes one step further than descriptive synthesis to assume a systematic design-to-translation methodology that links nanoparticle design variables to biological reactions and clinical viability.

Conversely, it is more translational, more mechanistically integrated, and broader. We systematically correlate physicochemical parameters, including size, surface chemistry, and release kinetics, with phase-specific biological responses during wound healing; make quantitative, comparative measurements across formulations and other nanomaterials; and critically evaluate translational barriers, including dose maximization, safety limits, biodistribution, and regulatory concerns. It is not just a synthesis of the description into a design-to-translation roadmap and a strategic roadmap for the rational design of next-generation silver-based wound therapies, but also links nano-bio interface design with clinical implementation pathways.

Physicochemical design and fabrication of silver nanomaterials

The physicochemical properties of silver are complex, as they determine how nanomaterials are fundamentally connected to their biological performance, how silver is delivered to biological systems, and how it behaves in different environments. Unlike formulations, surface chemistry, silver nanoscale systems can be precisely engineered in size, bulk silver or simple ionic shape, and release behavior, tailored to specific clinical needs, enabling multifunctional applications [17, 32, 33]. These are crucial for interpreting biological outcomes and guiding the development of fundamental design principles for future therapeutic platforms.

Silver nanomaterials used in biomedicine

Silver nanomaterials are used in several major areas, with physicochemical and biological properties spanning structural biomedicine, exhibiting classes with distinct characteristics. Broadly, categories include silver nanoparticles, which can be classified into four principal anisotropic silver nanostructures, ultra-small silver nanoclusters, and hybrid/composite silver-based systems. This class differs in surface area, ion-release behavior, crystallographic features, colloidal stability, and interaction with biologics. Classification is important to determine antimicrobial efficacy because each class of systems ultimately affects cytocompatibility and regenerative performance. They are the most widely studied and commonly used. range from 1 to 100 nm. Silver nanoparticles are typically incorporated into wound dressings, coatings, and antimicrobial formulations [34, 35]. Their high surface area-to-volume ratio not only promotes interaction with microbial membranes but also enables efficient surface release of silver ions, functionalization with polymers, and attachment of targeting ligands and biomolecules. These geometries’ faces, influencing dissolution behavior and reactive oxygen species generation, expose specific crystallographic and cellular interactions.

In contrast, anisotropic nanostructures such as nanoplates, their exposed crystal facets, nanorods, and nanowires can alter dissolution and possess shape-dependent properties due to reactive cellular interactions and oxygen species generation [36–38]. For instance, a significant proportion of high-energy silver nanoplates exposing reactive crystal surfaces may exhibit enhanced antimicrobial activity; however, their increased reactivity also elevates cytotoxic risk if not carefully engineered [38]. Ultra-small silver nanoclusters, and molecular complexes generally below 2 nm, bridge nanoparticles, and are attractive due to their unique optical properties and potentially improved clearance [39–41]. The progress in the design of hybrid and composite silver nanomaterial systems marks a significant milestone. Spatial confinement of systems into polymeric networks by integrating silver nanoparticles and hydrogels, or biodegradable scaffolds, ceramics, thereby controlling antimicrobial activity and modulating release dynamics [42–45]. These systems can also be enhanced through mechanical reinforcement or synergistic antimicrobial activity via bioactive communication. Such systems are in regenerative medicine, where infection control is particularly relevant, secondary to structural integrity and biological integration [46–48].

Fabrication strategies and scalable synthesis

The synthesis of silver nanomaterials significantly influences their size distribution, morphology, surface chemistry, and reproducibility. Silver nanomaterials are primarily fabricated through four main routes: physical synthesis, chemical reduction, emerging scalable flow-based systems, and biological/green synthesis. Chemical reduction remains a means of biomedical translation and is the most widely used method, enabling control over size, although residual reagents may limit surface chemistry [49–51]. This approach offers nucleation, growth, dispersity, and surface stabilization with relatively good control over. Critical process parameters in chemical reduction include silver precursor concentration, identity and ratio stabilizer/capping ligand, reducing agent type and strength, pH, temperature, and mixing conditions, all of which determine the final particle size, shape, reaction time, crystallinity, and colloidal stability, and complicate their biomedical applications if purification is insufficient due to surface chemistry. However, residual reagents may remain associated with the nanoparticles. These methods enable well-dispersed movement and shape of particles, but residual reagents can influence these properties, complicating their application in biomedical fields. Physical synthesis techniques, such as evaporation-condensation and laser ablation, produce silver nanomaterials with minimal chemical contamination and high purity. In these methods, vaporization-condensation conditions, energy input, and collection environment are particularly important in determining particle size and morphology. However, these methods face scalability and cost challenges [52–56].

Conversely, biological or microorganism-based, or biomolecule-mediated, green synthesis methods utilize plant extracts to reduce silver ions, often generating particles with biocompatible surface coronas. While appealing biological approaches often exhibit batch-to-batch variability in their sustainability and safety, and lack control over nanoscale characteristics [40, 55–60]. In such systems, pH, incubation time, biomolecule content, and temperature are key factors affecting reproducibility, particle heterogeneity, extract composition, and surface functionality. Scalability and reproducibility are paramount in the context of clinical translation. Emerging fabrication methods, including microfluidics, enable detailed control over reaction parameters and fabrication conditions, such as residence time, channel mixing, and flow-rate ratios, and facilitate production in compliance with good manufacturing practice (GMP)-compatible standards [61–64]. These methods are gaining importance as silver nanomaterials transition from laboratory prototypes to regulated medical products.

Physicochemical properties governing biological performance

Silver nanomaterials exhibit characteristics such as size, crystallinity, surface charge, shape, and collective behavior, which dictate their biological activity. In addition, Ag⁺ release kinetics are a critical functional link between material design and parameters that directly affect biological performance. Particle size influences cellular uptake, exhibits greater antimicrobial activity and clearance, and affects biodistribution; particles generally have lower potency but also pose a higher risk of cytotoxicity [28, 64, 65]. The effects related to shape arise from exposure and membrane interactions, while differences in facet surface charge govern protein adsorption, colloidal stability, and cellular interactions within biological fluids. The kinetics are particularly crucial in the healing of silver ion release processes. Rapid ion release can have effects but may also cause host cell destruction and inhibit, conferring potent antimicrobial regeneration. Conversely, release can maintain antimicrobial protection, promote cell-controlled, sustained proliferation, and facilitate tissue remodeling [66–68]. Crystallinity and surface coatings further modulate dissolution behavior and Ag⁺ release, making these parameters central to integrated material design.

The size of nanoparticles plays a crucial role in influencing the kinetics of Ag⁺ release and their antimicrobial efficacy. Nanoparticles with diameters smaller than 20 nm typically release ions more rapidly due to their elevated surface-area-to-volume ratios, often achieving over 90% reduction at bacterial concentrations of 5–10 µg/mL in vitro. In contrast, generally require higher nanoparticle concentrations (> 50 nm, > 20 µg/mL) to achieve comparable bactericidal effects; they may exhibit reduced cytotoxicity toward fibroblasts. These findings suggest compatibility and highlight a size-dependent requirement for balancing antimicrobial efficacy with host-cell cytocompatibility, the importance of dimensions for cytocompatibility, and the need to link particle biodistribution considerations (Table 1).

Anisotropic nanostructures exhibit high-energy crystal facets, including nanoplates and nanorods, that contribute to facet-dependent reactivity and enhance antimicrobial efficacy, thereby increasing Ag⁺ release. Research indicates that these structures can achieve bactericidal efficiency 1.5-2 times greater than that of their spherical counterparts at equivalent mass concentrations. Nevertheless, the heightened facets may also require controlled increases in oxidative reactivity in mammalian cells under these stresses, highlighting the need for surface modification. Collectively, physicochemical parameters must be interpreted not only as material descriptors, as these observations show, but also as predictors of cytotoxicity risk, antimicrobial activity, biodistribution, and regenerative performance. Surface functionalization and intelligent design for healing applications

Further modification of silver nanomaterials through surface functionalization is a powerful strategy to enhance their multifunctionality. Major functionalization strategies include polymer coatings, stimuli-responsive surface engineering, and bioactive ligand conjugation. Polymer coatings, such as those based on polyethylene glycol, chitosan, or collagen, improve colloidal stability, enhance interactions with host tissues, and reduce nonspecific toxicity [69–71]. Incorporating bioactive ligands, such as peptides or growth factor mimics, can effectively trigger angiogenesis and promote cell migration. These coatings improve biocompatibility and stability, although they may also modify Ag⁺ release kinetics, thereby influencing biological performance.

Additionally, stimuli-responsive designs further enhance therapeutic potential [72]. Such ligand-based functionalization regenerative signaling can enhance targeting, but its cost and manufacturing demands may increase formulation complexity. These on-demand activities within systems enable selective responses to local pathological cues; translational standardization, reproducibility, and scale-up remain challenging.

Silver nanomaterials are responsive to pH or inflammation, thereby reducing off-target effects, and can selectively release silver ions in infected or inflamed microenvironments. This spatiotemporal framework for matching silver activity to different wound-healing phases and microenvironmental control is particularly important in these conditions. Such intelligent systems align closely with clinical needs, as they dynamically adjust to the changing phases of healing [73–75]. Ideal design criteria for silver nanomaterials in wound healing

To ideally achieve wound-healing antimicrobial efficacy, controlled ion-release kinetics, angiogenic support, and anti-inflammatory capacity, the material should combine biocompatibility and structural adaptability. Silver nanomaterials meet several criteria due to their tunable physicochemical properties, broad mechanisms of action, and compatibility with a diverse range of antimicrobial biomaterial matrices. The immunomodulatory effects of silver nanomaterials vary with the phase of the healing process. During the initial inflammatory phase, these materials demonstrate antimicrobial properties and modulate NF-κB, thereby reducing pathogen load and the severity of cytokine storms [3, 10]. As the healing process progresses to the proliferative stages, the controlled release of ions facilitates angiogenesis and fibroblast migration by mitigating excessive oxidative damage [32, 76]. Biological mechanisms of silver nanomaterials in wound healing

The barriers to effective antimicrobial therapy are infections and biofilms, which remain significant obstacles to wound healing. Alternatives such as silver nanomaterials have also emerged as promising because they exhibit multi-targeted action with a lower risk of resistance [81]. This antimicrobial activity is regulated by the microenvironment, material architecture, and microbial phenotype. Oxidative stress and antimicrobial effects occur at the nanoscale due to ion release, and at the level of classes of microorganisms, direct physical interactions have a synergistic effect. These properties are especially applicable in complex wound situations. This multidrug effect is particularly clinically relevant in conditions in which the combination of inflammatory signals, microbial diversity, and severe tissue damage [38, 82–86] coexist and evolve.

Antibacterial mechanisms

Silver nanoparticles are active against Gram-negative bacteria, but their activity against Gram-positive bacteria depends on the architecture of bacterial cell walls and sensitivity through mechanistic pathways [77, 87–90]. The interaction between silver nanomaterials and bacterial cell envelopes is one of the most significant processes. Ag + 0.1-1.0 ppm studies indicate that Ag + exhibits antimicrobial activity, controlled release, and cytocompatibility in cell types used in wound healing (Table 1). The ability to maintain sub-cytotoxic levels for 4872 h has been shown to enhance wound healing in animal models, with a 20–35% faster rate than controls. This alone emphasizes the importance of release kinetics in determining peak concentration.

Electrostatic interactions between silver nanomaterials and negatively charged bacterial membranes destabilize them, increasing permeability and leakage of intracellular components, thereby facilitating Ag+ internalization [91–94]. Ag+ ions bind to the thiol groups of proteins and enzymes, and, when internalized, interfere with major metabolic pathways. The effects of silver on enzymes are especially detrimental to those involved in respiration, DNA replication, and redox homeostasis [95, 96]. Silver nanomaterials also generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) through direct contact with surfaces and by disrupting bacterial antioxidant defenses [97]. High oxidative stress impairs lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids, thereby increasing bactericidal activity.

One of the key benefits of silver nanomaterials is their multi-targeted mode of action, which simultaneously interferes with various microbial functions, as opposed to traditional antibiotics that typically target only one molecular target. This does not rule out resistance development but decreases the chances. Mechanisms of diminished membrane permeability, silver resistance, or tolerance have been reported to include Ag+ sequestration via metal-binding systems, active efflux, and biofilm protection [98, 99]. In certain bacteria, silver-resistance determinants can also be co-located with genes, and antibiotic resistance is of concern since co-selection on elements can occur under long-term or sublethal exposure, as well as through shared mobile genetic elements [98, 99]. However, when silver is used, clinically significant silver resistance is especially rare in controlled nanoscale formulations [100–104]. More studies on multidrug-resistant pathogens are needed to support translational applications, such as the use of silver nanomaterials against methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus and carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae.

Antifungal mechanisms

The wound care complication that is not well known is fungal infections, diabetic wounds, particularly in immunocompromised patients, clinically important yet often overlooked, and burns. Silver nanomaterials are antifungal agents against Candida pathogenic, Aspergillus, and Cryptococcus species [119, 120]. Silver reacts with the fungal cell wall and cell membranes, but their structures vary; these structures (like those of bacterial envelopes) are also destabilized by silver. Silver nanomaterials can act as antifungals by a variety of synergistic mechanisms. These cause destruction of fungal cells, increased membrane permeability and compromised membrane integrity, and disruption of ergosterol biosynthesis. Furthermore, oxidative phosphorylation can cause silver breakage, which can disrupt mitochondrial membrane potential and electron transfer, increasing intracellular oxidative stress and hindering ATP generation. All these impacts on metabolic activities and suppress growth undermine important fungal activities. Silver has been shown to inhibit hyphal elongation, virulence, tissue invasion, and spore germination, which are two primary processes that facilitate fungal growth [121–123]. These are especially strong in the wound environment, where fungi can contribute to inflammation and slow re-epithelialization. It is important to note that antifungal activity is highly reliant on shape, surface chemistry, and nanosize of particles-systems with a higher number of silver ions. The smaller the particle size and the higher the release rate, the greater the antifungal activity; however, this must be balanced against host-cell compatibility. Overall, the use of silver nanomaterials broadens their antifungal spectrum, enhances their clinical utility, and underscores their roles as polyfunctional anti-infective agents in multifaceted wound care [124, 125].

Antiviral mechanisms

Although there is still no such extensive development of antiviral applications of silver nanomaterials as with antibacterial or antifungal ones, evidence is growing that nano-silver can disrupt viral infectivity at different points in the viral life cycle [90, 126]. Silver nanomaterials have been shown to bind to viral surface proteins, thereby preventing viral attachment and entry into host cells. This process is particularly relevant to enveloped viruses, where silver reacts with glycoproteins that are important for binding to host receptors [127]. In addition to blocking viral entry, silver nanomaterials have been shown to disrupt viral replication by binding viral nucleic acids or cellular factors essential to viral assembly [128, 129]. These effects have been demonstrated in vitro for viruses such as influenza, herpes simplex virus, and coronaviruses. The use of silver nanomaterials as antivirals against coronaviruses has been reported mainly in vitro or in initial experimental studies. Nevertheless, their clinical effectiveness is yet to be established [129].

Even though the antiviral use of silver nanomaterials is still in the clinical translation stage, there is considerable promise for their therapeutic use in contexts where virus reactivation or persistent viral presence can complicate treatment, especially in immunosuppressed patients [129, 130]. Surface functionalization and particle size are closely related to antiviral efficacy in materials. In particular, ultra-small nanoparticles and nanoclusters interact with viral components, although considerations of biodistribution and toxicity are of utmost importance [131–133]. These antivirals. Anti-biofilm mechanisms

Biofilms are also a major problem in wound healing and infection control. In biofilms, microorganisms are entrenched in a self-produced EPS matrix that confers resistance to immune clearance, antibiotics, and physical removal. Chronic wounds are associated with biofilm-related infections, and the presence of these bacteria on orthopedic implants and indwelling medical devices leads to prolonged inflammatory responses and tissue injury. Silver nanomaterials have unique characteristics, such as the ability to prevent biofilm formation and disrupt preformed biofilms [136–140]. Silver interferes, thereby decreasing the likelihood of biofilm maturation and quorum sensing in the initial stages of microbial adhesion.

Such effects, including metabolic suppression, membrane perturbation, and oxidative stress, weaken the biofilm structure and enhance microbial susceptibility [141, 142]. One of them is their most clinically valuable properties, i.e., the antibiofilm potential of silver nanomaterials. To induce tissue regeneration and biomaterial integration, Silver inhibits biofilm-induced inflammation and infection, thus providing a favorable background [18, 143]. Immunomodulatory roles of silver nanomaterials in wound healing

Effective tissue repair requires tightly regulated immune responses and inflammation and is impaired by excessive tissue repair [80, 153–155]. Chronic wounds and the prolonged activation of innate immune pathways by persistent infiltration of inflammatory cells, implant-associated inflammation, excessive pro-inflammatory cytokines, and ongoing oxidative stress [156–160]. These maladaptive responses are also inflammatory, perpetuating a cycle of non-healing, permitting recurrent infections and biofilm resistance, thereby causing pathology and impairing tissue repair [5, 161–163].

Here, the silver nanomaterials regulate immune responses in a size-, surface chemistry-, and dose-dependent manner [164–166]. Silver nanomaterials can reduce excessive inflammation without interfering with immune function at therapeutically optimized concentrations. This preventive infectivity, as immunocompetence, even with inflammation contained to prevent tissue damage, must be preserved; it is especially useful and even helpful in treatment [17, 89, 167, 168]. Silver nanomaterials can therefore act as dynamic regulators of the healing environment rather than as antimicrobial agents.

Modulation of innate immune responses

In particular, macrophages, innate immune cells, are key players in orchestrating inflammatory responses and tissue repair [169, 170]. Macrophages can adopt pro-regenerative (alternatively activated, M2) variants, switch between pro-inflammatory (classically activated, M1) and other phenotypes, and assume additional phenotypes. Continuous M1 activation correlates with abnormal healing, as indicated by excessive cytokine secretion and impaired tissue regeneration.

Silver nanomaterials can regulate macrophages in a context-dependent manner. In particular, they inhibit pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-Alpha, IL-1Beta, IL-6) through the NF-kB and MAPK pathways and through redox regulation of intracellular levels. Immune functions are vital. This biased regulation of exorbitant inflammation. also represses NF-KB activation and the expression of downstream cytokines. Ag + controlled exposure; optimum dosing enhances M1-M2 polarization of tissue remodeling and IL-10 expression. This condition is most applicable in chronic wounds, sustained M1 dominance inflames and retards healing [171, 172].

Regulation of cytokine signaling and oxidative stress

Neutrophils, which are among the first responders to tissue damage and infections, represent an immunomodulation, a key cellular target for silver nanomaterial mediated. Neutrophils are essential for microbial clearance, and proteolytic enzymes can delay healing and cause tissue damage. In models of diabetic and chronic wounds, silver nanomaterials have demonstrated the ability to suppress persistent levels of IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, while preserving growth factor signaling pathways, including TGF-β and VEGF. This selection of cytokine networks limits MMP-mediated matrix degradation to the proliferative phase and results in sustained release of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and excessive neutrophil accumulation. In vitro studies

In vitro studies demonstrate that silver nanomaterials exert antimicrobial effects through membrane disruption, intracellular Ag⁺ accumulation, and ROS generation, while maintaining cytocompatibility in optimized concentration ranges [186, 187]. At sub-cytotoxic levels, suppress pro-inflammatory signaling and silver nanomaterials regulate redox balance, keratinocyte migration, and thereby promote fibroblast viability. These findings underscore that physicochemical parameters, and ion-release kinetics, particularly size and surface chemistry, govern the balance between antimicrobial efficacy and host compatibility.

Preclinical animal models

It has been demonstrated in preclinical models that silver nanomaterials can accelerate wound healing by incorporating infection control, tissue regeneration, and immune modulation [188–190]. Overall, the reported results on healing in complex wound conditions suggest that silver nanomaterials can enhance the healing process, but their effectiveness is highly dependent on formulation design, dose, and release kinetics. These results are linked to the control of macrophage polarization, promotion of angiogenesis toward pro-regenerative phenotypes, and extracellular matrix deposition, which, mechanistically, is achieved by suppressing the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines [98, 191].

Clinical applications

Silver-based systems deliver a therapeutic concentration of Ag+ within a therapeutic window that prevents microbial growth, promotes collagen formation, and suppresses protease-mediated matrix breakdown. AQUACEL® Ag + and ACTICOAT - are clinically proven systems that offer lasting antimicrobial action, enhanced healing results due to regulated ion delivery and biofilm management, and regulation of inflammation [192]. Future developments in spatiotemporal control of Ag+ delivery are anticipated to be achieved through emerging stimuli-responsive, biodegradable silver-based scaffolds that enable precise localization of antimicrobial action during wound-healing stages and reduce cytotoxicity and accumulation. These innovations would likely enable the replacement of passive antimicrobial dressings with smart, bioadaptable therapeutic devices that offer improved clinical effectiveness. Non-cutaneous therapeutic applications

The multi-target antimicrobial efficacy, immune modulation, and Ag+ release of silver nanomaterials are supported by mechanistic principles that can be applied in cutaneous repair as well as to orthopedic, dental, and internal applications. The latter pathologic problems are more pronounced in the context of implant-associated and reconstructive practices, where infection, inflammation, and poor tissue incorporation worsen clinical outcomes [55, 81, 196]. The orthopedic implants, dental restorations, and internal repair systems are very prone to microbial colonization and inflammation-induced complications, resulting in compromised mechanical stability and biological integration. Traditional antimicrobial approaches, such as systemic antibiotics and passive surface finishes, are not always adequate due to biofilm formation and uncontrolled host immune responses [46]. One of the main benefits of nanoscale silver systems is that they enable controlled, localized release of Ag+ with minimal systemic exposure. Such a localized process is especially crucial in non-cutaneous tissues, where sustained infection and inflammation are known to cause implant loosening, fibrosis, and thrombosis, among other complications. As such, silver nanomaterials are currently being considered as built-in anti-infective and regenerative elements of implantable systems. They are aided by the development of material engineering and better understanding of nano- bio interactions, which allow targeting of the antimicrobial and host responses. Importantly, successful translation involves adapting silver delivery to tissue-specific requirements, such as mechanical compliance, degradation kinetics, and long-term biocompatibility [46, 197]. Thus, design approaches that focus on controlled release, spatial isolation, and incorporation into functional biomaterial architectures are needed to enhance clinical applications without compromising safety and efficacy.

Orthopedic applications

Silver nanomaterials offer a means to reduce implant-associated infections in orthopedic and musculoskeletal applications, such as joint replacement, fracture repair, and spinal implantation. These devices are very vulnerable to infection, which in most cases leads to implant failure, revision surgeries, and higher morbidity in patients. The establishment of bacterial biofilms on implant surfaces, which shield pathogens from antibiotics and immune clearance and increase inflammation at the implant-tissue interface, is a significant issue [198]. A silver nanomaterial coating prevents rapid bacterial adhesion and biofilm formation by disrupting bacterial membranes and continuously releasing Ag+. Coating titanium, cobalt-chromium, or stainless-steel implants with silver nanoparticles enables the formation of antimicrobial surfaces without compromising mechanical integrity. The coatings deliver localized Ag + release at the implant interface, resulting in the highest possible antimicrobial activity with the lowest systemic exposure [199, 200]. This local approach minimizes the use of systemic antibiotics and minimizes the development of resistance [201]. In addition to infection control, silver nanomaterials regulate inflammatory signaling and oxidative stress at the bone-implant interface and promote regenerative processes. Chronic or long-term inflammation can suppress osteoblast differentiation, hinder matrix mineralization, and induce fibrous encapsulation instead of stable osseointegration. Properly designed systems suppress the effects of inflammatory signaling and oxidative stress, reestablishing an environment conducive to osteoblastic functions and bone remodeling. Nevertheless, therapeutic efficacy is dose-dependent, necessitating controlled-release strategies to prevent cytotoxic effects on osteoblasts and mesenchymal stem cells.

Oral and dental applications

The mouth cavity is a difficult environment, as it is constantly exposed to microorganisms, mechanical forces, and complex immune mechanisms. Dental implants and restorative materials are especially vulnerable to infection and inflammatory complications and particularly vulnerable in high-risk patients [202, 203]. To overcome these issues, silver nanomaterials are added in dental composites, implant surfaces, and periodontal delivery systems. These materials prevent harmful oral microbiota from causing harm by disrupting their membranes and regulating the release of Ag without causing tissue incompatibility. They reduce bacterial load, inhibit pro-inflammatory mediators, thereby reducing tissue damage and promoting regeneration. They are highly anti-browning, and this is especially beneficial in peri-implant infection prevention, which is also a significant factor in dental implant failure. Surface functionalization and controlled release of Ag have helped reduce anxieties surrounding cytotoxicity, discoloration, and aesthetic constraints.

Soft tissue and internal organ repair

Silver nanomaterials are also being sought in both soft tissue and internal organ repair due to the predominance of infection and inflammation in the healing criterion. Gastrointestinal, cardiovascular, and urogenital system surgery is also particularly prone to infection and inflammatory dysregulation [204]. Silver and silver-containing scaffolds and coatings minimize microbial colonization and inflammation at the surgical sites and improve tissue integration and repair. They enhance the effects of surgery by inhibiting bacterial growth and promoting tissue remodeling. Cardiovascular devices with silver coatings prevent bacterial adhesion and reduce complications from inflammation, such as thrombosis and restenosis. Silver nanomaterials can also promote the regeneration of soft tissue through the regulation of inflammatory response and induction of angiogenesis and extra-cellular matrix reconstruction. Incorporation into biodegradable scaffolds provides temporary antimicrobial and immunomodulatory properties during the initial phases of healing, with low exposure levels over the long term. Challenges and future directions

The greatest benefit of using silver nanomaterials in the medical industry is the potential to integrate antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and regenerative functions into a single material platform. Such functions are not independent; they are mechanistically connected and reinforce one another. This crosstalk is inherent to the rational design of materials, and plays a crucial role in predicting clinical performance in a wide range of healing conditions [49, 219]. Despite the high therapeutic potential of multifunctional silver nanomaterials, their therapeutic application is limited by safety, toxicity, and regulatory issues that remain to be fully addressed [220, 221]. These physicochemical characteristics, which confer high antimicrobial and immunomodulatory activities, also raise concerns about potential interactions with host tissues and biological systems. The crucial question that clinician-scientists and regulatory agencies should address is how long a therapeutic window can be established to enable the use of silver nanomaterials to achieve long-term clinical benefits without compromising cellular viability, tissue function, or systemic homeostasis. To address these issues, silver-based nanotechnologies must be used responsibly and effectively in regenerative medicine. The future strategy for mitigating silver’s cytotoxicity should focus on developing biodegradable carrier systems that can control the relocalization of Ag+ ions. Paradoxically, by entrapping these ions in polymeric scaffolds, such as PLGA, chitosan, or hydrogel networks, it is possible to reduce burst release and maintain ion concentrations within desirable therapeutic levels. These systems may also allow temporary exposure to antimicrobials and reduce long-term accumulation in tissues.

Integration of antimicrobial, immunomodulatory, and regenerative functions

Infection, inflammation, and regeneration are critical elements in tissue healing, and these are closely intertwined. Chronic infection enhances the inflammation, which suppresses cell proliferation and matrix remodeling. Unresolved inflammation, on the other hand, provides a niche where microbes thrive and form biofilms. Silver nanomaterials interfere with different nodes of this network. Antimicrobial effects reduce microbial load, thereby directly triggering inflammatory responses via pathogen-associated molecular patterns. At the same time, excessive cytokine production and oxidative stress are counteracted by immunomodulatory effects, preventing collateral tissue damage.

All these activities, when combined, rejuvenate environments conducive to regenerative processes, including angiogenesis and the differentiation of fibroblasts and stem cells. It is worth noting that regenerative signaling can augment immune homeostasis and, in turn, increase tissue integrity and barrier function. This synergy underscores the enhanced effectiveness of multimodal silver nanomaterials compared to unimodal approaches to healing in complex conditions. They do not just treat symptoms; rather, they address the pathological feedback loops that sustain non-healing conditions.

The process of healing is dynamic, and the therapeutic requirements of tissue change with time. Early stages involve solid antimicrobial defense and controlled inflammation, and late stages involve cell proliferation, deposition of matrix, and remodeling [81, 222, 223]. Silver activity in space and time is therefore important to ensure that the benefits are maximized and risks are reduced. More complex designs of silver nanomaterials can be used to control this through custom release kinetics, stimulus responsiveness, and spatial confinement within a scaffold or coating. For example, engineered materials designed to release higher levels of silver in response to acidic or enzyme-rich environments can deliver specific antimicrobial activity while minimizing cytotoxicity during the regenerative post-exposure period. Such an adaptive behavior is highly compatible with clinical needs and reduces the risk of cytotoxicity or inadequate tissue maturation.

Toxicity and safety considerations

Cytotoxicity is the greatest impediment to clinical application of silver nanomaterials. Very high concentrations of silver ions and nanoparticles can interfere with mitochondrial function, induce oxidative stress, and cause apoptotic or necrotic cell death in mammalian cells [224]. Particle size, surface chemistry, and exposure time are key factors determining these effects. Smaller particles with greater surface reactivity release silver ions more rapidly, exhibit better antimicrobial activity, but at the cost of an increased risk of host cell toxicity [225, 226]. In some situations, genotoxic effects, including DNA damage and chromosomal aberrations, have been detected in vitro, raising long-term safety concerns. These effects are also caused by several intertwined events, such as the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), the direct interaction of Ag+ ions with DNA and nuclear proteins, mitochondrial dysfunction, and disruption of DNA repair pathways, all of which contribute to genomic instability. Surface functionalization methods such as PEGylation, chitosan coating, and polymer encapsulation have been shown to mitigate these effects by minimizing nonspecific cellular uptake and controlling Ag+ release, thereby reducing oxidative stress, increasing fibroblast survival, and maintaining antimicrobial activity, underscoring the significance of safety-by-design.

Nevertheless, it is increasingly recognized that numerous undesirable effects occur when Ag + is dosed outside a physiological context, e.g., at excessively high concentrations, with prolonged exposure, or with uncontrolled (burst) Ag+ discharge, which are not applicable in a clinical situation [227]. Here, content composition can be understood to encompass key formulation parameters, such as silver loading, silver release kinetics, surface functionalization, and carrier matrix design. Such parameters have been optimized, particularly by controlling Ag+ release within a restricted therapeutic range and preventing burst release, thereby greatly reducing cytotoxicity and genotoxicity by mitigating oxidative stress and cellular damage [228]. These silver nanomaterials can be used to achieve controlled delivery and to entrap nanoparticles within biocompatible scaffolds, thereby improving the safety profile of the nanoparticles by controlling ion availability and limiting nonspecific adsorption by cell types [229]. Dose optimization and standardized test regimens play a crucial role in translation by separating clinically relevant risks from experimental artifacts. Temporary or stimuli-responsive silver delivery systems are also another promising approach to targeted therapy [230]. pH- or enzyme-sensitive systems enable local release of Ag + in infected microenvironments and reduce off-target release. This spatiotemporal constraint states that the antimicrobial effect is coordinated with the phases of inflammation associated with infections, rather than protracted exposure to the system.

Stimuli-responsive silver nanoplatforms

The recent progress in intelligent biomaterials has made possible the creation of stimuli-responsive silver nanoplatforms capable of selective release of Ag+ ions in reaction to pathological microenvironmental signals. The typical characteristics of infected and chronic wounds include localized acidosis (pH = 5.5–6.5), elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, and increased activity of proteolytic enzymes, such as matrix metalloproteinases [231]. Such pathological manifestations offer the potential to activate antimicrobials on demand. The pH-responsive systems take advantage of the acidic environment of wounds to accelerate Ag+ release through either proton-driven polymer degradation or destabilization of silver-ligand interactions. These systems reduce ion leakage and enhance antimicrobial activity in infected tissues under physiological conditions [232]. Of particular interest are protease-reactive systems that use protease-cleavable linkers or degradable polymer matrices to achieve silver release, particularly in protease-enriched chronic wounds. This will prevent non-specific exposure and enhance local control of infection. The transporters in ROS-sensitive systems are oxidation-sensitive and degraded under severe oxidative conditions, thereby facilitating dynamic release in response to inflammation levels. The strategies aim to maintain antimicrobial activity while conserving host cells by preventing high doses and extended administration. In combination, stimuli-responsive designs offer spatiotemporal resolution, reduced cytotoxicity, and expanded therapeutic windows, improving upon passive silver-release designs and advancing toward adaptive antimicrobial designs. The lessons of the above mechanisms inform the design of future silver nanomaterials. These values enable precise control of particle size and morphology, desired surface functionalization to regulate immune interactions, and loading into biodegradable matrices to support tissue regeneration. It is worth noting that the next generation of platforms should be designed with translational constraints in mind, including manufacturability, regulatory compliance, and clinical usability [28, 197, 233]. Mechanistic integration provides guidance for applying silver nanomaterials beyond experimental systems, explicitly linking material properties to biological processes and clinical outcomes.

Clinical translation pathways

Silver nanomaterials should be used in the clinic only through a gradual process, preceded by laboratory tests and followed by registration with the relevant authorities. This will be initiated with rigorous in vitro characterization, including accurate control of particle size distribution, surface chemistry, and Ag+ release kinetics, as well as standard antimicrobial and cytocompatibility assays. The most promising candidates can then be subjected to preclinical tests, first in small-animal infected wound models assessing antimicrobial efficacy and control of inflammation, and then in large-animal models, including porcine skin, which is closer to human wounds.

A key to successful translation is scalable, GMP-compliant manufacturing, which results in batch-to-batch reproducibility of physicochemical properties and ion-release behavior. A need exists and must be supported by comprehensive toxicological, biodistribution, and long-term exposure research to achieve regulatory convergence with medical device or combination product approaches. Clinically approved silver dressings should be compared, like nanocrystalline or silver-impregnated matrices. Despite the antimicrobial benefits of these products, most advanced multifunctional silver nanoparticle systems remain in the preclinical stage.

To bridge this gap, there is a need for strong safety profiles, therapeutic windows, and clinical benefits compared to other silver-based technologies. The biodistribution and clearance of silver nanomaterials are of relevance in the long-term safety of silver nanomaterials, particularly when there is repeated or sustained exposure. Following local applications, e.g., wound dressings or implant surfaces, minimal silver is absorbed into the systemic circulation [131, 234]. Systemic administration (e.g., by intravenous injection), as seen in preclinical trials, results in rapid distribution throughout the bloodstream and preferential deposition in organs, with the liver and spleen accumulating the most, followed by the kidneys. The clearance of these models can range from a few days to weeks, depending on the size of the nanoparticles, their surface coating, dose, and dissolution properties [131, 234].

The observations highlight the importance of distinguishing between conditions of topical exposure of clinical relevance and those of systemic dosing used in experimental studies. In preclinical trials, silver is detected in organs such as the liver, spleen, and kidney, and its clearance is a slow process. The general effects of this accumulation are little known. Although clinical research has not established any substantial predictors of severe systemic toxicity of therapeutic exposure to silver, there might be some insidious or cumulative effects that might occur, thus avoiding its use. This implies that design approaches focusing on biodegradation, controlled dissolution, and ultimate clearance of the material are crucial for successful translation. The use of ultra-small nanoclusters and biodegradable composite systems is particularly promising in this regard, as they reduce the risk of permanent tissue deposition by enhancing renal clearance and controlled dissolution [42–45, 235]. Improvement in biocompatibility can also be achieved through innovative designs that employ immune shielding or surface passivation. Functionalization of surfaces with polyethylene glycol, biomimetic surfaces, or cell membrane-derived shells can reduce unwanted immune activation and nonspecific uptake, increase tissue selectivity, and reduce the inflammatory burden.

Regulatory and scalability challenges

Biological safety, regulatory, and manufacturing factors are key determinants in the translational pathway of silver nanomaterials. Regulatory bodies require a detailed report on the material’s structure, stability, and biological behavior, but standardized evaluation programs for multifunctional nanomaterials are not yet available. The synthesis processes and material properties are heterogeneous, making it difficult to assess regulatory requirements and compare across studies. Scalability and quality control in manufacturing are also concerns [236–239].

Clinical-grade silver nanomaterial production must employ good manufacturing practices and should have analogous physicochemical profiles and predictable biological behavior. These problems are being addressed by recent advances in scalable synthesis and surface functionalization, although translating these materials into medical practice will require additional collaboration among materials scientists, clinicians, and regulators. Author perspectives

The next breakthrough will be bioresponsive silver nanomaterials capable of regulating the spatiotemporal release of Ag+. The site-specific activation is achieved through intelligent release mechanisms that respond to pH changes, bacterial enzymes, oxidative stress, or inflammatory biomarkers, thereby increasing antimicrobial efficacy and reducing host cytotoxicity. Close regulation of ion dynamics and therapeutic dosing will be important to balance antimicrobial activity with fibroblast growth and angiogenesis.

In addition, real-time monitoring of infection status and the healing process can be achieved using silver nanomaterials in smart wound dressings with biosensors, microfluidic channels, or wireless monitoring systems. These multifunctional devices might help support individual therapy and reduce unnecessary exposure to silver. Translation will also involve standardized safety assessments, resistance controls, and scalable manufacturing plans to meet regulatory requirements.

Challenges and future directions

Amid such developments, significant challenges remain. These dose-dependent cytotoxicity issues, prolonged biodistribution, and tissue retention have necessitated well-defined therapeutic windows and standardized safety parameters. But also of critical concern is the ability of multifunctional nanomaterials to be scaled, reproducible and compliant with regulatory needs. There are several priority questions to be answered in the future. Firstly, the quantitative correlations between the design parameters and biological outcomes should be scientifically established under various wound conditions. Secondly, standardized large-animal models that are clinically meaningful are required to bridge the gap between experimental results and clinical practice.

The proposed future study should be oriented at the quantitative correlation between the factors of nanomaterial design and their biological functionality in different wound environments. Such correlations will allow creating predictive measures to deter the maximum effect of antimicrobials and minimize cytotoxicity. In addition, there is a pressing need for standardized large-animal models and experimentally applicable platforms to bridge the gap between laboratory findings and clinical therapeutic application.

The initial step to be taken is the development of controlled-release nanomaterial systems which retain antimicrobial activity at a certain range of safety to allow the regulatory approvals of the nano-enabled wound dressing. It is predicted that, in the long term, the field will shift toward stimulus-responsive and bioadaptive nanoplatforms that respond to signals in the wound microenvironment, such as pH, enzymatic activity, or oxidative stress. Incorporating such advanced nanomaterials into other advanced wound-monitoring systems may one day render wound management sensitive to individual and phase-specific factors. These developments render silver nanomaterials to be attractive next-generation intelligent wound therapies, as long as design optimization, safety assessment, and translational validation are tackled concomitantly.

Such developments pose a great challenge. Cytotoxicity, prolonged biodistribution, and tissue retention are dose-dependent and require clearly defined therapeutic windows and standardized safety procedures. Also, the scalability, reproducibility, and regulatory compliance of multifunctional nanomaterials are major challenges. In the future, several priority questions need to be answered. To begin with, the quantitative relationships between design parameters and biological outcomes should be scientifically determined in different wound settings. Secondly, standardized, clinically relevant large-animal models are needed to bridge the gap between experimental results and clinical practice.

Conclusions

Silver nanomaterials are versatile therapeutic delivery platforms that can simultaneously modulate immune responses to fight infection, control inflammation, and promote tissue regeneration. The Nanoscale engineering has made silver a nano-biological interface to be programmable. Its activity can be tuned in terms of size, morphology, surface chemistry and release kinetics. Unlike other previous reviews which emphasized the antimicrobial activity of a substance or material shape, this article takes a more translational approach which relates the physicochemical design of materials to the wound phase biology and clinical application. In this paper, we provide a mechanistically and translationally coupled model that bridges material design to clinical translation and biological functionality.
