Salvage Androgen Deprivation Therapy Use After Definitive Radiotherapy With vs Without Androgen Deprivation Therapy in Prostate Cancer
Authors: Jiaye Shen, Aaron J. Katz, Yahui Xie, Ronald C. Chen, Mutlay Sayan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundFor patients with unfavorable intermediate or high risk localized prostate cancer receiving definitive radiotherapy, androgen deprivation therapy reduces recurrence and improves survival but is underutilized due to toxicity concerns. Patients who forgo initial ADT may later require salvage ADT. Robust real world risk estimates can guide counseling.Patients and MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study using SEER–Medicare. Men aged 66 years diagnosed from 2010 to 2021 and treated with external beam radiotherapy were included. Exposure was concurrent ADT with radiotherapy versus radiotherapy alone. Salvage ADT was defined as initiation after the initial treatment period. We estimated cumulative incidence of salvage ADT accounting for death as a competing risk and used Fine–Gray models to obtain adjusted sub‐distribution hazard ratios, controlling for demographics, tumor characteristics, and facility factors.ResultsAmong 5,092 patients with unfavorable intermediate‐risk disease, 61.17% received concurrent ADT with definitive RT. Among 6,970 patients with high‐risk disease, 89.60% received concurrent ADT with definitive RT. At 5 years, the adjusted cumulative incidence of salvage ADT was 3.53% (RT with ADT) versus 6.07% (RT alone) for unfavorable intermediate‐risk, and 5.92% vs 19.17% for high‐risk patients. On multivariable analysis, concurrent ADT with definitive RT was associated with a lower risk of salvage ADT in both the unfavorable intermediate‐risk group (SHR, 0.56; 95% CI,A 0.45–0.70; p < 0.01) and the high‐risk group (SHR, 0.26; 95% CI, 0.21–0.31; p < 0.01). Higher PSA was also associated with increased risk for salvage ADT for both risk groups, and higher Gleason score was also associated with increased salvage ADT in high‐risk patients.ConclusionsOmission of initial ADT during RT is associated with an increased risk of salvage ADT in unfavorable intermediate‐risk and high‐risk prostate cancer. These real‐world, population‐based estimates help inform patient counseling and facilitate personalized treatment decisions.

Introduction

Androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) plays a central role in the management of localized prostate cancer, particularly when combined with radiation therapy (RT). Multiple randomized clinical trials have established the benefit of adding ADT to RT, demonstrating improved disease control and survival outcomes for patients with intermediate‐ and high‐risk prostate cancer [1, 2]. Current guidelines strongly recommend concurrent ADT with RT for high‐risk prostate cancer and often for unfavorable intermediate‐risk disease [3, 4, 5, 6]. Despite these established benefits, ADT is associated with significant side effects, including metabolic syndrome [7, 8], cardiovascular morbidity [9, 10], sexual dysfunction [11, 12], decreased bone mineral density [13, 14], and reduced quality of life [15, 16]. Consequently, some patients may strongly prefer to avoid ADT, opting for RT alone despite the potential risk of suboptimal disease control. Indeed, despite multiple positive randomized trials, uptake of concurrent ADT with definitive RT remains low [17].

Although avoiding initial ADT may appear to be an attractive option for some patients to avoid potential side effects, it would be informative to know the risk of salvage ADT if patients pursue RT alone. For example, if almost all high‐risk patients who receive RT alone require salvage ADT, this is likely to impact patient and physician decisions regarding initial therapy and potentially reduce the hesitancy to receive initial RT with concurrent ADT. However, these rates are not well described in the published literature.

Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the risk of requiring salvage ADT among patients who initially receive RT alone compared to those who receive RT with concurrent ADT for unfavorable intermediate‐ and high‐risk localized prostate cancer. By leveraging population‐based data, we aim to provide real‐world evidence that can inform patient counseling and facilitate shared decision‐making between clinicians and patients.

Methods

Data Source

We performed a retrospective cohort study utilizing the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER)‐linked Medicare database. SEER data include patients' demographics and cancer incidence and survival information, while Medicare claims capture patients' treatment and use of other health services. In the present study, SEER data included information from 21 cancer registries accounting for approximately 35% of the United States population. This study adheres to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines. An institutional review board waiver was obtained for the use of de‐identified administrative data.

Study Cohort

We included patients diagnosed at age ≥ 66 years with a first primary of unfavorable intermediate‐ or high‐risk prostate cancer between 2010 and 2021. Patients were required to survive at least 12 months following diagnosis. To ensure comorbidity assessment and complete claims data capture, patients were required to have continuous enrollment in Medicare fee‐for‐service (Parts A and B) ≥ 12 months before and after prostate cancer diagnosis. Patients with any enrollment in Medicare Advantage during the 12 months before or after prostate cancer diagnosis were excluded (Supplement 1).

We limited the cohort to patients who received external beam radiation therapy (EBRT) as their initial treatment, with at least 20 fractions to infer definitive therapy. We defined concurrent ADT as ADT initiated before EBRT (with EBRT beginning within 1 year of diagnosis), during EBRT, or within 90 days after EBRT completion; other patients were classified as not receiving concurrent ADT. Patients treated initially with stereotactic body radiation therapy, brachytherapy, surgery, ADT alone, chemotherapy, or managed with active surveillance were excluded. Patients with metastatic or node‐positive disease at diagnosis were also excluded.

Definition of Salvage ADT

For patients without ADT during initial treatment, salvage ADT was defined as the first administration occurring at least 90 days after the final radiation session. For patients who received ADT during initial treatment, salvage ADT was defined as a new administration beginning at least 270 days after the last dose of ADT during the initial treatment period, following established methodology [18]. ADT initiation was identified through Medicare claims using relevant HCPCS, CPT, ICD, and National Drug Codes (NDC) (detailed in Supplement 2).

Statistical Analysis Plan

Baseline Clinical Characteristics

A prespecified statistical analysis plan was followed. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline clinical and demographic characteristics of the study cohort, stratified by National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) risk group (unfavorable intermediate‐risk vs high‐risk) [19]. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as means with standard deviations (SD).

Adjusted Risk of Salvage ADT Initiation

The primary endpoint was the time to initiation of salvage ADT. Fine‐Gray competing risks regression models were used to evaluate the association between initial ADT exposure and the cumulative incidence of salvage ADT initiation, analyzed separately within the unfavorable intermediate‐risk and high‐risk prostate cancer groups. Time‐to‐event was defined as the number of days from the first radiation therapy delivery to the initiation of salvage ADT. Death was treated as a competing risk. Patients were censored at Medicare fee‐for‐service disenrollment, or at the end of available claims data (December 31, 2021). Multivariable models adjusted for age at diagnosis, race, PSA level, Gleason score, clinical T stage, area of residence, year of diagnosis, and National Cancer Institute (NCI) Comorbidity Index (a validated claims data‐based comorbidity score) [20]. Sub‐distribution hazard ratios (SHRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and p‐values were estimated, with robust sandwich variance estimators applied to account for potential model misspecification. Statistical significance was defined as a two‐sided p‐value < 0.05, and all analysis was performed using SAS version 9.4.

Adjusted Estimates of Salvage ADT Use

Covariate‐adjusted estimates of salvage ADT use were calculated for patients with unfavorable intermediate‐risk and high‐risk prostate cancer, stratified by initial ADT exposure. Cumulative incidence curves were generated to estimate the probability of salvage ADT initiation over time, treating death as a competing risk. Patients were censored at Medicare fee‐for‐service or at the end of available claims data (December 31, 2021). Estimates with the corresponding 95% CIs were adjusted for all covariates included in the multivariable fine‐grey model.

Sensitivity Analysis

To address the clinical relevance of mortality in evaluating subsequent treatment burden, we performed a sensitivity analysis using salvage ADT‐free survival as a composite endpoint, defined as salvage ADT initiation or death from any cause, whichever occurred first. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used, with the same time origin, censoring rules, stratification by NCCN risk group, and covariate adjustment as in the primary analysis. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals were estimated using robust sandwich variance estimators.

Results

Baseline Distribution of Clinical Characteristics

Baseline clinical and demographic characteristics are summarized in Tables 1, 2, 3. Among 5,092 patients with unfavorable intermediate‐risk disease, 3,115 (61.17%) received ADT during initial treatment. Patients who received concurrent ADT (vs. those who did not receive concurrent ADT) were more likely to have higher clinical T stage, PSA, and Gleason score (Table 2). Among 6,970 patients with high‐risk disease, 6,245 (89.60%) received ADT during initial treatment. Patients who received concurrent ADT were more likely to have Gleason ≥ 9 disease (Table 3).

Among patients who ultimately initiated salvage ADT, the median time from first EBRT delivery to salvage ADT initiation was 37.9 months (interquartile range (IQR), 23.3–65.3) among patients who received initial ADT and 49.0 months (IQR, 21.5–71.3) among those who did not receive initial ADT in the unfavorable intermediate‐risk cohort. Adjusted Risk of Salvage ADT Initiation

As shown in Figure 1, the adjusted cumulative incidence of salvage ADT initiation was higher among patients who did not receive initial ADT compared with those who did, in both the high‐risk (p < 0.01) and unfavorable intermediate‐risk (p < 0.01) groups. In the unfavorable intermediate‐risk group, the 5‐year adjusted cumulative incidence of salvage ADT initiation was 6.07% (95% CI, 5.02%–7.12%) among patients who received RT alone initially, and 3.53% (95% CI, 2.88%–4.18%) for RT/ADT. In Fine‐Gray competing risks multivariable regression models, receipt of ADT during initial treatment was associated with a lower risk of salvage ADT initiation (Table 4). Among patients with unfavorable intermediate‐risk disease, initial ADT use was associated with a lower risk of salvage ADT initiation (SHR, 0.56; 95% CI, 0.45–0.70; p < 0.01) compared with no initial ADT. PSA level of 10–20 ng/mL was also associated with a higher risk of salvage ADT initiatio (SHR, 1.68; 95% CI, 1.33–2.12; p < 0.01) compared with PSA < 10 ng/mL.

At 5 years, the adjusted cumulative incidence of salvage ADT initiation among patients with high‐risk disease was 19.17% (95% CI, 16.31%–22.04%) for those who received RT alone initially, compared to 5.92% (95% CI, 5.34%–6.51%) for those who received initial RT/ADT. On multivariable analysis, initial ADT use was associated with a lower risk of salvage ADT initiation (SHR, 0.26; 95% CI, 0.21–0.31; p < 0.01) (Table 4). In addition, compared to patients with PSA < 10 ng/mL, those with PSA 10–20ng/ml (SHR, 1.38; 95% CI, 1.12–1.71; p = 0.01) or > 20ng/ml (SHR, 1.55; 95% CI, 1.24–1.94; p < 0.01) had higher risk of salvage ADT. Higher Gleason score was also associated with greater risk of salvage ADT.

In the sensitivity analysis evaluating salvage ADT‐free survival, defined as time to salvage ADT initiation or death, results were consistent with the primary competing‐risk analysis. Initial ADT use was associated with a lower risk of salvage ADT initiation or death in both the unfavorable intermediate‐risk cohort (HR, 0.77; 95% CI, 0.68 to 0.87; p < 0.01) and the high‐risk cohort (HR, 0.52; 95% CI, 0.45 to 0.61; p < 0.01) (Table 5).

Discussion

In this study, we found that the concurrent ADT during definitive radiotherapy was associated with a lower risk of salvage ADT in both unfavorable intermediate‐risk and high‐risk prostate cancer. Specifically, for patients who received RT alone for definitive therapy, the risk of salvage ADT within 5 years of follow‐up is 6.07% for patients with unfavorable intermediate‐risk and 19.17% for high‐risk disease. The use of concurrent ADT during RT use was associated with an absolute reduction in the risk of salvage ADT of 2.54% in the unfavorable intermediate‐risk group and 13.25% in the high‐risk group. The clinical significance of this finding is that it provides concrete, population‐based estimates to guide patient counseling. For patients who prefer to avoid upfront ADT due to concerns about side effects, these results offer quantifiable probabilities of later ADT use based on real‐world data. In sensitivity analyses incorporating death into the endpoint, initial ADT use remained associated with a lower risk of salvage ADT initiation or death in both risk groups, supporting the robustness of our primary findings while incorporating mortality into a clinically relevant composite endpoint. Among patients who ultimately initiated salvage ADT, the timing of salvage ADT initiation varied by risk group and initial ADT use; these descriptive findings should be interpreted as complementary to the primary cumulative incidence analysis.

Several randomized trials and meta‐analyses have demonstrated the oncologic benefits of combining ADT with radiotherapy for patients with unfavorable intermediate‐risk and high‐risk prostate cancer. The MARCAP Consortium meta‐analysis [1] and long‐term data from trials [21, 22, 23, 24, 25] have shown that ADT improves clinical outcomes in these populations. More recently, Kishan et al. reported in a pooled analysis that delaying or omitting ADT, even in select patients, may compromise cancer control, potentially increasing the need for future treatment escalation [1]. Similarly, Patel et al. demonstrated that initial treatment intensification with ADT significantly reduces the need for future treatment escalation, including salvage ADT, reinforcing the importance of appropriate upfront therapy in mitigating long‐term treatment burden [2]. Our findings align with and add to this body of evidence by providing real‐world, population‐based estimates of how initial treatment decisions influence the future need for hormonal therapy. Importantly, we extend prior trial results by quantifying absolute risks of salvage ADT use, which can aid in counseling patients who are hesitant about initiating ADT at diagnosis.

The clinical implications of these findings are particularly relevant to shared decision‐making. While many patients with unfavorable intermediate‐ or high‐risk prostate cancer are appropriately counseled to receive ADT in combination with RT, some patients decline ADT due to concerns about side effects. Until now, limited real‐world data have been available to inform patients of the long‐term likelihood of requiring hormonal therapy if ADT is initially omitted. By quantifying the absolute risk of salvage ADT initiation over time, our findings provide valuable, evidence‐based estimates that can help align treatment decisions with patient preferences. These data may also help clinicians balance the potential benefits of upfront ADT against the possibility of future treatment escalation, particularly for patients expressing strong preferences to avoid ADT at diagnosis.

This study has several limitations. First, as with any observational study, patient selection cannot be fully accounted for with statistical analysis adjusting for key clinical and demographic factors. That is, patients with high‐risk cancer who received RT alone may be selected due to, for example, lower‐volume disease or other factors that are not available for analysis. Therefore, the observed rate of salvage ADT could represent an underestimation. Similarly, the descriptive timing analysis should be interpreted cautiously because it was limited to patients who ultimately initiated salvage ADT and may reflect differences in baseline risk, treatment selection, surveillance, and physician practice patterns rather than a direct effect of initial ADT on the timing of subsequent therapy. Second, our analysis was restricted to patients aged 66 years or older, thus limiting generalizability to younger populations in whom the rate of salvage ADT could also be higher due to longer survival. Additionally, emerging prognostic tools such as the Decipher genomic classifier and multimodal artificial intelligence (MMAI)‐based risk assessment tools were not incorporated into this analysis, as these tests became clinically available only in recent years and were not captured in the SEER‐Medicare database. This is particularly important, as the ArteraAI Prostate Test—a multimodal AI‐based platform now endorsed by NCCN guidelines—has been shown to predict which patients with unfavorable intermediate‐risk disease benefit from ADT [26].

In conclusion, our study provides novel, population‐based estimates of the 5‐year risk of salvage ADT use in patients with unfavorable intermediate‐risk and high‐risk prostate cancer treated with definitive RT, with or without concurrent ADT. We found that omission of initial ADT was associated with an increased likelihood of future ADT initiation, with the largest absolute difference observed among patients with high‐risk disease. These findings can inform patient counseling by providing real‐world, quantifiable probabilities of future ADT use, thereby supporting shared decision‐making that balances patient preferences, treatment toxicities, and long‐term oncologic outcomes.
