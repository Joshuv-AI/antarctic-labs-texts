Evaluation of primary oral health-related factors predicting depression before and after the COVID-19 pandemic using KNHANES data
Authors: Yeon–Hee Lee, Su-Jin Jeong, Ronald Kulich, David Keith
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe impact of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic on oral health in individuals with depression remains unclear. This study aimed to assess changes in clinically diagnosed depression and identify oral health-related factors contributing to depression before and after COVID-19 using nationally representative data from South Korea.MethodsThis cross-sectional study used Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES) data from 2019 (pre-COVID-19) and 2021 (post-COVID-19). Participants were stratified by physician-diagnosed depression status. Survey-weighted multivariable logistic regression was used to identify oral health-related predictors of depression, adjusting for age, sex, BMI, systemic health, and socioeconomic variables.ResultsThe weighted prevalence of physician-diagnosed depression rose from 4.44% in 2019 to 5.11% in 2021. Oral health issues, including toothache, chewing discomfort, chewing problem severity, and unmet dental care needs, were consistently worse in those with depression in both years. Unmet dental care needs rose from 29.4% to 40.5%, and chewing discomfort from 26.3% to 27.6% after the pandemic. Toothache and chewing problem severity were significantly associated with depression in both 2019 (OR: 1.48 and 1.25; 95% CI: 1.09–2.01 and 1.10–1.41) and 2021 (OR: 1.51 and 1.18; 95% CI: 1.01–2.27 and 1.01–1.36). Unmet dental care needs were a significant predictor only in 2021 (OR: 1.44; 95% CI: 1.00–2.07). These findings persisted after adjusting for systemic diseases and socioeconomic factors.ConclusionThis study highlights persistent disparities in oral health among individuals with depression before and after the COVID-19 pandemic in South Korea. These findings suggest the need for public health strategies that incorporate oral health into broader mental health frameworks, especially in pandemic contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24240-8.Keywords: COVID-19, KNHANES, Oral health, Mental health, Depression, Dental

Introduction

Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) was an unprecedented global pandemic that led to large-scale lockdowns worldwide. It was declared a pandemic by the World Health Organization (WHO) on March 11, 2020, which marked the beginning of a public health crisis of historic proportions [1]. ​As of April 5, 2024, more than 700 million confirmed COVID-19 cases and over 7 million deaths worldwide. COVID-19 was associated with high mortality and severe symptoms in its early stages, leading to prolonged global anxiety [2]. Mortality rates varied, with reported rates of approximately 1.1% in the United States, 5.1% in China, 2.3% in Europe, and 0.1% in South Korea [3]. Until the World Health Organization (WHO) officially declared the end of the global COVID-19 emergency in May 2023, South Korea remained significantly affected by the pandemic for approximately 38 months. During this period, various public health measures such as physical distancing, mandatory face masks, and quarantine of confirmed cases significantly reduced social contact and increased feelings of isolation, thereby lowering individuals’ sense of belonging within society [4].

Depression is a common serious mental health disorder that negatively affects feelings, thoughts, and actions. According to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, depression is characterized by persistent feelings of sadness and a lack of interest in previously enjoyable activities, accompanied by cognitive and physical symptoms [5]. Diagnosis is typically based on criteria such as mood, behavioral changes, sleep disturbances, appetite changes, and difficulty concentrating. Its prevalence can increase owing to several factors, including systemic and oral health problems, large-scale pandemics, social pressures or restrictions, economic stress, and trauma [6]. Illness or death of a loved one is a well-established risk factor for depression, with sudden or unexpected loss posing an even greater psychological burden [7]. Global crises such as the COVID-19 pandemic have been linked to increased mental health disorders. The depression prevalence in South Korea increased significantly from 4.3% before to 5.2% during the pandemic [8]. According to a 2022 WHO brief, the global prevalence of depression alone increased by approximately 27.6% during the COVID-19 pandemic, while anxiety increased by 25.6% [9]. This difference may be partly explained by methodological differences: South Korea’s prevalence was based on self-reported physician diagnosis, while WHO used model-based estimates. Nevertheless, both showed a clear increase during the pandemic.

Oral health is a key component of general health and wellbeing. It includes the absence of oral pain, tooth loss, infection, and other conditions that impair the ability to chew, speak, or socialize comfortably [10]. Good oral health is maintained through proper oral hygiene, regular dental checkups, and timely dental treatments. Poor oral health is linked to not only systemic infections and physical discomfort but also increased mental vulnerability and psychological distress [11]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, painful dental conditions rose significantly in the United States—bruxism (> 60%), temporomandibular disorder symptoms (> 60%), chipped/cracked teeth (63%), dental caries (39%), and periodontal disease (37%)—indicating an increased burden of oral health issues [12]. Poor oral health may contribute to the onset or worsening of depressive symptoms through multiple biological and psychosocial pathways. Chronic oral pain and functional limitations can lead to social withdrawal, low self-esteem, and reduced quality of life, well-established contributors to depression [13]. Despite growing recognition of this oral–mental health interconnection, few studies have examined how this relationship evolved across the pandemic period using nationally representative data.

Given these trends, the aim of this study was to determine changes in the prevalence of clinically diagnosed depression and identify oral health-related factors contributing to depression before and after the COVID-19 pandemic using nationally representative data from South Korea. We used the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES), a comprehensive public health database, which offers distinct advantages, including a large sample size, standardized data collection, and rich clinical and sociodemographic variables. We hypothesized that depression rates would increase following the pandemic, and that oral health challenges, including toothache, functional limitations, and unmet dental care needs, would be significantly associated with depression in both periods. To test this hypothesis, we analyzed KNHANES data from 2019 (pre–COVID-19) and 2021 (post–COVID-19). The findings will contribute to the growing body of evidence on oral–mental health disparities by offering a nationally representative, pandemic-related perspective.

Materials and methods

KNHANES is a cross-sectional, nationwide surveillance program conducted annually by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency since 1998 [14]. This program collects comprehensive health-related data from the noninstitutionalized civilian population of Korea, including sociodemographic characteristics, medical histories, anthropometric measurements, biochemical profiles, and health behaviors [15]. Data are obtained through personal interviews, physical examinations, and laboratory tests. KNHANES employs a complex, stratified, multistage probability-cluster sampling design to ensure national representativeness.

To examine whether participants had ever been diagnosed with depression by a physician, we analyzed 2019 and 2021 KNHANES data to measure and compare the prevalence of depression among Korean adults before and after the pandemic. Depression was defined as a self-reported diagnosis by a physician, based on participants’ responses to health interview survey questions. For oral health, we used variables derived from both oral examinations and self-reported oral health questionnaires. In this study, we defined 2019 as the pre-COVID-19 period and 2021 as the post-COVID-19 period, as 2021 reflected the phase following the initial outbreak and major public health disruptions in South Korea. Although the WHO officially ended the global COVID-19 emergency in 2023 [16], we selected 2021 to assess early recovery trends and pandemic-related changes using the most recent data available at the time of analysis.

From the initial sample of 6,846 individuals in 2019 (weighted n = 51,385,737) and 4,741 individuals in 2021 (weighted n = 51,531,005), we selected participants aged 19 years or older. This resulted in 5,526 individuals (weighted n = 42,601,803) in 2019 and 3,928 individuals (weighted n = 43,342,405) in 2021. After excluding participants with missing data for any study variable, 4,993 (weighted n = 40,741,652) and 3,491 (weighted n = 40,936,095) individuals aged ≥ 19 years were included in the 2019 and 2021 KNHANES datasets, respectively. Variables

Weighted frequency is a key concept in national survey datasets that use complex sampling designs, such as KNHANES [17]. It refers to the estimated number of individuals in the total population that a particular response represents, after accounting for the complex survey design and sampling weights. In KNHANES, each respondent is assigned a sampling weight to adjust for unequal probabilities of selection and nonresponse and ensure that the results are representative of the Korean population. Therefore, weighted frequency provides a population-level estimate rather than a simple respondent count. For KNHANES variable analysis, binary variables were summarized using frequency, weighted frequency, and weighted percentage. All oral health variables were independently reviewed by two raters (Y.H.L. and S.J.J.), with any discrepancies resolved by consensus. The mean Cohen’s kappa across all categorical variables was 0.82, indicating substantial reliability.

Depression

Depression diagnosed by a physician, as defined in KNHANES, was based on participants’ responses to the survey variable DF2_dg, which asked “Have you ever been diagnosed with depression by a physician?” Respondents were required to choose one of three options: yes, no, or unsure. Responses marked as “unsure” were treated as non-responses and excluded from the analysis. Frequency, weighted frequency, and weighted percentage were calculated based on those who answered yes, reflecting self-reported physician-diagnosed depression.

Oral health-related factors

Several oral health-related variables from KNHANES, including both self-reported and clinically assessed indicators, were analyzed. The KNHANES questionnaire assessed toothache episodes (O_pain) and whether participants had undergone a dental checkup in the past year (OR1_2). Discomfort during chewing was evaluated using the variable O_chew_d, while the presence and status of dental prostheses were recorded under O_PROS. Clinical need for dentures was assessed using O_DENT, and unmet dental care needs were captured using BM1214, reflecting the presence of untreated oral conditions requiring professional care. Self-reported oral health status was measured using OR1, with scores ranging from 1 (very good) to 5 (very poor). Functional difficulties, including chewing (BM7_re) and speaking (BM8_re) problems, were also recorded, each rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (not uncomfortable at all) to 5 (very uncomfortable). Higher scores represented worse perceived health or more severe functional limitations. Objective clinical indicators included the numbers of remaining natural teeth (O_TC), dental implants (O_IMP_N), and units requiring fixed prostheses (O_BR_N), such as crowns or bridges.

Twelve oral health-related variables were assessed. Six binary variables with yes-or-no response formats included toothache in the past year (O_pain), dental checkups in the past year (OR1_2), chewing discomfort (O_chew_d), status of dental prosthetics (O_PROS), need for dentures (O_DENT), and unmet dental care needs (BM1214). Three categorical variables assessed using a 5-point Likert scale included self-reported oral health status (OR1), chewing problems (BM7_re), and speaking problems (BM8_re). The remaining three variables were continuous: number of remaining natural teeth (O_TC), number of dental implants (O_IMP_N), and number of units requiring fixed prostheses (O_BR_N). The selected oral health variables reflect clinically relevant symptoms and functional limitations that were consistently assessed in the KNHANES oral examination modules conducted in both 2019 and 2021. These variables were included as they represent key domains of oral health that are recognized as important factors associated with mental well-being [18–20]. All clinical oral health assessments in KNHANES were conducted by public health dentists affiliated with the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency or supported by local governments. Examinations were performed under standardized conditions in mobile examination centers, following calibrated protocols and using structured case report forms to ensure accuracy, reliability, and consistency across participants.

Nonoral health-related factors

Systemic health factors

Systemic factors included the total number of physician-diagnosed systemic diseases, body mass index (BMI; HE_BMI), and self-reported general health status (D_1_1). These variables were used to assess participants’ overall health conditions and investigate their associations with depression. We also examined differences in the distribution of these variables between the 2019 and 2021 datasets and compared the strength of their associations with depression in each year. The total number of systemic diseases was calculated based on participants’ responses to whether a physician had ever diagnosed them with any of the following 16 conditions: hypertension (DI1_dg), dyslipidemia (DI2_dg), stroke (DI3_dg), myocardial infarction or angina (DI4_dg), arthritis/osteoarthritis/rheumatoid arthritis/osteoporosis (DM1234_dg), tuberculosis (DJ2_dg), asthma (DJ4_dg), diabetes (DE1_dg), thyroid disease (DE2_dg), cancer (DC_dg), atopic dermatitis (DL1_dg), kidney or renal disease (DN1_dg), hepatitis B or C and liver cirrhosis (DK894_dg), allergic rhinitis or sinusitis (DJ86_dg), otitis media (DH4_dg), and eye diseases including cataract, glaucoma, macular degeneration, or diabetic retinopathy (DH2367_dg).

Lifestyle and socioeconomic factors

Lifestyle factors included alcohol consumption frequency over the past year (BD1_11_re: none, < 1/month, 1/month–2–3/week, ≥ 4/week), smoking status (BS1_1_re: never, former, current), and engagement in aerobic physical activity (pa_aerobic: not meeting vs. meeting WHO physical activity recommendations). Socioeconomic variables encompassed income quartile (incm_re: low, middle, high), residential region (region_re: metropolitan vs. provincial; town_t: urban vs. rural), education level (edu_re: ≤ elementary, middle/high school, ≥ college), occupational status including unemployment and noneconomic activity (occp_re: professional/managerial, service/sales, agricultural/fishery, manual labor, unemployed), and marital status (marri_2_re: never married, married/cohabiting, separated/widowed/divorced).

Statistical analysis

Sampling weights based on the complex survey design of each KNHANES dataset were applied to all statistical analyses to produce unbiased estimates representative of the Korean population [21]. We compared data from the pre–COVID-19 (2019) and post–COVID-19 (2021) periods and assessed differences between participants with and those without physician-diagnosed depression within each survey year. Baseline characteristics in 2019 and 2021 were summarized using descriptive statistics. Categorical variables were compared using Rao–Scott chi-square (χ2) tests, while continuous variables were assessed using Student’s t-tests to identify statistical differences between the two survey years and between depression and non-depression groups. These methods were also used to evaluate within-year differences between depression and non-depression groups. The overall depression prevalence and its distribution across baseline characteristics were compared between the two COVID-19 periods using χ2 tests. Survey-weighted univariate logistic regression analyses were first conducted to evaluate the individual associations between each predictor and physician-diagnosed depression. Subsequently, survey-weighted multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent predictors of depression, particularly oral health-related factors. All potential confounding variables were included in the multivariable models regardless of their univariate significance to ensure comprehensive adjustment. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R software (version 4.3.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographics

In the total population, the weighted proportion of individuals with physician-diagnosed depression increased from 4.44% before to 5.11% after the pandemic (p = 0.7157). The estimated weighted mean participant ages were 48.06 and 48.80 years in 2019 and 2021, respectively. In 2019, the mean age in the depression group was 52.82 years (95% confidence interval [CI]: 50.05–55.59), significantly higher than that in the non-depression group (47.82 years; 95% CI: 46.81–48.83; p = 0.0003). However, in 2021, the mean age in the depression group was 48.68 years (95% CI: 47.53–49.84), with no significant difference from that in the non-depression group (50.57 years; 95% CI: 47.67–53.47; p = 0.1721) (Table 1). Before and after COVID-19, the male-to-female ratio was nearly 1:1, with 49.9% men and 50.1% women.

Interestingly, in both periods, the proportion of women was significantly higher in the depression group. Oral health-related factors

Oral health burden after COVID-19

In the depression group, several oral health-related factors demonstrated increased prevalence or worsening from 2019 to 2021. Specifically, the proportion of individuals with toothache episodes in the past year increased from 29.72% to 42.01%, whereas complaints of chewing discomfort increased slightly from 26.34% to 27.58%. The proportion of individuals reporting the presence of dental prosthetics (e.g., dentures or bridges) increased from 41.49% to 48.22%. This increase reflects higher self-reported prevalence of existing dental prosthetics and does not necessarily indicate a rise in new prosthetic procedures during the pandemic period.

In the depression group, several oral health-related factors demonstrated increased prevalence or worsening from 2019 to 2021. Specifically, the proportion of individuals reporting toothache episodes in the past year increased from 29.72% to 42.01%, and complaints of chewing discomfort rose slightly from 26.34% to 27.58%. The proportion of individuals with existing dental prosthetics increased from 41.49% to 48.22%, likely reflecting the accumulation of untreated oral conditions rather than an increase in new prosthetic procedures. Notably, the prevalence of unmet dental care needs also rose markedly from 29.37% to 40.51%.

Depression-related oral health disparities

Among oral health-related factors, toothache in the past year, chewing discomfort, chewing problem severity scores, and unmet dental care needs were significantly higher in the depression group than in the non-depression group in both years. Toothache in the past year was significantly more common in the depression group in both years. Before COVID-19, 29.27% of participants in the depression group reported toothache, compared to 21.86% in the non-depression group (p = 0.0105). After COVID-19, these rates increased to 42.01% in the depression group and 30.84% in the non-depression group (p = 0.0089).

Chewing discomfort was consistently and significantly more common in the depression group than in the non-depression group in both years. Before COVID-19, 26.34% of participants in the depression group reported chewing discomfort, compared to 17.36% in the non-depression group (p = 0.0010). After the COVID-19 period, the respective rates were 27.58% and 17.02% (p = 0.0008). Chewing problem severity scores were significantly worse in the depression group than in the non-depression group before (2.43 vs. 2.10, p = 0.0008) and after (2.24 vs. 2.16, p = 0.0014) COVID-19. Similarly, speaking problem scores were consistently higher in the depression group before (1.82 vs. 1.49, p = 0.0002) and after (1.73 vs. 1.42, p = 0.0016) COVID-19, indicating greater functional speech limitations. The number of remaining natural teeth was lower in the depression group before (24.32 vs. 25.25, p = 0.0312) and after (24.63 vs. 25.17, p = 0.2523) COVID-19, although the difference was statistically significant only in the pre–COVID-19 period (Fig. 1).

Survey-weighted univariate logistic regression analysis of oral health-related factors predicting depression

Survey-weighted univariate logistic regression analyses, adjusted for age and sex, were conducted to evaluate the individual associations between oral health-related, systemic, and socioeconomic variables and physician-diagnosed depression in both the pre–COVID-19 and post–COVID-19 periods. Before COVID-19, significant predictors of depression included chewing discomfort (odds ratio [OR], 1.70; 95% CI: 1.24–2.35; p = 0.0012), toothache in the past year (OR, 1.48; 95% CI: 1.09–2.01, p = 0.0130), greater speaking problem severity (OR, 1.40; 95% CI: 1.21–1.61, p < 0.0001), greater chewing problem severity (OR, 1.25; 95% CI: 1.10–1.41; p = 0.0004), and decreased number of remaining natural teeth (OR, 0.98; 95% CI: 0.96–0.99; p = 0.0109).

After COVID-19, predictors of depression included chewing discomfort (OR, 1.86; 95% CI: 1.28–2.70; p = 0.0012), unmet dental care needs (OR, 1.64; 95% CI: 1.17–2.31; p = 0.0047), toothache in the past year (OR, 1.63; 95% CI: 1.12–2.36; p = 0.0105), presence of dental prostheses (OR, 1.51; 95% CI: 1.12–2.04; p = 0.0072), and greater speaking (OR, 1.32; 95% CI: 1.14–1.52; p = 0.0002) and chewing (OR, 1.27; 95% CI: 1.11–1.45; p = 0.0006) problem severity (Table 3).

Multiple logistic regression analysis of oral health-related factors predicting depression

Multiple survey logistic regression analyses were conducted with adjustments for age, sex, and additional significant covariates, including all physician-diagnosed systemic diseases and the number of systemic conditions.

Before COVID-19, oral health-related predictors included toothache in the past year (OR, 1.48; 95% CI: 1.09–2.01), chewing discomfort (OR, 1.70; 95% CI: 1.24–2.35), greater chewing (OR, 1.25; 95% CI: 1.10–1.41) and speaking (OR, 1.40; 95% CI: 1.21–1.61) problem severity, and fewer remaining natural teeth (OR, 0.98; 95% CI: 0.96–0.99). Socioeconomic predictors included female sex (OR, 2.18; 95% CI: 1.55–3.06), lower income (top 25% income quartile: OR, 0.36; 95% CI: 0.22–0.58), lower education (college or above: OR, 0.31; 95% CI: 0.20–0.47), unemployment (OR, 3.79; 95% CI: 2.38–6.03), and married status (OR, 2.48; 95% CI: 1.52–4.03).

After COVID-19, a crucial set of predictors emerged. Significant predictors included toothache in the past year (OR, 1.51; 95% CI: 1.01–2.27; p = 0.0464), unmet dental care needs (OR, 1.44; 95% CI: 1.00–2.07; p = 0.0494), and increased chewing problem severity (OR, 1.18; 95% CI: 1.01–1.36; p = 0.0329) (Fig. 2). These findings indicate that, in the post–COVID-19 period, unmet dental care needs, worsening chewing problems, and recent toothache episodes were all independently associated with a higher likelihood of depression. Non-oral health factors

Depression-related systemic health disparities

Before COVID-19, the mean BMI was significantly higher in the depression group than in the non-depression group (24.59 vs. 23.95, p = 0.0294). Interestingly, this trend was reversed after COVID-19, with the non-depression group showing a higher mean BMI (24.15 vs. 23.92), albeit without a statistically significant difference (p = 0.4846).

Self-reported general health status was significantly worse in the depression group than in the non-depression group before (3.34 vs. 2.79, p < 0.0001) and after (3.16 vs. 2.81, p < 0.0001) COVID-19.

Notably, when examining the prevalence of 16 systemic diseases, all conditions were more frequently observed in the depression group, regardless of statistical significance. Moreover, several conditions showed higher prevalence in the depression group after COVID-19 than before COVID-19. These included dyslipidemia (28.81% to 30.47%); stroke (2.51% to 4.00%); myocardial infarction/angina (3.59% to 7.22%); arthritis, osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, osteoporosis, and tuberculosis (26.37% to 28.56%); asthma (6.05% to 6.10%); thyroid disease (5.34% to 6.90%); atopic dermatitis (4.97% to 10.89%); and allergic rhinitis or sinusitis (26.12% to 32.70%). The number of systemic diseases was also significantly higher in the depression group than in the non-depression group before (3.28 vs. 1.36, p < 0.0001) and after (3.47 vs. 1.42, p < 0.0001) COVID-19 (Supplementary Table 1).

Lifestyle and socioeconomic Factors by depression status and COVID-19 period

Interestingly, the proportions of individuals who reported never consuming alcohol and those identified as non-smokers were significantly higher in the depression group before and after COVID-19. This finding may be partially explained by the higher proportion of women in the depression group, as women are generally less likely to engage in drinking and smoking behaviors (Supplementary Table 1).

Income and education levels by depression status and COVID-19 period

Regarding income quartile, before COVID-19, the proportion of individuals in the highest income quartile (top 25%) was significantly higher in the non-depression group than in the depression group (26.15% vs. 15.11%, p < 0.001). After COVID-19, this proportion increased in the depression group, although the difference was not statistically significant (25.14% vs. 25.27%, p = 0.4924).

Regarding education level, before COVID-19, the proportion of individuals with college-level education or higher was significantly lower in the depression group than in the non-depression group (24.72% vs. 44.97%, p < 0.001). This pattern persisted after COVID-19 (33.16% vs. 43.46%). Notably, the percentage of individuals with higher education in the depression group increased from the pre–COVID-19 to the post–COVID-19 period.

Marital status and employment by depression status and COVID-19 period

The proportion of single or unmarried individuals was significantly higher than that of married individuals in both groups in both periods (p < 0.0001 for all comparisons). Notably, the prevalence of single or unmarried status in the depression group increased from 18.38% before to 25.82% after COVID-19. The proportion of unemployed individuals was significantly higher in the depression group than in the non-depression group during both periods: pre–COVID-19, 54.18% vs. 34.47%, p < 0.0001; post-COVID-19, 50.69% vs. 35.67%, p = 0.0018.

Residential location by depression status and COVID-19 period

There was no significant difference in the distribution of living areas (city/province or urban/rural) between the depression and non-depression groups in either period.

Socioeconomic and demographic factors

Socioeconomic and demographic predictors of depression after COVID-19 included female sex (OR, 2.35; 95% CI: 1.57–3.49), lower educational level (college graduate or above: OR, 0.55; 95% CI: 0.35–0.85), unemployed status (OR, 1.91; 95% CI: 1.11–3.28), and married status (OR, 1.61; 95% CI: 1.03–2.50). These findings indicated that demographic vulnerabilities such as sex and employment status continued to play a critical role in depression risk during COVID-19. In addition, married status was associated with higher odds of depression, particularly during the pandemic (OR, 1.61; p = 0.0351).

Systemic health factors

Before COVID19, among systemic health predictors, dyslipidemia (OR, 2.02; 95% CI: 1.49–2.76), arthritis or related conditions (OR, 2.49; 95% CI: 1.72–3.62), asthma (OR, 2.00; 95% CI: 1.31–2.89), cancer (OR, 1.83; 95% CI: 1.09–3.08), allergic rhinitis or sinusitis (OR, 1.49; 95% CI: 1.06–2.08), otitis media (OR, 1.94; 95% CI: 1.19–3.18), eye-related conditions (OR, 2.29; 95% CI: 1.65–3.18), older age (OR, 1.02; 95% CI: 1.01–1.03), higher BMI (OR, 1.05; 95% CI: 1.01–1.09), number of systemic diseases (OR, 1.52; 95% CI: 1.43–1.60), and self-reported general health status (OR, 2.24; 95% CI: 1.83–2.75) were significantly associated with depression (Supplementary Table 2).

After COVID19, systemic health conditions also contributed to the likelihood of depression. Dyslipidemia (OR, 1.88; 95% CI: 1.34–2.62), stroke (OR, 2.10; 95% CI: 1.02–4.32), myocardial infarction or angina (OR, 3.29; 95% CI: 1.88–5.76), arthritis or related musculoskeletal conditions (OR, 2.86; 95% CI: 2.02–4.07), tuberculosis (OR, 2.27; 95% CI: 1.09–4.70), asthma (OR, 2.21; 95% CI: 1.24–3.92), thyroid disease (OR, 2.12; 95% CI: 1.14–3.94), atopic dermatitis (OR, 2.83; 95% CI: 1.50–5.32), allergic rhinitis or sinusitis (OR, 1.93; 95% CI: 1.37–2.71), and eye-related chronic diseases such as cataract, glaucoma, or macular degeneration (OR, 1.76; 95% CI: 1.15–2.69) showed statistically significant associations with depression. Interestingly, alcohol consumption two to four times per week was associated with a lower likelihood of depression (OR, 0.68; 95% CI: 0.50–0.94). In addition, the number of systemic diseases was strongly predictive of depression (OR, 1.57; 95% CI: 1.50–1.66).

Discussion

This study leveraged nationally representative data from South Korea to examine disparities in oral health by depression status in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic. A key strength of this analysis is the use of two temporally distinct KNHANES datasets, one collected in 2019 (pre–COVID-19) and one collected in 2021 (post–COVID-19). This enabled direct comparison of depression-related oral health disparities across a pivotal period in global public health. Our findings showed that individuals with depression consistently exhibited poorer oral health outcomes, including more frequent toothache, chewing discomfort, and unmet dental care needs, than did those without depression. Notably, certain indicators such as unmet dental needs worsened after the pandemic. Moreover, some oral health-related variables remained independent predictors of depression even after adjusting for systemic and socioeconomic factors. These results underscore the persistent and evolving nature of oral health inequities among individuals with depression, particularly under pandemic-related stressors and service disruptions. However, causal attribution is limited, and the findings should be interpreted with caution due to the cross-sectional design.

The mouth is both a mirror and a gateway to the mind. While this study did not establish a bidirectional relationship, our findings highlight specific oral health-related factors that were significantly associated with depression before and after COVID-19. Individuals with depression reported more frequent toothache and chewing discomfort and greater chewing problem severity; this reflected the potential effects of behavioral neglect, physiological vulnerability, and reduced access to care [22]. Notably, chewing problem severity remained significantly greater in the depression group in both years and increased in prevalence from 2019 to 2021; this suggested pandemic-related worsening of functional oral limitations. Impaired oral functions may contribute to the development or worsening of depression through multiple pathways. Reduced masticatory function has been linked to nutritional imbalances, particularly deficiencies in fruits, vegetables, and essential micronutrients associated with depressive symptoms [23]. Furthermore, speaking difficulties can impair social participation and communication, leading to social withdrawal, isolation, and loss of self-confidence, which are well-established predictors of depression [24]. Chronic oral dysfunction may also lead to frustration, stigma, and lower quality of life, further exacerbating depressive symptoms [25]. Notably, unmet dental care needs emerged as a significant predictor of depression after the pandemic. This finding aligns with those in previous reports linking unmet dental needs to psychological distress [26, 27]. The COVID-19 pandemic further exacerbated barriers to dental care access globally [28], and evidence suggests that vulnerable groups, including individuals with mental health conditions, may have shown disproportionate reductions in care utilization [29]. The pandemic may have worsened oral health burdens in depressed populations, underscoring the need to address care accessibility in vulnerable groups.

The COVID-19 pandemic led to widespread disruptions in healthcare delivery systems globally, including routine dental care. Public health measures such as lockdowns, resource reallocation, and patient avoidance behavior reduced access to both preventive and urgent dental care [30]. Vulnerable populations, particularly individuals with mental health conditions, were disproportionately affected due to compounded barriers, including fear of infection, limited mobility, and financial strain [31]. These systemic challenges likely intensified preexisting oral health inequities, particularly among individuals with depression, as reflected in broader declines in dental care access and oral functional status following the pandemic. Mental and oral health are closely interrelated, with psychological distress potentially aggravating oral symptoms and vice versa. Our findings are consistent with previous studies indicating that structural barriers to dental care contribute to elevated psychological distress and lower quality of life [26, 32]. Nonetheless, these findings should be interpreted with caution given the study’s repeated cross-sectional design, which does not track the same individuals over time. As such, the differences observed between 2019 and 2021 may reflect population-level patterns rather than individual-level trajectories. Longitudinal studies are warranted to determine whether these disparities persist or evolve over time, particularly in the context of future public health emergencies.

The association between depression and poor oral health has been reported across various populations and settings, with studies indicating that individuals with depressive symptoms are more likely to experience tooth loss, dental caries, periodontal disease, and unmet dental care needs [33]. A large U.S. national survey found that adults with depressive symptoms had significantly fewer remaining teeth and were more likely to report poor self-rated oral health [34]. Similarly, a European population study revealed that depression was independently associated with periodontal disease and chewing difficulties, even after adjusting for age and socioeconomic status [35]. Another study using Korean national data showed that depressive symptoms were significantly associated with unmet dental care needs, particularly among women and older adults [36]. However, these studies were primarily cross-sectional and did not examine changes over time or under the impact of major societal disruptions, such as the COVID-19 pandemic. In contrast, our study makes a unique contribution by comparing depression-related oral health disparities across two nationally representative time points, before and after the pandemic, within the same population. This temporal approach allowed us to observe not only consistent disparities but also the emergence and intensification of specific risk factors such as unmet dental care needs. These findings may help inform oral health strategies tailored for East Asian populations.

In our study, individuals with depression had a significantly higher burden of chronic systemic conditions, including cardiovascular, endocrine, respiratory, and musculoskeletal disorders, many of which negatively affect mental and oral health. These comorbidities may impair oral health through systemic inflammation, reduced functional capacity, or side effects of medications such as antidepressants and corticosteroids [37]. Poorly controlled diabetes and cardiovascular disease are particularly notable risk factors for periodontal disease via inflammatory pathways [38]. In addition to biological mechanisms, social disadvantages also play a key role. Our findings confirmed that female sex, lower educational level, unemployment, and low income were independently associated with depression, consistent with prior findings [39]. These structural vulnerabilities are well-documented predictors of poor oral health, including fewer dental visits and greater tooth loss [27]. Thus, the intersection of chronic illness with socioeconomic hardship in individuals with depression reflects a pattern of synergistic epidemics that likely drives the persistent oral health inequities identified in our analysis.

This study has several strengths. It used data from KNHANES, a nationally representative, population-based survey of noninstitutionalized Korean adults [37]. Using KNHANES data, previous studies also reported an increase in depression prevalence during the early pandemic period, from 4.3% to 5.2% (2018 vs. 2020) and from 4.9% to 5.2% (2016 vs. 2020) [8, 40]. Jeong et al. further reported that stress among individuals with depression in Korea worsened during the COVID-19 pandemic [40]. However, to our knowledge, this is the first Korean study to directly examine the association between oral health and depression before and after the pandemic. By comparing data from two distinct periods—pre–COVID-19 and post–COVID-19—this study enables a clearer understanding of how depression-related oral health disparities evolved over time. Multiple regression analyses were conducted to adjust for a comprehensive set of covariates, including demographic, socioeconomic, and systemic health variables, thereby reducing potential confounding.

Nonetheless, this study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design of KNHANES precludes causal inference. It is unclear whether depression leads to poor oral health or whether declining oral health contributes to depressive symptoms. In addition, while this study revealed differences across the two time points, the cross-sectional nature of the KNHANES limits any causal interpretation. The findings reflect associations at the population level rather than changes within individuals over time. Second, the reliance on self-reported data introduces potential recall and reporting bias, as is common in large-scale health surveys [41]. Third, depression was defined based on participants’ self-report of physician diagnosis, without confirmation of whether standardized diagnostic criteria, such as the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, or the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth or Eleventh Revision, were applied. Therefore, clinical diagnostic validity cannot be assured. However, this approach is widely used in epidemiological research and has been applied in numerous studies utilizing KNHANES data. Fourth, the dataset lacked detailed information on the severity, chronicity, and treatment status of depression, as well as on dental care utilization, including visit frequency, treatment types, and access to public versus private services. This limited our ability to assess dose–response relationships and care-seeking behaviors. Finally, residual confounding from unmeasured variables—such as oral hygiene practices, dietary quality, and psychosocial support—may have influenced the observed associations.

The widening oral health gap among Koreans with depression likely reflects deeper health inequities. This trend highlights the urgent need for integrated care models that connect mental health and dental services, such as co-located clinics, routine cross-screenings, and targeted oral health education within psychiatric care [42]. In South Korea, the National Health Insurance system provides limited coverage for dental services, primarily for preventive and basic procedures such as scaling, simple extractions, and partial dentures. In contrast, most restorative and prosthetic treatments (e.g., crowns, bridges, implants) require out-of-pocket payment. While psychiatric diagnoses are generally included under national insurance coverage, access to comprehensive mental health services—including psychotherapy and long-term follow-up care—remains restricted due to workforce shortages, uneven service distribution, and persistent social stigma. Expanding public insurance coverage for basic dental treatments and investment in mobile or telehealth solutions can also improve access for vulnerable populations. Longitudinal analyses using updated KNHANES data are essential to assess the influence of post-pandemic changes in oral health and care accessibility on the long-term trajectory of depression. Establishing a longitudinal cohort based on KNHANES-linked follow-up data would allow researchers to explore causal relationships, mediating pathways, and the durability of pandemic-era disparities. Such efforts could ultimately inform more effective, equity-driven policies in both dental and mental healthcare systems.

Conclusion

This study provides nationally representative evidence of persistent and worsening disparities in oral health among individuals with depression before and after the COVID-19 pandemic. By comparing pre- and post-pandemic data, we identified not only consistent disadvantages in oral health within the depression group but also emerging factors such as unmet dental care needs. Functional limitations, including chewing difficulties, became more prevalent and may contribute to worsening depressive symptoms through biological and psychosocial pathways. These findings highlight the importance of integrating oral health considerations into broader mental health and public health strategies, particularly in the context of post-pandemic recovery. To address these disparities more effectively, future public health initiatives should prioritize integrating oral healthcare into mental health services. Co-located clinics offering both dental and psychiatric care, routine oral health screening during mental health consultations, and cross-disciplinary training for providers could enhance access and continuity of care. In crisis settings such as pandemics, mobile dental units, tele-dentistry platforms, and targeted outreach for high-risk groups may serve as effective delivery models. These integrated approaches have the potential to reduce systemic barriers and improve health-related quality of life. Ultimately, they may help address the interconnected burden of mental and oral diseases more holistically.
