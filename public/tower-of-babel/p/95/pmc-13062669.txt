Are the long-term oncologic outcomes different between appendiceal cancer and right-sided colon cancer? An exact matching analysis of a 10-year institutional cohort
Authors: Gunwoo Lee, Eun Jung Park, Soo Young Oh, Young Il Kim, Min Hyun Kim, Jong Lyul Lee, Chan Wook Kim, Yong Sik Yoon, In Ja Park, Seok-Byung Lim, Chang Sik Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeDue to its rarity, treatment guidelines for appendiceal cancer have traditionally followed those established for colorectal cancer, despite showing distinct histologic and clinical features. This study aimed to compare the clinicopathologic characteristics and long-term oncologic outcomes of appendiceal cancer with those of right-sided colon cancers.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed the records of patients with stage I–III appendiceal, cecal, or ascending colon cancer who underwent curative resection between 2010 and 2020 at our center. A 1:3:3 exact matching for age, sex, TNM stage, and adjuvant chemotherapy was performed. Survival outcomes were analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier and Cox regression methods.ResultsOverall, 245 patients with appendiceal cancer (n = 35), ascending colon cancer (n = 105), and cecal cancer (n = 105) were analyzed. Appendiceal cancer exhibited a higher proportion of T4 tumors and fewer harvested lymph nodes compared with ascending or cecal cancers. The mean follow-up duration was 9.5 years. The 5- and 10-year overall survival rates were lower in appendiceal cancer (66.2% and 52.9%) than in ascending (91.2% and 78.4%) or cecal cancer (88.5% and 78.3%). Similarly, the 10-year disease-free survival rate was lower in appendiceal cancer (59.2%) compared with ascending (83.1%) and cecal cancers (78.4%). Cox regression analysis identified age (≥65 years), perforation, nodal metastasis, and lymphovascular invasion as independent predictors of poor prognosis.ConclusionAppendiceal cancer exhibited significantly worse long-term survival compared to cecal or ascending colon cancer. Tumor perforation, nodal metastasis, and lymphovascular invasion were adverse prognostic factors for overall and disease-free survival.Keywords: Appendiceal neoplasms, Colorectal neoplasms, Survival analysis, Treatment outcome, Prognostic factors

INTRODUCTION

Appendiceal cancer is a rare malignancy, accounting for less than 1% of all gastrointestinal tumors, with an estimated annual incidence of approximately 0.1 to 1.3 per 100,000 individuals [1,2]. In recent decades, its incidence has gradually increased [3], likely due to advances in diagnostic imaging and the frequent incidental discovery of tumors during appendectomy [4,5]. Histologically, appendiceal neoplasms demonstrate marked heterogeneity, encompassing colonic-type adenocarcinoma, signet ring cell carcinoma, appendiceal mucinous neoplasms, and neuroendocrine tumors, each with distinct biological behavior and clinical outcomes [6,7,8,9]. Despite these pathological differences, treatment and staging strategies for appendiceal adenocarcinoma have historically followed those established for colorectal cancer, particularly right-sided tumors of the ascending colon and cecum [10,11].

Recent classification efforts, exemplified by the 2019 World Health Organization update and the 9th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging manual, have aimed to distinguish appendiceal tumors from colorectal cancer by considering histologic subtype and tumor grade in prognosis [12,13]. The prognostic impact of tumor location has also become increasingly recognized. Multiple studies, including population-based analyses, have reported that stage-matched appendiceal cancers often exhibit poorer overall and disease-free survival (DFS) than colon cancers, particularly in non-mucinous and signet ring cell histologies [13,14,15].

Although appendiceal cancer has clinically distinct characteristics compared with colorectal cancer, its oncologic outcomes and prognosis remain underestimated due to its rare incidence. In addition, the oncologic impact of the higher perforation rate in appendiceal cancer compared to colorectal cancer warrants further investigation. Therefore, this study aimed to compare the long-term oncologic outcomes of appendiceal cancer with those of right-sided colon cancer.

METHODS

Study population and patient selection

We conducted a retrospective review of patients with colorectal cancer who underwent right hemicolectomy at Asan Medical Center, Seoul, South Korea, between January 2010 and December 2020. We included patients pathologically diagnosed with stage I–III colorectal adenocarcinoma located in the appendix, ascending colon, or cecum, who had undergone R0 curative resection without evidence of distant metastasis. Additionally, only patients discharged within 30 days after surgery were included to minimize potential bias in oncologic outcomes from postoperative complications unrelated to the disease. Patients diagnosed with pseudomyxoma peritonei from appendiceal mucinous neoplasms were excluded. Tumors located in the hepatic flexure or left-sided colon, including the transverse colon, descending colon, sigmoid colon, and rectum, were also excluded. In addition, patients with synchronous double primary cancers, stage IV disease, or those who underwent surgery with palliative intent were not included in the study.

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Asan Medical Center (No. 2025-0336) in accordance with Good Clinical Practice guidelines and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All patient data were anonymized prior to analysis, and the requirement for written informed consent was waived by the IRB.

Data collection and evaluation parameters

Clinical and demographic variables collected included sex, age, body mass index (BMI) at the time of surgery, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status (PS) classification, TNM stage, preoperative CEA level, prior history of surgery for malignancy, and receipt of adjuvant chemotherapy. For the matched cohort, additional variables were reviewed, including the initial diagnostic modality (e.g., colonoscopy or CT), surgical approach, operative time, length of hospital stay, and time to initiation of a soft diet. Postoperative complications were graded using the Clavien-Dindo classification system [16]. The regimens of adjuvant chemotherapy were evaluated. Pathologic data included histologic subtype, tumor and regional lymph node stages, number of harvested and positive lymph nodes, lymphovascular invasion, perineural invasion, and tumor size. The presence of intraoperative perforation was documented from operative and pathologic records. OS was evaluated from the date of operation to death. Recurrence was assessed based on radiologic or reoperation records.

Surgical techniques and postoperative chemotherapy

All patients underwent right hemicolectomy with curative intent. The resection included the terminal ileum, cecum, ascending colon, and proximal transverse colon, with the mesocolon preserved en bloc. Regional lymphadenectomy routinely encompassed nodes along the ileocolic and right colic vessels and, when appropriate, was extended toward the root of the middle colic artery along the superior mesenteric vein. Division of the middle colic branch was performed only when the surgeon deemed it necessary to achieve adequate margins or nodal clearance. In patients with a previous appendectomy, dissection was adjusted to release pericecal adhesions and remove any residual mesoappendix to ensure oncologic completeness.

When a laparoscopic approach was used, five trocars were generally placed: one 12-mm camera port and four 5-mm working ports. In contrast, robotic procedures involved a 12-mm camera port just below the umbilicus, robotic arms positioned in the right mid-abdomen, right upper quadrant (third arm), and left lower quadrant, as well as a 5-mm assistant port in the left mid-abdomen. The specimen was retrieved through an additional incision, followed by the construction of an extracorporeal ileocolic anastomosis, which was either side-to-side or end-to-side.

Patients with stage II disease exhibiting high-risk factors or stage III disease who achieved R0 resection were considered for adjuvant chemotherapy according to institutional protocols and National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines, in consultation with a medical oncologist. Regimens included FL (5-fluorouracil/leucovorin), FOLFOX (5-fluorouracil/leucovorin plus oxaliplatin), UFT-E/leucovorin, capecitabine, and XELOX (capecitabine plus oxaliplatin). Chemotherapy was generally initiated within 4 to 8 weeks after surgery [17].

Exact matching

Exact matching of patients with appendiceal cancer was performed against those with ascending colon or cecal cancer. The matching was done in a 1:3:3 ratio (appendiceal cancer:ascending colon cancer:cecal cancer) using the following variables: sex (male vs. female), age (<65 years vs. ≥65 years), TNM stage (I vs. II vs. III), and administration of adjuvant chemotherapy (yes vs. no).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SAS ver. 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc.) and IBM SPSS Statistics ver. 28.0 (IBM Corp.). Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher exact test, while continuous variables were compared using 1-way analysis of variance. Survival analyses, including overall survival (OS) and DFS, were conducted using the Kaplan-Meier method, with group comparisons made using the log-rank test.

Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate prognostic factors for OS and DFS through univariate and multivariate analyses. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals were calculated. A 2-sided P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Among 2,308 patients initially assessed, a total of 2,046 were included. Of the included patients, 35 had appendiceal cancer, 1,557 had ascending colon cancer, and 454 had cecal cancer. After performing 1:3:3 exact matching, the final matched cohort consisted of 35 patients with appendiceal cancer, 105 with ascending colon cancer, and 105 with cecal cancer (Fig. 1). The mean follow-up duration was 9.5 ± 3.2 years.

Patient characteristics

Prior to exact matching, patients with appendiceal cancer were significantly younger, with 82.9% under 65 years of age, compared to 46.9% and 54.2% in the ascending and cecal cancer groups, respectively (P < 0.001). There were no significant differences in sex, BMI, ASA PS class, prior history of cancer operations, or preoperative CEA levels. However, the TNM stage of appendiceal cancer differed significantly from that of ascending and cecal cancers (P = 0.004). Stage I accounted for 17.1% of appendiceal cancers, compared with 25.8% of ascending colon cancers and 26.0% of cecal cancers. The use of adjuvant chemotherapy also did not significantly differ between the groups (Table 1).

Perioperative outcomes

The type of operation (laparoscopic, open, or robotic) was comparable across all 3 groups (P = 0.711). Operation time for ascending colon cancer was longer than for cecal cancer (P = 0.022), while no significant differences were observed between the other groups. There were no significant differences in the length of hospital stay or the time to initiation of a soft diet among the 3 groups. In contrast, postoperative complication rates of grade III or higher were higher in cecal cancer than in ascending colon cancer, but did not significantly differ between appendiceal cancer and either of the other groups. Additionally, there were no significant differences in the use of adjuvant chemotherapy among the 3 groups (Table 3).

Pathologic outcomes

According to histologic characteristics, mucinous adenocarcinoma was the most common type in the appendiceal cancer group, whereas moderately differentiated adenocarcinoma predominated in both ascending colon and cecal cancers (P < 0.001). The proportion of mucinous adenocarcinoma was 40.0% in appendiceal cancer, compared to 3.8% in ascending colon cancer and 10.5% in cecal cancer.

Although there was no significant difference in the overall TNM stage, the T stage differed significantly among the groups (P = 0.023). A higher proportion of T4 lesions was observed in appendiceal cancer, accounting for 37.2%, compared with 14.3% in ascending colon cancer and 13.4% in cecal cancer. The mean number of harvested lymph nodes was 23.6 ± 10.5 in appendiceal cancer, which was significantly lower than 33.4 ± 12.9 in ascending colon cancer and 31.2 ± 14.2 in cecal cancer (P = 0.001). In contrast, the number of positive lymph nodes did not differ significantly among the groups. Rates of lymphovascular and perineural invasion were also comparable. The primary tumor size was smaller in appendiceal cancer than in ascending colon and cecal cancers (P = 0.027). Perforation of the primary cancer at the time of surgery occurred in 45.7% of appendiceal cancer cases, which was more frequent than 2.9% in ascending colon cancer and none in cecal cancer (P < 0.001). Peritoneal metastasis was most common in appendiceal cancer, whereas hepatic metastasis was most common in ascending and cecal cancers (Table 4).

Oncologic outcomes

In the long-term survival analyses, both OS and DFS showed that appendiceal cancer was associated with poorer survival compared to ascending colon and cecal cancers. The 5-year OS rate was 66.2% in the appendiceal cancer group, compared to 91.2% and 88.5% in the ascending colon and cecal cancer groups, respectively. The 10-year OS rate was 52.9% for appendiceal cancer versus 78.4% and 78.3% for ascending colon and cecal cancers, respectively (P = 0.024) (Fig. 2A). In addition, the 5-year DFS rates were 59.2%, 90.6%, and 84.4% in the appendiceal, ascending colon, and cecal groups, respectively (Fig. 2B). The 10-year rate of DFS for appendiceal cancer was 59.2%, which was lower than 83.1% for ascending colon cancer and 78.4% for cecal cancer (P = 0.010).

Prognostic factors for survival

In both univariate and multivariate analyses, age, lymph node metastasis, perforation, and lymphovascular invasion were significant prognostic factors for OS and DFS. Age was a significant prognostic factor for OS and DFS; patients aged ≥65 years had poorer OS (HR, 3.293; P = 0.019) and DFS (HR, 2.528; P = 0.048) than younger patients. In terms of nodal metastasis, the N2 stage was strongly associated with inferior OS (HR, 13.154; P < 0.001) and DFS (HR, 13.177; P < .001). In addition, lymphovascular invasion was independently associated with a worse prognosis for both OS (HR, 2.618; P = 0.050) and DFS (HR, 4.086; P = 0.002). Furthermore, perforation was a significant prognostic factor for both OS (HR, 3.810; P = 0.033) and DFS (HR, 5.362; P = 0.004). Sex, tumor location, T stage, histologic grade, adjuvant chemotherapy, the number of harvested lymph nodes, and perineural invasion were not significant prognostic factors for either OS or DFS (Table 5).

DISCUSSION

The long-term oncologic outcomes of appendiceal cancer were worse than those of right-sided colon cancer for both OS and DFS. Appendiceal cancer had a higher rate of T4 stage tumors and perforation of the primary tumor compared with ascending colon or cecal cancer. Tumor perforation, nodal metastasis, lymphovascular invasion, and old age more than 65 years old were prognostic factors affecting long-term survival [18].

Due to its rare incidence and anatomical location adjacent to the colon, appendiceal cancer has traditionally been treated similarly to colon cancer. Resection of the primary tumor with lymphadenectomy is considered the standard surgical procedure for both appendiceal and colon cancers. The NCCN guidelines suggest that chemotherapy for localized or metastatic appendiceal cancer should follow the guidelines for colon cancer [19]. Similarly, the American Society of Clinical Oncology acknowledges that evidence for appendiceal cancer is limited and that current management is often extrapolated from colorectal cancer protocols [20]. However, our study demonstrates that the long-term survival of patients with appendiceal cancer is significantly poorer than that of patients with ascending or cecal colon cancer. The 5-year rate of OS for the appendiceal cancer group was 66.2%, which was lower than the 91.2% and 88.5% observed in the ascending colon and cecal cancer groups, respectively. The 10-year rates of OS and DFS for appendiceal cancer were also worse than those for ascending or cecal colon cancer. Similarly, in a multicenter retrospective study, Son et al. [21] reported that the 5-year DFS rate for appendiceal cancer patients was lower than that for colon cancer patients, at 57.9% and 85.2%, respectively. These findings question the adequacy of applying colorectal cancer protocols to appendiceal malignancies, especially since major clinical guidelines do not yet provide disease-specific recommendations.

The clinical features of appendiceal cancer significantly differ from those of colorectal cancer. Appendiceal cancer is characterized by a higher frequency of T4 tumors and increased rates of intraoperative perforation. In our study, the pathological T4 rate for appendiceal cancer exceeded 37%, compared to 13% for colon cancer. Furthermore, in stage II patients, the rate of T4 tumors in the appendiceal cancer group was nearly three times that of the ascending and cecal cancer groups. This high proportion of T4 tumors is a distinguishing feature of appendiceal cancer and reflects its greater susceptibility to tumor perforation. Additionally, the tumor perforation rate was significantly higher in the appendiceal cancer group than in the ascending colon cancer group (45.7% vs. 2.9%). Appendiceal abnormalities are rarely detected during colonoscopy, and diagnostic biopsies are seldom obtained in patients with appendiceal carcinoma [22,23]. The difficulty of early detection of appendiceal cancer via colonoscopy may contribute to the higher T stages observed in primary tumors. While right-sided colon cancers are often identified through screening colonoscopy, most appendiceal cancers are diagnosed by CT scans following the onset of clinical symptoms, indicating a lack of effective early detection strategies for this disease.

The late detection and the tumor’s biologically fragile location may increase the risk of peritoneal dissemination and recurrence. In a Japanese retrospective study, all cases of peritoneal recurrence occurred in pT4 appendiceal cancer [24]. Consistent with previous findings, our results showed that peritoneal recurrence was most common in the appendiceal cancer group. Furthermore, delayed cancer surgery—specifically the interval between appendectomy and right hemicolectomy with lymphadenectomy—can increase the risk of peritoneal recurrence. In addition, patients with periappendicular abscesses exhibited a higher incidence of appendiceal tumors than those with complicated acute appendicitis without abscess [25]. Delayed interval appendectomy for periappendiceal abscess can postpone the diagnosis of appendiceal cancer and worsen the prognosis [26]. Because appendiceal cancer is confirmed through pathological diagnosis, early diagnosis and initial complete tumor resection are challenging. In our study, only six patients (17.1%) in the appendiceal cancer group underwent initial right hemicolectomy with lymphadenectomy. Although radiologic examinations rarely allow a definite diagnosis of appendiceal cancer, surgeons should carefully assess the need for performing a right hemicolectomy with lymphadenectomy when the findings are highly suspicious. In addition, the average interval from appendectomy to complete resection was 28.2 days, consistent with previous reports of a higher incidence of neoplasms in patients undergoing interval appendectomy [23,27]. Peritoneal metastasis occurred in eight patients (22.9%) with appendiceal cancer, compared with one patient (1.0%) in the ascending colon cancer group and four (3.8%) in the cecal cancer group. The median interval from surgery to peritoneal metastasis was 1.87 years in the appendiceal cancer group, compared with 1 year and 0.75 years in the ascending colon and cecal cancer groups, respectively. Therefore, the higher rate of T4 stage tumors and postoperative inflammatory processes resulting from repeated operations for complete tumor resection may contribute to the increased risk of peritoneal recurrence in appendiceal cancer [21,28].

According to the current AJCC 9th edition staging, the pathologic features of mucinous and signet ring cell appendiceal histologies indicate a hierarchical prognosis compared to non-mucinous histologies [15]. The poorer survival prognosis in appendiceal cancer may be influenced by the higher prevalence of mucinous histology compared to colon cancer [29]. In our study, a higher proportion of mucinous histologic subtypes was observed, both of which are associated with poor prognosis in appendiceal cancer. Furthermore, the predominance of mucinous histology in appendiceal cancer may contribute not only to increased biological aggressiveness but also to reduced responsiveness to standard adjuvant chemotherapy regimens. Several studies have reported that these subtypes, characterized by a higher rate of mucinous histology, are associated with chemoresistance [30], which may partially explain the poorer outcomes despite equivalent chemotherapy administration [31].

Molecular profiling reported in other studies has suggested that appendiceal tumors possess distinct genomic characteristics. APC mutations are rare, whereas GNAS mutations are common in appendiceal cancer compared with colorectal cancer [32]. Particularly, mucinous appendiceal adenocarcinoma frequently harbors mutations in GNAS, RAS, and TP53 [33]. Tokunaga et al. [32] reported high mutation rates in appendiceal adenocarcinoma for KRAS (55%), TP53 (40%), and GNAS (31%), while APC mutations were less frequent (10%). These genetic alterations in RAS, GNAS, TP53, and WNT signaling modifiers can increase the risk of tumorigenesis and metastatic potential [34]. The distinct characteristics of GNAS mutations may be associated with a worse prognosis in appendiceal cancer compared with colorectal cancer. GNAS mutation-predominant mucinous appendiceal adenocarcinoma exhibits a higher rate of somatic alterations, increased peritoneal metastasis, and reduced response to chemotherapy. GNAS mediates cAMP-PKA signaling, which promotes mucin production and may create a tumor microenvironment conducive to metastasis and drug resistance [35]. A recent study on the genetic landscape of appendiceal cancer proposed that appendiceal adenocarcinomas can be categorized into low- and high-grade based on mutational burden and expression profiles [29]. These findings suggest that relying on conventional colorectal-based staging and treatment frameworks may not adequately address the distinct molecular characteristics of appendiceal cancer, highlighting the need for revised treatment approaches. Ongoing trials investigating appendiceal cancer using genomic and transcriptomic analyses correlated with clinical outcomes (NCT05364788, NCT05734430) are expected to clarify its genetic characteristics.

In this study, we compared patients with appendiceal, ascending colon, and cecal cancers. Although ascending and cecal cancers are commonly classified as right-sided colon cancers, it is important to evaluate oncologic outcomes based on their specific sites of origin. Embryologically, the cecum develops from the cecal diverticulum of the caudal midgut loop around the fifth week of gestation and is distinct from the ascending colon. Analysis of the SEER (Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results) database revealed that cecal cancer has a significantly higher prevalence than ascending colon cancer, while cancer-specific survival is significantly better in patients with ascending colon cancer compared to those with cecal cancer [36]. Accordingly, survival outcomes for appendiceal cancer were worse than those for both ascending and cecal cancers. However, no significant differences in OS or DFS were observed between cecal and ascending colon cancers in this study.

This study benefits from analyzing the clinicopathologic features and oncologic outcomes of appendiceal cancer using a large, 10-year institutional cohort. However, several limitations exist. It was a retrospective, single-center analysis subject to potential selection bias. Although we employed 1:3:3 exact matching to compare appendiceal cancer with right-sided colorectal cancer, the small appendiceal cancer cohort may limit statistical power and weaken the robustness of the findings. Additionally, while the surgical approach did not differ significantly among groups, the appendiceal cancer group exhibited a slightly shorter operative time and fewer harvested lymph nodes compared to the cecal cancer group. Although the number of harvested lymph nodes was not independently associated with survival in multivariate analysis, it remains difficult to determine whether the extent of lymph node dissection for appendiceal cancer should be standardized to that of colorectal cancer due to the retrospective study design. Future studies should prospectively compare oncologic outcomes between appendiceal and colon cancer with equivalent D3 lymph node dissection. Furthermore, although analysis of genetic mutations is necessary to interpret survival outcomes fully, our study was limited to assessing only clinicopathologic characteristics. Future research should involve multicenter, large-cohort, prospective validation studies, considering the rarity of this disease. Moreover, integrating molecular markers into prognostic models could improve precision in risk stratification and guide individualized management strategies.

In conclusion, appendiceal cancer was associated with significantly worse oncologic outcomes compared to right-sided colon cancer. Its distinct clinicopathologic characteristics—including a higher rate of tumor perforation, T4 stage presentation, nodal metastasis—were associated with poorer survival outcomes than those observed in colorectal cancer. Although appendiceal cancer is currently managed according to colorectal cancer guidelines, tailored treatment approaches that reflect its unique clinical features, along with strategies for early diagnosis, are necessary to improve oncologic outcomes.
