Association between adipokine and metabolic dysfunction-related fatty liver disease in plateau-dwelling populations
Authors: Jizhuo Yang, Junyi Liu, Shaoxiong Yang, Zhuohang Che, Ying Qian, Xuehui Zhang, Jianzhong Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPrevious research on the relationship between adipokines and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) has predominantly focused on low-altitude populations, despite the well-documented impact of environmental stressors on adipokine expression. This study sought to explore the relationship between adipokines and MASLD across various metabolic subtypes and to develop a predictive model for MASLD screening in high-altitude regions. This study included 750 indigenous plateau residents from the China Multi-Ethnic Cohort (CMEC). Logistic regression analyses were conducted to examine the associations of leptin, A-fatty acid binding protein, and visfatin with MASLD. Among the three adipokines, only leptin demonstrated a significant association with MASLD in both obesity and non-obesity populations. The predictive model based on leptin yielded an area under the curve of 0.86 (95% CI: 0.83–0.90). This study provides evidence that leptin serves as a diagnostic biomarker for identifying MASLD among indigenous plateau populations, applicable to both obesity and non-obesity individuals.

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), which supersedes the previous nomenclature of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), is a key hepatic manifestation within the spectrum of metabolic syndrome and is strongly associated with obesity.1 With a worldwide pandemic of obesity, MASLD has become the most common chronic liver disease in the world, affecting around 30% of the global population.2,3 The presence of MASLD poses a significant threat to public health. Currently, the diagnosis of MASLD is primarily dependent on imaging examinations and serum biomarkers. However, magnetic resonance spectroscopy is costly, computed tomography involves radiation exposure, and the sensitivity of ultrasound examinations varies.4 Commonly used biomarkers such as alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) also lack sufficient sensitivity and specificity for reliable MASLD diagnosis.5 Consequently, there remains an urgent need to develop novel, reliable biomarkers or scoring systems to facilitate the early detection and accurate diagnosis of MASLD.

While obesity is a recognized major risk factor for MASLD, the disease risk demonstrates considerable inter-individual variability even among those with comparable body mass index (BMI),6 a significant subset of patients presents with MASLD despite having a normal BMI.7 Given these limitations of BMI in accurately predicting MASLD, research attention has increasingly shifted toward exploring alterations in the endocrine function of adipose tissue. Leptin, a cytokine secreted by adipocytes, plays important roles in regulating energy homeostasis, metabolism, reproduction, and neuroendocrine function.8 A-Fatty acid binding protein (A-FABP), a lipid chaperone primarily expressed in adipocytes, participates in regulating lipid transport processes.9 Visfatin, a member of the adipokine, has been demonstrated to modulate glucose metabolism, lipolysis, insulin sensitivity, and inflammatory responses.10 Accumulating evidence suggests that these adipokines contribute to hepatic steatosis and may serve as potential biomarkers for identifying MASLD.9,11,12 Notably, these prior investigations were predominantly conducted in low-altitude populations. Plateau environments are characterized by low temperatures and chronic hypoxia, which have been demonstrably linked to significant alterations in systemic adipokine expression profiles.13,14 Nevertheless, it remains unclear whether the plateau environment influences the association between adipokines and MASLD. Furthermore, most previous investigations of the adipokine-MASLD association did not account for the metabolic profiles of participants, and the confounding role of obesity remains underexplored.

Therefore, this study recruited indigenous Han Chinese residents permanently residing at high altitude, with or without obesity, from the Yunnan cohort of the China Multi-Ethnic Cohort (CMEC). Our objective was to investigate the associations of adipokines with MASLD, accounting for obesity status, and to develop a practical predictive model for MASLD screening in high-altitude populations.

The dose-response curves between adipokines and MASLD are shown in Figure 2. In the overall cohort, the dose-response curves indicated that there were nonlinear relationships between leptin, A-FABP, and MASLD (p for nonlinear <0.05). BMI stratification demonstrated positive correlations of leptin and A-FABP with MASLD in the obesity population (p for overall <0.05). Conversely, within the non-obesity population, a nonlinear relationship was found between leptin levels and MASLD (p for nonlinear <0.05). The inflection point for leptin was determined using the “segmented” package. This revealed a value of 7.17 (5.21–9.15). To further assess this nonlinear relationship, a two-piecewise logistic regression model was employed. After implementing a comprehensive adjustment for the relevant covariates, it was determined that there was an inflection point in leptin level and the risk of MASLD (p for log-likelihood ratio <0.05) (Table 3). When the leptin level was less than 7.17, an elevated level of leptin was identified as a notable factor contributing to an elevated risk of MASLD (OR = 1.83, 95% CI = 1.31–2.69). Leptin is a potential diagnostic biomarker for metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease

As shown in Figure 4A, a nomogram model was developed to predict the risk of MASLD based on demographic characteristics, BMI, and leptin. Each predictive variable was assigned a specific score on a point scale. The summation of these individual scores corresponded to a total points value, which was subsequently projected to a risk scale indicating the predicted probability of MASLD. The predictive model demonstrated adequate performance, achieving an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.86 (95% CI: 0.83–0.90) (Figure 4B). Discussion

The study demonstrated that, among the three adipokines, only leptin was associated with MASLD in both obesity and non-obesity populations. Specifically, a nonlinear association was found between leptin and the prevalence of MASLD in non-obesity populations, whereas a linear positive correlation was consistently observed in the obesity group. Conversely, the association between A-FABP and MASLD was exclusively observed in individuals with obesity. The predictive model based on leptin and BMI demonstrated adequate performance, with an AUC of 0.86 (95% CI: 0.83–0.90). These findings highlight the complexity of leptin as a predictive measure for MASLD risk, emphasizing the necessity for more nuanced analyses based on individual BMI conditions.

Our study revealed that, among individuals residing at high altitudes, leptin exhibited the strongest correlation with the prevalence of MASLD compared to the other adipokines. This discovery aligns with prior studies.11,15 However, the confounding role of obesity remains conflicted. Our research found that when the whole-person stratum was stratified by BMI, leptin was associated with MASLD in both obesity and non-obesity populations. This finding appears consistent with the report by Nobili et al.,15 which posited a positive correlation between elevated leptin and liver injury severity independent of age and BMI. However, a meta-analysis revealed that the correlation between leptin levels and MASLD with hepatic fibrosis became non-significant after excluding patients with obesity.16 The contradictory findings across studies are likely attributable to inconsistent methodological approaches, particularly in the adjustment for metabolic confounders and the definition of the study cohort. Obesity is a major risk factor for MASLD. The true association in the research may be obscured by individuals with MASLD, excluding those with obesity.

Our findings indicate that leptin was an independent risk factor for MASLD in both obesity and non-obesity populations. One potential explanation for this phenomenon could be related to the role of leptin as a significant pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic factor.17 Leptin was initially thought to be a satiety regulator. However, it is actually a multifunctional molecule.18 Mechanistically, studies have demonstrated that leptin can promote the activation of hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) through paracrine signaling pathways. These activated HSCs subsequently exacerbate liver inflammation and fibrosis by upregulating the secretion of TGF-β1, angiopoietin-1, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and type I collagen.17,19,20 Furthermore, HSCs themselves can also secrete leptin and express the Ob-Rb receptor, establishing a positive feedback loop. This vicious cycle perpetuates HSC proliferation and inhibits their apoptosis, thereby driving the progression of hepatic inflammation and fibrosis.20 In addition, hypoxia, a hallmark of high-altitude environments, has been shown to upregulate the expression of leptin, VEGF, matrix metalloproteinase-2 (MMP2), and MMP9.21 Consequently, the high-altitude hypoxic environment may also contribute to the progression of MASLD by promoting angiogenesis through the regulation of leptin and fibrotic factor expression levels. We speculate that chronic hypoxia may amplify leptin’s pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic signaling in the liver, thereby increasing its metabolic impact even at moderately elevated levels in non-obese individuals. Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms through which these environmental pressures interact with leptin to influence MASLD require further investigation.

This study reveals a saturation effect in the association between leptin levels and MASLD among non-obese individuals. Specifically, the positive correlation became non-significant once circulating leptin concentrations surpassed a certain threshold. This appears inconsistent with the results of linear regression. The discrepancy likely arises from the differing underlying assumptions of these statistical models and the inherent complexity of the biological data. While linear regression assumes a monotonous linear relationship across the entire data range, piecewise regression is capable of modeling distinct linear associations within different data intervals. In biological systems, the relationship between a biomarker and a disease outcome is often complex and non-linear. Consequently, imposing a single linear model may fail to capture the true dose-response relationship, potentially leading to an underestimation or overestimation of the variable’s effect. The use of breakpoints in piecewise regression affords greater flexibility in modeling such intricate data patterns. We hypothesize that the observed threshold saturation phenomenon in non-obese individuals is attributable to the onset of leptin resistance. Previous research indicates that under conditions of high circulating leptin levels, the peripheral effects of leptin may be attenuated due to reduced leptin sensitivity and receptor saturation.22,23 Although the precise mechanisms of leptin resistance remain incompletely understood, they may involve leptin receptor desensitization, downregulation of intracellular signaling pathways, and elevated expression levels of inflammatory mediators.23 The leptin threshold saturation effect observed in this study may provide a partial explanation for the significantly lower levels of steatosis, inflammation, fibrosis, and metabolic syndrome exhibited by patients with lean MASLD compared to patients with obese MASLD.24 In contrast, our analysis revealed a consistent linear positive correlation between leptin and the prevalence of MASLD in the obesity group. This result is likely due to selective leptin resistance—a concept initially proposed to explain leptin-mediated increases in blood pressure among obese individuals.25 Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that even in circumstances of elevated leptin levels, induced by a high-fat diet (HFD), leptin continues to promote the infiltration of macrophages into epididymal adipose tissue.26,27 However, since current research on hyperleptinemia has been conducted in obese animals, it remains unclear whether hyperleptinemia in non-obese states can produce the same biological mechanisms. Although the cause of the threshold saturation effect remains unclear, our findings consistently suggest that maintaining lower leptin levels is more beneficial for preventing MASLD, regardless of whether individuals are obese or non-obese.

Our study further demonstrated a positive correlation between A-FABP and MASLD among obesity populations, consistent with findings from previous research. A significant positive association has been observed between A-FABP levels and hepatic steatosis in individuals exhibiting metabolic abnormalities.9 It has been shown that circulating A-FABP concentrations and hepatic A-FABP gene expression increase progressively with MASLD severity.28,29 In particular, Wu et al. demonstrated that upregulated A-FABP expression in liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs) promotes the progression of liver fibrosis.30 Mechanistically, A-FABP in LSECs may facilitate the M1 polarization of macrophages and enhance the infiltration of CXCR3+ macrophages through the activation of the NF-κB/p65 signaling pathway.31 Furthermore, A-FABP can induce oxidative stress in endothelial cells, leading to endothelial dysfunction, which contributes to the progression of MASLD.32 Consequently, some researchers have proposed that A-FABP may serve as a non-invasive prognostic biomarker for assessing the progression of MASLD.33 However, emerging evidence indicates that A-FABP is not liver-specific but acts as a key mediator of lipid-induced inflammatory responses across multiple tissues.34 For instance, Zhang et al.35 found that A-FABP triggers chondrocyte degeneration by activating the NF-κB pathway and mediating catabolic responses in chondrocytes. Similarly, A-FABP plays a critical role in renal inflammation and fibrosis in mouse models of hyperuricemic nephropathy by modulating the JAK2-STAT3 and NF-κB p65 pathways.36 Notably, both chemical and genetic inhibition of A-FABP have been shown to ameliorate atherosclerosis, cardiac fibrosis, and type 2 diabetes.34,37,38,39,40 In conclusion, although further longitudinal studies are required to determine whether A-FABP can serve as a distinct prognostic biomarker for MASLD, targeting A-FABP—a multifunctional inflammatory signaling molecule involved in multiple organs—may represent a promising therapeutic approach for inflammatory and metabolic diseases.

Our study demonstrated that visfatin is not associated with an increased risk of MASLD among high-altitude residents, despite its elevated levels in individuals with obesity. Some studies suggest that visfatin acts as a protective factor against MASLD. One case-control study revealed a statistically significant difference in serum visfatin levels between the MASLD and control groups.41 However, the evidence regarding visfatin levels in MASLD/MASH remains conflicting. While a U.S.-based observational study reported lower serum visfatin in patients with MASH compared with obesity controls, other investigations have demonstrated elevated circulating visfatin levels in MASLD.42,43,44 In line with our results, Halil et al.45 also found no significant association between visfatin and MASLD. We propose several possible reasons for the contradictory results. First, the inclusion criteria for each study were inconsistent, and participants may have differed in their burden of comorbid metabolic conditions. Serum visfatin levels are known to be influenced by metabolic risk factors, such as obesity, T2D, and hypertension.42,46 Second, drugs that target metabolism can affect fat factor levels, potentially masking underlying pathologies.47 Therefore, future studies in larger populations are needed to validate these findings.

In conclusion, this study aimed to evaluate the associations between adipokines and MASLD across these metabolic subtypes and to develop a predictive model for MASLD screening in high-altitude regions. We demonstrated that leptin serves as a sole independent marker for MASLD in plateau-dwelling individuals, irrespective of their obesity status. Additionally, the predictive model based on leptin showed adequate predictive performance in the study subjects. This model could serve as a reliable, early screening tool for MASLD in high-altitude populations.

Limitations of the study

Our study has the following limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes the determination of causality. Second, despite considering various potential confounding factors during the selection process, other unconsidered confounding factors may still exist due to the observational nature of the study. Third, since this study did not confirm MASLD through liver biopsy, the prevalence of the condition may have been underestimated. In light of these limitations, future studies utilizing longitudinal cohort data are warranted to validate the leptin cutoff established in the current analysis and to evaluate its predictive value for incident MASLD.

Experimental model and study participant details

Study participants were selected from the Han Chinese population residing in a high-altitude region (average altitude: 2200 m; mean annual temperature: 13°C) of the Yunnan cohort from the CMEC. The study comprised 384 individuals classified with obesity (BMI ≥ 28kg/m2). For comparison, a control group of 366 participants with normal BMI (BMI < 24kg/m2) was randomly selected from the same population. Exclusion criteria included individuals with a history of acute or chronic hepatitis (regardless of viral or drug-induced origin) and excessive alcohol consumption (defined as ≥ 70 g/week for women or ≥ 140 g/week for men).The characteristics of participants are shown in Table 1. Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Medical Ethics Committee of Kunming Medical University (KMMU2020MEC078). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to study participation. Comprehensive details regarding the study design, sampling plan, and baseline characteristics of the cohort have been previously published.48

The diagnosis of MASLD requires the presence of hepatic steatosis, as detected by imaging or histology, coupled with at least one of five cardiometabolic risk factors, and contingent upon the rigorous exclusion of other chronic liver diseases, including significant alcohol consumption.3 The five cardiometabolic risk factors for diagnosing MASLD are defined as follows: (i) HDL-C ≤ 1.0 mmol/L (for men) and ≤ 1.3 mmol/L (for women) or ongoing lipid-modifying treatment; (ii) TG ≥ 1.70 mmol/L (150 mg/dL) or receipt of lipid-lowering agents; (iii) blood pressure ≥ 130/85 mmHg or treatment with specific antihypertensive medications; (iv) FBG ≥ 5.6 mmol/L or 2-h postprandial blood glucose ≥ 7.8 mmol/L or HbA1c ≥ 5.7% or type 2 diabetes (T2D) or treatment for T2D; (v) BMI ≥ 23 kg/m2 or waist circumference > 90 cm (male) or 80 cm (female).

Method details

Data collection and measurement

All participants underwent structured interviews administered via standardized electronic questionnaires. The questionnaire can be obtained from the corresponding author upon request. The interviews were conducted by investigators who had received comprehensive training to ensure the accurate and uniform collection of data on general demographic characteristics, socioeconomic status, and lifestyle behaviors. Standardized protocols were followed for physical examinations to accurately measure height, weight, and other relevant anthropometric indicators. Registered medical professionals who had undergone rigorous training performed ultrasound examinations to ensure high inter-rater reliability and data quality control. Fasting blood glucose (FBG), serum concentrations of triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) were measured using a fully automated biochemical analyzer. Serum levels of the target adipokines were measured using commercially available Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) kits following the manufacturers' instructions: (i) Leptin: Measured using ELISA kits from R&D Systems (Catalog No. DLP00; Minneapolis, MN, USA); (ii) A-Fatty Acid Binding Protein (A-FABP): Measured using ELISA kits from R&D Systems (Catalog No. DFBP40; Minneapolis, MN, USA); (iii) Visfatin: Measured using ELISA kits from RayBiotech Systems (Catalog No. EIA-VIS; Peachtree Corners, GA, USA).

Assessment of covariates

The assessed covariates included work (categorized as agriculture and non-agricultural), annual household income (categorized as < RMB 20,000 and ≥ RMB 20,000), smoking status (yes or no), drinking status (yes or no), marital status (classified as married/cohabiting and others), and education level (categorized as middle school and below, and others).

Physical activity (PA) data, encompassing the duration and type of various activities performed over the previous 12 months, were collected via a standardized questionnaire. The total amount of physical activity was aggregated across four domains: occupational, transportation-related, leisure-time, and household-related. The metabolic equivalent of task (MET) value for a specific physical activity type was multiplied by the daily duration of that activity, and the resulting values were summed to calculate the total daily physical activity metabolic equivalents. Participant physical activity levels were stratified into low (Q1), moderate (Q2–Q3), or high (Q4) groups according to MET quartiles.

Dietary data, including the frequency and consumption amounts of specific food items, as well as the usage of edible oil and salt, were collected utilizing a comprehensive food frequency questionnaire (FFQ). We subsequently calculated the mean daily total energy consumption according to the Chinese Food Exchange Tables and the Chinese Food Composition Tables (2018). BMI was determined using the conventional formula: weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Multivariate logistic regression models were employed to examine the relationships of three adipokines with MASLD, generating adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). To rigorously minimize confounding bias, the final models were adjusted for a comprehensive set of potential covariates, including: gender, age, work, annual household income, smoking status, alcohol consumption, marital status, education level, total daily physical activity (MET score), total energy intake, HbA1c, TG and HDL-C. The exploration of potential non-linear relationships between the circulating levels of adipokines and the risk of MASLD utilized the restricted cubic spline (RCS) model. The “segmented” R package was implemented to ascertain the inflection point, based on the likelihood-ratio test and bootstrapping methods. Subgroup analyses incorporating stratification by demographic/lifestyle factors (gender, age, work, alcohol consumption, smoking status, and physical activity) were conducted to evaluate modification effects. For the development and validation of the predictive model, participants were randomly allocated into a training set and a validation set at a 7:3 ratio. A predictive nomogram model was subsequently established based on key variables of MASLD, and it was validated using calibration and a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. Details of comparisons between groups are described in the figure legends. All p-values reported were two-sided, with statistical significance determined using statistical software packages SPSS (version 27.0) and R (version 4.4.1).
