Investigation of mechanical properties of 316 l steel samples at slm process with ultrasonic influence
Authors: I. A. Ivanov, S. V. Salikhov, E. B. Cherepetskaya, V. V. Cheverikin, A. A. Gapeev, P. N. Ivanov, I. A. Shibaev, Y. G. Semyonov, V. V. Vasilevykh, A. V. Karavaev, V. V. Dremov
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The SLM process is characterized by high crystallization rates and the formation of columnar grain structures. In this work, two fiber lasers were used to reduce the characteristic grain sizes and improve the physical and mechanical properties of the synthesized samples during 3D printing with metal powder of 316 L steel. Along with a continuous ytterbium laser, a quasi-continuous-wave (QCW) laser was used, which varied the repetition rate and duration of signals. Absorption of pulses of the second laser in the near-surface layer of the melt pool led to generation of powerful short ultrasonic pulses in it and, as a consequence of their action, grain refinement in the formed structure of 316 L steel was observed. The average grain size at additional influence of QCW laser decreased from 50 μm to 25 μm. Mechanical testing showed that under certain QCW modes, the ultimate tensile strength increased by ~ 13–15% and the elongation at failure increased from ~ 34% to ~ 40%.

Introduction

Selective Laser Melting (SLM) is one of the additive manufacturing techniques1–3 and is a layer-by-layer metal powder-based manufacturing process that can be used to create parts of high complexity1–3. According to ISO/ASTM 52900:20214, SLM is classified as a laser-based powder bed fusion process (PBF-LB/M) in metal additive manufacturing. In the present work, however, the term SLM will be used throughout for clarity and consistency with common usage in the literature. The most commonly used material for SLM is 316 L austenitic stainless steel, which is characterized by its improved anti-corrosion properties, creep resistance, strength and ability to operate in aggressive environments in a variety of industries including medical, aerospace and automotive.

An obstacle to the widespread implementation of SLM technology is the manifestation of porosity and anisotropic properties in printed products, which is closely related to the large columnar grains growing along the build direction in most industrial alloys5,6. This is due to the fact that SLM is characterized by high temperature gradients (10^5–10^7 K/m) and high cooling rates (10^5–10^7 K/s) in a melt pool of approximately 100 μm in size7,8.

One of the possible ways to transition from columnar-shaped grains in metals and alloys to equiaxed grains and to reduce porosity of samples synthesized by additive technology methods is the application of high-intensity ultrasound during the crystallization process9–11. Similar phenomena as a result of ultrasonic action during solidification of metal systems have been observed both in welding12–16 and traditional casting17–19.

The main number of articles12–21 is devoted to ultrasonic influence on the crystallization process of light metal alloys. As noted in12–21, the main mechanisms of grain refinement are cavitation and acoustic currents. The ultrasonic field in the melt creates variable acoustic pressure. When some critical pressure value is reached, pulsating bubbles and acoustic flows occur in the liquid. The bubbles vibrate depending on the magnitude of the variable pressure, grow, merge and collapse, creating local shock waves with high pressure and temperature in the nearby region20–22, which markedly affect the properties of the solidifying material and its microstructure.

Studies of ultrasonic grain refinement for aluminum and magnesium alloys were carried out in23–27. Here, acoustic pressure in the melt zone was created by a sonotrode operating at a frequency of 20 kHz at intensities exceeding the cavitation threshold for the corresponding alloy.

Subsequently, ultrasonic impact on the melt pool using sonotrodes was applied in additive technologies9,1028–30. In28,29, it was shown for 304 L alloy that the application of sonotrode-based ultrasonic impact at SLM using a 1 kW sonotrode operating at 50 kHz and attached to a substrate leads to a decrease in Young’s modulus anisotropy from 8% to 2% in the samples and microhardness anisotropy from 30% to 6%, as well as to an increase in nanohardness by 6%.

The authors30 studied the effect of ultrasonic fields of different frequencies (25 kHz, 33 kHz and 41 kHz) on the microstructural and mechanical properties of Inconel 718 alloy obtained by directed energy deposition (DED). In30, it was obtained that when ultrasonic vibrations are introduced in a directional manner, the formation of cavitation bubbles affects the Marangoni flow in the molten bath and leads to a reduction in the number of defects caused by layer-by-layer sputtering, improves the microstructure and increases the microhardness.

In9,10, the DED process with additional ultrasonic influence at a frequency of 20 kHz, created by a sonotron fixed on the substrate, for printing with powders of Ti-6Al-4 V alloys and 316 L steel was also considered. It is shown that the effect of ultrasound on the process of melt pool crystallization leads to a transition from columnar grains to equiaxed grains and a reduction in the number of defects. The explanation of these effects is also based on the processes of cavitation bubble formation and the emergence of acoustic currents when the melt pool is subjected to alternating pressure.

An alternative mechanism of grain refinement was proposed in31. Here, ultrasonic influence was introduced directly into the melt pool of austenitic stainless steel 12Cr18Ni10Ti (AISI 321 H). Experimental study of AISI 321 H microstructure formation was carried out using a QCW laser source with complex intensity modulation, as a result of which two processes occur simultaneously: melting of a region of the order of 100 μm in size and generation of powerful ultrasonic pulses in it with pressure amplitude up to tens of MPa. A decrease in grain size in the laser melting zone was observed. Microscopic modelling showed a significant decrease in the yield strength of AISI 321 H steel near the melting temperature up to 40 MPa. Shear deformations arising under the action of ultrasonic pressure pulse, in the region with low yield strength, lead to grain refinement.

In32,33, a pulsed laser was used in the DED fusion process of Ti-6Al-4 V powder in addition to a continuous laser, which also led to a reduction in grain size and porosity.

The present paper contains the results of research, which is a continuation of the work31. The technology of 3D printing with metal powder of 316 L steel using two lasers is considered. The first laser is continuous, produces fusion of powder and creates a melt pool with dimensions of about 100 μm. The second laser is QCW. The interaction of nanosecond pulses from the second laser with the near-surface layer of the melt pool results in the generation of short, powerful ultrasonic pulses that create shear deformations in the just-crystallized zone necessary for grain refinement (see11.

The main focus of this work is on the change in mechanical properties of synthesized 316 L steel specimens due to additional high frequency ultrasonic impact on the melt pool.

Materials and methods

Material and experimental setup for SLM with ultrasound

The starting material for 3D printing was 316 L stainless steel powder with a particle size distribution of 10–63 μm. Chemical composition of the powder: 17.54% Cr (wt%), 10.61% Ni, 1.44% Mn, 2.63% Mo, 0.03% C, 0.02% Ti, 0.29% Si, 0.019% P, 0.007% S, the rest Fe as a base. The sphericity of the particles was 88%.

Two lasers were employed in the setup: a continuous-wave (CW) ytterbium fiber laser YLR-400-WC (IPG Photonics, USA) and a quasi-continuous-wave (QCW) MOPA fiber laser MFPT-300 M+ (Maxphotonics Co Ltd, China). The centrewavelength of the CW laser is 1070 nm. The maximum power is 400 W, with the possibility of changing it from 12% to 100% of the maximum power. The diameter of the laser beam was 80 μm. The centre wavelength of the QCW laser was 1066 nm. The pulse duration range varied from 10 ns to 500 ns with a possible pulse repetition rate variation from 1 kHz to 4 MHz. Depending on the pulse repetition rate and pulse duration, the peak power ranged from 5 to 10 kW. The diameter of the QCW laser beam was 120 μm.

The spatial delay of the QCW beam relative to the CW beam was 100–200 μm (The schematic is shown in Fig. 1). Creation of this delay was necessary for the QCW laser radiation to be absorbed in the near-surface layer of the already formed melt pool. As a result of absorption of short laser pulses with peak power of 5–10 kW and subsequent expansion of additionally heated area with possible insignificant ablation, generation of powerful nanosecond ultrasonic pulses with pressure amplitude up to 100 MPa takes place32,34. The ultrasonic impact on the melt pool of these pulses, following at a frequency of up to 1 MHz, leads to the structural features described below of the solidifying material.

The operating modes of each laser were selected as follows. At scanning speeds of 600–800 mm/s and 40 μm layer thickness, the CW laser power was chosen to minimize porosity. Optimal printing modes were achieved at CW laser volumetric energy densities in the range of 70–110 J/mm3. Methods of sample research

The density of the synthesized samples was determined by hydrostatic weighing method based on ASTM B962-08. Laboratory analytical scales GR-202 with the AD-1653 density determination kit (AND, Japan) with ± 0.01 mg readability was used.

Tensile strength, yield strength, and relative elongation were determined in accordance with ASTM E8/E8M-08 using an Instron 150LX universal testing machine (max. load 150 kN). The strain rate was 10− 3 s− 1. The elongation of the specimens was measured using a non-contact video extensometer. For each printing condition, at least three specimens were tested to ensure reproducibility, and the average values of UTS, yield strength, and elongation (with standard deviations) are reported.

Specimens for EBSD were cut from the gauge sections after tensile failure, then mounted, ground, and polished using successive diamond suspensions down to 1 μm on the Tergamin polishing machine (Struers). They were cleaned in ethanol (no etching used). The structure of the samples was analyzed on a TESCAN VEGA LMH electron scanning microscope with a LaB6 cathode and an Oxford Instruments Advanced AZtecEnergy X-ray energy dispersive microanalysis system and an Oxford Instruments NordLysMax2 EBSD attachment with AZtec version 3.5 software. The Oxford Instruments Pure Element and Organic Database and the NIST Phases and Elements Database were used to identify the phases of the material. Scanning was carried out with a step of 0.05 μm. The mean angular deviation (MAD) used in automatic indexing is the quality of the solution match. The smaller the number, the better the match between the detected Kikuchi bands and the simulated phase lines. According to the accepted rules of phase identification, a value of MAD less than 1 indicates a good match. In this work we used the coefficient of MAD < 0.3.

As shown in Fig. 3, the samples printed with additional QCW pulses exhibit higher strength than those printed with only the CW laser. Specifically, the ultimate tensile strength (UTS) of the CW-only samples was 564–573 MPa, whereas with the pulsed laser it increased to ~ 650 MPa. At the same time, it should be noted that the conditional yield strength in these samples did not change practically. Thus, the conditional yield strength of the samples obtained in the absence of ultrasonic influence was equal to 438 and 444 MPa, whereas when using pulsed-periodic laser the value of the yield strength of the samples was 419 and 437 MPa. The elongation at failure increased to ~ 39% and 42% with the pulsed laser, versus ~ 34% for the CW-only samples.

A comparative analysis of the density of the obtained samples of 316 L steel without and with the influence of pulsed-periodic laser showed that when the powder was fused by one laser the density of the samples was 7.87 ± 0.02 and 7.86 ± 0.02 g/cm3, whereas when ultrasonic influence was added the density of the printed samples was 7.90 ± 0.02 and 7.89 ± 0.02 g/cm3.

The calculated density value of samples without and with pulsed-periodic laser exposure was 99.5% and 99.9% of the theoretical density, respectively. The calculated density was determined in accordance with the chemical composition of 316 L steel powder used and was ~ 7.91 ± 0.01 g/cm3.

Microstructural analysis

Figure 4 shows the grain structure of the sample without and with exposure to pulsed-periodic laser. The microstructure of the sample obtained with the pulsed-periodic laser is more dispersed. The average grain size for the samples obtained by printing with continuous laser and with the addition of pulsed-periodic laser is 50 ± 5 μm and 25 ± 5 μm, respectively. It is also worth noting that the structure obtained with the influence of pulsed-periodic laser has a more homogeneous grain size distribution. The grain shape anisotropy is maintained for the two samples, but in the samples obtained with the pulsed-periodic laser the grain shape anisotropy is less pronounced.

Figure 5 shows EBSD images of the structure of the samples without and with the influence of pulsed-periodic laser. It can be clearly seen that the images contain finely dispersed particles of the second phase, presumably chromium carbide particles.

To confirm the nature of the second-phase particles, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) was performed in addition to EBSD. The chemical composition and crystallographic orientation of these particles were consistent with chromium-rich carbides, and they were identified by matching experimental Kikuchi patterns with the Oxford Instruments database. Given that the chemical signatures and EBSD phase indexing excluded the presence of oxides or δ-ferrite, we confidently classify these particles as Cr-carbides. The average particle size, based on image analysis, was 255 ± 35 nm, which agrees well with our model prediction.

When printed with a continuous laser, individual particles are present in the sample, located predominantly along grain and sub-grain boundaries. In samples obtained with additional exposure to a pulsed-periodic laser, the number of finely dispersed chromium particles increases significantly. The particles are located not only along the boundaries, but also a large number of carbide particles appear in the volume of subgrains. The regularity of the particle arrangement in the grain volume suggests that additional exposure to the pulsed-periodic laser leads to the release of chromium carbide particles mainly at dislocations (Fig. 5a, b).

EBSD and mechanical analysis indicate that the additional pulsed QCW laser (generating ultrasonic pressure pulses) increases the tensile strength and produces a finer equiaxed grain structure.

Conclusion

This study confirms the efficacy of applying ultrasonic treatment during the Selective Laser Melting (SLM) process to enhance the mechanical properties of 316 L stainless steel samples. The experiments revealed that ultrasonic influence significantly reduces the grain size in the material’s microstructure, which positively affects its mechanical characteristics.
