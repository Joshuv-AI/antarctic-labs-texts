Ethanol production from rice straw: Comparative study of single and combined use of CaO/Ag nanocatalyst with Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Aspergillus terreus
Authors: Amany M. Hamad, Ahmed A. El-Sherif, Asmaa M. Ahmed, Heba Allah Abdelnabi Eid Mohamed, Engy Shams-Eldin, Maha A. Mohamed, Esraa Ahmed Abu El Qassem Mahmoud, L.M. Kasem, Ahmed E. Ibrahim, Heba M. Fahmy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This research investigates a sustainable method for bioethanol synthesis from ammonia-pretreated rice straw utilizing a CaO/Ag nanocatalyst. The addition of ammonia elevated the available cellulose level of rice straw from 37.7% to 55.87%, thereby improving its suitability for enzymatic hydrolysis. The fungal isolate Aspergillus terreus (At PP590607) and the yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae (Sc OR668931) were utilized both separately and in conjunction, with or without the CaO/Ag nanocatalyst. Antimicrobial testing revealed MICs of CaO/Ag nanoparticles ranging from 1 to 10 µg/ml, with sub-MIC doses employed in subsequent experiments. This nanocatalyst enhanced the liberation of fermentable reducing sugars, especially in conjunction with S. cerevisiae. Nonetheless, it demonstrated a considerable inhibitory effect on the net product of the resulting reducing sugars, from 5.2 mg/L to 3.8 mg/L sugar, when paired with A. terreus. The maximum ethanol output was achieved with S. cerevisiae alone, but silver nanoparticles exhibited slight adverse effects on yeast fermentation. The HPLC and DNS assays measured ethanol and sugar concentrations, respectively, while SEM imaging demonstrated that ammonia pretreatment compromised lignocellulosic architecture, enhancing saccharification. While concurrent saccharification and fermentation with both microorganisms reduced ethanol yields, the incorporation of nanocatalysts markedly improved production. ITS sequencing and BLAST verified the microbial identities with 98.1% and 99.6% similarity for A. terreus andS. cerevisiae, respectively. The research highlights the need to enhance microbe–nanocatalyst interactions to improve fermentation efficiency. It advocates an environmentally sustainable and economically viable method for transforming lignocellulosic agricultural byproducts into renewable bioethanol through integrated microbial and nanotechnological techniques.Graphical abstractConceptual workflow of bioethanol production from ammonia-pretreated rice straw using Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Aspergillus terreus, with and without CaO/Ag nanocatalyst supplementation.

Introduction

Annually, large amounts of carbon dioxide (CO₂) have been released into the atmosphere, increasing at an average rate of 2.11%. If this trend continues, atmospheric CO₂ accumulation is expected to reach a toxic threshold of approximately 1,200 ppm within 53 years, posing severe risks to human health and natural ecosystems (Hossain 2022). Carbon emissions from fossil fuel combustion have increased by nearly 57.5% in recent years, primarily contributing to this rise in atmospheric CO₂ (Siddik et al. 2021). A defining feature of modern industrial society is its heavy reliance on fossil fuels; however, the depletion of nonrenewable reserves, rising production costs, and rapid global population growth have intensified the demand for energy to sustain industrial development (Ali et al. 2024). To meet future energy needs while minimizing environmental impact, it is essential to relocate toward sustainable, low-emission energy sources and integrate them into industrial operations.

Many countries, such as the United States, India, China, and those in Europe, are developing renewable energy sources, including biofuels. Biofuels emerge as a promising solution, linking renewable energy sources with poverty reduction, agricultural development, and economic growth, as well as climate change mitigation and environmental protection (Hasan et al. 2023). Bioethanol is a promising drop-in biofuel produced by fermenting sugar and/or starch-enriched biomass sources using microorganisms (Edeh, 2021). In 2020, the United States led global ethanol production with 52.2 billion liters, followed by Brazil with 31.35 billion liters. Canada contributed 1.6% of global output, primarily from corn and wheat. Argentina, India, Thailand, and the EU produced 870 million liters, 2.98 billion liters, 1.5 billion liters, and 5.47 billion liters, respectively (Hoang and Nghiem 2021). By 2030, the percentage of biofuels used in transportation is expected to reach 14%, as mandated by the Renewable Energy Directive (RED II) (Hawrot-Paw et al., 2022). Furthermore, ethanol is blended with gasoline at three primary levels, with E10 essentially replacing pure gasoline (E0) in the United States (Johnson et al. 2021), the relevance of bioethanol development in Indonesia, as high-blend biofuels may become viable in the future, given that the automotive industry will require 5–10 years to produce compatible flexible-fuel vehicles (FFVs) by 60% (Purwanto and Lutfiana 2024). The United States, Brazil, and the European Union are major adopters of ethanol-blended fuels. Biofuels are divided into four generations. First-generation ethanol, made from edible crops such as sugarcane and corn, is the most common but competes with food production and requires significant water resources. Second-generation ethanol, produced from lignocellulosic Biomass such as crop residues, is more sustainable as it avoids food competition. Third-generation bioethanol is derived from marine organisms, and fourth-generation bioethanol is derived mainly from algae (Aghaei et al., 2022).

Lignocellulosic biomass is an attractive feedstock for ethanol production due to its abundance, low cost, easy availability, and renewable nature. Rice straw is the most important source of lignocellulosic biomass, accounting for 40–60% of paddy plants. Yearly, the global production of rice straw is estimated to exceed 731 million tons (Ali et al. 2024). Egypt contributes approximately 4–5 million tons of rice straw production per year (Nassar et al. 2022). There are about 3.1 million tons annually burned, and that causes the “black cloud” phenomenon, which causes air pollution, governmental waste management concerns, the destruction of soil and greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, carbon footprint, air pollution, and climate change issues (Andini et al. 2018; Nassar et al. 2022; Ali et al. 2024). Given that rice straw (RS) contains significant amounts of cellulose and hemicellulose, it is one of those plentiful renewable lignocellulosic wastes with the potential to produce bioethanol (Ashoor et al. 2023; Ningthoujam et al. 2023). However, this lignocellulosic biomass has a highly complex structure composed of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin, which limits the efficiency of its hydrolysis into fermentable sugars for bioethanol production (Areepak et al., 2022). Therefore, biomass pretreatment is a critical step for breaking down the complex structure of lignocellulosic materials, thereby improving conversion efficiency and enhancing bioethanol productivity (Jiradechakorn et al. 2023). Alkaline pretreatment is widely applied for lignin removal via the saponification process and can reduce cellulose polymerization by cleaving glycosidic bonds.

Additionally, it increases biomass porosity and swelling, thereby improving enzyme accessibility (Xu and Huang 2014; Alvarez-Gonzalez et al. 2025). Due to their safety, non-corrosive nature, and affordability, ammonia-based pretreatments are favored among alkaline agents, as they modify cellulose and hemicellulose structures, increase porosity, and remove or alter lignin (Pasha et al. 2022). Moreover, ammonia pretreatment removes lignin while preserving most hemicellulose, which improves enzymatic hydrolysis and alters cellulose crystallinity (Aftab et al. 2019; Pasha et al. 2022). Following pretreatment, cellulose can be hydrolyzed into sugars, which can then be fermented into bioethanol (Prajapati and Kango 2023). Using microorganisms to disintegrate polysaccharides and ferment concurrently is another affordable strategy known as simultaneous saccharification and fermentation, SSF (Brodeur et al. 2011). Fungi have sugar transporters that play a vital role in second-generation bioethanol production by hydrolyzing lignocellulosic Biomass, converting polysaccharides into fermentable simple sugars (Pereira et al. 2025). Then, Saccharomyces cerevisiae (baker’s yeast) was used to ferment the simple sugar into ethanol and carbon dioxide (Tse et al. 2021). To reduce the cost of biofuel production, the development of eco-friendly and economically feasible processes should be implemented on a large scale. Modern technologies increasingly incorporate nanotechnology as a promising approach to optimize biofuel production by maximizing output, reducing costs, and accelerating process development (Shahbaz et al. 2022). Nanoparticles are the most popular catalysts, as it has been shown that the ability of certain microorganisms to transfer electrons to acceptors is more efficient in their presence, particularly under anaerobic conditions (Beckers et al. 2013; Sanusi et al. 2019). Nanocatalysts such as silver nanoparticles, carbon nanotubes, magnetic nanoparticles, and metal oxides show promise for sustainable fuel production.

Additionally, immobilizing enzymes on these nanoparticles enhances the reusability of cellulases and other biocatalysts in biofuel production (Sarsan et al. 2023). This study focuses on low-cost and eco-friendly bioethanol production from ammonia-pretreated rice straw, investigating the effect of a CaO/Ag nanocatalyst on simultaneous saccharification and fermentation (SSF) using a cellulolytic Aspergillus strain alone or in co-culture with baker’s yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae), in the presence or absence of the nanocatalyst. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the impact of CaO/Ag on SSF using ammonia-pretreated rice straw as a low-cost substrate for the co-production of reducing sugars and bioethanol.

Materials and methods

Chemicals, media, microorganisms, and nanocatalysts

The chemicals used in this investigation include pure water, ammonium hydroxide solution, yeast-dextrose-agar (YDA, BIOLAB Inc., Hungary), and Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA, BIOLAB Inc., Hungary). A. terreus and S. cerevisiae were assayed in a current fermentative procedure. An Aspergillus strain was isolated from rice straw debris, and commercial fresh yeast was produced in the bakery. The amendment of the Aspergillus strain into this fermentative process could be beneficial as a cost-effective producer of cellulase. The CaO/Ag nanocatalyst employed in this investigation is recognized as an environmentally friendly heterogeneous nanocatalyst. It has been synthesized at a low cost using chicken eggshells and fenugreek leaf extract, as reported by El-sherif et al. (2023). Based on their study, this nanocatalyst facilitated the conversion of waste cooking oil (WCO) into biodiesel with fuel-quality attributes. The summarized design modulation of the current work is outlined in the graphical abstract, which outlines the integrated experimental design employed to assess the influence of the CaO/Ag nanocatalyst on bioethanol production from ammonia-pretreated rice straw. The diagram illustrates the entire workflow beginning with the collection and alkaline pretreatment of rice straw (RS) using ammonium hydroxide, followed by structural characterization using SEM/EDX to observe morphological and elemental changes. MIC assays were conducted to determine the inhibitory threshold of the CaO/Ag nanocatalyst on the tested fungi (A. terreus and S. cerevisiae). Subsequent steps involved simultaneous saccharification and fermentation (SSF) using S. cerevisiae and A. terreus either separately or in combination, with and without the nanocatalyst. The performance of each condition was evaluated by estimating reducing sugars via the DNS assay and analyzing fermentation products. The final output of this workflow is the production of bioethanol, with emphasis on understanding the dual catalytic and inhibitory roles of the nanocatalyst.

Isolation and inoculum preparation of Aspergillus and Saccharomyces

An Aspergillus strain was isolated from Egyptian rice straw-soil debris using serial dilution and then cultivated on Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA) amended with 350 mg/L rose bengal and 100 mg/L chloramphenicol. The plate incubation period was 7 days at 25 °C (Reynolds 2005).

Commercial fresh baker’s yeast (i.e., Saccharomyces) was cultivated on Sabouraud medium and then purified to obtain an axenic culture for alcoholic fermentations. Each microorganism isolate was then grown on YDA. Fungal-purified slants were then incubated for 7 days at 25 °C and preserved as a stock culture at 4 °C until use.

MIC determination of cao/ag nanocatalyst upon testing fungi

Different concentrations of the tested sample from CaO/Ag (1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, …, 10 µg/ml) were applied onto central wells of agar plates inoculated with tested fungi (Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Aspergillus terreus) that were seeded into SDA. A turbidity test on the broth medium confirmed the data from the agar diffusion method. Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values were determined as the lowest concentration of nanocatalyst that inhibited the fungal growth after 7 days of incubation at 37 °C.

MIC determinations were conducted at 37 °C in accordance with the standard antifungal susceptibility testing (CLSI M27-A3), which specifies incubation at 35–37 °C to ensure reliable growth of the test microorganisms (Pfaller and Diekema 2010). Microbial growth was calculated by measuring the diameter of the inhibition area (Velmurugan et al. 2012). The tested nanoparticles in our treatments were applied with a value lower than the lowest MIC of both tested fungi during the following steps.

Rice straw preparations and characteristics

Before grinding, the rice straw (RS) obtained from a farm was cut into tiny fragments using scissors. It was then vacuum-dried for 1 h at 120 °C after passing through a 60-mesh sieve (Chen et al. 2012). The high temperature of 120 °C was used to dry and sterilize rice straw. A recent study reported using a comparable temperature (Karuna et al. 2014; Abdelhamed et al. 2024).

Previous studies have investigated the characteristics of rice straw (Chen et al. 2018; Sakhiya et al. 2021). After obtaining a smooth powder, the properties of rice straw have been investigated using compositional analysis. Following the procedure provided by Thiex et al. (2012), the ash content was determined at 550 °C for 6 h in a muffle furnace. The oven-dry method has been used to determine moisture content (Nirmaan et al. 2020). Moreover, the surface microstructure and elements distribution of the rice straw were investigated using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) coupled with an energy dispersive X-ray detector (EDX) (Abdel-Hamied et al. 2022), Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, (electromagnetic radiation with wavelengths is nearly 51–510 μm) with matter and x-ray diffraction (XRD, uses the height of the individual particles 2θ peaks in relation to each other) was used as structural analyses determination tools. SEM images were captured using a Zeiss LEO Supra 55VP Field Emission and a Zeiss 1530 SEM. Using the following equation, the crystallinity index of the rice straw was calculated using the peak intensity method (Li et al. 2012; Tsegaye et al. 2019).

The compositional characterization of rice straw was carried out using established analytical procedures commonly applied to lignocellulosic Biomass. Ash content was determined gravimetrically after combusting the samples at high temperatures, while moisture content was measured by oven-drying to a constant mass. These standardized methods are widely reported in the literature and allow reliable comparison of biomass composition data across bioethanol and biorefinery studies (Sun and Cheng 2002; Kumar et al. 2009; Sluiter et al. 2010).

Rice straw treatment by ammonium hydroxide

Rice straw has been chemically pretreated to upgrade cellulose and hemicellulose, and reduce lignin content, thereby increasing the production of reducing sugars necessary for ethanol production. In this context, a 10% ammonium hydroxide solution was used to treat the rice straw (Xu et al. 2024). At room temperature, the rice straw was soaked in a diluted solution of ammonium hydroxide for two days. The ratio of dry solid straw to ammonium solution was 1:15 w/v. After that, the rice straw was washed several times with tap water and distilled water until it reached neutrality. The washed rice straw was then dried in an oven at 60 °C. The previously indicated characterizations were repeated on the dried rice straw to investigate the outcomes of ammonium treatment. The pretreated solid portion of rice straw was subsequently employed as a carbon substrate for the microbial bioassay to reduce sugar and bioethanol production.

Saccharification and fermentation of ammonia-pretreated rice straw

The solid part of the RS pretreated with ammonium was used as a substrate for the bioassay to reduce sugar and ethanol production. There were six conical flasks, each containing 100 mL of sterile broth medium and 1 gram of dried, ammonium-pretreated RS. Afterward, two loops of S. cerevisiae and a 0.5 mm diameter disc of the Aspergillus strain were separately introduced into flasks. Notably, Saccharomyces sp. and Aspergillus sp. were tested individually and in simultaneous combinations; each was supplemented with the CaO/Ag nanocatalyst (3 µg/ml, below the MIC of the nanocatalyst towards both tested fungi). For comparison, a set of samples was maintained without adding the tested nanocatalyst (i.e., control) and consisted of three inoculated flasks. The flasks of the combined inocula of Saccharomyces sp. and Aspergillus sp., with and without NPs, were then incubated for 7 days in a rotary shaker at 120 rpm, at 25 ± 2 °C, and pH 4 to manage the fermentative process.

Fermentation was carried out at 25 °C under solid-state conditions, which is consistent with standard practice for filamentous fungi in SSF systems and allows for stable growth and enzyme production (Pandey et al. 2000; Singhania et al. 2010). The initial fermentation pH was set to 4. Although this value is lower than the optimum pH reported for many fungal cellulases (around pH 5), acidic conditions were selected to reduce microbial contamination and to maintain stable solid-state fermentation conditions. In integrated fungal bioprocesses, the operational pH is often chosen as a compromise between the enzyme’s optimal pH and overall process requirements. In this context, Antonov et al. (2017) demonstrated that although cellulases generally exhibit maximal activity near pH 5, acidic pH values (~ 3.7) are frequently applied in fungal bioprocesses to satisfy overall process constraints. Therefore, pH 4 was selected as a practical compromise for stable fermentation and enzyme production in this study.

Analytical measurements

Sample filtration was performed to separate the solid residue (i.e., rice straw with dry cell biomass of the grown microorganisms) from the aqueous filtrate containing fermentation products after the incubation period. The Biomass of microorganisms was measured in grams per dry weight. The separated aqueous filtrates were analyzed using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, 13033 Refractive Index Detectors) to determine the amounts of acetaldehyde, acetic acid, and ethyl alcohol. The dinitro salicylic acid (DNS) spectrophotometric test was performed to quantify the amount of reducing sugars, as reported by Rekha and Saravanathamizhan (2021).

DNA extraction and molecular identification of the bioethanol-producing isolate

Total genomic Aspergillus DNA was extracted using the DNeasy Genomic Extraction Kit (Qiagen, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. At the same time, S. cerevisiae yeast DNA extraction was performed as described by Sambrook et al. (2001). DNA from the plated mycelial extensions of the most potent microorganism in bioethanol production was obtained after the microorganism grew on potato dextrose Agar at 25 °C ± 2 °C for three days. Mycelial threads were recovered, centrifuged for approximately five minutes at 15,000 rpm, and carefully washed with 500 µL TE buffer. The resulting pellets were ground in a conical grinder with an optimized extraction buffer constituted: (25 mM EDTA, 200 mm Tris-HCl, 0.5% SDS, pH-8.5, 250 mM NaCl, 150 µl of 3 M sodium acetate to final adjustment of pH at 5.2 and then incubated the homogenate at 20 ͦ C for 10 min followed by centrifugation at 10000 rpm for 15 min. DNA was precipitated away from its supernatant by adding an equal volume of isopropanol. The resulting pellets were finally washed with 70% ethanol. The formed DNA pellets were carefully dried and then dissolved in 20 µL of TE buffer, which was stored as a buffer until subsequent use. DNA was diluted to 15 ng/µL for manipulation in the following sequencing procedure. ITS1 (5’TCCGTAGGTGAACCTTGCGG3’) and ITS4 (5’TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGC3’) primers were used in the PCR technique for the proper amplification of the target region of ITS, targeting the 5.8 S ribosomal RNA. The final PCR product was sequenced at Macrogen, Inc. (Seoul, Republic of Korea) using an ABI 3730xl DNA sequencer.

Statistical analysis

The data were collected in a standardized randomized system with three trial replicates. Subsequently, the data were subjected to an analysis of variance, and the means of values were compared using the IBM SPSS Statistics 27 program. Post hoc comparisons were conducted using the Least Significant Difference (LSD) test to analyze the differences between group means further. The data are represented graphically as the mean of three replicates ± standard deviation (SD).

Results and discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of incorporating CaO/Ag nanocatalysts in promoting or inhibiting the conversion of ammonia-pretreated RS into reducing sugars and bioethanol. Assessing the change in RS that underwent pretreatment is the first and most crucial step before the saccharification and fermentation procedures.

Silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) have exhibited activity against bacteria, fungi, and viruses (Arshad et al. 2024). In a study reported by Mallmann et al. (2015), green-synthesized AgNPs exhibited high activity against yeasts, including C. albicans and C. tropicalis, with a similar antimicrobial activity to that of antifungal amphotericin B. They may be considered an alternative protocol for treating fungal infections, including those caused by Aspergillus strains. Although the concentrations of CaO/Ag nanocatalyst applied during SSF were below the MIC values for Saccharomyces cerevisiae (3.5 µg/mL) and Aspergillus terreus (5.5 µg/mL), a reduction in bioethanol production and sugar utilization was observed. This suggests a sublethal inhibitory effect rather than complete microbial growth suppression. Silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) are known to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS), disrupt membrane integrity, and interfere with enzyme function and DNA replication even at concentrations beneath MIC thresholds (Pal et al. 2007; Kim and Ryu 2013; Rai et al. 2009). For instance, Lee et al. (2019) demonstrated that AgNPs induced ROS-mediated metabolic impairment in fungal cells, leading to reduced glucose uptake and delayed cell cycle progression. Similarly, AgNPs have been reported to cause lipid peroxidation, thiol group binding, and DNA damage, which impair essential metabolic processes (Morones et al. 2005; Sharma et al. 2009). These mechanisms likely explain the observed antagonistic effects on fermentation, despite maintained microbial viability. Thus, MIC determinations alone do not account for metabolic disruptions occurring at sub-MIC levels, which must be considered when optimizing nanocatalyst concentration.

To contextualize our findings within the existing research on nanocatalysts in fermentation, we compared our results with those of similar studies. Silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) have been reported to reduce ethanol yields and increase organic acid byproducts when applied during microbial fermentation, primarily due to the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) that impair cellular metabolism even at sublethal concentrations (Pal et al. 2007; Rai et al. 2009). Specifically, Lee and Jun (2019) demonstrated that AgNPs induce ROS-mediated metabolic stress in fungal and yeast cells, leading to reduced sugar uptake and fermentation efficiency. Unlike previous studies, our work explicitly investigates the co-culture of A. terreus and S. cerevisiae in simultaneous saccharification and fermentation (SSF) using a CaO/Ag nanocatalyst. It uniquely identifies sub-MIC metabolic inhibition and byproduct accumulation as key factors limiting ethanol yield. This comparison underscores the novelty of our approach and highlights both alignment and divergence with previous observations, reinforcing the broader applicability and distinct contribution of our findings.

Rice straw changes following alkaline pretreatment

Rice straw’s composition

Rice straw preparations are crucial for balancing the drying conditions of rice straw to enhance chemical treatment efficiency while preventing excessive degradation of its structural components (Remli et al. 2014). Additionally, the chosen drying conditions (vacuum-dried for 1 h at 120 °C) effectively remove moisture without significantly altering the chemical structure of rice straw, which is composed of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin (Aski et al. 2019; Nakyp et al. 2024). Thus, this method ensures that the feedstock retains its key components necessary for biofuel production while preventing excessive thermal degradation.

Regarding the chemical compositions of untreated raw and pretreated rice straw, the results are listed in Table 2. According to the findings, the rice straw’s composition before treatment was as follows: 38.88% cellulose, 24.34% hemicellulose, 3.28% acid detergent lignin, 16.46% ash, and 5.78% moisture. Previous studies have investigated the characteristics of rice straw (Chen et al. 2018; Sakhiya et al. 2021). The developmental level and environmental variables during growth affect the constituent contents of rice straw (Van Hung et al. 2020). After ammonium hydroxide pretreatment, the relative cellulose content in rice straw increased from 38.88%% to 55.87%, while the hemicellulose content decreased from 24.2% to 21.72%.

Ash content affects biofuel conversion in a limiting manner (Kumari and Singh 2022); however, it can be improved through treatment. The following is evidence of the compelling effect of ammonia treatment in this study. As an inorganic material, the fraction of silica-containing ash drops from 16.46% to 7.33%. The accessibility of saccharifying and fermenting microorganisms, as well as the increase in cellulosic fiber content, were affected by this reduction in ash (Takano and Hoshino 2018).

Nonetheless, there is no meaningful change in the lignin content following treatment. Regardless of the lignin content in the processed rice straw, improving the cellulose fraction is a positive outcome. This increase does not indicate a net gain in cellulose, but rather a concentration effect resulting from the partial removal of hemicellulose, which is consistent with the outcomes of alkaline pretreatment. Similar patterns are widely reported in the literature, where compositional shifts reflect changes in biomass structure (Sun and Cheng 2002).

Kumar et al. (2020) reported comparable results regarding the increase in cellulose content (42.9 to 46.3%). As such, authors found that RS pretreated with ammonia, compared to other alkalis, with a 10% concentration identified as the optimum, yielded a high amount of reducing sugars after enzymatic hydrolysis. Given the significance of efficiently exploiting these biomass polysaccharides to produce ethanol, this study suggests that 10% ammonia pretreatment achieves acceptable outcomes with minimal hemicellulose loss.

Chemical treatment is one of the most vital steps to alter the composition and structure of botanical Biomass, which can also be effective in the removal of lignin and hemicellulose (Samsalee et al. 2023) Alkali treatment is the most abundant method due to its potentiality in increasing cellulose accessibility and lignin removal efficiency (Kim et al. 2016) The pretreatment process also cause an increase in the degree of crystallinity due to the swelling of lignocellulose material that leads to the elimination of amorphous components (Samsalee et al. 2023).

The ammonia-pretreated rice straw (Figs. 1C, D) acquired a significantly chaotic, rough, and scratchy surface structure in the current work. The SEM observations in this study are consistent with those of previous studies (Tsegaye et al. 2019; Kumar et al. 2020). The SEM examination by Kumar et al. (2020) revealed structural breakdown, characterized by fissures in the lignin and an inconsistent surface, in rice straw that had undergone ammonia pretreatment. Consequently, the saccharification process can be significantly enhanced by the activity of microorganisms and their enzymes using ammonia-pretreated RS, in which the cross-linked fibers have been disassembled.

Concerning the surface elemental distribution of untreated and ammonia-pretreated RS (Fig. 1A and B), an overview is provided in the Tables shown in the inset. Following the ammonia pretreatment, the surface of the RS underwent variations in which the C, Mg, and Si elements increased in the following proportions: (49.7% to 54.3%), (0.56% to 1%), (0.9% to 4.37%), and (46.86% to 42.53%), respectively. It is also noted that the following percentages have dropped on the surface of untreated RS: O (51.36% to 47.89%), AL (1.14% to 0.47%), and Ca (0.82% to 0.36%). Furthermore, following ammonia processing, several elements on the surface of RS disappeared (Fig. 1A, with a star symbol beside). These findings closely resemble the EDX analysis of highly alkaline petha wastewater (PWW)-pretreated RS reported by Kumari and Singh (2022).

Of all these, the most significant is the observed enhancement in the C element content on the surface of RS after ammonia pretreatment, which aligns with the results mentioned above (Table 1) of an increase in cellulose percentage (38.88% in RS to 55.87%).

The most noteworthy observation in the present study is the correlation between the above-mentioned results of raising the cellulose percentage (from 38.88% in RS to 55.87%) and the noticed rise in the C element content on the surface of RS following ammonia treatments. Nevertheless, there is some debate on the increase in Silica. According to Van Soest (2006), one possible reason for this silica increase is that ammonia only splits the silicified cuticular section of paddy straw, which is inadequate to dissolve the Silica. Furthermore, Kaur and Phutela (2014) also noted an increase in silica content due to ammonia pretreatment of paddy straw, attributed to the disintegration and generation of unconstrained Silica.

In some cases, Silica may be reprecipitated during the washing or drying step onto the fiber surface, primarily if a strong alkaline medium is not used to dissolve it completely. Abu Bakar et al. (2020) demonstrated that Silica dissolves in an alkaline medium. This implies that the treatment with ammonia was weak in dissolving Silica. It is noteworthy that biomass pretreatment can reduce overall ash content through the elimination of particular inorganic constituents (Lei et al. 2016). The chemically stable Silica, on the other hand, may either remain unaffected or redeposit during treatment, increasing its relative proportion.

Furthermore, Awoyale et al. (2021) indicated that pretreatment alters the elemental composition of biomass ash by selectively removing soluble minerals such as K, Ca, and Mg, whereas SiO₂ tends to remain in the solid residue. Rice straw’s chemical functional groups by FTIR

FTIR spectroscopy was employed in the wavenumber range of 400–4000 cm–1 to detect alterations in the chemical functional groups of RS following ammonia pretreatment (Fig. 4). Many functional groups existed in the cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin structures of the RS. The FT-IR spectrum is often used to analyze the structures and chemical bonding of lignocellulose.

However, the treated and untreated RS showed similar FTIR patterns; the pretreatment sample exhibited some bands with higher transmittance percentages (i.e., less absorbance). Also, the ammonia pretreatment caused the pretreated RS spectrum to lose some functional groups’ bands. The transmittance of the C-H stretching vibration at around 2916 cm− 1 increased by 10.52% compared to the untreated RS. Notably, this peak may have a role in defining the crystalline extension of cellulose (Tarchoun et al. 2019; Hartati et al. 2021).

According to Bhattacharyya et al. (2020), the aliphatic esters in lignin and hemicelluloses are responsible for the shoulder peak at 1726 cm− 1 in the spectrum of untreated RS. The C = O stretch of carboxylic acids, esters, and aldehydes explains the peak around 1730 cm ¹. This peak is related to hemicellulose (especially acetyl groups) and lignin, which are crucial in rice straw.

A noticeable shift to 1730 cm− 1 in the ammonia-pretreated RS spectrum, along with a slight increase in transmittance, suggests the breakdown of ester-linked molecules between hemicellulose and lignin. As a result of ester bond breakage, lignin or hemicellulose is partially eliminated from RS after pretreatment by ammonia (Phitsuwan et al. 2017). A peak in the region 1300–1000 cm¹ showed (C-O stretching, C-O-C stretching). However, this region, which represents C-O stretching in cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin, is informative. Changes in this region, particularly around 1000 cm ¹, can indicate alterations in cellulose content or crystallinity that might indicate the presence of pure cellulose (Lu et al. 2014; Phitsuwan et al. 2017; Liu et al. 2022).

Moreover, the syringyl moiety functional group (1373 cm− 1) of lignin is more apparent in the spectra of untreated RS (Bhattacharyya et al. 2020). Most notably, lignin can be identified by its characteristic C-H deformation bands of methoxyl groups (2850 cm-1, 1476 cm-1, 1430 cm-1) and an aromatic skeletal stretching band (1625 cm− 1).

The broad peak in the region (3400–3200 cm¹) indicates the presence of hydroxyl groups (O-H) in cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin, and N-H groups from the ammonia pretreatment. The reduction in the intensity of this peak in PRS compared to RS could suggest a decrease in hydrogen bonding or a change in the hydroxyl group content due to the pretreatment (Binod et al. 2012; Phitsuwan et al.,2017). However, the presence of N-H from ammonia might complicate the interpretation in this region. The peak around 2900 cm⁻¹ is declared (C-H stretch). Changes in this region might indicate alterations in the aliphatic side chains of cellulose and lignin (Phitsuwan et al. 2017).

Further transmittance is exhibited by these letter bands in the spectrum of ammonia-pretreated RS, implying that pretreated RS might have undergone a lignin content reduction (Shen et al. 2018; Ma et al., 2022). This conclusion contradicts the quantitative investigation of the chemical composition of pretreated RS in terms of lignin content (Table 1). Compositional analyses are typically expressed on a percentage basis, which reflects the relative proportions of each component within the total Biomass. As a result, when specific components such as hemicellulose or extractives are significantly reduced through pretreatment, other components—like lignin—may appear to increase in relative percentage, even if their absolute content remains the same or decreases slightly. This apparent increase is a mathematical artifact of normalization and should not be interpreted as a genuine accumulation of lignin.

On the other hand, a noteworthy result that aligns with the chemical composition analysis and the study reported by Boonsombuti et al. (2020) is that the pretreated RS contained more cellulose but less hemicellulose than the untreated RS. This is evidenced by the bands’ heightened transmittance at 1050 cm− 1 and 1240 cm− 1. The ammonia pretreatment process changes the cellulose structure, sometimes increasing its crystallinity. The peak at 900 cm− 1 is also attributed to β−1,4-glycosidic linkages, revealing the typical cellulose structure (Phitsuwan et al. 2017). It is a peak for amorphous cellulose’s antisymmetric out-of-plane ring stretch (Liu et al. 2022). Rice straw’s XRD and crystallinity index

The XRD pattern of both the untreated and ammonia-pretreated RS is shown in Fig. 4, with scales ranging from 5–35°/2θ degrees in increments of 0.02°/2θ. The untreated RS shows just two peaks; one is at 8.4° and moderately intense, while the second peak has a high intensity and is located at 20°. According to Ningthoujam et al. (2023), these results suggest the presence of amorphous compounds containing carbon, including lignin, hemicellulose, and cellulose. Once the RS was pretreated with ammonia, the moderate-intensity peak at 8.4° on the XRD pattern shifted and was instead observed at 5°. Furthermore, the high-intensity peak became apparent at 22° with improved sharpness. The noteworthy observation is that two more peaks are noticed within the pretreated RS (PRS) XRD pattern. One of these peaks is moderately intense, located at 16°, while the other is extremely small, at 34°. Little elements of mineral origin (e.g., Silica) may be responsible for the small peak at 34° in the pattern of pretreated RS (Bettaieb et al. 2015; Ningthoujam et al. 2023). The four peaks (5°, 16°, 22°, and 34°) detected in the PRS XRD pattern correspond to planes with Miller indices of 011, 002, and 004, respectively. It is evident from the XRD patterns that RS crystallinity was enhanced after ammonia treatment. Considering the presence of the peak at 16°, it is reasonable to believe the 22° high-intensity peak might exhibit duplication. Furthermore, the four peaks shown in the PRS XRD pattern, with particular emphasis on the duplex of the main, can be interpreted as indicating the presence of both cellulose I (crystalline) and II (amorphous) (Tarchoun et al. 2019). Consistent with the results of the current study, Ningthoujam et al. (2023) reported that pretreating RS with NaOH exposed the covered active sites, leading to the development of crystalline cellulose.Fig. 4X-ray diffractograms of untreated RS (right scale) and ammonia-pretreated RS (PRS, left scale). Concerning crystallinity index (CrI) percentages computed from the XRD patterns, the ammonia-pretreated RS exhibits a higher crystallinity index (CrI = 84.8%) compared to the untreated RS sample (CrI = 59.3%). Considering all of the characterization findings and evidence described in this study, it can be inferred that the lignin and hemicellulose underwent significant degradation following the ammonia pretreatment of RS.

Both A. terreus fungus-inoculated batches, regardless of the presence of the nanocatalyst, exhibited no ethanol yield. Given that Aspergillus is known to produce cellulase, these results align with the purpose of its inoculation. A. terreus is generally inactive in ethanol production; however, the production of acetic acid in the presence of CaO/Ag nanocatalysts suggests alternative metabolic pathways.

On the other hand, the CaO/Ag nanocatalyst-supplied batches with S. cerevisiae and A. terreus co-inoculation demonstrated a bioethanol yield of 0.285 ± 0.009%, which was significantly (P < 0.05) higher compared to control batches without a nanocatalyst (0.049 ± 0.003%). This latter result aligns with the above-mentioned results regarding the amount of reducing sugars in these two samples. Bioethanol production was negligible (0.049 ± 0.003%) in the batch with co-inoculation of S. cerevisiae and A. terreus, whereas it was markedly high (1.013 ± 0.038%) in batches with S. cerevisiae yeast inoculation alone. The discrepancy between the high reducing sugar concentration generated by A. terreus and the relatively low bioethanol yield in co-inoculated batches can be attributed to microbial antagonism and metabolic inhibition. The co-presence of A. terreus and S. cerevisiae may lead to competitive or inhibitory interactions that limit yeast fermentation capacity (Persons et al. 2013). Furthermore, accumulation of fermentation byproducts such as acetaldehyde and acetic acid, which were detected in these batches, can exert toxic effects on S. cerevisiae and reduce ethanol synthesis efficiency (Aranda and del Olmo 2004; Arroyo-López et al. 2010). These findings underscore that the mere presence of fermentable sugars does not guarantee ethanol production, as microbial dynamics and stressors from nanomaterials or byproducts critically affect the fermentation outcome.

The inoculation of S. cerevisiae yeast with A. terreus fungus may induce an antagonistic effect on bioethanol production, potentially elucidating the diminished yield of bioethanol in batches co-inoculated with both fungus and yeast. Carbon is diverted to organic acids by A. terreus, while glucose is mainly converted to ethanol by S. cerevisiae. Competing pathways may be present in mixed cultures, causing a division of carbon flux that produces moderate yields of ethanol and high yields of acid.

According to the HPLC results (Table 3), the undesirable byproduct acetic acid was detected in all batches that were inoculated with S. cerevisiae + A. terreus, A. terreus fungus supplemented with nanocatalyst, and S. cerevisiae + A. terreus supplemented with nanocatalyst. Bioethanol production is negatively affected by acetic acid, which is toxic to sugar-fermenting yeast (Chakraborty et al. 2024). Although there was no notable difference in acetic acid percentages between the batches inoculated with S. cerevisiae + A. terreus and those supplemented with CaO/Ag nanocatalyst, it is noteworthy that bioethanol production was enhanced when S. cerevisiae and A. terreus were co-inoculated and supplied with the nanocatalyst.

Amira et al. (2023) reported that immobilized S. cerevisiae increased ethanol production from wheat bran and coffee pulp compared to free cells. In the case of immobilized S. cerevisiae cells, the concentration of ethanol was increased steadily with increasing fermentation time and attained a maximum of 71.39 mg after 72 h of fermentation, compared with the amount of ethanol formed with either A. flavus or A. niger. The current work aligns with previous results, as the best ethanol production was achieved with S. cerevisiae, which recorded 1.013 ± 0.038%, exceeding the other batches.

Based on these findings, the saccharification and fermentation process in the same batch impacted S. cerevisiae’s ability to ferment the reduced sugars in the presence of the A. terreus fungus, resulting in reduced bioethanol production. Mixed cultures (S. cerevisiae and A. terreus) showed slightly higher ethanol in the CaO/Ag nanocatalyst batch. However, a large portion of sugars still favored growth or acid production, not ethanol.

Overall, the nanocatalyst increased the release of reducing sugars but did not consistently improve ethanol production. This suggests possible bottlenecks in fermentation, potentially caused by oxidative conditions or metabolic shifts.

Among the tested batches, S. cerevisiae fermentation without CaO/Ag nanocatalyst resulted in the highest bioethanol production, indicating it as the most effective condition under the present experimental setup.

Conclusion

In conclusion, economical and environmentally friendly nano-based sccarification and fermentation methods were successfully compared in this study to determine how the utilized CaO/Ag nanocatalyst affects the yield of reducing sugars and bioethanol from a commercially available lignocellulosic waste product, namely rice straw. The ammoni-pretreated rice straw was thoroughly examined for its compositional, surface morphological, chemical functional groups, and physical properties using advanced characterization techniques like composition %, SEM-EDX, FTIR, and XRD. The findings validated the efficacy of the 10% ammonia pretreatment, demonstrating favorable results. These included a notable increase in the relative cellulose content of rice straw, an enhancement in the carbon element content, distinct surface morphologies for treated rice straw, and an improved crystallinity index.

The utilized CaO/Ag nanocatalyst appears to stimulate the growth of the molecularly identified A. terreus (At PP590607), while it marginally hinders the growth of S. cerevisiae (Sc OR668931). This means the two fungal species, S. cerevisiae and A. terreus, were affected differently by the utilized CaO/Ag nanocatalyst. Given the marked improvement in sugar reduction in batches inoculated with S. cerevisiae in the presence of the nano-catalyst compared to the control batches, this study suggests the potential use of CaO/Ag nanoparticles as an additional chemical treatment or hydrolysis step. Most importantly, this study also concludes that S. cerevisiae was either slightly inhibited or exposed to a new metabolic pathway by means of the CaO/Ag nanocatalyst. This was supported by three HPLC findings: first, a modest reduction in yeast biomass; second, the ethanol concentration was about half the yield observed in the absence of the nanocatalyst; and third, a substantially elevated amount of acetaldehyde, an intermediate product, was detected in batches employing both S. cerevisiae and the nanocatalyst. This may indicate that the CaO/Ag nanocatalyst used here slows down fermentation in batches where both S. cerevisiae and the nanocatalyst are used.

Overall, the results presented in this study open the door to future discussions on optimizing the production of bioethanol and reducing sugar using the CaO/Ag nanocatalyst. Additionally, it is crucial to thoroughly investigate how the two fungal isolates interact with each other and with the environmentally friendly CaO/Ag nanocatalyst, particularly concerning their influence on reducing sugar and bioethanol production.
