Cognitive differences and ethical concerns in artificial intelligence in healthcare: a comparative text mining study of public and healthcare professional discussions
Authors: Yucheng Cao, Zeyu Peng, Xu Deng, Yang Tang, Zhixian Feng, Yu Gao, Lili Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aims to investigate the cognitive differences and ethical concerns regarding artificial intelligence in healthcare between the public and healthcare professionals and to provide empirical evidence for ethical governance and risk communication.MethodsA text mining approach was adopted to comparatively analyze Chinese public social media and professional medical forums. A total of 4,042 valid texts were included, comprising 2,088 from the public corpus and 1,954 from the healthcare professional corpus. SnowNLP sentiment analysis, the Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) topic model, and co-word network analysis were applied to reconstruct and compare the structures of concern between the two groups.ResultsBoth groups were dominated by positive sentiment toward artificial intelligence in healthcare, although healthcare professionals showed a higher positive tendency (75.2%) than the public (68.0%), while the public exhibited a higher degree of caution. LDA topic analysis and co-word network analysis showed that both groups focused heavily on the role positioning and replacement boundaries of artificial intelligence. However, their specific focal concerns differed significantly. Healthcare professionals placed greater emphasis on occupational impact and practice-related concerns, mainly involving clinical application, responsibility, and humanistic care. In contrast, the public focused more on technical conditions and risk perception, emphasizing practical uses in healthcare settings, the reliability of data and models, and issues of responsibility and risk.ConclusionAlthough both the public and healthcare professionals hold relatively positive attitudes toward artificial intelligence in healthcare, their cognitive structures exhibit clear group differences. The subsequent ethical governance and advancement of artificial intelligence in healthcare require clarifying its functional boundaries while simultaneously addressing the practice-related concerns of healthcare professionals and the risk-related concerns of the public.Keywords: Artificial intelligence in healthcare, Ethical concerns, Cognitive differences, Text mining, Topic modeling, Public, Healthcare professionals

Introduction

As artificial intelligence (AI) gradually penetrates healthcare scenarios such as disease screening, medical imaging recognition, assistance in diagnosis, clinical decision support, and process management, it has become an important component of the digital transformation of healthcare [1]. In response to this trend, existing studies have mostly focused on the technological potential of artificial intelligence in improving efficiency, optimizing resource allocation, and providing assistance in precise diagnosis and treatment [2]; however, given the inherent characteristics of healthcare settings, including high risk, high responsibility, and low tolerance for error, discussions surrounding artificial intelligence in healthcare cannot remain limited to a single dimension of technological performance evaluation, but must extend to the levels of cognition, evaluation, and acceptance in real-world diagnosis and treatment settings [3]. Furthermore, the application of artificial intelligence in medical practice often involves both technological feasibility and ethical complexity, which concerns not only the implementation of the technology, but also deeply involves multidimensional core issues such as role boundaries, attribution of responsibility, data security, and the protection of patient rights and interests [4].

Regarding these issues, existing studies have explored the attitudes, acceptance, risk perception, and ethical controversies of the public or healthcare professionals toward artificial intelligence in healthcare from different perspectives, but certain limitations remain in terms of research perspectives and the treatment of study populations [5, 6]. For example, the study by Dingel et al. mainly focused on technology acceptance and intention to use, and largely interpreted social responses to artificial intelligence in healthcare from the perspective of adoption intention, which to some extent compressed the multidimensional content of cognitive evaluation in healthcare contexts, including risk judgment, responsibility attribution, and value trade-offs [7]. The study by Cao et al. further addressed ethical issues such as privacy protection, safety, algorithmic fairness, responsibility allocation, and humanistic care, thereby broadening the understanding of ethical concerns regarding artificial intelligence in healthcare; however, overall, it still paid relatively limited attention to the structural differences in focal concerns among different stakeholders from a comparative group perspective [6]. Against this background, current evidence still cannot clearly define the commonalities and substantive differences between the public and healthcare professionals in their focus on artificial intelligence in healthcare [8].

In fact, the public and healthcare professionals play fundamentally different roles in the healthcare ecosystem, which determines inherent differences in the dimensions through which they evaluate technology and in their core concerns [9]. Specifically, the public usually bases its judgments on diagnosis and treatment outcomes, safety, and social effects, placing greater emphasis on the reliability of the technology and the potential real-world risks it may bring [10]; in contrast, healthcare professionals, as practitioners in clinical settings, focus more on how artificial intelligence is embedded in diagnosis and treatment processes, the standards for defining responsibility, and its specific impact on existing workflows [11, 12]. Therefore, systematically comparing the cognitive differences and ethical concerns between these two groups not only helps translate macro-level ethical controversies into identifiable and addressable practical issues, but also provides solid empirical support for the development of precise ethical governance frameworks and risk communication strategies [13].

In light of this, this study takes the public and healthcare professionals as the comparative dimension and aims to conduct an in-depth analysis of their cognitive differences and ethical concerns regarding artificial intelligence in healthcare. To achieve this goal, this study introduces text mining and comprehensively applies methods including SnowNLP sentiment analysis, the LDA topic model, and semantic network analysis to reconstruct the dominant structures of concern in the two groups. On this basis, this study focuses on two core questions: first, what major cognitive themes are formed by the public and healthcare professionals, respectively, regarding artificial intelligence in healthcare; second, what differences exist in the focal ethical concerns of the two groups. Ultimately, the findings of this study are intended to provide solid empirical evidence for the ethical governance of artificial intelligence in healthcare and related communication.

Methods

This study adopts text mining to conduct a comparative analysis of Chinese-language corpora related to artificial intelligence in healthcare on domestic internet platforms. Based on the user-group attributes of the platforms, this study constructs two parallel comparative corpora, namely the “public” corpus and the “healthcare professional” corpus. Specifically, the public corpus is mainly collected from mass social media and question-and-answer platforms such as Weibo, Xiaohongshu, and Zhihu, whereas the healthcare professional corpus is extracted from professional medical forums such as iiyi and Dingxiangyuan, whose core users consist of physicians, nurses, and other clinical practitioners. To ensure the reliability and validity of the comparative analysis, both corpora follow standardized data processing and analytical procedures, including data collection, text cleaning, Chinese word segmentation, time-series analysis, SnowNLP-based sentiment analysis, LDA (Latent Dirichlet Allocation) topic modeling, and semantic network analysis. Ultimately, by systematically quantifying and comparing the characteristic differences between the two corpora in terms of sentiment polarity, latent thematic structure, and semantic association networks, this study aims to provide an in-depth analysis of the cognitive differentiation between the public and healthcare professionals regarding artificial intelligence in healthcare and their core ethical concerns.In addition, the analytical code and preprocessing procedures used in this study have been organized and archived to ensure the verifiability of the research process.

Text mining analysis

Data sources and preprocessing

To explore public views on the ethics of artificial intelligence in healthcare, the research team collected publicly visible texts from multiple platforms, with data collection completed on March 11, 2026. Specifically, data from Weibo, Zhihu, and Xiaohongshu were scraped using a program written in Python 3.8.9, whereas data from the two medical forums, iiyi and Dingxiangyuan, were manually collected after webpage searches. In terms of the search strategy, in order to improve the comprehensiveness and completeness of the corpus, this study primarily used ethics-oriented terms in combination with basic search terms related to artificial intelligence in healthcare settings. The keywords included “AI healthcare,” “AI seeking medical care,” “AI physician,” “AI consultation,” “intelligent consultation,” “AI diagnosis,” “AI misdiagnosis,” “AI healthcare privacy,” “AI healthcare data security,” “AI replacement of physicians,” “Is AI safe for seeking medical care?”, and “Is AI physician reliable?”. This strategy was intended to collect, as broadly as possible, ethical expressions related to artificial intelligence in healthcare across different platforms, especially discussions surrounding core issues such as safety, privacy, data security, responsibility attribution, and role boundaries.

The initial numbers of texts collected from each platform and the numbers retained after the first round of deduplication were as follows: 1,207 texts were collected from Weibo, of which 632 were retained after deduplication; 1,344 texts were collected from Xiaohongshu, of which 670 were retained after deduplication; 2,642 texts were collected from Zhihu, of which 1,908 were retained after deduplication; 147 texts were collected from iiyi, with no duplicates; and 1,869 texts were collected from Dingxiangyuan, with no duplicates. After the first round of deduplication, a total of 5,226 Chinese texts were retained, including 3,210 texts in the public corpus and 2,016 texts in the healthcare professional corpus. On this basis, a second round of manual cleaning was conducted. After removing texts unrelated to the research topic, texts containing only symbols, or texts lacking substantive semantic information, the final sample included in the analysis consisted of 2,088 texts in the public corpus and 1,954 texts in the healthcare professional corpus, for a total of 4,042 texts.

Word segmentation, word frequency, and sentiment analysis

After preprocessing was completed, this study used jieba in Python to segment the Chinese texts and, in combination with a stop-word list, removed high-frequency function words, modal particles, and other functional words without substantive analytical meaning. On this basis, a word-frequency matrix was constructed to calculate word frequencies, and the top 50 high-frequency words in the public corpus and the healthcare professional corpus were extracted separately to describe the distribution of core vocabulary in discussions of artificial intelligence in healthcare in the two corpora. Sentiment analysis was conducted in Python using SnowNLP, with each individual comment or post serving as the basic unit of analysis. SnowNLP generated a sentiment score for each text, ranging from 0 to 1, with higher scores indicating a more positive orientation and lower scores indicating a more negative orientation. In this study, 0.4 to 0.6 was defined as the neutral interval: scores below 0.4 were classified as negative, scores above 0.6 were classified as positive, and scores between 0.4 and 0.6 were classified as neutral [14, 15]. Considering that sarcasm, negative transitions, and context-dependent expressions are present in social media texts and may affect the accuracy of automated sentiment recognition, this study conducted a manual review of a small number of texts with ambiguous boundaries or obvious misclassifications on the basis of machine classification. The manual review was conducted independently by two researchers, and inter-rater agreement was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient (0.85), which indicated good agreement. For texts with discrepancies, consensus was reached through discussion, and the final classification results were determined accordingly. Based on the final classification results, the distributions of positive, neutral, and negative sentiments in the public corpus and the healthcare professional corpus were calculated separately to compare the overall attitudinal tendencies of the two groups of texts toward artificial intelligence in healthcare.

Topic modeling

To identify potential discussion themes in the public corpus and the healthcare professional corpus, this study conducted LDA topic modeling separately for the two corpora in a Python environment. Text preprocessing included jieba-based Chinese word segmentation, the import of a custom dictionary, stop-word removal, synonym merging, and the removal of blank words, words with a length of fewer than two characters, and texts containing fewer than two words after segmentation [16]. These procedures referred to commonly used workflows in previous Chinese text mining studies and were intended to improve the quality of word segmentation and reduce noise interference [17]. Subsequently, gensim was used to construct the dictionary and bag-of-words corpus, and low-frequency and high-frequency words were removed through filter_extremes, with the parameters set as no_below = 5, no_above = 0.8, and keep_n = 5000. These parameters were determined with reference to commonly used settings in previous studies and were standardized according to the corpus size of this study to improve the stability of topic extraction [18]. For the selection of the number of topics, model performance under different numbers of topics was compared separately, with the candidate range set from 2 to 10. The model parameters were uniformly set as passes = 10, iterations = 500, minimum_probability = 0, alpha="auto”, and eta="auto”, and random_state = 42 was specified to improve the reproducibility of the results [18]. Model evaluation took both perplexity and coherence into account, with coherence calculated using the c_v metric; on this basis, the optimal number of topics was further determined by combining the aggregation of topic keywords and semantic interpretability [19]. The public corpus and the healthcare professional corpus followed the same analytical procedure, while the optimal number of topics was determined separately according to the characteristics of each corpus. Finally, the top 20 high-probability keywords for each topic were extracted, and the topics were named and interpreted in light of their semantic connotations.

Co-word network analysis

To further characterize the co-occurrence relationships and semantic organizational structures among core words in the public corpus and the healthcare professional corpus, this study constructed co-word networks based on the top 50 high-frequency words in each corpus. Network construction used each individual text as the unit of co-occurrence and applied a binary co-occurrence method to calculate the co-occurrence relationships between words: if two words appeared in the same text, this was counted as one co-occurrence; repeated occurrences of the same word within a single text were counted only once to avoid the excessive influence of repeated word use on co-occurrence strength [20]. On this basis, a symmetric word co-occurrence matrix was generated, and the network was constructed using co-occurrence frequency as the edge weight. The same preprocessing and network construction procedures were applied to both corpora to ensure the comparability of the results. Regarding network indicators, this study calculated node degree, weighted degree, degree centrality, closeness centrality, betweenness centrality, eigenvector centrality, PageRank, and clustering coefficient to identify core words located at the center of the network, bridging words connecting different semantic clusters, and the overall aggregation characteristics of the network [21]. The interpretation of the results mainly focused on the central positions of core words, the connections among words, and the overall aggregation and organizational structure of the network.

Strategy for integrating results

After independently completing the analyses of the public corpus and the healthcare professional corpus, this study further conducted an integrated comparison at two levels, namely LDA topic analysis and co-word network analysis. Specifically, this included comparing the main topics identified by the topic models of the two corpora and their focal points, comparing the core words, major connections, and overall organizational structures in the co-word networks of the two corpora, and on this basis summarizing the common concerns and key differences between the public and healthcare professionals regarding artificial intelligence in healthcare, thereby forming an integrated interpretation of the results.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study used publicly available text data from online platforms and did not involve direct interaction with individuals or access to identifiable private information. Given the non-interventional nature of the study, formal ethical approval and informed consent were not required. All data were analyzed in an aggregated and anonymized manner, in accordance with ethical principles for internet-based research.

Results

Time-series analysis

To present the changing trends in the number of comments on artificial intelligence in healthcare among the public and healthcare professionals during the study period, a descriptive analysis of the time-series distribution of annual comment counts was conducted. The results showed (see Fig. 1) that public comments appeared earlier, with related discussions already emerging in 2015; however, the overall level remained low from 2015 to 2021, with annual comment counts fluctuating between 7 and 36, including slight increases in 2017 and 2019 and a slight decline in 2020–2021. Since 2022, the number of public comments has increased markedly, further rising to 204 in 2023, remaining basically stable in 2024 (198 comments), and rapidly climbing to a peak of 861 comments in 2025. The number of public comments in 2026 was 587; however, because these data were counted only through March 2026, they are not suitable for direct comparison with full-year data and should instead be understood as indicating that a relatively high level of discussion was still maintained by the time of data collection. In contrast, comments by healthcare professionals began to appear in 2016 and remained at an extremely low level from 2016 to 2022, with only 1–4 comments per year, increasing only slightly to 13 in 2023. Since 2024, they have entered a stage of rapid growth, rising to 163 comments in 2024 and increasing substantially to 816 in 2025; in 2026, despite being counted only through March, the number had already reached 942, indicating that the level of discussion among healthcare professionals increased more markedly in the later stage of the study. Overall, public comments showed a trend characterized by “early low-level fluctuations–mid-stage continuous increase–late-stage rapid growth,” whereas comments by healthcare professionals showed a trajectory of “long-term low level–late-stage concentrated growth.”

High-frequency word analysis results

The high-frequency word analysis showed that the top-ranking words in both the healthcare professional and public discussions included “artificial intelligence,” “physician,” “replacement,” “healthcare,” and “patient,” indicating that both groups focused on the relationship between artificial intelligence and physicians, healthcare applications, and patient-related issues, thus showing a certain degree of commonality (see Table 1). At the same time, the high-frequency words in the two groups also exhibited clear differences. In the healthcare professional corpus, words such as “assistance,” “cannot,” “will not,” “completely,” “clinical,” “diagnosis and treatment,” “tool,” and “analysis” appeared more frequently, suggesting that these discussions were more centered on the role of artificial intelligence in clinical practice, the boundaries of replacement, and its attributes as a tool. The inclusion of terms such as “bear the blame,” “unemployment,” and “humanistic care” in the high-frequency word list also reflected concern among healthcare professionals about responsibility, occupational impact, and the humanistic dimension of care. In contrast, in the public corpus, words such as “hospital,” “issue,” “model,” “DeepSeek,” “data,” “information,” “examination,” “responsibility,” and “risk” were more prominent, indicating that the public paid greater attention to the practical application of artificial intelligence in healthcare settings, the technological basis of its operation, and potential risks. Overall, discussions among healthcare professionals were more closely related to clinical practice and occupational circumstances, whereas public discussions were more oriented toward the effects of use and risk perception in healthcare application settings.

Sentiment analysis results

The sentiment analysis results showed that both groups were dominated by positive sentiment toward artificial intelligence in healthcare, although the positive tendency among healthcare professionals was slightly higher than that among the public (see Fig. 2). In the healthcare professional corpus, positive sentiment accounted for 75.2%, negative sentiment for 16.6%, and neutral sentiment for 8.2%; in the public corpus, positive sentiment accounted for 68.0%, negative sentiment for 21.2%, and neutral sentiment for 10.8%. In terms of group comparison, healthcare professionals showed a higher degree of positive attitudes toward artificial intelligence in healthcare, whereas the proportion of negative sentiment was relatively higher in the public corpus, suggesting that the public held a more cautious overall attitude toward artificial intelligence in healthcare. These results indicate that both groups recognized the potential value of artificial intelligence entering the healthcare field, but the public still retained more concerns about its practical application.

LDA topic analysis results for healthcare professionals

According to the model evaluation results, the 2-topic model showed the highest coherence (0.433) and the lowest perplexity (46.035), and its overall performance was better than that of the other topic-number settings. Therefore, adopting two topics for the subsequent analysis was considered more appropriate for this study (see Fig. 3; Table 2). This result suggested that the discussion structure in the healthcare professional corpus was relatively concentrated and that two topics were sufficient to summarize the main content effectively.

Distribution probabilities of LDA topic keywords among healthcare professionals

The representative words of Topic 1 included “artificial intelligence,” “physician,” “replacement,” “cannot,” “assistance,” “will not,” “completely,” “work,” “tool,” “clinical,” and “diagnosis and treatment.” Based on the combination of these words, this topic mainly centered on whether artificial intelligence could replace physicians and on its role boundaries in healthcare. It focused on judging whether artificial intelligence had the capability to independently undertake responsibilities in diagnosis and treatment, and whether it was more suitable as an auxiliary tool or as a replacement force. The strength of this topic was 0.784, indicating that this was the most dominant topic in discussions among healthcare professionals.

The more distinctive words in Topic 2 included “development,” “industry,” “data,” “future,” “technology,” “humanistic care,” “warmth,” “responsibility,” and “bear the blame.” Compared with Topic 1, this topic more strongly reflected healthcare professionals’ concern with the practical impacts and practice-related issues brought about by the development of artificial intelligence in healthcare. Among these, words such as “development,” “industry,” “future,” “technology,” and “data” reflected discussions of technological development and industry change, whereas words such as “humanistic care,” “warmth,” “responsibility,” and “bear the blame” suggested that healthcare professionals were also concerned with the effects of the application of artificial intelligence in healthcare practice on care delivery, occupational circumstances, and responsibility. The strength of this topic was 0.216, indicating that although this topic accounted for a relatively small proportion of the overall corpus, it constituted another important aspect of healthcare professionals’ discussions.

Overall, discussions among healthcare professionals on artificial intelligence in healthcare mainly focused on two levels: first, the role positioning and replacement boundaries between artificial intelligence and physicians; second, the occupational impact and practice-related concerns brought about by the development of artificial intelligence in healthcare. Among these, the former represented the more central direction of discussion, whereas the latter reflected healthcare professionals’ further concern about the practical changes brought by the integration of technology into healthcare practice.

Co-word network analysis results for healthcare professionals

The co-word network analysis results showed (see Fig. 5; Table 4) that the healthcare professional corpus formed a closely connected, high-density co-word network, with discussions being relatively concentrated overall. In the network, “artificial intelligence” occupied the absolute core position, with the highest weighted degree, eigenvector centrality, and PageRank. “Physician” and “replacement” were also located in the core layer, with weighted degrees reaching 3,612 and 2,571, respectively, and their eigenvector centrality values were also significantly higher than those of other nodes, suggesting that discussions among healthcare professionals mainly revolved around the relationship between artificial intelligence and physicians, as well as the issue of replacement.

Key node indicators of the co-word network among healthcare professionals

From the overall structure, nodes such as “healthcare,” “patient,” “assistance,” “humans,” “cannot,” “completely,” “development,” “clinical,” and “technology” all showed relatively high degree and closeness centrality, indicating that these words were widely distributed in the central area of the network and maintained direct connections with most nodes, thereby constituting the basic semantic framework of discussions among healthcare professionals. Among them, “assistance” had a relatively high weighted degree, indicating that healthcare professionals’ understanding of the role of artificial intelligence was highly concentrated on its positioning as a tool for assistance, and that related discussions were consistently connected with clinical practice and judgments about professional roles.

From the distribution of nodes at the application level, words such as “diagnosis,” “diagnosis and treatment,” “treatment,” “clinical,” “analysis,” “tool,” “data,” “medical imaging,” “medical record,” and “judgment” all showed relatively high levels of connectivity, indicating that discussions among healthcare professionals about artificial intelligence were mainly situated in specific healthcare settings, with a focus on diagnostic analysis, diagnosis and treatment support, medical record processing, medical imaging recognition, and clinical judgment. Although the centrality of these nodes was lower than that of core words such as “artificial intelligence,” “physician,” and “replacement,” they occupied stable positions in the network, reflecting that healthcare professionals’ attention to artificial intelligence in healthcare had a clear orientation toward professional practice.

At the same time, some words related to occupational impact and responsibility also entered the outer core of the network, such as “work,” “industry,” “future,” “unemployment,” “bear the blame,” and “humanistic care.” “Work” and “future” showed relatively high levels of connectivity, indicating that healthcare professionals’ discussions of the development of artificial intelligence extended to occupational change and industry trends. Although “unemployment” and “bear the blame” had relatively lower centrality, they remained connected to the core word cluster, suggesting that healthcare professionals had already paid attention to occupational risks and issues of responsibility in the application of artificial intelligence. “Humanistic care” also showed a relatively high level of connectivity, indicating that while discussing technological applications, healthcare professionals continued to pay attention to the humanistic attributes of healthcare services.

Overall, the co-word network of the healthcare professional corpus showed a structural pattern centered on “artificial intelligence” and extending toward keywords such as “physician,” “replacement,” “assistance,” “healthcare,” and “clinical.” The words with high centrality in the network were mainly concentrated in aspects such as role positioning, replacement boundaries, clinical application, occupational impact, and humanistic care, reflecting that discussions among healthcare professionals about artificial intelligence in healthcare had a strong occupational perspective and a clear orientation toward practice.

Co-word network analysis results for the public

The public corpus formed a highly dense co-word network with a high overall level of connectivity (see Fig. 6; Table 5). In the network, “artificial intelligence” occupied the absolute core position, with the highest weighted degree, eigenvector centrality, and PageRank, indicating that “artificial intelligence” had the strongest centrality in public discussions. “Physician,” “healthcare,” “patient,” and “replacement” were also located in the core area of the network, with weighted degrees reaching 2,820, 1,864, 1,645, and 1,482, respectively, suggesting that public discussions mainly revolved around the relationship between artificial intelligence and physicians, healthcare settings, patient interests, and the issue of replacement.

Key node indicators of the co-word network among the public

From the network structure, nodes such as “hospital,” “diagnosis,” “issue,” “model,” “data,” and “assistance” all showed relatively high degree, degree centrality, and closeness centrality, indicating that these words maintained direct connections with most nodes in the network and were widely distributed in the central area. Among them, “hospital” and “diagnosis” had relatively high weighted degrees, suggesting that the public’s understanding of artificial intelligence in healthcare relied largely on specific healthcare settings, with discussion focusing on hospital applications and the diagnostic process. Words such as “model,” “data,” and “DeepSeek” entered the central layer of the network, indicating that public discussions were no longer limited to general technological impressions, but also involved specific technological forms, data foundations, and representative artificial intelligence products.

From the application- and practice-related nodes, words such as “seeking medical care,” “medicine,” “technology,” “treatment,” “information,” “clinical,” “examination,” “diagnosis and treatment,” “medical record,” “system,” “tool,” and “use” all maintained strong connections with the core network, indicating that public discussions of artificial intelligence in healthcare had extended to more specific healthcare service processes, involving aspects such as diagnosis support, clinical application, information processing, medical record management, and system use. Although the centrality of these nodes was lower than that of core words such as “artificial intelligence,” “physician,” “healthcare,” and “patient,” they occupied stable positions in the network, reflecting that public attention to artificial intelligence in healthcare exhibited relatively clear scenario-based characteristics.

At the same time, some words related to judgment, responsibility, and risk also occupied relatively important positions in the network, such as “cannot,” “will not,” “completely,” “judgment,” “responsibility,” “complexity,” “risk,” and “suggestion.” Among these, “cannot,” “will not,” and “completely” showed relatively high levels of connectivity, indicating that the public continuously focused on the capability boundaries and limits of replacement of artificial intelligence when discussing artificial intelligence in healthcare. “Responsibility” and “risk” entered the group of highly connected words, suggesting that the public had already developed relatively clear concern about risk control and responsibility attribution in the application of artificial intelligence. Words such as “complexity,” “professional,” “capability,” and “knowledge” also maintained strong connections, reflecting continued public discussion of whether artificial intelligence could undertake complex healthcare tasks and whether it possessed professional judgment capability.

Overall, the co-word network of the public corpus showed a structural pattern centered on “artificial intelligence” and extending toward keywords such as “physician,” “healthcare,” “patient,” “replacement,” “hospital,” and “diagnosis.” The words with high centrality in the network were mainly concentrated in aspects such as healthcare settings, replacement boundaries, technological application, responsibility and risk, and capability judgment, indicating that public discussions of artificial intelligence in healthcare focused not only on its practical uses in real healthcare processes, but also on its limits of application and potential impacts.

Integrated comparative results of cognitive differences between the public and healthcare professionals

The integrated results of the LDA topic analysis and co-word network analysis showed that both healthcare professionals and the public focused on role positioning and replacement boundaries in artificial intelligence in healthcare. Both groups discussed whether artificial intelligence could enter healthcare practice, what kinds of tasks it could undertake, and where the boundaries of its capability should be set, indicating that their shared core concern lay in the functional position of artificial intelligence in the healthcare system and its application boundaries.

Further comparison revealed differences in the focal concerns of the two groups. Healthcare professionals placed greater emphasis on the occupational impact and practice-related concerns following the integration of technology into healthcare practice, with related discussions mainly involving clinical application, humanistic care, and responsibility, reflecting that their cognition was more closely tied to the internal operation of healthcare practice and the stability of professional roles. In contrast, the public placed greater emphasis on technical conditions and risk perception, with related discussions mainly focusing on healthcare settings, technological application, and responsibility and risk, reflecting that their cognition was more focused on the reliability, applicability, and potential risks of artificial intelligence in the actual process of seeking medical care.

Discussion

This study showed that both healthcare professionals and the public were dominated by positive sentiment toward artificial intelligence in healthcare (75.2% among healthcare professionals and 68.0% among the public), indicating that both groups recognized the potential value of artificial intelligence in the healthcare field and that artificial intelligence in healthcare had already acquired a certain social basis of acceptance. At the same time, the relatively higher proportion of negative sentiment among the public reflected a more cautious attitude [22]. This result was highly consistent with the overall trend in previous international studies, namely that different sectors of society generally hold conditionally favorable attitudes toward the application prospects of artificial intelligence in healthcare [23]. Combined with the findings of this study, the core of discussion in both groups remained focused on the role positioning and replacement boundaries of artificial intelligence. In the healthcare context, which is characterized by high risk, high responsibility, and strong professionalism, both groups were more concerned with which parts of healthcare practice artificial intelligence could enter, what kinds of tasks it could undertake, and where the boundaries of its capability should be set [24]. A global review likewise showed that patients and the public generally hold positive yet reserved attitudes toward clinical artificial intelligence and explicitly prefer its application to be supervised by human physicians [25]. This shared probing and negotiation over the boundaries of human–artificial intelligence collaboration not only provided empirical evidence from the Chinese digital context, but also enriched the broader global dialogue on the coexistence of social acceptance and practical concerns regarding artificial intelligence in healthcare.

Further comparison revealed that healthcare professionals placed greater emphasis on the occupational impact and practice-related concerns following the entry of artificial intelligence into healthcare practice, with related discussions mainly involving clinical application, humanistic care, and responsibility. In the healthcare professional corpus, words such as “assistance,” “clinical,” “diagnosis and treatment,” and “tool” were relatively prominent, and expressions such as “responsibility,” “bear the blame,” “unemployment,” and “humanistic care” also continued to appear, indicating that their discussions did not stop at whether artificial intelligence could be applied in clinical settings, but further extended to issues such as occupational circumstances, responsibility structures, and care relationships [26]. This was closely related to the practical position of healthcare professionals. As potential direct users of artificial intelligence technology and parties closely linked to responsibility, their understanding of artificial intelligence in healthcare was more likely to be grounded in specific workflows, professional division of labor, and clinical care experience. Therefore, they were more concerned with how the entry of artificial intelligence into clinical settings would affect the boundaries of their own responsibilities, patterns of responsibility, and the implementation of humanistic care [24]. Previous studies have also suggested that healthcare professionals usually hold a cautiously supportive attitude toward artificial intelligence in healthcare. While recognizing its value as a tool, they also continue to pay attention to responsibility attribution, changes in professional roles, and adjustments in human–machine collaboration [27]. This study was consistent with this overall trend and further showed that, in the context of Chinese online discourse, healthcare professionals often expressed such concerns through more reality-oriented expressions such as “bear the blame” and “unemployment.”

In contrast, the public placed greater emphasis on the technical conditions required for the implementation of artificial intelligence and on risk perception, with related discussions mainly focusing on healthcare settings, technological application, and responsibility and risk. In the public corpus, words such as “hospital,” “model,” “data,” “information,” and “examination” were relatively prominent, and words such as “responsibility,” “risk,” and “judgment” also occupied relatively important positions, indicating that the public was more concerned with whether artificial intelligence could truly enter healthcare settings, how it could participate in the process of seeking medical care, and whether the relevant applications had a sufficiently reliable technological foundation and an acceptable level of risk [28]. This difference was likewise related to the public’s position. As the receiving end of healthcare services, the public’s judgment of artificial intelligence in healthcare was more likely to revolve around actual effects of use, with greater concern about whether it was safe, whether it was reliable, whether it would affect the quality of judgment in the process of seeking medical care, and who should bear responsibility once problems occurred [29]. Previous studies have likewise shown that the public generally recognizes the application potential of artificial intelligence in healthcare, but retains concerns about its safety, reliability, and responsibility issues in practical application [10]. This study was consistent with this trend and further suggested that the public’s focal concerns were mainly concentrated on whether technology could be stably applied in real healthcare settings and whether the related risks were within a range that was understandable, acceptable, and controllable [30].

From a theoretical perspective, this study further suggests that the ethics and governance of artificial intelligence in healthcare cannot be assessed solely through a single dimension such as “acceptance” or “trust,” but should instead be understood as a differentiated structure of concern formed by different actors based on their respective positions. When considered in light of the World Health Organization’s ethical principles and governance framework for artificial intelligence in healthcare, the role positioning, risk concerns, responsibility attribution, and governance requirements identified in this study correspond respectively to the core principles of autonomy, safety, transparency, and responsibility and accountability. On this basis, through comparative text analysis, this study supplements current ethical and governance frameworks for artificial intelligence in healthcare with empirical evidence on differences in the focal concerns of different actors [31]. At the same time, the above results provide several implications for the subsequent advancement of artificial intelligence in healthcare. First, the role positioning and application boundaries of artificial intelligence in healthcare still need to be further clarified. Healthcare institutions should, in light of different healthcare tasks, clearly define the applicable settings, permissions for use, and decision boundaries of artificial intelligence so as to reduce ambiguity in the understanding of its role positioning among healthcare professionals and the public [32, 33]. Second, the issue of responsibility attribution involved after artificial intelligence enters healthcare practice still needs to be further refined. It may be useful to explore the establishment of result review, exception reporting, and risk response mechanisms that are aligned with the application of artificial intelligence, and to clarify the division of responsibilities among healthcare professionals, healthcare institutions, and technology developers in different situations, so as to respond to healthcare professionals’ concerns about bearing responsibility and the public’s concerns about responsibility-related risks [34]. Third, communication directed toward the public still needs to be more specific and transparent. In combination with actual healthcare settings, the practical role of artificial intelligence in the process of seeking medical care, its technological limitations, and corresponding safeguard measures should be clearly explained in order to enhance the public’s understanding of related applications [35, 36]. Overall, the subsequent advancement of artificial intelligence in healthcare needs to simultaneously respond to the practice-related concerns of healthcare professionals and the risk concerns of the public.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the research materials were mainly derived from Chinese social media platforms, and the processes of text generation and dissemination were inevitably influenced by specific cultural backgrounds, platform rules, and regulatory environments. Therefore, the study conclusions have evident contextual dependence, and their external generalizability should be interpreted with caution. Second, this study adopted multiple text mining methods, and the units of analysis, identification logic, and interpretive levels corresponding to different methods were not entirely consistent. Therefore, the analytical results are more appropriately understood as mutually complementary evidence rather than as strictly equivalent comparisons in terms of measurement. Third, social media texts are characterized by anonymity, fragmentation, and contextual absence. Although the study was able to capture naturally generated discourse materials, it was difficult to fully identify the actual identities, professional backgrounds, prior experiences, and specific contexts of the speakers, which to some extent limited the precision of text interpretation. Finally, the evidence base of this study mainly came from text analysis at the discourse level. Therefore, caution is still needed when further inferring institutional arrangements, governance logic, or ethical norms from language patterns, so as to avoid overextension. Future studies should combine questionnaire surveys, interview research, and empirical materials from real application settings to further validate and calibrate the relevant judgments.

Conclusion

Both the public and healthcare professionals held relatively positive attitudes toward artificial intelligence in healthcare, but the public demonstrated a higher degree of caution. Discussions in both groups revolved around the role positioning and replacement boundaries of artificial intelligence, but clear divergence existed in their specific focal concerns: healthcare professionals were more concerned with occupational impact and practice-related concerns, mainly involving clinical application, humanistic care, and responsibility; the public was more concerned with technical conditions and risk perception, mainly involving healthcare settings, technological application, and responsibility and risk. Overall, on the basis of a shared positive attitude, social cognition of artificial intelligence in healthcare showed clear group differences.
