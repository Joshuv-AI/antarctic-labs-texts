Synergistic effects of diabetic neuropathy and kidney function decline on second metatarsal skeletal health and foot function: a pilot study
Authors: Victor A Cheuy, Saghi Sadoughi, Hasung Moon, Jalen Ellis, Rodney Gross, David R Sinacore, Mary K Hastings, Richard B Souza, Galateia J Kazakia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA discouraging increase in diabetes-related nontraumatic lower-extremity amputations (NLEA) has occurred over the last decade. Individuals with diabetic neuropathy (DMPN) and CKD are at the highest risk for NLEA. Despite the critical implications, little is known about the factors that initiate the cascade of events leading to NLEA, limiting early and effective interventions. We aimed to develop and implement a technique for quantifying metatarsal bone density and microstructure using HR-pQCT. Twelve participants were enrolled: n = 3 healthy controls (CON; 57±4 yr, BMI 30±13 kg/m2, eGFR 106±4 mL/min/1.73 m2), n = 3 who had type 2 diabetic neuropathy with estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) stage 1 (DMPN; 66±2 yr, BMI 27±3 kg/m2, eGFR 96±2 mL/min/1.73 m2), and n = 6 who had type 2 diabetic neuropathy with eGFR defined stages 2-4 (DMPN+eGFR2-4; 69±10 yr, BMI 28±3 kg/m2, eGFR 64±20 mL/min/1.73 m2). Participants underwent assessments including HR-pQCT of the second metatarsal, dynamic plantar pressure measurements, and multi-segment foot biomechanics analysis during gait. Between-group and pooled correlational analyses were performed. The DMPN+eGFR2-4 group exhibited the highest cortical porosity at the mid-diaphysis, the highest pressure-time integrals at the hindfoot and for the total foot, the lowest intersegmental foot excursions, and lowest maximum ankle power. Correlation analyses revealed strong relationships between eGFR and cortical porosity (r=−0.868, p<.001), stance hindfoot excursion (r=0.662, p=.027), swing toe excursion (r=0.759, p=.007), swing forefoot excursion (r=0.823, p=.002), swing hindfoot excursion (r=0.745, p=.007), and ankle power (r=0.717, p=.013). Skeletal deterioration, abnormal loading, and poor biomechanics underscore the compounded effects of DMPN and eGFR decline on foot health, likely a consequence of CKD-mineral bone disorder syndrome. These findings suggest potential targets for intervention to prevent NLEAs in this high-risk population. Further research is needed to explore the interaction between DMPN and CKD and targeted therapeutic strategies to improve clinical outcomes.

Introduction

A discouraging increase in the total rate of diabetes-related nontraumatic lower-extremity amputations (NLEA) has occurred over the last decade, driven by both a 62% increase in minor (i.e., toe or foot) and a 29% increase in major (i.e., above foot/below knee or above knee) NLEA.1 Foot ulcers precede 80% of these diabetes-related NLEAs.2 Peripheral neuropathy and structural changes to the foot cause focal areas of high plantar pressure that go undetected due to lack of sensation, and with the repetition of walking, result in ulceration, representing the most prevalent causal pathway to NLEA.3–6

The highest rate of NLEA occurs in individuals with diabetic neuropathy (DMPN) who have end-stage kidney disease. Individuals with both DMPN and CKD are 15 times more likely to have a NLEA compared to those with diabetes alone.7,8 Of critical importance, the associated three-year mortality rate after an NLEA in people with DMPN and CKD is a staggering 71%.9 CKD is one of the most common comorbidities in people with diabetes, where 40% of individuals with diabetes have CKD, and in turn, diabetes is the leading cause of kidney failure, accounting for almost 50% of all new cases.10

To date, foot health remains an understudied area of research in DMPN and CKD, even though it represents not just a symptom of disease progression, but also a potential modifiable target. Almost all the knowledge of foot deterioration is based on data from trials involving patients with either diabetes or CKD. However, the synergistic effects of diabetes, peripheral neuropathy, and kidney health create a unique, unifying pathology driving peripheral bone deterioration and movement pattern changes leading to structural changes, ulceration, and NLEA. Investigating these relationships will elucidate the mechanistic underpinnings of foot structure and plantar pressure changes, and potentially identify targets for intervention to improve current therapies in preventing NLEA.

This pilot study examined the combined effects of DMPN and kidney health using an innovative combination of three assessment tools—in vivo visualization and assessment of metatarsal bone microarchitecture through HR-pQCT, high-resolution pedography of gait, and comprehensive multi-segment foot biomechanics analysis. The aims of this pilot study were to determine differences between healthy controls (CON), individuals with diabetic neuropathy only (DMPN), and individuals with DMPN and eGFR consistent with CKD stages 2-4 (DMPN+eGFR2-4) with respect to metatarsal cortical and trabecular structure, gait plantar pressure, and intersegmental foot motion.

Materials and methods

Participants

Twelve participants provided informed consent and participated in this cross-sectional study (Table 1). Participants were divided into three groups: healthy controls (CON, n = 3), diabetic neuropathy only and eGFR stage 1 (DMPN, n = 3), and diabetic neuropathy and eGFR stage 2-4 (DMPN+eGFR2-4, n = 6). The inclusion criteria for all groups were 40-80 yr of age, no active neuropathic ulcers, no lower extremity amputations, no recent bouts of immobilization or bed rest, and no non-DMPN or renal medical condition or medication with known impact on bone metabolism. For both groups with DMPN, the inclusion criteria were diagnosed type 2 diabetes and presence of peripheral neuropathy, defined as a Michigan Neuropathy Screening Instrument (MNSI) score > 2.11 The MNSI is a bilateral examination including a foot inspection, a test of ankle reflexes, and sensation tests of the dorsum of the great toe using a 128-Hz tuning fork (vibration) and a 5.07 g Semmes-Weinstein monofilament (pressure). Kidney function was determined and staged using the 2021 CKD Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) creatinine equation for estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR).12

Clinical history and questionnaires

Demographic information including age, sex, race, ethnicity, height, weight, and duration of diagnosed diabetes was obtained from the participants. The most recent HbA1c and eGFR values were obtained from the medical record. Comorbidity status was assessed by the Charlson comorbidity index (CCI), counting the number and severity of 19 possible conditions associated with mortality and healthcare utilization.13–15 Foot-related physical performance was assessed using the Activities of Daily Living subscale of the Foot and Ankle Ability (FAAM) questionnaire.16 Scoring is on a scale of 0%–100%, 0 being the inability to perform any usual daily activities and 100 being full ability to function.

High-resolution peripheral quantitative computed tomography (HR-pQCT)

The second metatarsal of one foot was imaged using an in vivo HR-pQCT scanner (XtremeCT II, Scanco Medical). The imaged foot was selected as the foot with the most diabetes-related complications (e.g., history of ulcers, wounds, and infection) and the least complications unrelated to diabetes (e.g., trauma or surgery). If both feet were equivalent to these conditions, then the right foot was selected.

The participant lay prone on an exam table with the imaged foot placed in plantarflexion into the foot cast, with the medial side aligned with the cast wall and foam padding around to maintain position and preventing motion (Figure 1A and B). A scout view was collected to ensure that the entire span of the second metatarsal was in view, with minimal overlap with adjacent bones, and that the second metatarsal was aligned parallel to the bore axis (Figure 1C). Three locations on the second metatarsal were imaged (Figure 1D-F): a distal site at a 4 mm offset from the most distal point, a proximal site at a 2 mm offset from the base of the proximal concavity, and a mid-diaphysis site at 50% between the distal and proximal reference lines. At each site, a single stack was acquired—168 slices covering approximately 10.2 mm—using the manufacturer’s standard in vivo protocol of source potential 68 kVp, tube current 1470 μA, and isotropic 60.7 μm nominal resolution. Scan time per stack is ~2 min; effective dose <5 μSv per scan. Scans were repeated, if motion or misalignment were detected on the first scan.

HR-pQCT positioning and data acquisition for the second metatarsal. (A) Participant placed prone with (B) the target foot in plantarflexion in the positioning cast. (C) Initial scout view to ensure alignment, with 3 ROIs placed at (D) distal, (E) proximal, and (F) mid-diaphysis locations along the metatarsal.

A Laplace–Hamming filter followed by a fixed global threshold was used to segment the bone.17–19 This segmentation approach was critical for the accurate quantification of microstructure at the second metatarsal, where fine microstructural features and signal to noise challenges make segmentation particularly challenging. After the trabecular and cortical binary masks were created, direct measurements of trabecular bone indices at the distal and proximal sites, and cortical bone indices at the mid-diaphysis site were performed using distance transform methods.20 The parameters of interest were trabecular volumetric BMD (Tb.vBMD), trabecular bone volume fraction (Tb.BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), trabecular number (Tb.N), cortical area (Ct.Ar), cortical volumetric BMD (Ct.vBMD), cortical porosity (Ct.Po), and cortical thickness (Ct.Th).

Plantar pressure

Participants underwent dynamic plantar pressure assessment while walking barefoot at their preferred comfortable walking speed using a high-resolution floor mat (HR Mat, Tekscan). Five clean stance phase strikes for each foot were used for analysis, defined as complete contact of a single foot within the mat. Plantar segmentation maps defined the total foot, forefoot, and hindfoot regions using the Tekscan automated 3-box analysis.21 Variables of interest extracted were peak pressure, peak pressure gradient (PPG) defined as the spatial change in pressure around the location of the peak pressure, force*time impulse (FTI), and pressure-time integral (PTI), all which assess risk of dermal injury in the DMPN foot.22,23

Gait biomechanics

Fifty reflective markers defined four segments on each leg: shank, hindfoot, forefoot, and second proximal phalanx (i.e., toe).24 Sagittal-plane kinematic and kinetic data were collected using a 10-camera 3D motion capture system (Vicon) and two force plates (AMTI). Participants performed barefoot walking at a comfortable speed, where each foot performed five successful trials. Participants practiced walking at their self-selected speed before gait analysis collection. The average speed of the practice trials was recorded as the participants’ self-selected speed. Walking trials were considered successful if the participant maintained a speed ±5% of the previously determined self-selected speed and a single foot made complete contact within a single force plate. All data were processed and analyzed using QTM (Qualisys AB), Visual3D (C-motion, Inc.), and MATLAB (MathWorks) software, using established methods.24

Statistical analyses

SPSS V29 (IBM Software) was used for all statistical analyses. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Data were summarized as mean and SD or counts (n) as appropriate. Fisher’s exact tests and ANOVA with Tukey’s HSD post-hoc pairwise comparisons were used to determine differences between groups. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to assess relationships across pooled data.

Results

Demographics and clinical measurements of the twelve participants are summarized in Table 1. No differences among the groups were found for sex, age, race, ethnicity, height, weight, or BMI. HbA1c and FAAM scores were also not different among groups. DMPN+eGFR2-4 had a lower eGFR compared to the CON and DMPN groups (DMPN+eGFR2-4 64 (20) vs CON 106 (4) and DMPN 96 (2), respectively; p = .007) by virtue of group assignment. Diabetes duration was not different between the DMPN and DMPN+eGFR2-4 groups, and both had higher MNSI (DMPN+eGFR2-4 5.8 (1.1) and DMPN 4.2 (1.0) vs CON 0.5 (0.9); p = .001) and CCI scores compared to CON (DMPN+eGFR2-4 3 (3) and DMPN 3 (0) vs CON 0 (0); p = .017). One participant from the DMPN group could not complete the plantar pressure or gait biomechanics assessments due to an unexpected surgical procedure.

Table 2 summarizes cortical structure indices at the mid-diaphysis and trabecular structure indices at the distal and proximal sites of the second metatarsal. Ct.Po was found to be higher in DMPN+eGFR2-4 compared to CON (4.03 (0.78)% vs 2.23 (0.38)%; p = .017). No other differences in cortical structure were found at the mid-diaphysis, and no differences in trabecular structure were observed at either the distal or proximal sites among groups.

Correlational analyses on the pooled data revealed relationships between eGFR and Ct.Po and intersegmental foot kinematics and kinetics (Figure 2). Estimated glomerular filtration rate had a strong inverse relationship with Ct.Po (r = −0.868, p < .001), indicating poorer kidney function was related to poorer bone microstructure. Estimated glomerular filtration rate also had strong direct relationships with stance phase hindfoot on shank excursion (r = 0.662, p = .27) and swing phase hindfoot on shank excursion (r = 0.745, p = .007), forefoot on hindfoot excursion (r = 0.823, p = .002), and toe on forefoot excursion (r = 0.759, p = .007). These results indicate that poorer kidney function was related to poorer intersegmental motion across the foot and across the gait cycle. Lastly, eGFR had a direct relationship with maximum ankle power (r = 0.717, p = .013), indicating poorer kidney function was related to poorer propulsive power at toe-off at the end of stance phase.

Discussion

The results of this pilot study indicate that patients with DMPN and kidney dysfunction may have the worst pedal bone microarchitecture, plantar pressure profiles, and foot biomechanics, which worsen with severity of kidney dysfunction. The DMPN+eGFR2-4 group exhibited the highest cortical porosity, highest cumulative pressure profile, and the least amount of intersegmental joint ranges of motion in the foot during gait compared to CON, but larger samples sizes will be required to fully characterize the combinatory impact of DMPN and kidney health.

This pilot study represents the first application of in vivo HR-pQCT in the forefoot, establishing the positioning, acquisition, and image processing methodologies necessary to evaluate the metatarsals. The ability to leverage HR-pQCT to quantify bone microarchitecture changes that precede gross structural changes provides the opportunity to identify patients with DMPN and CKD who may have poor foot outcomes. To our knowledge, the effect of DMPN and kidney health on foot bone deterioration has not been studied using HR-pQCT and represents a large gap in knowledge that directly impacts the ability for early recognition, treatment, and prevention of NLEA. An established protocol for collection and analysis of metatarsal HR-pQCT scans not only opens the door for larger scale studies investigating microarchitectural changes in the foot, but presents further avenues for research understanding how these bone measures relate to bone fragility, fracture risk, and structural deformities that precede ulcers.

Previous literature has found that BMD in the hip, spine, and feet of people with type 2 diabetes is often normal or even elevated, compared to those without diabetes.25 Seemingly paradoxically, persons with type 2 diabetes also have an increased risk for hip, spine, and foot fractures.26–28 The lack of consistency between BMD and fracture risk may suggest that the composite nature of standard measures obscures the redistribution of mineral within the microarchitecture of the bone, which may ultimately be responsible for the increases in bone fragility.29 The increase in cortical porosity—a major determinant of strength, stiffness, and fracture toughness—captures microarchitectural changes otherwise missed by lower resolution non-HR-pQCT methods, and also supports current evidence identifying abnormalities in trabecular and cortical microarchitecture.30 Previous HR-pQCT studies at the tibia and radius in people with type 2 diabetes have generally found trabecular bone hypertrophy compared to controls (Tb.BV/TV +11%-15%, Tb.Th +6%-14%, Tb.N +6%-25%) and cortical bone deterioration compared to controls (Ct.Po +6%-50%, Ct.vBMD −2%-3%).29,31–39 Similarly, CKD has long been associated with bone loss and increased risk for fragility fractures, and HR-pQCT based studies in CKD have also identified abnormalities in trabecular and cortical microarchitecture of the tibia and radius.40–42 In hemodialysis patients, almost 30% reductions in Tb.BV/TV and Tb.N compared to controls have been documented.43,44 Measures of cortical bone show similar deterioration, with an over 40% decrease in Ct.Th and Ct.Ar compared to controls.43,44

It is well known that excessive plantar pressure combined with insensate skin are critical contributors to skin breakdown in people with DMPN.4,45–47 The inability to perceive the high loads on the feet during repetitive activities of daily living, like walking, leads to the development of foot ulcers, recurrent ulcers, non-healing, and infection, which all precede an overwhelming majority of NLEA.48–50 Variables quantifying plantar pressure can be assessed over three major domains—magnitude, time, and direction.45 The significantly higher PTI in the DMPN+eGFR2-4 group represents a combination of the magnitude and time domains throughout stance phase, indicative of the elevated cumulative pressure experienced at the hindfoot (over 70% higher compared to CON) and the total foot overall (over 40% higher compared to CON). As the prevalences of DM and CKD continue to grow, PN remains the most frequent complication of both conditions, affecting over 50% of adults with DM and 70% of pre-dialysis patients, respectively.51–54 While this study did not find a difference in neuropathy severity based on MNSI scores between DMPN and DMPN+eGFR2-4, previous work has shown that the overlap between DM and kidney dysfunction is thought to contribute to a more severe PN phenotype compared to both DM or CKD alone, characterized by more severe total neuropathy scores, reduced nerve conduction amplitudes, and reduced nerve excitability.54,55 Given the growing comorbidity of DM and CKD, the study results highlight the importance of monitoring and managing PN and plantar pressures in patients with DMPN and kidney dysfunction to prevent severe foot complications.

This pilot study also represents the first evidence pedal gait impairments across stages of eGFR severity. Intersegmental joint motions in the foot were significantly impaired in the DMPN+eGFR2-4 group, evident during both stance and swing phases of gait. These compromised ranges of motion and ankle power may be linked to poor skeletal quality and structural support, muscle weakness and atrophy, and advanced glycation end product accumulation, all of which are downstream cumulative effects of DM, PN, and kidney dysfunction.56–61 The resultant altered gait patterns not only affect mobility and balance, but contribute to the high plantar pressures observed and increased risks of ulceration and NLEA.24,62 Previous studies have also shown reduced sagittal-plane excursions during gait in patients with DMPN, as well as reduced ankle power up to 20% compared to controls.63–71 The DMPN+eGFR2-4 group in this study exhibited a 45% reduction in ankle power, providing preliminary evidence that comorbid kidney dysfunction may exacerbate the negative effects of DMPN alone. The combined inefficiencies of the ankle and poor intersegmental kinematics could be capturing the impaired ability of the foot to transition from a flexible to a rigid lever, leading to dysfunction in the dynamic propulsion and stability required during gait for efficient energy transfer, minimization of stress, and weight distribution.64,72

The pedal structural and functional changes observed may be linked to CKD-mineral and bone disorder (CKD-MBD). CKD-MBD is a systemic disorder defined as (1) abnormalities in concentrations of calcium, phosphorus, PTH, or vitamin D metabolism; (2) abnormalities in bone turnover, mineralization, volume, linear growth, or strength; and (3) vascular and extravascular calcification.73,74 These abnormalities typically become apparent at stage 3a, but may start as early as stage 2 CKD.75 While CKD-MBD is primarily recognized for its effect on bone, its potential downstream effects on exacerbating muscle and movement dysfunctions underscore how CKD-MBD could negatively affect pedal physical function and mobility.75 Additional research is needed to better understand the direct relationships between CKD-MBD and the diabetic, neuropathic foot as it relates to preventing NLEA.

This pilot study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the sample size is small, and while typical of pilot study design, restricts the ability to generalize the findings to a larger population and impacts the statistical power of the study. Though statistically significant results were found throughout, the findings should be considered preliminary and require further research to leverage larger cohorts to improve the depth of analyses. The study used a cross-sectional design, which limits the ability to determine cause and effect, and it is uncertain what the individual contributions of DM, PN, or kidney dysfunction are on the outcomes of interest. While self-selected gait speed was controlled for within each participant to determine a successful walking trial, speed was not controlled for in the between-group analyses, which could influence the results. Albuminuria data and indicators of vascular disease (eg, ankle brachial index and vascular ultrasound) were also not available from the participants. As a pilot investigation, this study prioritized feasibility and HR-pQCT method development, with an intentionally focused scope of assessments defined primarily by cost and participant burden. There are several valuable outcomes not able to be incorporated in this initial phase, but each representing a concrete next step that will both strengthen biological interpretation and enhance comparability with the broader bone literature. DXA-derived areal BMD (aBMD) at the LS and hip were not available in this study focused on foot-specific measures, but future protocols including DXA will enable benchmarking against established fracture-risk thresholds, contextualize foot-specific changes within systemic skeletal changes, and facilitate cross-study harmonization. Similarly, adding HR-pQCT at conventional sites (e.g., radius, tibia) would provide microarchitectural and cortical porosity benchmarks that are widely reported and permit discrimination of systemic vs site-specific changes. Incorporation of serum markers, such as PTH, collagen type I C-telopeptide (CTx), and procollagen type I N-propeptide (PINP), would offer a robust insight into calcium-phosphate homeostasis, bone resorption, and bone formation, complementing imaging by characterizing bone remodeling dynamics pertinent to CKD. Incorporating these markers can strengthen biological interpretation and clarify the mechanistic link between systemic remodeling dynamics and site-specific structural outcomes. Lastly, establishing the HR-pQCT methods at the metatarsals provides a foundation for future studies to leverage finite element analysis to gain insight into complex bone structure-function relationships and produce bone strength estimations.

Conclusion

The results of this pilot study provide insight into pedal skeletal quality and function changes that differ by presence of DM, PN, and kidney dysfunction. Innovative application of HR-pQCT to the foot identified abnormalities of bone microarchitecture previously obscured by lower resolution bone BMD measurements. Relationships between declining kidney function and poorer bone quality, abnormal plantar pressures, and reduced intersegmental foot motion and ankle dysfunction underscore the combined impact of diabetes, neuropathy, and reduced kidney function on foot biomechanics and health. Given the alarming increase in NLEA over the past decade and the risk patients with DMPN and declining kidney function face, these results highlight the need for future research to identify targeted therapeutic strategies to preserve skeletal health and joint mobility to prevent severe foot complications, and improve the monitoring and treatment of kidney dysfunction to benefit NLEA prevention.
