Fear of Reinjury Is Associated With Worse Physical Function Measured by PROMIS Following Minimally Invasive Achilles Rupture Repair
Authors: Steven M. Hadley, John J. Peabody, Rachel Bergman, Eric H. Durudogan, Sarah J. Westvold, Frederic Washburn, Muhammad Y. Mutawakkil, Milap Patel, Anish R. Kadakia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Prior literature suggests that patients suffering from fear of reinjury (FORI) following Achilles rupture may fail to return to sport and report worse Achilles Tendon Total Rupture Scores (ATRS); however, this prior study included patients who underwent both surgical and non-surgical management. This study therefore aims to determine whether self-reported fear (srFORI) is associated with worse outcomes as measured by validated Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) measures of physical function (PF) and pain interference (PI) and ATRS following operative Achilles repair specifically.Methods:This investigation was a retrospective review of all patients who underwent Achilles repair at a single institution (2018-2023). Tendinopathies, open injuries, concomitant fractures, tendon transfers, and gastrocnemius recessions were excluded. A total of 119 patients completed questionnaires containing PROMIS and ATRS, and additional surveys querying srFORI measured at the time of survey participation. Primary outcome was PROMIS. Secondary outcome was ATRS.Results:Mean follow-up duration was 30.3 ± 8.3 months. No significant differences existed in baseline characteristics (P > .05). Twenty-nine of 119 patients (24.4%) self-reported FORI. SrFORI was associated with significantly worse PF relative to those without srFORI (53.0 ± 6.1 vs 59.0 ± 8.1, mean difference (MD) −6.08, 97.5% CI −9.79, −2.36, P < .001). SrFORI was similarly associated with significantly worse ATRS (72.6 ± 17.8 vs 88.7 ± 12.3, MD −16.18, 97.5% CI: −22.89, −9.47, P < .001). There were no significant differences in PI (44.9 ± 7.0 vs 43.9 ± 6.1, MD 1.03, 97.5% CI: −2.04, 4.11, P = .48). Adjusted regression revealed that srFORI was associated with significantly worse PF (MD 6.2 points lower, P < .001) and ATRS (MD 16.5 points lower, P < .001). Both differences in PF and ATRS reached MCID. With the numbers available, no significant difference in overall complications could be detected between groups (7.8%, 7/90, vs 10.3%, 3/29, P = .70).Conclusion:Nearly 25% of patients self-reported FORI following minimally invasive Achilles tendon repair, which may be associated with clinically and statistically significant deficits in functional outcomes as measured by PROMIS and ATRS following repair.Level of Evidence:Level III, retrospective cohort study.

Introduction

Fear of reinjury (FORI) may limit patient function and recovery following a variety of orthopaedic conditions, including ankle fractures, anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) ruptures, meniscus tears, shoulder instability, and chronic lateral ankle instability.1-11

Prior evidence suggests that patients who suffer Achilles rupture similarly may experience FORI. Kaalund et al 7 reported that 15% (6/39) of badminton players who suffered Achilles tendon ruptures did not return to pre-injury activity levels because of FORI. Similarly, a retrospective study evaluating 28 patients (average age 63) who sustained Achilles ruptures playing pickleball revealed that 53.6% (15/28) failed to return to pickleball, and 14 of those 15 (93.3%) cited FORI as the primary explanation. 12 Moreover, Larsson et al 13 found that approximately half of patients suffer from FORI after Achilles tendon rupture, and that FORI was associated with significantly worse outcomes as measured by the Achilles Tendon Total Rupture Score (ATRS). However, these studies assessed both nonoperative and operative management. Other studies found that lack of motivation and delayed readiness to return to play during rehabilitation were associated with poorer performance and return to sport, emphasizing the role of psychological barriers in recovery from injury.14,15

No prior studies have compared patient-reported outcomes (PROs) among patients with and without FORI following operative Achilles tendon rupture repair specifically. Notably, no studies have compared validated Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) computerized adaptive tests (CATs) of physical function (PF) and pain interference (PI) between those with and without FORI following Achilles tendon repair. PROMIS is a more global metric of function compared with the ATRS, which was developed to assess function specific to the Achilles tendon. 16 PROMIS is also widely being used more frequently in routine orthopaedic clinical practice, unlike ATRS.17,18 PROMIS further exhibits improved reliability and generalizability compared with other commonly utilized PRO metrics for foot and ankle orthopaedics.19-24

This study therefore aims to determine the association between self-reported fear of reinjury (srFORI) and patient functional outcomes following Achilles rupture repair as measured by PROMIS and ATRS. We hypothesized that srFORI would correlate with worse PROMIS scores and ATRS than those without srFORI. A clearer understanding of the role of srFORI in recovery after Achilles tendon repair may help physicians optimize patients’ functional outcomes following surgical treatment of Achilles tendon ruptures.

Methods

This investigation was an institutional review board–approved retrospective review of all patients who underwent Achilles rupture repair from 2018-2023 at a single academic institution. Two reviewers independently examined all patient records. Tendinopathies, open injuries, concomitant fractures, tendon transfers, and gastrocnemius recessions were excluded. All patients underwent repair using minimally invasive surgical techniques with either the Percutaneous Achilles Repair System (PARS; Arthrex) or the Midsubstance SpeedBridge system (MSB; Arthrex) according to previously described techniques.25-27 Two fellowship-trained foot and ankle orthopaedic surgeons performed all cases. All patients also underwent an accelerated functional rehabilitation protocol previously described by Willits et al. 28 A total of 316 patients meeting criteria were contacted to complete a postoperative questionnaire containing PROMIS CATs and ATRS, as well as an additional survey querying current (at time of survey completion) srFORI with a binary yes/no response option via REDCap. 29 The exact wording of the question was “do you experience limitations due to fear of reinjury?” This question was used to identify self-reported FORI (srFORI). Patients were contacted a total of 5 times via phone and email prior to ceasing contact. A total of 136 patients consented to participation, although 17 were subsequently excluded because of insufficient follow-up (<1 year postoperatively) and/or incomplete survey responses. Therefore, 119 patients who completed all survey questions and had ≥1 year follow-up were included for final analysis.

Outcomes

The primary outcome measure in this study was PROMIS scores (version 2.0). PROMIS is an adaptive questionnaire that uses age-matched norms calibrated to a US population mean score of 50, with higher scores on PF indicating better physical function, with conversely higher scores on PI designating increased pain.19,20 The minimal clinically important difference (MCID) is a change of 3 to 30. 20

The secondary outcome was ATRS scores. ATRS was developed specifically for measuring function of the Achilles tendon following rupture using 10 questions graded on an 11-point Likert scale. 16 A perfect score is 100, indicating no functional deficits. 16 The initial validation of this study indicated a healthy population mean score of 99. 16 The MCID for ATRS is a change of 8 to 10.16,30-33

Complications were also analyzed between groups. Surgical complications included deep vein thrombosis (DVT), pulmonary embolism (PE), superficial wound complication, deep wound infection, re-rupture, sural neuritis, and nerve block complications.

Prior literature indicated that MSB may have a higher incidence of postoperative heel pain than PARS. 27 However, a modified placement of the MSB anchors (superior to inferior instead of posterior to anterior) in the calcaneus has been shown to reduce the incidence of heel pain in MSB substantially. 27 Incidence of postoperative heel pain was collected and analyzed between groups.

Statistical analysis

Hypothesis-driven testing with R, version 4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing), was used. t tests were used to compare continuous variables by srFORI. χ2 and Fisher exact tests were used to compare categorical variables and complications between groups. To evaluate the association between srFORI and PROs further, multivariable regression was performed, adjusted for age, sex, body mass index, surgical technique, and time since surgery at the time of survey completion. To account for multiple primary outcomes and reduce the risk of type I error, statistical significance was defined as a 2-sided P value <.025 using Bonferroni correction.

Results

A total of 119 patients were included in final analysis. There were no significant differences in baseline characteristics between groups (Table 1), including the number of patients in each group who underwent repair with PARS and those who underwent repair with MSB. Of the 119 total patients, 29 (24.4%, 95% CI 17.5%-32.8%) self-reported FORI and 90 (75.6%, 95% CI 67.2%-82.5%) described no srFORI. Mean follow-up duration was 30.3 ± 8.3 months (median 24.8 months, range 13.3-64.4). Follow-up duration was not significantly different between groups (mean 33.6 ± 10.0 vs 29.3 ± 7.3; median 24.7 months vs 25.0 months; range 13.3-64.4 months vs 14.1-63.0 months, P = .33). There were no significant differences in incidence of srFORI by duration of follow-up (P = .29) or patient age at time of surgery (P = .89). Baseline characteristics were not significantly different between those who responded to the survey (n = 119) and those who did not (n = 197, Supplementary Table S1), including nonresponders who had minimum 12 months’ follow-up (n = 99, Supplementary Table S2).

SrFORI was significantly associated with deficits in PROs (Table 2). Those with srFORI described significantly worse mean PROMIS PF compared with those without srFORI (53.0 ± 6.1 vs 59.0 ± 8.1, mean difference −6.08, 97.5% CI: −9.79, −2.36, P < .001). Similarly, srFORI was associated with significantly worse mean ATRS compared with those without fear (72.6 ± 17.8 vs 88.7 ± 12.3, mean difference −16.18, 97.5% CI: −22.89, −9.47, P < .001). Both differences in mean PF and mean ATRS reached MCID criteria. With the numbers available, no significant differences were found in PI between groups (44.9 ± 7.0 vs 43.9 ± 6.1, mean difference 1.03, 97.5% CI: −2.04, 4.11, P = .48). Discussion

To our knowledge, no prior study has compared PROMIS scores between patients with and without srFORI following operative repair of Achilles tendon rupture. This study demonstrated an incidence of srFORI of 24.4% and identified that the presence of srFORI was associated with both statistically and clinically significant reductions in PROMIS Physical Function and ATRS scores compared with patients without srFORI. Self-reported FORI was associated with a PROMIS PF of 53.0 vs a PF of 59.0 in the no srFORI group in unadjusted analysis and a 6.2-point lower PROMIS PF score in adjusted regression, both of which exceed MCID criteria. These findings are therefore representative of clinically relevant worse PF in patients with srFORI. 20 Similarly, the mean ATRS in the fear group was 72.6 vs 88.7 for those without srFORI in unadjusted analysis, and srFORI was associated with a 16.5-point lower ATRS score in adjusted analysis, both of which meet MCID criteria for ATRS. 16 These findings suggest that the presence of srFORI may correlate with clinically meaningful postoperative functional limitations.

Average PROMIS PI was not significantly different between groups. This finding aligns with prior literature evaluating FORI and outcomes of various foot and ankle operations—in which discrepancies have been described for PF although not found significant for PI.5,11 Together, these results might suggest that postoperative functional deficits may be driven by psychological rather than pain-related barriers.

The findings of this study may corroborate prior literature revealing functional deficits driven by FORI following Achilles tendon rupture.7,12,13 Recent literature has evaluated ATRS scores and return to sport among patients treated with both surgical and non-surgical management.7,12,13 This study suggests that among patients who undergo operative repair, srFORI may be associated with worse recovery and postoperative function. Furthermore, significantly worse PROMIS scores might suggest that patients have global physical deficits in addition to the Achilles tendon–specific functional deficits as measured by the ATRS.

Interestingly, our cohort found that among patients who underwent operative tendon repair, the incidence of srFORI was lower than the reported rates in prior studies; for example, Lencer et al 12 reported a rate of 50%, and Larsson et al 13 reported a rate of 56%. However, both studies included patients treated both surgically and non-surgically, and the study conducted by Lencer et al12,13 focused on patients who played pickleball with an older average age (63.9 vs 38.4 years in this study’s cohort). Given that recovery from Achilles tendon rupture requires lengthy rehabilitation, it is reasonable that incidence of FORI is higher in older populations. Compared with findings reported by Larsson et al, 13 the lower incidence of srFORI in this study’s cohort could reflect a psychological benefit of operative repair; patients may perceive a repaired tendon as more structurally secure, potentially increasing confidence postoperatively.

The significant deficits in functional outcomes associated with srFORI following Achilles tendon ruptures suggest that the psychological barriers to recovery may be inadequately addressed in this population. Addressing srFORI may have the potential to enhance outcomes substantially for these patients. Although this study did not evaluate strategies to reduce srFORI, future investigations from this study team aim to evaluate and implement potential strategies to combat srFORI. As surgeons, there may be benefit in screening for srFORI, perhaps including a quick binary yes/no questionnaire administered at routine postoperative clinic visits. Physical therapists additionally see patients frequently in the postoperative period and may be able to more easily identify behaviors indicative of FORI, which can be communicated with the operating surgeon. Further, Kvist and Silbernagel 8 described the importance of reassurance and tailoring rehabilitation exercises to build and promote confidence as patients regain function in the postoperative period. There also may be value in screening patients with srFORI for comorbid depression and anxiety, as some evidence suggests that these patients suffer worse outcomes following surgery. 34 Surgeons can also consider connecting high-risk patients with sports psychologists when possible. Culvenor et al 35 reported that, when combined with an appropriate rehabilitation regimen, psychological interventions such as guided imagery, relaxation, coping modeling, and visual imagery may reduce fear and anxiety following ACL reconstruction. Chmielewski et al 3 suggested that patients who exhibit FORI following ACL reconstruction who address psychological barriers early in rehabilitation may progress more quickly in the recovery process. The findings reported in the ACL literature indicate the potential benefit of rehabilitation protocols that address the psychological in additional to the functional components of recovery, and employing these techniques may improve outcomes for patients following Achilles tendon repair as well.35,36

With the numbers available, the incidence of surgical complications was not significantly different between groups. This finding does not support a relationship between perioperative complications and the functional outcome differences observed. The low incidence of complications in this study reinforce the safety of minimally invasive Achilles tendon repair, regardless of which system was utilized (PARS vs MSB). 27 The complication rates in this study are also lower than the reported complication incidences for open Achilles repair, particularly with respect to deep wound infections.37-41 The re-rupture rate of 1.7% (2/119) in our study is similar to rates reported for open repair and lower than reported re-rupture rates for those who undergo nonoperative management.26,27,42-46

The 3 patients with reported heel pain in this cohort also described srFORI. However, prior research indicates that heel pain following Achilles rupture repair does not negatively affect PROs, and srFORI was still associated with significantly worse PROMIS PF and ATRS in the absence of heel pain (Supplementary Table S3). 26 Therefore, there is no conclusive evidence to determine any relationship between postoperative heel pain and srFORI.

Irrespective of srFORI, patients were able to achieve PROMIS and ATRS scores near or superior to population means.26,39,40,47,48 Because Achilles ruptures often occur in physically active individuals, these findings indicate that minimally invasive Achilles tendon repair may enable physically active patients to return to high levels of activity.26,49 The PROMIS and ATRS scores reported in this study are similar to PROs reported for both open and minimally invasive techniques.26,39,40,47,48

Limitations

This study is not without limitations. The assessment of fear was performed with a binary yes/no response option that may limit the reliability of the responses. However, a binary measure allowed us to recognize patients who self-identify as having FORI without imposing additional response burden or requiring administration of instruments that are not routinely collected in standard postoperative clinic visits. Further research should work toward implementing a validated metric to identify FORI (ie, Tampa Scale of Kinesiophobia, Fear Avoidance Beliefs Questionnaire), though these PROs are not used in routine clinical practice. This study is also limited by its retrospective design, and future prospective studies will help identify patients at higher risk for FORI. Further, this study did not have baseline PROMIS and ATRS metrics to compare postoperative outcomes. However, PROMIS is calibrated to a population mean score of 50, and thus the results of this study still provide meaningful interpretative value even in the absence of baseline scores. Patients may have interpreted survey questions differently, which would introduce measurement bias. The survey response rate of 38%, 119/316 (or 55%, 119/218, of those with ≥1 year follow-up), also may introduce nonresponse bias. However, with the numbers available, baseline characteristics between responders and nonresponders were not significantly different (Supplementary Table S1 and Supplementary Table S2). Moreover, persistent functional deficits may generate fear rather than fear contributing to functional deficits, and the cross-sectional measurement of the exposure and outcomes is unable to differentiate the two. Lastly, although every patient had minimum 1-year follow-up, the follow-up duration was not the same for every patient, as time of survey completion was not standardized; therefore, completion of PROs was performed at a single time point rather than longitudinally throughout the postoperative period. However, mean follow-up duration was not significantly different between groups, and there were no significant differences in incidence of srFORI when stratified by duration of follow-up.

Strengths

Despite these limitations, this study has many strengths. Importantly, this study is the first to describe the association between srFORI and worse PROs among a cohort of patients with Achilles tendon rupture who underwent operative repair. This study is also the first to compare PROMIS scores between those with and without srFORI following Achilles rupture. Because PROMIS is a metric of global function (and therefore not injury-specific), is validated specifically in Achilles rupture, and is widely adopted in routine clinical practice, this study provides invaluable insight into the association between srFORI and worse overall functional outcomes. This investigation is also the first to suggest srFORI as a potential barrier in recovery following minimally invasive Achilles tendon repair.

Conclusions

One quarter of patients following minimally invasive Achilles rupture repair self-reported FORI, which may be associated with clinically and statistically significant deficits in functional outcomes as measured by postoperative PROMIS PF and ATRS. Future efforts should consider addressing FORI and other psychological barriers to recovery.
