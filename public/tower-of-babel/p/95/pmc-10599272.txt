Assessment of the application for renewal of the authorisation of a feed additive consisting of dicopper chloride trihydroxide for all animal species (Nutreco Nederland B.V.)
Authors: EFSA Panel on Additives and Products or Substances used in Animal Feed (FEEDAP), Vasileios Bampidis, Giovanna Azimonti, Maria de Lourdes Bastos, Henrik Christensen, Birgit Dusemund, Mojca Durjava, Maryline Kouba, Marta López‐Alonso, Secundino López Puente, Francesca Marcon, Baltasar Mayo, Alena Pechová, Mariana Petkova, Fernando Ramos, Roberto Edoardo Villa, Ruud Woutersen, Georges Bories, Jürgen Gropp, Jaume Galobart, Matteo Lorenzo Innocenti, Jordi Ortuño, Fabiola Pizzo, Barbara Rossi, Jordi Tarrés‐Call, Maria Vittoria Vettori, Anita Radovnikovic
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractFollowing a request from the European Commission, EFSA was asked to deliver a scientific opinion on the assessment of the application for the renewal of the authorisation of dicopper chloride trihydroxide as a nutritional feed additive. The additive is currently authorised for use in all animal species (3b409). The applicant is requesting a modification in the current authorising specification: (i) replacing the alpha crystal form paratacamite with clinoatacamite; (ii) removing the ratio between crystal polymorphs from the authorising specification; (iii) changing the minimum for particles below 50 μm from 1% to 5%. The FEEDAP Panel considers that the proposed modifications do not introduce concern for the safety for the target species and that the additive remains safe for the target species and the consumers when used up to the maximum authorised levels. Regarding the safety for the environment, the use of the additive in feed for terrestrial animals and land‐based aquaculture is considered safe under the proposed conditions of use. The data available do not allow the FEEDAP Panel to reach a conclusion on the safety of the additive for marine sediment when it is used in sea cages. The additive is not corrosive to skin, but is an eye and skin irritant. Due to the nickel presence, it is considered a skin and respiratory sensitiser. There is no need for assessing the efficacy of the additive in the context of the renewal of the authorisation.

Introduction

Background and terms of reference

Regulation (EC) No 1831/2003 1 establishes the rules governing the Community authorisation of additives for use in animal nutrition. In particular, Article 14(1) of that Regulation lays down that an application for renewal shall be sent to the Commission at the latest one year before the expiry date of the authorisation.

The European Commission received a request from Nutreco Nederland B.V. 2 for the renewal of the authorisation of the additive consisting of dicopper chloride trihydroxide (Intellibond C), when used as a feed additive for all animal species (category: nutritional additives; functional group: compounds of trace elements).

According to Article 7(1) of Regulation (EC) No 1831/2003, the Commission forwarded the application to the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) as an application under Article 14(1) (renewal of the authorisation). EFSA received directly from the applicant the technical dossier in support of this application. The particulars and documents in support of the application were considered valid by EFSA as of 24 November 2021.

According to Article 8 of Regulation (EC) No 1831/2003, EFSA, after verifying the particulars and documents submitted by the applicant, shall undertake an assessment in order to determine whether the feed additive complies with the conditions laid down in Article 5. EFSA shall deliver an opinion on the safety for the target animals, consumer, user and the environment and on the efficacy of the feed additive consisting of dicopper chloride trihydroxide, when used under the proposed conditions of use (see Section 3.1.3 ).

Additional information

The additive is dicopper chloride trihydroxide. The FEEDAP Panel issued an opinion on the safety and efficacy of this product when used in feed for all animal species (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2011). The additive is currently authorised for use in feed for all animal species (3b409). 3

Dicopper chloride trihydroxide is also authorised for use as an active substance in pesticide products (fungicide) under Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009. 4

Data and methodologies

Data

The present assessment is based on data submitted by the applicant in the form of a technical dossier 5 in support of the authorisation renewal request for the use of dicopper chloride trihydroxide (Intellibond C) as a feed additive.

The dossier was received on 11.06.2021 and the general information and supporting documentation are available on Open.EFSA at https://open.efsa.europa.eu/questions/EFSA-Q-2021-00547

The FEEDAP Panel used the data provided by the applicant together with data from other sources, such as previous risk assessments by EFSA or other expert bodies, peer‐reviewed scientific papers, other scientific reports and experts' knowledge, to deliver the present output.

The European Union Reference Laboratory (EURL) considered that the conclusions and recommendations reached in the previous assessment regarding the methods used for the control of the copper content in animal feed are valid and applicable for the current application. 6

Methodologies

The approach followed by the FEEDAP Panel to assess the safety and the efficacy of the additive is in line with the principles laid down in Regulation (EC) No 429/2008 7 and the relevant guidance documents: Guidance on the renewal of the authorisation of feed additives (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2021).

Assessment

Dicopper chloride trihydroxide is currently authorised for use as a nutritional additive (functional group: compounds of trace elements) – source of copper in feed for all animal species and categories. The additive is the active substance. The applicant is requesting the renewal of the authorisation and changes in the specification.

Characterisation

Characterisation of the additive

The additive consists of dicopper chloride trihydroxide (synonym: tribasic copper chloride; TBCC) with a chemical formula Cu2(OH)3Cl and molecular weight 213.57 g/mol. The Chemical Abstracts Service (CAS) number is 1332‐65‐6, EC number 215‐572‐9 and Classification, Labelling & Packaging number (CLP) is 029–017‐001.

Dicopper chloride trihydroxide occurs in three forms. The α‐ and β‐crystals and the amorphous form, which show different physico‐chemical properties (e.g. solubility). The current assessment relates only to the α‐crystal form.

The current authorisation specifies that the additive must contain a minimum of 90% of dicopper chloride trihydroxide and a copper content of minimum 53%, that the crystal polymorph ratio of alpha crystals atacamite and paratacamite is in a 1:1 to 1:1.5 ratio and that the crystalline product should contain a minimum of 95% of alpha crystals. The particles that have a diameter below 50 μm should be below 1%.

In the first application (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2011), dicopper chloride trihydroxide was manufactured via a reaction between an acidic solution of cupric chloride, hydrochloric acid and copper tetramine dichloride. In the current application, the applicant proposes a different manufacturing process 8 ■■■■■ At the end of the production, a small amount of non‐genetically modified starch (ca. 5%) is added to reduce the dusting potential.

In the current application, the applicant is requesting a modification in the authorising specification: (i) replacing the alpha crystal form paratacamite with clinoatacamite; (ii) removing the ratio between crystal polymorphs from the authorising specification; (iii) changing the minimum for particles below 50 μm from 1% to 5%. The applicant wishes to maintain the minimum of 95% of alpha crystal. The applicant also requested the product to be called ‘granulated dicopper chloride trihydroxide’. The applicant justified the request for the above changes as follows.

Based on scientific evidence 9 and new X‐ray diffraction analysis, 10 the applicant states that what was originally identified as paratacamite is in fact a different polymorph, clinoatacamite. Moreover, the applicant stated that the manufacturing process has been changed since the original authorisation, which results in a relative ratio of atacamite to clinoatacamite of around 1:6 to 9, in contrast of 1:1 to 1:1.5. In addition, the applicant is requesting that in a new specification, particles below 50 μm can be present up to 5%, as this reflects better what is achieved in practice.

To demonstrate the absence of paratacamite in the product originally authorised, the applicant carried out the X‐ray diffraction analyses. Powder X‐ray diffractograms were conducted on three batches of dicopper chloride trihydroxide, produced before the change in the manufacturing process, representative of the material as described in the current authorisation. 10 The crystalline phases identified were atacamite and clinoatacamite at 40% and 60%, respectively. The results of the applicant's analyses indicate that the original dicopper chloride trihydroxide was comprising of atacamite and clinoatacamite, and that paratacamite was not present. The ratio of atacamite to clinoatacamite in the three batches was around 1:1.5. The applicant also tested the ratio of crystal polymorphs in the five batches of the additive, produced after the change in the manufacturing process. The change was reported to have an impact on the dissolution rate, which increased by ~ 20%, with respect to the original additive. In these batches, atacamite was present at ~ 10–15%, while clinoatacamite was present at 85–90%, thus confirming the min 95% of alpha crystals in the crystalline product. All eight samples tested contained copper in a range from 54.0% to 59.3%.

To confirm the new specification proposed, the applicant provided X‐ray diffraction analyses of five recent batches of the additive. 11 The sum of atacamite and clinoatacamite (referred to as the alpha‐crystal content) was from 97.4% to 98.9%. Small amounts of botallackite 12 were present in two batches tested (0.3% and 1.2%, respectively) and a minor amount of copper oxide as tenorite was also identified in all five batches of the dicopper chloride trihydroxide (levels ranging from 0.2% to 2.5%). Paratacamite was not identified. The sum of all mineral species reported amounted to 100% in all five batches.

Copper content in the additive ranged from 56.3% to 56.5% and the additive was considered to have 94.7% to 95% purity 13 (with respect to the theoretical max copper content of 59.51% in pure crystal). 14 Moisture content was between 0.20% and 0.38%.

Overall, the crystal polymorphs analyses and copper content indicate that the product complies with the new specification proposed by the applicant.

The applicant provided multi‐elements analyses in five bathes of the additive. 15 Cadmium and mercury content was below the limit of quantification (LOQ) 16 in all five samples. Lead content ranged from 17.6 to 33.4 mg/kg, while arsenic was detected in two samples at 1.1 and 1.36 mg/kg. Levels of zinc were from 36.7 to 55.4 mg/kg and nickel from 9.45 to 15.5 mg/kg.

The level of dioxins was from 0.088 to 0.17 ng WHO‐PCDD/F‐PCB‐TEQ/kg in all five batches. 17 The sum of dioxins and dioxin‐like polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) ranged from 0.14 to 0.21 ng WHO‐PCDD/F‐PCB‐TEQ/kg. The sum of six non‐dioxin‐like PCB 18 (upper bound) ranged from 0.41 to 0.52 μg/kg.

The detected amounts of the above‐described impurities do not raise safety concerns.

Physical state, physico‐chemical and technological properties of the additive

The additive is a green crystalline powder with a tapped bulk density 14 of 1,032.2 kg/m3. Dusting potential was determined by the Stauber‐Heubach method 19 in three batches and ranged from 217.5 to 302.5 mg/m3, with an overall average at 256.7 mg/m3 (mg airborne dust per m3 of air). The copper content in the dust 20 was on average 53.7% (50.0–56.8%).

The solubility of dicopper chloride trihydroxide in water varies with pH. It is insoluble at neutral pH but soluble under acidic conditions. The applicant provided a comparative dissolution test at pH 4.7 (acetate buffer) with the additive produced via the former and the new manufacturing process. The additive produced by the new manufacturing process showed ~ 20% higher dissolution rate than the original material. 21

Data were submitted to support the request to modify the current specification of particles below 50 μm from 1% to 5%. The particle size distribution of five batches of dicopper chloride trihydroxide was tested by laser diffraction method. 22 Average particle size was in a range of 188–218 μm. The additive contained 0.12–1.47% volume of particles with a size below 50 μm and there were no particles below 1 μm.

Since the above data were not generated in line with the Guidance on technical requirements for regulated food and feed product applications to establish the presence of small particles including nanoparticles (EFSA SC, 2021), the decision criteria to establish the presence of small particles based on the above information could not be applied; therefore, the applicant was requested to provide information in that regard. The applicant submitted a study with scanning electron microscopy analysis of three recent batches of the additive to demonstrate the absence of small and nanoparticles. 23 No particles have been detected under 500 nm, allowing to conclude that the additive does not contain small particles/nanoparticles and the conventional risk assessment can be applied.

New data were provided to support the capacity for homogeneous distribution of the additive in feed. The additive was added to a commercial poultry feed at an inclusion level of 0.75 g/kg. 24 The coefficient of variation (CV) calculated from the analyses of 10 samples of pelleted feed was 7.6%.

Safety

The FEEDAP Panel evaluated the safety of the additive in the opinion on the safety and efficacy of dicopper chloride trihydroxide (tribasic copper chloride, TBCC) as a feed additive for all species (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2011). In this assessment, it was concluded that the additive is safe for the target species, the consumers and the environment. In the absence of data, the additive was considered as a potential irritant to skin and eyes, and a potential skin sensitiser. The risk of respiratory exposure was considered to be minimal.

The applicant reported that due to the new manufacturing process, the dissolution rate of the additive in the acetate buffer at pH 4.7 increased by approximately 20%. Other copper compounds that have higher solubility/dissolution rates than the additive under assessment are already authorised (e.g. copper sulfate pentahydrate 25 ). Therefore, this increase in the dissolution rate will not cause any safety concern for the target species and the consumer when the additive is used at currently authorised levels in feed.

The applicant conducted a literature search with the aim to demonstrate that the additive remains safe for the target species, consumers and user 26 under the authorised conditions of use. The search was limited to publications from 31 December 2010 to 6 January 2021 and the search terms and search strategy were provided. Web of Science portal was used as a primary database for searching articles. The main search terms used were related to the active substance in combination with terms relevant for target animals, consumer and user. PubMed/TOXLINE was used for a secondary search for target species and toxicology. This secondary search was conducted as a verification for the completeness of the Web of Science Search by using the most relevant keywords. Results of the literature search are presented below.

Safety for the target species

At the time of the original application (2011), three tolerance studies were provided by the applicant, two in chickens for fattening and one in laying hens (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2011). Based on the results of these studies, the FEEDAP Panel concluded that the additive is a safe source of copper for all animal species/categories up to the maximum total copper content authorised in feedingstuffs.

From the literature search (described above), the applicant selected 35 publications as relevant to support safety for the target species. Following a review, the FEEDAP Panel excluded 13 publications for reasons described here: low level of copper supplementation, inappropriate length of the study or inappropriate experimental design and insufficient performance data. No adverse effects were reported in the rest of the publications; only two papers were identified that described minor negative performance effects, at levels above the maximum authorised, in fish and in laying hens, which are further described below.

In a study on the effects of dicopper chloride trihydroxide on performance, copper status, antioxidant activities, immune responses and intestinal microbiota of blunt snout bream (Shao et al., 2012), a poorer zootechnical performance was identified at higher copper supplementation levels. Weight gain in fish supplemented with copper levels higher than authorised in EU (34–148 mg Cu/kg) was found to be lower than those in fish fed diets with 11.8 mg Cu/kg feed (p < 0.05). In the previous opinion on the additive under assessment, the FEEDAP Panel derived a theoretical margin of safety (MOS) for fish species of 4, considering the maximum authorised level of 25 mg Cu/kg and a maximum tolerable copper concentration of 100 mg/kg feed for fish (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2011). This case indicates that there could be interspecies sensitivity differences where theoretical MOS may be lower than the one derived for copper exposure in fish; however, additional studies would be necessary to properly evaluate them.

In another study on bioavailability of copper in laying hens (Kim et al., 2016), it was reported that the use of 308 mg Cu/kg feed caused a significant decrease in egg production. However, this supplementation level is more than tenfold above current maximum authorised level for poultry (25 mg Cu/kg feed).

Conclusions on safety for the target species

In the light of current knowledge, the additive under assessment remains safe for target animals under the current conditions of the authorisation.

Safety for the consumer

In the previous opinion, the FEEDAP Panel assessed the copper concentration in liver from poultry, growing cattle and piglets, and from plasma copper and ceruloplasmin levels in cattle and concluded that the available data demonstrated the bioequivalence between copper sulfate and dicopper chloride trihydroxide (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2011). Based on this observation, the FEEDAP Panel assumed that no substantial differences in the disposition behaviour were present between copper sulfate and dicopper chloride trihydroxide, particularly with regard to the deposition of copper in edible tissues. Consequently, it was concluded that consumer exposure to copper from food of animal origin would not be significantly modified by the replacement of copper sulfate by dicopper chloride trihydroxide.

In the current dossier, no specific studies to demonstrate the safety of the additive for the consumer were submitted.

From the literature search 27 conducted by the applicant, 35 papers were retrieved, some of which reporting also copper deposition data from animal studies. The FEEDAP Panel evaluated these studies and noted that, in general, limited deposition data in tissues/organs of animals were available at copper feed levels authorised in EU; some studies were conducted with higher copper supplementation levels, and others used too low copper supplementation levels. Three publications reported data on Cu deposition that are considered by the FEEDAP Panel to be relevant for the current assessment which are described below.

The deposition of copper in weaned piglets at supplementation level of 160 mg Cu/kg for 38 days resulted in copper deposition in liver of 33 mg Cu/kg (Lin et al., 2020). In another study, weaned piglets were supplemented with copper from the additive under assessment at 160 mg Cu/kg feed for 42 days which resulted in liver deposition of 80 mg Cu/kg (Villagómez‐Estrada et al., 2020). The FEEDAP Panel noted that in these studies, the supplementation level was slightly above the EU authorised level (160 mg Cu/kg complete feed versus 150 mg Cu/kg complete feed in the first 4 weeks after weaning). Despite the non‐compliance with the EU legislation, considering that the supplementation level is higher although close to the authorised one, the results of these studies could be considered as supportive evidence of copper liver deposition resulting from the use of dicopper chloride trihydroxide in weaned piglets.

A publication from Shao et al. (2012) reported copper deposition in muscle (0.4 mg Cu/kg fresh tissue) and liver (26 mg Cu/kg fresh tissue) in blunt snout bream (Megalobrama amblycephala) fed practical diet with copper supplementation at 25 mg Cu/kg feed from tribasic copper chloride for 56 days.

The FEEDAP Panel noted that the copper deposition levels reported in these studies are comparable to the previously reported values for copper contents in animal tissues and products from feed supplementation studies (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2011, 2016).

The toxicology of copper compounds has been previously reviewed by the FEEDAP Panel, the NDA Panel and the Scientific Committee (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2015, 2016; EFSA NDA Panel, 2015; EFSA SC, 2023) and by Ellingsen et al. (2015). To the knowledge of the FEEDAP Panel, there are no new relevant toxicological studies on copper that would modify the latest review (EFSA SC, 2023). Under normal circumstances, copper homoeostasis ensures that copper overload in humans does not occur. An excess of copper has been recorded and shown to cause problems only under certain specific conditions, notably genetic disorders such as Wilson disease (EFSA NDA Panel, 2015).

The FEEDAP Panel in 2016 reported that ‘The main food group contributing to copper intake in adults is grains and grain‐based products’. ‘The food group meat and meat products are also an important contributor to copper intake, with an average contribution up to 19% in males and up to 16% in females.’

Dietary upper levels (ULs) for copper have already been established by the Scientific Committee on Food (SCF) for different population classes (SCF, 2003). The SCF derived a tolerable upper intake level (UL) of 5 mg Cu/day for adults and extrapolated UL for toddlers, children and adolescents (e.g. UL of 1 mg/day for toddlers). More recently, the EFSA Scientific Committee re‐evaluated the existing health‐based guidance values for copper and exposure assessment from all sources and established an acceptable daily intake (ADI) for copper (EFSA SC, 2023). In establishing this ADI, the EFSA Scientific Committee placed emphasis on hepatic copper retention as an early marker of potential adverse effects. The EFSA Scientific Committee concluded that no hepatic retention of copper is expected to occur with an intake of 5 mg Cu/day (for a 70 kg adult) and established an ADI of 0.07 mg/kg bw. Dietary exposure to total copper does not exceed the health‐based guidance values (HBGV) in adolescents, adults, elderly and the very elderly. The FEEDAP Panel notes that this ADI is established for the general population and that ULs for all age groups will be established by the NDA Panel in line with the NDA Panel Guidance on establishing and applying tolerable upper intake levels for vitamins and essential minerals (EFSA NDA Panel, 2022).

Safety for the user

The applicant carried out a literature search 28 and submitted new studies on the effects of the additive on eyes and skin. 29 The 14 references retrieved from the literature search on the effects of dicopper chloride trihydroxide on skin and eyes 30 refer to unpublished studies that were not made available and therefore not assessed.

Effect on respiratory system

No specific studies were provided by the applicant regarding the effect of the additive on the respiratory system. The highest dusting potential measured for the additive under assessment was up to 302.5 mg/m3. Therefore, the FEEDAP Panel considered that the exposure through inhalation is likely.

Considering the highest measured concentration of 56.8% copper in the dust, 172 mg Cu/m3 may be released by the dust during handling of the additive; the occupational exposure limit (OEL, 8‐h time‐weighted average – TWA) of 0.01 mg/m3 (respirable fraction; European Commission, 2014) for copper is exceeded.

The highest nickel content measured in the additive was 15.5 mg/kg. Considering a dusting potential of 302.5 mg/m3 and assuming a similar proportion of nickel in the dust like in the additive, the nickel content in the dust would be up to 0.005 mg Ni/m3. Therefore, the nickel content in the dust would not exceed the transitional limit value of 0.1 mg Ni/m3 for the inhalable fraction and 8 h time‐weighted average (8 h TWA) exposure established in Directive (EU) 2022/431. 31 However, due to the presence of nickel, the additive is considered to be a respiratory sensitiser.

Effect on eyes and skin

The skin irritation potential of the additive was investigated in a study 32 performed according to OECD Testing Guideline (TG) 439. Based on the results obtained, the additive is considered a skin irritant.

The additive was further tested in a study to evaluate the skin corrosion potential following OECD TG 431. 33 Based on the results obtained, the additive is not considered to be corrosive to skin.

The eye irritation potential of the additive was investigated in an irritation study 34 performed according to OECD TG 492. Based on the results, the substance was considered potentially irritant or corrosive to the eye, but no prediction can be made regarding the UN‐GHS classification.

The eye irritation potential of the additive was further investigated using an isolated bovine cornea (BCOP) 35 according to the OECD TG 437. The additive induced ocular irritation, but no prediction could be made regarding the UN‐GHS classification.

No specific studies were submitted by the applicant to investigate the potential skin sensitisation of the additive; however, based on the presence of nickel, the additive should be considered a dermal sensitiser.

Conclusions on safety for the user

The FEEDAP Panel concludes that the additive is hazardous by inhalation. The additive is irritant to skin and eyes. It is not corrosive to skin. Based on the presence of nickel, the additive is considered to be a skin and respiratory sensitiser.

Safety for the environment

In 2011, the FEEDAP Panel concluded that dicopper chloride trihydroxide did not represent additional risk to the environment compared to other already authorised sources of copper (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2011).

Dicopper chloride trihydroxide is not soluble in water at neutral pH and there are uncertainties on its toxicokinetics in the various animal species. Nevertheless, there is evidence that the majority of the copper ingested by food producing animals is excreted and found in the manure (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2016). Chlorides are ubiquitous in environmental compartments and have not been further assessed.

Under this assumption, the environmental risk assessment is made assuming that the whole amount of the additive ingested by the animals is excreted and is focused on copper.

To assess the environmental risk of copper from the additive, the Panel compared the worst‐case predicted environmental concentrations (PECs), 36 calculated at the maximum authorised level of copper, with the natural background concentration considered as 90th percentile value from FOREGS Geochemical Baseline Database. 37 If the predicted concentrations fall below 10% of this value, no further risk assessment is needed for trace metals. The determination of natural background concentration for metals in water is described in the Guidance for implementing environmental quality standards (EQSs) for metals, as outlined in the Water Framework Directive implementation strategy. 38

In Europe, based on 840 topsoil samples collected for the FOREGS survey, the 90th percentile for soil was 34 mg Cu/kg dw (range from 0.81 to 256 mg Cu/kg dry weight – dw).

The 90th percentile of copper in inland surface water from 808 water samples collected throughout Europe for the FOREGS survey was 2.45 μg/L (range from 0.08 to 14.6 μg/L).

According to the OSPAR Commission data, 39 the natural background concentration of copper in the marine sediment is 20 mg Cu/kg dw. From the study by Monteiro et al., the background copper concentration in marine sediment from different EU countries was 16.1 mg Cu/kg dw (Monteiro et al., 2010).

Environmental safety from use in feeds for terrestrial farm animals

The worst‐case PECsoil – after 1 year application calculated at the maximum authorised level (150 mg Cu/kg) is 2.5 mg Cu/kg soil dry weight (dw) for piglets, 40 while for the other species, it was in a range of 0.23—0.50 mg Cu/kg dw. It is noted that the piglets will be supplemented at the maximum authorised dose of 150 mg Cu(II)/kg only during the first 4 weeks after weaning, followed by 100 mg Cu/kg from week 5th to 7th after weaning (considering the production process that last for 7 weeks). This means that the actual worst‐case scenario would be somewhat lower than this value. Since PECsoil is below 3.4 mg Cu/kg dw (10% of the natural background concentration in soil), no further risk assessment is needed.

Environmental safety from use in aquaculture feeds

Calculated concentration of the additive in surface water (PECswaq) is 0.063 μg/L for aquaculture, which is below 0.245 μg/L (10% of the natural background concentration in surface waters). The risk to surface freshwater is acceptable when using copper in aquaculture feed on land‐based fish farms under the current conditions of use.

Calculated PECsed in marine sediment is 55 mg/kg dw for ‘other marine species’ (at use level 25 mgCu/kg feed). Considering the reported background concentration of 20 mg Cu/kg dw, the use of the additive in sea cage‐based fish farming may pose a risk to marine environment. The data available do not allow to conclude on the safety of the additive when used in sea cages.

Conclusions on safety for the environment

The FEEDAP Panel concludes that the use of the additive in animal nutrition at the proposed use levels for the terrestrial species and land‐based aquaculture systems is considered safe for the environment. The data available do not allow the conclusion to be made on the safety of the additive for marine sediment when it is used in sea cages.

Efficacy

The present application for renewal of the authorisation does not include a proposal for amending or supplementing the conditions of the original authorisation that would have an impact on the efficacy of the additive. The only relevant change would be an increase in the dissolution rate at pH 4.7 as a result of the new manufacturing process. However, the FEEDAP Panel considers unlikely that this would have an impact on the efficacy of the additive. Therefore, there is no need for assessing the efficacy of the additive in the context of the renewal of the authorisation.

Post‐market monitoring

The FEEDAP Panel considers that there is no need for specific requirements for a post‐market monitoring plan other than those established in the Feed Hygiene Regulation 41 and Good Manufacturing Practice.

Conclusions

The applicant demonstrated that the additive currently in the market complies with the conditions of authorisation in terms of copper content and alpha crystals. The applicant is requesting a modification in the current authorising specification: (i) replacing the alpha crystal form paratacamite with clinoatacamite; (ii) removing the ratio between crystal polymorphs from the authorising specification; (iii) changing the minimum for particles below 50 μm from 1% to 5%. The FEEDAP Panel considers that the proposed modification of the specification would not add any hazard to those already assessed.

The FEEDAP Panel concludes that the additive remains safe for the target species and the consumers.

The FEEDAP Panel concluded that the additive poses a risk by inhalation. The additive is a skin and eye irritant. It is not corrosive to skin. The additive is considered to be a skin and respiratory sensitiser.

The use of the additive in animal nutrition at the proposed use levels for the terrestrial species and land‐based aquaculture systems is considered safe for the environment. The data available do not allow the FEEDAP Panel to reach a conclusion on the safety of the additive for marine sediment when it is used in sea cages.

The proposed modifications of the specification of the additive are not considered to have an impact on the efficacy. Therefore, there is no need for assessing the efficacy of the additive in the context of the renewal of the authorisation.
