The associations of consultant sex on herding‑like prescribing behaviour: evidence from a web-based experiment with female primary care physicians in England
Authors: Sandro Tiziano Stoffel, Matthias Schwenkglenks, Berna Cennet Özdemir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSex and gender influence medical decision making, yet little is known about how the sex of a consultant shapes the prescribing behaviour of female primary care physicians (PCPs). This study examined whether consultant sex affects female PCPs’ likelihood of following recommendations that conflict with clinical guidelines and explored the factors underlying trust in consultants.MethodsWe conducted a web-based experimental study using case vignettes on sleeping medication and antibiotics. A total of 482 female PCPs in England were randomly assigned to conditions with no recommendation or with recommendations from male or female consultants. The outcomes included prescribing decisions, confidence, perceived difficulty, and effort.ResultsIn the absence of recommendations, female PCPs rarely prescribe non-guideline medications, reflecting cautious baseline behaviour. Consultant recommendations increased prescribing across both vignettes, with higher rates in both consultant conditions than in the control condition (sleeping medication: 43.3% male vs. 33.6% female vs. 30.4% control, χ2(2, N = 482) = 6.61, p = 0.037; antibiotics: 14.9% male vs. 14.5% female vs. 5.3% control, χ2(2, N = 482) = 9.55, p = 0.008). Regression analyses confirmed that both male and female consultant recommendations significantly increased the odds of prescribing antibiotics compared with the control condition (male: OR = 3.33, 95% CI [1.43–7.76]; female: OR = 3.29, 95% CI [1.39–7.79]). Only the male consultant’s recommendation significantly increased the prescribing of sleeping medication (OR = 1.89, 95% CI [1.13–3.15]), whereas the female consultant’s recommendation had no significant effect (OR = 1.22, 95% CI [0.71–2.10]). However, direct comparisons between male and female consultants were not statistically significant. Across conditions, participants generally reported high confidence in their decisions and perceived low difficulty and effort. Consultant recommendations in the antibiotic vignette were associated with lower confidence and higher perceived difficulty and effort.ConclusionsConsultant recommendations, independent from consultant’s sex, increased the likelihood of non-guideline prescribing among female PCPs. The absence of statistically significant differences between male and female consultants suggests that the presence of a recommendation itself may be more influential than consultant sex. Strengthening physicians’ confidence and supporting guideline-based decision-making may help reduce susceptibility to social influence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-026-04716-z.

Background

Sex and gender influences on healthcare are increasingly recognized as essential for understanding health outcomes and medical decision-making [1]. Its importance has increased with the increasing number of female physicians [2]. While sex refers to biological differences and gender refers to socially constructed roles, responsibilities, and expectations [1], the two are often conflated [3] despite their distinct and interactive effects on health and healthcare experiences [4].

Studies have shown that female physicians differ from their male colleagues in clinical practice. They are more cautious, adhere more closely to guidelines, and follow evidence-based practice [5–8]. Patients treated by female surgeons experience fewer adverse outcomes [9]. Female physicians also spend more time with patients [10], use patient-centred communication [11], and engage more in shared decision-making [12]. This style has been linked to higher patient satisfaction and fewer malpractice claims [13].

Medical errors remain a major concern because of their impact on patients and healthcare costs [14–16]. In the United States, both patients and physicians frequently report serious errors [17], and systematic reviews confirm their prevalence across settings [18]. In Switzerland, 11 percent of patients experience at least one error over two years [19]. Errors are more common among newly graduated doctors but also occur among senior physicians [20–22]. The contributing factors include slips, lapses, violations, communication failures, and workload [22, 23]. Behavioural economics highlights additional mechanisms such as omission and status quo bias [24], representativeness bias [25], lack of empathy [26], overconfidence [27], and social influence [28]. Social influence can lead to herding behaviour, where physicians follow colleagues’ recommendations rather than making independent, evidence-based decisions. This phenomenon is well documented in financial markets [29–31] but has rarely been studied in medicine. Only three studies have examined peer influence on prescribing [28, 32, 33].

Yang and colleagues reported that Taiwanese physicians’ adoption of new antipsychotics was shaped by colleagues’ prescribing [32]. Saposnik and colleagues reported that 78% of Spanish neurologists treating multiple sclerosis patients followed a peer’s recommendation even when it contradicted guidelines [28]. Our own experimental work demonstrated that primary care physicians were more likely to follow incorrect advice from consultants than from peers and that female physicians were also influenced by consultants’ recommendations [33]. These findings parallel evidence from financial markets, where women also display herding tendencies [34]. However, little is known about whether the sex of the advice giver affects this behaviour in medicine.

Current study

This study examined whether female primary care physicians’ susceptibility to incorrect recommendations depends on the sex of the advising consultants. We carried out a randomized online experiment to examine whether consultant recommendations might lead to prescription errors in primary care, a sector where prescriptions cost £10.4 billion in England during 2022–2023 [35]. Effective prescribing requires physicians to assess each medication’s dosage, frequency, and type [36].

Research question

Does the herding-like behaviour of female primary care physicians depend on the sex of the advice giver?

Methods

To answer this research question, we developed two working hypotheses (WH) that were tested in a randomized, online experiment with 475 female PCPs. A three-group between-subjects design was employed, consisting of a control condition without a consultant recommendation, a treatment condition with a recommendation from a male consultant not supported by best practice guidelines, and a treatment condition with a recommendation from a female consultant not supported by best practice guidelines. The focus on incorrect recommendations was chosen because of their established link to medical errors [33].

The first hypothesis was derived from the focus theory of normative conduct [37], which suggests that individuals adopt descriptive social norms by imitating the behaviour of others in similar situations [38, 39]. In this context, physicians may follow a peer’s recommendation rather than making an independent, evidence-based decision. Accordingly, we hypothesized that female PCPs in the treatment conditions would be less likely to select guideline-supported prescriptions than would those in the control condition (WH1). The second hypothesis was based on evidence that women often stereotype other women as more trustworthy [40], that implicit bias may increase trust in female consultants [41], and that in-group bias fosters greater trust in members of the same social group [42]. We therefore hypothesized that a non-guideline-supported recommendation from a female consultant would more strongly reduce the likelihood of selecting a guideline-supported decision than would an equivalent recommendation from a male consultant (WH2).

Study design

The study design followed Stoffel and colleagues [33]. The experiment was conducted between September and October 2025. Eligible study participants were presented with two case vignettes in random order.

The two case vignettes, adapted to the English setting by medical experts for a recent study [33], involved common prescribing decisions. The first described a 70-year-old patient with insomnia, where the non-guideline option was prescribing sleeping tablets [43]. The second described a 27-year-old woman with a 10-day cough producing yellow sputum but no underlying lung disease, where the non-guideline option was prescribing antibiotics [44]. For each vignette, participants chose between two options: one supported by best practice guidelines (no medication) and one not supported (sleeping tablets or antibiotics).

For each vignette, participants were randomly assigned to one of three experimental conditions using the Qualtrics randomizer [45]. The platform allocated respondents with equal probability (1:1:1) to the control, male consultant, or female consultant condition. Randomisation occurred automatically at survey entry, and no stratification or blocking procedures were applied.

Recruitment procedures

Female PCPs were recruited through the M3 Global Research online panel. No personal identifiers were collected to ensure anonymity. The participants received £9 for completing the survey, which required approximately five minutes.

Outcome variables

The primary outcome was the proportion of PCPs prescribing medication in each vignette. Prescription options were presented in random order, and proportions were compared across conditions. The secondary outcomes included confidence in prescription decisions, perceived difficulty of the decision, and effort invested in decision-making. Confidence was measured on a five-point Likert scale ranging from “Not at all” to “Extremely”. Difficulty was measured on a five-point scale, and effort was measured on a five-point scale ranging from “None” to “Great” [46]. Finally, the study participants were asked two close-ended questions about factors associated with trust in consultants, as well as measures of risk preference and decision style. Risk preferences were assessed with a single item [47], rated on a 0–10 scale (0 = risk averse, 10 = risk prone). Decision styles were measured via the Decision Style Scale (DSS), which comprises five rational style and five intuitive style items [48], with scores ranging from 5–25; higher values indicating stronger tendencies toward the respective style [49]. The questionnaire and dataset is available on Open Science Framework (OSF) [50].

Sample size and statistical analysis

The sample size was based on a previous experimental study using equivalent vignettes [33]. Assuming independent responses per vignette, 475 participants were estimated to provide 80% power to detect differences of at least 17% in prescribing behaviour across conditions, using a two-sided chi-squared test with α = 0.05 [51].

We first applied chi-squared tests for each vignette to assess differences in prescribing behaviour across conditions and subsequently employed mixed-effects logistic regression models to account for repeated measures and formally test hypotheses. These models included fixed effects for the experimental condition, vignette type, and their interaction, with a random intercept for participants. Secondary outcomes (confidence, difficulty, effort) were analysed with mixed-effects ordered logistic regression models. Likelihood ratio tests indicated that the inclusion of random effects improved the model fit relative to standard logistic models. The estimated odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) from these regression analyses are presented in the result section, while the complete regression outputs are provided in the Tables S3 and S4 in the additional file 1.

Study participants

Figure 1 presents the study flowchart. A total of 3,074 female PCPs in England registered with the M3 Global Research Panel were invited to participate between September and October 2025. Of these, 677 (22.0%) accessed the survey, and 648 (95.7%) provided informed consent. One hundred forty-four participants (22.2%) were excluded because they had not worked as general practitioners for at least two years, and 22 (3.4%) did not complete the survey. Prescribing medication in the two case vignettes

The estimated odds ratios (ORs) in Table 2 show that, compared with the control group, participants were significantly more likely to prescribe antibiotics when the vignette featured either a female consultant (OR = 3.29, 95% CI: 1.39–7.79) or a male consultant (OR = 3.33, 95% CI: 1.43–7.76). The direct comparison between male and female consultants indicated no statistical difference (OR = 1.01, 95% CI: 0.51–1.99).

For sleeping medication, prescribing was significantly higher in the male consultant condition relative to control (OR = 1.89, 95% CI: 1.13–3.15), whereas the female consultant condition did not differ from control (OR = 1.22, 95% CI: 0.71–2.10). However, the male–female consultant comparison was not statistically significant (OR = 1.55, 95% CI: 0.91–2.62), i.e. we could not confirm that consultant sex differentially affected sleeping‑medication prescribing.

Trust in consultants

As shown in Tables S5 and S6 in the Additional file 1), study participants most frequently attributed their trust in consultants to perceived medical expertise (90.7%) and clinical experience (76.1%). Other commonly cited factors included communication skills (66.8%), shared values (66.4%), and professional credentials (61.6%). When asked about consultants more generally, 42.2% of the respondents agreed that consultants possess greater knowledge than do PCPs, whereas only 32.2% agreed that they have more advanced training.

Discussion

This study examined how female primary care physicians’ prescribing decisions are influenced by consultant recommendations, paying particular attention to whether consultant sex plays any role in shaping responses to advice that conflicts with clinical guidelines.

Our findings indicate that female PCPs are not passive recipients of consultant advice but respond systematically to the social context in which recommendations are given. In the absence of a recommendation, they rarely prescribe non-guideline medications, reflecting cautious baseline behaviour aligned with clinical guidelines. This finding is consistent with prior evidence that female physicians are generally more cautious, adhere more closely to guidelines, and follow evidence-based practices closer than their male colleagues [5–8]. Consistent with the first working hypothesis, consultant recommendations in general increased the likelihood of non-guideline prescribing. Male consultants significantly increased prescribing in the sleeping medication vignette, and both male and female consultants increased prescribing in the antibiotics vignette. However, different to the second working hypothesis, the direct comparisons between male and female consultants showed no statistically significant differences for either vignette. This indicates that the presence of a recommendation itself, rather than consultant sex, may have been the primary driver of prescribing changes.

Although the second working hypothesis predicted stronger influence from female consultants due to in-group bias and gender-based trust expectations, this pattern did not emerge. Several explanations are plausible. The study was powered to detect relatively large effects and may not have been sensitive to smaller sex-based differences. Strong normative authority cues that overwhelm expectations relating to sex may also be present in the consultant role. Furthermore, processes that function independently of consultant sex include perceived seniority, specialization, and defensive prescribing under perceived scrutiny.

These findings extend earlier work showing that physicians are influenced by peers’ or consultants’ recommendation [28, 32, 33] by highlighting that social cues embedded in the interaction, such as consultant identity, may shape prescribing decisions, even if the specific role of consultant sex remains inconclusive.

Several additional factors may also explain the increased prescribing observed in the treatment conditions. Participants may have inferred greater seniority, specialization, or clinical authority from the consultant role itself, independent of sex. Normative expectations surrounding consultant–PCP hierarchies, or defensive prescribing under perceived scrutiny, may likewise have contributed. Although our trust measures provide descriptive insight into factors PCPs associate with consultant credibility, they do not directly test these mechanisms. Female PCPs reported that medical expertise and clinical experience were the most important factors underlying trust in consultants. However, their beliefs about consultants’ superior knowledge and training were mixed, with many respondents expressing neutrality or disagreement. This suggests that PCPs may follow incorrect recommendations not because of clear evidence of superior expertise but because of professional norms and perceived authority. These interpretations are still preliminary and emphasise the need for more studies on the ways in which social expectations, hierarchy, and knowledge interact to influence prescription behaviour.

The implications are significant. Social influence can increase the risk of inappropriate prescribing. This finding resonates with behavioural economics research showing that social influence and herding can bias decision-making [28–30]. In our study, the effect of male consultants’ recommendations replicates findings from financial markets, where women also display herding tendencies [34]. Interventions are therefore needed to strengthen female PCPs’ confidence in their own clinical judgment and reduce reliance on authority cues. Training that explicitly addresses the role of social dynamics in clinical decision-making, combined with decision support systems that reinforce guideline-based practice, may help mitigate these risks. More broadly, our findings underscore that prescribing behaviour is shaped not only by clinical knowledge but also by social context and that addressing gendered patterns of influence is important for improving patient safety.

This study has several strengths. It investigated herding-like behaviour in two different treatment decision settings in primary care, thereby extending previous research on social influence in medicine [28, 32, 33]. Compared with earlier work by our group, it also assessed factors associated with trust in consultants and beliefs about their knowledge and training, which helps explain the observed prescribing patterns. Consistent with prior studies, we found that physicians’ trust is shaped not only by perceived competence but also by communication [11]. The use of the same experimental design and vignettes as those used in our previous study further strengthens comparability. Interestingly, the proportion of female PCPs prescribing in the control condition was lower in this study than in the earlier experiment, and the effect of a recommendation from an unspecified consultant was also weaker. This finding suggests that specifying the consultant’s sex may alter the dynamics of influence.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The study relied on a web-based experimental design with hypothetical case vignettes and treatment intentions as outcomes, which limits behavioural validation in real-world practice [22, 23]. The sample was recruited through a commercial survey vendor, which may restrict generalizability. As in the previous study, we did not include questions about participants’ perceptions of the recommendations or their sources, which could have provided additional explanatory insight. Furthermore, although the sample size was adequate to detect overall differences in prescribing behaviour across conditions, the study may have been underpowered to detect smaller differences between male and female consultants. As a result, the absence of statistically significant sex-specific effects should be interpreted with caution. Finally, the web-based format may have offered a limited clinical context, potentially influencing prescribing decisions. Although we attempted to address this by assessing confidence in prescription recommendations and most participants reported being very or extremely confident, this does not fully compensate for the complexity of real-world decision-making.

Conclusions

Overall, our study reveals that consultant recommendations can increase the likelihood of non-guideline prescribing among female PCPs in England. While some patterns suggest that consultant sex may shape these dynamics, the evidence does not support strong causal claims about sex-specific effects. Instead, the results highlight the broader influence of consultant input and the need to better understand how social and hierarchical cues shape clinical decision making. Future research should examine whether these dynamics occur in real clinical settings and explore strategies to strengthen physicians’ confidence in their own judgment while reducing reliance on authority cues.
