Integrated metabolomics and transcriptomics analysis of flavonoids in different parts of Houttuynia cordata
Authors: Jingjian Liu, Juncheng Lin, Huimin Liu, Changhu Ke, Fangling Ren, Lun Wu, Pengju Wang, Fang Peng, Tingwen Ming, Zhihao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHouttuynia cordata (H. cordata) is an important plant used for both medicinal and edible purposes. However, the metabolic profile and biosynthetic pathways of its flavonoids are still poorly understood. Therefore, metabolomics and transcriptomics were employed to analyze the roots, stems, and leaves of H. cordata, aiming to clarify this issue.ResultsA total of 471 metabolites were identified, with flavonoids being the predominant class. Key flavonoids, including quercetin, kaempferol, and rutin, showed the highest relative abundance in leaves, subsequently confirmed by targeted absolute quantification. Transcriptomic analysis revealed a large number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) among tissues. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis revealed that the phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis pathways were significantly enriched among the DEGs. The majority of DEGs within these pathways, such as those encoding PAL, 4CL, CHS, and CHI, were found to be up-regulated in the leaves. These genes were further validated by real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR), which showed consistent results with the transcriptomic data.ConclusionsThis work provides a comprehensive overview of the metabolite distribution and gene expression in different parts of H. cordata. Our findings highlight the leaf as the primary site for flavonoid accumulation and biosynthesis in H. cordata, laying the foundation for the utilization of candidate genes and future functional characterization of these pathways.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12961-y.Keywords: Houttuynia cordata, Metabolomics, Transcriptomics, Flavonoid biosynthesis

Introduction

Houttuynia cordata (H. cordata) is a common perennial herbaceous plant widely distributed in Asia and North America [1]. In China, its main distribution includes central, southeastern, and southwestern provinces, especially Sichuan, Hubei, Hunan, and Jiangsu. H. cordata not only has good edible value but also attracts much attention as a traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) [2]. Modern research has found that H. cordata is rich in various active ingredients, such as flavonoids, volatile oils, alkaloids, and polysaccharides [3]. Among these, flavonoids are particularly noteworthy for their significant pharmacological effects, including anti-inflammatory, anti-tumor, and antiviral properties [4–6]. Therefore, clarifying the compounds in H. cordata, especially flavonoids, and their biosynthetic pathways is of great importance for the development and rational utilization of this plant.

The medicinal quality of herbal plants is influenced by factors such as cultivation location, method, and harvesting time [7]. Furthermore, chemical constituents often vary across different plant parts, leading to tissue-specific medicinal properties [8]. In H. cordata, tissue-specific chemical variation has been observed. For instance, Lin et al. reported significant differences in volatile compounds among roots, stalks, and leaves using headspace solid-phase microextraction (HS-SPME) [9]. Liu et al. demonstrated that a polysaccharide-whey protein complex derived from leaves exhibited superior antioxidant activity compared to its stem-derived counterpart [10]. Although Qi et al. investigated metabolites in different parts of H. cordata using HS-SPME, their study was limited by the analytical techniques available at the time [11].

Recent advances in omics technologies have enabled powerful integrative approaches to study plant secondary metabolism. The combination of metabolomics and transcriptomics has been successfully applied to elucidate the molecular basis of metabolite biosynthesis in various medicinal plants, such as Chrysanthemum, Paeonia veitchii, and Gardenia jasminoides [12–14]. In H. cordata, two recent studies have significantly advanced our understanding of flavonoid biosynthesis. Yang et al. published a near-complete genome assembly of H. cordata, and identified key genes involved in flavonoid biosynthesis and houttuynin metabolism [15]. Liu et al. performed combined metabolomic and transcriptomic analyses on two H. cordata accessions with contrasting flavonoid contents, revealing differences at the metabolite and gene expression levels and pinpointing candidate genes that potentially influence flavonoid accumulation [16]. However, both studies have certain limitations. On one hand, their analysis of flavonoid metabolites was primarily based on relative quantification, lacking absolute content data for key compounds. On the other hand, their criteria for identifying differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were relatively lenient, which may increase the likelihood of false-positive results.

Flavonoids are a large class of plant secondary metabolites with diverse structures, including the chalcones, flavones, flavonols, flavandiols, anthocyanins, and condensed tannins [17]. They play essential roles in plant growth, development, and stress responses, such as protection against pests, diseases, UV radiation, and oxidative stress [18–20]. The flavonoid biosynthesis pathway is relatively well-conserved across plant species [21]. It begins with the phenylpropanoid pathway, where phenylalanine is converted to p-coumaroyl-CoA via sequential actions of phenylalanine ammonia lyase (PAL), cinnamate 4-hydroxylase (C4H), and 4-coumarate-CoA ligase (4CL) [22]. Chalcone synthase (CHS) and chalcone isomerase (CHI) then catalyze the formation of flavanones, which are subsequently converted to dihydroflavonols by flavanone 3-hydroxylase (F3H) and flavonoid 3'-hydroxylase (F3'H) [23]. Dihydroflavonols serve as a key branching point: they can be oxidized by flavonol synthase (FLS) to produce flavonols, or reduced by dihydroflavonol 4-reductase (DFR) to initiate anthocyanin and proanthocyanidin biosynthesis [24]. Anthocyanidin synthase (ANS) converts leucoanthocyanidins to unstable anthocyanidins, which are stabilized by glycosylation via UDP-glucose. Flavonoid 3-O-glucosyltransferase (UFGT) and transported into vacuoles by glutathione S-transferases (GSTs) for storage [25, 26]. Proanthocyanidins are formed from leucoanthocyanidins by leucoanthocyanidin reductase (LAR) or from anthocyanidins by anthocyanidin reductase (ANR), followed by polymerization [27, 28]. Despite the well-established nature of this pathway in model plants, its specific regulatory mechanisms in H. cordata remain poorly understood.

In this study, we conducted an integrated metabolomic and transcriptomic analysis to systematically investigate the tissue-specific distribution of flavonoids and the expression patterns of related genes in the roots, stems, and leaves of H. cordata. We adopted more stringent criteria for screening DEGs and performed absolute quantification of key flavonoids, including rutin, quercetin, and kaempferol, providing precise evidence for the differential distribution of these metabolites. This study aims to elucidate the distribution patterns of flavonoids in different parts of H. cordata and lay a foundation for understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying their biosynthesis, thereby offering theoretical support for the rational utilization and genetic improvement of this important medicinal plant.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

H. cordata was collected in Yichang City, Hubei Province, China in April 2024 and identified by Professor Zhi-Hao Li from Sinopharm Dongfeng General Hospital, Hubei University of Medicine. A voucher specimen was deposited at the Herbarium of Wuhan Botanical Garden, Chinese Academy of Sciences (voucher number: HIB0165042), with identification confirmed by Shuxia Fu. H. cordata used in this study were collected from the same batch and were in the vegetative growth phase. The plants grew in a wild, semi-shaded environment near moist valleys. The collection sites were approximately 380 m above sea level and are characterized by a subtropical monsoon climate. For each group, six samples were prepared for metabolomics analysis and four for transcriptomics analysis, with each sample consisting of a pooled mixture from six individual plants [29]. After collection, all samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen to preserve their structural and biological integrity. They were then stored at −80 °C for subsequent analysis. H. cordata is a common plant in China. The collection and study of samples in this work comply with relevant Chinese regulations, and no specific permits were required.

Non-targeted metabolomics

All chemicals and reagents used in this study, including methanol and acetonitrile (LC–MS grade), were purchased from ANPEL. Ultra-pure water was prepared using a Milli-Q water purification system (Millipore, Bedford, MA, USA). The sample preparation for H. cordata was carried out through a series of steps. Initially, the sample was thawed at 4 °C. Then, 1 mL of an extraction solvent consisting of water, acetonitrile, and isopropyl alcohol in equal volumetric ratios (1:1:1, v/v/v) was added to 100 mg of the sample. The mixture was vortexed and centrifuged for 1 min, followed by ultrasonic-assisted extraction at a low temperature for 30 min. After centrifugation at 11,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected. The supernatant was then stored at −20 °C for 1 h and centrifuged again under the same conditions. The resulting supernatant was transferred to a vacuum drying chamber and evaporated to dryness. The dried residue was reconstituted in 200 µL of 30% acetonitrile solution, thoroughly mixed, and centrifuged at 14,970 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. Finally, the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter and transferred into HPLC–MS injection vials [30].

Sample analysis was performed using a UPLC-Orbitrap-MS system consisting of a Vanquish UPLC and an HFX mass spectrometer. Chromatographic separation was carried out on a Waters HSS T3 column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm particle size) maintained at 40 °C. The mobile phase flow rate was 0.3 mL/min, and the injection volume was 2 μL. The mobile phase consisted of phase A (Milli-Q water with 0.1% formic acid) and phase B (acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid). The gradient elution program was as follows: 0–1 min, 100% A (v/v); 1–13 min, linear gradient to 5% A and 95% B (v/v), maintained until 13 min. To assess system stability and ensure data reliability, a quality control (QC) sample was prepared by pooling equal volumes of all test samples.

Mass spectrometric detection was conducted using a Q Exactive HFX Orbitrap hybrid quadrupole mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a heated electrospray ionization (HESI) source, enabling high-resolution mass spectra acquisition. Data were acquired using the full MS data-dependent MS2 (ddMS2) mode. The source parameters were set as follows: sheath gas pressure, 40 Arb; auxiliary gas pressure, 10 Arb; spray voltage, + 3000 V (positive) and –2800 V (negative); source temperature, 350 °C; and ion transfer tube temperature, 320 °C. Full-scan mass spectra were collected over an m/z range of 70–1050 at resolutions of 70,000 (MS1) and 17,500 (MS2).

Data preprocessing was performed using Progenesis QI software (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA). The workflow included baseline filtering, peak detection and matching, retention time correction, and peak alignment, ultimately generating a data matrix containing retention time, mass-to-charge ratio, and peak intensity [31]. Peaks with corresponding MS2 data were identified by matching against a commercial database, an in-house secondary mass spectrometry database (self-built by Honest Biotech Co., Ltd.), and diagnostic fragmentation patterns.

Multivariate statistical analyses were conducted using the R package (version 1.32.0). Principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least-squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) were performed using the ropls function in R [32]. For OPLS-DA, data were Pareto-scaled, and permutation tests (200 permutations) were conducted to evaluate model validity and avoid overfitting [33]. Volcano plots were generated using ggplot2 (version 3.4.2; https://ggplot2.tidyverse.org/), and heatmaps were created using pheatmap (version 1.0.12; https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/pheatmap/index.html).

Transcriptomics

Total RNA was isolated from the samples using the RNAprep Pure Plant Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. RNA purity and concentration were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA), and RNA integrity was evaluated using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, California, USA). cDNA library construction and sequencing were performed by Honest Biotech Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Briefly, total RNA was first treated with RNase-free DNase, followed by mRNA enrichment using mRNA Capture Beads (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China). The enriched mRNA was then fragmented and used as a template for first-strand complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis with random hexamer primers. Second-strand synthesis was subsequently performed, and the resulting double-stranded cDNA was purified using VAHTSTM DNA Clean Beads (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China). After purification, end repair was carried out, and sequencing adapters with index sequences were ligated. Fragments of appropriate size were selected, amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR), and purified to generate the final cDNA library. The library was sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Honest Biotech Co., Ltd.) with a paired-end read length of 150 bp, generating approximately 6 Gb of clean data per sample.

Raw sequencing reads were filtered to remove those containing adapter sequences, ambiguous nucleotides ('N'), or low-quality bases. Reads with extreme signal intensity, overall low quality, or length shorter than 20 nucleotides were discarded. Bases with a quality score (Q) < 10 at the 3' end, as well as adapter sequences, were trimmed. The resulting high-quality clean reads were then assembled de novo into unigenes using Trinity software [34]. After de novo assembly, the quality of the transcriptome assembly was evaluated. A total of 128,330 unique transcripts were obtained, with an N50 length of 1,582bp, an average length of 1,246bp, and a GC content ranging from 40 to 60%. The coding potential of unigenes was predicted using CPC2. For functional annotation, all unique transcripts were aligned against multiple public databases using BLAST (BlastX algorithm) with an E-value threshold of 1.0e-5 [35], including the NCBI non-redundant protein (Nr) database, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database, Gene Ontology (GO), and evolutionary genealogy of genes: Non-supervised Orthologous Groups (eggNOG) database. Differential expression analysis between sample groups was performed using the DESeq2 package [36].

Correlation analysis between metabolomics and transcriptomics

The pathways involved in differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs) and DEGs were intersected to obtain a common pathway, and R Software Venn package was used for visualization [37]. The common pathway was further displayed in a bar chart. Gene co-expression networks were constructed using the weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) approach with R packages (version 3.2.2) [38]. Here, we used the normalized quantile function in the R software package to normalize the gene expression data. We selected the expression matrix of 31,909 genes with the sum FPKM value in all tissues > 1.0 from all genes as the input file for WGCNA to identify gene modules with strong co-expression.

Targeted metabolomics

For targeted validation, approximately 100 mg of sample was extracted with 0.5 mL of 80% aqueous methanol containing 0.2% vitamin C. The mixture was vortexed, ultrasonicated at room temperature for 30 min, and centrifuged at 13,700 × g for 10 min. The residue was re-extracted once under the same conditions, and the combined supernatants were appropriately diluted prior to UPLC-MS/MS analysis. The UPLC-MS/MS system consisted of a Vanquish UPLC and a Q Exactive high-resolution mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) equipped with an electrospray ionization source operating in negative ion mode. Full-scan mass spectra were acquired over an m/z range of 100–900 at a resolution of 70,000. Metabolites were identified by matching retention time and accurate molecular mass (mass error < 10 ppm) of authentic standards. The chromatographic conditions are as follows: the chromatographic column is Waters HSS T3 (50 × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm). Mobile phase A is an ultrapure water solution containing 0.1% formic acid, and B is an acetonitrile solution containing 0.1% formic acid. The flow rate is 0.3 mL/min. The column temperature is 40 ℃. The injection volume is 2 μL. Gradient elution program: 0 min (90% A, 10% B), 2 min (90% A, 10% B), 6 min (40% A, 60% B), 9 min (40% A, 60% B), 9.1 min (90% A, 10% B), 12 min (90% A, 10% B). Details of authentic standards are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Assessment by real-time quantitative PCR(RT-qPCR)

To validate the expression patterns of key genes involved in flavonoid biosynthesis identified by RNA-seq, ten candidate genes were selected for RT-qPCR analysis. Total RNA was extracted from roots, stems, and leaves using the FastPure® Plant Total RNA Isolation Kit (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer's protocol. RNA purity and concentration were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA), and RNA integrity was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis. First-strand cDNA synthesis and quantitative PCR were performed in a one-step reaction using the HiScript II One Step qRT-PCR SYBR Green Kit (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China) on an ABI StepOne Plus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA). The 20 μL reaction mixture contained 10 μL of 2 × One Step SYBR Green Mix, 1 μL of One Step SYBR Green Enzyme Mix, 0.4 μL of 50 × ROX Reference Dye 1, 0.3 μL each of gene-specific forward primers (10 μM), 0.3 μL of gene-specific RT primer, approximately 100 ng of total RNA template, and RNase-free ddH₂O to adjust the final volume. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: reverse transcription at 50 °C for 10 min, initial denaturation at 95 °C for 1 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 60 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s. Melting curve analysis was performed from 55 °C to 95 °C to verify amplification specificity. Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as the internal reference gene for normalization [15]. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences for all candidate genes are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Result

Metabolite analysis of different parts of H. cordata

A total of 471 metabolites were detected across all samples of H. cordata, and their classification is presented in Fig. 1A. Flavonoids were the most abundant class, accounting for 14.0% of all detected compounds, followed by carboxylic acids and derivatives (13.6%), prenol lipids (6.8%), and other categories. To assess the reliability of the metabolomic data, we first evaluated the stability and reproducibility of the analytical platform. The QC samples clustered tightly in the PCA score plot (Fig. S1A), and 86.6% of the detected peaks exhibited a relative standard deviation (RSD) below 30% in QC samples (Fig. S1B), indicating good repeatability and a stable analytical process [39]. We then analyzed the overall metabolic differences among the three tissue types. OPLS-DA of the roots, stems, and leaves samples revealed clear separation between groups and strong clustering within groups (Fig. 1B–D), suggesting significant metabolic differences among tissues. The validity of the OPLS-DA models was confirmed by permutation testing (Fig. S2A–C) [40].

Metabolite classification pie chart and OPLS-DA plot. A Metabolite classification pie chart. B-D OPLS-DA scoring diagram of leaves and roots, leaves and stems, stems and roots, respectively (n = 6)

To further investigate the metabolite distribution across different tissues of H. cordata, pairwise comparisons were performed. Volcano plots were used for preliminary screening of metabolites with significant changes between groups, based on the criteria of fold change (FC) > 1.5 or FC < 0.67 and P < 0.05 (Fig. S3A–C). For more rigorous identification of DEMs, a combined criterion of variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1 and P < 0.05 was applied. The numbers of DEMs identified in each pairwise comparison are summarized in Table 1. In the comparison between leaves and roots, 26 DEMs were identified, of which 23 were up-regulated and 3 were down-regulated in leaves. Flavonoids were the predominant class, accounting for 53.8% of these DEMs, and were highly accumulated in leaves (Fig. 2A). The main flavonoid DEMs included rutin, quercetin, kaempferol, and their derivatives. In the comparison between leaves and stems, 21 DEMs were identified, with 17 up-regulated and 4 down-regulated in leaves. Flavonoids remained the dominant class, comprising 52.4% of the DEMs (Fig. 2B). And flavonoids such as rutin, quercetin, kaempferol, and their derivatives exhibited higher levels in leaves than in stems. In the comparison between stems and roots, 32 DEMs were identified, with 29 up-regulated in stems and 3 down-regulated. Flavonoids again constituted a major portion of these DEMs (37.5%), with higher accumulation in stems compared to roots, primarily including rutin, quercetin, and their derivatives (Fig. 2C). Pairwise Venn diagrams of total metabolites and DEMs across different tissues are presented in Fig. 2D and E. It should be noted that all percentages reported above represent the proportions of DEMs by number, not by abundance.

Targeted metabolomics validation and co-expression network analysis of flavonoids

To validate the findings from non-targeted metabolomics, we performed targeted absolute quantification of key flavonoids in roots, stems, and leaves of H. cordata. As shown in Fig. 7A, the absolute concentrations of all six compounds exhibited consistent tissue-specific accumulation patterns, with the highest levels in leaves, moderate levels in stems, and the lowest in roots. This trend is in strong agreement with the relative abundance patterns observed in the non-targeted metabolomic analysis. The concordance between relative and absolute quantification not only confirms the reliability of our metabolomic data but also provides precise biochemical evidence for the preferential accumulation of these bioactive flavonoids in the leaves of H. cordata. To identify gene modules associated with flavonoid accumulation, we performed WGCNA using genes and key flavonoids. As shown in Fig. 7B, the MEturquoise module exhibited the strongest positive correlations with these key flavonoids, indicating that genes within this module are closely related to flavonoid biosynthesis. Key genes, including CHS, CHI, and F3H were identified within this module, further supporting their crucial roles in flavonoid accumulation in H. cordata.

Discussion

Tissue-specific accumulation of flavonoids in H. cordata

H. cordata is widely used as both a medicinal and edible plant in China [41, 42]. However, the metabolite composition in its different parts has not been thoroughly characterized. In our work, non-targeted metabolomics analysis revealed that flavonoids are the primary differential metabolites in the roots, stems, and leaves, with the highest relative abundance observed in the leaves. Further absolute quantitative analysis of flavonoids confirmed that key flavonoids such as rutin, quercetin, and kaempferol are most concentrated in the leaves, followed by the stems, and are least abundant in the roots. The enrichment of flavonoid compounds, including rutin, quercetin, and kaempferol, in the leaves may reflect their ecological roles in above-ground tissues. For instance, leaves, as the most exposed organs, may require higher levels of these protective flavonoids to prevent UV-induced damage [43]. This hypothesis is supported by the enrichment of the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway, particularly in comparisons related to the leaves.

Key genes involved in flavonoid biosynthesis

Transcriptomic analysis revealed that genes encoding key enzymes in the phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis pathways, such as PAL, 4CL, CHS, CHI, and F3H, exhibited the highest expression in leaves, moderate expression in stems, and the lowest expression in roots. These expression trends were further validated by RT-qPCR. The up-regulation of these structural genes in leaves suggests their critical role in driving tissue-specific flavonoid biosynthesis. PAL and 4CL catalyze the initial steps of the phenylpropanoid pathway, providing precursors for flavonoid synthesis [44, 45]. CHS and CHI are responsible for the formation of naringenin, a central intermediate in flavonoid metabolism [46, 47]. Subsequently, F3H converts flavanones into dihydroflavonols, which are precursors for various flavonoid subclasses, including flavonols and anthocyanins [48]. In our study, the high expression of these genes in leaves was closely associated with elevated levels of downstream products such as rutin, quercetin, and kaempferol, indicating their involvement in the biosynthesis of flavonoids in H. cordata.

The biosynthesis of flavonoids in H. cordata

In summary, based on the analysis of the existing flavonoid biosynthesis pathways in plants, we have mapped out the possible metabolic pathways of flavonoid-related metabolites in the H. cordata [49, 50] (Fig. 9). The pathway initiates with the phenylpropanoid metabolic route. The high expression of PAL and 4CL in leaves, coupled with the substantial accumulation of downstream flavonoids in the same tissue, indicates that this pathway is most active in leaves. Subsequent catalysis by CHS and CHI yields naringenin. Here, the enrichment of naringenin in leaves aligns with the elevated expression of CHS and CHI in this tissue, suggesting these early steps are particularly vigorous in leaf tissue. Naringenin is then converted by F3H into dihydroflavonols. From dihydroflavonols, the pathway diverges into two main branches. The first branch proceeds via FLS into flavonol biosynthesis, generating the aglycones kaempferol and quercetin. The consistent accumulation of these flavonols in leaves, alongside the high expression of FLS, supports the activity of this branch in leaves. The second branch, directed by DFR and ANS, leads to anthocyanin and proanthocyanidin biosynthesis. However, compared to flavonols, these end products were less prominent in our metabolic profiling. Additionally, UGT genes are highly expressed in leaves, a step responsible for converting unstable aglycones into stable, soluble glycosides suitable for vacuolar storage [51]. Therefore, the tissue-specific accumulation of flavonoids in H. cordata reflects the coordinated regulation of the entire biosynthetic pathway: from the early entry steps (PAL,4CL), through core ring formation (CHS, CHI, F3H), to branch-specific synthesis (FLS) and final modifications (UGTs). Leaves serve as the primary site where this complete biosynthesis is most active, producing and modifying flavonoids, likely to fulfill protective functions in these exposed above-ground organs.

Comparison with previous studies, limitations, and future directions

Recent studies have significantly advanced our understanding of flavonoid biosynthesis in H. cordata. Yang et al. [15] published a high-quality genome assembly of H. cordata and identified key biosynthetic genes through integrative multi-omics analysis. Liu et al. [16] compared two H. cordata accessions with contrasting flavonoid content and screened out candidate genes including CHI, F3H, FLS, and ANS. However, both studies have certain methodological limitations. Their metabolite analyses were primarily based on relative quantification, lacking absolute content data for key compounds. Moreover, the selection criteria for DEGs (|FC|≥ 2) were relatively permissive, potentially increasing the probability of false-positive results. In contrast, this study employed a more stringent DEGs screening threshold (|log₂FC|≥ 2), enhancing the robustness of transcriptomic data, and performed targeted absolute quantification of key flavonoids (rutin, quercetin, kaempferol, etc.), providing precise concentration data to support the non-targeted metabolomics findings. Despite these advances, this study has certain limitations. First, the findings are correlative in nature, and causal relationships between gene expression and metabolite accumulation have not yet been established. The proposed biosynthetic model remains speculative and requires experimental validation. Second, constrained by database limitations, some H. cordata-specific metabolites, such as houttuynoids, were not detected in this work [52]. Future research should focus on functional validation of the candidate genes identified in this work, such as employing virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) technology for validation in H. cordata itself, to confirm the roles of key structural genes and transcription factors in flavonoid biosynthesis [53]. Furthermore, CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene knockout, particularly targeting genes encoding downstream modifying enzymes such as UGTs, could provide direct evidence for the involvement of these genes in flavonoid glycosylation and transport processes [54].

Conclusions

This study provides integrated metabolomic and transcriptomic evidence that leaves are the primary site of flavonoid biosynthesis and accumulation in H. cordata. The coordinated high expression of key structural genes (PAL, 4CL, CHS, CHI) in leaves correlates with the elevated levels of flavonoid metabolites, including kaempferol, quercetin, and rutin. Targeted absolute quantification further confirmed the tissue-specific accumulation patterns of these bioactive compounds, with highest concentrations in leaves, followed by stems, and lowest in roots. These findings provide insights into the tissue-specific distribution of flavonoids in H. cordata and highlight leaves as a valuable source of these compounds. Collectively, this work lays a foundation for further functional characterization of candidate genes and supports the rational utilization and genetic improvement of H. cordata resources.
