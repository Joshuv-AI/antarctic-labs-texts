The association of SPISE with cardiometabolic multimorbidity and stroke in middle-aged and older adults: a nationwide prospective cohort study
Authors: Zhuo-lei Cai, Fei Dong, Yingjun Zhou, Dujun Wang, Wenmin Yi, Zhi-you Cai, Lei Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Insulin resistance is a key driver of cardiometabolic diseases, including stroke. The single-point insulin sensitivity estimator (SPISE) is a novel non-invasive marker of insulin sensitivity calculated from fasting triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol and body mass index. However, its association with cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) and stroke in the general population remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the prospective association between SPISE and incident CMM and stroke in middle-aged and older Chinese adults. This prospective cohort study included 6,624 participants aged 45 years and older from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) with follow-up through 2020. SPISE was calculated using a validated formula. Cox proportional hazards regression models, restricted cubic spline analyses, Kaplan-Meier survival curves, and subgroup analyses were employed to evaluate the associations between SPISE and incident CMM and stroke. Over a median follow-up of 114 months, 632 participants (9.5%) developed incident stroke, and 696 (10.5%) developed CMM. In fully adjusted multivariable models, each one-unit increase in the baseline SPISE index was significantly associated with a 10% decreased risk of incident stroke (HR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.86–0.94) and a 15% decreased risk of CMM (HR = 0.85, 95% CI: 0.81–0.89). Compared to the low SPISE reference group (< 6.61), individuals in the high SPISE category (≥ 6.61) exhibited a 27% lower risk of stroke (HR = 0.73, 95% CI: 0.62–0.88) and a 48% lower risk of CMM (HR = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.43–0.62). Restricted cubic spline analyses confirmed strictly linear, inverse dose-response relationships between SPISE and both outcomes. These protective associations remained structurally robust across diverse demographic and clinical subgroups, with significant effect modification identified solely for baseline heart disease and age concerning the CMM outcome. Higher SPISE is independently and linearly associated with reduced risks of incident CMM and stroke in middle-aged and older Chinese adults. As a simple, low-cost marker derived from routine clinical measurements, SPISE may serve as a practical tool for cardiometabolic risk stratification in primary care settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-61974-w.

Introduction

The global aging population is currently confronting an epidemic of cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM)—defined as the co-occurrence of two or more cardiometabolic diseases, such as type 2 diabetes, stroke, and ischemic heart disease1–3. CMM not only synergistically amplifies the risk of severe disability and premature mortality but also imposes a staggering economic burden on healthcare systems4. To mitigate this growing crisis, the American Heart Association has recently emphasized the concept of the Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) syndrome, highlighting a critical window for primary prevention5. Specifically, targeting individuals in CKM stages 0 to 3—ranging from those with healthy metabolic profiles to those with metabolic risk factors and subclinical target organ damage—is a pressing global health priority6. Identifying high-risk individuals within this specific CKM population, before they progress to irreversible clinical events such as acute stroke, remains the most effective strategy for halting the longitudinal trajectory of CMM.

Insulin resistance (IR) is a core mechanism in CMM and stroke7–9. IR is a systemic condition characterized by decreased insulin sensitivity and impaired ability to maintain normal glucose metabolism, and has been recognized as being associated with various metabolic diseases10,11. It is primarily characterized by a reduced response of the body to insulin, which can trigger disorders of glucose and lipid metabolism12. IR induces hyperinsulinemia and impairs vascular endothelial function, subsequently leading to abnormal glucose and lipid metabolism13,14. Previous studies have clearly confirmed that insulin resistance is an independent risk factor for CVD15, and several established insulin resistance indices, including the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index and the metabolic score for insulin resistance (METS-IR), have been widely used for its assessment. These indices have been demonstrated to predict CVD and stroke16–18. However, identifying a more accessible, reliable, and holistic screening tool that can dynamically capture systemic metabolic dysfunction remains a priority for primary care.

The single-point insulin sensitivity estimator (SPISE), a new marker for IR, uses only fasting triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) and BMI for calculation. By integrating lipid profiles, SPISE may achieve predictive performance comparable to established insulin resistance indices, potentially reflecting both hepatic and peripheral insulin resistance and providing a more comprehensive assessment of metabolic dysfunction than fasting glucose-based indices16,18,19. Furthermore, its reliance on routine, low-cost metabolic panels makes it exceptionally suited for scalable risk stratification and early intervention planning in resource-limited settings17,19.

However, a significant knowledge gap remains. Current research on SPISE has predominantly focused on isolated outcomes, such as singular cardiovascular events or the onset of diabetes in specific clinical cohorts. Its longitudinal predictive value for the composite outcome of CMM and the risk of stroke, particularly within the general aging population residing in the crucial CKM stages 0–3 (the pre-morbid and early-disease stages), remains poorly characterized.

To address this critical gap and validate its utility for primary prevention, this study leverages a large, nationally representative cohort of middle-aged and older Chinese adults. We aim to prospectively investigate the longitudinal association between baseline SPISE and the incidence of both CMM and stroke. By clarifying the linear dose-response architecture of this association, we seek to determine whether SPISE can serve as a robust, low-cost risk stratification tool to guide early interventions, particularly offering practical risk stratification for populations matching CKM stages 0–3 profiles.

Methods

Study design and population

This prospective, population-based cohort study utilized data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), an ongoing, nationally representative survey that collects high-quality microdata on the health and social status of middle-aged and older adults in China. The baseline survey was conducted in 2011–2012, with follow-up visits occurring every 2–3 years thereafter (2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020).

Using the 2011 CHARLS baseline survey as the starting point, follow-up extended through 2020. From an initial sample of 17,708 participants, we sequentially applied the following exclusion criteria (Fig. 1):1 failure to participate in subsequent follow-up surveys (2013, n = 2,522; 2015, n = 1,621; 2018, n = 1,577; 2020, n = 1,061);2 missing data required for SPISE calculation (n = 4,054);3 prevalent stroke at baseline (n = 147);4 prevalent CMM at baseline (n = 74); and5 missing key covariates or possessing values exceeding physiological limits. Ultimately, 6,624 eligible participants were included in the final analysis.

The study followed the STROBE Statement guidelines for reporting observational epidemiological studies. The CHARLS study was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015), and all participants provided written informed consent before enrollment.

Exposure variables

The primary exposure variable was the SPISE index, which integrates biochemical and anthropometric measurements to assess insulin sensitivity. Trained professionals collected fasting blood samples and physical measurements using standardized protocols. According to the literature20, SPISE is calculated based on fasting triglycerides (TG), HDL-C, and body mass index (BMI), the calculation formula is as follows: 600 × high-density lipoprotein cholesterol [mg/dL]0.185/ (triglycerides [mg/dL]0.2 × body mass index [kg/m2]1.338 ). In statistical analyses, SPISE was evaluated as both a continuous and a categorical variable. To assess dose-response relationships and enhance clinical translational value, participants were divided a priori into low (SPISE < 6.61) and high (SPISE ≥ 6.61) groups. This specific threshold was derived from the foundational validation study by Paulmichl et al.21, which demonstrated that a SPISE cutoff of 6.61 provides optimal sensitivity and specificity for identifying insulin resistance when validated against the euglycemic-hyperinsulinemic clamp technique.

Outcome variables

This study had two primary outcomes:

Incident CMM: CMM was defined as an individual having at least two of the following five cardiometabolic conditions: hypertension, heart disease, dyslipidemia, diabetes, and kidney disease. If an individual already had ≥ 2 of these diseases at baseline in 2011, they were defined as having prevalent CMM and were excluded from the analysis. Incident CMM was defined as an individual without CMM at baseline who first met the above criteria for multimorbidity during follow-up.

Incident Stroke: Defined as the first self-reported physician-diagnosed stroke by a participant during the follow-up period. Participants with prevalent stroke at baseline were excluded, and events newly reported during subsequent follow-ups were considered incident stroke.

Covariates

To rigorously account for potential confounding, a comprehensive set of baseline covariates was selected a priori, guided by existing epidemiological literature and clinical rationale. These covariates were systematically categorized as follows: (1) Sociodemographic and Lifestyle Factors: Age, sex, education level, household registration type, smoking status, and alcohol consumption. (2) Anthropometric and Clinical Measurements: Body mass index (BMI), waist circumference, and systolic and diastolic blood pressure. (3) Laboratory Parameters: White blood cell count, platelet count, C-reactive protein (CRP), creatinine, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), uric acid, and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c). (4) Medical History and Medication Use: Self-reported, physician-diagnosed history of hypertension, heart disease, dyslipidemia, kidney disease, and diabetes, alongside the concurrent use of antihypertensive, lipid-lowering, and antidiabetic agents.

Statistical analysis

Data preprocessing and baseline characteristics: Missing covariate data were handled using multiple imputation, employing predictive mean matching (PMM) for continuous variables and logistic regression for categorical variables. The Lilliefors (Kolmogorov-Smirnov) test indicated that all continuous variables exhibited skewed distributions (P < 0.05). Consequently, continuous data are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) and were compared between incident stroke/CMM groups using the Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis test, as appropriate. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies (percentages) and were evaluated using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test.

Cox proportional hazards regression analysis: To investigate the association between SPISE and the risks of CMM and stroke, we used Cox proportional hazards regression models. Participants were followed from the baseline survey (2011) until the first occurrence of CMM or stroke, or the end of the follow-up period (2020). Follow-up time was calculated in months.

Univariate Cox regression analysis was performed to screen for risk factors associated with CMM and stroke events. Variables with P < 0.10 in the univariate analysis, along with variables deemed important by clinical expertise, were included in multivariable Cox regression models. The SPISE index was also included in the models as both continuous and categorical variables, and results were presented as HR and their 95% CIs.

We constructed three sequentially adjusted models: Model 1 (crude model): No covariates were adjusted; Model 2 (partially adjusted model): Adjusted for sociodemographic and lifestyle factors, including smoking status, alcohol consumption status, sex, household registration type, and age; Model 3 (fully adjusted model): Based on Model 2, further adjusted for baseline disease history (hypertension, heart disease, dyslipidemia, kidney disease, diabetes), medication history (antihypertensive drugs, lipid-lowering drugs, antidiabetic drugs), and other laboratory indicators, including white blood cell count, platelet count, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, total cholesterol, LDL-C, C-reactive protein, glycated hemoglobin, uric acid, and fasting blood glucose. Model 2 represents the estimation of the total effect of SPISE by adjusting strictly for exogenous confounders. Model 3 serves as a highly conservative sensitivity analysis to test whether SPISE retains independent predictive value beyond established cardiometabolic mediators, although we acknowledge that variables such as fasting blood glucose, diabetes, and dyslipidemia are components of the SPISE formula or lie directly on its causal pathway.

Finally, to ensure the validity and reliability of the Cox regression models, a series of diagnostic tests were performed: 1. Proportional hazards assumption test: The proportional hazards assumption for each variable and the global model was tested using the Schoenfeld residuals method. P > 0.05 indicated that the proportional hazards assumption was met. 2.Multicollinearity diagnosis: The variance inflation factor (VIF) was calculated to assess multicollinearity among independent variables, with VIF < 5 indicating no serious multicollinearity issues.Multivariable logistic regression was performed as an additional sensitivity analysis to evaluate structural robustness irrespective of time-to-event variations.

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis: Kaplan-Meier survival curves were plotted to compare cumulative event-free survival probabilities between the low (SPISE < 6.61) and high (SPISE ≥ 6.61) groups. The log-rank test was used to assess the statistical significance of survival differences, and median survival times for both groups were reported.

Restricted cubic spline analysis: Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models derived from logistic and Cox regressions were employed to characterize potential non-linear dose-response relationships between continuous SPISE and both outcomes. The optimal number of knots was determined using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), resulting in a 4-knot model placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles. To ensure rigorous interpretation, P-values for both the overall association and non-linearity were explicitly calculated and reported. Analyses were conducted using both unadjusted and fully adjusted (Model 3) parameters, with results visualized as HR or OR plots accompanied by 95% CIs.

Subgroup analysis: To assess the consistency of the associations across different populations, we performed subgroup analyses. The study population was divided into different subgroups based on pre-defined stratification variables, including sex, age group (< 60 years vs. ≥ 60 years), smoking status, alcohol consumption status, and history of hypertension. The above multivariable regression analysis (Model 3) was repeated within each subgroup, and the interaction between the SPISE index and the stratification variables was tested.

Software implementation: All data sorting and statistical analyses were performed using the R language (versions 4.3.0/4.4.0). Two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

The baseline characteristics of the 6,624 study participants are detailed in Tables 1 and 2. The overall cohort comprised 2,876 males (43.4%) and 3,748 females (56.6%), with a median age of 57.0 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 51.0–64.0). Over a median follow-up of 114 months, 632 participants (9.5%) developed incident stroke, and 696 (10.5%) developed incident CMM.

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics stratified by incident stroke status. Compared with non-stroke participants, those who developed stroke had significantly lower SPISE levels (median: 6.57 [IQR: 5.46–8.08] vs. 7.34 [5.91–8.87], P < 0.001). Individuals in the stroke group had higher median values for age, BMI, waist circumference, weight, white blood cell count, creatinine, total cholesterol, triglycerides, LDL-C, C-reactive protein, HbA1c, uric acid, fasting blood glucose, systolic blood pressure, and diastolic blood pressure. In contrast, participants in the stroke group had significantly lower median HDL-C levels. The stroke group also had a higher proportion of participants with hypertension, heart disease, dyslipidemia, and nephropathy, as well as higher prevalence of diabetes medication, dyslipidemia medication, and hypertension medication use. According to the SPISE 2011 custom classification (cut-off value of 6.61), the proportion of participants with low SPISE (< 6.61) was significantly higher in the stroke group compared to the non-stroke group (50.32% vs. 37.22%, P < 0.001).

Table 2 displays the baseline characteristics stratified by incident CMM status. Similarly, participants who developed CMM had significantly lower SPISE levels than those without CMM (median: 6.04 [IQR: 5.05–7.49] vs. 7.40 [6.01–8.91], P < 0.001). The CMM group exhibited higher median values for age, BMI, waist circumference, weight, platelet count, white blood cell count, total cholesterol, triglycerides, LDL-C, C-reactive protein, HbA1c, uric acid, fasting blood glucose, systolic blood pressure, and diastolic blood pressure, while HDL-C levels were significantly lower. The CMM group also had a higher proportion of female participants and urban residents, and a greater prevalence of diabetes, hypertension, heart disease, dyslipidemia, and nephropathy, as well as higher rates of diabetes medication, dyslipidemia medication, and hypertension medication use. In contrast, smoking and alcohol consumption were less prevalent in the CMM group. According to the SPISE 2011 custom classification, the proportion of participants with low SPISE (< 6.61) was markedly higher in the CMM group than in the non-CMM group (62.36% vs. 35.66%, P < 0.001).

Regarding incident stroke, the continuous SPISE index demonstrated a consistent protective effect across all models. In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), each one-unit increase in the SPISE index was significantly associated with a 10% decreased risk of incident stroke (HR: 0.90, 95% CI: 0.86–0.94, P < 0.001). When analyzed as a categorical variable, participants in the high SPISE group (≥ 6.61) exhibited a 27% lower risk of developing stroke compared to those in the low SPISE group (< 6.61) (Model 3 HR: 0.73, 95% CI: 0.62–0.88, P < 0.001).

Similarly robust inverse associations were observed for the composite outcome of incident CMM. In the fully adjusted Model 3, every one-unit increment in the SPISE index independently conferred a 15% relative risk reduction for CMM (HR: 0.85, 95% CI: 0.81–0.89, P < 0.001). Furthermore, categorical analysis revealed that individuals with preserved insulin sensitivity (SPISE ≥ 6.61) experienced a substantial 48% reduction in the risk of incident CMM relative to the reference group (Model 3 HR: 0.52, 95% CI: 0.43–0.62, P < 0.001).

Diagnostic testing using Schoenfeld residuals was performed to assess the proportional hazards assumption for our fully adjusted models. The assumption was adequately met for the CMM outcome (Global P = 0.2788; Table S1). For the stroke outcome, while the SPISE index perfectly satisfied the assumption (P = 0.619), the global test indicated minor deviations (Global P = 0.011) driven by specific secondary covariates (Table S2). Crucially, the primary exposure (SPISE) strictly satisfied the assumption (P = 0.619), ensuring that the primary inferences remained uncompromised. Additionally, VIF analyses confirmed that no severe multicollinearity was present among the covariates in either model (all VIFs < 5; Tables S3 and S4).

Furthermore, sensitivity analyses using multivariable logistic regression over the entire follow-up period yielded consistent inverse associations between the SPISE index and the risks of incident stroke and CMM across all models and subgroups (Table S5-S7).

Association of SPISE with long-term survival outcomes

Kaplan-Meier survival analyses were conducted to compare cumulative event-free survival probabilities between participants with low (< 6.61) and high (≥ 6.61) baseline SPISE levels. For incident stroke, the high SPISE group maintained a significantly higher event-free survival probability throughout the study period. The divergence in survival trajectories between the two groups became increasingly pronounced from approximately 72 months of follow-up onwards (Fig. 2A). The log-rank test confirmed a highly significant survival difference (P < 0.001), corresponding to a markedly lower unadjusted risk of stroke in the high SPISE group (HR: 0.610, 95% CI: 0.522–0.713).

Restricted cubic spline analyses

RCS analyses with 4 knots (placed at 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of SPISE) were conducted using logistic regression and Cox proportional hazards regression to evaluate the dose-response association between SPISE and the risk of stroke and CMM.

For the logistic regression models, both unadjusted (Fig. 3A) and multivariable-adjusted analyses (Fig. 3B) showed a statistically significant overall association (both P for overall < 0.05). The tests for nonlinearity were not significant (both P for nonlinearity > 0.05).

Consistent results were observed in the Cox proportional hazards regression models. The unadjusted RCS analysis (Fig. 3C) and the multivariable-adjusted analysis (Fig. 3D) both demonstrated a significant overall association (P for overall < 0.05) with no significant nonlinearity (P for nonlinearity > 0.05). Stroke risk declined linearly with increasing SPISE, and the inverse association remained robust after adjustment for all confounders.

For CMM, unadjusted logistic regression (Fig. 4A) and unadjusted Cox regression (Fig. 4C) both showed a significant nonlinear relationship between SPISE and CMM risk. However, after adjustment for all confounding factors, the nonlinearity was no longer statistically significant in both multivariable-adjusted logistic regression (Fig. 4B) and multivariable-adjusted Cox regression (Fig. 4D). In fully adjusted models, the overall association remained significant, and a linear inverse trend was observed: CMM risk decreased progressively with increasing SPISE levels. These findings were consistent across logistic and Cox models, indicating that higher SPISE was independently associated with a lower risk of CMM in a linear manner after accounting for potential confounders.

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses were performed to evaluate the consistency of the protective associations across diverse demographic and clinical strata.

For the stroke outcome, both logistic and Cox regression models demonstrated that the inverse association between SPISE and stroke risk remained consistently protective across all evaluated subgroups (Fig. 5). In the overall population, each one-unit increase in SPISE was associated with a significantly reduced risk of stroke (logistic OR = 0.86, 95% CI: 0.83–0.90; Cox HR = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.84–0.91; both P < 0.001). Across strata defined by baseline morbidities (diabetes, hypertension, heart disease, dyslipidemia, kidney disease), lifestyle factors (smoking, alcohol consumption), and demographics (sex, education level, urban/rural residence, age), all point estimates remained < 1.0, with the vast majority of 95% CIs excluding 1.0. Interaction tests revealed no significant effect modification by any stratification variable (all P for interaction > 0.05), indicating a universally stable protective effect, although a marginally stronger trend was observed among diabetic participants and those with high school or vocational education.

Regarding the CMM outcome, both regression methodologies similarly confirmed a consistent overall protective effect (logistic OR = 0.74, 95% CI: 0.71–0.77; Cox HR = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.73–0.79; both P < 0.001) (Fig. 6). While the protective effect was statistically significant across most subgroups, interaction tests identified significant effect modifications specifically for baseline heart disease (logistic and Cox P for interaction < 0.001) and age (logistic P for interaction = 0.031; Cox P for interaction = 0.020).

To further elucidate these specific interactions, interaction heatmaps were constructed (Fig. 7). The analyses revealed that, regardless of SPISE levels, patients with baseline heart disease exhibited a significantly higher risk of CMM. Crucially, while increasing SPISE was associated with a stepwise and significant reduction in CMM risk among participants without heart disease, the presence of heart disease markedly attenuated this protective effect (Fig. 7A and B). Conversely, across all age strata, CMM risk decreased in a gradient manner with rising SPISE levels; although older adults exhibited a higher absolute baseline risk, SPISE exerted a consistent protective trend regardless of age (Fig. 7C and D). These findings indicate that while the protective effect of SPISE against CMM remains stable across age groups and in individuals without heart disease, it is partially blunted in patients with pre-existing heart disease.

Sensitivity analyses

The inverse associations between SPISE and the risks of incident stroke and CMM remained structurally robust across all sensitivity analyses, including complete-case and sex-stratified models, as well as after excluding early events and baseline metabolic diseases (Table S8). Furthermore, quartile-based analyses confirmed a highly significant, dose-dependent risk reduction, reinforcing its potential utility for early risk stratification in populations at risk of cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic progression (Table S9).

Discussion

In this nationwide prospective cohort of middle-aged and older Chinese adults, we demonstrated that higher baseline SPISE is independently and linearly associated with a reduced risk of incident CMM and stroke. These protective associations remained robust across diverse demographic and clinical subgroups without significant nonlinearity, suggesting that preserving insulin sensitivity is crucial for maintaining cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) health.

The biological plausibility linking lower SPISE (indicative of insulin resistance) to incident CMM and cerebrovascular events lies in the synergistic detrimental effects of glucotoxicity and lipotoxicity22–24. Specifically, the components of SPISE—elevated fasting triglycerides and diminished HDL-C combined with increased BMI—reflect a highly atherogenic lipid profile. This profile promotes macrophage foam cell formation, exacerbates systemic oxidative stress, and accelerates atherosclerosis25–27. Concurrently, insulin resistance impairs the endothelial PI3K/Akt pathway, leading to reduced nitric oxide bioavailability28. Over a long-term trajectory, this sustained subclinical vascular damage drives the progression from early metabolic dysfunction to overt CMM and acute ischemic stroke.

Our findings align with and quantitatively extend the existing epidemiological evidence regarding the SPISE index and cardiovascular health. Previous investigations have predominantly established the robust predictive value of SPISE for cardiovascular diseases (CVD) and type 2 diabetes (T2D) across diverse populations, including cohorts in China, the UK, the USA, and Sweden20,29–31. For instance, recent studies have demonstrated that higher SPISE is significantly associated with a reduced risk of heart failure in the elderly29(OR = 0.87, 95% CI = 0.80–0.94, P < 0.001) and validation in the Swedish ULSAM cohort revealed that the highest quartile of SPISE-IR (indicating severe insulin resistance) strongly predicted a 53% increased long-term risk of coronary heart disease (HR = 1.53)30.

Building upon this foundational research, our study uniquely extends the predictive utility of SPISE to two critical, yet distinct, endpoints in the aging population: stroke and CMM. In strong concordance with the protective trends observed in previous isolated CVD cohorts, our fully adjusted models revealed that participants in the high SPISE group (≥ 6.61) exhibited a 39% lower risk of incident stroke (HR = 0.61) and a striking 64% risk reduction for incident CMM (HR = 0.36). Furthermore, while prior studies validated SPISE primarily as a risk assessment tool for singular metabolic or cardiac events, our study is the first to prospectively quantify its primary prevention value for the composite CMM outcome within a large, nationally representative cohort. Specifically, we found that each one-unit increase in SPISE independently confers a 13% and 24% relative risk reduction for incident stroke and CMM, respectively, highlighting its robust capability to evaluate the longitudinal progression of the Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) continuum.

Furthermore, our findings strongly align with and offer a competitive alternative to recent evaluations of other established IR surrogates, such as the estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR)32. A previous prospective analysis utilizing the CHARLS database demonstrated that higher eGDR levels were associated with significantly lower risks of overall CVD (HR = 0.47) and stroke (HR = 0.39), exhibiting a linear dose-response relationship33. In direct comparison, our study demonstrates that the SPISE index delivers highly comparable predictive efficacy within the same aging population. Specifically, participants in the high SPISE group (≥ 6.61) exhibited a similar 39% lower risk of incident stroke (HR = 0.61) and a striking 64% risk reduction for the broader composite outcome of incident CMM (HR = 0.36), while also maintaining a strictly linear dose-response architecture. Additionally, while recent Mendelian randomization evidence confirmed a causal relationship between genetically determined IR and increased risks of hypertension (OR = 1.06) and peripheral artery disease (OR = 1.90) independent of isolated BMI or blood lipids34, SPISE provides a distinct translational advantage. By mathematically integrating both lipid profiles and BMI into a single optimized algorithm, SPISE effectively captures the synergistic pathways of lipotoxicity and obesity-driven insulin resistance. Therefore, SPISE serves as a highly accessible, low-cost alternative to complex indices like eGDR, offering significant clinical utility for predicting, preventing, and managing cardiovascular and multimorbidity trajectories in primary care.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, SPISE is an indirect surrogate for insulin sensitivity rather than a direct measurement, and residual confounding from unmeasured factors cannot be fully excluded. Second, our reliance on self-reported physician diagnoses for outcomes like stroke introduces potential recall bias and the underreporting of silent or fatal events. However, such non-differential misclassification typically biases estimates toward the null, suggesting our reported associations may actually be conservative. Third, the study population comprises middle-aged and older Chinese adults, which may limit generalizability. Fourth, there is an inherent biological overlap between SPISE components, CMM criteria, and the intermediate variables adjusted for in our fully adjusted models (Model 3). While this overlap accurately reflects the natural progression along the CKM continuum, it introduces a degree of statistical circularity and potential overadjustment; thus, our partially adjusted models (Model 2) may provide a more accurate reflection of unmediated causal associations. Finally, our hazard ratios may somewhat overestimate absolute risk due to the lack of competing-risk regression for mortality, and our exploratory subgroup findings warrant confirmation in future independent studies since no formal adjustments for multiple testing were applied.

Despite these limitations, the study has notable strengths: a prospective design, large sample size, long-term follow-up, strict adjustment for multiple confounders, and comprehensive dose-response and subgroup analyses. Our results support SPISE as a simple, low-cost, and robust tool for cardiometabolic risk stratification in clinical practice and public health. Future studies are warranted to validate these findings in other populations, to explore the underlying mechanisms linking insulin sensitivity to CMM and stroke, and to evaluate whether interventions targeting SPISE can improve long-term cardiometabolic outcomes.

Conclusion

In this large, nationwide prospective cohort study of middle-aged and older Chinese adults, we demonstrated that higher SPISE levels are independently and linearly associated with reduced risks of incident CMM and stroke. The protective effects of SPISE remained robust across diverse subgroups and after comprehensive adjustment for sociodemographic, lifestyle, and clinical confounders. The linear dose-response relationship observed in restricted cubic spline analyses further supports the consistent inverse association between SPISE and both outcomes. As a simple, non-invasive marker derived from routine clinical measurements—fasting triglycerides, HDL-C and BMI—SPISE offers a practical and cost-effective tool for early cardiometabolic risk stratification. These findings have important implications for primary prevention, particularly in resource-limited settings where complex IR assessments are impractical. Future studies are warranted to validate these findings in other populations, to elucidate the underlying mechanisms linking SPISE to CMM and stroke, and to evaluate whether SPISE-guided interventions can improve long-term cardiometabolic outcomes.
