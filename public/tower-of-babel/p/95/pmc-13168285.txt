Ultra-widefield OCTA assessment of retinal and choroidal microcirculation in cerebral small vessel disease
Authors: Yu Zhou, Chao Gao, Xiaoxing Zhang, Luan Tao, Jing Pan, Xi Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cross-sectional study investigated widefield retinal and choroidal structure, as well as three-dimensional vascular density—including the choroidal vascularity index (CVI), choroidal stromal volume (CSV), and choroidal vascular volume (CVV)—in patients with cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD). A total of 40 CSVD patients (66 eyes) and 30 healthy controls (53 eyes) underwent magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and comprehensive ophthalmic examinations, which included ultra-widefield (UWF) swept-source optical coherence tomography angiography (SS-OCTA) for quantitative retinal and choroidal assessment. After adjusting for confounders, generalized estimating equation models revealed that choroidal thickness and CVI in the nasal-superior quadrant, nasal quadrant, inferior quadrant and nasal-inferior quadrant were independently and inversely associated with CSVD risk. Superficial and deep vascular complexes in the inferior and nasal-inferior quadrants showed similar inverse associations, indicating that nasal and inferior quadrants of both the retina and choroid are most strongly linked to CSVD risk. With increasing CSVD burden, choroidal stromal and vascular volume reduction progressed from localized to global involvement. These findings collectively indicate that the choroid may serve as an accessible window for evaluating cerebral microvascular health, with UWF SS-OCTA offering potential for detecting and monitoring CSVD-related alterations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-45896-1.

Introduction

Cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) is a progressive degenerative disorder of small-caliber cerebral blood vessels, characterized by pathological alterations in cerebral microarteries, small arteries, capillaries, and venules1. It is a leading cause of increasing global incidence of stroke and vascular dementia2. The prevalence of CSVD rises with age, affecting approximately 5% of individuals aged 50 years to nearly 100% of those over 90 years old. It contributes to approximately 25% of all strokes and 45% of dementia cases, which significantly impacts patients’ health and quality of life3,4. Due to its often insidious onset and nonspecific clinical manifestations, current diagnosis primarily relies on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). However, there is a considerable lag between imaging evidence and clinical symptoms. Early identification and dynamic monitoring are therefore crucial for the prevention and management of CSVD.

Direct visualization of cerebral small vessels remains unattainable due to their minute diameter, and conventional neuroimaging such as MRI—though serving as the primary diagnostic modality for CSVD—may fail to capture early pathological changes. Consequently, the development of alternative clinical imaging biomarkers for CSVD is urgently needed. The retinal and cerebral microvasculatures share similarities in embryological origin, anatomical structure, and physiological characteristics5. Advances in non-invasive, high-resolution retinal imaging, particularly optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA)6, now enable detailed, depth-resolved visualization of the retinal and choroidal microvasculature at a capillary-level scale. This capability, coupled with the anatomical and physiological parallels between the retinal and cerebral circulations — including their common origin from the internal carotid artery system — underpins the concept of the retina as an accessible “window” to the brain, offering a unique opportunity to non-invasively infer the state of the cerebral microvasculature7,8.

Ultra-widefield optical coherence tomography angiography (UWF OCTA)9 is an emerging retinal vascular imaging technology that enables observation and quantitative analysis of retinal microvascular structure and blood flow perfusion. It offers advantages such as non-invasiveness, high resolution, and rapid imaging speed. Previous studies have indicated a correlation between retinal blood flow changes and CSVD severity10. However, studies utilizing conventional OCTA have been largely confined to the central retina, with typical scan areas ranging from 3 × 3 mm to 12 × 12 mm, and recent advancements extending to 15 × 9 mm or 15 × 15 mm formats11. In this study, we utilized ultra-widefield OCTA12 to acquire tomographic and angiographic images of the retina and choroid covering a 24 mm×20 mm area (equivalent to a 120° field of view intraocular angle). Our objective was to explore changes in the structure and vascular density of the widefield retina and choroid in patients with CSVD.

Methods

Study Population

This study enrolled patients diagnosed with cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) from the outpatient clinics of Ruijin Hospital, Shanghai, between September 2024 and November 2025. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles for medical research involving human participants outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Ethics Committee of Ruijin Hospital Luwan Branch (Approval No.: LWEC2023056). All participants were fully informed of the study protocol and provided written informed consent. The trial was registered with and approved by the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration No.: ChiCTR2400087669). Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age over 18 years, (2) diagnosis of CSVD confirmed by a neurologist according to the Standards for Reporting Vascular Changes on Neuroimaging (STRIVE-1) criteria based on neuroimaging features and clinical characteristics13. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) corneal pathologies, glaucoma, age-related macular degeneration (AMD), uveitis, vitreous-related diseases, high myopia (spherical equivalent ≥ −6.00 D or axial length ≥ 26 mm), or systemic conditions potentially causing ocular complications, presence or history of other retinopathies, or poor general health compromising examination compliance, (2) definite contraindications to MRI (e.g., psychiatric disorders, impaired consciousness, claustrophobia), (3) inability to complete the examination due to nystagmus, fixation deficiencies, or other reasons.

For the healthy control group, to ensure age-comparability with the typically older CSVD patient population and to minimize age-related confounding in the analysis of microvascular parameters, we applied a more specific age criterion. Potential controls were screened for the following inclusion criteria: (1) age over 45 years; (2) no history or clinical evidence of stroke, transient ischemic attack, dementia, or other major neurological disorders; (3) no significant abnormalities on routine cranial MRI; (4) absence of severe systemic diseases (e.g., uncontrolled hypertension, diabetes mellitus with end-organ damage, autoimmune diseases) that could affect cerebral or ocular vasculature; and (5) ability to complete all study examinations. Exclusion criteria for controls mirrored those applied to the CSVD group, particularly regarding ocular pathologies and MRI contraindications.

Data collection

Basic demographic and clinical information was collected, including sex, age, height, weight, educational background, history of smoking and alcohol consumption, as well as medical histories such as hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia.

MRI examination

Cranial MRI scans were performed on enrolled patients using either a 1.5T (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) or 3.0T (United Imaging Healthcare, Shanghai, China) MRI system. The acquired sequences included diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), T1-weighted imaging (T1WI), T2-weighted imaging (T2WI), 3D fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), and susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI). All MRI data were evaluated by the same radiologist in a blinded manner for the presence of lacunar infarcts, white matter hyperintensities, cerebral microbleeds, and perivascular spaces. Specifically, the radiologist was blinded to all clinical, cognitive, and OCTA data of the participants during the assessment.

The total MRI burden of CSVD was scored according to the rating method established by Staals et al.14, as follows: (1) presence of ≥ 1 lacunar infarct (LI); (2) presence of ≥ 1 cerebral microbleed (CMB) in deep brain regions; (3) presence of > 10 enlarged perivascular spaces (EPVS) in the basal ganglia region; (4) deep white matter hyperintensities with a Fazekas score15(a visual rating scale ranging from 0 to 3 for periventricular and deep white matter lesions) of 2–3, or periventricular white matter hyperintensities with a Fazekas score of 3. One point was assigned for each item present, resulting in a total possible score of 4 points.

Cognitive assessment

Global cognitive function was evaluated with the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA, Beijing version). In line with the instrument’s standard scoring guidelines to correct for the known confounding effect of education, one point was added to the MoCA scores of participants with fewer than 12 years of formal education. A total score of less than 26 was indicative of cognitive impairment16.

Ophthalmic examination

All participants underwent comprehensive ocular examinations, including intraocular pressure measurement (Canon TX-20, Japan), best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) assessment, slit-lamp examination, axial length measurement (Lenstar LS900, Haag-Streit AG, Switzerland), fundus photography (CLARUS 700, Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Germany), as well as optical coherence tomography (OCT) and OCT angiography (OCTA) (BM-400 K BMizar, TowardPi Medical Technology, China). Each type of examination was performed by the same designated operator.

Participants were imaged using a 400 kHz swept-source OCTA system. OCTA data were acquired using a raster scanning protocol consisting of 1,536 (horizontal) × 1,280 (vertical) B-scans and covered a 24 × 20 mm area centered on the fovea. Measurements were obtained using the system’s built-in software. The entire retinal and choroidal area were divided into a 3 × 3 grid composed of nine rectangular sectors, namely temporal-superior, superior, nasal-superior, temporal, macular, nasal, temporal-inferior, inferior, and nasal-inferior (Fig. 1).

The following parameters were quantified: (1) Vessel density (VD) was defined as the percentage of vascular area per unit area of the total scanning area. To avoid interference from large retinal blood vessels, we adopted retinal blood flow density data with large vessels excluded. (2) Superficial vascular complex (SVC) was defined from the internal limiting membrane (ILM) to the inner plexiform layer (IPL)/inner nuclear layer (INL) boundary. (3) Deep vascular complex (DVC) was defined from the IPL/INL boundary to the outer plexiform layer (OPL)/outer nuclear layer (ONL) boundary. (4) Choriocapillaris was defined as a 10-µm slab immediately beneath the Bruch’s membrane (BM)–retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) complex. (5) Choroidal vascularity index (CVI) was defined as the ratio of choroidal vascular volume to total choroidal volume within a specified region, equivalent to three-dimensional vessel density, and expressed as a percentage. Higher values indicate denser vasculature. (6) Choroidal thickness (CT) was measured as the vertical distance between Bruch’s membrane (BM) and the choroid-scleral interface (CSI). (7) Choroidal vascular volume (CVV) represented the absolute volume of choroidal vessels within a specified region, expressed in units of 10⁶ µm³. Higher values indicate greater vascular volume. (8) Choroidal stromal volume (CSV) referred to the volume of choroidal stroma within a specified region, also expressed in units of 10⁶ µm³. Higher values indicate greater stromal volume. Only images with signal strength index (SSI) ≥ 7 (range 0–10) were included for analysis. Details on motion and projection artefact control, as well as image quality exclusion criteria, are provided in the supplementary methods.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA). Categorical variables are summarized as frequencies (counts), and continuous data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Comparisons between the CSVD and control groups were performed using the chi-square test for categorical variables. For continuous variables, the independent samples t-test was used for normally distributed data, and the Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data. Comparisons among the five groups (divided by MRI total burden scores) were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis analysis. When significant differences were detected, Dunn’s post hoc test with Bonferroni correction was applied for pairwise comparisons between groups. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of retinal and choroidal parameters. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated for each parameter. A generalized estimating equation was used to account for intraocular dependencies within subjects, with adjustments for age, sex and hypertension. P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

Of 46 initially screened CSVD patients, 6 were excluded (poor OCTA image quality, n = 3; ocular comorbidities, n = 3), resulting in 40 enrolled patients. An additional 21 eyes (14 from CSVD patients, 7 from controls) were excluded due to poor image quality, yielding a final dataset of 66 eyes from 40 CSVD patients and 53 eyes from 30 healthy controls.

Table 1 summarizes the baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the study participants. The CSVD group was significantly older than the control group (70.10 ± 6.81 years vs. 60.21 ± 7.91 years, P < 0.01). No significant differences were observed between groups regarding sex distribution (P = 0.30), education duration (median 9 vs. 12 years, P = 0.44), smoking status (P = 0.71), alcohol consumption (P = 0.28), diabetes (P = 0.56), or hyperlipidemia (P = 0.29). However, hypertension was significantly more prevalent in the CSVD group compared to controls (67.5% vs. 6.7%, P < 0.01). The CSVD group also showed significantly lower Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) scores than controls (19.95 ± 4.04 vs. 24.77 ± 2.01, P < 0.01), indicating poorer cognitive function.

Regarding ocular parameters assessed at the eye level, there were no significant differences between CSVD eyes (n = 66) and control eyes (n = 53) in axial length (23.62 ± 1.18 mm vs. 23.67 ± 1.27 mm, P = 0.85) or best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) (0.03 ± 0.08 logMAR vs. 0.01 ± 0.05 logMAR, P = 0.05).

Analysis of MRI markers revealed prevalence rates of cerebral microbleeds (CMB), white matter hyperintensities (WMH), lacunar infarcts (LI), and perivascular spaces (PVS) at 25%, 75%, 82.5%, and 45%, respectively. Three-marker combinations included LI, WMH, and PVS (10 patients, 25%); CMB, WMH, and LI (4 patients, 10%); and CMB, LI, and PVS (1 patient, 2.5%). Comparison of choroidal parameters in different scores of CSVD and normal controls groups

Choroidal capillary density and three-dimensional choroidal vascular metrics, including choroidal vascularity index (CVI), choroidal stromal volume (CSV), and choroidal vascular volume (CVV), were compared across CSVD patients stratified by total MRI burden scores (Supplementary Table I).The results revealed significant differences in choroidal capillary density between the burden groups in the temporal-superior (P = 0.001) and nasal-superior (P = 0.005) quadrants. In contrast, the large choroidal vessel parameters (CVV, CSV, CVI) exhibited significant differences across all measured quadrants (all P < 0.05).

The comparative results of choroidal thickness (CT) among groups with different MRI total burden scores are presented in Fig. 3. Compared with the healthy control group, the group with a burden score of 2 exhibited significant choroidal thinning in the nasal-superior (P = 0.038), nasal (P < 0.001), nasal-inferior (P < 0.001), inferior (P = 0.002), and macular (P = 0.005) regions. In groups with higher burden scores (3–4 points), significant thinning was also observed in the superior (P < 0.001 for score 3, P = 0.031 for score 4), nasal-superior (P < 0.001 for score 3, P = 0.017 for score 4), temporal (P < 0.001 for score 3, P = 0.005 for score 4), macular (P < 0.001 for score 3, P = 0.04 for score 4), and nasal (P < 0.001 for score 3, P = 0.003 for score 4) regions. Furthermore, in CSVD patients with a score of 3, significant thinning was also present in the temporal-superior (P = 0.027), inferior (P < 0.001), and nasal-inferior (P < 0.001) regions. No statistically significant differences in choroidal thickness were found in the temporal-inferior quadrant across any inter-group comparisons (all P > 0.05).

Supplementary figures I, II and III compare the three-dimensional choroidal vascular metrics across different stages of CSVD. Compared with the healthy control group, patients with an MRI total burden score of 2 exhibited significantly reduced choroidal stromal volume (CSV) and choroidal vascular volume (CVV) in the nasal (PCSV=0.016, PCVV=0.007), macular (PCSV=0.020, PCVV=0.003), nasal-inferior (PCSV=0.046, PCVV=0.011), and inferior (PCSV=0.007, PCVV=0.002) regions. Concurrently, the choroidal vascularity index (CVI) was also significantly lower in the macular (P = 0.008) and inferior (P = 0.011) regions. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis of Retinal vascular density and choroidal parameters in CSVD

ROC analysis of vessel density (VD) in the superficial vascular complex (SVC) revealed that the superior region yielded the highest area under the curve (AUC) of 0.709 (P < 0.001), indicating its optimal diagnostic performance for distinguishing CSVD patients from controls (Supplementary Table II). A parallel analysis of VD in the deep vascular complex (DVC) also identified the superior region as having the highest AUC (0.677, P = 0.001). Statistically significant AUCs were observed for SVC VD in most examined regions (e.g., temporal-superior, superior, macular, nasal). In contrast, for DVC VD, significance was confined to the superior and inferior regions, with the inferior region exhibiting an AUC below 0.5.

CT and CVI showed good diagnostic performance in all detected regions, with peak AUCs noted in the nasal-inferior area (CT: 0.834; CVI: 0.747, P < 0.001 for both). In overall comparison, choroidal thickness outperformed CVI in terms of AUC in most regions, namely the superior, nasal-superior, temporal, macular, nasal, inferior, and nasal-inferior quadrants (Table 2).

Associations of choroidal and retinal microcirculatory parameters with CSVD Risk

Based on the binary multivariable generalized estimating equation model (Table 3), we adjusted for age, hypertension, sex, and the inter-eye dependency within subjects. After these adjustments, choroidal thickness showed significant inverse associations in the nasal-superior (Exp(β) = 0.985, P = 0.007), nasal (Exp(β) = 0.986, P = 0.010), inferior (Exp(β) = 0.979, P = 0.023), and nasal-inferior (Exp(β) = 0.969, P = 0.014) regions. No significant associations were found in other regions (all P > 0.05). Choroidal vascularity index demonstrated significant inverse associations in the nasal-superior (Exp(β) = 0.843, P = 0.024), nasal (Exp(β) = 0.854, P = 0.021), inferior (Exp(β) = 0.814, P = 0.031), and nasal-inferior (Exp(β) = 0.838, P = 0.016) regions. In contrast, the associations of choroidal thickness and CVI in other regions, such as the temporal and macular areas, with CSVD lost statistical significance after adjustment.

Supplementary Table Ⅲ presents the associations between retinal vessel density and CSVD risk. After adjusting for age, hypertension, sex, and inter-eye correlations using generalized estimating equation models, significant inverse associations with CSVD risk were consistently observed in the inferior and nasal-inferior quadrants for both superficial and deep vascular complexes. Specifically, SVC density in the inferior (Exp(β) = 0.892, P = 0.023) and nasal-inferior (Exp(β) = 0.942, P = 0.011) regions, and DVC density in the inferior (Exp(β) = 0.923, P = 0.038) and nasal-inferior (Exp(β) = 0.949, P = 0.010) regions were independently and inversely associated with CSVD risk.

Age-stratified subgroup analysis

To determine whether the association between choroidal parameters and CSVD risk is modified by age, we performed an age-stratified subgroup analysis. Patients with CSVD were divided into two subgroups based on the mean age of the cohort: a younger CSVD subgroup (mean age 64.00 ± 4.77 years, 31 eyes) and an older CSVD subgroup (mean age 75.17 ± 3.43 years, 35 eyes). Generalized estimating equation models were applied within each subgroup, with adjustment for hypertension and inter-eye correlations.

In the younger subgroup, choroidal thickness in the nasal-superior (Exp(β) = 0.986, P = 0.048) and nasal-inferior (Exp(β) = 0.973, P = 0.043) regions was significantly and inversely associated with CSVD risk. In the older subgroup, this inverse association extended more broadly, involving the superior, nasal-superior, temporal, macular, nasal, inferior, and nasal-inferior sectors (all P < 0.05). Regarding choroidal vascularity index (CVI), a significant inverse association with CSVD risk was observed only in the nasal-inferior region (Exp(β) = 0.862, P = 0.037) in the younger subgroup. In contrast, the older subgroup demonstrated a widespread inverse association, involving the temporal-superior, superior, nasal-superior, nasal, temporal-inferior, inferior, and nasal-inferior regions (all P < 0.05).

Discussion

The present study identified significant, age-independent alterations in choroidal thickness and three-dimensional vessel density in patients with CSVD. Furthermore, with increasing total MRI burden scores, a concurrent reduction in choroidal stromal and vascular volumes was observed, exhibiting a spatial progression from localized to global involvement. This pattern suggests that the severity of choroidal pathology may reflect the overall status of the cerebral microvasculature. To our knowledge, this study is the first to comprehensively evaluate widefield alterations in both structural and vascular parameters of the retina and choroid across multiple anatomical regions in patients with CSVD using ultra-widefield SS-OCTA.

The retina has long been regarded as an observable window into the central nervous system, and its value in assessing CSVD has been increasingly recognized17,18. Multiple studies have confirmed that the retinal neural structure and microvascular system can serve as a window for evaluating the severity of CSVD19,20. Specific manifestations include thinning of the retinal nerve fiber layer, ganglion cell inner plexiform layer, and outer retinal layers, which independently correlate with white matter hyperintensity volume21,22, Similarly, reduced capillary density in the radial peripapillary capillaries (RPC) and macular region is significantly associated with the severity of WMH, enlarged perivascular spaces(EPVS), and the presence of lacunes23,24.

In contrast to the well-established retinal findings, the relationship between CSVD and choroidal structure and function remains poorly understood. Only a limited number of studies have preliminarily reported that patients with WMH may exhibit concomitant choroidal thinning and reduced blood flow perfusion25. More critically, the imaging field of view in existing research has been constrained to the central macular area or a limited peripheral region26. The widefield swept-source OCTA technology employed in this study overcomes this limitation by enabling ultra-widefield (24 × 20 mm) fundus imaging, extending to the mid-periphery and even parts of the far-periphery of the retinal and choroidal vasculature27, This technical advance allows for systematic and comprehensive evaluation of choroidal thickness, vascular volume, and blood flow perfusion across the entire field of view in CSVD patients.

Previous studies have suggested a potential association between quantitative metrics of the the choroid and CSVD. Geerling et al.28, in an age-matched study with a limited sample size (10 patients with white matter lesions and 10 controls), reported a significant between-group difference in the standard deviation of choriocapillaris reflectivity, indicating the sensitivity of this parameter to microcirculatory alterations. Our study provides the first spatial distribution evidence supporting the correlation between choriocapillaris microcirculation and CSVD severity. We observed significant differences in choriocapillaris density within the temporal-superior and nasal-superior quadrants among groups with different CSVD burden scores. This finding not only confirms the indicative value of choriocapillaris microcirculatory parameters for CSVD but also offers a more systematic and clinically applicable imaging basis for understanding microcirculatory changes in the ocular fundus associated with CSVD.

When evaluating the overall relationship between choroidal structure and white matter burden, the study by Shen et al. provides a more integrated perspective, revealing a significant negative correlation between white matter hyperintensity volume (WMH-V) and the optic nerve head choriocapillaris perfusion area (ONH CC PA)29. The advantage of the present study lies in its utilization of widefield imaging technology, which enabled the identification of marked spatial heterogeneity in CSVD-associated choroidal alterations. After adjusting for age, hypertension, sex and inter-eye correlations using a generalized estimating equation model, our results demonstrated that choroidal thickness and the choroidal vascularity index in the nasal-superior quadrant, nasal quadrant, inferior quadrant and nasal-inferior quadrant were all independently and inversely associated with CSVD risk. Superficial vascular complexes and deep vascular complexes in the inferior quadrant and nasal-inferior quadrant were all independently and inversely associated with CSVD risk. By overcoming the limited field of view inherent in conventional imaging techniques, this study demonstrates for the first time that parameters in the nasal and inferior quadrants—in both the retina and choroid—show the strongest associations with CSVD risk.

The anatomical basis of choroidal vessels, along with pathological injury and systemic dysregulation, may explain the predilection for nasal and inferior quadrant involvement. The nasal and inferior quadrants of the choroid are vascularized by terminal branches of the posterior ciliary arteries with minimal collateral anastomoses. Their proximity to watershed zones renders these regions particularly susceptible to hypoperfusion and confers a low hemodynamic reserve30,31. Consequently, these regions are particularly vulnerable to hypoperfusion and hemodynamic fluctuations when CSVD elevates microcirculatory resistance in the internal carotid artery system32. Furthermore, core pathological features of CSVD—including endothelial dysfunction33, pericytes loss34, and associated inflammation and oxidative stress35—can directly compromise the structural integrity and regulatory function of choroidal vessels. Additionally, CSVD may induce central autonomic dysfunction36, impairing ocular blood flow regulation. However, the exact underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood and warrant further investigation through integrated basic and clinical studies.

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes the establishment of causal or temporal relationships between choroidal parameters and CSVD. Therefore, we cannot determine whether the observed choroidal changes precede or follow cerebral microvascular damage. Second, the sample size, while adequate for initial exploration, remains relatively modest, which may limit the statistical power to fully adjust for all potential confounders and to detect subtler associations. Third, the cohort exhibits inherent heterogeneity: the CSVD group was significantly older and had a higher prevalence of hypertension than the control group. Although these variables were included as covariates in our statistical models, residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded. Fourth, while we performed an age-stratified subgroup analysis (Table 4), a key methodological limitation of this analysis is that both CSVD subgroups (aged ≤ 65 and > 65 years) were compared against the same, relatively younger control group, rather than against separate, age-matched control subgroups. This limits our ability to definitively disentangle the pathological effects of CSVD from the effects of normal aging on the observed choroidal parameters. Fifth, this was a single-center study, and all participants were of Asian ethnicity, which may affect the generalizability of our findings to other populations and healthcare settings. Sixth, we did not collect detailed data on systemic medications (e.g., antihypertensives, statins, or other vasoactive drugs) that could potentially influence choroidal vascular tone and perfusion. Although we recorded the prevalence of hypertension and other comorbidities, the lack of medication data represents a potential unmeasured confounder in our analysis of choroidal parameters. Finally, the use of two different MRI field strengths (1.5T and 3.0T) represents a technical limitation of this study. Although all images were evaluated by the same radiologist using consistent criteria, residual scanner-related variability cannot be fully excluded. Future large-scale, multi-center, prospective studies with meticulously matched cohorts and harmonized imaging protocols are needed to validate and extend our findings.

In summary, this study confirms that choroidal morphology and perfusion characteristics serve as significant, quantifiable ocular biomarkers for CSVD. These findings position choroidal parameters as a non-invasive target for optimizing early screening and dynamic monitoring of CSVD, while also providing a novel theoretical and practical foundation for developing early intervention strategies to slow disease progression.
