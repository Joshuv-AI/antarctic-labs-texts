Effect and safety of nutritional supplements on clinical outcomes in patients with acute pancreatitis: a systematic review and network meta-analysis
Authors: Kaifeng Su, Ruifeng Duan, Yang Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe effectiveness of nutritional supplements is controversial compared to standard supplementation in the clinical management of individuals with acute pancreatitis (AP). We performed a network meta-analysis (NMA) to compare and rank their efficacy.MethodsFour databases (Web of Science, Cochrane, Embase, PubMed) were searched for randomized controlled trials. Random-effects models to derive outcome estimates, with risk ratios (RRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) calculated for each comparison. The surface under the cumulative ranking (SUCRA) was used to rank the relative efficacy of all therapies.ResultsSeventeen trials including 1,313 patients were analyzed. ω-3 fatty acid supplementation emerged as the most effective intervention. It significantly shortened hospital stay (mean difference = -5.82 days; 95% CI -10.62 to -1.01) and intensive care unit (ICU) stay (mean difference = -6.09 days; 95% CI -8.98 to -3.20). It also reduced ICU stay in patients with severe acute pancreatitis (SAP) (mean difference = -6.09 days; 95% CI -8.98 to -3.02). Moreover, ω-3 fatty acids reduced the risk of sepsis (RR = 0.35; 95% CI 0.14–0.89) in AP and SAP (RR = 0.30; 95% CI 0.10–0.93). Synbiotics dramatically reduced the incidence of bacteremia (RR = 0.27; 95% CI = 0.09–0.86). It also reduced ICU stay with the enteral route (mean difference = -3.22 days; 95% CI, -3.95 to -2.49). However, none of the regimens produced significant improvements in mortality, infectious complications, time to oral feeding, or multiorgan failure (MOF).ConclusionsThis NMA preliminary suggests that ω-3 fatty acid supplementation demonstrates the greatest efficacy in reducing the length of hospital and ICU stays in individuals with AP. Hence, it should be considered for routine use in AP, as it may reduce the future medical burden. Furthermore, increasing ω-3 fatty acids and synbiotics may contribute to a reduced incidence of sepsis and bacteremia, respectively.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12937-026-01295-w.

Introduction

Acute pancreatitis (AP) arises as a systemic immune-mediated inflammatory response triggered by pancreatic and peripancreatic autodigestion. It has become one of the most common gastrointestinal diseases [1]. AP is characterized by its sudden onset, rapid progression, and multiple complications. About one-fifth of AP individuals develop severe acute pancreatitis (SAP), which has a mortality rate of up to 30% [2]. This poses significant challenges to clinical treatment. When it comes to the prevention and treatment of several illnesses that have a considerable influence on health, nutrition is crucial [3]. As a result of this growing awareness, nutrition is now appropriately covered in the recommendations for several illnesses. There are several examples in history, but AP is arguably the most comprehensive and obvious [4]. In actuality, AP is followed by both specific and nonspecific metabolic changes that result in hypermetabolism, hypercatabolism, and a negative nitrogen balance. Malnutrition should be prevented and treated with the proper nutritional support to improve the disease’s progression and prevent complications [5].

Nutritional supplements currently used in clinical practice mainly include microecological agents (probiotics, prebiotics, synbiotics) and immune nutritional supplementation (ω-3 fatty acids, glutamine, nucleotide). A randomized controlled trial (RCT) showed that administration of a freeze-dried formulation of live probiotics combined with oat fiber in the treatment group was associated with lower rates of sepsis and reduced surgical requirements [6]. Similarly, in 2007, the same research group demonstrated that early enteral administration of a symbiotic formulation (comprising probiotics and prebiotics) reduced the incidence of systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), infections, and mortality rate in the late phase of SAP [7]. Glutamine modulates nitric oxide metabolism, influences lymphocyte and monocyte activity, regulates cellular maturation, and promotes the synthesis of heat shock proteins (HSPs) [8]. Besides modulating the immune system, it works directly on the intestinal barrier. An investigation showed that by decreasing intestinal permeability in the early stages of AP, enteral glutamine treatment improves intestinal barrier function [9]. Studies have demonstrated that glutamine has a positive effect in preventing complications. The European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN) guidelines recommend an L-glutamine dosage of 0.20 g/kg per day for AP individuals [4, 10]. Nevertheless, the role of glutamine in critical patients is still controversial [11]. ω-3 fatty acids exhibit potent anti-inflammatory effects. Adding them to parenteral feeding reduces the inflammatory response by influencing proinflammatory cytokine and eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) levels [12]. Furthermore, research has shown that intravenous ω-3 fatty acid supplementation is linked to shorter hospital stays, lower death rates, and fewer infection complications in AP [13]. Thus, nutritional support strategies have become increasingly important in treating AP [14]. Nevertheless, there is currently a lack of comparative clinical studies on the efficacy of various microecological and immunonutritional agents. Additionally, individual studies often fail to identify the most effective microecological agents and immunonutritional supplements for improving clinical outcomes in individuals with AP. Several studies suggest using network meta-analysis (NMA) to evaluate comparative treatment effects when head-to-head trials are unavailable [15–17]. NMA builds on these methodologies by synthesizing direct and indirect comparisons across a network of interventions to estimate relative treatment effects.

Therefore, the present study implemented a systematic review and NMA to evaluate the relative effects of these nutritional supplements on clinical outcomes in AP, ultimately selecting the most effective therapies. This may offer a new therapeutic strategy for treating AP.

Materials and methods

Search strategy

The current NMA followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension Statement for Reporting of Systematic Reviews Incorporating Network Meta-analyses of Health Care Interventions [18]. Meanwhile, this protocol has been registered on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (CRD420251051101).

Literature was retrieved in the Cochrane Library, Embase, Web of Science, and PubMed up until May 12, 2025. Combining MeSH terms and keywords, the search strategy was tailored to each database according to their specific indexing features. The literature retrieval applied the following terms: (“pancreatitis” [MeSH] OR “acute pancreatitis” [Title/Abstract] et al.) AND (“probiotics” [MeSH] OR “probiotic agent” [Title/Abstract] et al.) AND (“prebiotics” [MeSH] OR “prebiotic agent” [Title/Abstract] et al.) AND (“synbiotics” [MeSH] OR “synbiotic agent” [Title/Abstract] et al.) OR (“immunonutrition” [MeSH] OR “immunonutrition diet” [Title/Abstract] et al.) AND (“omega 3 fatty acid” [MeSH] OR “n-3 Oil” [Title/Abstract] et al.) AND (“glutamine” [MeSH] OR “L Glutamine [Title/Abstract] et al.) AND (“fatty acid” [MeSH] OR “Aliphatic Acid” [Title/Abstract] et al.) AND (“nucleotides” [MeSH] OR “free nucleotide” [Title/Abstract] et al.) AND (“fish oil” [MeSH] OR “Liver Oils, Fish” [Title/Abstract] et al.) AND (“random” [MeSH] OR “randomized” [Title/Abstract] et al.). Details are presented in Table S1.

In addition, literature references were included retrospectively to supplement access to relevant literature. The reporting of this study conforms to the PRISMA-NMA checklist (Table S2).

Inclusion criteria

I) RCTs. II) Subjects were diagnosed with AP per the diagnostic and treatment guidelines for AP in each country, regardless of nationality, sex, age, etc. III) The treatment group (standard nutrition added additional nutritional supplements, includ-ing probiotics, prebiotics, synbiotics, glutamine, omega-3 fatty acids, and immunonu-trition, respectively). The control group received conventional treatment (standard nu-trition without any nutritional supplements or only including a placebo). IV) Clinical outcomes comprised the length of hospital stay (Defined as the duration from hospital admission to discharge because of pancreatitis), mortality (Death of a patient due to pancreatitis during hospitalization), the length of intensive care unit (ICU) stay (The patient's condition deteriorated during hospitalization due to pancreatitis, necessitat-ing admission to ICU for treatment; following improvement with treatment, the pa-tient was discharged from the ICU), multiorgan failure (MOF) (During the patient's hospitalization, pancreatitis failed two or more organ systems), infectious complica-tions (Any infectious events occurring during hospitalization for pancreatitis, includ-ing infected pancreatic necrosis, pneumonia, urinary tract infection and abscesses et al), time to successful oral feeding (pancreatic patients could take food through the mouth without any abdominal pain or other discomforts), sepsis (Fever, low arterial blood pressure, systemic inflammatory response, and positive bacterial blood cultures), and bacteremia (Positive blood culture).

Exclusion criteria

I) Types of studies that are not RCTs, such as case-control, cross-sectional, and cohort studies, reviews, and animal experiments. II) Interventions interfered with by other treatments. III) Unavailability of the full text or duplicate publications.

Data extraction

Literature was screened independently by two researchers. Information was collected and cross-checked. Discrepancies were resolved via consensus-based mutual discussion or with the assistance of an experienced third researcher. Any missing information was supplemented by contacting the authors whenever possible. Collected data comprised the first author, sex, year of publication, intervention, sample size, age, and clinical outcomes.

Risk of bias (ROB) assessment

The Cochrane Collaboration ROB tool was employed to evaluate the quality of each study by two investigators. The literature was evaluated for ROB primarily in terms of randomization methods, allocation concealment, blinding of outcome evaluators, completeness of outcome data, blinding, selective reporting of study results, and other potential sources of bias.

Data analysis

This NMA was conducted utilizing Stata 15.0 software with a random-effects model.

In the context of an NMA, inconsistency refers to the statistical disagreement between direct and indirect evidence within the network, which may suggest a lack of transitivity [16]. For inconsistency testing, a forced loop was used to compare direct estimates from the pairwise meta-analysis with indirect estimates from the NMA. Effect estimates were reported as weighted mean differences (for continuous outcomes) and risk ratios (for dichotomous outcomes), along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Interventions were ranked based on their efficacy using the surface under the cumulative ranking (SUCRA) curve, with higher SUCRA values indicating greater efficacy [17]. For each outcome, league tables were created to present the estimated treatment effects and to rank all included treatments according to their mean ranks and SUCRA values. Publication bias was evaluated utilizing Egger’s regression asymmetry test [19]. A P-value below 0.05 was suggestive of publication bias.

Results

Literature retrieval

An initial database retrieval identified 711 articles, of which 296 duplicates were removed; 374 additional articles were excluded after title and abstract screening. Of the remaining 41 full-text articles, 24 were excluded due to ineligibility, leaving 17 articles included in the NMA. The selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

NMA

Length of hospital stay

Our NMA yielded 15 two-by-two comparisons, one of which had statistically significant differences. Compared to conventional treatment, incorporating ω − 3 fatty acids could potentially reduce the length of hospital stay. A statistically significant reduction was observed, with an MD of -5.82 (95% CI: -10.62 to -1.01). The other interventions demonstrated no statistical significance compared to conventional treatment (Table 2).

Safety assessment

Only one article reported a serious adverse event (SAE) associated with probiotics. The overall outcome showed that intestinal ischemia occurred in 9/152 (6%) of the probiotic group versus 0/144 (0%) in the control group, demonstrating a significant difference (p = 0.004) [20]. Lasztity et al. [21] reported that abdominal pain, nausea, and other discomforts occurred in both the ω-3 fatty acids group and the control group, with no significant difference in their frequency. Karakan et al. [22] reported that three cases experienced bloating and gas symptoms within the first 48 h of feeding in the prebiotics group; the symptoms resolved spontaneously over the following days. Rohith et al. [23] reported that vomiting, feeding intolerance, and loose stools occurred in both the synbiotic and placebo groups, with no significant difference between them. The other articles have not reported adverse events (AE) associated with nutritional supplements.

Discussion

Our research found that treating AP individuals with ω-3 fatty acids could reduce the length of hospital stay compared to conventional treatment. ω-3 fatty acids had superior effects in decreasing the length of hospital stay compared to other supplemental nutrients. Furthermore, ω-3 fatty acids and synbiotics may also reduce the length of ICU stay. The SAP subgroup analysis also confirmed that ω-3 fatty acids were associated with reduced ICU length of stay in SAP patients. The enteral nutrition route subgroup analysis also showed that synbiotics could reduce ICU length of stay. Although there are insufficient articles on the parenteral nutrition route for future subgroup analysis, three studies have reported that intravenous ω-3 fatty acid supplementation may reduce inflammation and improve clinical outcomes in patients with AP. ω-3 fatty acids are also a better choice than other treatments. Additionally, our study revealed that ω-3 fatty acids could decrease sepsis and that synbiotics could reduce bacteremia in individuals with AP. Likewise, the SAP subgroup analysis also found ω-3 fatty acids could reduce sepsis in SAP patients.

However, our investigation revealed that none of the nutritional supplements in our study impacted other clinical outcomes, including mortality, infectious complications, time to successful oral feeding, and MOF.

ω − 3 fatty acids, primarily derived from fish oil, contain EPA and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA). These substances act as precursors to inflammatory mediators and effector molecules [24]. Some studies have found that ω − 3 fatty acids can suppress inflammation and improve the infectious process by reducing the production of proinflammatory eicosanoids and cytokines [25, 26]. Numerous experimental and clinical studies have demonstrated the capacity of ω − 3 fatty acids to regulate these immunological processes [27–29]. Several epidemiological studies and clinical trials have shown that ω − 3 fatty acids regulate the production of proinflammatory cytokines, improve infectious progression, and restore liver function [30–33]. Wolbrink et al. [24] studied the effect of C-reactive protein (CRP) levels in individuals receiving ω − 3 fatty acids and found that these levels decreased more rapidly. Its reduction was linked to milder disease severity and better clinical outcomes in individuals with predicted SAP. Moreover, ω-3 fatty acids may impact inflammation by promoting the synthesis of anti-inflammatory cytokines and pro-resolving mediators, while suppressing NF-κB activation [34, 35].

Sepsis is a potentially fatal and dysregulated host response to infections or infectious agents. It is a prevalent and recurrent illness that has grown to be a severe public health burden in critical care medicine [36, 37]. It is also a common complication in SAP. According to our previous research, ω-3 fatty acids may decrease sepsis’ incidence in individuals with AP. Wang et al. [38] discovered that ω-3 polyunsaturated fatty acid supplementation has the potential to reduce sepsis-related mortality and shorten ICU stays. The favorable impact of ω-3 fatty acids on sepsis is likely attributed to several mechanisms. Critical illness is thought to disrupt the intestinal milieu, leading to dysbiosis, impaired immune function, and other undesirable effects in sepsis individuals [39]. Recent research suggests that the gut microbiome represents a potential therapeutic target in sepsis due to its ability to modulate the immunophenotype and improve survival in septic mice [40]. Watson et al. [41] showed that supplementing with ω-3 fatty acids may cause a reversible increase in bacteria, such as Roseburia, Lactobacillus, and Bifidobacterium, which produce short-chain fatty acids. Furthermore, an animal study showed that administering ω-3 fatty acid supplements to mice altered the gut microbiota and alleviated endotoxemia by reducing bacteria that produce lipopolysaccharides (enterobacteria) and increasing those that suppress them (Bifidobacterium) [42]. Thus, ω-3 fatty acids may favorably modulate sepsis by altering the gut microbiota and enhancing the synthesis of anti-inflammatory mediators, including short-chain fatty acids. Additionally, Liu et al. [43] found that ω-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids ameliorated sepsis development by activating the AMPK/mTOR pathway in rat models, highlighting their potential as a therapeutic agent.

Thus, we speculate that ω − 3 fatty acids may help reduce hospital and ICU stays by inhibiting the inflammatory response and decreasing sepsis in individuals with AP.

However, our analysis revealed that they did not significantly improve other clinical outcomes, including mortality, infectious complications, and MOF. Palmer et al. [44] investigated the role of ω − 3 fatty acids in critically ill adult subjects and obtained similar results. In our previous studies, we found that supplements of ω − 3 fatty acids were routinely used in parenteral nutrition for most individuals with AP. Cao et al.‘s research [45] has revealed that parenteral nutrition is proinflammatory and has been shown to have a higher risk of infection, pancreas-related complications, organ failure, and mortality in individuals with SAP. Some experimental studies have found that total parenteral nutrition can result in rapid and severe atrophy of lymphoid tissue related to the intestine, leading to increased bacterial translocation [46–48]. Contrarily, enteral nutrition decreases bacterial translocation, preserves the host’s immunocompetence, and protects the gastrointestinal mucosa and microbial ecology [49].

Additionally, the current AP guidelines strongly advise against routine parenteral nutrition for most patients. They recommend enteral nutrition as the first-line nutritional support, reserving parenteral nutrition for patients unable to tolerate enteral feeding [49]. Therefore, it remains unclear whether the observed outcomes in the aforementioned studies were solely due to ω − 3 fatty acids. There is limited clinical research comparing the supply of ω − 3 fatty acids from parenteral and enteral nutrition in individuals with AP. Hence, further study of the specific mechanism is required.

Synbiotics are a combination of probiotics and prebiotics. Probiotics are live bacteria that are beneficial for our health; examples include Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus. Prebiotics are carbohydrates that are not absorbed through the intestinal mucosa and serve as a source of nutrition for probiotics. Some evidence indicates that synbiotics increase not only bacterial abundance but also modulate genus-level composition and overall microbial diversity, potentially supporting gut microbiota homeostasis [50, 51]. An RCT reported that the synbiotic-treated group showed significantly preserved gut microbiota and organic acids, primarily acetate, along with elevated acetate and lactate levels, which may suppress intraluminal toxins [52]. Furthermore, Fecal acetic acid improves intestinal barrier function and prevents bacterial translocation [52]. Acetic acid exhibits strict bactericidal effects on pathogenic bacteria and upregulates tight junction-related genes, such as claudin-1, occludin, and ZO-1, in the intestinal epithelium [53, 54]. Previous research has illustrated that intestinal dysbiosis is associated with the severity of AP and precedes the onset of systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) [55]. Generally, the normal microbiota in AP is replaced by “AP-associated microbiota,” which is characterized by an increase in pathogenic bacteria and a decrease in beneficial bacteria [56]. These findings may explain why synbiotics could reduce bacteremia in AP individuals.

Our research has several limitations: (I) The sample sizes in most trials were small; (II) Heterogeneity existed between studies in the definition of AP and its severity. Some studies included moderate to severe AP, while others only included SAP; (III) There was an absence of detailed data on antibiotic use, which may affect the outcomes, specifically the incidence of infectious complications; (IV) The absence of data on potential supplementary therapies, including surgery, drainage, and debridement, may influence the observed outcomes; (V) Different nutritional supplement dosages in each trial may impact final analytical outcomes; (VI) As the majority of included studies were conducted in European and Asian countries, the applicability of the findings to other populations may be restricted; (VII) Owing to the limited number of studies, no evidence networks with outcome measures formed closed loops; hence, we anticipate that future research will conduct additional RCTs on this topic to validate our findings.

Conclusions

Our NMA preliminary revealed that ω-3 fatty acid therapy could reduce the length of hospital and ICU stays in AP individuals and ranked first among the nutritional supplements examined. Additionally, we have preliminarily found that ω-3 fatty acid therapy could inhibit the rate of sepsis in AP and that synbiotic therapy could reduce the incidence of bacteremia in AP. According to these results, we suggest considering the routine incorporation of ω-3 fatty acids to alleviate the progression of AP, which could reduce future medical costs. Increasing the use of ω-3 fatty acids and synbiotics may reduce the need for prophylactic and therapeutic antibiotics, leading to a decrease in multidrug-resistant organisms. Nonetheless, future multicenter, large-scale, and prospective studies are necessary to fully determine the clinical relevance and broader implications of the observed results.
