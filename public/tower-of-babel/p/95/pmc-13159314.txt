The reorganization mechanism of brain functional networks by long term actual exercise and exercise imagination training: spectrum and network topology analysis based on EEG
Authors: Jinglong Wu, Wangjun Zou, Jinyan Zhang, Binbin Gao, Ruiqing Li, Zhilin Zhang, Jian Zhang, Tianyi Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMotor learning optimizes motor skills and promotes neuroplasticity through motor execution (ME) and motor imagery (MI). However, the mechanisms underlying long-term training-induced reorganization of functional brain networks remain unclear. Existing studies predominantly focus on short-term effects, and their analytical methods are limited to spectral properties, with insufficient exploration of complex network interactions. Elucidating differential regulation of functional brain networks by different training modes is important for understanding the characteristics of motor learning stages and optimizing rehabilitation strategies.MethodsThis randomized controlled trial compares seven-day ME and MI training effects on cortical reorganization and behavioral performance. Forty-eight healthy right-handed participants were randomly assigned to four groups: ball rotation task (BRT), video guide imagery (VGI), tactile stimulation (Tactile), and control group (Control). All groups underwent a one-week training intervention. Behavioral performance was quantified by ball-rotation scores, while brain function was evaluated using resting-state electroencephalography (EEG) pre- and post-training. Power spectral density (PSD) in theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–13 Hz), and beta (13–30 Hz) bands, phase-lag index (PLI) connectivity, and graph-theoretical metrics (clustering coefficient, node degree) were analyzed. Statistical comparisons used paired t-tests, ANOVA, Kruskal–Wallis tests and post hoc test (α = 0.05).ResultsAll groups showed significant motor improvement (p < 0.001), with BRT demonstrating superior gains versus others (F = 32.279, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.6876). Frontal β-band power increased significantly in BRT, suggesting enhanced skill output via neuronal recruitment, whereas VGI exhibited attenuated β-power, indicating optimized neural resource integration. θ-band frontal-parietal functional connectivity was enhanced in both the BRT and the VGI groups, supporting motor-cognitive integration. Parietal β-band connectivity weakened, implying enhanced neural efficiency and reduced visual dependence. α-band clustering coefficients decreased in BRT, indicating network reorganization toward distributed processing, while θ-band frontoparietal node degree increased in VGI, reflecting elevated cognitive control demands.ConclusionME and MI enhance motor skills through distinct network reorganization: ME sustains neuronal recruitment via beta-enhancement and local network dispersion, whereas MI optimizes efficiency through beta-suppression and frontoparietal integration. These frequency-specific mechanisms provide preliminary neurophysiological insights that may inform future development of personalized rehabilitation approaches for motor disorders. Future investigations should validate these findings in clinical populations with motor impairments.

Introduction

Motor learning refers to the adaptive process of motor skill acquisition, optimization and long-term maintenance through repeated practice [26]. This process facilitates functional recovery in patients with neurological disorders [14]. As a continuous phenomenon, motor learning is categorized into short-term (< 1 h), intermediate-term (> 1 h to < 24 h), and long-term (> 24 h to 5 weeks) phases based on temporal dynamics [25]. Both motor execution (ME) and motor imagery (MI) enhance motor learning, garnering increasing attention in rehabilitation and skill acquisition research [38]. Motor execution entails explicit actions with physical output, while motor imagery constitutes a dynamic cognitive process involving simulated action without overt movement. [23, 37].

Evidence indicates that motor execution (ME) alters functional brain activity through neuroplastic mechanisms [8]. In a study of soccer skill acquisition, 8 weeks of physical training enhanced functional connectivity within temporal, frontal, and cerebellar regions, with improved connectivity significantly correlating with superior motor performance [11]. Similarly, long-term dance training in Parkinson's disease (PD) patients induced functional reorganization in cortical areas, particularly the supplementary motor area (SMA) [13]. Furthermore, study of finger key press sequence movements showed that after multiple days of training, the brain showed highly distinguishable changes in activity patterns in primary and secondary cortical motor areas [48]. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that ME enhances specialized brain circuit functions and reorganizes neural activity in core motor control nodes.

Motor imagery training also enhances motor function, yielding gains comparable to physical practice [18, 27], while eliciting similar neural reorganization patterns [9, 47]. Neuroimaging studies demonstrate that both physical and motor imagery training induce functional changes in overlapping regions—including primary motor cortex (M1), supplementary motor area (SMA), premotor cortex (PMC), and cerebellum—during their respective task states [15, 30, 54]. Notably, M1 activation during motor imagery remains attenuated relative to motor execution. Furthermore, combined motor execution and imagery training modulates motor planning regions such as dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), inferior frontal cortex (IFC), and posterior parietal cortex (PPC), though the brain regions altered by the two training modes are not consistent [24]. These findings collectively reveal task-state-dependent differential engagement of motor networks [17].

Electroencephalography (EEG), with its high temporal resolution advantage, is widely employed to characterize neurophysiological mechanisms in motor skill acquisition and rehabilitation [49], enabling observation of neuroplastic changes. Researchers typically employ spectral analysis to quantify these changes, including power spectral density (PSD) or relative power (RP) alterations in frequency bands of interest [42]. For instance, Van Der Cruijsen et al. demonstrated that theta and alpha bands exhibit stronger associations with motor learning than beta bands during sequential motor task learning [44]. However, such spectral methods inadequately capture the functional changes in brain networks with altered information interactions. Consequently, EEG-based graph theory analysis has emerged as a critical tool for characterizing complex brain networks, revealing connectivity patterns and identifying hub regions essential for information processing [4, 5, 19, 31, 40]. Yu et al. compared functional brain networks under a motor imagery task with those in the resting state, and found that connectivity of beta rhythms in the networks became sparse [53], while Baltazar et al. studied the brain core networks of dystonia patients undergoing actual motor rehabilitation, confirming the hypothesis that the disease is a network disorder [3]. Despite these advances, the differential impact of motor execution versus imagery on network regulation remains controversial [21], and similarities in their regulatory mechanisms require further investigation. Crucially, in many studies, researchers have only used spectral or categorical analyses to examine how motor learning in different modalities induces neuroplasticity, which is insufficient to understand the complex information exchange and integration that occurs between different regions of the brain.

Current research reveals inconsistent findings regarding neural adaptations induced by motor execution versus imagery paradigms, highlighting the need for systematic investigation of their distinct underlying mechanisms. Although motor imagery training has gained empirical support as an effective intervention for neuromotor rehabilitation and skill acquisition—and typically involves extended practice periods—several critical limitations persist in the literature. First, significant gaps remain in our understanding of long-term neuroplastic changes following motor learning. Second, the specific mechanisms by which motor imagery reconfigures functional brain activity remain poorly characterized. Third, existing methodological approaches often lack comprehensive assessment of both spectral and network-level dynamics, limiting our ability to capture the full complexity of training-induced neuroplasticity.

Therefore, to develop effective motor intervention, characterizing neuroplastic changes across training modalities and considering their long-term effects is essential. In this randomized controlled trial, 48 participants were randomly assigned to four experimental conditions designed to dissociate the effects of physical execution, mental simulation, and sensory input during motor learning. The Ball Rotation Task (BRT) group physically manipulated the balls with their hands, engaging sensorimotor networks through actual movement execution. The Video-Guided Imagery (VGI) group observed task-relevant videos while simultaneously performing kinesthetic motor imagery, activating motor planning circuits without overt movement. The Tactile group maintained physical contact with the balls but performed no rotation, controlling for somatosensory input without action execution. Finally, the Control group received no training intervention. We hypothesized that both BRT and VGI training would enhance motor performance relative to control conditions, but through distinct neurophysiological mechanisms, this difference in neural mechanisms is expected to be reflected in the unique pattern of power spectrum distribution and functional network reorganization across frequency bands. Spectral and graph-theoretic analyses quantified rhythmic activity and network properties in task-relevant regions of interest (ROIs), elucidating ME and MI effects on functional reorganization and exploring long-term neural mechanism underlying motor adaptation induced by exercise execution and motion image.

Materials and methods

Participants

Forty-eight healthy right-handed adults (25 male, 23 female; aged 20–28 years, mean ± SD = 23.25 ± 1.86) participated in this study. All participants met the following criteria: (a) no history of neurological/psychiatric disorders, (b) absence of sensorimotor impairments, and (c) no history of psychosis. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. The study protocol received official approval from the Ethics Committee of Beijing Institute of Technology.

Experimental design and procedure

Participants completed a 7 day motor training protocol comprising three phases: (1) pre-test assessment, (2) 5 day training intervention, and (3) post-test assessment. Participants were randomly and equally assigned to four experimental groups: (1) Ball Rotation Task (BRT group), (2) Video Guide Imagery (VGI group), (3) Tactile intervention (Tactile group), and (4) Control condition (Control group) (Fig. 1A).Fig. 1Experimental tasks and procedures. During pre-testing, all participants completed resting-state EEG (rs-EEG) recordings followed by motor performance assessment in an electromagnetically shielded chamber(Fig. 1B).The procedure began with rs-EEG acquisition, during which participants were instructed to close their eyes while brain signals were recorded for at least 5 min. Subsequently, motor performance was assessed by having participants rotate two wooden balls sequentially with their right and left hands. Each trial consisted of a 20 s rotation period followed by a 10 s rest interval, with five consecutive trials performed for each hand. Participants were explicitly instructed to rotate the balls as rapidly as possible while avoiding drops. If a ball fell onto the table, the participant was required to resume rotation immediately. If a ball fell to the floor, the trial was terminated and repeated, with an additional trial conducted after the initial five trials to ensure five valid trials. The experimenter recorded the total number of rotations during all valid trials, with one rotation defined as a complete positional exchange of the ball in the hand.

During the training intervention phase, participants from each assigned group underwent five consecutive days of motor training. All groups except the Control group completed ten daily training sessions (Fig. 1C), with each 3 min training block comprising 2 min of active training followed by 1 min of rest. Participants performed the training under experimenter supervision, with their right hands occluded by cardboard boxes to eliminate visual feedback. For each training block, participants in BRT group physically rotated the ball as rapidly as possible while maintaining grip; participants in VGI group observed a ball-rotation video while simultaneously imagining the rotational movements; participants in Tactile group held the ball without performing rotations; participants in Control group received no training throughout the five-day period.

To ensure participants actively engaged in motor imagery rather than passively watching the videos, we implemented a multi-faceted verification protocol: (1) During each training day, participants completed an questionnaire after each training session to rate the vividness and clarity of their imagery on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = no image/can't feel the movement, 5 = perfect image/can feel the movement as if actually performing it); and (2) Experimenter monitoring ensured participants maintained focused attention during video viewing. The average vividness score across the five training days was 4.05 (SD = 0.86), confirming consistent and high-quality motor imagery engagement throughout the intervention period. All training sessions were scheduled at consistent times (either morning or afternoon) corresponding to each participant's initial assessment schedule from Day 1.

After the last training session, all participants will undergo a post-training assessment. The procedure and instructions are the same as for the pre-test assessment.

Both pre-test and post-test assessments recorded behavioral data of rotational counts per 30 s interval across all trials. EEG acquisition and signal preprocessing

EEG signals were recorded using a Neuroscan SynAmps2 EEG system with a 64-channel electrode cap. Signals were acquired at a sampling rate of 5000 Hz with electrodes positioned according to the international 10–20 system (Fig. 2A). All electrode impedances were maintained below 15 kΩ throughout recordings. The reference electrode was placed between Oz and CPz, and the ground electrode was placed on AFz.Fig. 2Data processing and analysis flow. A. EEG data acquisition. B. EEG preprocessing. C. EEG data preprocessing was performed using the MATLAB-based open-source toolbox EEGLab v2020.0 [10]. Raw EEG signals were first bandpass filtered using a Hamming windowed sinc FIR filter between 1 and 45 Hz. The filtered data were then down-sampled from 5000 to 1000 Hz to reduce computational load. Noisy segments and bad channels were identified and removed based on visual inspection and automated artifact detection. Independent component analysis (ICA) was subsequently applied using the Infomax algorithm to decompose EEG signals into 60 temporally independent components. Artifact-related components (e.g., ocular movements, blinks, electromyographic activity, and electrocardiographic interference) were systematically identified by experienced researchers and excluded from reconstruction. On average, 13.8 ± 5.6 artifactual components were removed per participant. The cleaned EEG data were then re-referenced to the common average reference (Fig. 2B).

Power spectral analysis was conducted across all trials by computing frequency-band-specific power. Discrete fast Fourier transform (FFT) with Welch’s method (window length: 2 s, 50% overlap) was employed for spectral estimation. The frequency spectrum was partitioned into four bands: theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–13 Hz), beta (13–30 Hz), and low gamma (30–45 Hz) (Fig. 2C).

Brain functional network analysis

The brain functional network serves as an objective measure of statistical dependencies among neural nodes or cortical regions. During imagination-related cognitive tasks, dynamic reconfigurations within these networks manifest as measurable alterations in topological organization. This study quantified functional connectivity by calculating the Phase Lag Index (PLI) between all pairwise EEG channel combinations across four frequency bands (theta, alpha, beta, and low gamma). Functional connections were determined based on PLI values exceeding predefined thresholds.

Brain functional networks provide a mathematical representation of statistical dependencies among neural nodes or cortical regions, where dynamic reconfigurations during cognitive tasks manifest as measurable alterations in topological organization. In this study, functional connectivity was quantified by calculating the Phase Lag Index (PLI) between all pairwise EEG channel combinations across four frequency bands (theta, alpha, beta, and low gamma). The PLI metric was selected because it is robust to volume conduction effects and effectively eliminates spurious zero-lag phase synchronization that may arise from common reference issues or volume conduction, as demonstrated in previous neurophysiological studies [51, 56].

Given the critical challenge of threshold selection in functional connectivity analysis [1], we adopted a multi-threshold averaging approach to mitigate threshold-specific bias while maintaining network sparsity. This method, validated in prior EEG connectivity studies [57], systematically applies multiple thresholds to generate sparse networks, then averages the resulting topological properties. Specifically, we selected nine threshold values ranging from 0.1 to 0.5 with an incremental step of 0.05. This range was deliberately chosen based on empirical evidence that low thresholds typically retain noisy connections, while high thresholds often produce disconnected networks with isolated nodes that compromise graph-theoretical analyses [1]. The step size of 0.05 represents a balance between computational feasibility and sufficient sampling density across the threshold spectrum.

For each threshold value, PLI matrices were binarized to construct sparse networks, excluding connections with weights below the threshold. Topological analyses were then performed on each threshold-specific network, and final metrics were derived by averaging across all nine threshold iterations. This approach effectively reduces threshold-dependent variability while preserving the essential network architecture. Network characterization was performed using GRETNA [46], which computed key graph-theoretical metrics including node degree and clustering coefficient. These parameters collectively provide quantitative descriptions of the networks' topological architecture (Fig. 2C).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.2) with relevant packages. The significance level was set at α = 0.05 for all primary analyses. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, as it is the most powerful test for normality with small samples. Parametric tests were applied to normally distributed data, while non-parametric alternatives were used for non-normally distributed data.

For behavioral data, paired t-tests compared pre- and post-training ball rotation scores within each group. Between-group differences in performance changes (ΔBR = post-test—pre-test) were analyzed using one-way ANOVA with Tukey's HSD post-hoc tests for normally distributed data. Effect sizes were reported as Cohen's d for t-tests and partial eta-squared (η2) for ANOVA.

For EEG spectral power, PLI connectivity, and network parameters, non-parametric approaches were predominantly employed due to non-normal distributions. Kruskal–Wallis tests with Dunn's post-hoc comparisons evaluated between-group differences. Effect sizes were calculated as η2 for Kruskal–Wallis tests, with Cliff's delta (δ) for pairwise comparisons. We implemented permutation-based correction (1000 iterations) for all post-hoc tests. All p-values reported in the main text and figures are permutation-corrected.

Discussion

This study investigated EEG signatures associated with the long-term effects of motor learning through motor execution and motor imagery. Results demonstrated significant performance gains in motor performance following five days of training. Crucially, spectral analysis revealed enhanced frontal beta-band activity in the BRT group contrasted with beta suppression in the VGI group post-training. Functional network construction identified dynamic reorganization of frontoparietal connectivity, accompanied by alterations in clustering coefficient distributions and nodal degree patterns. Collectively, these findings indicate that both physical execution and mental simulation facilitate motor skill acquisition through distinct yet complementary neuroplastic mechanisms, modifying both behavioral performance and brain activity.

Across all experimental groups, ball rotation scores demonstrated significant improvement post-training, with the motor training group (BRT) exhibiting substantially greater enhancement than other conditions. These findings suggest that even one week of targeted training suffices to refine ball rotation performance, with physical practice yielding superior behavioral gains compared to alternative interventions. Consistent with prior investigations [2, 37], the observed behavioral improvements align with the functionally equivalent hypothesis—wherein motor imagery and physical execution share similar processes and draw on the same action representation [39]. This mechanistic overlap likely underlies the comparable behavioral enhancements observed across physical training, motor imagery, and tactile intervention groups.

The results of this study demonstrated that the VGI group exhibited a significant decrease in frontal beta rhythm after training, while the BRT group showed enhanced beta rhythm activity, with statistically significant differences between the two groups (p < 0.05). Beta-band oscillations play a critical role in sensorimotor integration and motor control [16], and their divergent changes may reflect heterogeneous mechanisms of neural regulation induced by the two training modalities. Specifically, the increased beta activity in the BRT group might be associated with the sustained motor output demands during actual motor training, which necessitate repeated neuronal recruitment to support the gradual optimization of motor skills [34]. In contrast, the reduced beta activity in the VGI group could indicate that motor imagery training accelerates the efficiency of neural resource integration through mental simulation. According to Steele's stage-dependent theory of motor learning [41], neural network activity strengthens during the initial stage of skill acquisition to support the encoding of novel tasks but diminishes during the consolidation phase due to task automation. Our findings align with this theory: the persistent increase in beta activity in the BRT group may reflect delayed consolidation through repetition-dependent maintenance of neuronal recruitment, while the decrease in beta activity in the VGI group suggests that mental simulation accelerates skill stabilization, bringing motor performance closer to a plateau. Notably, the study by Aoyama et al. [2] suggests that that the difficulty of motor imagery training should exceed 1.5 times the participant's initial average ability in order to maximize neuroplasticity. Although the training difficulty for the VGI group in this study (matched to the participants' initial maximum ability) did not reach this threshold, the results indicate that it still effectively triggered neural adaptive reorganization during the consolidation phase. This adaptability may arise from mental simulation refining existing motor representations [22], reducing the consumption of redundant neural resources and thereby accelerating performance stabilization. Consequently, the divergent beta-band changes induced by the two training modalities may stem from their differentiation in the stages of motor learning: actual motor training reinforces neuroplasticity through repetitive actions, sustaining neuronal recruitment, whereas motor imagery training optimizes neural resource allocation efficiency through mental simulation, hastening adaptive neural integration.

This study further elucidated differential effects of training modalities on frontoparietal functional networks. In the theta band (4–8 Hz), both BRT and VGI groups exhibited significantly enhanced functional connectivity between and within frontoparietal regions compared to the control group, though their network dynamics displayed distinct patterns. The frontoparietal network, long associated with higher-order cognitive control [29], demonstrated strengthened theta-band connectivity post-training in BRT and VGI groups relative to controls, indicating improved information transfer between motor and cognitive regions through both physical and imagined practice. Notably, the BRT group showed attenuated parieto-occipital connectivity compared to controls, potentially reflecting reallocation of visuospatial attentional resources—physical training may enhance functional integration in parietal motor control areas while reducing reliance on visual feedback [35]. In the beta band (13–30 Hz), connectivity within the parietal region was weakened in all groups, reflecting task automation as well as increased neural efficiency [16]. Titone et al. further linked beta-band connectivity reduction during motor learning to adaptive resting-state network reorganization, providing a mechanistic basis for these shared changes [43]. However, intergroup differences revealed distinct pathways: BRT and Tactile groups exhibited additional parieto-occipital beta connectivity attenuation, likely reflecting optimized visuomotor integration. Specifically, physical (BRT) and tactile training reduced dependence on visual monitoring through repetitive motion or somatosensory feedback, manifesting as weakened parieto-occipital beta connectivity—a finding consistent with Phunruangsakao et al., who demonstrated that tactile feedback reduces cognitive load in visually intensive tasks [32]. Conversely, VGI group’s beta suppression may stem from rapid integration of redundant parietal resources, this observation aligns with the finding of Calcagno et al. that functional network reorganization of the beta band in visuomotor integration tasks closely relates to optimization of neural efficiency [6].

This study further revealed distinct patterns of brain topological network reorganization across training modalities. Physical motor training (BRT group) and motor imagery training (VGI group) drove divergent network parameter changes through distinct neural mechanisms, manifested in group differences in alpha-band clustering coefficients and theta-band nodal degrees. Alpha-band oscillations are closely associated with sensorimotor functions and predictive value for cognitive-motor performance [12]. Actual motor training (BRT group) induced reduced alpha-band clustering coefficients, potentially reflecting optimized functional connectivity reorganization in motor regions—a shift from centralized to distributed network topology. This finding aligns with Zhang et al., who demonstrated that physical training reduces visual monitoring dependency through repetitive motor output while enhancing functional integration in motor control areas via dorsal attention network (DAN)-sensorimotor network (SMN) synergies [55]. In contrast, the VGI group exhibited increased alpha-band clustering coefficients, consistent with prior observations of motor imagery enhancing functional connectivity in sensorimotor cortices. Investigations of bilateral hand motor imagery have identified elevated nodal degrees and clustering coefficients in right sensorimotor regions during imagined movements [58]. This suggests that increased alpha-band clustering coefficients in the VGI group may indicate stronger functional integration among relevant brain areas facilitated by motor imagery training.

Theta-band (4–8 Hz) nodal degree alterations similarly reflected reorganization within cognitive control and motor planning networks. An fMRI study comparing motor execution and imagery tasks identified distinct hub nodes and connectivity patterns between the two modalities [50], suggesting differential functional connectivity profiles during actual versus imagined motor training that manifested as theta-band network parameter divergences. Theta oscillations have been associated with attentional engagement, working memory, and cognitive control processes [7]. Specifically, the increased theta-band nodal degrees in frontal electrodes (F2, FC2, FCz, Cz) observed in the VGI group may reflect heightened cognitive control and attentional resource allocation required for motor imagery. Conversely, reduced nodal degrees in these regions for the BRT group are likely to indicate diminished cognitive control demands during physical task execution. Furthermore, temporal region network changes in the BRT group may reflect suppression of task-irrelevant inputs [28]. Collectively, these findings suggest that network restructuring in the BRT group reflects neural efficiency improvements characteristic of skilled performance stages, whereas changes in the VGI group underscore persistent reliance on visuomotor integration control mechanisms.

A limitation of the current study design is the inability to fully dissociate the neural effects of motor imagery from those of action observation in the VGI group. During VGI training, participants were required to perform kinesthetic motor imagery while simultaneously observing videos of ball rotation movements. Recent neurophysiological research has demonstrated that action observation alone can significantly modulate cortical excitability and functional connectivity in motor networks, particularly in the alpha and beta frequency bands. For instance, Guidali et al. [20] demonstrated in healthy subjects that visuomotor paired associative stimulation (m-PAS), which repeatedly pairs action observation with transcranial magnetic stimulation over the primary motor cortex, not only enhances motor-evoked potentials in a muscle-specific fashion by engaging the Action Observation Network (AON), but also modulates large-scale functional connectivity patterns in the alpha and beta frequency bands during action observation. Similarly, Picardi et al. [33] showed that the mere repeated observation of hand-grasping actions can induce functional reorganization within motor circuits, with effects on corticospinal excitability that correlate with stroke chronicity. These findings align with our observation of reduced beta-band power and enhanced frontoparietal theta-band connectivity in the VGI group, suggesting that both motor imagery and action observation likely contributed to the observed neural reorganization. However, our experimental design lacked a dedicated action observation-only control condition, precluding definitive attribution of effects exclusively to motor imagery components. Future research should incorporate such control conditions (e.g., passive observation without imagery instructions) to disentangle these intertwined mechanisms. Additionally, employing neurostimulation techniques, as demonstrated by Guidali et al. [20]. and Picardi et al. [33], could help establish causal relationships between specific training modalities and their neuroplastic effects.

This study still has several limitations that warrant consideration as well. First, the training protocol spanned only five days, placing it within the intermediate-term phase of motor learning rather than representing stable long-term neuroplasticity. The observed neural adaptations likely reflect rapid functional reorganization during skill acquisition rather than consolidated structural changes. Second, the present study's sample consisted exclusively of young, healthy, right-handed adults, which, while enhancing experimental control and clarifying underlying neural mechanisms, limits the generalizability of our findings to older adults, left-handed individuals, and clinical populations with motor impairments such as stroke survivors or patients with Parkinson's disease. Future research should incorporate longitudinal assessments at multiple post-training intervals (e.g., one week, one month, three months) to distinguish transient from enduring neural changes and recruit diverse samples across age groups, handedness, and clinical conditions to establish the robustness and clinical applicability of neuroplastic changes. Third, this study employed the standard PLI rather than the more robust weighted PLI (wPLI) as the functional connectivity index. While PLI effectively eliminates zero-lag synchronization, wPLI provides a more nuanced estimation of connection strength by considering the shape of the phase difference distribution [45]. This choice limits our sensitivity to subtle variations in connection strength and may affect the characterization of weak connection network properties. Future research should consider implementing wPLI or other improved connectivity metrics, particularly when analyzing the contribution of weak connections to network dynamics.

Additionally, while EEG was exclusively utilized in this study, subsequent investigations could integrate complementary neuroimaging modalities (e.g., functional magnetic resonance imaging and transcranial magnetic stimulation) to enhance spatial resolution and provide comprehensive mechanistic insights. Furthermore, this experiment focused solely on manual motor paradigms; extending this work to other body regions (e.g., lower-limb movements) would advance our understanding of domain-specific neural mechanisms and optimize rehabilitation strategies for clinical populations (e.g., stroke patients). Nevertheless, our methodology provides readily replicable protocols and establishes a quantitative foundation for investigating long-term functional connectivity (FC) patterns following motor imagery (MI) and motor execution (ME) interventions—a currently understudied research domain.

Conclusions

The integration of EEG-based motor execution and imagery training with functional brain network analysis provides a mechanistic framework for understanding neuroplasticity in healthy adults. While these findings offer preliminary neurophysiological insights for potential clinical translation, direct application to rehabilitation settings requires validation in patient populations with motor impairments and assessment of functional recovery outcomes. Future research should prioritize longitudinal studies in clinical cohorts to determine the therapeutic relevance and stability of these neurophysiological signatures, and implementing complementary connectivity estimation technique. Furthermore, advanced network construction methods capable of capturing weakly connected neural assemblies should be adopted to enhance representations of brain network organization. This approach is particularly imperative for studies using limited electrode configurations, as it would simultaneously enhance representational accuracy and cross-study comparability.
