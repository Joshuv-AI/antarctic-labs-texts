Moroccan Arabic adaptation and cross cultural validation of the Schizophrenia Quality of Life short version questionnaire SQoL 18 in individuals with schizophrenia
Authors: Kachouchi Aymen, Sebbani Majda, Karroumi Saadia, Adali Imane, Manoudi Fatiha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAssessing quality of life (QoL) is an important aspect of the management of chronic diseases such as schizophrenia. However, there is no validated Moroccan specific QoL instrument for schizophrenia patients.ObjectivesThis study aimed to adapt and assess psychometric characteristics of The Schizophrenia Quality of Life questionnaire (SQoL-18).MethodsWe conducted a cross sectional study between May 2022 and October 2022 of 43 patients with schizophrenia attending the psychiatry department at the University hospital Mohamed VI Marrakech, Morocco. The SQoL-18 underwent translation into Moroccan Arabic language via the forward and backward translation method. The resulting version was subsequently evaluated for content validity by a panel of experts including psychiatrists and epidemiologists. Internal consistency analysis was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha, while external validity was assessed by examining correlations with the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS), the Global Assessment of Functioning and WOOQOL-BREF.ResultsThe Moroccan Arabic version of SQoL-18 was found to have satisfactory content validity. The global Cronbach’s alpha was 0,866 and the specific Cronbach’s alpha for the dimensions of SQoL-18 were satisfactory except for one dimension (Self Esteem dimension). Elevated QoL levels are globally associated with reduced severity levels of schizophrenia.ConclusionThese results demonstrate satisfactory psychometric properties of the Moroccan Arabic version of SQoL-18.Electronic supplementary materialThe online version of this article (10.1007/s44192-026-00436-1) contains supplementary material, which is available to authorized users.

Background

Schizophrenia, a severe psychiatric disorder, is estimated to impact approximately 26–45 million people globally, causing significant disability [1]. Its prevalence ranges between 0.4 and 1% posing a significant public health challenge at both National and International levels. In Morocco, the National Survey of Prevalence of Mental Disorders among individuals aged 15 years and older identified a 5.6% incidence of psychotic disorder, with over 200.000 Moroccans aged 15 and above affected by schizophrenia [2]. It typically commences during adolescence or early adulthood, with symptoms varying among individuals and fluctuating over time. Nevertheless, the prognosis generally tends to be severe and enduring [3]. Individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia in low and middle income countries raise significant concern due to their restricted access to mental health services. Merely 50% of these patients with schizophrenia receive the necessary treatment [4]. Hence, interventions focused on enhancing the quality of life seem beneficial.

The concept of Quality of life (QoL) is multifaceted and intricate, encompassing various indicators like satisfaction, life style and mental well-being and liberty of choice [5]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) in 1994, QoL is defined as “an individual’s subjective perception of their place in society, considering the cultural and value frameworks that influence them, in relation to their aspirations, expectations, norms, and worries” (WHO). Evaluating this aspect necessitates a scale that is appropriately tailored and validated.

Studies the QoL in patients with schizophrenia began since the 1960 with the policy of deinstitutionalization [6]. QoL makes it possible to evaluate functioning globally and is not limited to a symptomatic approach. It encompasses different dimensions of an individual’s life, including: social interactions, physical health, functional abilities, psychological status, economic status and personal well-being [7]. Despite the potential benefits of utilizing QoL measures in schizophrenia treatment, such as enhancing treatment adherence, improving satisfaction with care, better health outcomes and health costs reduction [8], the assessment of QoL remains underused in clinical practice.

This underutilization is attributed to various factors. Firstly, there is a lack of consensus in defining QoL [9]. Additionally, some studies suggest that the cognitive impairment experienced by patients might impact the reliability of self-administrated questionnaires assessing QoL [10]. Moreover, the use of generic scales that fail to consider the specificity of schizophrenia has also been cited as a limitation [5].

To date, no specific QoL instrument for schizophrenia based on the perspectives of patients has been adapted and validated in Moroccan Arabic. Therefore, the objective of this study was to adapt a specific QoL instrument for schizophrenia, namely the Schizophrenia Quality of Life short-version questionnaire (SQoL-18), for use in Morocco and to assess the psychometric properties of the adapted version.

Methods

Study participants

This cross-sectional study prospectively evaluated 43 patients who attended attending the psychiatry department at the University Hospital Mohamed VI Marrakech, Morocco, from May 2022 to October 2022. Participants were outpatients receiving care at the psychiatric department. All had been engaged in psychiatric follow-up for at least six months at the time of the study.

The study’s inclusion criteria consisted of individuals aged 18 or above, diagnosed with schizophrenia based on the criteria outlined in the fifth edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) [11]; and proficient in speaking Moroccan Arabic. The exclusion criteria encompassed individuals experiencing an acute psychotic episode or diagnosed with schizoaffective disorder, depression, bipolar disorder, comorbid intellectual disability, substance-induced psychotic disorder (as defined by DSM-5), or any sensory or cognitive impairments that could hinder their ability to undergo evaluation Participants were informed about the purpose of the study and the voluntary nature of participation. Informed consent was obtained. Given the high prevalence of illiteracy, trained investigators administered the questionnaires through face-to-face interviews in a confidential setting.

Approval for the study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the University of Cady Ayyad Faculty of Medicine and Pharmacy Marrakech, and the patients provided informed consent to participate.

Given the vulnerability of individuals with schizophrenia, informed consent was obtained through a structured process ensuring comprehension of study objectives and voluntary participation. Explanations were provided in Moroccan Arabic, and participants were assured that refusal would not impact their clinical care.

Socio demographic information

Socio demographic variables such as: gender, age, level of education, marital status and family history were gathered as part of the data collection process.

Clinical characteristics

This section included the age of onset, duration of disease, use of licit and illicit substances, psychotic symptoms based on the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) [12], and the Global Assessment of Functioning (GAF) scale.

The GAF score serves to characterize an individual’s overall level of functioning and capability engage in daily activities [13]. This scale spans from 0 to 100, where lower scores indicate severe challenges in performing daily tasks, whereas higher scores indicate minimal difficulties in everyday functions [14].

Measurement of QoL

QoL was measured using the WHOQOL-BREF (World Health Organization Quality of Life—BREF version) and the SQoL-18.

This instrument was designed to gauge the QoL, health, and overall well-being of both individuals and populations. It comprises 26 self-administered questions focusing on individuals’ perceptions of their health and well-being during the preceding two weeks. Linguistic adaptation of the SQoL18.

The authors granted permission to adapt the instrument with the consent of the authors and the MAPI Trust Research. Permission to translate and culturally adapt the SQoL-18 into Moroccan Arabic was obtained from the original authors prior to the start of the study.

The Forward and backward translations of the French version of SQoL-18 questionnaire were completed by expert translators. Firstly, two independent Moroccan Arabic experts translated the original French version into Moroccan Arabic. The experts examined and compared these two versions to reach a single version by consensus. Secondly, one French expert translated this intermediate Moroccan Arabic version again into French (backward translation) and compared the results with the original French version to ensure conceptual, semantic and technical equivalence. Finally, all expert translators participated in the proofreading test for the new version. Because the sentences of the SQoL-18 were written in clear and direct language, there was no difficulty in translating the items. Following the forward/ backward translation of the instrument, we conducted a key-informant consensus meeting (researchers and translators).

A convenience sample of 12 patients was used for the pretest.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the JAMOVI package. The data were summarized using measures such as means and standard deviations for quantitative variables, while frequency distributions were utilized for qualitative variables.

The psychometric evaluation consisted of assessing the validity for each dimension and item using the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient and inter-item and item correlation dimensions. Cronbach’s alpha value ≥ 0.5 was considered acceptable, and a value ≥ 0.7 was considered good [22].

The correlation between the score of each dimension and its corresponding items was assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation, and, in line with previous validation studies [23], a correlation coefficient of r ≥ 0.4 was considered acceptable for construct validity. Inter-item correlations were evaluated separately for the items in each dimension using Spearman’s rank correlation. An exploratory principal component factor analysis was used to extract factors with proper a value criterion of 1. The load factors were obtained using Varimax rotation by the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin method for the adequacy of the sample and the Bartlett-test for the sphericity.

External (convergence) validity was checked using Spearman’s correlation. The statistical significance level was 5%.

Results

Transcultural adaptation (content validity = expert panel)

The adaptation process did not reveal any difficulty in translating items from French to Moroccan Arabic. However, the expert panel was aware of the possibility of difficulty in data collection among patients given the high prevalence of illiteracy within the intended population. The expert panel attempted to conserve the experiential meaning of the items as much as possible during the adaptation process (online Appendix).

Psychometric properties of the Moroccan Arabic version of the SQoL18

Reliability (internal consistency measure)

The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient calculated for the 18 items was 0.866, surpassing the reference value of 0.70, indicating strong internal consistency indicating satisfactory reliability. Cronbach’s alpha scores ranged from 0.51 (for self-esteem) to 0.93 (for family relationships). The Cronbach’s alpha coefficients demonstrated satisfactory levels of internal consistency for seven dimensions, with the exception being the self-esteem dimension (r = 0,51) (Table 2).

Construct validity

In the initial exploratory factor analysis, the rotated matrix results were obtained using the main axis and three common factors. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin measure was approximately 0.72, indicating an acceptable sampling adequacy, while the Bartlett test of sphericity was statistically significant ( <0.001), confirming that the variables were significantly correlated. During the analysis, items with high factor loadings (> 0.60) were selected for consideration.

External validity

The SQoL-18 did not exhibit any statistically significant association with age, showing a correlation coefficient of 0.336. As anticipated, the SQoL-18 dimension scores did not demonstrate a correlation with the duration of illness. Regarding the associations between SQoL-18 dimension scores and clinical features (PANSS), it was observed that higher levels of QoL were generally linked with lower levels of severity in schizophrenia. We also found a large positive correlation between SQoL-18 dimension scores and functioning GAF scores (r = 0,788) (p < 0,001). We also compared the domains of the WHOQOL-BREF with the dimensions of the SQoL-18, the SQoL-18 dimension scores for psychological well-being (PsW) and physical well-being (PhW) Displayed strong correlations with the dimension scores of the WHOQOL-BREF, specifically assessing similar domains. Discussion

QoL assessment in mental health has gained importance since the deinstitutionalization movement and the clarification of the definition of health. This study investigated the psychometric properties of the SQoL-18 in its Moroccan Arabic version.

Most health questionnaires were developed in Western countries and require cross-cultural adaptation to ensure better cultural relevance and to encourage clinicians to use them in routine clinical practice [24]. This problem arises even more in terms of mental health specifically in patients with schizophrenia. It is essential to adopt a broader approach to QoL assessment that takes into account patients’ perspectives, emotions, and experiences rather than focusing solely on symptomatology [25].

The main constraints in the cross-cultural adaptation of QoL scales are linked to the socio-cultural specificities specific to each country [26]. Several cross-cultural adaptation methods have been proposed in the literature, the most important of which are the recommendations of Guillemin [27]. Validating an instrument in another language presents numerous challenges. The process requires a rigorous and comprehensive methodology to ensure that the original meaning of the text aligns appropriately with the target culture [28].

Our findings demonstrate satisfactory validity and reliability of the Moroccan Arabic version of the SQoL-18 for assessing the QoL among patients diagnosed with schizophrenia. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient obtained for the 18 items was 0.866, aligning with the results from a previous validation study of the SQoL-18 (α = 0,88) [17]. We also found that Cronbach’s alpha was satisfactory for seven of eight dimensions and acceptable for the Self Esteem dimension. In the Spanish version, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients demonstrated satisfactory values (> 0.7) for four of the eight dimensions in Bolivia and Chile, and for five dimensions in Peru, as reported by Caqueo-Urizar [29]. In the initial version, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficients ranged from 0.72 to 0.84 for the entire sample [17]. In our study, it should be noted that one dimension (Self Esteem) presented low Cronbach’s alpha coefficients.

In a review of literature that describes the difficulties of translating the scales into classical or dialectal Arabic, it was interesting to note that words connected to how we can describe the Self Esteem such as: taste, symptoms, liking, love, and hostility were problematic and words with emotional connotations like “sense”, “feelings”, “very”, “rely on”, “share”, “effort”, and “influence” were identified as challenging to comprehend in this study’s findings [30]. The results of the assessment of Self-esteem in schizophrenia seem to be paradoxical, some patients can simultaneously have high scores on measuring both positive and negative self-esteem [31]. The literature also posits that stigma, social functioning, depression and psychotic symptoms are detrimental to self-esteem in patients with schizophrenia [31]. These results should be confirmed using larger samples in other studies. These observations underscore the importance of culturally adapting sensitive items and validate the need for future studies on larger samples to confirm these results.

Regarding external validity, our findings showed that SQoL-18 dimension scores were not correlated with age or duration of illness, similar to the results of the Spanish validation study [29]. The Moroccan Arabic SQoL-18 showed a negative correlation with disease severity, confirming that better QoL scores are associated with lower symptom severity. The SQoL-18 index showed a negative correlation with both the PANSS total and general psychopathology scores. Several studies have established an association where higher overall clinical symptoms tend to be related to a less favorable QoL in individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia [25]. These results confirm the main characteristics formulated in the initial validation study of the SQoL-18.

The SQoL-18 dimension scores for psychological well-being and physical well-being showed a high correlation with dimension scores of the WHOQOL-BREF assessing the same kind of domains : psychological health (r = 0,649; p < 0,001) and physical health (r = 0,565; p < 0,001). The SQoL-18 index exhibited a strong correlation with the GAF score (r = 0,788; p < 0,001) as well as the Environment dimension of the WHOQOL-BREF.

There was a weak correlation between the WHOQOL-BREF Social Relationships domain and the SQoL-18 Relationships with Friends dimension, and no significant correlation with the Family Relationships dimension. This indicates that the social dimension remains difficult to evaluate either with family members or with neighbors and friends [25, 32]. On the family side, two types of families have been identified in schizophrenia: families with low emotional expression and families with high emotional expression.

In Morocco, approximately 77% of families of patients with schizophrenia show high emotional expression. Even in the Moroccan social context, families live with a spirit of solidarity and mutual aid, socioeconomic challenges in traditional societies may hinder community cohesion [33]. The family is also affected by stigma and is torn between contradictory attitudes towards the patient such as: overprotection, rejection and aggressiveness [34]. Families also grapple with a lack of information; 76% expressed having no awareness about the patient’s illness, and merely 11% of family members acknowledged perceiving the condition as conducive to leading a typical life [35].

On the side of friends, schizophrenia is often associated with dangerousness and acting out, a source of stigmatization and isolation of patients [36]. Repeated relapses and hospitalizations further weaken social ties, contributing to the progressive narrowing of the patient’s social environment. The social environment has gradually become more limited.

In summary, although the suitability indices of the Moroccan Arabic SQoL-18 were acceptable, it is important to understand the following difficulties.

Strengths and limitations

To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the initial endeavor in Morocco to validate a Moroccan Arabic questionnaire addressing the QoL among patients diagnosed with schizophrenia. This version will allow for a more objective evaluation of the QoL of patients with schizophrenia in Morocco.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. The sample size was relatively small and recruited primarily from public mental health services, limiting generalizability. This sample may not reflect the broader population of individuals with schizophrenia, particularly those treated in private settings with different socio-economic profiles. Future research with larger, multicenter samples is necessary to confirm these findings.

Moreover, the methodology required that the questionnaire be administered by investigators due to high illiteracy rates in the target population. This differs from the original self-administered format and may introduce interviewer bias, potentially affecting responses. The future development of a self-administered classical Arabic version for literate populations would represent a significant improvement.

Additionally, the absence of an assessment of responsiveness (the ability of the tool to detect meaningful changes over time) constitutes another important limitation. Understanding how the SQoL-18 reflects clinical changes longitudinally is crucial for its use in therapeutic monitoring. Future longitudinal studies are therefore needed to complete this validation process.

In terms of clinical implications, the availability of a validated Moroccan Arabic instrument can significantly enhance patient-centered assessment, incorporating patients’ own perceptions of their quality of life beyond mere symptom evaluation. This paves the way for a more holistic and culturally adapted approach to care.

For future research, it would be relevant to enrich the questionnaire by including dimensions specific to Moroccan culture, such as spirituality and religious beliefs, which play a central role in patients’ subjective well-being. Moreover, examining the impact of social, familial, and environmental factors, as well as psychiatric and somatic comorbidities, on quality of life measured by this tool would be valuable.

Conclusion

The current findings offer the initial evidence on the Moroccan Arabic validation and psychometric traits of the SQoL-18 among Moroccan patients diagnosed with schizophrenia. With the encouraging outcomes obtained, it is anticipated that this concise self-administered tool will prove beneficial in clinical settings within these populations.
