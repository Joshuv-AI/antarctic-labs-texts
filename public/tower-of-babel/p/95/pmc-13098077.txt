Complete Blood Count Profile in Steady State Sickle Cell Disease Patient: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Josué Louokdom Simo, Romaric De Manfouo Tuono, Ingrid Worti Sulem Yong, Prisca Tipane Angandji, Maryline Seuko Njopwouo, Claude Tagny Tayou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsSickle cell disease is a genetic disorder whose pathophysiology is primarily characterized by vasoocclusive crises and hemolytic crises. The stationary phase describes the interval between two vasoocclusive crises. The aim of this systematic review and meta‐analysis was to synthesize studies published over the past two decades on the complete blood count profile in patients with sickle cell disease in the steady state, and to emphasize the need for prospective studies to better characterize hematological profiles and establish potential reference values specific to this patient population. The goal was to provide a consolidated and contextual overview of the available evidence for this patient population.MethodsThe search period for the included studies spanned from 2000 to 2025. This systematic review and meta‐analysis was conducted following PRISMA guidelines. The included studies were descriptive cross‐sectional designs. Articles were retrieved from PubMed, Embase, Google Scholar, and Web of Science, with a total of 21 studies analyzed. Data were extracted and analyzed using R version 4.3.2, applying a random effects model for means and confidence intervals. The JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist was used to assess the risk of bias. Forest plots were employed to present overall and individual study effect sizes with 95% confidence intervals, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsThe risk of bias across these domains was low to moderate, and no study was excluded based on the quality assessment. The mean red blood cell count, estimated using a random effects model, was 3.42 T/L [2.83; 4.13], ranging from 2.54 T/L [2.33; 2.77] to 11.40 T/L [10.21; 12.73]. The mean hemoglobin was 8.83 g/dL [8.29; 9.41], with values ranging from 7.51 g/dL [7.32; 7.71] to 11.62 g/dL [11.29; 11.96]. The mean leukocyte count was 10.87 G/L [9.47; 12.49], with a range from 7.10 G/L [6.43; 7.84] to 15.64 G/L [13.72; 17.83]. The mean platelet count was 332.07 G/L [285.32; 386.48], ranging from 121.44 G/L [120.44; 122.44] to 450.00 G/L [446.55; 453.48].ConclusionThe overall results from the analyses indicate consistent differences in hematological parameters compared to the reference values established by professional societies. These findings underscore the importance of conducting prospective studies to better characterize hematological profiles and to explore potential reference values specific to patients with sickle cell disease in the steady state.Keywords: complete blood count profile, sickle cell disease, steady state

Introduction

Sickle cell disease is the most widespread hereditary hematological pathology of genetic origin in Africa and in the world, causing significant public health problems [1, 2, 3]. It is characterized genotypically by genetic disorders associated with structurally abnormal hemoglobin (Hb), which results in the episodic formation of sickle‐shaped red blood cells (RBCs) and several clinical manifestations [4, 5, 6]. The main clinical consequences associated with the pathophysiology are vasoocclusive crises and hemolytic crises [7, 8]. Moreover, they are also characterized by chronic inflammation [7, 9], associated with leukocytosis, leukocyte activation with a consequence on leukocyte adhesion to the vascular subendothelium [10, 11]. They have biological repercussions, particularly on blood cells [1].

Indeed, several studies report an intrinsic elevation and high count of leukocytes, granulocytes, and activated endothelial cells, followed by an increased expression of endothelial cell adhesion molecules associated with high levels of cytokines and elevated acute phase proteins [12, 13, 14]. Moreover, anemia, commonly seen in patients with sickle cell disease, is associated with overall disease severity [15, 16]. Whether high Hb levels or values are associated with higher rates of severe pain in patients with sickle cell disease [17], lower steady‐state Hb is generally responsible for an increased risk of stroke in these same patients [18]. Previous reports have demonstrated that elevated white blood cell counts appear to be a risk factor for several serious complications of sickle cell disease, such as increased rates of severe pain, acute chest syndrome, and mortality [19, 20, 21].

Thus, sickle cell disease constitutes a global burden and requires coordinated and effective management. For this, on a biological level, the performance of the hemogram constitutes the examination carried out in first intention and should be interpreted in accordance with the clinical picture of the patient. The identification of the stationary phase is essential to ensure the follow‐up of the patient, the stationary period defined as “the period free of crisis extending from at least 3 weeks since the last clinical event and 3 months or more since the last blood transfusion, to at least 1 week before the start of a new clinical event” [22].

However, although not associated with severe clinical forms, it must still be emphasized that the profile of the blood count in sickle cell patients in the stationary phase is typical and particular; and for the most part in the latter, the results obtained from studies are interpreted on the basis of reference values as defined by societies such as the WHO, whose reference values are themselves defined from the normal subject without sickle cell disease [19]. The main objective of this study is that it would allow us to establish, based on this previously conducted research, a diagnostic baseline and even reference ranges for this specific population group.

This systematic review and meta‐analysis was conducted to synthesize available evidence from descriptive studies reporting on the hematological profile of patients with sickle cell disease in the steady state. Although numerous individual studies have described these parameters, the findings remain fragmented and sometimes heterogeneous, limiting the ability to draw comprehensive conclusions. By systematically aggregating and quantitatively analyzing the existing data, this study seeks to provide a clearer and more structured overview of hematological patterns observed in this population. Such an approach may help clarify the range and consistency of reported findings, identify areas of variability, and highlight gaps requiring further investigation. In this context, the present meta‐analysis contributes to strengthening the evidence base and may serve as a foundation for future prospective and analytical studies.

Methods

Ethical Statement

Not applicable for this study.

Search Strategy and Study Selection

The recommendations of the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses) guideline were used as guidance for this systematic review and meta‐analysis [23]. An electronic and manual search was conducted to identify and select studies. Published articles of cross‐sectional, descriptive studies were included. PubMed, Embase, Google Scholar, and Web of Science were the electronic databases chosen to identify published studies on the blood count profile in stationary‐phase sickle cell disease patients from 2000 to 2025. Keywords were used to define search terms and were used in accordance with the title/abstract, allowing us to select relevant studies.

Boolean operators such as “OR” and “AND” were used separately and in combination to define search strategies. An example of a search combination used was: “(Sickle Cell Disease) AND (Steady State) AND (blood count profile).” Data obtained in a loop were excluded.

Study Inclusion Criteria

All included studies were original research published in English and contained minimal information regarding sample size and data on the complete blood count profile in stationary‐phase sickle cell disease patients. Interest was focused on studies that presented a complete and detailed complete blood count profile in sickle cell disease patients. The elements used to define the complete blood count parameters were defined below in the “Outcome Measurement” section.

Exclusion Criteria

Irrelevant and duplicate articles were excluded. Articles lacking necessary information, such as age and year of study, were also excluded. Those written in a language other than English were excluded.

Search Method

An electronic and manual search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, Google Scholar, and Web of Science databases using the defined keywords and studies from the period 2000–2025 were included. The studies obtained after the first search (353) were screened by two independent reviewers/researchers from the title and abstract to perform a first screening of articles to be included in the full‐text review. After excluding studies that did not meet the study objectives, the abstracts and titles of 103 articles were screened according to the eligibility criteria, and 21 were selected for full‐text assessment. The articles selected for inclusion in the systematic review and meta‐analysis were based on original articles. To ensure the quality of the review, the abstracts of the selected articles were thoroughly checked for analysis and purification. A careful assessment of each research article was subsequently carried out by each of the reviewers.

Data Extraction

An Excel 2016 spreadsheet was used for data extraction. The two previous authors independently searched the PubMed, Embase, Google Scholar, and Web of Science databases. This tool extracted information, such as study details, including authors' names, country and year of publication, study population setting and characteristics (size, age, and sex), methods employed (e.g., study design, tools, and type of interventions), and main results obtained. PRISMA guidelines were strictly adhered to during the conduct of the study.

Risk of Bias Assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was independently assessed by two reviewers using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist. This tool evaluates the appropriateness of the sampling strategy, the validity and reliability of measurements, the identification and management of potential confounding factors, and the suitability of statistical analyses [26]. The checklist consists of eight structured items assessing key methodological domains: clarity of inclusion criteria; detailed description of study participants and setting; validity and reliability of exposure measurement; use of objective and standard criteria for condition measurement; identification of potential confounding factors; strategies to address confounding; validity and reliability of outcome measurement; and appropriateness of statistical analysis. Each item is rated as “Yes,” “No,” “Unclear,” or “Not applicable.” Rather than generating a numerical summary score, the tool supports a domain‐based qualitative assessment of risk of bias, allowing for a transparent and structured interpretation of methodological strengths and limitations across studies [26]. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion and, when necessary, consultation with a third reviewer. Risk of bias was assessed at the study level and reported narratively; a general score was not assigned, in accordance with current methodological recommendations [27]. The risk of bias assessment was considered in the interpretation of pooled estimates, and sensitivity analyses were conducted where necessary to evaluate the robustness of the findings.

Data Synthesis, Management, Quality Assessment, and Analysis

R statistical software version 4.3.2 (The R Foundation for Informatics, Vienna, Austria) was used as the primary tool for statistical analyses. Since the included studies were from diverse backgrounds, heterogeneity between studies was assessed and interpreted as follows: low heterogeneity defined for I² values < 0.25 (25%), moderate heterogeneity defined for values between 0.25 (25%) and 0.5 (50%), and finally significant heterogeneity defined for values > 0.5 (50%) [28]. The mean and confidence interval from the included studies were analyzed and presented using the random effects model. To facilitate interpretation with the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist, the proportion of items rated positively (“Yes”) was calculated for each study. Studies were then classified according to their risk of bias: low risk if 80%–100% of items were rated “Yes,” moderate risk if 50%–79.9% were rated “Yes,” and high risk if 20%–49.9% were rated “Yes” [29]. For presentation of results, forest plots were used to estimate the overall effect size and the effect size for each study, along with their confidence intervals (CIs), to provide a visual representation of the data. The confidence interval was fixed at 95% for data presentation, and the significance test was set for p‐values < 0.05.

Discussion

Sickle cell disease is a disease caused by a point mutation at the sixth position of the hemoglobin β chain, resulting in the replacement of glutamine with valine at this location [49, 50]. This disease is very heterogeneous, with marked variations in both disease manifestations and clinical severity [51]. Although numerous studies have described the blood count profile in patients with sickle cell disease, including those in the steady state, none have been specifically conducted with the objective of establishing a reference framework for patient management in this phase. This systematic review and meta‐analysis aims to synthesize the literature published over the past two decades on the complete blood count profile in sickle cell patients in the steady state. The overall findings highlight consistent differences in hematological parameters compared to the reference values established by professional societies, underscoring the importance of synthesizing and contextualizing these data within the existing literature in patients with sickle cell disease in the steady state.

Analysis of the red blood cell count and erythrocyte indices reveals erythrocytopenia, accompanied by a low mean hematocrit, reflecting the intrinsic nature of the disease characterized by subclinical hemolysis [52]. Several authors also report erythrocytopenia during stationary sickle cell disease, even if it is not profound [16, 53]. Overall, the studies report normocytosis and normochromia in red blood cells. However, the red blood cell distribution width (RDW) indicates a notable degree of anisocytosis. RDW reflects the variability in erythrocyte volume and is traditionally used in laboratory hematology to assist in the differential diagnosis of anemias [54]. Its elevation in sickle cell disease reflects a profound disruption of erythrocyte homeostasis, involving both impaired erythropoiesis and reduced red blood cell survival. This dysregulation can be attributed to various underlying metabolic abnormalities, including telomere shortening, oxidative stress, inflammation, poor nutritional status, dyslipidemia, hypertension, erythrocyte fragmentation, and impaired erythropoietin function [55, 56, 57].

Furthermore, the analysis of hemoglobin, fetal hemoglobin, and reticulocytes highlights the presence of anemia in sickle cell disease, which primarily results from subclinical hemolysis, although its severity may be reduced during the steady state. These patterns have also been reported by several authors [19, 35, 58]. Furthermore, elevated fetal hemoglobin levels may be influenced by several factors, including treatment regimens, age differences among patients, and the underlying disease itself [21, 38, 59]. Variations of this parameter are also reported by authors [38, 60, 61, 62]. Furthermore, the tendency for reticulocytosis in sickle cell patients reflects bone marrow hyperactivity in response to peripheral hemolysis. This is also associated with the stress caused by anemia, which stimulates the release of immature red blood cells from the bone marrow, thereby increasing the number of reticulocytes in peripheral blood [63]. A slight decrease in nitric oxide metabolites (NOm) was observed in patients with sickle cell disease. Erythrocyte lysis releases free hemoglobin into the plasma, promoting inflammatory and oxidative effects that contribute to endothelial dysfunction [64, 65]. Hemolysis leads to the release of heme, reactive oxygen species (ROS), and arginase into the bloodstream, which increases oxidative stress and reduces nitric oxide metabolite (NOm) levels [66].

The analysis of the white blood cell lineage generally shows a slight elevation of its parameters, reflecting leukocytosis and changes in subpopulations. Sickle cell disease is inherently an inflammatory condition, with patients exposed to multiple intrinsic factors that promote inflammation even during the steady state, including viral and bacterial infections, oxidative stress, and hemolysis [67, 68, 69, 70]. This is also attributed to the activation of endothelial cells, which leads to increased expression of endothelial adhesion molecules [12, 13, 14].

Finally, the analysis of the platelet line and related indices generally shows a normal trend, although variations such as thrombocytosis and macroplateletosis can occur. These alterations are accompanied by changes in platelet distribution width and mean platelet volume. Elevated platelet counts in patients with sickle cell disease are thought to result from factors such as splenic sequestration, hyposplenism or autosplenectomy, and chronic underlying inflammation. Studies suggest a correlation between platelet parameters and the pathophysiology of sickle cell disease [71, 72]. Freedman and Karpatkin also report an increase in platelets in stable sickle cell patients [73]. Furthermore, the thrombocytosis observed, along with an elevated mean platelet volume (MPV), is thought to result from increased platelet activation in patients with sickle cell disease. Indeed, platelets play a key role in hemostasis and are also central mediators of inflammation [74].

The strength of this study lies in its ability to consolidate existing evidence and provide a foundation for prospective studies aimed at further characterizing hematological parameters in patients with sickle cell disease during the steady state. However, as a review, the study is limited by the lack of full control over the included studies, and the influence of factors such as age, sex, and disease‐modifying therapies could not be fully accounted for. Consequently, while the findings contribute valuable descriptive and contextual information, they should be interpreted cautiously and are not intended as direct guidance for clinical or therapeutic decision‐making.

Conclusion

The stationary phase refers to the interval between vaso‐occlusive crises. The aim of this systematic review and meta‐analysis was to synthesize studies published over the past two decades on the complete blood count profile in patients with sickle cell disease in the steady state. While the overall results indicate consistent differences in hematological parameters compared to reference values established by professional societies, these findings should be interpreted as descriptive and contextual, rather than prescriptive for clinical practice. Variations related to age, sex, and disease‐modifying therapies such as hydroxyurea could not be fully accounted for, and the heterogeneity of the included studies further underscores the need for caution. These observations highlight the importance of conducting prospective studies to better characterize hematological profiles and to explore potential reference values specific to sickle cell patients in the steady state.
