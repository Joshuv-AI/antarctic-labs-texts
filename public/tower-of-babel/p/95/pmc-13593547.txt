Introduction

Hereditary hemochromatosis is a systemic iron-overload disorder characterized by progressive iron deposition in multiple organs. Cardiac involvement is clinically important because myocardial iron accumulation may lead not only to cardiomyopathy and heart failure but also to a broad spectrum of electrophysiological abnormalities, including conduction disturbances, sinus node dysfunction, supraventricular tachyarrhythmias, and ventricular arrhythmias. Iron-mediated oxidative injury, cellular dysfunction, and replacement fibrosis are considered the key mechanisms of these manifestations.1, 2, 3

Among rhythm disorders, atrial fibrillation is a recognized manifestation of cardiac iron overload. In addition, involvement of the conduction system has been described, with atrioventricular block, bundle branch block, and bradyarrhythmias reported in advanced disease. These observations suggest that iron deposition may affect multiple components of the cardiac electrical system, extending beyond the working myocardium alone.1, 2, 3

However, despite the recognized association between hemochromatosis and arrhythmias, the underlying atrial substrate has not been well characterized. In particular, direct electroanatomic assessment of the left atrium using high-density voltage mapping has rarely been reported, and whether cardiac iron overload can produce advanced atrial cardiomyopathy disproportionate to the expected remodeling seen in paroxysmal atrial fibrillation remains uncertain. We report a case of hereditary hemochromatosis in which multimodality evaluation and high-density mapping demonstrated diffuse cardiac electrical involvement, including extensive left atrial low-voltage substrate, conduction system disease, and sinus node dysfunction.1,4, 5, 6

Case report

A 57-year-old man with hereditary hemochromatosis was referred for catheter ablation of symptomatic paroxysmal atrial fibrillation. He first developed hypogonadism and liver dysfunction in his 30s and was diagnosed with secondary hypogonadotropic hypogonadism at 36 years of age. Diabetes mellitus was diagnosed at 44 years of age. At 56 years of age, hereditary hemochromatosis was diagnosed after a liver biopsy and genetic testing, and therapeutic phlebotomy and iron chelation therapy were initiated. Around the same time, he developed heart failure and symptomatic paroxysmal atrial fibrillation. His clinical course was notable for multisystem manifestations of iron overload, including hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus, osteoporosis with vertebral compression fractures, hepatic dysfunction, and biopsy-proven hepatic iron overload. Cardiac involvement had also been suspected because of prior heart failure and progressive conduction abnormalities. He had no history of hypertension, significant valvular heart disease, obesity (body mass index 16.6 kg/m2), or obstructive sleep apnea.

Laboratory testing demonstrated marked systemic iron overload. Ferritin levels were persistently elevated, with a peak value of 4844 ng/mL, and transferrin saturation reached 97%. Genetic testing identified a homozygous TFR2 variant (c.2336T>C, p.Leu779Pro), classified as a variant of uncertain significance but considered a possible contributor to the phenotype in the setting of severe iron overload.

Preprocedural electrocardiography demonstrated sinus rhythm with complete right bundle branch block, left axis deviation, and first-degree atrioventricular block (Figure 1A). Preprocedural 24-hour Holter monitoring demonstrated 107,589 beats/d without significant sinus pauses or other evidence of sinus node dysfunction. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a left atrial anteroposterior diameter of 36 mm and a left atrial volume index of 46.4 mL/m2. Left atrial strain analysis demonstrated reduced left atrial reservoir strain (19%) (Figure 1B). Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated diffusely reduced myocardial signal intensity on T2∗ imaging, consistent with myocardial iron overload (Figure 2). Endomyocardial biopsy from the right ventricular septum demonstrated myocardial iron overload with intracellular hemosiderin deposition and interstitial fibrosis. Hematoxylin and eosin staining showed coarse golden-brown cytoplasmic granules within cardiomyocytes; Berlin blue staining confirmed abundant intracellular iron deposition; and Masson trichrome staining demonstrated interstitial fibrosis (Figure 3A–3C).

The patient subsequently underwent an electrophysiology study and catheter ablation. High-density electroanatomic mapping of the left atrium was performed using the EnSite X EP System (Abbott, Abbott Park, IL) with an Advisor HD Grid X Mapping Catheter (Abbott, Abbott Park, IL) during high right atrial pacing (Figure 4). Scar was defined as a region with a bipolar voltage of ≤0.1 mV, and low-voltage area was defined as a region with a bipolar voltage of <0.5 mV. Extensive low-voltage areas were observed over the posterior wall, where pacing at 10 V/1.0 ms failed to achieve local capture. Broad low-voltage involvement was also present within the pulmonary veins, with preserved capture identified only at the left inferior pulmonary vein and the anterior carina regions of both pulmonary veins. Right atrial bipolar voltage mapping was also performed before slow pathway ablation during atrial tachycardia (Supplemental Figure 1). Pulmonary vein isolation was performed using radiofrequency energy with a TactiFlex Contact Force Ablation Catheter (Abbott, Abbott Park, IL), the EnSite X mapping system, and an Ampere RF generator (Abbott, Abbott Park, IL). Radiofrequency applications were delivered at 50 W with a target contact force of 10–15 g. The target ablation index was 55 for the anterior wall and 50 for the posterior wall. Circumferential pulmonary vein isolation was therefore performed with confirmation of bidirectional block.

During isoproterenol infusion and programmed stimulation, a narrow QRS tachycardia was reproducibly induced. Electrophysiological findings were consistent with slow-fast atrioventricular nodal reentrant tachycardia. Limited slow pathway ablation was performed with junctional rhythm observed during energy delivery. Despite ablation, both the AH jump and inducibility of slow-fast atrioventricular nodal reentrant tachycardia persisted. Because the patient had no documented clinical supraventricular tachycardia and had preexisting advanced conduction disease, further aggressive slow pathway ablation was intentionally deferred to minimize the risk of atrioventricular conduction injury.

On the day after the procedure, sinus node dysfunction became clinically apparent with long pauses associated with presyncope/syncope, requiring temporary pacing support. A permanent pacemaker was subsequently implanted. A dual-chamber pacemaker (Azure XT DR MRI SureScan, Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN) was implanted using 2 SelectSecure Model 3830 leads (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN). Left bundle branch area pacing was selected because of the patient’s baseline complete right bundle branch block and progressive conduction disease. The atrial lead was positioned in the atrial septum targeting the Bachmann bundle region to facilitate physiological interatrial activation in the setting of diffuse atrial disease. The device was programmed in AAI⇄DDD mode, with a lower rate limit of 60 beats/min and an upper tracking rate of 120 beats/min. Postimplantation electrocardiography demonstrated shortening of the P-wave duration from 98 to 78 ms, with preserved positive P-wave polarity in the inferior leads. During 3 months of follow-up after pacemaker implantation, device interrogation demonstrated no recurrence of atrial fibrillation or atrioventricular nodal reentrant tachycardia. The atrial pacing percentage was 100%, whereas the ventricular pacing percentage was 0%. The patient experienced no recurrent syncope or heart failure hospitalization during follow-up.

Discussion

This case highlights 3 important clinical messages. First, hereditary hemochromatosis may manifest as disproportionate atrial cardiomyopathy with advanced left atrial substrate disease despite clinically early-stage atrial fibrillation. Second, iron overload may involve the specialized conduction system in addition to the atrial myocardium, resulting in progressive conduction disease and sinus node dysfunction. Third, multimodality evaluation combined with high-density electroanatomic mapping may provide mechanistic insight into diffuse cardiac electrical involvement in systemic iron-overload disorders.

Cardiac manifestations of hereditary hemochromatosis have traditionally been described as iron-overload cardiomyopathy leading to ventricular dysfunction, restrictive or dilated physiology, and heart failure. However, arrhythmias and conduction disturbances are also well recognized. Iron deposition within the myocardium may promote oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, cellular injury, and replacement fibrosis, thereby altering impulse formation and conduction. These mechanisms may predispose to both tachyarrhythmias and bradyarrhythmias.1, 2, 3

Atrial fibrillation is one of the most frequently reported arrhythmias in cardiac hemochromatosis, yet the underlying atrial substrate has not been sufficiently characterized. To our knowledge, peer-reviewed reports describing detailed high-density electroanatomic mapping of the left atrium in hereditary hemochromatosis are extremely limited. In routine clinical practice, extensive low-voltage areas are more commonly observed in persistent atrial fibrillation, advanced age, structural heart disease, or after prior atrial intervention. In contrast, our patient had paroxysmal atrial fibrillation but demonstrated broad low-voltage involvement of the posterior wall and pulmonary vein antral regions. This finding suggests an atrial cardiomyopathic process beyond conventional atrial fibrillation–related remodeling alone and raises the possibility of iron-overload–associated atrial cardiomyopathy.4, 5, 6

The reduction in left atrial strain further supported clinically meaningful atrial dysfunction. Low-voltage areas reflect abnormal electrical properties, whereas impaired strain reflects reduced mechanical performance. Their concordance suggests coupled electromechanical remodeling of the left atrium, supporting the presence of diffuse atrial cardiomyopathy. Such findings may be particularly relevant in infiltrative or metabolic cardiomyopathies, in which structural injury may precede or exceed the burden of clinical atrial fibrillation.4,6

Another notable feature of this case was progressive involvement of the cardiac conduction system. Baseline electrocardiography demonstrated advanced conduction abnormalities, and sinus node dysfunction became clinically evident after rhythm control, ultimately requiring permanent pacemaker implantation. Although direct histological confirmation of conduction tissue iron deposition was not available, and formal assessment of sinus node function (including sinus node recovery time and corrected sinus node recovery time) was not performed during the electrophysiology study, the coexistence of advanced baseline conduction abnormalities, diffuse left atrial substrate disease, and subsequent clinically manifest sinus node dysfunction raises the possibility of widespread electrophysiological involvement beyond isolated atrial disease. However, because sinus node dysfunction became clinically apparent only after catheter ablation, a procedure-related contribution cannot be completely excluded. Inducible atrioventricular nodal reentrant tachycardia should be interpreted cautiously, as this arrhythmia may occur in structurally normal hearts, and a direct relationship with iron overload cannot be definitively established.2,3

This case also has procedural implications. When unexpectedly advanced low-voltage substrate is encountered in patients with apparently early-stage atrial fibrillation, clinicians should consider occult infiltrative, inflammatory, or metabolic disorders. Recognition of the underlying substrate may influence ablation strategy, expectations regarding recurrence, and surveillance for progressive conduction disease requiring device therapy.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. This is a single case report, and causality cannot be definitively established. Direct histological confirmation from atrial tissue was not available. In addition, the relative contributions of aging, atrial fibrillation burden, and iron overload to the observed substrate abnormalities cannot be fully separated. Nevertheless, the concordant findings across multimodality assessment and high-density mapping support diffuse cardiac involvement associated with iron overload.

In conclusion, hereditary hemochromatosis may present as diffuse atrial cardiomyopathy and conduction system disease involving the atrial myocardium, specialized conduction tissue, and sinus node. High-density mapping combined with multimodality evaluation may help identify disproportionate substrate remodeling in patients with clinically early-stage atrial fibrillation.

Clinical implications

This case highlights several practical implications for electrophysiologists and general cardiologists. Extensive left atrial low-voltage areas in patients with apparently early-stage atrial fibrillation should prompt consideration of underlying infiltrative, inflammatory, or metabolic disorders rather than atrial fibrillation progression alone.

Hereditary hemochromatosis may involve multiple components of the cardiac electrical system, resulting not only in atrial arrhythmias but also in progressive conduction disease and sinus node dysfunction. Careful electrocardiographic assessment and longitudinal rhythm surveillance are therefore important, particularly in patients with baseline conduction abnormalities.

Multimodality evaluation integrating iron indices, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, pathology, left atrial strain analysis, and high-density electroanatomic mapping may improve recognition of diffuse cardiac involvement and help guide individualized management strategies.

Limitations

Several limitations of this report should be acknowledged.

First, this is a single case report, and the findings may not be generalizable to all patients with hereditary hemochromatosis or cardiac iron overload.

Second, although multimodality data strongly suggested diffuse cardiac involvement, a direct causal relationship between myocardial iron deposition and the observed electrophysiological abnormalities cannot be definitively established.

Third, direct histological confirmation from atrial tissue or specialized conduction tissue was not available. Therefore, iron deposition within the left atrium, sinus node, or His-Purkinje system was inferred from the overall clinical and pathological context rather than directly proven.

Fourth, formal assessment of sinus node function, including sinus node recovery time and corrected sinus node recovery time, was not performed during the electrophysiology study because there was no clinical suspicion of sinus node dysfunction before the procedure. In addition, right atrial voltage mapping was obtained during atrial tachycardia rather than sinus rhythm, precluding direct assessment of the sinus node region. Therefore, whether the subsequent sinus node dysfunction represented preexisting intrinsic disease or became clinically apparent after catheter ablation cannot be definitively determined.

Fifth, the relative contributions of age, cumulative atrial fibrillation burden, structural remodeling, and systemic iron overload to the extensive low-voltage substrate cannot be completely separated.

Sixth, inducible atrioventricular nodal reentrant tachycardia should be interpreted cautiously, as this arrhythmia can occur in structurally normal hearts and may not be directly attributable to iron overload.

Finally, long-term follow-up data regarding arrhythmia recurrence, pacemaker dependency, and progression of conduction disease were limited at the time of reporting.

Conclusion

Hereditary hemochromatosis may manifest as a diffuse electrophysiological disorder involving the atrial myocardium, specialized conduction system, and sinus node. In this case, extensive left atrial low-voltage substrate despite paroxysmal atrial fibrillation suggested disproportionate atrial remodeling beyond conventional atrial fibrillation progression. The coexistence of conduction disease and postprocedural sinus node dysfunction further supported widespread electrical involvement associated with iron overload. Multimodality assessment combined with high-density electroanatomic mapping may provide important mechanistic and clinical insights when unexpected substrate abnormalities are encountered in apparently early-stage atrial fibrillation.
