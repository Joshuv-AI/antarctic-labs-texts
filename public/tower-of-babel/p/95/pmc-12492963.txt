Pleural effusion following thoracoscopic sympathectomy in a patient with palmar hyperhidrosis
Authors: Pei Zhou, Qing Peng, Jun Li, Du Fan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThoracoscopic sympathectomy can be used to treat primary hyperhidrosis (PH). Nonetheless, there is a paucity of literature addressing the postoperative complications associated with this procedure. We report a case of a 21-year-old male patient who developed prolonged bilateral pleural effusion after undergoing thoracoscopic sympathectomy for PH. This case aims to raise awareness of this rare complication and discuss effective management strategies for it.Keywords: Thoracic sympathectomy, Primary hyperhidrosis, Complications, Pleural effusion

Background

Primary hyperhidrosis (PH) is a disorder of the sympathetic nervous system that causes excessive sweating in areas such as the hands, axillae, face, and feet. Its cause remains unclear, but it can impair social and professional life and contribute to depression [1]. Thoracoscopic sympathectomy is the most effective treatment for PH, relieving symptoms when conservative measures prove ineffective [2, 3]. The advantages are shorter time, fewer cosmetic defects, and a lower complication rate. While it effectively alleviates postoperative symptoms, the procedure carries inherent risks, including complications such as compensatory sweating, pneumothorax, and pulmonary atelectasis. Moreover, atypical complications may occur, such as injury to the great vessels or brachial plexus and the development of Horner’s syndrome [4]. Rare complications include chylothorax and pleural effusion [5]. We report a case of post-surgical pleural effusion presenting with chest pain, which was significantly relieved by thoracentesis.

Case presentation

A 21-year-old male was admitted after experiencing chest pain for over two weeks following a thoracoscopic bilateral sympathectomy for PH. Preoperative computed tomography (CT) scans were routine (Fig. 1A and B). The surgery is conducted through small incisions in the chest wall, located between the third and fourth ribs on both sides. A thoracoscope is inserted through one of the incisions to observe the sympathetic nerve chain T4 along the spine, and the pleura is electrocoagulated along the surface of the third and fourth ribs to sever the nerve branches, thereby alleviating excessive sympathetic nerve activity. After the procedure is completed, the thoracoscope and surgical instruments are removed, and the small incisions are closed with sutures. and the surgery had no intraoperative complications. Shortly after the procedure, he developed significant right-sided chest pain. A CT scan revealed a small amount of bilateral pleural effusion (Fig. 1C and D). Despite antibiotic treatment at a local clinic, his symptoms persisted. A repeat CT scan upon admission showed moderate pleural effusion on the right and a small effusion on the left (Fig. 1E and F). Laboratory tests indicated an elevated white blood cell count and neutrophil count. Additionally, the total protein level was 62.9 g/L, lactate dehydrogenase was 155.4 U/L, and glucose was 6.03 mmol/L. Other laboratory tests were within normal limits. On day 2, a thoracentesis revealed an exudative pleural effusion based on Light’s criteria (Table 1). The pleural effusion-to-serum protein ratio was 0.76 (right)/0.66 (left) (greater than 0.5), and the pleural effusion-to-serum lactate dehydrogenase ratio was 1.84(right)/0.75 (left) (greater than 0.6). These results confirm that the bilateral effusions are exudative. The triglyceride levels in bilateral pleural effusion are measured at less than 1.1 mmol/L, which effectively excludes the possibility of chylothorax. Tests for acid-fast bacilli, tuberculosis deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA), cytology, and bacterial cultures were negative. The cause of the pleural effusion remained unclear, prompting a multidisciplinary consultation regarding potential complications from the sympathectomy. During hospitalization, the patient was placed on a high-protein diet. Initially, the amount of pleural effusion was 500–600 mL/d, which gradually decreased. The chest tube was removed because no fluid was draining from the chest cavity on day 7, and the patient experienced no complications at discharge or during follow-ups, and imaging showed that no accumulation of fluid.

Discussion

Thoracic sympathectomy is a viable and safe treatment for PH, most patients report high satisfaction with compensatory sweating as the primary side effect. While pleural effusion is uncommon in this surgery, individual differences and variations in surgical techniques may lead to such complications in some cases. Thus, emphasizing case-specific features is crucial for understanding postoperative risks, and we should not overlook the rare complications that may arise. In a prospective study, only 4 of 940 post-thoracic sympathectomy complications required thoracic drainage for less than 24 h, and 1 patient developed a mild pleural effusion that improved with physiotherapy [6]. Masters, A. reports a case of unilateral non-infectious reactive pleural effusion [7]. Kwong, K. F. found asymptomatic pleural effusion in only one patient in a study of 397 patients after transthoracic sympathectomy [5]. Bilateral postoperative pleural effusions that require management are extremely rare and there are few reports of such cases in the literature.

Increased pleural vascular permeability, usually accompanied by inflammation, is a key mechanism in exudative pleural effusions. The loss of sympathetic nerve function often leads to an imbalance in the regulation of vascular smooth muscle. Under normal circumstances, the sympathetic nerves regulate the contraction and dilation of blood vessels by releasing neurotransmitters such as noradrenaline [8]. After sympathectomy, loss of neuromodulation leads to vascular smooth muscle dysregulation, resulting in vasodilation and increased wall pressure, which contribute to greater fluid and macromolecule leakage. Additionally, sympathectomy may exacerbate local inflammatory responses, promoting the release of inflammatory mediators, such as histamine and cytokines, which further enhance vascular permeability [9]. The sympathetic nervous system also affects the function of the lymphatic system [10]. After sympathetic nerve resection, the contractility of the lymphatic vessels may decrease, obstructing the drainage of lymph fluid and leading to localized fluid accumulation. These mechanisms elevate vascular permeability and promote fluid leakage, which may trigger pleural effusion. There are many etiologies of exudative pleural effusion, such as inflammatory, tumour, and tuberculosis. In this case, the patient’s pleural fluid adenosine deaminase level was not elevated. Antacid staining, tuberculosis DNA testing, and cytological analysis of the pleural effusion all returned negative results. Normally, postoperative chylothorax is suspected when chest tube drainage is persistent and shows a milky or cloudy appearance. Diagnosis is confirmed through laboratory analysis of the pleural fluid, specifically by measuring triglyceride levels. A triglyceride concentration in pleural fluid exceeding 1.1 mmol/l is typically indicative of chylothorax [11]. In this case, the triglyceride levels in the pleural fluid of both sides were less than 1.1 mmol/l. Consequently, tuberculosis, tumour and chylothorax involvement were not considered at this time. Typically, protein levels in pleural fluid reflect pleural permeability, while lactate dehydrogenase levels reflect the severity of pleural inflammation. It is worth noting that the protein level and lactate dehydrogenase level in the patient’s pleural effusion were significantly elevated. Therefore, it is believed that the cause of the pleural effusion in this patient was the release of local inflammatory mediators, which increased vascular permeability and led to the extravasation of protein. Preoperative nutritional risk assessment should be emphasized in patients undergoing surgery, and accurate identification of sympathetic nerves is essential during surgery. One week after surgery, patients are advised to undergo lung imaging for early detection of complications. If pleural effusion is detected, prompt nutritional support and thoracic drainage are essential to prevent adhesion formation.

Conclusion

Although the incidence of postoperative complications of thoracoscopic sympathectomy is low, the potential for rare complications, such as pleural effusion, which can present diagnostic challenges, remains a concern. In addition, early thoracentesis and comprehensive nutritional support are crucial for effectively managing these patients.
