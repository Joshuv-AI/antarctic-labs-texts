SRSF3 determines Treg cell fate in antitumor immunity and autoimmunity
Authors: Rong Jia, Jihua Guo, Linyan Yan, Can Lin, Jun Jia, Jun Shao, Dunfang Zhang, Na Liu, Wanjun Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Human FOXP3 shows two isoforms due to exon 2 skipping, and the exon 2 is critical for FOXP3 function in Treg cells. However, the factor(s) regulating its splicing remain unknown. Here, we show that SRSF3 regulates FOXP3 exon 2 function and determines Treg cell fate and function in autoimmunity and tumor immunity. Mechanistically, SRSF3 promoted FOXP3 exon 2 inclusion and protein expression and thus safeguarded Treg cells, as deletion of Srsf3 gene specifically in Treg cells resulted in profound deficiency of Treg cells in mice, leading to lethal systemic inflammation. Treg cells in human tumor showed high SRSF3 expression. SRSF3 was required for human Treg cell–suppressive function. Humanized mice generated with human FOXP3 exon 2 and adjacent introns showed significantly reduced tumor formation with the decreased Treg cell function and consequently increased intratumoral CD8+ T cell infiltration. We have thus revealed a previously unrecognized role of SRSF3 in controlling Treg cell fate and function in antitumor immunity and autoimmunity by regulating FOXP3 exon 2 inclusion and protein expression.

INTRODUCTION

Regulatory T cells (Treg cells) play crucial roles in maintaining self-tolerance and preventing autoimmunity and inflammation. On the other hand, they can also hamper effective antitumor immunity. Heavy infiltration of Treg cells are often found in cancers and associated with poor prognosis of patients (1). Anti–Treg cell approaches have shown therapeutic effects on suppressing tumor growth (2). FOXP3 has been identified as a master controller in the generation and function of Treg cells (3, 4). Lacking Foxp3 gene causes immune dysfunction in both human [immune dysregulation, polyendocrinopathy, enteropathy, x-linked (IPEX) syndrome] and mouse (scurfy mice) (5). Human FOXP3 has multiple isoforms generated by alternative splicing of pre-mRNA, including full-length (FOXP3FL), exon 2 skipping (FOXP3Δ2), and both exon 2 and 7 skipping (FOXP3Δ2Δ7) (6). FOXP3FL and FOXP3Δ2 isoforms are two major isoforms, and both of them could exhibit immunosuppressive activity of Treg cells to naïve CD4+ T cells (7). However, depletion of full-length FOXP3 by exon 2–specific small interfering RNA (siRNA) significantly weakens the suppressive ability of human Treg cells (8). Mice with exon 2 depletion developed systemic autoimmune disease (9). Exon 2 is located in the central part of N-terminal repressor region and required for FOXP3-mediated transcriptional repression (10, 11). Exon 2 can mediate the autoinhibitory role of FOXP3 N-terminal domain (12). Moreover, in contrast to FOXP3FL isoform, FOXP3Δ2 isoform has no effect on nuclear factor κB and retinoic acid receptor-related orphan nuclear receptor α (RORα)-mediated gene expression (11, 13). FOXP3Δ2 lacks nuclear export signal in exon 2 and is mainly localized in the nucleus (14). Therefore, exon 2 is important for the function and subcellular localization of FOXP3. Loss of exon 2 weakens the function of FOXP3. Unlike human, mouse Foxp3 is only expressed as a full-length protein. Therefore, it is puzzling why human has FOXP3 isoform of exon 2 exclusion. In addition, the underlying regulatory mechanism of exon 2 alternative splicing in human Treg cells remains unknown.

The molecular mechanisms of FOXP3 expression in Treg cells have attracted great interests (15–17). A number of studies have shown that the expression of FOXP3 is tightly controlled on transcription level by transcriptional factors (18–21) and DNA methylation (e.g., conserved noncoding DNA sequence elements) (22, 23). In posttranslational level, arginine methylation at positions 48 and 51 by protein arginine methyltransferase 1 (PRMT1) is important for FOXP3 function (24). Deubiquitination of lysine residues by deubiquitinase USP7 and hyperacetylation by p300 increase FOXP3 protein stability (25, 26). In addition, deacetylation of Foxp3 promoter by the OX40 costimulation pathway inhibits FOXP3 expression (27). FOXP3 expression is regulated by multiple signaling pathways, such as transforming growth factor–β, T cell receptor (TCR), and CD28 (28, 29). However, how alternative splicing contributes to the regulation of human FOXP3 expression remains unclear.

Treg cells exhibit an increased number and immunosuppressive function in the tumor tissues, in which they inhibit antitumor immunity and help tumor cells escape from the immune surveillance and attack and induce immunotherapy resistance (30). Treg cell–targeted therapies represent a promising antitumor approach; however, the efficiency of anti–Treg cell treatment remains to be determined (31). Thus, it is essential to understand the regulatory mechanisms of Treg cell function and stability in the tumors, especially in human cancers.

In this study, by generating humanized mice in which human FOXP3 exon 2 and adjacent 50-bp introns replaced corresponding mouse Foxp3 genome sequence, we determined the effects of exon 2 exclusion in Treg cells on tumorigenesis and intratumoral CD8+ T cell infiltration. We found that splicing factor serine and arginine-rich splicing factor 3 (SRSF3) is the key factor for the inclusion and translation of FOXP3 exon 2 in Treg cells. We determined a key role of SRSF3 in maintaining the function of Treg cells by generating Treg cell–specific Srsf3–knockout (KO) mice, as the KO mice developed lethal systemic inflammation. We demonstrated high levels of SRSF3 expression in Treg cells in human tumor tissues, which showed a positive correlation with human cancer progress. We have thus revealed a previously unrecognized role of SRSF3 in determining Treg cell fate and function in tumor immunity and autoimmunity through regulation of FOXP3 exon 2.

RESULTS

FOXP3 exon 2 skipping in Treg cells reduces tumorigenesis

The human FOXP3 gene produces two major types of isoforms, namely, with or without exon 2, through the alternative splicing of exon 2. However, the mouse Foxp3 gene has no exon 2 skipping (Fig. 1A). It is thus mysterious why exon 2 of human FOXP3 gene can be skipped to generate a short FOXP3 protein. To understand how exon 2 is skipped in the human FOXP3 gene, we compared the DNA sequences of exon 2 and nearby introns of human FOXP3 with those of mouse Foxp3. DNA sequence of exon 2 is highly conserved between human and mouse. In terms of amino acid sequence encoded by exon 2, there is only a two–amino acid difference between human and mouse (Fig. 1B and fig. S1). The nearby introns are poorly conserved between human and mouse. We established a mouse Foxp3 minigene system containing the genomic sequence from exon 1 to exon 3 of mouse Foxp3 gene and performed a serial mutation in introns nearby exon 2 according to the human sequence. Mutation mt4, which is located in the polypyrimidine tract region, induced substantial exon 2 skipping (Fig. 1C). Moreover, we also established a corresponding human FOXP3 minigene system and made a mutation according to the mouse sequence in the mt4 intron region that led to almost completely abolishing exon 2 skipping (Fig. 1D). However, the replacement of mouse exon 2 with human exon 2 had a minor effect on exon 2 skipping (fig. S2). These results indicate that this intron region is responsible for human FOXP3 exon 2 skipping. Human FOXP3 exon 2 is weakened in splicing due to small changes in intron 1.

To understand the function of Foxp3 exon 2 exclusion in vivo, we generated a humanized mouse whose Foxp3 exon 2 and adjacent upstream and downstream 50-bp intron sequences were replaced by human corresponding FOXP3 exon 2 and adjacent intron sequences (Fig. 1E). These humanized mice did not show obvious spontaneous inflammation at ages of 4 and 9 weeks (fig. S3). A reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) result showed that Treg cells from humanized mice (Foxp3-Hu) did express both Foxp3 exon 2 included and excluded isoforms, while wild-type (WT) mice did not (Fig. 1F). The humanized mice appeared normal in size (Fig. 1E) and exhibited similar frequency and numbers of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in the spleen and lymph node (fig. S4). There is no significant difference in the activation of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (fig. S5), the expression levels of interferon-γ (IFN-γ) in CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (fig. S6), and the expression levels of interleukin-4 (IL-4) in CD4+ T cells (fig. S7) between humanized and WT mice. The numbers of Foxp3+ Treg cells were also compatible to WT mice (Fig. 1G). However, the expression levels of both full-length FOXP3 (full-Foxp3) and total FOXP3 (all-Foxp3) in Treg cells in the spleens of humanized mice were significantly decreased compared with those in WT mice (Fig. 1G). The data indicate that Foxp3 exon 2 regulates the expression of FOXP3.

We also made a point mutation mouse (fig. S8). This mouse carries mutations in intron 1 based on mt4 in Fig. 1B, which drove mouse Foxp3 exon 2 skipping. We analyzed the inclusion of Foxp3 exon 2 in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). Mutant mice also showed obvious skipping of Foxp3 exon 2 compared to WT mice by RT-PCR analysis (fig. S8D). Moreover, flow cytometry analysis showed that mutant mice have similar Treg cell population to WT mice but significantly lower levels of FOXP3 expression than WT mice (fig. S8E). These results confirmed that the intron region corresponding to mt4 is responsible for FOXP3 exon 2 skipping and expression.

As Treg cells play a key role in affecting the development of tumor by suppressing antitumor immunity especially the function of CD8+ T effector cells (1), we next investigated the effects of Foxp3 exon 2 in Treg cells on the tumorigenesis in mice. For this, we inoculated 0.5 × 105 MC38 mouse colon cancer cells subcutaneously into left axilla of humanized (Foxp3-Hu) or WT mice. Unexpectedly, humanized mice showed significantly slower tumor growth and decreased tumor weight compared to the WT mice (Fig. 1, H to J). Notably, even we inoculated more (2 × 105) MC38 cells subcutaneously into left axilla of the mice to monitor animal survival, we observed that humanized mice showed significantly better overall survival than WT mice (Fig. 1K). Tumors dissected from the humanized mice showed a significantly larger number of CD8+ T cell infiltration than those from WT mice (Fig. 1L). Treg cells from humanized mice’s tumors showed significantly lower expression levels of total Foxp3 protein than those from WT mice, although the frequency of Treg cells in tumors is similar in both groups (Fig. 1L). Moreover, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis confirmed that Foxp3 expression was significantly down-regulated in the intratumoral Treg cell from humanized mice (table S1 and fig. S9). These results indicate that Foxp3 exon 2 skipping decreases Foxp3 expression in Treg cells, which compromises Treg cell function leading to more infiltration of CD8+ T cells into the tumor tissues to suppress tumor growth. Moreover, scRNA-seq analysis also showed that Foxp3 exon 2 skipping may extensively affect Treg cell function. For example, Tnfrsf1b (also called TNFR2) and Gzmb (encoding granzyme B protein), two crucial genes for intratumoral Treg cell function (32–34), as well as a number of genes related to the regulation of inflammatory response and mitochondrial respiratory, were significantly down-regulated in the intratumoral Treg cells from humanized mice (fig. S9 and table S6).

We then extended our studies to patients with cancer to investigate whether FOXP3 exon 2 expression is associated with the prognosis and survival of the patients. We analyzed the splicing of exon 2 in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) datasets from the Spliceseq database with a log-rank test method. Consistent with the findings in the humanized mice, we found that patients with lower exon 2 inclusion showed significantly better overall survival than those with higher exon 2 inclusion in TCGA colon, head and neck, breast, esophageal, pancreatic, and rectum cancers (fig. S10). These results collectively suggest that the switch from the expression of only full-length FOXP3 to both full-length and exon 2–skipped FOXP3 decreases function of Treg cells and consequent tumorigenesis in animal models and also positively associated with the prognosis and survival of patients with cancer.

Splicing factor SRSF3 promotes FOXP3 exon 2 inclusion

On the basis of the effects of exon 2 inclusion on Treg cell function in the humanized mice and consequent tumorigenesis, we next investigated the underlying mechanisms of human FOXP3 exon 2 inclusion in Treg cells. As splicing enhancers are often located in alternative exon itself, we thus made serial mutations in FOXP3 exon 2 to map the regulatory motifs for exon 2 inclusion in the human FOXP3 minigene system (Fig. 2A). Mutant hmt10 showed notably decreased inclusion of exon 2 compared with WT (hwt), while mutants hmt1 + 2 and hmt9 showed notably increased inclusion of exon 2 (Fig. 2B). This indicated that WT (original) sequence of hmt10 (hwt10) contained an exon splicing enhancer (ESE) for the inclusion of exon 2, while WT sequences of hmt1 + 2 or hmt9 contained exon splicing suppressors for the skipping of exon 2 (Fig. 2B).

Alternative splicing of pre-mRNA is mainly regulated by splicing factors (35, 36). To identify splicing factors involved in the regulation of alternative splicing of exon 2, human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293 cells were cotransfected by minigene and splicing factor expression plasmids. We screened four splicing factors and found that SRSF3 promoted exon 2 inclusion, while heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein L (hnRNP L) and serine and arginine-rich splicing factor 7 (SRSF7 or 9G8) repressed the inclusion of exon 2 (Fig. 2C and fig. S11). Knockdown of SRSF3 with specific siRNA promoted the skipping of exon 2 (Fig. 2D), and SFI003, an SRSF3 inhibitor, also strongly enhanced the skipping of exon 2 in human Treg cells (fig. S12) or humanized mouse Treg cells (fig. S13). Mouse Treg cells do not express exon 2–skipped isoform of Foxp3 gene. Therefore, the inhibition of SRSF3 did not induce the skipping of exon 2 in WT mouse Treg cells (fig. S13). RNA pull-down assay confirmed that SRSF3 binds to the ESE in hwt10 (Fig. 2E), suggesting that SRSF3 enhances exon 2 inclusion by binding to the ESE in hwt10. On the other hand, we also performed RNA precipitation assay with an anti-SRSF3 antibody in 293 cells transfected with a human FOXP3 minigene and found that the SRSF3 protein binds to FOXP3 exon 2 in both pre-mRNA and spliced mRNA (fig. S14). Moreover, we analyzed the effects of SRSF3 on FOXP3 exon 2 splicing and expression in human Treg cells. We found that silence of SRSF3 also significantly down-regulated the inclusion of exon 2 and the expression level of full-length FOXP3 protein (Fig. 2, F and G). Conversely, overexpression of SRSF3 promoted the inclusion of exon 2 and the expression level of full-length FOXP3 protein (Fig. 2, F and G). The results collectively confirm that SRSF3 is required for exon 2 inclusion and the expression of full-length human FOXP3.

SRSF3 promotes the translation of FOXP3 full-length protein

Next, we tried to explore the role of SRSF3 in FOXP3 mRNA translation. First, we constructed plasmids expressing full-length or exon 2–skipped human FOXP3 isoforms tagged by 3 × Flag. FOXP3 open reading frames (ORFs) are fused with three Flag tags and driven by a cytomegalovirus (CMV) promoter (Fig. 3A). Then, we tested the effects of SRSF3 on the expression of human FOXP3 protein in 293 cells. SRSF3 overexpression markedly enhanced the expression of human full-length FOXP3, without changes in transcription levels (Fig. 3B). The expression of exon 2–skipped FOXP3Δ2 was also somewhat enhanced by SRSF3 overexpression. Accordingly, silence of SRSF3 down-regulated the expression of full-length FOXP3 (Fig. 3C). Moreover, the silence of SRSF3 also significantly reduced mouse Foxp3 expression, while SRSF3 overexpression significantly enhanced mouse Foxp3 expression (Fig. 3D). This indicates that SRSF3 is also essential for Foxp3 expression and Treg cells in mouse whose Foxp3 exon 2 is not alternatively spliced. These results indicate that SRSF3 is essential for the expression of FOXP3 full-length protein besides the inclusion of exon 2.

SRSF3 is overexpressed and correlated with FOXP3 up-regulation in human intratumoral Treg cells

The increase in intratumoral Treg cells has been recognized as one of the most important mechanisms to suppress antitumor immunity with their increase in FOXP3 expression and are associated with cancer progress in patients with cancer (37, 38). Moreover, intratumoral Treg cells have also exhibited enhanced suppressive capacity, which is also associated with higher expression of FOXP3 protein (39). We then hypothesized that intratumoral Treg cells might exhibit increased FOXP3 expression that was regulated by SRSF3. We thus studied the FOXP3 and SRSF3 expression in Treg cells in the tumor and the adjacent normal tissues of patients with oral squamous cell carcinoma or with breast cancer (Fig. 4, A to H). We found that intratumoral Treg cells expressed significantly more FOXP3 protein than Treg cells in adjacent normal tissues in both oral squamous cell carcinoma (Fig. 4, A and C) and breast cancer (Fig. 4, E and G). Intratumoral Treg cells also had significantly increased SRSF3 expression than Treg cells in adjacent normal tissues in both oral squamous cell carcinoma (Fig. 4, B and D) or breast cancer (Fig. 4, F and H). Consistently, high SRSF3 expression is significantly correlated with poor overall survival in head and neck, breast, and esophageal cancer (fig. S15, A to C). Tumors express significantly more SRSF3 than normal tissues, and higher-grade tumors show larger amounts of SRSF3 expression in head and neck and esophageal cancer (fig. S15, D and E). These findings suggest a key function of SRSF3 in the regulation of FOXP3 expression in Treg cells in human cancer and reveal a potential target for immunotherapy to human cancer.

SRSF3 is required for the suppressive function of human Treg cells

As FOXP3 is essential for the suppression function of Treg cells (3, 4, 29, 40), we next examined the effect of SRSF3 knockdown on the suppressive function of Treg cells by in vitro suppression assay (41). We cocultured normal peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PMBC) with Treg cells that were pretreated with control (NS) or SRSF3 siRNA and showed that the proliferation of CD8+ T cells cocultured with SRSF3-deficient Treg cells was significantly up-regulated compared with those cocultured with control Treg cells (Fig. 4I). We also analyzed the levels of cytokines in CD8+ T cells cocultured with different types of Treg cells. As expected, Treg cells with knockdown of SRSF3 significantly compromised the suppression of IFN-γ, tumor necrosis factor–α (TNF-α), and IL-2 in CD8+ T cells mediated by control Treg cells (Fig. 4J). The data indicate that SRSF3 promotes FOXP3 expression and the suppressive function of human Treg cells.

SRSF3 is required for Treg cell stability and function

Next, we explored the roles of SRSF3 in Treg cells in vivo. We crossed Srsf3-flox mice with the Foxp3YFP-cre mice to produce Treg cell–specific Srsf3-KO mice, including both Foxp3YFP-CreSrsf3flox/flox homozygous [Srsf3-conditional knockout (cKO)] and Foxp3YFP-CreSrsf3flox/+ heterozygous (Srsf3+/−) mice (Fig. 5, A and B). Srsf3 was specifically deleted in Srsf3-cKO Treg cells (Fig. 5C). Srsf3+/− mice developed normally. The birth rates of Srsf3-cKO mice are similar to Srsf3+/− mice. However, Srsf3-cKO male mice showed severely retardation in growth and significantly reduced body weight (Fig. 5, D and E). All Srsf3-cKO male mice died at 3 to 5 weeks of age (Fig. 5F). In postnatal day 7, the Srsf3-cKO mice kept normal body and thymus weight without significant inflammation in skin, liver, heart, and kidney tissues (fig. S16, A to C). The profile of thymocytes remained intact in the Srsf3-cKO mice (fig. S16D). However, the Srsf3-cKO mice showed significantly lower population of Treg cell with significantly decreased expression of Foxp3 and CD25 in the thymus compared to Cre control mice (fig. S16, E and F). In postnatal day 11, the thymus in the Srsf3-cKO mice also exhibited decreased frequency of Treg cells (Fig. 5G). Srsf3-cKO male mice gradually developed systemic inflammation in multiple vital organs including the skin, liver (Fig. 5I), kidney, heart, and submandibular glands (fig. S17). By 4 weeks of age, the Srsf3-cKO mice exhibited splenomegaly and lymph node enlargement, as well as thymus atrophy, compared with Srsf3+/− male mice (Fig. 5I). Flow cytometry analysis revealed that Srsf3-cKO male mice lost most of Treg cells in the spleen and lymph nodes (Fig. 5J). In addition, the frequencies of CD8+ T cells, but not CD4+ T cells, were significantly increased in the spleen and lymph nodes of the Srsf3-cKO mice (fig. S18). CD4+IFN-γ+ [T helper cell 1 (TH1)], CD8+IFN-γ+ T cells, and CD4+IL-4+ (Th2) cells were also significantly enhanced in the spleens and lymph nodes in Srsf3-cKO mice compared to Srsf3+/− mice (figs. S18 and S19). The levels of serum anti–double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) antibody also significantly increased in Srsf3-cKO mice (Fig. 5H). Srsf3-cKO male mice showed significantly increased CD62LlowCD44high CD8 T cells in the spleen and lymph nodes compared with Srsf3+/− mice (Fig. 5K). In addition, we found that the inhibition of SRSF3 expression by its specific inhibitor SFI003 (42) significantly reduced the tumor formation of MC38 in C57BL/6N mice, accompanied by the decreased expression of SRSF3 and FOXP3 in intratumoral Treg cells (fig. S20). The data collectively reveal that SRSF3 controls the homeostasis of Treg cells through promoting FOXP3 expression.

SRSF3 regulates gene expression and RNA splicing in Treg cells

SRSF3 is a multifunction protein. To extensively understand its role in Treg cells, we analyzed the gene expression and the alternative splicing profile in Treg cells of heterozygous female mouse whose partial Treg cells showed SRSF3 expression deficient (fig. S21A). We compare the gene expression and the alternative splicing profile between Treg cells with 25% highest SRSF3 expression and Treg cells with 25% lowest SRSF3 expression by using a single-cell third-generation full-length sequencing method. Treg cells with high SRSF3 expression have significantly more up-regulated genes compared with those with low SRSF3 expression (fig. S21B and table S4). The SRSF3-deficient Treg cells showed significantly reduced expression of Bcl10, Card11, Rnf213, and Setd2. These genes are required for Treg cell differentiation and function (fig. S21C) (43–46). In addition, many down-regulated genes in SRSF3-deficient cells are anti-apoptosis, including Arl6ip1, Babam2, Cd44, Wnk3, Anp32b, Dad1, Hspd1, Hspa5, Hsp90ab1, Mif, Npm1, Pa2g4, and Tmbim6. Some genes promoting cell proliferation are also down-regulated, including Ccnd2, Fignl1, Mapkap1, Nono, Plaur, and Ptma. Two important antiferroptosis genes, Sod1 and Tfrc, are also down-regulated. As expected, RNA splicing is the major affected pathway in SRSF3-deficient Treg cells. Moreover, translation, ribosome biogenesis, and activation of immune response pathways are also affected (fig. S21D). In terms of alternative splicing, the SRSF3-deficient Treg cells showed 856 obviously changed alternative splicing events (table S5), in which exon skipping and alternative first exon are two major affected events (fig. S21, E and F). Pathway analysis indicated that SRSF3 deficiency affected multiple important cellular biological progresses, such as DNA damage response, DNA repair, autophagy, and cell cycle (fig. S21G).

DISCUSSION

Unlike Foxp3 in murine Treg cells, human FOXP3 is found to express in the form of two bands in Western blot assay in human CD4+CD25+ Treg cells (47). Allan and colleagues (48) demonstrated that human Treg cells express at least two isoforms, FOXP3FL and FOXP3Δ2, and simultaneous overexpression of both isoforms in CD4+CD25− T cells could significantly increase the expression of Treg cell–associated cell surface markers, including CD25, CD45RO, CCR4, cytotoxic T-lymphocyte associated protein 4 (CTLA4), glucocorticoid-induced TNFR-related protein (GITR), and human leukocyte antigen-DR (HLA-DR). FOXP3FL may contribute more to suppressive function of Treg cells than FOXP3Δ2. Mice with exon 2 deletion developed systemic autoimmune disease (9). Rosa et al. (8) showed that down-regulation of exon 2–contained isoform significantly weakened the suppression ability of Treg cells. Treg cells expressing exon 2 represent a subset characterized by higher levels of Treg cell–associated markers (CD25, CTLA4, and GITR) and more proliferative and metabolic active. Treg cells from patients with multiple sclerosis expressed much less FOXP3FL than healthy controls (49). However, the underlying molecular mechanisms responsible to the formation of two isoforms and differential expression of FOXP3Δ2 versus FOXP3FL protein in human Treg cells are unknown. In the present study, we have revealed that FOXP3 exon 2 contains translational suppression motifs that account for the lower expression level of FOXP3FL in Treg cells. Mechanistically, we have found that SRSF3 controls alternative splicing and translational of human FOXP3. Significantly, we have uncovered that intratumoral Treg cells in human oral cancer and breast cancer exhibit significantly higher levels of SRSF3 that is positively linked to the increased FOXP3 expression.

Because FOXP3 full-length with exon 2 is important for Treg cell stability (9), it is puzzling why human Treg cells express FOXP3 isoform with exon 2 exclusion. Recently, it has been shown that FOXP3Δ2 isoform plays a different role in thymus-derived Treg cells and peripherally induced Treg cells by using an exon 2 deletion mouse model (12). We here found by the generation of humanized mice, whose Treg cells expressed two isoforms similar to human Treg cells, that humanized Treg cells showed decreased FOXP3 expression levels in spleen Treg cells and less tumorigenesis compared with mouse Treg cells in the WT mice.

SRSF3 (also known as SRp20) is a member of the serine/arginine-rich splicing factor family and essential regulator of pre-mRNA alternative splicing (50, 51). It has been reported that SRSF3 interacts with the 3′ untranslated region (3′UTR) region of a set of lipopolysaccharide (LPS)–induced genes and suppresses their translation and then represses the innate immune response to LPS stimulation (52). Another study showed that SRSF3 binds to the 5′UTR region and suppresses programmed cell death 4 (PDCD4) protein translation (53). However, SRSF3 is also involved in enhancing translation (54). We have revealed here that SRSF3 not only enhances the inclusion of FOXP3 exon 2 but also increases the translation of FOXP3FL.

Treg cells in the tumor tissues have higher suppressive capacity with higher levels of Foxp3 expression than those from healthy individuals (55). Saito et al. (39) found that patients with abundant infiltration of T cells with low FOXP3 expression (FOXP3lo) showed significantly better prognosis than those with predominant infiltration of Treg cells with high expression of FOXP3 (FOXP3hi), which are effector Treg cells, in colorectal cancer. Therefore, depletion of FOXP3hi Treg cells from cancer tissues may enhance antitumor immunity (1, 56). However, the regulatory mechanisms of Treg cells expressing high levels of FOXP3 in tumor tissues remain unclear. Here, we have revealed that the maintenance of high level of FOXP3 expression depends on SRSF3 in human Treg cells. This finding was further supported by the observation of intratumoral Treg cells expressing significantly higher SRSF3 and FOXP3 than those in adjacent normal tissues, such as in oral squamous cell carcinoma and breast cancer. SRSF3 promotes splicing and translation of FOXP3 exon 2–included isoform. Patients with cancer and high levels of inclusion of FOXP3 exon 2 was significantly correlated with poor overall survival and metastasis in multiple types of human cancers, indicating a general feature. Thus the findings highlight the levels of FOXP3 exon 2 as a potential biomarker for the prognosis of patients with cancer. We have previously demonstrated that SRSF3 is overexpressed in oral squamous cell carcinoma, breast cancer (57), and other cancers and is a proto-oncogene required for tumor cell proliferation (58). Here, we reveal a previously unknown role of SRSF3 in regulating FOXP3 expression and function in Treg cells, especially in intratumoral Treg cells, revealing a previously unidentified function of SRSF3 in tumor pathogenesis and development. Thus, suppressing SRSF3 expression may block both tumor cell proliferation and immunosuppression caused by Treg cells simultaneously and thus provide a possible antitumor strategy.

In sum, we have found that SRSF3 is essential for Treg cell stability via promoting FOXP3 exon 2 inclusion and translation (fig. S22). The findings should have substantial implication in understanding the function of FOXP3 in human Treg cells and also in developing immunotherapy for human cancer.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

The goal of this study is to determine the roles and mechanisms of FOXP3 isoforms and its regulator SRSF3 in Treg cells. We made a Foxp3-humanized mouse by CRISPR-Cas9 to analyze the effects of FOXP3 exon 2 skipping in tumorigenesis. Then, the functions of SRSF3 in Treg cells and FOXP3 exon 2 splicing and translation were analyzed by quantitative RT-PCR, RNA pull-down, scRNA-seq, and in vitro suppression assay and were further confirmed in an Srsf3 conditional KO mouse model. Cells or mice used were randomly assigned to the experimental groups. The numbers of experimental replicates are indicated in figure legends.

Plasmids

ORFs of human FOXP3 full-length and Δ2 isoforms were amplified from Jurkat cells and cloned into p3 × FLAG-CMV14 plasmid at NotI and BamHI sites. Genomic DNA of human FOXP3 from exon 1 to exon 3 was amplified from HEK 293 cells and cloned into pEGFP-N1 at an EcoRI site to produce a minigene expression plasmid. T7-SRSF3 expression plasmid was provided by Z.-M. Zheng (National Cancer Institute/National Institutes of Health, USA). SRSF1-GFP expression plasmid was purchased from Genechem (China). ORFs of hnRNP L and 9g8 were amplified from HEK 293 cells, fused with T7 tag, and then cloned into the same vector as T7-SRSF3 expression plasmid.

Cells

Human HEK 293 cells were grown in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) medium with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic. Human Jurkat cells were grown in RPMI 1640 medium with 10% FBS and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic. Mouse colon cancer MC38 cells were bought from Cyagen Biosciences (Guangzhou, China) and grown in DMEM medium with 10% FBS.

Mice

C57BL/6N Foxp3-humanized mice was generated by replacing mouse Foxp3 exon 2 and adjacent upstream and downstream 50-bp intron sequences with human corresponding exon 2 and adjacent intron sequences by using the Biocytogen’s Extreme Genome Editing system (Biocytogen, China) which is based on CRISPR-Cas9. Humanized mice were confirmed by PCR and Southern blot.

C57BL/6N Foxp3 intron 2 point mutation mice were generated by mutating mouse Foxp3 intron 2 according to mt4 by using a CRISPR-Cas9 method (Cyagen, China). Mutation mice were confirmed by PCR. C57BL/6J-Srsf3fl/fl and B6.Foxp3YFP-cre mice were purchased from Cyagen and used for producing C57BL/6J-Srsf3fl/fl Foxp3YFP-cre (Treg cell Srsf3-KO) mice.

All mice were maintained in the specific pathogen–free animal facility at Hospital of Stomatology, Wuhan University. All animal studies were approved by the institutional Animal Ethics Committee, Hospital of Stomatology, Wuhan University (reference numbers: S07924060A and S07922070E).

Tumor model

MC38 cells were inoculated subcutaneously into left axilla of humanized or WT mice. Tumor sizes were measured every 2 to 3 days. Tumor volume was calculated as length × width2 × 0.52. For the survival experiment, tumor burden was measured every day, and animals were euthanized at the humane endpoints according to guidelines such as the maximum allowed tumor size or consistent weight loss of 15% or more. For the experiment of SFI003 inhibitor treatment, mice were administered 0.3 mg of SFI003 around tumors every day for 10 days, followed by 0.6 mg of SFI003 treatment every day for 6 more days.

Reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA of cells was extracted with the Total RNA Miniprep Kit (AxyPrep). One microgram of RNA was treated with deoxyribonuclease I (DNase I; Invitrogen) for 15 min and then reverse transcribed using random hexamers at 37°C for 1 hour with the Superscript II reverse transcriptase kit (Invitrogen, USA) and amplified using the following primer pairs:

5′-CCATGCCTCCTCTTCTTCCTTGAAC-3′ and 5′-CGTGGGCATCCACCGTTGAG-3′ for FOXP3 (amplification of human exon 1 to 3 genomic DNA), 5′-CCATGCCTCCTCTTCTTCCTTGAAC-3′ and 5′-GCTCCTCGCCCTTGCTCACCA-3′ for the detection of alternative splicing of exon 2 in human minigene plasmid, 5′-CCCACACCTCTTCTTCCTTGAACC-3′ and 5′-GCTCCTCGCCCTTGCTCACCA-3′ for the detection of alternative splicing of exon 2 in mouse minigene plasmid, 5′-GTCATCAATGGAAATCCCATCACC-3′ and 5′-TGAGTCCTTCCACGATACCAAA-3′ or 5′-GAAGGTGAAGGTCGGAGTC-3′ and 5′-GAAGATGGTGATGGGATTTC-3′ for glyceraldehyde phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH).

Western blot

Total protein samples were separated in 10% SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis gel and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. The membrane was blotted with the following antibodies: mouse monoclonal anti-Flag (F1804; M2; Sigma-Aldrich), mouse monoclonal anti-T7 (69522; Novagen), rabbit monoclonal anti-FOXP3 antibody (D25D4; 5298; Cell Signaling Technology), mouse monoclonal anti-SRSF1 (96; sc-33652; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA), mouse monoclonal anti-SRSF3 (7B4; 33-4200; Invitrogen), mouse monoclonal anti–hnRNP L (4D11; sc-32317; Santa Cruz Biotechnology), or horseradish peroxidase–labeled mouse anti–β-actin antibody (ac-15; A3854; Sigma-Aldrich).

RNA pull-down assay

Biotin-labeled FOXP3 exon 2 RNA oligonucleotides wt10 5′ GCCACAUUUCAUGCACCAGCTT 3′ and mt10 5′ GCCACUUAUGAAGGACCAGCTT 3′ were synthesized in Takara Bio Inc. (Dalian, China). The total 293 and Jurkat cell extracts were prepared using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Two-microliter biotin-labeled RNA oligonucleotide (20 μM) was immobilized onto 10 μl of NeutrAvidin agarose beads (50% aqueous slurry, Pierce). The beads were then incubated with 400 μl of prediluted total cell extract in 1× binding buffer [20 mM tris, 200 mM NaCl, 6 mM EDTA, 5 mM potassium fluoride, 5 mM β-glycerophosphate, and aprotinin (2 μg/ml) (pH 7.5)] in the presence of 200 U of ribonuclease inhibitor (Promega) at 4°C for 2 hours. After the beads were washed with 1× binding buffer, the bound proteins were eluted by SDS sample buffer and analyzed by Western blot.

Cell or tissue isolation from patients’ tissues

Five patients diagnosed with oral squamous cell carcinoma in School and Hospital of Stomatology in Wuhan University and eight patients with breast cancer in Hubei Cancer Hospital were involved in this study (tables S2 and S3). Cancer tissues were collected for routine pathologic diagnosis. Blood samples from healthy donors were collected from School and Hospital of Stomatology in Wuhan University. Informed consent was obtained from every participant. The experimental protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee at School and Hospital of Stomatology in Wuhan University and the Ethics Committee at Hubei Cancer Hospital (reference numbers: [2016]20, [2022]B68, and [2018]KYLL(S23)).

Flow cytometry analysis

Fresh isolated cancer or adjacent normal tissues were minced into small pieces, digested with collagenase I and DNase I for 2 hours, then passed through a cell strainer, and centrifuged for 5 min at 400g to collect single cells. Cells were washed with RPMI 1640 medium and resuspended in flow cytometry staining buffer (MultiSciences Biotech, China). Then, cells were labeled with phycoerythrin (PE)–conjugated mouse anti-human CD4 (RPA-T4; 555347; BD Pharmingen) and eFluor 710–conjugated mouse anti-human TCR (IP26; 46-9986-42; Thermo Fisher Scientific) antibody, fixed with fixation/permeabilization buffer, followed by eFluor 660–conjugated mouse anti-FOXP3 (PCH101; 50-4776-42; eBioscience) and rabbit monoclonal anti-SRSF3 (EPR16976; ab198291; Abcam) and Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated goat polyclonal anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibody (ab150077; Abcam) labeling.

Thymus, spleens, and lymph node tissues were collected from various mouse lines. Mouse cells were stained with eFluor 660–conjugated rat anti-mouse Foxp3 (FJK-16s; 50-5773-80; eBioscience, recognizing full-length Foxp3 protein), PE-conjugated rat anti-mouse Foxp3 (NRRF-30; 12-4771-82; eBioscience, recognizing all Foxp3 proteins), fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)–conjugated rat anti-mouse CD4 (RM4-5; 11-0042-85; Invitrogen), allophycocyanin (APC)-conjugated rat anti-mouse CD8a (53-6.7; 100711; BioLegend), Alexa Fluor 700–conjugated hamster anti-mouse TCRβ (H57-597; 109224; BioLegend), eFluor 660–conjugated rat IgG2a isotype antibody (eBR2a; 50-4321-82; eBioscience), FITC-conjugated rat IgG2a isotype antibody (eBR2a; 11-4321-82; eBioscience), Alexa Fluor 700–conjugated hamster IgG isotype (HTK888; 400926; BioLegend), APC-conjugated rat IgG2a isotype antibody (RTK2758; 400511; BioLegend), PE-conjugated rat IgG2a isotype antibody (eBR2a; 12-4321-80; eBioscience), APC-conjugated rat anti-mouse CD44 (IM7; 17-0441-81; eBioscience), APC-conjugated rat IgG2b isotype antibody (eB149/10H5; 17-4031-82; eBioscience), and PE-conjugated rat anti-mouse CD62L (MEL-14; 12-0621-81; eBioscience).

Transfection in 293 and Treg cells with siRNA or plasmid

The sequence of human SRSF3 siRNA is 5′-AGAGCUAGAUGGAAGAACA-3′. HEK 293 cells were transfected with plasmid or 20 nM siRNA in the presence of Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen). Treg cells were transfected with plasmid or 100 nM siRNA using a P3 primary cell 4D-Nucleofector X kit (V4XP-3024; Lonza) by the 4D-Nucleofector transfection system (Lonza).

Purification of human Treg cells and suppression assay

Human PBMCs were obtained from adult healthy donors. Human CD4+CD25+CD127low Treg cells were positively selected by using an immunomagnetic Human CD4+CD25+ CD127low T Cell isolation kit (18063; STEMCELL Technologies) from human PBMCs. PBMCs from the same donor were labeled by carboxyfluorescein diacetate succinimidyl ester (CFSE; 1 μg/ml; Invitrogen) and mixed with Treg cells at various ratios in round-bottomed 96-well plates. Cells were cultured for 4 days in the presence of anti-human CD3 antibody (0.5 μg/ml). Then, CD8+ T cells were gated by APC-conjugated mouse anti-CD8 antibody (RPA-T8; 301014; BioLegend). The CFSE dilution in CD8+ T cells was measured by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS).

Intracellular cytokine staining

In suppression assay, CD8+ T cells were stained by APC-conjugated mouse anti-CD8 antibody and fixed by eBioscience IC fixation buffer (00-8222-49). The intracellular levels of TNF-α, ILF-γ, and IL-2 were analyzed by FACS with BioLegend antibodies, including PE-labeled anti-human TNF-α (MAb11; 502908), PE-labeled anti-human IFN-γ (4S.B3; 502508), and PE-labeled anti-human IL-2 (MQ1-17H12; 500306) antibody. A BioLegend PE-labeled mouse IgG1 isotype (MOPC-21; 400139), a PE-labeled rat IgG2a isotype antibody (RTK2758; 400507), and a PE-labeled rat IgG1 isotype antibody (RTK2071; 400407) were used as controls. For mouse cells, PE-labeled anti-mouse IFN-γ (XMG1.2; 505807), PE anti-mouse IL-4 (11B11; 504103), and APC anti-mouse IL-4 (11B11; 504105) antibodies were used.

Data analysis of TCGA datasets

The association between the inclusion of FOXP3 exon 2 and the overall survival of patients with cancer was analyzed in TCGA datasets. The inclusion of exon 2 was evaluated by a PSI (percent-splice-in) value for each patient from the Spliceseq database (https://bioinformatics.mdanderson.org/TCGASpliceSeq) (59). Patients without exon 2 PSI were excluded from the analyses. PSI is the ratio of normalized exon 2 inclusion level versus both inclusion and exclusion levels. A high or low PSI value of inclusion of exon 2 was determined by the number of SDs from the mean value. The numbers of cases with low and high inclusion of exon 2 are 299 and 43 in head and neck cancer, 379 and 109 in breast infiltrating ductal carcinoma, 63 and 83 in esophageal carcinoma, 62 and 11 in pancreatic adenocarcinoma, 80 and 66 in colon adenocarcinoma, or 29 and 30 in rectum adenocarcinoma, respectively.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

The levels of mouse serum anti-dsDNA antibody were analyzed by an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kit (CSB-E11194m, CUSABIO). Briefly, sample or standard sera were added to the microtiter plate precoated with anti-mouse IgG, followed by adding biotin-conjugated dsDNA and avidin-conjugated horseradish peroxidase, and then developed by trimethylboron substrate solution. The concentration of anti-dsDNA antibody was determined by fitting four-parameter logistic curves.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

Single intratumoral immune cells were isolated by using Percoll and gradient centrifugation for scRNA-seq analysis. scRNA-seq assays were performed by Wuhan Ruixing Biotechnology Co. Ltd. in the DNBelab C high-throughput scRNA-seq platform (BGI). Raw data were analyzed by dnbc4tools software. scRNA-seq data were aligned to the GRCm38 reference genome. Treg cells were marked by Foxp3, Cd4, and Ctla4 genes. Differentially expressed genes of Treg cells between WT and humanized mice were identified by using Seurat.

For single-cell third-generation RNA-seq analysis of spleen Treg cells, spleen Treg cells were isolated by using a CD4+CD25+ Regulatory T cell isolation kit (Miltenyi Biotec). Oxford Nanopore Technologies assay was performed by Wuhan Ruixing Biotechnology Co. Ltd. Raw data were analyzed by the wf-single-cell software. scRNA-seq data were aligned to the GRCm39 reference genome. Differentially expressed genes between Srsf3-high and Srsf3-low Treg cells were identified by using Seurat. Differentially spliced events between Srsf3-high and Srsf3-low Treg cells were identified by using ASProfile.

RNA immunoprecipitation

Cells transfected with human FOXP3 minigene were treated with ultraviolet-crosslink and lysed with RIPA buffer. Cell lysate was immunoprecipitated with anti-SRSF3 antibody. Mouse IgG was used as control. Precipitated RNA was purified and analyzed by RT-PCR using FOXP3-specific primers.

Statistical analysis

Statistical difference of means between two groups were calculated with paired or unpaired Student’s t test (GraphPad Prism). P < 0.05 was considered significant. Survival analysis was performed with a log-rank test with GraphPad Prism software. The Kaplan-Meier method was used to produce survival curves.
