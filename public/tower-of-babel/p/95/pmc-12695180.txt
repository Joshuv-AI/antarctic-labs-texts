Revisiting the role of intra-arterial thrombolysis after EVT: a mechanistic blind spot in an otherwise robust meta-analysis
Authors: Hui Wang, Meng-Jie Shi, Fangshi Xu
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the article by Guo and colleagues titled “Efficacy and safety of intra-arterial thrombolysis after endovascular reperfusion for acute ischemic stroke: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized trials” published in the International Journal of Surgery (2025;111(6):4002–4008)[1]. The authors are to be commended for their comprehensive synthesis of randomized evidence examining intra-arterial thrombolysis (IAT) following endovascular thrombectomy (EVT) in acute large-vessel occlusion (LVO) stroke. Their findings offer valuable insights into the area of persistent clinical uncertainty. While the conclusions are well-supported by the data presented, we respectfully wish to raise several points that we believe merit further consideration, particularly regarding mechanistic interpretation and clinical generalizability. These pivotal issues included outcome stratification based on different clinical decisions to assess whether the patients benefit from treatment in different clinical scenarios, differences in therapeutic efficacy of IAT affected by multiple factors, and the evaluation bias caused by temporal heterogeneity stemming from technological development.

First, the study implicitly treats IAT as a uniform adjunctive intervention across trials. However, in clinical practice, the intent behind IAT use can vary significantly ranging from routine protocol-driven administration to targeted, post-thrombectomy rescue therapy for incomplete or distal reperfusion. Without stratifying outcomes based on the clinical indication, procedural timing, or imaging triggers for IAT administration, the meta-analysis may conflate fundamentally different uses of the therapy. Understanding whether benefit arises from planned augmentation or reactive salvage is essential to informing patient selection in real-world practice[2].

Second, the efficacy of intra-arterial thrombolysis is inherently dependent on anatomical proximity between the catheter delivery site and the thrombotic material. For instance, when thrombolytic agents are infused into more proximal segments of the middle cerebral artery, their efficacy may be significantly attenuated if the remaining thrombus is located in more distal or tortuous branches. Unfortunately, the analysis does not incorporate information on infusion site alignment, segmental occlusion characteristics, or post-procedural perfusion patterns, all of which may critically modulate treatment response[3].

Third, we note that the seven trials included span a period of rapid evolution in EVT techniques, device performance, and peri-procedural workflow. Improvements in imaging triage, thrombectomy efficacy, and supportive care could have differentially influenced outcomes in the EVT-only arms. Without accounting for this temporal heterogeneity, there is a risk of over- or underestimating the added value of IAT due to background improvements in stroke care. A time-adjusted meta-regression could help clarify whether the observed benefit is consistent across different eras of endovascular practice[4]. In summary, while Guo et al. have provided important aggregate evidence supporting the potential role of IAT, further refinement in understanding its mechanism of action, procedural context, and anatomical targeting is necessary. We encourage future meta-analyses and trials to include such granularity, which will be essential in defining the subgroups most likely to benefit from this adjunctive approach.
