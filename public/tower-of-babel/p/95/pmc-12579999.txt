Introduction

Testicular germ cell tumors (GCTs) are rare cancers, predominantly affecting young men, with an incidence of 1–2 per 100,000. Risk factors include family history, cryptorchidism, and a history of contralateral testicular.

tumors [1]. Treatment strategies vary depending on histology (seminoma vs. non‐seminoma), stage, and risk stratification (IGCCCG classification). Seminomas benefit from radiotherapy and cisplatin‐based chemotherapy, with radiotherapy and single‐agent carboplatin recommended for early‐stage seminomas, while advanced cases require cisplatin‐based chemotherapy. Non‐seminomas: BEP (bleomycin + etoposide + cisplatin) and retroperitoneal lymph node dissection are standard [2]. Advances in chemotherapy have significantly improved the cure rate even in metastatic cases. However, teratomas, containing mature somatic elements, show low sensitivity to chemotherapy. Rarely, teratomas can undergo somatic malignant transformation (SMT), leading to histological variants [3, 4]. SMT is a rare condition present in approximately 2%–6% of non‐seminomas, with a variety of histological forms including sarcoma, adenocarcinoma, and squamous cell carcinoma [5].

We report a rare case of liposarcoma arising from a mature teratoma following testicular tumor surgery.

Case Presentation

A 22‐year‐old man presented with a left scrotal swelling, which had been progressively enlarging over the previous year.(day X) He visited our hospital with fever and further scrotal enlargement. His medical history was significant for depression, with no notable family history or current medications. Physical examination revealed marked swelling of the left scrotum. Tumor markers were elevated (hCG: 4330 mIU/mL, AFP: 1075 ng/mL, LDH: 1979 U/L). Contrast‐enhanced CT showed an enlarged left testis with heterogeneous enhancement (Figure 1a), enlarged para‐aortic lymph nodes (Figure 1b), multiple pulmonary metastases (Figure 1c), and liver metastases (Figure 1d).

Treatment

On day X + 1, the patient underwent a high inguinal orchiectomy. Histopathological examination revealed a mixed germ cell tumor comprising 80% embryonal carcinoma and 20% choriocarcinoma (pT3, LVI1, margin negative). The embryonal carcinoma component of the mixed germ cell tumor consisted of immature epithelial‐like tumor cells, which were cuboidal, showed papillary and tubular structures, and stained positively for CD30 and AFP (Figure 2a). The choriocarcinoma component consisted of proliferating tumor cells with clear cytoplasm and large nuclei, accompanied by hemorrhage and necrosis, and stained positively for HCG (Figure 2b). Considering the staging (pT3N0M1b, Stage IIIC), chemotherapy was initiated on day X + 4 with five courses of BEP (bleomycin, etoposide, cisplatin) followed by three courses of PE (cisplatin, etoposide). X + 7 months later, retroperitoneal lymph node dissection (RPLND) was performed, and pathological findings showed no viable tumor cells. The RPLND was performed using a full template, and there were no abnormalities in the perinephric adipose tissue during surgery.

(a) Embryonal carcinoma component of the mixed germ cell tumor. Immature epithelial‐like tumor cells, which are cuboidal in shape, exhibit papillary, and tubular structures. (b) Choriocarcinoma component of the mixed germ cell tumor. Tumors which have pale cytoplasm and large nucleus.

X + 9 months later, partial hepatectomy (S3, S6, S7, S8) was performed, and pathology revealed remnants of mature teratoma post‐chemotherapy. X + 1 year later, thoracoscopic lung resection was performed, and pathology revealed no malignancy. The patient remained stable on regular imaging and tumor marker surveillance. However, X + 3 years later, a rapidly enlarging left renal mass was detected on follow‐up CT, despite stable tumor markers. Open left nephrectomy was performed (Figure 3a). Histopathology revealed multiple nodules of a yellow‐white solid tumor containing translucent mucinous material (Figure 3b). Immunohistochemical staining showed positivity for MDM2 and α‐SMA, indicating a sarcomatous component comprising well‐differentiated (Figure 4a), dedifferentiated (Figure 4b), and myxoid liposarcoma (Figure 4c), alongside residual teratoma elements (Figure 4d,e). Because the tumor contains a mixture of teratomas, such as glandular epithelium and squamous epithelium (Figure 4f). The final diagnosis was liposarcoma arising from a mature teratoma. The patient is currently alive without evidence of recurrence or metastasis.

Discussion

The recurrence rate after RPLND for testicular cancer is approximately 20%, and RPLND is considered safe and potentially curative (cure rate > 70%) for Stage II GCTs, including seminomas [6]. Pluripotent stem cell‐derived GCTs often present with various histological types at metastatic sites during the course of treatment, and residual mature teratomas may be observed after chemotherapy, especially in metastatic nonseminomatous GCTs [7]. SMT is a well‐described entity that refers to the transformation of the somatic teratomatous component of a nonsecretory germ cell tumor (NSGCT) into a histology indistinguishable from somatic malignancies. It is estimated that SMTs occur in 3% to 6% of patients with GCTs with teratoid differentiation.

GCTs, with sarcomas, particularly rhabdomyosarcomas, being the most common subtype [5, 8]. Malignant transformation occurs by differentiation of totipotent germ cell elements into somatic tissues or by malignant transformation of pre‐existing teratomatous elements. Because mature teratomas in metastatic lesions originate from primary tumors containing mature elements, it has been speculated that the appearance of induced differentiation in metastatic lesions is actually the result of selective destruction of teratomatous elements by chemotherapy and not caused by differentiation of totipotent germline cells [9]. SMTs can arise at primary sites, metastatic sites, or extragonadal sites. Their pathogenesis involves malignant transformation of totipotent germ cells or pre‐existing teratomatous elements [10]. SMTs often affect adjacent organs and major blood vessels, necessitating complex surgical resection and reconstruction to improve the chances of cure. Standard GCT chemotherapy regimens (e.g., cisplatin‐based) are generally ineffective against SMTs, making surgical resection the mainstay of treatment [8]. Cases of transformation of a mature teratoma into a liposarcoma, especially after surgery for testicular cancer, appear to be very rare and have not been reported in the literature [11].

SMT is considered to be less responsive to cisplatin than GCT, and the role of chemotherapy in SMT has not been fully evaluated. Prophylactic complete resection of residual masses after chemotherapy for GCT may prevent malignant transformation of residual teratomas and prevent the development of TMT. Surgery is considered the first‐line treatment for both the prevention and treatment of SMT [5]. The presence of teratomas in primary tumors also suggests a potentially lethal tumor phenotype that is inherently resistant to chemotherapy and prone to somatic transformation, and can only be cured by timely surgical resection [12].

In this case, timely surgical resection likely contributed to the prolonged survival. However, SMT treatment remains challenging, and effective treatments for advanced cancer remain limited.

Conclusion

Liposarcoma arising from mature teratoma after surgery for testicular cancer is extremely rare, and to our knowledge, there have been no reports of this condition. Because of poor response to systemic chemotherapy, early and aggressive surgical resection may improve prognosis. Careful long‐term follow‐up and timely intervention are essential for patients at risk for SMT.
