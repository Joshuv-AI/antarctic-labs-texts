A cyanide-free route towards the electrosynthesis of nitriles
Authors: Jiahui Xian, Lulu Wang, Ziyu Mi, Meng Wang, Sze Xing Tan, Xun Cao, Mingsheng Zhang, Shibo Xi, Jiajian Gao, Yanwei Lum, Pengfei Ou, Wan Ru Leow
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe manufacture of nitriles is critical to various industrial applications, but poor selectivity results in the significant release of pollutive byproducts such as CO2, NOx and toxic cyanide. Here, we report the electrochemical conversion of benzaldehyde to benzonitrile at near-unity selectivity, during which Co3O4 clusters-decorated CuxO electrocatalyst activates the C−N bond of an unstable intermediate species. Operando spectroelectrochemical studies and theoretical calculations reveal that benzaldehyde couples with NH3 to form the phenylmethanimine intermediate bound atop Cu2+ active sites, which facilitates hydrogen transfer from the imine group to nearby surface oxygen. Our electrochemical system can convert aromatic and aliphatic aldehydes to the corresponding nitriles at Faradaic efficiencies of 78–97%. This can be paired with cathodic NOx reduction, which produces a commensurable amount of NH3 to that consumed even at a current density of 200 mA cm−2. Our work offers a clean synthetic route to otherwise-pollutive chemicals, and also expands the repertoire of organonitrogen compounds that can be accessed by electrocatalysis.Subject terms: Electrocatalysis, Electrocatalysis, Electrocatalysis

Introduction

Nitriles have far-ranging applications in pharmaceuticals, agrochemicals, dyes, and polymers1. For example, benzonitrile is manufactured at 10 million kg per year due to its importance as a building block in pharmaceuticals and melamine resins2. Currently, nitriles are manufactured through the ammoxidation of alkanes3,4, alcohols or aldehydes5 with ammonia and oxygen at high temperatures (Fig. 1a). These conditions require heavy energy consumption and result in low selectivity, leading to the release of CO2, NOx, and over-oxidation products6. Other synthesis methods, such as the Sandmeyer reaction and Rosenmund-von Braun reaction, require stoichiometric amounts of toxic cyanide reagents7,8 (Fig. 1b); more than one million tons of cyanide are consumed per year by the chemicals industry9, which generate hazardous wastes due to incomplete cyanide consumption or production of cyanide derivatives10. Thus, it is important to develop cleaner methods of nitriles synthesis that can utilize renewable energy and benign conditions, while avoiding cyanide reagents.

Electrochemical benzaldehyde-to-benzonitrile conversion.

Schematics illustrating benzonitrile production via a ammoxidation and b cyanation. c Our proposed sustainable electrosynthesis of benzonitrile from benzaldehyde, which can be integrated into reaction pathways that enable the nitrilation of more challenging alcohol and alkyl groups, and paired with cathodic NOx reduction as a supply of NH3 reactant.

Recently, there has been great interest in electrochemical C−N coupling reactions, as they offer a sustainable route for producing valuable organonitrogen compounds such as urea, amides, amines, oximes, and amino acids11–25. Comparatively, nitriles have a higher degree of unsaturation; a different electrochemical route must be designed to access this type of C≡N bond. Previously, it has been reported that copper salt can catalyze the thermal ammoxidation of aldehydes to nitriles; Cu2+ binds to the aldimine intermediate to form an iminylcuprate complex, which then dissociates to Cu+ and the nitrile product26. This led us to consider the possibility of enabling “electrochemical ammoxidation” by building Cuδ+ into our electrocatalyst. We also note that the oxidative nature of the reaction means that it can be coupled to the cathodic nitrogen oxides reduction reaction (NOxRR) to NH3, which suggests that waste NOx in industrial exhaust can be utilized as a N-source to enhance the sustainability of the reaction (Fig. 1c).

In this work, we demonstrate the electrocatalytic conversion of benzaldehyde to benzonitrile using NH3 as a benign nitrogen source under ambient conditions. This is achieved with an electrocatalyst comprising Co3O4 clusters-decorated CuxO nanorod array (Co3O4/CuxO), which enabled Faradaic efficiency of 98.7(±1.1)% and near-unity selectivity under an applied potential of 1.35 V vs. RHE. The electrocatalyst remains stable with Faradaic efficiency averaging 95.4(±2.5)% over 100 h. Operando spectroelectrochemical and density function theory (DFT) studies show that benzaldehyde couples with NH3 to form an unstable phenylmethanimine intermediate, which subsequently undergoes Cuδ+-mediated electrochemical dehydrogenation to benzonitrile. The electrochemical benzaldehyde-to-benzonitrile conversion can be integrated into reaction pathways that enable the nitrilation of more challenging alcohol and alkyl groups. Our electrochemical system can also convert other aromatic and aliphatic aldehydes, including those derived from CO2, to the corresponding nitriles with Faradaic efficiencies of 78–97%. Finally, we demonstrated tandem benzonitrile electrosynthesis and NOxRR at current densities up to 200 mA cm–2.

Results

Electrocatalyst design and characterization

We prepared the Co3O4/CuxO electrocatalyst by chemical oxidation of copper foam in an aqueous NaOH and (NH4)2S2O8 solution, which embellished the surface with Cu(OH)2 nanorods, followed by immersion in a solution containing ZIF-67 metal-organic framework and annealing in air at 350 °C (see characterization of the Cu(OH)2 and ZIF-67/Cu(OH)2 nanorod arrays in Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). Co K-edge X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) spectroscopy indicated that the ZIF-67 was successfully converted to Co3O4, as the absorption edge of Co3O4/CuxO displayed identical features to that of the Co3O4 standard (Supplementary Fig. 3). The Fourier-transformed extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectra revealed oscillations corresponding to Co−O, the Co−Co bond with octahedral centers, and the Co−Co bond with octahedral and tetrahedral centers, which further confirms the formation of Co3O4 cluster27. High-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) with corresponding EDX mapping showed that the Co3O4 cluster took the form of a slightly concave octahedron attached to the tip of the CuxO nanorod (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 4). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed an array of cluster-on-nanorod morphology on the copper foam surface (Fig. 2b). The X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern showed peaks at 35.5°, 38.7°, 61.5° corresponding to (11-1), (111) and (11-3) lattice planes of CuO, as well as 36.5° and 61.5° corresponding to (111) and (220) lattice planes of Cu2O (Supplementary Fig. 5)28. This is corroborated by high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM), which revealed crystalline phases with lattice spacings of 0.242 and 0.234 nm corresponding to Cu2O(111) and CuO(111), respectively. It should be noted that both Cu2O(110) and Cu2O(111) facets were observed (Supplementary Fig. 6), with the dominant one being the (111) surface. The Co3O4 cluster also appeared to be highly crystalline, with lattice spacings of 0.246 and 0.287 nm corresponding to (311) and (220) planes (Fig. 2c).

Electrochemical conversion of benzaldehyde to benzonitrile

The electrochemical C−N coupling of benzaldehyde and ammonia to benzonitrile was conducted in a three-electrode H-type cell, with a working electrode comprising Co3O4/CuxO on copper foam, an Ag/AgCl reference electrode, and a platinum plate counter electrode (Supplementary Fig. 7). The optimal anolyte concentration comprised 0.04 M benzaldehyde, 2.0 M NH3, and 0.1 M NaOH in a 41:9 water:acetonitrile mixture (Supplementary Figs. 8 and 9). The catholyte is an aqueous 0.1 M NaOH solution, and is separated from the anolyte by Nafion® 117 membrane. The Faradaic efficiencies towards benzonitrile showed a volcano-type tendency in the potential range of 1.15–1.65 V vs. RHE, with the optimal Faradaic efficiency at 98.7(±1.1)% at 1.35 V vs. RHE (Fig. 2d). Beyond 1.55 V vs. RHE, the Faradaic efficiency decreases significantly due to competing NH3 oxidation reaction. This is corroborated by the linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) curve, which revealed the NH3 oxidation peak to be about 1.5 V vs. RHE under reaction conditions (Supplementary Fig. 10). The yield of benzonitrile reached 48.6(±6.7)% after 1 h of reaction at 1.55 V vs. RHE, and further increased to 75.0(±1.5)% after 3 h (Supplementary Fig. 11). The selectivity towards benzonitrile was ~100% with no obvious byproducts detected (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13). No benzonitrile was detected at open-circuit voltage (OCV), without applied potential or in the absence of NH3, which confirms the electrochemical nature of the reaction and the important role of NH3 as the nitrogen source (Supplementary Fig. 14).

We investigated the robustness of the Co3O4/CuxO electrocatalyst by subjecting it to a constant potential of 1.35 V vs. RHE for 100 h, during which portions of the electrolyte were periodically removed for analysis and replaced with fresh electrolyte. Under these conditions, the electrocatalyst achieved a stable current density of ~10 mA cm−2, with an average Faradaic efficiency of 95.4(±2.5)% (Fig. 2e). Post-reaction characterizations of the electrocatalyst (i.e., SEM, EDX, XANES, EXAFS, and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), see Supplementary Figs. 15–18) showed no obvious change in the structure or composition, which suggests its compatibility for long-term operation.

Investigation of reaction mechanism

Having established the efficacy of Co3O4/CuxO towards benzonitrile production, we studied the role of different components in the electrocatalyst. The CuxO electrocatalyst without Co3O4 clusters was also prepared (see characterization in Supplementary Figs. 19 and 20) and yielded a Faradaic efficiency of 71.8(±3.1)% at 1.35 V vs. RHE, which suggests that CuxO functions as the main catalytic component driving the benzaldehyde-to-benzonitrile conversion (Supplementary Fig. 21). Only trace benzonitrile product was detected when pristine copper foam (CF) was used as the working electrode (Supplementary Fig. 22), which suggests that either Cu2+ or Cu+ should function as the electrocatalytic active site in CuxO. We performed operando electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) to study the electrode/electrolyte interface during reaction (Fig. 3a)29. At potentials between 0.5 and 1.0 V vs. RHE, we observed a signal in the middle-frequency region (100–102 Hz) that corresponds to the electrooxidation of the CuxO catalyst (i.e., Cu0→Cu+→Cu2+). This is consistent with the cyclic voltammetry (CV) curve, which showed two distinct oxidation peaks at 0.60 and 0.85 V vs. RHE corresponding to Cu0→Cu+ and Cu0/Cu+→Cu2+, respectively28 (Supplementary Fig. 23). At potentials >1.05 V vs. RHE, the signal is observed in the low-frequency region (10−1–100 Hz), which is related to the appearance of oxidation species on the surface of the electrode. This signifies that the structural evolution of Cuδ+ is complete and the benzonitrile electrosynthesis occurs. In situ Raman spectroscopy showed distinctive peaks at 218 and 294 cm−1 at OCV, which can be attributed to Cu2O and CuO, respectively (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Figs. 24 and 25). In the absence of benzaldehyde and NH3, the Cu2O peak disappeared at 1.35 V vs. RHE, indicating the oxidation of Cu+ to Cu2+. With benzaldehyde and NH3, the Cu2O peak can still be observed at 1.35 V vs. RHE, while the CuO peak showed reduced intensity compared to that at OCV, which suggests that Cu2+ catalyzed the benzaldehyde-to-benzonitrile conversion and was reverted to Cu+ in the process.

The reaction mechanism was further probed using in situ attenuated total reflection surface-enhanced infrared absorption spectroscopy (ATR-SEIRAS), by tracking the adsorbed intermediates and products on CuxO/CF at potentials from OCV to 1.65 V vs. RHE (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 26). Under OCV, peaks were observed at 1700, 1371, 1233, and 1138 cm−1, which could be assigned to the C=O stretching vibration of benzaldehyde, alcoholic O−H group, the N−H twisting vibration of CH2−NH structure, and the NH2 stretching vibration of CH2−NH2 structure, respectively23,30. This implies that NH3, which has a relatively strong nucleophilicity, can spontaneously attack benzaldehyde to form amino(phenyl)methanol, which undergoes dehydration to generate the unstable *benzyl-azane intermediate. As the potential increased to 1.15 V vs. RHE, the NH2 peak at 1138 cm−1 is diminished while the N−H peak at 1233 cm−1 is slightly enhanced. We observed the emergence of peaks corresponding to the N−H stretching vibration of CH=NH structure at 1296 cm−1, C=N stretching vibration at 1624 cm−1 and C≡N stretching vibration at 2259 cm−1, which indicates the formation of phenylmethanimine intermediate and benzonitrile product23,30. As the potential increases up to 1.65 V vs. RHE, the NH2 and N−H absorption peaks of unstable amino(phenyl)methanol and *benzyl-azane intermediates are weakened, whereas the C=N bond and C≡N peaks of phenylmethanimine and benzonitrile are generally strengthened. This signified that amino(phenyl)methanol and *benzyl-azane intermediates rapidly converted to the critical phenylmethanimine intermediate, which subsequently could be dehydrogenated to generate the benzonitrile. Liquid chromatography-quadrupole time of flight mass spectrometry (LC-QTOF) also ascertained the presence of phenylmethanimine intermediate and benzonitrile product in the post-reaction anolyte (Fig. 3d). The use of 15N-labelled NH3 yielded 15N-labelled phenylmethanimine and benzonitrile, which reinforced the important role of NH3 as a nitrogen source in benzonitrile electrosynthesis (Supplementary Fig. 27). Taken together, we postulate that NH3 readily undergoes C−N coupling with the carbonyl group of benzaldehyde to form an unstable phenylmethanimine intermediate, which subsequently undergoes Cuδ+-mediated electrochemical dehydrogenation to yield the benzonitrile product (Fig. 3e).

We further investigated the Cuδ+-mediated dehydrogenation step through density functional theory (DFT) calculations. A CuO(111) slab model with four atomic layers was constructed, upon which several initial adsorption geometries were examined. The most stable configuration features the imine nitrogen of phenylmethanimine (Ph−CH=NH) binding to a single two-coordinate Cu atom (Configuration 1 in Supplementary Fig. 28). This geometry suggests a possible adsorption mode in which the =NH moiety is in proximity with the surface, potentially enabling the abstraction of the N-bound hydrogen. Within the computational hydrogen electrode (CHE) framework, this hydrogen is removed as a coupled proton–electron pair rather than transferred to or stabilized on any surface atoms31. The resultant molecule adopts an sp3-like configuration and relocates to a bridge site coordinated by two Cu atoms (Supplementary Fig. 29c, d). The hydrogen atom from the CH= moiety is then abstracted by the surface to form the nitrile product (C≡N), culminating in the final state where the nitrogen atom binds atop a single Cu atom (Supplementary Fig. 29e, f). This second hydrogen transfer is calculated to be thermodynamically more favorable than the first, as reflected by the downhill reaction energy (ΔG). Overall, the ΔG of phenylmethanimine dehydrogenation on CuO(111) is 0.70 eV (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Table 2). In contrast, phenylmethanimine dehydrogenation on Cu2O(110) has a higher ΔG of 1.16 eV (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Figs. 30 and 31), suggesting that Cu2+ species may be more effective for phenylmethanimine dehydrogenation than Cu+ species under the modeled conditions. This is substantiated by Bader charge analysis of the adsorbed phenylmethanimine on CuO(111). As reaction progresses, the phenylmethanimine molecular fragment exhibits an increasingly positive charge; from +0.16 in the initial adsorbed state to +0.85 in the first dehydrogenated intermediate and +2.10 in the final nitrile product (Supplementary Table 3). Correspondingly, CuO(111) exhibits increasingly negative charge (−0.16 → −0.85 → −2.10), which is indicative that the surface Cu2⁺ accepts electrons from phenylmethanimine and is reduced to Cu⁺.

To elucidate the influence of the Co3O4 cluster, we similarly examined the phenylmethanimine dehydrogenation step on the Co3O4(311) surface (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Figs. 32 and 33). The calculated ΔG of 0.96 eV lies between those of CuO(111) and Cu2O(111), indicating an intermediate oxidizing strength within this computational model. It should be noted that these DFT results are based on idealized static slab models and mainly provide qualitative trends under real catalytic conditions. Experimentally, the Faradaic efficiency of benzaldehyde-to-benzonitrile conversion using Co3O4 clusters supported on carbon paper is 32.6(±3.8)% (Supplementary Fig. 34), which suggests that the Co3O4 clusters provide additional active sites that contribute to the enhanced Faradaic efficiency of Co3O4/CuxO. This is corroborated by the increased double-layer capacitance (Cdl) of Co3O4/CuxO compared to CuxO (2.06 versus 1.70 mF cm−2, see Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 35). We also investigated the electronic structure of Co3O4/CuxO through XPS analysis (Fig. 4b, c and Supplementary Figs. 36 and 37). High-resolution Co 2p XPS spectrum showed a negative shift compared to that of pure Co3O4 powder, signifying a lower average Co oxidation state. Cu 2p XPS spectrum of Co3O4/CuxO showed a higher energy shift compared to that of CuxO, which indicates a higher average Cu oxidation state and suggests electron transfer from Cu to Co within the Co3O4/CuxO material. The higher average Cu oxidation state promotes the Cuδ+-mediated dehydrogenation step, thereby enabling higher Faradaic efficiencies for electrochemical benzaldehyde-to-benzonitrile conversion (Supplementary Fig. 34). Therefore, the decoration of Co3O4 clusters on CuxO electrocatalyst not only increases the active sites, but can potentially modulate the electronic structure of the electrocatalyst, resulting in a higher average Cu oxidation state promoting the Cuδ+-mediated dehydrogenation step.

Electrosynthesis of various nitriles and utilization of NOx

The broad applicability of Co3O4/CuxO electrocatalyst was demonstrated through the electrochemical conversion of various aldehydes to the corresponding nitriles at near-unity selectivities (Supplementary Fig. 38). An application scenario is the electrosynthesis of 2,6-dichlorobenzonitrile, an important herbicide with a market size of $23 million per year32,33; we achieved Faradaic efficiency of 81.2(±1.7)% at 1.35 V vs. RHE. The presence of electron-withdrawing groups such as −Cl, −Br, and −CN generally led to higher Faradaic efficiencies of 93–97%, as the enhanced electrophilicity of these benzaldehydes promotes C−N coupling with nucleophilic NH3. For electron-donating −CH3 and −OCH3 groups, Faradaic efficiencies were lower at 84.2(±6.1) and 77.8(±2.5)%. The Co3O4/CuxO electrocatalyst can be extended to the electrosynthesis of aliphatic nitriles, such as acetonitrile with a Faradaic efficiency of 86.2(±4.7)%. It should be noted that the acetaldehyde precursor can be obtained from electrochemical CO2 reduction reaction, thereby enabling the electrosynthesis of nitriles from renewable sources34. We also demonstrated that nitrile electrosynthesis can be integrated into reaction pathways starting from precursors with alcohol and even alkyl groups (Fig. 4d). For example, the electrochemical benzyl alcohol-to-benzonitrile conversion can be achieved Faradaic efficiency at 43.1(±2.0)% (Supplementary Fig. 39). The electrochemical toluene-to-benzonitrile conversion was conducted in two stages; toluene was first oxidized at 100 mA cm−2 for 2 h using a CoOx/IrO2 on Ti mesh electrode to activate the C(sp3)−H bond and achieve sufficient benzaldehyde intermediate, after which NH3 was added and the reaction continued at 25 mA cm−2 to enable benzonitrile using the Co3O4/CuxO electrocatalyst (Supplementary Fig. 40).

Finally, we considered that the electrochemical valorization of waste NOx to nitrogenous chemicals can mitigate pollution and reduce dependence on valuable NH3 as a nitrogen source. We therefore coupled cathodic NOxRR with anodic benzonitrile electrosynthesis in a single electrochemical cell, with the former acting as a NH3 supply for the latter. The cathode was oxide-derived Cu (OD-Cu) synthesized by electrochemically reducing CuxO (Supplementary Fig. 41), and the catholyte comprised 0.1 M KNO2 in 1 M NaOH. As NH3 in the anolyte can crossover the Nafion membrane and lead to a higher-than-actual NOxRR Faradaic efficiency, we employed a decoupled electrolyzer connected by O2/H2O redox couple, which can better separate the catholyte and anolyte in order to accurately quantify the amount of NH3 produced at the cathode (Supplementary Fig. 42). Notably, the NH3 consumption at anode was comparable to NH3 production at cathode even at current densities up to 200 mA cm−2, which signifies that waste NOx can be viably incorporated into valuable nitriles (Fig. 4e, Faradaic efficiencies can be found in Supplementary Fig. 43). This also means that the achieved partial current densities and production rates are competitive with other reported nitrile electrosynthesis, while maintaining near-unity selectivity (Supplementary Table 4).

Discussion

We report the electro-conversion of aromatic and aliphatic aldehydes to valuable nitriles using a Co3O4 clusters-decorated CuxO electrocatalyst, which achieves high Faradaic efficiencies of 78–97% and near-unity selectivities, thereby avoiding the release of CO2, NOx, and toxic cyanide into the environment. Operando spectroelectrochemical and DFT studies provide strong evidence for the following mechanism: (1) the application of positive potential enables structural evolution of Cu+ to Cu2+, (2) benzaldehyde couples with NH3 to form the phenylmethanimine intermediate bound atop the Cu2+ active sites, (3) this configuration facilitates hydrogen transfer from the imine group to nearby surface oxygen, yielding the nitrile product while regenerating Cu+. The electrochemical aldehyde-to-nitrile conversion can be adapted to enable the nitrilation of more challenging alcohol and alkyl groups. We envision that the sustainability of our electrochemical system can be further enhanced by using precursors derived from waste; we demonstrated tandem benzonitrile electrosynthesis and NOxRR at current densities up to 200 mA cm−2, which meant that waste NOx can be converted to NH3 and used as a feedstock for nitrile production in the same reactor.

Methods

Materials

All the chemicals and solvent were commercially available and directly used without further purification. Cu foam (Cu > 99%, thickness 0.5 mm, 100 PPI) was purchased from Latech Scientific Supply Pte. Ltd. Hydrochloric Acid (37%, HCl), Sodium hydroxide (≥98%, NaOH), Ammonium persulphate (≥98%, (NH4)2S2O8), Cobalt(II) nitrate (≥98%, Co(NO3)2·6H2O), Potassium nitrite (≥96%, KNO2), Benzaldehyde (purified by redistillation, ≥99.5%), Ammonium hydroxide solution (puriss., 30–33% NH3 in H2O), Ammonium-15N hydroxide solution (3 N in H2O, 98 atom% 15N), Benzonitrile (≥98%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. 2-methylimidazole (>98%) was purchased from TCI. Acetonitrile (HPLC grade, ≥99.9%, MeCN) was purchased from Fisher Scientific Korea Ltd. Deuterium oxide (D, 99.9%, D2O) and Dimethyl sulfoxide-D6 (D, 99.9%, DMSO-d6) were purchased from Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, Inc. 4-Chlorobenzaldehyde (>99%), 4-Chlorobenzonitrile (>97%), 4-Bromobenzaldehyde (>99%), 4-Bromobenzonitrile (>98%), 4-Cyanobenzaldehyde (>98%), 1,4-Dicyanobenzene (>98%), 2,6-Dichlorobenzaldehyde (>99%), 2,6-Dichlorobenzonitrile (>97%), 4-Methylbenzaldehyde (>99%), p-Tolunitrile (>98%), 4-Methoxybenzaldehyde (>99%), 4-Methoxybenzonitrile (>99%), Acetaldehyde (puriss, p.a., anhydrous, ≥99.5%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

Synthesis and characterization of the Co3O4/CuxO nanorod array electrocatalyst

The Co3O4/CuxO electrocatalyst was synthesized by the modified chemical oxidation followed by annealing procedure. Cu foam was ultrasonically cleaned in 3 M HCl solution for 10 min, then in a mixture of acetone and deionized water for another 10 min. The pretreated Cu foam was soaked into a 40 mL aqueous solution containing 4 g NaOH and 1.5 g (NH4)2S2O8 for 1 h, then washed with deionized water and ethanol. The resultant Cu(OH)2 nanorod array was prepared after drying at 60 °C. Cu(OH)2 was immersed into 2 mmol cobaltous nitrate hexahydrate in methanol solution for 10 min, after which 8 mmol 2-methylimidazole in methanol solution was added under stirring and allowed to stand for 6 h. The resultant ZIF-67/Cu(OH)2 was taken out, washed with deionized water and methanol, dried at 60 °C, then annealed at 350 °C for 3 h, 2 °C min−1 to form the Co3O4/CuxO electrocatalyst.

Electrocatalyst characterization

To characterize the Co3O4/CuxO electrocatalyst (XRD) was detected on Bruker® D8 ADVANCE with Cu Kα source. SEM images were obtained on a Hitachi SU8220 FESEM at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV. Energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectroscopy was collected with an on-board AEMTEK® Octane Elite EDS System, with an accelerating voltage of 20 kV. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images were obtained on JEOL JEM-2200FS, and elemental mapping were recorded by JEOL JED-2300 instrument. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was measured on a PHI Quantum 2000 XPS system. X-Ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) was conducted on XAFCA beamline at the Singapore Synchrotron Light Source.

Evaluation of electrochemical performance

The electrochemical benzaldehyde-to-benzonitrile conversions were conducted in H-type electrolytic cell (purchased from Tianjin Aida). The electrolyte was prepared individually before each experimental run. The anolyte comprised 0.04 M benzaldehyde, 2.0 M NH3, and 0.1 M NaOH in 41:9 water:acetonitrile mixture (pH = 13.16(±0.07)). The catholyte was 0.1 M aqueous NaOH catholyte. The overall reactor configuration comprised 15 mL of anolyte and 15 mL of catholyte, separated by Nafion® 117 membrane (size 2.5 cm by 2.5 cm, thickness 0.18 mm). The Nafion® 117 membrane was activated in 0.5 M H2SO4 solution at 80 °C for 2 h and stored in MilliQ water (18.2 MΩ). The working electrode was Co3O4/CuxO electrocatalyst on copper foam (1 × 1 cm2), the counter electrode was a platinum plate, and the reference was an Ag/AgCl (saturated KCl) electrode. Unless otherwise stated, the duration of the electrochemical reaction is 1 h. All electrochemical measurements were performed using the Metrohm Autolab potentiostat PGSTAT204 without iR correction. The cyclic voltammetry (CV) and linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) curves were measured at a scan rate of 5 mV s−1. In situ electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) tests were tested at the potentials of 0.45 to 1.65 V vs. RHE with a frequency range from 10−1 to 105 Hz with an alternating-current amplitude of 10 mV. Electrochemical double-layer capacitance (Cdl) measurement was carried out by CV method with different scan rates in the non-Faradaic region. The Cdl value was obtained from the slopes of the current density–scan rate plots. The 100-h stability test was conducted at 1.35 V vs. RHE, during which portions of the electrolyte were periodically removed for analysis and replaced with fresh electrolyte. In broad applicability tests and the integrated electrosynthesis system starting from other precursors, different precursors were added to the anolyte using a similar electrocatalytic procedure.

The qualitative and quantitative analysis of the products produced in anolyte was determined using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, Agilent 1260) equipped with a DAD detector and an Eclipse Plus C18 column (4.6 × 150 mm). The mobile phase is ultrapure water/acetonitrile (v/v = 70%:30%) with a flow rate of 1.0 ml min−1 and column temperature of 25 °C. The formation of products in anolyte was further ensured by 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR, Bruker AV400SB) with water suppression and liquid chromatography-quadrupole time of flight mass spectrometry (LC-QTOF, Agilent 6545) with 90% ultrapure water and 10% acetonitrile as flowing eluent.

Operando spectroelectrochemical studies

In situ ATR-SEIRAS was conducted using PerkinElmer Frontier FT-IR spectrometer equipped with a mercury cadmium telluride (MCT) detector. The measurements were carried out in a commercial electrochemical cell containing benzaldehyde and NH3 in 0.1 M aqueous NaOH electrolyte. The electrode was CuxO electrocatalyst on an Au film chemically deposited on the reflecting plane of the ATR Si prism. The background spectrum without applied potential was acquired at electrolyte condition before each systemic measurement. In situ spectra were collected under potentials of OCV and 1.15–1.65 V vs. RHE, by averaging 50 scans with a resolution of 4 cm−1 and subtracting the infrared background.

In situ Raman spectra were recorded using a Horiba LabRam Odyssey Nano Raman Spectrometer system with 633 nm laser. The Raman cell was equipped with a working electrode comprising CuxO deposited on carbon paper, an Ag/AgCl reference electrode, and a Pt wire counter electrode. The cell was filled with benzaldehyde and NH3 in 0.1 M NaOH electrolyte. An Olympus N2667700 water immersion objective was submerged in the electrolyte to collect in situ Raman spectra under different electrochemical conditions.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations

All periodic slab calculations were carried out using density functional theory (DFT) as implemented in the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP)35. The Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE)36 exchange-correlation functional within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) was employed, along with projector-augmented wave (PAW)37 potentials and a plane-wave energy cutoff of 500 eV. Dispersion interactions were accounted for using the DFT-D3 method38.

Slab models of CuO(111), Cu2O(111), and Co3O4(311) surfaces were constructed with four atomic layers, where the bottom three layers were fixed during structural optimizations. The CuO(111) slab has lattice parameters a = 17.30 Å, b = 18.95 Å, and c = 28.26 Å, with interaxial angles α = β = 90° and γ = 107.76°. The Cu2O(111) surface adopts a = 18.12 Å, b = 18.12 Å, and c = 31.10 Å, with α = β = 90° and γ = 120.00°. The Co3O4(311) slab exhibits a = 19.85 Å, b = 22.93 Å, and c = 26.31 Å, with α = β = 90° and γ = 106.78°. In all models, the c-axis is oriented normal to the surface plane, and a vacuum region of approximately 20 Å was added along this direction to prevent spurious interactions between periodic images. Brillouin zone sampling was performed using a Γ-centered 1 × 1 × 1 k-point mesh for all adsorption geometry optimizations39. Structural relaxations were conducted until the forces on each atom were below 0.02 eV/Å and the total energy change between successive steps was less than 10−5 eV. To account for the on-site Coulomb interactions in the localized 3d orbitals, the DFT + U method40 was applied with U = 7.0 eV for Cu in CuO41 and U = 4.0 eV for Co in Co3O442.

To rigorously assess the influence of k-point sampling for slabs of this size, we performed a convergence test on the Cu2O model (Supplementary Table 1). Increasing the k-point mesh from 1 × 1 × 1 to 2 × 2 × 1 resulted in only marginal variations (≤0.01 eV) in the computed energy profiles, thereby confirming that Γ-point sampling is sufficient for this lateral cell dimension. On this basis, and given the nearly identical cell sizes across all three systems, the Γ-point (1 × 1 × 1) mesh validated for Cu2O was consistently used for the CuO and Co3O4 slabs in all subsequent calculations.

For all surfaces, only coordinatively unsaturated (CUS) metal sites were considered as potential adsorption sites. Guided by the surface symmetry and the locations of CUS sites, we evaluated three, two, and three distinct adsorption geometries on the CuO(111), Cu2O(111), and Co3O4(311) surfaces, respectively.

The dehydrogenation step was modeled using the CHE approach, where the chemical potential of H+ + e− is referenced to 0.5H231. This method avoids explicitly modeling H adsorption sites. The Gibbs free energy was corrected by including zero-point energy and entropy contributions obtained from vibrational frequency calculations.
