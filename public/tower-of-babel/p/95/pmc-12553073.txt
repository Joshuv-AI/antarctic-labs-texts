Effects of dietary supplementation with selenium in organic form and iodine in dairy goats and their transfer to milk and dairy products
Authors: I Azorín, J Madrid, S Martínez-Miró, MB López, M López, F Hernández
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•High-dose organic selenium plus iodine in goats enriched milk.•With high dietary iodine, milk, cheese, and yogurt qualified as “high in iodine”.•High organic selenium made fresh cheese eligible as a “selenium source”.•Lower iodine doses may still deliver meaningful mineral enrichment.Keywords: Small ruminants, Feed additives, Milk, Dairy products

Introduction

Mineral supplementation of the diets of dairy goats is widely adopted in intensive production systems to promote animal health and prevent disease (Simões et al., 2021). Specifically, selenium (Se) and iodine (I) are essential micronutrients often deficient in soils and crops globally, with health implications for both humans and animals (Dijck-Brouwer et al., 2022). Both are important minerals that contribute to the maintenance of critical vital functions, since selenium contributes to enzymatic activity and selenoproteins synthesis (Amin et al., 2022), whereas iodine is a key component of thyroid hormones (Bertinato, 2021). Additionally, selenium also protects the thyroid gland through its antioxidant role and supports thyroid hormone metabolism (Köhrle, 2023).

Beyond animal welfare, dietary mineral supplementation offers an opportunity to enrich animal-derived products, thereby mitigating nutritional deficiencies in the human population (Aghwan et al., 2016; Liu et al, 2020). In this context, supplementation with minerals such as selenium and iodine in the diet of dairy cattle is increasingly recognised because milk is an effective medium for mineral transfer (Nikooyeh & Neyestani, 2017; Anastasova et al., 2020). Among livestock, dairy goats are increasingly recognised globally for producing milk with valuable nutritional, nutraceutical, and functional properties (Rai et al., 2022). A notable characteristic of goat milk is its higher proportion of short- and medium-chain fatty acids and smaller fat globule size, making it a valuable alternative to cow milk due to improved digestibility (Almášiová et al., 2023). Organic selenium sources, such as selenised yeast, are more effective for increasing selenium concentrations in milk due to their better transfer and absorption within the body. Organic selenium is rapidly incorporated into milk proteins (Ling et al., 2017). Meanwhile, iodine is actively concentrated and secreted by the mammary gland (Bertinato, 2021), making it a favourable candidate for enrichment in milk and dairy products.

It is important to emphasize that prolonged dietary intake of excessive amounts of selenium or iodine has been associated with toxicity in both animals and humans, resulting in infertility, neurological disorders, and thyroid dysfunction (Kieliszek & Błażejak, 2016; Bertinato, 2021). In ruminants, dietary selenium levels ranging from 5 to 8 mg/kg DM have been identified as potentially harmful (Arshad et al., 2020), whereas iodine exerts toxic effects at doses around 50 mg/kg DM; however, this threshold is influenced by several factors, including the higher tolerance observed in dairy cattle due to the iodine excretion through milk (Underwood & Suttle, 1999). In humans, Upper Intake Levels (ULs) for selenium and iodine have been established to prevent potential toxic effects associated with excessive intake. According to EFSA (2023), the UL for selenium is set at 255 μg/day for adults, including pregnant and lactating women. For iodine, the Scientific Committee on Food (SCF, 2006) established a UL of 600 μg/day for the same population group. In children, the ULs for both micronutrients are extrapolated from adult values based on body weight. Thus, while the enrichment of animal-derived foods with selenium and iodine may serve as an effective strategy to address dietary deficiencies, responsible consumption remains essential to ensure consumer safety.

Several studies have been conducted in dairy goats to evaluate the effects of supplementing diets with different forms and concentrations of selenium, particularly in the context of its transfer to milk and dairy products (Pechová et al., 2008; Petrera et al., 2009; Tozzi et al., 2016). Some studies have also explored the potential of selenium, either alone or combined with other micronutrients, such as vitamin E, to enhance milk production and quality (Tufarelli & Laudadio, 2011; de Vasconcelos et al., 2023; Barcelos et al., 2023). Similarly, iodine supplementation in the diet of dairy goats has been investigated to increase iodine concentration in milk (Nudda et al., 2009). However, the limited studies available on the combined supplementation of both minerals for milk enrichment have primarily focused on improving the growth of goat kids, often administering both minerals in bolus form rather than as additives in the feed (Rashnoo et al., 2020). To our knowledge, no studies to date have investigated the combined enrichment of selenium and iodine in goat dairy products through dietary supplementation. In this regard, some research has examined the simultaneous supplementation of selenium and iodine in goat diets, but these have mainly assessed the effects on growth performance, blood haematology, thyroid hormone levels, antioxidant status (Qin et al., 2011; Aghwan et al., 2013), and carcass characteristics (Aghwan et al., 2016). Therefore, it is necessary to evaluate how the combined supplementation of both microminerals at high doses could impact not only the physiological aspects of the animal, but also the quantity of these in milk and its derived products, such as cheese or yogurt, which may have important implications for human nutrition and health (Amin et al., 2022; Niero et al., 2023).

We hypothesised that dietary supplementation with selenium at concentrations approaching the legal upper limit established by the European Union, together with iodine at the maximum authorised level, may positively influence the health and performance of goats and support the production of dairy goat products enriched with both trace minerals, thus meeting labelling requirements with the possibility of offering value-added dairy products to the market. Accordingly, this study aimed to assess how supplementation with 0.46 mg of selenium, partially supplied as organic form, and 5.68 mg of iodine per kilogram of dry matter (DM) in the diet of dairy goats affects the overall composition and quality of fresh milk, cheese and yogurt, as well as its effects on the productive performance and metabolic status of the animals.

Materials and methods

All the procedures in this study were approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Murcia (protocol no. A13170805), in accordance with the Directive 2010/63/EU (European Union 2010) and the University of Murcia Code of Good Research Practice (Murcia, Spain).

Study design and sampling procedures

The trial was conducted over 64 days on a commercial farm operated by Alimentos del Mediterráneo, S. Coop. (ALIMER), located in Lorca (Murcia, Spain). A total of 140 multiparous Murciano–Granadina goats in mid-lactation with homogeneous body weight (47.88 kg ± 5.92 kg) and milk yield (1.84 kg ± 0.72 kg/day), were randomly assigned to two experimental groups (n = 70 per group). Animals were housed in two separate straw-bedded pens with free access to drinking water.

Two total mixed rations (TMRs) were formulated with alfalfa hay and a concentrate mixture (Table 1), differing only in selenium and iodine content. The control diet (CON) included 0.23 mg of inorganic selenium and 0.57 mg of iodine per kg (DM). Meanwhile, the experimental diet (SeI) included 0.46 mg of selenium (0.23 mg inorganic + 0.23 mg organic) and 5.68 mg of iodine per kg (DM). These concentrations reflect the complete diet composition (12% moisture) and comply with European Union regulatory limits.

Individual body weight and milk production were measured at the start of the trial (day 0) and at its conclusion (day 64). Goats were milked twice a day (08:00 h and 16:00 h) using an iodine-free post-milking teat disinfectant. For chemical composition and somatic cell count (SCC), 150 mL of individual milk were collected, while an additional 100 mL were frozen at ˗20 °C for mineral analysis (Ca, P, Se, and I). These milk samples were obtained on days 0, 42, and 64. Bulk tank milk (20 L) from each group was collected on days 49, 56, and 63 for cheese and yogurt production.

Blood sampling was performed on days 0 and 64 from 10 goats per group via jugular venipuncture after morning milking. Two lithium-heparinised VACUETTE® tubes were collected per goat (Greiner Bio-One International GmbH, Kremsmünster, Austria): one for whole blood mineral (Ca, P, and Se) and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) assays, and the other for plasma metabolic profile and thyroxine (T4) analysis (plasma stored at ˗80°C). Lastly, one EDTA VACUETTE® tube (Greiner Bio-One International GmbH) was used for haemoglobin (Hb) analysis in whole blood.

Production of dairy products

Fresh cheese and yogurt were produced at the Food Technology Area, University of Murcia. Milk was pasteurised in a plate heat exchanger (Redefine Food Solutions, S.L., Bullas, Murcia, Spain) at 75 °C for 20 s (cheese) or 95 °C for 240 s (yogurt).

Cheese was prepared in a double-zero tank (Pierre Guerin Technologies, Mauzé, France), with 10 L of pasteurised milk per batch. At 33–34 °C, 0.3 mL/kg of anhydrous CaCl2 (Chr. Hansen, France) and 0.3 mL/kg of calf rennet (80% chymosin, 145 IMCU/mL) (Caglio Star España, S.A., Murcia, Spain) were added. After 40 min rest, the curd was cut (25% speed, 3 min), rested (5 min), salted (80 g), stirred (1 min), rested (10 min), stirred again (1 min), and drained. The curd was placed in perforated moulds and refrigerated at 4 °C for 24 h without pressing. Four blocks of cheese (400–500 g each) were obtained per batch and treatment, which were divided proportionally to determine the physicochemical composition, texture, and sensory evaluation.

Yogurt was prepared in a stainless-steel vat by mixing 1 L of pasteurised milk at 95 °C with 100 g of milk powder and 20 g of sugar. After mixing well and tempering to 45 °C, 1 g/100 L of YOMIX-300 starter culture (Streptococcus thermophilus and Lactobacillus bulgaricus, Chr-Hansen, Madrid, Spain) was added uniformly. The mixture was dispensed into plastic cups with lids and incubated at 45 °C for 4 h, then refrigerated at 4 °C. Six yogurts per treatment were produced per batch—three were used for sensory testing and three for the physicochemical analysis.

Analytical methods

Analysis of TMRs

To analyse the TMRs, samples were dried in an oven at 60 °C for 48 h and milled to a 1 mm size with a Retsch ZM 200 Ultra Centrifugal Mill (Retsch, Hann, Germany). Crude protein (CP), ether extract (EE), and ash content were analysed according to the Association of Official Analytical Chemists (AOAC, 2006) methods: AOAC 2001.11 for CP, AOAC 920.39 for EE, and AOAC 942.05 for ash. Starch content was determined via the polarimetry technique according to the methods of Commission Regulation (EC) No. 152/2009 (European Commission 2009). Neutral detergent fibre and acid detergent fibre were analysed per Van Soest et al. (1991), and acid detergent lignin was determined gravimetrically after 72% sulphuric acid treatment.

Mineral content of samples (Ca, P, Se, and I)

Samples were hydrolysed with HNO3 in a microwave digestion system (Milestone Ethos X Microwave, Sorisole, Italy). Ca, P, and Se were analysed in TMR, whole blood, fresh milk, fresh cheese, and yogurt samples. Minerals were quantified by inductively coupled plasma–mass spectrometry (ICP–MS; Agilent 7900, Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) using the standard addition method. Iodine content was measured in TMR, fresh milk, fresh cheese, and yogurt samples using a modified Meyer et al. (2008) method. Sample matrices were disintegrated using a 0.5% ammonium solution before measuring in the ICP–MS equipment.

Biochemical analysis and GSH-Px blood assay

Biochemical parameters, including cholesterol, triglycerides (TGs), glucose, urea, and total proteins, were analysed in plasma using an automatic chemistry analyser (Olympus AU600, Olympus Diagnostica Europe GmbH, Ireland) and Beckman kits (Beckman Coulter Inc., Fullerton, USA). Cholesterol was quantified using the cholesterol dehydrogenase method. TGs were hydrolysed using a combination of microbial lipases to produce glycerol and fatty acids. Glucose was measured using the hexokinase G-6-PDH method. Urea was calculated using the enzymatic method adapted from Talke and Schubert (1965), and total proteins were quantified using the adapted Weichselbaum (1946) method. The total thyroxine (T4) levels were determined using a chemiluminescence immunoassay (Immulite 1000 Immunoassay System, Siemens Medical Solutions Diagnostics, Deerfield, USA).

GSH-Px content in whole blood samples was assessed using the Ransel test kit (Randox Laboratories Ltd., Crumlin, UK) following the method described by Paglia and Valentine (1967). Hb was analysed using an Advia 120 haematological analyser (Siemens Healthcare Diagnostics SL, Spain).

Analytical procedures in goat fresh milk and dairy products (cheese and yogurt)

SCC analysis was performed using fresh milk samples following the manufacturer’s FOSS method with a FossomaticTM 5000 instrument (Foss Electric, Hillerød, Denmark). Fresh milk, and yogurt samples were analysed for chemical composition via infrared spectroscopy using the MilkoScan FT 6000 system (Foss Electric). The Standard 141B:1996 of the International Dairy Federation (IDF, 1996) was applied to determine CP, EE, lactose, and non-fat solids. The chemical composition of cheese was determined according to IDF standards: IDF Standard 4:2004 (International Dairy Federation (IDF) 2004) for dry extract (using 3 g ± 10 mg grated samples), IDF Standard 5:2004 for EE content (International Dairy Federation (IDF) 2004), and IDF Standard 25:2008 for CP content (International Dairy Federation (IDF) 2008) using the by Kjeldahl method. pH of milk, fresh cheese, and yogurt samples was determined using a Crison® pH meter (micro–pH 2001, Barcelona, Spain). For cheese, 5 g ± 0.1 mg of grated samples was pre-crushed for 10 min in 30 mL of distilled water prior to pH measurement. Syneresis in yogurt was assessed by centrifuging 50 g of sample at 3000 rpm for 20 min and measuring the released whey. Cheese yield was expressed as the number of kilograms of cheese obtained per 100 kg of milk. Water activity in cheese was determined using a Novasina Thermoconstanter (TH-2, RTD-200 model, Novasina AG, Switzerland), with measurements performed in triplicate at 25 °C room temperature. The color and lightness of fresh cheese and yogurt were measured in triplicate using a CR–400 MINOLTA colorimeter (Konica Minolta Sensing, Ramsey, USA), with results expressed as CIELab values: a* (redness), b* (yellowness), and L* (lightness).

Texture analysis of fresh goat cheese

Texture profile analysis of cheese was performed using a TA-XT Plus Texture Analyser (Stable Micro Systems Ltd., Godalming, UK) fitted with a 500 N load cell and controlled with Exponent Stable Micro System software (version 5.1.1.0) to process the results. The analysis was performed in triplicate using rindless, cube-shaped cheese samples (3 × 3 × 3 cm) at room temperature (20 °C). Each sample underwent two consecutive compressions to a depth of 15 mm at compression speed of 1 mm/s. The following texture parameters were calculated, as described by Bourne (1978): hardness (expressed as N), cohesiveness (dimensionless), gumminess (hardness x cohesiveness, N), elasticity (mm), chewiness (gumminess × elasticity, N mm), and adhesiveness (N s).

Sensory evaluation of dairy products

Sensory evaluation was conducted by 10 expert assessors for each batch of cheese and yogurt produced. Cheese samples were evaluated on a 9-point scale for intensity, assessing the following parameters: odor, cream odor, goat milk odor, goat milk flavor, cream flavor, salty flavor, other flavors, consistency, graininess, juiciness, fatty nature, and overall acceptability. Meanwhile, yogurt samples were evaluated using a 10-point scale for the following parameters: consistency, odor, acid odor, goat milk odor, acid flavor, goat milk flavor, cremosity, metallic flavor, astringency, other flavors, and overall acceptability. All cheese and yogurt samples were randomly coded and presented in standardised portions—1 cm-thick slices for cheese and plastic cups for yogurt.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics software (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of data distributions. Non-normally distributed variables were subjected to logarithmic transformation prior to further analysis. A general linear model with repeated measures was employed to evaluate body weight, milk production, and parameters from whole blood, plasma, and milk composition. In this model, treatment type (CON or SeI) was used as the inter-subject factor and measurement time points as the intra-subject factor. For milk chemical composition, Tukey’s multiple comparison test was applied to assess pairwise differences across three times within each diet. The physicochemical and sensory characteristics of cheese and yogurt were analysed using a mixed model procedure within the same statistical software, incorporating treatment type as a fixed effect and the production batch as a random effect. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Animal performance was not affected by dietary supplementation with iodine and organic selenium (Table 3). No variations were observed in body weight or milk yield between groups at the end of the trial (p > 0.05).

For mineral milk composition, a diet effect on selenium and iodine content were found (p < 0.001), with enriched diet showing higher values than the control diet. In addition, the time factor also had an effect, increasing selenium and iodine levels in milk as the feeding period progressed (p < 0.05), and also a diet × time interaction was detected for both selenium and iodine (p < 0.001). Thus, selenium and iodine concentrations were substantially higher in the SeI group, reaching 3.93 µg Se/100 g and 98 µg I/100 g by day 64. At day 42, however, the concentrations of these microminerals in the supplemented group were already comparable to those at day 64 and higher than at day 0.

Regarding the other minerals analysed, a day effect was observed for calcium and phosphorous levels (p < 0.001), generally finding lower concentrations at the beginning of the test.

Mineral supplementation with organic selenium and high-dose iodine did not influence the nutritional composition of cheese, pH, cheese yield, or water activity. However, the cheese lightness (L*) parameter was slightly higher in the SeI group (p < 0.05). Selenium content nearly doubled (16.0 µg/100 g vs. 7.56 µg/100 g), while iodine content was approximately five times higher compared to the CON group (99.4 µg/100 g vs. 20.1 µg/100 g). The texture profile of the cheese was not influenced by organic selenium and iodine supplementation for any of the parameters evaluated (p > 0.05).

Discussion

The National Research Council (2007) recommends a dietary selenium intake of 0.3 mg/kg in growing and lactating goats. For iodine, the recommended concentration is 0.5 mg/kg for growing and non-lactating goats, increasing to 0.8 mg/kg for lactating goats. In our study, the diet was supplemented with these trace minerals within the maximum levels permitted in feed under European Union legislation, set at 0.5 mg/kg selenium (with a maximum of 0.2 mg/kg organic selenium) and 5 mg/kg iodine.

Animal performance

Selenium and iodine are essential trace elements that strongly influence animal health and productivity (Rashnoo et al., 2020). In addition to other relevant functions, the supply of these minerals is crucial for maintaining adequate milk production (Niero et al., 2023). From our results, no change in milk yield was observed following combined dietary supplementation with iodine and organic selenium. In addition, no changes in body weight were detected, and animals showed no signs of disease or poor health throughout the test period. Notably, studies assessing the effects of combined dietary selenium and iodine supplementation at moderate-to-high levels on goat performance are scarce. Rashnoo et al. (2020) reported that daily intraruminal bolus supplementation with a moderate level of 0.25 mg selenium combined with 0.40 mg iodine improved milk yield in goats compared with the non-supplemented group. However, individual supplementation of selenium or iodine also improved milk production to the same extent. Consistent with our results, other studies that combined selenium and iodine supplementation in cows showed no effect on milk production levels (Azorín et al., 2024).

General blood metabolic profile and blood minerals

The general metabolic profile of blood was unaffected by organic selenium and high-dose iodine supplementation. Nevertheless, blood urea levels increased at the end of the experiment in the CON and SeI groups. These levels were elevated (> 40 mg/dL) compared to the average levels reported by Posada et al. (2012) in dairy goats (10.5 mg/dL to 27.6 mg/dL), which may be attributed to a slightly elevated protein content in the diets (Akhtar et al., 2020).

In our study, dietary supplementation with a higher overall selenium content, through the addition of organic selenium, positively influenced the selenium level in blood after 64 days, increasing by 22.8% compared to the CON group. Several studies have reported similar results using organic selenium in the diets of dairy goats (Weiss, 2003; Weiss and Hogan, 2005; Barcelos et al., 2023; de Vasconcelos et al., 2023). However, some studies have indicated that blood selenium levels may increase similarly, regardless of whether the dietary selenium source is organic or inorganic (Petrera et al., 2009; Pavlata et al., 2011; Kachuee et al., 2014). Bickhardt et al., (1999) considered 80 µg Se/L to be threshold value for selenium deficiency in goat whole blood, and Pavlata et al. (2005) set it at 100 µg Se/L. The initial selenium levels in our trial were 283.5 µg Se/L and 284.4 µg Se/L for the CON and SeI groups, respectively, indicating an adequate baseline selenium status. In animals with adequate selenium status, supplementation typically does not result in significant changes in blood selenium levels (Pavlata et al., 2011), which may explain the lack of immediate response.

Determining GSH-Px activity in blood is considered more accurate for assessing selenium status in the body since blood selenium concentration does not always reflect the mineral content of the entire organism (Pavlata et al., 2011). Selenium is a key component of many selenoproteins, including GSH-Px, and the activity level of this enzyme provides information about the selenium and antioxidant status of the body (Shi et al., 2018). The increase in GSH-Px activity in blood following dietary supplementation with organic selenium in goats aligns with some previous reports (Pavlata et al., 2011; Shi et al., 2018). However, other studies have shown that both organic and inorganic selenium sources are similarly effective in increasing GSH-Px levels (Aspila, 1991). In our case, iodine supplementation also did not interfere with achieving a higher activity of this enzyme, contrary to the findings of Qin et al. (2011), who reported a significant decrease in GSH-Px when dietary iodine was supplied at 4.0 mg I/kg.

One of primary roles of selenium and iodine is to ensure the proper function of the thyroid gland, which stores the most significant amount of both minerals (Lossow et al., 2019; Köhrle, 2023). Analysis of serum thyroxine hormone (T4) level is a common method used to diagnose iodine deficiency in goats (Clark et al., 1998; Naveen et al., 2024), and the evaluation of blood selenium levels is also relevant in this context, as selenium is essential for the conversion of T4 into the active form, triiodothyronine (T3). In our study, the initial T4 hormone concentration in the CON and SeI groups of goats were 3.94 μg/dL and 4.61 μg/dL, respectively. These values are within the normal ranges reported in previous research (Lucaroni et al., 1989; Kumar et al., 1994; Paulíková et al., 2011). T4 levels increased slightly in both groups at the end of the trial (4.24 µg/dL and 4.76 µg/dL for CON and SeI groups, respectively), with no significant effect of iodine and selenium supplementation. Thyroid hormone concentrations in ruminants are not easily affected by short-term changes in selenium or iodine intake (Hemingway et al., 2001; Guyot et al., 2011). However, some studies in goats have reported higher T4 levels following dietary supplementation with iodine or selenium around 50 days of testing (Qin et al., 2011; Shi et al., 2018), or with longer trial duration in the case of iodine (Wichtel et al., 1996). In addition, incremental moderate iodine levels in dairy goats for eight weeks have been reported to increase circulating T3 and the T3 /T4 ratio, while improving insulin sensitivity and reducing blood urea nitrogen (Nudda et al., 2013). Thus, these endocrine changes could be accompanied by modifications in energy- and protein-related blood metabolites, suggesting an impact on metabolic status. In our study, despite a high-iodine level was used in the supplemented diet, no negative consequences were observed in this sense; although, a larger number of animals may have been required to show possible metabolic effects.

Similarly, long-term deficiencies are typically required to observe remarkable effects, since ruminants efficiently store iodine and selenium in their thyroid gland and maintain the thyroid concentration levels over extended periods (Guyot et al., 2011; Köhrle, 2023; Naveen et al., 2024). Considering the complexity and multifactorial regulation of thyroid hormones metabolism, extended study periods may be necessary to assess the full impact of selenium and iodine supplementation on thyroid function (Qin et al., 2011). Meanwhile, EFSA (2025) indicates that the maximum iodine levels currently permitted under European Union legislation are considered safe for all target species, including goats. Part of the excess iodine is usually excreted via urine and faeces, while in dairy animals, iodine is also transferred to milk, which could affect human intake if these products exceed tolerable limits.

Milk quality and composition

In addition to its nutritional benefits, selenium plays a key role in udder health, as it enhancing the function of mammary gland defence cells (Arshad et al., 2020), and is commonly used to prevent mastitis in dairy herds (Enjalbert et al., 2006). In our study, selenium supplementation did not affect the SCC in milk. However, some studies have reported a reduction in SCC following supplementation with organic selenium in ruminant diets (de Vasconcelos et al., 2023). Iodine supplementation also did not influence SCC levels, consistent with Nudda et al. (2009), who revealed no changes in SCC in goats following dietary supplementation with 0.9 mg I/goat/day.

The overall physicochemical profile of milk was unaffected by organic selenium and iodine supplementation, except for a reduction in lactose content in the SeI group. The selenium and iodine concentrations in milk increased significantly in response to the supplemented diet. Sampling time during the experiment also influenced the chemical composition of milk due to physiological changes associated with different stages of lactation at the time of sampling for most parameters (Auldist et al., 1995). Significantly, selenium and iodine contents in milk were affected by a significant interaction between diet and sampling time for these trace minerals. This confirms that the ration supplemented with organic selenium and high iodine levels increased their concentrations in milk sampled at 64 days. Numerous studies have demonstrated a direct relationship between the intake of these minerals and their concentrations in milk (Aspila, 1991; Nudda et al., 2009; Moschini et al., 2010; Bagnicka et al., 2017; Barbé et al., 2020; Sun et al., 2021; Azorín et al., 2024). The choice of the selenium source is also an important consideration when supplementing the diet (Barbé et al., 2020), since organic sources are more bioavailable to the organism than inorganic ones. Selenomethionine from selenised yeast is readily incorporated into milk proteins by substituting for methionine during protein synthesis in the mammary gland (Pehrson, 1993; Petrera et al., 2009; Gong et al., 2014; Picciotti et al., 2021; Barcelos et al., 2023). Consequently, as expected, our experiment showed that the milk from the SeI group contained 1.73 times more selenium at day 64 compared to the start of the trial, with more than four times more iodine.

Although recommended daily intake varies, selenium and iodine guidelines generally recommend 55 µg Se/day (Institute of Medicine, 2000) and 150 µg I/day (EFSA, 2014) for adults. The peak values (expressed in volume) in our study in the SeI group (4.07 µg Se/100 mL and 107.3 µg I/100 mL) indicate that 100 mL of enriched milk would provide 7 % and 72 % of the respective daily intake values. According to the UE Regulation 1169/2011 (European Union 2011), beverages may be labelled as a ‘source of’ a nutrient if they provide at least 7.5 % of the recommended intake per 100 mL, and ‘high content’ if they provide at least twice the amount. Therefore, the enriched milk from the SeI group at day 64 qualifies as a ‘source of iodine’ and nearly a ‘source of selenium’ while clearly meeting the criteria for ‘high iodine content’. Notably, the iodine level in 100 mL of enriched milk at the end of the study represents 18% of the tolerable upper daily intake of 600 µg I/day in humans (Scientific Committee on Food, 2006).

Cheese and yogurt

Dairy products represent a suitable category of food to be fortified with essential micronutrients due to the structure and composition of their matrices (Picciotti et al., 2021). However, scientific data on trace element concentrations in dairy products is limited, particularly from goat milk (Slačanac, 2011; Almášiová et al., 2023).

In our study, fresh cheese did not show changes in physicochemical characteristics following dietary supplementation with organic selenium and high-dose iodine, consistent with previous findings in goats and cows (Pechová et al., 2008; Ling et al., 2017; Ianni et al., 2019; Azorín et al., 2024). The only exception was a slight increase in lightness (L*), which was higher in cheese from the SeI group but did not visibly affect its appearance. Minerals are partially retained in the curd during cheese-making, associated in different proportions with the colloidal suspension of casein micelles (Hernández-Ledesma et al., 2011), with selenomethionine incorporated non-specifically (Phipps et al., 2008; Ling et al., 2017). In our study, cheese from the SeI group contained 16.0 µg Se/100 g compared to 7.56 µg Se/100 g in the CON group, demonstrating that supplementation of dairy goat diets with selenised yeast was effective in increasing selenium levels in milk and cheese. The selenium content in cheese was four times higher than in milk (from 3.93 µg/100 g to 16.0 µg/100 g). Aligning with our results, Tozzi et al. (2016) concluded that supplementation with selenised yeast enhances the selenium content of dairy goat products, observing the highest average concentration of selenium in cheese (35.3 µg Se/100 g) when the diet was supplemented with 0.14 mg Se/kg. In contrast, Pechová et al. (2008) reported no selenium increase in milk or dairy products following long-term organic selenium supplementation (with the lactate-protein complex at a dose of 0.94 mg Se/kg), likely due to a saturation of the selenium level requirements in the organism or different absorption mechanisms of the selenium source. Additionally, many studies in dairy cows have shown effective selenium transfer to dairy products when organic selenium sources are used in dietary supplementation (Phipps et al., 2008; Liu et al., 2015; Ling et al., 2017; Ianni et al., 2019; Azorín et al., 2020; Azorín et al., 2024). Meanwhile, fresh cheese may be more suitable for iodine enrichment than aged cheese, as it contains more whey, where iodine is predominantly concentrated (Moschini et al., 2010; van der Reijden 2017; Niero, 2023). However, processing factors, such as high temperatures or fine curd cutting, can lead to iodine losses (Norouzian, 2011; van der Reijden 2017). In our study, cheese from the high-iodine SeI group contained 99.4 µg I/100 g compared to 20.1 µg I/100 g in the CON group, confirming effective mineral transfer from the diet to the fresh cheeses.

Dietary supplementation with organic selenium and high-dose iodine also did not change the physicochemical characteristics of yogurt. The higher mineral content in yogurt compared to milk is mainly due to the increase in total solids during the manufacturing process (Almášiová et al., 2023). In this study, a significant increase in iodine concentration was observed in the SeI group compared to the CON group (106 µg I/100 g vs. 24.4 µg I/100 g). Although the selenium concentration was numerically higher in the SeI group (4.90 µg Se/100g) than in the CON group (3.59 µg Se/100 g) this difference was not statistically significant.

Under the European Union nutritional claims for minerals (Regulation 1169/2011), solid food must contain at least 15% of the nutrient reference value per 100 g to be labelled as a ‘source of’ or 30% for ‘high in’. The enriched fresh cheese from the SeI group met the criteria for ‘source of selenium’ (≥8.25 µg/100 g), ‘source of iodine’ (≥22.50 µg/100 g), and ‘high in iodine’ (≥45 µg/100 g), and nearly reached the threshold for ‘high in selenium’ (16 µg/100 g vs. the required 16.5 µg/100 g). Yogurt, prepared from the milk of the SeI group could be labelled as a ‘source of iodine’ and ‘high in iodine’, with no statement for selenium. Consumption of 50 g of enriched cheese from the SeI group would provide 14.5% and 33.1% of the recommended daily selenium and iodine in the adult population, respectively. Following the same conditions, 50 g of enriched yogurt would provide 35.3 % of the iodine requirement. These findings suggest that moderate iodine supplementation could effectively enrich milk as well as cheese and yogurt, thereby producing products better aligned with typical dietary patterns.

It is necessary to highlight that excessive intake of selenium or iodine can pose health risks, as both elements have well-defined upper intake limits beyond which toxicity may occur (EFSA, 2023; SCF, 2006). Although their presence in milk and dairy products can contribute positively to nutritional requirements, uncontrolled consumption may lead to imbalances. Therefore, informing consumers about the concentrations of these elements is crucial to promote awareness and ensure a responsible intake. This approach helps safeguard health by minimizing the risk of deficiencies as well as harmful excesses.

On the other hand, sensory analysis of the finished products is critical to guarantee consumer acceptability in real market conditions. To ensure consumer acceptance, supplementation should not compromise the physicochemical properties and sensory characteristics of cheese and yogurt (Picciotti et al., 2021). In this study, the lack of differences between cheese samples from both groups is a positive result, indicating that dietary supplementation and further processing did not introduce undesirable characteristics that could affect cheese quality. For yogurt samples, the higher odor, acid odor and cremosity observed in the SeI group did not negatively impact overall consumer acceptability.

Finally, texture analysis of cheese samples was also performed. Cheese texture is widely recognised as one of the most critical parameters for determining the quality and identity of cheese type, and many studies have focused on improving methods for its assessment (Benedito et al., 2000). In our study, dietary supplementation in goats exhibited no effect on the texture parameters of the fresh cheese, consistent with the results of previous studies conducted in dairy cows (Azorín et al., 2020; Azorín et al., 2024). Our findings align with those of Liu et al. (2015), who reported no changes in the texture profile of mozzarella cheese following dietary supplementation of selenised yeast (0.3 mg Se/kg) in cows.

In any case, further long-term studies are required to assess the potential effects on possible changes acceptability properties of animal products. In addition, while the enrichment effect is evident, future studies should also consider the economic perspective, since Madududu et al. (2024) reported that consumers may be willing to pay up to 25% more for dairy products with health-related attributes, which supports the market potential of micromineral-enriched animal products.

Conclusions

Dietary supplementation of organic selenium in combination with iodine in dairy goats resulted in adequate enrichment of selenium and iodine in milk and dairy products. Iodine was the most efficiently transferred mineral to milk, cheese, and yogurt at the end of the trial, with all products qualifying for the nutritional claim ‘high in iodine’. Fresh cheese from the SeI group could also be labelled as a ‘source of selenium’. Although selenium transfer to yogurt was not as substantial, an excellent mineral enrichment was achieved for iodine. No adverse effects of supplementation on animal health and productivity were observed. The iodine concentrations found in the milk and dairy products suggest that lower levels of dietary iodine supplementation would be sufficient for meaningful enrichment. However, to improve the evidence found in this study, as well as the limited scientific information available in dairy goats, future studies exploring different levels of selenium and iodine supplementation, as well as longer-term trials under commercial conditions, are warranted. Such studies would help clarify their effects on productivity, animal health, and the nutritional and functional properties of milk and dairy products. The naturally enriched dairy products produced in this study may represent a commercially valuable dietary option for increasing daily selenium and iodine intake among the general population, and especially in people at risk of deficiency in these microminerals. In any case, animal products enriched with these microminerals should be consumed responsibly, in order to maximize their nutritional benefits while avoiding potential health risks associated with excessive intake.
