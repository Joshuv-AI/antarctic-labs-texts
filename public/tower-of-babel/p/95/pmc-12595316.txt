Development and reliability and validity test of the body image and social function scale for patients after thyroid cancer surgery
Authors: Qingqing Diao, Jieyu Lu, Sailei Shi, Jing Yang, Yating Song, Yong Wang, Zejuan Gu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to develop and validate a thyroid cancer-specific instrument to assess body image and social functioning in patients after thyroidectomy.MethodsUsing a multi-stage approach guided by Cash's Body Image Disturbance Theory and Leary's Social Avoidance-Distress Model, we developed the Thyroid Cancer Body Image and Social Impact Scale (TC-BISIS). Item generation involved literature review, semi-structured interviews with 18 thyroid cancer survivors, expert consultation (n = 15), and preliminary testing (n ​= ​30). Psychometric properties were evaluated in 686 thyroid cancer patients with a mean age of 39.397 ​± ​12.278 years and approximately 73.40% female divided into two samples: Sample 1 (n ​= ​321) for item analysis and exploratory factor analysis (EFA), and Sample 2 (n ​= ​365) for confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and longitudinal measurement assessment at one, two, and three months post-surgery. Measurement invariance across gender was also assessed.ResultsEFA identified a five-factor structure explaining 62.43% of variance: Scar Cognition and Emotional Response, Voice Change Experience, Social Avoidance Behavior, Social Role Function Impact, and Intimate Relationship Impact. CFA confirmed excellent model fit (CFI ​= ​0.987, TLI ​= ​0.986, RMSEA ​= ​0.018, SRMR ​= ​0.039). The 30-item TC-BISIS demonstrated good internal consistency (α ​= ​0.866–0.886), strong criterion validity with the Body Image Scale (r ​= ​0.809), and measurement invariance across three time points and between male and female patients.ConclusionsThe TC-BISIS is the first validated instrument specifically addressing body image and social functioning in thyroid cancer survivors. Its strong psychometric properties support its utility in clinical practice for identifying patients requiring targeted psychosocial interventions and in research for evaluating intervention effectiveness. The scale enables comprehensive assessment of thyroid-specific concerns that are often overlooked in this population frequently labeled as having a “good cancer.”Keywords: Thyroid cancer, Body image, Social functioning, Psychometric properties, Scale development

Introduction

Thyroid cancer is the most common endocrine malignancy globally, with an increasing incidence rate, especially among younger populations and women.1,2 Ranking as the ninth most common cancer worldwide, thyroid cancer presents a unique profile among malignancies due to its relatively favorable prognosis coupled with significant quality-of-life implications. The global age-standardized incidence rate has nearly tripled in the past three decades, with particularly concerning trends among individuals under 45 years of age.3 This dramatic increase is attributed to multiple factors, including enhanced detection through advanced imaging technologies, increased surveillance, environmental exposures, hormonal factors affecting women disproportionately, and changing diagnostic criteria.4 Advances in early diagnosis and surgical treatment have significantly improved survival rates, with 5-year survival exceeding 98% for localized disease, making postoperative quality of life an increasingly critical aspect of patient care.5,6

Body image distress (BID), defined as distress and dissatisfaction arising from altered physical appearance, is prevalent among cancer survivors who have undergone surgical interventions, significantly impacting psychological well-being and social interactions.7,8 Specifically, patients recovering from thyroid cancer surgery frequently experience multifaceted physical alterations that extend beyond visible scarring and altered neck contours. These changes include voice disturbances (affecting 25–87% of patients), swallowing difficulties, neck tightness, shoulder dysfunction, and sensory changes around the surgical site.9 Such alterations can profoundly impact patients’ self-perception and social engagement, particularly considering that thyroid cancer disproportionately affects younger women, a demographic group in which physical appearance often carries significant psychological and social implications.10

While surgical intervention remains the primary treatment for most thyroid cancer patients, it is important to acknowledge that this population may experience additional appearance and functional changes beyond those directly related to surgery. These can include weight fluctuations, hair thinning, skin changes, and fatigue resulting from thyroid hormone replacement therapy or radioactive iodine treatment.11 Additionally, some patients report cognitive changes (“brain fog”) and mood alterations that may affect social functioning and self-perception.12 For patients requiring more aggressive treatments in cases of advanced disease, side effects from external beam radiation therapy or systemic therapies may further impact physical appearance and functionality. These diverse treatment-related changes, though sometimes less visible than surgical alterations, nonetheless contribute to the complex body image concerns experienced by thyroid cancer survivors.

The psychological impact of these physical changes is substantial, with research indicating that some thyroid cancer survivors report persistent concerns about their appearance and functional changes that can last 5–10 years or even longer after successful treatment, with a significant proportion of survivors continuing to experience body image distress and functional concerns during long-term follow-up.13,14 Importantly, these body image distress frequently transcend mere cosmetic concerns, manifesting as diminished self-confidence, sexual difficulties, workplace challenges, and social isolation.15 Despite growing recognition of these issues, targeted interventions and comprehensive assessments of BID and social functioning in this patient group remain limited, creating a significant gap in holistic cancer care.16,17

Studies addressing body image among cancer survivors have predominantly focused on breast cancer and certain head and neck cancer populations (particularly those involving facial structures and oral cavity), with significantly less attention devoted specifically to thyroid cancer patients despite their unique post-treatment challenges.18,19 This disparity is particularly concerning given that thyroid cancer survivors often face a unique stigma of having an “easy” or “good” cancer due to favorable survival statistics, potentially minimizing their psychosocial concerns and reducing supportive care opportunities.20 Research clearly highlights that altered body images can lead to anxiety, depression, social withdrawal, reduced intimacy, occupational difficulties, and diminished quality of life.21

Existing measurement tools demonstrate significant limitations when applied to thyroid cancer survivors. General instruments such as the Body Image Scale (BIS) and the Multidimensional Body-Self Relations Questionnaire (MBSRQ) are widely used yet often lack specificity to thyroid surgery-related alterations.22,23 While the BIS developed by Hopwood et al.22 has value for cancer patients broadly, it shows considerable limitations for thyroid cancer patients. Initially validated in breast cancer populations, the BIS includes broadly applicable items but fails to capture thyroid cancer-specific concerns such as neck scarring visibility, voice changes, hormone-related body composition alterations, and the unique psychological impact of having a “good cancer” diagnosis that often minimizes patient distress. Studies by Zhao et al.16 have demonstrated that generic cancer body image scales inadequately detect the nuanced body image distress experienced by thyroid cancer survivors, particularly related to their altered neck appearance and thyroid hormone-related physical changes. Similarly, the MBSRQ developed by Cash23 emphasizes overall body satisfaction but inadequately captures the distinctive concerns related to visible neck scarring, voice changes, and swallowing difficulties that characterize post-thyroidectomy experiences.

Other available instruments such as the Thyroid Cancer-Specific Quality of Life (THYCA-QoL) assessment and the University of Washington Quality of Life Questionnaire (UW-QOL) include limited items addressing body image and social function but lack comprehensive assessment of these domains.24,25 Furthermore, these tools often fail to adequately capture the complex interplay between physical changes, psychological adaptation, and social reintegration that characterizes the thyroid cancer survivorship journey. Moreover, social functioning—a critical dimension of quality of life reflecting patients’ interactions and roles within social contexts including family relationships, intimate partnerships, professional environments, and broader community engagement—has not been adequately integrated into available measurement tools tailored specifically to thyroid cancer survivors.

Hence, there is a clear gap in precise assessment instruments addressing both BID and social functionality specifically in thyroid cancer postoperative populations. This gap is particularly significant considering the growing evidence suggesting that body image distress and social functioning challenges may persist long after physical recovery, potentially affecting treatment adherence, surveillance participation, and overall quality of survivorship.26,27

Developing and validating a targeted measurement tool for thyroid cancer survivors is of critical clinical significance. First, it fills the research gap by providing a tailored assessment instrument specifically designed for thyroid cancer patients, thereby overcoming limitations of existing generic scales and facilitating more accurate identification of at-risk individuals. Second, accurate evaluation of BID and social functioning can facilitate timely psychological and social interventions, significantly enhancing postoperative rehabilitation and overall quality of life. Such targeted interventions may include specialized psychological support, scar management techniques, voice rehabilitation, and social reintegration strategies that address the specific needs of this patient population.28

From a health care delivery perspective, this instrument could serve as a valuable screening tool in routine clinical practice, enabling health care providers to systematically assess and address psychosocial concerns that might otherwise remain undetected. Lastly, this research contributes theoretically to the body image and social cognition literature, providing valuable insights into how cancer-related physical changes influence interpersonal and societal interactions, ultimately promoting comprehensive cancer care and survivorship interventions that extend beyond physiological recovery to encompass psychological adaptation and social well-being.

Methods

Theoretical framework

The development of the Thyroid Cancer Body Image and Social Impact Scale (TC-BISIS) was guided by two complementary theoretical frameworks that provide a comprehensive foundation for understanding the complex interplay between body image distress and social functioning in thyroid cancer survivors.

In considering theoretical frameworks for cancer-related body image concerns, we examined White's model which offers a heuristic cognitive-behavioral approach to understanding body image dimensions in cancer populations.29 While White's model provides valuable insights through its multidimensional approach encompassing appearance, body integrity, body functioning, and cognitive-behavioral processes, we determined that additional frameworks were needed to fully address the unique challenges faced by thyroid cancer survivors, particularly regarding visible neck alterations, voice changes, and the specific pathways from body image distress to social impairment.

The primary theoretical underpinning is Cash's Body Image Disturbance Theory,30 which conceptualizes body image as a multidimensional construct comprising three interrelated components: perceptual (how individuals perceive their physical appearance), cognitive-affective (thoughts and feelings about one's body), and behavioral (actions taken in response to appearance concerns). According to Cash, body image distress emerges when negative evaluations of appearance lead to emotional distress and maladaptive behaviors.31 This theory is particularly relevant for thyroid cancer survivors who experience visible changes such as neck scarring and functional alterations like voice changes. The cognitive-affective component of Cash's theory informed the development of the “Scar Cognition and Emotional Response” and “Voice Change Experience” dimensions of our scale, while the behavioral component provided the foundation for the “Social Avoidance Behavior” dimension.

Complementing this framework, we incorporated Leary's Social Avoidance-Distress Model, which establishes a clear pathway from body image distress to social functioning impairment.32 This model proposes that perceived changes in appearance or abilities trigger social anxiety, which subsequently leads to avoidance behaviors and ultimately results in functional impairment across various life domains. Leary's theory explains how body image distress specifically translate into social consequences, providing the theoretical basis for the “Social Role Function Impact” and “Intimate Relationship Impact” dimensions of our scale.

The integration of these two theoretical frameworks enabled us to develop a comprehensive measurement tool that captures the complete trajectory from cognitive-emotional responses to physical changes (dimensions 1–2), through behavioral adaptations (dimension 3), to ultimate impacts on social functioning domains (dimensions 4–5). This approach aligns with contemporary understanding of body image as not merely a perceptual or aesthetic concern, but as a complex psychosocial construct with significant implications for functional outcomes and quality of life in cancer survivors.19,33 Throughout our scale development process, we systematically mapped each item to specific components of these theoretical frameworks. For the Voice Change Experience dimension, we ensured that items capture not just the physical changes themselves, but also patients’ psychological responses to these changes. Similarly, items within the Social Avoidance Behavior, Social Role Function Impact, and Intimate Relationship Impact dimensions were designed to explicitly connect behavioral and functional outcomes to body image concerns following thyroid surgery, thereby creating a theoretically coherent measurement tool that reflects the progressive impact of body image distress on social functioning.

Scale development process

Item pool generation

Literature review

A comprehensive literature review was conducted to identify existing knowledge and measurement gaps regarding body image and social functioning in thyroid cancer survivors. Electronic databases including PubMed, CINAHL, PsycINFO, Web of Science, and Embase were systematically searched using combinations of keywords: “thyroid cancer”, “thyroidectomy”, “body image”, “appearance concern”, “scar”, “voice change”, “social function”, “social avoidance”, “quality of life”, “intimate relationship”, “measurement”, “scale”, and “questionnaire”. The search was limited to English and Chinese, peer-reviewed articles published between January 2000 and December 2024.

The initial search yielded 412 articles, which were screened for relevance based on title and abstract, resulting in 127 articles for full-text review. After applying inclusion criteria (original research focusing on psychosocial aspects of thyroid cancer survivorship) and exclusion criteria (studies exclusively examining physiological outcomes), 26 articles were selected for detailed analysis. Content analysis of these articles, along with review of existing scales including the Body Image Scale (BIS), the Multidimensional Body-Self Relations Questionnaire (MBSRQ), the Thyroid Cancer-Specific Quality of Life (THYCA-QoL) assessment, and the University of Washington Quality of Life Questionnaire (UW-QOL), informed the development of our preliminary item pool. In this process, we systematically mapped identified concerns to our theoretical frameworks, categorizing potential items according to Cash's three components (perceptual, cognitive-affective, and behavioral) and Leary's pathway from social anxiety to functional impairment. This theory-driven approach ensured that our preliminary item pool comprehensively captured the theoretical constructs while remaining relevant to the specific experiences of thyroid cancer survivors. Based on the theoretical framework and literature analysis results, we initially developed a preliminary item pool consisting of 20 items across five dimensions: (1) scar cognition and emotional responses, (2) voice change experiences, (3) social avoidance behaviors, (4) social role functioning impacts, and (5) intimate relationship impacts.

Semi-structured interviews

To ensure comprehensive content coverage and establish content validity, semi-structured interviews were conducted with 18 thyroid cancer survivors (12 females, 6 males) who had undergone surgery within the previous 6–24 months. Female participants ranged in age from 29 to 55 years (mean age ​= ​44.33 ​± ​8.68 years), while male participants ranged from 38 to 59 years (mean age ​= ​49.67 ​± ​8.87 years). The mean time since surgery for all participants was 7.50 ​± ​3.28 months. Participants were recruited from the thyroid surgery outpatient clinic of a tertiary general hospital using purposive sampling to ensure diversity in age, time since surgery, and cancer stage.

The interview guide included open-ended questions addressing: (1) experiences with physical changes following thyroid surgery; (2) emotional responses to these changes; (3) impact on social interactions and activities; (4) changes in work or school functioning; and (5) effects on intimate relationships. These interview domains were directly derived from our theoretical frameworks, with questions designed to elicit responses corresponding to Cash's perceptual, cognitive-affective, and behavioral components, as well as Leary's progression from appearance concerns to social anxiety, avoidance behaviors, and functional impairment. Sample questions included: “Could you describe any changes you've noticed in your appearance since your thyroid surgery?” 、“How have any changes in your voice or neck mobility affected your daily activities or work performance?” and “How have these changes affected your comfort level in social situations?” All interviews were conducted by two trained researchers (DQQ and LJY) with backgrounds in psycho-oncology and qualitative research methods. Each interview was conducted by one researcher in a private room at the hospital's outpatient clinic and lasted approximately 45–60 minutes. A detailed version of the semi-structured interview guide can be found in Supplementary File 1.

The interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using thematic content analysis by two independent researchers.34 The coding framework for analysis was structured according to our theoretical components, allowing us to systematically categorize participants' experiences into theoretically meaningful categories. Key themes emerged related to concerns about surgical scars, voice changes, neck discomfort, body image disturbance, and social functioning impacts. Several participants described specific concerns that informed our scale development. For example, one female participant stated: “I constantly wear scarves or high-necked tops to hide my scar. I feel like people are staring at it even when they probably aren't.” This statement exemplifies both Cash's behavioral component (hiding the scar) and the cognitive-affective component (perceived negative evaluation by others). Another male participant described functional impacts: “My voice gets tired so easily now. I had to step down from leading team meetings at work because I can't talk for that long without my voice becoming hoarse.” This illustrates the progression described in Leary's model from physical changes to functional impairment in social role domains. Regarding psychological impact, a 35-year-old female participant shared: “Every time I look in the mirror, the scar reminds me that I'm a cancer patient. It's like wearing a visible badge of illness that I can't take off.” This reflects Cash's perceptual and cognitive-affective components, highlighting how visible changes affect self-perception and emotional well-being.

These rich qualitative insights directly informed the content and wording of our scale items, ensuring they captured the lived experiences of thyroid cancer survivors. These rich qualitative insights revealed additional aspects of the thyroid cancer experience not fully captured in the literature, leading to the addition of 12 new items to the preliminary item pool. This expanded the item pool to 32 items while maintaining the five-dimensional structure to better reflect thyroid-specific concerns.

Expert consultation

Content validity was established through consultation with 15 experts in relevant fields, including surgical oncology (n ​= ​3), endocrinology (n ​= ​3), oncology nursing (n ​= ​3), psycho-oncology (n ​= ​3), and health measurement/psychometrics (n ​= ​3). Experts were provided with detailed descriptions of our theoretical frameworks and asked to evaluate how well each item reflected the intended theoretical construct in addition to its relevance for thyroid cancer survivors. Experts rated each item on a four-point scale (1 ​= ​not relevant, 2 ​= ​somewhat relevant, 3 ​= ​quite relevant, 4 ​= ​highly relevant) and provided qualitative feedback on item clarity, relevance, and comprehensiveness. The item-level content validity index (I-CVI) was calculated as the proportion of experts rating an item as three or 4, while the scale-level content validity index (S-CVI) was calculated as the average of the I-CVI values across all items.35,36

Items with an I-CVI < 0.78 were eliminated or revised according to expert recommendations. The resulting S-CVI for the overall scale was 0.92, exceeding the threshold of 0.80 for acceptable content validity.37 Based on expert feedback, four items were eliminated, seven were substantially revised, and two new items were added, resulting in a 30-item preliminary scale.

Examples of eliminated items included “I feel my neck appears asymmetrical after surgery” and “I avoid wearing certain types of clothing that expose my neck,” which experts deemed redundant with other items capturing similar concepts. Examples of substantially revised items included changing “I feel unattractive because of my scar” to the more specific “I feel that the scar on my neck makes me less attractive,” and modifying “I worry about my voice changes” to the more behaviorally anchored “I avoid situations that require speaking loudly (such as giving speeches or talking on the phone).” The two newly added items were “My scar negatively affects my overall perception of myself” and “Changes in my body make me feel anxious when communicating with my partner,” which experts suggested to better capture body image impact on self-concept and intimate communication, respectively. These revisions further strengthened the theoretical foundation of the scale by ensuring that items precisely mapped to specific theoretical components while maintaining clinical relevance.

Preliminary survey

The 30-item preliminary scale was pilot-tested with 30 thyroid cancer survivors (20 females, 10 males) recruited from the same hospital setting. Female participants ranged in age from 29 to 59 years (mean age ​= ​45.50 ​± ​8.61 years), while male participants ranged from 32 to 59 years (mean age ​= ​48.90 ​± ​9.53 years). The mean time since surgical treatment for all participants was 7.50 ​± ​2.95 months (range: 2–12 months). The 2:1 female-to-male ratio in our sample reflects the epidemiological pattern of thyroid cancer, which has a higher incidence in women than men (approximately 3:1 in the general population).38 Participants completed the questionnaire and participated in cognitive interviews to assess item comprehension, response processes, and completion time. Based on feedback regarding item redundancy, ambiguity, and relevance,39 three items were revised, while maintaining the same total number of items. For example, “I feel self-conscious about my voice when speaking” was revised to the more specific “Changes in my voice make me uncomfortable when speaking in public” to better capture situational discomfort. Similarly, “I worry about my appearance in social settings” was refined to “My scar/voice changes make me feel ashamed or embarrassed in social settings” to more explicitly connect appearance concerns to thyroid cancer-specific changes. The third revised item, “I feel different from others since my surgery,” was modified to “My scar makes me feel different from others” to focus specifically on scar-related perceptions rather than general post-surgical feelings. These revisions resulted in a 30-item scale ready for psychometric evaluation.

The final preliminary TC-BISIS comprised 30 items across five dimensions: Scar Cognition and Emotional Response (7 items), Voice Change Experience (6 items), Social Avoidance Behavior (7 items), Social Role Function Impact (5 items), and Intimate Relationship Impact (5 items). Revilla et al. recommend a five-point response scale (“strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”). Such a scale enables the participants to accurately express their thoughts and opinions, as well as have a smoother of response selection experience.40 Therefore, each item was rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 ​= ​Completely disagree to 5 ​= ​Completely agree), with higher scores indicating greater body image distress and social functioning impairment. The English and Chinese versions of the TC-BISIS are available in Supplementary File 2.

Psychometric evaluation

Participants and procedures

Following approval from the Institutional Review Board of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (Approval No. 2025-SR-479), the psychometric properties of the TC-BISIS were evaluated in a sample of thyroid cancer patients who had undergone surgery. Participants were recruited from the same hospital setting between January 2025 and July 2025. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) histologically confirmed diagnosis of thyroid cancer; (3) having undergone thyroidectomy within the previous 1–6 months; (4) ability to read and understand Chinese; and (5) willingness to provide informed consent. Exclusion criteria included: (1) cognitive impairment affecting ability to complete questionnaires; (2) history of other malignancies; (3) pre-existing psychiatric disorders; and (4) severe postoperative complications requiring extended hospitalization.

A total of 686 eligible patients provided written informed consent and were divided into two independent samples: Sample 1 (n ​= ​321) for item analysis and exploratory factor analysis (EFA), and Sample 2 (n ​= ​365) for confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and additional validity and reliability assessments. Sample sizes were determined based on established psychometric guidelines, with the EFA sample exceeding the recommended subject-to-item ratio of 10:1,41 and the CFA sample surpassing the minimum recommendation of 200 participants42 and the 5:1 ratio of subjects to free parameters.43 There were no significant differences between the two samples in terms of demographic and clinical characteristics (P ​> ​0.05 for all comparisons). Participants in Sample 2 were followed longitudinally at one, two, and three months post-surgery to assess measurement invariance over time.

Measures

Demographic and clinical information

Participants provided demographic information including age, gender, education level, marital status, and employment status. Clinical data including receipt of radioiodine therapy, type of surgery, and presence of comorbidities were extracted from medical records with the participants’ consent.

Thyroid cancer body image and social impact scale (TC-BISIS)

The preliminary 30-item TC-BISIS was administered to all participants. The scale comprises five hypothesized dimensions: Scar Cognition and Emotional Response (7 items), Voice Change Experience (6 items), Social Avoidance Behavior (7 items), Social Role Function Impact (5 items), and Intimate Relationship Impact (5 items). Each item is rated on a five-point Likert scale from 1 (Completely disagree) to 5 (Completely agree), with higher scores indicating greater disturbance.

Body image scale (BIS)

The 10-item Body Image Scale developed by Hopwood et al.22 was used to assess criterion validity. The BIS measures body image distress in cancer patients and has demonstrated good psychometric properties across various cancer populations. Items are rated on a four-point scale from 0 (not at all) to 3 (very much), with higher scores indicating greater body image distress. The Cronbach's alpha in the current study was 0.89.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) and Mplus version 8.3 (Muthén & Muthén, Los Angeles, CA). Statistical significance was set at P ​< ​0.05 for all analyses. The demographic characteristics of participants were described using descriptive statistics (frequency, percentages, means, and standard deviations). Missing data represented less than 3% of total responses and were handled using multiple imputation with five imputation datasets. Sample sizes were determined based on recommendations of a minimum 10:1 participant-to-item ratio for factor analysis, exceeding this threshold for both samples.

Item analysis

Three complementary methods were used to evaluate and refine scale items using Sample 1 (n ​= ​321). (1) Critical ratio method: Participants were ranked by total scale score, with the highest 27% forming the high-score group and the lowest 27% forming the low-score group. Independent samples t-tests compared item scores between these groups, with items demonstrating t-values < 3.0 or P ​> ​0.05 considered for elimination.44 (2) Correlation coefficient method: Pearson correlation coefficients between each item and the total scale score were calculated. Items with coefficients ≤ 0.30 were considered for elimination.45 (3) Internal consistency analysis: The impact of each item on the scale's overall Cronbach's alpha coefficient was assessed. Items whose removal resulted in an increased alpha coefficient were considered for elimination.46

Exploratory factor analysis

Using Sample 1 data (n ​= ​321), exploratory factor analysis (EFA) with principal axis factoring and oblique rotation (Promax) was conducted to identify the underlying factor structure of the TC-BISIS. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy and Bartlett's test of sphericity were examined to confirm the suitability of the data for factor analysis. Factor extraction was guided by multiple criteria: eigenvalues > 1.0, scree plot examination, parallel analysis, and theoretical interpretability. Items with factor loadings < 0.40 were considered for elimination.47

Confirmatory factor analysis

The factor structure identified through EFA was subsequently validated using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) with Sample 2 (n ​= ​365). Model fit was evaluated using multiple indices: comparative fit index (CFI; good fit > 0.95), Tucker–Lewis index (TLI; good fit > 0.95), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA; good fit < 0.06), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR; good fit < 0.08).48

Measurement invariance

Measurement invariance across gender was assessed using multi-group confirmatory factor analysis with gender as the grouping variable, following the procedure to establish configural, metric, and scalar invariance between male and female patients. Additionally, longitudinal measurement invariance was assessed using data from Sample 2 participants at one (T1), two (T2), and three (T3) months post-surgery. A series of increasingly constrained models were tested: (1) configural invariance (same factor structure across time points); (2) metric invariance (equal factor loadings); and (3) scalar invariance (equal item intercepts). Model comparisons were based on changes in CFI (ΔCFI ≤ 0.01) and RMSEA (ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015).49

Validity assessment

Content validity was established through expert consultation as described earlier. Criterion validity was assessed by examining Pearson correlation coefficients between the TC-BISIS and the BIS using Sample 2 Data at time point T1. Correlations between 0.40 and 0.59 were considered moderate, 0.60–0.79 strong, and ≥ 0.80 very strong.50

Reliability assessment

Internal consistency reliability was evaluated using Cronbach's alpha coefficients of sample 2 at all three time points (T1, T2, and T3) for both the overall scale and each subscale, with values ​≥ ​0.70 considered acceptable, ≥ 0.80 good, and ≥ 0.90 excellent.51

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 686 thyroid cancer patients who had undergone surgery participated in this study. The sample was divided into two independent groups: Sample 1 (n ​= ​321) for item analysis and exploratory factor analysis, and Sample 2 (n ​= ​365) for confirmatory factor analysis and additional validity and reliability assessments. Table 1 presents the demographic and clinical characteristics of both samples. The mean age of participants was 38.45 ​± ​12.02 years in Sample 1 and 40.23 ​± ​12.45 years in Sample 2. In both samples, approximately 75% of participants were female, reflecting the higher prevalence of thyroid cancer among women. Most participants (58.57% in Sample 1 and 55.34% in Sample 2) had undergone total thyroidectomy, with the remainder having received partial thyroidectomy. No significant differences were observed between the two samples in terms of demographic or clinical characteristics (P ​> ​0.05 for all comparisons), confirming their comparability for scale validation purposes.

Item analysis

The initial 23-item TC-BISIS was subjected to item analysis using data from Sample 1 (n ​= ​321). Results of the critical ratio analysis showed that all items had t-values > 3.0 (range: 9.448–13.144) and P ​< ​0.001, indicating good discriminative ability between high and low groups. The item–total correlations ranged from 0.529 to 0.669, exceeding the threshold of 0.30 for all items. Internal consistency analysis revealed that removal of any item would not increase the overall Cronbach's alpha coefficient, which was 0.937 for the initial scale. Based on these results, all 30 items were retained for subsequent exploratory factor analysis.

Exploratory factor analysis

Exploratory factor analysis was conducted on the 30-item scale using data from Sample 1 (n ​= ​321). The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin measure of sampling adequacy was 0.938, and Bartlett's test of sphericity was significant (χ2 ​= ​4919.57, df ​= ​435, P ​< ​0.001), confirming the suitability of the data for factor analysis. Initial extraction identified five factors with eigenvalues > 1.0, explaining 62.43% of the total variance. This five-factor solution was further supported by parallel analysis and scree plot examination.

After Promax rotation, all items demonstrated factor loadings ≥ 0.40 on their respective factors, with no problematic cross-loadings detected. The factor structure aligned with the theoretically hypothesized five dimensions covering scar perception, voice changes, social avoidance, role function, and intimate relationships. Factor loadings ranged from 0.591 to 0.822, indicating robust item-factor relationships. Table 2 presents the factor loadings from the exploratory factor analysis with Promax rotation. All 30 items were retained for subsequent confirmatory factor analysis.

Confirmatory factor analysis

The five-factor structure with 30 items identified through EFA was further validated through confirmatory factor analysis using data from Sample 2 (n ​= ​365). The initial model demonstrated excellent fit to the data: CFI ​= ​0.987, TLI ​= ​0.986, RMSEA ​= ​0.018 (90% CI: 0.010–0.025), and SRMR ​= ​0.039. These fit indices substantially exceeded conventional thresholds for good model fit (CFI/TLI > 0.95, RMSEA < 0.06, SRMR < 0.08), providing strong support for the hypothesized five-factor structure of the TC-BISIS.

All standardized factor loadings were statistically significant (P ​< ​0.001) and ranged from 0.529 to 0.686, further confirming the robust construct validity of the scale. The correlation coefficients between factors were weak to moderate (ranging from 0.301 to 0.459), indicating related but distinct dimensions that capture different aspects of body image distress and social functioning. The excellent model fit achieved without any post-hoc modifications provides compelling evidence for the structural validity of the five-dimensional TC-BISIS as theoretically derived. Measurement invariance

Measurement invariance across gender was examined using multi-group confirmatory factor analysis with Sample 2 data at T1. The results supported configural, metric, and scalar invariance, indicating that the TC-BISIS demonstrates good measurement invariance across male and female thyroid cancer patients. This supports its use for gender comparisons. Based on the established measurement invariance, we proceeded to examine gender differences in TC-BISIS scores. Male participants (n ​= ​97) reported a mean total score of 90.22 (SD ​= ​9.12), while female participants (n ​= ​268) reported a mean total score of 91.57 (SD ​= ​10.57). Independent samples t test revealed no statistically significant difference between genders (t ​= ​−1.116, P ​= ​0.117). This finding suggests that while thyroid cancer surgery impacts body image and social functioning in both genders, the overall magnitude of this impact may be similar across gender groups at the initial assessment point.

Longitudinal measurement invariance was assessed using data from Sample 2 (n ​= ​365) across three time points: one month (T1), two months (T2), and three months (T3) post-surgery. Table 4 presents the fit indices for measurement invariance testing at each step. The model testing began with configural invariance, which examines whether the same factor structure holds across time points. The fit indices indicated an acceptable model fit. Metric invariance was tested by constraining factor loadings to be equal across time points. The results showed similar fit indices, with changes in CFI (ΔCFI ​= ​0.002) and RMSEA (ΔRMSEA ​= ​0.000) meeting the recommended thresholds (ΔCFI ≤ 0.01, ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015). This suggests that the factor loadings are invariant across time. Scalar invariance, which constrains item intercepts to be equal across time points, was then examined. The results showed a significant drop in model fit, with ΔCFI ​= ​0.140 and ΔRMSEA ​= ​0.017 exceeding the acceptable thresholds. These results indicate that scalar invariance was not supported across the three time points.

Criterion validity

Criterion validity was assessed by examining correlations between the TC-BISIS and the Body Image Scale (BIS) using data from Sample 2 at T1. The total TC-BISIS score showed a strong positive correlation with the BIS total score (r ​= ​0.809, P ​< ​0.001), indicating good criterion validity. At the subscale level, correlations between the TC-BISIS dimensions and the BIS ranged from moderate to strong: Scar Cognition and Emotional Response (r ​= ​0.556, P ​< ​0.001), Voice Change Experience (r ​= ​0.492, P ​< ​0.001), Social Avoidance Behavior (r ​= ​0.561, P ​< ​0.001), Social Role Function Impact (r ​= ​0.532, P ​< ​0.001), and Intimate Relationship Impact (r ​= ​0.503, P ​< ​0.001). These correlations support the criterion-related validity of the TC-BISIS.

Reliability

Internal consistency reliability was evaluated using Cronbach's alpha coefficients for Sample 2 at all three time points. The overall TC-BISIS demonstrated good internal consistency at all time points (α ​= ​0.866–0.886). Each subscale also showed acceptable to good reliability, with Cronbach's alpha coefficients ranging from 0.722 to 0.820 across the three assessments. These results indicate strong internal consistency reliability of the TC-BISIS.

Final scale structure

The final TC-BISIS consists of 30 items across the five dimensions previously described, with each dimension capturing a distinct aspect of post-thyroidectomy experience. Each item is rated on a five-point Likert scale from 1 (Completely disagree) to 5 (Completely agree), with higher scores indicating greater body image distress and social functioning impairment. The total score ranges from 30 to 150, with subscale scores calculated as the sum of respective item scores. Discussion

Main findings

This study developed and validated a novel, theory-based instrument—the Thyroid Cancer Body Image and Social Impact Scale (TC-BISIS)—designed specifically to assess body image distress and social functioning in patients following thyroid cancer surgery. The 30-item TC-BISIS demonstrated robust psychometric properties, including excellent structural validity, good internal consistency, satisfactory criterion validity, and measurement invariance across time. To our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive scale that specifically addresses the unique body image distress and social functioning challenges faced by thyroid cancer survivors.

Our findings align with Cash's theoretical proposition that body image distress encompasses perceptual, cognitive-affective, and behavioral components. The first two dimensions of our scale—Scar Cognition and Emotional Response, and Voice Change Experience—capture the cognitive-affective aspects of body image distress, while the Social Avoidance Behavior dimension reflects the behavioral component. Similarly, consistent with Leary's model, our results confirm that body image distress following thyroid surgery can precipitate social anxiety and avoidance behaviors, ultimately affecting broader social functioning domains as captured in our Social Role Function Impact and Intimate Relationship Impact dimensions.

The weak to moderate inter-factor correlations (r ​= ​0.301–0.459) provide empirical support for the interrelationship between body image distress distress and social functioning, while still confirming that these are distinct but related constructs. This finding is consistent with previous research suggesting that body image distress following cancer treatment often extend beyond mere appearance evaluation to affect various domains of psychosocial functioning.8,15

A significant strength of the TC-BISIS is its comprehensive coverage of concerns specific to thyroid cancer survivors. Unlike generic body image measures such as the BIS or MBSRQ, the TC-BISIS incorporates items addressing neck scarring and voice changes—physical alterations uniquely associated with thyroid surgery. Previous research has highlighted the importance of voice-related concerns following thyroidectomy, with studies reporting voice disturbances in 25–87% of patients.52 Our Voice Change Experience dimension specifically addresses this critical aspect of post-thyroidectomy recovery that is often overlooked in generic quality of life assessments.

Furthermore, the TC-BISIS extends beyond measuring body image distress alone to assess its impact on various domains of social functioning. This comprehensive approach acknowledges the complex interplay between physical changes, psychological adaptation, and social reintegration in the cancer survivorship journey. Our findings parallel those of Lin et al.,53,54 who found that physical changes following head and neck cancer surgery often lead to challenges in workplace functioning, social engagement, and intimate relationships.

Our measurement invariance testing revealed that while the TC-BISIS demonstrates configural and metric invariance across time points, scalar invariance was not achieved. This finding has important implications for how the scale should be used in longitudinal assessment. The established configural invariance confirms that the overall factor structure remains stable over time, suggesting that the fundamental constructs being measured remain consistent throughout the recovery process. Metric invariance indicates that the strength of relationships between items and their underlying factors is consistent, supporting meaningful comparisons of correlations and regression coefficients across time points.

However, the lack of scalar invariance suggests that item intercepts may vary across time, potentially reflecting the evolving nature of body image concerns and social functioning throughout the recovery process. This means that while changes in latent factor correlations remain interpretable, direct comparisons of raw scores or latent means across time points should be approached with caution. These findings might not necessarily indicate measurement problems but could reflect genuine changes in how patients interpret and respond to certain aspects of body image and social functioning as they progress through their recovery journey. Future research should explore which specific aspects of body image and social functioning show the most pronounced changes in measurement properties over time, as this could provide valuable insights into the recovery process. For clinical applications, we recommend using the scale primarily for tracking patterns of change rather than making absolute comparisons of scores across different time points.

In addition to longitudinal measurement invariance, our findings also demonstrated strong measurement invariance across gender groups. The TC-BISIS maintained configural, metric, and scalar invariance between male and female patients, indicating that the scale measures the same constructs in the same way regardless of gender. This is particularly important given the gender disparities in thyroid cancer prevalence, with women having significantly higher incidence rates than men.1 Despite these epidemiological differences, our results suggest that both male and female thyroid cancer survivors experience similar dimensions of body image distress and social functioning challenges following surgery. Our gender-based analyses revealed no significant differences between male and female participants in overall TC-BISIS scores, suggesting that both genders may experience comparable levels of body image concerns and social functioning challenges following thyroid cancer surgery. This finding is notable given that body image concerns are often presumed to affect women more severely than men in many other contexts. The lack of gender differences in our sample may reflect the unique nature of thyroid surgery scarring, which affects a visible area of the body that holds significance for both men and women.

The establishment of gender measurement invariance enhances the clinical utility of the TC-BISIS by ensuring that gender differences in scale scores reflect true differences in the underlying constructs rather than measurement bias. This property is essential for making valid comparisons between male and female patients and for developing gender-sensitive interventions that address the potentially different needs and concerns of men and women following thyroid cancer surgery. Previous research has suggested that men and women may differ in how they experience and express body image distress,55 with women often reporting greater appearance-related distress and men focusing more on functional impairments. The TC-BISIS's proven invariance across gender ensures that it can accurately capture these nuanced differences, providing clinicians with valuable information for tailoring psychosocial support.

From a theoretical perspective, our findings have important implications for understanding body image and social functioning in the context of cancer survivorship. The five-factor structure of the TC-BISIS provides empirical support for an integrated theoretical model that combines Cash's body image components with Leary's social functioning pathway. Specifically, our results suggest that body image distress in thyroid cancer survivors progresses from cognitive-affective responses to physical changes (Factors 1–2), through avoidance behaviors (Factor 3), to functional impairment across life domains (Factors 4–5). This progression aligns with both theories while extending them to address the unique challenges of thyroid cancer survivorship. The theoretical mapping of our scale dimensions was largely confirmed by our empirical findings, suggesting that these theoretical frameworks provide a valid foundation for understanding the psychosocial impact of thyroid cancer surgery.

Implications for nursing practice and research

The development and validation of the TC-BISIS have several important clinical implications. First, this instrument addresses a significant gap in the assessment of psychosocial concerns among thyroid cancer survivors, providing clinicians with a reliable and valid tool to identify patients who may benefit from targeted psychosocial interventions. Given that thyroid cancer survivors are often labeled as having a “good cancer” due to favorable survival rates, their psychosocial needs may be overlooked in clinical practice.14 The TC-BISIS can help legitimize these concerns and facilitate more comprehensive cancer care.

Second, the multidimensional structure of the TC-BISIS allows for a nuanced assessment of different aspects of body image distress and social functioning, enabling clinicians to tailor interventions to address specific areas of concern. For example, patients scoring high on the Scar Cognition and Emotional Response dimension might benefit from scar management techniques and cognitive-behavioral interventions addressing appearance concerns, while those with elevated scores on the Social Role Function Impact dimension might require vocational rehabilitation or workplace accommodations.56

Third, the establishment of measurement invariance supports the utility of the TC-BISIS for monitoring changes in patients' concerns over time. This property makes the scale particularly valuable for evaluating the effectiveness of psychosocial interventions in clinical trials and for tracking individual patients’ recovery trajectories in clinical practice. Health care providers can use the TC-BISIS as part of routine follow-up care to identify persistent or emerging concerns that warrant intervention.

Fourth, our findings suggest that thyroid cancer survivors may experience body image and social functioning concerns across multiple domains, highlighting the need for multidisciplinary care approaches. Comprehensive support programs for thyroid cancer survivors should address not only physical recovery but also psychological adaptation and social reintegration. Such programs might include scar management, voice therapy, psychological counseling, vocational rehabilitation, and relationship support.

Finally, the strong association between TC-BISIS scores and the BIS suggests that body image distress among thyroid cancer survivors share common features with those experienced by other cancer populations, while the thyroid-specific dimensions of the TC-BISIS capture unique aspects of the thyroid cancer experience. This finding underscores the value of both general and cancer-specific psychosocial assessment in comprehensive cancer care.

Overall, based on our findings, we recommend integrating the TC-BISIS into routine follow-up care for thyroid cancer survivors, particularly during the first year post-surgery when psychosocial concerns are often most pronounced. Patients with elevated scores should receive dimension-specific interventions: scar management techniques and cosmetic strategies for scar-related concerns; early referral to speech-language pathologists for voice issues; cognitive-behavioral therapy for social avoidance; vocational rehabilitation and workplace accommodations for role function impacts; and relationship counseling for intimate relationship challenges. Regular reassessment using the TC-BISIS at critical time points (such as return to work or completion of adjuvant treatment) allows for monitoring changes and adjusting interventions accordingly. Comprehensive care should also include preoperative psychoeducation about potential body image and social functioning challenges, facilitation of peer support connections through support groups, and education for partners and family members about providing appropriate support. This multifaceted approach acknowledges the complex interplay between physical changes and psychosocial adaptation, potentially improving overall quality of life for thyroid cancer survivors.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations that should inform future research. The sample was recruited from a single hospital in China, potentially limiting generalizability to other cultural contexts or health care settings, and we strongly recommend multi-center validation studies across diverse health care systems to enhance external validity. A larger sample would provide greater confidence in the stability of the factor structure and allow exploration of clinical variables’ influence on TC-BISIS scores. Additionally, although our sample included participants with various comorbidities, we did not analyze or control for the potential impact of these conditions on scale outcomes, which could bias the results. Future research should examine how comorbid conditions might influence TC-BISIS scores and potentially develop appropriate adjustment procedures or normative data for different comorbidity profiles. Despite conducting initial measurement invariance testing across gender groups and finding no significant gender differences in TC-BISIS scores, the uneven distribution with significantly more women than men (reflecting the typical epidemiological pattern of thyroid cancer) limited our ability to conduct more comprehensive gender-specific validations. Future studies should aim to recruit larger samples of male thyroid cancer survivors to enable more robust gender-based analyses and potentially identify any gender-specific aspects of body image concerns and social impacts that our overall score comparison might not have captured.

Our exclusion of participants with pre-existing psychiatric disorders, although methodologically justified for this initial validation, limits the generalizability of our findings, as psychiatric conditions such as depression and anxiety are common among cancer patients. Future validation studies should include participants with comorbid psychiatric conditions to ensure the scale's applicability across the diverse clinical spectrum of thyroid cancer survivors. Criterion validity was assessed by examining correlations between the TC-BISIS and the Body Image Scale (BIS), which served as our primary criterion measure. While the BIS provided an established reference point for body image assessment, we acknowledge that comprehensive validity assessment would ideally include additional measures of related constructs. Future validation studies should incorporate measures of anxiety, depression, and quality of life to establish a more complete nomological network.

The relatively short three-month follow-up period represents another limitation; this timeframe captures only the initial adaptation phase and cannot adequately reflect the complex trajectory of psychosocial recovery following thyroid cancer treatment. Body image concerns and social functioning challenges may evolve significantly beyond this period, particularly as patients negotiate long-term survivorship issues. Longitudinal studies with extended follow-up periods of 1–5 years are needed to track body image and social functioning concerns over extended survivorship periods and identify factors associated with persistent versus resolving issues. Although we made several refinements to item wording based on expert feedback during the revision process, our statistical analyses necessarily reflect data collected with the original item formulations. This methodological constraint represents a limitation, as the psychometric properties of these refined items could not be reassessed within the current study. Future validation studies should incorporate these improved item formulations to verify their psychometric properties and potentially enhance the scale's conceptual validity. Additionally, our inclusion criteria limiting participants to the early post-treatment period may not capture the full spectrum of body image concerns that can persist for years after thyroid cancer treatment. Future validation studies should examine the scale's performance across more diverse post-treatment timeframes to ensure its relevance for long-term survivors. Furthermore, our scale was developed and validated in a Chinese population using Mandarin Chinese. Given the global prevalence of thyroid cancer and cultural variations in body image perceptions and social functioning, future research should focus on translating and validating the TC-BISIS in other languages and cultural contexts. Such cross-cultural validation studies would enhance the scale's global applicability and could potentially identify culture-specific aspects of body image distress and social impacts following thyroid cancer treatment. Finally, research should evaluate the TC-BISIS's clinical utility by examining its sensitivity to change following psychosocial interventions and its ability to identify patients at risk for poor outcomes, incorporating it as an outcome measure in intervention studies targeting body image distress and social functioning in thyroid cancer survivors.

Conclusions

The TC-BISIS represents a significant advancement in the assessment of body image distress and social functioning among thyroid cancer survivors. This theoretically grounded, psychometrically sound instrument addresses a critical gap in survivorship care by capturing the unique concerns of patients following thyroid cancer surgery. The strong psychometric properties of the TC-BISIS, including its excellent structural validity, good reliability, satisfactory criterion validity, longitudinal measurement invariance, and measurement invariance across gender groups, support its utility for both clinical practice and research applications. By enabling comprehensive assessment of thyroid-specific body image distress and their impact on social functioning, the TC-BISIS can facilitate more targeted psychosocial interventions and contribute to improved quality of life for the growing population of thyroid cancer survivors. Health care providers are encouraged to incorporate this instrument into routine survivorship care to identify and address these important yet often overlooked aspects of the thyroid cancer experience.
