Disappearance of histone H3F3A mutation in metastatic giant cell tumor of bone during long term follow up
Authors: Takashi Ariizumi, Naoki Oike, Yudai Murayama, Tomohiro Miyazaki, Hajime Umezu, Hiroyuki Kawashima, Akira Ogose
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGiant cell tumor of bone (GCTB) is a locally aggressive tumor that rarely metastasizes, although a small subset undergoes malignant transformation. H3F3A driver mutations have been recognized as characteristic molecular features of GCTB. We describe a case of a refractory GCTB with disappearance of the H3F3A mutation and that later developed a malignancy within the preexisting tumor.Case presentationA 22-year-old male was diagnosed with GCTB and underwent multiple surgeries for recurrence and lung metastases. The tumor relapsed in the knee, soft tissue, and lungs and required repeated resections. All relapsed tumor tissues were positive for H3F3A mutation. A large lung metastatic tumor developed 35 years after the initial surgery. The specimen from the last lung metastasis showed no significant changes in the morphology and characteristics of GCTB, despite the disappearance of the H3F3A mutation. Subsequently, liver and bone metastases appeared, and chemotherapy was initiated for presumed malignant transformation. Despite treatment, the disease progressed, and the patient died.ConclusionThe disappearance of the H3F3A mutation may be associated with malignant transformation in GCTB, whereas our findings suggest that it does not correspond to the tumor’s morphological or biological characteristics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05196-6.Keywords: Case report, Giant cell tumor of bone, H3F3A, RANKL, TRAP

Background

Giant cell tumor of bone (GCTB) is a locally aggressive but rarely metastasizing neoplasm composed of neoplastic mononuclear stromal cells with mixed macrophages and osteoclast-like giant cells [1]. Neoplastic mononuclear stromal cells are of the mesenchymal lineage and pre-osteoblast phenotype and are a heterogeneous population that may consist of cells at multiple stages of differentiation [2]. Therefore, stromal cells express bone sialoprotein, osteonectin, osteopontin, osterix, and Runt-related transcription factor 2 [3–10]. Neoplastic stromal cells also express the receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa B ligand (RANKL) [11], which is an essential regulator of osteoclastogenesis [12]. Giant cells are large multinucleated cells that exhibit many osteoclastic properties [2]. Therefore, giant cells express osteoclast-specific markers, such as tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) [13, 14] and cathepsin K [15].

Specific H3F3A driver mutations in GCTB have been recently described, and they have been helpful in differentiating GCTB from other giant cell-containing tumors, including aneurysmal bone cysts, chondroblastomas, and telangiectatic osteosarcomas [16, 17]. At least 95% of GCTBs harbor pathogenic H3F3A gene mutations, approximately 90% of which are H3.3 p.Gly34Trp (G34W). Accordingly, H3.3 G34W is a reliable surrogate marker for molecular analysis [1, 18], although the mechanism by which H3.3 mutations drive tumorigenesis in GCTB remains unclear [1, 16, 17, 19].

Giant cell tumor of bone occasionally undergoes malignant transformation [20], typically occur after treatment (e.g., radiation therapy) or multiple local recurrences [21, 22]. However, the mechanisms underlying such malignant transformation has not been fully elucidated.

We describe herein a patient with recurrent and metastatic GCTB in whom the histone H3F3A mutation disappeared and a secondary malignancy later developed.

Case presentation

The patient’s graphical clinical course was shown in Fig. 1. In 1979, a 22-year-old male presented to our hospital with left knee pain. The patient underwent open biopsy, and the pathological diagnosis was GCTB. The patient underwent tumor curettage (sample 1), and a bone graft was performed. Preoperative chest radiography revealed a suspicious lesion in the right lung. In 1981, partial resection of the increase lung nodules was performed (sample 2), and histology showed conventional GCTB with no malignant features. Although there was no clear treatment standard for metastases of conventional GCTB, the patient was treated with combination chemotherapy comprising doxorubicin, vincristine, and cisplatin. In 1984, chemotherapy with bleomycin, cyclophosphamide, and actinomycin D was initiated because of the progression of lung metastases and local recurrence. Subsequently, the lung metastases resolved. In 1989, he underwent tumor resection (sample 3) and knee joint arthrodesis because of local recurrence. In 1999, local recurrence (sample 4) occurred in the soft tissue of the left thigh, and left lung metastases occurred in 2002 (sample 5) and 2005 (sample 6). All relapsed tumors were resected.

Clinical patient course. VCR, vincristine; DXR, doxorubicin; CDDP, cisplatin; BLM, bleomycin; CPA, cyclophosphamide; ACD, actinomycin D; GEM, gemcitabine; PTX, paclitaxel

The patient’s clinical course was uneventful until 2014, when follow-up radiography and computed tomography (CT) showed a large metastasis to the right lung. The tumor (sample 7) was resected, and the histological features indicated conventional GCTB with numerous partial mitotic changes and penetration into the bronchial tubes. Adjuvant denosumab was administered considering the multiple tumor relapses. In 2015, a palpable mass was detected in the right side of the abdomen, and CT revealed a large liver mass. Histological analysis of the specimens obtained by core needle biopsy (sample 8) from the liver lesion showed that highly cellular lesions contained large numbers of mononuclear cells, and multinucleated giant cells almost disappeared. Tc-99 m MDP bone scintigraphy revealed multiple abnormally increased areas of activity in the skull, vertebrae, right scapula, ribs, pelvis, and right femur. We considered this a malignancy in GCTB because the development of the tumor was not a typical occurrence in GCTBs. Despite chemotherapy with gemcitabine and paclitaxel, the disease progressed, and the patient died of multiple organ failure 36 years after the first hospital visit.

Histopathological and immunohistochemical examination

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples were obtained from surgically resected and biopsy specimens (samples 1–8). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed for histopathological diagnosis. Osteoclasts were detected using TRAP staining performed according to Burstone’s Azo dye method [23], with some modifications [24]. Immunohistochemical staining for RANKL and H3.3 G34W was performed, and the detailed procedures are provided in Supplementary File 1.

The specimen from the initial surgery (sample 1) showed a diffuse proliferation of oval- to spindle-shaped mononuclear stromal cells with a fibrous matrix. Osteoclast-like multinucleated giant cells were scattered within the background of mononuclear cells. The nuclei of the mononuclear cells were similar to those of the giant cells. Mononuclear stromal cells showed diffuse H3.3 G34W immunoreactivity. Similar histological features were observed in samples 2–6. The histological appearance was similar to that of conventional GCTB, with multinuclear giant cells sparsely spread in ovoid, round, or spindle mononuclear stromal cells. Discussion

This report analyzes the phenotypic and molecular characteristics of a GCTB with malignant transformation over a long-term disease course. This case differs from conventional GCTB with respect to several aspects. Clinically, metastasis to the liver and bones is uncommon among GCTBs. Although benign giant cell tumors can metastasize to the lungs [25], reported rates of pulmonary metastasis vary across studies, ranging from approximately 1% to 9% [22, 26–28]. Further, to our best knowledge, metastases to sites other than the lungs have not been previously reported. Thus, our case may not have been a GCTB but rather a giant cell-rich osteosarcoma. Giant cell tumors may be incorrectly diagnosed histologically based on similarities between tumors [29]. Although H3.3 mutations may occur in osteosarcomas [30], all H3.3-mutant osteosarcomas have osteoblastic or fibroblastic histology. Sample 1 (initial surgery sample) from the current case showed a typical finding in conventional GCTB. No osteoids were detected within the tumor, and the multinucleated giant cells stained positively for TRAP. Zhao et al. reported that multinucleated giant cells in giant cell–rich osteosarcomas stained negatively for TRAP [31]. This suggests that the current tumor is different from a giant cell–rich osteosarcoma. Another notable finding was that the last lung metastasis was more aggressive than previous relapses. Therefore, we considered the possibility that another giant cell–rich lung cancer similar to GCTB may have developed in the lungs. Although a primary osteoclast-like giant cell–rich carcinoma of the lung pathologically similar to GCTB was possible, RANKL expression in mononuclear cells and TRAP expression in multinucleated cells were characteristic of GCTB. One study reported positive histone H3 expression in the nuclei of giant cells in osteoclast-like giant cell–rich carcinomas of the lungs. This result is distinctly different from that of histone expression in GCTB [32]. Thus, giant cell–rich carcinomas of the lung was ruled out based on the differences in RANKL, TRAP, and histone H3.3 G34W expressions.

There were a few histological points that were unclear. First, TRAP staining revealed total absence of multinucleated giant cells in sample 4. TRAP is an enzyme unique to osteoclasts, and the osteoclastic phenotype is shown in multinucleated giant cells of GCTB [33]. However, this can be explained with respect to the fixation and preservation of the FFPE blocks. Poor or inadequate sample processing decreases TRAP enzyme activity, and thus, TRAP-positive multinucleated giant cells may not be detected. Second, the liver metastasis specimen was also characterized by a low number of cells that stained positively for both RANKL and TRAP. Thus, the phenotypic characteristics of conventional GCTB may have disappeared with malignant transformation, although the histological changes in the liver metastasis might also have been influenced by continuous denosumab administration. Our findings are consistent with those reported by Yamagishi et al. who found that osteoclast-like giant cells were absent in GCTB after denosumab administration [34]. Although osteogenesis has been reported previously, mononuclear cell proliferation remains the main finding, and osteogenesis was not observed in the current case. These results suggested that the histologic features of liver metastases were not solely the result of changes after denosumab administration, but rather the result of changes associated with malignant transformation or a coexistence of the two conditions.

Notably, H3.3 mutation disappeared during the clinical course. H3.3 G34W mutation was negative in all specimens collected since the last lung metastasis in the immunohistochemical examination. H3.3 G34W immunohistochemistry is a valuable diagnostic modality for GCTB [35]. However, because the possibility of antigenicity loss in immunostaining cannot be ruled out, this result does not directly confirm abnormalities in H3F3A itself. Therefore, direct sequencing using PCR was performed in the present case, and the results showed that H3F3A mutation was absent at the genetic level. These findings suggest that the H3F3A point mutation was lost during the course of tumor progression. Sanger sequencing is less sensitive, whereas next-generation sequencing provides more reliable data for analysis [19]. However, most samples in this case were old, poorly preserved paraffin-embedded tissues, and the available frozen material was limited, restricting further analyses. Therefore, the presence of low-frequency subclonal H3F3A-mutant cells cannot be completely excluded. The use of a high-sensitivity platform such as next-generation sequencing, if suitable tissue had been available, would have further strengthened the interpretation of complete mutation loss. The disappearance of H3F3A G34W in GCTB has been reported in previous studies [18, 36]. Yoshida et al. reported cases of H3F3A mutation loss in malignant GCTB and proposed several hypotheses to explain this occurrence; these include the collision theory, clonal replacement theory, and clonal activation theory [36]. In contrast, Fernanda et al. reported a case of malignant GCTB with positive H3F3A mutation [37]. Ishihara et al. reported that p53 alterations and dysfunction in histone methylation, as evidenced by H3K27me3 loss, may play an important role in the malignant progression of GCTB [38]. Based on these findings, malignant transformation is considered to be caused not only by the loss of H3F3A mutations, but also by the involvement of various other genetic alterations. The specimen from the last lung metastasis showed no significant changes in the morphology and characteristics of GCTB, such as RANKL expression in mononuclear cells and TRAP expression in multinucleated cells, despite the disappearance of the H3F3A mutation. These findings indicate that the loss of H3F3A mutation, while potentially associated with malignant transformation in GCTB, may occur independently of overt morphological changes. This observation underscores a dissociation between histological features and molecular alterations, suggesting that clonal evolution may play a critical role in tumor progression. The mechanisms underlying malignant transformation in GCTB remain poorly understood and require further investigation.

Conclusion

Although uncommon, H3F3A mutations may disappear in a GCTB. Our patient later developed a malignant tumor within the GCTB. In this case, a prolonged clinical course over 36 years with repeated recurrences and multiple surgical interventions was observed, and the patient later developed a malignant tumor within the GCTB.
