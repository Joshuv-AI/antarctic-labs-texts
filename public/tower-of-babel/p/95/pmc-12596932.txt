Rieske Oxygenase‐Catalyzed Biotransformations in Recombinant Cupriavidus necator Fueled by Formate Oxidation
Authors: Marleen Hallamaa, Hannah Pia Franziska Meier, Matteo Vajente, Mattia Ghirardi, Jan Deska, Sandy Schmidt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe use of single carbon (C1) molecules, such as carbon dioxide or formate, is crucial in the transition from a linear, petroleum‐based economy to a circular bioeconomy. Formate can serve as both a carbon and energy source, further enhancing its attractiveness as a feedstock. Cupriavidus necator, a lithoautotrophic microbial chassis strain, provides an opportunity to leverage formate for the synthesis of valuable products. However, its ability to grow on formate and the subsequent coupling of that process to recombinantly produced redox enzymes for the efficient production of high‐value‐added products in a biotransformation has not yet been established. Here, we report the development of a formate‐driven C. necator whole‐cell chassis that recombinantly produces Rieske oxygenases (ROs) and elaborate on possible stress responses of the cells during formatotrophic cultivation. The whole‐cell chassis efficiently catalyzes the oxyfunctionalization of olefins fueled by formate oxidation. For instance, styrene is dihydroxylated to (R)‐1‐phenylethane‐1,2‐diol in an excellent 95% yield and with good enantioselectivity (74% ee) under formatotrophic conditions. The product yield and optical purity obtained demonstrate the synthetic usefulness of formate‐fueled whole‐cell bio‐transformations in C. necator.Keywords: biocatalysis, cofactor regeneration, Cupriavidus necator, formate, Rieske oxygenases

Introduction

Biocatalysis is widely accepted as a green alternative in the chemical industry due to its sustainability advantages, such as biodegradable catalysts, high selectivity, and the ability to operate under mild conditions.[ 1 ] To further advance the role of biocatalysis in the green transition, significant interest has emerged in the past decade in single‐carbon (C1)‐based biomanufacturing.[ 2 , 3 ] In this process, biocatalysts convert C1 molecules, such as carbon dioxide (CO2), into valuable products. The direct utilization and conversion of gaseous C1 feedstocks is complex due to the needed advanced reactor setups for gas fermentations, and the difficulties in transporting and storing such gaseous feedstocks. In this context, formate has significant potential as a microbial C1 feedstock because it is easy to handle, transport, and store[ 4 ] and can serve as both a carbon and energy source.[ 5 ] Formate can be obtained directly from CO2 using renewable electricity through an electrochemical conversion process.[ 2 , 5 , 6 ] Furthermore, as a water‐soluble salt, formate can be introduced in the process without any special equipment, and reactors commonly used in whole‐cell processes can be employed.[ 7 ]

These benefits have sparked extensive research into developing synthetic formatotrophic growth via different assimilation pathways. The pathways have been implemented in Escherichia coli (E. coli) and other commonly used microorganisms.[ 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 – 12 ] While the synthetic formatotrophs show promise, their growth rates are not yet compatible with their industrial application. One alternative is to use natural formatotrophs directly as a platform for formate‐based production.[ 13 ]

Cupriavidus necator H16 (C. necator) represents such an alternative, especially given its well‐studied chemolithoautotrophic metabolism.[ 14 ] Notably, this bacterium can grow using formate as its sole source of carbon and energy by employing the Calvin–Benson–Bassham (CBB) cycle.[ 15 , 16 ] Formate oxidation is catalyzed by an endogenous molybdenum‐dependent formate dehydrogenase (FDH) and is accompanied by the reduction of NAD+ to NADH.[ 17 ] One challenge, on the other hand, lies in the fact that formatotrophic growth can be diminished due to formate toxicity[ 13 , 18 ] and the alkalinization of the growth medium due to proton consumption during formate uptake.[ 19 ] Therefore, continuous feeding of formate and monitoring of pH and growth are required.

In addition, genetic manipulation of C. necator has traditionally been challenging due to its status as a nonmodel organism and the limited availability of molecular tools. However, recent progress in developing more efficient synthetic biology toolkits[ 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 – 26 ] has fueled a plethora of engineering studies to produce high‐value products, such as isopropanol,[ 27 , 28 ] resveratrol,[ 29 ] and phytase enzymes,[ 30 ] alkanes,[ 31 ] and methyl ketones[ 32 ] from CO2. In addition, C. necator has attracted significant interest as a potential source of bioplastic[ 33 , 34 ] due to its ability to accumulate excess carbon in the form of polyhydroxybutyrate (PHB)[ 33 ] and of single‐cell protein.[ 35 , 36 ] From a biocatalytic perspective, the ability of C. necator to oxidize H2 for NAD(P)H regeneration is of particular interest because it provides an atom‐economic cofactor regeneration system for heterologous redox enzymes.[ 37 ] A few promising studies have focused on using its oxygen‐tolerant hydrogenases for H2‐driven reductions of hydroxyacetone,[ 38 ] cyclic enones,[ 39 ] and xylose.[ 40 ]

Metabolic engineering has facilitated fermentative formate conversion into crotonate[ 13 ] and biofuels[ 41 ] by C. necator. However, there is a paucity of reports on whole‐cell biotransformations demonstrating cofactor regeneration fueled by formate oxidation in C. necator. This is despite its potential benefits, such as providing both the carbon necessary for biomass formation and the energy required for cofactor supply. Consequently, a successful implementation would facilitate access to a broader range of products by leveraging the action of heterologous redox enzymes and expanding the potential for C1‐driven biocatalytic processes (Figure 1 ).

The oxidation to CO2 by the soluble FDH requires NAD+ as a cofactor, which is regenerated by the reductase of the ROs. SFDH: soluble formate dehydrogenase. CBB cycle: Calvin–Benson–Bassham cycle.

Rieske oxygenases (ROs) are well‐known biocatalysts for diverse O2‐dependent oxyfunctionalization reactions, making them very useful biocatalysts due to their broad reaction scope and high selectivities.[ 42 ] ROs are the only enzymes known to catalyze the stereoselective formation of vicinal cis‐diols in one step. They have also been found to catalyze other diverse oxidative transformations, such as monooxygenations,[ 43 , 44 ] N‐dealkylations,[ 45 , 46 ] dehydrogenations,[ 47 ] and cyclizations.[ 48 , 49 – 50 ] Due to their dependency on a complex electron transport chain and requirement for cofactor regeneration,[ 51 , 52 ] ROs are well‐suited for synthetic applications in recombinant whole‐cell catalysts.[ 53 , 54 ]

In this study, we present a formate‐driven C. necator whole‐cell chassis that recombinantly produces ROs and efficiently catalyzes the oxygenation of limonene and various aromatic compounds, in biotransformations fueled by formate oxidation (Figure 1). Additionally, we offer valuable perspectives for potential cellular stress responses under formatotrophic conditions and overall cell morphology. This approach provides new perspectives for formate‐driven biotransformations in C. necator and broadens the biocatalytic toolbox beyond traditional model organisms, such as E. coli or baker's yeast. Moreover, a comparison of the yields and selectivities achieved in formate‐driven biotransformations in C. necator allows for direct evaluation of efficiency with those previously established in E. coli. This comparison reveals the limitations and strengths of different host organisms for biocatalytic applications.

Results and Discussion

Strain Generation and Cultivation of C. necator on Formate

We started our investigations by generating C. necator strains for the recombinant production of two model ROs (Table S1, Supporting Information). We chose cumene dioxygenase (CDO, from Pseudomonas fluorescens IP01) and naphthalene dioxygenase (NDO, from Pseudomonas sp. NCIB 9816‐4) as both have been extensively studied before and are known to catalyze the oxygenation of diverse aromatic compounds and terpenes.[ 53 , 54 , 55 – 56 ] The genes encoding the CDO and NDO subunits were cloned into our in‐house created plasmid pMVRha to allow recombinant expression in a recently established C. necator strain (C. necator ΔRM) with increased electroporation efficiency.[ 20 ]

Next, we examined the growth of C. necator ΔRM on formic acid, followed by the growth of the RO expression strains. For this, we used a straightforward fed‐batch setup with a syringe pump to continuously feed the carbon source (Figure S4, Supporting Information). We chose formic acid as the feed source instead of sodium formate, as the slow, continuous addition of formic acid helps prevent gradual alkalization of the medium while avoiding excessive formate accumulation. To determine optimal growth conditions, we initially conducted screening experiments focusing on the appropriate feed rate of formic acid (Figure S3, Supporting Information) and the composition of the growth medium.

We found that a higher phosphate concentration is crucial for buffering the medium and protecting the cells from abrupt pH changes (data not shown). However, the stronger phosphate buffer complicates determining an ideal feed rate because growth was inhibited even though pH levels remained within an acceptable range, as formate is still toxic for the organism. Ultimately, we developed a straightforward protocol for formatotrophic growth of C. necator strains consisting of three key steps: 1) an adaptation phase in minimal medium following inoculation; 2) low, continuous formate feeding (8 mmol L–1 h–1), initiated once the culture reached an optical density (OD600) of 0.3; and 3) an increase in feed rate (43 mmol L−1 h−1) upon reaching OD600 of 2.5. Following this strategy resulted in OD600 values of up to 10 within 71 h (Figure 2 , C. necator ΔRM). In rich medium (lysogeny broth, LB), C. necator ΔRM grows to OD600 values of up to 7 within 24 h. The longer lag phase observed under formatotrophic conditions is presumably due to the time required for the expression of endogenous FDHs, which are essential for formate oxidation. Since formate serves as the inducer for FDH expression,[ 15 , 57 ] adaptation to formatotrophic conditions typically takes overnight[ 19 , 58 ] before growth can resume efficiently.

Once a suitable growth protocol for the C. necator wild type was established, strains expressing CDO and NDO were grown under the same conditions. However, several adaptations were necessary, as the cultures bearing plasmids grew slowly under these conditions. To reduce stress on the cultures, antibiotics were omitted from the medium, relying on the proven stability of our developed plasmid.[ 20 ] Additionally, we induced protein expression only during the later stages of growth (typically at an OD600 between 4 and 7) to further minimize the metabolic burden during the growth phase. Overall, we found that reducing culture stress effectively addressed the challenges of combining recombinant protein expression with formatotrophic growth. Using this strategy, we achieved high cell densities (OD600 of 9 within 70 h), which correspond to data obtained from cultivating the plasmid‐empty strain C. necator ΔRM (Figure 2). Furthermore, the soluble expression of CDO and NDO was confirmed under formatotrophic growth conditions (Figure S6, Supporting Information).

Formatotrophic RO‐Catalyzed Biotransformations in C. necator

After establishing efficient formatotrophic growth conditions and confirming the soluble production of both CDO and NDO, we set up RO‐catalyzed whole‐cell biotransformations of limonene and various aromatic compounds. Our aim was to demonstrate the feasibility of formatotrophic biotransformations in C. necator while comparing them to cells grown under heterotrophic conditions in a rich medium. To this end, we cultivated the C. necator ΔRM pMVRha‐cdo and C. necator ΔRM pMVRha‐ndo strains, which produce CDO and NDO, respectively, on formate or in a rich medium (LB) up to an OD600 of 12. The resulting resting cells after harvest were then used to perform whole‐cell biotransformations, adding formate or fructose for intracellular cofactor regeneration.

To further highlight the benefit of prior FDH induction, formate was also added to the oxygenation reactions to regenerate NADH. While growth is sustained when using formate as an energy source, using formate to drive biotransformations in C. necator resting cells has not been reported yet. Therefore, a direct comparison of cofactor regeneration efficiency using energy‐dense substrate (fructose) and energy‐poor substrate (formate) is necessary to prove that C1 compounds can be used as a driving force for oxygenation reactions (fructose: ΔfHo = −1265.6 kJ/mol vs. formate: ΔfHo = −425.9 kJ/mol).[ 59 ] However, Assil‐Companioni et al. demonstrated that C=C bond reductions catalyzed by an ene‐reductase can efficiently proceed in C. necator when H2 is used for cofactor regeneration,[ 39 ] indicating that the cells can provide sufficient amounts of reduced nicotinamide cofactor to drive enzymatic conversions at least under autotrophic conditions.

The initial biocatalyst evaluation focused on the oxygenation of indene 1, a common model substrate in NDO‐ and CDO‐related studies.[ 51 , 52 , 53 – 54 ] This allowed us to understand how the enzymes behave in this new host and determine if C. necator ΔRM is a suitable host for whole‐cell biocatalysis. We initially screened a range of substrate concentrations and cell densities to identify the optimal reaction conditions to achieve high conversions (Table 1 and Table S5, Supporting Information).

As expected, the conversion of 1 increased with higher cell densities used in the biotransformations (e.g., Table 1, entries 5 and 6), and noninduced cells showed no detectable product formation (Table S6, Supporting Information), indicating that the native metabolism does not contribute to the product formation. Interestingly, formatotrophically cultivated cells fueled with formate performed at least as efficiently as their heterotrophic counterparts using fructose (e.g., Table 1, entries 1 and 4). However, some differences in product distribution and overall yield were observed between the two systems, which are potentially due to metabolic inhibition when formate, a known cytotoxin, is used as the sacrificial compound. Previous studies have shown that growth of the wild‐type strain of C. necator on formate triggers the upregulation of stress‐response genes.[ 58 ] Therefore, these differences may also be caused by varying expression levels of the ROs due to the inhibitory effect of formate on the metabolism during cultivation.

Subsequent evaluations of key biotechnological metrics (Table 2 ) support this hypothesis, showing a general decrease in specific activity and maximum conversion rate when switching from fructose to formate. Nevertheless, NDO under formatotrophic conditions (HCO2 −‐NDO) outperformed fructose‐based systems in terms of total product yield (Table 1 entries 2 and 5, Table 2), potentially due to slower background degradation of both the starting material and the products by the native metabolism. The time‐course study (Figure 3 ) reveals differences in the mass balances between these two systems that further support this hypothesis, suggesting that the inhibited state of the cell might, in fact, be beneficial for the overall selectivity.

Encouraged by these results, we proceeded to evaluate the performance of the whole‐cell biocatalysts across a representative scope of substrates without further optimization. This allowed for a direct comparison with similar previously reported biotransformations using NDO/CDO‐expressing E. coli [ 54 ] or isolated enzymes.[ 51 ] Notably, C. necator exhibited similar activity compared to other host systems or even purified enzymes. For example, the product profiles, conversions, and enantiomeric excesses for indene 1 and styrene 2 (Figure 4B, substrates 1 and 2) were consistent with previously reported values.[ 51 , 54 ] Most notably, the performance of formate‐grown cells was on par with that of heterotrophically grown cells, indicating that switching to formate does not substantially reduce catalytic efficiency. In fact, HCO2 −‐NDO achieved an impressive 95% yield and 74% ee in the oxygenation of 2 (Figure 4B, substrate 2, NDO) without additional process optimization.

Next, we examined the structurally related compounds toluene 3 and 2‐nitrotoluene 4 to determine whether the enzymes could oxygenate aromatic rings. In both conditions, NDO favored the more challenging C(sp 3)—H bond activation over aromatic ring dihydroxylation, yielding benzyl alcohol 3a and 2‐nitrobenzyl alcohol 4a, respectively. This type of reactivity mirrors the detoxification pathways observed in the human liver, where cytochrome P450s oxidize toluene to benzyl alcohol, which is then metabolized to hippuric acid via benzaldehyde.[ 60 ] A similar degradation pathway may account for the low yield of 3a in our system. Despite complete substrate conversion, benzyl alcohol is the only detectable product in small quantities, suggesting that the native metabolic pathways of C. necator may further convert it. Supporting this hypothesis, it has previously been suggested that NDO converts ethylbenzene to 1‐phenylethanol, thereby participating in its detoxification in Pseudoxanthomonas spadix BDa59.[ 61 ] Although the HCO2 −‐NDO system yields only modest amounts of 3a, further optimization could increase production. Nevertheless, this microbial oxidation route shows promise, particularly given the difficulty of toluene oxidation under ambient conditions.[ 62 ] Currently, 3a is primarily produced through the hydrolysis of benzyl chloride or the reduction of benzaldehyde, processes that have environmental drawbacks. Therefore, exploring more sustainable microbial alternatives is worthwhile.

In contrast, CDO predominantly catalyzed the dihydroxylation of the aromatic ring in 2 and 3 to yield 2b and 3b, respectively (Figure 4B, substrates 2 and 3, CDO). For 3, however, the dihydroxylation is followed by a rapid elimination to produce o‐cresol 3b regioselectively with a 74% yield using heterotrophic cells, as opposed to a 13% yield under formatotrophic conditions. Cresols are traditionally derived from coal tar; however, rising demand has led to the development of synthetic routes, such as the hydrolysis of chlorotoluene, vapor‐phase methylation of phenol, and alkali fusion of toluenesulfonates, methods that often yield isomeric mixtures.[ 63 ]

Thus, developing greener, more selective methods for o‐cresol production is still relevant. Although toluene‐o‐xylene monooxygenase (ToMO) shows promise in the biotransformation of toluene to cresol, it still suffers from poor regioselectivity, even after several rounds of site‐directed mutagenesis.[ 64 ]

We then evaluated the performance of whole‐cell biocatalysts in the oxygenation of the aliphatic substrate (+)‐limonene 5. Utilizing limonene is of particular interest because it is an inexpensive, abundant hydrocarbon found in high concentrations in citrus peels, which the juice industry typically discards as waste.[ 65 ] Consequently, the functionalization of limonene is highly desirable. The industrial synthesis of the caraway/dill odor, (+)‐carvone, depends on converting limonene to carvone oxime with nitrosyl chloride.[ 65 ] However, carveol 5a, produced by our formatotrophic CDO whole‐cell system at a 21% yield, could also serve as an alternative precursor for carvone production (Figure 4B, substrate 5, CDO). Although the obtained yield is relatively low, this transformation demonstrates the potential of using formatotrophic whole‐cell systems to selectively produce pure trans‐ 5a. This compound, which contains two chiral centers, could also serve as a valuable intermediate for synthesizing other complex molecules, such as the antitumor agent eleutherobin.[ 66 ] In contrast, the NDO‐expressing strain failed to produce any detectable products, consistent with findings by Gally et al. who also observed no identifiable products from NDO‐expressing E. coli during limonene oxygenation.[ 54 ]

Analysis of Cellular Stress Responses of C. necator Grown on Formate

To further evaluate the potential of formatotrophic whole‐cell biotransformations in C. necator and analyze limitations caused by possible cellular stress responses when grown on formate, we examined cell morphology using transmission electron microscopy (TEM).[ 67 ] C. necator accumulates PHB under nitrogen‐ and phosphate‐limiting conditions,[ 33 , 34 ] and the granules can account for up to 80% of the total cell dry weight (CDW) in minimal medium when sufficient carbon is present, but phosphate or nitrogen is limited.[ 68 , 69 ] Thus, a low level of PHB formation in formatotrophically grown cells may indicate a limitation in carbon supply. Since PHB production has not been characterized in formatotrophically grown C. necator cells, this analysis may also open novel possibilities for future PHB production using formate as the sole carbon and energy source.

We analyzed C. necator ΔRM cells grown under heterotrophic (LB) and formatotrophic (HCO2 −) conditions by TEM at different growth phases. A C. necator ΔRM culture grown under nitrogen‐limiting conditions served as a positive control for PHB granule production (Figure S11, Supporting Information). C. necator PHB–4, which is unable to accumulate PHB,[ 70 ] was used as a negative control. C. necator ΔRM and C. necator PHB–4 were cultured under formatotrophic conditions, and samples were collected at various time points to evaluate the PHB production during continuous formate addition. The cells were immediately fixed, and a sample for TEM was prepared in EPON resin, as described in the Supporting Information.

We collected samples from the C. necator ΔRM culture at OD600 values of 2.8 and 11.8 and from the C. necator PHB–4 culture at an OD600 of 2.9 and 5.3. These samples were collected at the same time points, revealing a slower growth rate in the PHB‐deficient strain. TEM images demonstrate minimal PHB production in C. necator ΔRM cells at an OD600 of 2.8 during exponential growth (Figure 5A–C). The average diameter of the PHB granules at OD600 2.8 is about 120 nm, with 0–3 granules per cell. At an OD600 of 11.8 (Figure 5D–F), the number of PHB granules increases visibly, and their average diameter increases to 205 nm. Up to 12 PHB granules can be identified per cell. These results suggest that, after three days of growth on formate, nitrogen depletion occurs at an OD600 of 11, resulting in the storage of excess carbon in PHB granules. The cells maintained their general shape, and their average length increased only slightly. This suggests that the extreme conditions and continuous addition of formate do not affect the cell morphology.

TEM images of formatotrophically grown C. necator ΔRM and C. necator PHB–4. A–C) C. necator ΔRM, OD600 2.8; D–F) C. necator ΔRM, OD600 11.8; G–I) C. necator PHB–4, OD600 2.9; and J–L) C. necator PHB–4, OD600 5.3.

As expected, PHB granules were not produced by C. necator PHB–4 (Figure 5G–L); however, at an OD600 of 2.9, the cells appear visibly longer, and the cell membrane appears less intact compared to the C. necator ΔRM strain (Figure 5G–I). The average cell length is even greater at an OD600 of 5.3. This altered morphology may result from stress caused by random mutations that were introduced when this strain was created or by metabolic imbalance due to the lack of the PHB production pathway.[ 70 ] Cell stress may also explain the observed slower growth of C. necator PHB–4 on formate compared to C. necator ΔRM. The positive control (Figure S11, Supporting Information) exhibits large, densely packed PHB granules that alter cell shape. In contrast, the granules observed in C. necator ΔRM (Figure 5D–F) are significantly smaller and do not affect cell shape.

In addition to the phenotypic analysis of formatotrophic C. necator cells, we performed whole‐genome sequencing to identify changes in genotype that potentially occurred during longer growth phases on formate. Although we anticipated observing potential stress‐induced mutagenesis targeting formate metabolism, the whole‐genome sequencing revealed no differences compared to the genome sequence of C. necator ΔRM and C. necator H16 grown in rich medium.

Overall, these results suggest that formatotrophic growth does not result in a significant altered cell morphology. However, the formation of PHB under formatotrophic conditions appears to be lower than when cells are grown in a rich medium. This suggests that less carbon is available under formatotrophic conditions for storage in the form of PHB. It cannot be ruled out that energy derived from intracellular PHB hydrolysis is used for cofactor supply rather than FDH‐mediated formate oxidation during whole‐cell biotransformations.

Conclusions

The microbial conversion of formate into valuable products presents an intriguing opportunity to utilize C1 compounds and overcome the challenges associated with gaseous C1 feedstocks. In this study, we established formate‐fueled, RO‐catalyzed biotransformations in recombinant C. necator. Using two well‐studied ROs (CDO and NDO, respectively), we achieved comparable activity levels to those of other established host systems, such as E. coli, or systems employing purified ROs. For instance, the product profiles, conversions, and enantiomeric excesses for the oxygenations of indene 1 and styrene 2 were consistent with previously reported values, achieving 95% yield and 74% ee in the NDO‐catalyzed oxygenation of 2 without additional process optimization. In addition, formatotrophic reactions were as efficient as their heterotrophic counterparts, demonstrating the potential of formate‐driven biotransformations in C. necator to expand the biocatalytic toolbox beyond traditional model organisms such as E. coli and baker's yeast. Encouragingly, our phenotype and genotype analyses of formatotrophically grown C. necator cells revealed no obvious morphology alteration or accumulation of genomic mutations. This suggests that C. necator is naturally well‐equipped to handle these demanding growth conditions. However, the observed lag phase during growth on formate indicates that the cells require an adaptation period during which the endogenous FDHs must be expressed. This seems to impose a metabolic burden on the cells, at least initially.

Additionally, our TEM analysis shows that slight PHB accumulation under formatotrophic growth conditions does not preclude the possibility that, during subsequent biotransformations, some of the stored energy in PHB is used for cofactor regeneration in RO‐catalyzed biotransformations. This observation could also be considered an opportunity because PHB is also a valuable product. Since the carbon stored in this intracellular biopolymer stems directly from formate, no carbon is lost, aligning with a circular carbon economy. Furthermore, the total microbial biomass after biotransformation could serve as an additional product in the form of single‐cell protein. This is currently being investigated as a replacement for feed protein.

Overall, our study highlights the potential of formate‐driven microbial cell factories based on C. necator that can effectively drive redox conversions to high‐value products, competing with established host organisms. This concept could be expanded in the future to include other oxidoreductases. Furthermore, optimizing growth rates, robustness to growth conditions, and overall yields via strain engineering and adaptive laboratory evolution will expand the scope of accessible products via microbial formate utilization.[ 71 , 72 , 73 , 74 , 75 , 76 , 77 , 78 , 79 , 80 , 81 , 82 , 83 , 84 – 85 ]
