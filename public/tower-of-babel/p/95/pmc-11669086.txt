The Alkynyl π Bond of sp-C Enhanced Rapid, Reversible Li–C Coupling to Accelerate Reaction Kinetics of Lithium Ions
Authors: Jingchi Gao, Xingru Yan, Xiangyao Gu, Xinlong Fu, Qian Chang, Zhihui Zhang, Yi Wang, Changshui Huang, Yuliang Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractGraphdiyne (GDY) is a promising anode for rechargeable batteries with high capacity, outstanding cyclic stability, and low diffusion energy. The unique structure of GDY endows distinctive mechanisms for metal-ion storage, and it is of great significance to further visualize the complex reaction kinetics of the redox process. Here, we systematically tracked the reaction kinetics and provided mechanistic insights into the lithium ions in the GDY to reveal the feature of the cation-π effect. It has been demonstrated that, unlike only one π bond in sp2-C, π electrons provided by one of the two alkynyl π bonds in sp-C can achieve proper interaction and speedy capture of lithium ions; thus, reversible Li–C coupling can be formed between electron-rich sp-C and lithium ions. In addition to interlayer intercalation in sp2-C regions, nanopores filling triangular-like cavities composed of highly conjugated sp-C contribute to the major capacity in flat voltage plateau regions. Therefore, a capture/pores filling-intercalation hybrid mechanism can be found in GDY. The coexistence of sp and sp2 carbon enables GDY electrodes with rapid Li+ diffusion, high capacity of over 1435 mAh g–1, extraordinary rate capability, and cyclic stability for more than 10000 cycles at 10A g–1. These results provide guidance for developing advanced carbon electrodes with optimized reaction kinetics for rechargeable batteries.

Introduction

Rechargeable electrodes with high energy density and ultralong cyclic stability have been extensively employed in the field of energy storage.1−5 In contrast to metal oxides, metallic alloys, and conversion electrodes,6−9 carbon-based materials, such as graphite, hard carbons, and soft carbons, have attracted great interest in rechargeable batteries owing to their abundant resources, outstanding electrical conductivity, superior chemical stability, and superior rate capability.10−13 Especially, carbon-based materials with conjugated sp2-C have unique features, such as ultralow working potential, extraordinary long-term capability, and abundant porous structure are the most feasible anodes for commercial applications. Nevertheless, the specific capacity of carbon-based electrodes can not meet the demands of ever-growing large-scale energy storage. Defect modulation with heteroatoms has been demonstrated to break through the capacity limitation of structures to some extent. Besides, the sp3-C defects enabled the electrodes with efficient ion diffusion and adsorption to boost the storage capacity. However, indistinct structure with sophisticated fabrication process, inferior conductivity, and randomly stacked pores of electrode induced by the introduction of heteroatoms and sp3-C defect,14−18 make it difficult to have an in-depth understanding of underlying structure-capacity correlation.

In contrast to other carbon-based materials with sp2 and sp3 hybrids, the appearing carbon allotrope graphdiyne (GDY) with sp-sp2 cohybrid carbon structure exhibits great unique features like 2D plane structure, 3D ion diffusion channels, outstanding chemical stability, and modifiable molecular pore. GDY shows inherent advantages, such as high specific capacity, superior cyclic stability, and favorable rate performance in rechargeable energy storage.19−24 GDY is a superior electrode with the formation of LiC3 in theory, which is beyond carbon-based materials·24,25 Interestingly, GDY with the coexistence of sp and sp2 hybridized carbon atoms endows electrodes with modulate electronic features to boost theoretical capacity and optimize electrochemistry performance.2,26−30 In addition, GDY can realize reversible self-expanding lithium-ion channels and strength reaction kinetic.31 However, the storage mechanism of GDY remains elusive, since there are few rare studies concentrated on the storage behavior at the molecular or atomic level. Given the nondestructive technology, it is essential to adopt a variety of in situ strategies to clarify the lithiation/delithiation kinetic dynamic.15,32,33 A distinct storage mechanism of GDY can facilitate the understanding of the difference betweens sp and sp2 hybridized carbon when they are used as electrodes of rechargeable batteries, displaying the advantages of sp hybridized carbon (sp-C) and providing guidance to devise high-performance electrodes.

Here, we tracked the reaction kinetic process and visualized lithium active sites in the redox process to uncover the characteristic of the cation-π effect. It has been proved that π electrons from one of the two alkynyl π bonds in sp-C, unlike only one π bond in sp2-C, realize expeditious lithium-ion capture, achieve appropriate interaction, and form reversible Li–C orbital coupling on account of incomplete charge transfer. Hence, superior reaction kinetics and reversible lithium active sites in the sloping voltage regions can be realized during the discharge process. In the subsequent stage, nanopores filling triangular-like sp-C cavities are observed to supply extra capacity, except for the interlayer space in sp2 regions with a similar behavior in graphite. As a consequence, “capture/pores filling-intercalation” hybrid mechanism is proposed in the redox process of GDY. GDY with sp and sp2 C displays an ultrahigh capacity of 1435 mAh g–1, superior rate capability, and cyclic stability for ultralong 10000 cycles at 10A g–1 with a dominant pseudocapacitive process and speedy Li+ diffusion when serving as an electrode of LIB. This underlying microstructure-dependent mechanism provides a guide and opens a new perspective for designing high-performance electrodes for advanced electrodes.

Results and Discussion

The microstructures and morphologies of GDY samples were investigated through various characterizations. As depicted in Figures 1a and S1, the as-synthesized GDY nanosheets display a porous morphology with a smooth surface and the thickness of approximately 10 nm, which can benefit from fast ion diffusion and excellent interface compatibility. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) images and the corresponding selected area electron diffraction (SAED) show highly crystalline characteristics of multilayer GDY and a typical hexagonal pattern lattice fringe with a lattice distance of 0.46 nm, which matches well with the simulated ABC stack mode of GDY (Figure 1b–1m).34,35 We could clearly identify an interlayer distance of 0.37 nm ascribed to adjacent curved GDY sheets. The XPS characterization was conducted to reveal the composition and chemical states of GDY (Figure S2). The C 1s spectra in the high-resolution XPS analysis (Figure 1n) can be deconvoluted into four peaks of C=C(sp2), C≡C(sp), C–O, and C=O, respectively.36,37 Moreover, the Raman spectrum demonstrates two distinct vibrational peaks located at 1921 and 2166 cm–1 ascribed to −C≡C- and −C≡C–C≡C– with sp-hybridized in conjugated alkyne bonds38 (Figure 1o). In addition, the existence of C≡C in Figure 1p indicates the successful synthesis of GDY.

It is worth noting that the active sites of the anode materials are critical parameters to determine the diffusion kinetic process and electrochemical performance. There are plenty of active sites, such as alkyne bonds with sp-C, triangular-like cavities centers, and benzene ring centers in the sp2-C region that could realize reversible lithium-ion uptake and remove to fulfill high-capacity electrodes (Figure 2a). Thus, the multiple active sites in sp and sp2 hybridized GDY have enormous advantages, such as extraordinary reversible capacity, ultrastable cyclability, and speedy charge transport. The Li+ storage behaviors are evaluated by coin type half-cells with lithium metal as a counter electrode. The cyclic voltammetry curves at the scan rate of 0.1 mV/s are displayed in Figure 2b. It is no doubt that the CV curves in the first cycle are significantly distinct from the following cycles. In the initial anodic process, a small and broad peak at 1.25 V is attributed to the formation of a solid electrolyte interface (SEI) during the lithium-ion insertion process. A distinct cathodic peak at 0.46 V corresponds to the substantial lithiation process of GDY. Apparently, the broad reduction peaks at 1.25 and 2.5 V are associated with the transformation from lithiation GDY to GDY in the cathodic process.29 This proves the reversible redox process of GDY for lithium insertion and removal. In subsequent cycles, GDY displays analogous redox behavior with overlapping curves, indicating good reversibility. GDY delivers a discharge capacity of 2077 mAh/g with an initial Coulombic efficiency of 69.1% (Figure S3). The irreversible capacity loss may be due to unstable flocculent-like SEI layers, edge plane defects, and the features of pores. Especially, unstable flocculent-like SEIs accelerate further electrolyte decomposition, interface side reaction, and irreversible Li+ consumption.39,40 It is worth noting that there is no conspicuous capacity attenuation in the latter process with a similar conclusion of CV. Intriguingly, GDY shows long cyclic stability with a capacity retention of 81.5% at 0.1 A/g after 100 cycles, as shown in Figure 2c. In addition, a reversible average capacity of 1331, 1078, 897, 803, 622, 431, and 264 mAh g–1 could be attained within the current density from 0.1 A/g to 10 A/g (Figure 2d). The relevant charge–discharge curves are displayed in Figure S4. To gain insight into the kinetic storage behaviors, CV curves were obtained at different scan rates, as shown in Figure 2e. We could clearly distinguish enhanced peaks density and slight shifts accompanied with rising discrepancy, verifying obvious voltage polarization at high scan rates. It is accepted that the storage mechanism can realize quantitative analysis on the basis of the power-law equation i = avb.19 A b value close to 1 reflects a fast capacitance reaction, whereas the b value of 0.5 represents a diffusion-controlled intercalation mechanism.14 The b values we speculate are 0.783, 0.845, 0.787, and 0.760 in the anodic and cathodic processes in Figure 2f. These results are close to 1, indicating a pseudocapacitance-controlled dynamic process and favorable Li+ storage kinetics. Further, the total redox behavior can be separated into capacitive-dominated behavior (k1v) and diffusion-controlled process (k2v1/2) to quantitatively analyze the kinetic process. The separated capacity contribution for the GDY from 0.1 to 0.8 mV/s is displayed in Figures S5–S9. Capacitive contribution capacity at 1 mV/s is exhibited in Figure 2g. Obviously, with the increasing scan rate from 0.1 mV/s to 1.0 mV/s, the capacitance ratios are continuously increasing and diffusion proportion are diminishing (Figure 2h). On account of outstanding kinetic process, excellent conductivity, enrichment active sites, and specific sp and sp2 hybrid in GDY, the electrochemistry performances, including specific capacity, rate capability, and cyclic stability, exceed those of advanced anodes in previous literatures, such as LTO@TiC, Fe2O3/GO, BTT-COF, Sn/DGT, and so on (Figure 2i).41−45 Benefiting from the outstanding kinetic process, GDY displays outstanding cyclic stability for over 10000 cycles at 10 A/g with a capacity retention of 74.2%, as shown in Figure 2j.

Raman spectrum is sensitive to the local atomic environment and phase transition. We implemented in situ Raman spectra to substantiate the structural evolutions during the lithiation and delithiation processes in the first cycle to attest the lithium-ion storage mechanism and the Li+ storage reversibility. Figure 3a–d shows the Raman spectrum during the discharge and charge process. Interestingly, the continuing interaction of lithium into GDY host leads to the position, intensity, and absence of D band and G band as well as conjugated alkyne units in Figure 3a,b. It has been demonstrated that the characteristic peaks located at 1921 and 2166 cm–1 are corresponding to −C≡C– and −C≡C–C≡C– in GDY, respectively.46,47 It can be observed that the diminished intensity and disappearance of alkyne bonds located at 2166 cm–1 from the open-circuit voltage (OCV) to 1.5 V are responsible for the introduction of Li+. In the subsequent lithiation process, the alkyne bonds located at 1921 cm–1 broadened and decreased, and the alkyne peaks were absent until the voltage approached 0.4 V. This is acknowledged that π electrons from one of the two alkynyl π bonds in high electronegativity sp-C could realize incomplete charge transfer to capture Li+ and produce proper interaction between sp-C and lithium ion. Besides, we could conclude that two −C≡C– in-between two adjacent benzene rings of highly conjugated GDY would achieve the cation−π effect and from Li–C orbital coupling, which is consistent with the weakened and disappearance of alkyne bonds in different voltage regions. In addition, we found the existence of D and G bands as well as the vanishment of alkyne bonds with area of 60 μm × 60 μm (Figures S10–S12). Moreover, we observed inconspicuous red shifts of the G-band from the OCV to 0.1 V, which accounts for the effective charge transfer to adjacent carbon layers in the sp2 region upon Li+ interaction. This induces occupied π* antibonding band by donor electrons and an asymmetric charge distribution for carbon layers in the perpendicular direction.48−50 At the final stages, the disappearance of G peak originates from lithium-ion interactions between the adjacent metallic-like features of the carbon layers and the resonant phonon scattering process.10 Meanwhile, the intensity of the D band is weakened and until it vanishes during the discharge process. This is attributed to the centers of diacetylenic triangular–like pores and sp2 carbon breathing motion in the rings at edge planes occupied by the introduction of Li+.51 Interestingly, a reversible phenomenon of the intensity and shape as well as the position of the D, G, and alkyne bands was observed during the delithiation process in Figure 4c,d. In the charge process, we could clearly distinguish that the D band as well as G band gradually recover and their intensity returns to the original states with increasing voltage, confirming that the lithium ions in plane triangular-like cavities consisting of high conjugated sp-C and interlayer distance in the sp2-C region are removed and extracted within the fully lithiation state to 1.0 V. As the charge process proceeded, the characteristic peaks at 1921 cm–1 appeared, and then the intensity of characteristic peaks become stronger. In the subsequent charging states, −C≡C–C≡C– characteristic peaks at 2166 cm–1 emerged and then constantly enhanced without sacrificing the characteristic peaks at 1921 cm–1. Our results demonstrate that the captured lithium ions are released progressively in different stages because of the attenuated interaction between sp-C and Li+. All these features clearly indicate the good reversibility of GDY electrodes. Besides, we conclude that a reversible delithiation process occurs in large regions (Figures S13–S15). On account of the strongest binding energy between lithium ion and sp-C among the three active sites and lower diffusion barriers, lithium ion will have priority to come into strong interaction between sp-C and Li+ with the formation of Li–C bonds and subsequent insertion into carbon layers among adjacent interlayers as well as filling into the centers of triangular-like cavities.52−54 In view of this novel discovery, a “capture/pore filling-intercalation” mechanism is put forward for lithium-ion storage in GDY. In addition, the in situ EIS is implemented in Figure 3e,f during the discharge states and charge states. It is noteworthy that Rs (solution resistance) and Rct (charge transfer resistance) gradually increased, and their visible boundaries could be seen during the lithiation stage. The value of Rct and Rs could return to the original state after delithiation, suggesting good reversibility. As displayed in Figures 3g,h and S16, we attribute the sloping region to the speedy lithium-ion capture as well as strong Li–C orbital coupling on account of incomplete charge transfer and ascribe the relative platform to the interaction into sp2-C region and nanopores filling the in-plane triangular-like cavities.

We also implemented the galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) to evaluate lithium-ion storage behaviors in different potential ranges, as shown in Figure 4a. Based on the results shown in Figure 4b,c, the lithium-ion storage mechanisms are mainly divided into two different regions, which is consistent with Raman results.55 The rapid lithium-ion coefficients may originate from rapid lithium-ion capture due to the high electronegativity of sp-C.56 Besides, the fast desolvation process on account of high electronegativity of sp-C in GDY is also conductive to rapid lithium diffusion and kinetic processes, as depicted in Figure 4d.57,58 As a consequence, the high electronegativity sp-C can accelerate kinetic processes, achieve a speedy desolvation process, and supply massive active sites, making it a possible obtain high capacity, superior cyclic stability, and excellent rate capability in contrast to other sp2-C electrodes. To further reveal the intercalation sites for the Li+ ion, we made full use of the 7Li nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) strategy to reveal distinguishing Li species in carbon layers during the cyclic process. The chemical shift peaks of 7Li emerge a positive shift during the discharge process and appear at around 1.25 ppm in the fully discharged state. This is assigned to incomplete charge transfer from sp-C regions to Li+ in the initial stage, forming a strong interaction between Li–C and sp2-C regions as well as nanopores filling in in-plane triangular–like cavities, thus affecting the local environments of Li atoms.51,59 The peaks of 7Li return to their original position, demonstrating the good reversibility for the GDY electrode in Figure 4e. As depicted in Figure 4f, we concluded that incomplete charge transfer in high-electronegativity sp-C could induce speedy lithium-ion capture and realize a fast kinetic process in view of an in situ characterization strategy.

In order to offer more visual evidence complementary to Raman results, we performed ex situ XPS with different Ar+ sputtering depths to figure out the chemical and composition evolution of the carbon layers during the first cycle. It was found that the C peak could be deconvoluted into C =C(sp2), C≡C (sp), C–O, C=O, and C=O–C in Figure 5a when the electrode was discharged to 1.2 V. We could distinguish some differences in the relative amount of C=C and C≡C with various sputtering times of 60 and 120 s. These results are from the spatial heterogeneity during lithium-ion intercalation processes.60,61 Surprisingly, the absence of C≡C demonstrates effective active sites for lithium storage at the discharge states of 0.4 and 0.01 V. During the charge process, we could notice the reversible recovery of C≡C, indicating high reversibility (Figure 5b). It is worthy that the binding energy of C 1s shows a slight positive shift when discharged at 0.4 V and a negative shift in the fully discharged state (Figure 5c). This phenomenon is attributed to effective charge transfer from high electronegativity sp-C atoms in GDY to Li ions owing to abundant π electron as well as the formation of Li–C bonds on account of proper interaction in the initial stage and effective negative charge transfer to the sp2 region in GDY nanosheets originating from the π* antibonding band occupied by electrons in the subsequent discharge process.48,62 The binding energy of C 1s shifts to an opposite direction during the charge process. Li 1s can be divided into three characteristic peaks of LiF, LiOH/Li2CO3, and LiCx, as shown in Figures 5d and S17–S21.63 This confirms that a strong interaction would form Li–C bonds during the lithiation process. We found that the amount of LiCx became more conspicuous during the discharge process (Figure 5e) and the relative amount of LiCx was reduced during the charge state (Figure S22). This indicates the good reversibility of the lithium-ion storage mechanism for GDY. Furthermore, the relative content of C=O constantly increases, while the amount of C–O is reduced during the discharge states. The reversible phenomenon is monitored during the charge states (Figures S23–S34). Besides, we could witness dominant C–F in the external and primary Li–F in the internal, demonstrating organic SEI composition on the surface and organic component in the external. The lithium-ion storage in GDY will undergoes rapid lithium-ion capture and forms a strong interaction with the formation of Li–C results. In the subsequent stage, lithium ions would insert into the layer distance in the sp2 region, similar to the behaviors of graphite and fill in in-plane triangular-like cavities. These are consistent with the Raman results.

In addition, we exploit in situ XRD to investigate the structural changes during the Li+ storage process. As depicted in Figure 6a–c, the peak position of (002) for GDY has no apparent shifts, confirming constant interlayer distance accompanying with the relative amount variation of lithium ions during the lithiation and delithiation processes.17,49,55,64,65 We could come into the analogous conclusion since an interlayer distance of 0.370 nm at the end of discharge or charge process and an interlayer distance of 0.370 nm with pristine states are observed in Figure 6d–f. As demonstrated in Figure 6g, the large interlayer distance in GDY during the discharge and charge processes could effectively restrain the volume expansion during the cyclic process, achieve a speedy 3D lithium-ion transfer path, and expose abundant active sites, resulting in exceptional cyclic stability and outstanding rate capability of GDY.

Conclusions

In summary, the complex reaction kinetic process and the lithium active site visualization are systematically traced to demonstrate the feature of cation-π effect in GDY. Since there is incomplete charge transfer from alkyne bonds to Li+, electrons from one of the two alkynyl π bonds in high electronegative sp-C for GDY could realize rapid lithium-ion capture and reversible Li–C orbital coupling to accelerate kinetic behaviors, which is in contrast to sp2-C with simply one π bond. More importantly, C≡C in conjugated GDY electrode realizes stepwise lithium-ion capture with proper interaction as well as the formation of reversible Li–C bonds with different voltage regions in the initial stage. In the subsequent low-voltage plateau, in-plane triangular–like cavities made up with conjugated sp-C could realize lithium-ion nanopores filling, except that intercalation into the interlayer distance in the sp2 regions are viewed with a similar behavior to that of graphite. Interestingly, it is a reversible process for lithiation and delithiation into GDY, accompanied by constant interlayer distance. The capture/pores filling-intercalation hybrid mechanisms endowed the sp and sp2 hybridized GDY electrodes with high specific capacity (1435 mAh g–1 at 0.1 A/g), and fast Li+ diffusion. This work revealed the unique feature of sp-C in accelerating ion diffusion and the exceptional functions of sp-sp2 cohybridized carbon atoms in different voltage plateau regions. The analytical models here have gained a deeper understanding of the storage mechanism as well as active sites visualization in the redox process and established a favorable correlation of structure-mechanism-performance in GDY. These distinct hybrid mechanisms will supplement the profound appreciation and open a new perspective to devise electrodes from the aspect of structure and electronic characters for rechargeable batteries to realize outstanding capability and brilliant security for energy storage systems.
