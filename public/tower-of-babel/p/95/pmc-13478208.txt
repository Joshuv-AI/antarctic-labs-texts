A portable Cas6f-based system for multiplex translational repression in bacteria
Authors: Giusi Favoino, Denis Pšenka, Lea Frideres, Daniel C. Volke, Pablo I. Nikel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Engineered small RNAs (sRNAs) enable programmable gene knockdowns and support metabolic engineering and multiplex regulation in model bacteria. Still, precise, tunable, and multiplex gene repression remains a challenge in synthetic biology. Common tools can impose genetic burden, depend on host RNA factors, or do not transfer well across species. Here we present MORTISE (Multiplex, ORthogonal Translation Interference SystEm), a compact Cas6f-based platform for programmable translational repression in Gram-negative bacteria. The system functions without host Hfq or RNases and operates robustly in Escherichia coli and Pseudomonas putida. We demonstrate repression in both species using chromosomal reporter assays, with performance improving when guide and target transcription are matched and when the translation initiation region is targeted. Single-promoter multiplexing enables simultaneous knockdowns and a cloning toolbox facilitates assembly of up to nine guides in a single step. Finally, MORTISE is leveraged to boost malonyl-coenzyme A–dependent production in P. putida, supporting pathway balancing.

Introduction

Over the years, synthetic biology shifted from single-gene edits to routine, multi-locus rewiring and installation of complex, modular circuits1–4. A broad toolkit enables extensive, precise rewiring of microbial metabolism at will. Substantial progress in the field has been driven by multiplex genome engineering methods, e.g., λ-Red recombineering, precision base-editing, and CRISPR/Cas9 technologies, and by multi-part DNA assembly strategies, e.g., USER cloning, Golden Gate cloning, and Gibson assembly5–12. Editing non-essential genes is straightforward13, yet precise and tunable control of competing or regulatory nodes remains challenging, especially in non-canonical microbial hosts14–18. In metabolic engineering, targeted knockdown strategies can be adopted to redirect flux, relieve feedback inhibition, and suppress by-product formation, thereby improving yield and productivity19–23. CRISPR-based interference (CRISPRi) and RNA-mediated knockdown are commonly used for this purpose24,25, but each approach has constraints. CRISPRi, based on a catalytically inactive Cas9 protein (dCas9), is a powerful and widely used strategy for transcriptional repression26,27. In some bacteria, however, high levels of dCas9 expression can cause growth or morphological defects28–30. For this reason, dCas9 expression level and guide design often require careful tuning31–33. In practice, inducible control is commonly used to modulate the onset and magnitude of silencing, especially for essential genes and growth-coupled phenotypes34, and it can also help manage burden in some contexts.

RNA-mediated knockdown using engineered trans-acting bacterial small RNAs (sRNAs) is an established toolset for programmable gene repression35–38. Synthetic MicC-derived sRNAs have been implemented for metabolic engineering in Escherichia coli towards combinatorial knockdown, and later platforms expanded this concept into reusable sRNA pools and fast multiplex, including genome-scale knockdown strategies39–41. These studies showed that bacterial sRNAs can act as compact and tunable regulators for pathway balancing and strain optimization. At the same time, many such systems were developed in E. coli using native Hfq-associated scaffolds, so portability often depends on host context. Importantly, Hfq dependence is not absolute. A truncated RyhB-derived regulator retained silencing activity without Hfq, indicating that at least some engineered sRNA architectures can function with reduced or altered reliance on canonical Hfq binding37. Mechanistically, bacterial sRNAs often repress most strongly when pairing overlaps with the Shine–Dalgarno sequence, the AUG START codon, or the immediate 5′-coding region, where they can directly occlude translation initiation. In several well-studied systems, translational repression alone is sufficient for silencing42, whereas RNA turnover can also involve RNase E-dependent target decay and, in some cases, RNase III-mediated cleavage of sRNA-mRNA duplexes43,44. Both CRISPRi and RNA-mediated knockdown also face some practical limits to multiplexing that can complicate balanced expression across targets. These aspects provide the conceptual background for evaluating whether an orthogonal RNA-processing factor could replace scaffold-dependent stabilization and support portable multiplex repression.

To address these gaps, we harnessed an efficient, orthogonal, and multiplexed translation knockdown system based on the RNA-processing protein Cas6f (formerly known as Csy4) from the type I-F CRISPR-Cas system. Cas6f is a 21.4 kDa endoribonuclease45 isolated from Pseudomonas aeruginosa UCBPP-PA14 that recognizes and cleaves a 16-nt stem-loop with very high efficiency and accuracy46–49. This protein processes its cognate pre-crRNA at the 3′-end, generating mature crRNAs that contain a unique spacer flanked by 8 nt and 20 nt repeats. Cas6f binds both substrate and product with sub-nanomolar affinity, with an equilibrium dissociation constant of ca. 50 pM48, and it remains stably associated with the cleaved hairpin after catalytic processing.

These properties make Cas6f an interesting enzyme to design an RNA-mediated tool for translational control. Stable encapsulation protects RNAs from host RNases that rapidly degrade naked or duplexed transcripts47. Precise cleavage could support the construction of multiplex arrays that are processed into individual guides. Because the entire recognition sequence lies 5′ of the cleavage site, processing leaves no extra nucleotides on the 3′-proximal fragment, preserving guide fidelity. Cas6f is small (188 amino acids), lacking any documented interactions with core metabolic processes, and its 16-nt recognition site is absent from the genome of many organisms, including E. coli and Pseudomonas putida. Cas6f has been leveraged in synthetic biology as an RNA-processing module to eliminate UTR-RBS cross-talk, standardize promoter output, and design operons50; to express multiple guide RNAs from a single transcript across diverse organisms and Cas systems51–56; as a general post-transcriptional regulator through insertion of Cas6f hairpins at defined mRNA sites57; as a translational ON-switch58; in a two-component repression system59; and for transcription activation60. However, Cas6f has not been implemented as a processing and stabilization module for RNA-mediated knockdown. We asked whether Cas6f could provide an orthogonal processing and stabilization layer for an established class of bacterial sRNA regulators. In this framework, the conceptual advance of MORTISE is the coupling of guide maturation, guide protection, and single-transcript multiplexing within a compact Cas6f-based translational interference architecture. An effective, tunable knockdown system for P. putida would be particularly valuable, given the limited performance of conventional RNA-mediated knockdown strategies in this host61,62.

Based on this premise, this study introduces MORTISE, a Cas6f-based platform for multiplexed, orthogonal translational interference with temporal control and broad portability in Gram-negative bacteria. We demonstrated high efficiency in E. coli and P. putida, and we provide a set of toolbox plasmids that enable rapid assembly of RNA-mediated knockdown cassettes with up to nine targets in a single Golden Gate assembly step. The MORTISE acronym evokes the mortise and tenon joint: Cas6f forms a precise ‘mortise’ that accepts the 16-nt recognition hairpin ‘tenon,’ locking each guide in place and enabling modular multiplex assembly. We then applied the system to metabolic engineering in P. putida to raise malonyl-coenzyme A (CoA) availability and boost flaviolin production. Overall, MORTISE delivers rapid, efficient, and programmable translational knockdown across microbial hosts and is readily adaptable to high-throughput functional genomics and fine-tuning of metabolic pathways.

Results and discussion

The MicC architecture does not support efficient translational repression in non-canonical bacteria

While some robust knockdown strategies exist for prokaryotes, tools optimized in E. coli often underperform in alternative hosts because their expression and interoperability have been specifically tuned for the E. coli cellular machinery. Deployment in other hosts, therefore, requires a fresh look into the approaches currently available for this purpose (Fig. 1A). CRISPRi, in which a catalytically inactive Cas protein sterically blocks transcription initiation or elongation, is a widely used strategy for programmable repression in bacteria26. In P. putida and other Gram-negative bacteria, inducible dCas9-based CRISPRi platforms have enabled tunable single-gene and multiplex repression27,63,64, but high dCas9 expression can sometimes impose metabolic burden and toxicity30, and off-target promoter binding has been reported in some contexts31,32. Cis-acting mRNA structures (Fig. 1A) offer high target specificity. These elements generally toggle between two conformations: one masks the ribosome-binding site (RBS), and the other exposes the RBS to allow mRNA translation. Switching can be triggered by a range of signals, including chemical ligands, temperature, or RNA binding. Despite their versatility, these systems require modification of the target gene and heavily depend on neighboring sequences, which complicate implementation and usually yield suboptimal performance. Trans-acting small RNAs (sRNAs) provide another route to modulate expression; in this case, sequences complementary to the target mRNA block translation upon binding and can also promote mRNA degradation. Importantly, the design of such sRNAs is straightforward.

Guided by these considerations, we set out to establish an RNA-mediated knockdown platform adopting Pseudomonas as a non-canonical bacterial host. We selected the E. coli MicC sRNA as the scaffold because of its high efficiency and because the target site resides entirely at the 5′-end of the structure37, which simplifies retargeting (Fig. 1B). The 3′-end folds into two hairpins that recruit the Hfq protein, an RNA chaperone37,67. To test RNA-mediated knockdown in P. putida, we built a constitutive and an inducible XylS/Pm expression module carrying a 24-nt target-binding sequence fused to the MicC scaffold. The binding sequence was designed to complement the 5′-terminus of the gfpmut3* gene, beginning at the START (ATG) codon. GFPmut3* is a mutant protein, ca. 20-fold more fluorescent than the wild-type68 and displaying a serine-to-arginine change in position 2 of the protein69; we refer to this variant as GFP for simplicity. The resulting cassette was cloned on a broad-host-range, giving rise to plasmid pSEVA638·MicC·α-GFP (Fig. 1C). In parallel, a gfpmut3* module under transcriptional control of the constitutive PA1-04/03 promoter was integrated at the Tn7 attachment site (attTn7). Strains carrying the fluorescent reporter as a genomic insertion, together with either plasmid pSEVA238·MicC·α-GFP induced with 3-methylbenzoate (3-mBz), pSEVA231·MicC·α-GFP, or an empty vector, were grown to the stationary phase. Translational GFP repression was quantified by normalizing the endpoint fluorescence to biomass (estimated as the optical density at 600 nm, OD600) to obtain specific normalized fluorescence values; expressing these values relative to the empty-vector control reports the fold change in fluorescence, which reflects repression. In these experiments, the constitutive architecture did not lead to any significant translational repression, while the inducible MicC-mediated interference reduced GFP fluorescence by ca. 39% relative to the control (Fig. 1D). We next asked whether this modest effect reflects host-factor dependencies and therefore benchmarked MicC against RNA-silencing and CRISPRi designs previously evaluated in P. putida.

Traditional RNA-based tools often depend on host RNA-processing factors. Hfq is commonly required for sRNA-mRNA complex stability and activity, and it promotes their interaction. Differences in Hfq availability, functionality, or auxiliary factors can compromise the portability of E. coli sRNA scaffolds70, especially because this RNA chaperone has been shown to display divergent functionality in P. putida71. Consistent with our results, when the E. coli MicC scaffold was evaluated across Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, knockdown of fluorescent reporters was generally low. Apura et al.62 reported 20%-40% repression in P. putida using a MicC-based structure. Cho et al.61, in contrast, built a broad-host-range sRNA system using the Bacillus subtilis RoxS scaffold with its cognate Hfq component, which functioned in P. putida and several other species; in this case, GFP knockdown exceeded that of MicC-Hfq but remained modest and incurred a substantial growth penalty, suggesting host-dependent toxicity. Importantly, mRNA repression was nearly undetectable in P. putida61. Although functional, the poor efficiency of MicC- and RoxS-based systems motivates the development of an RNA-mediated knockdown system tailored to P. putida and other Gram-negative bacteria.

Design, architecture, and molecular mechanism of a Cas6f-based translation interference toolset

We implemented a multiplex knockdown system based on Cas6f processing to obtain a more effective translational knockdown platform. To this end, we first designed a standardized interference cassette. In this architecture (Fig. 2A), the cassette is transcribed as a single polycistronic RNA in which 20-nt long target-binding sequences (TBSs) alternate with a 16-nt hairpin that constitutes the Cas6f recognition sequence (termed rs[Cas6f]). The cas6f gene is expressed separately and constitutively from the moderate-strength Ptrc promoter. During transcription, Cas6f cleaves immediately at the 3′-end to each recognition hairpin and remains tightly bound to the newly generated 3′-end. This processing reaction converts the precursor into a pool of sRNAs in which the 5′-segments are complementary to the intended targets, while the 3′-segments are sequestered by Cas6f, protecting the sRNAs from exonucleases and possibly eliminating the need for host chaperones, e.g., Hfq, although this possibility has not been experimentally tested. Hfq is not only an RNA chaperone, but it also supports other processes, e.g., promoting interactions between Hfq-binding sRNAs and their RNA targets72–74. Antisense and synthetic sRNA studies indicate that 15–25 nt of perfect complementarity directed to the Shine-Dalgarno motif at the RBS and START-codon region is sufficient to block translation with single-gene precision75–77. Based on prior work on Hfq-binding bacterial sRNAs, we expected guides targeting the translation initiation region to silence primarily by ribosome occlusion, mediated by base pairing of the guide to its cognate mRNA and blocking translation initiation (Fig. 2A). In canonical bacterial sRNA regulation, base pairing across the Shine-Dalgarno sequence, the AUG START codon, or the immediate 5′-coding region is often sufficient for strong translational repression, and target mRNA destruction is typically not required for the initial silencing event42. RNase-dependent decay could nevertheless reinforce repression in some systems78,79, particularly for non-canonical targeting architectures or when the sRNA guides RNase E-mediated cleavage. We therefore considered RNA decay as a possible secondary contribution to the phenomenon. In E. coli and related Proteobacteria, for instance, RNase E cleaves nearby single-stranded regions, which then undergo 3′-to-5′ exonucleolytic degradation80, thereby lowering the effective mRNA concentration81. Ribosome occlusion is expected to predominate because it is factor-independent, supporting portability across bacterial hosts.

Validation of Cas6f-mediated knockdown

To quantify MORTISE performance, we first targeted a chromosomal fluorescent reporter. Based in the configuration adopted for the preliminary MicC tests, we integrated gfpmut3* at the conserved attTn7 site of E. coli and P. putida (Fig. 2B), thereby avoiding positional and strain-specific transcription effects. In this setup, the transcription of gfpmut3* was driven either by the synthetic constitutive PA1-04/03 promoter82 or by an inducible, phage-derived RNA polymerase promoter matched to the cognate polymerase encoded in the chromosome (Fig. 2C). The Cas6f interference module was supplied on a medium-copy, broad-host-range plasmid83 bearing either the oriV(pBBR1) or oriV(RSF1010) origin of replication and the aphA1 marker for kanamycin or neomycin resistance (Fig. 2B). The strains and plasmids used in these assays are listed in Supplementary Tables S1, S2. Our plasmid nomenclature followed the Standard European Vector Architecture (SEVA) conventions84,85: the first digit denotes the antibiotic resistance cassette (e.g., # 2 for kanamycin), the second digit indicates the origin of replication [e.g., # 3 for oriV(pBBR1) or # 5 for oriV(RSF1010)], and the third digit identifies the promoter that drives sRNA expression; the targeted transcript is indicated by a terminal α suffix (Fig. 2C). For E. coli experiments, we used strain SEGA058, which expresses the T7 RNA polymerase gene from the IPTG-responsive PlacUV5 promoter86. For P. putida, we employed a strain expressing the orthogonal Phi15 RNA polymerase gene from the XylS/Pm system, which enables tight and stable induction of the Pphi15 promoter with 3-mBz87. The corresponding isogenic backgrounds, P. putida KT2440 and E. coli MG1655, were used for testing the constitutive MORTISE system.

Each knockdown cassette was transcribed from the same promoter that controlled gfpmut3* in the inducible architecture (i.e., PT7 or Pphi15) or from the strong synthetic P14c promoter in the constitutive setup (Fig. 2C). The transcript comprised an rs[Cas6f] motif at the 5′-end, a 20-nt sequence complementary to the first 20 nt of the gfpmut3* mRNA starting at the ATG codon, and a second rs[Cas6f] structure lying at the 3′-end (Fig. 2B). Cas6f cleavage at both recognition sites generated a mature 36-nt sRNA with a 5′-terminus that precisely matches the coding region of the target, minimizing additional nucleotides that could weaken duplex stability or promote off-target binding. Oligonucleotides used for cassette assembly are listed in Supplementary Table S3. Furthermore, we constructed three promoter variants that differed only in the promoter placed upstream of the sRNA cassette. In the inducible design, matching promoters synchronized guide-RNA and reporter-mRNA output; the P14c promoter served as the benchmark for maximal constitutive knockdown. Relative fluorescence values were calculated against the empty-vector control pSEVA2513 (Fig. 2D, E); the corresponding normalized fluorescence data are provided in Supplementary Fig. S1A–D.

In E. coli, the inducible T7-based system achieved a 57% repression of GFP, whereas the constitutive configuration mediated 91% repression (Fig. 2D). This difference likely reflects the very high transcriptional output afforded by the T7 RNA polymerase, which produces an excess of GFP mRNA relative to the available Cas6f and limits complete target engagement. In P. putida, in contrast, the inducible MORTISE system outperformed the constitutive one, achieving 86% repression compared to 69% (Fig. 2E), consistent with a more favorable balance among Cas6f levels, sRNA abundance, and target expression under inducible conditions. These results expose the importance of tuning transcriptional rates within the knockdown module and help explain why other knockdown strategies failed when transferred to microbial hosts beyond E. coli.

To evaluate the potential physiological burden associated with the MORTISE platform, we compared the growth of E. coli and P. putida carrying either an empty vector or the MORTISE plasmids (Supplementary Fig. S2). In both hosts, the plasmid carrying the complete knockdown cassette (sRNA and cas6f) caused a modest growth defect relative to the empty vector control, which was mainly reflected in longer lag phases, especially for E. coli. In E. coli SEGA058, strong T7 promoter-driven gene expression imposed the strongest burden (with a ca. 2-fold longer lag phase). In all the other conditions, the effect was rather limited, and all strains reached comparable final OD600 as the control condition.

In order to explore if MORTISE-mediated repression depends on the combined action of the sRNA and Cas6f rather than on the sRNA alone, we generated two control plasmids based on pMORTISE·251::α-GFP. One carried a catalytically inactive Cas6f variant harboring the H29A substitution48 (plasmid pMORTISE[dCas6f]·251::α-GFP), and one lacking the cas6f gene entirely (plasmid pMORTISE[∆Cas6f]·251::α-GFP) (Supplementary Fig. S3A). These plasmids were tested in P. putida KT2440 attTn7::gfp and, in contrast to the original MORTISE plasmid, neither the dCas6f nor the no-Cas6f variant constructs could repressed GFP (Supplementary Fig. S3B). These results show that MORTISE-mediated repression requires an active Cas6f protein and cannot be achieved by the guide RNA alone.

In P. putida, MORTISE outperforms prior sRNA tools in efficiency and growth compatibility, reflecting portability conferred by orthogonality to host-specific factors. Furthermore, the system is also effective in E. coli, particularly when the promoter driving cas6f expression matches the strength of the promoter controlling the target gene. These findings validate MORTISE as a robust, portable platform for targeted translational repression and indicate that matching guide and target transcription is a primary lever for maximizing knockdown across hosts.

Versatility of the Cas6f architecture across RNA scaffolds

We tested whether Cas6f can be combined with alternative scaffolds to enable translational knockdown. To this end, we started by evaluating in P. putida an architecture shown to be highly efficient in E. coli35. A plasmid was constructed that carries a 20-nt targeting sequence flanked by a 5′-hammerhead ribozyme and a 3′-optimized Hfq-binding scaffold derived from the Spot42 sRNA of E. coli (Supplementary Fig. S4A). In its native context, Spot42, also known as Spf, acts as a regulator of carbohydrate metabolism and catabolite repression. The 5′-ribozyme cleaves co-transcriptionally to generate a base-pair-precise 5′-end and to remove the upstream sequence that could destabilize sRNA folding. The resulting construct was placed under XylS/Pm control (plasmid pS238·GFP-sRNA), and the expression was induced with 3-mBz across a range of concentrations (0.01 to 10 mM). To quantify Spot42 performance in P. putida, we targeted the same chromosomally integrated, constitutively expressed gfpmut3* gene used previously. Although induction of the system was responsive to the addition of 3-mBz, GFP repression was rather low, with a maximum of 23% relative to the empty-vector control (Supplementary Fig. S4B). We hypothesized that weak sRNA output from the inducible system limited repression efficacy. Accordingly, we replaced the XylS/Pm system with the strong constitutive P14c promoter, yielding a new Spot42-ribozyme architecture (pS2514·Spot42-ribozyme) (Fig. 3A). This modification increased repression both in E. coli (93%, Fig. 3B) and P. putida (56%, Fig. 3C). The host-dependent disparity was unexpected, but we identified cultivation temperature (i.e., 37 °C for E. coli and 30 °C for P. putida) as a likely factor affecting the results, because temperature is known to affect the folding and catalytic rate of the hammerhead ribozyme. To test this possibility and to assess compatibility with Cas6f processing, we replaced the hammerhead with a Cas6f recognition site and co-expressed cas6f (resulting in the Spot42-Cas6f construct, Fig. 3A). Upon binding, Cas6f should cleave at the recognition hairpin and produce the same 5′-end as the ribozyme. Given the higher catalytic rate of Cas6f, we anticipated faster, temperature-robust maturation of the knockdown structure. We asked whether the Hfq-binding scaffold could also be replaced by the Cas6f recognition hairpin (in a ribozyme-Cas6f construct, Fig. 3A). As indicated above, the Spot42 scaffold recruits Hfq, which protects sRNAs and engages RNase E via a specific binding interface in E. coli88. Because Cas6f remains bound to the processed 3′-end, the Cas protein could shield the resulting sRNA structure, analogously to the Hfq functionality. Indeed, replacing the Hfq scaffold with the Cas6f recognition site enabled GFP repression in both hosts, reaching 97% in E. coli (Fig. 3B) and 34% in P. putida (Fig. 3C). These results indicate that Cas6f can functionally replace the 5′ ribozyme and improve guide maturation, while the Spot42 scaffold contributes positively to translational silencing in P. putida.

These findings show that the Cas6f module can be integrated into existing RNA-mediated knockdown designs both to substitute for Hfq and to enhance sRNA processing in Gram-negative bacteria. In P. putida, using the Cas6f recognition element for both maturation and protection, as in MORTISE, remains the most effective configuration.

We built four toolbox variants that differ in the promoter driving sRNA transcription and in the origin of replication (Fig. 4A), respecting the SEVA nomenclature. One such MORTISE plasmid includes the strong constitutive P14c promoter (# 1). Three inducible options expand host and use-case flexibility: PT7, optimized for use in E. coli (# 3); Pphi15, optimized for P. putida (# 4); and PrhaBAD, which is activated by RhaR/RhaS in the presence of rhamnose (# 2). All PrhaBAD components, including rhaR and rhaS, are encoded on the plasmid itself, allowing function without phage polymerases and supporting hosts that lack those specific systems. This modular layout allows rapid customization and deployment of MORTISE across diverse experimental contexts without extensive recloning.

For multiplex knockdown, multiple sRNA cassettes must be easily and quickly assembled in one construct. Golden Gate cloning is well-suited for building repetitive arrays; however, arbitrary 4-nt fusion sites can add additional bases at the 5′-end of the target-binding sequence and weaken pairing specificity. To avoid this potential problem, we anchored the fusion sites within rs[Cas6f], the 16-nt Cas6f recognition sequence (Fig. 4B). All 13 overlapping 4-nt segments of rs[Cas6f] (positions 1-4, 2-5, 3-6, through 13–16) were generated with a sliding-window approach and evaluated with the NEBridge Ligase Fidelity Tool89 to exclude palindromic or high-mismatch overhangs. Using this strategy, up to eight sRNA modules can be easily assembled in a single reaction; an optional terminal linker maintains a uniform 55-nt oligonucleotide length when required for DNA synthesis or processing. If the final-unit length constraint is not relevant, a ninth sRNA module can replace the linker. Supplementary File S1 provides detailed guidance for sRNA design as a function of target number.

We validated multiplex assembly by constructing plasmids with one to eight sRNA units plus the optional linker. Initial cloning efficiency, scored by loss of fluorescence, remained high across all constructs with > 98% non-fluorescent colonies even for the most complex arrays (Fig. 4C). Eight non-fluorescent colonies per assembly were then screened by colony PCR (Fig. 4C and Supplementary Fig. S5). Constructs bearing one, two, three, or six modules consistently showed 100% correct inserts. Slightly reduced accuracy (yet > 98%) was observed for four-, five-, and seven-module assemblies. Even with an eight-module configuration ca. 40% of the assemblies (three of eight colonies) were correct. Incorrect clones were incomplete assembly events that removed one or more intended parts (Supplementary Fig. S5C), rather than constructs displaying a wrong sequence. Given the high overall efficiency and the clear dropout readout, selecting a correct clone is straightforward and time-efficient, a feature absent in other RNA-mediated knockdown systems.

These data establish that MORTISE enables multiplexed repression in both bacterial species.

Ribosome occlusion independent of host RNases as the mechanistic basis of MORTISE silencing

To test whether MORTISE silences gene expression primarily by preventing ribosome loading onto the target mRNA, we quantified GFP knockdown in E. coli and P. putida using sRNAs complementary to distinct regions of the gfpmut3* transcript. Six guides were designed to this end: GFP1 targeted − 20 to − 1 relative to the START codon (ATG); GFP2 covered − 2 to + 17; GFP3 matched + 1 to + 21 (the first 20 nt of the coding sequence); and GFP4, GFP5, and GFP6 annealed at +70, +316, and +549, respectively (Fig. 6A). The sequences and specific binding energies of the guides, ranging from – 25 to – 30 kcal mol–1, are reported in Supplementary Table S4. In E. coli, GFP1, GFP2, and GFP3 each reduced GFP fluorescence by > 70% (Fig. 6B). These guides overlap the translation initiation region: GFP1 and GFP2 cover the Shine-Dalgarno sequence, whereas GFP3 overlaps the first codon of the open reading frame. Recognition of both elements by the translation initiation complex is essential for the onset of translation95. In contrast, sRNAs positioned 70 nt or more downstream produced no detectable repression, consistent with a requirement for steric occlusion of the ribosome. Although GFP4, GFP5, and GFP6 showed a declining trend in knockdown with increased distance from the RBS, differences relative to the negative control are not significant (Fig. 6B). The results in P. putida mirrored those in E. coli. GFP1, GFP2, and GFP3 each reduced GFP fluorescence by ca. 50% relative to the empty vector, while guides positioned at + 70 or farther downstream produced no detectable repression (Fig. 6C). To test whether endogenous ribonucleases contribute to MORTISE activity, we evaluated repression in two strains derived from P. putida KT2440 with deleted or reduced activity of RNase III or RNase E96 (Fig. 6D). RNase III, encoded by rnc, is a conserved double-stranded RNA endonuclease whose substrate selection depends on helix length, base-pair stability, and specific nucleotide pairs97–99. Because MORTISE guides form an RNA duplex with the target, RNase III could, in principle, cleave the duplex. In the RNase III-deletion strain used here, nucleotides 210 to 222 were removed, and a STOP codon was inserted, yielding a truncated 74-amino-acid protein; this design preserves expression of the essential downstream era gene while inactivating RNase III. This strain96 is denoted P. putida KT2440 rnc(Δ210_222::STOP) (Fig. 6D). RNase E, encoded by rne, is an essential single-stranded endoribonuclease central to mRNA decay and to the processing of many native sRNAs, thereby modulating their stability and activity44,100–103. In the RNase E mutant, the C-terminal coding region from amino acids 589 to 1,087 was deleted [P. putida KT2440 rne(Δ1765_3262)]96, removing the degradosome and Hfq interaction domain and reducing catalytic function without compromising viability (Fig. 6D).

Next, we integrated the same chromosomal gfpmut3* reporter (Fig. 6A) into each mutant background and introduced either an empty vector or a MORTISE plasmid encoding a single sRNA complementary to the first 20 nt of the gfpmut3* mRNA. In both RNase-deficient strains, GFP knockdown matched the wild-type background (Fig. 6E), indicating that these individual ribonucleases are not required for MORTISE-mediated mRNA silencing. This experimental outcome aligns with a mechanism in which Cas6f primarily represses translation by sterically blocking ribosome access rather than accelerating mRNA decay, with auxiliary RNases playing little or no role. Moreover, RNase III, which could act on RNA duplexes, most likely cannot access the guide-target duplex due to the steric shielding provided by Cas6f bound at the processed 3′-end. Independence from host RNA-processing machinery benefits metabolic engineering because the same architecture can function across diverse genetic backgrounds; the same construct also performs effectively in E. coli. Consequently, the Cas6f-based platform provides a broadly portable translational knockdown tool that does not rely on species-specific RNA processing pathways, simplifying application across strains. Collectively, these findings support a model in which Cas6f alone mediates guide RNA maturation and sterically occludes ribosome loading, with little or no contribution from host RNases.

Translational repression increases malonyl-CoA–dependent production in engineered P. putida

To demonstrate the biotechnological utility of MORTISE, we aimed to increase the intracellular concentration of malonyl-CoA, a key precursor for lipids, polyketides, and fatty acid-related products104. Intracellular malonyl-CoA is typically low and limits the production of malonyl-CoA–derived molecules; raising its pool is therefore a strategy to improve yields in metabolic engineering105. Because many competing pathways are essential (e.g., fatty acid synthesis), a temporally controlled repression strategy is advantageous.

Building on this background, we used MORTISE to inhibit further malonyl-CoA consumption. We targeted fabD and fabF (encoding malonyl-CoA–ACP transacylase and β-ketoacyl-ACP synthase II, respectively; two key steps of fatty acid synthesis) and sucA (encoding subunit E1 of the 2-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase in the tricarboxylic acid cycle). We introduced these perturbations with the MORTISE platform in strains SEM11 + rppA and SEM11 ΔhexR Δgcd Δpha + rppA to test whether RNA-mediated knockdown could be adopted as a general strategy to enhance flaviolin production. We constructed a set of plasmids derived from pMORTISE·251 carrying a guide against each gene individually (plasmids pMORTISE·251::α-fabD, pMORTISE·251::α-fabF, and pMORTISE·251::α-sucA) or the three simultaneously (plasmid pMORTISE·251::α-fabD-fabF-sucA, Fig. 7B).

Flaviolin production in strain SEM11 ΔhexR Δgcd Δpha + rppA was slightly higher than in P. putida SEM11 + rppA, consistent with redirected flux toward malonyl-CoA (Fig. 7C). Repression of any single target (fabD, fabF, or sucA) substantially increased flaviolin production in both genetic backgrounds (Fig. 7C), confirming that each intervention elevates the intracellular malonyl-CoA pool. Each target boosted the relative flaviolin content (normalized to the empty-vector control) by > 40%. However, the plasmid harboring a triple sRNAs against fabD, fabF, and sucA did not yield a synergistic increase beyond the effect of the individual knockdowns. This observation can be explained by two factors. Simultaneous expression of three sRNAs partitions Cas6f across competing guides, reducing per-target knockdown; and excessive interference with lipid biosynthesis can trigger metabolic feedback that lowers malonyl-CoA formation105, offsetting gains from diverting flux away from the tricarboxylic acid cycle and fatty acid synthesis. This non-additivity likely reflects guide competition at the RNA level and pathway-level feedback, highlighting the need to calibrate repression depth when tuning central metabolite availability. Nevertheless, MORTISE increased flaviolin production through temporal downregulation of genes involved in essential metabolic pathways, demonstrating practical utility for metabolic engineering. This observation provides indirect evidence for reduced flux through these nodes rather than a direct measurement of endogenous protein levels, but the biosensor response is consistent with partial functional repression of the targeted genes. These results establish Cas6f-mediated translational control as a practical lever for tuning essential flux nodes in production hosts.

MORTISE offers a compact alternative to established gene repression platforms

MORTISE is a compact, plasmid-based Cas6f platform that enables programmable, multiplexed translational knockdown without prior modification of the target gene. The system does not rely on host Hfq or endogenous RNases and performs in both E. coli and P. putida, demonstrating portability across Gram-negative bacteria with minimal retuning. Positioning MORTISE relative to CRISPRi, a widespread technology used for repression, clarifies when each approach is preferable. CRISPRi, based on dCas9, is a powerful strategy that can approximate null phenotypes in P. putida. Prior studies reported ca. 80% repression when targeting the − 35 region of a promoter, an 11-fold reduction under rhamnose induction, and ca. 90% knockdown under XylS/Pm transcriptional control of dCas927,110–112. However, CRISPRi performance can be influenced by dCas9 dosage and guide design. In some bacterial contexts, high dCas9 expression imposes a burden or growth defects28, and some guides can trigger off-target silencing and toxicity, further contributing to the burden. Careful temporal control of dCas9 expression levels and guide selection is therefore important for efficient CRISPRi32,113. In our hands, MORTISE imposed negligible growth costs under both constitutive and inducible expression of the cognate constructs. Mechanistically, CRISPRi acts at the transcriptional level and often exhibits delayed onset while preexisting mRNA decays, whereas Cas6f operates at translation initiation and delivers strong repression without engaging host RNA processing pathways. In practice, MORTISE provides precise, titratable reductions in protein output with a lighter genetic load. This profile suits pathway balancing and gene tuning, where partial knockdown is often preferable to full silencing, and it fills a complementary niche alongside CRISPRi by offering distinct practical advantages.

In the context of CRISPRi repression, dCas12a-based platforms also support multiplexing through autonomous processing of crRNA arrays and can deliver strong transcriptional repression with a single promoter and array114,115. MORTISE is conceptually parallel in that guides are generated from a single RNA transcript, but it is mechanistically distinct. Cas6f processing yields sRNAs that act at translation initiation, avoiding DNA targeting and removing the need for a large DNA-binding effector. In this architecture, the small endoribonuclease Cas6f both processes and remains bound to compact sRNAs that directly repress translation, reducing reliance on high expression of large auxiliary proteins.

Multiplexing further differentiates the platforms. CRISPRi in P. putida achieved dual knockdowns of mRFP1 and YFP in the 55-65% range, but required a separate PEM7 promoter for each sRNA that, in our hands, led to increased cellular burden and complicated transcriptional balancing27. In MORTISE, all guides are encoded on a single polycistronic transcript; Cas6f processes this precursor into individual sRNAs and remains bound to each fragment for repression. A single promoter and one effector gene suffice irrespective of target number, which reduces plasmid size, transcriptional load, and design complexity while maintaining efficacy.

Methods

Bacterial strains and growth conditions

The bacterial strains used in this study are listed in Table S1. E. coli strains were routinely cultured at 37 °C, while P. putida strains were incubated at 30 °C. Lysogeny broth (LB, 10 g L–1 tryptone, 5 g L–1 yeast extract, and 5 g L–1 NaCl) was used for overnight (16 h) cultures and pre-cultures. Cultures were incubated in a MaxQTM 8000 incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific Co., Waltham, MA, USA) with constant shaking at 250 rpm. P. putida strains were routinely cultivated in a minimal salt medium (MSM)116, containing 3.88 g L–1 K2HPO4, 1.63 g L–1 NaH2PO4, 2 g L–1 (NH4)2SO4, and 0.1 g L–1 MgCl₂∙6H2O, added with a trace element solution composed of 10 mg L–1 ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 2 mg L–1 ZnSO4 ∙ 7H2O, 1 mg L–1 CaCl2 ∙ 2H2O, 5 mg L–1 FeSO4 ∙ 7H2O, 0.2 mg L–1 Na2MoO4 ∙ 2H2O, 0.2 mg L–1 CuSO4 ∙ 5H2O, 0.4 mg L–1 CoCl2 ∙ 6H2O, and 1 mg L–1 MnCl2 ∙ 2H2O, with 30 mM glucose. E. coli strains were cultivated in M9 minimal medium, containing M9 salts117 (3 g L–1 KH2PO4, 0.5 g L–1 NaCl, 6.78 g L–1 Na2HPO4, and 1 g L–1 NH4Cl), 0.24 g L–1 MgSO4·7H2O, 11 mg L–1 CaCl2, and 30 mM glucose. When required, filter-sterilized antibiotic solutions were added (gentamicin, 10 μg mL–1; kanamycin, 50 μg mL–1). For selection of ΔgalK::mRFP1 derivatives of E. coli MG1655, M9 agar plates were supplemented with 0.2% (w/v) 2-deoxygalactose, 0.2% (w/v) glycerol, and gentamicin.

Reagents and plasmid construction

A complete list of plasmids used in this study is provided in Supplementary Table S2. All oligonucleotides were synthesized by Integrated DNA Technologies Inc. (Leuven, Belgium), and their sequence are listed in Supplementary Table S3.

Colony PCR was carried out using OneTaqTM DNA Polymerase (New England Biolabs Inc., Ipswich, MA, USA). MORTISE toolbox plasmids were assembled from SEVA backbones by uracil-excision (USER) cloning118. Oligonucleotides were designed using the AMUSER tool119. In amplification steps for USER cloning, Phusion U Hot StartTM DNA Polymerase (Thermo Fisher Scientific Co., Waltham, MA, USA) was used according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Plasmid DNA was purified using the NucleoSpinTM plasmid EasyPure and PCR amplicons using NucleoSpinTM gel and PCR clean-up kit (Macherey-Nagel GmbH, Düren, Germany), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Sanger sequencing was conducted by Eurofins Genomics (Ebersberg, Germany) using Mix2SeqTM kits; Oxford Nanopore sequencing was performed by Plasmidsaurus (San Francisco, CA, USA).

Golden Gate cloning120 was used to build MORTISE plasmids carrying one or multiple target sRNAs. Each target sequence was ordered as a forward and reverse complementary oligonucleotide, as detailed in Supplementary Data 1, and annealing was performed by resuspending the two oligonucleotides in nuclease-free water (Milli-Q) to a concentration of 100 µM and adding 2 µL each of forward and reverse oligonucleotide to T4 ligase buffer in a final volume of 50 µL. The mix was incubated at 95 °C for 5 min and then cooled down to room temperature. The annealed insert suspension was diluted 1:200 in Milli-Q water; 2 µL of each annealed insert were used in a Golden Gate reaction with the NEBridge Golden Gate Assembly Kit (BsaI-HF v2) and the backbone plasmid, essentially by following the manufacturer’s instructions. For the highest efficiency of cloning, the following program was used: 30 cycles of 37 °C for 5 min and 16 °C for 5 min, 65 °C for 10 min (T4 ligase inactivation), 37 °C for 30 min (digestion of backbone DNA without the insert), and 80 °C for 20 min (BsaI inactivation). The 20-nt sequence complementary to each target mRNA were selected from the first 20 nt of the corresponding coding sequences, unless otherwise specified. The binding energy of each RNA duplex was calculated using the RNAup tool121 and is listed in Supplementary Table S4.

Plasmids were transformed in chemically-competent E. coli DH5α λpir by adding a 5 µL aliquot of the cloning mix to 50 µL competent cell suspension and incubated on ice for 15 min122–124. Then, heat shock was performed by incubating the transformation mix in a 42 °C water bath for 35 s. After cooling for 5 min on ice, 1 mL of LB was added and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C with agitation at 600 rpm. In the last step, the cells were spread on an LB agar plate with antibiotics and incubated overnight at 37 °C. For plasmids containing sRNAs targets, non-fluorescent colonies after overnight incubation on LB plates were selected for colony PCR and sequencing.

Transformation of E. coli and P. putida

Bacteria were inoculated in LB and incubated in an orbital shaker overnight (16 h) for P. putida or until OD600 reached 0.4 for E. coli. Cells were pelleted, washed four times with 1 mL 0.3 M sucrose, and resuspended in 400 µL of the same solution. For electroporation, 50 µL of the resuspended cells were mixed with 1 µL of the plasmid suspension and the mix was transferred to a cuvette for electroporation. A single pulse was applied at 1.8 kV for ca. 5 ms. Immediately after pulsing, 1 mL LB was added for recovery, and cells were incubated at their optimal temperature, with agitation at 600 rpm, for 2 h. In the last step, cells were plated on LB agar with antibiotics and incubated overnight at 30 °C (P. putida) or 37 °C (E. coli).

Strain engineering

To construct all the strains carrying attTn7::gfpmut3*, the fluorescent reporter gene was inserted into the unique attTn7 site with a mini-Tn7 transposon via electroporation using plasmid pBK-miniTn7-gfp2, carrying the transposon125, and the pTnS2 helper plasmid126. Colonies exhibiting green fluorescence on LB agar with gentamicin after overnight incubation were selected for further processing. P. putida SEM11 PP_0013::phi15rnap(RBS-D), termed P. putida P15, was used for inducible expression of the RNA-mediated knockdown system and gfpmut3*. This strain harbors the Phi15 RNA polymerase gene as a stable insertion87. Plasmid pPUT-ST, described by Lammens et al.87, was modified to remove the P14g promoter and the BCD2 element127 and used to insert the gfpmut3* module under control of the Pphi15 promoter at the attTn7 locus.

To construct E. coli MG1655 attTn7::gfpmut3* ΔgalK::mRFP1, the galactokinase gene (galK) was replaced with a constitutively expressed mRFP1 using recombineering. The recombination machinery was expressed from the temperature-sensitive plasmid pSIM19, which encodes the λ Red proteins (Exo, Beta, and Gam) under control of the cI857 repressor128. E. coli was electroporated with plasmid pSIM19 and recovered at 30 °C in LB. The mRFP1 gene, including a promoter and terminator, was amplified by PCR from the pQURE6 plasmid129, with primers containing 50-nt arms homologous to the chromosomal galK flanking regions. The PCR product was purified using the NucleoSpin Gel and PCR Clean-up kit. An overnight culture of E. coli MG1655 attTn7::gfpmut3* carrying plasmid pSIM19 was diluted (1:100) into 50 mL LB and grown to OD600 = 0.5. The λ Red system was induced by heat shock at 42 °C for 15 min, followed by incubation on ice for 20 min. Cells were washed with ice-cold water, resuspended in 15% (v/v) glycerol, and electroporated with 200 ng purified donor DNA (1.8 kV, ca. 5 ms). After recovery in LB at 37 °C for 1 h to promote loss of plasmid pSIM19, cells were plated on M9 agar with 0.2% (w/v) 2-deoxygalactose and 0.2% (v/v) glycerol to select for ΔgalK::mRFP1 recombinants. Colony PCR130, using the OneTaqTM mix, was employed to verify the successful integration of mRFP1 in the galK locus of E. coli.

The P. putida SEM11 strains containing the biosensor encoded by rppA at the attTn7 locus108 were modified to remove the kanamycin resistance gene using plasmid pE-FLP, which expresses the FLP recombinase gene. The recombinase was induced overnight at 37 °C, as required by the pE-FLP-dependent protocol131. After loss of kanamycin resistance, P. putida strains SEM11 + rppA and SEM11 ΔhexR Δgcd Δpha + rppA were electroporated with the empty vector and the sRNA-containing MORTISE plasmids pMORTISE·251::α-fabD, pMORTISE·251::α-fabF, pMORTISE·251::α-sucA, and pMORTISE·251::α-fabD-fabF-sucA.

Plate reader experiments and fluorescence quantification

For plate reader experiments, cells were pre-grown in 3 mL of minimal medium (either MSM or M9, as required for each experiment) in 12 mL test tubes. After measuring OD600, cells were transferred to 96-well microplates and incubated at 30 °C in a plate reader (Synergy H1, BioTek Instruments). GFP fluorescence was monitored at an excitation wavelength (λexcitation) of 485 nm and an emission wavelength (λemission) of 516 nm. RFP fluorescence was monitored at λexcitation = 587 nm and λemission = 615 nm. Fluorescence was normalized to cell density by monitoring OD600 over time. All growth parameters were calculated using the QurvE software132.

Imaging procedures

Images of agar plates with fluorescent colonies were captured with a UVP ColonyDoc-It Imaging Station (Analytik Jena, Jena, Germany). To visualize the emission of fluorescent colonies, the settings were: filter 2 (GFP filter), epi-lighting blue, and base lighting darkfield. Fluorescence microscopy was performed in a Leica DM4000 B microscopy system at 100 × resolution (F1 type immersion oil) fitted with a TX2 filter63 after the E. coli culture reached stationary phase in LB.

Implementation of a malonyl-CoA biosensor

For flaviolin quantification, the strains containing rppA and the sRNA or negative control plasmid were inoculated in 1 mL of MSM with 30 mM glucose and 5 g L–1 MOPS in 24-deep-well plates for 48 h in an orbital shaker at 30 °C. Next, 100 µL of each culture was diluted 1:2 and transferred to a 96-well plate; the remaining culture was centrifuged for 10 min at 5000 × g. The supernatant containing flaviolin was transferred to a new 96-well plate. Finally, the flaviolin concentration in the supernatant (estimated as the absorbance at 340 nm) and the cell density (OD600) were measured in triplicate in an automated microplate reader (Epoch 2, Biotek Instruments).

Data and statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with two or three biological replicates, each with three technical replicates (as specified in the corresponding figure or table legend), and the mean value of the corresponding parameter ± standard deviation is reported unless indicated otherwise. Data analysis was performed with Microsoft ExcelTM (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA) and GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Statistical analyses were also performed in GraphPad Prism 10.
