Efficacy of ultrasound guidance for arteriovenous access cannulation in haemodialysis patients: a systematic review with quantitative synthesis
Authors: Tsuei-Wun Chang, Hsin-Yi Chen, Yu-Chi Chen, Chiung-Ying Huang, Wei-Ting Wu, Ke-Vin Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUltrasound (US)-guided cannulation has been proposed to improve success rates and safety compared with conventional palpation-guided techniques in haemodialysis patients. This systematic review with quantitative synthesis evaluated the efficacy of US-guided versus palpation-guided cannulation for arteriovenous access.MethodsRandomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing US-guided with conventional cannulation were included. Searches were conducted across five electronic databases from inception to May 2025. Quantitative syntheses were performed where appropriate using Review Manager 5.4, calculating pooled risk ratios (RRs) and mean differences (MDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). To address potential unit-of-analysis errors, data were stratified by reporting unit, namely patient-level versus cannulation-event-level reporting. A random-effects model was employed to provide conservative estimates given the high heterogeneity.ResultsFive RCTs involving 142 unique patients, totaling 2,894 cannulation events, met the inclusion criteria. US-guided cannulation showed a marginal improvement in the first-attempt success rate (RR = 1.08; 95% CI, 1.00–1.16; p = 0.04). However, after excluding the largest event-based reporting study, Wu et al. [24], the leave-one-out sensitivity analysis showed that the effect was no longer statistically significant (RR = 1.07; 95% CI, 0.98–1.16; p = 0.14). The observed overall effect was predominantly driven by trials using cannulation events as the unit of analysis, whereas studies reporting per-patient outcomes showed no significant differences. No definitive clinical or geographical conclusions could be drawn, as apparent variations across trials were identified as statistical artifacts resulting from these unit-of-analysis errors. No statistically significant differences were found in complication rates. Due to substantial methodological heterogeneity in procedure duration definitions, these data were synthesized qualitatively and showed no consistent benefit. High heterogeneity was observed for first-attempt success (I² = 91%) and complications (I² = 89%).ConclusionsAlthough US-guided cannulation shows potential for increasing first-attempt success, the current evidence is limited by significant unit-of-analysis errors and clinical heterogeneity related to the operator learning curve. When accounting for these statistical artifacts, no definitive evidence supports superior efficacy in specific clinical or geographical settings. Future large-scale RCTs should adopt standardized, per-patient reporting to confirm these findings.Protocol registrationPROSPERO Registration Number: CRD42023424434.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12882-026-05193-5.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease affects nearly 10% of the global population and represents a growing challenge for healthcare systems worldwide. As the incidence of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) continues to rise, haemodialysis remains the predominant modality of renal replacement therapy [22]. Successful haemodialysis depends on reliable vascular access, most commonly achieved via arteriovenous access (AVA), including arteriovenous fistulas (AVF) and arteriovenous grafts (AVG). AVFs are generally preferred due to their superior long-term patency and lower complication rates; however, they require a maturation period and necessitate repeated cannulation with large-bore needles [1, 21].

Traditional cannulation techniques rely on tactile palpation and visual assessment, often performed without direct visualization of the vessel lumen. These palpation-based methods can be particularly challenging in patients with deep, tortuous, or immature vessels and are associated with higher rates of infiltration, hematoma, and early access failure [8]. Cannulation failure may necessitate the insertion of a central venous catheter, which significantly increases the risk of infection, hospitalization, and mortality [3]. Reported complication rates during initial AVF use can be as high as 90%, while maturation failure rates remain significant [22].

Ultrasound (US)-guided cannulation has emerged as a promising technique to improve vascular access outcomes by enabling real-time visualization of vessels during needle insertion [4, 11]. The growing availability of portable point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) devices allows for US-guided procedures to be integrated into routine dialysis care, potentially reducing the reliance on centralized imaging facilities [18]. Previous studies have suggested that US-guided cannulation may improve first-attempt success rates, reduce complications, and enhance staff confidence [7, 12, 14, 21]. However, the consistency of these findings remains uncertain across different clinical settings and operator experience levels [8, 18, 23].

This systematic review with quantitative synthesis aimed to evaluate the efficacy of US-guided cannulation compared with traditional palpation-guided techniques in adult haemodialysis patients with arteriovenous access (both AVF and AVG). Outcomes of interest included cannulation success rates, complication incidence, and procedure duration. In addition, this review examined the potential clinical implications of integrating POCUS into routine dialysis practice, while critically evaluating the impact of data reporting units, the operator learning curve, and the overall quality of evidence on clinical efficacy.

Methods

Study protocol

This systematic review with quantitative synthesis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, as shown in Table S1. The study protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the registration number CRD42023424434.

Research design

This systematic review with quantitative synthesis was guided by the following PICO question: Among adult haemodialysis patients with arteriovenous access (AVF or AVG) (population), does US-guided cannulation (intervention), compared with conventional palpation-guided cannulation (comparison), improve clinical outcomes (outcome), specifically the first-attempt cannulation success rate, complication rates, and total procedure duration? The study population comprised adult patients (≥ 18 years) undergoing haemodialysis via either an arteriovenous fistula (AVF) or an arteriovenous graft (AVG). The intervention was US-guided cannulation performed by nurses or dialysis technicians, whereas the comparison was conventional palpation-guided techniques based on tactile or visual assessment. The primary outcome was the first-attempt cannulation success rate. Secondary outcomes included complication incidence (e.g., hematoma, bleeding, or infiltration) and total procedure duration (defined as the time from initial skin contact or ultrasound scanning to successful needle placement).

Literature search

A comprehensive literature search was conducted independently by two reviewers across five major electronic databases: the Cochrane Library, PubMed, CINAHL, Web of Science, and Embase. The search included studies published from database inception up to May 2025. We have updated and rectified the search strategy for Web of Science to ensure it specifically targets US-guided cannulation in haemodialysis (Table S2). Although the PROSPERO registration initially indicated English-language restrictions, the final search was conducted with no language or publication date filters to maximize sensitivity; any inconsistencies between the main text and supplemental tables have been corrected.

Keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) were developed based on the PICO framework and included terms such as “haemodialysis,” “dialysis,” “arteriovenous fistula,” “arteriovenous graft,” “vascular access,” “ultrasound-guided cannulation,” “ultrasonography,” “point-of-care ultrasound,” and “randomized controlled trial.” Boolean operators (“AND,” “OR”) were applied to refine the search strategy and balance sensitivity with specificity. The detailed search strings for each database, including the corrected string for Web of Science, are provided in Table S2.

All identified references were managed using EndNote 21 for deduplication. After removal of duplicates, articles were screened for eligibility by title and abstract, followed by full-text assessment. In addition, the reference lists of relevant systematic reviews and included articles were manually screened to capture any additional eligible studies.

Eligibility criteria and study selection

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: randomized controlled trials (RCTs) enrolling adult patients (≥ 18 years) undergoing haemodialysis via arteriovenous fistula (AVF) or arteriovenous graft (AVG); interventions involving US-guided cannulation performed by nurses or dialysis technicians in chronic haemodialysis settings; and reporting of at least one relevant clinical outcome, including first-attempt cannulation success rate, complication incidence, or procedure duration.

Studies were excluded if they involved animal or in vitro models; vascular access procedures unrelated to routine haemodialysis arteriovenous access (e.g., peripheral intravenous cannulation, central venous catheterization); surgically created or iatrogenic AVF/AVG models outside routine dialysis care; or were non-original articles such as reviews, study protocols, conference abstracts, or reports lacking sufficient clinical outcome data.

Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts against the eligibility criteria to identify potentially relevant studies, with reasons for exclusion recorded. Full-text articles were then assessed independently for final eligibility. Any disagreements at any stage were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer to ensure transparency and reproducibility.

Quality appraisal

For the studies that met the inclusion criteria, data were extracted independently by two authors. Extracted information included the first author, year of publication, study design, country, sample size (stratified by number of patients and total cannulation events), inclusion and exclusion criteria, operator training protocols, ultrasound modality, study duration, measured outcomes, and funding source. To ensure transparency regarding the data structure, we specifically recorded whether the primary outcomes were reported on a per-patient or per-cannulation basis. The methodological quality of each included RCT was independently assessed using the Revised Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool for Randomized Trials (RoB 2) [19]. The tool evaluates five domains: (i) bias arising from the randomization process, (ii) bias due to deviations from intended interventions, (iii) bias due to missing outcome data, (iv) bias in measurement of the outcome, and (v) bias in selection of the reported result. Each domain was rated as “low risk,” “some concerns,” or “high risk.” Given the nature of US-guided cannulation, blinding of personnel was generally not possible; this was critically considered within the ‘measurement of the outcome’ and ‘deviations from intended interventions’ domains. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or, if necessary, by consulting a third author.

Data synthesis and analysis

Quantitative synthesis was conducted using Review Manager (RevMan) version 5.4 when outcome definitions and data structures were sufficiently comparable. For dichotomous outcomes (e.g., cannulation success, complications), risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. For continuous outcomes (e.g., procedure time), mean differences (MDs) with 95% CIs were used. To address the unit-of-analysis error inherent in studies reporting aggregate cannulation events, we employed a random-effects model for all analyses. This approach provides more conservative estimates and wider confidence intervals, partially accounting for the potential correlation of repeated measures within the same patient (clustered data).

Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic according to the Cochrane Handbook [10] and categorized as follows: I2 < 25% (no heterogeneity), 25–49% (low), 50–74% (moderate), and > 75% (high). To explore sources of clinical heterogeneity, subgroup analyses were primarily conducted based on the unit of analysis (patient-level vs. event-level reporting). Although regional differences were initially considered, we prioritized the unit-of-analysis stratification to critically evaluate how the clustering of cannulation events influenced the pooled estimates, as previous regional observations were deemed to be potential statistical artifacts arising from these reporting differences.

Given the small number of included studies, meta-regression and publication bias assessments (e.g., funnel plots) were not performed. Sensitivity analysis was conducted using the leave-one-out method to evaluate the robustness of pooled estimates. Furthermore, we compared results from patient-level studies against the total pooled data to determine if the reported significance was driven by the inflation of sample sizes in event-based studies.

GRADE quality assessment

The certainty of the evidence for each outcome was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach. The assessment covered five domains: (i) risk of bias, (ii) inconsistency, (iii) indirectness, (iv) imprecision, and (v) publication bias. In our assessment, the ‘risk of bias’ and ‘imprecision’ domains were scrutinized for potential unit-of-analysis errors; specifically, evidence was downgraded if the findings were predominantly driven by studies with unadjusted clustered data (cannulation events) rather than unique patients. The overall certainty of the evidence was rated as high, moderate, low, or very low. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion between the two reviewers (T.W.C. and H.Y.C.). The resulting GRADE evidence profiles reflect the caution required when interpreting the pooled results of first-attempt success and procedure duration, given the observed high heterogeneity and statistical clustering.

Results

Description of selected studies

Figure 1 displays the PRISMA flowchart illustrating our literature search. Initially, we identified 122 non-duplicated citations. Following title and abstract screening, 8 articles were subjected to further scrutiny for eligibility. Based on a thorough full-text assessment, three articles were excluded (Table S3): two were not randomized controlled trials, and one lacked relevant outcome data. Consequently, five RCTs were included in the final analysis. The numerical consistency of the selection process has been verified (122 screened, 114 excluded, 8 assessed, 3 excluded, 5 included).

Characteristics of included studies

The five included RCTs (Table 1) were conducted in various clinical settings globally, including the USA, UK, Australia, Singapore, and China. Collectively, these studies enrolled 142 unique adult patients (ranging from 18 to 65 years) with AVF or AVG access, representing a total of 2,894 cannulation events. Individual study sample sizes varied from 9 to 50 participants, while the number of reported cannulation events reached as high as 2,327 in a single study [24].

The follow-up periods ranged from 1 to 24 months, reflecting differences in study design and outcome assessment timelines. The US-guided cannulation interventions utilized various devices, including handheld and portable point-of-care systems. Significant variations in study protocols and operator experience were observed across the included trials. Some studies utilized structured competency evaluations as part of their implementation, while others were pilot trials where operators were in the early phases of the learning curve. Notably, the reporting units varied significantly, with some trials focusing on longitudinal cannulation events and others on per-patient success rates, which contributed to the observed clinical and statistical heterogeneity.

Quality of studies

Methodological quality was appraised using RoB 2.0 [19]. All studies were assigned an overall risk of bias rating of “some concerns,” predominantly due to the inherent difficulty of blinding participants and personnel in US-guided interventions. This lack of blinding resulted in potential concerns regarding deviations from intended interventions and outcome measurement bias.

Conversely, domains assessing missing outcome data and selective reporting were judged to be at low risk, as nearly complete data were reported for all prespecified endpoints. While no study was considered at high risk of bias, we acknowledge that the high weighting of studies using clustered event data (unit-of-analysis error) influenced the overall certainty of the findings, which was considered during the GRADE evaluation. Figure S1 illustrates the risk of bias assessment summary.

Assessment of evidence certainty

We further assessed the quantitative synthesis results using the GRADE framework to determine the certainty of evidence. Although the initial pooled analysis indicated an improvement in the first-attempt success rate, the certainty of evidence for this outcome was rated as very low. This downgrading was primarily due to serious risk of bias arising from unit-of-analysis errors (treating repeated cannulations as independent events), significant inconsistency (I2 = 91% for the primary success-rate outcome), and imprecision in the estimated effect sizes. Specifically, the observed efficacy was heavily influenced by studies utilizing cannulation events as the unit of analysis rather than unique patients. Once these statistical artifacts—previously misinterpreted as regional differences—were accounted for, the apparent benefit became non-significant. Similarly, no significant differences were observed in complication rates or procedure duration; the evidence for these outcomes was also rated as low to very low certainty due to high clinical heterogeneity and the limited number of unique participants. Detailed GRADE evidence profiles are provided in Table S4. These ratings underscore the need for extreme caution when interpreting the pooled results, as the perceived technical benefits are likely confounded by unit-of-analysis errors and the operator learning curve.

Cannulation success rate

The pooled analysis of five RCTs, encompassing a total of 2,894 cannulation events, initially showed a marginal improvement in first-attempt success rates with US-guided cannulation compared to conventional palpation-guided techniques (RR = 1.08; 95% CI, 1.00–1.16; p = 0.04) (Fig. 2). However, this finding must be interpreted with extreme caution due to the high heterogeneity (I2= 91%) and potential unit-of-analysis errors.

Our investigation into the influence of reporting units revealed that the observed statistical significance was predominantly driven by studies reporting aggregate cannulation events as independent observations. Sensitivity analysis using the leave-one-out method demonstrated that removing the largest event-based study, Wu et al. (2022), rendered the overall effect non-significant (RR = 1.07; 95% CI, 0.98–1.16; p = 0.14). This confirms that the pooled efficacy is highly sensitive to the reporting unit; event-based trials artificially narrow confidence intervals by treating repeated cannulations as independent events, thereby likely overestimating the effect.

Furthermore, previous interpretations suggesting regional variations in efficacy were identified as statistical artifacts resulting from these unit-of-analysis errors rather than genuine clinical disparities. The persistent high heterogeneity reflects fundamental differences in analysis units (patient-level vs. event-level), operator proficiency, and ultrasound protocols (Fig. 2).

Complications

Four studies reported cannulation-related complications, such as hematoma, post-cannulation bleeding, and infiltration (Fig. 3). Pooled analysis revealed no statistically significant difference in complication rates between US-guided cannulation and conventional palpation-guided techniques (RR = 0.34; 95% CI, 0.06–1.90; p = 0.22).

Considerable heterogeneity was observed (I² = 89%; P < 0.001), likely reflecting inconsistencies in complication definitions, varying reporting quality, and differences in clinical practice across centers. Furthermore, the wide confidence interval and lack of statistical significance may be attributed to the limited number of unique patients involved in these trials, which reduces the statistical power to detect rare adverse events. The findings suggest that while US-guided cannulation does not appear to increase complication risks, its potential protective effect remains unconfirmed due to current data limitations.

Cannulation time

Regarding procedure duration, a quantitative meta-analysis was not performed due to substantial methodological heterogeneity in outcome definitions across the included trials. Specifically, Chen et al. (2023) measured the total workflow from initial draping to the successful placement of the second needle (mean 5.41 min), whereas Kumbar et al. (2018) reported the raw cannulation time for a single needle (mean 41.1 s). Because these definitions are fundamentally incompatible, representing a broad clinical workflow versus a specific needle insertion event, a pooled mean difference was deemed statistically invalid. Qualitatively, studies such as Chen et al. (2023) and Schoch et al. (2023) noted that US-guided procedures may initially require longer durations due to device setup and vessel scanning requirements, particularly during the initial stages of the operator learning curve in less experienced cohorts.

Patient-reported outcomes and additional findings

Several studies assessed secondary outcomes such as patient-reported pain and nursing staff confidence. While these outcomes generally favored US-guided cannulation, the data were insufficient for meta-analysis due to the lack of standardized assessment tools. Notably, although technical success may improve, the overall patient experience remains confounded by procedure duration; a prolonged procedure due to operator inexperience may offset the comfort gained from fewer needle punctures. These findings highlight the need for standardized measurement tools to capture patient-centered effects.

Summary of findings

Overall, the results suggest that US-guided cannulation may be associated with improved first-attempt success in adult haemodialysis patients; however, the strength of this conclusion is substantially limited by unit-of-analysis errors, high clinical heterogeneity, and the influence of the operator learning curve. Previous regional conclusions were found to be unsubstantiated when accounting for statistical clustering. Further high-quality trials using per-patient reporting and standardized training protocols are essential.

Discussion

This systematic review with quantitative synthesis evaluated evidence from RCTs comparing US-guided cannulation with conventional palpation-guided techniques in adult haemodialysis patients. The findings indicated that US-guided cannulation was associated with improved first-attempt success rates in the pooled analysis, which aligns with discussions surrounding different cannulation protocols for AVF and AVG [20]. However, this finding must be interpreted with extreme caution, as the significance was primarily driven by studies with inherent unit-of-analysis errors. High clinical and statistical heterogeneity in the primary success-rate outcome (I2 = 91%) further underscores the preliminary nature of these results [9, 10].

US-guided cannulation theoretically improves success by allowing real-time visualization of vascular anatomy, which is particularly advantageous in patients with challenging vascular features, such as deep, tortuous, or immature vessels [14, 21]. While trials such as Wu et al. (2022) reported a high first-attempt success rate of 98.7% compared with 88.3% in the conventional group, the disproportionate weighting of studies reporting aggregate events (2,894 attempts) rather than unique patients (142 patients) likely inflated the statistical significance (p = 0.04). We emphasize that the previously observed discrepancies between trials should not be attributed to geographical or biological factors. Instead, these variations are identified as statistical artifacts resulting from unadjusted clustered data. The reliance on event-based reporting in studies like Wu et al. (2022) likely leads to an overestimation of the treatment effect. We acknowledge that such data dependencies can mask individual-level confounders. For instance, a single patient with difficult access in the control group undergoing multiple failed attempts could disproportionately favor the intervention arm. Due to the lack of individual patient-level data across all trials, this inherent bias cannot be fully adjusted, suggesting that the current p = 0.04 reflects specific clinical environments rather than a universal technical advantage.

Beyond statistical factors, the clinical efficacy of US-guided cannulation is inextricably linked to operator proficiency. The substantial heterogeneity observed in success rates (I2 = 91%) and complications (I2 = 89%) highlights that ultrasound-guided cannulation (UGC) is not a standalone technology but is highly dependent on a structured instructional framework to be effective. Trials that implemented rigorous pre-study training with competency evaluations [2, 24] tended to report higher success rates, whereas pilot studies where operators were still navigating the learning curve showed less pronounced benefits [17]. The more modest results seen in pilot studies reflect an active learning curve where staff were still gaining technical confidence; therefore, successful implementation requires bridging the gap between technology and clinical efficacy through competency-based training and overcoming initial technical barriers [15].

Regarding procedure duration, the reported times varied significantly across trials based on the chosen definition of “procedure time.” For instance, Chen et al. (2023) utilized a broad definition encompassing the entire workflow, whereas Kumbar et al. (2018) reported narrower technical cannulation events. Qualitatively, US-guided cannulation can involve additional time for device setup and vessel scanning, which may initially offset the technical benefits of visualization, particularly in pilot studies where operators are navigating the learning curve. Furthermore, the cannulation method plays a role: US-guided cannulation is likely more helpful in rope-ladder cannulation, where multiple puncture sites must be visualized, but less advantageous in buttonhole cannulation [17].

While the pooled analysis showed no significant reduction in complications, individual trials suggested a trend toward fewer hematomas and bleeding events [13]. However, the limited number of unique patients reduces the statistical power to detect rare adverse events. Moreover, technical success does not automatically translate to improved patient-reported outcomes (PROs). Schoch et al. (2024) reported comparable satisfaction across groups, indicating that if procedure time is prolonged due to operator inexperience, the discomfort from device setup may negate the benefit of fewer needle punctures.

Successful implementation depends on addressing logistical challenges and fostering institutional support. Dedicated training programs have been shown to enhance both skill and nursing confidence [5, 8]. Although procedural times initially increase, protocol adherence remains high and confidence improves with experience, suggesting that sustainable integration is feasible with adequate training [17].

This review has several major limitations. First, there is a paucity of evidence due to the limited number of published RCTs and the relatively small total patient population (N = 142 across five trials). Second, a persistent unit-of-analysis error—treating repeated cannulations on the same patient as independent events—poses a threat to the validity of the primary outcome’s significance. This issue was prevalent across multiple included trials [6, 14, 17, 24], involving small patient pools, which may artificially narrow confidence intervals. Third, the high heterogeneity and inconsistent outcome definitions further restrict the generalizability of the findings. Consequently, the results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive clinical evidence. We have corrected our earlier interpretations to reflect that observed variations are more likely due to data clustering than to regional differences. Future research must prioritize methodological rigor by using the patient as the unit of analysis. Large-scale, multicenter RCTs are needed to evaluate not only technical success but also long-term access patency, cost-effectiveness, and holistic patient experiences [16, 23].

Conclusion

This systematic review with quantitative synthesis suggests that US-guided cannulation may improve first-attempt success rates in adult haemodialysis patients in the pooled analysis. However, the overall certainty of this evidence is very low, as the observed statistical significance is predominantly driven by studies with unit-of-analysis errors (clustered event-based data) and is highly sensitive to these reporting methods. When accounting for these statistical artifacts—previously misinterpreted as clinical disparities—no definitive evidence supports superior efficacy in specific clinical or geographical settings.

Furthermore, no clear benefits were observed regarding complication rates or procedure duration, which may be significantly influenced by the operator learning curve and device setup time. While US-guided cannulation shows potential as an adjunct or alternative approach to conventional palpation-guided techniques, its clinical value remains to be fully confirmed through more methodologically rigorous research. Future large-scale, multicenter trials using standardized patient-level reporting and comprehensive patient-reported and cost-effectiveness measures are essential to support its wider adoption in routine haemodialysis care.
