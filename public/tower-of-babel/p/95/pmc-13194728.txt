A framework for a national cancer imaging repository in Nigeria
Authors: Adenike Adegoke-Elijah, Olufemi Macauley, Kudirat Oyewumi Jimoh, Axel Zevallos-Aquije, Olufunmilayo I. Olopade, Benjamin Aribisala
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cancer is a growing global health concern responsible for approximately 10 million deaths annually, with low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) accounting for over 70% of cancer-related mortality. Nigeria, the most populous country in Africa, bears the highest incidence and mortality burden. Although Artificial Intelligence (AI) holds promise for improving early detection and treatment, its success depends on access to large, high-quality imaging datasets. Nigeria lacks a centralized repository for cancer imaging data to support AI development. This study proposes a framework for establishing a national cancer imaging repository to facilitate data-driven cancer research and AI model development in Nigeria, using twelve (12) tertiary healthcare institutions across the six geo-political zones as pilot implementation centres. It suggests a set of protocols to guide the standardization of the repository’s operations. A robust data ingestion and pre-processing pipeline was also designed to ensure that the repository maintains only high-quality images, adheres to ethical standards and supports interoperability with existing databases. Using a set of criteria, the study suggests twelve hospitals spread across the country’s six geopolitical zones to serve as the pilot data collection sites. The study also outlines the infrastructural requirements for the initial repository deployment and provides a roadmap for national scale-up in the future. The proposed architecture was benchmarked against The Cancer Imaging Archive (TCIA) to validate the proposed framework. The comparison assessed metadata standards, data governance, access protocols, and security features. The results show that the framework aligns with international best practices while addressing Nigeria’s infrastructural and regulatory constraints. The proposed repository framework provides a secure and scalable foundation for AI-powered cancer diagnosis and research in Nigeria. Its alignment with global standards enhances its potential to support national cancer control strategies and foster innovation in low-resource settings.

Introduction

Geographically, Nigeria is situated in West Africa, sharing borders with the Republic of Benin to the west, Cameroon and Chad to the east, and the Niger Republic to the north. Nigeria has a population of over 202 million, and accounts for approximately 20% of the population of Africa1,2 and about 44%3 of the population of West Africa4. projects significant population growth in Nigeria, reaching about 440 million by 2050, and thus becoming the fourth most populous country.

Cancer is a malignant disease arising from uncontrolled cell division in the body to form a mass of tissues5. Cancer remains a leading cause of mortality worldwide, resulting in approximately 10 million deaths globally in 2022, translating to almost one in six deaths (16.8%) and one in four deaths (22.8%) from non-communicable diseases (NCDs)6–8. The incidence rates are expected to rise significantly, suggesting a 47% rise by 2040, with new cases rising to 28.4 million by 20409. Predisposing factors like tumorigenesis, the immune system, biological stressors, as well as gene-environment interaction and low life expectancy, could influence the risk of different groups of cancer10. Other risk factors are geographical location and occupational settings11.

The burden of cancer is not evenly distributed, with about 70% of cancer-related deaths occurring in low-middle-income countries (LMIC), including countries in sub-Saharan Africa. Cancers are often detected at advanced stages in these countries due to limited access to early detection, diagnosis, and treatments, leading to higher mortality rates in the region. Cancer is a major public health and economic problem in Africa in the 21 st century12. The factors responsible for the higher burden of cancer in developing countries include growth and ageing of the population, adoption of cancer-associated lifestyles, and exposure to different infections13. Other factors are increase in smoking habits, physical inactivity, overweight, changing reproductive patterns (including lower parity and later age at first birth) associated with urbanization and economic development, and “westernized” diets14. In Nigeria, cancer remains a notable public health challenge with increasing incidence rates and high mortality. According to the World Health Organization, cancer cases in Africa are projected to rise by 70% by 2030, with Nigeria bearing a significant portion of the burden15. This projection underscores the need for an innovative approach to enhance cancer control in Nigeria.

Towards developing a platform for cancer management, the Nigerian government established cancer registries across its length and breadth. Cancer registries are means by which information on all cancer cases is collated, coded, and classified to produce statistics on cancer occurrence in a defined population, within a specific period. According to16, there are thirty-three (33) cancer registries in Nigeria, with thirteen (13) of them in the class of Population-Based Cancer Registries (PBCRs), while twenty (20) are Hospital-Based Cancer Registries (HBCRs). These cancer registries were purposely created to describe the extent and nature of cancer burden, and subsequently serve as a source of materials for etiological studies applicable to planning, monitoring, and assessing the effectiveness of National Cancer Control Programmes (NCCP). Whether a cancer registry is population-based or hospital-based, its primary focus remains providing statistics for cancer management, particularly in treating patients and implementing planning, monitoring, and control processes. To effectively address the cancer burden in Nigeria, a more comprehensive approach is needed, one that encompasses cancer control through prevention, early detection, treatment, and robust research programs.

Artificial Intelligence (AI) has revolutionized numerous fields, notably medicine, where its applications have been demonstrated to rival experts’ performance in various tasks17. AI has also been applied in omics data analysis, where high-dimensional datasets are used to uncover disease subtypes and inform personalized treatment plans18. This technological advancement presents a promising solution to cancer control in Nigeria. One of the important fields where AI has made significant strides is medical image analysis. AI algorithms can interpret and analyze medical images of various modalities, such as X-rays, CT scans, MRIs, and ultrasounds, much faster and more accurately than human radiologists19. In cancer detection, AI can be used to analyse radiological images and identify early signs of cancer. For example, AI-driven systems can analyse mammograms to detect breast cancer, or CT scans to identify lung nodules, thus assisting radiologists in making more precise diagnoses. Beyond diagnoses, AI algorithms can also be utilized for image segmentation and detailed mapping of organs, tissues, and tumours, which is crucial for surgical planning and radiation therapy. Furthermore, AI can predict cancer progression by tracking image changes over time, providing insights to aid personalised treatment plans. The success of AI in radiological image analysis is hinged on its ability to learn complex patterns and features from a diverse set of images. Medical images are complex, and subtle differences between normal and abnormal tissues require nuanced analysis. AI models, especially deep learning algorithms, must be trained on vast datasets that capture various medical conditions to achieve the accuracy and reliability required in healthcare. Without sufficient data, these models may fail to generalize effectively, leading to unreliable predictions, especially in less common conditions. Therefore, this necessity explains the importance of building and maintaining large and standardized repositories for medical images to harness the potential of AI in healthcare delivery fully.

Nigeria lacks a large-scale image repository that could be used to train advanced AI models and develop innovative solutions tailored to the specific needs of Nigerian patients. Most Nigerian researchers interested in data-driven medical research, therefore, depend on online repositories consisting of data from non-Nigerians, and this can lead to misdiagnosis when used for Nigerian patients, as existing studies indicate that tumour biology, breast density, and cancer presentation differ across ethnic groups, with African populations often having distinct tumour characteristics compared to Caucasian counterparts20,21. To overcome these challenges, this study proposes a comprehensive framework for a cancer medical image repository in Nigeria, designed to bridge the gap between AI technologies and local healthcare needs. By establishing a standardized and large-scale repository of medical images sourced from various healthcare institutions across Nigeria, the proposed framework ensures that AI models can be trained on data representative of the Nigerian population. Such a repository would not only empower local researchers to develop and validate AI algorithms suited to the country’s unique demographic and pathological characteristics but also support early cancer detection, personalized treatment planning, and the development of population-specific screening programs. Ultimately, this study proposed a foundational framework that may support enhanced diagnostic research capacity, more timely access to cancer imaging data, and the future development of AI-assisted decision-support tools, with the longer-term potential to contribute to improved cancer care delivery and population-level cancer control in Nigeria.

While the immediate objective of the proposed framework is to develop a secure, standardized, and large-scale repository for storing and managing cancer medical images across Nigeria, the design also anticipates and supports future applications of artificial intelligence (AI) and deep learning, which is an essential tool for developing accurate and reliable AI models.

Literature review

A cancer image repository is a centralized database of medical images related to cancer22, typically collected from various clinical or research institutions. These images include MRI, PET, CT, and histopathology images, and can be accompanied by relevant metadata such as clinical history, diagnostic information, and the patient’s demography. The repository, which typically contains many images, is essential for developing and training machine learning algorithms capable of identifying patterns in medical images and diagnosing cancer, aiding treatment, and predicting patient outcomes. The collaborative approach of managing centralized images from multiple sources fosters collaborations and enables researchers to access diverse datasets, thereby aiding innovation in cancer treatment and diagnosis. The repositories also provide benchmark datasets for testing new algorithms and validating new models, which is essential for enhancing the accuracy and reliability of algorithms in medical imaging. Several prominent cancer repositories have been established and substantially influenced cancer research. Four available repositories are discussed due to their widespread use in numerous studies and their significant contributions to advancing cancer imaging and diagnosis. Additionally, these repositories were selected because they provide open access to users, promoting global collaboration in research.

The Cancer Imaging Archive (TCIA) is an open-access and prominent repository established and managed by the National Cancer Institute. It consists of over 50million radiological images collected from 50,000 subjects, and contributed by over 85 institutions, such as universities, research institutions, and hospitals23,24. TCIA contains a vast collection of anonymized imaging data, such as CT, MRI, PET, and digital pathology scans, that are organized by tumour types25. Apart from the imaging data, the repository also contains accompanying clinical and genomic data for many patients. The images are from various cancers such as breast, lung, brain, and prostate cancer. TCIA supports AI and machine learning research; its high-quality data has been used to train and validate several AI models. The datasets have been used for automatic detection of tumour26, image segmentation27 and radiomics-based survival prediction28.

The LIDC-IDRC (Lung Image Database Consortium and Image Resource Initiative) is a comprehensive open-access database of 1,018 thoracic CT scans developed to support lung nodules detection29. The National Cancer Institute did the project in collaboration with the Food and Drug Administration(FDA) and multiple academic and medical institutions30. The database contains 7,371 lesions annotated as nodules by at least one radiologist, with 2,669 of these lesions identified as nodules with a size greater than or equal to 3mm. LIDC-IDRI provides an essential dataset helpful in developing and validating classification problems31 and comparing the lung nodule detection algorithm32.

National Cancer Institute (NCI) Imaging Data Commons is a cloud-based repository that hosts and shares medical CT, MRI, and PET scans, with associated clinical and genomic data33. The repository also provides access to histopathology collections, with tools for data exploration, visualization, and analysis. It aggregates imaging datasets from multiple sources, thus providing researchers with centralized access to vast and diverse data for cancer research34.

TCGA-GBM (The Cancer Genome Atlas-Glioblastoma Multiforme) is a comprehensive dataset focused on glioblastoma multiforme (GBM)35. GBM is one of the most lethal, aggressive, and treatment-resistant brain cancers36. The TCGA-GBM dataset was created within the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) project to understand the genetic and molecular characteristics of the disease for improved diagnosis and treatment for patients. The dataset contains multi-modal data comprising genomic data, epigenomic data, transcriptomic data, proteomic data, and MRI imaging scans of GBM patients linked with the associated genomic data for understanding the correlations between the molecular characteristics of tumours and the features of the images37. The repository contains data collected from over 500 patient samples and captures the genetic diversity of GDM tumours. Therefore, it exists as one of the most comprehensive datasets for understanding the variability of glioblastoma and response to treatment. In addition to molecular and imaging data, TCGE-GBM includes clinical information about the datasets, such as age, treatment outcomes, survival rates, and tumour grade, which helps researchers associate molecular characteristics with clinical behaviour, for insights into prognosis and treatment responses. The dataset is a benchmark for developing and validating AI models for diagnosing GBM and predicting patient outcomes38,39.

Apart from the four notable repositories discussed, CHAIMELEON (Cancer HeterogeneitY Analysis In Multi-centeraL EurOpeaN repositories) is a pan-European project focused on the creation of an extensive multi-modal database of cancer medical images, with primary focus on lung, breast, prostate, and colorectal cancers. The repository contains diverse data, including imaging data (CT, MRI, PET), clinical and molecular data40. CHAIMELEON is an ongoing project that aims to collect over 40,000 cases across multiple European medical institutions41. The project focuses on harmonizing and standardizing data from diverse sources to ensure interoperability and compatibility across multiple data sources42. The repository is designed to facilitate the development and validation of AI-driven tools using the CHAIMELEON dataset, for improved cancer detection, diagnosis, and treatment planning43.

In addition to publicly available cancer imaging databases, several frameworks and infrastructures have been developed globally to aid the standardized collection, storage, and utilization of cancer imaging data. Frameworks exist as foundations for the development of new repositories. The UK Biobank Imaging Enhancement program44 provides a model for integrating large-scale imaging data into population-based research. The framework supports images of various modalities (MRI, DEXA, CT)and links these data with comprehensive electronic health records, genetic profiles, and patient lifestyle information. The framework has a centralized data management, rigorous quality control pipelines, and a well-defined ethical and data access governance structure. However, its implementation depends on an advanced digital health ecosystem and large-scale funding, features that are not readily available in many LMICs. The Euro-Bioimaging infrastructure45 provides a distributed network of imaging facilities and data services across Europe. While the infrastructure does not host imaging data directly, it offers standardized access to imaging technologies, protocols, and training resources. Its coordination of interconnected nodes across Europe, approach to interoperability, service harmonization, and cross-border collaboration provide valuable governance and operational model that could guide the development of large scale frameworks for cancer imaging, particularly in Africa. The Imaging Data Commons (IDC) is part of the U.S. National Cancer Institute Research Data Commons, which offers a cloud-based platform for hosting, sharing and analyzing medical imaging data. IDC integrates with other NCI data platforms (genomics, proteomics, and clinical data) to enable multi-modal research. The framework emphasizes data harmonization, AI readiness, and cloud native access.

46presents a framework for collecting, processing, storing, and analyzing imaging data in a secure, standardized, and interoperable manner. DICOM and non-DICOM images are captured using diagnostic devices. The acquired images are transmitted securely to a Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS), where they are anonymized using the Clinical Trials Processor (CTP) DICOM Pixel Anonymizer. After anonymization, the images are standardized and stored using a vendor-neutral abstraction layer to enhance interoperability across diverse equipment and institutions. The images are thereafter stored in a cloud-based or on-premises object storage system. Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources (FHIR) was also used to structure the clinical metadata to foster interoperability with other databases, with REDCap used for secure role-based access. A NoSQL/MongoDB database was adopted to store semi-structured metadata.A routing system (dispatcher) ensures proper sequencing and classification of examinations by directing them to use suitable AI models or manual review, if a compatible AI model is unavailable. The framework adopts DICOMWeb to facilitate the manipulation of DICOM format images in a web-based environment. It has been successfully implemented in diagnostic cases involving tuberculosis, melanoma, and Zika virus. The study presents a framework adaptable to necessary domains, by emphasizing the harmonization of imaging data from different sources, ensuring interoperability with existing databases, and the need for an extensive pipeline to preprocess the images before storing them in cloud storage.

Despite the advancements in cancer imaging repositories and frameworks in developed countries and their importance in enhancing cancer control, many low and middle-income countries (LMICs), including Nigeria, face many challenges in setting up this infrastructure. The challenges include limited access to imaging equipment47, lack of data storage and sharing infrastructures, shortage of trained personnel48, and financial constraints. Apart from the listed, the absence of standardization in imaging practice and the lack of harmonized protocols in data collection present key challenges to data integration into a centralized repository49. Nigeria, just like many LMIC countries, faces significant challenges in providing comprehensive cancer care to its citizens. There is an acute shortage of cancer treatment centres for a population of over 200 million50,51. Nigeria also experiences a severe shortage of oncologists, radiologists, and other cancer care specialists. Many healthcare centres also lack modern diagnostic equipment such as CT scanners, MRI and PET scanners52,53, leading to misdiagnosis and delays in diagnosis. Cancer cases in Nigeria are also marked by late presentation54,55 due to limited awareness and organized screening programs, which leads to reduced survival rates. The development of a comprehensive National Cancer Imaging Repository (NCIR) has the potential to alleviate some of these challenges. The repository will provide researchers with a large dataset for training and validating AI models to aid image interpretation, thereby improving diagnostic accuracy in areas with few radiologists. The repository will also enhance research capabilities by facilitating large-scale studies of the centralized image data to identify the specific cancer pattern in Nigeria, thus leading to the development of tailored screenings and treatment protocols for Nigerian patients. The proposed repository offers an invaluable training resource to new oncologists and radiologists, thereby addressing the shortage of specialists in the country. The comprehensive repository would also serve as an invaluable resource to policymakers for accurate insights into cancer trends, thus helping in resource allocation and healthcare planning. The standardized repository will also aid international collaborations, potentially bringing more resources and expertise into Nigerian cancer care. Standardization and harmonization are issues that must be addressed in the proposed centralized cancer image repository. Cancer imaging data are collected from various healthcare institutions using varying equipment, including older machines and state-of-the-art systems. These imaging devices therefore produce images in different formats (e.g. DICOM, JPEG, TIFF). The difference in equipment and setting used during image capturing could also lead to varying quality and resolution of images. Different facilities also record clinical metadata such as patient demographics, diagnostic information, treatment histories, and follow-up outcomes in different formats, which could lead to conflicting data, data analysis, and comparison. These differences necessitate the standardization and harmonization of dataset collected across different facilities to make data from different sources interoperable and comparable.

Given its scalability, flexibility, and ease of access, the proposed repository adopts a cloud-based architecture rather than a purely on-premise deployment. Firstly, the cloud-based architecture provides elastic storage and computing resources required for imaging data, which grows over time as it accumulates from multiple institutions nationwide. Secondly, the cloud-based solutions are cost-effective at the pilot phase, eliminating the need for substantial investment in physical hardware and ongoing maintenance. Another advantage of cloud infrastructure is the secure remote access to data, which enables seamless collaboration and data sharing among geographically dispersed hospitals, researchers, and clinicians using the repository. Furthermore, major service providers, such as Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure, etc., provide integrated tools that support the development of AI models, including high-performance GPUs and TPUs, which align with the long-term goal of enabling AI-driven cancer diagnosis on the repository. These providers also adhere to international standards of data protection, thus ensuring security, regulatory compliance, and robust user authentication. Considering these benefits, cloud infrastructure offers a practical and sustainable approach to implementing the proposed national repository in the pilot phase. This study develops a cloud-based framework for a cancer image repository to promote the use of artificial intelligence and state-of-the-art tools for cancer control in Nigeria. It adopts twelve (12) tertiary hospitals as the source of the imaging data. To enhance data standardisation across all the facilities, a set of national protocols for collecting, transmitting, and storing the images is also proposed. This study also develops a six-layer architecture that ensures that imaging data of different modalities are successfully transferred from the donating tertiary hospitals (PACS centres) to the centralised repository, and authorised users can access the data. The architecture has many features such as image harmonisation, in-transit and at-rest data encryption, data validation, image anonymization, and Role-Based Access Control(RBAC) to promote the integrity and security of the data from authorised users.

Stakeholders engagement and governance

The proposed repository’s success depends on the stakeholders’ active participation and collaboration in the project. To ensure the repository’s relevancy and sustainability, key stakeholders involved in the project must engage in strategic meetings to discuss the strategies to ensure the smooth running of the repository. The stakeholders are the Federal Ministry of Health (FMoH), the Nigeria Institute of Cancer Research and Treatment (NICRAT), participating hospitals, radiologists, pathologists, data scientists, and IT professionals. The strategic meeting will involve signing a memorandum of understanding with the selected teaching hospitals. The agreements formalize the terms of collaboration, data sharing agreements, and the roles of participating institutions in contributing imaging data to the repository. A national steering committee, comprising representatives of all the stakeholders, would be constituted to provide oversight governance of the repository and ensure compliance with ethical standards. The committee would formulate data governance policies, establish standard operating procedures, and review progress reports from the implementing hospitals. In addition, the research ethics committee in each institution shall be engaged to review the project protocol, ensuring confidentiality, informed consent, and compliance with the guidelines of the National Health Research Ethics Committee. The proposed governance structure ensures that the development and management of the repository aligns with national health priorities, respects patients’ rights, and fosters transparency and accountability among all partners.

Development of national protocols

A core requirement for establishing the repository is the development of standardized protocols governing the acquisition, annotation, storage, transfer, and reuse of imaging data across heterogeneous healthcare institutions in Nigeria. Such protocols are necessary to address variations in imaging equipment, technical capacity, and documentation practices, and to ensure interoperability, data quality, and ethical compliance across all contributing centres. The National Imaging Protocols serve as formal operational guidelines that harmonize practices across participating institutions while aligning with internationally recognized imaging and data governance standards.

The proposed protocols are organized into the following functional domains: Data Acquisition Protocol: Defines eligible imaging modalities, file formats (DICOM), minimum technical parameters (e.g., resolution, scan settings, patient positioning), and submission frequency. Modality selection reflects Nigeria’s infrastructural realities and clinical priorities, with emphasis on breast ultrasound, CT, and mammography based on availability and clinical relevance56–58. Architecture of the proposed repository

The proposed repository adopts a six-layer, modular architecture designed to support secure ingestion, processing, storage, and controlled access to cancer imaging data aggregated from twelve geographically distributed PACS centres. This layered design reduces system complexity, enhances scalability, and supports interoperability across institutions. Data Source Layer: Serves as the entry point for imaging data by interfacing with institutional PACS to retrieve images and associated metadata. At this stage, data undergo initial anonymization and preliminary annotation, followed by modality-based routing and secure transmission to the central repository using DICOMWeb-compatible services.

Data Transfer Layer: Facilitates secure movement of imaging data from source institutions to the central repository through encrypted channels. This layer manages ingestion workflows, error detection, and data integrity checks prior to processing.

Data Processing Layer: Performs validation, harmonization, secondary anonymization, and data transformation to ensure consistency across heterogeneous data sources. In addition to structural and semantic harmonization, this layer supports AI-assisted annotation and quality refinement of imaging data, including automated region-of-interest suggestions, annotation consistency checks, and normalization of annotation formats in accordance with national protocols. These processing tasks collectively prepare curated datasets for reliable storage and downstream analytical use.

Data Storage Layer: Provides scalable cloud-based storage for imaging data and structured metadata. DICOM images are stored in object-based systems, while metadata are managed in relational databases to support efficient querying and indexing. Backup, recovery, and encryption-at-rest mechanisms are integrated.

Data Access and Security Layer: Implements authentication, role-based authorization, access control policies, and auditing mechanisms to ensure secure and compliant data access. Encryption is enforced for data both in transit and at rest.

User Interface Layer: Provides web-based tools for authorized users to search, visualize, and retrieve imaging data using flexible query parameters, enabling research and clinical analysis without direct database interaction.

Data ingestion and preprocessing pipeline

The data ingestion and preprocessing pipeline operationalizes the national protocols by ensuring that imaging data collected from participating institutions are standardized, validated, anonymized, and harmonized prior to storage.

Data Acquisition: Designated focal personnel at each institution retrieve eligible imaging data from local PACS in accordance with national acquisition protocols. All images are submitted in DICOM format, with associated metadata structured using HL7 FHIR standards.

Data Curation and Validation: Incoming data undergo automated checks for format compliance, metadata completeness, and protocol adherence. Non-compliant submissions are flagged for correction. The validation logic is summarized in Algorithm 1.

Data Anonymization: A two-tier anonymization strategy is adopted, combining site-level de-identification with centralized anonymization at the processing layer. At the point of data collection, identifiable fields are removed, while essential non-identifiable attributes needed for research are retained. To accommodate the scale of data expected from multiple institutions, the framework relies on scalable, standards-compliant imaging solutions capable of efficiently processing high volumes of DICOM data. Representative implementation examples are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Data Harmonization: Imaging datasets are normalized across acquisition parameters, metadata schemas, and semantic representations using standardized ontologies (e.g., SNOMED CT, RadLex). Vendor-specific fields are translated or removed to ensure uniformity. This figure outlines how imaging data from the twelve hospitals are unified into a common format to enhance interoperability. First, acquisition parameters such as pixel spacing, orientation, and slice thickness are normalized. Next, patient demographics, clinical history, and study details are mapped into a shared HL7 FHIR format. Implementation plan

The deployment of the NCIR follows a phased implementation strategy designed to ensure feasibility, scalability, and sustainability. Key components include pilot site selection, infrastructure deployment, capacity building, and a roadmap for national expansion.

Pilot Site Selection: Twelve tertiary healthcare institutions were selected based on oncology service capacity, geographic coverage, operational readiness, and participation in national cancer initiatives (Table 2). Formal data-sharing agreements will be established through Memoranda of Understanding.

Infrastructure Deployment: Each site will deploy PACS integration tools, anonymization and annotation software, and secure data transfer services. Centralized cloud infrastructure will provide scalable storage, processing capacity, and GPU resources to support data curation and future AI workflows.

Training and Capacity Building: Structured training programs will equip radiologists, data managers, and IT personnel with skills in PACS integration, anonymization, annotation, and ethical compliance. Continuous professional development will support long-term sustainability.

National Scale-up: Following pilot evaluation, additional institutions will be onboarded using standardized deployment protocols. Picture Archiving and Communication Systems (PACS) provide an integrated digital platform for the acquisition, storage, retrieval, and visualization of medical images across multiple imaging modalities. PACS enable efficient image management by supporting rapid access, concurrent viewing by multiple users, and seamless sharing of imaging data across clinical locations, thereby replacing traditional film-based workflows with digital processes.

In the context of the proposed National Cancer Imaging Repository, PACS serve as the primary source of clinical imaging data at participating institutions. The availability of functional PACS infrastructure at the selected data collection centres enables standardized extraction of imaging data, facilitates interoperability, and provides a practical foundation for scalable, multi-institutional data aggregation.

Typically, PACS are made up of image acquisition devices for capturing various imaging modalities (e.g., MRI, CT scans, fluoroscopy, mammography, ultrasound, angiography), Communication Networks that aid the transfer of imaging data between the components of PACS and other remote locations for the proposed cancer repository, and Display Workstations that allow medical professional to view, analyse and carry out preliminary tasks on the image before being transferred-view images at PACS workstations. The deployment of PACS across multiple locations in Nigeria is therefore essential for the development of a national repository for cancer images.

Anonymization Tools: For the initial anonymization process, this study recommends the deployment of Clinical Trial Processor (CTP) by all participating institutions for automatic de-identification of the images. The software is suggested due to its easy configuration to specific institution requirements and ability to handle large datasets.

Annotation Capabilities at Participating Sites: Participating institutions are equipped with DICOM-compliant visualization and annotation environments that allow clinicians and trained personnel to perform preliminary region-of-interest delineation and metadata tagging. These environments are designed to support standardized annotation schemas and interoperable formats, ensuring compatibility with centralized processing and harmonization workflows. Representative examples of such annotation environments are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Training and capacity building

The success of the NCIR depends not only on infrastructure deployment but also on the technical capacity of the participating institutions. Therefore, a dedicated training and capacity-building program will be implemented to equip healthcare professionals, data managers, and IT personnel with the necessary skills. Training activities will focus using PACS integration tools, anonymization, and basic annotation platforms (e.g., OHIF Viewer, Cornerstone.js), secure data uploading procedures, and compliance with ethical standards, including patient consent protocols. Workshops and hands-on sessions will be organized in-person and virtually, ensuring consistent technical proficiency across all pilot centres. Additionally, selected personnel will receive advanced training on centralized annotation techniques and basic principles of AI-assisted image pre-processing to prepare for future scaling. Continuous professional development will be encouraged through refresher courses, periodic assessments, and participation in relevant national and international workshops and conferences.

National scale-up

The project is strategically positioned for national scale-up after the successful pilot implementation in the initial 12 tertiary hospitals. The scale-up phase will involve the gradual onboarding of additional public and private tertiary healthcare institutions across the six geo-political zones of Nigeria. Established selection criteria, including institutional capacity, oncology service availability, regional representation, and alignment with national cancer control priorities, will guide this expansion. To ensure a seamless scale-up, the proposed framework includes a standardized deployment protocol, cloud-based infrastructure for scalable data storage and processing, and a central coordination unit to oversee integration and compliance. Stakeholder engagement will remain a core strategy, with collaboration from the Federal Ministry of Health, National Cancer Control Programme, National Institute for Cancer Research and Treatment(NICRAT), and other relevant regulatory bodies to align with national health data governance and ethical standards. Capacity-building initiatives, such as continuous training for radiologists, data managers, and IT personnel, will accompany the scale-up to strengthen institutional readiness. Furthermore, the scale-up will incorporate feedback from the pilot phase to optimize system performance, usability, and interoperability, ultimately aiming to establish a robust, inclusive, and sustainable repository.

Validation of the framework

To ensure the robustness, relevance, and credibility of the proposed NCIR, the architecture was validated by benchmarking it against internationally recognized standards, using The Cancer Imaging Archive (TCIA) as a reference model. TCIA is widely acknowledged for its comprehensive infrastructure, high-quality curated datasets, and strong governance protocols. The above features thereby make it an ideal benchmark for evaluating similar platforms. The benchmarking exercise assessed the repository across several factors, such as supporting imaging modalities, primary storage medium, information stored, data anonymisation techniques, data submission process, user access policies, governance structures, and Geographical scope. By identifying areas of alignment and divergence with TCIA, the validation process confirms that the proposed repository meets global best practices and highlights context-specific innovations tailored to the Nigerian healthcare and data landscape. The comparison ensures that the designed architecture is interoperable with existing global infrastructures, facilitating research collaboration and data exchange. Validation is thus an essential component that guides continuous improvement during pilot implementation and national scale-up.

Limitations of the study

While the proposed repository presents a critical step toward strengthening cancer research and AI development in Nigeria, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the initial deployment is limited to 12 tertiary institutions, which may not fully capture the imaging diversity across all regions of the country. Second, the project relies on the readiness and cooperation of partner hospitals, and variability in infrastructure or data quality across sites may pose early integration challenges. Third, while the proposed architecture has been designed with future AI integration in mind, actual model training and performance validation are beyond the scope of the current phase. Finally, dependence on cloud infrastructure raises cost, data security, and long-term sustainability considerations, which will need to be continually reviewed as the repository scales nationally.

Results and discussion

The implementation of the proposed framework would enable the establishment of a centralized and standardized repository for cancer imaging data sourced from twelve tertiary healthcare institutions across the six geo-political zones of Nigeria. The repository supports imaging data from multiple modalities, including breast ultrasound, computed tomography (CT), and mammography, organized according to anatomical site and cancer type. The application of standardized data acquisition protocols across participating centres, together with the deployment of harmonization tools within the data processing layer, is expected to enhance interoperability and ensure greater uniformity of imaging data aggregated from heterogeneous institutional sources. The proposed repository could support the development and evaluation of AI-based analytical models trained on locally representative data, reduces reliance on externally sourced datasets, and facilitate population-specific imaging research. In addition, the centralized nature of the repository could enhance the identification of context-specific cancer imaging patterns, thereby informing future research into locally relevant screening and diagnostic strategies. The framework is inherently scalable, allowing for the phased integration of additional centres beyond the pilot phase. Furthermore, the adoption of cloud-based infrastructure, combined with role-based access control and encryption mechanisms for data in transit and at rest, is intended to promote operational efficiency, data security, and controlled access for authorized users.

While the proposed framework is promising, it faces several limitations that must be acknowledged. The framework lacks real-world implementation and is thus not operational, and as such, its effectiveness and efficiency remain theoretical until validated through the pilot study. Financial constraints also constitute a significant challenge to the implementation of the repository in resource-limited settings such as Nigeria, as this requires substantial financial, technical, and infrastructural investments.

Conclusion

This study presents the design of a framework for the National Cancer Repository in Nigeria, aimed at addressing the scarcity of localized cancer imaging datasets in cancer research and promoting the integration of artificial intelligence for early cancer detection, diagnosis, and optimization of treatment tailored to Nigerian populations. The framework emphasizes standardized data acquisition and curation across the selected tertiary hospitals, a robust data preprocessing pipeline, and cloud-based infrastructure that supports AI-assisted image annotation and storage of cancer imaging data, with a view to supporting AI model training in the future. Theoretically, the study contributes a structured framework for implementing a national-level imaging repository in a low-and middle-income country (LMIC) context, which could be reproduced within a resource-limited environment. Practically, the study lays a groundwork for large-scale, standardized medical imaging data collection in Nigeria, facilitating research collaborations, capacity building, and eventual development of an AI model tailored to Nigerian populations. The proposed repository offers several practical advantages for cancer imaging research and data management. It provides a centralized platform for accessing high-quality, anonymized imaging data, helping to reduce data fragmentation and support standardized data sharing across institutions. By enabling structured and interoperable datasets, the repository is designed to support downstream analytical workflows, including the future application of AI-assisted tools that may help improve diagnostic efficiency and manage radiologists’ workloads. Furthermore, the use of cloud-based compute resources and internationally recognized interoperability standards, such as DICOM and HL7 FHIR, positions the repository for scalable growth and cross-institutional integration, capabilities that are currently limited within existing cancer imaging infrastructure across much of sub-Saharan Africa.

Despite its strengths, the study acknowledges several limitations. The implementation is currently limited to 12 pilot hospitals, which may not capture the full heterogeneity of cancer cases across the country. Additionally, reliance on cloud infrastructure raises concerns around operational cost, data security, and long-term sustainability, which must be addressed through continuous stakeholder engagement and government support.

At this stage, the proposed framework represents an enabling infrastructure rather than a validated clinical intervention. Future research shall implement the development and benchmarking of AI models for cancer detection using the curated Dataset to assess the performance on local populations. Further studies shall also evaluate the impact of the repository on clinical workflow efficiency, research output, and the potential improvement in early cancer detection across partner institutions.
