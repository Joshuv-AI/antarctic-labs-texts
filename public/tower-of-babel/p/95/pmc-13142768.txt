Slowing artemisinin resistance in Africa
Authors: Maciej F. Boni, Issiaka Soulama, Jimmy Opigo, Oliver J. Watson, Bernhards Ogutu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Diversifying antimalarial drug use across Africa offers the most immediate strategy to slow antimalarial resistance and limit potential increases in treatment failure rates.

After 25 years of investment in global malaria control, the per capita death rate from malaria in the world’s most affected regions has dropped by a factor of 3. Nevertheless, malaria remains the third biggest infectious disease killer globally with 600,000 deaths occurring annually, mostly in African children under the age of five. Part of the reduction in malaria burden and transmission over the past two decades can be attributed to the gradual global deployment of artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs) since 2005. These 3-day treatment courses combine one short-lived high-potency artemisinin derivative and one longer-acting partner drug, and they are highly efficacious at clearing parasites by the third day of treatment as long as the parasites are not resistant to either the artemisinin component or the partner drug. As for all treatment deployments for high-burden diseases, drug resistance was expected, and it emerged in two separate and independent phases in Southeast Asia and East Africa.

Between 2007 and 2010, clinical signs of parasite resistance to artemisinin were identified in Cambodia when patients presenting with uncomplicated Plasmodium falciparum infections were not clearing their parasites by their third day of ACT treatment. Calls for malaria elimination efforts for Southeast Asia were made as early as summer 2007. The scale of the challenge ahead was tens of thousands of malaria cases per year. A combination of community health workers in Cambodia, the Cambodian national malaria program, the global institutions that finance malaria control and response, and scientists and clinicians involved in evaluating these responses rose to this challenge to push back artemisinin-resistant malaria in most of Southeast Asia. These parasites still circulate at high levels in Myanmar as the political situation in Myanmar prevented the initiation of most malaria elimination programs.

A decade later, these efforts would have to reconstitute themselves in Africa. In August 2020—when most parts of the world were waiting under lockdown for the clinical trial results of the first candidate SARS-CoV-2 vaccines—the first report of artemisinin-resistant P. falciparum genotypes circulating in Africa was published (1). The scale of this new malaria-control problem was now hundreds of millions of malaria cases endangered by the possibility of slow parasite clearance under treatment with artemether-lumefantrine (AL), the most commonly used ACT in Africa. The good news was that this response did not require the immediacy and speed of a COVID-level emergency—where death counts could increase 10-fold in a matter of weeks—but required instead a year-to-year mitigation program that would lead to a makeover of malaria treatment guidelines by the mid-2020s.

Once in Africa, the danger of spreading partial artemisinin resistance (ART-R), as it came to be called, was recognized internationally. In November 2022, the World Health Organization (WHO) released a strategy for responding to antimalarial drug resistance in Africa. The pillars of this strategy were surveillance, limiting drug pressure, limiting the spread of resistant parasites, and stimulating more innovation. Support for these measures has been reaffirmed in three recent perspectives (2–4), and the urgency expressed in these calls to action is clear. Historical patterns of previous waves of drug resistance and evolutionary principles tell us that ART-R genotypes will increase in frequency and spread geographically; there is also a risk of additional de novo resistance mutations. In the absence of any intervention in Africa, natural selection will continue pushing in one direction: higher resistance levels to AL. This is not a prediction made solely on historical patterns of antimalarial resistance, but one reflecting clear temporal changes in the measured susceptibility to both artemisinin and lumefantrine (5). The falciparum genotypes that circulated 10 to 20 years ago, genotypes on which AL had very high efficacy, will not be selected for again unless we change course. Calls to action to begin the process of slowing and reversing ART-R are timely and well placed—the cost of maintaining the status quo is simply too high.

RECOMMENDATIONS FOR RESISTANCE MITIGATION

Many of the recommendations discussed over the past 3 years underline the importance of gathering more data on resistant parasites, practicing better data sharing, improving vector control, improving malaria control and prevention, deploying triple ACTs, and recommending single low-dose primaquine to reduce transmission. National Malaria Control Programs (NMCPs) across Africa will be able to pursue some of these recommendations. Other recommendations will not be able to be implemented because of insufficient funding. Malaria elimination, as an example, worked well in Southeast Asia but will only work in several corners of Africa and not as a continent-wide approach. These recommendations all point in the right direction—better malaria control—but they do not differentiate between approaches that are specifically targeted at slowing or reversing the current course of drug resistance and those that are aimed at reducing malaria burden in general.

Missing in these conversations are recommendations for actions that should be implemented now—actions that will meet near-term priorities in drug-resistance mitigation and are affordable, acceptable, and effective at the NMCP-level in Africa. These actions need to be tailored to resistant parasites. The following are four focus areas to pursue over the next 5 years as resistance mitigation efforts are launched.

First, diversification of antimalarial drug use is crucial in pushing back a rising and spreading wave of drug resistance. This means that multiple high-efficacy, first-line antimalarial therapies (MFT) will need to be deployed in most African countries to ensure that selection pressure is not pushing the parasites to evolve in a single direction (Fig. 1). This can currently be achieved by deploying four ACTs: AL, artesunate-amodiaquine (ASAQ), dihydroartemisinin-piperaquine (DHA-PPQ), and artesunate-pyronaridine (ASPY). MFT is already supported by WHO implementation guidelines (6), and the rationale for this and any type of simultaneous drug deployment is that malaria parasites carrying resistant alleles to a particular drug will only have a 25% chance, or 33% chance, or 50% chance of seeing that drug in the next patient they infect (depending on how many therapies are in simultaneous use). This slows down the evolutionary trajectory of resistant alleles and increases the likelihood that resistant parasites are cleared (killed) by alternate drugs. Simultaneous deployment can be achieved by controlling price, influencing patient/prescriber choice, separating therapies by public/private sector use, rewriting guidelines to assign specific ACTs to particular age groups, or rotating ACTs at a national or subnational level on a preset schedule. The rotation period must be short so that parasites are not given a long runway to evolve resistance to the components of a particular ACT.

Additional important approaches to drug diversification include deployment of triple therapies as soon as they are approved—a triple combination therapy ensures that parasites see a diverse array of drugs in the shortest possible time window. Deployment of the novel non-artemisinin–based therapy ganaplacide-lumefantrine, once approved, will help diversify the set of antimalarial compounds used and play the vital role of reducing selection pressure from artemisinin.

Second, surveillance is critical. Molecular surveillance for drug-resistant genotypes is already in place in most African countries and needs continuous support to expand and shorten its turnaround time. In these surveillance systems, the genotype always appears before the phenotype, and routine components of molecular surveillance will allow us to identify resistance before it manifests as a high-percentage treatment failure phenotype. Surveillance for piperaquine resistance will be the most important addition to current African molecular surveillance programs and will enable MFT policies to be implemented with seat belts in place for the moment that piperaquine resistance emerges. Surveillance for lumefantrine and amodiaquine resistance is already passively in place, with most molecular genotyping studies looking at the key alleles associated with resistance to these two partner drugs. Both AL and ASAQ have been used extensively in Africa over the past 20 years, multiple resistant allele combinations have emerged, and efficacy studies have shown that AL and ASAQ efficacies can fall below 85%. Pyronaridine resistance has not yet been described and cannot thus be interrogated with the current molecular surveillance system. Piperaquine resistance, on the other hand, has both a rich set of loci characterizing its resistance phenotype and clinical studies showing that DHA-PPQ efficacy on PPQ-resistant artemisinin-resistant parasites can drop as low as 25% (7). These genotypes are known to have the potential to emerge and rise to high levels quickly. Rapid-turnaround molecular surveillance for PPQ resistance must be in place so that adjustments to DHA-PPQ use can be made if the threat of sub-50% efficacy becomes imminent. With this in place, DHA-PPQ can be comfortably added to the menu of ART-R response options. All countries need to set up surveillance as they prepare to roll out new strategies such as MFT to have a clear baseline for evaluation of the social impact of these interventions and to inform next steps for future interventions.

Third, because of the past 20 years of AL use in most of Africa, most drug-resistance response strategies will need to include a medium-term reduction in the usage of AL, lasting probably 5 years or more. This particular feature of the ART-R response is based on mathematical modeling projections for Rwanda (8) and Uganda (9), where genotypes that are known to be somewhat resistant to lumefantrine and less resistant to amodiaquine are circulating at high levels. High-grade lumefantrine resistance has not been confirmed in vivo among these genotypes; however, longitudinal data from Uganda show year-on-year increasing tolerance to lumefantrine (5). The long duration of use and the high AL coverage in Rwanda and Uganda specifically indicate that these environments have exerted strong selection pressure over 20 years for genotypes exhibiting any level of lumefantrine tolerance. The exceptions to this rule, of course, are settings where the national policy has been ASAQ use for the past decade or more, in countries like South Sudan, Eritrea, and Burundi. In these settings, the best options for therapy switches, rotations, and MFT deployments are not yet known.

Fourth, several countries have proposed a geographically stratified MFT approach with some regions of a country assigned to one ACT and other regions assigned to a different ACT. In our initial analysis on this topic with colleagues from Rwanda, we found that geographically based ACT deployment did not improve treatment failure outcomes because patients do not travel between regions frequently enough to receive a different ACT (10). This policy separates an entire country into zones for different ACTs, resulting in each zone using a single first-line ACT with evolutionary pressure for drug resistance pointed in a single direction. Without sufficient travel between zones to achieve any properly diversified use of therapies, this approach may fail to achieve the aims of MFT. A geographically stratified strategy that includes ACT rotations—e.g., where one zone receives AL for 1 year, ASAQ for the subsequent year, and so on—corrects this problem and again forces the parasite population to encounter multiple ACTs during a short time period. With rotations in place, routine molecular surveillance may also help explain the rise and fall of various resistant genotypes.

The next 10 years present an enormous challenge in malaria management as we attempt to slow, delay, and eventually reverse the evolution and spread of ART-R parasites. One of these challenges is that management of drug resistance falls into a very specific niche of infectious disease control; standard and familiar epidemic control tools are not guaranteed to push back drug resistance. Most infectious disease epidemics are self-limiting, but drug-resistance epidemics are not. A drug-resistance epidemic is not a forest fire—it is a broken dam. Response and mitigation efforts are most effective if implemented early when drug-resistant genotypes are still rare. The later we act, the more challenging it is to drain the floodwaters and repair the dam. Early action and response have already begun in Rwanda, Uganda, Tanzania, and Burkina Faso. We have several years ahead of us before we understand whether these responses were implemented early enough and which aspects of the response policies were most successful. As known and new ART-R genotypes continue to be discovered across the continent, other countries in Africa will begin considering implementation of ART-R response policies—these policies should specifically target delaying and slowing the spread of drug resistance.
