Cost-reduction strategies for cardiac implantable electronic devices: Increased battery capacity and beyond
Authors: Matthew T Bennett
Published: 2024

In light of the financial challenges facing many health care systems, payers increasingly prioritize methods to deliver care at reduced costs.1 For cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIEDs), the expenses span initial implantation, follow-up care, and generator replacement.2 Although negotiating lower purchase prices for CIEDs and optimizing procedural and follow-up efficiencies can mitigate some of these costs, the most significant cost savings arise from extending the interval between device implantation and generator replacement. Prolonging this interval reduces a patient’s lifetime number of generator replacements, decreasing both the direct costs of purchasing fewer generators and the procedural costs associated with replacements.3,4 Furthermore, fewer replacements lower the risk of device-related complications, which in turn reduces system-wide expenditures.5,6

In this issue of Heart Rhythm O2, Williams et al7 model the comprehensive costs associated with different battery capacities (1.0, 1.6, and 2.1 ampere-hours [Ah]). Their model included the costs associated with device implantation, replacement, follow-up, and complications over a 6-year follow-up period. These estimates were based on patients with cardiac resynchronization devices who had profiles typical of a Medicare cohort, including expected survival and risk of procedure-related complications. The 6-year event-free battery survival estimates included in the model were 10%, 44%, and 77% for 1.0-, 1.6-, and 2.1-Ah battery capacity. This increased time from implantation to generator change and the reduced procedure frequency translated to a comprehensive cost reduction from $56,647 to $41,527. When this cost difference was extended to the entire Medicare system, it was estimated that the cumulative 6-year cost savings would exceed 500 million dollars.

These findings underscore the critical importance of extending the time between device implantation and generator replacement. Although not directly evaluated in this study, it is reasonable to assume that a longer device lifespan would reduce the overall number of generator changes throughout a patient’s life, thereby minimizing procedure- and complication-related anxiety and disruption to patient and caregiver livelihood.8

Beyond battery capacity,9 multiple strategies can extend the interval between CIED implantation and replacement. These strategies fall into 3 categories: minimizing battery draw during device follow-up clinic visits, advancing battery technology, and reducing the risk of premature lead or generator failure. When clinically feasible, programming optimizations—such as reducing the pacing base rate, limiting rate responsiveness, the use of automatic lead threshold testing,10 deactivating nonessential features (eg, pre-event electrogram storage, ventricular-sensed response, and rate regularization), or adjusting medications that unnecessarily slow the sinus rate or alter atrioventricular nodal conduction—can significantly improve battery longevity.11

Implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) shocks are another factor that shortens battery life.11 Following current programming guidelines to minimize unnecessary shocks,12 prescribing optimal heart failure or antiarrhythmic medications, and performing ablation, where indicated, can also reduce ICD shocks by decreasing the risk of ventricular arrhythmias.

Advances in battery technology have significantly increased the usable capacity of ICDs since their initial introduction. Although lithium remains the standard anode material, the cathode has evolved from silver vanadium oxide to newer combinations, including silver vanadium oxide/carbon monofluoride hybrids and manganese dioxide, offering enhanced energy usable battery capacity.2 Device system advisories and premature lead or generator failure can also shorten device lifespan by necessitating additional interrogations or early device replacement. Rigorous preclinical testing and clear clinical guidelines that balance the risks of device system failure with the hazards of repeat procedures can mitigate these risks.13

Lastly, periprocedural infections remain a concern, potentially reducing device longevity. Implementing comprehensive infection control protocols, such as preventing pocket hematoma, administering preprocedure antibiotics, using antibiotic-eluting envelopes for select patients, and adhering to stringent infection prevention measures, can significantly lower the incidence of system infections and the need for premature device removal.14

In conclusion, a multifaceted, patient-centered approach that includes devices with larger battery capacities, clinical strategies to minimize battery consumption, infection prevention protocols, and improvements in battery and system reliability can effectively reduce the frequency of CIED replacements. This reduction will not only yield substantial cost savings but also mitigate the morbidity associated with frequent procedures. Such strategies should be integral to every CIED management program.
