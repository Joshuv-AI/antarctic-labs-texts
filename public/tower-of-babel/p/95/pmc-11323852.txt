Endoscopic endonasal drainage of a middle fossa epidural abscess in conjunction with endoscopic sinus surgery: illustrative case
Authors: Hirotaka Hasegawa, Kenji Kondo, Sayaka Ishihara, Yuki Shinya, Satoshi Kiyofuji, Motoyuki Umekawa, Nobuhito Saito
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDRecent anatomical studies have reported the feasibility of the endoscopic endonasal approach to the middle fossa. However, its clinical applicability has been discussed in only a few cases. This article describes the case of a middle fossa epidural abscess successfully drained through a fully endoscopic endonasal corridor and discusses the key technical points.OBSERVATIONSThe authors describe an 8-year-old boy who presented with worsening headache, fatigue, emesis, and fever and was diagnosed with a left middle fossa epidural abscess associated with sphenoid sinusitis. Surgical access to the medial middle fossa is challenging, traditionally requiring a standard frontotemporal craniotomy with significant extradural dissection if approached extradurally or mobilization of the temporal lobe if approached intradurally. Endoscopic endonasal surgery has significantly evolved over the last few decades, making a variety of midline/paramidline skull base regions accessible. In recent years, the endoscopic endonasal middle fossa approach has been explored mainly through cadaveric and experimental studies, demonstrating promise.1, 2 However, there remains a paucity of data regarding its clinical applicability.

An epidural abscess is a neurosurgical emergency requiring prompt treatment. It can occur predominantly as a result of progressive craniofacial infections, such as sinusitis, otitis media, and dental abscess.3–5 The standard therapeutic approach includes combined rhinological and neurosurgical management, as well as adequate long-term (typically a 6-week course) antibiotic therapy.3, 6–8 Although advances in antibiotic therapy have improved outcomes, neurosurgical interventions continue to have a significant role in eradicating the source of infection and thus are likely required.3, 4, 7–9 If a case is appropriately treated, the prognosis is not necessarily dismal;4 however, any delays in diagnosis and initiation of the treatment can result in morbidity and mortality.4, 7, 10

Epidural abscesses can occur in pediatric patients, mainly in association with sinusitis, either directly through the sinus wall or via the valveless venous system connecting the sinus mucosa and the dural veins.6, 10, 11 Although the therapeutic strategy for pediatric epidural abscess is largely similar to that for adults, there are several points to carefully consider. While the literature suggests that craniotomies are unlikely to result in craniofacial asymmetry,12–15 there remains uncertainty regarding the potential negative effects on cranial growth in pediatric patients, considering that the cranial base continues to develop and change for at least 10 years postnatally and that the cranial vault does not fully fuse until an even later age.16–18 Additionally, diagnosing epidural abscesses in children can be challenging; since they are not always the best sources of information, diagnosis tends to be delayed, providing enough time for suppuration to be significantly established, possibly leading to an undesirable clinical course.6

We recently encountered the case of a middle fossa epidural abscess in an 8-year-old boy. In addition to performing endoscopic sinus surgery to address the causative sinusitis, we included middle fossa drilling through the established nasal corridor to accomplish surgical drainage of the epidural abscess in a single procedure. Here, the detailed surgical techniques are shared.

Illustrative Case

History and Examination

An 8-year-old boy with no known medical history was admitted to an outside hospital for the treatment of bacteremia after his blood culture turned out to be positive for non-typeable Haemophilus influenzae. Prior to admission, he had a 4-day history of worsening headache, fatigue, emesis, and fever following a 2-week history of rhinorrhea. Initial image surveillance revealed sinusitis involving the sphenoid sinus and the bilateral ethmoid sinuses, with an extradural fluid collection at the medial tip of the right middle fossa, which was compatible with an epidural abscess (Fig. 1). Despite slight improvement, he remained lethargic with a persistent headache and fever even after fluid resuscitation and initiation of antibiotic therapy (intravenous ceftriaxone [330 mg/kg/day] and metronidazole [30 mg/kg/day]). Consequently, he was transferred to our institution 2 days after the admission, aiming for surgical intervention for the middle fossa abscess. We accordingly sought a surgical plan to accomplish abscess drainage and sinus surgery simultaneously.

The fluid exhibits restricted diffusion (B). On a coronal CT (C), the abscess is located in the middle fossa, slightly above the foramen rotundum (yellow arrowhead), and seems accessible using a transnasal corridor through the maxillary strut (red dotted lines). Surgical Procedure

After the induction of standard general anesthesia, the patient was secured on the surgical bed in a supine position. A 3-pin skull clamp was used, with the head rotated and tilted so the nostril was comfortably accessed. Surgical navigation (StealthStation S7, Medtronic) was set up before the procedure. Straight, 30°, and 70°, 4-mm-diameter rigid endoscopes (VISERA 4K UHD, Olympus Medical Systems Corp.), either held in the hand (rhinologist) or secured in a robotic endoscope holder (neurosurgeon; UniARM, Mitaka Kohki Co., Ltd.), were used. A microdebrider (Straightshot M4 microdebrider, Medtronic) was utilized by the rhinologist for the nasal part of the procedure. A curved, 3-mm-diameter diamond burr (Midas Rex, Medtronic) was used for skull base drilling. Nasal packing with 0.02% epinephrine was performed as the surgical preparation.

The nasal part of the procedure was started on the left side by medializing the middle turbinate and resecting the uncinate process, followed by the removal of the anterior ethmoid cells. The medial wall of the left maxillary sinus was then fully opened. After additionally removing the posterior ethmoid cells, the sphenoid sinus was entered through the middle nasal corridor, during which frank pus was encountered and thus was sent to culture (Fig. 2A). These procedures were repeated on the right side without fully opening the right maxillary sinus to accomplish bilateral sinus surgery (Fig. 2B).

Intraoperatively, there was frank pus (blue arrow) coming out of the sphenoid sinus while removing the left posterior ethmoid cells (A). The left sphenoid, maxillary, and ethmoid sinuses were opened through the middle nasal corridor (B). The mucosa of the posterior wall of the maxillary sinus was dissected to expose the bone in front of the pterygopalatine fossa (C), which was then removed to widely expose the pterygopalatine fossa (D). The purple dotted line(D) indicates a periosteal incision to allow for gentle lateral retraction of its contents to expose the bone behind them. While laterally retracting the pterygopalatine fossa, a small amount of frank pus (green arrow, E) was confirmed between the periosteum and the bone. The epidural space was entered by drilling the maxillary strut, allowing for drainage of the abscess (white arrow, F). A 7-Fr feeding tube was inserted in the epidural space for meticulous irrigation (G). Transparent views (HandI) and surgical simulation images (J–L) correspond with the intraoperative images (C–F). As the neurosurgical part of the procedure, the pterygopalatine fossa was widely exposed by drilling the posterior wall of the maxillary sinus (Fig. 2C). The superomedial aspect of the periosteum of the pterygopalatine fossa was incised (Fig. 2D) to allow for gentle lateral retraction of its contents to expose the area lateral to the pterygoid wedge (Fig. 2E). Care should be taken to not put too much pressure on the connective tissues to avoid injury to the pterygopalatine ganglion. Surgical navigation was utilized to confirm important anatomical landmarks, including the maxillary nerve, vidian canal, and superior orbital fissure, leading to identification of the maxillary strut. The maxillary strut was then meticulously drilled under an oblique view using the 70° scope to approach the middle fossa. There was a gush of purulence once entering the extradural space (Fig. 2F). After additional drilling to slightly widen the bony window, meticulous irrigation of the abscess cavity was performed using normal saline containing amikacin (200 mg/500 mL; Fig. 2G). The cavity was left open for continuous drainage. No nasal packing was performed. Postoperative Course

Immediate postoperative computed tomography (CT) confirmed the created bony fistula communicating with the sphenoid sinus and the middle fossa (Fig. 3A and B). The patient tolerated surgery without neurological deficit, suggesting dysfunction of the vidian nerve or maxillary nerve, such as hypolacrimation and hypoesthesia of the face and palate. His headache and fever promptly improved by postoperative day 1. Postoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed the disappearance of the epidural abscess (Fig. 3C and D). The patient was transferred back to the previous hospital on postoperative day 4 and was eventually discharged home without neurological deficit after completing a 6-week course of intravenous antibiotic therapy. The patient remained free from symptoms without radiological evidence of recurrence at 1 year following surgery.

Postoperative coronal (A) and axial (B) CT scans demonstrate the bony window through the maxillary strut (red dotted lines) just above the foramen rotundum (yellow arrowheads). Postoperative contrast T1-weighted (C) and diffusion-weighted (D) MRI revealed the disappearance of the epidural abscess.

Discussion

Observations

With the present technique, we achieved successful drainage of the middle fossa epidural abscess, in addition to clearing up the sinusitis, in a single surgery with minimal invasiveness, reducing the infectious source and contributing to prompt symptom improvement. Although the classic recommendation advocates a large craniotomy for complete eradication of intracranial abscesses, recent studies appear to favor less invasive approaches. Endoscopic endonasal drainage is one such technique, which has been successfully used for anterior fossa epidural abscesses according to the literature.19–21 A small eyebrow craniotomy has been recently performed for surgical drainage of an anterior fossa epidural abscess, without a large craniotomy.22 In our case, however, a trajectory through an eyebrow incision was inapplicable given the anatomical location of the middle fossa. There is a case report in which burr hole drainage was successfully performed for a middle fossa epidural abscess,5 though it would require an incision and a burr hole in the temporal area outside the hairline, which might not only injure facial nerve branches but also affect cosmetic outcomes and thus remains controversial. Having said that, a standard frontotemporal craniotomy may sound too aggressive for pediatric patients. From the standpoint of balancing surgical invasiveness and benefits, our method is deemed reasonable. To our knowledge, this is the first technical report describing fully endoscopic endonasal drainage of a middle fossa epidural abscess.

Lessons

The endoscopic endonasal approach may be applicable to middle fossa epidural abscesses, resulting in prompt clinical improvement. There are a few points to consider for successful access to the middle fossa. First, the intraoperative use of surgical navigation is desirable, as the surgical route may not be familiar to many neurosurgeons. Second, the maxillary strut is a key structure for approaching the middle fossa,23 though its anatomical proximity to several critical structures, including the maxillary nerve, vidian nerve, and orbit, is a significant concern. There have been several recent reports describing endoscopic endonasal middle fossa approaches, which serve as good references.1, 2, 23, 24 Third, to establish a safe and effective route, the anatomical variation of the lateral recess of the sphenoid sinus needs to be understood. Although the middle fossa was reached only with gentle retraction of the pterygopalatine fossa in our case, which had a Li type 2 lateral recess,25 establishing an endonasal corridor to the middle fossa in cases with a Li type 3 lateral recess might require more thorough transposition of the pterygopalatine fossa, as detailed by Pinheiro-Neto et al.24 A curved powered drill under an oblique-viewing endoscope will be the best fit in cases in which a narrow curved trajectory is needed. Fourth, given the small nasal corridors in pediatric patients and the concomitant sinus surgery, support from experienced otorhinolaryngologists is essential. Although the need for endoscopic sinus surgery for acute sinusitis–associated intracranial infections remains unclear,3, 9 it theoretically reduces the infectious source and may bring a certain benefit in disease control.6, 26 Despite these efforts, it should be noted that appropriate long-term antibiotic treatment is essential to prevent recurrence.

There are several limitations that should be considered when interpreting our report. First, the present method could only be applicable to medially located middle fossa abscesses, which are usually associated with sphenoid sinusitis; laterally located ones, such as those associated with otitis media, may be unreachable through the present corridor. Second, the efficacy and safety of the present method need to be further assessed with case accumulation since the risk of recurrence may not be negligible if the surgery consists of drainage alone.27 Although we routinely use amikacin-containing saline irrigation for cases with infection, it should be noted that the efficacy of intraoperative antimicrobial irrigation remains uncertain.28

In conclusion, the present technique allows for endoscopic endonasal drainage of a middle fossa epidural abscess on top of endoscopic sinus surgery to clear the infectious origins, possibly leading to prompt symptom improvement.
