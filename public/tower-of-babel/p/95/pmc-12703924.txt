Comparative pharmacokinetic and bioequivalence of nine oral ivermectin formulations in dogs
Authors: Suyoung Kim, Syed Al Jawad Sayem, HyunYoung Chae, Sung-Won Park, Ling Gui, Seung-Chun Park, JeongWoo Kang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ImportanceCanine heartworm disease remains a major global health issue. Despite the wide availability of ivermectin (IVM) formulations, pharmacokinetic and bioequivalence data are limited.ObjectiveThis study evaluated the pharmacokinetics and bioequivalence of eight oral IVM formulations (B–I) compared with the innovator product (A) in dogs.MethodsForty-five healthy dogs (mean body weight: 14.3 kg) were divided into nine groups (n = 5). Each received 6.2 µg/kg of IVM orally. Blood samples were collected up to 72 h post-dosing, and plasma IVM concentrations were quantified by liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry. Pharmacokinetic parameters were analyzed using a one-compartment model. Bioequivalence was assessed non-compartmentally using 90% confidence intervals for maximum concentration (Cmax) and area under the plasma concentration–time curve (AUC72h), with acceptance limits of 80–125%.ResultsAbsorption (Ka = 0.09–0.16 h−1), elimination half-life (T1/2 = 4.89–14.97 h), and systemic exposure (Cmax = 6.07–7.59 ng/mL) varied among formulations. Formulation C showed prolonged elimination (14.97 h) and higher exposure (AUC72h = 214.16 ng·h/mL), whereas formulation H exhibited faster absorption but lower exposure (AUC72h = 114.36 ng·h/mL). Simulated dosing indicated that most formulations maintained plasma levels above the minimum effective concentration (0.2 ng/mL), except formulation E. Only formulation G met full bioequivalence with the reference product.Conclusions and RelevanceAll formulations achieved plasma concentrations sufficient for prophylaxis, but only B, C, and G satisfied bioequivalence criteria. Larger sample sizes and standardized evaluation guidelines are recommended for multi-formulation bioequivalence studies.

INTRODUCTION

Ivermectin (IVM), a macrocyclic lactone derived from Streptomyces avermitilis, is a well-known anti-parasitic agent with broad-spectrum activity [1]. Its primary mechanism involves selectively binding to glutamate-gated chloride channels present in invertebrates but absent in mammals [2]. This binding increases chloride ion influx, leading to hyperpolarisation of neuronal and muscular membranes, causing parasite paralysis and death [3]. IVM also interacts with gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA)-gated channels; however, its inability to cross the blood-brain barrier at therapeutic doses ensures a wide safety margin in mammals [4]. This selectivity minimises its effect on mammalian GABA-gated channels, further contributing to its broad therapeutic index and safe use across species [5].

First introduced in 1981 by Merck Sharp & Dohme as an anti-parasitic agent [6], IVM remains the leading anti-parasitic medication for livestock worldwide. Its remarkable efficacy against both endoparasites and ectoparasites, even at very low doses (μg/kg), contributes to its wide therapeutic margin [178]. Widely used in mammals, IVM requires varying doses depending on the target parasite: approximately 6 μg/kg is effective for monthly heartworm prevention in dogs while treating intestinal nematodes (e.g., strongyloidiasis, ascariasis, trichuriasis, and ancylostomiasis) typically requires 200–400 µg/kg, adjusted for pathology, age, and weight. Ectoparasite treatment (e.g., scabies, larva migrant, and myiasis) often requires higher doses, typically 200–600 µg/kg, with the potential for repeated administration. IVM maintains a strong safety profile owing to its selective binding to invertebrate neuronal ion channels and limited mammalian blood-brain barrier penetration [9]. However, acute (4–12 h) or mild (2–3 days) toxicity symptoms, including lethargy, disorientation, seizures, and respiratory distress, may occur, particularly in individuals with MDR1 gene mutations [10]. The dose varies by species and parasite type, highlighting the importance of dose-specific pharmacokinetic studies [7].

The pharmacokinetics of IVM have been studied in various species, including cattle, sheep, goats, pigs, horses, and dogs [1]. One study investigated six healthy lactating Istrian Pramenka sheep, a Slovenian dairy breed, each nursing a 5–9-week-old suckling lamb, following a subcutaneous (SC) dose of 0.2 mg/kg IVM. IVM concentration-time profiles in sheep plasma, milk, and lamb plasma revealed significant inter-animal variability attributed to differences in absorption, metabolism, and body composition. These findings enhanced the understanding of IVM kinetics in lactating sheep, with detectable levels persisting for over 35 days post-treatment [11]. Another study assessed the pharmacokinetics of a new commercial IVM formulation in six healthy adult goats following intravenous (IV) and SC administration. IV data were best characterised by a two-compartment model, while SC administration demonstrated high absorption with a bioavailability of 91.8%, indicating the effectiveness of the formulation in goats [12]. However, pharmacokinetic studies following monthly oral administration of low dose IVM (6.2 µg/kg) for heartworm prevention in dogs remain limited.

Currently, nine IVM formulations are used in Korea, each with different ingredients, yet none have undergone bioequivalence testing. Such studies are essential for ensuring that generics are therapeutically equivalent to the reference drug [13]. These studies typically evaluate pharmacokinetic parameters such as maximum concentration (Cmax) and the area under the plasma concentration-time curve (AUC), with a standard bioequivalence range of 80.00%–125.00%, based on the 90% confidence intervals (CIs) for the pharmacokinetic metric ratio between the test and reference products [1415].

Therefore, this study aimed to 1) conduct a comparative pharmacokinetic analysis for heartworm prevention and 2) evaluate the bioequivalence of the original IVM and eight generic formulations in dogs. These findings are intended to provide valuable insights into the pharmacokinetic profiles and clinical compatibility of IVM formulations.

All formulations contained two common active ingredients: IVM and pyrantel pamoate. Pyrantel did not affect the pharmacokinetics of IVM, a well-controlled study in dogs demonstrated that IVM and pyrantel pamoate, when administered together in a chewable tablet formulation, did not interfere with each other’s therapeutic action or pharmacokinetics [15]. To focus on the pharmacokinetics of IVM, all formulations of IVM were administered orally at a dose of 6.2 µg/kg body weight.

Animal experimental procedure and treatments

The animal experiment was conducted at the Korea Animal Testing (KUlF, Korea) specialising in preclinical and clinical trials. The animals were housed individually in an environment with a 12 h light/dark cycle, and the ambient temperature was maintained at 25°C–28°C. The animals’ unrestricted access to food and water throughout the study. Forty-five male beagle dogs weighing an average of 14.3 kg and aged between 2 and 3 years, were randomly divided into nine groups of five animals each and treated as described below.

Innovator (formulation A): IVM (6.2 µg/kg) and pyrantel (5 mg/kg) were administered orally at 30-day intervals, with one tablet per month, resulting in 12 administrations per year. Animals in the formulation A to I groups were administered the IVM test formulations following the same methodology and dosage as the innovator product for the comparative pharmacokinetics and bioequivalence trial (Fig. 1). The animal study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Ministry of Agriculture, Food and Rural Affairs, Korea (approval number: 2022-321) and conducted in accordance with animal testing guidelines.

Collection and processing of blood samples

The IVM tablet formulation, specifically designed for canines, was administered orally as a single dose per group based on body weight. This was followed by a 12-day blood monitoring period and a subsequent 7-day observation phase for potential abnormalities. Blood samples (3 mL each) were collected from the jugular vein of each dog at the following time intervals: 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10, 24, 48, and 72 h, using vacutainer heparin tubes (Becton Dickinson and Company, USA). Plasma samples were obtained by centrifuging the blood at 2,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. To each 0.5 mL of plasma, 1.5 mL of 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile was added for plasma protein extraction. The mixtures were vortexed for 20 min and then centrifuged at 5,000 × g for 30 min. The supernatant was evaporated under nitrogen at 50°C until the volume was reduced to 500 µL. The processed samples were subsequently stored at −70°C.

Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis

Plasma IVM concentrations were determined using a Shimadzu LC-MS 8045 triple-quadrupole mass spectrometer coupled with a Nexera X2 UHPLC system and an ESI interface. For chromatographic separation, an XBridge BEH C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 2.5 µm; Waters, USA) was used at 40°C with a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min and an injection volume of 2 µL. A gradient elution was applied over a 10-min run using mobile phase A (0.1% formic acid in water) and mobile phase B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile): 0.00–1.00 min at 10% B, 1.01–5.00 min at 95% B, 5.01–7.00 min at 95% B, and 7.01–10.00 min at 10% B. The mass spectrometer was operated in positive ESI mode, and IVM was quantified using multiple reaction monitoring. The optimized parameters included an interface temperature of 150°C, a heating gas flow of 10 L/min, a DL temperature of 250°C, a heating block temperature of 400°C, a drying gas flow of 10 L/min, and a nebulizing gas flow of 3 L/min.

Validation of IVM quantification methods

The specificity of IVM quantification was assessed using a standard IVM solution, IVM-spiked dog plasma, and untreated dog plasma to evaluate potential matrix interference at the retention time of IVM. A stock solution of IVM was prepared by dissolving IVM in 0.1% aqueous formic acid to obtain a concentration of 1 mg/mL. This stock solution was subsequently diluted to produce a series of standard IVM solutions. Known volumes of these standard solutions were added to the untreated dog plasma to generate spiked plasma samples with IVM concentrations of 50, 100, 200, and 300 ng/mL. All samples were filtered through 0.2 µm polyvinylidene fluoride syringe filters prior to LC-MS/MS analysis. Each sample was analysed in triplicate, and IVM concentrations were quantified using LC-MS/MS following sample injection.

Comparative pharmacokinetic analysis and bioequivalence

A comparative pharmacokinetic analysis was conducted to evaluate nine IVM formulations, including the reference (Innovator formulation A) and eight generic test formulations B–I, for their potential to prevent heartworm. The study utilised a one- or non-compartment open model to assess the absorption, distribution, and elimination kinetics of each formulation and identify the most clinically advantageous formulation. The concentration-time profiles obtained from the plasma of individual animals were analysed using WinNonlin software (version 6.1; Statistical Consultants, USA). To determine which formulations were bioequivalent to Innovator A, a bioequivalence study was conducted following the internationally accepted guidelines set by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), European Medicines Agency (EMA), and World Health Organization. The assessment was based on the 90% CI of the test-to-reference ratios for Cmax and AUC, which were required to fall within the bioequivalence acceptance range of 80%–125%. Pharmacokinetic parameters for each animal were evaluated through non-compartmental analysis (NCA). This approach enabled the determination of key pharmacokinetic parameters, including time to maximum concentration (Tmax), AUC, Cmax, and elimination half-life (T1/2). Additionally, NCA enabled the estimation of the Cmax of IVM and the Tmax. The terminal-phase disposition rate constant (λ) was estimated using least-squares linear regression on the natural log-transformed IVM concentrations over time. The half-life (T1/2λ) was subsequently calculated using the formula T1/2 = 0.693/λ.

Statistical analysis

The results were expressed as the mean from five replicates. Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA, followed by Dunnett’s Test to compare the difference between the control formulation (A) and all other formulations (B–I). The analysis was conducted using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) and R statistical software (version 4.1.2; R Core Team, 2021). A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For bioequivalence assessments between formulations, a 90% CI approach was applied using ANOVA on log-transformed data, incorporating sequence, subject variability, period, and formulation as fixed effects. Non-parametric methods were deemed inappropriate for this evaluation [16].

RESULTS

Standard solution preparation

IVM was weighed, dissolved in methanol (MeOH) to prepare a 1.0 mg/mL stock solution in a volumetric flask, and stored at 4°C in sealed polypropylene tubes. A working solution was prepared by diluting the stock solution with MeOH to the desired concentration.

Validation of IVM quantification methods

The IVM peak derived from the standard solution eluted at approximately 7.2 min (Fig. 2). Under optimised LC-MS/MS conditions, symmetric IVM peaks with improved morphology were observed in both standard and plasma samples. Notably, the mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) and retention time of IVM in spiked plasma samples were comparable to those obtained from standard solutions (Fig. 2). The optimised method enabled rapid, efficient, and straightforward extraction without derivatisation, reducing analysis time. Linearity, selectivity, accuracy, and precision were evaluated to validate the assay, with results summarised in Table 1. Linearity was evaluated using a calibration curve from six standard solution concentrations (0, 1, 5, 10, 20, and 50 ng/mL), with an r2 > 0.99 indicating excellent linearity (Table 1). Recovery and precision were assessed at 2, 5, and 10 ng/mL using triplicate samples, yielding mean recoveries of 91.2%–107.8% and coefficients of variation (CV) below 10%. Sensitivity was determined by calculating the limits of detection (LOD) and quantification (LOQ) based on signal to noise ratios of 3 and 10, respectively. The LOD and LOQ for IVM were 0.5 and 1.7 ng/mL, respectively.

Even though formulations H and I were better described by a two-compartment model based on their biphasic profiles, we calculated the Akaike Information Criterion for both one- and two-compartment models using formulations H and I. Since the AIC values were nearly similar for both model types, we chose the one-compartment model for consistency in comparison. Another reason is that the dosing for all formulations had already been fixed in this study. Most formulations-maintained plasma IVM concentrations above 0.2 ng/mL for up to 72 h. The absorption rate constant (Ka) was 0.09–0.16 h−1 across all formulations. Formulation H had the highest Ka (0.16 h−1), 37% higher than that of formulation A, while formulation G (0.09 h−1) was 17% lower than that of formulation A, indicating slower absorption (Table 2). The elimination rate constant (K10) varied between 0.05 and 0.14 h−1, with formulation C (0.05 h−1) showing a 56% reduction compared to formulation A, suggesting prolonged elimination. Absorption half-life (T1/2ka) ranged from 4.45 to 7.36 h, with formulation G (7.36 h) 20.5% longer and formulation C (4.61 h) 24.5% shorter than that of formulation A. Elimination half-life (T1/2k10) varied from 4.89 to 14.97 h, with formulation C (14.97 h) being 2.29-fold longer than formulation A, indicating slower elimination. The Vd ranged from 0.31 to 0.59 L/kg, with formulation C (0.59 L/kg) being 1.85-fold greater than formulation A, suggesting higher tissue penetration. Cmax varied between 6.07 and 7.59 ng/mL, with formulation B concentration (7.59 ng/mL) being 3.0% higher and formulation C (6.19 ng/mL) 16.0% lower than that for formulation A. Tmax or time to reach Cmax, was shortest for formulation H (6.72 h, 26.2% faster than formulation A) and longest for formulation C (11.32 h, 24.3% slower than formulation A). The AUC-time curve from zero to t ranged from 114.36 to 214.16 ng·h/mL, with formulation C (214.16 ng·h/mL) 17.6% higher and formulation H (114.36 ng·h/mL) 37.2% lower than formulation A. Mean residence time (MRT) was longest for formulation C (28.24 h, 1.55-fold greater than formulation A), suggesting extended drug retention, while formulation H (13.47 h) was 26.1% shorter. These differences in absorption, elimination, and systemic exposure influence the pharmacokinetic behaviour of IVM and its potential efficacy in dogs. Thus, pharmacokinetic parameters were used to simulate plasma concentration-time profiles of IVM over 12 months, following monthly dosing at 11 intervals, to prevent heartworm larvae based on different compartment model. The data presented are based on a long-term simulation spanning approximately one year, assuming monthly dosing intervals. The simulation was performed using the first-order elimination equation [Ct = Cmax × e^(−kt)], which is widely accepted for modeling drug concentration over time. To ensure reliability, input parameters were derived from both non-compartmental and one-compartmental pharmacokinetic models based on actual observed data.

Fig. 4 shows simulated formulations A–I, showing peak plasma concentration and minimum plasma concentrations over 12 months, with monthly doses to confirm clinical efficacy. Peak plasma concentrations were reached shortly after each dose, followed by a predictable decline. Notably, plasma concentrations remained above 0.2 ng/mL of mean effective concentration (MEC), the critical threshold for L3 heartworm prophylaxis, for at least 48 h post dose [17]. Non-compartmental pharmacokinetic parameters used in bioequivalence were applied to simulate IVM plasma changes once monthly for 12 months (Fig. 5).

Bioequivalence analysis

To evaluate bioequivalence, Cmax and AUC data were obtained from pharmacokinetic parameters calculated using a non-compartmental model (Table 3). Bioequivalence was assessed based on the 90% CI of Cmax and AUC72h ratios, with the acceptable range set at 80%–125% [15]. The reference formulation (A) showed a Cmax ratio of 104.09%. Among the test products, formulations C (105.69%), D (95.56%), E (94.12%), F (100.19%), G (94.16%), and H (95.58%) had Cmax ratios within the acceptable range. However, bioequivalence was confirmed only for C, D, E, F, G, and H based on the 90% CI falling within 80%–125%. Specifically, formulation G showed a Cmax ratio of 94.16% with a 90% CI of 82.11%–106.21%, fully within the accepted limits. Formulations B and I failed to meet Cmax bioequivalence due to CI ranges of 90.91%–148.21% and 77.87%–104.33%, respectively (Fig. 6A). Regarding AUC72h, formulation A exhibited a ratio of 112.98%. Only formulations G (96.84%; 90% CI, 81.37%–112.31%) and I (106.65%; 90% CI, 89.82%–123.49%) met the criteria for AUC72h bioequivalence. Formulations B (116.79%; 97.21%–136.38%), C (114.64%; 94.75%–134.54%), D (72.82%; 57.44%–88.20%), E (82.32%; 66.05%–98.60%), F (88.36%; 73.38%–103.33%), and H (85.91%; 71.55%–100.27%) failed to achieve bioequivalence, with their CI ranges extending outside the predefined limits (Fig. 6B). Importantly, only formulation G met the bioequivalence criteria for both Cmax and AUC72h, indicating comparable systemic exposure and pharmacokinetic behavior to the innovator formulation.

DISCUSSION

IVM is primarily an anti-parasitic drug but also has antibacterial, antiviral, and anti-inflammatory properties, with potential applications beyond parasitic infections. Its impact on the immune system and potential contributions to antibiotic resistance highlights the need for further research to ensure its safety and efficacy. Therefore, the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of IVM have been extensively investigated across various animal species [1819]. Oral administration is frequently used for preventing heartworm disease and endoparasites in dogs [20]. However, pharmacokinetic data available for this usage remain limited. This study aims to evaluate the pharmacokinetics of nine IVM formulations following oral administration in dogs and assess their bioequivalence based on the collected data.

Accurate determination of pharmacokinetic data following oral administration of IVM in dogs requires precise measurement of IVM concentration in the blood. IVM, a drug utilised in both human and veterinary medicine, is traditionally analysed using high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). A rapid, simple, and sensitive HPLC method was developed for the simultaneous determination of triclabendazole and IVM, providing an effective analytical procedure for clinical pharmacokinetic studies. This method demonstrates high accuracy with minimal relative standard deviation, while robustness testing shows no significant variation under minor experimental modifications [21]. Building on this foundation, this study employed an advanced LC-MS/MS technique. This method represents a significant improvement in the detection of IVM in plasma, providing faster, more sensitive, and more accurate results than conventional methods. The LC-MS/MS technique demonstrated a linear response across a concentration range of 0–50 ng/mL, with the IVM peak occurring at approximately 7.2 min (Fig. 2). The retention times and m/z of IVM -spiked plasma samples were consistent with those of the standard solution. Analytical linearity was confirmed with an r2 value of 0.9997, well above the minimum requirement of 0.9900 [22], demonstrating an excellent correlation between drug concentration and signal response (Table 1). Precision, expressed as the %CV, falls within the acceptable range for analytical method validation [23]. Recovery analysis, based on three injection doses, further revealed the reliability of the method in quantifying IVM from dog plasma. LOD and LOQ were determined using the calibration curve slope and SD of mean responses in accordance with recognised validation standards. Additionally, compliance with the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety’s guidelines reinforces the methodological integrity and reproducibility of the procedure [24].

The pharmacokinetic profiles of nine IVM formulations, including one innovator product (formulation A) and eight test formulations B–I, were comparatively analysed in dogs following a single oral dose of 6.2 µg/kg. Plasma IVM concentrations were measured over 72 h. Fig. 3 depicts the resulting concentration-time profiles. The mean plasma concentration–time profiles for each formulation group (n = 5) are shown in Fig. 3 along with SD error bars. There is some variation, especially in the elimination phase and around the Cmax, but this is to be expected in pharmacokinetic studies with small animal groups. Biological variables like variations in absorption, gastrointestinal transit time, and metabolic rates are probably the cause of the observed inter-individual differences [25]. The overall profiles show consistent trends across formulations, with distinct Cmax peaks and elimination patterns, despite the relatively large SD at some time points. This suggests that even at this exploratory stage, the pharmacokinetic behavior is still interpretable and significant for comparative analysis. Transparency is provided and realistic biological variability that could arise under comparable experimental conditions is highlighted by the inclusion of SD bars. Studies in veterinary species show that bioavailability is influenced by formulation type, lipid solubility, and metabolic pathways [2627]. Additionally, formulations with an extended MRT can increase drug retention and prolong anti-parasitic efficacy [28]. Formulation H and I exhibited biphasic profiles, best described by a two-compartment model. For consistency in parameter estimation, a one-compartment open model with first-order absorption was applied to all formulations (Table 2).

Cunningham et al. [17] identified the minimum protective plasma concentration of IVM as a critical parameter for effective heartworm prevention. Through extensive laboratory and field studies, they demonstrated that a sustained plasma level of 0.2 ng/mL provides 100% protection against Dirofilaria immitis infection in dogs. This threshold is especially important for the development of sustained-release formulations, as it ensures consistent prophylactic efficacy against canine heartworm disease [17]. This analysis revealed that most formulations sustained plasma IVM concentrations above 0.2 ng/mL throughout the study. The Ka ranged from 0.09 h−1 (formulation G) to 0.16 h−1 (formulation H), indicating variation in absorption speeds. Similarly, the K10 ranged from 0.0463 h−1 (formulation C) to 0.1419 h−1 (formulation H), suggesting variability in the elimination phase. Consequently, the T1/2ka ranged from 4.45 h (formulation H) to 7.36 h (formulation G), whileT1/2k10 ranged from 4.89 h (formulation H) to 14.97 h (formulation C). The apparent Vd varied considerably, ranging from 0.31 L/kg (formulation B) to 0.59 L/kg (formulation C). The apparent clearance (CL/F) also varied, with formulation H exhibiting the highest value (0.054 L/h/kg) and formulation C the lowest (0.027 L/h/kg). The Tmax ranged from 6.72 h (formulation H) to 11.32 h (formulation C), while the Cmax ranged from 6.07 ng/mL (formulation D) to 7.59 ng/mL (formulation B). The AUC-time curve from zero to infinity ranged from 115.12 ng·h/mL (formulation H) to 225.78 ng·h/mL (formulation C), while the MRT ranged from 13.47 h (formulation H) to 28.24 h (formulation C). These findings indicate significant inter-formulation variability in the pharmacokinetic parameters of IVM following oral administration in dogs.

Twelve-month pharmacokinetic simulation with 11 repeated doses was conducted using both one-compartmental (Fig. 4) and non-compartmental open models (Fig. 5) to assess long-term drug exposure. Heartworm transmission occurs when a mosquito ingests L1 larvae from an infected dog. This stimulates their development into L2 in the stomach of the mosquito and then into L3 in the infective stage in the salivary glands over 10–20 days. When the mosquito bites another dog, it injects L3 larvae into the skin, where they mature into L4 larvae within 7–14 days. Plasma concentration analysis revealed that trough levels, despite fluctuations, consistently exceeded the MEC of 0.2 ng/mL, ensuring continuous drug exposure necessary to prevent heartworm development. On the 5th day post-dose, drug concentrations in all formulations exceeded 0.68 ng/mL, remaining well above the MEC of 0.2 ng/mL for at least 48 h. This sustained drug exposure ensures effective prevention against L3 heartworm, with the non-compartmental open model demonstrating particular reliability as a prophylactic strategy. These simulations demonstrate that monthly administration maintains continuous therapeutic coverage for most tested formulations. The pharmacokinetic profiles of IVM vary significantly with formulation composition, necessitating rigorous bioequivalence assessment to evaluate pharmacokinetic behaviour across different formulations [29]. These findings indicate the significant inter-formulation variability in IVM pharmacokinetics in dogs, underscoring the necessity of comprehensive bioequivalence evaluation when considering generic alternatives in clinical practice [22]. This variability aligns with that of previous reports on generic IVM formulations [30] and highlights the importance of considering formulation type and its effect on systemic exposure [31]. Further in vivo efficacy trials and pharmacodynamic modelling are warranted to establish the clinical relevance of these pharmacokinetic differences [32].

Regulatory standards from both the U.S. FDA and the EMA require that the 90% CIs for the geometric mean ratios of both Cmax and AUC fall within the accepted bioequivalence range of 80%–125%. Meeting this range ensures that the test product delivers the drug at a similar rate and extent as the reference (innovator) product, supporting its use as a therapeutic alternative [33]. Among the formulations tested, only formulation G met the bioequivalence criteria for both Cmax (94.16%; 90% CI, 82.11%–106.21%) and AUC72h (96.84%; 90% CI, 81.37%–112.31%). These results suggest that formulation G closely matches the absorption profile of the innovator, making it a suitable candidate for substitution under current regulatory requirements. The remaining formulations showed only partial bioequivalence. Formulations C, D, E, F, and H met the Cmax criteria but failed to achieve equivalence for AUC72h. This implies they reach comparable peak concentrations, but the overall drug exposure over time is not consistently in line with the reference product. On the other hand, formulation I met the AUC72h criteria (106.65%; 90% CI, 89.82%–123.49%) but did not satisfy the Cmax requirement, pointing to a slower or altered absorption rate that could affect how quickly the drug takes effect. These findings emphasize the need to assess both the speed and extent of absorption in bioequivalence studies. Using a NCA alongside established FDA and EMA criteria provided a thorough evaluation, ultimately identifying formulation G as the only one fully comparable to the innovator. This reinforces the critical role of detailed pharmacokinetic analysis in ensuring that generic formulations are both safe and therapeutically effective.

The finding that only one generic formulation (formulation G) met the bioequivalence criteria highlights important concerns regarding inter-product variability. The failure of other formulations to meet the accepted range for either Cmax or AUC72h may reflect differences in formulation quality, excipient composition, manufacturing processes, or dissolution behaviour. Variability in absorption rates, bioavailability, and elimination kinetics—particularly among formulations C, D, E, F, H, and I—suggests inconsistency in drug release and systemic exposure. While all formulations reached plasma concentrations above the therapeutic threshold, the failure to meet strict regulatory bioequivalence standards raises questions about the interchangeability of these products. These findings underscore the need for stricter quality control in generic formulation development and emphasize the importance of evaluating not just active pharmaceutical content but also formulation performance under physiological conditions.

Although differences in pharmacokinetic parameters such as Cmax and AUC72h were observed among the tested formulations, these variations are unlikely to have a meaningful impact on clinical efficacy. Importantly, all formulations maintained plasma IVM concentrations above the established MEC (0.2 ng/mL) required for the prevention of D. immitis infection, as reported by Cunningham et al. [17]. This sustained exposure suggests that, despite statistical variability, the therapeutic efficacy of most formulations is preserved under practical dosing conditions. Given the broad therapeutic index of IVM, the observed differences in systemic exposure can be considered clinically negligible. Nevertheless, further pharmacodynamic investigations and long-term field studies are warranted to confirm the therapeutic equivalence of non-bioequivalent formulations in diverse clinical scenarios.

This investigation represents the first simultaneous bioequivalence and pharmacokinetic comparison of nine commercially available oral IVM products at the established therapeutic dose in dogs. By leveraging a cohort of five animals per formulation—consistent with precedents in canine and other species pharmacokinetic research—we were able to efficiently screen a large number of products and identify those meeting preliminary bioequivalence criteria. However, for subsequent formulation development and confirmatory studies, adherence to regulatory bioequivalence guidelines will require expanding group sizes (e.g., ≥ 12 dogs per arm) to ensure adequate statistical power and narrow CIs. We therefore recommend that future work incorporate larger cohorts or complementary in vitro–in vivo correlation studies to robustly validate generic IVM formulations.

In summary, this study showed significant variability in the pharmacokinetic profiles of oral IVM formulations administered to dogs. Differences in systemic exposure (AUC), CL/F, and Vd suggest that while absorption may be similar across formulations, elimination kinetics and overall bioavailability differ substantially. Bioequivalence analysis revealed that only formulation G met the criteria for both Cmax and AUC72h, indicating its potential interchangeability with the reference product. Other formulations demonstrated partial or no bioequivalence, emphasising the need for careful selection in clinical settings. These findings highlight the need for comprehensive multidimensional bioequivalence testing that extends beyond traditional pharmacokinetic metrics. Variability in terminal half-life, clearance, and drug persistence affects treatment outcomes, especially for drugs with narrow therapeutic endpoints. Given the important role of IVM in veterinary medicine, clinicians should exercise caution when substituting generic formulations.
