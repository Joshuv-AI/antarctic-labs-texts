Clinician and patient attitudes toward locoregional treatment of oligometastatic oesophago-gastric cancers: a mixed-methods study
Authors: AS Lakha, A Gordon-Weeks, SR Markar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe role of locoregional treatment in oligometastatic oesophago-gastric (OG) cancer remains uncertain, with conflicting trial evidence and limited guidance for clinical practice. This study aimed to evaluate clinician and patient attitudes toward locoregional management, assess clinical equipoise, and explore trade-offs between survival gain and quality of life (QOL) reduction in response to locoregional therapies.Materials and methodsA cross-sectional survey of clinicians involved in multidisciplinary management of OG cancer was conducted between August and December 2025. Scenario-based questions assessed acceptability of combined survival gain (6-15 months) and QOL reduction (5%-20%) as a modified discrete choice experiment. A clustered logistic regression model evaluated the independent effects of survival gain and QOL reduction on clinician acceptability. Patient and public involvement workshops were undertaken to understand patient preferences in potential future trial design.ResultsThirty clinicians from 24 centres participated. Sixty percent reported offering locoregional treatment following stability on systemic therapy. Approximately 93% of respondents indicated willingness to participate in a randomised controlled trial (RCT). Acceptability of treatment strategies increased with greater survival gain and decreased with increasing QOL reduction. In clustered regression analysis, each additional 3 months of survival gain increased the odds of acceptability by ∼75%, whereas each additional 5% QOL reduction decreased the odds by ∼45%.ConclusionThere is substantial variation in clinician attitudes toward locoregional treatment of oligometastatic OG cancer, but marked equipoise and high trial willingness exist. Acceptability is strongly influenced by the balance between survival benefit and QOL impact. These findings support the design of prospective RCTs incorporating patient-centred outcomes.Key words: gastric cancer, oesophageal cancer, patient involvement, survey

Introduction

Oesophago-gastric (OG) cancers are common, with over an annual incidence of 15 000 new cases per year in the UK.1,2 They are often diagnosed at an advanced stage, with up to 50% presenting with metastatic disease, the outcome for which is poor (5%, 5-year survival), despite advancements in systemic therapies.3 The current standard of care for metastatic OG cancer primarily involves palliative systemic treatment, with locoregional therapies (surgery, ablation, and targeted radiation) reserved for symptom management rather than as a curative approach. Nevertheless, the evolving recognition and anatomical definition of an ‘oligometastatic’ disease (OMD) state has prompted a reevaluation of the continuum between localised disease and widespread malignancy.

The oligometastatic paradigm suggests that selected patients with a limited metastatic burden may benefit from more aggressive locoregional therapies, potentially achieving prolonged disease control or enhanced survival when these are combined with systemic treatments. In several solid tumours, including colorectal,4 renal,5 and non-small-cell lung cancers,6 patients with OMD are regularly considered for surgical resection or targeted radiotherapy for both primary and metastatic sites. However, OG cancers present unique challenges including major morbidity from curative resection,7 high micrometastatic disease burden,8 and early peritoneal carcinomatosis,9 which disables extrapolation from guidelines for oligometastasis from other primary sites. For OG cancer, recent work to define OMD10,11 is now informing clinical practice; however, the role of locoregional therapy in oligometastatic OG cancer remains unclear and largely nonevidence based. Furthermore, recent breakthroughs in both targeted therapies and systemic immunotherapies have fundamentally altered the first-line therapeutic landscape. Novel agents directed against claudin 18.2,12 PD-L1,13 and HER214 provide yet more options to prolong survival in patients with metastatic disease.15,16

The evidentiary base supporting locoregional strategies in OG cancer is sparse and heterogeneous, comprising mainly retrospective analyses and highly selected patient cohorts.17,18 Reported clinical outcomes are variable, and there are ongoing concerns regarding treatment-associated morbidity, effects on quality of life (QOL), and the risk of postponing systemic therapy. As a result, clear consensus is lacking on patient selection criteria, optimal therapeutic modality, and the sequencing of locoregional interventions. Prospective trials including IKF-RENAISSANCE,19 AIO-FLOT3,20 and ESO-Shanghai 1321 showed conflicting results, incorporated patients with varying definitions of OMD, and unclear surgical quality assurance. To address this ambiguity, the European clinical practice guidelines for the definition, diagnosis, and treatment of oligometastatic oesophago-gastric cancer (OMEC-4) have recently been established.11 However, while the OMEC guidelines provide essential consensus-based frameworks for defining this disease state, standard international clinical guidelines still offer minimal direction regarding definitive, evidence-based treatment algorithms due to the ongoing deficit of completed, high-level phase III randomised controlled trials (RCTs) conducted in the context of OMEC-4. Therefore, this study sought to investigate clinician attitudes toward locoregional management of oligometastatic OG cancer through a national survey, utilising scenario-based acceptability questions to explore trade-offs between survival and QOL outcomes. In addition, patient and public involvement (PPI) activities were conducted to capture patient perspectives and contextualise clinician views. Collectively, these findings aim to assess perceived equipoise and guide the development of future prospective trials within this challenging and rapidly evolving area of OG cancer management.

Materials and Methods

Study design

This study comprised a cross-sectional survey (August to December 2025) of clinicians involved in the management of OG cancer, supplemented by PPI activities (October 2024). The primary aim was to explore clinician attitudes towards the use of locoregional treatment strategies in the setting of oligometastatic OG cancer and to assess perceived equipoise and recruitment feasibility for RCTs from both patient and clinician perspectives.

Survey development

The survey was developed by the study team, informed by existing literature on OMD and locoregional treatment strategies in upper gastrointestinal malignancies. Survey items were designed to capture respondent expertise, centre characteristics, current clinical practice, attitudes toward locoregional treatment following disease stability on systemic therapy, and willingness to participate in a multicentre RCT. Multiple participation was controlled by excluding duplicate entries before anonymisation for analysis.

Clinical scenarios were included to explore acceptable treatment strategies across differing presentations of OMD. Questions included a combination of single-response, multiple-response, and free-text items. The survey was piloted internally for clarity and clinical relevance before dissemination. Clinical scenarios were included to explore acceptable treatment strategies across differing presentations of OMD. Scenarios were constructed by the study steering committee following a review of available literature, mapping out realistic parameters based on survival ranges observed in recent phase II protocols.20 This was presented as a modified discrete choice experiment, comprising 12 distinct scenario-based questions, systematically cross-referencing all variations of survival gain (6, 9, 12, or 15 months) against QOL reduction thresholds (5%, 10%, or 20%). A link to the full survey is available in Supplementary Materials (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100382).

Participants and survey distribution

Eligible participants included consultant surgeons, medical and clinical oncologists involved in the management of patients with OG cancer, particularly those participating in multidisciplinary team (MDT) decision making. The survey was distributed electronically via the Association of Upper Gastrointestinal Surgery, professional networks, and clinical contacts. Participation was voluntary, sampling was nonprobabilistic (convenience sampling), and responses were anonymised at the point of analysis.

Patient and public involvement

PPI activities were undertaken in parallel with the clinician survey to capture patient perspectives on locoregional treatment in the oligometastatic setting. A broad range of PPI contributors comprising patients with OG cancer and relatives of patients with OG cancer (with both operable and nonoperable disease) were engaged through structured discussion sessions focusing on treatment priorities, attitudes toward treatment-related risk, and perspectives on clinical uncertainty and trial participation. Findings from these sessions are reported descriptively.

Data handling and analysis

Survey responses were exported into Microsoft Excel and analysed descriptively, with plots generated in R, and findings reported in line with the Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies.22 Respondent characteristics and centre-level variables were summarised using counts and percentages. For attitude-based questions, responses were reported as frequencies and proportions. Free-text responses were reviewed and summarised to provide contextual insights but were not subjected to formal qualitative thematic analysis due to the limited volume of responses.

Scenario-based questions were used to assess clinician acceptability of hypothetical treatment outcomes trading off survival gain against treatment-related QOL reduction as a modified discrete choice experiment. Each respondent was presented with a set of predefined scenarios (Supplementary Material, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100382) and asked to indicate which they considered acceptable. Scenarios varied systematically according to survival gain (6, 9, 12, or 15 months) and QOL reduction (5%, 10%, or 20%). These QOL intervals were selected to correspond with established clinical anchors used in validated oncological instruments, such as the EORTC QLQ-C30,23 where scores are standardly evaluated on a 0-100 scale. In these frameworks, a 5%-10% change represents a small but clinically meaningful alteration in functional status, whereas a 20% drop denotes a major, highly perceptible clinical deterioration. A heatmap was used to illustrate acceptance of treatment strategies across different OMD scenarios, with colour intensity representing the proportion of respondents endorsing each approach.

Responses were analysed using logistic regression, treating each respondent–scenario pair as a binary outcome (acceptable versus not acceptable). To account for repeated assessments by individual respondents, standard errors were clustered at the respondent level. Survival gain and QOL reduction were scaled to clinically interpretable units (per 3-month increase in survival gain and per five-percentage-point increase in QOL reduction).

Model coefficients were reported as log-odds with cluster-robust standard errors, and results summarised visually using a forest plot. Predicted probabilities of acceptability were generated for selected illustrative scenarios to aid interpretation. 95% confidence intervals (bounded 0-1) were obtained using the delta method.24 Given the exploratory nature of the study and the modest sample size, analyses were descriptive and hypothesis generating, with no formal inference regarding clinical decision-making intended.

Results

Thirty clinicians completed the survey. Respondents included upper gastrointestinal surgeons and medical and clinical oncologists working across 24 centres with varying annual case volumes of OG cancer (Table 1). Most (17/24) centres were UK based, with additional centre representation from Italy, Pakistan, Romania, and Denmark. All respondents were involved in MDT-level decision making for patients with oligometastatic OG cancers. The median number of OG cases reportedly seen by each centre was 170 (100-200) per year.

Current use of locoregional treatment

Practice patterns regarding locoregional treatment of oligometastatic OG cancer varied considerably. Sixty percent of respondents reported that their centre currently offers organ-specific therapy following disease stability on systemic treatment, while others (40%) reported that such approaches are not routinely considered. These treatments include surgery, ablation, radiotherapy, and stereotactic ablative radiotherapy offered on a case-by-case basis following discussion in a MDT, or tumour board meeting. Absence of guidelines and lack of published evidence for not offering locoregional therapies were reasons cited among respondents where these options were not offered at their centre.

Attitudes toward clinical equipoise and trial recruitment

Approximately 79.3% expressed either strong or some equipoise regarding the role of locoregional treatment in the oligometastatic setting. Reservations were related to limited evidence and concerns regarding treatment-related morbidity. Despite these uncertainties, 93% of respondents indicated that their centre would consider participation in a multicentre randomised trial comparing systemic therapy with locoregional treatment in selected patients. None of the respondents believed that lack of eligible patients was a barrier to trial participation.

Patient attitudes to locoregional therapies

A series of patient workshops were organised with members of Heartburn Cancer UK and the Oxfordshire Oesophageal and Stomach patient Organisation in October 2024. This included patients and family members of patients with OG cancers diagnosed at various stages with experience of a range of treatments, including surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy. Patients consistently expressed strong preference for the use of health-related QOL metrics as a primary outcome for trials looking at treatment of metastatic OG cancer. This formed the basis of the discrete choice experiment design. Patients were supportive of a clinical trial to give individuals the opportunity to access aggressive local treatment strategies applied with curative intent—aiming for long-term disease control—which they otherwise would not routinely access in standard palliative pathways. Patients were keen to ensure that locoregional therapies would not significantly alter their post-operative recovery above what is anticipated for an OG resection alone. Finally, all patients expressed a willingness to be randomly assigned to systemic therapy alone versus systemic therapy and locoregional treatment(s).

Acceptable treatment strategies by treatment outcome

Clinician preferences for or against locoregional treatment varied according to QOL loss versus survival gain. This was assessed using a modified discrete choice experiment set of questions where respondents indicated which combined outcome scenario would be acceptable when deciding whether to offer locoregional treatment of OMD. Most clinicians favoured maximal survival gain (15 months) with minimal QOL loss (up to –10%) (Figure 1). Two-thirds of clinicians felt a QOL reduction of 20% was unacceptable in any scenario of survival gain presented (up to 15 months gain). Interestingly, more modest survival gains (6 or 9 months) were also deemed acceptable in the presence of minimal QOL loss (−5%) by 33.3% and 36.7% of respondents, respectively.

In a clustered logistic regression accounting for repeated assessments by individual clinicians, greater survival gain was associated with increased odds of scenario acceptability, while increasing QOL reduction was associated with reduced odds (Figure 2). When scaled to clinically meaningful units, each additional 3 months of survival gain increased the odds of acceptability by ∼ 75%, whereas each additional 5% reduction in QOL reduced the odds by ∼ 45%.

Model-based predicted probabilities illustrated these trade-offs. Scenarios offering limited survival gain with substantial QOL reduction were associated with low predicted acceptability, whereas scenarios offering larger survival gains with modest QOL impact demonstrated substantially higher predicted probabilities of acceptability (Table 2). Model-based predicted probabilities for acceptability ranged from 5.6% [6-month gain with 20% QOL loss; 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.2% to 10.0%] to 65.0% (15-month gain with 5% QOL loss; 95% CI 48.0% to 81.9%). Intermediate scenarios showed graded probabilities consistent with greater tolerance for QOL deterioration only when substantial survival benefit was expected (Table 2).

Discussion

This study clearly demonstrates considerable variation in clinician perspectives regarding locoregional treatment of oligometastatic OG cancer, underscoring ongoing clinical equipoise in the absence of robust prospective evidence. Utilising an international clinician survey with scenario-based trade-off questions and informed by PPI, we demonstrate that both current practices and clinicians’ acceptance of intensive treatment strategies are highly contingent on the perceived balance between potential survival benefits and anticipated impacts on QOL.

A central finding is the pronounced heterogeneity in existing practice patterns. While most respondents indicated their centres offer organ-specific locoregional therapy following disease stability on systemic treatment, a considerable proportion do not. This variability aligns with the lack of definitive guideline recommendations, emphasising the influence of institutional culture and individual clinician judgement in managing oligometastatic OG cancer. Notably, the majority of respondents expressed a willingness to participate in multicentre RCTs involving locoregional therapy for OMD, indicating that, despite varied approaches, genuine equipoise exists and that prospective research is warranted. Interestingly, while 93% of respondents expressed institutional willingness to participate in a future multicentre RCT, historical precedents suggest that transitioning this into successful trial accrual remains a significant challenge. In clinical practice, the true pool of eligible patients is highly restricted, comprising only those who demonstrate adequate disease stability or response to systemic induction therapy while maintaining technical resectability or suitability for targeted radiation. Within this highly select and vulnerable window, strong treatment preferences held by either the clinician or the patient may eliminate equipoise, complicating randomisation. Therefore, the optimism captured in our survey must be balanced against documented real-world operational challenges in trial execution.

The scenario-based acceptability analysis portrays clinicians’ nuanced decision-making processes in this context of uncertainty. Clinician endorsement of locoregional treatments increased as projected survival benefit rose and diminished with greater expected QOL detriment, suggesting rational, incremental assessments rather than absolute positions regarding aggressive intervention. These tendencies were corroborated by both descriptive heatmaps and clustered logistic regression models, supporting the internal consistency of the results.

Clustered logit analysis further indicates that both survival gain and QOL impact independently inform clinician acceptability. Although exploratory, these findings reinforce that clinicians are prepared to accept treatment-associated morbidity in selected patients when the anticipated survival advantage is meaningful. This has direct implications for trial design, suggesting future studies should prioritise endpoints reflecting clinically relevant survival outcomes and incorporate comprehensive QOL assessments that capture real-world clinical trade-offs.

PPI added valuable perspectives. PPI contributors indicated a readiness to consider therapies associated with morbidity in uncertain settings, provided that risks and benefits are communicated transparently. The convergence between clinician equipoise and patient openness supports the ethical foundation for prospective trials and highlights the necessity of integrating patient-centred outcomes in research protocols, early on in the trial design process.

This study has several limitations, including a modest sample size, potential respondent self-selection bias favouring those with a particular interest in locoregional therapies, and the scenario format assessing acceptability thresholds rather than mutually exclusive choices—thus, not constituting a formal discrete choice experiment. Accordingly, the regression analyses should be interpreted as exploratory. Additionally, the scenario format assessed acceptability thresholds purely based on survival and QOL trade-offs, rather than offering mutually exclusive choices across nuanced clinical presentations. Real-world practice involves multidisciplinary review and integration of clinical parameters such as histological subtype (adenocarcinoma versus squamous-cell carcinoma), anatomic distribution of metastases, timing (synchronous versus metachronous), and depth of response to therapies. The scenario model therefore has limited ecological validity and should be interpreted as an exploration of threshold preferences rather than a clinical decision tool. Furthermore, although centres from various countries participated, the results may be most applicable to health care systems comparable to those in the UK and Western Europe. Finally, while these findings are informative, they should be interpreted as such, rather than as normative criteria for treating OMD.

Nonetheless, the results of this study have several implications for contemporary clinical practice. Firstly, the variability observed in clinician perspectives highlights the importance of implementing structured MDT discussions that explicitly consider both anticipated survival benefits and QOL impacts when evaluating locoregional therapy in oligometastatic cases. The nuanced preferences regarding treatment acceptability identified in this survey indicate that clinical decisions are rarely straightforward and frequently involve complex trade-offs that may not always be articulated during routine care. Secondly, the concordance between clinician equipoise and patient receptiveness to uncertainty strengthens the ethical justification for offering locoregional treatment within controlled trial environments rather than through unstandardised or institution-specific approaches. Both AIO-FLOT3 and ESO-Shanghai did suggest a benefit to locoregional therapies for both gastro-oesophageal adenocarcinoma and squamous-cell carcinomas respectively, in the phase II setting. Taking this forward in a phase III European trial with a well-defined patient population appears necessary to address the current evidence gap.

Future investigations should prioritise prospective randomised trials assessing locoregional treatment strategies in carefully selected cohorts of patients with oligometastatic OG cancer. Study designs ought to incorporate the trade-offs identified herein by focusing on clinically meaningful improvements in survival as well as robust, longitudinal assessments of QOL as coprimary or principal secondary endpoints. Formal discrete choice experiments involving both clinicians and patients could further optimise attribute selection and guide endpoint prioritisation. Additionally, qualitative research examining MDT decision-making processes may elucidate contextual influences on therapeutic recommendations. Finally, use of a unified European consensus definition of OMD, as defined by the OMEC-411 project, will ensure clinically relevant populations are enrolled, further strengthening the utility of any trial findings.

In summary, this study reveals substantial variation in clinician attitudes toward locoregional treatment of oligometastatic OG cancer, alongside notable equipoise and willingness for prospective evaluation. Clinician acceptability is chiefly influenced by the trade-off between survival benefit and QOL impact, a dynamic echoed by patient viewpoints. These results underscore the imperative for rigorously designed randomised trials incorporating patient-centred outcomes to inform future clinical practice.
