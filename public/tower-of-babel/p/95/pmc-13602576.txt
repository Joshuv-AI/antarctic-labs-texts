Comparison of the effects of haptonomy application and mindfulness-based stress reduction on psychological well-being, perceived stress, and fear of childbirth in primiparous women: a randomized controlled trial*
Authors: Burcu Küçükkaya, Simge Öztürk, Ebru Ci̇rban Ekrem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThis study aimed to examine and compare the effects of haptonomy and a mindfulness-based stress reduction program (MBSR) on psychological well-being, perceived stress, and fear of childbirth among primiparous pregnant women.Materials and methodsThis prospective, assessor-blinded, three-arm randomized controlled trial was conducted between January 1, 2024, and October 1, 2024. A total of 120 primiparous pregnant women were randomly assigned to the haptonomy group (n = 40), MBSR group (n = 40), or control group (n = 40). At baseline, participants completed the Personal Information Form, Psychological Well-Being Scale (PWBS), Perceived Stress Scale (PSS), and Wijma Delivery Expectancy/Experience Questionnaire Version A (W-DEQ-A). PWBS, PSS, and W-DEQ-A were reassessed at weeks 4 and 8. The interventions were conducted individually through face-to-face sessions, supported by home-based video/audio materials. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, chi-square test, repeated-measures ANOVA, and Bonferroni post hoc analyses.ResultsNo statistically significant differences were observed between groups at baseline (p > .05). However, significant differences emerged at weeks 4 and 8 for all primary outcomes (p < .001). Compared with the control group, participants in both intervention groups demonstrated significantly higher psychological well-being scores and significantly lower perceived stress and fear of childbirth scores. At week 8, the haptonomy group demonstrated greater improvement than the MBSR group across all outcome measures.ConclusionBoth haptonomy and MBSR were effective in improving psychological well-being and reducing perceived stress and fear of childbirth among primiparous pregnant women. Haptonomy demonstrated greater effectiveness than MBSR across all primary outcomes.Trial registrationThis trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06699550; registered on November 19, 2024; retrospectively registered). The study protocol was finalized before participant recruitment; trial registration was completed subsequently due to administrative delays.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12906-026-05452-w.Keywords: Primiparous women, Haptonomy, Mindfulness-based stress reduction program, Psychological well-being, Perceived stress, Fear of childbirth

Introduction

Stress and fear of childbirth during pregnancy are recognized as major psychosocial factors that may contribute to complications in both the prenatal and postnatal periods [1, 2]. Maternal stress, anxiety, and fear related to childbirth may adversely affect fetal/neonatal neurodevelopment, increase labor pain perception and analgesia use, prolong labor, and contribute to higher rates of operative delivery, including instrumental birth and cesarean Sects [3–5]. Furthermore, antenatal stress and fear of childbirth have been associated with obstetric and psychiatric complications including preeclampsia, preterm labor, post-traumatic stress disorder, postpartum anxiety, depression, psychosis, and an increased demand for psychiatric interventions [6–8]. These conditions not only affect maternal health but may also disrupt the quality of the mother–infant relationship by impairing maternal–infant bonding [9]. Indeed, maternal bonding, when negatively influenced by stress, anxiety, depression, or childbirth-related fears during pregnancy, may remain compromised in the postpartum period as well [3]. Previous studies further support that psychological disorders in pregnancy are associated with reduced maternal–infant attachment [10–12]. Pharmacological interventions such as antidepressants or benzodiazepines are sometimes used to treat antenatal mental health disorders. However, due to the risk of placental transfer and potential adverse effects on fetal health, non-pharmacological approaches are increasingly preferred in clinical practice [13]. These include yoga, music therapy, Lamaze, cognitive-behavioral therapies, hypnosis, mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR) programs, and haptonomy [14, 15].

Haptonomy is a relatively novel and underexplored intervention that aims to reduce maternal stress and childbirth fear while enhancing prenatal bonding [1, 16, 17]. Defined as an approach emphasizing emotional contact through touch, haptonomy focuses on strengthening the relationship between parents and the unborn child [18]. At the same time, it facilitates maternal adaptation to pregnancy and psychological preparation for labor [19]. Although limited in number, existing studies indicate that haptonomy may reduce fear of childbirth [1, 20], protect against maternal depression [21], and enhance prenatal attachment [22]. Additional benefits reported include smoother pregnancy and childbirth experiences and strengthened marital relationships [21]. Recent empirical studies have also suggested that haptonomy decreases perceived stress and childbirth fear, alleviates maternal anxiety and depressive symptoms, and improves maternal–infant bonding [23–25]. Despite these promising findings, haptonomy remains a developing area of research with insufficient evidence and no standardized clinical guidelines, either nationally or internationally [26, 27]. This highlights the need for more rigorous research and the establishment of evidence-based practice recommendations.

Another non-pharmacological approach with strong empirical support is mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR). Originally developed by Kabat-Zinn, MBSR is a structured cognitive-behavioral program that cultivates awareness of present-moment physical and psychological experiences, encouraging acceptance without judgment [28]. By enhancing awareness of habitual reactions, improving stress tolerance, and reducing cortisol levels, MBSR fosters self-confidence, self-compassion, attentional control, empathy, positive emotions, and psychological well-being, while simultaneously decreasing depression, anxiety, stress, and fear of childbirth [12, 29, 30].

Taken together, haptonomy and MBSR appear to offer substantial benefits for maternal psychological health by reducing antenatal mental distress, strengthening maternal–infant bonding, and potentially improving pregnancy, childbirth, and postpartum outcomes. Their integration into maternity care may also provide valuable guidance for maternal and child health nurses, particularly in care planning and mental health promotion. Nevertheless, despite supportive evidence regarding their individual effectiveness, comparative research examining the relative efficacy of haptonomy versus MBSR during pregnancy is lacking. Furthermore no previous studies conducted in Türkiye have investigated the impact of these interventions on psychological well-being, perceived stress, and fear of childbirth among primiparous women. Accordingly, the present study aims to examine and compare the effects of haptonomy and MBSR interventions on psychological well-being, perceived stress, and childbirth fear in primiparous pregnant women.

Study hypotheses

H0

There is no significant difference between haptonomy, mindfulness-based stress reduction, and control groups regarding psychological well-being, perceived stress, and fear of childbirth.

H1

Primiparous pregnant women receiving haptonomy intervention will have significantly higher mean scores on the Psychological Well-Being Scale compared to those not receiving the intervention.

H2

Primiparous pregnant women receiving haptonomy intervention will have significantly lower mean scores on the Perceived Stress Scale compared to those not receiving the intervention.

H3

Primiparous pregnant women receiving haptonomy intervention will have significantly lower mean scores on the Wijma Delivery Expectancy/Experience Questionnaire (W-DEQ), Version A compared to those not receiving the intervention.

H4

Primiparous pregnant women receiving MBSR intervention will have significantly higher mean scores on the Psychological Well-Being Scale compared to those not receiving the intervention.

H5

Primiparous pregnant women receiving MBSR intervention will have significantly lower mean scores on the Perceived Stress Scale compared to those not receiving the intervention.

H6

Primiparous pregnant women receiving MBSR intervention will have significantly lower mean scores on the Wijma Delivery Expectancy/Experience Questionnaire (W-DEQ), Version A compared to those not receiving the intervention.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was designed as a three-arm, parallel-group, assessor-blinded randomized controlled trial with a 1:1:1 allocation ratio to investigate the effects of haptonomy and mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR) programs on psychological well-being, perceived stress, and childbirth fear among primiparous pregnant women. Due to the behavioral nature of the interventions, participant blinding was not feasible. All interventions were conducted individually by certified researchers trained in the respective intervention protocols to ensure intervention fidelity and consistency across sessions. A project-supported scholarship undergraduate research assistant assisted with scheduling and outcome assessments but was not involved in intervention delivery. This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the CONSORT 2025 guidelines for randomized controlled trials, and the completed CONSORT 2025 checklist is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Study population and sample

The study population comprised primiparous pregnant women attending routine prenatal check-ups at the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology, Bartın State Hospital, Women’s Health and Pediatric Unit. The intervention was conducted between January 1, 2024 – October 1, 2024, in the hospital’s training room and the practice laboratory of the Faculty of Health Sciences, Bartın University.

Sample size was determined using G*POWER 3.1.9.7 software. Based on the effect size (0.70) reported in the study of Ozbek and Pinar (2022), with a 5% margin of error and 0.81 power, the required sample was calculated as 111 primiparous women. Based on power analysis, a minimum of 37 participants per group (haptonomy, MBSR, control) was required [25]. To account for possible attrition, the final sample included 120 participants (40 in each group).

Randomization and blinding

Eligible participants were randomly assigned to one of three groups: haptonomy intervention, mindfulness-based stress reduction program (MBSR), or control group using a simple randomization method with a 1:1:1 allocation ratio. An independent researcher who was not involved in participant recruitment, intervention delivery, or outcome assessment generated the random allocation sequence using a computer-based random number generator (StatTrek Random Number Generator). To ensure allocation concealment and prevent foreknowledge of group assignment, sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes containing group assignments were prepared by the independent researcher and opened only after participant enrollment was completed. One researcher screened participants for eligibility and obtained informed consent, while another independent researcher performed group allocation. Outcome assessments and data collection were conducted by a project-supported scholarship undergraduate research assistant who remained blinded to participant group assignments throughout the study period. The research assistant was responsible for participant scheduling, follow-up coordination, and data collection procedures but was not involved in intervention delivery. Due to the behavioral nature of the interventions, blinding of participants and intervention providers was not feasible. However, the statistician responsible for data analysis remained blinded to group allocation throughout the analysis process. Data collection instruments

Four instruments were used. Additionally, educational brochures and instructional videos were developed for haptonomy and MBSR interventions. Content validity was ensured through expert review.

Personal information form

Developed by the researchers based on literature [10, 18], consisting of 19 questions on sociodemographic, obstetric, and complementary therapy-related characteristics. Pre-pregnancy BMI values were calculated based on self-reported pre-pregnancy weight and measured height. Weekly physical activity duration was assessed using a self-reported item included in the Personal Information Form.

Psychological Well-Being Scale (PWBS)

Developed by Diener et al. (2009), adapted into Turkish by Telef (2013), with Cronbach’s alpha = 0.87 [31]. It consists of 8 items rated on a 7-point Likert scale; higher scores indicate greater well-being [31]. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the scale was found to be 0.96

Perceived Stress Scale (PSS)

Originally developed by Cohen et al. (1983), validated in Turkish by Eskin et al. (2013) [32, 33]. It includes 14 items on a 5-point Likert scale (0–4), measuring stress and inadequacy perception; Cronbach’s alpha = 0.84 [33]. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the scale was found to be 0.92.

Wijma Delivery Expectancy/Experience Questionnaire (W-DEQ-A)

This scale was developed by Wijma et al. (1998) and validated in Turkish by Korukcu et al. (2009) [34, 35]. It contains 33 items scored on a 6-point Likert scale (0–5), with total scores ranging 0–165. Higher scores indicate greater childbirth fear, with cut-offs for mild, moderate, severe, and clinical levels. Cronbach’s alpha = 0.88 for primiparas [35]. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the scale was found to be 0.93.

Intervention procedures

Educational brochure

This brochure contains the definition, benefits, and stages of haptonomy, which aims to guide pregnant women in the application of haptonomy before research [36–38]. MBSR Educational Brochure, prepared by the researcher, contains information about the definition, benefits, and effects of the MBSR program during pregnancy [39–42]. Expert opinion was sought regarding the suitability of the brochures prepared by the researchers.

Educational video

The researcher filmed a haptonomy training video to guide pregnant women during haptonomy practices with their partners at home. The video content included the definition of haptonomy, its benefits, the characteristics of the environment in which haptonomy would be practiced, the stages of haptonomy, and information to be considered during practice, and was accompanied by music selected with expert opinion. The video was filmed in accordance with the literature [21, 38, 43] and then expert opinion was obtained [38]. MBSR can be applied formally and informally. During the implementation of the formal MBSR program, video and audio recordings of MBSR practices, including body scan, breath awareness, and sounds and thoughts meditation, were sent to pregnant women to accompany them.

Haptonomy intervention

The haptonomy program was delivered by certified researchers who completed formal haptonomy training. The intervention was conducted over 8 weeks, and the haptonomy intervention implementation steps are shown in Fig. 2. Participants attended one weekly face-to-face individual session lasting approximately 45–60 min for 8 weeks. Partners were encouraged to participate in home practice sessions using instructional videos. Sessions were scheduled across weekdays and weekends to manage participant flow. To facilitate intervention delivery, appointments were arranged individually according to participant availability throughout weekdays and weekends. Home-based instructional videos and educational materials were also provided to reinforce practice between face-to-face sessions. A project-supported scholarship undergraduate research assistant assisted with participant scheduling and follow-up assessments; however, all haptonomy sessions were delivered exclusively by the certified researchers.

Weekly haptonomy intervention protocol delivered over 8 weeks

MBSR program

The MBSR program was delivered by a certified researcher trained at the Mindfulness Institute. The intervention was conducted over 8 weeks, and the MBSR program intervention implementation steps are shown in Fig. 3. Participants attended one weekly individual face-to-face session lasting approximately 45–60 min for 8 weeks and completed daily home mindfulness exercises supported by audio recordings. Sessions were scheduled across weekdays and weekends to manage participant flow. Participants additionally received audio recordings and guided mindfulness materials to support daily home practice between weekly sessions. A project-supported scholarship undergraduate research assistant assisted with participant scheduling and follow-up assessments; however, all MBSR sessions were delivered exclusively by the certified mindfulness-trained researcher.

Weekly MBSR program intervention protocol delivered over 8 weeks

Control group

Control participants received routine antenatal care only. They completed the same data collection at baseline, week 4, and week 8, but no intervention was provided during the trial. At the study’s conclusion, following completion of the study, participants in the control group were offered access to the intervention materials. The control group was offered access to the programs. Routine antenatal care included standard physician examinations, fetal monitoring, routine antenatal counseling, nutritional recommendations, and standard childbirth education provided by the hospital. All participants had access to the hospital’s routine antenatal education program, which consisted of four standard group sessions covering nutrition, physical changes during pregnancy, preparation for labor, breastfeeding, and newborn care. These routine educational sessions were independent of the study interventions. In contrast, participants assigned to the haptonomy and MBSR groups additionally received eight weekly individual intervention sessions as part of the randomized controlled trial.

Data collection

At baseline, all groups of pregnant women received preliminary information; for each of the three groups, personal characteristics data were recorded using a questionnaire. The mothers were informed that data would be collected using the Psychological Well-Being Scale (PWBS), Perceived Stress Scale (PSS), and Wijma Delivery Expectancy/Experience Questionnaire (W-DEQ) A version at baseline and during the 4th and 8th week follow-ups. The haptonomy group practiced haptonomy for 8 weeks, while the MBSR group practiced mindfulness for 8 weeks (Fig. 4). The baseline, 4th, and 8th week follow-ups were conducted in the pregnant class. The primary outcomes of this randomized controlled trial were psychological well-being, perceived stress, and fear of childbirth, measured using the Psychological Well-Being Scale (PWBS), Perceived Stress Scale (PSS), and Wijma Delivery Expectancy/Experience Questionnaire Version A (W-DEQ-A), respectively. These outcomes were assessed at baseline, week 4, and week 8. Secondary exploratory analyses examined the correlations among these psychological outcomes following the interventions. Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics and G*Power software. Descriptive statistics were presented as mean, standard deviation, frequency, and percentage. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Baseline categorical variables were compared using the Pearson chi-square test, while baseline continuous variables were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Secondary exploratory Pearson correlation analyses were performed to examine associations among psychological well-being, perceived stress, and fear of childbirth scores at weeks 4 and 8. To evaluate changes in psychological well-being, perceived stress, and fear of childbirth across baseline, week 4, and week 8 assessments, repeated-measures ANOVA was used to examine within-group changes over time and between-group differences. Bonferroni post hoc tests were performed for pairwise comparisons when significant differences were identified. A significance level of p < .05 was considered statistically significant with 95% confidence intervals. No missing outcome data were observed; therefore, all randomized participants were included in the final analyses. Only participants who completed all follow-up assessments were included in the final analysis.

Ethical consideration

Prior to data collection, ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Social and Human Sciences at Bartın University (Date: July 17, 2023; Decision No: 2023-SBB-0435). Institutional permissions were granted by the Dean’s Office of the Faculty of Health Sciences at Bartın University and the Bartın Provincial Health Directorate to access antenatal classes and conduct data collection. All primiparous women included in the study were informed about the purpose of the research, and it was emphasized that participation was entirely voluntary. Written informed consent forms were obtained from all participants. For the instruments used in the study, written permissions were secured from the original authors who had established their validity and reliability. The study was conducted in full compliance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was supported by the Scientific and Technological Research Council of Türkiye (TÜBİTAK-ARDEB) under the Rapid Support Program (1002-A), Project No: 223S428. The trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06699550; registered on November 19, 2024; retrospectively registered).

No intervention-related adverse events, physical complications, or psychological harms were reported during the haptonomy or MBSR intervention periods. All participants completed the interventions safely under researcher supervision.

Discussion

This randomized controlled trial aimed to compare the effects of haptonomy and mindfulness-based stress reduction on psychological well-being, perceived stress, and fear of childbirth among primiparous pregnant women.

Mindfulness practice encourages pregnant women to detach from past experiences and future anxieties, focusing instead on the present moment and cultivating acceptance of ongoing experiences in both mind and body [44]. This practice has been shown to decrease stress, anxiety, and depression while increasing self-efficacy, thereby reducing childbirth fear, pain, and opioid use during labor [44, 45]. In the present study, mindfulness practice significantly reduced perceived stress and childbirth fear while improving psychological well-being at both week 4 and week 8 assessments compared with baseline. These results are consistent with previous studies. For instance, Pan et al. (2019) found that mindfulness reduced depression and perceived stress during pregnancy and the postpartum period while enhancing mindfulness levels [46]. Hulsbosch et al. (2023) reported that an 8-week mindfulness intervention significantly improved mindfulness and reduced stress and depression in pregnant women [47]. Similarly, Hassdenteufel et al. (2023) observed that an 8-week mindfulness program reduced pregnancy-related anxiety, with effects persisting up to six weeks postpartum [48]. Additional evidence from Wang et al. (2023) indicated that mindfulness reduced anxiety, stress, and childbirth fear, while increasing life satisfaction and awareness [49]. Dinç et al. (2024), in a systematic review and meta-analysis, confirmed the effectiveness of mindfulness in reducing childbirth fear [50]. Moreover, Hulsbosch et al. (2021) demonstrated that mindfulness interventions reduced negative perceptions of childbirth [51]. Taken together, these findings support the present results, suggesting that mindfulness reduces perceived stress and enhances psychological well-being by increasing self-awareness, thereby alleviating childbirth fear.

Secondary correlation analyses further demonstrated that psychological well-being and reduced perceived stress and fear of Childbirth and improved psychological well-being was associated with lower perceived stress and reduced fear of childbirth across intervention groups. Similarly, Dorosti et al. (2023) reported that five haptonomy sessions significantly reduced childbirth fear while enhancing maternal–infant bonding [24]. Alivand et al. (2024) also showed that haptonomy and cognitive behavioral therapy significantly reduced childbirth fear and postpartum depression in a randomized controlled trial [52]. Likewise, Krusche et al. (2018) and Sun et al. (2018) found that mindfulness significantly reduced anxiety, stress, depression, and childbirth fear among pregnant women, with Sun et al. (2018) additionally noting stronger effects on depression among primiparous women compared with multiparous women [30, 53]. The findings of the present study are therefore consistent with the broader literature [24, 30, 52, 53].

In the current study, both interventions significantly improved psychological well-being and reduced perceived stress and fear of childbirth. This finding is consistent with previous evidence suggesting that maternal psychological well-being functions as an important protective factor during pregnancy. For example, Wang and colleagues reported that fear of childbirth is strongly associated with psychological distress and emphasized that mindfulness-based interventions may reduce childbirth-related fear by improving emotional regulation and coping mechanisms [54]. Similarly, a recent randomized controlled trial by Kaya et al. demonstrated that structured antenatal education significantly reduced fear of childbirth and perceived stress while improving mental well-being among primiparous pregnant women [55]. In addition, Gerymski et al. found that perceived stress was positively associated with fear of childbirth and negatively associated with life satisfaction among pregnant women, highlighting the interrelated nature of maternal psychological outcomes [56]. A recent systematic review also concluded that mindfulness-based interventions significantly reduce stress, anxiety, and fear of childbirth while improving psychological well-being during the perinatal period [57]. Collectively, these findings support the interpretation that improvements in psychological well-being may contribute to lower stress perceptions and reduced childbirth fear by enhancing emotional resilience, coping capacity, and maternal self-efficacy during pregnancy.

Building on the findings of Ozbek and Pinar (2022) [25], the present study expands the existing evidence in several important ways. Although their randomized controlled trial demonstrated that haptonomy effectively reduced perceived stress and fear of childbirth while improving prenatal attachment, our study employed a three-arm randomized controlled design that enabled direct comparison of haptonomy, mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR), and routine antenatal care. In addition, psychological well-being was evaluated as an additional primary outcome, providing a more comprehensive assessment of maternal mental health during pregnancy. Repeated assessments at baseline, week 4, and week 8 allowed the evaluation of both short-term and sustained intervention effects. Furthermore, by including only primiparous women, the potential influence of previous childbirth experiences was minimized, allowing a more homogeneous evaluation of intervention efficacy. Collectively, these methodological and conceptual advances provide novel evidence regarding the comparative effectiveness of non-pharmacological interventions designed to support maternal psychological health.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study include its three-arm randomized controlled trial design, which enhanced methodological rigor and enabled direct comparison between two non-pharmacological interventions and a control group. Another important strength is the inclusion of only primiparous pregnant women, which helped reduce potential confounding factors related to previous childbirth experiences. Additionally, repeated assessments conducted at baseline, week 4, and week 8 allowed for the evaluation of both short-term and sustained intervention effects. The use of validated measurement tools to assess psychological well-being, perceived stress, and fear of childbirth also strengthened the reliability of the findings. Furthermore, to our knowledge, no previous randomized controlled trial has directly compared the effects of haptonomy and mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR) among primiparous pregnant women. Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged. The study was conducted at a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader populations. In addition, participants were recruited from women attending prenatal education services, which may have introduced selection bias by excluding women who did not access such services. Finally, long-term postpartum outcomes were not evaluated; therefore, future multicenter studies with extended follow-up periods are recommended.

Conclusion and recommendations

The findings of this study demonstrated that both haptonomy and MBSR significantly reduced perceived stress and childbirth fear while improving psychological well-being among primiparous pregnant women. Notably, haptonomy was found to be more effective than mindfulness and the control condition in enhancing psychological well-being and reducing stress and childbirth fear. Based on these results, it is recommended that both haptonomy and mindfulness practices be incorporated into prenatal education programs to support maternal psychological health. Accordingly, nurses working in prenatal education schools should be trained in these approaches and provide relevant guidance to pregnant women.
