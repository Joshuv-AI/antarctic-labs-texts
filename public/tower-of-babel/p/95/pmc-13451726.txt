Efficacy and Safety of Sintilimab in Combination With Anlotinib and Chemoradiotherapy as First-Line Treatment for Driver Gene-Negative Oligometastatic Non-small Cell Lung Cancer: A Real-World Cohort Study
Authors: Jian Jiang, Chen Ni, Chunfeng Wu, Yu Song, Huiping Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of sintilimab combined with anlotinib and chemoradiotherapy as first-line treatment for driver gene-negative oligometastatic non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC).MethodsThis retrospective study included patients with driver gene-negative oligometastatic NSCLC (single organ, ≤2 lesions) treated at the Affiliated Zhangjiagang Hospital of Soochow University (7/2021–12/2023). All patients received 4 cycles of sintilimab plus albumin-bound paclitaxel/carboplatin and achieved partial response or stable disease. Subsequently, patients received concurrent radical radiotherapy with or without anlotinib, or continued the original regimen. Progression-free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS), objective response rate (ORR), disease control rate (DCR), and adverse events were analyzed.ResultsA total of 88 patients were included: 27 in the sintilimab plus chemotherapy (SC) group, 32 in the sintilimab plus chemoradiotherapy (SCR) group, and 29 in the anlotinib plus sintilimab and chemoradiotherapy (ASCR) group. The radiotherapy group (SCR + ASCR) showed significantly longer median PFS and OS than the non-radiotherapy group (SC) (7.7 vs. 16.5 months, P < 0.001; 20.1 vs. 31.8 months, P = 0.004). The ASCR group had significantly higher 12-month PFS, 18-month OS, and 24-month OS rates than the SCR group (P = 0.005, 0.014, and 0.046, respectively). ORR and DCR were significantly lower in the non-radiotherapy group (P < 0.001 and P = 0.007). Treatment-related adverse events were manageable.ConclusionIn driver gene-negative oligometastatic NSCLC patients benefiting from first-line sintilimab plus chemotherapy, adding concurrent radical radiotherapy significantly improved survival with acceptable toxicity. The addition of anlotinib further enhanced long-term outcomes. These findings are preliminary and require prospective validation.

Introduction

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide, with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounting for approximately 85% of cases.1,2 Due to insidious clinical onset, more than half of patients already have distant metastases at diagnosis, resulting in a 5-year survival rate of only 17.7%.2,3 In recent years, advances in imaging have increased focus on the “oligometastatic” state—an intermediate phase between localized primary tumors and widely metastatic disease. This concept, first proposed by Hellman and Weichselbaum in 1995, is defined by the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) as no more than five metastatic lesions involving up to three organs.4,5

For driver gene-negative oligometastatic NSCLC, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) combined with platinum-based doublet chemotherapy represent the standard first-line treatment. However, even among initial responders, complete response rates remain limited, and most patients eventually progress. Optimizing strategies to further prolong progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) is therefore a key clinical research focus. ASTRO/ESTRO guidelines recommend radiotherapy and surgery as primary local treatments for oligometastatic disease. 6 Previous studies suggest that consolidative radiotherapy for residual lesions after effective systemic therapy can improve local control and survival, yet optimal timing, magnitude of benefit, and safety require further validation.7,8

Anlotinib is an oral multi-target tyrosine kinase inhibitor that promotes tumor vascular normalization, improves immune cell infiltration, and exhibits synergistic effects with PD-1 inhibitors. 9 Subgroup analyses from the ALTER0303 trial indicated clinical benefit of anlotinib monotherapy in advanced NSCLC patients with oligometastases (e.g., liver, brain).10-12 However, the efficacy and safety of combining anlotinib with immunotherapy and local radiotherapy—forming a triple-intensification model of “immunotherapy and anti-angiogenesis and radiotherapy”—in driver gene-negative oligometastatic NSCLC remain unexplored.

This study was designed to provide a preliminary assessment of the efficacy and safety of first-line therapy with sintilimab plus anlotinib in combination with concurrent chemoradiotherapy for patients with driver gene-negative oligometastatic NSCLC.

Methods

Patients

Patients with pathologically confirmed advanced NSCLC were enrolled at the Affiliated Zhangjiagang Hospital of Soochow University (Jiangsu, China) between July 2021 and December 2023. The study was approved by the Hospital’s Medical Ethics Review Committee (202107-KS010) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and STROBE statement. This was a retrospective cohort study. Patients were classified into three groups based on the treatment they actually received after the 4-cycle induction period: (1) continuation of sintilimab plus chemotherapy (SC), (2) addition of concurrent chemoradiotherapy (SCR), or (3) addition of anlotinib with concurrent chemoradiotherapy (ASCR). Treatment assignment was determined by physician-patient shared decision after comprehensive discussion of radiotherapy feasibility, tolerance to intensified therapy, and individual patient preference. An independent committee assessed efficacy based on modified RECIST (mRECIST) and safety using NCI-CTCAE version 5.0. The primary endpoint was PFS; secondary endpoints were ORR, DCR, and OS. Inclusion Criteria

1) age 18–75 years; 2) histologically confirmed NSCLC; 3) clinical stage IV (AJCC 8th edition, 2018); 4) driver genes (EGFR/ALK/ROS1) negative (not mandatory for squamous cell carcinoma); 5) no prior thoracic treatment; 6) ECOG PS 0–1; 7) single distant organ metastasis with ≤2 lesions; 8) at least one measurable metastasis (largest diameter ≤3 cm); 9) prior receipt of 4 cycles of sintilimab plus nab-paclitaxel/carboplatin with confirmed partial response (PR) or stable disease (SD).

Exclusion Criteria

1) any prior anti-tumor therapy; 2) impaired liver/kidney/bone marrow function or comorbidities that could worsen organ dysfunction during treatment; 3) severe cardiopulmonary disease; 4) history of pulmonary fibrosis, pulmonary hypertension, or severe obstructive airway disease; 5) symptomatic brain or vertebral metastases; 6) malignant pleural, peritoneal, or pericardial effusion.

Radiotherapy Regimen

Radiotherapy was delivered using image-guided radiation therapy (IGRT) with a 6-MV X-ray on a Halcyon 3.0 linear accelerator (Varian Medical Systems, Siemens Healthineers), with daily cone-beam CT (CBCT) for setup accuracy. Gross tumor volume (GTV) included the primary tumor (GTVp), regional metastatic lymph nodes (GTVnd), and distant metastatic lesions (GTVm). Clinical target volume (CTV) for the primary tumor (CTVp) was defined as GTVp plus a 0.6–0.8 cm margin. CTV for nodes (CTVnd) and metastases (CTVm) were GTVnd/GTVm plus a 0.5 cm margin. Planning target volume (PTV) was CTV plus a 0.5 cm margin. The primary tumor and metastatic nodes received up to 60 Gy in 2Gy fractions. OAR constraints (RTOG-based): For lung, V20Gy ≤10% (SBRT) or ≤30% (IMRT), and mean dose <8 Gy (SBRT) or <20 Gy (IMRT). Spinal cord maximum dose <30 Gy (SBRT) or ≤45 Gy (IMRT). Esophagus maximum dose <52 Gy (BED10) for SBRT and ≤60 Gy for IMRT. For heart, maximum point dose ≤105% of the prescription dose for SBRT; for IMRT, V40Gy <30% and mean dose <20 Gy.

The choice between SBRT and IMRT was based on the following principles: SBRT was applied to distant oligometastatic lesions (GTVm) with a maximum diameter ≤5 cm and adequate distance from critical OARs (spinal cord, esophagus, main bronchus, etc.). Peripheral lesions were preferred for SBRT, while central lesions were treated cautiously per RTOG 0813. IMRT was applied to primary tumors (GTVp) and regional metastatic lymph nodes (GTVnd), particularly when lesions were adjacent to critical mediastinal structures such as the great vessels, esophagus, or trachea. For intrapulmonary metastases adjacent to the chest wall, ribs, or mediastinum, SBRT was prioritized with 4D-CT respiratory management to minimize normal tissue exposure.

BED was calculated using the formula: BED = nd × [1 + d/(α/β)], where nd is the total dose, d is the dose per fraction, and α/β is 10 Gy for tumor tissue (BED10) and 3 Gy for late-responding normal tissues (BED3).

Systemic Treatment Regimens

SC Group: Nab-paclitaxel (Jiangsu Hengrui Medicine) 100 mg/m2 weekly; carboplatin (Shandong Qilu Pharmaceutical) AUC 5 on day 1 of each 3-week cycle (2 cycles total); sintilimab (Suzhou InnoCare BioPharma) 200 mg every 3 weeks until progression or intolerable toxicity (maximum 2 years).

SCR Group: Nab-paclitaxel 100 mg/m2 weekly starting the first weekend after radiotherapy initiation (6 doses total); sintilimab 200 mg every 3 weeks until progression/intolerable toxicity (maximum 2 years). Radiotherapy was delivered concurrently with chemotherapy and immunotherapy as described above.

ASCR Group: Nab-paclitaxel and sintilimab as in the SCR group; anlotinib (Chia Tai Tianqing Pharmaceutical) 8–10 mg daily starting on the first day of radiotherapy (2 weeks on/1 week off per 3-week cycle) until progression/intolerable toxicity. Radiotherapy, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and anlotinib were delivered concurrently in this group.

Data Collection and Definitions

Efficacy was assessed via CT, MRI, or PET-CT as indicated. Tumor response was evaluated per RECIST 1.1 and iRECIST.13,14 PFS was defined as time from treatment initiation to first progression or death from any cause. OS was time from initiation to death from any cause. Secondary endpoints included 6-, 12-, 18-month PFS rates; 18-, 24-, 36-month OS rates; ORR (confirmed CR+PR); DCR (CR+PR+SD); pattern of first failure; and treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs). Toxicity was graded per NCI CTCAE v5.0 15 and RTOG/EORTC criteria. 16 Data were retrospectively extracted from the hospital’s electronic medical record system.

Statistical Analysis

Patients included in efficacy/safety analyses had to receive at least one cycle of systemic therapy; those in radiotherapy groups also had to complete planned radiotherapy. Baseline characteristics, response rates, survival, and TRAEs were compared using Pearson’s chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. OS and PFS were estimated by Kaplan-Meier method; hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs were calculated using Cox proportional hazards models. All covariates entered into the Cox models were baseline characteristics. The comparison between PR and SD subgroups was based on response status determined at the completion of the 4-cycle induction period, which served as a baseline stratification criterion for study entry. As all patients were classified prior to the start of the subsequent treatment phase, this analysis does not involve time-dependent covariate bias. Analyses were performed with SPSS version 31 (IBM Corporation, Chicago, IL, USA).

Median OS was 20.1 months in SC, 27.2 months in SCR, and 34.1 months in ASCR (P=0.006) (Figure 2B). 18-month OS rates were 66.7%, 71.9%, and 96.6%; 24-month rates were 25.9%, 59.4%, and 82.8%; 36-month rates were 0%, 31.3%, and 34.5%, respectively. No significant difference was seen between SC and SCR (P=0.172), but SC differed from ASCR (P<0.001). Radiotherapy improved mOS (20.1 vs. 31.8 months, P=0.004) (Figure 3C). A trend favored anlotinib for OS (22.6 vs. 34.1 months, P=0.057) (Figure 3D). ASCR had higher 18-month (P=0.014) and 24-month OS (P=0.046) than SCR, but no significant overall mOS benefit (P=0.43).

Treatment Response

Tumor responses are shown in Table 3. At first assessment, 9 patients (10.2%) achieved CR, 42 (47.8%) PR, and 25 (28.4%) SD. Significant differences among groups were observed for PR, SD, and PD (P<0.001, <0.001, and 0.021, respectively). Overall ORR was 58% (P<0.001 across groups). ORR differed between non-radiotherapy (SC) and radiotherapy (SCR+ASCR) groups (P<0.001); adding anlotinib did not significantly improve ORR (SCR vs. ASCR, P=0.313). Overall DCR was 86.4% (P=0.021 across groups), with a significant difference between non-radiotherapy and radiotherapy groups (P=0.007). Anlotinib provided no additional DCR benefit (P=0.614).

Subgroup Analysis of PFS and OS

Compared with the non-radiotherapy group (SC), the radiotherapy group (SCR+ASCR) showed improved mPFS (7.7 vs. 16.5 months, P<0.001) (Figure 3A) and mOS (20.1 vs. 31.8 months, P=0.004) (Figure 3C). Anlotinib-treated patients (ASCR) had longer mPFS than those not receiving anlotinib (SC+SCR) (8.3 vs. 17.9 months, P=0.016) (Figure 3B) and a trend toward better mOS (22.6 vs. 34.1 months, P=0.057) (Figure 3D). Among concurrent chemoradiotherapy patients, ASCR had higher 12-month PFS than SCR (P=0.005), but no mPFS difference (P=0.994). ASCR also had higher 18- and 24-month OS rates (P=0.014, P=0.046), though overall mOS did not differ (P=0.43). Discussion

Currently, no standard multimodal regimen integrating systemic and local therapy exists for driver gene-negative oligometastatic NSCLC. This retrospective study evaluated the efficacy and safety of synchronous radiotherapy added after confirmed benefit from prior immunochemotherapy. The results demonstrate that sintilimab plus concurrent chemoradiotherapy (SCR/ASCR) may be a valuable option for carefully selected patients with good ECOG status, oligometastases confined to a single organ, and limited lesion number/volume. In this selected population, sintilimab plus concurrent chemoradiotherapy (SCR/ASCR) was associated with significantly better OS, PFS, and ORR compared with non-radiotherapy (SC). Adding anlotinib further improved long-term survival rates without compromising safety. These findings suggest that this multimodal approach may be a valuable option for carefully selected patients.

Radiotherapy induces tumor cell death via apoptosis, necrosis, and autophagy, promoting release of tumor-associated antigens, 17 enhancing antigen presentation,18,19 and increasing tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes.20-22 These mechanisms activate antitumor immunity. Clinical evidence 23 indicates radiotherapy can convert the NSCLC tumor microenvironment to an inflammatory phenotype, sensitizing tumors to immune checkpoint inhibitors.

Growing evidence confirms that adding local radiotherapy to systemic therapy after initial benefit improves survival.7,8,24-26 A key finding here was the crucial synergistic role of local radiotherapy with immunochemotherapy. At median follow-up of 26.05 months, radiotherapy patients (SCR+ASCR) achieved median PFS of 16.5 months and median OS of 31.8 months, significantly longer than the non-radiotherapy group (PFS 7.7 months, OS 20.1 months; both P<0.01). SBRT and IMRT showed comparable efficacy for oligometastases (P>0.05 for PFS/OS), suggesting modality choice can be individualized without compromising outcomes.

However, several questions remain regarding optimal integration of radiotherapy with immunochemotherapy: timing, modality, target volume/dose, need to suspend systemic therapy, limits on lesion location/number, and optimal systemic regimen. These factors can influence outcomes, and high-quality large RCTs are lacking to establish standards. The NRG-LU002 phase II/III trial 27 enrolled 682 driver gene-negative oligometastatic NSCLC patients (≤3 lesions) with SD or better after 4 cycles of first-line therapy. Adding local consolidative therapy to maintenance systemic therapy did not improve PFS (HR 0.93, P=0.66) or OS (HR 1.05, P=0.81) and increased toxicity. These results contrast with our study and some prior reports, and do not support routine local therapy in this population. Survival benefit in our study may stem from strict inclusion criteria (single-organ oligometastases, ≤2 lesions), continued immunotherapy during chemoradiotherapy, use of nab-paclitaxel/carboplatin, and radical-like involved-field radiotherapy to primary and nodal sites concurrent with oligometastasis irradiation.

Combining immunotherapy and radiotherapy carries risks of synergistic toxicity, including systemic reactions and thoracic events, notably increased pneumonitis risk.28-31 The most common TRAEs here were leukopenia (93.2%), hypothyroidism (85.2%), nausea/vomiting (79.5%), and fatigue (54.5%), mostly grade 1–2, consistent with prior safety profiles of sintilimab, anlotinib, and concurrent chemoradiotherapy.32-35 Grade ≥3 leukopenia was more frequent with radiotherapy (54.1% vs. 29.6%, P=0.034), and severe pneumonitis showed an increasing trend (21.3% vs. 3.7%, P=0.055). Close monitoring of blood counts and pulmonary symptoms during radiotherapy is essential, with timely supportive care (e.g., G-CSF, anti-infectives) to maintain treatment tolerance. No patient discontinued due to AEs, indicating acceptable safety with appropriate management (e.g., weekly low-dose chemotherapy to reduce toxicity, low-dose glucocorticoids to mitigate inflammation), with temporary interruptions or dose reductions as needed.

Notably, adding anlotinib to SCR (ASCR group) showed promising long-term survival improvements: 12-month PFS (96.6% vs. 68.8%), 18-month OS (96.6% vs. 71.9%), and 24-month OS (82.8% vs. 59.4%) were significantly higher than SCR (all P<0.05), though median PFS/OS differences were not statistically significant. This aligns with synergistic mechanisms of anti-angiogenics with immunochemoradiotherapy: anlotinib inhibits VEGF signaling, normalizes tumor vasculature, improves perfusion/oxygenation, enhances immune infiltration, and increases radiosensitivity.36,37 The OS trend favoring anlotinib (34.1 vs. 22.6 months, P=0.057) supports the potential of this triple-therapy regimen, especially for patients needing durable disease control; longer follow-up may reveal significant OS differences. The survival rates observed in the ASCR group compare favorably with those reported in most phase III trials. However, this comparison should be interpreted in the context of the limited sample size and the selective nature of our cohort.

Regarding treatment response, ORR and DCR were significantly higher in radiotherapy groups (SCR+ASCR) than in SC (ORR: 77.0% vs. 14.8%; DCR: 93.4% vs. 70.4%; both P<0.01), confirming radiotherapy enhances immunochemotherapy efficacy. Anlotinib did not significantly improve ORR or DCR (P=0.313, P=0.614), suggesting its survival benefit may arise from delaying progression rather than improving initial response, consistent with the disease-stabilizing effect of anti-angiogenic agents.10,38

Prognostic factor analysis showed that better ECOG status, lower N stage, greater response to prior therapy, and objective response to current treatment independently predicted improved OS. These factors can help clinicians stratify patients and select regimens—e.g., patients with ECOG 0 or cN0-2 may benefit more from intensified regimens like ASCR, whereas those with poor performance status may require dose adjustments or less intensive therapy to balance efficacy and safety.

Importantly, the findings presented here should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than practice-changing. The single-center design, small sample size, and potential for selection bias preclude definitive conclusions regarding the optimal treatment strategy for this patient population. Our results are intended to inform the design of future prospective studies rather than to establish a new standard of care.

Limitations

We acknowledge several limitations. First, this was a single-center, retrospective study with a small sample size (n=88), which may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability. The inclusion of patients who achieved PR/SD after 4 induction cycles, while ensuring cohort homogeneity, may have enriched treatment-responsive patients and introduced immortal time bias. Numerical imbalances in baseline ECOG PS across groups, though not statistically significant, may have contributed some confounding. Second, the median follow-up of 26.05 months may be insufficient for long-term survival assessment, particularly for the ASCR group. The limited sample size also constrained multivariable modeling, and subgroup analyses should be considered exploratory. Third, PD-L1 and TMB data were unavailable for most patients, and smoking history lacked quantitative documentation, preventing adjustment for these important factors. Fourth, adverse events were retrospectively extracted, which may have introduced reporting or grading bias. Fifth, radiotherapy was delivered at a single institution, and reproducibility across centers requires confirmation. Finally, our strict oligometastatic criteria(single organ, ≤2 lesions) limit applicability to patients with more extensive disease, and lesion number was not further stratified. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and require validation in larger, multicenter, prospective cohorts.

Conclusion

In patients with strictly defined driver gene-negative oligometastatic NSCLC (single organ, ≤2 lesions) who responded to initial induction therapy, the addition of concurrent radical radiotherapy to sintilimab was associated with a significant survival benefit compared with chemotherapy alone. Adding anlotinib (ASCR regimen) may further improve long-term survival rates in patients seeking durable disease control. Our findings also suggest a potential synergistic role of radiotherapy when combined with immunochemotherapy. Individualized selection of radiotherapy modalities and close monitoring of TRAEs may help to optimize outcomes. Future large-scale randomized trials are needed to validate these findings and explore predictive biomarkers for precision medicine.
