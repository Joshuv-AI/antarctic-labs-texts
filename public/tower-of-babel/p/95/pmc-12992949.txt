Anatomy of Hamate Articular Surface
Authors: Annelise Chaparro, Christian Shigley, Jerry Huang, Chelsea Boe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo better understand variability in the articular anatomy of the distal hamate, particularly the central ridge angles (CRAs) and the radii of curvature.MethodsThe surgically relevant features of the distal hamate include the CRA, the width of the articular surface, and the radius of curvature of each of its facets. In total, 118 CT wrists obtained between 2016 and 2020 were analyzed (63 men and 55 women with mean age of 64.8). Visage software was used to determine the width of the distal hamate articular surface in the coronal plane and the CRA as well as the radius of curvature of the fourth and fifth metacarpal facets in the coronal and sagittal planes. We also analyzed the fourth and fifth metacarpal facets to determine the incidence of a convex instead of a concave articular surface.ResultsAnalysis revealed variability in all measurements. The mean coronal articular width was 14.8 mm, whereas the mean CRA was 155°. With regards to the CRA, 10.2% had an angle > 1 standard deviation below the mean, and 14.4% had an angle > 1 standard deviation above the mean. The mean fourth metacarpal facet width was 6.6 mm compared to 8.9 mm for the fifth metacarpal facet. On the coronal plane, the majority of the fourth metacarpal facet was concave. However, for the fifth metacarpal facet, more than 50% were convex.ConclusionsThere is considerable variability in the general population with regards to the hamate distal articular surface.Type of study/level of evidenceDiagnostic IV.Key words: Anatomy, Finger fractures, Hamate, Hemihamate

Proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joint fracture dislocations are difficult injuries to manage and can result in stiffness and pain, leading to impaired function.1 PIP function is dependent on adequate range of motion (ROM) with flexion and extension as well as radioulnar stability afforded by its ligamentous attachments.1 The PIP joint has a long moment arm and has the greatest ROM when compared to the distal interphalangeal joint (DIP) and the metacarpophalangeal joint (MCP).2,3 This, along with limited tolerance for stress in the rotational, axial, and angular planes, contributes to the frequency of PIP injuries.4 It is estimated that approximately 85% of motion required for functional grip is accounted for by the PIP joint, which demonstrates the importance of preserving this joint following traumatic fracture dislocations.3,5 Treatment options for PIP fracture dislocations ideally allow for early ROM to prevent stiffness.6 Failure to treat these injuries appropriately can result in persistent stiffness with loss of range of motion, joint subluxation, and posttraumatic arthritis.7,8

There are a wide range of treatment options for PIP fracture dislocations. In cases with articular surface involvement, surgical fixation options include open reduction and internal fixation, closed reduction pinning, and external fixation.9 For fractures with considerable articular comminution with an unreconstructable volar lip, hemihamate arthroplasty (HHA) may be indicated.

Hemihamate arthroplasty was first published in 1999 by Hastings et al,10 in which the authors described the use of autologous osteochondral autograft harvested from the ipsilateral hamate to treat acute and chronic PIP joint fracture dislocations. The hamate central ridge and articular contours with the fourth and fifth metacarpal bases were determined to be the key anatomic features of the hamate that resembled the volar articular surface at the base of the middle phalanx frequently damaged in PIP fracture dislocations.11 The technique involves determining the dimensions of the graft that will need to be harvested by measuring the size of the bony defect. The surgeon must also note where the central articular ridge lies relative to the ulnar and radial dimensions to ensure the correct location of the central ridge during hamate graft harvest.12

HHA is a technically demanding surgical technique that has been associated with variable outcomes with some studies demonstrating high rates of posttraumatic arthritis at long term follow up.13,14 Although posttraumatic arthritis likely is due to many factors including the inciting trauma, the surgical intervention and graft complications such as necrosis, it is possible that anatomical mismatch between the graft and the native PIP joint could be a contributing factor.15

Drain et al11 performed an analysis of the middle phalanx base compared to the distal hamate articular surface based on cadaver specimens and found that there was considerable variability in the hamate ridge angle, the articular surface area, and the shape of the facets. They found that 39 of 40 specimens had a concave fourth facets, whereas the fifth facet was convex in 31 of 40 specimens.11 In another study, Calva et al15 evaluated computed tomography (CT) scans in 31 patients and measured the consistency of the angulation of the hamate articular surface compared to that of the middle phalanx in digits 2–5 but did not describe in detail the specific hamate anatomic features of the distal hamate articular surface relevant to HHA.

The purpose of this study is to examine CT scans including the distal ridge of the hamate in order to evaluate and quantify surgically relevant anatomic features. The goal is to better understand the variability of the anatomy of the distal ridge of the hamate as it applies to hemihamate reconstruction of the base of the middle phalanx.

Methods

This study was approved by the institutional review board. CT scans of the wrist for patients treated within the authors hospital system from 2016 to 2020 were analyzed using Visage imaging software. Patients were excluded if they were under 18 years of age or had a fracture of the hamate or any previous surgical intervention to the hamate. Demographic data, including age, date of birth, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), and hand dominance, were obtained via chart review. CT scans were analyzed using Visage software’s measurement tools. Articular width was measured using the ruler tool (Fig. 1). The angle of the central ridge of the hamate in the coronal plane was measured using the angle tool (Fig. 2). The width of the articular surface between the fourth and fifth metacarpal heads in the coronal place was measured using the ruler tool (Fig. 3). The radius of the curve of these articulations in the sagittal plane was measured using the circular region of interest tool (Fig. 4). The shape of the facet in the coronal plane was determined by evaluating the curve of the facet on the coronal view of the CT scan and deemed to be concave or convex (Fig. 5).

Results

A total of 150 CT scans were screened, with 118 meeting inclusion criteria. The final cohort included 55 females and 63 males with an average age of 65 years. The average height among females was 64.5 inches, and BMI was 27.7. The average height among males was 69.3 inches, and BMI was 28.8.

The average width of the articular surface in the coronal plane was 14.9 mm with a range of 10.9–18.7 (95% confidence interval [CI] ± 3.6) (Fig. 6). The average width for females was 13.8 mm (95% CI ± 2.9 mm), and the average width for males was 15.5 mm (95% CI ± 3.5 mm). The mean central ridge angle (CRA) was 154.5° with a range of 131.7–175 (95% CI ± 16.92) (Fig. 7). The mean CRA for females was 153.2° (95% CI ± 19.3). The mean CRA for males was 155.4° (95% CI ± 16.2). The mean width of the fourth metacarpal base facet was 6.5 mm (95% CI ± 2.3), with mean of 6.5 mm (95% CI ± 2.1) among females and 6.7 mm (95% CI ± 2.3) among males (Fig. 8). The mean width of the fifth metacarpal base facet was 8.9 mm (95% CI ± 2.9). The mean was 8.2 mm (95% CI +/- 2.7) for females and 9.5 mm (95% CI ± 2.4) for males (Fig. 9).

Discussion

Previous research has demonstrated suitability of using the dorsal hamate articular surface as an osteochondral autograft for fractures involving the middle phalanx articular base.7,11,15 This similarity is largely due to the central ridge of the distal hamate articular surface creating a bicondylar articular surface as well as the biconcavity of the fourth and fifth facets.11,12 Mismatch of the distal hamate and middle phalanx base may result in abnormal PIP joint congruency and contact pressures contributing to pain, stiffness, decreased ROM, and ultimately degenerative joint changes. This may account for the heterogenous results reported in the literature with regards to development of degenerative arthritis after HHA.11,16, 17, 18 Knowledge of this variability may improve patient selection and may justify preoperative CT imaging to evaluate suitability of the distal hamate as an autograft.

The CRA represents one of the key anatomic features that denotes the resemblance of the distal hamate to the middle phalanx base.11 Calva et al (2016) evaluated the consistency of this angle between the subjects hamate and middle phalanx base of digits 2–5 using CT scans to compare the two; however, they did not describe in detail the variability of the CRA itself. In our study, we focused on just the distal hamate central ridge and did not evaluate the middle phalanx anatomy. In our study, the mean CRA was 154.5°. Here, 10.2% of patients had an angle >1 standard deviation below the mean (less than 145°), and 14.4% had an angle >1 standard deviation above the mean (more than 165°). This variability in acuity of the angle suggests that the hemihamate distal articular surface may not consistently be an acceptable substitute for the middle phalanx proximal articular surface. Further study could evaluate these measures with respect to the middle phalanx base to see if the same amount of variability exists.

In addition to the CRA, the shape of the fourth and fifth facets indicates an important anatomic feature that contributes to the congruency of the graft with the middle facet base. The shape of the fourth metacarpal facet in the coronal plane was concave in 92.7% women and 60 out of 63 men. The shape of the fifth metacarpal facet was much more variable. This finding is notable because HHA relies on the biconcavity of the distal hamate to replace the biconcavity of the middle phalanx proximal articular surface. The middle phalanx proximal articular surface is noted to be consistently concave at both the radial and ulnar facets. We noted in the current study that more than half of patients had a convex fifth metacarpal facet. This is similar to a previous study that noted that nearly 80% of patients had a convex shaped fifth facet.11 This variability suggests that these patients may not have suitable anatomy for HHA or may require additional contouring of the graft to prevent abnormal chondral contact at the reconstructed joint. As joint convexity versus concavity is difficult to determine on plain radiographs, there may be a role for preoperative CT to evaluate the suitability of the hamate in those considering HHA.

In regard to the coronal articular surface width, ROC, width of the fourth and fifth facets, there was variability between genders as expected given the typical differences in height and weight between the two groups. These differences are less clinically relevant as the graft is often cut from the donor site to the size needed.

Preoperative CT scan may quickly predict hamate anatomy amenable to HHA. The challenge remains how to address unreconstructible volar lip fractures if HHA is not an ideal anatomic option, as there are few alternatives. Calfee et al13 reported radiographic outcomes on 14 out of 33 patients with mean follow up of 5 years (range 1–7) and described arthritic changes in six out of 14 patients. In a case series of eight patients with minimum 48 month follow up, Afendras et al14 reported mean arc of motion of 67° with a range of 45° to 95°. Posttraumatic arthritis was present in 50% of patients (4 out of 8) with severe arthritic changes in two patients and mild changes in two patients. Kato et al19 reported 3 out of 13 patients developed arthritic changes visible on radiographs with a mean of 14 month follow up. Najid Mazhar et al20 reported 5 out of 15 patients had radiographic degenerative joint disease at mean follow up of 29.7 months with mean PIP arc of motion of 63.6°. Preoperative CT scan may be helpful to counsel patients with mismatches that they may be at an increased risk of joint stiffness and development of early osteoarthritis following HHA. These patients may benefit instead from a salvage procedure just as primary joint arthroplasty or fusion to avoid future surgical interventions.

Although joint incongruity can lead to increased contact pressure and contribute to posttraumatic arthritis, not all patients with joint incongruity develop symptoms of arthritis. Incongruity alone does not alone predict development of symptomatic degenerative joint changes following HHA.15,21 Thus, preoperative imaging to confirm suitability of a HHA graft would not be anticipated to completely absolve risk of developing posttraumatic arthritis and would not affect other proposed contributing factors such as devascularization and denervation of the graft from the graft harvest.22 However, it could potentially predict patients in whom the graft would be likely to create an incongruous joint with a readily available and relatively ubiquitous CT technology.

This study validates that there is considerable variability in the general population with regards to the hamate distal articular surface. However, there are several limitations to this study. Given the limited scans that did not uniformly include the PIP joint, we were unable to complete a matched analysis for each patient so did not include analysis of the base of the middle phalanx. Moreover, as this was a CT study, articular cartilage thickness and morphology cannot be assessed and may affect the convexity and concavity of the joint surface. This could be better addressed in conjunction with a magnetic resonance imaging or cadaveric study to address chondral in addition to bony morphology. Although there is variability in the CRA, it is unclear the correlation of mismatch between the CRA and clinical outcomes. Future clinical investigation is necessary to evaluate the effect of hamate mismatch on outcomes following hemihamate reconstruction arthroplasty. This would help guide us on the value of preoperative imaging in predicting ROM and risk of posttraumatic arthritis.
