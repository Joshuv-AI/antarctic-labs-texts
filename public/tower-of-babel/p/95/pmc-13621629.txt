Integrated lipidomic and transcriptomic profiling of the host response in human malaria
Authors: Wael Abdrabou, Dariga Bolatbay, Issiaka Soulama, Maria Nikulkova, Mame Massar Dieng, Saruul Zorigt, Manar Alshaikh, Samuel Sindié Sermé, Salif Sombié, Noelie Bere Henry, Désiré Kargougou, Sam Aboubacar Coulibaly, Vinu Manikandan, Aïssatou Diawara, Jane M Carlton, Youssef Idaghdour

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIdentifying the mechanisms of host–parasite interactions in vivo in malaria is essential for the development of antimalarial strategies tailored to clinically and physiologically relevant contexts.ResultsWe analyzed 396 paired global serum lipidomes from pediatric patients sampled before and during blood-stage malaria infection in Burkina Faso, spanning three ethnic groups (Gouin, Mossi, and Fulani). Consistent infection-induced remodeling of the host lipidome was identified across populations, including depletion of 47 host-derived lipid species that correlated with parasitemia. Notably, we observed that Plasmodium falciparum selectively scavenges linoleic acid–containing phospholipids to support its proliferation and validated this with parasite culture assays. This integrative multi-omics analysis combining lipidomic profiles with host–parasite transcriptomes further identified a Plasmodium falciparum transcriptional program associated with lipid turnover in vivo.ConclusionsThese results provide a high-resolution profile of lipidomic perturbations in children with malaria and demonstrate how integrated clinical phenotyping, cross-ethnic population sampling, and multi-omics can reveal key host–parasite interactions and clinically relevant metabolic changes within the human host.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13059-026-04180-1.Keywords: Malaria, Host-parasite interactions, Metabolomics, High resolution lipidomics, Linoleic acid, Phospholipids, Ethnicity

Background

Advancements in metabolomic and lipidomic profiling have transformed our ability to identify and characterize lipid species with unprecedented depth [1–6]. Recently, lipid metabolism has emerged as a key molecular mechanism underpinning the pathophysiology of malaria infection [7–10], which remains a major global health crisis with limited prospects for new interventions [11]. During the blood stage, Plasmodium falciparum depends heavily on host-derived metabolites, specifically lipids, for membrane biogenesis and remodeling of infected red blood cells (iRBCs) [12–17]. Throughout its intraerythrocytic development, the parasite continually synthesizes and remodels fatty acid–based lipids, especially phospholipids (PLs) and triacylglycerols (TAGs) [17–21]. Yet, the specific lipid molecules that promote or alter parasite proliferation or potentially modulate host immune responses remain poorly defined. Hence, generating a high-resolution paired lipidome map before and during infection is essential to identify the specific host lipids P. falciparum scavenges, and to reveal its molecular preferences and strategies for optimizing proliferation within the host.

Understanding how P. falciparum infection alters lipid metabolism has been the focus of multiple studies, primarily using in vitro and animal models [15, 18, 19, 21–23]. Most in vivo studies have also emphasized global metabolic changes rather than mapping the role of distinct lipid classes or fatty acid profiles in host–parasite interactions [24, 25]. Addressing this knowledge gap is essential, as identifying the lipid pathways most affected during infection will not only advance mechanistic insight into malaria biology but also highlight potential biomarkers of susceptibility and new molecular entry points for intervention. In vitro research has shown that specific host-derived fatty acids (FAs) and PLs are essential for the proliferation and sexual commitment of P. falciparum during the intraerythrocytic developmental cycle [15, 23]. Combinations of FAs, including palmitic acid (C16:0), stearic acid (C18:0) and oleic acid (C18:1, n-9) have been found to promote and sustain parasite growth in culture [15, 23]. Additionally, in vivo studies have revealed shifts in plasma concentrations of certain lipid classes during P. falciparum and P. vivax infection, including the depletion of monounsaturated PL, lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC), acylcarnitines (e.g., palmitoylcarnitine (C16:0), stearoylcarnitine (C18:0) and oleoylcarnitine (C18:1)), and the elevation of TAG plasma levels [18, 24–29].

PLs are the primary constituents of the parasite’s cell membrane. During the trophozoite stage, the lipid composition of the parasite includes approximately 34% phosphatidylcholine (PC), 20% phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), and 11% phosphatidylserine (PS), although this distribution varies across developmental stages [30]. TAGs represent another essential lipid class. Despite comprising only about 2.7% of the total lipid content, TAGs play critical roles in protecting the parasite from oxidative stress [31–33]. TAGs also help detoxify heme, act as reservoirs for fatty acids required for phospholipid remodeling, and buffer against the harmful effects of saturated fatty acid accumulation [34]. Notably, the functional properties of both PLs and TAGs are influenced by key molecular features, especially acyl chain length and degree of saturation level [35–38]. Thus, the availability of host-derived lipids with favorable molecular properties may significantly enhance parasite growth and proliferation, thereby influencing the course of malaria.

Previous in vitro and animal studies have shown that certain fatty acids, such as palmitic (C16:0), stearic (C18:0), and oleic acid (C18:1), support parasite growth and sexual development [15, 18, 20, 21, 23, 39]. In vivo, malaria infection alters circulating lipid levels, including reduced monounsaturated PLs, lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC), and acylcarnitines, alongside elevated TAGs (20, 25–30). PLs are central to the parasite membrane, with phosphatidylcholine (34%), phosphatidylethanolamine (20%), and phosphatidylserine (11%) dominating during the trophozoite stage [40]. Although TAGs comprise only ~ 3% of total parasite lipids, they play key roles in oxidative stress defense, heme detoxification, and phospholipid remodeling [31–33, 41]. The bioactivity of both PLs and TAGs depends on molecular features such as acyl chain length and levels of saturation [35–37, 42], and the availability of host lipids with favorable properties may impact parasite fitness.

To investigate lipid metabolism perturbations in natural P. falciparum infection in the most vulnerable age group, we performed paired serum lipidomic profiling in children from three West African ethnic groups. In 100 children from the Gouin group, we generated and analyzed 200 high-resolution lipidomes before and during infection, identifying major lipidomic shifts associated with infection and parasitemia. We replicated key findings, including the depletion of linoleic acid–containing PLs, in two independent cohorts (Mossi and Fulani), revealing a consistent metabolic signature. Integrative lipidomic-transcriptomic analysis suggests that this depletion is driven to a large extent by parasite metabolic processes. Our experimental assay shows that supplying exogenous linoleic acid–containing phospholipids can enhance P. falciparum growth under lipid-restricted conditions. Together, these findings suggest that the parasite can leverage host lipid pools during infection, providing insight into metabolic interactions that shape malaria pathogenesis and may reveal potential intervention strategies.

Results

Study design and discovery cohort

A discovery cohort of 100 healthy non-Plasmodium infected children (2–12 years of age) from the Gouin ethnic group was enrolled. In Burkina Faso, malaria follows a seasonal pattern allowing sampling of children before infection (at the end of the dry season) and during P. falciparum infection (at the beginning of the rainy season) with minimal variation between the two states due to other factors such as diet. Sampling was done following comprehensive clinical examination and malaria rapid detection tests which indicated the absence of P. falciparum infection. These results were subsequently confirmed using microscopy and gene expression data. Children were then monitored on a bi-weekly basis and sampled again upon the onset of malaria symptoms and the detection of P. falciparum (Fig. 1a). The paired study design, carefully defined inclusion and exclusion criteria, and meticulously designed experimental protocols (see Methods) were all implemented to minimize unwanted sources of variability and improve the precision of the statistical analyses. Demographic and clinical characteristics of the study participants are provided in Additional file 2: Tables S1 and S2.

Global serum metabolomic profiling

To explore the impact of P. falciparum infection on the host serum lipidome, 200 global lipid profiles were generated from 100 Gouin children before and during infection (90% matched) using a combination of flow injection analysis, differential mobility spectrometry, and multiple reaction monitoring for high resolution characterization of lipid molecules (see Methods). Curation of the metabolomic data (Additional file 1: Fig. S1) resulted in retention of 991 complex lipids spanning 11 lipid classes detected in both non-infected and infected states: triacylglycerol (TAG; n = 518), phosphatidylcholine (PC; n = 95), phosphatidylethanolamine (PE; n = 119), phosphatidylinositol (PI; n = 28), lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC; n = 18), lysophosphatidylethanolamine (LPE; n = 17), free fatty acid (FFA; n = 25), monoacylglycerol (MAG; n = 26), diacylglycerol (DAG; n = 58), cholesterol ester (CE; n = 26) and Sphingolipid (ceramide (CER; n = 12), hexosylceramide (HCER; n = 12), lactosylceramide (LCER; n = 12) and dihydroceramide (DCER; n = 13)). TAGs and PLs made up 52% and 28% of the lipid molecules detected, respectively (Fig. 1a).

The impact of P. falciparum infection on the host lipid profile

Principal component analysis (PCA) showed that P. falciparum has a significant impact on the global lipidome with the first two PCs accounting for 31.2% of the variation in the data (Fig. 1b). Pairwise comparisons of the total concentration of each lipid class between before and during infection states revealed a significant drop in total LPC and LPE (Fold change (FC) = −3.4; Benjamini–Hochberg (B-H) FDR adjusted p < 0.0001), and significant elevation of total TAG (FC = 1.5; B-H FDR adjusted p < 0.0001) during infection (Fig. 1c and Additional file 2: Table S3). Next, we performed molecule-by-molecule ANCOVA analysis of the 991 lipid molecules and identified 129 significantly differentially abundant lipids during infection (FC ≥|1.5|, B-H FDR < 0.1, Fig. 1d).

PLs made up approximately 73.5% of all depleted lipid molecules (n = 83) during infection; LPC (21%; n = 17), PE (19%; n = 16), CE (19%; n = 16), LPE (15%; n = 12), PC (10%; n = 8), and PI (10%; n = 8). In contrast, 89% of enriched lipid molecules (n = 46) during infection were neutral lipids; TAG (76%; n = 35) and DAG (13%; n = 6) (Fig. 1d and Additional file 2: Table S4). These results revealed a major impact of P. falciparum infection on the composition of the host lipidome and hint at major perturbations in glycerolipid (e.g., TAG and DAG) and phospholipid (e.g., PC and PE) biosynthesis pathways in the host and/or parasite. To identify lipid molecules that are potentially selectively exploited by the parasite, we tested the associations between parasitemia and serum levels of individual lipid molecules within the infected samples using linear regression analysis adjusting for age, sex and white blood cell counts. The analysis revealed 47 statistically significant lipid molecules associated with parasitemia (B-H FDR ≤ 0.1; Fig. 1e). These lipids comprised entirely of two classes: TAGs and PLs (PE, PC, PI and LPE) (n = 31 and 16, respectively) (Figs. 1e, 2a, Additional file 1: Fig. S2 and Additional file 2: Table S4).

Depletion of parasitemia-associated phospholipids and triacylglycerols

Next, we focused on the molecular features of PLs, the primary building blocks of the parasite's cell membrane, which are both depleted during infection and negatively correlated with parasitemia, suggesting they are scavenged from the host to support parasite growth. PLs associated with parasitemia consists of 16 molecules including 7 positively correlated (enriched) PLs (4 PCs and 3 PEs; e.g., PC (20:0/18:1); Pearson correlation coefficient r = 0.38, Bonferroni-adjusted p = 0.005, Fig. 2a) and 9 negatively correlated (depleted) PLs (e.g., PE (18:2/18:2); Pearson correlation coefficient r = −0.43, Bonferroni-adjusted p = 0.0005, Fig. 2a). Typically, the sn-1 position in PL molecule glycerol-3-P backbone contains a saturated acyl chain (e.g., C16:0, C18 and C20:0) whereas the sn-2 position contains a saturated, monounsaturated or polyunsaturated acyl chain [43]. A close examination of the acyl chain characteristics of the parasitemia-associated PLs revealed a striking pattern: 7 out of 9 of the depleted PLs contain (C18:2) acyl chain (linoleic acid), in sharp contrast to the enriched PLs, none of which contain this acyl chain (e.g., PE (18:1/18:2); Fig. 2a, b). These results suggest that the depletion of linoleic acid-containing PLs in peripheral blood may be driven by the host’s immune response to infection, the metabolic demands of P. falciparum, or a combination of both. Notably, of the 110 PUFA-containing PLs present in the serum of children before infection, 33 are linoleic acid-containing PLs, 32 of which are significantly depleted during infection (B-H FDR < 0.1, FC ≥|1.1|; Fig. 2c and Additional file 2: Table S5). The top five most depleted linoleic acid-containing PLs are shown in Fig. 2c.

To test for enrichment for each acyl chain detected in our data, we performed lipid ontology enrichment analysis for each of the 30 acyl chains in enriched and depleted PLs separately using Fisher’s exact tests implemented in Lipid Mini-On [44] (see Methods). This analysis, which considered acyl chains irrespective of the phospholipid subclass they make, confirmed our initial observation and revealed a strong, statistically significant enrichment of linoleic acid in the depleted PLs (FC = 7.92; B-H FDR adjusted p < 0.00001; Fig. 2d and Additional file 2: Table S6). Strikingly, none of the other 29 acyl chains in depleted PLs showed significant enrichment. Similarly, no significant enrichment was observed for any of the 30 fatty acid acyl chains in the enriched PLs (Fig. 2e and Additional file 2: Table S6). Interestingly, among the parasitemia-associated triacylglycerols (TAGs; n = 31), which included 9 positively correlated (enriched) and 22 negatively correlated (depleted) species, all TAGs containing linoleic acid were significantly depleted at higher parasitemia levels (e.g., TAG56:3-FA18:2, Pearson correlation coefficient r =-0.40, Bonferroni-adjusted p = 0.002; Additional file 1: Fig. S2).

Depletion of linoleic acid-containing phospholipids during infection in replication cohorts

To determine whether the host lipidome perturbations observed in Gouin children are consistent across other ethnic groups with distinct backgrounds, we conducted two independent studies in Mossi and Fulani populations, two prominent West African ethnic groups with different genetic, cultural, and lifestyle characteristics [45] and both inhabiting another geographic location with a high prevalence of malaria in Burkina Faso. Mossi have an agrarian lifestyle and practice subsistence farming while Fulani are traditionally pastoralists, known for their nomadic way of life and cattle herding. Importantly, we used the same study design and methodologies employed for the Gouin discovery cohort in both Mossi and Fulani. In total, we recruited 53 Fulani and 45 Mossi children and generated 196 paired metabolomic profiles before and during P. falciparum infection (see Methods and Additional file 2: Table S7 for detailed characteristics of both cohorts).

A total of 927 lipid molecules were detected across 196 samples, 90.3% of which (n = 837) were detected in the Gouin discovery cohort. In both Mossi and Fulani, we detected the same number of PUFA-containing PLs as in Gouin (n = 110), 30 of which contain linoleic acid (33 in the Gouin dataset). Despite the geographic and seasonal heterogeneity between the discovery and replication cohorts, which encompassed disparate transmission windows and varying parasitemia levels, and the distinct dietary and sociocultural profiles of the three ethnic groups, we identified a robustly conserved metabolic signature (Fig. 2f, g). Specifically, consistent with the observation in Gouin children, 28 out of 30 and 26 out of 30 linoleic acid–containing PLs were statistically significantly depleted during infection in Fulani and Mossi, respectively (Fig. 2f, g and Additional file 2: Table S7).

Replication of our findings from the Gouin cohort in two independent childhood cohorts provides further evidence that the depletion of the host's reservoir of linoleic acid–containing PLs during blood-stage is a hallmark of falciparum malaria. Our next set of host-parasite co-transcriptomic experiments in the Gouin cohort tested if these infection-driven dynamic molecular changes are associated with P. falciparum and/or host transcriptional signatures.

Transcriptional signatures associated with parasitemia-associated lipids

To investigate the contribution of host and parasite transcription to the observed lipidomic perturbations, we generated dual host-parasite RNASeq profiles using total RNA extracted from the same peripheral blood samples of Gouin children from which the lipidomic data were generated (Fig. 3a and Additional file 1: Fig. S3). A paired host-parasite lipidomic-transcriptomic dataset provides an opportunity to deduce the extent and nature of interplay between the host and parasite transcriptional profiles and infection-induced lipid metabolism. We paid careful attention in our procedures of extraction and process of host-parasite RNA and in our computational analysis to minimize bias and enable quantitative analysis of host-parasite lipidomic-transcriptomic data (see Methods, Fig. 3a). Co-extraction of host-parasite RNA was successful as indicated by the presence of both human and P. falciparum 18S and 28S rRNA units in the Bioanalyzer profiles (Fig. 3b and Additional file 1: Fig. S3). Host-parasite RNASeq library preparation was done following depletion of globin mRNA and abundant rRNA and tRNA (see Methods). Raw sequencing reads were filtered, trimmed, and mapped to the human reference (Ensembl GRCh38 release-84) [46, 47] and the PF3D7 P. falciparum reference (PlasmoDB Release 28) [48, 49] genomes. Quality control resulted in retaining 12,375 human and 1,923 P. falciparum uniquely mapped transcripts for downstream analyses (see Methods, Fig. 3c and Additional file 3: Table S8).

To identify host and parasite transcripts correlated with the 47 parasitemia-associated lipid molecules, we performed cross-correlation analysis between levels of these molecules and transcript abundance of the 12,376 host and 1,923 parasite transcripts separately. For the host data, the analysis revealed 12,821 statistically significant lipid-human transcript associations corresponding to 6,504 unique human transcripts at FDR 5% and an absolute Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient cutoff of |r|≥ 0.42 (5,616 negative and 7,205 positive associations, Fig. 3d and Additional file 3: Table S9). For the parasite data, the analysis revealed 2,246 statistically significant lipid-parasite transcript associations corresponding to 589 unique P. falciparum transcripts at FDR 5% and an absolute Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient cutoff |r|≥ 0.36 (1,388 positive and 858 negative associations, Fig. 3d and Additional file 3: Table S10).

Next, we investigated the functional characteristics of the 6,504 human transcripts and the 589 P. falciparum transcripts whose expression levels were significantly correlated with parasitemia- associated lipids using Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) and the PF3D7 P. falciparum reference (PlasmoDB Release 28) [48, 49], respectively. This analysis revealed no enrichment in the human host data for any fatty acid lipid metabolic pathway in contrast to statistically significant enrichment in the P. falciparum data with four KEGG pathways implicated in fatty acid lipid metabolism: Glycerolipid (ec00561; fold enrichment = 2.17, p-value = 5.62e-3), Glycerophospholipid (ec00564; fold enrichment = 1.70, p-value = 6.12e-3), fatty acid degradation (ec00071); fold enrichment = 2.20, p-value = 1.00e-2), and fatty acid biosynthesis (ec00061; fold enrichment = 2.48, p-value = 1.93e-2) (Fig. 3e, f and Additional file 3: Table S11). In total, 26 P. falciparum transcripts were implicated in these pathways (Fig. 3f and Additional file 3: Table S12).

Phospholipid de novo biosynthesis and remodeling in P. falciparum

Investigating the function of the 26 P. falciparum transcripts using the PF3D7 P. falciparum reference (PlasmoDB Release 28) and the Malaria Parasite Metabolic Pathways database [48–50] revealed that 22 out of the 26 transcripts encode a set of enzymes implicated in the de novo biosynthesis and remodeling pathways of phospholipids and glycerolipids. These metabolic pathways are CDP-DAG pathway, Kennedy pathway, Lands cycle and lysophospholipid degradation (Additional file 3: Table S11). Among the transcripts involved in de novo biosynthesis pathways, we identified PF3D7_0215300, PF3D7_0401900, and PF3D7_1479000, which encode acyl-CoA synthetases PfACS8, PfACS6 and PfACS1a. These enzymes play a critical role in catalyzing the formation of fatty acyl-CoAs, a key step in the de novo synthesis of lysophosphatidic acid and phosphatidic acid, both key precursors in the Kennedy pathway for phospholipid biosynthesis. We also identified PF3D7_1444300, which encodes PfLPAAT, an acyltransferase enzyme [51] that catalyzes the formation of phosphatidic acid from fatty acyl-CoAs and glycerol-3-phosphate [52, 53]. This enzyme is responsible for the synthesis of phosphatidic acid, the smallest phospholipid and a central precursor for the biosynthesis of all other phospholipids [54, 55]. Together, these results suggest that the major and specific lipid alterations observed during infection may be driven by parasite transcriptional programs geared towards the synthesis and utilization of linoleic acid-containing PLs during the intra-erythrocytic developmental stage.

Exogenous linoleic acid-containing phospholipids promote parasite proliferation

To test whether linoleic acid–containing phospholipids can support parasite growth under lipid-limited conditions, we performed a controlled supplementation experiment using PC (18:2/18:2) at a concentration comparable to the total in vivo pool of linoleic acid–containing PLs detected in the discovery cohort (Additional file 1: Fig. S4a and Mendeley Data). Synchronized asexual parasites were cultured at ~ 1% parasitemia (5% hematocrit) in complete RPMI medium (CM), lipid-poor medium lacking AlbuMAX [CM(− AlbuMAX)], CM(− AlbuMAX) with vehicle (ethanol/water), or CM(− AlbuMAX) supplemented with 18 µM PC (18:2/18:2) (Additional file 1: Fig. S4a, b). Across three independent biological replicates, cultures supplemented with PC (18:2/18:2) exhibited a reproducible and statistically significant increase in parasitemia from 1.0% to 4.3% (P = 0.008), whereas CM(− AlbuMAX) and CM(− AlbuMAX) + vehicle showed no significant increase over the same time interval of five days (Additional file 1: Fig. S4b). Although PC (18:2/18:2) did not fully restore growth to levels observed in CM (which increased from 1.7% to 6.0%; P = 0.005, Additional file 1: Fig. S4b), the magnitude of rescue under lipid-limiting conditions was robust and consistent across replicates. Importantly, ethanol at the concentration used did not promote parasite growth, confirming that the proliferative response was attributable to phospholipid supplementation rather than solvent effects. These findings demonstrate that supplying a linoleic acid–containing phospholipid is sufficient to enhance parasite proliferation under lipid-restricted conditions, consistent with the depletion of these PLs observed in vivo.

Discussion

Parasites have evolved remarkable adaptations to evade host immune responses [56–59] and optimize their utilization of host resources [17]. In the case of P. falciparum, these adaptations are essential for survival and proliferation during the transition from the liver to asexual blood-stage development. Here, we report major alterations in the lipidomic composition of children upon P. falciparum infection and provide evidence that the parasite exploits the host nutrient microenvironment to fuel its growth. The most striking finding, replicated across three independent West African ethnic groups, was the selective depletion of host-derived linoleic acid-containing PLs, which were negatively associated with parasitemia. Disruptions in linoleic acid metabolism have been implicated in the immune response to other major infections, such as influenza and SARS-CoV-2, where hydroxylation processes produce pro-inflammatory eicosanoids [60–62]. These comparative studies highlight the central role of PUFA-containing phospholipids in host defense molecular processes and suggest that the sequestration of these lipids is a critical pivot point in host–pathogen interactions.

In the specific context of P. falciparum biology, PUFA-containing PLs are known to contribute to membrane elasticity and plasticity, facilitate adaptation to mechanical stress, and enhance the plasma membrane's capacity for deformation and fission, particularly under cholesterol-limited conditions [43, 63–65]. Using untargeted metabolomic profiling of the same Gouin cohort, we previously reported the depletion of serum eicosanoids such as 12-HETE and 12-HETR during infection [24]. Taken together, this suggests that the changes in host-derived linoleic acid-containing PLs observed here are likely driven by the parasite's high metabolic demand for membrane biogenesis and specialized molecular processes rather than general host inflammation.

Previous studies have shown that linoleic acid enhances the sexual development of the parasite Toxoplasma gondii, although it remains unclear whether this effect is mediated through signaling functions or by meeting the parasite’s basic nutritional needs [66]. Earlier studies have reported that infected erythrocytes exhibit an increase in total unesterified fatty acid content, including linoleic acid, across multiple host species (avian, primate, and rodent) [67, 68]. Additionally, diglycerides containing linoleic acid are significantly elevated in simian erythrocytes infected with P. knowlesi [68]. Linoleic acid is an essential fatty acid that can be obtained exclusively through plant-based diets due to the unavailability of delta-12 fatty acid desaturase 2 responsible for the conversion of oleic acid to linoleic acid in mammals [69, 70]. Linoleic acid is the main precursor of all ω−6 (n-6) PUFAs such as arachidonic acid [71], it is one of the fatty acids that play a direct role in membrane permeability [72] and has the highest concentration among all PUFAs in human serum [23, 73]. Our results call for systematic investigations of the relationship between dietary linoleic acid intake and disease severity in malaria as done previously for coronary heart disease [74].

To investigate whether the depletion of linoleic acid–containing PLs during infection is linked to host or parasite lipid metabolism, we performed an integrative host–parasite co-lipidomic-transcriptomic analysis, which revealed P. falciparum transcriptional changes in association with this depletion. Among the transcripts identified, we highlight two phospholipases and a set of lysophospholipase-encoding transcripts that catalyze the deacetylation of phospholipids and lysophospholipids, respectively, and result in the generation of a pool of FAs for phospholipid remodeling [75]. Importantly, we identified a set of three acyl-CoA synthetases – encoding transcripts (PfACS8, PfACS6 and PfACS1a) [76]. Nonsynonymous variation in PfACS8 has been shown to modulate the protective effect of sickle hemoglobin HbS [77], raising the possibility that linoleic acid-containing phospholipid depletion or processing may be subject to this striking host–parasite genetic interaction.

Our findings warrant further investigation into whether P. falciparum ACS enzymes exhibit preferential specificity for linoleic acid. Long-chain acyl-CoA synthetase (ACSL) inhibitors have been investigated as therapeutic agents in cancer, metabolic disorders [78–80], and parasitic infections including Giardia and Cryptosporidium parvum [81, 82]. Notably, a recent study identified several Medicines for Malaria Venture (MMV) compounds as potent antimalarial candidates targeting PfACS10, further underscoring the therapeutic potential of ACS enzymes in malaria [83]. Interestingly, disruption of the PfACS10 gene, which is highly expressed during the late asexual developmental stages (http://plasmodb.org) [84–86], has been shown to reduce the parasite’s internal pool of FAs, particularly those with a C18 chain length [83].

The identification of a consistent, cross-ethnic lipidomic signature associated with P. falciparum infection reveals a metabolic vulnerability with translational relevance. The observed depletion of host-derived linoleic acid–containing phospholipids, coupled with the upregulation of parasite PfACS, supports prioritizing these enzymes as targets for small-molecule inhibition aimed at disrupting the parasite’s capacity to scavenge and remodel essential host-derived lipids. In parallel, targeting transport mechanisms mediating the selective uptake of PUFA-containing phospholipids across the parasitophorous vacuole membrane represents an additional therapeutic avenue. The apparent dependence on exogenous linoleic acid to sustain membrane plasticity further highlights a point of metabolic fragility that may be exploitable for intervention. Beyond pharmacological strategies, these findings provide a biological rationale for investigating how host nutritional status and dietary PUFA availability influence malaria pathogenesis, with potential implications for adjunct nutritional interventions in endemic settings.

This study aimed to identify changes in lipid metabolism during P. falciparum blood-stage malaria. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the origin of the altered serum lipid profile remains uncertain; contributions from hepatic secretion, lipoprotein remodeling, or the release of metabolites from apoptotic erythrocytes and other tissues cannot be excluded. Nevertheless, the longitudinal matched design of our study control for this inter-individual variability and confounding factors. Second, our functional assay was designed as a proof-of-principle test to evaluate whether replenishing a single linoleic acid–containing PL could partially compensate for the lipid-limited conditions created in vitro and thereby support parasite proliferation. By supplementing lipid-poor medium with PC (18:2/18:2) at physiologically relevant concentrations, we observed a reproducible enhancement of parasite growth relative to matched vehicle and un-supplemented controls. While the effect size was modest compared to full serum-derived lipid availability, this result aligns with the concept of sufficiency rather than complete metabolic replacement and demonstrates that the parasite can utilize exogenous linoleic acid-containing PLs to sustain proliferation. Because our assay tested a single PL species at one concentration, it does not establish exclusivity or specificity relative to other phospholipid classes or fatty acid compositions.

A primary challenge in field studies is the potential for seasonal dietary shifts to confound host lipidomic profiles. Although data collected from our study cohorts indicated largely consistent dietary intake across sampling time points, self-reported dietary information lacks the precision to entirely exclude subtle nutritional influences. To resolve this, future research should prioritize controlled experimental malaria models in mice, which allow for the strict titration of dietary lipids to confirm that the observed alterations are intrinsic metabolic features of Plasmodium infection and host response. Furthermore, investigating the biophysical context of serum phospholipids, specifically their partitioning within various lipoprotein fractions, and expanding analysis to other lipid classes, such as neutral lipids and sphingolipids, will be essential to map the full breadth of host-parasite metabolic competition. Methodologically, the use of stable-isotope-labeled lipid tracing in these controlled settings would provide a high-resolution map of lipid flux from the host to the parasite. Additionally, randomized, controlled supplementation trials are warranted to evaluate whether modulating host linoleic acid availability directly influences parasite growth and disease severity. Such studies will be essential to decouple host nutritional status from the parasite-driven metabolic demands identified in this study.

Conclusions

While the depletion of linoleic acid-containing phospholipids was a convergent feature of P. falciparum infection across all three ethnic groups, we observed distinct inter-cohort heterogeneity in the absolute magnitude of these lipidomic shifts. This variability is likely multi-factorial, stemming from both stable baseline determinants and dynamic, infection-specific variables. At baseline, ethnic-specific dietary features and host genetics may establish disparate steady-state concentrations of certain lipids. Upon infection, this landscape is further modified by parasitemia-driven metabolic flux, where higher parasite densities observed in the Gouin cohort create a more profound sink for host phospholipids. Furthermore, the magnitude of the host systemic inflammatory response and differences in oxidative stress levels [57] between cohorts may influence the rate of lipid peroxidation versus metabolic scavenging. The fact that the linoleic acid signature remains statistically dominant despite these confounding geographic, seasonal, and host-related variables underscores the robustness of this metabolic requirement in P. falciparum pathogenesis.

Additionally, PLs in human serum are presented predominantly within lipoprotein complexes rather than as isolated molecular species, and our experiment does not recapitulate this biophysical context. Nonetheless, the observed functional rescue, together with the selective depletion of linoleic acid–containing PLs in vivo and the enrichment of parasite transcripts involved in acyl-CoA synthesis and phospholipid remodeling, provides convergent evidence that these lipids represent a physiologically relevant nutrient source that the parasite can access and use. We acknowledge that further studies, including dose–response experiments, comparison with non-linoleic containing PLs, metabolic labeling to trace incorporation, and genetic or chemical perturbation of the implicated parasite enzymes, will be required to dissect the precise molecular determinants of uptake and utilization. However, within the scope of our study, these results support the hypothesis that P. falciparum depletes linoleic acid-containing PLs to meet its metabolic demands during blood-stage infection. In summary, our study offers a high-resolution view of lipidomic changes during natural P. falciparum blood-stage infection and highlights the power of integrating population-based sampling, multi-omics, and in vitro validation to dissect host-parasite interactions. Additionally, we provide three paired lipidomic datasets from malarial children across West African ethnic groups as a valuable resource for the malaria community.

Methods

Study design

A total of 198 Burkinabe children from three ethnic groups (Gouin, Fulani and Mossi) in the health districts of Banfora and Saponé were recruited for this study. Non-infected children were enrolled at the end of the dry season and followed longitudinally during the rainy season when malaria transmission starts. Study inclusion criteria are age of 2 to 12 years, no P. falciparum infection at the time of enrollment, no fever (axillary temperature ≥ 37.5 °C) within the preceding 48 h. Exclusion criteria were symptoms of active infection or illness, use of anti-malarial treatment within the preceding two weeks, use of any immune-modifying drugs within the preceding six months, receiving blood transfusion within the preceding three months and history of immunosuppressive or chronic diseases.

Sample collection and phenotyping

Peripheral blood samples were collected twice from study participants, before P. falciparum infection and during infection. Following the second blood sampling, the children received artemether/lumefantrine antimalarial treatment (Coartem) following the guidelines of the Ministry of Health in Burkina Faso. For the discovery cohort (Gouin children), a total of 200 blood samples (100 children, 90% matching) were collected in Banfora. For the replication cohorts, a total of 106 and 90 blood samples were collected from the same group of 53 Fulani and 45 Mossi children, respectively, before and during infection (100% matching). Samples of the discovery and replication cohorts were collected in different years. Peripheral blood samples from each participant were split between SST, Tempus, and EDTA tubes, which were used for serum processing, RNA sequencing, and hematological testing, respectively. P. falciparum infection status was first determined by the SD BIOLINE Malaria Ag P.f/Pan rapid detection test and confirmed by bright field microscopy and PCR using P. falciparum specific primers as previously described [87]. Parasitemia was calculated using Giemsa staining of blood smears following the World Health Organization guidelines. Hemoglobin genotyping PCR was used to identify and exclude participants with sickle cell traits. All study participants were assessed during enrollment by a pediatrician on site. Clinical parameters recorded are listed in Additional file 2: Tables S1 and S2.

Metabolomics profiling and data analysis

Serum sample preparation and analytical methods

Serum samples were isolated on site by centrifugation of blood samples in SST tubes for 30 min at 1500 RCF. Separated serum aliquots of 0.5 mL were stored in cryovials immediately at −80 °C and were then transported on dry ice to NYU Abu Dhabi, UAE then shipped to Metabolon Inc. (Durham, NC, USA) for global lipidomic profiling using Metabolon’s complex lipid platform, which employs the triple quadrupole Sciex SelexIon-5500 QTRAP instrument. Lipids were extracted in conjunction with deuterated internal standards using an automated BUME extraction as described previously [88]. Nitrogen was used to dry lipid extracts which were then reconstituted in 0.25 mL of dichloromethane:methanol solution containing 10 mM ammonium acetate (50:50). Infusion-MS analysis of the extracts was performed on a Shimadzu LC with nano PEEK tubing and the Sciex SelexIon-5500 QTRAP. Each sample was analyzed twice via both positive and negative mode electrospray. The 5500 QTRAP employs flow injection analysis, differential mobility spectrometry, and multiple reaction monitoring. The combination of three techniques allows for the precise characterization of detected lipid species (e.g., phospholipids or triglycerides) down to the individual fatty acid level [6]. Samples were profiled in Multiple Reaction Monitoring mode with a total of more than 1,100 MRMs. Samples were randomized throughout the process to minimize any variation resulting from instrument inter-day tuning differences for runs spanning multiple days.

Complex lipid data curation

Concentrations of individual lipid molecules were calculated by taking the ratio of the signal intensity of each molecule to the signal intensity of its assigned internal standard, then multiplied by the concentration of internal standard added to each sample. Fatty acid composition of lipid classes that contain more than one fatty acid per molecule such as phosphatidylcholine (PC), phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), and phosphatidylinositol (PI) were determined by calculating the proportion of each class composed of individual fatty acids. The concentrations of triacylglycerol (TAG) molecules were calculated individually based on each neutral loss of fatty acid (FA) moiety, instead of the sum of all the FA fragment ion intensities. Each compound was corrected in run-day blocks by registering the medians to equal one and normalizing each data point proportionately. Missing values were imputed with the observed minimum after normalization.

Annotation of lipid molecules

Lipid molecules were annotated as follows: phospholipids (PC, PE, PI, PS), plasmalogens and diacylglycerols (DAG) molecules were annotated by lipid class, followed by the number of carbons and double bonds in the first and second FA moieties (e.g., PC (20:0/18:1, DAG (12:0/16:0) and PE(O-18:0/16:0). Triacylglycerols (TAG) molecules were annotated by sum composition (total carbons C and double bonds across all three FA moieties) followed by the composition of a single FA moiety (e.g., TAG 52:4-FA20:4). Lysophospholipids (LPC and LPE), sphingolipids, cholesterol esters and monoacylglycerols were annotated by their sum composition (e.g., LPE (22:4), SM (20:0), CE (16:1) and MAG (22:6)).

Normalization, unsupervised and supervised statistical analysis

Curated lipidomic data (total lipid classes and individual lipid molecules concentrations) were log transformed and IQR normalized using SAS/JMP Genomics version 10.0 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC) to remove potential technical artifacts and outliers (Additional file 1: Fig. S1, Additional file 2: Tables S1 and S2 and Mendeley Data). Batch normalization of the lipidomics data was also performed to correct for variation resulting from instrument inter-day tuning differences for runs spanning multiple days. Unsupervised statistical analyses such as PCA and hierarchical clustering were done to explore the correlation structure in the data and global influence of the infection on the serum lipidome. Supervised statistical analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism v8.0 and SAS/JMP Genomics version 10.0 (SAS Institute). Intra-ethnic analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) with repeated measures was used to test for statistically significant differential abundance of each lipid molecules between before and during infection states accounting for age and sex. Linear regression was used to test for association between the abundance of each lipid molecule and parasitemia in the infected children accounting for age and sex. A Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate threshold of 0.1 was used to infer statistical significance.

Acyl chain characteristics and lipid ontology analysis

Lipid ontology analysis of parasitemia-associated PLs was performed using the web interface, Lipid Mini-On (https://omicstools.pnnl.gov/shiny/lipid-mini-on/.) [44]. Lipid Mini-On determines the overrepresentation of specific lipid groups based on their intrinsic features (e.g. lipid class, subclass, head group, FA chain length, FA level of unsaturation) using the lipid naming and inferred structure. Statistical significance of enrichment was inferred using Fisher’s exact tests and FDR-adjusted p values below 0.05.

Host-parasite transcriptomic profiling

Host-parasite total RNA isolation

Blood samples collected in Tempus tubes (ThermoFisher Scientific) were stored at −80 °C and shipped in dry ice to stabilize RNA. RNA isolation was done using Tempus Spin RNA isolation kits (ThermoFisher Scientific). RNA quantification and quality-checking were done using Qubit and 2100 Bioanalyzer instruments. One µg of high-quality total RNA was subjected to globin-mRNA depletion using the GlobinClear kit (ThermoFisher Scientific) followed by cDNA library preparation using 500 ng globin-depleted RNA and KAPA Stranded RNA-Seq kit with RiboErase (Kapa Biosystems). cDNA libraries were quality-checked and quantified using a 2100 Bioanalyzer instrument. cDNA libraries were then multiplexed and sequenced (100 bp paired-end) using the TruSeq SBS Kit v3—HS (Illumina) on an Illumina instrument.

RNA-seq experiment and data analysis

Raw reads from total RNA sequencing data were processed using Trimmomatic v0.36 [89] to remove sequencing indexes, adapter sequences and reads with low quality scores (Phred quality score < 20). FastQC (0.11.5) was used before and after trimming to visualize the quality matrix. Filtered and trimmed reads were then mapped to the human reference genome (Ensembl GRCh38 release-84) using STAR v2.5.0c [46, 47] and the Plasmodium falciparum (PF3D7) genome (PlasmoDB Release 28) [48, 49]. Multiple alignment strategies were tested including alignment of the data to the human genome first then mapping the unmapped reads to the parasite genome, and vice versa. In all scenarios, stringent alignment criteria were applied to retain only uniquely mapped reads of high quality. These procedures yielded similar results and did not alter the inferences made from the integrative multi-omics analysis described below. Human and parasite transcript abundance was estimated as fragments per kilobase of exon per million mapped fragments (FPKM) using cufflinks v2.2.1. FPKM were then converted to transcripts per million (TPM) as described previously [90]. Only transcripts with a minimum of 1 TPM in at least 10 samples were retained for downstream analyses. Raw counts were log10 transformed and IQR normalized using SAS/JMP Genomics version 10.0 (SAS Institute). Distribution and unsupervised analyses of the data before and after normalization were used to assess the quality of the data. Four normalization methods (mean, median, IQR and quantile normalization) were tested and all gave similar results. After quality control, 77 dual host-parasite total RNA sequencing profiles were retained for downstream analysis (Additional file 1: Fig. S1 and Additional file 3: Table S8).

Integrative lipidomic-transcriptomic and pathway enrichment analysis

Lipidome-transcriptome cross-correlation analysis was performed using the cross-correlation procedure implemented in SAS/JMP Genomics 10.0 (SAS Institute) using the data from 77 Gouin children. In total, 12,371 human and 1,923 Plasmodium falciparum transcripts were cross-correlated with 47 parasitemia-associated lipids, separately. The correlation was assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (Qiagen) and the PF3D7 P. falciparum reference (PlasmoDB Release 28) [48, 49] were used to identify human and P. falciparum metabolic pathways implicated in human and parasite lipid metabolism, respectively. A Benjamini–Hochberg FDR threshold of 0.05 was used to infer statistical significance.

Parasite culture

P. falciparum culture was performed as previously described [91]. Asexual stages of P. falciparum parasite line 3D7 were cultured in complete RPMI consisting of RPMI 1640 Medium with GlutaMAX™ and HEPES (ThermoFisher Scientific) supplemented with 0.05 mg/ml hypoxanthine (Invitrogen), 2 mg/ml sodium bicarbonate (Invitrogen), 10 µg/ml gentamicin (Invitrogen), and 0.5% AlbuMAX (Invitrogen). Erythrocytes were obtained by collecting venous blood from healthy donors in EDTA tubes, and white blood cells were removed through centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min. The erythrocytes were washed using complete RPMI media (CM) and maintained at 50% hematocrit. To prepare a trophozoite-rich culture, the asexual parasites were synchronized twice using a 5% D-sorbitol (Sigma-Aldrich) solution at a 7–10-h interval [92]. The cultures were maintained at 37 °C in an atmosphere of 5% O2, 5% CO2, and 90% N2. To assess induced parasite growth in the presence of linoleic acid-containing phospholipids, we prepared a complete RPMI media free of AlbuMAX (CM (-AlbuMAX) supplemented with PC (18:2/18:2) (purchased from Avanti Polar Lipids). PC (18:2/18:2) was initially dissolved in a mixture of ethanol/water (1:1, v/v) then added to the AlbuMAX-free RPMI media to reach a final concentration of 18 µM with a final concentration of ethanol in the media of 25 µl/ml (v/v). The concentration is also comparable to the in vivo concentration of PC (18:2/18:2) detected in our discovery cohort.

Quantification of parasite proliferation

Synchronized asexual parasites were plated in triplicate into 24-well plates at ~ 1% parasitemia (5% hematocrit) in the following prepared media: complete RPMI medium, CM; CM (-AlbuMAX) supplemented with 18 µM of PC (18:2/18:2). CM, CM (-AlbuMAX) and CM (-AlbuMAX) supplemented with equal volume of ethanol/water used to dissolve the phospholipid into the media were used as controls. Total parasitemia was calculated using microscopic counts of thin smears of erythrocyte samples fixed with methanol, stained with Giemsa, and microscopically evaluated by standard methods [93]. Parasitized RBCs were counted among 1,000 RBCs on the thin smear and parasite load expressed as % parasitemia “(parasitized RBCs/total RBCs) × 100”. Differences in the fold change of % parasitemia between day 1 and day 6 of the experiment were evaluated using paired t-test in GraphPad Prism v8.0. Microscopic counts of parasitized RBCs are provided in Additional file 4: Table S13.
