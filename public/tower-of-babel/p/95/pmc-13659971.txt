Legal and ethical responsibility for AI-assisted diagnostic and referral errors: a scoping review of comparative approaches with implications for Saudi healthcare
Authors: Alanwar Obaid Almutairi, Alanoud Sajda Owad Almutairi, Faisal Sajda Owad Almutairi, Majid Ali, Long Ming
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArtificial intelligence (AI) systems are increasingly embedded in clinical diagnostics and referral pathways, yet when these tools contribute to patient harm, traditional medico-legal doctrines are strained by algorithmic opacity, automation bias, and distributed decision-making. Beyond legal uncertainty, AI integration raises fundamental ethical concerns regarding clinician moral agency, patient autonomy, and distributive justice. This scoping review maps how jurisdictions worldwide allocate legal and ethical responsibility for AI-assisted diagnostic and referral errors, identifies governance mechanisms that support accountability, and examines the implications for Saudi Arabia’s healthcare system under Vision 2030.MethodsFollowing the Arksey and O’Malley framework and PRISMA-ScR reporting guidelines, literature was identified through systematic searches of MEDLINE (via PubMed), Scopus, Web of Science, and CINAHL supplemented by targeted grey-literature retrieval from the World Health Organization, the European Commission, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration, and Gulf Cooperation Council regulatory sources. Sources were included if they substantively addressed legal liability, ethical accountability, or regulatory frameworks related to AI-assisted diagnostics or referrals. After screening and deduplication, 77 records were included. Data were extracted across eight thematic domains and analysed through both legal-doctrinal and ethical lenses informed by the principlist framework (autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice).ResultsFour dominant liability models were identified: negligence-based (27 studies), strict product liability (16), fault-based (12), and hybrid/tiered (11), with the remaining 11 records examining multiple or unspecified frameworks. Across all frameworks, clinicians bore disproportionate responsibility despite limited control over algorithmic processes, creating a “liability sink” phenomenon with significant ethical implications for moral distress and professional integrity. Only two studies directly addressed Saudi Arabia, revealing that 89% of clinicians lacked AI-related legal training, and consent forms failed to disclose AI involvement. Ethical analysis exposed tensions between innovation-driven policy goals and the principles of patient autonomy, non-maleficence, and justice.ConclusionsEffective AI governance is likely to depend on integrating legal and ethical frameworks. For Saudi Arabia, the limited jurisdiction-specific evidence supports the provisional development of a contextualised shared-responsibility model that could classify AI by clinical risk, draw on principlist ethical standards in regulatory design, address the moral dimensions of the liability sink, and align with Vision 2030’s innovation objectives and Islamic bioethical values. Background

Artificial intelligence (AI), frequently implemented through machine-learning-based clinical decision-support systems, has been promoted as a tool to improve diagnostic accuracy, reduce perceptual and interpretive errors, and increase clinical efficiency across multiple health data modalities, such as medical imaging and electronic health records (EHRs) [1, 2]. Despite these potential benefits, diagnostic errors and information technology-related mistakes remain persistent patient safety concerns across healthcare settings. Consequently, any technological innovation introduced into diagnostic or referral processes must be assessed not only for performance but also for its implications for patient safety in real clinical settings [1, 3]. Within traditional medical malpractice law, liability is typically determined through four elements: duty of care, breach of the professional standard of care, causation, and damages. The integration of AI into diagnostic or referral decision-making, therefore, raises fundamental legal questions regarding how “reasonable professional care” should be interpreted when clinicians rely on, override, or operationalise algorithmic recommendations [4, 5].

A central governance issue is the attribution of legal responsibility when AI contributes to harmful diagnostic or referral decisions. The difficulty is not merely that errors may occur. AI introduces new causal pathways that complicate liability attribution, including algorithmic opacity (“black-box” decision processes), data-dependent performance drift, automation bias among clinicians, and failures within the socio-technical systems in which AI is deployed [6–8]. As a result, policymakers and regulators across different jurisdictions have begun to formulate approaches through legislation, regulatory guidance, professional standards, and policy frameworks. These approaches address three core questions: the legal classification of diagnostic AI systems, the allocation of responsibility among clinicians, healthcare institutions, and technology developers, and the evidentiary and governance mechanisms necessary to operationalise accountability [2, 6, 9]. Nevertheless, much of the literature highlights that many jurisdictions still lack AI-specific health-law provisions. Consequently, courts and regulators frequently rely on general medical negligence and product liability doctrines originally designed for conventional medical technologies rather than adaptive algorithmic systems [10–17].

Importantly, the challenges posed by AI in healthcare extend beyond legal doctrine into the domain of biomedical ethics. The four principles of biomedical ethics (autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, and justice) [18] are each implicated when opaque algorithmic systems influence clinical decisions that affect patients’ bodies, health, and well-being. Patient autonomy is compromised when neither clinicians nor patients can understand how AI-generated recommendations are reached. The principle of non-maleficence is challenged when foreseeable algorithmic harms persist due to inadequate validation or biased training data. Beneficence supports AI adoption when it demonstrably improves outcomes, yet overly cautious legal frameworks may prevent beneficial technologies from reaching patients. And justice demands equitable distribution of both AI’s benefits and its risks across populations, including attention to algorithmic bias that disproportionately affects marginalised groups. An integrated legal-ethical analysis is, therefore, essential for developing governance frameworks that are not only legally robust but also morally defensible.

Among the most consequential governance challenges arising from the convergence of these legal and ethical concerns is what recent scholarship has formalised as the “liability sink”. We adopt this concept throughout the present review and define it as follows: a structural arrangement in which clinicians bear disproportionate legal and moral responsibility for AI-influenced clinical decisions despite having limited understanding of or control over the algorithmic processes that generated those decisions. The liability sink reflects a mismatch between where causal contribution to harm originates (across developers, institutions, and deployment contexts) and where legal accountability ultimately settles (on the individual clinician). It is associated with moral distress, defensive practice, and professional burnout. The concept is referenced throughout the manuscript without further redefinition. The liability sink has implications across three dimensions. Legally, causation analysis must extend beyond individual physician conduct to encompass distributed responsibility among developers, institutions, and clinicians. Ethically, it positions the clinician’s moral agency as a protected interest alongside patient autonomy and welfare. Organisationally, it places positive duties on institutions to provide the technical infrastructure, training, and governance processes needed to support clinicians who use AI tools.

This review is motivated by a translational policy question: how can insights derived from national legal documents and policy guidance regarding responsibility for AI-assisted diagnostic and referral errors be systematically mapped and adapted to the Saudi healthcare context in a legally feasible, ethically grounded, and socially legitimate manner? Saudi Arabia’s Vision 2030 positions AI as a strategic priority for healthcare transformation [19, 20], yet rapid adoption without adequate legal and ethical frameworks risks patient harm, clinician moral distress, and erosion of healthcare trust. Comparative research on medical liability indicates that responsibility frameworks are shaped by jurisdiction-specific cultural, social, political, and legal factors, including religious and institutional influences. As a result, regulatory models cannot simply be transferred between countries without contextual modification [13]. At the same time, normative analyses of AI governance emphasise that reforms related to liability, product regulation, and professional oversight should be embedded within coherent policy frameworks that align with broader goals such as data protection, patient rights, and healthcare system safety [6, 8, 14].

Although existing scholarship provides valuable jurisdiction-specific insights into the allocation of responsibility for AI-related medical harm, the literature also reveals significant fragmentation. Many analyses examine either professional negligence or product liability frameworks in isolation, while emerging AI-supported referral workflows, particularly those embedded in telemedicine systems, introduce additional actors and data governance considerations that may not be fully captured by traditional malpractice frameworks [6, 9, 11]. Moreover, comparative medical liability research emphasises that legal responsibility structures are deeply embedded in broader legal cultures and institutional arrangements, meaning that regulatory approaches cannot be simply replicated across jurisdictions without contextual adaptation [12, 13].

The ethical dimensions of AI liability have received insufficient integrated analysis. Most studies examine legal and ethical questions in isolation, and few connect liability frameworks to principlist ethical reasoning. The gap is especially acute in the Saudi context, where Vision 2030’s digital transformation agenda [19, 20] intersects with an evolving regulatory landscape and a healthcare workforce that lacks formal legal training on AI.

In response to these gaps, the present scoping review maps how different jurisdictions allocate legal and ethical responsibility for diagnostic and referral errors associated with AI systems. Building on these comparative insights, the review proposes a translational framework tailored to the Saudi context. The framework integrates three elements: classification of AI tools by clinical role and level of autonomy; allocation of responsibilities among clinicians, healthcare institutions, and technology developers, informed by principlist ethical reasoning; and the establishment of governance mechanisms (auditability, professional training, and post-deployment monitoring) that enable attribution of responsibility. Together, these elements aim to support patient safety, clinician wellbeing, and rights-oriented healthcare governance [2, 6, 8, 16].

Methods

Study design and framework

This scoping review follows the methodological framework proposed by Arksey and O’Malley [21] as refined by Levac et al. [22], and reports findings in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [23]. The scoping review design was selected because the research question spans multiple disciplines (health law, bioethics, regulatory science, clinical informatics), the relevant literature encompasses heterogeneous study types, and the objective is to map the breadth of available evidence rather than synthesise effect sizes [21].

Research questions

The review was guided by four research questions: (1) How do different jurisdictions allocate legal responsibility for AI-assisted diagnostic and referral errors among clinicians, healthcare institutions, and technology developers? (2) What ethical frameworks and principles are invoked or implicated in these allocation decisions? (3) What governance mechanisms are proposed or implemented to support accountability? (4) What are the implications for Saudi Arabia’s healthcare system?

Identifying relevant studies

A multi-database search strategy was employed to identify relevant literature. We systematically searched MEDLINE (via PubMed), Scopus, Web of Science, and CINAHL from inception to January 2026 using a combination of controlled vocabulary and free-text terms related to artificial intelligence, diagnostic and referral errors, medical liability, legal responsibility, ethical accountability, and the Saudi healthcare context. To capture recent regulatory developments and grey literature, we additionally hand-searched the websites of major health and regulatory bodies (including the World Health Organization, the European Commission, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration, and Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) regulatory authorities) and performed backward and forward citation chaining of key articles and reviews. A complete search strategy is presented in the supplementary file accompanying this paper.

Study selection

Sources were included if they substantively addressed: (a) legal liability, regulatory frameworks, or professional responsibility related to AI-assisted diagnostics or referrals in healthcare; or (b) ethical analysis of accountability, autonomy, justice, or professional integrity in the context of AI-assisted clinical decisions. Sources were excluded if they focused solely on algorithm performance without legal, ethical, or governance implications; addressed only surgical robotics or administrative AI; or were opinion pieces without analytical grounding. After screening at the abstract and full-text levels and removal of duplicates across platforms, 77 unique sources were retained. The screening process is presented in the Appendix (PRISMA flow diagram).

Charting the data

Data were extracted across eight thematic domains: AI error types, liability frameworks, stakeholder responsibilities, jurisdictional approaches, Saudi healthcare context, legal gaps, proposed solutions, and healthcare impact. An ethical analysis layer was applied to each domain, examining findings through the principlist framework of autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, and justice [18], supplemented by attention to clinician moral agency and professional integrity.

Collating, summarising, and reporting results

Findings were synthesised thematically and compared across five jurisdictional groupings: the European Union, the United States, Asia, the Middle East and GCC, and Africa. Ethical dimensions were analysed as a cross-cutting theme throughout the results, with emphasis on integrating ethical reasoning into the analysis.

Results

Characteristics of included studies

The final synthesis draws on 77 [6, 20, 24–98] records examining legal and ethical responsibility for AI-assisted diagnostic and referral errors across diverse jurisdictions and legal traditions (Table 1). The majority employed legal analysis methodologies (74.0%), with smaller contributions from systematic reviews, empirical studies, comparative analyses, and policy evaluations. Geographic distribution showed concentrations in the European Union (22 studies), Asia (16), the United States (13), and Africa (12). Notably, only two studies directly addressed the Saudi Arabian legal context [44, 45], with an additional five addressing the broader GCC or Middle Eastern region [24, 59, 81, 82, 86], highlighting a critical gap in jurisdiction-specific scholarship. Publication dates ranged from 2008 to 2026, with over 80% published between 2022 and 2026.

The predominance of legal analysis (57 of 77 studies, 74.0%) reflects an important feature of this evidence base. Legal scholarship is largely normative. It analyses what existing law requires, identifies doctrinal gaps, and argues for how reformed law should look. Empirical evidence on real-world clinical outcomes, patient experiences, or the practical effects of liability frameworks is comparatively limited in this literature. Readers should, therefore, interpret the findings of this review primarily as a map of the dominant legal and ethical arguments in the field. They should not interpret them as a synthesis of empirical evidence on what AI liability frameworks actually achieve in clinical practice. The 20 included studies with empirical, comparative, or systematic review components (constituting 26.0% of the synthesis) are noted where relevant throughout the Results section.

Types of AI-assisted diagnostic and referral errors

Diagnostic errors included misdiagnoses due to algorithmic bias [26, 37], missed diagnoses when AI failed to detect abnormalities [27, 38], and delayed diagnoses from overreliance on AI outputs [33, 80]. Referral errors manifested as inappropriate or missed referrals based on algorithmic predictions [27, 30, 54, 80]. System-level failures included algorithmic bias affecting minority groups, older patients, and women [31, 33, 34, 36, 37, 46, 48, 78, 87, 91]; black-box decision-making complicating error identification [27–29, 31, 32, 35, 39–43, 46, 53, 55, 73, 77]; and unrepresentative training data perpetuating healthcare disparities [26, 28, 30, 31, 34, 35, 42, 43, 50, 52, 55, 63, 78, 87, 95]. Verification errors (clinicians failing to override incorrect AI outputs) and supervision errors (inadequate monitoring of autonomous systems) were also identified [91, 96, 97].

From an ethical standpoint, algorithmic bias violates the principle of justice by systematically disadvantaging marginalised populations. Black-box opacity undermines autonomy by preventing meaningful understanding of consequential decisions. The persistence of known error patterns in deployed systems raises questions about non-maleficence, whether stakeholders are discharging the duty to avoid foreseeable harm by permitting the deployment of inadequately validated systems.

Legal frameworks for AI liability

Negligence-based liability remained the most common approach [26–30, 33, 36–39, 41, 43, 46, 47, 52, 56, 59, 65, 67, 73, 75, 84, 85, 90]. Strict product liability, particularly in European contexts, treated AI as medical devices [26, 30, 31, 35, 40, 42, 51, 53, 57, 63, 69, 70, 76, 95]. Fault-based systems shifted the burden of proof in civil-law jurisdictions [29, 32, 33, 40, 51, 61, 62, 71, 82, 89]. Hybrid models emerged in Indonesia (three-tiered) [35], China (four-level) [33, 71], and the EU AI Act [27, 74, 93]. Establishing causation proved formidable across all frameworks [26, 31, 35, 42, 47, 63]. Modified approaches included rebuttable presumptions [68, 70], burden-shifting [32], and prima facie evidence [54]. Alternative compensation included no-fault schemes [35, 48, 49], risk-pooling [29, 51, 76], and mandatory insurance [40, 75, 82] (Table 2).

Stakeholder responsibilities and the “liability sink” problem

Physicians bore primary responsibility across jurisdictions [26–34, 36–41, 43–45, 50–53, 58, 59, 61, 64–67, 71, 73, 75, 76, 79, 85, 89]. Developers faced liability through product liability for design defects [26, 29–31, 33, 34, 37, 43, 46, 50, 52, 58, 59, 61, 64–67, 73, 76]. Institutions bore responsibility for implementation and supervision [20, 25, 30, 35, 39, 49, 51, 64, 83, 94].

The liability sink phenomenon (defined in the Background) was consistently observed across the four dominant frameworks. Physicians faced liability for both following erroneous AI advice and deviating from recommendations without justification [26, 29, 30, 39, 48, 51, 56, 67, 82, 84, 90]. This double bind characterised the included studies regardless of whether the underlying liability regime was negligence-based, strict liability, fault-based, or hybrid. The ethical implications of this pattern are examined in the Discussion.

Informed consent and patient autonomy

Informed consent requirements increasingly encompassed AI-specific disclosures [31, 33, 38, 44, 50, 58, 60, 64–67, 73, 74, 85, 91], yet black-box opacity complicated these processes [29, 38, 58, 73, 78, 85]. In Saudi Arabia, consent protocols do not include AI-specific disclosures [44]. The principle of autonomy requires that patients make informed decisions based on an adequate understanding. When AI contributes to reasoning through processes that neither clinicians nor patients can interpret, the epistemic conditions for meaningful consent are eroded, transforming the consent process from a genuine exercise of self-determination into a formality.

Legal and ethical gaps

Six categories of inadequacy were identified: (1) inadequacy of malpractice doctrines for AI [28, 39, 40, 50, 52, 54, 62, 65, 72, 90]; (2) algorithmic opacity [27, 29, 31, 32, 35, 38–43, 46, 48, 53, 55, 58, 60, 62, 63, 72, 77, 84, 89]; (3) undefined liability allocation [26, 27, 36, 37, 43, 58–60, 73, 83]; (4) informed consent deficiencies [33, 38, 43, 44, 60, 73, 80, 85, 91]; (5) regulatory fragmentation [26, 27, 40, 42, 49, 53, 57, 63, 73, 74]; and (6) clinician preparedness gaps [34, 44]. These converge to create an “accountability vacuum,” which is a governance environment in which patients harmed by AI have no effective path to redress, undermining both the principle of justice and the broader social contract between healthcare systems and the populations they serve [81]. Alignment with international standards for trustworthy AI [98] and emerging medico-legal methodologies [25] may help address these gaps, though significant jurisdictional adaptation remains necessary.

Healthcare impact of legal and ethical uncertainty

Patient safety was undermined by both delayed adoption and premature deployment of inadequately validated systems [28, 29, 35–37, 41, 44, 48, 55, 57, 63, 65, 75, 88]. Clinician behaviour was distorted by defensive medicine, skill degradation, and moral distress associated with the liability sink [28, 30, 43, 51, 56, 57, 63, 65, 90, 91]. Innovation was hindered by regulatory ambiguity and market fragmentation [27, 37, 41, 46, 49, 55, 58, 66, 72, 73, 85, 86]. Equity of access was compromised as liability concerns disproportionately delayed AI adoption in under-resourced settings [27, 44, 55, 63, 81, 88], directly violating the justice principle and having implications for sustainable development goals in healthcare [81].

Sensitivity analysis: empirical-only subset

To test whether the review’s main findings depend on the inclusion of normative legal scholarship, we conducted a sensitivity analysis. We re-examined the synthesis after restricting the evidence base to the 20 studies (26.0%) that contained empirical, comparative, or systematic review components. These included the empirical Saudi study by Alanazi [44], the regional comparative analysis by Solaiman et al. [45], the systematic and scoping reviews by Aldhafeeri [27], Cestonaro et al. [43], Oliva et al. [57], and Čartolovni et al. [58], the empirical experimental work by Bahner et al. [96] and Wang et al. [97], and the policy-analytic and economic studies cited throughout.

Three observations emerged from this sensitivity analysis. First, the identification of four dominant liability frameworks (negligence-based, strict product liability, fault-based, and hybrid or tiered) remained well-supported, as these frameworks are described in the systematic reviews [27, 43, 57, 58]. Second, the liability sink phenomenon and its associated moral distress are supported by empirical work [44, 96, 97] in addition to the seminal normative analysis by Lawton et al. [51]. The conclusion that this is an ethical concern rather than a purely doctrinal one, therefore, does not depend on normative scholarship alone. Third, however, the Saudi-specific findings narrow substantially. They reduce to a single national mixed-methods study [44] and one regional governance proposal [45]. This finding strengthens, rather than undermines, the call for Saudi-focused empirical research that we have placed in the Implications for Saudi Arabia subsection. The recommendations for Saudi reform should, therefore, be regarded as informed by a primarily normative literature. They should be tested empirically before formal implementation.

Discussion

This scoping review mapped the legal and ethical landscape governing responsibility for AI-assisted diagnostic and referral errors across multiple jurisdictions. The findings reveal a fragmented yet converging global discourse. Four dominant liability models were identified: negligence-based, strict product liability, fault-based, and hybrid or tiered frameworks, each with distinct advantages and limitations when applied to algorithmic clinical decision-making. A consistent finding across all jurisdictional groupings was the operation of the liability sink, regardless of the underlying liability regime. The review further exposed critical gaps in the Saudi Arabian context, where clinician preparedness for AI-related legal and ethical challenges remains markedly low and informed consent protocols do not account for AI involvement in clinical decisions [44]. Ethical analysis through the principlist framework [18] revealed systemic tensions between innovation-driven policy objectives and the foundational principles of autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, and justice. These findings collectively underscore the urgent need for integrated legal and ethical governance frameworks that distribute accountability proportionately across all stakeholders in the AI deployment chain.

Liability frameworks and their limitations

The predominance of negligence-based liability identified in this review aligns with earlier analyses by Price et al. [30, 56] and Sullivan and Schweikart [39], who argued that traditional tort doctrines remain the default mechanism for adjudicating AI-related clinical harm in common-law jurisdictions. However, the present findings extend this body of work by demonstrating that negligence-based approaches are not merely suboptimal but fundamentally misaligned with the distributed causality inherent in AI-assisted clinical workflows. When diagnostic reasoning is mediated by algorithmic processes that neither the clinician nor the patient can fully interpret, the conventional standard of “reasonable professional care” becomes conceptually unstable [28, 65]. Froomkin et al. [28, 72] cautioned that tort-induced overreliance on AI could paradoxically increase harm, a concern substantiated by the automation bias literature reviewed here [91, 97]. The strict product liability frameworks favoured in European jurisdictions [26, 42, 53, 70] offer an alternative by shifting focus from clinician conduct to system design, yet they encounter difficulties when AI systems are classified as services rather than products, or when continuous learning algorithms blur the boundary between manufacturing defects and emergent behaviour [57, 77]. The hybrid and tiered models emerging in the EU AI Act [27, 74, 93], China [33, 71], and Indonesia [35] represent promising attempts to calibrate regulatory intensity to clinical risk, though their effectiveness remains empirically untested.

The liability sink and clinician moral distress

The liability sink is the most ethically consequential pattern documented in this review, and its analytical force derives from how it reframes ethical responsibility under conditions of distributed cognition. From a non-maleficence perspective, concentrating liability on clinicians who cannot interrogate algorithmic outputs exposes them to moral injury, a form of distress that is qualitatively distinct from conventional clinical uncertainty [29, 53]. From a justice perspective, the distribution is inequitable when developers and institutions that design, validate, and deploy these systems escape proportionate accountability [34, 36, 43, 58, 59]. Smith and Fotheringham [29, 53] noted that existing remedies in English law are inadequate to address harms arising from defective AI aids, and Maliha et al. [41] emphasised the need to balance safety and innovation without placing clinicians in untenable positions. Addressing the liability sink, therefore, requires both legal redistribution of responsibility and the creation of epistemic conditions for responsible clinical judgment. These conditions include transparency requirements, interpretability standards, and institutional support [27, 46, 47, 61, 74, 85, 98].

Informed consent and patient autonomy

The finding that informed consent protocols across jurisdictions, and particularly in Saudi Arabia [44], fail to account for AI involvement in clinical reasoning resonates with concerns raised by Zhang [33], Wałdoch [91], and Cartolovni et al. [58]. Meaningful informed consent presupposes that patients can understand the basis on which clinical decisions affecting their health are made. When AI systems contribute to diagnostic or referral reasoning through processes that are opaque even to the treating clinician, the epistemic foundation of consent is fundamentally compromised. This is not merely a procedural deficiency but a substantive threat to patient autonomy as articulated in the principlist tradition [18]. The challenge is compounded in contexts where algorithmic bias may systematically disadvantage certain patient populations [31, 33, 34, 36, 37, 46, 48, 78, 87, 91], raising justice concerns that intersect with autonomy in complex ways. Bolden [36] and Mensah et al. [37, 46, 47] have documented how biased training data can produce discriminatory diagnostic outputs, effectively denying equitable care to marginalised groups without their knowledge or consent. The development of AI-specific consent frameworks that communicate the nature, limitations, and potential biases of algorithmic involvement in clinically meaningful terms represents an urgent governance priority.

Comparative jurisdictional approaches

The comparative analysis revealed a spectrum of regulatory philosophies ranging from the EU’s precautionary, rights-centred approach to the US market-driven model, with Middle Eastern, Asian, and African jurisdictions occupying various intermediate positions. The EU’s comprehensive framework, anchored by the AI Act, the Medical Device Regulation, and the General Data Protection Regulation, represents the most developed attempt to integrate risk classification, transparency requirements, and accountability mechanisms [26, 27, 34, 35, 40, 42, 45, 46, 53, 57, 63, 66, 68, 69, 72, 74, 76, 77, 93]. However, as Duffourc and Gerke [77] observed, even the proposed EU directives leave significant gaps in liability allocation. The US approach, while fostering innovation through the FDA’s Software as a Medical Device framework, relies heavily on post-market tort litigation to address harms, an approach that Price et al. [30, 56, 65] and others have shown to be reactive rather than preventive. African jurisdictions face the most acute governance challenges, with legislative frameworks largely unprepared for AI-assisted clinical decision-making [6, 37, 46, 47, 52, 55, 63, 79, 80, 87, 88]. The emerging international consensus represented by the FUTURE-AI framework [98], which emphasises fairness, universality, traceability, usability, robustness, and explainability, provides a valuable reference point for harmonisation, though its translation into binding legal obligations remains incomplete across all regions.

Weighing principlist tensions in the Saudi context

The principlist framework provides a useful organising structure for ethical analysis of AI in healthcare, but it is most rigorous when its principles are interrogated against counterarguments and against one another rather than asserted in isolation. Three specific tensions warrant explicit engagement.

Autonomy and the comparative-explainability counterargument

Throughout this review, we have argued that algorithmic opacity threatens informed consent and patient autonomy. A reasonable counterargument deserves attention: patients rarely understand the full mechanistic reasoning behind conventional clinical decisions either. A patient consenting to chemotherapy seldom understands the molecular pharmacology of the agents involved; a patient consenting to surgery seldom understands the surgeon’s intraoperative decision-making heuristics. On this view, the autonomy concern with AI may be overstated, since informed consent has always operated at a level of abstraction rather than full mechanistic transparency.

We acknowledge the force of this counterargument but suggest it is incomplete for three reasons. First, the standard of consent is not a full mechanistic understanding but the disclosure of material information that a reasonable patient would consider relevant. The involvement of an AI system, the population on which it was trained, the existence of known performance disparities, and the option to receive non-AI-mediated care are all plausibly material in a way that the precise pharmacokinetics of a chemotherapeutic agent are not. Second, the conventional clinician is, in principle, accountable, open to questioning, and capable of explaining their reasoning when challenged; an opaque AI system is not. Third, the comparative-explainability argument can justify minimal disclosure rather than no disclosure: if anything, it suggests that consent processes for AI should match the level of meaningful explanation already expected in conventional care, not fall below it. The autonomy concern is, therefore, best understood not as absolute but as a comparative and procedural one, i.e., AI integration should not regress consent practices below their existing standard.

Justice and specifying who is affected in the Saudi context

The general claim that algorithmic bias disproportionately affects marginalised populations is well established in the international literature [34, 36, 46, 48, 78, 87, 91]. In the Saudi context, however, this generic framing requires specification. Several populations are plausibly more vulnerable to algorithmic bias in AI-assisted diagnosis and referral. Expatriate workers, who comprise a substantial share of the Saudi workforce and a highly diverse patient population spanning South Asia, Southeast Asia, North Africa, and the Levant, are likely underrepresented in the training datasets used to develop imported AI systems, which are primarily trained on North American and European populations. Rural and Bedouin populations in regions such as Najran, Jazan, Al-Jouf, and the Empty Quarter face both reduced access to AI-equipped tertiary centres and the possibility that AI tools deployed there were validated on metropolitan populations with different epidemiological and demographic profiles. Female patients, particularly in conservative settings where same-gender clinician preferences shape consultation patterns, may be affected differentially if AI systems were trained on data reflecting different gender-distribution patterns in care-seeking behaviour. Older Saudi patients with multimorbidity may be under-represented in training data dominated by younger working-age cohorts. Justice considerations, therefore, demand not only that AI systems be validated locally before deployment, but that local validation include explicit subgroup analysis across these populations. In the absence of such analysis, the equitable-access promise of Vision 2030 [19] risks being undermined by hidden distributive harms.

Beneficence and non-maleficence: the Vision 2030 dilemma

The most acute principlist tension in the Saudi context arises between beneficence and non-maleficence. Beneficence requires making beneficial AI technologies available to patients as rapidly as feasible. This obligation is reinforced by Vision 2030’s strategic positioning of AI as a catalyst for healthcare transformation [19, 20]. Non-maleficence, by contrast, requires avoiding foreseeable harm by ensuring adequate validation, transparency, and accountability before deployment. These principles can pull in opposite directions in practice. Slowing AI deployment to allow for rigorous local validation, robust consent procedures, and clinician training delays the benefits of AI for current patients (a beneficence cost) but reduces the risk of population-scale algorithmic harm and the moral hazard of the liability sink (a non-maleficence gain). Accelerating deployment in line with Vision 2030 timelines maximises beneficence under a particular operational assumption, that AI tools imported from international markets perform adequately in the Saudi population, but risks substantial non-maleficence costs if that assumption proves unwarranted.

Principlist analysis does not automatically adjudicate this tension, but it can help clarify what responsible governance might involve. One defensible orientation, offered here as a working proposition rather than a settled conclusion, would treat the beneficence–non-maleficence dilemma not as a binary choice between speed and safety but as a structured trade-off that could be managed through stratified deployment: rapid adoption may be justifiable for low-risk applications with strong international validation evidence; staged adoption with local validation could apply to higher-risk applications; and pilot-phase adoption with explicit informed consent and outcome surveillance might suit novel applications without local evidence. Regulatory sandboxes [24, 55] would be one possible institutional embodiment of this stratified logic. Justice considerations also suggest that the benefits and risks of each tier could be distributed equitably across the populations identified above, rather than concentrated geographically or demographically. The principlist framework, when properly applied, therefore, neither endorses unrestrained innovation nor demands precautionary paralysis; it requires a deliberate, justifiable, and population-sensitive calibration of the four principles against one another.

Beyond principlism

We acknowledge that the principlist framework, while widely used in biomedical ethics [18], is not exhaustive. Care ethics, virtue ethics, and Islamic jurisprudential reasoning each generate distinct considerations relevant to AI governance, for example, the Islamic legal maxim la darar wa la dirar (“no harm shall be inflicted or reciprocated”) reinforces non-maleficence but also imposes positive duties of istiḥsān (juristic preference) and the maqasid al-shariah (objectives of Islamic law) that do not map neatly onto principlist categories [45]. Future ethical analysis of Saudi AI governance would benefit from explicit engagement with these complementary frameworks alongside principlism.

Implications of the study

Implications for Saudi Arabia

For Saudi Arabia, the findings carry particular urgency given Vision 2030’s strategic positioning of AI as a catalyst for healthcare transformation [19, 20]. The near-absence of AI-specific civil liability provisions, combined with critically low levels of clinician legal literacy regarding AI [44], creates conditions in which rapid technology adoption may outpace the governance infrastructure necessary to protect patients and clinicians alike. The Saudi-specific empirical evidence base remains narrow, comprising primarily a single mixed-methods clinician survey [44] and one regional governance proposal that includes Saudi Arabia within a broader GCC framework [45]. Wider regional evidence (n = 5 studies) provides additional context but does not substitute for jurisdiction-specific empirical work [24, 59, 81, 82, 86]. The framework outlined below is, therefore, best understood as a working hypothesis to be tested, refined, and validated through dedicated Saudi-specific empirical research, rather than as a settled policy prescription.

Within these constraints, a contextualised shared-responsibility framework for Saudi Arabia could provisionally consider several elements: risk-based classification of AI systems aligned with the SFDA’s existing medical device regulatory architecture; AI-specific informed consent requirements that respect both principlist ethics and Islamic bioethical values [45]; structured professional training programmes designed to build critical AI literacy among clinicians; institutional governance mechanisms such as AI oversight committees and local validation protocols; and adaptive regulatory instruments such as regulatory sandboxes that permit controlled innovation while maintaining safety oversight [24, 35, 45, 55]. Each of these elements draws on emerging international experience and would benefit from empirical validation in the Saudi context before formal implementation. Implementing any of these proposals in their current form risks outpacing the available evidence. Several lines of Saudi-specific empirical research are, therefore, best understood as prerequisites rather than as supplements to policy development. These include: (i) larger-scale, multi-centre surveys of clinician knowledge, attitudes, and training needs regarding AI legal and ethical responsibility, extending beyond the single-institution evidence currently available; (ii) qualitative studies of patient understanding and acceptance of AI-specific consent processes within the Saudi cultural and religious context; (iii) audit studies of current institutional governance practices for AI deployment across Ministry of Health, military medical services, and private-sector providers; (iv) prospective evaluation of pilot regulatory sandboxes and AI-specific consent protocols before national scale-up; and (v) jurisprudential analysis of how Saudi courts and the Sharia-informed civil-liability tradition currently address algorithmic harm in the absence of AI-specific legislation. Without such empirical groundwork, well-intentioned legal reform risks producing rules that are poorly calibrated to clinical realities, professional expectations, or patient values in the Kingdom.

Implications for the Gulf Cooperation Council

At the GCC level, the findings are consistent with the development of harmonised regional governance standards that could leverage member states’ comparative advantages while addressing shared challenges. Solaiman et al. [45] have proposed a “true lifecycle approach” to AI governance that positions the GCC as a potential model for global governance, and the present findings provide tentative support for the feasibility of this approach. Regional coordination on AI classification standards, mutual recognition of conformity assessments, shared post-market surveillance infrastructure, and harmonised training requirements may, in principle, help accelerate the development of robust governance frameworks while limiting regulatory fragmentation that could impede cross-border innovation and care delivery [24, 81, 82, 86]. These regional implications, like their national counterparts, would benefit from dedicated empirical investigation before being translated into binding policy instruments.

Global implications

Globally, this review points toward a possible paradigm shift from individual-focused liability models to systemic accountability frameworks that reflect the distributed nature of AI-related clinical harm. The apparent convergence across jurisdictions toward risk-based classification, shared responsibility, and transparency requirements suggests an emerging international discourse that could, in time, inform the development of binding international standards. Integrating ethical reasoning into the design of legal governance, rather than treating ethics and law as parallel but separate domains, would represent a meaningful advancement. The FUTURE-AI principles [98] and emerging medico-legal methodologies [25] offer promising foundations for such integration, though the empirical case for any particular configuration remains to be developed.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. As a scoping review, this study prioritised breadth of coverage over depth of quality appraisal, and no formal risk-of-bias assessment was conducted for individual studies. A notable methodological limitation is the predominance of legal-analytical and doctrinal studies among the included sources, with relatively few empirical investigations of real-world clinical or patient-level outcomes. Consequently, the practical effectiveness of the liability frameworks and governance mechanisms identified in this review remains largely untested in clinical settings. The sensitivity analysis presented in the Results confirms that the main conceptual findings of this review (the four dominant frameworks and the liability sink phenomenon) are not solely artefacts of the normative literature, but also that Saudi-specific recommendations rest on a thin empirical base. A further important limitation concerns the language scope of the search. The reliance on English-language sources and machine-translated materials may have introduced selection bias, potentially excluding relevant scholarship published in Arabic, Mandarin, or other languages. The principlist ethical framework, while widely recognised and applied in biomedical ethics [18], does not exhaust the range of relevant moral perspectives, and alternative frameworks such as care ethics, virtue ethics, or Islamic jurisprudential reasoning may yield additional insights. Furthermore, the rapid pace of regulatory development in AI governance means that some findings may have been superseded by legislative or policy changes occurring after the search period. Future research should prioritise empirical studies that examine the real-world impact of AI liability frameworks on clinical practice, patient outcomes, and innovation trajectories, with particular attention to the Saudi and broader GCC contexts, where jurisdiction-specific evidence remains scarce.

Conclusions

This scoping review demonstrates that legal and ethical responsibility for AI-assisted diagnostic and referral errors remains inadequately addressed across jurisdictions. Existing frameworks struggle to accommodate the distributed causality, algorithmic opacity, and automation bias that characterise AI-mediated clinical decision-making. The liability sink emerges from this review not as a peripheral concern but as the central governance challenge requiring concerted attention from legislators, regulators, professional bodies, and healthcare institutions in any jurisdiction adopting AI-assisted diagnostic and referral systems. The comparative analysis reveals a global trajectory toward risk-based classification, shared responsibility, and transparency mandates, although significant gaps persist in translating these principles into operational governance mechanisms. Realising the promise of Vision 2030 in Saudi Arabia, and analogous innovation agendas in other jurisdictions, may depend on building governance infrastructure that protects patients, supports clinicians, and integrates legal and ethical reasoning from the outset; the specific shape of such infrastructure remains an open empirical and policy question.
