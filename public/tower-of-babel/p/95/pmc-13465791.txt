Single-nucleus transcriptomics dissects beef quality variation: coordinated reprogramming of myofiber metabolism, FAPs Fate, and ECM–vascular signaling
Authors: Jiawei Du, Juntao Guo, Tongfei He, Ni Gao, Haobin Ma, Hafiz Qadeer Ahmed, Huaxuan Li, Huabin Zhu, Linsen Zan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBeef quality traits significantly varies among cattle breeds. These differences cannot be fully explained using traditional omics techniques as they lack cell-level detail. To address this gap, we integrated phenotypic data, histological analyses, and single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) of the longissimus dorsi muscle to uncover the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying breed-specific variation.ResultsHistology and molecular markers indicated that Wagyu (WY) had the highest intramuscular fat(IMF) and the lowest shear force followed by Angus (AN) with the intermediate while Qinchuan cattle (QC) showed the lowest levels. SnRNA-seq resolved 12 cell types, revealing breed-specific cellular compositions. Pseudotime analysis revealed myoblast differentiation through hybrid states to type IIX (IIX), type IIA (IIA), and type I (I) myofibers, with lineage-specific remodeling of glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation, and Ca²⁺ handling directing QC toward IIX fibers and WY toward Type I fibers. Reclustering of PDGFRα⁺ fibro-adipogenic progenitors (FAPs) revealed a bifurcation between adipogenic and fibrogenic fates, with WY favoring lipid-metabolism and adipogenesis-enriched FAPs alongside stronger FGF/ANGPTL–integrin signaling, while QC showed extracellular matrix (ECM)–integrin and TGF-β/TNFα–NF-κB pathways. Ligand–receptor analysis indicated that, in contrast to QC’s adhesion-centric ECM axis, WY-specific laminin/collagen–integrin and IGF/ANGPT inputs enhance metabolic–mechanical coupling. These results suggested that breed-dependent phenotypes arise from coordinated shifts in FAP fate, myofiber metabolism, and ECM–vascular signaling.ConclusionBeef-quality variation reflects coordinated reprogramming along three axes: myofiber metabolic programs, FAP fate specification, and basement-membrane/integrin–vascular coupling, forming a phenotypic gradient from WY, which is pro-adipogenic and anti-fibrotic, through AN, which is intermediate, to QC, which is pro-fibrotic with high stiffness.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12932-3.Keywords: Meat quality, snRNA-seq, FAPs, Muscle fiber composition, Myofiber metabolism

Introduction

Beef quality is a major factor of value and market competitiveness in the beef industry [1, 2]. It is influenced by genetic background, nutrition, and management. The longissimus dorsi (LD) muscle is commonly used for commercial evaluation to measure beef quality and traits. Intramuscular fat, Shear force, water-holding capacity, tenderness, juiciness, and flavor, are major selection targets in beef breeding [3].

Breed differences in these traits suggest that there is complex molecular regulation and micro-environmental influences. WY exhibit high IMF in the LD with fine, uniformly distributed marbling, with rich flavor and texture [4, 5]. Angus has vigorous muscle development with moderate fat deposition and well known in global markets [6]. QC, a major indigenous Chinese breed with robust muscle growth and a unique genetic background, offer an excellent model for studying the mechanisms behind meat-quality traits [7].

While bulk RNA-seq, genomic selection, and QTL mapping have identified regulators of meat quality, these conventional methods cannot resolve functional partitioning and transcriptional heterogeneity among muscle cell types, thereby limiting our understanding of phenotypic variation [8, 9]. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) identifying all different cellular compositions, detecting rare cells population, and reconstructing cell-fate trajectories making it a powerful tool to study the micro-regulatory networks underlying complex traits [10].

In addition to myofibers, skeletal muscle contains satellite cells, FAPs, endothelial cells, immune cells, and other cell types [11, 12]. These cells collectively work to maintain myofiber homeostasis, supports regeneration/repair, and regulate lipid deposition, forming the cellular basis of meat-quality traits [13]. However, a clear framework linking single-cell data to meat-quality traits is still lacking. It is not yet well understood whether different breeds show consistent differences in cell composition, how such differences influence key biological pathways activities, and to what extent they mechanistically explain phenotypic variation.

Focusing on WA, AN, and QC, we quantified key meat-quality traits of the LD including shear force (SF), water-holding capacity (WHC), and IMF. We combined these measurements with high-throughput single-cell transcriptomics to construct breed-specific single-cell atlases. We further explored interbreed differences in cellular composition, functional subpopulations, key signaling pathways, and transcriptional regulatory networks, integrated these differences with phenotypes to explain meat-quality variation at both cellular and molecular levels. Our research explored the multilayer regulation of meat quality and suggests potential targets for molecular breeding and beef quality improvement.

Methods

All animal experiments strictly complied with institutional, national, and international guidelines for the care and use of animals in research. The experimental cattle were obtained from the breeding farm of Northwest A&F University. All procedures involving animals were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Northwest A&F University (Ethics Committee Name: Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Northwest A&F University; Approval Number: DK2021042).

For tissue collection, cattle were humanely slaughtered at the experimental abattoir of Northwest A&F University. Animals were individually restrained and rendered unconscious by captive-bolt stunning, followed immediately by exsanguination via severing of the carotid arteries and jugular veins. Loss of consciousness was verified by the absence of corneal reflex, rhythmic breathing, and vocalisation before bleeding. No pharmacological anaesthetics were used, in order to avoid potential effects of anaesthetic agents on post-mortem muscle metabolism and meat quality traits.

IMF content determination

IMF content was measured using Soxhlet extraction method based on the Chinese National Food Safety Standard of China GB 5009.6–2016 (Determination of Fat in Foods). Briefly, fascia and visible adipose tissue was removed from LD muscle then minced, and homogenized. About 3–5 g of the homogenate was weighed and subjected to extraction with petroleum ether until all fat was removed. The remaining contents was dried to constant weight, and IMF content was calculated as grams of fat per 100 g of fresh tissue. Each sample was analyzed twice, and the average value was used for further analysis.

Shear force measurement

Shear force was determined based on the Chinese agricultural industry standard NY/T 1180–2006 (Determination of Meat Tenderness—Shear Force Method). Fascia was removed from regularly shaped meat samples (≥ 6 cm × 3 cm × 3 cm; muscle fiber length ≥ 3 cm) and then sealed with self-sealing polyethylene bags, and cooked at 80 °C water bath until the core temperature reached 70 °C (measured with a probe thermometer, MITIR, Zhejiang, China). Samples were then cooled to room temperature and stored overnight at 4 °C. Cylindrical cores (diameter 1.27 cm, ≥ 6 per sample) were cut parallel to the orientation of muscle fibers and sheared across the fibers in perpendicular direction at a speed of 1 mm/s. The peak shear force (N) was recorded, and the average value was used for analysis.

Drip loss rate determination

Drip loss was calculated based on the Chinese agricultural industry standard NY/T 1333–2007 (Determination of Meat Quality of Livestock and Poultry). Rectangular meat samples (approximately 5 cm × 3 cm × 2 cm) were prepared, surface moisture was removed, and the initial weight (W₀) was recorded. Each sample was hung vertically along the muscle fibers orientation using fine iron wire. Samples were then placed in a sealed polyethylene bag without touching the bag, and stored at 4 °C for 48 h. After storage, surface moisture was blotted off and the final weight (W₄₈) was recorded. Drip loss (%) was calculated as: (W0 − W48)/W0 × 100%.

PH determination

Muscle pH was measured with a portable pH meter (Testo 205; Testo, Shanghai, China) at three different positions within each sample (approximately 2 cm deep), and then the average value was recorded. The instrument was calibrated before every measurement using pH 4.01 and 7.00 buffer solutions, with pH 10.01 buffer when needed.

Frozen sections and Oil Red O

Fresh tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, then immersed in 30% sucrose for dehydration, embedded in OCT compound, and cryosectioned at − 20 °C to a thickness of 8–10 μm. The sections were treated with 60% isopropanol, stained with Oil Red O working solution for 8–10 min, rinsed with tap water, counterstained with hematoxylin, and finally mounted using glycerol gelatin.

Paraffin sections and H&E, Masson, PAS

Tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h and processed through routine dehydration, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 5 μm thick sections. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was carried out by following the standard procedures. For collagen contents assessment, Masson’s trichrome staining was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (blue or green signal area or intensity). Glycogen contents were assessed Periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) staining, with hematoxylin used for nuclear staining.

Tissue section immunofluorescence

Frozen tissue sections were treated with citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for antigen retrieval when necessary, then blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with Primary antibodies against MYH2(HUABIO, HA721862, 1:200) and MYH7(Sangon Biotech, D121014, 1:100). After washing with PBS, fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies were applied for 1 h in the dark. Nuclei were then counterstained with DAPI.

Principal component analysis

Intramuscular fat, shear force, drip loss, and pH were standardized using z-scores and examined by principal component analysis (PCA) in R (version 4.x) using the prcomp function with scaling (scale = TRUE). The variance explained by each principal component and the loading values were calculated, and PCA score plots were generated to illustrate separation among breeds.

Pearson correlation analysis

For each sample, Pearson correlation coefficients (r) and two-tailed p-values were computed for the four meat quality traits using the cor.test function in R. Correlation patterns were visualized as a heatmap with the pheatmap package, where colors represent r values and asterisks mark significance levels.

Total RNAs extraction, cDNA synthesis and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was isolated using RNAiso Plus (Takara, Mountain View, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Then, 1 µg of RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using an RT reagent kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara). The PCR reaction (15 µL) consisted of 7.5 µL TB Green II, 1.2 µL cDNA, 0.3 µL each of forward and reverse primers, and RNase-free water. Reactions were performed on a Bio–Rad CFX Connect Real-Time PCR System. Gene expression levels were examined using the 2^-△△Ct method, with GAPDH as the internal control. All primers were manufactured by TSINGKE Biological Technology Co. (Xi’an, Shaanxi, China). Primer information is detailed in Table 1.

Sample collection, nuclear isolation, and snRNA-seq data processing

LD muscle samples from 30-month-old Qinchuan, Angus, and Wagyu cattle were processed for single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) (n = 3 per breed, females). Tissue samples were minced on ice and gently homogenized using a Dounce homogenizer in pre-chilled nuclear lysis buffer containing 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 10 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl₂, and 0.025% non-ionic detergent in 1× PBS. The homogenates were incubated on ice for 1 min to allow mild lysis, and the resulting nuclei suspension was diluted with nuclei wash and resuspension buffer consisting of 1× PBS supplemented with 1% BSA and RNase inhibitor at a final concentration of 0.2 U/µl. Nuclei were collected by centrifugation at 500 rcf for 10 min at 4 °C, and the pellet was gently resuspended in nuclei wash and resuspension buffer followed by an additional wash under the same centrifugation conditions. The final nuclei suspension was filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer to remove debris and aggregates, counted, and adjusted to the appropriate concentration before immediate loading onto the 10x Genomics Chromium 3′ Gene Expression platform. snRNA-seq libraries were constructed using the 10x Genomics Chromium 3′ kit and sequenced on the NovaSeq 6000 platform. Sequencing reads were processed using Cell Ranger in pre-mRNA mode and aligned to the bovine reference genome (ARS-UCD1.2) to generate UMI count matrices. Downstream quality control and integration were performed in Seurat. Low-quality barcodes (nFeature_RNA < 200 or > 3000, nCount_RNA < 200 or > 10,000, percent.mt > 10%) were removed. Data were normalized using NormalizeData and ScaleData, and batch correction was performed with Harmony. PCA was used to construct the neighbor graph, followed by UMAP dimensionality reduction and Louvain clustering for subsequent analyses.

FAPs subcluster reclustering and enrichment analysis

PDGFRA⁺/COL high-expressing FAPs cells were extracted and processed independently through NormalizeData, ScaleData, PCA, UMAP, and secondary clustering yielding four sub populations (FAPs1–FAPs4). Differentially expressed genes for each subcluster were identified using FindAllMarkers. Functional enrichment analysis and GSEA were performed using clusterProfiler (version 4.12.6) for GO-BP, KEGG, and MSigDB Hallmark gene sets with significance defined at p < 0.05.

Pseudotime analysis

Pseudotime analysis was conducted on myoblasts and different myofiber types including Hybrid cells. Using Monocle2 (v2.32.0), dimensionality reduction was executed with highly variable genes using DDRTree, and pseudotime was inferred with OrderCells. Myoblasts were set as the root to trace the differentiation trajectory from myoblast to hybrid and then to Type IIX/IIA/I fibers. Branch-specific genes were identified using BEAM (Branch Expression Analysis Modeling) and a branched heatmap was generated, grouping genes into four dynamic modules.

Cell communication analysis

After normalization, integration, and cell-type annotation in Seurat, the expression matrix and labels for each breed were imported into CellChat (v2.1.2). Using the CellChatDB (human) database, homologous genes were mapped to cattle genes. Communication probabilities and the pathway aggregation were calculated using the default settings (computeCommunProb/ computeCommunProbPathway/aggregateNet), with cell subsampling within each type to balance cell numbers. Outputs included global circle plots, interaction strength heatmaps, sending/receiving strength, and pathway “relative information flow.” Cross-breed differences were assessed using compareInteractions, rankNet, and netAnalysis_signalingRole.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using a t test in GraphPad Prism 6 software. The results are presented as the mean ± SD, and p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Phenotypic, histological, and molecular characteristics of meat quality traits in different cattle breeds

To explore differences in meat quality between breeds, integrated approach was used by utilizing phenotypic, histological and molecular analysis of QC, AN, and WY. Quantitatively, WY demonstrated the highest IMF, whereas shear force showed opposite trend (QC > AN > WY); drip loss at 48 h was lowest in WY, and pH values slightly differed among breeds (Fig. 1A). Oil Red O staining with quantitative analysis demonstrated that intramuscular lipid accumulation was highest in WY, lowest in QC, and intermediate in AN. Quantification of MYH2/MYH7 immunofluorescence signals indicated that WY contained a higher proportion of slow oxidative fibers, whereas QC was relatively enriched in fast glycolytic fibers. Masson’s trichrome staining further showed significantly greater collagen area in AN and WY than in QC. In addition, PAS staining revealed significant breed-specific differences in PAS-positive area, with QC showing the highest level, AN the lowest, and WY an intermediate phenotype (Fig. 1B). PCA of the four traits separated the breeds, with PC1 explaining 90.2% of variance, primarily driven by the opposing behavior of IMF and shear force (Fig. 1C). Pearson correlations further illustrate negative associations of IMF with shear force and drip loss, whereas pH values correlated weakly with the other traits (Fig. 1D). At the molecular level, qPCR demonstrated higher expression of slow-fiber markers (MYH7, TNNT1) and the lipid-metabolic gene FABP4 in WY, while fast-fiber markers (MYH2, TNNT3) were elevated in QC; AN generally showed intermediate levels (Fig. 1E). Collectively, WY is characterized by high IMF, slow-fiber enrichment, and reduced collagen deposition, corresponding to lower shear force and better water-holding capacity whereas QC shows the opposite trend (low IMF, fast-fiber enrichment, and greater collagen). AN showed intermediate values between WY and QC.

Single-cell transcriptomic profiling of the longissimus dorsi delineates the cellular atlas and breed-specific differences

SnRNA-seq was performed to explain breed-specific meat-quality differences, on the LD from QC, AN, and WY. UMAP analysis identified 12 distinct cell populations (Fig. 2A), including Type I, Type IIA, Type IIX, hybrid myofibers, myoblasts, FAPs, endothelial cells, pericytes, immune cells, and a minor fraction of mature adipocytes. Cell-type identities were confirmed using marker genes (Fig. 2D–E): MYH7, MYH2, and MYH1 marked Type I/IIA/IIX fibers, respectively; PDGFRA and collagen genes were enriched in FAPs; PECAM1 was specific to endothelium. Although the overall clustering structure was similar across breeds (Fig. 2B), cell composition varied (Fig. 2C) as WY showed the highest FAPs proportion, QC was characterized by enrichment of Type IIX fast fibers, and AN was intermediate. Differential gene expression (DEG) analysis revealed that myofibers were enriched in genes related to muscle structure and energy metabolism, while FAPs and vascular cells were enriched in extracellular matrix remodeling and angiogenesis pathways (Fig. 2F). In summary, this single-cell atlas of QC, AN, and WY highlights breed-specific differences in myofiber composition and FAP abundance, laying the groundwork for future studies on signaling pathways, developmental dynamics, and cell to cell interactions.

Breed-specific FAP subpopulations and signaling reveal dual adipogenic–fibrogenic fates

FAPs are the major regulators of IMF and fibrosis as IMF improves flavor whereas fibrogenesis reduces tenderness, making FAP fate decision critically important for beef quality. Four subpopulation were revealed by reclustering (FAPs1–FAPs4; Fig. 3A), with FAPs2 most abundant, followed by FAPs1, and FAPs3/4 relatively rare (Fig. 3B). Marker-gene expression and enrichment analyses suggested functional heterogeneity among the four FAP subpopulations (Fig. 3C–E; Fig. S1C–D). FAPs1 was characterized by higher expression of adipogenesis- and lipid-associated genes, including APOD and CFD, consistent with an adipogenic identity. FAPs2 showed strong expression of ECM- and fibrosis-related genes such as COL1A1, COL3A1, FMOD, and S100A4, suggesting a matrix-remodeling/fibrogenic program. FAPs3 expressed genes including MME and NUPR1, indicating a distinct intermediate/stress-responsive state that may be related to early adipogenic or repair-associated features. FAPs4 was enriched for genes such as TGFB3, POSTN, and FSTL1, consistent with an activated stromal remodeling state. Breed-specific trend (Fig. S1A) showed that WY with APOD-enriched FAPs1 and weak COL1A1/COL3A1 corresponding low fibrosis/active lipid acquisition. AN showed strong COL1A1/COL3A1 in FAPs2/4 which is related to vigorous ECM remodeling, limited adipogenesis while QC showed lowest APOD levels, higher MME/NUPR1 expression in FAPs3/4, and moderate to high COL1A1/COL3A1 suggesting stress/repair and fibrotic activity.

Pseudotime analysis placed FAPs2 at the progenitor state, branching into two trajectories: an adipogenic route (FAPs2 → FAPs3 → FAPs1) and a fibrotic route from FAPs2 to FAPs4 (Fig. 3F; Fig. S1B). Cell-cell Communication analysis revealed the densest network in WY showing strong myofiber/endothelial interactions), intermediate in AN, and weakest/ fibrosis-skewed in QC (Fig. 3G–H, Fig. S1E). In WY, FAPs1 upregulated pro-adipogenic FGF–FGFR and ANGPTL–integrin axes, while FAPs2 amplified ECM–integrin with TGF-β and TNFα–NF-κB signals, creating a permissive paracrine-rich environment (Fig. 3I; Fig. S1F). Information flow ranked the adipogenic microenvironment gradient as WY the highest, AN intermediate while QC is the lowest (Fig. 3J). Consistently, GSEA showed WY vs. AN FAPs1 enriched for NOTCH, bile/fatty-acid metabolism, and adipogenesis pathways (Fig. 3K), and WY vs. QC FAPs1 enriched for IL6–JAK–STAT3, TNFα–NF-κB, TGF-β, Complement, and Adipogenesis (Fig. 3L). Altogether, differences in FAP subsets and their signaling are associated with breed-specific variation in myofiber metabolism and ECM properties, and may contribute to differences in IMF, tenderness, and water-holding capacity.

Pseudotime mapping and metabolic remodeling reveal fast/slow fiber fates and breed specificity

To correlate myofiber programs to meat-quality traits, we examined lineage and metabolic states contrasting slow oxidative with fast glycolytic profiles, since these are directly linked to IMF, shear force, and WHC. Pseudotime analysis indicated a conserved “single-origin, three-branch” structure across breeds progressing from myoblast to hybrid fibers and then diverging to Type IIX, IIA, I fibers (Fig. 4A). Marker gene expression confirmed branch identities (Fig. 4B; Fig. S2B): MYH1 peaked early representing the IIX branch, MYH2 peaked mid-trajectory representing the IIA branch, and MYH7 increased late representing the I branch. Terminal fate preferences differed by breed (Fig. S2A): QC favored IIX fibers, WY favored type I fibers, and AN lean toward IIA fibers. A branch based heatmap grouped post-commitment genes into four dynamic clusters (Fig. 4C): Cluster 1 increased towards type I branch which was enriched for mitochondrial respiration/oxidative phosphorylation (Fig. S2C). Cluster 3 increased toward Type IIA/IIX branches with sarcomere assembly, contractile machinery, and Ca²⁺ transport (Fig. S2E). Cluster 2 included translation/ribosome biogenesis, collagen-containing ECM, and ER protein processing (Fig. S2D). Cluster 4 included transcriptional regulation, small GTPase/Wnt, and chromatin remodeling (Fig. S2F). These dynamics matched with breed specific cell composition (Fig. 2C). WY contained more Type I myofibers and endothelial/pericyte fractions with lower IIX, aligning with Cluster 1 oxidative metabolism and a vascular niche. QC was enriched with IIX/IIA fast fibers and FAPs, corresponding to Cluster 3 contractile maturation and Cluster 2 ECM remodeling, reflecting glycolytic and fibrotic tendencies. AN showed intermediate with more IIA/Hybrid, aligned with Cluster 4 and transitional programs. Pathway activity scoring by cell type (Fig. 4D; Supplementary Fig. S2G) further supporting these patterns as WY leading in oxidative phosphorylation, Ca²⁺ handling, and a WHC-related gene set in Type I and precursor/transition cells while QC had the highest glycolysis and proteasome activity in Type IIX/IIA and enhanced ECM remodeling in myofibers and FAPs. AN remained intermediate with an IIA/transition bias. GO-BP analysis confirmed these findings depicting (Fig. S3A) WY’s slow-oxidative and Ca²⁺-homeostasis advantages align with higher IMF, better tenderness, and superior WHC and QC’s fast-glycolytic and ECM-remodeling bias corresponds to higher shear force and poorer tenderness/WHC while AN falls between these extremes.

Breed-dependent intercellular signaling in the longissimus dorsi highlights ECM–integrin and growth-factor differences

Cell–cell communication links niche signals with cellular fate and metabolism, eventually shaping IMF, tenderness, and WHC. Single-cell ligand–receptor analysis showed dense, FAP–myofiber–centric interaction networks across all breeds (Fig. 5A–C). Quantitative information flow showed the densest outputs from FAPs to Type I/IIX myofibers and vascular cells in WY, intermediate in AN, and weakest/sparse network in QC (Fig. 5B–C). WY myofibers and vascular cells received the strongest basement-membrane/ECM and trophic inputs (LAMININ/COLLAGEN/HSPG; IGF/ANGPT), whereas AN was intermediate with angiogenic/remodeling bias, and QC was weakest and skewed toward adhesion inputs (CD99/CADM) concentrated in Type IIX (Fig. S3A). Pathway level comparisons highlighted that WY-specific enrichment of ECM–integrin axes involving laminin, collagen I/III/VI, FN1 and HSPG2 interacting with ITGA/ITGB1, CD44, DAG1 together with DHEAS–PPARD lipid-metabolic signaling. This combination indicates efficient mechano–metabolic coupling that supports lipid deposition, matrix compliance, and water retention (Fig. 5D–E; Fig. S3C–D). AN showed moderate ECM–integrin input combined with angiogenic and guidance pathways including SEMA3C–NRP1, PLXNA and ANGPTL1–integrin, while QC instead relied on CADM1–CADM1, CD99–CD99, and SDC4-centered adhesion with weak basement-membrane engagement, consistent with a stiffer and less supportive niche. Module level analysis ranked WY the highest for EGF/collagen/IGF/laminin signaling with FAP-directed inputs to myofibers that bridged endothelium and pericytes cells. AN was intermediate and angiogenesis-weighted while QC was overall weakest with EGF/IGF largely unidirectional to IIX (Fig. 5F; Fig. S3B). At the transcriptional level, WY upregulated CIDEC, COL1A1/COL1A2/COL3A1, LOXLs, MMP14/TIMP2, and AQP1/AQP4, consistent with lipid-droplet fusion, regulated collagen assembly/crosslinking, and enhanced water transport. AN preferentially expressed vascular-remodeling genes whereas QC lacked this coordinated reinforcement (Fig. 5G). Collectively, WY established a microenvironment that influence higher IMF, lower shear force, and improved water-holding. AN is intermediate whereas QC showed insufficient ECM support and weaker mechano–metabolic coupling, linked with lower IMF and higher shear force.

Discussion

In the modern beef industry, “expected eating quality” is shifting from traditional judgment to understanding cell micro environment and measureable molecular traits. Although IMF improves flavor, juiciness, and tenderness, traditional traits or average gene expression cannot explain why tenderness and water-holding capacity differ at similar IMF levels, nor do they reveal precise intervention to improve these traits [14, 15]. Current study linked and highlighted the breed-level phenotypic differences (QC/AN/WY) to the single-cell scale, focusing on three interconnected factors, myofiber metabolic programs, FAP dual fates, and basement-membrane/integrin/vascular signaling. This framework aligns with the classic triad of meat quality (IMF, myofibers, connective tissue) and, via cell-type and signaling network analyses, yields intervention-ready targets and actionable metrics [16].

PDGFRα⁺ FAPs are main source and central regulators of intramuscular adipogenesis and fibrosis in skeletal muscle [17–19]. FAPs are the major determinants of adipogenic versus fibrogenic trajectory as observed in mouse and human skeletal muscle [20, 21]. The immune system influences this decision as type-2 innate immune signals (IL-4/IL-13) expand FAPs to support regeneration and temporarily limiting adipogenesis, whereas TNF-α limits chronic fibrosis by inducing FAP apoptosis [22, 23], thereby balancing the adipogenesis versus fibrosis. Our finding elucidated that reciprocal shifts in pro-adipogenic versus pro-fibrotic signaling are consistent with this model [22].

Adipose development and blood vessel formation mutually support each other, with ANGPTL proteins regulating fatty-acid flux and FGF signaling often acting in parallel with vascular development. In our network analysis, ANGPTL and FGF interactions matched lipid-metabolism programs, suggesting a self-reinforcing loop connecting blood supply, lipid metabolism, and fat droplets. Adipogenesis and angiogenesis mutually promote each other during tissue remodeling, ANGPTL regulate fatty acid flow and FGF signaling often works alongside vascularization [24, 25]. The network analysis depicts that ANGPTL/FGF-centered interactions aligned with lipid-metabolic programs, proposing a mechanistic basis for a self-reinforcing loop connecting blood supply, lipid metabolism and lipid droplet [26, 27].

WY FAPs are inclined towards a pro-adipogenic pole marked by matrix compliance and vascular–metabolic coupling whereas QC lies at a pro-fibrotic pole with high stiffness and pro-inflammatory/TGF-β signaling. AN showed an intermediate state, combining adipogenic drive with matrix remodeling. This breed gradient (WY > AN > QC, by adipogenic bias) explains systematic differences in fat accumulation and fibrosis, suggesting ways to adjust immune, mechanical, and vascular–metabolic signals to promote fat growth without fibrosis [18, 28].

Our findings demonstrated breed-specific differences in muscle fiber composition, with WY showing a higher proportion of slow oxidative fibers, QC showing a predominance of fast glycolytic fibers, and AN exhibiting an intermediate phenotype, which may influence postmortem meat quality. Slow-oxidative fibers, characterized by high mitochondrial activity and gradual metabolic changes, are linked with slower pH decline, improved tenderness, and greater water-holding capacity whereas in contrast fast-glycolytic fibers acidify rapidly, resulting to stress shortening and more variable quality [29, 30].

Water-holding capacity is strongly influenced by calcium regulation and myofibril structure as the stronger glycolysis accelerates pH drop and increases drip loss [31, 32]. The breed specific differences observed in Ca²⁺ handling and glycolytic programs are aligned with structural model. Connective tissue also defines baseline tenderness as the collagen contents and cross-linking make meat tougher and more heat-stable. ECM remodeling is more pronounced in breeds enriched in fast fibers which increases baseline toughness [33, 34]. This observation is consistent with our findings as stronger ECM-remodeling signals in fast-fiber–rich breeds [35]. The tenderization potential is further limited by the endogenous proteolytic system. Under physiological Ca²⁺, µ-calpain degrades key myofibrillar proteins and serves as the main driver of postmortum tenderization [36, 37]. This helps explaining why slow-oxidative, homeostatically stable muscles respond more predictably to postmortem aging [36, 38].

On the other hand, adipogenesis and angiogenesis are mutually integrated and dependent as adipose-tissue development/remodeling occur alongside local vascularization. This same principle is applicable to intramuscular fat deposition in skeletal muscle [24, 39].

In WY, we observed a denser network of basement-membrane, integrin, and vascular interactions, consistent with a microenvironment that may better support nutrient and oxygen delivery, matrix flexibility, and lipid-droplet maturation. By contrast, intercellular communication in QC was more concentrated on cell-adhesion pathways and showed weaker basement-membrane/integrin engagement, which is consistent with a stiffer extracellular matrix and less favorable baseline conditions for water-holding capacity and tenderness. These breed-related differences suggest that muscle microenvironment remodeling may contribute to variation in meat-quality traits. However, meat quality is a complex phenotype shaped not only by cellular and molecular programs, but also by nutrition, management, and postmortem factors. Therefore, the signaling features identified here should be regarded as candidate mechanisms associated with breed-specific variation, rather than as sole determinants of meat quality. Future studies will be needed to validate whether laminin–integrin signaling and endothelial–FAP interactions can serve as robust indicators of muscle microenvironment states relevant to beef quality.

Conclusion

Our results showed that WY was characterized by a higher proportion of slow-oxidative myofibers, a stronger adipogenic FAP signature, and enhanced basement-membrane/integrin–vascular coupling, together with higher IMF, lower shear force, and better water-holding capacity. In contrast, QC was characterized by a greater abundance of fast-glycolytic fibers, more fibrogenic FAP features, and a stiffer ECM-associated niche, accompanied by lower IMF, higher shear force, and poorer water-holding capacity, whereas AN displayed an intermediate profile. Together, these findings suggest that breed-associated variation in meat quality is linked to coordinated differences in myofiber metabolism, FAP fate, and ECM–vascular signaling. This lineage–niche framework provides a basis for interpreting the cellular and molecular architecture underlying breed differences in beef quality, while further studies are needed to determine the extent to which these effects are independent of IMF.
