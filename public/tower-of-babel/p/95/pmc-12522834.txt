Glutamic acid-lysine (EK) rich motif of RabD2 self-associates and regulates adhesion through multivesicular bodies in Entamoeba histolytica
Authors: Navyaka Padavala, Kuldeep Verma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEntamoeba histolytica, an enteric pathogen, causes disease by adhering to and destroying the host tissues. The interaction between the parasite and host tissue enables the rewiring of gene expression and global membrane trafficking in the parasite. A fine balance between endocytosis and exocytosis of cargoes/receptors is required to establish infection in the host. Multivesicular bodies (MVBs) act as sorting platforms, delivering cargoes/receptors to lysosomes for degradation or secreting their contents through plasma membrane fusion. Some small GTPases are known to control MVB biogenesis in various organisms. However, the functional contribution of Rab GTPases in MVB biogenesis is poorly studied in E. histolytica.ResultsHere, we identified a novel atypical protein RabD2, with an N-terminal glutamic acid-lysine rich motif and a C-terminal conserved Rab domain. Our biochemical and cell biological assays provide evidence that RabD2 self-associates, and this interaction is controlled by the N-terminal EK-rich motif and the GTPase activity mutants. RabD2 localizes on the surface of MVBs and controls their biogenesis. In line with these findings, overexpression of RabD2 upregulates ubiquitination levels, directing the decreased abundance of the heavy chain of GalNAc lectin, ultimately leading to decreased adherence of E. histolytica trophozoites to host cells.ConclusionsThus, amoebic RabD2 is a new class of Rab protein that forms high-ordered self-association variants and controls adhesion to the host cells, which impacts the pathogenesis of E. histolytica through the biogenesis of MVBs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12915-025-02415-4.

Background

Entamoeba histolytica is a unicellular protozoan parasite, which is commonly known to cause intestinal and extraintestinal amoebiasis. The infections are reported across the globe with nearly 100,000 fatalities annually [1]. Moreover, its prevalence is much higher in children below 5 years of age and in low socio-demographic index countries [2]. Endocytosis is one of the fundamental pathways for nutrient uptake, growth, and virulence in this parasite. Multivesicular bodies (MVBs) are a specialized subset of endosomes that are characterized by membrane-bound intraluminal vesicles [3]. Protein localization on MVBs means that the protein is sorted into the MVB lumen or membrane by a process called endosomal sorting. This process is regulated by various proteins, such as the ESCRT (endosomal sorting complex required for transport) machinery proteins, cytoskeleton members, and vacuolar sorting receptors. The genesis of MVBs relies on the sequential recruitment of ESCRT-0, ESCRT-I, ESCRT-II, ESCRT-III, and ESCRT-III-associated proteins [4]. This process begins with recognition of the mono-ubiquitinated proteins by ESCRT-0 at the cytosolic face of endosomal compartments. The comparative genomic study revealed that ESCRT machinery proteins are highly conserved across the eukaryotic lineage [5]. However, the ESCRT-0 proteins are only found in opisthokonts (metazoans and fungi), which suggests an alternative mechanism for sorting the cargo to MVBs in other organisms, including Amoebozoa [6]. The amoebic parasite genome encodes 19 genes of ESCRT machinery proteins, which are devoid of the ESCRT-0 complex proteins. The VHS (Vps27-Hrs-STAM) domain is an evolutionarily conserved region within the ESCRT‐0 complex, composed of Hrs and STAM (humans), and Vps27 and Hse1 in (yeast). It is presumed that the parasite uses an unconventional VHS domain protein/s to recognize ubiquitinated proteins [7]. Similar to A. thaliana and D. discoideum, E. histolytica encodes Tom1 and is considered to be a scaffold to interact with ubiquitin and other ESCRT machinery proteins [8–10]. The ESCRT-I member, EhVps23, an orthologue of yeast Vps23 and mammalian TSG101, is predicted to have a UEV domain (ubiquitin E2 variant domain present in TSG101), which potentially interacts with LBPA and ubiquitin [11]. The ESCRT-III protein, EhVps32, interacts with EhVps20 and then recruits EhVps24. EhVps2 has been shown to control the intraluminal vesicle (ILV) size [12]. Previously, it has been shown that the ESCRT member EhVps23 is involved in vesicular transport, phagocytosis, motility, and secretion. Based on immunoprecipitation and proteomic analysis, it was hypothesized that ESCRT members couple with multiple Rab proteins to take part in MVB functions [11].

More than 100 Rab proteins are encoded by E. histolytica [13]. These proteins act as molecular switches and control various membrane transport events such as vesicle tethering, budding, and fusion. Rab GTPase cycles between active (GTP-bound) and inactive (GDP-bound) states intrinsically by utilizing the specific regulators such as GEFs and GAPs [14]. Previously, it has been shown that EhRab5 and EhRab7a form unique endocytic pre-phagosomal vacuoles (PPVs) upon interaction with the host cells [15]. These PPVs are indicative of MVB-like structures that transport the acid hydrolases and iron to lysosomes [16]. In higher eukaryotes, Rab7, Rab11, Rab22, and Rab27 are reported to localize on MVBs and control their fusion with lysosomes and plasma membrane [17–20], although the direct role of Rab GTPases in MVB biogenesis is poorly studied.

The unbiased transcriptomic screen identified that specifically two sets of Rab GTPases, RabD2 (EHI_164900) and Rab35 (EHI_146510), were upregulated in the virulent strain of E. histolytica (HM: 1: IMSS) upon the pathogen’s interaction with human colon tissue [21]. In a recent microarray-based study conducted in both non-pathogenic (UG10) and pathogenic (HM: 1: IMSS) strains, it was found that RabD2 is upregulated only in the pathogenic strain of E. histolytica [22]. Further, upregulation of RabD2 is also linked with cytopathic activity against the host [23]. The RabD2 homologue is absent in non-virulent species such as E. dispar and E. moshkovskii. Altogether, it suggests that RabD2 could be an important regulator for the pathophysiology of pathogenic E. histolytica species. Therefore, understanding the function of RabD2 will bring new insights into the pathophysiology of E. histolytica. In this study, we have employed quantitative, biochemical, and cell biological assays which provide evidence that RabD2 self-associates, and its interaction alone is controlled by the N-terminal glutamic acid-lysine (EK) rich motif and nucleotide status of GTPase. We showed that RabD2 localizes on the MVBs and controls their biogenesis. Furthermore, we found that RabD2 overexpression and activity upregulate ubiquitination levels, which leads to the decreased protein abundance of the heavy chain of GalNAc lectin (Hgl), a prominent antigenic marker for E. histolytica infection [24, 25]. Further, our results showed that adherence of pathogens to host cells is reduced due to the low levels of plasma membrane Hgl. Collectively, our findings demonstrate that the novel RabD2, consisting of the EK-rich motif, undergoes high-ordered association and regulates the E. histolytica pathogenicity through MVB biogenesis.

Results

Large RabD2 contains an atypical glutamic acid-lysine-rich motif and is expressed as SDS-resistant, high molecular mass protein bands

To study the details of putative large RabD2 (EHI_164900) from E. histolytica, primers were designed based on the hypothetical sequence available in the database (amoebadb.org). The high-fidelity PCR was performed using respective primers (see Methods section), and a 795 bp (approximately) amplified product was obtained from the cDNA pool of E. histolytica (Additional File 1: Fig. S1A). The amplified product was cloned into a tetracycline inducible amoebic expression vector (pEhTex-HA, vector control without any insert) with an N-terminal 3X hemagglutinin (HA) epitope sequence, which is translated into amino acids. The cloned RabD2 hypothetical molecular mass is around 30 kDa, which is slightly higher than the conventional Rab GTPase (20–25 kDa). These high molecular weight Rab GTPases are known as large Rab GTPases [26]. The mammalian Rab44, Rab45, and Rab46 belong to the large Rab GTPase family [27–29]. Further, Rab2A, together with the known large Rab GTPases Rab44, Rab45, and Rab46, were retrieved from the protein database, and comparative sequence alignment was performed. It was observed that RabD2 consists of a conserved G domain followed by an additional 69 amino acids at the N-terminus (Additional File 1: Fig. S1B). The N-terminal 69 residues are non-Rab sequences consisting of negatively charged residues (aspartic acid + glutamic acid = 31 residues) and positively charged residues (arginine + lysine = 19 residues). In addition, this region is rich in glutamic acid (15 residues) and lysine (12 residues) amino acids that are distributed throughout the N-terminal 69 residues in protein sequences. This specific characteristic abundance of glutamic acid and lysine residues suggested us to name it the EK-rich motif (Fig. 1A). Interestingly, bioinformatics analysis also revealed the presence of a potential intrinsically disordered region within the EK-rich motif of RabD2. Further, pEhTex-HA-RabD2 plasmid was transfected, and stable expressing trophozoites were selected in the presence of G418. The pEhTex tetracycline-inducible system allows regulated expression of the target gene/s over two orders of magnitude and is commonly used in expression studies [30–32]. To study the expression of RabD2, transfected trophozoites were incubated with tetracycline, and their lysate was subjected to western blotting using anti-HA antibody and cysteine synthase 1 (CS1) as a loading control (Fig. 1B). Surprisingly, our western blot analysis revealed high molecular mass bands, including the expected mass of 33 kDa (HA tagged with RabD2). Although high molecular mass bands were prominent compared to the expected mass. Further, HA-RabD2 expressing trophozoites were incubated without and with tetracycline, and its expression levels were assessed by western blotting. The results indicated that RabD2 expression is elevated (4.5 fold) in the presence of tetracycline (Fig. 1C and D). However, we observed low-level expression of HA-RabD2 even in the absence of tetracycline, suggesting minor leaky gene expression, as previously reported [33, 34]. To validate the high molecular mass bands observed in Fig. 1B and C, HA-RabD2 trophozoites were subjected to immunoprecipitation to enrich the protein, followed by western blot analysis. Our results confirmed the clear presence of multiple bands at higher molecular mass of HA-RabD2 (Fig. 1E). It was observed that the abundance of high molecular mass bands was varying among the experiments, suggesting the possibility of dynamic post-translation modifications or formation of transient high-ordered protein complexes under physiological steady-state conditions. We next analysed the subcellular localization of HA-RabD2 in trophozoites. Confocal imaging revealed that RabD2 is localized on the variable-size vesicles (diameter, 0.8 ± 0.12 µm) and vacuoles (diameter, 2.04 ± 0.65 µm), which are distributed throughout the cell (Fig. 1F and G). Endogenously, RabD2 self-associates, and it is sensitive to urea

We formerly observed the SDS-resistant high molecular mass species of RabD2 due to its ectopic expression. To determine the nature of endogenous RabD2, amoebic RabD2 antibody was generated using the full-length RabD2 protein in rabbit and was validated (Additional File 1: Fig. S2, Additional File 2: Supplementary Methods). Furthermore, the validated antibody was used to confirm that the endogenous RabD2 is also SDS-resistant and reveals the high molecular mass bands (Fig. 2A). The anomalous migration of proteins on SDS-PAGE could be a cause of post-translational modifications [35]. There are two common post-translational modifications, sumoylation (∼8 kDa SUMO small ubiquitin-like modifier) and ubiquitination (∼10 kDa ubiquitin), that covalently link to a target protein and results in an increase in the mass of the modified protein [36, 37]. Both the post-translational modification pathways are conserved in E. histolytica [38, 39]. The RabD2 sequence analysis also predicted seven potential sites (K24, 29, 43, 48, 53, 58, 63) with high confidence for sumoylation (ΨKXE/D (ψ: hydrophobic amino acid, K: target lysine, X: any amino acid, E and D: glutamic and aspartic acids, respectively) by SUMOplot (abcepta.com/sumoplot) and JASSA analysis tools [40]. Similarly, in the case of ubiquitination, ubiquitin is covalently linked with lysine (K) of the target protein through a three-step enzymatic cascade [41]. The inhibitors 2-D08 and MG132 were widely used to selectively inhibit global sumoylation and ubiquitination, respectively [42, 43]. The sumoylation inhibitor 2-D08 blocks the E2-conjuating enzyme which aids the covalent transfer of SUMO to its target substrates, thereby resulting in global reduction of the sumoylated proteins [44] whereas the ubiquitination inhibitor MG132 inhibits the activity of 26S proteasome leading to the accumulation of poly-ubiquitinated proteins by blocking their proteasomal degradation [45]. The untransfected amoebic trophozoites were incubated with 50 µM 2-D08 or 10 µM MG132 for 16 h, and subsequently, it was identified that the levels of RabD2, typically the high molecular mass species remained unchanged (Fig. 2B and C). These results suggest that RabD2 neither undergoes sumoylation nor ubiquitination under physiological conditions. Few studies suggest that Rab GTPases undergo self-association or oligomerization in mammalian cells [27, 28]. However, the molecular basis of self-association in Rab GTPases and their functional relevance has not been tested in any living system. To evaluate the self-association of RabD2 under steady-state conditions, wild-type amoebic trophozoites were grown in the logarithmic phase, and the whole cell lysate (WCL) was subjected to incubation with potent reducing agents like 10 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) and 10% β-mercaptoethanol (β-ME) for 30 min. We were unable to observe any remarkable difference in high molecular mass bands upon the treatment with reducing agents. Next, we hypothesized the possibility of ionic interactions that might be pivotal for the self-interaction of RabD2. Urea and guanidine hydrochloride (GnHCl) are chaotropic agents and commonly used as strong denaturants for proteins [46, 47]. The whole cell lysate was independently incubated with 6 M urea and 9 N GnHCl for 30 min. Our immunoblot result suggests that upon the treatment with urea, an intense band was observed at 30 kDa, which is likely to be the hypothetical mass (monomeric) of RabD2 (Additional File 1: Fig. S3A). Further, a similar experiment was performed at longer incubation time (16 h) with urea and other reducing agents (Additional File 1: Fig. S3B). Surprisingly, we observed enrichment of RabD2 at expected hypothetical molecular mass (30 kDa) resulting from the decay of high molecular mass band (35 kDa). Finally, we wanted to study the effect of urea on the denaturation of endogenous RabD2 in a time-dependent manner. Trophozoites were lysed and incubated with urea for multiple time points. Our findings clearly suggest that the decay of high molecular mass bands, particularly around 60–70 kDa, resulted in an intense 35 kDa band at 30 min. Eventually, the decay of the 35 kDa band into the monomeric (30 kDa) RabD2 at incremental time points was observed (Fig. 2D and E). Further, the bacterially purified and proteolytically cleaved (PreScission Protease) RabD2 protein from the recombinant GST-RabD2 was employed in varying concentrations (0.1–0.8 µg) and subjected to immunoblotting. Our results showed that with increasing concentrations of RabD2 protein, the high molecular mass bands appeared with enriched intensities at approximately 35, 45, and 60 kDa (Fig. 2F and G). This result provides another line of evidence that RabD2 undergoes high-ordered self-organization in vitro. RabD2 self-associates through an atypical N-terminal glutamic acid-lysine (EK)-rich motif, which is controlled by the constitutively active and dominant negative mutants

We used a hypothetical protein sequence of RabD2 for in silico analysis and identified that RabD2 contains an EK-rich motif (1–69 amino acid residues) at the N-terminal (Fig. 3A). To evaluate whether the N-terminal EK-rich motif is important for the self-association of RabD2, the EK-rich motif deletion construct (∆69RabD2) was generated and ectopically expressed in amoebic trophozoites. The HA-∆69RabD2 transfected trophozoites were selected, and their lysate was subjected to western blotting. Our results indicate that HA-∆69RabD2 migrates only at the hypothetical molecular mass (approximately 24 kDa), and a significant reduction in the high-ordered self-association bands was observed (Fig. 3B and C). Next, we investigated whether the nucleotide binding status of RabD2 control its higher-order version. We employed a constitutively active mutant with defective GTP hydrolysis (HA-RabD2QL) and a dominant negative mutant with defective GTP-binding (HA-RabD2TN). These standard mutations represent two functionally opposite states of Rab GTPases that are well established to show the nucleotide binding status in biochemical and cell biology studies [48, 49]. The RabD2 mutant (QL and TN) constructs were ectopically transfected, and their expression and subcellular localization were determined by immunoblotting and confocal microscopy. Strikingly, our immunoblot results showed that HA-RabD2TN has significantly reduced higher molecular mass bands compared to both wild-type HA-RabD2 and HA-RabD2QL mutant (Fig. 3D and E). These results suggest that RabD2 GTP bound version is remarkably contributing to the high-ordered organization of this protein. Further, we have quantified the size of RabD2 positive compartments in RabD2 wild type and mutant trophozoites (Additional File 1: Fig. S4). Our confocal imaging data indicates that the HA-RabD2 [diameter, 1.85 ± 0.74 µm, n = 364 compartments (including vesicles and vacuoles)/20 cells] and its mutants HA-RabD2QL (diameter, 2.07 ± 0.9 µm, n = 348 compartments/20 cells) and HA-∆69RabD2 (diameter, 2.24 ± 1.09 µm, n = 182 compartments/20 cells) mostly localizes on variable sizes of vesicles and vacuoles (Fig. 3F). Conversely, the self-association-deficient mutant HA-∆69RabD2 localizes on variable sizes of vacuoles and shows a global reduction of 50 and 52.2% in total number of vacuoles compared to the wild-type RabD2 and RabD2QL mutant, respectively (Fig. 3G). The localization of RabD2QL was similar to wild-type with a slightly high fraction of large-size (4.5–5.5 µm) vacuoles. Since the dominant negative mutation in Rab GTPases are known to interact with GDI (guanine nucleotide dissociation inhibitor), it is observed that their localization is mostly confined to the cytosol. Similarly, HA-RabD2TN is also localized in the cytosol, thereby attributing to its negligible appearance on the vacuoles and vesicles and thence excluded from this quantification. Altogether, our results suggest that the EK-rich motif and the dominant negative mutant of RabD2 influence its high-ordered self-association and subcellular distribution in the amoebic trophozoites.

RabD2 localizes on MVBs and controls their biogenesis

Our results indicate that the endogenous RabD2 localizes on large-size vacuoles suggestive of MVB-like structures, where discrete RabD2 vesicles (ILV) were observed within the large vacuoles (Additional File 1: Fig. S5A). Therefore, we sought to determine the association of RabD2 with MVBs. Lysobisphosphatidic acid (LBPA) is an atypical lipid, often used as a marker for MVBs. It plays an important role in both the formation of these structures and in regulating protein turnover through degradation and recycling pathways. Previously, it has been shown that LBPA is enriched in the internal membranes of late endosomes and MVBs in mammalian cells and E. histolytica trophozoites [50, 51]. Hence, to test whether RabD2 is endogenously localized on MVBs, untransfected amoebic trophozoites were probed with anti-RabD2 and anti-LBPA. Interestingly, our confocal images clearly showed RabD2 positive vacuoles were discreetly filled with LBPA (Fig. 4A). The localization of RabD2 was observed to be abundant on the surface as well as within these vacuoles. Next, we performed transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to confirm that RabD2 localizes on MVBs. Indeed, our immunogold-labelled TEM micrograph showed that endogenously RabD2 localizes on the surface of MVB and ILV (Fig. 4B). In eukaryotes, MVBs often originate from early endosomes, which are marked by the presence of Rab5 [52]. Therefore, HA-RabD2 expressing trophozoites were employed and probed with endogenous amoebic Rab5. Our confocal images clearly showed that RabD2 colocalizes with Rab5 on the surface of MVBs and ILVs (Additional File 1: Fig. S5B). Next, we sought to ask whether HA-RabD2 overexpression modulates the MVB abundance in the trophozoites. Therefore, HA-RabD2 and pEhTex-HA (vector control without any insert) trophozoites were subjected to TEM analysis. Our quantitative results suggest that HA-RabD2 expression showed a significantly high number of MVBs compared to pEhTex-HA (Additional File 1: Fig. S6A and B). These observations suggest that RabD2 could potentially be involved in MVB formation. Further, we asked whether RabD2 activity and its self-association are functionally important for MVB formation. HA-RabD2 and its mutants were probed with LBPA under steady-state conditions. We found that HA-RabD2, HA-RabD2QL, and HA-∆69RabD2 mutants were localized on the surface of MVBs. Conversely, the dominant negative mutant HA-RabD2TN remained in the cytosol along with the diffused distribution of LBPA (Fig. 4C). Next, we quantified the total number of LBPA-positive compartments and their varying size distribution in wild-type RabD2 and its mutants. Our quantification data suggest that HA-RabD2TN showed 17.69% reduced LBPA positive compartments when compared to the wild-type HA-RabD2 and a more prominent 52.5% reduction when compared to the constitutively active mutant HA-RabD2QL, respectively. Interestingly, we found that the size of LBPA compartments dramatically reduced in the HA-RabD2TN mutant (diameter, 1.86 ± 0.71 µm) when compared with wild-type HA-RabD2 (diameter, 2.73 ± 1.21 µm), HA-RabD2QL (diameter, 2.18 ± 1.25 µm), and HA-Δ69RabD2 (diameter, 2.22 ± 0.9 µm) mutants (Fig. 4D). Altogether, our results suggest that the activation status of RabD2 may play a crucial role in the association of LBPA with MVBs. Since MVB turnover is an inherent consequence of MVB biogenesis, any impairment in the biogenesis process will inevitably affect the later stages of endocytic pathway that ultimately influences receptor degradation and protein recycling [3]. Based on TEM data, we observed that RabD2 immunogold particles were enriched at the invagination site of ILVs, as marked by yellow arrowheads (Fig. 4B). Further, we systematically analysed the HA-RabD2 localization during inward membrane invagination (negative curvature) and budding events in fixed cells. Our confocal images indicate that RabD2 was markedly enriched at the sites of membrane invagination and elongated buds (Additional File 1: Fig. S7A and B), suggesting its potential role in the early stages of MVB biogenesis. The negative curvature of endocytic membrane is regulated by several lipids [53, 54] and remain poorly studied in eukaryotes. Next, we employed a lipid overlay assay using PIP strips to screen for potential interacting partners of RabD2. Notably, our results (Additional File 1: Fig. S7C, Additional File 2: Supplementary Methods) showed that RabD2 specifically binds to phosphatidic acid (PA). To determine whether this interaction occurs under physiological conditions, we performed a PA pulldown assay using lysates from wild-type trophozoites. Our immunoblot analysis indeed confirmed that RabD2 co-precipitates with PA (Additional File 1: Fig. S7D, Additional File 2: Supplementary Methods). Therefore, to gain more details on the function of RabD2 in MVB biogenesis, RabD2 knockdown (KD) trophozoites were generated. The efficiency of RabD2 downregulation was measured in RabD2KD trophozoites compared with Trigger trophozoites (vector control). Our immunoblot quantification results showed significant reduction (around 50%) in overall RabD2 expression levels (Fig. 4E and F). Further, RabD2KD trophozoites were probed for LBPA localization. Interestingly, our confocal micrographs clearly showed diffused LBPA distribution, which is similar to that of HA-RabD2TN (Fig. 4G). This diffused LBPA distribution suggests a defect in the classical MVB formation pathway. To gain a better understanding of MVB structures, RabD2KD and Trigger (vector control) trophozoites were subjected to TEM imaging. Surprisingly, we observed that the RabD2KD trophozoites showed a large number of tiny vesicles, some of which are the MVBs. Interestingly, the large size dark MVBs with large ILVs were prominent in Trigger trophozoites compared with RabD2KD trophozoites (Fig. 4H). In addition, it was observed that in the absence of RabD2, the size of ILVs reduced and were found in close proximity with the MVB membrane. Upon quantification, we identified that RabD2KD trophozoites showed a significant reduction in the diameter of MVBs (Fig. 4I), which implies an improper maturation of MVBs. RabD2 upregulation causes higher ubiquitination, reduced protein level of the heavy chain of GalNAc lectin (Hgl), and impaired adherence

It is very well established that specific sets of cargoes and receptors undergo MVB-dependent protein degradation in eukaryotes [55–57]. Therefore, we looked for the ubiquitination status in amoebic trophozoites upon the ectopic expression of RabD2. Surprisingly, we have observed that the overall ubiquitination is enhanced in RabD2 expressing trophozoites compared to uninduced RabD2 and pEhTex-HA trophozoites (Additional File 1: Fig. S8). Further, our immunoblot analysis suggests that the constitutively active HA-RabD2QL mutant exhibits high levels of ubiquitination compared to the dominant negative HA-RabD2TN and HA-∆69RabD2 mutants (Fig. 5A and B). These results indicate that HA-RabD2 and HA-RabD2QL are likely to induce ubiquitination, which potentially controls the degradation of specific receptors in amoebic trophozoites. The GalNAc lectin, a tetrameric complex constituting the Hgl, is required for host cell adhesion, cytotoxicity, and complement resistance [58–60]. Therefore, we asked how RabD2 expression and its activity impact the levels of Hgl in amoebic parasites. Interestingly, we found that the HA-RabD2QL mutant significantly causes decreased protein abundance in Hgl levels, which is 52.8% reduced when compared to HA-RabD2TN and 54.7% reduced when compared to the HA-∆69RabD2 mutants (Fig. 5C and D). Furthermore, we sought to investigate whether RabD2 and its mutants influence the intracellular localization of Hgl. Our confocal microscopy images showed that HA-RabD2, HA-RabD2QL, and HA-∆69RabD2 colocalized with Hgl on vacuoles (Additional File 1: Fig. S9), suggesting that RabD2 is involved in the transport of Hgl in the parasite. Interestingly, it was noticed that the accumulation of Hgl was observed within HA-RabD2 and HA-RabD2QL vacuolar compartments. These results suggest that both RabD2 wild-type and constitutively active mutant are involved in reducing the levels of Hgl. Therefore, we asked whether this decreased protein abundance of Hgl controls the adherence of trophozoites to the host cells. The wild-type HA-RabD2 and its mutant trophozoites were incubated with Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells for adhesion (Additional File 1: Fig. S10). Interestingly, our quantification results showed that HA-RabD2QL expression significantly reduced its adherence to the CHO cells, which is 28.05% when compared with HA-RabD2TN and a highly significant 44.16% when compared with HA-∆69RabD2 (Fig. 5E). These results raise the possibility that the poor adherence of HA-RabD2QL mutant trophozoites to CHO cells is due to the relatively low levels of Hgl at the cell surface. The plasma membrane levels of Hgl were observed in previous studies using non-permeabilized trophozoites [61, 62]. Further, cell surface-associated Hgl was investigated in wild-type HA-RabD2 and its mutants. Our imaging results showed that plasma membrane abundance of Hgl dramatically reduced in HA-RabD2QL and HA-RabD2WT compared with HA-RabD2TN and HA-∆69RabD2 mutants (Fig. 5F and G). Similarly, our quantification data indicate that the percentage of trophozoites with plasma membrane localization of Hgl was significantly reduced in HA-RabD2WT and HA-RabD2QL mutants (Fig. 5H). To validate this observation, a cell surface biotinylation assay was conducted to evaluate the levels of Hgl in the plasma membrane. Hgl has previously been established as a bonafide marker in biotinylation-based membrane isolation protocols [32, 63]. Using a similar experimental pipeline, the plasma membrane fractions were isolated from RabD2 wild-type and its mutant trophozoites. Our immunoblot analysis revealed a marked reduction in Hgl levels in HA-RabD2 and RabD2QL mutants compared to HA-RabD2TN and HA-∆69RabD2 (Fig. 5I). Next, we asked how RabD2 knockdown influences the expression, localization of Hgl and adherence to the host cells. We found that the overall Hgl levels remained unchanged in RabD2KD compared with Trigger vector control. Interestingly, our imaging data revealed the mislocalization of Hgl to tubular compartments in RabD2KD trophozoites (Additional File 1: Fig. S11A and B). Further, we tested whether RabD2KD has an effect on adhesion to the CHO cells. Our results indicate that RabD2KD cause significant reduction of adherence to CHO cells when compared to vector control (Additional File 1: Fig. S11C and D). Discussion

This study presents three major findings. First, we identified the presence of a novel EK-rich motif at the N-terminus of the conserved Rab domain that participates in self-association. Second, RabD2 localizes on MVBs and controls their biogenesis. Third, we found that RabD2 self-association and GTPase activity mutants regulate ubiquitination levels, accompanied by the downregulation or reduced level of the plasma membrane receptor Hgl.

Rab GTPases are ubiquitous in eukaryotes and are crucial for the transport of cargoes between compartments via vesicular budding, fusion, and docking [14]. The amoebic RabD2 protein is relatively longer than the conventional small Rab GTPases due to its EK-rich motif at the N-terminus of the Rab domain. The presence of this motif is unique in the Rab proteins. In mammals, a subset of Rab GTPases is of higher molecular mass compared to classical small Rab GTPases. These high molecular mass Rab GTPases, known as large Rab GTPases, consist of an N-terminal EF-hand domain (EFD), a coiled-coil domain (CCD), and a C-terminal conserved Rab G domain. Rab GTPases 44, 45, and 46 belongs to this class in mammals [26]. It has been demonstrated that the N-terminal domains are required for the self-association of these GTPases under physiological conditions [27, 28]. Conversely, Rab40 also contains an additional region at the C-terminus, the suppressor of cytokine signaling (SOCS) box [64]. Our study identified that the novel N-terminal EK-rich motif (potential disordered region) is crucial for controlling the self-association of RabD2. Surprisingly, we observed that RabD2 forms high-order oligomers that are resistant to SDS and other denaturants. Additionally, we found that the chaotropic agent urea denatures RabD2 into its monomeric state. Both GnHCl and urea are chaotropic agents that disrupt the non-covalent bonds within the protein’s tertiary structure [65]. Previously, it has been shown that urea and GnHCl are structurally similar and denature proteins by forming hydrogen bonds with the NH2-group and COOH-group of peptides [66]. In contrast, lysozyme stability sharply decreases upon GnHCl treatment, whereas in the presence of urea, the protein remains folded and stable [67]. Urea solubilizes and denatures proteins by disrupting the non-covalent interactions that stabilize their secondary and tertiary structures. Urea denatures proteins through both direct and indirect mechanisms [68, 69]. The direct mechanism involves hydrogen bonding of urea with polar amino acids of the protein, leading to both weak intermolecular bonds and interactions between amino acid residues. Urea can also compete with the protein for water molecules, reducing protein solvation and hydration thereby exposing its hydrophobic core. The indirect mechanism involves altering the solvent environment by urea, affecting the thermodynamics and kinetics of protein folding and unfolding. Urea can change the structure and hydrodynamics of water, making it more like a non-polar solvent, which favours the denatured state of the protein. Collectively, it can be speculated that thermodynamic parameters, the overall amino acid composition, and peptide charge within the EK-rich motifs confer to maintaining protein stability in the presence of different chaotropic agents. The N-terminal region of RabD2 provides a negatively charged side chain of glutamic acid (E) and a positively charged side chain of lysine (K) that may contribute to salt-bridge formation. It has been shown that the ionic interactions between the positive (aspartic acid, glutamic acid) and negative (lysine, histidine) side chains of charged amino acids are crucial for structural integrity and function [70, 71]. Interestingly, we observed that high-ordered self-association of RabD2 is controlled by the EK-rich motif and dominant-negative mutant. Based on these data, it can be hypothesized that RabD2 self-association requires both EK-rich motif for interaction surface and nucleotide-induced conformational changes. The GDP-bound mutant (RabD2TN) likely adapts a different conformation in which the EK-rich motif is improperly positioned or partially masked, leading to reduced association. This suggests that the RabD2QL active form (GTP-bound) conformation properly exposes the EK-rich region, enabling effective dimerization or high-ordered multimer formation. This model can be further tested by biochemical and high-resolution structural analysis to determine how the GDP-bound conformation reduces the EK-rich interface and obstructs self-association. The deletion of EK-rich motif in RabD2 showed a significant reduction in the number of vacuolar compartments. This observation raises the possibility that the self-association of RabD2 fine-tunes the budding or fission events that likely generates smaller vesicles. It has been shown that deletion of N-terminal domains in multiple large Rab proteins indeed affect their subcellular localization [28, 72]. However, future studies would be needed to understand how these additional domains control the membrane trafficking by interacting with their effectors or GEFs. MVBs are ancient organelles and are found across various eukaryotic lineages [6]. The direct role of Rab GTPases in MVB formation is poorly studied from lower to complex eukaryotes. The mammalian Rab11, Rab27a, and Rab27b have been shown to be involved in MVB fusion and exosome biogenesis, although their functional roles are cell type-specific [18, 20]. We identified that RabD2 localizes on LBPA-positive compartments and MVBs, through confocal and TEM imaging. Previously, RabD2 was identified as an interacting partner of endogenous EhVps23 in immunoprecipitation-based proteome studies. EhVps23, an orthologue of yeast Vps23 and mammalian TSG101, undergoes ubiquitination and is recruited to MVBs and phagosomes [11]. Our results corroborate with previous studies where EhVps23 localizes on MVBs. Recent studies showed that the EhVps35 C, a member of the retromer complex, tie-up with ESCRT machinery proteins [10]. RabD2 was also identified in EhVps35 C interactome along with ESCRT-I and II members. We also observed that overexpression of RabD2 causes a significant upregulation of MVBs and are localized at the abscission site of ILV where EH domain-containing protein (EHD1) is localized [31]. Previously, it has been shown that the small GTPase ARF6 recruits phospholipase D2 to promote local synthesis of PA during MVB formation [73]. Our data also indicate that RabD2 binds with PA and potentially coordinates with ESCRT proteins upon the formation of MVBs. Recently, a direct interaction between PA and the ESCRT component Chm7 was established at the nuclear envelope [74]. Further, it would be interesting to study how the RabD2 crosstalk's with ESCRT machinery proteins and how membrane lipids like PA controls the organization of MVBs. Interestingly, while ESCRT complexes I, II, III, and III-associated are ubiquitous and conserved in eukaryotic lineages. However, ESCRT 0 members appear to be missing in this lineage and have specific requirements to initiate the process. It has been shown that EhTom1, similar to D. discoideum DdTom1 (VHS and GAT), interacts with TSG101 and facilitates the sorting of ubiquitinated proteins onto MVBs [10]. Future study is required to establish a direct link between ESCRT and retromer complex proteins with RabD2.

The crosstalk between MVBs and ubiquitination is crucial for regulating protein sorting and degradation within the cells [57]. We have shown that the ectopic expression of RabD2 causes upregulation of ubiquitinated proteins in amoebic trophozoites. This raises the question of how RabD2 overexpression causes this phenomenon. First, it can be predicted that RabD2 activates ubiquitin conjugation machinery proteins. It has been found that the SOCS box (non-Rab region of Rab40c), an evolutionarily conserved LPLP motif, is required for the binding to Cullin5 to form ubiquitin E3 ligase complex, which regulates protein ubiquitination [64, 75]. Second, RabD2 may recruit ESCRT complex proteins to effectively target ubiquitinated proteins to the endosomes. The RabD subfamily proteins are conserved in protozoa and plant species and may have been evolutionarily lost in mammals. In Arabidopsis thaliana, RabD1 and RabD2 GTPases localize on the Golgi/TGN and control the trafficking to specific endosomes [76]. We can also predict that RabD2 vesicles may originate from the Golgi and traffic the cargoes to endosomes or MVBs. Interestingly, it has been shown that plant ESCRT machinery proteins transport cargoes from TGN to the endosomes. Third, RabD2 activity in the late endocytic pathway may trigger receptor proteins to be directed towards lysosomal fusion and degradation. In mammals, Rab7 is required for the biogenesis of MVBs and degrades the cargoes through lysosomal fusion [17]. Rab7b, on the other hand, has been shown to promote the lysosomal degradation of Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) [77]. Interestingly, we cannot rule out the possibility that RabD2 is also involved in the proteasomal degradation pathway, as it has been shown that ubiquitinated proteins are also degraded through the same path. The contrasting observation is suggesting that proteasomal function and ubiquitination levels cannot be correlated in amoebic parasites. Because proteasome function is highly upregulated in the trophozoites during encystation, while ubiquitination levels remain fluctuating in the same process [78]. Further, it would be interesting to identify the downstream effector molecules of RabD2 and their roles in the above possibilities.

The upregulation of amoebic RabD2 was specifically identified upon interaction with human colon explants and under oxidative stress conditions [21]. These observations suggest that host tissue interaction potentially causes a stress response leading to global transcriptional reprogramming and it likely changes the protein modifications in the trophozoites. This situation may allow the trophozoites in the host environment to adapt by upregulating the virulence factors, improving strategies for evading host immunity and adapting in the hostile environment by degrading their own proteins through ubiquitination [79]. However, ubiqutination-mediated protein degradation pathway is studied in several parasites [80–82] and poorly studied in amoebic parasite. Recently, antibodies to E. histolytica ubiquitin have been identified in patients with invasive amoebiasis [83]. In addition, it has been shown that the Entamoeba ubiquitin also reduces robust immune response in animal models [84]. These observations raise a possibility that the alteration in parasites ubiquitination may be involved in the pathogenesis. Further, evidence is required to determine the precise role of ubiquitination in the virulence of the parasite. The MVB pathway contributes in sorting the various cargo/s to lysosome or releasing the extracellular vesicles (EVs). It is possible that the trophozoites ubiquitinated proteins containing MVBs that fuse with the plasma membrane and exocytose EVs into the host environment. Interestingly, it has been observed that E. histolytica EVs downregulate the human neutrophil respiratory burst and NETosis [85]. It can be speculated that EV secretion from the trophozoites is potentially involved in immune modulation. A recent study showed that the composition of amoebic EVs play a significant role in male-biased immunopathology during the formation of amoebic liver abscesses [86]. In the current study, reduced levels of Hgl were identified in the constitutively active RabD2QL mutant. Conversely, increased abundance of Hgl was observed in the catalytically inactive and self-association deficient mutants. However, Hgl punctas were observed within RabD2 positive compartments in both wild-type and constitutively active mutant trophozoites. Collectively, it can be hypothesized that with increased MVB formation, more endocytosed proteins including Hgl are sequestered into ILVs within MVBs. These MVBs have multiple fates: either fusing with lysosomes for degradation, fusing with the plasma membrane to release exosomes or regulate quality control of misfolded proteins from the Golgi [87]. Previously, it has been shown that Hgl is constitutively degraded through the endo-lysosomal pathway [62]. Further, it would be interesting to study whether Hgl ubiquitination is required for its constitutive degradation. This could be relevant because the cytoplasmic domain of Hgl shares sequence similarity with integrins [59], which are known to be regulated through ubiquitin-mediated pathways [88]. In mammalian systems, it has been observed that ubiquitin can be non-canonically linked to the N-GlcNAc sugar by forming an oxyester bond [89]. The high abundance of N-linked glycan moieties observed in E. histolytica ubiquitin [90], it is plausible to hypothesize that the GalNAc moieties of the heavy subunit of the Gal/GalNAc lectin could also be targeted for ubiquitination through similar non-canonical glyco-ubiquitin linkages. Interestingly, a recent study found a 5.8-fold increase in Hgl abundance in the EV population upon the interaction with host neutrophils [85]. It could be possible that the downregulation or degradation of membrane receptors is considered a double-edged sword in which trophozoites reduce adherence to tissue, favouring evasion from the host immune system. This might be a potential mechanism for the parasite's long-term survival, replication, differentiation, and establishing the infection in the host. We cannot rule out the other possibility for constitutive exocytosis of Hgl, which could lead to global depletion of Hgl in the trophozoites and modulate immune surveillance. Interestingly, we found that plasma membrane abundance of Hgl and adherence of trophozoite is reduced in both wild-type RabD2 and RabD2QL mutant. On the other hand, RabD2 downregulation does not affect the overall levels of Hgl but causes its mislocalization and accumulation within tubular compartments. Notably, a comparable Hgl distribution phenotype was observed in trophozoites overexpressing a EhRabAQL mutant, where Hgl was accumulated specifically in the ER and Golgi [91]. These observations point to a possible disruption in Hgl trafficking from the ER and Golgi to the cell surface or to the MVBs. In this context, the recent study has highlighted the interaction of tetraspanins with Hgl and its involvement in cargo selection and its packaging into the MVBs in E. histolytica [92]. However, the overexpression of tetraspanins 12 and 13 significantly reduced the adherence and upregulated the protease secretion. It is noteworthy to check the involvement of RabD2 in the secretion of extracellular vesicles from E. histolytica against the host cell killing and immune modulation.

Conclusions

In this study, we illustrate a novel RabD2 protein featuring a unique EK-rich motif that drives its high-order multimerization. These assemblies are orchestrated by the EK-rich motif and modulated by RabD2 activity mutants. Notably, RabD2 localizes to MVBs, and its expression elevates cellular ubiquitination. This pathway facilitates the degradation of a key cell surface antigenic receptor Hgl, ultimately influencing parasite adherence to host cells. Further studies on the crosstalk between parasite ubiquitination and antigenic receptors downregulation provide insights into how parasites use these strategies to establish the infection in the host intestine.

Methods

Cell culture

Entamoeba histolytica HM-1: IMSS trophozoites were cultured under axenic conditions in BIS-33 medium supplemented with 15% (v/v) adult bovine serum (RM10913, HiMedia Labs, India), 2% (v/v) Diamond's vitamins, 100 units of penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin, 0.25 µg/mL and amphotericin B (15,240,062, Gibco, USA), and the amoebic trophozoites were maintained at 35.5 °C [93].

cDNA pool preparation and cloning

Logarithmic phase trophozoites were used to extract the total amoebic RNA using the PureLink™ RNA Mini Kit (12,183,020, Invitrogen, USA) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. The obtained total RNA was used in the synthesis of cDNA with a High-Capacity RNA-to-cDNA™ Kit (4,387,406, Invitrogen, USA). Fifty nanograms of the pooled cDNA, together with amoebic RabD2 (EHI_164900) primers (forward primer 5’gcacccgggATGAGTGATACTAAAGAGACTGTTCC3’ and reverse primer 5’gacctcgagTTAACAACATCCTCCTTTCTTTT3’) was used in PCR amplification using Phusion® High-Fidelity PCR master mix (M0531S, New England Biolabs, USA). The amplified products were electrophoresed on a 1% (w/v) agarose gel and the predicted molecular weight band (approximately 792 bp) was excised and digested with SmaI and XhoI restriction enzymes (R0141S, R0146S New England Biolabs, USA). The pEhTex-HA plasmid was also digested with the aforementioned enzymes and was subjected to ligation with the RabD2 PCR product using T4 DNA ligase (M0202S, New England Biolabs, USA). The ligated product was transformed into E. coli DH5-α, and recombinant clones (pEhTex-HA-RabD2) were identified and confirmed using unidirectional Sanger sequencing.

Site-directed mutagenesis and generation of deletion mutants

The RabD2 mutant primers were designed using the NEBaseChanger tool (nebasechanger.neb.com). To create the mutant constructs, 25 ng of HA-RabD2 plasmid was amplified using the constitutively active RabD2Q136L (RabD2QL) primers (forward primer 5’TACAGCTGGACTGGAACGTTTTG3’ reverse primer 5’TCCCAAATTTGAAGTTTAATATG3’) and dominant negative RabD2T91N (RabD2TN) primers (forward primer 5’TGTTGGTAAAAACTGTTGTATGAATAGATATGTTAG3’ reverse primer 5’GAAGACTCTCCTACCATAATAAC3’) using Q5 Site-directed mutagenesis kit (E0554S New England Biolabs, USA) as per manufacturer’s instructions. The deletion construct ∆69RabD2 was generated by PCR amplification using these primers (forward primer 5’gcacccgggAATGAATCTAATGAACAAC3’ reverse primer 5’gacctcgagTTAACAACATCCTCCTTTCTTTT3’).

Electroporation and selection of stable expressing trophozoites

E. histolytica trophozoites at the mid-logarithmic growth phase were harvested by replacing the culture media with cold 1X phosphate saline buffer (PBS) pH 7.4 and placing the tube on ice for 10 min (to detach cells). The cell suspension was then centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min, and the pellet was suspended in ice-chilled incomplete cytomix buffer (10 mM K₂HPO₄/KH₂PO₄ (pH 7.6), 25 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), 120 mM KCl, 0.15 mM CaCl₂, 2 mM EGTA, and 5 mM MgCl₂) and was subsequently centrifuged at 900 rpm for 5 min. The pellet was resuspended in complete cytomix buffer (incomplete cytomix buffer supplemented with 10 mM reduced glutathione and 4 mM ATP) along with 80 µg of plasmid DNA in a 0.4 cm electroporation cuvette (1,652,088 BioRad, USA) with modifications [94]. The trophozoites were then electroporated at 500 V voltage, 500 µF capacitance, and a time constant of infinity (∞) using Gene Pulser Xcell Eukaryotic System (BioRad, USA). Immediately, the contents of the electroporation cuvette were transferred into warm complete culture media (BIS-33 supplemented with 15% adult bovine serum and 2% Diamond's vitamins) without antibiotics. The stable transfectants were screened using G418 (A1720, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) selection, which involves gradual increment of G418 from 2 to 6 µg/ml with regular change of media for 48 h over a period of 12–15 days and the stable trophozoites were maintained at 6 µg/ml G418. Before every experiment performed, the stable trophozoites were shifted to complete BIS-33 medium supplemented with 20 µg/ml G418 and 5 μg/ml tetracycline to induce the expression of the target gene for at least 36–48 h.

Indirect immunofluorescence assay and confocal microscopy

Trophozoites at the mid-logarithmic phase were washed with cold 1X PBS and harvested by centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 5 min. The pellet was then suspended in warm BIS-33 medium and transferred onto a clean multitest 8-well slide (6,040,805, MP Biomedical, USA), and incubated at 37 °C in a water bath for 20 min. The adhered trophozoites were fixed with 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde (158,127, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 15 min, followed by a wash with 1X PBS and then permeabilized with 0.1% (v/v) Triton X-100 (in 1X PBS) for 9 min. Blocking was carried out in 5% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (26,140,079, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) prepared in 1X PBS (blocking solution) for 30 min. Trophozoites were incubated with specific primary antibodies; anti-HA monoclonal antibody (sc-7392, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA) (1:150) or anti-RabD2 (1:500) or anti-LBPA (Z-PLBPA, Echelon Biosciences) (1:100) or anti-Hgl (1:100, 3F4 and 7F4) or anti-Rab5 (1:50) for 90 min and subsequently three washes with blocking solution were given. The secondary antibodies conjugated with Alexa Fluor 488, 568, and 647 (Alexa Fluor 488 anti-mouse, A11029; Alexa Fluor 488 anti-rabbit, A11034; Alexa Fluor 568 anti-mouse, A11031; and Alexa Fluor 647 anti-mouse, A21235; Invitrogen, USA) were prepared in blocking solution (1:500) and incubated for 60 min in the dark. To probe the DNA, DAPI (10236276001, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) (1:1000) was co-incubated with secondary antibodies. Finally, mounting of trophozoites with coverslips was done using 5 µl of Prolong Diamond Antifade (P36970, Invitrogen, USA), and the slides were dried at room temperature overnight and employed for microscopy.

The image acquisition was done using the LSM 900 laser scanning confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany) and the SP8 laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica Microsystem, Germany) equipped with multiple laser lines (405, 488, 561, and 640 nm). The × 63 oil immersion objective (with 1.4 NA) was used, and all the images were acquired at 8–16 bit resolution, 512 × 512 or 1024 × 1024 frames, with a standard scan speed in individual en face (xy axes) planes throughout the cellular z axis at 0.30-μm intervals.

Image quantification

The confocal Z-stacks were employed to visualize intracellular fluorescent vesicles and vacuoles. All images were calibrated to ensure precise spatial measurements and were subsequently analysed using ImageJ. Individual vesicles and vacuoles were manually delineated with the region-of-interest (ROI) tool and their size was measured using the measurements tool from the ImageJ. In each plane, at least five-ten discrete objects were quantified. However, overlapping vesicles or vacuoles within the same plane or across adjacent planes were excluded manually from this analysis. The association between RabD2 and LBPA was quantified using two approaches. First, hollow RabD2 vacuoles that were filled with LBPA were identified and measured. Second, pixel colocalization images were generated, and the number of colocalized objects was manually quantified. All the values were merged and plotted for representation.

HA-tagged protein pulldown

Mid-logarithmic phase trophozoites expressing pEhTex-HA (vector control without the gene of interest) and wild-type HA-RabD2 were immunoprecipitated using the Pierce HA-Tag IP/Co-IP Kit (26,180, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The trophozoites were harvested and lysed using the lysis buffer provided in the kit supplemented with E-64 (E3132, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and PIC (04693116001, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 30 min on ice with periodic mixing at regular intervals. The lysate was then clarified by centrifuging at 12,000 g for 10 min. The clarified lysate was incubated with 25 µl pre-equilibrated HA-tagged magnetic beads for 3 h at 4 °C under rotation. Subsequently, the beads were washed twice with wash buffer and the bound protein were eluted in elution buffer containing SDS sample loading dye and by boiling the beads at 95 °C for 10 min. The elute containing the immunoprecipitated complex was analysed by separating the sample on SDS-PAGE followed by western blotting.

Transmission electron microscopy

The transmission electron microscopy was performed by harvesting 5 × 105 mid-logarithmic phase trophozoites, and the cell pellet was fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 24 h at 4 °C as described previously [95, 96]. Post fixation, the pellet was resuspended in aqueous osmium tetroxide for 3 h, followed by 6 washes with deionized distilled water. The cell pellet was then dehydrated in a series of alcohol, embedded in araldite resin, and incubated at 80 °C for 72 h for polymerization. An ultra-microtome (Leica Ultracut UCT-GA-D/E-1/00) was used to make 60-nm ultrathin sections, which were mounted onto nickel grids (G5526, Merck, USA); following this, blocking was carried out with PBSG (PBS with 0.2% gelatin and 0.5% BSA) for 1 h at room temperature. The sections were then incubated with anti-RabD2 (1:30) primary antibody for 2 h at room temperature, followed by 10-nm gold-conjugated secondary antibody (A31561 Invitrogen, USA) (1:50) for 1 h at room temperature. The grids were incubated with saturated aqueous uranyl acetate and counterstained with Reynolds lead citrate to achieve contrast. The images were acquired using a Talos L120C transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at CCMB, Hyderabad, India.

Western blotting

E. histolytica trophozoites were harvested and lysed using the lysis buffer [50 mM Tris HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM PMSF, 1% Triton X-100, 10 µM E-64 (E3132, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and proteases inhibitor cocktail (04693116001, Sigma-Aldrich, USA)] at 4 °C for 30 min. The whole cell lysate (25–35 µg) was resolved at 120 V on a 12–15% acrylamide gel in reducing conditions. The proteins were electrophoretically transferred onto a PVDF membrane (10600023 Cytiva) at 90 V for 90 min at 4 °C. The transferred proteins on the membrane were incubated with blocking solution [5% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSAV-RO, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) in 1X PBS] for 1 h at room temperature. The primary antibodies employed are anti-HA monoclonal (3724 Cell Signalling Technology) (1:1000), anti-RabD2 polyclonal (1:1000), anti-ubiquitin monoclonal (05–944, Merck, USA) (1:1000), and anti-CS1 polyclonal (1:1000) which were incubated at 4 °C overnight. Post incubation, the membrane was washed and incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-rabbit (111–035-144, Jackson Immuno Research Laboratories, USA) (1:1000) and anti-mouse (315–035-048, Jackson Immuno Research Laboratories, USA) (1:1000) followed by detection of immune-reactive proteins using chemi-luminescence (ImageQuant™ 500, USA).

Treatment of sumoylation, ubiquitination inhibitors, reducing, and chaotropic agents

To study the effect of sumoylation and ubiquitination inhibitors on amoebic parasites, 2 × 106 trophozoites were incubated with 10 µM MG132 (M7449, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) or 50 µM 2-DO8 (SML1052, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 16 h at 37°C. The trophozoites were then lysed using lysis buffer, followed by detection using western blotting as described above.

To study the RabD2 self-association, logarithmic phase wild-type trophozoites were harvested, lysed in lysis buffer and further incubated under rotation with a series of reducing and chaotropic agents; 1mM or 10 mM DTT, 10% βME, 6 M urea, 9 N guanidine hydrochloride, respectively, for specified time points at 4 °C. After each time point, samples were prepared and subjected to western blotting.

Biotinylation and purification of cell surface proteins

Surface biotinylation of the trophozoites expressing wild-type HA-RabD2 and its mutants was performed using the EZ-Link Sulfo-NHS-SS-Biotin kit (A44390, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with some modifications. Briefly, 2 × 107 trophozoites were washed twice with chilled 1X PBS and incubated with 6 mg of EZ-link Sulfo-NHS-SS-Biotin prepared in ice-chilled 1XPBS for 30 min with gentle rotation. Biotin binds to the primary amines present on the surface of the trophozoite without being endocytosed by the cell due to the cold environment. Post labelling, the cells were harvested by centrifuging at 1000 rpm for 6 min and the excess unbound biotin was quenched by washing the cells twice with Tris buffer (provided inside the kit). The cells were then lysed for 30 min on ice with the lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors mix (04693116001, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and E-64 (E3132, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and the lysate was clarified by centrifuging at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. The clarified lysate was then loaded onto the NeutrAvidin Agarose resin and incubated for 60 min in cold under rotation. The resin was then washed twice with 1XPBS and the bound membrane proteins were eluted in 70 µl elution buffer containing 50 mM DTT and SDS-PAGE sample buffer for 30 min at 4 °C under rotation, followed by boiling the resin at 95 °C for 10 min. The eluted proteins were then analysed by SDS-PAGE.

Adhesion assay

The assay to investigate the adherence of E. histolytica trophozoites with CHO cells was adapted from a previous study [97]. Briefly, the amoebic trophozoites and CHO cells 1 h post trypsinisation) in a ratio of 1:20 were harvested, washed, and co-incubated in 1 ml PSF (calcium and magnesium free, Puck saline F; 137 mM NaCl, 5.3 mM KCl, 1.1 mM Na2HPO4, 1.1 mM KH2PO4, and 6.1 mM glucose) and centrifuged at 150 g for 5 min. The resulting mixture was incubated for 90 min on ice. Post incubation, 0.8 ml of the supernatant was discarded and the remaining cell pellet was gently broken down by rubbing the tube between hands and was transferred onto a clean 8-well slide. The cells were fixed with 4% PFA, and the slide was mounted with Prolong Diamond Antifade. Confocal microscope images were acquired for quantification. E. histolytica trophozoites adhering to at least three or more CHO cells, forming an Entamoeba-CHO cell rosette, were considered as an adherence positive event. The adherence was expressed as percentage that was calculated by dividing the number of rosettes (Entamoeba associated with three or more CHO cells) by the total number of Entamoeba and then multiplying by 100.

Immunoblot quantification

The immunoblots were quantified using the ImageJ software by maintaining uniformity in the area and measuring the intensity, thereby deducing the background. The values obtained from ImageJ were utilised to plot the bar using GraphPad Prism 9, and their respective p-values are mentioned in the figure legend.

RabD2 antibody production

The animal used in antibody generation study was in compliance with guidelines provided by the Committee for Control and Supervision of Experiments on Animals (CCSEA), Government of India and approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee (protocol no. EAF/2023/PP/18). The full-length purified amoebic RabD2 protein was used as an antigen to produce the antibody. An 8-week-old New Zealand rabbit was subcutaneously injected with a primary dose of 500 µg purified RabD2 protein prepared in Freund’s adjuvant (F5881, Sigma-Aldrich, USA). After 50 days, 750 µg of RabD2 protein was reinjected as the first booster dose. Later, a series of three extra booster doses with the same amount (750 µg) were injected with a time interval of 20 days each. Finally, serum was separated from the collected blood, and the antisera containing the anti-RabD2 antibody was further used for validation experiments. The pooled antisera containing the RabD2 antibodies were subjected to affinity purification using the protein A/G magnetic beads (88,802, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The pre-immune serum was collected from the animal before injecting the RabD2 protein.
