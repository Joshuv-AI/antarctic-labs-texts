Public readiness for anaphylaxis emergencies in Al-Baha Region, Saudi Arabia: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Mohammad I. Barnawi, Muaid H. Morad, Nujud A. Alzahrani, Nada H. Alqarni, Daniyah A. Alghamdi, Hanin A. Alamri, Wajd S. Alghamdi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTContext:Anaphylaxis is a life-threatening systemic allergic reaction requiring prompt recognition and epinephrine administration. Public preparedness for anaphylaxis has not been previously assessed in Al-Baha region of Saudi Arabia.Aim:To measure the level of public knowledge, awareness, and practice readiness regarding anaphylaxis among adult residents of Al-Baha region.Setting and Design:A cross-sectional, descriptive-analytical survey conducted in Al-Baha region between February and April 2026.Material and Methods:A content-validated, reliable bilingual (Arabic/English) 27-item questionnaire was administered online to adults aged 18 years and older. Knowledge was scored out of 28 points and classified using Bloom’s modified cut-off framework into low (≤16), moderate (17–23), and high (≥24) categories.Statistical Analysis:Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics v25. Descriptive statistics, Chi-square tests, t-tests, one-way analysis of variance, and binary logistic regression were used. P <0.05 was considered statistically significant.Results:A total of 702 participants were included. The mean knowledge score was 14.4 ± 5.7/28 (51.4%). Knowledge levels were low in 65.9%, moderate in 25.9%, and high in 8.1%. Only 29.6% identified epinephrine as the first-line treatment, while 29.8% incorrectly considered antihistamines an alternative. Awareness of EpiPen was 25.2%, and only 23.1% correctly identified the injection site. Healthcare practitioner status, female gender, and student status were independent predictors of adequate knowledge (adjusted odds ratio (aOR): 2.40, 1.97, and 2.19, respectively; all P < 0.05).Conclusion:Anaphylaxis preparedness in Al-Baha is inadequate, particularly regarding epinephrine use and EpiPen administration. Healthcare practitioner status was the strongest predictor of adequate knowledge. Targeted community education through healthcare providers and primary care settings is urgently needed.

Introduction

Anaphylaxis is a life-threatening, multi-system allergic reaction requiring immediate intramuscular epinephrine administration.[1] Global incidence is rising, but public knowledge of appropriate emergency responses remains consistently poor across populations.[23] In Saudi Arabia, multiple regional surveys have documented critical knowledge gaps, particularly regarding epinephrine primacy and auto-injector use.[456] No data exist for Al-Baha region. This study measures community anaphylaxis knowledge, identifies specific deficits, and determines independent demographic predictors of inadequate preparedness. For healthcare practitioners (HCPs), these findings identify the highest risk community groups for prioritizing anaphylaxis education during clinical encounters. For patients and the public, this evidence supports the development of targeted, accessible resources that address specific misconceptions (e.g., inappropriate substitution of antihistamines for epinephrine) most likely to cause harm during an emergency.

Subjects and Methods

Study design and setting

A descriptive-analytical, cross-sectional study was conducted in Al-Baha region, southwestern Saudi Arabia (estimated population 476,172). Data were collected via an online questionnaire distributed between February 2026 and April 2026. The study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist.

Participants and eligibility

We included all male and female adult residents (aged ≥18 years) of Al-Baha region who could read Arabic or English and who provided electronic informed consent. Those below 18 years of age, residing outside the region, unwilling to participate, or who returned incomplete questionnaires were excluded. Recruitment aimed to include participants across age groups, gender, and socioeconomic strata. The questionnaire was distributed through university notice boards and social media platforms, including Instagram, WhatsApp, Snapchat, and Facebook, to achieve the widest possible reach across the region.

Sample size

Sample size was calculated using the single-proportion formula: n = Z2 × P (1 − P)/d2, where Z = 1.96 (95% confidence interval [CI]), P = 0.50 (assumed prevalence), and d = 0.05 (5% margin of error). The minimum required sample was 384 participants, raised to 422 after a 10% non-response allowance. The achieved sample of 702 exceeded this target.

Questionnaire development and scoring

The study instrument was developed de novo as a bilingual self-administered questionnaire comprising 27 items in two sections. Section 1 (eight items) captured sociodemographic data: age, gender, nationality, education level, marital status, employment status, HCP status, and personal or family history of anaphylaxis. Section 2 (19 items) assessed knowledge, awareness, attitudes, and emergency practice readiness.

Before main data collection, a pilot study was conducted among 20 adult volunteers meeting the same eligibility criteria. Participants completed the questionnaire and provided written feedback on item clarity, language accessibility, and completion time. Based on this feedback, three items were slightly reworded. Pilot data were excluded from all subsequent analyses.

Of the 19 Section 2 items, 15 were single-response (one point for a correct answer, zero for an incorrect answer). Three items used a “select all that apply” format with partial credit: (i) causes of anaphylaxis - four correct options (drugs, insect stings, food, and latex), maximum four points; (ii) food allergens - five correct options (tree nuts, seafood, eggs, cow’s milk, and wheat), maximum five points; and (iii) prevention strategies - four correct options (avoiding the allergen, carrying an epinephrine auto-injector, reading food labels, and informing restaurants or schools), maximum four points. The aggregate maximum score was 28 points. The remaining item was an unscored information source question.

The content validity index (CVI) was established through expert panel review (n = 6; specialists in allergy, emergency medicine, and family medicine). The Item-CVI ranged from 0.83 to 1.00, and the Scale-CVI average was 0.94, exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.90. Internal consistency was satisfactory, with an overall Cronbach’s alpha of 0.813 (95% CI: 0.793–0.833); subscale alpha values ranged from 0.616 to 0.744, acceptable for a multidimensional community knowledge instrument.

Knowledge was classified using a modified Bloom’s cut-off framework scaled to 28 points: low (<60%, ≤16 points), moderate (60–84.1%, 17–23 points), and high (≥84.2%, ≥24 points).

Ethical approval

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Faculty of Medicine (Approval No. IRB/SUR/BU-FM/2025/7). Electronic informed consent was obtained from all participants before questionnaire access. Participation was voluntary, anonymous, and without financial compensation. The study was classified as minimal risk.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered into Microsoft Excel 2021 and exported to IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) Statistics v25.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages; continuous variables as means with standard deviation (SD). We used Pearson’s Chi-square test to evaluate associations between knowledge categories and demographic variables. Mean scores were compared between two groups with independent-samples t-tests and across multiple groups with one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Additionally, binary logistic regression was performed to identify independent predictors of adequate knowledge. The outcome was dichotomized as adequate knowledge (score ≥17, moderate or high) versus low knowledge (score ≤16). All demographic variables were entered simultaneously using the forced-entry method; adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% CIs were reported. Two-tailed P values below 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

Of 708 individuals who accessed the survey, 702 (99.2%) provided valid consent and completed all items. The sample was predominantly female (n = 542; 77.2%) and Saudi nationals (n = 687; 97.9%). The 18–30 year age group formed the largest stratum (n = 340; 48.4%). University-level education was the most common attainment (n = 549; 78.2%). The employed (n = 278; 39.6%) and student (n = 242; 34.5%) categories together accounted for 74.1% of the sample. HCPs comprised 20.8% (n = 146) of participants. A personal or family history of anaphylaxis was reported by 179 participants (25.5%). Full demographic data appear in Table 1.

Overall knowledge scores

The mean total score was 14.40 ± 5.68 (range 2–27) out of a maximum of 28 points, equating to 51.4% of the maximum. No participant achieved the maximum score. The median score was 14 (interquartile range 10–18), with a right-skewed distribution. Using the Bloom’s framework, 463 participants (65.9%) were classified as low knowledge, 182 (25.9%) as moderate, and 57 (8.1%) as high. Only 29.6% correctly identified epinephrine as the first-line treatment, and only 9.1% correctly answered that antihistamines are not an appropriate substitute. In contrast, 29.8% explicitly endorsed antihistamines as adequate replacements for epinephrine. Physical activity as an anaphylactic trigger was recognized by 31.1%, while the typical clinical presentation was correctly identified by 62.7%.

EpiPen-specific knowledge was particularly limited. Only 25.2% had heard of the epinephrine auto-injector device, 15.2% reported knowing how to use it, and 23.1% correctly identified the outer thigh as the injection site. Following EpiPen administration, 44.2% correctly identified immediate transfer to the emergency department as the required next step. Among food trigger items, the most frequently recognized allergens were eggs (70.8%), seafood (69.7%), and tree nuts (68.4%). Latex was identified by only 17.4%. A majority of participants (62.0%) incorrectly selected dust as a distractor, suggesting frequent confusion between systemic anaphylaxis and upper airway conditions such as allergic rhinitis or asthma.

Sources of information

Healthcare providers were the most frequently cited source of anaphylaxis information (70.7%), followed by social media platforms (46.4%), health awareness campaigns (45.6%), family or friends (34.0%), artificial intelligence tools (33.8%), and school or university (24.5%). Multiple selections were permitted. Figure 3 presents the full distribution.

Gender was significantly associated with knowledge level (chi2 = 25.63, df = 2, P < 0.001): female participants achieved a higher mean score (15.12 ± 5.61) than male participants (11.94 ± 5.23; t = 6.40, P < 0.001). Age group showed the strongest bivariate association (chi2 = 118.11, df = 10, P < 0.001); the 18–30 cohort recorded the highest mean score (17.10 ± 5.31), while the group aged over 60 years recorded the lowest (8.36 ± 2.98). Personal or family history of anaphylaxis was not significantly associated with knowledge category (chi2 = 3.95, df = 4, P = 0.413). Healthcare practitioner status was the strongest independent predictor: HCPs had 2.40 times the odds of adequate knowledge compared with non-HCPs (aOR 2.40; 95% CI 1.57–3.66; P < 0.001). Female gender was independently associated with higher odds of adequate knowledge (aOR 1.97; 95% CI 1.18–3.31; P = 0.011). Student employment status conferred a 2.19-fold higher odds of adequate knowledge relative to employed adults (aOR 2.19; 95% CI 1.18–4.06; P = 0.013). Older age was independently associated with lower odds: adults aged 31–50 years had 59% lower odds (aOR 0.41; 95% CI 0.23–0.72; P = 0.001) and those aged 51 years or older had 64% lower odds (aOR 0.36; 95% CI 0.16–0.80; P = 0.013) compared with the 18–30 year reference group. Education level, unemployment, and personal history of anaphylaxis were not significant independent predictors after adjustment.

Discussion

This survey provides the first published data on community anaphylaxis preparedness in Al-Baha region of Saudi Arabia. The mean knowledge score of 14.40/28 (51.4%) and the classification of 65.9% of the sample as low knowledge are broadly comparable to findings from Al-Ahsa, where low knowledge was documented among the majority of respondents,[45] and the Western Region of Saudi Arabia, where term recognition did not translate into emergency management knowledge,[6] but represent the first such data from the Al-Baha highland region, adding geographic diversity to the national evidence base.

The proportion correctly identifying epinephrine as first-line treatment (29.6%) was lower than the 37.9% recorded in the Al-Ahsa PLoS One survey[4] and substantially lower than the 46.2% found among trained paramedics in the United States.[7] In our sample, 29.8% of participants believed antihistamines can adequately substitute for epinephrine. This misconception poses a direct clinical hazard and has been consistently linked to delayed epinephrine administration and preventable anaphylaxis fatalities.[89] Comparable findings were reported in the Western Region study, where epinephrine recognition was similarly low, suggesting this deficit is a consistent and widespread pattern across Saudi Arabia rather than a region-specific phenomenon.[6] Antihistamines address only cutaneous histamine-mediated symptoms and cannot reverse bronchospasm, laryngeal edema, or distributive cardiovascular collapse. Any community education program must explicitly correct this belief.

EpiPen literacy was severely limited: device awareness (25.2%), knowledge of correct use (15.2%), and injection site identification (23.1%) were all below levels that would support a viable community auto-injector strategy. Both the World Allergy Organization (WAO) 2020 guidance and the European Academy of Allergy and Clinical Immunology (EAACI) 2021 guidelines recommend prescribing an epinephrine auto-injector for every patient at risk of recurrence.[1011] Where patients and bystanders cannot correctly use a prescribed device, the prescription provides limited real-world benefit.

A consistent finding across Saudi anaphylaxis surveys is the disparity between attitudinal recognition and actionable knowledge. In this sample, 85.0% agreed that anaphylaxis is life-threatening and 77.5% acknowledged that food allergy can be fatal, figures broadly matching those from Al-Ahsa and the Western Region.[456] However, only 28.1% correctly responded to a clinical vignette requiring epinephrine administration. This attitude–knowledge gap is a recognized pattern in health literacy research: favorable health attitudes do not translate into protective behaviors unless supported by specific knowledge and self-efficacy.[12] The high attitudinal awareness present in this community is, however, a useful foundation for educational programs, as receptivity to learning already exists.

The incorrect selection of dust as an anaphylactic trigger by 62.0% of participants reflects probable conflation of familiar upper airway conditions, allergic rhinitis and asthma, both prevalent in the southwestern Arabian Peninsula,[13] with systemic anaphylaxis. Health promotion materials for this region should explicitly draw this distinction.

Multivariable logistic regression confirmed HCP status as the strongest independent predictor of adequate anaphylaxis knowledge (aOR 2.40; P < 0.001), after controlling for all other demographic variables. This aligns with findings from Al-Qassim primary care physicians,[1415] where specialist training remained the primary driver of preparedness. The independent effect of HCP status confirms that deploying HCPs as community educators or anaphylaxis advocates is the most evidence-consistent initial strategy. Healthcare providers were also the dominant information source in this survey (70.7%), confirming their position as a critical communication channel. This finding is particularly relevant to the family medicine and primary care context: opportunistic anaphylaxis education during routine consultations represents a scalable, low-cost delivery channel that does not require additional infrastructure.

Female gender was independently associated with higher knowledge (aOR 1.97; P = 0.011). The female predominance in the sample (77.2%) is consistent with the online survey modality used across comparable Saudi studies,[45] and the gender-based knowledge difference may partly reflect differential engagement with health-related digital surveys, though other socio-cultural factors cannot be excluded. Dedicated outreach targeting male community members is advisable.

Younger age (18–30 years) was an independent protective factor, with adults aged 31–50 years (aOR 0.41) and those aged ≥51 years (aOR 0.36) having significantly lower odds of adequate knowledge after adjustment. This age gradient likely reflects greater educational engagement, digital health literacy, and proximity to university training environments among younger adults. The independent effect of student status (aOR 2.19; P = 0.013), confirming that active academic enrollment is associated with better knowledge irrespective of age, reinforces educational institutions as priority venues for structured anaphylaxis curricula.

Importantly, education level was not an independent predictor after adjustment, suggesting that it was a confounder for age and employment status in bivariate analysis. This highlights the value of multivariable analysis in Knowledge, Attitude, and Practice (KAP) studies, which commonly overinterpret bivariate associations.

The absence of a significant independent association between personal or family history of anaphylaxis and knowledge level (P = 0.065 in multivariable analysis) is an important finding. Experiential exposure to a life-threatening allergic event does not, without structured follow-up education, reliably improve preparedness. Post-anaphylactic patient education should therefore be a mandatory component of emergency department and allergy clinic follow-up protocols.

The substantial uptake of social media (46.4%) and artificial intelligence tools (33.8%) as information sources reflects contemporary health information-seeking behavior. While these platforms offer scale and accessibility, they carry the risk of propagating inaccurate content. Professionally curated, evidence-based anaphylaxis resources distributed through verified channels should form part of any regional awareness strategy.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths include a large sample (N = 702) substantially exceeding the minimum requirement, broad distribution across demographic strata achieved through multi-channel recruitment, a content-validated and internally consistent bilingual instrument (overall α =0.813) with a structured scoring framework, the inclusion of multivariable regression to identify independent predictors, and the regional originality of this dataset. Limitations include the disproportionate female representation (77.2%), which may restrict generalizability to adult males; potential under-representation of older adults with limited digital access, evidenced by the small >60 cohort (2.0%); and the cross-sectional design, which precludes causal inference. Self-reported EpiPen competency may be subject to social desirability bias. Restriction to one region limits transferability, although contextual overlap with adjacent highland Saudi populations is expected. Al-Baha’s demographic profile, predominantly young, university-educated, and with a mixed urban-rural population in a highland setting, may share characteristics with other southwestern Saudi governorates such as Bisha and Abha, suggesting that the findings have relevance beyond the immediate study region, pending replication.

Conclusion

Community knowledge of anaphylaxis in Al-Baha region is largely inadequate, with nearly two-thirds of participants scoring below the low knowledge threshold. The most severe gaps involve failing to recognize epinephrine as the primary treatment, poor auto-injector literacy, and the dangerous belief that antihistamines are an acceptable substitute. Multivariable analysis confirms that HCP status is the strongest independent predictor of adequate preparedness, followed by younger age and active student status. Education level is not an independent predictor after adjustment. Based on these findings, we recommend a multi-pronged regional approach: HCP-delivered community education sessions, anaphylaxis modules integrated into school health curricula, evidence-based digital health messaging correcting the antihistamine misconception, and structured patient education at post-anaphylaxis follow-up. Opportunistic anaphylaxis education during routine primary care and family medicine consultations represents a particularly scalable, low-cost delivery channel, given that healthcare providers were identified as the dominant information source in this community. Repeat assessment after targeted interventions is recommended to evaluate impact.
