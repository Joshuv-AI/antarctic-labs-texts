Monitoring the ramp use of cage-free laying hens with deep learning technologies
Authors: Bidur Paneru, Xiao Yang, Anjan Dhungana, Samin Dahal, Casey W Ritz, Woo Kim, Tianming Liu, Lilong Chai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMislaid eggs are management and economic challenges in Cage-free (CF) housing systems, producing about 10-15 % floor eggs, although approximately 40 % of table egg laying hens are now in CF production systems. Ramps may ease the use of nesting boxes by minimizing physical extension to hens, thereby reducing floor eggs, and increasing the number of eggs laid in the nest box. The objective of our study was to develop a deep learning method for monitoring hens’ ramp use and the potential impact of ramp access to the nest box on the number of floor eggs and the number of eggs laid in the nest boxes. A total of 600 Lohmann LSL Lite hens were raised from day 1 to 413 in three identical research rooms (7.3 m L × 6.1 m W × 3 m H) following the Lohmann LSL Lite management guide. Each study room had four nest boxes placed at the four corners of the room. Two nest boxes were provided with ramp access (R), and two nest boxes had no ramp access (NR) in each study room and were replicated among three study rooms. The ramp use was video recorded at 15 frames per second (fps). We trained two You Only Look Once (YOLO) models, YOLOv5u and YOLO11, object detection models for 200 epochs each. A total of 2,000 images were used for training (70 %), validation (20 %), and testing (10 %) for the model. All models achieved a precision, recall, and mean average precision at 0.50 intersection over union (mAP@0.50) of at least 0.94. YOLO11n(nano) achieved the highest precision (0.9940), recall (0.9934), and mAP@0.50 (0.9848). Our best model provides a baseline for automatic ramp use detection with 0.99 precision. Ramp access did not lower the floor egg production statistically (p = 0.5468). Across bird weeks, ramp access (R) and no ramp access (NR) revealed opposite patterns in floor and nest-box egg production. Ramp access generally resulted in significantly higher floor egg production in several weeks (52, 54, 58), but also produced higher nest-box egg counts in other weeks (49, 51, 52, 54, 56, 58), whereas no-ramp access showed more nest-box access in weeks when ramp access floor egg were high (48, 50, 53, 55, 57, 59). Overall, ramp access did not consistently increase or decrease egg laying in either location, but shifted the proportion of eggs between the floor and nest-boxes depending on bird age (week). Future studies are warranted to investigate the effect of ramp use on nesting behavior and floor eggs from egg laying to the end of the laying cycle. Data on floor eggs and nest box eggs with ramp access to the nest box from commercial aviary systems, as well as the CF system, also need to be compared.Keywords: Cage-free housing, nesting behavior, ramp use, floor eggs, machine vision

INTRODUCTION

Hens in conventional cages (CC) experience extreme behavioral restrictions, such as not being able to perform foraging, dustbathing, and perching (Hughes and Duncan, 1988), which are considered a key part of the normal behavioral repertoire of chickens (LayWel, 2006) and a commitment made in 2016 from wholesale and retail grocers such as Walmart and Kroger to go 100% CF by 2026 (Chai et al., 2019, United Egg Proudcers UEP, 2017; Lusk, 2019; Dong, 2025). CF housing allows hens to exhibit their natural and applied behaviors, such as scratching, foraging, dust bathing, and perching, which are impossible for the birds in CC and very few extent in battery cages (Lay et al., 2011; Shields and Greger, 2013; Stadig et al., 2015). These behaviors and activities of the hens in the CF system contribute to improved activity, leg health (as indicated by footpad dermatitis, gait score, and hock burn), and enhanced welfare compared to CC housing (Lay et al., 2011; Shields and Greger, 2013; Stadig et al., 2015; Yilmaz Dikmen et al., 2016; Hartcher and Jones, 2017).

However, CF housing is not an ideal solution as it comes with challenges such as mislaid or floor eggs (Jones et al., 2015; Oliveira et al., 2019), abnormal behaviors, including aggressive feather pecking leading to associated damages (Coton et al., 2019), pilling, and keel bone fractures. Earlier studies have focused on understanding the mislaying behavior of a CF laying hen and reported that it is a multifactorial problem. The reported rate of floor egg production in CF housing ranges up to 10-15% (Campbell, 2023), leading to concerns on how to address this challenge that contributes to food safety, egg prices in the market, and sustainable egg production.

The floor eggs are prone to contamination and damage. Their presence not only compromises egg quality and marketability but also poses food safety risks. The FDA reported that a total of sixty-five people were infected with the outbreak strain of Salmonella in eggs across nine states in the U.S. from August to September of 2024 (FDA, 2024). The manual collection of floor eggs is laborious, which increases operational costs and affects the economic viability of CF production. Different management strategies on whether floor eggs can be reduced or managed with different management practices have been reviewed by (Campbell, 2023). Some of the management strategies applied to reduce floor eggs include improving nest box design and accessibility, providing early access to nest boxes for young hens, ensuring proper lighting and environmental conditions, and training hens to use nest boxes. One of the ways to reduce floor eggs in a CF facility could be providing ramp access to nest boxes, which could minimize a physical barrier for the hens to reach nest boxes and encourage nest box use.

Previous studies have highlighted the positive impact of ramp access to nest boxes during early life and the laying period in an aviary system. Research has shown that hens reared with access to elevated structures, such as with ramps, during early life are more likely to use elevated nest boxes and lay fewer eggs on the floor (Pullin et al., 2024). The researchers found that when ramps were installed in an aviary system, hens reduced the number of flights and increased the number of walks, which helped movement between tiers. Although the percentage of nest box eggs with ramp access was not statistically higher, (Nannoni et al., 2022) highlighted that the improved mobility from ramp access suggests easier nest box access, potentially reducing floor eggs, which is biologically important. Furthermore, providing ramps during the laying period has been associated with improved hen mobility and access to resources in aviary systems, which can contribute to improved egg laying patterns and reduced floor eggs (Toscano et al., 2024). However, few studies have specifically examined how hens use ramps to access nest boxes in CF systems.

Ensuring whether hens will use the ramps or not is challenging as it requires frequent farm visits and manual observation, consuming a significant amount of time for the producers. This task can be easily and automatically detected using a machine learning approach, as precision livestock farming is gaining popularity in the poultry industry. One of the approaches is to use object detection models to detect different behaviors of the hens. Different versions of object detection models developed by Ultralytics, such as YOLO (You Only Look Once), have been used to detect CF brown laying hens (Guo et al., 2023), CF dead hens (Bist et al., 2023a), tracking floor eggs in CF facility (Subedi et al., 2023a), and monitor problematic behavior such as mislaying (Bist et al., 2023b), piling (Bist et al., 2023c), pecking (Subedi et al., 2023b) and applied behaviors such as dustbathing (Paneru et al., 2024a), and perching (Paneru et al., 2024b) as well as the spatial distribution of CF laying hens (Yang et al., 2023). There is a need to develop an automated system to monitor the ramp use by laying hens in a CF facility, and we aim to address this challenge through a machine learning approach. The target behavior to be detected in this study would be a ramp use by cage-free laying hens. In the context of our research, we define ramp use when hens use ramps to get access to the nest box and come back to the litter floor using three layers of ramps. While detecting a ramp use using image datasets, any hens standing on any of the three layers of the ramp at a given time point would be defined as a ramp use, indicating that hens utilize the ramps either to get access to nest boxes or get off the nest boxes to come back to the litter floor. This automated system will save farmers time monitoring the hens and could be applied in real-time, which will help them make better decisions and develop better management strategies when considering the use of ramps in CF housing.

Hence, we proposed to utilize two deep-learning-based object detection models, i.e. YOLOv5u based on YOLOv5 (Jocher & Glenn, 2020) and YOLO11 (Jocher et al., 2024) to compare the performance metrics (precision, recall, mean average precision; mAP, and F1-score) of the two models in detecting ramp utilization and to evaluate the number of floor eggs and number of eggs laid in nest boxes when hens have access to ramps versus without access to ramps. Therefore, our study objectives were to develop a deep learning method for monitoring hens’ ramp use and the potential impact of ramp access to the nest box on the number of floor eggs and the number of eggs laid in the nest boxes in CF houses. We hypothesize that the latest object detection model, YOLO11, will have better performance compared to earlier versions of the object detection model, YOLOv5u, and ramp access to nest boxes will increase the percentage of eggs laid in nest boxes and reduce the number of floor eggs compared to nest boxes without ramp access.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Experimental Setup

Our study was conducted in three identical poultry research rooms at the University of Georgia in Athens, GA, with each room housing 200 Lohmann LSL Lite hens. The study rooms were equipped with A-frame perches from day 1, nest boxes were added one week before the pre-laying phase, and the litter floor was covered with 5cm depth pine shavings from day 1. Hens had ad libitum access to feed and water from day 1 and were raised until day 413 in these environments. Each room measured 7.3 meters in length, 6.1 meters in width, and 3 meters in height (Figure 1). Hens were provided with 6 feeders placed at equidistant, nipple drinkers, perches, and nest boxes were provided to promote natural behaviors. Environmental conditions such as temperature, humidity, light duration and intensity, and ventilation were automatically controlled and recorded using the Chore-Tronics Model 8 System (Chore-Time Equipment, Milford, Indiana, USA). Lighting was provided 24 hours on day 1-2 (20-40 lux), 16 hours from day 3 - 6 (20-30 lux), 14 hours for week 2 (10-20 lux), reduced 1 hour each week following week 2 until week 6 for 10 hours (4-6 lux) and maintained same lighting schedule until week 17. Lighting was provided 12 hours a day (10-15 lux) for week 18 and increased by 1 hour of lighting until week 22 for 16 hours a day (10-15 lux) and followed the same schedule until the end of the production cycle. Overall, hens’ care and management were maintained, and followed by Lohmann LSL Lite management guidelines (Lohmann Tierzucht, 2016). Feed was formulated at the UGA feed mill using soybean and corn according to management nutrition guidelines, and it was produced every month to maintain freshness. The animal care and use protocol for this study was approved by the University of Georgia’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the University of Georgia (AUP#: A2023 02-024-A1).

Cage-Free Research facility at University of Georgia, Athens, GA, housing Lohmann LSL Lite hens during layer phase 2 to layer phase 3 (week 48-59), where nest boxes were provided with and without a ramp.

Treatments

Each study room was equipped with four nest boxes Medium Extended (91.44 cm) Reversible Roll Out Chicken Nest Box (up to 32 hens) of the same type placed at the four corners of the study room. Two nest boxes were provided with ramp access (R), and two nest boxes had no ramp access (NR) in each study room and were replicated among three study rooms. The nest boxes with or without ramp access were rotated weekly within each study room to avoid nest box location bias by the hens. The experiment was conducted over 12 weeks (from week 48 to week 59). Ramps were consistently placed at a 30-degree angle leading to the nest boxes to reduce a physical barrier for the birds to access the nest boxes. We use object detection models, i.e., YOLOv5u and YOLO11, to detect ramp use behavior and compare the performance metrics of the two models while we recorded the number of mislaid eggs and the number of nest box eggs manually from each treatment daily and analyzed the effect of treatment, bird week, and interaction of treatment and bird week on the number of mislaid eggs and nest box eggs separately.

Videos and Image Data Collection

Night-vision surveillance cameras (PRO-1080MSB, Swann Communications USA Inc., Santa Fe Springs, CA) were used to capture a video of the study rooms. Especially, 4 cameras on the ceiling, 3m above the litter floor, placed right above nest boxes at the four corners of each study room. The cameras continuously recorded RGB videos for 24 hours a day throughout the study period and were stored in .avi format with a resolution of 1920 × x 1080 pixels at 15 frames per second (fps). However, for image analysis purposes, data collection was done from 5:00 AM to 9:00 PM. As the birds age, dust accumulation in the CF environment becomes a huge problem, which causes video blurring. Therefore, routine camera cleaning was done while caring for the birds. All recorded videos were saved on a digital video recorder (DVR-4580, Swann Communication USA Inc., Santa Fe Springs, CA) throughout the bird’s development from starter to layer phase 3. However, we only used the videos from layer phase 2 to layer phase 3 (week 48-59), as we focused on the ramp use study during this growth phase.

Image Collection, Data Pre-processing, and Model Training

Video datasets obtained from the experiment were converted into individual image files in .jpg format using Python 3.13, at a frame extraction rate of 15 fps, and 3000 images were randomly selected. The resulting images were then manually reviewed to make sure images included ramps and birds using a ramp, and overall image quality, resulting in 2000 images. We used an open-source image labeling tool, ‘cvat.ai’, for labeling training and validation image datasets, and labeled manually by drawing bounding boxes around the hens using ramps by a single trained researcher on this work to maintain consistency while drawing bounding boxes. The hens using the ramp were labelled as ‘ramp use’ and the annotations were downloaded in YOLO format for model training (70%), validation (20%), and testing (10%) (Paneru et al., 2024a; 2024b, 2025). Images that were under testing datasets were never seen by the model during training and validation to see how the model performs on completely unseen data before. Five variants of both YOLOv5u and YOLO11 models, i.e., nano (n), small (s), medium (m), large (l), and extra-large (x) were trained on the annotated images for 200 epochs, with a batch size of 16 and a constant learning rate of 0.01. Python 3.13 was utilized for training, validation, and testing purposes. The workflow for ramp use detection using both YOLOv5u and YOLO11 models is shown below (Figure 2).

YOLOv5u Model Architecture and Features

YOLOv5u model is a cutting-edge version of the regular YOLOv5 model developed by Ultralytics (Jocher, Glenn, 2020). YOLOv5’s architecture consists of three main parts, i.e., a backbone for feature extraction, a neck for feature fusion, and a head for final object detection predictions. The model uses Cross-Stage Partial Darknet (CSPDarknet) as its backbone, Path Aggregation Network (PANet) for the neck, and the standard YOLOv3 head. The model consisted of five model variants, namely nano (nu), small (su), medium (mu), large (lu), and extra-large (xu). YOLOv5u integrates the anchor-free, objectness-free split head, an approach first introduced in YOLOv8, that greatly enhances the balance between detection accuracy and processing speed in object detection tasks. Based on its practical performance and enhanced features, YOLOv5u serves as an effective choice for delivering reliable solutions in both academic research and real-world applications. We aim to compare its performance with the latest version of the object detection model, YOLOv11, in detecting ramp use by laying hens. YOLOv5u introduces a more adaptable and dynamic detection approach, leading to improved performance across a wide range of conditions. Its key strength lies in striking a well-calibrated balance between speed and accuracy, two factors that typically conflict, which allows for real-time detection without compromising precision. In addition, YOLOv5u comes with multiple pre-trained models, making it suitable for tackling varied challenges beyond just a single use case.

YOLO11 Model Architecture and Features

YOLOv11 is the latest and pivotal model in the Ultralytics YOLO series of real-time object detection, combining speed, accuracy, and efficiency through innovations like C3k2 blocks, Cross-Stage Partial (CSP) bottleneck that employs a kernel of size 2, Spatial Pyramid Pooling Fast (SPPF) inspired by YOLOv8, and Cross-Stage Partial with Spatial Attention (C2PSA), which integrates spatial attention into feature extraction process in the backbone (Jocher et al., 2024). The model architecture is composed of three main components: the backbone, neck, and head. Backbone serves as the core feature extractor, using convolutional neural networks to convert the input image into multi-scale feature representations. The neck acts as a processing bridge, combining layers designed to enhance and fuse features from various scales. Finally, the head generates the model’s prediction, delivering precise object localization and classification using the refined features. The model has five model variants, namely nano (n), small (s), medium (m), large (l), and extra-large (x). Model Evaluation Metrics

To evaluate the model performance, we calculated precision, recall, mean average precision (mAP), and F1 score as the key indicators. Precision (Eq. 1) refers to how well the model correctly identifies hens using ramps without including incorrect predictions. It is calculated as the ratio of true positive predictions, such as true detected hens using the ramp, to the total number of positive predictions made.

Recall (Eq. 2) shows the model’s capability to correctly predict all relevant positive instances within the datasets. It is calculated as the ratio of true positive predictions, such as true detected hens using the ramp, to the total number of actual positive instances in the dataset.

Mean average precision (mAP) (Eq. 3) is a crucial evaluation metric that measures the model’s detection performance across different classes. It uses an intersection over union threshold of 0.50 and a broader range from 0.50 to 0.95 (mAP@0.50-0.95).

Within this equation, APi signifies the average precision of the ith category, and C represents the total number of categories.

The F1 score (Eq. 4) is a vital metric in object detection, standing for the harmonic mean of both precision and recall as shown in equation 4. A higher F1 score indicates better detection performance. When the F1 score is 1, it signifies that object (i.e., hens that were using the ramp) detection is highly accurate without any negative outcomes.

Training and Validation Loss Function

Both YOLOv5u and YOLO11 models utilize a custom loss function called ‘YOLO Loss’ during training and validation. This loss function combines several components to penalize incorrect predictions of hens using ramps and enhance accuracy. The YOLOv5u and YOLO11 loss is a weighted sum of box loss, classification loss, and distribution focal loss (dfl), with the importance of each decided through backpropagation and gradient descent to reduce prediction errors and improve model performance. Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed in JMP Pro 17 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) using generalized linear mixed models (GLMM) to account for repeated measures and random block effects. The response variables were the weekly counts of floor eggs and nestbox eggs, analyzed separately. The experimental design was specified as a randomized block design, with treatment (ramp vs. no ramp), week (12 levels), and their interaction as fixed effects, and room (3 levels) as a random effect. The week was treated as a repeated measure within each room × treatment combination. Models were fitted using a mixed model personality with alternative covariance structures [compound symmetry (CS), first-order autoregressive (AR(1)), and unstructured (UN)] compared based on Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC). The covariance structure yielding the lowest AIC was selected for final inference. Statistical significance was declared at P ≤ 0.05. Results are expressed in least square means (LSMeans) ± standard error (SE) of the number of eggs (floor eggs or nestbox eggs) per week.

Effect on Floor Eggs

Both treatments showed fluctuating number of floor eggs (F(1,466) = 0.35,p=0.56) with LSMeans ± SE: Ramp (R) = 24.04 ± 3.60 and No Ramp (NR) = 26.81 ± 3.60 over time. Week had a significant effect on the number of floor eggs produced (F(11,466) = 1.94, p = 0.033), showing variation across weeks with LSMeans ± SE ranging from 23.69 ± 2.72 in week 49 to 33.85 ± 2.72 in week 59. There was a highly significant treatment × week interaction on the number of floor eggs produced (F(11,466) = 3.75, p < 0.0001), suggesting that the effect of treatment differed across weeks, and the LSMeans and Standard error for treatment × week interaction are reported in Table 3 and Figure 9.

The possible reasons behind the higher number of floor eggs with ramp access in weeks 52, 54, and 58 might be the combination of the nesting preference of the hens (hens preferred nest box without ramp access in those weeks) and preferred the isolated and dark space under the nest box with ramp access as ramp access didn’t only facilitate hens to lay eggs on the nest boxes but rather created secured and isolated dark space to lay eggs below the nest box in the litter floor.

No ramp access (NR) resulted significantly higher number of nest-box egg in weeks 48, 50, 53, 55, 57, and 59, while Ramp access resulted significantly higher number of nest-box egg in weeks 49,51, 52, 54, 56, and 58. This indicates that ramp access did not consistently increase or decrease the number of nest-box egg laying across weeks; instead, its effect was week-dependent. The data suggest that bot management practices influence how consistently hens lay eggs in the nest box, with NR sometimes favoring nest use and R sometimes favoring nest use. One of the reasons why hens lay a greater number of eggs in the nest box without ramp access could be due to the gregarious nesting behavior of the hens, which means hens prefer to lay eggs in a certain nest-box even though it is occupied by other hens and no facilities such as ramp access are provided. It could be also because hens has established nesting preference by the time this study was started at week 48 as these hens were provided a nesting box from week 17 and started laying eggs from week 18.

This result was opposite to our expectation, and it could be due to the fact that ramps created a more isolated and secure space under the nest box for the hens to lay eggs, as our nest box was placed at 45 cm height from the litter floor, and ramps were placed at 30°angle to the nestboxes. A previous study that looked at the egg deposition pattern and animal welfare indicators in a three-tier aviary system with different ramp settings also found no significant effect on egg deposition pattern, egg quality, or keel bone damage with ramp access (Nannoni et al., 2022) and reported that hens used the ramps when available. Another study reported that hens that received ramp access during the laying period in an aviary system had lower keel bone fractures, greater feather coverage, and better footpad health without any negative impacts on production than those that did not receive ramp access (Toscano et al., 2024). In this study, the height of nest boxes was different from commercial CF houses, where the nest boxes are in a much higher location (e.g., 2 m), requiring hens on the litter floor to travel a short distance to access the nesting space. The studies of ramp use and hens’ nesting behaviors in the commercial aviary system are needed as a comparison.

CONCLUSIONS

Our study focused on utilizing object detection models based on deep learning developed by Ultralytics to automatically detect ramp use of CF laying hens and assessed the impact of ramp access to nest boxes on the number of floor eggs and the number of eggs laid in the nest boxes. Small variants of YOLO11 (YOLO11n) models stand out among the models used in the study to detect ramp use automatically with the highest precision (0.9940), recall (0.9934), mAP@0.50 (0.9848), and F1-score of 1. All models used in the study achieved at least a precision, recall, and mAP@0.50 of 0.94, suggesting higher precision than in a study that utilized different versions of YOLO models for behavior and welfare indicator detection in the past. Our findings provide a baseline for automatically detecting ramp use in a CF scenario with a precision of 0.99 and open the opportunity to track ramp use and nesting behavior in the near future. Ramp access (R) shifted the location of egg laying rather than consistently increasing or decreasing total eggs, producing a greater number of floor eggs in some weeks and a greater number of nest-box eggs in others. No-ramp access (NR) tended to show the opposite pattern, with higher number of nest-box use in weeks when ramp access (R) hens lay more floor eggs, showing that the effect of ramps depends on birds’ age (week).
