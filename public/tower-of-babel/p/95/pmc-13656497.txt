Association between preoperative sleep quality and postoperative nausea and vomiting in patients undergoing laparoscopic cholecystectomy: a prospective observational study
Authors: Turgay Atay, Mehmet Akif Yazar, Mustafa Aydemir, Ömer Keklicek, Betül Özçelik, Muhammed Ali Akbulut
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPostoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) remains a common and distressing complication after surgery. Although established risk factors are well defined, the role of preoperative sleep quality has not been fully elucidated. This study aimed to evaluate the association between preoperative sleep quality and PONV in patients undergoing laparoscopic cholecystectomy.MethodsThis single-center prospective observational study included 145 patients undergoing elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy. Preoperative sleep quality was assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) one day before surgery. PONV risk was evaluated using the Apfel score. The incidence of PONV within the first 24 h postoperatively was recorded, and nausea severity was assessed using a numeric rating scale (NRS). Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of PONV.ResultsOverall, PONV occurred in 119 of 145 patients (82.1%, 95% CI 75.0–87.5). Vomiting occurred in 48 patients (33.1%). Patients who developed PONV had significantly higher PSQI scores than those who did not (median 6 vs. 3, p < 0.001). A PSQI score ≥ 5 was more frequent among patients with PONV (70.6% vs. 26.9%, p < 0.001). PSQI scores were moderately correlated with postoperative nausea severity (ρ = 0.626, p < 0.001). In multivariable analysis, PSQI score remained independently associated with PONV (OR 1.33, 95% CI 1.12–1.58, p = 0.001), whereas the Apfel score was not statistically significant.ConclusionsPreoperative sleep quality was independently associated with both the occurrence and severity of PONV. These findings suggest that sleep quality may represent a relevant factor associated with early postoperative outcomes. However, further studies with broader adjustment for potential confounders, external validation, and formal evaluation of predictive performance are needed before PSQI can be incorporated into routine PONV risk assessment.Trial registrationNot applicable. Background

Postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) remains one of the most frequently encountered complications in the postoperative period. Its incidence is estimated to be approximately 30% in the general surgical population and may rise to 70–80% in high-risk patients [1]. Beyond being a distressing symptom, PONV is associated with decreased patient satisfaction, prolonged post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) stay, unanticipated hospital admissions, and increased healthcare costs [2].

Risk factors for PONV can be broadly categorized into patient-related, surgical, and anesthesia-related components. Female sex, younger age, non-smoking status, and a history of motion sickness or previous PONV are well-established patient-related predictors. In addition, certain procedures—such as laparoscopic, gynecological, bariatric, and cholecystectomy surgeries—are associated with a higher risk. From an anesthetic perspective, volatile agents, nitrous oxide (N2O), and postoperative opioid use are recognized contributors, whereas the roles of factors such as anxiety, menstrual cycle, and fasting duration remain less clearly defined [3].

Sleep is essential for maintaining physiological homeostasis, including immune regulation, metabolic balance, and neurocognitive function. Impaired sleep quality has been linked to increased pain sensitivity, cardiovascular instability, and delayed recovery. In the perioperative setting, poor sleep has been associated with adverse outcomes such as postoperative delirium, heightened pain perception, prolonged hospitalisation, and impaired recovery. Despite growing attention to postoperative sleep disturbances, preoperative sleep quality remains relatively under-recognised in clinical practice. Nevertheless, a considerable proportion of patients report inadequate sleep prior to surgery, often accompanied by increased anxiety levels [4]. Evidence also suggests that preoperative anxiety is associated with a higher risk of PONV [5], suggesting that sleep disturbances may represent a potential contributing factor.

Preoperative sleep impairment may increase susceptibility to PONV through multiple mechanisms, including alterations in central neurotransmitter systems—particularly serotonergic pathways—and amplification of the physiological stress response. Given the established role of serotonin receptors and neuroendocrine activation in the pathophysiology of PONV, this association is biologically plausible [6]. Nevertheless, direct clinical evidence linking preoperative sleep quality to PONV remains limited.

Based on this background, we hypothesized that poorer preoperative sleep quality would be associated with a higher incidence of PONV. The primary aim of this study was to evaluate the relationship between preoperative sleep quality and PONV in patients undergoing laparoscopic cholecystectomy. Secondary objectives were to assess postoperative nausea severity, to examine the association between Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) scores and nausea severity, and to determine whether PSQI was independently associated with PONV after adjustment for the Apfel score, as well as its relationship with preoperative anxiety.

Methods

Study design and ethics

This prospective, single-center observational study was conducted between September 2025 and February 2026 at Konya City Hospital, University of Health Sciences, in patients scheduled for laparoscopic cholecystectomy. The study was carried out in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant national and international ethical guidelines. Ethical approval was obtained from the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the University of Health Sciences, Konya City Hospital (approval number: 2025/176; date: 08 September 2025). All participants were informed about the study objectives, procedures, and potential risks and benefits. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to enrollment. This study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement.

Patient selection

A homogeneous surgical population with a moderate-to-high risk of PONV was targeted by including patients undergoing laparoscopic cholecystectomy. Eligible participants were adults aged 18–65 years scheduled for elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy under general anesthesia, with an expected postoperative follow-up of at least 24 h. All patients were classified as American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I-II, were able to provide written informed consent, and had sufficient cognitive ability and Turkish literacy to complete the PSQI questionnaire.

Exclusion criteria included emergency surgery, a history of neurological or psychiatric disorders (e.g., major depression, bipolar disorder, or diagnosed sleep disorders), regular use of sleep medications or sedative–hypnotic agents, current treatment with anxiolytics, antipsychotics, or antidepressants, preoperative use of antiemetic drugs, and the use of anesthetic techniques other than general anesthesia. Patients with severe renal, hepatic, or cardiac insufficiency, those who developed intraoperative complications (e.g., perforation or significant bleeding), and those who declined to participate were also excluded.

Anesthesia and analgesia protocol

All patients were instructed to fast for 6–8 h prior to surgery. All procedures were performed by experienced consultant surgeons from the same hepatobiliary surgery unit. Anesthetic management followed a standardized institutional protocol and was delivered by attending anesthesiologists experienced in laparoscopic surgery. Upon arrival in the operating room, standard monitoring was established, including electrocardiography (ECG), peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2), and noninvasive blood pressure (NIBP). General anesthesia was induced with intravenous (IV) midazolam 2 mg, fentanyl 1 µg/kg, propofol 2 mg/kg, and rocuronium 1 mg/kg. Following induction, tracheal intubation was performed, and patients were mechanically ventilated in volume-controlled mode with a tidal volume of 8 mL/kg. The respiratory rate was adjusted to maintain end-tidal carbon dioxide (EtCO2) between 35 and 45 mmHg. Anesthesia was maintained with sevoflurane (0.8-1.0 minimum alveolar concentration [MAC]) and a remifentanil infusion (0.15–0.2 µg/kg/min). A fresh gas mixture of 50% oxygen and 50% air was administered at a flow rate of 2 L/min. N2O was not used.

For intraoperative analgesia, tramadol (1 mg/kg IV) and paracetamol (10 mg/kg IV) were administered. All patients received ondansetron (4 mg IV) at the end of surgery for PONV prophylaxis. At the completion of surgery, anesthetic agents were discontinued, and patients were extubated and transferred to the PACU for continuous monitoring.

In the PACU, patients were evaluated for PONV within the first postoperative hour. In cases of nausea or vomiting, dexamethasone (4 mg IV) was administered as rescue antiemetic therapy.

Postoperative analgesia consisted of paracetamol (1 g IV every 8 h) during the first 24 h. Rescue analgesia was provided with tramadol (1 mg/kg IV) as required within the same period.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of the study was the incidence of PONV within the first 24 h after surgery. PONV was defined as the presence of any nausea (numeric rating scale [NRS] > 0) and/or vomiting during this period.

Secondary outcomes were postoperative nausea severity measured using the NRS, the correlation between PSQI score and nausea severity, PONV incidence and nausea severity according to PSQI categories (≥ 5 vs. < 5), the independent association of PSQI score with PONV after adjustment for the Apfel score, and the correlation between PSQI and Amsterdam Preoperative Anxiety and Information Scale (APAIS) anxiety scores.

Measurements

Demographic and clinical data, including age, sex, height, weight, ASA physical status, comorbidities, smoking status, and current medications, were recorded during the preoperative period.

Preoperative sleep quality was assessed at least one day before surgery using the PSQI [7, 25]. The validated Turkish version of the PSQI was used in this study. The PSQI is a self-reported instrument consisting of 19 items, with total scores ranging from 0 to 21, where higher scores indicate poorer sleep quality.

The Apfel score was calculated for all patients to estimate baseline risk for PONV. The Apfel score consists of four components: female sex, non-smoking status, a history of PONV or motion sickness, and postoperative opioid use. Each component is assigned one point, resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 4 [1].

The occurrence of PONV was recorded during the first 24 h postoperatively based on patient self-reports and nursing documentation. Nausea severity was quantified using an 11-point numeric rating scale (NRS; 0 = no nausea, 10 = worst imaginable nausea), as commonly used in PONV assessment [8]. The occurrence of vomiting was also recorded. Although the number of vomiting episodes was intended to be recorded, these data were not consistently available for all patients and were therefore not included in the analysis. PONV outcomes were obtained from routine PACU and ward nursing records. The nursing staff responsible for postoperative assessments were not involved in the administration of the PSQI and did not have access to patients’ PSQI scores.

Preoperative anxiety was assessed one day before surgery using the APAIS [9, 26]. The validated Turkish version of the APAIS was used in this study. Completion of the APAIS required additional time and direct patient interaction in the preoperative setting. Due to constraints related to clinical workflow and patient preparation, APAIS assessment could not be completed in all participants. Consequently, APAIS data were available for 92 patients, and analyses involving anxiety were performed using complete-case data without imputation. To evaluate the potential impact of missing APAIS data, demographic characteristics, PSQI scores, and PONV incidence were compared between patients with and without APAIS assessments.

Sample size calculation

Sample size estimation was based on the primary analytical objective of evaluating the association between preoperative sleep quality and PONV using multivariable logistic regression analysis.

Given the limited availability of prior data directly examining this relationship, an events-per-variable (EPV) approach was adopted rather than a conventional effect size–based power calculation. Based on a reported PONV incidence of 31.1% following laparoscopic cholecystectomy [10], and considering two variables to be included in the model (preoperative sleep quality and Apfel score), a minimum of approximately 20 events per variable was targeted.

Accordingly, a total sample size of 132 patients was estimated to provide an adequate number of outcome events. To account for potential missing data and loss to follow-up, an additional 10% was included, resulting in a planned sample size of 145 patients.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range [IQR]), as appropriate, while categorical variables were expressed as number (percentage) [n (%)].

Between-group comparisons were performed using the independent samples t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables, as appropriate. Effect sizes were calculated as r for the Mann–Whitney U test (r = Z/√N) and as Cramér’s V for chi-square analyses.

Correlations between continuous variables were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient.

To evaluate the independent association between preoperative sleep quality and PONV, a multivariable binary logistic regression model was constructed. The PSQI score was entered as the main predictor variable, and the Apfel score was included as a covariate to adjust for established PONV risk factors. The number of outcome events was considered adequate relative to the number of predictor variables included in the model.

Results

A total of 165 patients were assessed for eligibility, of whom 9 were excluded prior to inclusion (5 did not meet the inclusion criteria and 4 declined to participate). Thus, 156 patients were enrolled. Subsequently, 11 patients were excluded after inclusion due to intraoperative or early postoperative complications (n = 4) or case cancellations or incomplete follow-up (n = 7), leaving 145 patients for the final analysis (Fig. 1).

The incidence of PONV was 82.1% (119/145; 95% CI 75.0–87.5). Vomiting occurred in 48 patients (33.1%; 95% CI 26.0–41.1). Among patients with PONV, 71 experienced nausea without vomiting, whereas 48 experienced both nausea and vomiting. No patient experienced vomiting in the absence of nausea. Rescue antiemetic therapy was required in 55 patients (37.9%).

For the primary outcome, PSQI scores were significantly higher in patients who developed PONV compared with those who did not (6 [4–10] vs. 3 [2–5.5], p < 0.001). In categorical analysis, a PSQI score ≥ 5 was more common among patients with PONV than among those without PONV (70.6% vs. 26.9%, p < 0.001), with a moderate effect size (r = 0.34; Cramér’s V = 0.35) (Table 2).

In univariable logistic regression analysis, PSQI score was significantly associated with PONV (OR 1.35, 95% CI 1.15–1.59, p < 0.001), whereas the Apfel score was not (OR 1.43, 95% CI 0.86–2.38, p = 0.171). In multivariable analysis, PSQI score remained independently associated with PONV (OR 1.33, 95% CI 1.12–1.58, p = 0.001), whereas the Apfel score was not significantly associated with PONV (OR 1.50, 95% CI 0.84–2.66, p = 0.170) (Table 3). Each one-point increase in PSQI score was associated with a 33% increase in the odds of PONV.

Exploratory receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis demonstrated an AUC of 0.580 (95% CI 0.455–0.700) for the Apfel score, 0.757 (95% CI 0.651–0.854) for PSQI, and 0.761 (95% CI 0.648–0.859) for the combined Apfel + PSQI model. These AUC values should be interpreted as apparent, in-sample estimates, as they were derived from the same dataset used for model evaluation. No bootstrap-based optimism correction or split-sample internal validation was performed; therefore, the AUC estimates may be optimistic. These findings suggest greater discrimination for PSQI than for the Apfel score alone, whereas the combined model demonstrated only a modest increase in discrimination compared with PSQI alone (Fig. 4).

In a sensitivity analysis using clinically meaningful nausea (NRS ≥4) as the outcome, 103 of 145 patients (71.0%) met this criterion. Patients with NRS ≥4 had significantly higher PSQI scores than those with NRS <4 (8 [5–11] vs 3 [2–6], p < 0.001). Poor sleep quality (PSQI ≥5) was also more frequent among patients with NRS ≥4 than among those with NRS <4 (76.7% vs 28.6%, p < 0.001). These findings suggest that the association between preoperative sleep quality and postoperative symptoms remained consistent when a more clinically meaningful definition of nausea was applied.

No significant differences were observed in surgical duration, anesthesia duration, intraoperative fluid administration, remifentanil dose, or postoperative rescue opioid requirement between patients with and without PONV (Table 4). Although postoperative rescue opioid use was numerically higher in patients without PONV than in those with PONV, this difference was not statistically significant. This finding should be interpreted cautiously, as rescue opioids may have been withheld or avoided in patients with active nausea or vomiting, reflecting postoperative clinical decision-making rather than a true inverse association.

Discussion

In this prospective observational study, preoperative sleep quality was significantly associated with both the incidence and severity of PONV in patients undergoing laparoscopic cholecystectomy. Importantly, PSQI scores remained significantly associated with PONV in multivariable analysis, highlighting the potential relevance of sleep quality in early postoperative outcomes.

The incidence of PONV in our cohort was 82.1%, which is higher than that reported in many previous studies. This relatively high incidence may be attributable to both methodological and clinical factors. In the present study, PONV was defined as any occurrence of nausea (NRS > 0) and/or vomiting within the first 24 h postoperatively, thereby capturing even minimal symptoms [11, 12]. This inclusive definition differs from more restrictive approaches that consider only moderate-to-severe or treatment-requiring events and may partly explain the higher incidence. Given the characteristics of the study population, all patients underwent laparoscopic cholecystectomy, a procedure inherently associated with a high risk of PONV. In addition, the predominance of established risk factors, including female sex, non-smoking status, perioperative opioid exposure, and intraoperative remifentanil administration, indicates that the cohort represented a moderate-to-high risk population [8, 13, 14]. Furthermore, although all patients received ondansetron prophylaxis, contemporary consensus guidelines generally recommend multimodal antiemetic prophylaxis for patients with multiple risk factors. Therefore, the use of single-agent prophylaxis in this high-risk cohort may also have contributed to the observed incidence.

The association between preoperative sleep quality and PONV may be explained by the interaction of several physiological mechanisms. Poor sleep quality has been linked to increased hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis activity and altered serotonergic signaling, both of which may influence susceptibility to nausea and vomiting [15]. In addition, pneumoperitoneum, a defining feature of laparoscopic surgery, induces neuroendocrine stress responses and enhances vagal afferent stimulation through peritoneal stretching [6]. In patients with impaired sleep, this additional perioperative stress may exert a synergistic effect on central emetic pathways.

Furthermore, sleep disturbance has been associated with heightened pain sensitivity and increased analgesic requirements [16]. Given the well-established role of opioid use as a risk factor for PONV [1], this relationship may indirectly contribute to increased susceptibility to PONV. Although studies directly evaluating the relationship between sleep quality and PONV are limited, existing literature indicates that sleep disturbances are associated with adverse postoperative outcomes, including increased pain, anxiety, and delayed recovery [17]. Similarly, higher preoperative PSQI scores have been linked to other negative perioperative outcomes such as postoperative delirium in major non-cardiac surgery [18]. Overall, our findings are consistent with the existing literature and suggest that preoperative sleep quality may represent a clinically relevant factor associated with PONV that warrants further investigation.

PONV should be evaluated not only as a dichotomous outcome but also in terms of symptom severity, which has important clinical implications [11]. In this context, preoperative sleep quality may be associated not only with the occurrence of PONV but also with the intensity of symptoms. Although direct clinical evidence linking sleep quality to PONV severity is limited, sleep disturbances have been associated with greater symptom burden and adverse postoperative outcomes [16, 19]. In the present study, a moderate and statistically significant correlation was observed between preoperative sleep quality and nausea severity (ρ = 0.626, p < 0.001). Furthermore, patients with poor sleep quality (PSQI ≥ 5) exhibited higher nausea severity compared to those with good sleep quality (median NRS: 6 [5–7] vs. 4 [0–5], p < 0.001).

Taken together, our findings support an association between preoperative sleep quality and both the occurrence and severity of PONV [16, 20]. Accordingly, assessment of sleep quality in the preoperative period may provide additional information regarding both the occurrence and severity of PONV. However, further studies evaluating predictive performance and external validity are required before sleep quality can be incorporated into routine PONV risk stratification strategies [8].

The use of multivariable models in predicting PONV risk is widely accepted as an important approach for evaluating the independent contributions of different risk factors [21, 22]. In this context, our multivariable analysis demonstrated that PSQI score remained significantly associated with PONV when evaluated alongside the Apfel score. In contrast, the lack of a significant association for the Apfel score within the same model represents a noteworthy finding. However, this observation is more likely related to the characteristics of the study population rather than a limitation of the well-established predictive value of the Apfel score [1, 8].

The inclusion of a homogeneous patient population undergoing the same surgical procedure, together with a standardized anesthesia protocol and routine antiemetic prophylaxis, may have reduced variability among established risk factors [1]. Furthermore, most patients shared several well-established PONV risk factors, including female sex (76.6%), laparoscopic surgery, and perioperative opioid exposure, which may have further restricted variability in baseline risk. In patient populations with an inherently elevated baseline risk of PONV, such as those undergoing laparoscopic cholecystectomy, this relative homogeneity may limit the discriminative performance of traditional risk stratification tools, including the Apfel score. In our cohort, Apfel scores were clustered within a relatively narrow range, with 94.5% of patients having scores between 0 and 2, only 5.5% having a score of 3, and none having a score of 4. This limited variability may have further reduced the discriminative performance of the Apfel score in this population. Accordingly, our findings suggest that preoperative sleep quality, as assessed by the PSQI, was independently associated with PONV, even within a relatively homogeneous high-risk cohort.

Although the present study was not designed as a prediction-model development study, exploratory ROC analysis demonstrated greater discrimination for PSQI than for the Apfel score alone (AUC 0.757 vs. 0.580). The combined PSQI + Apfel model yielded an AUC of 0.761, indicating only a modest increase in discrimination compared with PSQI alone. However, these AUC values represent apparent, in-sample estimates and were not adjusted for optimism using bootstrap resampling or split-sample internal validation. Because external validation and formal model-performance evaluation were beyond the scope of this study, these findings should be considered exploratory and should not be interpreted as sufficient evidence for routine clinical implementation.

Consistent with existing evidence, female sex, non-smoking status, a history of PONV or motion sickness, and postoperative opioid use remain well-established risk factors for PONV [8, 13]. However, sleep quality is not currently included in standard risk stratification tools. The independent association observed between PSQI score and PONV in our study suggests that sleep quality may provide additional information beyond traditional risk factors. Accordingly, preoperative sleep quality may represent a complementary factor that warrants further investigation in future studies.

The potential role of preoperative anxiety should be considered when interpreting the relationship between sleep quality and PONV. In the present study, APAIS data were available only for a subset of patients, limiting a comprehensive evaluation of the interaction between anxiety, sleep quality, and PONV. A weak but statistically significant correlation was observed between PSQI and APAIS scores, whereas no significant differences in anxiety levels were identified between sleep quality groups. This may indicate that sleep quality and anxiety represent related but partially distinct aspects of perioperative vulnerability.

Previous studies have reported associations between preoperative anxiety and adverse postoperative outcomes, including increased pain and, in some cases, a higher incidence of PONV [23, 24]. In contrast, no clear association between anxiety and PONV was observed in our cohort, while sleep quality remained significantly associated with PONV. This finding suggests that sleep quality may contribute additional information beyond psychological factors alone. From a clinical perspective, preoperative sleep quality assessment may provide additional information beyond traditional risk factors. However, our findings are not sufficient to justify routine modifications of antiemetic prophylaxis based solely on PSQI scores. At present, sleep quality assessment is not incorporated into our standard preoperative evaluation, and prophylactic antiemetic strategies are guided by established risk factors and current consensus guidelines. Nevertheless, these findings support further investigation of sleep quality as a potential complementary factor in future studies of PONV and perioperative outcomes.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, sleep quality was assessed using the PSQI, a self-reported instrument that may be subject to measurement bias. Similarly, the assessment of PONV was partly based on patient-reported outcomes, which may have led to underreporting or misclassification of mild symptoms. Second, the observational design of the study limits the ability to draw causal inferences. Although adjustment was performed using the Apfel score, additional factors such as age, sex, body mass index, perioperative opioid exposure, pain severity, surgical duration, anesthesia duration, and anxiety may also influence the risk of PONV. While no significant differences were observed in several perioperative variables examined in the present study, residual confounding cannot be excluded, and the observed associations should therefore be interpreted with caution. Third, the single-center design, relatively modest sample size, incomplete APAIS data, and reliance on a single preoperative sleep assessment may limit the generalizability and external validity of the findings. Furthermore, because the PSQI evaluates sleep quality over the preceding month, the results primarily reflect habitual preoperative sleep quality rather than sleep on the night immediately before surgery. Although the sample size was determined using an events-per-variable approach and the number of outcome events was sufficient to support the multivariable logistic regression model, the limited number of predictors included in the analysis should be considered when interpreting the adjusted estimates. Finally, the ROC-derived AUC values should be interpreted as apparent in-sample estimates, as no bootstrap-based optimism correction, split-sample internal validation, or external validation was performed.

Conclusion

Poorer preoperative sleep quality was independently associated with higher odds of PONV and greater postoperative nausea severity in this single-center cohort of patients undergoing laparoscopic cholecystectomy. These findings suggest that sleep quality may represent a relevant factor associated with early postoperative outcomes. However, given the observational design, the possibility of residual confounding, and the lack of external validation, the results should be interpreted with caution. Further studies with broader adjustment for potential confounders, external validation, and formal predictive-performance analyses are needed before PSQI can be incorporated into routine PONV risk assessment.
