The Impact of Endoscopic Ultrasound and Multidisciplinary Team Evaluation on the Management of Pancreatic Cystic Lesions
Authors: Mohamad Dbouk, Osama Altayar, Mark J. Hoegger, Juan Reyes Genere, Arvind Rengarajan, Vincent M. Mellnick, Anup S. Shetty, Linda Chu, Atif Zaheer, Ghazal Zandieh, Vladimir Kushnir, Gabriel Lang, Michael Goggins, William Hawkins, Adeel Khan, William Burns, Kelly Lafaro, Kian‐Huat Lim, Katrina Pederson, Elham Afghani, Koushik Das
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundRetrospective evaluations of multidisciplinary team (MDT) review of pancreatic cystic lesions (PCL) demonstrate improved PCL management. Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) evaluates PCL for worrisome features; however, its influence on MDT is unclear and may be attenuated by anchoring/confirmation bias. We aimed to evaluate the impact of EUS and the overall performance of MDT evaluation of PCL.MethodsFour mock MDT sessions at two institutions were presented imaging from 40 consecutive PCL cases (20 “high‐risk” with advanced neoplasia, 20 “low‐risk” with pathologic low‐grade dysplasia or ≥ 3 years of non‐progression on surveillance), with and without upfront EUS/FNA (cytology, CEA, Amylase), in a randomized, crossover design. Group 1 adjudicated without EUS, Group 2 adjudicated with EUS, and Group 3 adjudicated with EUS after initially adjudicating without it.ResultsThe cohort included branch‐duct (n = 20), mixed type (n = 6), and main duct (n = 4) IPMN, MCN (n = 5), and non‐mucinous PCL (n = 5). Group 2 (EUS) correctly identified mucinous PCL with 97% sensitivity and 70% specificity, significantly higher than Group 1 (No EUS) (Sensitivity 91%, Specificity 20%; p = 0.04). Group 3 (Unblinded to EUS) performed significantly worse in mucinous PCL identification compared with Group 2, indicating cognitive bias (Sensitivity 97%, Specificity 10% (p = 0.0003)). Group 2 correctly identified high‐risk PCL that required surgical intervention with 85% sensitivity and 85% specificity, significantly higher than Group 1 (Sensitivity 60%, Specificity 75%; p = 0.0016) and Group 3 (Sensitivity 73%, Specificity 80%, p = 0.0485).ConclusionWhile susceptible to cognitive bias, EUS significantly improves MDT assessment of PCL. MDT establishes a high bar for iterative additions of biomarkers to PCL analysis.

Introduction

Pancreatic cystic lesions (PCL) are frequently, incidentally diagnosed in up to 26%–38% of patients above the age of 70 undergoing abdominal imaging [1]. While pancreatic resection remains potentially curative, the vast majority of these lesions are indolent [2, 3, 4], and surgery may carry a 1%–4% mortality and 40%–50% adverse event rate [5]. Thus, segregating patients who carry high‐risk versus low‐risk lesions that would benefit from early surgical resection is of critical importance. While several, management guidelines for PCLs exist [4, 6, 7, 8], their performance remains only fair in validation studies [9, 10] in part due to their reliance on imperfect clinical/imaging factors, which may be highly specific but insensitive (e.g., jaundice), or highly sensitive but non‐specific (e.g., PCL size). Thus, the principal of an expert multidisciplinary team (MDT) approach to PCL management is to integrate these imperfect data elements to optimize the prediction of PCL type and malignant potential.

An MDT approach involves expert radiologists, hepatobiliary surgeons, gastroenterologists, and variably oncologists/pathologists who review clinical history, cross‐sectional imaging, and endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) in a conference to develop consensus on a diagnosis and management plan. Such MDT approaches are widely deployed, and have been shown to have superior diagnostic accuracy for PCL stratification when compared to conventional non‐MDT approaches [11, 12, 13] or algorithmic application of guidelines [9, 10, 14]. While EUS is invaluable in evaluating PCL for International Association of Pancreatology (IAP) “worrisome” or “high‐risk” features [15] and sampling cyst fluid with fine needle aspiration (FNA) for carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), amylase and cytology, the accuracy of EUS alone to discriminate between benign versus malignant and mucinous versus non‐mucinous lesions ranges widely, contributing to variances in clinical guidelines for recommending EUS FNA [8, 16]. Additionally, as with any imperfect clinical piece of data, EUS/cyst fluid results may be susceptible to anchoring and other bias, either elevated when confirming or discounted when refuting a pre‐held diagnostic belief.

Utilizing a multicenter, mock‐MDT, blinded review of confirmed PCL cases, we assessed the overall accuracy of this approach to identify PCL type, malignant potential, and appropriateness for surgical resection. In addition, utilizing crossover analysis, we assessed both the marginal value of EUS imaging/sampling data with “traditional” cyst fluid analysis (CEA, amylase, cytology) in MDT assessments and the potential for anchoring bias when EUS data was not initially available.

Methods

Development of Patient Cohorts

Consecutive patients with PCL presented (1/2016–8/2021) at the weekly MDT at Washington University were identified. Cases were selected consecutively to eliminate bias from recall or surgical referral (Figure S1). After a power calculation (Supplementary Methods), a total of 40 patients, 20 high‐risk (pathologic confirmation of advanced neoplasia (high grade dysplasia or cancer) or the development of interval evident PC) and 20 low‐risk (resection with pathology demonstrating low grade dysplasia, pancreatic pseudocyst, or other non‐malignant pathology or “clinically benign” lesions with ≥ 3 years of serial imaging follow‐up without evidence of malignant transformation) was developed. Cases were required to have imaging/clinical follow‐up data, EUS evaluation at Washington University, and MRI data available. During the study period, PCL FNA was only routinely sent for CEA, amylase, and cytology at the institution. The patients were divided into two groups (cohorts 1 and 2) of 20 cases each as described below, with 10 patients having high‐risk lesions and 10 having low‐risk lesions in each cohort. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at Washington University in Saint Louis.

Study Design

A multicenter, case control, blinded study was conducted in which four mock MDT conferences were performed to review the cases described above (Figure 1). Four unique MDT sessions (2 from Washington University (MDT A, B) and 2 from Johns Hopkins Hospital (MDT C, D)) were held. Each session included a different gastroenterologist, radiologist, and hepatobiliary surgeon, all with significant expertise in PCL and PC management, who routinely participated in their institutions' MDT conference. No one involved in the preparation of the cases was involved in the mock MDT conferences. The participants were not aware of the number of high‐ or low‐risk cases included in each cohort. For the first session at each institution (MDT A, C), cohort 1 was presented with EUS data up front, and cohort 2 was presented without EUS data. For the second MDT session (MDT B, D), cohort 2 was presented with upfront EUS data, and cohort 1 was presented without EUS data. For the 20 cases presented at each MDT without upfront EUS, after a consensus was reached by the group, EUS results were provided to determine if the EUS data affected their decision. Thus, the 40 cases were presented in three groups with 120 evaluations—Group 1 (No EUS), Group 2 (EUS), and Group 3 (Unblinded to EUS) (Figure 1). This process was replicated with the same cases, imaging, and presentations at Johns Hopkins Hospital, resulting in 240 unique MDT patient evaluations. A unanimous consensus was reached on all cases in assessing if the lesion was mucinous, what the PCL subtype was, whether the lesion harbored advanced neoplasia, and whether the lesion should be referred for surgical resection. The performance of PCL management guidelines (American Gastrointestinal Association (AGA) [4] and IAP [15]) was also evaluated and compared to the performance of the three MDT groups. The criteria from each guideline were applied retrospectively to classify each PCL.

Crossover study schema evaluating the impact of endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) on multidisciplinary team (MDT) decision‐making. Two centers (Washington University and Johns Hopkins) each used two independent MDTs (A–D) to review two case lists (Cohorts 1 and 2). In Group 2 (EUS), each site balanced order by having one MDT review Cohort 1 with EUS available, while the paired MDT reviewed Cohort 2 with EUS. The MDTs then crossed over to Group 1 (No EUS) to evaluate the complementary cohort without EUS information. In Group 3 (Unblinded to EUS), the same MDTs were provided the EUS results for the cases they had just reviewed without EUS and reconsidered their decisions (unblinded to EUS). MDT consisted of a gastroenterologist, hepatobiliary surgeon, and abdominal radiologist. Cohorts 1 and 2 consisted of 20 patients each, with half “high‐risk” PCL with advanced neoplasia and half “low‐risk” PCL with low grade dysplasia or no progression on imaging.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous and categorical variables (presented as frequencies and percentages) were analyzed using χ‐squared tests or 2‐tailed Student's T‐tests as appropriate. AUCs were compared using DeLong's method. Kappa statistics were used to compare the degree of agreement between the Washington University and Johns Hopkins Hospital MDT groups. Agreement is presented using Cohen's kappa (k) and interpreted using the Landis and Koch interpretation (< 0: no agreement; 0–0.20: slight; 0.21–0.40: fair; 0.41–0.60: moderate; 0.61–0.80: substantial; 0.81–1: almost perfect). To assess the generalizability of diagnostic performance beyond the enriched study cohort, positive and negative predictive values for MDT evaluation with and without EUS were recalculated using Bayes' theorem. A sensitivity analysis was performed in which PPV and NPV were estimated across a range of clinically plausible prevalence values (1%–50%) to illustrate how the predictive performance of each diagnostic strategy would be expected to vary according to the pre‐test probability of high‐risk disease in a given clinical setting. Statistical analysis was performed in R version 4.4.0 and GraphPad Prism 10.

Results

Cases

Of the 40 consecutive patients included in the study, 57.5% were female, with a mean age of 62 ± 13.2 years (Table 1). Twenty patients had high‐risk cysts (11 with confirmed HGD on pathology, 9 with histologically confirmed PC) and 20 patients had low‐risk cysts (14 with no progression after ≥ 3 years of follow‐up (median follow‐up 5.8 years (IQR 4.2–7.8) and no escalation of imaging/EUS surveillance) and 6 with pathologically proven low‐grade dysplasia (LGD)). Given the design of the cohort to include 50% high‐risk cases, the majority of the cysts were mucinous (87.5%). The most common cyst type was a branch‐duct intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm (BD‐IPMN) (50%), followed by mixed‐type IPMN (MT‐IPMN) (15%), and mucinous cystic neoplasm (MCN) (12.5%) (Table 1, Table S1).

EUS Improves MDT Evaluation of PCL

When upfront EUS data was available (Group 2 (EUS)), MDT was 97% (CI 90.1%–99.7%) sensitive, and 70% (CI 34.8%–93.3%) specific for the identification of mucinous versus non‐mucinous cysts (AUC 0.836; CI 0.74–0.91). This is significantly higher (p = 0.04) than when EUS data was not available (Group 1 (No EUS)) (sensitivity 91% (CI 82.3%–96.8%), specificity 20% (CI 2.5%–55.6%), AUC 0.557 (CI 0.44–0.67)) (Table 2). Similarly, with the benefit of EUS data, Group 2 (EUS) was significantly better in identifying cysts with advanced neoplasia (Group 2 vs. Group 1: Sensitivity 80% (CI 64.4%–90.9%) versus 62.5% (CI 45.8%–77.3%), specificity 82.5% (CI 67.2%–92.7%) versus 75% (CI 58.8%–87.3%), AUC 0.812 (CI 0.71–0.89) versus 0.688 (CI 0.57–0.79), p = 0.029) (Table 2). Most critically, up front EUS data allowed Group 2 (EUS) to have an 85% (CI 70.2%–94.3%) sensitivity and specificity, AUC 0.85 (0.75–0.92) (Table 2) in appropriately referring patients for surgical evaluation, which was significantly higher than Group 1 (No EUS) (Sensitivity 60% (CI 43.3%–75.1%), specificity 75% (CI 58.8%–87.3%), AUC 0.67 (CI 0.56–0.77), p = 0.0016).

EUS Evaluation Appears Vulnerable to Cognitive Bias in MDT Evaluation

If MDT deliberations were unaffected by cognitive bias, then the addition of EUS data when initially not available (Group 3 (Unblinded to EUS)) would be expected to result in near identical recommendations as compared to when the EUS data was available initially (Group 2 (EUS)). However, Group 3 (Unblinded to EUS) performance in determining if a PCL was mucinous was significantly poorer (p = 0.0003) with a particular drop in specificity (Group 2 vs. Group 3: Sensitivity 97% (CI 90.1%–99.7%) versus 97% (CI 90.1%–99.7%), Specificity 70% (CI 34.8%–93.3%) versus 10% (CI 0.3%–44.5%), AUC 0.836 (CI 0.74–0.91) versus 0.54 (CI 0.42–0.65); p = 0.0003) (Table 2). While Group 2 (EUS) and 3 (Unblinded to EUS) performed similarly in the appropriate identification of advanced neoplasia (Group 2 vs. Group 3: Sensitivity 80% (CI 64.4%–90.0%) versus 75% (CI 58.8%–87.3%), Specificity 82.5% (CI 67.2%–92.7%) versus 82.5% (CI 67.2%–92.7%), AUC 0.81 (CI 0.71–0.89) versus 0.79 (CI 0.68–0.87); p = 0.59), Group 3 (Unblinded to EUS) performed significantly more poorly in appropriately recommending patients for surgery (Group 2 vs. 3: Sensitivity 85% (CI 70.2%–94.3%) versus 72.5% (CI 56.1%–85.4%), Specificity 85% (CI 70.2%–94.3%) versus 80% (CI 64.4%–90.9%), AUC 0.85 (CI 0.0.75–0.92) versus 0.76 (CI 0.65–0.85) p = 0.0485). Even though Group 3 (Unblinded to EUS) overall performed worse than Group 2 (EUS) in terms of correctly classifying cyst type (Group 2 vs. 3: 72.5% (58/80) versus 65% (52/80)), referring high‐risk PCLs for surgery (Group 2 vs. 3: 85% (34/40) versus 72.5% (29/40)), and incorrectly referring low‐risk PCLs for surgery (Group 2 vs. 3: 15% (6/40) versus 20% (8/40)), the differences were not statistically significant (Table 3).

Inter‐Institutional Variability in MDT Evaluation of PCL

Given that MDT was completed at two quaternary care referral centers, we evaluated the inter‐observer agreement between institutions in their assessment of PCL. When EUS was initially available (Group 2 (EUS)), Kappa analysis demonstrated almost perfect agreement in recommendations for surgery (kappa 0.9) and substantial agreement for the presence of advanced neoplasia (kappa 0.75) and for classifying PCL as mucinous or non‐mucinous (kappa 0.62). However, when EUS results were not available (Group 1 (No EUS)), there was an overall downward shift in the degree of agreement regarding surgical referral (kappa 0.5), presence of advanced neoplasia (kappa 0.35) and mucinous classification (0.44). A similar increase in the variability of recommendations was observed when comparing the subsequent unblinding of MDT to EUS data (Group 3 (Unblinded to EUS)), primarily for identification of advanced neoplasia (kappa 0.46) and recommendations for surgery (kappa 0.56) but not for classification of PCL as mucinous or non‐mucinous (kappa 0.65) (Table 4).

Performance of Consensus Guidelines Compared to MDT Management of PCL

The performance of the AGA and IAP guidelines was evaluated in the study cohort and there was no statistically significant difference between the AGA (AUC 0.675 (CI 0.5–0.85) and IAP guidelines (AUC 0.625 (CI 0.45–0.8) (p = 0.57). Compared to the MDT groups, there were no differences between the performance of Group 1 (No EUS) and the two guidelines (Figure 2A). Group 2 (EUS) performed significantly better than the AGA (p = 0.029) and IAP (p = 0.005) guidelines (Figure 2B). Group 3 (Unblinded to EUS) performed better than the IAP guidelines (p = 0.047) but was not significantly different from the AGA guidelines (p = 0.24) (Figure 2C).

Simulation of MDT Performance in a Population With Low Prevalence of High‐Risk PCL

At the enriched study prevalence of 50%, the positive predictive value (PPV) for Group 1 (No EUS) was 71.4% and the negative predictive value (NPV) was 66.7%, compared with 82.1% and 80.5% for Group 2 (EUS), respectively. A sensitivity analysis across a range of prevalence values (1%–50%) demonstrated that PPV was consistently higher for Group 2 (EUS) than for Group 1 (No EUS) at every prevalence threshold. NPV remained above 95% for both strategies if the prevalence of high‐risk PCLs was 10% or lower, but if the prevalence was higher than 10%, then Group 2 (EUS) had higher NPV compared to Group 1 (No EUS) (Table S2).

Discussion

Despite the development of numerous guidelines for the management of PCL [4, 15, 17, 18], their performance remains poor given the underlying imperfect clinical/imaging characteristics they depend upon. MRI/CT criteria, EUS imaging/cyst fluid sampling analysis, and clinicopathologic features in PCL are all lacking. Thus, MDT evaluation allows for the heuristic weighing of each of these imperfect pieces of data to provide a clinically informed recommendation that is “greater than the sum of its parts.” Multiple studies have demonstrated that MDT evaluation of PCL allows for superior diagnostic accuracy and alters the management of PCL in 30% of cases when compared with initial community evaluation [11, 12, 13]. Utilizing two independent expert centers, we demonstrate that MDT with upfront EUS with basic cyst fluid analysis was able to appropriately identify patients that require surgery with 85% accuracy, which was significantly higher than current guidelines (p = 0.02 (68% accuracy with AGA) and p = 0.005 (51% accuracy with IAP)). Indeed, utilizing this approach, patients were incorrectly recommended surgery in only 15% of cases, as opposed to the 62%–68% [9, 19] of benign resections seen in contemporary surgical cohorts of PCL. This not only demonstrates the excellent clinical performance of MDT but also may establish a benchmark against which iterative advancements in PCL biomarker/imaging development may be measured.

European guidelines have argued against the routine utilization of EUS for PCL evaluation given its modest performance when utilized alone in assessing if a lesion is mucinous or harbors advanced neoplasia [17, 20]. Additionally, it is unresolved as to when in the workup of PCL EUS should be optimally utilized (i.e. in response to a new worrisome feature, in lieu of imaging). In the current study, MDT evaluation with EUS significantly increased the recognition of high‐risk PCL and the correct surgical referral for these lesions, despite the historically fair accuracy of EUS FNA and cytology for PCLs [16, 21]. EUS was able to increase the rate of correct classification of PCLs from 61% to 73% and increase the rate of correct referral of high‐risk cysts to surgery from 60% to 85%, while decreasing the rate of incorrectly referring low‐risk cysts to surgery from 25% to 15%. Recent studies have reported that EUS‐FNA improved surveillance recommendations and surgical referrals in up to ∼30% of patients [22, 23]. However, to our knowledge, no prior study has prospectively modeled the real‐world integration of EUS in the multidisciplinary evaluation of PCL risk stratification.

As the data from EUS evaluation may be subjectively interpreted (i.e., variable cutoffs of CEA to define a lesion as mucinous), it may be susceptible to cognitive biases. This may take several forms including: anchoring bias (i.e. insisting a lesion is an MCN given demographics, despite a low CEA), confirmation bias (i.e. insisting an IPMN with a mural nodule requires resection, despite a negative cytology), or a loss of aversion bias (i.e. weighing the impact of a missed cancer against the potential surgical morbidity for a resected benign lesion). While cognitive biases are common in clinical practice, real world/retrospective analysis makes assessing residual confounding exceptionally difficult. The current MDT modeling study was carefully designed to see if deliberation without EUS data would affect the interpretation of EUS data once available: if unbiased, then MDT with EUS data (Group 2 (EUS)) and MDT without initial EUS, unblinded to EUS (Group 3 (Unblinded to EUS)) should be identical. On the contrary, Group 2 (EUS) demonstrated a sensitivity/specificity of 97% and 70% for mucinous lesions, but Group 3 (Unblinded to EUS) was significantly poorer (Sensitivity 97%, Specificity 10% (p = 0.0003)). As interpretation of indeterminate CEA values may be particularly subjective, the low specificity when EUS data was not initially available may be due to a desire not to exclude any case without an undetectable CEA level. Importantly, Group 2 (EUS) demonstrated significantly better performance than Group 3 (Unblinded to EUS) in correctly recommending patients for surgery (p = 0.0485). Taken together, this suggests that when performing MDT review, the upfront availability of EUS analysis is important and, if not available, for clinicians to be conscious of potential biases that may influence interpretation. The decision on when a PCL case should be presented at MDT may be highly variable given the practice pattern variation in the clinicians who may manage frequently asymptomatic, incidental PCL. However, certainly if being presented due to the identification of a potential worrisome feature or high‐risk stigmata, the availability of up‐front EUS prior to the MDT would help inform and potentially improve the accuracy of that discussion.

MDT performance was consistent across two large referral centers for surgical recommendations (kappa 0.9) and presence of advanced neoplasia (kappa 0.75) when EUS‐FNA data was available upfront. However, there was increased variability when EUS was not available for these assessments (0.5, 0.35 respectively). This variability could be the result of multiple factors including clinicians being forced to make decisions with fewer data points, as well as institutional differences in the routine utilization or availability of EUS/cyst fluid analysis in the initial evaluation of PCL. Importantly, it demonstrates that EUS significantly reduces heterogeneity, even at the expert‐level, further underscoring the value of including EUS data in the initial MDT discussions for PCL evaluation.

Our results also provide a general performance metric for MDT based management of PCLs, in which MDT with EUS data upfront was able to correctly refer patients to surgery with 85% sensitivity and specificity and an AUC of 0.85. This could be considered as a baseline clinical decision benchmark for PCL prognostication among experts, against which innovations in this space could be compared. For example, recent pre‐clinical studies on PCL biomarkers such as cyst fluid telomerase (AUC 0.849 for advanced neoplasia) [24] or novel methylated DNA markers (AUC 0.86 for advanced neoplasia) [25] have similar pathologic performance as that of clinical MDT review. While the comparison of new technologies against the rote application of CEA values, cytology results, or guidelines may make novel approaches appear significantly superior, it may be more realistic to judge them against the clinical decision benchmark of MDT, which is frequently how decisions for surgical resection are made.

In addition to these pre‐clinical approaches, next generation sequencing (NGS) testing of cyst fluid has increasingly been utilized for PCL assessment and risk stratification [26, 27]. A recent “real world” evaluation of commercial cyst fluid NGS analysis reported on 441 patients, with 26/441 having progressed to advanced neoplasia, and 42/441 having pathologic confirmation [28]. The authors report a sensitivity of 72.7%, specificity of 97.8% and AUC of 0.85 (0.76–0.95) for detecting advanced neoplasia when they included all cases (pathological confirmation and benign follow‐up), which is similar to our MDT approach evaluated in the current study. However, when evaluating only pathology proven cases, the performance of cyst fluid NGS was considerably lower with an AUC of 0.597 (sensitivity 44%, specificity 75%) [28].

There are several limitations to this study. The design employed “mock” MDT sessions as opposed to utilizing “real” clinical MDT discussion. Even though it was a “mock” study, the identification of advanced neoplasia and recommendation for surgery (Table 2) were not uniformly identical as physicians worried certain patient's comorbidities may increase perioperative morbidity; this demonstrates that the deliberation was not merely algorithmic. The average MDT session ran 114 min with an average case discussion of 2.85 min, similar to the case discussion in contemporary clinical MDT at our centers. This novel study design allowed for: the selection of a cohort equally divided between high‐risk and low‐risk lesions, the maintenance of blinding for all clinicians involved, the crossover evaluation of EUS data, and the ability to control for residual confounding, all of which would have been impossible in a retrospective or prospective model. Additionally, the “mock” design allowed for analysis between experts/centers for the invaluable evaluation of interobserver reliability. Another possible limitation to the study is the cohort size; however, the cohort was larger than our conservative sample size calculation and the study was replicated at another site (JHH). In clinical practice, the distribution of PCLs between high‐risk and low‐risk lesions is not 1:1, with low‐risk lesions being much more prevalent. However, relying only on the real‐world prevalence of high‐risk PCLs would not have allowed a confident evaluation of the diagnostic performance measures of the MDT groups. Additionally, the study participants were blinded to the distribution of high‐ and low‐risk cases.

In summary, EUS significantly increases the recognition of high‐risk PCL and correct surgical referral in MDT evaluation over MDT with clinical and cross‐sectional imaging data alone. Cognitive bias can significantly affect decision‐making when EUS data are not initially available. MDT assessment with EUS evaluated in a well‐controlled, prospective fashion is highly accurate and establishes a high bar for iterative additions of biomarkers to PCL analysis.
