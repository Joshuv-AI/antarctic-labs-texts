Phenolic, amino acids, and fatty acids profiles and the nutritional properties in the fresh and dried fruits of black rosehip (Rosa pimpinellifolia L.)
Authors: Milad Pashaei, Hamid Hassanpour
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Recently, increased attention has been paid to the raw materials of plants as a source of biologically active substances. Black rosehip (Rosa pimpinellifolia L.) fruits could be a good resource for potential functional components in the food, cosmetic and pharmaceutical industries. Also, drying can influence the composition of heat-sensitive phytochemicals. However, less attention is given to comparing black rosehip bioactive compounds particularly compositions of fatty acid, amino acids, and phenolic content in fresh and dried fruits. So in this study, the amino acid constituents (by amino acid analyzer), fatty acids (by GC–MS), mineral elements (by atomic absorption spectrometer), antioxidant (by DPPH) and phenolic compounds (by HPLC) present in fresh and dried fruits of black rosehip naturally grown in Iran were comprehensively investigated. The results showed that dried fruits had a lower level moisture by 51.55%, and a higher level of total phenolic compounds and total sugar by 786.20 mg GAE/100 g and 15.77 g/100 g, respectively. Chlorogenic acid and gallic acid were the major phenolic compounds (109.3 mg/g). Whilst, linoleic acid, oleic acid, and arachidic acid (85.34%) were the most dominant fatty acids. The most dominant amino acids were glutamic acid, phenylalanine, and arginine (29.41 g/100 g). Also, Fe and Mn as micro-elements were the most dominant elements. In general, the results illustrated the potentials and differences of black rosehip fruits grown in the Arasbaran region as promising resources for food sources, pharmaceutics, cosmetics, and breeding programs. Also, these findings confirm that black rosehip fruits contain significant amounts of secondary metabolites that may positively affect human health.

Introduction

Demand for healthy food and increasing awareness of people about the importance of a healthy diet have demanded the food industries to provide diverse and healthy sources for food production1. On the other hand, population growth and the effects of climate changes on arable lands have negatively affected food resources worldwide2. It is also essential to find various health and nutrition solutions and to find new food resources. Natural resources, mainly plants with high levels of bioactive compounds, have been one of the solutions to overcome food safety problems2. Plants that wildly grow in many regions possess several functional compounds suitable for human consumption, meanwhile with therapeutic effects that prevent the development of several diseases in consumers1,2. It is estimated that more than 30 thousand of the edible plant species growing naturally in native habitats have mostly been overlooked and their potential gain a little attention as a valuable resource for food and medical uses3,4.

Rosehip (Rosa pimpinellifolia L.) belongs to the Rosaceae family is one of the most promising plants that grows wildly in vast regions of Europe, Asia, the Middle East and North America5. Rosehip fruits have several bioactive functional compounds such as tocopherols, phenolics, and fatty acids and used in the food and pharmaceutical industries5. The fresh rosehip fruits are most often processed into many products such as jams, herbal tea, jellies, high-value cosmetic oils, animal nutrition, and most importantly in canning factories and use for therapeutic purposes1,5. Among the rosehips, the black rosehip has attracted less attention. However, the black rosehip fruit is used by the native people in various staple dishes or to treat ailments. Tea made from black rosehip fruit has been used as traditional pharmaceutics for flu, cough, stress, sore throat and pneumonia6. It has been shown that black rosehip is rich in bioactive compounds with multi-functional features such as anti-microbial activities, anti-cancer effects, and antioxidants6, so this fruit is an important raw material for the food and pharmaceutical industries.

It has been shown that environmental conditions could influence the bioactive compositions of rosehip7. Recently, numerous studies have been conducted on the bioactive properties of rosehips in various regions and countries. To mention some, Murathan et al.8 studied bioactive compounds of four rosehip species growing in the East Anatolia region of Turkey and reported that the level of total phenol, antioxidant activity, and total sugar significantly varies between species. The study conducted by Fascella et al.9 on four rose species (Rosa canina, R. corymbifera, R. micrantha, and R. sempervirens) grown in Sicily (South Italy) revealed significant variability among the rose species. The highest total phenol (6784.5 mg GAE/100 g DW) and total anthocyanin (2.94 mg CGE/100 g DW) were observed in R. canina9. In Kazakhstan, Ikhsanov et al.10 reported that R. canina fruits contained high levels of amino acids such as glutamine, aspargine, alanine, and proline, fatty acids including linoleic and oleic acids and ascorbic acids. Also, studies conducted in Croatia11 and Bosnia and Herzegovina12 revealed that R. canina contained higher total phenol, antioxidat activity and elements. In a study conducted in the Adiyaman province of Turkey to evaluate the physiological and biochemical properties of naturally growing rosehips, it has been shown that total phenol content and antioxidant activity range significantly among genotypes13. In rosehips grown in Serbia, the fatty acid constituents are different in plant samples collected from different locations14. Besides the effect of different locations and growing environments, other factors such as cultivars, storage conditions, processing technology, and drying process affect the physiological and biochemical characteristics especially the composition of heat-sensitive phytochemicals of fruits. The fruit drying process extends the shelf life by reducing the water activity inhibiting the growth of microorganisms, and enzymatic and other deteriorative reactions15. A popular, low-cost, and efficient way to reduce moisture is the air-drying method. However, the drying process leads to changes in various fruit compositions and affects its nutritional value15. Goztepe et al.16 showed that phenolic compositions and antioxidant activity significantly change when different methods of drying are applied to the samples.

Climate conditions of Iran made the country as origin of many plant species growing naturally in dry lands, mountainous regions, margin of the sea, and forests. The Arasbaran zone a semi-humid region located in the northwest regions of the country covers more than 174 thousand hectares and has vast plant diversity17. The recent interest in functional foods that contain significant amounts of bioactive compounds would lead to increased knowledge about specific metabolites such as polyphenols, fatty acids and amino acids, for which black rose hips were proposed as a raw material source. On the other hand, awareness is growing that foods and their chemical components and bioactive compounds can help maintain optimal health and prevent chronic diseases. For this reason, the chemical and bioactive compounds of wild black rosehip can be a potential source of raw materials for human health. However, these health-promoting compounds may be changed due to genetic variability and environmental conditions.

As mentioned above, the importance of environmental conditions and processing technology, particularly fruit drying, in changing the properties and nutritional of rosehip fruits has been great. However, upon reviewing of existing literature, it becomes apparent that a scarcity of studies have been done on bioactive compounds particularly compositions of fatty acid, amino acids, and phenolic content of dried and fresh black rosehip grown in Iran. On the other hand, to the best of our knowledge, this study is the first that investigates black rosehip in Iran. So, this study aimed to determine comprehensively the biochemical properties of black rosehip fruits from semi-humid forests of the Arasbaran regions and to compare biochemical traits, compositions of fatty acids and amino acids, mineral contents, and compositions of phenolic compounds in fresh and dried black rosehip fruits.

Materials and methods

Fruit samples of wild black rosehip (Rosa pimpinellifolia L.) were collected from the Arasbaran region (38.9200290, 46.8707536, 1679 m above sea level) located in East Azerbaijan, Iran. This area is characterized by hot and dry summers and relatively cold and snowy winters. The amount of precipitation is 432.5 mm. The average min/max air temperature is 11.2/20.3 °C and the average air relative humidity is 60.7%. The soil of this area is mostly forest brown soil and limestone. The Samples were identified and authenticated by Professor Ahmad Alijanpour, department of forestry in Urmia University, Iran and voucher specimens (No. 1621) were deposited at the herbarium of the horticultural department, Urmia University, Urmia, Iran. The Fruits were collected (at a commercially ripe stage) early morning from all sides of the shrubs to ensure homogeneous sampling (approximately 2000 g) in October 2023 and immediately after harvest, the fruits were packed in polyethylene bags and transferred to the Lab into the ice. On receipt, stalk residues and damaged fruits were removed and washed in deionized water. The samples were then divided into two sets; one set (1000 g) was immediately frozen using liquid nitrogen and stored at − 70 °C, and the second (1000 g) was air-dried (the most popular method used for the preparation, conservation and storage of plant raw material for a longer period) at room temperature (~ 25 °C). Drying was performed by spreading the fruits in a single layer on cardboard paper in a ventilated shadow place at an average temperature of 25 °C.

Determination of ash, moisture, and fiber content

To measure the ash content, fruits were finely ground and then mixed and dried carefully. Then 10 g was weighed and heated in a muffle furnace for about 3–5 h at 600 °C. It was cooled in a desiccator and weighed to completion of ashing. To ensure completion of ashing, it was heated again in the furnace for half an hour more, cooled and weighed. Fiber content in fresh and dried rosehip fruits was determined by AOAC methods18 using acid-alkaline digestion.

Estimation of total sugar

To obtain extracts, 25 mg of fruit powder was dissolved in 10 ml of 80% methanolic solvent. The resulting solution was centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min. Subsequently, the supernatant was collected and adjusted to a final amount of 10 ml to determine the total sugar content of the samples. Then 100 µl of the methanolic extracts were transferred to a vial and 3 ml of Anthrone solution (150 mg of Anthrone and 100 ml of 72% sulfuric acid (W/W)) was added. The vials are put in the water bath for 10 min and then left to be cooled at room temperature. The absorbance of the solution was measured at 625 nm. The standard sample was absorbance of glucose with 0 to 120 mg/l at 625 nm4.

Determination of antioxidant activity, total phenolic, anthocyanin, and flavonoid content

To determine these parameters, methanol solvent (the most commonly used extraction solvent due to its high polarity which could produce high extraction yields) was used to prepare the extracts. To obtain extracts, 1 g of the powdered sample was added to 10 ml of methanol and held at 0 °C for 10 min. The slurry was centrifuged at 10,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C, then the supernatant was used. The DPPH approach described by Sharma and Bhat19 was followed to measure antioxidant activity. The 2 ml of the DPPH solution (0.004 g of DPPH in 100 ml of 80% methanol) was added to the 15 µl of the prepared extracts. After 15–30 min, the absorbance was measured at 517 nm. The colorimetric method described by Chang et al.22 with a little modifications was performed to calculate the total flavonoid content (TF). The mixture of 30 µl methanolic extract and 150 µl sodium nitrate (5%) was prepared and remained in darkness for 15 min. Then, 1000 µl of 1 M potassium acetate and 300 µl of the 10% aluminum chloride solution were added and the absorbance was measured at 380 nm and expressed as mg quercetin per 100 g.

Phenolic compound analysis

The black rosehip fruits were ground using a 1.5 ml extract solution (2:28:70, formic acid: water: methanol). The obtained extracts were shaken for 10 min in a Thermomixer (Eppendorf, USA) and were centrifuged at 10,000 g at 4 °C. Then 0.2 μm membrane was used for filtering of 1 ml extract (Agilent Technologies, USA). The Arabbi et al.23 method was used for the identification and quantification of phenolic compositions using analytical reversed-phase HPLC in a Hewlett–Packard 1100 system with a quaternary pump coupled to a diode array detector. The used column was a 250 × 4.6 mm (id), 5 μm Prodigy ODS3 reversed-phase silica column (Phenomenex Ltd., Torrance, CA). The mobile phase included water: tetrahydrofuran: trifluoroacetic acid (98:2:0.1) (solvent A), and solvent B consisted of methanol: tetrahydrofuran: trifluoroacetic acid (98:2:0.1 by volume). The gradient profile was 17% B for 2 min enhancing to 25% B after 5 min, to 35% B after a further 8 min and to 50% B after a further 5 min. The injection volume and the flow rate were 20 μl and 1.0 ml/min, respectively. The phenolic compounds (gallic acid, caffeic acid, chlorogenic acid, rutin, P-coumaric acid, rosmaric acid, quercetin, cinnamic acid, and apigenin) within the samples were identified based on their relative retention times and UV spectra with those of standard compounds and were determined utilizing an external standard method.

Fatty acids analysis

The fresh fruits were ground to a fine powder using liquid N2 and dried fruits were powdered in a domestic blender. Powdered samples were Soxhlet extraction using petroleum ether heated to reflux to obtain fresh rosehip and dried fruit oil. Then, the solvent was evaporated under reduced pressure to obtain a crude fixed oil. Finally, after methyl esterification, fatty acid composition analysis was carried out using 7890A GC coupled to 5979C MS (Agilent Technologies, USA). Then, 1 g of the extracted oil was dissolved in 40 ml of n-hexane and completely mixed with 40 mL of methanolic solution of KOH (0.4 M). The obtained mixture was kept for 30 min and 40 ml of distilled water were added and finally presented the biphasic system. Then the upper aqueous phase was collected to obtain fatty acid methyl ester (FAMEs) for GC–MS measurement. The extracts were also concentrated at 35 °C using a Rotavapor (Büchi, Flawil, Switzerland) system and the organic solvent was evaporated to yield FAMEs. All of the extractions were performed in three technical repetitions.

These analytes were separated by a DB-5 quartz capillary column (30 m £ 0.25 mm £ 0.25 mm). The used carrier gas was high-purity N2 (99.99%), which was applied at a flow rate of 1 mL/min, and the ratio of N2 and sample gas was 5:1. The injection temperature was 270 °C and the inlet sample quantity was 0.5 ml. Temperature programming in GC system was as follows: 70 °C for 5 min; 25 °C/min to 200 °C; 2 °C/min to 250 °C; 20 °C/min to 300 °C for 3 min. Mass spectra were recorded at the source and quadrupole temperature of 230 and 150 °C, with positive electron ionization (EI +) source at 70 eV ionization energy, in a full scan of 33,500 m/z. The fatty acid composition was determined based on the comparison with the standard24.

Amino acids analysis

The amino acid composition was analyzed by a Hitachi model L-8900 amino acid analyzer (Hitachi Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) with a column packed with Hitachi custom ion-exchange resin 2622 (4.6 mm × 60 mm, particle size 5 μm). The Choi et al.25 method was used for extraction and analysis of amino acids with modification. Briefly, about 2 g samples were macerated in a glass and 1% sulfosalicylic acid (50 ml) was added with ultrasonic for 20 min. Then filtered with 0.22 μm membrane and 20 μL of solution was used for determination. Flow rates of lithium citrate buffer and ninhydrin were 0.35 mL/min and 0.30 mL/min, respectively. The column temperature was from 30 to 70 °C and the reaction coil temperature was 135 °C. The amino acid compositions of the black rosehip fresh and dried fruits were compared with those of standards.

Elemental composition analysis

Digestion of the sample was carried out in a microwave oven. Fresh and dried fruits (0.1 g) were added to the 5 ml HNO3 (69%), 2 ml H2O2, and 2 ml deionized water. The conditions of digestion were as follows: 5 min at 120 °C, 5 min at 150 °C, and 5 min at 190 °C. Then, the digested samples are cooled (25 °C) and sonicated (to remove bubbles) by ultrasound bath cleaner (KQ3200E, China) for 5 min. The obtained solutions were transferred into 50 mL volumetric flasks and made up to volume with deionized water. Determinations of elements were carried out by a high-resolution continuum source atomic absorption spectrometer (HR-CS AAS) (Analitik Jena ContrAA 300, Analitik Jena AG, Jena, Germany). For each element, the air, acetylene, optics, and electronics were adjusted for maximum absorption. To prepare the elements standards, the standard solutions were applied and diluted in 5% HNO326. The nitrogen (N) value in fresh and dried rosehip fruits was determined by the Kjeldahl method27.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were carried out based on three replications and data were expressed as means ± standard deviation. The obtained means were compared by the Duncan test at a significance level of p < 0.05. Also, the Pearson correlation analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics software (version 27.0.1).

Results and discussion

Biochemical characteristics

The average value of ash, moisture, and dietary fiber content for fresh black rosehip fruits was 5.04%, 15.77%, and 7.78%, respectively (Table 1). Dried fruits had significantly higher ash (p < 0.05) and lower moisture content (p < 0.05), however, they had no statistically significant differences in fiber content (p < 0.05) (Table 1). The results showed that dried fruits had higher 11.76% ash and lower 51.55% moisture content compared to fresh fruits. These results on the ash content in fresh and dried fruits are higher than the results of Benković-Lačić et al.11 in Croatia (0.93–2.37% for fresh fruits) and Vasilj et al.12 in Bosnia and Herzegovina (2.15–2.41% for fresh and 2.99–4.59% dried fruits) studies. The ash of rosehip fruits from different locations was studied by Benković-Lačić et al.11, who they reported a range of 0.93–2.37% ash content. The fruits with low moisture have a high nutritional value and also, better stability during storage and are safe from spoilage and microbial infection28. The average level of total sugar content was 11.68 g/100 g and significantly increased to 15.77 g/100 g in dried fruits (p < 0.01) (Table 1). Eroğul and Oğuz9 reported about 30–41 g/100 g FW total sugar in rosehips grown Adiyaman region. Murathan et al.8 reported 18.78 g/100 g FW for rosehip obtained from the East Anatolia region in Turkey, whilst in Bosnia and Herzegovina, Vasilj et al.12 reported 1.99–7.83 g/100 g for fresh and 7.81–12.41 g/100 g for dried rosehip fruits. It seems that ecological conditions, differences in harvest season, and experimental conditions could affect the level of sugar content. The amount of sugar content in rosehip fruits studied here is comparable to other species of Rosa such as 25 g/100 g for R. villosa, and 15.5 g/100 g for R. dumalis8. The high level of sugar content in dried rosehip fruits and other wild fruits compared to fresh fruits was reported previously29,30. From a technological point of view, the sugar content is an important factor, that influences the gel consistency in gelatin products such as juices and jams12. However, further studies should be carried out on the sugar profiles of black rosehip to determine sugar composition to make a decision on its use in the food industry.

Antioxidant activity, total phenol (TP), flavonoid (TF), and anthocyanin (TA) content

The results showed that the antioxidant activity and TP content of fresh black rosehip fruits were 65.3% and 432.48 mg GAE/100 g, respectively. TA and TF content were obtained at 82.48 mg cyanidin 3-glucoside/100 g and 314.33 mg quercetin/100 g, respectively (Table 1). TP and TF content significantly increased by 81.78% and 18.66% respectively, whereas total TA content and antioxidant activity significantly declined by 51.5% and 14.96% respectively, in dried black rosehip fruits (p < 0.01 and p < 0.05) (Table 1). Antioxidant activity has a positive correlation with the nutritional value of foods and it mainly depends on environmental stresses, genotype, soil conditions, and climate changes31. In a previous study conducted by Shameh et al.32, the antioxidant activity of red rosehip fruits was reported at 37.86 mg AAE/g and TF content was reported at 5.11 mg quercetin/g. TP content of red rosehip fruits has been reported to be 160–230 mg GAE/g for the north-west33, 80–104 mg GAE/g for the central regions of Iran and 929.27 mg GAE/100 g for black rosehip inTurkey34. Previously, the TA content of red rosehip fruits measured 2.75 mg/g in Turkey8. These observations reflect the high concentration of bioactive compounds in the fresh and dried black rosehip fruits.

Accordingly, it has been indicated that dried fruits have a high level of TP content and decreased antioxidant activity, however, TF is non-significant in dried fruits compared to fresh fruits16. As demonstrated by previous studies on different types of fruits35,36, drying damage the cell walls and as result the phenolic compounds could be extracted better. Pashazadeh et al.36 found that drying decreases or does not affect TA content while it raises the amount of phenol compounds in rosehip fruits. Similar results have been also found in autumn olive berries35. Although phenolic compounds and flavonoids are heat sensitive and their content may decrease in dried fruits37, however in this study phenolic and flavonoids compounds were significantly higher in dried fruits. While, the antioxidant activity based on the DPPH method and TA content were significantly low in dried fruits. The air-drying process and prolonged drying using solar energy might destroy anthocyanin and some antioxidant compounds as they may oxidize at high temperatures, high oxygen concentrations and exposure to sunlight38. Anthocyanins are also degraded enzymatically in the presence of polyphenol oxidase (PPO) during the drying process at moderate temperature39. It also was revealed that changes in the structure of bioactive compounds, during drying, lead to the formation of lower molecular weight compounds, which decreased their antioxidative activities38.

Phenolic compositions

The differences in phenolic compounds between fresh and dried black rosehip fruits were statistically significant for all the phenolic compositions (p < 0.01; Table 2). The results of the present study showed that chlorogenic acid is the major phenolic compound in fresh black rosehip fruits. Among nine phenolic compounds that were characterized in fresh fruits, the highest level is related to chlorogenic acid (94.04 mg/g) followed by gallic acid with 15.27 mg/g. The results of the present study are in agreement with a study by Shameh et al.32, who found chlorogenic acid (68.5 µg/g) and gallic acid (65.8 µg/g) as major phenolic compounds in fresh red rosehip fruits. The different results obtained from different studies could be caused by growing conditions, environmental stresses, genotypes, and genetic diversity11,29,37. The level of phenolic compounds in dried fruits of black rosehip was different compared to fresh fruit. The content of gallic acid, caffeic acid, chlorogenic acid, and rosmaric acid declined by 9.21–71.77%, whereas the content of rutin, comaric, quercetin, cinnamic acid, and apigenin were elevated in dried fruits. The results showed that the content of chlorogenic acid decreased from 71.17% (94.03 mg/g) to 51.59% (31.81 mg/g) and gallic acid from 11.56% (15.27 mg/g) to 8.16% (5.03 mg/g). The chlorogenic acid and comaric were the major phenols in dried fruits of rosehip (82.01%) (Table 2). In contrast to our finding, Goztepe et al.16 reported catechin and rutin as the major compounds of red rosehip fruits and found that drying could change the content of some phenolic compounds. However, similar to our findings the level of gallic acid is declined in dried fruits16. A loss of phenolic content during the drying process may be attributed to the binding of polyphenols with other compounds (like proteins) or to transformations in the chemical structure of polyphenols which cannot be extracted or determined using appropriate methods40.

According to the results, chlorogenic acid is the major phenolic compound in both fresh and dried black rosehip fruits. It protects the liver and kidney from oxidative damage and has anti-bacterial, anti-tumor, and anti-inflammatory properties. It also has cholesterol-reducing characteristics, which reduce the risks of cardiovascular diseases41. However, its content decreased during the drying process. Chlorogenic acid is considered a favourite substrate of the catecholase activity of polyphenol oxidase. Thus, the oxidation process may affect the concentration of these compounds37. The main importance of gallic acid is because of its anti-inflammatory features that make it a promising candidate for use as efficient pharmaceutics in the treatment of inflammatory diseases42. Similar to chlorogenic acid, p-comaric acid has antioxidant properties and protects cells from oxidative damage, it is a viable food additive and is used to inhibit bacterial and fungal activity in foods43. Although the content of chlorogenic acid, gallic acid, and p-comaric acid was changed in dried fruits, but the overall content of these compounds was stable (about 91%) in fresh and dried fruits of black rosehip. The degradation of phenolic compounds such as p-coumaric acid may also be related to oxidation reactions and high temperatures during drying. Ozcan et al.38 reported that higher temperatures might exert negative effects on the availability of these compounds.

To date, little information has been reported about the amino acid compositions of rosehip fruits and other Rosa species. Of rare reports, Liu et al.52 reported nine amino acids in Rosa roxburghii and Rosa sterilis fruits. Finally, the results showed that black rosehip fruits are a good source of hydrophobic amino acids that have many bioactive functions and can be effective in reducing the risk of diseases. Also, previous studies have shown that amino acids are useful in reducing cardiovascular disease and cancer as well as increasing the immune system53. Therefore, amino acids are vital for human nutrition and health and may have beneficial effects against oxidative damage. Amino acids are involved in the synthesis of hormones and the production of nitrogenous substances, which are vital to body homeostasis. The results of the present study showed that fresh and dried fruits of black rosehip are rich in most essential amino acids that can be used as an adjuvant for food deficient in essential amino acids.

Some amino acids including histidine, tyrosine, and methionine, have antioxidant activity, and the type of histidine shows strong free radical inhibition due to the breakdown of its imidazole ring54. Also, high concentrations of hydrophobic amino acids such as valine, leucine, and phenylalanine in fresh and dried fruits of black rosehip, can donate their excess electrons and are applied to quench free radicals or reduction of metal cations. Overall, amino acids with aromatic residues can donate protons to electron-deficient free radicals, and this feature enhances the radical scavenging potential54. However, more studies are needed on the effect of fresh and dried fruits of black rosehip in dealing with ROS and various diseases.

Elemental compositions

The results of the elemental analysis of black rosehip fruits are presented in Table 7. The results showed that 99.81% of rosehip fruits were macro and 0.19% were microelements. In the macro elements, K and Ca with 46.72 and 27.31% were the most dominant elements and in microelements Mn and Fe with 41.4 and 36.74% were the most dominant, respectively. Potassium (K) maintains cell membrane potential and its deficiency leads to hypertension and as a result, the risk of cardiovascular diseases increases55. Therefore, the use of rosehip fruits in diets can prevent potassium deficiency. Also, Calcium is essential for bone health and other metabolic processes, and its deficiency in the diet leads to an increased risk of osteoporosis55. Hence, the consumption of black rosehip fruits containing high calcium can help prevent this disease. As wealthy resources of Fe, black rosehip fruits could help to treat iron deficiency anaemia, which is common in developing countries49. Similar to our results, Benković-Lačić et al.11 reported Ca, K, Fe, Mn, and Al as the most dominant elements in red rosehip grown Croatia, however, Ca and K were not dominant in wild rosehip fruits. However, similar to our study, Vasilj et al.12 in Bosnia and Herzegovina, and Popović-Djordjević et al.56 in Serbia indicated that Ca and K are dominant macro elements in cultivated and wild red rosehip fruits. Conclusions

Black rosehip fruits of semi-humid regions of Iran showed significant antioxidant activity, total phenol, flavonoid, and sugar content. The results showed that Chlorogenic acid and gallic acid were the major phenolic compounds. Both fresh and dried fruits of black rosehip had more than 75% unsaturated fatty acids, and 50% essential amino acids, and were rich in K, Ca, Fe, and Mn elements. Dried fruits had less moisture, total anthocyanin, antioxidant activity and a higher total phenol, flavonoid, and sugar content. The obtained results determined that drying changed the quantity of individual phenolic compounds, fatty acids, amino acids, and elements. Some of the compounds were increased while others were decreased or remained constant. Our findings emphasize the considerable variation in individual and total phenolic, amino acids, fatty acids and elemental content found in fresh and dried fruits of black rosehip that may positively affect human health. Since black rosehip bioactive compounds are reported to reveal different health-promoting effects, knowledge of both the composition and concentrations can be useful to consumers. Consumers mostly use its dried fruit, because dried fruits of black rosehip, despite higher bioactive compounds, have a long shelf life and are available throughout the year. However, the consumption of fresh black rose hips is more beneficial than dried ones because of their antioxidant activity. Based on the results, black rosehip grown in the Arasbaran region can be incorporated into health supplements or functional food formulations. They could be used as natural sources of bioactive compounds relevant to human health and commercially used as bioactive ingredients in food manufacturing. Moreover, they may be used as raw materials to extract useful secondary metabolites for pharmaceutical applications and the formulation of dietary supplements, benefitting human health. However, further studies may be carried out to support this idea including the determination of more biological activities using different in vitro and in vivo models.
