Assessment and localization of the posterior superior alveolar artery in lateral window approach for maxillary sinus floor elevation
Authors: Horatiu Urechescu, Marius Pricop, Cristiana Cuzic, Nicoleta Gena Oncescu, Alisia Pricop, Felicia Streian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMaxillary sinus (MS) floor elevation using the lateral window approach is a predictable technique for bone augmentation in the posterior maxilla. However, injury to the posterior superior alveolar artery (PSAA) during osteotomy can result in significant intraoperative bleeding and compromise surgical visibility. This study aimed to evaluate the presence, anatomical location, and vertical distance of the PSAA using cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) and to analyze its relationship with the vertical position of the simulated lateral window relative to the sinus floor, relevant to sinus floor elevation procedures.MethodsA retrospective observational analysis was performed on 1152 MS assessed on CBCT scans obtained for various diagnostic purposes. Scans fully visualizing the MS and lateral wall were included. The PSAA was identified and classified according to its anatomical location: intraosseous, submucosal, or extraosseous. The vertical distance between the artery and the sinus floor was measured, and the vertical position of the simulated lateral window relative to the sinus floor was categorized into four classes (0–3 mm, 3–8 mm, 8–11 mm, and > 11 mm). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the distribution of PSAA visibility, anatomical location, and lateral window height classes. Associations between PSAA anatomical location and vertical window position were examined using the Chi-square test for independence.ResultsThe PSAA was identified in 68.1% of evaluated MS and was more frequently visible in males than in females. The artery was most commonly located beneath the Schneiderian membrane (48.6%), followed by intraosseous (17.6%) and extraosseous (1.8%) courses. The mean distance from the sinus floor was 8.3 ± 4.3 mm. Higher vertical positions of the simulated lateral window were significantly, although weakly, associated with an intraosseous arterial course.ConclusionsThe PSAA is identifiable on CBCT in approximately two-thirds of MS, most commonly following a submucosal course, with a mean distance of 8.3 mm from the sinus floor. PSAA location was significantly associated with the vertical position of the simulated lateral window relative to the sinus floor, supporting the value of CBCT-based anatomical assessment for preoperative lateral window planning in MS floor elevation procedures.Keywords: Posterior superior alveolar artery, Cone-beam computed tomography, Maxillary sinus floor elevation, Lateral window, Vascular anatomy

Background

Rehabilitation of the posterior edentulous maxilla often presents a significant challenge in oral and maxillofacial surgery due to limited bone height resulting from anatomical constraints or post-extraction bone resorption. Maxillary sinus (MS) floor elevation has become the gold-standard technique to address this limitation, providing a predictable and well-documented method for creating sufficient bone volume to support dental implants. This procedure plays a pivotal role in contemporary implant dentistry, ensuring long-term functional and esthetic outcomes. Over the years, various augmentation techniques have been proposed to improve bone support in the posterior maxilla; however, sinus floor elevation remains the most widely accepted and reliable approach [1, 2].

Lateral wall sinus floor elevation involves creating a bony window in the lateral wall of the MS to elevate the Schneiderian membrane and place a bone graft beneath it, thereby increasing vertical bone height for implant placement. New bone formation is influenced by both the residual alveolar bone height and the choice of grafting material. Consequently, clinicians tend to individualize biomaterial selection according to patient-specific parameters, including residual bone height and sinus anatomy, rather than relying on a universally accepted grafting material [3]. Performing a lateral window osteotomy in the posterior maxilla remains a challenging procedure due to the complex anatomical structures within the MS. The introduction of endoscopic assistance, precision drilling systems, and other ongoing innovations has reduced intraoperative complications, improved clinical outcomes, and transformed sinus floor elevation into a safer, faster, and more predictable procedure, shifting it from a complex, high-risk intervention to a patient-friendly and reliable surgical technique [4]. When performed correctly, sinus floor elevation procedure demonstrates a high success rate exceeding 90%, particularly in patients with ≥ 4 mm of residual bone height [5].

Tooth loss in the posterior maxilla, particularly in the first molar region, is a common indication for MS floor elevation due to the combined effects of alveolar bone resorption and MS pneumatization [6, 7]. Several studies have reported that this site is the most frequently associated with the need for sinus lift procedures [8, 9].

Arterial supply of the MS walls is provided primarily by three main arteries, subdivisions of the maxillary artery: the infraorbital artery (IOA), the posterior lateral nasal artery, and the posterior superior alveolar artery (PSAA), all of which may be encountered during lateral approach sinus elevation surgery. The lateral maxilla is supplied by branches of PSAA and the IOA that form an anastomosis, alveolar antral artery, which also supplies the Schneiderian membrane [10, 11].

Due to its anatomical course and potential involvement in the surgical field, the PSAA represents a critical structure to identify when planning and performing lateral window sinus floor elevation. Accidental injury to this vessel can result in intraoperative bleeding, compromised visibility, prolonged surgical time, and reduced graft stability.

The lateral osteotomy window is generally created 2–3 mm above the floor of the MS, with its superior border and overall length determined by the number and size of the implants planned for placement [12]. The height of the lateral window for MS floor elevation can be classified as small (< 5 mm), medium (5–8 mm), or large (> 8 mm). Small windows provide minimally invasive access with limited visibility, while medium windows are commonly used for reduced-access or single-implant sites. Large windows are indicated for conventional access in multi-implant cases or when wide sinus exposure is required [13–16].

The PSAA and the sinus septa are critical anatomical variables for lateral window MS floor elevation. Variations in the PSAA’s course and diameter influence intraoperative bleeding risk and may affect postoperative bone healing by modulating local perfusion of the grafted area. Preserving this vascular supply—together with preoperative assessment of sinus septa—supports stable graft integration and predictable bone regeneration.

Preoperative assessment of the PSAA—its presence, location, and distance from the sinus floor—using cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) is therefore an essential step in minimizing complications and improving surgical predictability. Knowledge of the vascular anatomy of the lateral maxilla, particularly the PSAA, enables clinicians to plan the lateral window more safely and strategically, thereby enhancing the success of MS floor elevation procedures.

While several CBCT-based studies have described the anatomical variability and prevalence of the PSAA [17–19], most have focused primarily on its radiographic visibility or its mean distance from the sinus floor, without correlating these findings with practical surgical parameters. The present study extends previous analyses [14, 17, 18] by introducing a clinically oriented, radiographic model that relates PSAA localization to the vertical position of the simulated lateral window relative to the sinus floor, reflecting commonly used concepts of lateral window design in sinus augmentation.

The aim of this study was to evaluate the radiological relationship between the PSAA and the MS floor on CBCT and to propose an anatomically based framework intended to support preoperative planning of lateral window sinus lift procedures.

This correlation is intended to assist clinicians in estimating the available vertical space for lateral window preparation and in anticipating potential vascular considerations, rather than to define prescriptive surgical protocols.

Materials and methods

This study was designed as a retrospective observational analysis of CBCT scans to evaluate the presence and anatomical characteristics of the PSAA. All CBCT scans met predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria and were reviewed to ensure complete visualization of the MS, including its anterior and lateral walls. The analysis focused on identifying the PSAA and assessing its relevance for lateral window sinus floor elevation procedures.

The research was conducted at the Municipal Emergency Hospital, Timisoara (Romania), using CBCT scans retrospectively retrieved from the institutional radiological database during the study period, in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (E-3790/16.09.2025). All patients provided informed consent for the use of their anonymized clinical and radiological data for research purposes.

This study is reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for cross-sectional studies.

The CBCT scans analyzed in this study were obtained as part of routine diagnostic protocols at our institution and were predominantly performed for the evaluation of various maxillofacial conditions, with only a minority acquired for preoperative planning of MS floor elevation. As the sample was not restricted to patients undergoing sinus floor elevation procedures, the risk of selection bias was reduced, thereby enhancing the generalizability of the findings.

Inclusion criteria comprised patients aged ≥ 18 years who underwent CBCT imaging of the posterior maxilla for any diagnostic purpose, with high-quality scans encompassing the MS and the lateral wall of the posterior maxilla, and images suitable for assessment of the PSAA in terms of visibility and anatomical delineation. The minimum age of 18 years was selected to exclude individuals with potentially incomplete MS development and vascular maturation. Exclusion criteria included patients under 18 years of age; CBCT scans presenting artifacts, insufficient resolution, or incomplete coverage of the MS region; patients with a history of maxillofacial trauma, surgical intervention, or pathology (e.g., tumors, cysts) affecting the anatomy of the posterior maxilla or MS; and those with active MS infection at the time of imaging. Fully edentulous maxillae were excluded because extensive alveolar resorption can obscure the sinus floor and distort the spatial relationship with the PSAA. There were no gender-based restrictions for inclusion in the study.

Considering the inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 1152 MS were evaluated on CBCT images, including 547 sinuses from male patients and 605 sinuses from female patients, all fully covering the MS along with its anterior and lateral walls. As this was a retrospective CBCT-based investigation, no a priori sample size calculation was performed, and all available scans meeting the eligibility criteria during the study period were consecutively included. The MS constituted the unit of analysis; when CBCT examinations included both sinuses, each was evaluated separately, and patient sex was recorded solely for stratified descriptive and subgroup analyses. This sample size exceeds those of comparable CBCT-based studies and was considered sufficient to ensure stable frequency estimates and reliable subgroup analyses. At this sample size, prevalence estimates can be obtained with good statistical precision (approximately ± 3% margin of error at a 95% confidence level), supporting robust estimation of PSAA prevalence and its distribution across subgroups. All CBCT examinations were performed using a Planmeca VISOG7 CBCT unit (Planmeca, Helsinki, Finland). Scans were acquired under routine diagnostic conditions with a tube voltage of 110 kV and a tube current of 8 mA, with an exposure time of approximately 12.5 s. The field of view (FOV) was selected according to the diagnostic indication and included volumes of 8 × 15 cm and 13 × 15 cm. Image acquisition was performed with an isotropic voxel size of 0.15 mm. Images were reconstructed using the manufacturer’s proprietary software (Romexis version 6.4.2.49) and subsequently analyzed using OnDemand3D (Cybermed Inc., Daejeon, Republic of Korea). All CBCT datasets were assessed under consistent viewing conditions within the same software environment and using the original acquisition parameters. Multiplanar reconstructions were evaluated at the native acquisition resolution, without additional slice thickening. Brightness and contrast settings were adjusted using standardized viewing presets and applied uniformly across all examinations to ensure reproducible visualization of anatomical landmarks. The importance of standardized CBCT visualization in ensuring consistent radiographic assessment has been highlighted in recent methodological literature [20]. Image analysis was independently performed by two oral and maxillofacial surgeons with substantial experience in three-dimensional radiographic assessment. In cases of discrepancy, the observers reviewed the images together to reach a consensus. Furthermore, both observers underwent a calibration process before data collection, jointly analyzing 30 randomly selected CBCT scans that were not part of the main dataset. Inter-observer reliability was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient to ensure consistency of the recorded measurements.

The PSAA was identified on coronal CBCT sections. Its location within the lateral wall of the MS was classified into three categories according to established radiological criteria [16, 17]: class 1 – intraosseous, when the canal was completely encased within bone, class 2 – submucosal, when visible immediately beneath the Schneiderian membrane, and class 3 – extraosseous, when located along the external cortical surface of the lateral sinus wall (Fig. 1).

Coronal CBCT views and schematic diagrams showing the PSAA in the lateral wall of the MS: (A) intraosseous, (B) submucosal, and (C) extraosseous

The vertical distance between the PSAA and the floor of the MS was measured on coronal sections using the software’s length measurement tool, as illustrated in Fig. 2. This parameter was selected because the sinus floor represents a reproducible anatomical landmark for PSAA localization on CBCT, and because alveolar ridge resorption does not directly affect this reference level, although sinus pneumatization following tooth extraction may vary between individuals and was not specifically assessed or controlled for in this retrospective analysis. For this reason, measurements were performed irrespective of the presence of the maxillary first molar, reflecting the anatomical focus of this observational study; tooth presence/absence in the region of interest was not used as an inclusion criterion. All evaluations were carried out in the coronal sections corresponding to the region of the maxillary first molar. For standardization purposes, the coronal section was selected at the anteroposterior level corresponding to the maxillary first molar region, based on the morphology of the lateral wall of the MS and the sinus floor as stable anatomical references, rather than on dental structures, allowing consistent assessment irrespective of tooth presence or absence.

Data from CBCT evaluations were initially organized in Microsoft Excel and subsequently imported into SPSS (version 26, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) for statistical analysis. Both descriptive and comparative statistical methods were applied.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the study variables. Categorical variables, including PSAA visibility and anatomical classification (intraosseous, submucosal, extraosseous), were reported as absolute frequencies and percentages, both overall and stratified by sex. Continuous variables, specifically the vertical distance between the PSAA and the floor of the MS, were expressed as mean ± standard deviation.

The comparative analysis aimed to determine how the design of the lateral window—particularly its vertical position—could influence the likelihood of encountering the PSAA during surgery. Given that the analyzed CBCT scans were obtained for diagnostic purposes rather than sinus lift planning, the simulated window classifications serve as an anatomical model rather than a direct surgical prediction. For this purpose, the vertical position of the simulated lateral window relative to the sinus floor was classified into four categories, with the inferior border of the window consistently set at 3 mm above the sinus floor, in accordance with established surgical protocols; rather than defining the window based on its effective height, this approach relied on window dimensions commonly reported in the literature to determine the vertical position of the simulated window.

Class 1 (0–3 mm) referred to cases in which the PSAA was located within 3 mm of the sinus floor and therefore lay below the inferior border of the simulated window, without interfering with the osteotomy site. For Classes 2, 3, and 4, the vertical position of the simulated lateral window was defined by adding the 3 mm distance from the sinus floor to the inferior border of the window to the standard window dimensions described in the literature. Accordingly, Class 2 (3–8 mm) corresponded to small windows, typically used for single-implant sites; Class 3 (8–11 mm) described medium windows providing broader access for one or two implants; and Class 4 (> 11 mm) represented large windows, generally required for multiple implants or wide sinus exposure (Table 1). To the best of our knowledge, this four-class radiographic classification of the vertical position of the simulated lateral window relative to the sinus floor has not been previously described in the literature and is proposed here for the first time as an exploratory anatomical model. This classification allowed evaluation of the relationship between the vertical position of the simulated lateral window and the risk of intersecting the PSAA during lateral window sinus floor elevation (Fig. 3).

In addition to frequency distribution, comparative analysis was used to determine which window classes were most frequent and whether statistically significant differences existed among them. The primary focus was on Classes 2, 3, and 4 (small, medium, and large windows), as Class 1 represented cases in which the artery was located within 3 mm of the sinus floor and did not interfere with the osteotomy window. The analysis aimed to establish the probability of PSAA interference for each window size and to determine whether the differences were statistically significant. These comparisons were carried out both for the overall sample and separately for males and females. Statistical significance of associations between window classes and PSAA location was tested using the Chi-square test for independence, with a p-value < 0.05 considered significant.

Results

Inter-observer reliability for PSAA identification and classification was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient (κ). The analysis demonstrated a κ value of 0.87, indicating almost perfect agreement between the two observers and confirming the consistency of the recorded measurements.

A total of 1152 MS were evaluated; results are presented overall and stratified according to the sex of the patient from whom each sinus originated.

Results of the Chi-square test assessing the association between the vertical position of the simulated lateral window relative to the sinus floor and the anatomical location of the PSAA

A Chi-square test of independence evaluated the association between the vertical position of the simulated lateral window relative to the sinus floor and the anatomical location of the PSAA. The analysis revealed a statistically significant association between the two variables, χ²(6) = 19.11, p = 0.004, indicating that the distribution of PSAA locations varied according to the vertical position of the simulated lateral window. Cramer’s V was 0.111, suggesting a weak association strength despite statistical significance.

Standardized residual analysis indicated that intraosseous PSAA localization (Class 1) was identified significantly more frequently than expected in the higher vertical position group corresponding to large windows (> 11 mm; residual = 2.3), suggesting a positive association between a higher vertical window position and intraosseous artery location. Conversely, the submucosal localization (Class 2) was slightly underrepresented in the same group (residual = − 1.7), approaching the significance threshold, while external cortical localization (Class 3) showed a mild but non-significant overrepresentation (residual = 1.8).

In contrast, lower vertical positions corresponding to small windows (3–8 mm) tended to show a lower frequency of intraosseous PSAA (residual = − 1.8), though this difference did not reach statistical significance. Overall, these findings indicate that as the vertical position of the simulated lateral window relative to the sinus floor increases, the PSAA more frequently follows an intraosseous course, potentially increasing the likelihood of arterial exposure. A subsequent hierarchical loglinear analysis including sex as a third factor confirmed these findings. The three-way interaction among PSAA location, vertical window position class, and sex was not statistically significant, χ²(4) = 4.59, p = 0.332, indicating that the relationship between window position and PSAA location was consistent across both sexes.

Chi-square subgroup analyses further showed that the association was not significant in males, χ²(6) = 6.72, p = 0.347, but reached significance in females, χ²(6) = 17.24, p = 0.008, with a moderate effect size (Cramer’s V = 0.211). However, due to violations of Chi-square assumptions related to low cell frequencies, these gender-specific results should be interpreted with caution. Discussion

In this CBCT-based study, the PSAA was identified in approximately two-thirds of evaluated sinuses, most frequently following a submucosal course, with a mean vertical distance of 8.3 mm from the sinus floor. A statistically significant, although weak, association was observed between the vertical position of the simulated lateral window relative to the sinus floor and PSAA localization, with higher vertical window positions being more frequently associated with an intraosseous arterial course. These findings underscore the relevance of preoperative CBCT assessment in lateral window planning, particularly when more superiorly positioned osteotomies are anticipated [17, 18, 21].

The predominance of a submucosal PSAA course observed in the present study is consistent with the anatomical configuration of the lateral maxillary wall, where the artery frequently runs immediately beneath the Schneiderian membrane as part of the alveolar antral vascular network. This distribution may be explained by the relatively thin bony component of the lateral sinus wall and by the close relationship between the PSAA and the sinus mucosa, which facilitates vascular supply to both the membrane and the underlying bone [22, 23]. In contrast, the greater frequency of an intraosseous PSAA course observed at higher vertical window positions may reflect anatomical variations in lateral wall thickness and sinus morphology, whereby a thicker bony wall accommodates an intraosseous arterial canal. This anatomical configuration provides a plausible explanation for the observed statistical association between window position and arterial localization.

The prevalence and localization patterns of the PSAA identified in this study are broadly comparable to previous CBCT-based investigations, which have reported variable anatomical distributions within the lateral maxillary wall, most commonly involving submucosal and intraosseous courses [17, 18, 21]. Reported PSAA detection rates in the literature range from approximately 60% to nearly 100%, depending on imaging protocols, voxel resolution, and population characteristics [18, 24, 25]. Recent CBCT studies have continued to document this variability. Tofangchiha et al. (2022) identified the PSAA in 76.3% of cases and reported an intraosseous course in 63.6% of identified arteries [26]. Torkzadeh et al. (2024) observed PSAA visibility in 77% of dentate and 72.7% of edentulous sinuses, with intraosseous localization representing 54.8% of cases [27]. Moreover, Alqhtani et al. (2025) reported a PSAA detection rate of 98.3% in their CBCT sample, with submucosal localization being the most frequent pattern in the molar region, followed by intraosseous and cortical positions [25]. The 68.1% detection rate observed in the present study falls within this reported range.

When stratified descriptively by sex, PSAA detection appeared higher in male-derived sinuses; however, the anatomical or imaging-related factors underlying this difference cannot be determined within the present study design and should therefore be interpreted with caution. The findings do not allow definitive conclusions regarding inherent sex-related anatomical differences of the lateral sinus wall.

The mean vertical distance between the PSAA and the sinus floor identified in this study (8.3 mm) is comparable to the values reported by Bedeloğlu et al., who described mean distances of 9.54 ± 4.27 mm at the first detection point and 7.91 ± 4.75 mm at the last detection point [17]. Contemporary CBCT analyses have reported mean PSAA–sinus floor distances of 6.87 mm (Tofangchiha et al., 2022) and approximately 6.18–6.31 mm in the molar region (Alqhtani et al., 2025) [25, 26]. Minor discrepancies across studies may reflect population-specific anatomical variation as well as methodological differences in measurement reference points and regional assessment. Importantly, few previous investigations have evaluated the relationship between lateral window position and PSAA localization; therefore, the present analysis offers an additional anatomical perspective by correlating arterial course with radiographically defined window position categories.

From a clinical perspective, the present findings highlight the importance of tailoring lateral window design to individual anatomical characteristics of the MS wall. Higher vertical positioning of the simulated lateral window was more frequently associated with an intraosseous arterial course, suggesting that extended or superiorly positioned osteotomies may increase the likelihood of encountering an intraosseous vascular canal. Preoperative CBCT assessment enables clinicians to anticipate the spatial relationship between the planned osteotomy and the PSAA, facilitating adjustments in window position aimed at minimizing vascular complications [17, 18, 21].

Beyond sinus floor elevation, the proposed anatomical framework may also have relevance in surgical scenarios involving sinus septa, variable lateral wall thickness, or complex sinus morphology that could influence osteotomy design. In implant planning requiring multiple fixtures or extended lateral access, awareness of the vertical relationship between the PSAA and the sinus floor may assist in optimizing surgical approach. These considerations should be interpreted as anatomical guidance rather than as direct predictors of surgical complications.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, its retrospective design and the use of CBCT scans acquired for routine diagnostic purposes rather than specifically for sinus lift planning may have influenced anatomical assessment. Dentate or edentulous status in the region of interest was not analyzed as a dedicated variable, and sinus pneumatization following tooth loss was not controlled for. The inclusion of both MS from the same individual, when available, means that observations may not be fully independent. As the unit of analysis in this CBCT-based anatomical investigation was the sinus rather than the patient, no multilevel or clustered statistical model was applied to adjust for potential intra-individual correlation. Consequently, residual statistical dependence between bilateral sinuses cannot be entirely excluded, and subgroup analyses—particularly those stratified by sex—should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Second, assessment of the vertical position of the simulated lateral window relative to the sinus floor was based on radiographic simulation rather than direct intraoperative measurement and may not fully replicate surgical dynamics. Although inter-observer agreement was high, intra-rater reliability was not evaluated. Third, the four-class radiographic classification applied in this study represents a study-specific, exploratory model intended to facilitate analysis of PSAA localization in relation to commonly reported surgical window dimensions; it is not a validated framework and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Moreover, the CBCT-based anatomical nature of this analysis did not allow direct correlation with intraoperative findings or clinical outcomes.

Within these limitations, the present CBCT-based analysis provides detailed insight into the spatial relationship between the PSAA and the lateral MS wall, particularly in relation to radiographically defined lateral window positions. The large sample size and the integration of arterial localization with clinically relevant window concepts represent key strengths of this study. By correlating vascular anatomy with surgical planning parameters, the findings contribute to a more anatomy-guided approach to lateral window sinus floor elevation.

Conclusions

This CBCT-based study showed that the PSAA can be identified in approximately two-thirds of MS and most frequently follows a submucosal course beneath the Schneiderian membrane. The mean vertical distance between the PSAA and the sinus floor was 8.3 mm. A statistically significant, although weak, association was observed between the vertical position of the simulated lateral window relative to the sinus floor and PSAA localization, with higher vertical window positions more frequently corresponding to an intraosseous arterial course. These findings support the value of CBCT-based anatomical assessment as a complementary tool for individualized lateral window planning in MS floor elevation procedures.
