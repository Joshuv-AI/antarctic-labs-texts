Enhancing completeness and forecasting of early post-mainshock sequences: A case study of the 2021 Qinghai Maduo MS7.4 earthquake
Authors: Jinmeng Bi, Ye Li, Ye Gao, Yuhang Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryEarly post-mainshock catalogs are often incomplete, causing biased parameter estimation and unreliable forecasts. Using the 2021 Qinghai Maduo M7.4 intraplate earthquake, we assess two approaches: data replenishment using the bi-scale empirical probability integral transformation (BEPIT) method and Bayesian spatiotemporal epidemic-type aftershock sequence (ETAS) model. Early catalog incompleteness is the primary cause of ETAS parameter instability. After replenishment, the a value rises from 3.9952 to 4.4836 and the b value from 0.57 to 0.69, indicating more small aftershocks. Bayesian ETAS provides probabilistic forecasts, early overestimation of larger events diminishes as data accumulate, and high-probability zones match observed aftershocks. Statistical tests confirm acceptable performance but show sensitivity to prior settings. As this study is based on a single sequence, generalizability requires validation across diverse tectonic regimes. Nevertheless, BEPIT replenishment and Bayesian updating enhance early post-mainshock parameter estimation and short-term forecasting for intraplate events, supporting rapid response and dynamic risk assessment.

Introduction

The spatiotemporal evolution of early aftershock sequences provides a critical window into understanding fault rupture processes, rapid regional stress field adjustments, and the inherent dynamics of earthquake sequences.1 Accurately characterizing seismicity in the hours to days following a mainshock is of paramount scientific. It is essential for determining sequence type (e.g., mainshock-aftershock or swarm), assessing strong aftershock risk, elucidating the physical mechanisms of fault interaction, and guiding emergency response.2 However, factors such as waveform overlap, processing facility overload, and damage to seismic stations caused by the mainshock often lead to the omission of numerous smaller aftershocks, resulting in incomplete earthquake catalogs.3,4,5 This systematic data incompleteness directly undermines the objective basis for seismicity analysis.

Recently, researchers have begun to analyze earthquake sequences from the multi-dimensional perspective of space-time-depth-magnitude (STDM) to reveal more complex seismic behavior.6 However, early catalog incompleteness remains a common challenge that hinders accurate parameter estimation in such multi-dimensional analyses. For instance, the maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) of parameters in widely used models describing spatiotemporal earthquake clustering, including the epidemic-type aftershock sequence (ETAS) model7 and the Reasenberg-Jones model,8 is highly sensitive to catalog completeness. Missing data in the early post-mainshock period can lead to substantial bias and instability in estimating key model parameters (the α parameter representing aftershock triggering efficiency, the μ parameter for background seismicity rate, and the c and p values in the Omori-Utsu decay formula).9 Such uncertainties can adversely affect the judgment of sequence characteristics, distort the understanding of aftershock decay and triggering potential, and ultimately severely degrade the reliability of short-term aftershock forecasts and seismic hazard assessments based on these models.10

To address the problem of early post-mainshock data incompleteness, several technical approaches have been developed. One involves detection techniques like waveform-matched filtering to identify more hidden events from continuous records.11 However, this method requires pre-determined event templates as input, which are not always available. Furthermore, waveform similarity decreases with increasing source distance, causing a bias toward detecting events similar to the templates. Consequently, incompleteness may persist even after large earthquakes.12 A second approach utilizes deep learning for aftershock detection and location.13 While capable of detecting small events, it suffers from high computational costs and limitations inherent to phase-picking methods, potentially leading to false detections and missed low signal-to-noise-ratio events. The third avenue is developing data-driven replenishment algorithms. Among these, the stochastic replenishment method based on the bi-scale empirical probability integral transformation (BEPIT), proposed by Zhuang et al.,9,14 is notable. This method operates under a simple assumption of statistical independence between magnitude and occurrence time, requiring no pre-specified process model or complete marked distribution. It is applicable to sequences in regions with sparse station coverage or weak early monitoring capabilities. Through iterative reweighting and negative binomial distribution simulation, it robustly reconstructs missing event information (time, location, and magnitude), effectively enhancing the stability of parameter estimation for models like ETAS. Nevertheless, the BEPIT technique still has certain limitations regarding its accuracy and reliability,15 necessitating further validation through more case studies, theoretical analysis, and experimental testing.

In recent years, real-time probabilistic aftershock forecasting based on the ETAS model has demonstrated operational potential for hazard mitigation in several regions,16,17,18,19 often outperforming other statistical and physics-based seismicity models.20,21 However, its forecasting performance is heavily constrained by the requirement for high-quality, complete data input during the early post-mainshock stage.10,22 A key challenge in improving model predictive capability lies in maximizing the use of information from early incomplete catalogs to reduce parameter estimation uncertainty.23,24,25 Adopting a Bayesian statistical framework, which explicitly handles parameter uncertainty arising from data incompleteness by incorporating prior distributions, offers a promising solution.22,26,27,28,29 The Bayesian approach allows the explicit assessment of model parameter posterior distributions using prior distributions, thereby quantifying uncertainties and mitigating the impact of missing data.28,30,31,32 Its effectiveness has been validated in several cases, including the 2016 Amatrice earthquake sequence in central Italy,28 seismic sequences in Greece from 1995 to 2018,33 induced seismicity at the St. Gallen deep geothermal field in Switzerland,31 and the 2008 Ölfus earthquake in Iceland.34

Focusing on the challenge of aftershock forecasting under early post-mainshock data incompleteness, this study takes the May 22, 2021, Qinghai Maduo M7.4 earthquake as a case study. Although the BEPIT method and the Bayesian spatiotemporal ETAS model have each demonstrated potential in data replenishment and uncertainty quantification, their application to intraplate earthquake sequences in the Tibetan Plateau remains unexplored. To address this gap, we apply the BEPIT technique proposed by Zhuang et al.9,14 to compensate for missing early data. We compare data characteristics and parameter variations before and after replenishment, testing the method’s stability and generalizability across different magnitude thresholds and time windows. From another perspective, we integrate prior information using a Bayesian spatiotemporal ETAS model28,35 to analyze model parameters and their corresponding uncertainties, exploring the model’s forecasting performance. This work aims to provide a scientific reference for maximizing the use of prior information during the data-deficient early phase and for post-earthquake trend analysis. By systematically and quantitatively revealing the extent to which early catalog incompleteness affects aftershock sequence parameter estimation and short-term forecasting, this work validates the applicability of these methods in an intraplate seismic setting and provides a methodological reference for the design of post-mainshock rapid forecasting procedures. Furthermore, it seeks to compare and evaluate the performance of these two technical pathways in mitigating data incompleteness and enhancing parameter stability and forecast reliability, thereby offering a scientific reference for maximizing the use of prior information during the data-deficient early phase and for post-earthquake trend analysis.

Results

Aftershock replenishment and sequence parameter calibration based on statistical seismology

Early post-mainshock data replenishment

The BEPIT technique operates on a simple assumption, the independence of earthquake magnitude from its occurrence time. This allows the method to be applied to replenish various types of seismic sequences, including aftershocks, foreshocks, and earthquake swarms.9 It aids in assessing the impact of missing data and correcting the biases thereby introduced. Bi et al.36 applied this technique to the 2021 Qinghai Maduo M7.4 earthquake sequence, as illustrated in Figure 1. Based on the detection rate curve shown in Figure S1B, missing data regions, which are temporal-spatial monitoring blind spots, were identified (represented by the blue polygon in Figure 1A). Replenishment was then performed using statistical relationships established from the complete records during the period of recovered monitoring capability. For the Maduo sequence, the number of seismic events above 1.0 within the first day increased from 522 to 3,562 after replenishment, a nearly 7-fold increase. The added events are predominantly small-magnitude aftershocks (green dots in Figure 1E). Their temporal distribution aligns with the early-stage decay law, substantially correcting the apparent activity level in the immediate post-mainshock period. The cumulative event count curve for the replenished sequence (Figure 1F) exhibits a smoother and more reasonable growth trend in the early stage, demonstrating the effectiveness of the replenishment. Furthermore, applying this technique to the aftershock sequence of the 2008 Sichuan Wenchuan M8.0 earthquake revealed that up to 30% of M ≥ 3 events were missing due to incomplete monitoring capability.14

Impact of replenishment on ETAS model parameters

Based on the earthquake sequences before and after replenishment, the parameters of the ETAS model were fitted using the MLE method. To ensure the completeness of the earthquake catalog and the participation of a sufficient number of aftershock events in the calculation, a continuous, sliding ETAS model fitting was adopted for the May 22, 2021, Qinghai Maduo M7.4 earthquake sequence. The fitting start time began at C0 (0.10 days), with a completeness magnitude Mc2.0. The fitting end time (sequence duration) started from 1.00 day after the mainshock and increased continuously to 20.00 days of the sequence in 1.00-day steps, resulting in a total of 20 sliding fitting periods.

To further examine the fitting performance of the ETAS model, the “residual analysis” method7 was employed. The fitting performance of the ETAS model before and after replenishment was tested using this method, as shown in Figure 2. The results indicate that compared to the original events before replenishment, the ETAS model fitting based on the replenished events performed better, reducing the impact of missing small earthquakes. As seen from the figure, in the early post-mainshock stage, the seismic sequence parameters change relatively drastically, and the range of standard deviation values changes noticeably, especially for the original sequence lacking small earthquakes, reflecting the rapid stress adjustment process in the source region during the early post-mainshock stage. As events continue, the sequence parameters gradually transition toward stability. Compared to the original sequence, the replenished ETAS parameters (blue line) converge to stable values more rapidly after the sequence begins. Taking parameter c as an example, the c value of the original sequence showed drastic fluctuations in orders of magnitude in the first 3 days (e.g., as high as 2.21 on day 1, then sharply dropping to 0.98 on day 2), while the c value of the replenished sequence was at a more reasonable order of magnitude (∼0.01–0.10) from the beginning and stabilized in a shorter time (after about 5 days). The replenished earthquake catalog yields more stable sequence parameter values, thereby leading to more accurate aftershock forecasting results. Zhuang et al.,9 using the April 15, 2016, Kumamoto, Japan, earthquake sequence as an example, discussed the impact of short-term aftershock incompleteness (STAI) on ETAS parameter estimation. They used the BEPIT to replenish missing data in the early post-mainshock stage and then compared the maximum likelihood estimates of ETAS parameters from the Japan Meteorological Agency (JMA) catalog dataset with those from the replenished data. When the magnitude threshold Mc ≥ 3, which approximates the static completeness magnitude of the JMA catalog, the estimated ETAS parameters for the two datasets were roughly similar. However, significant differences existed for values where Mc < 3. Directly estimating ETAS parameters from the original dataset without considering the completeness magnitude could lead to significant errors.9 Similarly, Seif et al.10 reached analogous conclusions based on simulated earthquake catalogs for Southern California, noting that using lower magnitude thresholds below the completeness level, especially for certain parameters, could lead to erroneous fittings. Building on this, Zheng et al.15 developed a semi-automatic earthquake selection technique. By replenishing five moderate-sized incomplete sequences in inland Japan after 2000, they found that the replenished dataset became usable within a relatively short time after the mainshock. When the magnitude threshold varied, the ETAS parameters were more stable and consistent, facilitating rapid aftershock prediction and related research work in the early post-mainshock period.

Probabilistic forecasting by utilizing early prior information

An alternative approach to early post-mainshock aftershock forecasting involves mitigating the uncertainties inherent in traditional methods and reducing the impact of data incompleteness. This is achieved by constructing models that maximize the use of prior information to enable near-real-time short-term aftershock prediction. This study employs the Bayesian spatiotemporal ETAS model as an example to conduct relevant research in this direction.

Distribution characteristics of spatiotemporal ETAS model parameters

To further analyze the variation characteristics of the spatiotemporal ETAS model parameters, the uncertainty of the model parameters was examined and evaluated using their posterior distributions. Prior distributions are assigned to θ using independent lognormal distributions, which ensure positivity. The prior means are set to [β = 1.8, c = 0.05 day, p = 1.5, d = 1.0 km, q = 1.5], with a coefficient of variation of 0.3 for each parameter to maintain a weakly informative yet sufficiently flexible specification. These values are selected based on regional seismicity characteristics and reference values from the literature.28,35 Specifically, the prior mean for β corresponds to a b value of approximately 0.78 (β = b·ln10), which lies within the typical b-value range for earthquake sequences in mainland China. The prior mean for c is set to 0.05 day to account for possible overestimation due to early-stage catalog incompleteness. The prior means for p, d, and q follow common practice in spatiotemporal ETAS modeling.28,35 In addition, we conducted tests using a controlled variable approach, in which a single prior parameter was varied to assess its impact on the sequence parameters. The results are consistent with those of Bi et al.,38 indicating that the choice of prior parameters has a significant influence on early-stage parameter fitting, but its effect gradually diminishes as the sequence evolves.

Posterior samples p(θ∣seq, Mc) are generated using an adaptive Metropolis-Hastings Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithm.39 Six parallel chains are run, each with 220 iterations. The first 20 iterations (approximately 10%) are discarded as burn-in. The proposal distribution was a multivariate normal with an adaptively updated covariance matrix,40 with the scaling factor adjusted every 50 iterations to achieve an acceptance rate of 0.2–0.4. MCMC convergence is assessed using the Gelman-Rubin statistic Rˆ. For all ETAS parameters (β, c, p, d, and q), the Rˆ values are below 1.1, indicating that the ratio of between-chain variability to within-chain variability is within an acceptable range and that all chains have converged to a common posterior distribution. In addition, the effective sample size (ESS) is used to evaluate the impact of autocorrelation on the precision of posterior estimates. The computed ESS values are all above 200, ensuring sufficient stability in the estimation of posterior means and quantiles.

To better validate the discrepancy between the number of earthquakes predicted by the Bayesian spatiotemporal ETAS model and the number actually observed, the N-test method for earthquake count evaluation within the Collaboratory for the Study of Earthquake Predictability (CSEP) framework was employed.41 An effective significance level of αeff = 0.025 was chosen for conducting a one-sided N-test. When the result meets the condition indicating under-prediction, it signifies a scenario of “too few” predicted aftershocks; conversely, meeting the condition for overprediction indicates a scenario of “too high” predicted aftershocks.

In addition, to better evaluate the forecasting performance, we quantified the results using the log likelihood ratio and calculated the probability gain accordingly. Overall, the Bayesian ETAS model did not show a gain in forecast performance compared to the stationary Poisson model, which is consistent with the characteristics of the Maduo sequence, namely the scarcity of large aftershocks and the overestimation in early forecasts.

It should be noted that the forecasting performance evaluation in this study primarily relies on the N-test (for event count) and probability gain calculations, without employing spatial consistency tests such as the L-test or S-test recommended by the CSEP framework. The Maduo aftershock sequence exhibits a strongly linear spatial distribution along the rupture fault, and the number of observed events within each 0.05° × 0.05° spatial cell is very low, making the spatial Poisson assumption underlying the L-test difficult to satisfy. The primary focus of this study is to evaluate the impact of catalog incompleteness on parameter estimation and temporal forecasting, rather than to rigorously test spatial prediction accuracy. The spatial probability maps (Figure 8) are provided as a qualitative reference for emergency response. Future work with denser aftershock sequences and finer spatial grids will be needed to systematically apply spatial consistency tests.

Discussion

Taking the 2021 Qinghai Maduo M7.4 earthquake as a case study, this work systematically investigated the impact of early catalog incompleteness on sequence parameter estimation and forecasting. It also evaluates how catalog replenishment and Bayesian modeling improve the reliability of aftershock sequence analysis. Our results indicate that the BEPIT technique can effectively replenish missing small-magnitude events in the early stage, significantly improving the stability and convergence speed of ETAS parameters. Meanwhile, the Bayesian spatiotemporal ETAS model can integrate prior information to provide relatively reliable short-term probabilistic forecasts. Nevertheless, this study has certain limitations that warrant further investigation.

Assumptions, limitations, and comparison with other data replenishment methods

The BEPIT method operates under the assumption that earthquake magnitude and occurrence time are statistically independent. Although this assumption is approximately satisfied for the Maduo sequence (as verified by Kruskal-Wallis test42), it may not hold perfectly in all real aftershock sequences. Some studies suggest that the proportion of larger events in the early aftershock period could be slightly elevated.4 If such dependence existed in the Maduo sequence, it could lead to a slight overestimation of early small-magnitude events. However, in this study, the replenished events are predominantly small events below M2.0, and the b value increased from 0.57 to 0.69 after replenishment, consistent with typical aftershock statistics. This indicates that any potential dependence has only a limited impact on the overall replenishment results. Future research could explore data replenishment methods that account for possible magnitude-time dependence.

Compared to waveform-based detection methods, the BEPIT technique does not require pre-defined event templates or complete distributions, making it highly practical for regions with sparse stations and limited monitoring capability. However, matched-filter techniques43,44,45,46 and deep-learning-based event detection methods13,47 also offer significant advantages in enhancing catalog completeness. Especially when waveform data quality is high and template events are available, these methods can identify hidden events more precisely.48,49 Future research could explore combining statistical replenishment with waveform detection methods to build a multi-source integrated framework for constructing complete catalogs.

Prior sensitivity, forecasting performance, and implications of intraplate sequence characteristics

The Bayesian spatiotemporal ETAS model exhibits a systematic tendency to overestimate higher magnitude events in the very early post-mainshock period. This may be attributed to the choice of prior parameters, the lack of early data, and the limited number of large events in the sequence itself. The selection of initial prior information has a certain influence on the model’s convergence speed and forecast accuracy in the early stage. If the initial priors deviate significantly from the “ideal” values of the actual sequence, even allowing a wide uncertainty range may still lead to slow parameter adjustment or unrealistic forecasts. Therefore, establishing an empirical prior parameter set based on historical earthquake sequences and regional tectonic characteristics is crucial for improving the reliability of early post-mainshock forecasting.

A regional classification standard could be developed considering active tectonic blocks in mainland China, characteristics of historical strong earthquake sequences, and regional seismic monitoring capabilities. Systematically collecting and compiling complete catalogs of typical historical sequences would allow inversion of their “quasi-true” ETAS parameter posterior distributions using mid-to-late stage complete data. Statistical induction of these could then determine prior values for core parameters for each region-sequence type combination, forming a prior earthquake sequence parameter database.

The Maduo earthquake is an intraplate strike-slip event within the Tibetan Plateau, which exhibits distinct differences from typical interplate or transform-fault earthquakes, such as the 2000 Iceland sequence35 and the 2018 Palu earthquake sequence in Indonesia.50 Intraplate earthquakes generally have lower maximum aftershock magnitudes, larger mainshock-aftershock magnitude differences, and an approximately one order of magnitude lower frequency of moderate-to-strong aftershocks (M ≥ 5.0).28 The observed characteristics of the Maduo sequence (only one M ≥ 4.0 event within 6 days) are consistent with these statistical findings. These differences can be primarily attributed to lower tectonic loading rates, a relatively uniform stress field, and less developed fault networks in intraplate regions.

The scarcity of large aftershocks in the Maduo sequence imposes inherent constraints on the evaluation of the Bayesian forecasting model. Verification of high-magnitude forecasts lacks sufficient observational samples, thereby limiting the power of statistical tests such as the N-test. Moreover, the scarcity of large aftershocks may affect the estimation accuracy of the parameters and increase the uncertainty of the posterior distribution. In contrast, for sequences with more abundant large aftershocks (e.g., the 2000 Iceland sequence studied by Darzi et al.35), the Bayesian spatiotemporal ETAS model exhibits faster parameter convergence and more reliable forecasting performance. As a case with limited large-aftershock productivity, the Maduo sequence provides a valuable reference for evaluating model performance under data-sparse conditions. Future studies should test this method across a variety of aftershock sequence types to comprehensively assess its applicability and limitations.

Furthermore, the characteristics of the sequence itself are a key factor influencing the forecasting performance of the Bayesian framework. The relatively low number of high-magnitude aftershocks in the Maduo sequence led to some deviation in early forecasts for the M ≥ 3.0 range. This underscores the model’s strong dependence on prior information: with reasonably set priors, the Bayesian updating procedure can gradually converge to a stable posterior distribution as data accumulate; conversely, if the initial prior is far from ideal, parameter adjustment may slow significantly, potentially leading to unrealistic estimates due to trade-offs between parameters.35 Therefore, employing a regionally informed, knowledge-rich Bayesian ETAS prior set may be crucial for obtaining reliable forecasts rapidly after a strong earthquake. The Maduo case provides a preliminary reference for developing such prior sets in intraplate settings, but systematic studies across multiple sequences are required. Particularly during the critical first 3 days post-mainshock, priors for subsequent intervals should be dynamically updated based on the posterior distribution of the previous forecast interval. Once the priors stabilize, their discrepancy from the optimal parameter set diminishes significantly, thereby improving timeliness while maintaining forecast accuracy.

Concluding remarks

Taking the 2021 Qinghai Maduo M7.4 intraplate earthquake as a single case study, this research systematically evaluates the impact of early post-mainshock data incompleteness on aftershock sequence parameter estimation and short-term forecasting and examines the effectiveness of two technical approaches, data replenishment and Bayesian modeling.

Early catalog incompleteness is the primary cause of ETAS parameter instability. In the raw incomplete catalog, the key parameter c fluctuated by two orders of magnitude (0.003–2.210) within the first 3 days, while the p value exhibited abnormally high values (>1.5) with significantly large standard deviations. After replenishing missing events using the BEPIT method, the stability of parameter estimation improved markedly. The c value converged to a stable range (0.02–0.03) within 5 days, and the p value stabilized between 1.15 and 1.18. These improvements, however, are demonstrated for this specific sequence and require verification in other cases.

The replenished catalog more realistically reflects the statistical characteristics of aftershock activity. The added events are predominantly small and moderate magnitude aftershocks. After replenishment, the a value increased from 3.995 to 4.484, and the b value increased from 0.57 to 0.69, indicating a significant increase in both the level of seismic activity and the proportion of small and moderate magnitude events, which is more consistent with the statistical patterns of typical aftershock sequences. This pattern is consistent with typical aftershock sequence behavior, but its reproducibility across different sequences remains to be tested.

The Bayesian spatiotemporal ETAS model can effectively integrate prior information to perform short-term probabilistic forecasting for this intraplate sequence. In this case study, although early forecasts tended to overestimate higher magnitude events, the predictions gradually approached actual observations as data accumulated. The spatial zones of high forecast probability aligned well with the actual distribution of aftershocks, preliminarily validating the model’s good adaptive and convergent capabilities under the specific conditions of the Maduo sequence.

Statistical tests confirm the reliability of the forecasting performance for this case. In this case study, statistical test results show that forecasting performance improved over time, with later forecasts showing good agreement between predicted and observed event counts. The model’s early-stage performance was sensitive to the initial prior settings, highlighting the importance of establishing region-specific prior parameter sets for enhancing the reliability of rapid post-mainshock forecasting.

Limitations of the study

This study is based solely on the Maduo earthquake sequence. Consequently, the aforementioned findings are preliminary, and their generalizability requires systematic validation across sequences of different tectonic settings (e.g., interplate, subduction, and intraplate with varying stress regimes) and different aftershock productivity types (e.g., mainshock-aftershock, swarm, or hybrid). Moreover, the BEPIT method assumes magnitude-time independence, an assumption that may not hold for all sequences. The Bayesian forecasting results are sensitive to initial prior settings, and the absence of large aftershocks in the Maduo sequence limits the statistical power of forecast evaluations. Future work should test these methods on more sequences and refine prior parameter databases specifically for intraplate regions.

Method details

Completeness analysis

To investigate the aftershock sequence of the May 22, 2021, Qinghai Maduo M7.4 earthquake, we utilized the earthquake catalog from the National Unified Official Catalog provided by the China Earthquake Networks Center. In the earthquake catalog used in this study, the magnitudes of aftershocks are all local magnitudes (ML), while the mainshock magnitude is the surface wave magnitude (MS), herein, they are all uniformly represented by M. Within 30 days following the mainshock, a total of 5803 aftershocks were recorded for the Maduo earthquake. This includes 3 aftershocks with M ≥ 5.0, 18 within M4.0–4.9, 165 within M3.0–3.9, and 806 within M2.0–2.9.

The magnitude completeness of the 2021 Maduo M7.4 earthquake sequence was analyzed using the “magnitude-rank” method51 in Figure S1A. The analysis indicates that the completeness magnitude Mc reaches M2.0 at approximately 0.10 days after the mainshock. Furthermore, the temporal variation of the earthquake detection rate during the study period was obtained through an iterative, event-by-event approach. The parameter μ, estimated from the OK1993 model,52 is continuous, the minimum completeness magnitude Mc can be approximated by the completely recorded μ+2σ at the 95.45% confidence level or by μ+3σ at the 99.74% confidence level, as shown for Maduo earthquake in Figure S1B. In the immediate post-mainshock period, the number of recorded aftershocks is limited and the detection capability is low. It subsequently recovers gradually, stabilizing at a stage where about 99.73% of detected aftershocks have a magnitude of approximately 2.0.

Bi-scale empirical probability integral transformation

The core concept of the BEPIT technique9,14 is based on the statistical independence between earthquake magnitude and its occurrence time.26,53,54 To verify the applicability of this assumption to the Maduo sequence, we tested independence using both qualitative and quantitative approaches. Qualitatively, we examined magnitude-time scatterplots (Figures 2A and 2E) to assess whether the magnitude distribution exhibited any obvious temporal trend. Quantitatively, we used the Kruskal–Wallis test42 to examine whether magnitude distributions differ across five time windows. The result (0.3234 ≥ 0.05) shows no significant difference among the groups, confirming the independence between magnitude and occurrence time. Therefore, the prerequisite for applying the BEPIT method is essentially satisfied for the Maduo sequence.

Under this assumption, the method aims to map the non-uniformly distributed seismic events observed in the time-magnitude domain into uniformly distributed points in a transformed domain via empirical probability integral transformation. This process facilitates the identification and replenishment of events missing due to monitoring limitations. The key to this algorithm lies in utilizing the empirical distribution from data-complete regions, typically records from the mid-to-late sequence when monitoring capability has recovered, to infer and fill in missing events in the early, incomplete regions.

The BEPIT technique assumes N = {(ti, mi): i = 1, 2, … …, n} is a marked point process in the time-magnitude domain [0, T]×M. It transforms the non-uniform pattern generated by the actual point process into a uniform pattern to replenish missing aftershock events.(Equation 1)ΓN:[0,T]×M→[0,1]×[0,1](Equation 2)(t,m)→(t′,m′)={1n∑j=1nI(tj<t),1n∑j=1mI(mj<m)}where I denotes the indicator function.

The procedure can be summarized in the following five main steps9,14,15: (1) Data Standardization: Standardize all earthquake events using the BEPIT method, transforming them via point process handling into the domain [0,1]×[0,1], and preliminarily estimate the time-magnitude range of missing aftershocks. (2) Empirical Distribution Function: Based on events within the time-magnitude range presumed to be completely observed, perform an iterative process to search for a uniform distribution of events while adjusting the region of missing events, thereby obtaining a new empirical distribution function. (3) Generating Random Events: Based on the empirical distribution function, generate independent and uniformly distributed aftershock events within the identified missing event region. (4) Adjusting the Replenished Dataset: Since the region lacking aftershocks may still contain some real events, avoid duplicate events in the dataset by removing the nearest replenished data point to each real event. (5) Magnitude Assignment: Assign magnitudes to each replenished aftershock event via linear interpolation, utilizing the relationship between magnitude and time.

The spatial coordinates of replenished events were assigned using the method employed by Zhuang et al.14 for the Wenchuan earthquake aftershock sequence. This approach leverages the spatial clustering characteristics of aftershock sequences, and the specific interpolation algorithm is as follows: (1) Selection of temporally neighboring events: For each replenished event (which was generated in the time-magnitude domain and requires spatial coordinate assignment), a fixed number of observed events that are closest to it in time are selected as “neighboring events.” This is based on the principle that aftershocks occurring close in time are often also close in space. (2) Delaunay triangulation: A Delaunay triangulation network is constructed using the spatial coordinates of the selected neighboring events. This network divides the spatial distribution of the neighboring events into triangular facets, with each vertex corresponding to a real observed event. (3) Random selection of a triangle: Among the Delaunay triangles formed in the previous step, one triangle is selected with equal probability. (4) Random generation of spatial coordinates: The spatial coordinates (longitude and latitude) of the replenished event are generated uniformly at random within the selected triangle.

ETAS model and parameter estimation method

The temporal Epidemic-Type Aftershock Sequence (ETAS) model is a classical statistical model for describing the temporal clustering characteristics of earthquake sequences.7 The model decomposes the earthquake occurrence rate into a background seismicity rate and a triggered seismicity rate, where each earthquake may trigger its own aftershocks. The conditional intensity function is defined as:(Equation 3)λ(t|θ,Ηt,Mc)=μ+∑j:tj<tKeα(Mj−Mc)·1(t−tj+c)pwhere Ht is the observation history up to time t, μ is the background seismicity rate (constant), K is the productivity coefficient of earthquake triggering, α is the magnitude-dependent triggering parameter, c and p are the parameters of the modified Omori-Utsu temporal decay law, Mc is the completeness magnitude threshold, Mj is the magnitude of the jth event.

Given the observed sequence {ti,Mi}i=1N, the parameter vector to be estimated is θ = [μ, K, c, α, p]. The maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) method is employed, and the likelihood function is given by:(Equation 4)lnL(θ)=∑i=1Nlnλ(ti|Ht,θ,Mc)−∫t1Tλ(t|Ht,θˆ,Mc)dtwhere t1 is the start time of the sequence and T is the end time. The point estimates and their uncertainties are obtained by maximizing the likelihood function.

To evaluate the goodness of fit of the ETAS model, residual analysis is adopted.7 This method transforms the original earthquake sequence into a homogeneous Poisson process in the transformed time domain {τi} via the transformation τi=∫t1tiλ(t|Ht,θˆ,Mc)dt. If the model fits well, the residuals should follow a homogeneous Poisson process with unit intensity, which can be examined by comparing the cumulative number of events with the diagonal line.

Bayesian spatiotemporal ETAS model

The spatiotemporal Epidemic-Type Aftershock Sequence (ETAS) model describes the earthquake occurrence rate as a non-homogeneous Poisson point process as a function of magnitude, space, and time. In this study, we adopt the Bayesian spatio-temporal ETAS model developed by Darzi et al.35 for parameter fitting and short-term forecasting. The conditional intensity function is defined as:(Equation 5)λ(t,x,y,M|θ,Ηt,Mc)=βe−β(M−Mc)λ(t,x,y|θ,Ηt,Mc)where βe−β(M−Mc) is the probability density function of magnitudes following the Gutenberg-Richter relation (truncated exponential), and λETAS represents the spatiotemporal triggering component:(Equation 6)λETAS(t,x,y|θ,Ηt,Mc)=μ(x,y)+∑j:tj<tKeβ(Mj−Mc)·Kt(t−tj+c)p·KR(1+rj2d2)qHere, μ(x,y) is the spatially varying background seismicity rate, K is the productivity coefficient controlling the number of triggered events, β governs the magnitude distribution and, following the common practice in spatiotemporal ETAS modeling to reduce parameter uncertainty,28 is set equal to the triggering productivity parameter α, c and p are the parameters of the modified Omori-Utsu temporal decay law; d and q characterize the spatial kernel decay, and rj=(x−xj)2+(y−yj)2 is the epicentral distance from the jth event to the target location.

The normalization coefficients Kt and KR are derived analytically to ensure that the temporal and spatial kernels integrate to unity over infinite domains:(Equation 7)Κt=(p−1)cp−1,p>1(Equation 8)KR=q−1πd2,q>1

The productivity coefficient K is calibrated such that the total number of events predicted by the model over the observation period equals the observed number N0, and is therefore not directly learned through Bayesian inference. Consequently, the parameter vector learned via Bayesian inference is θ = [β, c, p, d, q], comprising five parameters.

An empirical prior distribution is first defined for these parameters. Given the earthquake sequence and Mc, sampling techniques are used to generate samples of θ from the posterior probability density distribution. A Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) simulation procedure based on the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm39,55 is employed. After accumulating a sufficient number of samples, the set of realizations for the model parameters θ directly reflects the characteristics of the posterior probability distribution, allowing the generation of event sequences occurring within the target forecast interval [Tstart, Tend].

Considering the associated uncertainties in the spatiotemporal distribution of the event sequence, the model provides probabilistic forecasts of aftershock occurrence and obtains robust estimates of the predicted number of earthquakes. The forecast outputs include: the probability distribution of the number of earthquakes in the target forecasting interval, summarized by percentiles (e.g., 16th, 50th and 84th) and various confidence intervals to characterize forecast uncertainty; the expected number of events in each spatial cell for different magnitude thresholds, along with average estimates for each cell and magnitude threshold, enabling the generation of short-term spatial seismicity forecast maps that include the corresponding uncertainty in each cell; and the probability of at least one earthquake exceeding a given magnitude threshold within the aftershock zone during the forecasting interval. These probabilistic outputs explicitly quantify the uncertainties arising from both model parameter estimation and the stochastic nature of the earthquake process.

For Bayesian inference, posterior distributions are visualized as histograms, and forecast uncertainties are described using the 16th, 50th (median), and 84th percentiles, together with the mean forecast.
