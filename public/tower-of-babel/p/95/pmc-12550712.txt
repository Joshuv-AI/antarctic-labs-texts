Reliability of self-reported medical history and selected health characteristics in the environmental health study for Western New York cohort
Authors: Samantha Rees, Laurene Tumiel Berhalther, James Olson, Lina Mu, Matthew R. Bonner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveWe examined the reliability of self-administered questions used to assess medical and family history and other characteristics in the Environmental Health Study for Western New York (EHS4WNY), a prospective cohort study designed to investigate industrial pollution and health.MethodsBetween 2018 and 2022, 13,306 adults completed a self-administered, either mailed or identical online questionnaire version. Two hundred and ninety-five participants completed duplicate baseline questionnaires. Reliability was assessed using Cohen's kappa (K), weighted kappa (Kw), percent agreement, and intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC).ResultsCommon medical conditions, including hypertension (K = 0.80; 95 % CI: 0.71, 0.89) and high cholesterol (K = 0.76; 95 % CI: 0.66,0.86) yielded excellent reliability. A personal medical history of colorectal cancer was moderately reliable (K = 0.67; 95 % CI: 0.05, 1.00). Family history of breast cancer had the highest reliability (K = 0.81; 95 % CI: 0.72, 0.91) and non-melanoma skin cancers had the lowest (K = 0.47; 95 % CI: 0.28, 0.65). Recalled height (ICC = 0.98; 95 % CI: 0.97, 0.98) and weight at age 18 (ICC = 0.96; 95 % CI: 0.94, 0.97) showed excellent reliability.ConclusionsThis questionnaire demonstrated fair-to-excellent test-retest reliability of self-reported medical and family history and selected factors. Although we did not assess validity, self-reported medical history collected via questionnaire can be used to generate reliable data in general population studies.

Introduction

Self-administered questionnaires are an indispensable tool for epidemiology, offering advantages over other data collection approaches (e.g., in-person interviews and medical records review). These advantages include timely and affordable data collection, particularly within a large population, where it may be infeasible to collect objective measures (e.g., clinical exams) or to systematically collect via existing data sources (e.g., medical records). Self-administered questionnaires may also reduce the possibility of interviewer bias, but are prone to participants leaving questions unanswered or misinterpreting the questions.(Althubaiti, 2016; Cho et al., 2017) Reliability of questionnaires is often examined within unique sub-populations (i.e., among individuals with a specific health condition or health care professionals)(Blair et al., 2002; Cho et al., 2017; Colditz et al., 1986; Parikh-Patel et al., 2003) and may not be generalizable to a general population. As such, general population cohorts likely consist of individuals with varying degrees of health literacy and knowledge. Previous reports have called for cohort studies to conduct reliability and validity assessments to ensure reliable and accurate data collection.(White et al., 1998; Wise and Hartge, 2021).

We report on a post hoc approach to assess the reliability of self-reported information within a broad population-based cohort study. We focused on the reliability of commonly used questions in epidemiological research, including questions that assess personal medical and family history of disease and other selected health characteristics.

Materials and methods

The Environmental Health Study for Western New York (EHS4WNY) is a general population cohort study designed to investigate industrial pollution and health in several western New York communities. Adults ≥18 residing in several western New York communities were invited to participate in the EHS4WNY by completing a mailed self-administered questionnaire. The baseline questionnaire was mailed to 106,244 residents, between June 2018 and December 2022, with instructions to either complete the paper or online version of the questionnaire. Non-respondents were mailed a second invitation, followed by a third mailing that contained a shortened version of the paper questionnaire. Of the 106,244 mailers sent, 9036 mailers were returned as undeliverable. The EHS4WNY cohort had a total sample size of 13,334 participants who completed the baseline questionnaire, leading to a response rate of 14 %. For this study, only the full paper and online versions were included because these versions had the same questions, in the same order. All questionnaires were approved by the University at Buffalo IRB (UBIRB#:STUDY00002003) and informed consent was implied upon completing the questionnaire.

Even with efforts to remove participants from the resend list, there were several instances where participants completed the baseline questionnaire multiple times. This report utilized data from these duplicate submissions to conduct a reliability analysis of the questionnaire. We compared an individual participant's initial baseline submission with their duplicate. Two hundred ninety-five participants completed duplicate baseline questionnaires and were included in the analysis. The median time between the returning the two iterations was 16 days (IQR = 1–32 days).

Questionnaire

The baseline self-administered questionnaire collected information regarding demographics, residential history, personal and family medical histories, work-related chemical exposures, lifestyle factors including smoking and alcohol use, and physical measurements such as height and weight. We pilot tested the questionnaire prior to implementation and was based on previous existing questionnaires. However, the questionnaire was not validated in our study population. Family and personal medical history was measured by asking participants to mark “yes” or “no” for specific health conditions. Family history was comprised of 13 health conditions, and personal medical history specified 44 health conditions with a write-in option for “other.” Alcohol use was assessed via two questions, measuring the frequency and volume of consumption within the past year. Smoking status (i.e., current, former, never) was measured by asking participants if they had smoked at least 100 cigarettes during their lifetime and if they currently smoke. Vaping was assessed by asking participants if they have ever used an electronic vaping product (yes/ no). Secondhand smoke exposure was measured by asking participants if they were exposed to smoke from other people's cigarettes or tobacco products either during childhood at home, adulthood at home, and in a social setting. Additionally, participants were asked to report their current height and weight and their height and weight at age 18.

Statistical analysis

We report descriptive statistics for the test-retest subgroup and the total cohort to evaluate the similarity of our sample of duplicates to the entire cohort by age, sex, educational attainment, income, and race. Due to sparse data, the paper and online questionnaire types were not evaluated separately.

For all questions with a binary response option (yes or no), a Cohen's kappa (K) was used. A weighted kappa (Kw) was used for questions that had three or more categorical responses, and for questions with continuous response options, an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC, one-way random effects model) was conducted.(Koo and Li, 2016) A kappa statistic less than 0.40 was considered poor, 0.40–0.75 fair-to-good, and > 0.75 was deemed excellent agreement.(Fleiss et al., 2003) The above-mentioned statistics are frequently used in the epidemiological literature to determine reliability, making our findings comparable. For binary and 3-level categorical variables, the percent exact agreement, or the proportion of participants with the same response for both iterations, was calculated. All analyses were completed in SAS software Version 9.4 or SPSS Version 28 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Family History of breast cancer yielded the highest reliability (K = 0.81, CI:0.72, 0.91) (Table 2). Brain cancer yielded the second-highest reliability with K = 0.77 (CI:0.59, 0.95). Family history of non-melanoma skin cancers had the lowest reliability (K = 0.47, CI:0.28, 0.65). Family history of colorectal cancer, lung cancer, and lymphoma all yielded similar reliability statistics with K = 0.62 (CI:0.45,0.80), K = 0.63 (CI:0.47, 0.78), and K = 0.65 (CI:0.39, 0.91), respectively. Melanoma, leukemia, prostate cancer, and stomach cancer had fair reliability ranging from K = 0.59 for melanoma to K = 0. 54 for leukemia. The reliability of reporting a family history of any cancer type was K = 0.70 (CI:0.60 0.80), which was generally higher than the individual cancer reliabilities. Family history of a heart attack before age 50 and diabetes generated fair-to-good reliability.

Vaping use had good reliability (K = 0.75, CI:0.59, 0.91) and 96 % exact agreement. The frequency of alcohol use had a Kw = 0.83 (CI:0.76, 0.91), whereas the volume of alcohol consumed had a K = 0.68 (CI:0.55, 0.80). Discussion

Overall self-reported medical history had the highest reliability and percent exact agreements compared to family history and selected health characteristic questions evaluated, with kappa's ranging from fair-to-excellent. We hypothesize that participants are able to better report their medical history than their family's medical history. For self-reported personal medical history of common health conditions—high blood pressure, high cholesterol, and heart attacks— our results were consistent with other reports.(Brownson et al., 1994; Dal Grande et al., 2012; Stein et al., 1993) Stein et al. (1993) assessed the reliability of the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System questionnaire and found that hypertension and high blood pressure had excellent reliability (K = 0.89 and K = 0.86, respectively). More recently, Dal Grande et al. (2012) conducted a reliability study on 154 participants aged ≥16 residing in Australia and found excellent reliability for high blood pressure (K = 0.82), high cholesterol (K = 0.79), and heart attack (K = 0.79).

Previous studies observed that reliability and validity scores of self-reported personal cancer diagnoses varied by cancer type, and our results echo the variation in reliability.(Bergmann et al., 1998; Cho et al., 2017; Loh et al., 2014) Loh et al. (2014) conducted a study examining the validity and reliability study of self-reported cancers in an Australian population and found that both validity and reliability of self-reported cancer diagnosis varied by cancer site. In their study, breast cancer had the highest reliability (K = 0.79), followed by colorectal and prostate cancer (K = 0.73), and melanoma had the lowest reliability score (K = 0.45). Breast cancer is often one of the most valid and reliable types of cancer collected via self-report(Bergmann et al., 1998; Cho et al., 2017; Loh et al., 2014) For other cancer types, such as lung and colorectal cancer, reliability scores were more variable. Leukemia/lymphoma, breast, and lung cancer had excellent reliability (K = 1.00; K = 0.90; K = 0.80, respectively). Understanding the variation in reliability scores across cancer types would be informative; however, we lacked the level of qualitative data needed to explore these nuanced differences. The prevalence of health conditions within our sample may account for some of the variation; for example, two participants reported being diagnosed with leukemia/lymphoma, meaning that only two participants needed to agree for 100 % agreement.

Reliability of family history questions also varied by cancer type, with breast cancer yielding the highest reliability. Our study was not designed to answer why there are differences in reporting by cancer type, but we speculate that reporting family history of breast cancer may have high reliability in the general population because women may speak openly with their families about these diagnoses and the success of breast cancer awareness campaigns.(Figueiredo et al., 2004) Women may have familiarity with providing their family history of breast cancer. At primary care appointments, medical providers should ask if their patients have had a family member with breast cancer since research has suggested a genetic component.(Nelson et al., 2019) Family history of non-melanoma skin cancers had the lowest reliability (K = 0.47), which may be due to non-melanoma skin cancers being perceived as less severe and, therefore, less likely to be shared in detail among family members. Also, there are a wide variety of skin cancer types (i.e., basal cell carcinoma, melanoma), that may lead to skin cancers being perceived as a homogenous disease.(Aitken et al., 1996) Both of which would have the potential to impact reliability. Our questionnaire's reliability for a family history of any cancer was the same as previous literature (K = 0.70).(Bravi et al., 2007).

All selected health characteristics generated good reliability scores with kappa's ≥ 0.64. Smoking status had excellent reliability (Kw = 0.92). Previous studies found similar reliability scores to ours ranging from K = 0.73–0.97 for smoking status.(Dal Grande et al., 2012; Karatela et al., 2006; Stein et al., 1993) Secondhand smoke exposure during childhood had higher reliability (K = 0.82) compared to smoke exposure as an adult (K = 0.64) or in a social setting (K = 0.64). Some possible explanations include strong memories of secondhand smoke exposure as a child or the historical nature of childhood secondhand exposure remaining constant. In contrast, smoke exposure in the social setting or adulthood may change over time and be considered mundane. Dal Grande et al. (2012) assessed the reliability of secondhand smoke exposure by asking participants, “Which best describes your home smoking situation?” and generated a reliability score of K = 0.59. Next, we found that self-reported vaping use had good reliability (K = 0.75). To the best of our knowledge, there was no reliability data for vaping use in the general population to compare our findings with. A systematic review on vaping use observed that all 40 studies included did not report reliability or validity for their collection tools.(Becker et al., 2021) Based on our results and the similar nature of traditional smoking status, we posit that self-reported vaping information can produce reliable data in our study. Alcohol use—frequency and volume—were consistent with other reliability studies, ranging from K = 0.72–0.82.(Dal Grande et al., 2012; Karatela et al., 2006; Stein et al., 1993).

Height and weight, currently and at age 18, yielded excellent reliability scores within our study. Our ICC scores for current height and weight (ICC = 0.96 and ICC = 0.98, respectively) were consistent with Dal Grande et al. (2012), who had reported ICC scores of 0.97 for height and 1.00 for weight. While the results of height and weight were reliable in our questionnaire and others, we are aware of the long-standing validity concerns regarding self-reported body mass index.(Dal Grande et al., 2012; Gorber et al., 2007; Lin et al., 2012; Rothman, 2008).

There are several strengths of this study. Self-administrated questionnaires are not prone to interviewer bias.(Choi and Pak, 2004) Our questionnaire assessed various environmental exposures and health outcomes that should reduce the possibility of participants guessing a specific hypothesis. Next, this study had a relatively large sample size for a test-retest study design. Finally, although this study was not an a priori-designed traditional test-retest design, we took advantage of the duplicate submissions of the baseline questionnaire to better understand the reliability of questions commonly used in large epidemiologic studies. In this report, we observed acceptable reliability scores, which was an important initial step to conduct before further using these variables to investigate exposure and health outcome associations.

This research is not without potential limitations. Due to the post hoc nature of this study design, there is potential for memory effects to occur; participants may have remembered their responses on the first iteration of the questionnaire when completing the second. If memory effects occur, then the reliability scores may be artificially high. However, the median time between the two iterations was 16 days (IQR = 1–32), which should be an adequate time interval to reduce this possibility. In test-retest study designs, time intervals between iterations are not consistent; some use shorter time frames like ours – Dal Grande et al. (2012) had a mean time frame of 16.8 days, and Stein et al. (1993) started re-interviews at 21 days—while others chose a longer time frame.(Blair et al., 2002; Bravi et al., 2007) We had approximately 77 participants, or 26.1 %, complete both questionnaire submissions within one day or less, which left 218, or 73.9 %, that had completed their submissions at least two days apart. A longer time frame between the two iterations increases the possibility that real changes could have occurred; 43 participants completed a second questionnaire ≥100 days apart. To explore the potential for memory effects we stratified personal and family medical histories by the median of 16 days (Supplemental Table 1). For personal medical history, those that completed the second iteration in <16 days had slightly higher reliability scores than the ≥16 days group. However, after excluding participants who completed the questionnaires within a very short interval (≤2 days) our results were consistent with the overall samples. Our sensitivity analyses suggested there may be small memory effects present but not significantly and not enough to explain away the overall reliability scores.

Although a random sample of the total cohort was not used, the descriptive characteristics in Table 1 only show minor differences in participant demographics between the total cohort and the test-retest group, indicating that selection-like biases may not be of great concern. In addition, reliability studies compare a single questionnaire item administered at two-time points, it would be unlikely that selection forces would influence both responses as is a common concern in other study designs exploring exposure-disease associations. We encountered considerable missing data for some questions, complicating statistical analyses and interpretation of the results. There were missing data ranging from 4 to 12 %, depending on the demographic characteristic. Self-reported income had the highest amount of missing data, consistent with missingness reported in other studies.(Brownson et al., 1994; Cho et al., 2017; Dal Grande et al., 2012; Stein et al., 1993) Non-response is often higher for self-report household income, potentially due to participants not wanting to share that personal information.(Kusnoor et al., 2018; Moore et al., 2000; Moore and Loomis, 2001)

Conclusion

Our questionnaire had fair-to-excellent test-retest reliability for self-reported personal and family medical history, lifestyle factors, and physical measurements in a general population, demonstrating that people varying degrees of health literacy can complete self-administered questionnaires that produce reliable information. The results suggest that self-administered questionnaires are a feasible method to ascertain reliable information from the general population. Future epidemiological studies should continue to evaluate the reliability of questionnaires used.
