Right Ventricular Perforation After Vertebroplasty

Vertebroplasty is widely performed for vertebral compression fractures, but cement leakage and embolization are recognized complications.1 Cardiac involvement is rare but potentially life-threatening.2

A 75-year-old woman presented with worsening dyspnea and hemodynamic compromise due to cardiac tamponade 6 months after percutaneous vertebroplasty for vertebral compression fracture, following an otherwise asymptomatic course.

Transthoracic echocardiography revealed multiple hyperechoic linear structures within the right ventricle, one of which appeared to penetrate the right ventricular free wall and protrude toward the pericardial space, suggesting right ventricular perforation (Video 1). Pericardial effusion with hemodynamic features consistent with cardiac tamponade was also observed (Video 2). In addition, the inferior vena cava was dilated (23 mm) with reduced inspiratory collapse (18 mm) (Video 3). Mitral inflow Doppler showed marked respiratory variation, with an E-wave velocity of 0.58 m/s during inspiration and 0.84 m/s during expiration, corresponding to a 31% respiratory variation, further supporting cardiac tamponade physiology.

Because the patient had no history of cardiovascular or endovascular intervention, the etiology was not immediately recognized; however, comparison of radiographs obtained after vertebroplasty and at admission demonstrated interval migration of bone cement into the cardiac silhouette (Figures 1A and 1B), providing an important diagnostic clue. Computed tomography clearly demonstrated an intracardiac high-density linear structure penetrating the right ventricular free wall (Figures 1C and 1D). At surgery, bone cement perforating the right ventricular free wall was directly visualized and removed, confirming the diagnosis (Figure 1E).

Delayed Intracardiac Migration of Bone Cement Causing Right Ventricular Perforation After Vertebroplasty

(A) Radiograph obtained immediately after vertebroplasty showing cement leakage from the treated vertebral body (white arrow). (B) Radiograph at presentation showing interval cranial migration of the cement fragment into the cardiac silhouette (white arrow). (C and D) Computed tomography demonstrating a linear hyperdense structure within the right ventricle penetrating the right ventricular free wall, with associated pericardial effusion. (E) Intraoperative image confirming bone cement perforating the right ventricular free wall after surgical exposure (white arrow).

Intracardiac cement embolism after vertebroplasty has been reported, and most cases are identified in the early period after the procedure.3 In contrast, this case remained clinically silent for 6 months before presenting as cardiac tamponade, representing a rare delayed manifestation.

Delayed, clinically silent bone cement migration can present as life-threatening cardiac tamponade. Serial radiography, echocardiography, and computed tomography were critical for recognizing delayed intracardiac cement migration and guiding urgent surgical management.
