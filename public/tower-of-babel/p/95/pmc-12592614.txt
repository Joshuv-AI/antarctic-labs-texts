Proteome-wide Mendelian randomization and colocalization analysis uncovers druggable targets for lung cancer across multiple phenotypes and complications
Authors: Yunpeng Chen, Xiaohong Dai, Peng Zhang, Lijuan Yao, Qing Wang, Zhihong Qin, Tianmin Gao, Junguo Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLung cancer remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality, necessitating novel therapeutic targets. The plasma proteome represents a key source for such targets.MethodsProteome-wide Mendelian randomization (MR) and colocalization analyses were conducted to assess the causal effects of plasma proteins on lung cancer subtypes and complications. Genetic instruments (cis-pQTLs) for 2,090 proteins were derived from plasma proteome data (54,306 UK Biobank and 35,559 Icelandic participants). Lung cancer phenotype data were obtained from FinnGen R10.ResultsMR identified seven plasma proteins showing significant causal associations with specific lung cancer phenotypes: high GGT1 increased non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) risk (OR 1.27, 95% CI 1.10–1.46; PFDR = 0.0261), GFRA2 increased the SCLC risk (OR 1.65, 95% CI 1.24–2.21; PFDR = 0.0462), and higher advanced glycosylation end-product specific receptor reduced the squamous cell carcinoma risk (OR 0.338 per SD increase, 95% CI 0.209–0.548; PFDR = 0.0138). Fifteen proteins showed associations with lung cancer complications. Colocalization strongly supported causal roles for eight proteins: FKBP1B (OR 1.15, 95% CI 1.09–1.22; PFDR = 0.00264), F11(OR 1.01, 95% CI 1.01–1.01; PFDR = 1.47 × 10− 23), ABO (OR 1.11, 95% CI 1.06–1.21; PFDR = 5.82 × 10− 9), F2 (OR 3.04, 95% CI 1.74–5.31; PFDR = 0.0102), and VSIG10L (OR 1.006, 95% CI 1.00–1.01; PFDR = 0.0159).ConclusionThis study reveals causal proteins for various lung cancer phenotypes and complications, emphasizing causal pathways and potential therapeutic targets for lung cancer and providing new insights into its etiology, prevention, treatment, and therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03910-4.

Introduction

Lung cancer is currently one of the most common malignancies worldwide, and with advances in immunotherapy, targeted therapies, etc., the 3-year relative survival rate for non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) has increased from 26% in 2004 to 40% in 2017, whereas that for small cell lung cancer (SCLC) has increased from 9% to 13% [1]. Patients with comorbidities or/and complications (e.g., chronic bronchitis, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis [IPF], pleural effusion, and deep vein thrombosis [DVT]) have even lower survival and quality of life. Thus far, approximately 340 people still die of lung cancer every day, ranking first among all cancers [1]. This places a heavy burden on the healthcare system and human health.

Lung carcinogenesis is a multistep process involving both environmental and genetic factors [2, 3]. Key mechanisms include activation of proto-oncogenes (e.g., HER, RAS, Myc, ALK, Sox, MDM2), inactivation of tumor suppressors (e.g., p53, Rb, p16, nm23, PTEN), and dysregulation of growth signaling, angiogenesis, apoptosis, and immune evasion. While targeted therapies and immune checkpoint inhibitors have improved outcomes for some patients with NSCLC [4]; however, a large proportion of patients with low expression of driver-negative, programmed cell death 1/programmed death ligand 1 still did not receive additional therapies [5, 6]. SCLC, a highly aggressive cancer, has been treated with a combination of platinum-containing etoposide therapies as the first-line treatment for the past three decades [7, 8]. The National Cancer Institute has identified SCLC as a priority for research due to its aggressive nature and poor post-relapse outcomes [9]. Therefore, compared with NSCLC, a breakthrough in treatment for SCLC is imminent. However, lung cancer treatment still needs further exploration.

The circulating proteome reflects biological processes from diverse tissues and may offer insights into disease mechanisms and therapeutic opportunities [10]. Although observational studies have identified numerous proteins associated with lung cancer [11, 12], these are susceptible to reverse causation and confounding. In contrast, Mendelian randomization (MR) uses genetic variants as instrumental variables to infer causality, mitigating these limitations and providing a robust framework for identifying potential drug targets [13–15]. Recent multi-omics/phenomics frameworks in lung cancer highlight how integrating genomics, transcriptomics and proteomics can refine subtype biology and accelerate target and biomarker discovery [16, 17]. Complementary evidence shows that Adenocarcinoma(LUAD) gene-expression signatures and omics-guided pipelines are associated with prognosis and translational prioritization, including in contexts such as IPF-related lung cancer [18–20].

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified genetic variants associated with proteins that are referred to as “protein quantitative trait genomic loci (pQTLs)” [21]. QTLs are typically identified using data on the population-based genetic and plasma protein level [21]. While previous studies have leveraged pQTLs to explore drug targets in SCLC [22] and other cancers [23], no study has yet investigated potential drug targets for each phenotype of lung cancer across the entire proteome. Therefore, to identify potential drug targets for each lung cancer phenotype and its complications, we performed MR analysis using data from 2,940 proteins provided by the UK Biobank Pharmaco Proteomics Project (UKB-PPP) and 4,907 proteins from deCODE Genetics, supplemented by colocalization analysis.

Methods

Study design

The overall study design is illustrated in Fig. 1. Briefly, two-sample MR analyses were conducted to examine the association between plasma proteins and lung cancer phenotypes and lung cancer complications. Colocalization analysis was employed to verify the causal associations between plasma proteins and lung cancer phenotype. The potential target value of these target proteins was analyzed in conjunction with existing studies. The study has been conducted in accordance with the standards set forth by the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology using MR guidelines.

Data sources for plasma proteins

Cis-pQTLs significantly associated with plasma protein levels across the genome (P < 5 × 10− 8) were selected as instrumental variables (IVs) from two large GWAS from the UKB-PPP [24] and deCODE genetics Health [25]. Cis-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were defined as SNPs within 1 Mb of the gene encoding the protein. UKB-PPP conducted the proteomic analysis of plasma samples from 54,306 participants using the Olink platform and collected data on 2940 proteins. deCODE carried out the proteomic analysis of plasma samples from 35,559 participants using the somascan platform and collected data on 4907 proteins [25]. For the two-sample MR analysis, index cis-SNPs were selected as IVs for 2090 proteins [24]. Given that colocalization analysis could only be performed for proteins from the UKB-PPP study owing to the availability of the summary-level data, genetic IVs selected from the UKB-PPP study were used for these overlapped proteins in the main results.

Data sources for lung cancer phenotypes and complications

Data on the association of protein-related SNPs with lung cancer were obtained from the FinnGen study. The most recent data from FinnGen R10 were used in this analysis [26]: NSCLC with 5315 and 314,193 controls, LUAD with 1590 cases and 314,193 controls, squamous cell carcinoma (SqCLC) with 1510 cases and 314,193 controls, and SCLC with 717 cases and 314,193 controls.

Five lung cancer-related conditions, namely, pleural effusion, DVT, bronchitis, IPF, emphysema, or chronic bronchitis, were analyzed. Data for pleural effusion, bronchitis, and IPF were obtained from the FinnGen R10 study. Data for DVT, emphysema, or chronic bronchitis were obtained from Catalog GWAS (Table 1).

IVs

IVs were selected through a four-step process (Tables S1 and S2): (1) Genome-wide significant cis-pQTLs (P < 5 × 10 − 8) were clumped using a reference panel to ensure independence (linkage disequilibrium threshold R2 < 0.001, window size = 10,000 kb). (2) SNPs with F statistic < 10 were excluded to mitigate weak instrument bias. (3) Allele frequency-checked palindromic SNPs were removed to avoid strand ambiguity. (4) MR-PRESSO global test (p < 0.05) identified outliers, which were iteratively removed until no significant outliers remained (maximum iterations = 3). The heterogeneity of single SNPs was evaluated using Cochran’s Q test. A significant Cochran’s Q statistic (P<0.05) suggested substantial heterogeneity within the analyses.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All MR analyses were conducted in accordance with three fundamental assumptions: first, the selected IV must demonstrate a robust correlation with exposure; second, the IVs must be free from confounding factors; and third, the IV can only effectively represent an effect through the specified plasma protein and cannot directly influence the outcome.

All statistical analyses were performed in the R software environment using the TwoSam-pleMR and MendelianRandomization software package (version 4.4.1).

Bayesian weighted MR (BWMR)

The BWMR method was employed to investigate the causal relationships between lung cancer phenotypes, comorbidities, and potential drug targets [27]. BWMR is a causal inference approach that utilizes summary statistics from GWAS, and it offers several key advantages:

The researchers developed a variance expectation maximization algorithm [27] to make BWMR-based causal inference calculations stable and efficient. The BWMR model also considers the uncertainty in estimating weak effects due to polygenic structure, which further improves the robustness of causal inference and can effectively address violations of IV assumptions caused by pleiotropy [27]. This is particularly important for exploring potential therapeutic targets across the proteome.

Briefly, the BWMR method can effectively address the challenges of pleiotropy and polygenicity in MR by providing a reliable statistical framework to investigate the causal relationships among lung cancer phenotypes, comorbidities, and potential therapeutic targets. The BWMR method will be employed to systematically evaluate the causal effects of various protein targets on lung cancer phenotypes and comorbidities using GWAS summary statistics across the entire proteome.

The inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method was the primary statistical technique employed. To validate primary IVW results and address pleiotropy, four complementary methods were also used: cML-MA [28, 29], ConMix [30], MR-RAPS [31], and dIVW [32], which not only improve the statistical power of MR analysis but also make the results more reliable. Causal effects were considered consistent if Cochran’s Q test P >0.05 (no heterogeneity); MR-Egger intercept P >0.05. These robust statistical methods help us extract the real therapeutic targets from the huge proteomics data, which provides a reliable basis for the subsequent targeted drug development.

Colocalization analysis

Colocalization analysis was performed to test whether the identified associations of proteins with lung cancer and its complications were driven by linkage disequilibrium. The analysis was based on a Bayesian model that assesses the support for five exclusive hypotheses: (1) no association with either trait, (2) association with lung cancer only, (3) association with complications only, (4) both traits are associated, but distinct causal variants were for two traits, and (5) both traits are associated, and the same shares causal variant for both traits. A posterior probability is provided for each hypothesis test (H0, H1, H2, H3, and H4). In this analysis, colocalization analysis was conducted using the R language “Coloc” with the following priors: prior probability of SNP association with exposure (p1): 1 × 10− 4, prior probability of SNP association with outcome (p2): 1 × 10− 4, and prior probability of shared causal variant (p12): 1 × 10− 5. A posterior probability PPH4 ≥ 0.75 was defined as strong evidence for colocalization, consistent with established standards in proteomic MR studies. To assess robustness, sensitivity analyses were conducted by varying p12 (5 × 10 − 6 to 5 × 10 − 5). Proteins with PPH4 ≥ 0.75 across all prior settings were considered high-confidence targets. Medium colocalization indication was defined as 0.5 < PH4 < 0.75.

Phenome-wide MR (Phe-MR) analysis of target proteins

The exploration of side effects for the three lung cancer-associated proteins was expanded by performing Phe-MR analyses for various diseases. pQTLs were derived from the same proteome GWAS as in the primary analysis with lung cancer phenotypes. Phe-MR findings can be interpreted as the risk/protective effect per SD increase in the plasma protein level, the same as with primary lung cancer outcomes. That is, if the effect direction of the additional indication is consistent with the effect direction in lung cancer, the identified protein that is therapeutically targeted for the treatment of lung cancer may also be “beneficial” for the additional indication, and vice versa. MR causal effects are considered significant at P ≤ 1.06 × 10− 5.

Results

In this study, all research analyses were based on the data presented in Table 1. MR analyses were performed on 4907 proteins in the deCODE Health database and 2940 proteins in the UKB-PPP, and the cis-genetic instruments for 971 and 1119 proteins were obtained, respectively. The minimal F statistic of the genetic instruments was > 300 in all outcome data.

MR analysis of whole plasma proteomes

The derived data were synthesized, and MR analysis was performed using Cis-pQTL as an IV. After FDR correction, 23 plasma proteins were successfully identified (Table S3, Figs. 2 and 3). Eight of these proteins were associated with lung cancer subtypes (Table 2 and S4). The odds ratio (OR) of lung cancer subtypes ranged from 0.338 (95% CI, 0.209–0.548, PFDR = 0.0138) for advanced glycosylation end-product specific receptor (AGER) to 1.90 (95% CI, 1.40–2.57, PFDR = 0.0486) for FUCA1 for each SD increase in the levels of the gene-predicted proteins (Table 2; Fig. 4). Among them, the upregulation of AGER, IL1RAP, ISLR2, and ANGPTL1 may contribute to the reduction in lung cancer risk, with the most significant MR result for AGER (PFDR = 0.0138).

Another 15 plasma proteins were causally associated with lung cancer complications (Table 2 and S4). Among them, F2 may be a common risk factor for DVT and IPF, BRSK2 may be a common risk factor for IPF and malignant pleural effusion, and higher PROS1 may be associated with lower risk of emphysema or chronic bronchitis and DVT risk. Among them, F11 (OR 1.01 (95% CI, 1.01–1.01), PFDR = 1.47 × 10− 23) and ABO (OR 1.11 (95% CI, 1.06–1.21), PFDR = 5.82 × 10− 9) were the most important outcomes for DVT in the MR analysis (Figs. 3 and 4). The identified association was directionally consistent between the discovery and replication studies for all proteins. The results of the sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of the primary MR analysis. All positive results passed MRPRESSO and heterogeneity analysis.

The results of the multiple test analyses indicated the absence of heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy (Table S5).

Association between relevant plasma proteins and lung cancer subtypes and complications

Colocalization analysis could not be performed because the Cis-pQTL data from the deCODE database did not intersect with validated IVs for disease screening. Thus, colocalization analysis of plasma proteins from UKB-PPP data was performed with the results of each lung cancer subtype and complication. The results showed that seven proteins had high colocalization analysis support (PPH4 > 0.75) (Figs. 4C and 5), namely, GFRA2, AGER, FKBP1B, F11, ABO, F2, and VSIG10L. Among them, GFRA2 and AGER in SCLC and SqCLC exhibited strong colocalization support. FKBP1B and VSIG10L showed strong localization support in bronchitis and emphysema or chronic bronchitis. F11, ABO, and F2 exhibited strong localization support with VTE. Among these proteins, F11 had the highest support in the colocalization analysis (PPH4 = 1.0), whereas GGT1 (PPH4 = 0.714) also showed moderately strong colocalization in NSCLC. Finally, 14 plasma proteins were analyzed for weak localization (Figs. 4C and S1–S2). All of them may be drug target proteins.

Phe-MR analysis of lung cancer-associated proteins

To assess whether lung cancer-associated proteins affect other indications, MR screening of the FinnGen R5 version of the full phenotype scan was performed for 2803 diseases. The Phe-MR results clearly show that genetically determined plasma protein levels per SD increase resulted in either an increase or a decrease in the risk of specific diseases. If the protein affected the disease in the same direction as it did lung cancer, the effect was considered “beneficial” and vice versa. Ultimately, 10 associations (PFDR < 0.05) were identified, of which 7 (70%) were considered beneficial (Table S6).

Genetically, high plasma AGER levels not only reduce the SqCLC risk but may also reduce the risk of disorders of the lacrimal system and orbit in diseases classified elsewhere, urticaria and erythema, wet age-related macular degeneration, and retinal vein occlusion (central or branch). However, it may be associated with an increased risk of other demyelinating diseases of the central nervous system, chronic iridocyclitis, and iridocyclitis in diseases classified elsewhere. High levels of GGT1 not only increase the risk of NSCLC but also of benign neoplasm in the transverse colon, sacrococcygeal disorders, not classified elsewhere (Table S7).

Discussion

Combined with the basis of the genomic GWAS study, the association of plasma protein with bronchitis, emphysema or chronic bronchitis, pleural effusion, DVT, and IPF was analyzed. This MR study tested and analyzed the correlation of nearly 3,000 plasma proteins with various lung cancer subtypes and complications and found that eight plasma proteins were directly associated with lung cancer and 15 plasma proteins were associated with lung complications. Upon further colocalization analysis of the UKB-PPP data, strong colocalization support for FKBP1B, F11, F2, ABO, and VSIG10L with lung cancer complications was found, whereas GGT1, GFRA2, and AGER showed causal association with lung cancer. Finally, to further explore the potential benefits and drawbacks of the treatment with these three lung cancer-associated proteins, a Phe-MR analysis was performed, which showed other beneficial indications and, more importantly, pointed out possible potential safety issues.

GGT1 plays a crucial role in glutathione (GSH) metabolism, which is fundamental in redox homeostasis in cancer cells. Recent evidence demonstrates that GGT1 overexpression activates the NF-κB pathway, which enhances tumor cell survival in NSCLC [33]. GGT1 is involved in cellular GSH metabolism, hydrolyzes L-γ-Glutamyl-p-nitroanilide (GPNA), releases the cytotoxic metabolite p-nitroaniline (PNA), which induces apoptosis in cancer cells, and regulates the sensitivity of cancer cells to GPNA/PNA. Importantly, GGT1-mediated GPNA/PNA cytotoxicity is significantly enhanced in KRAS-mutant lung cancers, suggesting its potential as a subtype-specific target [33]. A large-scale national population-based cohort study in Korea [34] found that persistently high GGT1 levels were associated with an increased risk of respiratory cancers, especially in men (HR 1.39, 95% CI 1.31–1.48). Therefore, the high GGT1 expression is crucially involved in promoting lung occurrence and development [33, 35, 36]. Figure S3 illustrates the primary associated mechanisms. This study further confirms the reliability of the causal relationship between GGT1 and lung cancer (Figs. 3B and 4A). Current challenges in the development of inhibitors focus on overcoming structural heterogeneity in the catalytic domain (Tyr-403/Lys-562) of human GGT1 (hGGT1) [37]. However, currently, only in vitro studies are available. 6-diazo-5-oxo-norleucine-specific inhibition of hGGT1 is a novel molecular mechanism of hGGT1 inhibition [38]. Therefore, GGT1 may be a drug target in NSCLC. Despite studies of hGGT1 inhibitors, further studies are needed to explore drug safety and efficacy and find suitable hGGT1 inhibitors.

GFRα2 (encoded by GFRA2) is a membrane-anchored GDNF family receptor (GFRα) featuring three extracellular domains (D1, D2, and D3) [39]. Upon binding its ligand neurturin (NRTN), it forms a complex that activates downstream RET or NCAM signaling pathways [39]—a mechanism of particular significance in neuroendocrine tumors. The strong causal association found between the GFRα2 isoform and SCLC (Figs. 4 and 5B, PPH4 = 0.773) likely stems from the neuroendocrine origin of SCLC. Despite the insufficient direct studies of GFRα2 in SCLC, its established mechanisms in other cancers, and the molecular features of SCLC suggest three potential pathogenic roles (Figure S4): As a GDNF receptor family member, GFRA2 likely regulates neuroendocrine signaling via ligand NRTN [40]. By forming complexes with overexpressed RET [41], GFRA2 may activate the PI3K/AKT pathway to promote tumor survival and invasiveness [40]. In addition, the abnormal activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway in SCLC was found to be associated with chemotherapy resistance [42]. It may inhibit the mitochondrial apoptosis pathway by upregulating members of the Bcl-2 protein family, leading to the resistance of SCLC cells to apoptotic signals [42]. Although therapeutic strategies targeting the GFRα2/PI3K/AKT axis remain in early-stage development, they represent promising novel approaches.

AGER expression is higher in normal lung tissues and significantly lower in LUAD and SqCLC, and the higher the AGER expression, the longer the overall survival of patients with NSCLC [43–45]. Our MR analysis revealed a strong causal protective effect of genetically predicted AGER levels against SqCLC (OR 0.338, 95% CI 0.209–0.548, PFDR=0.0138, PPH4 = 0.822, Figs. 4 and 5C). As a context-dependent tumor suppressor, AGER activates the p53-p21CIP1 pathway, inhibiting CDK2 kinase activity and blocking RB phosphorylation, leading to G1 cell cycle arrest and suppressing lung cancer cell proliferation. Additionally, RAGE activates the ERK1/2-Snail/Slug/Twist pathway, downregulating E-cadherin and upregulating mesenchymal markers (N-cadherin and vimentin), promoting epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) and metastasis [46]. The inhibition of the RAGE-ERK pathway may reduce cancer cell invasion rates by 58% [46]. In tumor-associated macrophages, the AGE–AGER axis promotes the section of interleukin-6 secretion via NF-κB activation, creating a pro-tumorigenic microenvironment [47]. AGER upregulates its expression via the lncAGER/miR-185 regulatory axis, thereby inhibiting the proliferation and migration of lung cancer cells while promoting apoptosis and autophagy [48]. Figure S5 demonstrates the principal relevant mechanisms. Consequently, although AGER correlates with SqCLC, its oncogenic role depends on ligand types, receptor subtypes, and downstream pathway activation—a complexity that also positions it as a therapeutic target.

In the colocalization analysis of plasma proteins and lung cancer complications, a strong causality was found between F11, F2, and ABO and DVT (Figs. 4 and 5E–G). This is consistent with the results of previous studies [49–52]. In patients with lung cancer, non-O blood groups are associated with a high risk of VTE [53]. However, the effect of ABO blood groups on lung cancer risk factors appears related to the population and type of pathology [54]. F2 directly activates endothelial cells and indirectly stimulates platelets to release angiogenic mediators, promoting tumor angiogenesis. Concurrently, F2/PAR signaling inhibits T/NK cell function, increases immunosuppressive factors, and activates tumor-associated macrophages. F2 also induces neutrophil extracellular traps to facilitate metastasis [55]. Through PAR-1 activation, F2 triggers NF-κB-mediated EMT and vasculogenic mimicry, with high PAR-1 correlating with poor NSCLC prognosis [56]. Therefore, novel F2 inhibitors show promise—particularly in lung cancer with thrombotic complications—warranting clinical evaluation of combination therapies.

FKBP1B (encoding FKBP12.6 protein) regulates calcium signaling through RyR2 receptor stabilization and is crucial in chronic hypoxia-induced pulmonary hypertension [57]. Its strong association with bronchitis (Figs. 4 and 5D, PPH4 = 0.947) presents new therapeutic avenues for the management of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. In this study, five lung cancer-associated proteins (FUCA1, ANGPTL1, CRELD1, ISLR2, and IL1RAP; Figs. 2A/C/D and 3 A–3 C) were identified; however, though subtype associations lacked colocalization support, potentially due to limited statistical power. ANGPTL1 represses lung cancer cell motility by abrogating the expression of the EMT mediator SLUG [58]. ITGA3 is highly expressed in early-stage KRAS-mutant AT2 cells, where it forms the α3β1 integrin complex and couples with SRC to drive integrin-mediated signaling. This KRAS–ITGA3–SRC axis sustains a transitional cell state crucial for LUAD initiation, representing both a pathological driver and a therapeutic target [59]. FUCA1 has recently been identified as a p53-responsive gene, functioning as a tumor suppressor through the regulation of glycoprotein fucosylation. Its downregulation in colorectal and breast cancers indicates a potential tumor-suppressive role and highlights its clinical therapeutic relevance [60]. Furthermore, CRELD1 has been implicated in cardiac pathophysiology and immune regulation, suggesting its broader relevance beyond oncology [61, 62]. Although their mechanistic roles in lung cancer are not fully understood, our genetic findings nominate them as potential causal targets for future investigation.

The expression level of IL1RAP was significantly associated with the histological subtypes of lung cancer (P < 1.5 × 10− 4) [63]. As a core component of IL-1 signaling, IL1RAP is recruited to IL-1R1 upon IL-1α/β binding, activating the NF-κB pathway to induce tumor inflammation, proliferation, metastasis, chemoresistance [64, 65]. The clinical evaluation of the anti-IL1RAP monoclonal antibody nadunolimab (CAN04) in NSCLC is ongoing (NCT03267316) [66]. Paradoxically, the present findings suggest the potential tumor-suppressive effects on SCLC, indicating divergent mechanistic roles across lung cancer subtypes—necessitating critical investigation into SCLC-specific mechanisms.

This study incorporated extensive case data on plasma proteins and related diseases and employed BWMR to cross-validate two independent outcome data, supplemented by Bayesian colocalization analysis, resulting in stable and precise inferential calculations. However, our study was restricted to individuals of European ancestry, which reflects the predominance of European-derived cohorts in currently available large-scale GWAS datasets [67]. While this reduces potential confounding due to population structure, it limits the generalizability of our findings to other ancestries. Indeed, even multi-ancestry GWAS remain heavily weighted toward European participants, meaning that European-derived instruments currently provide the most statistically robust associations. Future studies leveraging larger and more diverse cohorts will be essential to validate these associations across different ancestral populations. Additionally, the limitations of this study are worth discussing. First, owing to data availability, colocalization analysis was performed only on proteins included in the UKB-PPP database; therefore, whether the MR associations of proteins in the deCODE Health database are supported by colocalization analysis remains unknown. Second, the analysis of SCLC may be inadequate owing to the small number of SCLC cases. Finally, although cis-qtl analysis minimizes the effect of horizontal pleiotropy, the associations obtained may also be affected by this phenomenon, particularly for proteins in which certain coding genes play a broad role.

Conclusions

The findings of this study provide several potential targets for the future treatment of various lung cancer pathology and complications. Moreover, the ABO blood group may be an independent risk factor for lung cancer and VTE. This study also indirectly found that IL1RAP expression levels and pathological mechanisms may vary significantly among different lung cancer pathologies, and GFRA2 may bring new hope for the treatment of SCLC. AGER inhibitors and GGT1 may also have potential value in drug research in the treatment of NSCLC. Although the mechanism of some of the proteins has been investigated, further studies are needed to evaluate the feasibility of these drug-targeted proteins. More in-depth and comprehensive proteomic studies on different phenotypes of lung cancer may bring new leaps in treatment. With the emergence of more comprehensive proteomics platforms, more and more precise drug targets will also be discovered, and the future is promising.
