Hydrogen reduced interstitial-vacancy cluster recombination in metals
Authors: Yu-Hao Li, Fang-Fei Ma, Hao-Xuan Huang, Yi-Chun Hua, Li-Min Liu, Hong-Bo Zhou, Guang-Hong Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe interplay between displacement defects governs the evolution of irradiation damage in materials and is of great fundamental interests with important practical implications, from microelectronics industry to advanced nuclear system. Hydrogen, a ubiquitous impurity, is known to segregate to vacancies, but its role in altering vacancy-interstitial recombination—the key process underlying defect annihilation—has not been established. Here, using tungsten as a model system, we show that hydrogen adsorption on the inner surfaces of vacancy clusters significantly suppresses recombination with self-interstitial atoms, thereby inhibiting defect annihilation. We identify a stress-mediated mechanism in which hydrogen adsorption transforms the local stress field of vacancy clusters, weakening their long-range attraction to self-interstitial atoms. Based on this mechanism, we develop a predictive model that quantitatively relates the relative reduction of recombination radius to the hydrogen inner surface density, independent of cluster size. By integrating atomistic parametrization with multiscale simulations, we investigate the co-evolution of hydrogen and displacement defects, which show quantitative agreement with recent experiments, including the hydrogen isotope retention, distribution and desorption. Our results establish a direct link between impurity-defect interactions and defect-defect recombination, providing a physically grounded framework for understanding and controlling irradiation damage in structural materials.Subject terms: Structural materials, Theory and computation

Introduction

Interaction of energetic particles with matter has been recognized for over 80 years and remains critically important across a broad range of fundamental science and engineering applications, from semiconductors in the microelectronics industry to structural materials in advanced nuclear system1,2. Such interaction produces displacement damage in the form of vacancies and self-interstitial atoms (SIAs), whose evolution governs the microstructure and performance of irradiated materials at a fundamental level. Therefore, a quantitative understanding and control of displacement defect behavior in irradiated materials is essential for the fabrication of high-quality semiconductor devices and the development of radiation-resistant structural material3–7.

Hydrogen (H), the most ubiquitous impurity in materials, is well known to induce various forms of structural damage, including surface blistering8–10, cracking11,12, and hydride formation13,14. These damages originate from the interaction of H with lattice defects15,16, among which the interplay between H and vacancies is of great importance and has attracted considerable attention. Although interstitial H atoms are known to segregate into vacancies to relieve local stress, how H segregation will affect the evolution of vacancies is still unclear. This is particularly important in applications of advanced nuclear system17,18, where H isotopes are often introduced simultaneously with displacement damage. Numerous studies have shown that the H addition, even with low concentrations, can dramatically aggravate the radiation damage in steels, leading to significant swelling compared with H-free samples19–22. This phenomenon has further been observed in tungsten (W) under various experimental conditions23–27, including the simultaneous irradiation, cyclic irradiation, and post annealing after irradiation, strongly confirming the promoting effect of H isotopes on the population of surviving defects. Despite being well identified in experiments, the underlying mechanisms of H-stabilized displacement damage remain under debate. Existing interpretations have primarily focused on H-assisted vacancy formation, because the presence of H reduces the formation energy of vacancy nearby due to the attractive interaction of H-vacancy complexes, providing a thermodynamic driving force for defect production28–30. Another possibility is the suppressing effect of H-decorated vacancies on Frenkel pair annihilation, as advocated by first-principle calculations31. Recently, molecular dynamic simulations have revealed that the number of created defects after collision cascade (either a single one or cumulative impacts) increases with increasing H isotope content owing to both primary defect production and subsequent defect recombination32,33. In addition to these atomistic endeavors, notable rate theory investigations have reproduced experimental data reasonably well and confirmed the increase in trap density induced by H isotopes26,34,35. It should be noted, however, that the direct link between the available atomic simulations and experimental observations is still lacking, and the rate theory relies heavily on the empirical models/parameters employed, without incorporating a physical dynamic mechanism. Consequently, the absence of a unified framework currently hinders accurate assessment of H effects on displacement damage; elucidating the underlying physics and developing multiscale predictive tools remain formidable challenges.

Here, using body-centred cubic (bcc) W as a model system, we demonstrate that H adsorption on the inner surface of vacancy clusters will significantly reduce the recombination radii of vacancy clusters and SIAs. This is driven by a stress-mediated mechanism, wherein H incorporation progressively neutralizes and then reverses the intrinsic tensile field of vacancy clusters. Based on this insight, we develop a predictive model that quantitatively relates relative recombination radius reduction to H inner surface density, independent of cluster size. By integrating this model with atomistic parameterization and object kinetic Monte Carlo (OKMC) simulations, we explicitly clarify the evolution of displacement damages with H, showing good quantitative agreement with recent experiments. These results reveal a previously underexplored pathway by which impurities regulate defect evolution—by modifying defect-defect interactions rather than only defect energetics—and provide a general framework for controlling irradiation damage through compositional and stress engineering.

Results

Interaction between SIAs and vacancies

Displacement damage takes the form of vacancies and SIAs, which can annihilate in a self-healing process. Under irradiation, most displacement defects recombine, whereas the remaining fraction determines the microstructural features that ultimately affect material performance36,37. Therefore, elucidating the recombination of SIAs and vacancies is key to understanding the evolution of displacement defects, which significantly impacts damage control5,38.

Physically, the annihilation of SIAs and vacancies can be characterized by two kinds of recombination radii39–44, a spontaneous recombination radius (R0) and a capture efficiency radius (Rc). The former is a “thermodynamic recombination radius” depending solely on their interaction range, and widely used both theoretically and computationally (e.g., OKMC and mean field rate theory). Namely, if an SIA approaches a vacant site at a distance longer than R0, no vacancy-interstitial interaction is considered; the diffusion of SIA is treated as a random walk in crystal materials and only depends on the energy barriers (see Fig. 1a). As for shorter distances, the recombination of vacancies and SIAs occurs spontaneously and almost instantaneously. For the case of capture radius, there is also a migration energy barrier within R0; at larger distances, the direct interaction of SIAs and vacancies diminishes, but can still bias diffusive jumps for SIAs. Thus, Rc is always supposed to be larger than R0. However, it should be noted that even without interaction, spontaneous recombination can still occur with a reduced probability. In this sense, the two recombination radii could be indistinguishable, and either could be used to characterize vacancy-SIA interactions for annihilation43,44. Due to its importance, several studies have estimated the recombination radius in bcc and fcc metals both experimentally39–41 and theoretically42–44. However, each study obtained a different value and a wide range of recombination radii from 1.0a0 to 3.3a0 have been predicted in computational studies, where a0 is the lattice constant of materials.

Herein, the general trends of recombination radii calculation in W with and without H are conducted by molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. The primary simulation configuration involves an SIA (i.e., a W-W dumbbell along <111> direction) and a mono-vacancy (Vac1) or highly symmetric vacancy clusters (Vac4, Vac6, Vac15, Vac27 and Vac65) in the simulation box with varying separation distances up to 3.8 nm and different relative orientations. To examine the recombination probability (Pi) and ensure the statistical convergence, we perform ~ 50 MD simulations in each configuration. After that, the total number of lattice sites (Ntot) from which spontaneous recombination takes place is determined, i.e. Ntot=∑iPi, and the effective recombination radius (Rrec) between SIA and vacancies can be calculated by Rrec=(3NtotΩ4π)1/3, where Ω denotes the atomic volume of bcc metals. In order to clarify the influence of H on the annihilation process, the H atoms are sequentially introduced into a mono-vacancy or vacancy clusters, leading to the formation of H-vacancy (H-Vac) complexes. Then, the recombination radius of SIA with H-Vac complexes is again obtained (see more details in Supplementary Note 1).

Figure 1a illustrates a typical diffusion trajectory of SIA towards a mono-vacancy in W. Note that only SIA jumps are observed during recombination migration, because its energy barrier (~0.04 eV) is much lower than that of vacancies (~1.73 eV), in agreement with previous studies45,46. Obviously, before being recombined with a mono-vacancy, the diffusion of SIA is completely random, and the trajectory is composed of one-dimensional (1D) migration along <111> direction with intermittent changes of orientation. However, once SIA jumps across the recombination radius, its trajectory deviates from <111> direction due to the strong interstitial-vacancy attractions. Such interaction originates from the coupling of stress fields induced by SIA and vacancies, and can be determined by lattice distortion. As seen in Fig. 1b, the most stable SIAs in non-magnetic bcc metals are a dumbbell (or crowdion) structure along <111> (or <11 h > ) direction, i.e., two atoms sharing one lattice site. This anisotropic configuration decreases the distance to the atoms along its dumbbell directions, resulting in an extended distortion and compressive stress field of the lattice structure, but increases the distance from the atoms perpendicular to the dumbbell axis. For a mono-vacancy (or vacancy clusters), as displayed in Fig. 1c, it will create a local tensile stress nearby because of the reduced atomic density. Therefore, the stress coupling between SIA and vacancies will eliminate the lattice distortion induced by displacement defects and further drive SIA to overcome its low migration energy barrier and move towards vacancies in W (see Fig. 1d).

To quantitatively analyze the defect annihilation, taking Vac15 as an example, we further studied the variation of recombination probability as a function of vacancy-interstitial distance as well as their relative angle, as illustrated in Fig. 1e. Roughly speaking, the average recombination probability between SIA and vacancy clusters decreases with the increasing of their distance, from ~100% within 6th nearest neighboring distance (~0.63 nm) to ~49% beyond 15th NN distance (~1.03 nm), which can be attributed to a diminished interaction between vacancies and SIA at a longer distance. Besides the vacancy-interstitial distance, the recombination probability is also closely associated with the angle between the dumbbell axis and the line connecting the centroid of vacancy clusters and dumbbell atoms. As shown in Fig. 1e, independent of the distances between Vac15 cluster and <111> dumbbell, the bcc W features a high recombination probability with angles near 0 degrees, corresponding to the case that the vacancy cluster is located on the 1D diffusion path of <111> dumbbell. On the other hand, the probability of vacancy-interstitial annihilation gradually decreases with the increase of the relative angle. This is because, as a high-energy-barrier process, the rotation of <111> dumbbell needs to occur for recombing SIA with the vacancy clusters perpendicular to the dumbbell axis. Therefore, strong vacancy-interstitial interactions and a low angle between their connecting line and dumbbell axis are both critical for determining the recombination probability in bcc W.

H-induced reduction of recombination radius

Next, we investigated the influence of H segregation into vacancies on the recombination of displacement defects. As shown previously28,47–50, the H atoms prefer to occupy the sites at the inner surface of mono-vacancy and vacancy clusters in W, and H2 molecule can only be formed at a high H-to-vacancy (H/Vac) ratio. As seen in Fig. 1e, the presence of H in Vac15 cluster decreases the probability of displacement defect annihilation, especially in the stress field overlapped region, leading to the reduction of recombination radius. To quantitatively estimate this effect, we examined the variation of recombination radius induced by H addition as a function of H/Vac ratio, as illustrated in Fig. 2a. It can be clearly seen that the presence of H reduces the recombination radius of displacement defects, and the effect of H becomes stronger with the increasing of H/Vac ratio. Furthermore, in comparison with mono-vacancy, the reduction of recombination radius is even more significant in vacancy clusters. This is due to the fact that the maximum H/Vac ratio in H-Vac complexes decays rapidly as the number of vacancies increases47,50. Thus, the H segregation in vacancies may prevent vacancies from recombining with the neighboring SIAs in W, suggesting an accelerating effect of H on the displacement damage.

a The H-induced relative reduction of recombination radius for different vacancy clusters. b Atomic-level stress of W in the vicinity of Hn-Vac15 cluster on {110} plane. The red and blue denote the tensile and compressive stress, respectively.

Since the long-range recombination of displacement defects should originate from the stress field interactions between vacancies and SIAs, broadening (shortening) stress distribution and increasing (decreasing) local stress may promote (suppress) the defect annihilation. Hence, similar to a previous study51, we further determined the influence of H on the distribution of atomic-level stress for Vac15 clusters in W. As seen in Fig. 2b, a tensile stress field is clearly observed in the vicinity of vacancy clusters, while the W atoms at the first shell of the inner surface show the compressive stress. Such a phenomenon can be rationalized by the local depletion in the surrounding electron density, which looks locally like a dangling bond in ionic materials and draws neighboring atoms toward the vacant site to compensate for this effect. Similar results on atomic forces are also obtained by DFT calculations (Supplementary Note 2). However, as illustrated in Fig. 2b, the atomic-level stress field globally transforms from tensile stress in pure vacancy clusters into compressive stress in H-Vac complexes, especially for the cases with a high H/Vac ratio. Therefore, the H segregation significantly suppresses the long-range attraction of vacancies with SIAs and reduces the probability of vacancy-interstitial recombination.

To quantitatively analyze the relationship between H-induced atomic-level stress variation and suppressing effect on defect annihilation, we examined the recombination probability of different sites in the vicinity of H-decorated Vac15 cluster. As seen in Fig. 3a, the influence of H on the recombination probability can be categorized into three regions, i.e., center, side, and corner, which depend on both the distance and the relative angle between the SIA and the vacancy cluster. In the center region (corresponding to the nearest-neighbor sites around the vacancy cluster with a wide range of relative angle), the recombination probability remains largely unaffected by H addition and consistently exceeds 70%, as the created SIA is located very close to the vacancy cluster. Moreover, the side and corner regions both involve lattice sites farther away from the vacancy cluster compared to the center region, but they differ in terms of the relative angle. The corner region corresponds to angular ranges of 0–20° and 70–90°, where the recombination probability exhibits minimal dependence on H, consistent with Fig. 1e. In contrast, as displayed in Fig. 3a, the recombination probability in the side region (20–70°) dramatically decreases with the increase of H numbers, which is attributed to the decrease of tensile stress. This is also confirmed by Fig. 3b, which shows that the reduction in recombination radius for different H-Vac complexes is nearly proportional to the variation in average stress toward the cluster center.

a The distribution of recombination probability for each W atom in the vicinity of a Vac15 cluster with and without H addition. b The variation of average stress as a function of the relative reduction of recombination radius for different H-Vac complexes.

In the above analysis, the qualitative nature of H effects on defect annihilation is clarified. However, how to quantitatively and accurately characterize the H-induced reduction of recombination radius remains a very challenging problem. It is important to note that the initial H adsorption on the inner surfaces of vacancy clusters is energetically favorable, and the interaction between H adatom and neighboring W atoms should be responsible for the H-induced variation of atomic-level stress. Therefore, to simplify the analysis but yet without losing generality, we assume that all H atoms stay in the form of adatoms at the inner surface of vacancy clusters. In this way, the inner surface stress (positive correlation to the atomic-level stress) is only dependent on the H density at the inner surface (units: H/Å²), which is defined as the number of H atoms divided by the inner surface area of the vacancy cluster. The inner surface area is calculated based on the Wigner-Seitz construction (see Fig. 4a), ranging from 30.52 Å² for a mono-vacancy to 185.64 Å² for a Vac15 cluster and 493.41 Å² for a Vac65 cluster, as done previously47. Figure 4b shows the H-induced relative reduction of recombination radius in W as a function of inner surface H density for various H-vacancy complexes. One clearly sees that there is a significant positive correlation between the recombination radius reduction and H density, regardless of the size of vacancy clusters and the number of H atoms. Accordingly, a simple linear function is proposed to determine the variation of recombination radius, i.e., △rr=Ana+1, where n and a denote the number of H atoms and inner surface area of vacancy clusters, respectively. The model prediction is in good agreement with MD data, as seen in Fig. 4b. This suggests that the H-induced surface stress release mechanism, rather than the simple filling effect, captures the salient physics of H effects on annihilation of displacement defects (see Supplementary Note 3), which provides an effective framework to describe the recombination between SIAs and H-decorated vacancies. Noted that the linear function of H-reduced recombination radii obtained here is also confirmed by two additional W‑H potentials52,53, confirming the robustness of the predictive model.

The black lines represent edges of the Wigner-Seitz cells. b The relative reduction of recombination radius as a function of H density at the inner surface.

Model verification and application

The model parameters are not sensitive to the size or configuration of vacancy clusters; thus, it is generally applicable to larger clusters and for examining H effects in various H-vacancy complexes. Since the precise H effects on annihilation of displacement defects could be strongly dependent on the kind of metal elements, the preliminary calculations of H segregating into vacancies, as well as its influence on recombination radius, have been performed in other bcc metals, including Mo and α-Fe. The similar positive correlations between surface H density in vacancy clusters and relative reduction of recombination radius have also been found (Supplementary Note 4), confirming the generality of this physical model in bcc metals.

The proposed model accurately describes the radii of SIA and H-decorated vacancies in W, as derived from a finite set of MD recombination calculations. Such predictions can provide atomic-precision data to be fed into large-scale simulations, e.g., kinetic Monte Carlo and cluster dynamics, enabling a multiscale approach that directly bridges atomistic insights with macroscopic phenomena. In the following, applying our predictive model-based multiscale simulations, we present one typical and important example comparing recent nuclear reaction analysis (NRA) and thermal desorption spectroscopy (TDS) results23, which determine the evolution of displacement damage in W with and without H isotope. In the experiments23, the polycrystalline W samples are sequentially or simultaneously irradiated by 10.8 MeV W ions and 300 eV H isotope (deuterium) ions at different temperatures. The irradiated samples are subsequently implanted with low-energy H isotope at 450 K in order to populate all the defects created beforehand, followed by NRA and/or TDS measurements with an oven heating ramp of 3 K min−1 to evaluate the amount of displacement damage. Note that the results of sequential irradiation results are largely consistent with our previous study54, with updated implantation modeling, and serve as a reference to demonstrate the difference between with and without H isotopes.

Combining our predictive model, primary irradiation damage simulations and an OKMC method, we carried out multiscale simulations to reproduce and interpret the experimental NRA and TDS results23. To make a direct comparison, the same irradiation and heating conditions as those employed in the experimental studies are used in the simulations (for details see Supplementary Note 5). Figure 5a shows the depth distribution of H concentration for both simultaneous and sequential irradiation. It is clearly evident that the H depth distribution obtained by OKMC simulations is well consistent with the NRA experiments23. Notably, the H atoms only populated the defects down to 0.8 µm during simultaneous irradiation (marked area). Within this region, the H concentration for the simultaneous case is much higher than that of sequential one after the additional H implantation (by a factor of ~1.98 in the first 0.5 μm), while the H concentrations for the two cases agree with each other beyond the marked area. This is in good agreement with the depth of “defect stabilization zone” (~0.7 μm) observed in experiments, and is in accordance with the maximum permeation depth of H during simultaneous irradiation (see Supplementary Note 6). As seen in Fig. 5b, the simulated TDS curves (lines) for both sequentially and simultaneously irradiated samples also match the experimental results (symbols) very well for the whole range of heating temperatures. In particular, the simulated total retention of H isotope (positive correlation with the number of surviving defects) after simultaneous irradiation at 450 K is much higher than that after sequential irradiation. This attests that the presence of H significantly increases the surviving defect population and thus exacerbates the displacement damage. Since the H effect on displacement damage is closely related to temperatures, we further determined the H isotope retention in simultaneously and sequentially irradiated samples as well as their ratio as a function of irradiation temperatures. As illustrated in Fig. 5c, the ratio decreases with increasing temperature, from 1.55 at 450 K to 1.07 at 800 K; again, these simulated results show good agreement with experimental data23. More information on H behaviors in irradiated W is presented in the Supplementary Note 6.

Overall, aside from some minor discrepancies at very high temperatures, which may be attributed to other intrinsic defects (e.g. dislocations and/or grain boundaries) and surface effects, our simulations reproduce the experimental observation of displacement damage stabilization by H isotope addition23. Such effects should be mainly attributed to the preventing effects of H segregation on defect annihilation, which can be quantified accurately by our model. These results also suggest that the modulation of recombination radius (e.g. external stress44 and/or composition design55), even by a slight variation, offers an effective strategy for controlling displacement damage in materials. This has broad implications for the fabrication of high-quality semiconductor devices and the development of radiation-resistant structural materials.

Discussions

In summary, we explicitly demonstrate that H segregation into vacancies prevents the annihilation of displacement defects, via atomistic simulations using bcc W as a prototypical model system. Importantly, a predictive model is proposed to quantify the H-induced reduction of recombination radius, based on the inner stress release of H absorption in relation to the Wigner-Seitz area of vacancy clusters. By combining this model along with atomistic parameters, a comprehensive multiscale modelling framework has been established to accurately describe H-stabilized displacement damage, yielding excellent agreement with experiments. Our findings clarify the long-sought physical insights underlying the co-evolution of H and displacement defects in structural materials, and provide a potential strategy for controlling irradiation damage.

Methods

MD simulations

MD simulations were performed in the large-scale atomic molecular massively parallel simulator (LAMMPS, version: 7Aug2019) code56. An embedded-atom method (EAM) potential developed by Wang et al. was selected for W-H systems57, which shows a good performance on the behaviors of irradiation defects as well as their interactions with H58,59. A supercell containing 5488 atoms (i.e., 14 × 14 × 14 duplicate of a conventional bcc unit cell) was used in our calculations to determine the recombination radius with and without H segregation. The timestep was adapted automatically to ensure that the maximum displacement of atoms per step is less than 0.01 Å, and the periodic boundary conditions (PBCs) were applied in all three directions. The bulk system was first equilibrated at 300 K under NPT ensemble of 5 ps. Since the <111> dumbbell is commonly considered the most stable configuration of SIA in non-magnetic bcc metals, such dumbbell structure was placed at the center of the simulation box, and the mono-vacancy or vacancy cluster (or H-Vac complex) was introduced at different positions. Then, the energy minimization using the conjugate gradient method was conducted with energy and force convergence criteria of 10−15 eV and 10−15 eV/Å, respectively. After that, the system was evolved under NVT ensemble at 300 K for 500 ps with a timestep of 0.001 ps, and whether the vacancy-interstitial recombination was examined by OVITO. Notably, as illustrated in Supplymentary Note 1, most vacancy-interstitial interactions occur within the first 200 ps, accounting for up to 97% of all recombination events, indicating that the simulation duration is sufficiently long. This can be attributed to the low migration energy barrier of SIA in W, allowing defect annihilation to take place within a very short time (without long-term migration and evolution) as long as the SIA and vacancies are coupled with the stress fields. Each configuration was simulated 50 times to ensure statistical convergence. Benchmark calculations with increased supercell size and temperature were performed, and the negligible effect on our results was observed (Supplementary Note 1). Moreover, benchmark calculations of configurations and energetics of H-Vac complexes were also carried out, and the obtained results were in good agreement with previous DFT and MD simulations.

First-principles calculations

Our first-principles calculations were conducted using the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP, version 6.3.2) code60. The projector-augmented wave potentials were used for W and H with the standard Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof exchange-correlation functional61,62. A slab model was selected to calculate the stress of W(110) surface, which consists of sixteen W atomic layers and a vacuum thickness of 30 Å. The slab model constructed with a (3 × 3) surface periodic structure at the theoretical equilibrium lattice constant contains a total of 144 W atoms and has dimensions of 8.24 Å, 9.51 Å and 67.23 Å along the [111], [001] and [11¯0] directions, respectively. The cutoff energy was set to be 400 eV, and the Brillouin zone integration was sampled with the 6×6×1 k-points mesh by the Monkhorst-Pack approach63. During the structure optimization, the bottom three atomic layers were fixed at the bulk lattice, while the H and other W atoms were relaxed until the forces on those atoms were less than 0.01 eV/Å.

OKMC simulations

Thermal desorption spectra of H isotope in irradiated W were simulated by a quantitative multiscale approach, which incorporates the atomistic scale of H-inhibited defect annihilation, primary displacement damage induced by high-energy W ions and OKMC simulations. General algorithms of the OKMC method and parameterizations of defects in W-H system were described in detail elsewhere47,54,64. It should be noted that all key parameters were either derived from first-principles calculations, obtained from independent MD simulations, or taken from well-established literature values. The only introduced quantity—the H-modified recombination radius—was determined from our MD simulations and/or predictive model in the present work, without any fitting to experiments. Actually, a sensitivity analysis of key OKMC parameters is conducted, which demonstrates that the recombination radius is the dominant factor controlling defect survival, directly supporting the central mechanism proposed in this work (for details see Supplementary Note 7).

The primary displacement damage was introduced based on the primary knock-on atom (PKA) spectrum and collision cascade at different PKA energies. The former one was calculated by the binary collision code IM3D65, but the latter was obtained through rigorous MD simulations66. Note that high-energy PKAs generate various types of irradiation defects, ranging from mono-vacancy and single SIA to nano-sized void and dislocation loop, which are well consistent with experimental observations67,68. Considering the experimentally relevant temperatures and H/Vac ratios considered here, the influence of H isotope on the defect production during cascade is not considered (for details see Supplementary Note 8). For simultaneous W and H irradiation, the simulation duration was 4 hours, followed by 39 hours of continuous H implantation to populate the created defects. For the sequential irradiation, the simulation consisted of 4 hours of W ion irradiation followed by 39 hours of H implantation. After irradiation, the samples were heated from 300 K (~room temperature) to 1010 K at a heating rate of 3 K min−1, corresponding to a duration of about 4 hours. The evolution of displacement defects during annealing was also considered in our OKMC simulations. The irradiation fluxes, temperatures and dose were also calibrated according to the corresponding experimental conditions23.

In order to simulate the simultaneous irradiation, the displacement damage profile was calculated with SRIM, and microstructures under different damage levels were spliced together to obtain the depth-dependent damage distribution (each layer thickness of 0.02 μm). Meanwhile, based on the average implantation range of 300 eV H isotope obtained by SRIM, the H atoms were introduced simultaneously at a depth of 5 nm from the surface. The corresponding flux was 0.65 × 1018 (m2s)−1, assuming a reflection coefficient of 0.5, consistent with experiments23. In this approach, the bottom interface of each layer served as an ideal sink for H atoms, and a time-dependent H permeation rate was derived and subsequently used as the net H injection rate for the next layer. Additional H implantation was then performed to populate the created defects in a 60 nm × 60 nm × 1500 nm box, followed by simulation of the corresponding thermal desorption processes. More details of OKMC simulations are presented in Supplementary Note 5.
