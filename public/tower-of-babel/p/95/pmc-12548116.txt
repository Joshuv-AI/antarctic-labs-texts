Nitrogen–phosphorus stoichiometry shapes seedling growth and yield performance of perennial cool-season forage grasses in saline–alkali soil
Authors: Nan Xu, Junpeng Niu, Zhipeng Guo, Quanzhen Wang, Zhao Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGrass production faces increasing challenges due to excessive fertilization, which contributes to greenhouse gas emissions and poor soil quality, especially in saline–alkali regions, threatening long-term sustainability. Most previous studies have focused on short-term effects of nitrogen (N) or phosphorus (P) applied individually, with limited understanding of their interactive and long-term impacts. This study investigated the combined effects of N and P fertilization on the seed germination and yield production of perennial cool-season forage grasses under saline–alkali soil conditions. Over three consecutive years, large-scale orthogonal field experiments were conducted with 18 N levels (0–480 kg N ha⁻¹) and 16 P levels (0–240 kg P₂O₅ ha⁻¹) across six grass species to determine the optimal N:P ratio for seed germination, seed yield and yield components. Results showed that P had a stronger impact on early seedling vigor, while N primarily influenced reproductive growth and yield. Species varied in their nutrient responses, with shallow-rooted species favoring low nutrients and deep-rooted species benefiting from moderate to high fertilization. Optimal N: P ratios were around 2:1 for seed germination and 2.86:1 for yield. Excessive or imbalanced N and P reduced growth and yield due to nutrient antagonism and stress. Background

Soil degradation has become a major constraint to global forage production and ecosystem integrity [1], particularly in salt-affected regions where excess sodium, high pH, and poor nutrient availability severely limit plant growth and soil health [2]. In these environments, the cultivation of perennial cool-season forage grasses not only supports sustainable livestock systems but also contributes to erosion control, organic matter retention, and the maintenance of plant biodiversity [3, 4]. However, However, the productivity of such grasses is often restricted by low soil nitrogen (N) and phosphorus (P) availability [5], highlighting the need for effective nutrient management strategies to enhance forage establishment and growth in saline–alkali soils.

Application of N and P fertilizers has been widely recognized as a viable means to improve plant growth and soil quality in degraded systems [6, 7]. Nitrogen is essential for chlorophyll synthesis, amino acid production, and protein assembly, while P plays a central role in ATP generation, membrane stability, and nucleic acid synthesis [8, 9]. Numerous studies have shown that N and P fertilization can enhance seed germination [10, 11], increase biomass production [12, 13], and improve nutrient uptake [14], with applications often outperforming single-nutrient treatments [9, 15]. In saline–alkali soils, these nutrients are taken up by plants through modifications in root architecture, enhanced secretion of organic acids and phosphatases, and the upregulation of nutrient transporter genes [16, 17]. However, if poorly managed, fertilization can disrupt rhizosphere chemistry, reduce nutrient-use efficiency, and even exacerbate soil degradation, ultimately limiting plant performance [18–20].

Maintaining a balanced N: P ratio in fertilization has therefore emerged as a key strategy to sustainably enhance plant growth while minimizing environmental risks [18]. The interaction between N and P inputs can be synergistic or antagonistic depending on their relative proportions and plant species [21, 22]. Synergistic effects are typically observed when both nutrients are supplied within optimal thresholds, whereas imbalanced ratios may lead to nutrient uptake inhibition or physiological stress [23]. Recent studies suggest that moderate N:P ratios, such as 1.2, improve plant yield and physiological function in species like Sapindus mukorossi [24] and millet [25], while excessive or skewed ratios adversely affect growth and resource allocation [26]. However, empirical evidence for optimal N: P fertilization ratio management in perennial forage grasses under saline–alkali conditions remains limited, especially in multi-year field experiments that assess impacts on germination, nutrient allocation, and yield components over time.

To address this critical knowledge gap, we conducted a large-scale, three-year field experiment in the Hexi Corridor of Gansu Province, China, which is a region characterized by saline-alkali soils and overfertilization [27–29]. Utilizing an orthogonal experimental design, we applied 18 levels of N and 16 levels of P across 6 forage grass species, encompassing key developmental stages of seed germination and reproduction stage. By fitting two-variable quadratic models and applying ridge-regression techniques, we quantified both synergistic and antagonistic interactions, identified modal optimal N: P combinations for seed germination and yield production, and assessed how N: P ratios influence plant N and P uptake. We hypothesize that (1) N and P interaction significantly shapes germination and yield under saline soils; (2) an optimal N: P fertilizer ratio exists that maximizes both seedling vigor and reproductive output; and (3) stoichiometric balance, more than absolute fertilization rate, dictates nutrient uptake. This study moves beyond traditional fertilizer recommendation by anchoring management in nutrient ratio optimization, providing a scalable framework to enhance productivity and environmental resilience in marginal soils.

Methods

Experimental site

Field experiments were conducted from 2003 to 2005 at the Grassland Research Station of China Agricultural University, located in the Hexi Corridor of Gansu Province, China (39° 37′ N, 98° 30′ E; elevation 1480 m). The region has an arid continental climate, with a long-term (1995–2004) average annual temperature of 7.3 °C and mean annual precipitation of 85.3 mm, most of which occurs in summer [30]. Weather conditions during the experimental period (2003–2005) were obtained from the Jiuquan Meteorological Observatory and are presented in Fig. S1.

The 6000 m2 field was prepared using conventional tillage methods [31]. Six perennial cool-season forage grass species were selected: Pascopyrum smithii, Psathyrostachys juncea, Festuca arundinacea, Phleum pratense, Bromus inermis, and Dactylis glomerata. Seeds were sourced from Jiuquan Daye Seed Industry Co. Ltd. and sown in April 2002 at a depth of 2.5 cm and a density of 5 × 106 seeds ha−1, with row spacing of 0.45–0.60 m. No seed yield was harvested in the first growing season (2002). Initial fertilization included 104 kg ha⁻¹ of (NH4)2CO3 and 63 kg ha−1 of P2O5, applied in bands 6 cm below and 5 cm beside the seed furrows. The soil at the site is classified as Mot-Cal-Orthic Aridisols (Xeric Haplocalcids in USDA taxonomy), characterized by low fertility and high salinity [30]. Initial soil property was represented in Table S1.

Experimental design

The overall experimental system utilized a multi-factor orthogonal experimental design incorporating six factorial combinations (Groups A–E, Tables S2), with each group testing two to six agronomic variables (Table S5): fertilization time (4 levels), irrigation (11 levels), N (18 levels), P (16 levels) and plant density (4 levels). This design helped us efficiently estimate the main effects and interactions of treatments while minimizing the number of required plots [32, 33]. Among these combinations, groups C, D and E involving factors of plant density, irrigation rate, and fertilization timing have been reported in our earlier studies [30]. While groups A and B (Tables S3, S4) were specifically designed to assess the coupling effects of N and P fertilizers at various levels and thus constitute the core dataset for the current analysis. Each group replicated 4 times with each plot measured 28 m2 (4 × 7 m). The 1.5 m buffer zone between adjacent plots to minimize edge effects.

Based on N application rates, we categorized 18 levels into three groups: low N (LN: 0–100 kg N ha−1 yr−1 containing 6 levels), medium N (MN: 107–153 kg N ha−1 yr−1 containing 6 levels) and high N (HN: 176–480 kg N ha−1 yr−1 containing 6 levels). Similarly,16 levels of P application were grouped into: Low P (LP: 0–63 kg P₂O₅ ha−1 yr−1 containing 6 levels), Medium P (MP: 77–105 kg P₂O₅ ha−1 yr−1 containing 5 levels), High P (HP: 124–240 kg P₂O₅ ha−1 yr−1 containing 5 levels). These groupings were defined post hoc based on the inflection points and response patterns observed from the quadratic regression and ridgeline analysis, rather than predefined thresholds, in order to reflect actual functional differences among fertilization intensities [34, 35]. Detailed group levels of N and P fertilizations were provided in Table S5.

To facilitate statistical modeling and ensure comparability across factors with different units and scales, the actual N and P application rates were linearly transformed into coded values (Table S14). The coding relationships were derived via linear regression as follows: N (kg ha−1) = 30.043 × level code + 150.013 (R2 = 0.999, p < 0.001); P (kg ha−1) = 22.013 × level code + 105.071 (R2 = 0.999, p < 0.001). This standardization enables stable estimation of effects in response surface and ridge regression analyses. Corresponding codes for N and P fertilization corresponding to N and P levels were given in Table S5.

Data collection

To systematically assess the effects of N and P fertilization, we collected indicators in seed germination, yield components and nutrients accumulation of six grass species.

Seed germination parameters

Seeds harvested in 2003 were used to evaluate early-stage germination traits for each forage species. For each treatment, three replicates of 50 surface-sterilized seeds were prepared. Seeds were disinfected using a 0.1% sodium hypochlorite solution for 15 min, followed by five rinses with distilled water.

Germination was conducted on germination paper (Germitest®, distributed by Shanghai Biotai Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), moistened with distilled water at 2.5 times the dry paper mass. Seeds were incubated in an illumination growth chamber (Model LRH-250-G, Guangzhou Guangda Laboratory Instrument Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China) under a photoperiod of 8 h light at 25 °C and 16 h dark at 20 °C. Germination was monitored daily for up to 28 days depending on the species, until no further germination was observed.

To assess germination energy (GE), germination index (Gi) and mean germination time (MGT), the number of seeds germinated each day was recorded. On Day 10, seedlings were harvested to evaluate multiple vigor-related indices. For mean seedling length (MSL) assessment, a separate test was conducted with four replicates of 100 seeds each. Seeds were aligned embryo-axis down along the centerline of a 30 cm × 40 cm germination paper, above which 12 parallel lines were drawn at 1 cm intervals. After moistening, the setup was placed in a 35 cm × 42 cm white enamel tray, covered with two additional moist filter papers, loosely rolled into a cylindrical shape, and stood upright in the tray with 1–2 cm of water at the base. Yield components

From 2003 to 2005, seed yield components and total seed yield were measured for six forage grass species. Each plot comprised 12–16 rows (4 m each), with outer rows and 1 m at both row ends excluded to reduce edge effects. Based on species, 16–20 central 1 m sampling units were defined per plot.

Fertile tillers were assessed from 10 to 12 randomly selected units. For spikelets per fertile tillers, florets per spikelet and seed numbers per spikelet, 14–18 units were sampled sequentially, with three random subsamples per unit (total n = 30–54). Seed weight was determined by weighing ten 100-seed subsamples after drying seeds to 7–10% moisture. Seed yield was calculated from full-plot harvests conducted between early July and early August. Detailed methods are provided in the previous study [30]. Summarized sample sizes of three years for our study are listed in Table S6.

Plant N and P concentrations

In 2005, whole-plant samples and harvested seeds were collected to determine N and P contents, following the procedure outlined in [38]. Plants were separated into roots and shoots (i.e., aboveground and belowground parts). Seeds and plants were subjected to heat treatment at 80 ℃ for 1 h to inactivate enzymes, followed by oven-drying at 70 ℃ for 24 h.

The dried samples were ground and analyzed for nutrient content. Nitrogen content was determined by the Kjeldahl digestion method and quantified using Nessler’s reagent colorimetric method. Phosphorus content was measured by the molybdenum blue colorimetric method. All assays were conducted using the UV-Vis spectrophotometer (model UV-2600) from the Shanghai Precision Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd. for colorimetric analyses. Nutrient concentrations are expressed as percentages of dry weight (%). Specifically, data on shoot and root N and P contents for Phleum pratense were unavailable under HN and HP treatments, while seed N and P contents for Phleum pratense and Bromus inermis under all N and P treatments were missing due to poor seed development and low yield under excessive nutrient conditions (Table S6).

N/P ratios were calculated separately for root, shoot, and seed tissues to characterize nutrient stoichiometry across plant organs. Data analysis

To investigate how N and P fertilization, and their stoichiometric balance influence seed germination, yield components, and nutrient uptake, we employed a structured three-tier analysis framework.

Analysis of variance

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to assess the effects of N and P application rates, as well as their interaction, on germination parameters, yield components, and plant nutrient contents. Treatment means were compared using Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test at a significance level of p < 0.05. Prior to model fitting, the assumption of homogeneity of variance was verified to ensure the validity of the statistical tests.

Path analysis for nutrient accumulation

To identify the direct and indirect effects of N and P fertilization on N and P accumulation in roots, shoots, and seeds, we applied path analysis [39]. This clarified internal nutrient redistribution mechanisms under different fertilization scenarios.

Influence of N:P ratio on yield and yield components

To evaluate reproductive performance under varying fertilization regimes, 105 two-variable quadratic models were constructed for seed yield and related traits (Table S9). Significant nonlinear interactions between N and P were observed for all yield components (p < 0.05), including fertile tiller number, seed number per spikelet, and floret formation (Figs. S2–S4).

Ridgeline analysis revealed that N: P ratios below 3 consistently produced antagonistic effects (K < 0), whereas ratios above 3 were associated with synergistic interactions (K > 0) (Fig. 4). Optimal N: P ratios for yield enhancement ranged between 3.2 and 3.4 across species and years. For instance, in F. arundinaceum (2004), the number of fertile tillers and seed yield increased significantly under high N (201–400 kg ha⁻¹) and rising P levels, with an optimal N: P ratio of 3.4 (Fig.5a, d). Similar synergistic patterns were observed in B. inermis (2003) and P. juncea (2005), where seed yield and tillering improved at N: P ≈ 3.3–3.4 (Fig. 5b, c). Additional traits such as florets per spikelet and seed number per spikelet followed this trend, plateauing or declining when N: P dropped below 3 (Fig. 3a, b).

Overall, the optimal N:P ratio for maximizing seed yield was estimated at 2.86:1, as determined by peak model values and modal optimization (Table S9). In contrast, P application had weaker and more inconsistent effects on plant N content, especially in seeds. While P addition enhanced P levels in roots and shoots, it did not reliably increase seed P content (Table 1). Across all species, the high-N and low-P (HN + LP) treatment with N: P ratios around 3:1 resulted in the highest total nutrient accumulation, aligning with the superior seed yield observed under these conditions (Fig. S5). These findings support the conclusion that N: P ratios near or above 3 promote coordinated nutrient uptake and efficient biomass and reproductive allocation, offering a practical strategy for productivity improvement in saline–alkali soils.

Discussion

Effects of N and P on seed germination and yield compoents

Our results show that P exerted a stronger effect than N on early seedling performance, even though both N and P fertilization significantly influenced seed germination (Fig. 2, Table S7). These findings are consistent with previous results that P limitation negatively affected seed germination of Holcus lanatus [42], rice [43], wheat [44] and Arabidopsis [45]. This is likely because P played an important role in ATP synthesis and energy transfer processes during early development [46].

In addition, yield components and yields of forage grasses improved with N and P fertilization, particularly under HN conditions (Figs. S2–S4). This is likely because enhanced N is highly demanded by plants to improve carbon remobilization [47, 48] and starch accumulation [49] in reproductive stage. In fact, N has a stronger influence on reproduction stage by influencing leaf photosynthetic capacity [50], tiller formation [51] and spikelet differentiation [52] and grain production [53].

Species-specific responses were evident through significant A × N and A × P interactions on all traits (P < 0.05), highlighting varied nutrient requirements among grasses (Table S7). For example, responses varied: P. pratense and P. smithii favored low N–low P input, while Festuca arundinacea and Dactylis glomerata responded better to moderate to high nutrient levels (Figs. 2, S2–S4). These differences are likely related to species-specific nutrient acquisition strategies and root system architecture. For instance, shallow-rooted species like P. pratense may perform better under low nutrient availability by avoiding subsoil N and P fixation [54]. In contrast, species with deeper or fibrous roots such as F. arundinacea may access recalcitrant N and P pools more effectively, particularly in moderately fertilized soils [55, 56].

N:P fertilization ratio decides seed germination and yield production

Excessive application of N (~ 210 kg N ha⁻¹) and P (~ 90 kg P2O5 ha⁻¹) negatively impacted seed yield, yield components, germination index, vitality index, and fertile tiller number (Figs. 1 and 5). This decline in performance may stem from the accumulation of inorganic ions, leading to osmotic stress and ion toxicity during seedling and reproductive growth [57, 58]. Excessive N and P can also be toxic by inhibiting the uptake of essential cations such as Ca2+, Mg2+, and K+ [59]. Furthermore, N and P overaccumulation may lead to poor nutrient utilization efficiency [60], environmental pollution [61], and increased risk of lodging [62], all of which compromise seed germination and yield production.

Similarly, seedling growth was inhibited when N: P ratios exceeded 2, while yield and yield components were negatively affected when the ratio fell below 3 (Figs. 1 and 5). This pattern supports the concept of threshold-dependent nutrient interactions [63], and this is possibly because the acquisition and utilization of one nutrient is dependent on the availability of another, implying that synergistic or antagonistic interactions between N and P are common and dependent on their ratio [64].

Our study suggests that an optimal N: P ratio of approximately 2.86:1 can enhance yield production. Comparable findings have been reported for an optimal N: P ratio of 3:1 for winter rapeseed [65]. This may be attributed to improved overall plant growth by optimizing and effective N and P absorption in roots, shoots, and seeds [66, 67]. In fact, The HN + LP treatment resulted in superior nutrient uptake and seed yield in P. juncea, P. pratense, B. inermis, and P. smithii (Fig. S5). While the optimal N: P ratio for improving seed germination is 2:1. Similar results that N and P fertilization with 2:1 ratio increased fertilizer use efficiency of wheat [68]. The possible reason could be that seed germination process requires less N than the reproductive stage [69]. While an optimal stoichiometric N: P fertilization ratio has been shown to improve seed germination and yield components, future research should incorporate the effects of rainfall variability and soil heterogeneity to further validate and refine these conclusions.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that optimizing N and P fertilization ratios is crucial for improving seed germination, early seedling development, and yield in forage grasses grown on saline–alkali soils. Phosphorus played a dominant role in promoting early seedling vigor, while N significantly enhanced reproductive growth and yield components. Species exhibited distinct nutrient responses related to root architecture and nutrient acquisition strategies. Optimal N: P ratios were identified at approximately 2:1 for seed germination and early growth, and 2.86:1 for yield maximization. Excessive nutrient application or imbalance led to physiological inhibition and reduced productivity. These findings support the implementation of balanced N and P fertilization to improve nutrient use efficiency and forage production in saline–alkali environments. Future research should integrate the effects of rainfall variability and soil heterogeneity to develop more resilient nutrient management practices. Finally, our experimental design and analytical approach provide a valuable framework for determining optimal N and P rates and exploring other agronomic factors in diverse agroecosystems.
