Early detection of cerebral palsy through context‐driven implementation in low‐ and middle‐income countries: From impossible to achievable
Authors: Joanne M George
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Short abstract This commentary is on the original article by Legas et al. on pages 1432–1439 of this issue.

Africa is the world's second‐largest continent, home to approximately 1.2 billion people. Notably, half of its population is under 18 years of age, and projections indicate that by 2050 more than 40% of the global child population will reside in Africa. 1 The prevalence of cerebral palsy (CP) is higher in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs) than in high‐income countries, with a disproportionately greater number of children presenting with more severe forms of CP in resource‐constrained settings. 2 Of the estimated 8.1 million children under 5 years living with CP, an overwhelming 98% reside in LMICs. 3 Early detection of CP enables timely referral to targeted early interventions that aim to improve outcomes for children and families, and has increasingly become the standard of care in high‐income settings following publication of international clinical practice guidelines. Although implementation challenges exist universally, the prospect of establishing early detection pathways in contexts facing both exceptional need and limited resources can appear insurmountable.

Legas et al. apply implementation science methodology using an evidence‐based, structured, Knowledge‐to‐Action framework. 4 Through systematic adaptation to the local context and iterative cycles of barrier identification and strategy refinement, they demonstrate substantial progress. In Ethiopia—Africa's second‐most populous nation, with more than 130 million people and over 80 spoken languages—the authors achieved a remarkable reduction in the mean age of CP identification from 5.6 years to under 1 year of age within their tertiary care setting. Although the technical components of this achievement are clearly articulated, the human elements remain unstated. Such progress requires courage, persistence, creativity, and sustained commitment. As Nelson Mandela aptly stated, ‘It always seems impossible until it's done.’ The insights shared from their implementation journey offer valuable guidance for accelerating early detection initiatives across both well‐resourced and resource‐limited healthcare systems.

A notable strength of their approach is the explicit emphasis on clinical reasoning as central to diagnostic formulation. Over‐reliance on assessment tools as prescriptive diagnostic algorithms risks overlooking crucial clinical information. While the Hammersmith Infant Neurological Examination (HINE) was selected and implemented for its brevity, feasibility, and low cost, the authors emphasized that HINE scores were interpreted within a broader clinical context. For instance, careful documentation of asymmetries enhanced diagnostic accuracy among children whose global HINE scores exceeded typical CP‐associated thresholds, enabling identification of unilateral CP. Experience as a HINE trainer affirms that clinicians frequently afford too much weight to the HINE score, and an important part of any HINE training is emphasizing both the value, and also the limitations, of a HINE score. The pattern of findings on the HINE will always be more important than the score.

Experts in early autism identification advocate that, in low‐resource settings, scalability and sustainability must be prioritized from the outset of any early detection implementation initiative. 5 Key components include training and supervision structures, ongoing monitoring and evaluation, and context‐driven program adaptation. Legas et al. address these principles effectively by embedding HINE training and CP early identification content into existing medical education pathways, thereby strengthening both the reach and long‐term impact of their implementation efforts.

The message is unequivocal: early detection of CP is achievable in every setting when implementation is grounded in evidence, adapted to the local context, and supported by sustainable training pathways.
