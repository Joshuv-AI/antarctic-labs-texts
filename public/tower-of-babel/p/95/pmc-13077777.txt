When Selectivity Increases Uncertainty: Context‐Dependent Pharmacology in Antibody Drug Development
Authors: Xiaozhi Liao, Yanguang Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAs antibody therapeutics become increasingly selective and multi‐specific, their pharmacological effects depend more strongly on biological context, creating new uncertainties in drug development. In this perspective, we discuss how context‐dependent pharmacology complicates preclinical‐to‐clinical translation, dose optimization, and rational combination therapy selection for antibody drugs. Recognizing context dependence is essential for realizing the full therapeutic potential of highly selective biologics and for advancing a more predictive, mechanism‐informed paradigm in antibody drug development.

Introduction

The evolution of antibody‐based therapies toward high selectivity promises improved targeting of disease‐relevant pathways and reduced off‐target toxicity. However, this growing precision introduces a new challenge: drug effects become increasingly dependent on biological context. This context dependence creates significant development uncertainty due to highly variable pharmacological responses across preclinical and clinical settings.

In this perspective, we examine how context‐dependent pharmacology of antibody therapeutics creates challenges in drug development, particularly in preclinical‐to‐clinical translation, dose optimization, and combination therapy selection. We highlight the importance of context‐aware in vitro, in silico, and clinical approaches for integrating biological context into the decision‐making processes. We explore how these strategies, including quantitative systems pharmacology (QSP), organ‐on‐a‐chip, adaptive trial designs, and biomarker‐driven stratification, help to mitigate these challenges. Overall, this perspective encourages developers to recognize the hidden development costs of increased selectivity and consider these approaches to overcome these challenges.

Selectivity and Context‐Dependent Pharmacology

Antibody therapeutics have transformed the treatment of oncology and autoimmune disease by enabling highly selective, often multi‐specific, targeting of disease‐relevant antigens. Achieving high selectivity is a central design goal, as it improves efficacy while minimizing off‐target effects [1]. However, as drug designs become more selective, drug behavior becomes increasingly governed by context‐dependent pharmacology, where pharmacological effect is highly dependent on the surrounding biological and pathological factors. While selectivity narrows the range of molecular interactions, it simultaneously amplifies the drug's dependency on contextual factors such as target expression levels, immune cell composition, and tissue microenvironment features [2, 3].

While chemotherapies act broadly with relatively uniform cytotoxic effects, selective antibodies rely on a coordinated interplay of multiple factors, making pharmacological outcomes more divergent. This increased uncertainty across preclinical and clinical settings creates significant challenges in drug development, particularly in preclinical‐to‐clinical translation, dose optimization, and combination therapy selection. To fully realize the potential of antibody therapeutics, it is essential to understand the context‐dependent pharmacology and implement advanced, context‐aware strategies to navigate the uncertainty.

Translational Gap in Preclinical Development

One primary consequence of context‐dependent pharmacology is the limited predictive value of traditional preclinical models for clinical efficacy and safety. This difficulty primarily arises from interspecies and inter‐system differences in the biological context that governs drug activity. Because the context of an animal model or a simplified in vitro system does not mirror the complex and dynamic environment of a human patient, preclinical findings often cannot accurately represent clinical outcomes [4].

To bridge this translational gap, organ‐on‐a‐chip technologies have emerged as a next‐generation experimental platform that attempts to reconstruct the in vivo physiological context to evaluate pharmacological outcomes more realistically [5]. Furthermore, QSP models provide a mechanistic framework where functional characteristics derived from experimental assays can be embedded within physiologically grounded model structures. By mechanistically linking drug exposure to downstream biological processes, QSP allows for the evaluation of preclinical findings conditional on clinically relevant contexts [6]. This capability enables the extrapolation of pharmacological outcomes across species and scales, transforming preclinical observations into clinical predictions.

This translational gap is particularly pronounced for bispecific T cell engagers (bsTCEs) [4], which represent an extreme form of context‐dependent pharmacology where the pharmacological activity relies on a coordinated interplay between the effector (immune) side and the target (disease) side. As illustrated in Figure 2, while traditional chemotherapeutic agents typically follow a relatively direct sequence from dose to response, bsTCEs require a cascade of mechanistic events, including dual target engagement, immune synapse formation, and T‐cell activation to elicit a clinical response. This exemplifies how context‐dependent pharmacology generates translational variability that cannot be captured by conventional preclinical approaches alone. Due to this multi‐factorial complexity, the advantage of QSP modeling is especially evident in bsTCEs. Through mechanistic representation of cell–cell interactions and the dynamic formation of immune synapses, QSP models can integrate clinically relevant contexts to predict pharmacological outcomes where empirical models fail.

The Complexity of Dose Optimization

Another challenge derived from context‐dependent pharmacology is dose optimization. Moving beyond the traditional maximum tolerated dose, Project Optimus reflects a regulatory shift toward finding an optimal safe dose that maximizes the benefit–risk profile [7]. For highly selective antibodies, the optimal safe dose is rarely a universal constant; it varies significantly across patients due to their diverse biological contexts. This context‐driven uncertainty makes identifying a single optimal dose for a broad population difficult and risks either sub‐therapeutic dosing or unnecessary toxicity.

QSP models address this challenge by incorporating patient heterogeneity into the mechanistic framework. By simulating heterogeneous clinical outcomes in virtual patient populations that reflect real‐world patient diversity, QSP models enable a systematic, cost‐effective exploration of alternative dosing regimens in silico to identify optimal doses conditional on specific biological contexts rather than relying on empirical population averages.

In clinical development, adaptive clinical trial designs provide the operational flexibility to navigate context‐driven uncertainty in real‐time. By using Bayesian frameworks, adaptive trials can learn from the observed outcomes of the first few patients to adjust the dosing strategy for subsequent participants [8]. This dynamic optimization process allows the study to converge on the ideal dose for specific subgroups, effectively managing the unpredictable variability inherent in highly selective modalities.

These examples illustrate how context‐driven heterogeneity in treatment response necessitates in silico simulations and flexible trial designs that can learn, predict, and adapt optimal dosing regimens in real time rather than assuming a uniform optimal dose across patients.

The Challenge of Combination Therapy Selection

A further challenge in developing highly selective antibodies driven by context‐dependent pharmacology is the rational selection of combination therapies. Many of these antibodies are designed to target specific nodes within a multi‐step biological process, such as the cytokine cascades in autoimmune diseases or checkpoint inhibition in oncology. While simultaneously targeting multiple nodes seems logical, it does not always yield additive benefits due to intricate immune crosstalk and feedback loops. Furthermore, identifying the specific biological context where a combination adds value is crucial. Otherwise, researchers may risk applying combination regimens to patient subgroups whose disease is driven by a single dominant node, thereby increasing treatment burden without enhancing efficacy [9].

QSP models address this complexity by mapping the entire biological network rather than focusing on isolated nodes. By simulating the crosstalk between different pathways, QSP helps to identify the ideal combinations with additive benefits, enabling a model‐informed selection process rather than an empirical trial‐and‐error approach.

Biomarker‐driven stratification helps to identify patients who truly require a multi‐targeted approach. A landmark example is the combination of nivolumab (anti‐PD‐1) and ipilimumab (anti‐CTLA‐4) in advanced melanoma. Data from the CheckMate 067 trial illustrates how biological context governs the necessity of dual‐pathway inhibition. In patients with low PD‐L1 expression or BRAF mutation, the combination provides a substantial survival advantage over monotherapy [10]. By stratifying patients based on these biomarkers, researchers can navigate the therapeutic uncertainty to ensure that deal combination regimens are administered only to patients who require them. This example illustrates how biological context governs the need for combination therapy, reinforcing the importance of understanding context‐dependent pharmacology to develop more precise and effective treatment regimens.

Toward a Context‐Aware Era of Antibody Drug Development

As antibody therapeutics, and targeted therapies more broadly, continue to evolve toward increasingly selective and multi‐specific design, context‐dependent pharmacology will become a defining feature rather than an exception. In drug development, the growing dependence on biological context challenges traditional empirical development paradigms, particularly in preclinical‐to‐clinical translation, dose optimization, and combination therapy selection. However, this shift also creates an opportunity for translational strategies that account for biological complexity and inherent variability of human systems.

The integration of QSP modeling, organ‐on‐a‐chip, adaptive trial designs, and biomarker‐driven stratification provides a roadmap for this evolution. Collectively, these approaches transform the antibody drug development from a series of empirical guesses into a mechanistic alignment of the drug's pharmacological context.

While QSP modeling offers transformative potential for addressing context‐dependent pharmacology, its implementation faces the hurdle of overparameterization. This challenge motivates iterative modeling strategies, where QSP models are initially built on well‐characterized core mechanisms and progressively refined as new experimental data become available, ensuring that added complexity is always empirically justified. Recognizing this limitation, it is worth noting that not all challenges in antibody drug development demand this level of complexity. Target‐mediated drug disposition (TMDD) or physiologically‐based pharmacokinetics (PBPK) models suffice when the focus is drug exposure and clearance rather than efficacy, and empirical pharmacokinetics/pharmacodynamics (PK/PD) models remain practical when informative biomarkers are available to account for biological variability as covariates.
