Enantioselective Determination of Carnitine Enantiomers in Food and Supplement Samples by Chiral Liquid Chromatography-Tandem Mass Spectrometry
Authors: Aogu Furusho, Yutaro Nakayama, Hiroki Mizuno, Eiji Sugiyama, Kenji Kojima, Kenichiro Todoroki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Carnitine (CAR) is an essential compound for animals and plays several physiological roles related to energy production. These functions are possessed by only the l-forms, while the d-forms are known to inhibit the uptake of the l-forms, thus causing a CAR deficiency. Therefore, it is required to determine dl-CAR in various samples such as foods, supplements, and drugs by an enantioselective analytical method. In this study, we developed a chiral liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) method using derivatization. The CAR enantiomers derivatized with 4-nitrophenylhydrazine were successfully separated on a CHIRALPAK ZWIX (−) column (resolution = 1.64) and detected with high sensitivity and selectivity by MS/MS. The developed method was well validated and applied to the analysis of eight kinds of foods and an l-CAR supplement. Only the l-forms were found in all the tested food samples and were abundant in meat, milk, and yogurt. The system was also applicable for a supplement sample and could detect the d-CAR contamination at the 2.5% level, suggesting its potential for use in the purity test of CAR preparations.

1. INTRODUCTION

Carnitine (CAR) is an intrinsic compound synthesized from lysine and methionine and is present in almost all human cells. Since CAR has one asymmetric carbon atom within the molecule, a pair of enantiomers exists, that is, the d-form and the l-form. The major form, l-CAR, transports long-chain fatty acids into the mitochondria and promotes energy production via the beta-oxidation of lipids.1,2) This function is considered to help with weight loss and improving physical functions, and l-CAR supplements are commercially available. As a pharmaceutical, levocarnitine (l-form) is used for the therapy of the CAR deficiency. In contrast, d-CAR has not been reported to have these functions and is known to disturb the absorption of the l-forms.3) Though the extent to which the d-form inhibits the l-form absorption has not been clarified in detail, the European and United States Pharmacopoeia have set the limitation of the d-form contamination in CAR preparations.4,5) Thus, an analytical method, which can distinguish CAR enantiomers, is required for quality control of the l-CAR formulation and drug substance.

Although CAR can be synthesized in our bodies, its shortfall must be obtained from foods.2) Animal-based foods, such as meat and milk, are known to abundantly contain CAR, though their enantioselective analyses have barely been performed. The determination of dl-CAR contents in food samples would be useful for the efficient intake of l-CAR and prevention of CAR deficiency induced by the d-forms.

So far, various methods have been developed for the enantioselective analysis of CAR.6–9) Liquid chromatography (LC) was mainly employed, with two approaches adopted: diastereomeric separation on an achiral column and direct separation on a chiral column. For the indirect method, the chiral derivatization reagents, such as (+)-1-(9-fluorenyl)-ethyl chloroformate10) and l-alanine-2-naphthylamide,11,12) were used to convert the CAR enantiomers to diastereomers, and the derivatives were separated on a conventional reversed-phase column. The direct separation of CAR enantiomers was conducted using an ovomucoid-conjugated column,13) a polysaccharide-based column,14) and a teicoplanin-bonded column.15) In these methods, the derivatized CAR enantiomers were detected by their UV absorption or fluorescence, and the optical purities of the drug substances were evaluated. However, quantitative values are sometimes affected by the optical purities of the derivatization reagents in the indirect methods, making it difficult to precisely determine trace amounts of the d-forms. Furthermore, UV and fluorescence detection have some issues, such as relatively low sensitivity and insufficient selectivity to identify CAR from other substances that were not separated by the LC column. For these reasons, enantioselective analyses of CAR in food or animal samples have barely been reported, and a highly sensitive and selective analytical method is needed to evaluate these samples.

In this study, a chiral LC-tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) method has been developed for the determination of CAR enantiomers in various samples. Because CAR has a quaternary ammonium cation, it is ionized by electrospray ionization (ESI) with a high efficiency, enabling sensitive detection by MS/MS. The derivatization of CAR was performed before the LC-MS/MS analysis to improve the chiral separation. The developed method was validated and applied to the analysis of food samples and CAR supplements.

2. EXPERIMENTAL

2.1. Materials

l-CAR, dl-CAR hydrochloride, 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (DNPH), LC-MS grade formic acid (FA), hydrochloric acid, and pyridine were purchased from FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation (Osaka, Japan). o-Benzylhydroxylamine (o-BHA), 2,2'-dipyridyl disulfide, 1-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-3-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC), 2-nitrophenylhydrazine (2-NPH), 3-NPH hydrochloride, 4-NPH, 4-nitro-7-piperazino-2,1,3-benzoxadiazole (NBD-PZ), and 2-picolylamine (2-PA) were the products of Tokyo Chemical Industry Corporation (Tokyo, Japan). LC-MS grade ammonium formate (NH4FA) was obtained from SERVA Electrophoresis GmbH (Heidelberg, Germany). Triphenylphosphine and LC-MS grade methanol (MeOH) were purchased from Kanto Chemical Co. (Tokyo, Japan). LC-MS grade acetonitrile (MeCN) was obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Water was purified using a PURELAB flex ultrapure water purification system (ELGA LabWater, High Wycombe, UK).

2.2. Sample pretreatment

2.2.1. Food samples

Ground beef, ground pork, ground chicken (all meats from Japan), milk, yogurt, cheddar cheese, lactic acid bacteria drink (Yakult), soy sauce, and miso were obtained at a local grocery store. Five grams each of the solid foods (meat, yogurt, cheese, and miso) were added to 20 mL of water and homogenized by an ultrasonicator. The homogenate was centrifuged at 2600 × g for 10 min, and an aliquot of the supernatant (50 μL) was added to 950 μL of MeOH. After the centrifugation at 7500 × g for 10 min at 4°C, the supernatant was collected and stored at −80°C. The liquid samples (milk, Yakult, and soy sauce) were diluted 20-fold with MeOH, and the centrifuged supernatant was stored at −80°C in the same manner as the solid food samples. Before derivatization, 100 μL of the MeOH extracts of the meat samples and 200 μL of those of the other food samples were dried under reduced pressure, then the residues were thoroughly dissolved in 20 μL of 50% MeCN.

2.2.2. Supplement

The CAR supplement was purchased at a drug store. The powder in three capsules (about 1000 mg) was added to 10 mL of water and thoroughly vortexed for 2 min. The suspension was centrifuged at 2500 × g for 15 min, and the supernatant was transferred to another tube. The residue was again suspended in 10 mL of water, and the supernatant after centrifugation was mixed with the first solution. This process was repeated three more times, and the supernatant (total of 50 mL) was diluted 1000 times with 50% MeCN. For the spiking test, the standard d-CAR equivalent to 0.5%, 1.0%, 2.5%, and 5.0% of l-CAR in the supplement was added to the supernatant by mixing with the dl-CAR solution.

2.3. Derivatization

For investigation of the derivatization reagents, standard dl-CAR samples were reacted with NPH analogs (2-NPH, 3-NPH, 4-NPH, and 2,4-DNPH), 2-PA, NBD-PZ, and o-BHA. The derivatization procedures for these reagents are summarized in Supplemental Information (Method S1 and Table S1). As for the food and supplement samples, 20 μL of the 50% MeCN extract was mixed with 20 μL of 100 μM dl-d9-CAR in 50% MeCN, 20 μL of 120 mM EDC/6% pyridine in 50% MeCN, and 20 μL of 100 mM 4-NPH in MeCN. The mixture was heated at 60°C for 30 min and diluted with 720 μL of 25 mM FA/NH4FA in H2O/MeOH/MeCN = 2/24/74. The solution was filtered using a 0.45 μm filter (Millex Syringe-Driven Filter Unit 0.45 μm; Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), and 5 μL of the solution was injected into the LC-MS/MS described in Section 2.4.

2.4. LC-MS conditions

LC-MS/MS analyses were performed by an ultra-performance liquid chromatographic system (ACQUITY H-class; Waters, Milford, MA, USA) connected to a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Xevo TQ-Smicro; Waters). For the enantiomer separation, a CHIRALPAK ZWIX (−) column (3.0 mm I.D. × 250 mm, 3 μm; Daicel Corporation, Osaka, Japan) was used in a NANOSPACE SI-2 3014 column oven (Shiseido, Tokyo, Japan) maintained at 25°C. The mobile phase was an isocratic solution of a H2O/MeOH/MeCN mixture (2/24/74, v/v) containing 25 mM FA/NH4FA at the flow rate of 0.15 mL/min. The detection conditions of the 4-NPH-derivatized CAR in MS/MS were as follows: 1.0 kV capillary voltage for ESI-positive ionization; 40 V cone voltage; 50 L/hr cone gas flow rate; 1000 L/hr desolvation gas flow rate; 150°C source temperature; 500°C desolvation temperature; 30 eV collision energy. The selected reaction monitoring (SRM) transitions were m/z 297.2 > 102.0 for quantification, 297.2 > 145.1 for qualification, and 306.2 > 102.1 for d9-CAR. The transitions for CAR non-derivatized and derivatized with the other reagents are summarized in Table S2. Data processing was performed using MassLynx v4.2 software (Waters).

2.5. Method validation

The calibration curves were constructed using a linear least-squares and 1/x2-weighted regression. The standard dl-CAR solutions (1, 5, 10, 50, 100, and 200 μM for each enantiomer) were derivatized with 4-NPH using the procedure described in Section 2.3., and 5 μL of the solution was measured by LC-MS/MS (n = 5). The injection amounts of the derivatized CAR were 0.125, 0.625, 1.25, 6.25, 12.5, and 25 pmol for each enantiomer. The IS ratio (CAR/d9-CAR), calculated with the peak area, was plotted versus the nominal concentration.

The intra- and inter-day precisions were evaluated using the l-CAR contents in the meat, milk, and supplement samples. The pooled meat sample was made by mixing equal amounts of the beef, pork, and chicken meat extracts, and the pooled milk sample was from four commercial products. The samples were measured five times per day (intra-day) and once per day for 5 days (inter-day). The relative standard deviation (RSD) values of the IS ratio of l-CAR were used for the evaluation.

The accuracy was evaluated by the analytical recovery using the standard-spiked meat, milk, and supplement samples. The residues of the meat and milk samples after evaporation were dissolved in water or in 100- or 200-μM dl-CAR solution. The water extract of the l-CAR supplement was diluted 500 times with 50% MeCN and mixed with an equal volume of water or in 200- or 400-μM dl-CAR solution. The recovery was calculated using the IS ratio with the following formula: (spiked sample – original sample)/standard sample × 100.

The matrix effects were tested by mixing the derivatized standard solutions with the food and supplement samples as the matrix. The dried residues of the meat and milk samples were dissolved in 60 μL of 50% MeCN and 20 μL of MeCN, and the 500-times diluted supplement extract (10 μL) was mixed with 50 μL of 50% MeCN and 20 μL of MeCN. These solutions were heated at 60°C for 30 min and dried under reduced pressure. The obtained residue was dissolved in 800 μL of the solution prepared by derivatizing the standard solutions of 200 μM. The matrix factor (MF) was determined as the ratio of the peak area of the 4-NPH-CAR enantiomers in the standard samples to those in the matrix samples. The IS-normalized MFs (ISMFs) were also determined using the IS ratio.

3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

3.1. Selection of the derivatization reagents

A CHIRALPAK ZWIX column was selected for the enantiomer separation in the present study. Its chiral selector has zwitterionic character, a quinoline skeleton, and an amide structure and is known to be effective for the chiral separation of zwitterionic compounds such as amino acids.16) Because CAR enantiomers possess an ammonium cation and a carboxylic group, the ZWIX column was considered suitable for the separation of the dl-CAR.

The CAR enantiomers were measured using the mobile phase of the H2O/MeOH mixture (2/98, v/v) containing 25 mM of FA and NH4FA, which was the first choice for the ZWIX column. As shown in Fig. 1, the intact d- and l-CAR eluted almost at the same time and were not separated. Several other mobile phases consisting of various ratios of H2O, MeOH, and MeCN could not separate the enantiomers (data not shown). Thus, the chemical modification with the derivatization reagents was investigated to alter the interaction between the CAR and the chiral selector. The structures of the tested reagents are shown in Fig. 2. All reagents reacted with the carboxylic group of the CAR and have been used for the LC analysis of organic compounds with fluorescence detection and MS detection in previous studies.17–20) The obtained chromatograms are shown in Fig. 1. All the derivatives were selectively detected by the MS/MS. The difference in intensity between the reagents was likely due to their derivatization procedures, reaction rates, and properties of the product ions used for the detection. Especially for the NPH analogs, the intact CAR was detected even after derivatization, suggesting that the low reaction rate caused the low peak intensity. Among the reagents, 3-NPH and 4-NPH achieved the enantiomer separation, and the l-forms eluted earlier than the d-forms. Both derivatives produce the characteristic fragment ions of m/z 102 and 145, which enabled the selective detection of the CAR using the SRM mode. However, their enantiomer separations were not completely achieved (the resolution values of 1.16 and 1.08, respectively), and the improvement of the LC conditions was needed to apply the method to actual samples.

Notably, 2-NPH and 2,4-DNPH were not suitable for the enantiomer separation of CAR. The ZWIX column possesses several interactions with analytes, that is, hydrophilic interaction, hydrogen bond, and π–π interaction. The enantiomeric separation is considered to be accomplished by the appropriate balance of these interactions. When a nitro group is bonded in the ortho-position relative to the hydrazine group, the degree of the interactions may become comparable between the enantiomers, making it impossible to separate the enantiomers. Further experimental and computational chemical investigations are necessary to clarify the retention and separation mechanisms.

The present research demonstrated that the enantiomer separation could be improved by the derivatization. To date, there have been very few studies on the effect of derivatization on chiral separation. Tagging compounds is also useful for controlling the retention time and improving the selectivity and sensitivity of the method. This concept of derivatization plus chiral separation is likely to contribute to the development of enantioselective methods for other compounds.

3.2. Optimization of the analytical conditions and derivatization

Among the LC conditions, the mixing ratios of H2O/MeOH/MeCN in the mobile phases were optimized. Figure 3 shows the chromatograms obtained by measuring the 4-NPH-CAR enantiomers using several mobile phases, and the CAR enantiomers were separated by all the tested mobile phases. Increasing the proportion of H2O in the mobile phases shortened the retention time and worsened the separation. Equal volumes of MeOH and MeCN in a mobile phase (49/49, v/v) gave the shortest retention time. The ZWIX column is a multi-interaction column with ionic interaction and π–π interaction, and the mobile phase containing equal volumes of MeOH and MeCN may weaken these interactions the most. The enantiomer separation of 4-NPH-CAR became better along with the increase in the MeCN ratio in the mobile phases. Because the separation factors did not significantly change under MeCN-rich conditions, the improved peak shape mainly contributed to the higher resolution values. The 3-NPH-CAR also showed similar elution properties (Fig. S1). Table 1 summarizes the separation factors and resolution values of 3-NPH-dl-CAR and 4-NPH-dl-CAR. The best separation was obtained with the H2O/MeOH/MeCN mixture in a 2/24/74 ratio for both derivatization reagents (Rs = 1.33 for 3-NPH and Rs = 1.35 for 4-NPH).

The flow rate was also investigated in the range of 0.1–0.4 mL/min to achieve a better separation with the resolution value higher than 1.5. The lower flow rate yielded the longer retention time and the better Rs, and the practical separation (Rs = 1.64) was obtained at 0.15 mL/min for about 30 min.

Under the final conditions, no characteristic compounds, including an unreacted reagent and its by-products, were observed at the retention time of the d-CAR by MS full-scan detection. In addition, the peak areas of d- and l-CAR were almost equivalent using the racemic standard, suggesting that the ion suppression by interferences did not occur.

Regarding the MS conditions, the capillary voltage was tested at 1.0 and 3.0 kV. The 4-NPH-CAR solution was measured using the optimized LC conditions, and the averaged peak intensity of three trials was 1.05 × 106 at 1.0 kV and 5.72 × 105 at 3.0 kV. This result indicated that the capillary voltage at 1.0 kV was suitable for the 4-NPH-CAR detection under the optimized LC conditions.

The derivatization procedure for 4-NPH was optimized in terms of the reaction temperature and reaction time. The reaction temperature was tested at 40°C, 60°C, and 80°C. The higher temperature provided a higher peak area (l + d), but the reaction solvent was observed to volatilize at 80°C, making it difficult to conduct a reproducible analysis. Regarding the reaction time, five reaction periods, 10, 20, 30, 60, and 90 min, were investigated at 60°C. The peak area (l + d) significantly increased from the 10-min reaction to the 20-min reaction and remained constant for reaction periods exceeding 20 min. Based on these results, the reaction condition was set at 60°C for 30 min.

For the precise determination of minor enantiomers, it is desirable that the d-forms elute earlier than the l-forms. A ZWIX (+) column, which is the opposite type column of the ZWIX (−) column, was tested to obtain the ideal separation pattern. Although the elution order of the enantiomers was successfully reversed, the separation deteriorated (Fig. S2). Since the chiral selector of the (+) column is a diastereomer of those of the (−) column, it was thought that the retention properties of the two columns were not completely reversed, resulting in the poor separation.

The enantiomer separation of CAR has already been achieved with several chiral columns, such as an ovomucoid-conjugated column,13) a polysaccharide-based column,14) and a teicoplanin-bonded column.15) These chiral columns could separate CAR enantiomers with resolution values higher than 1.5. Although the resolution obtained in the present study was slightly lower than those in the previous ones, the developed method can sufficiently separate 4-NPH-CAR enantiomers using a ZWIX column.

3.3. Method validation

The developed method was validated using a standard CAR solution, meat, milk, and supplement samples. A pooled meat sample prepared by mixing beef, pork, and chicken was used as a representative solid sample, while a pooled milk sample made from four products was used as the representative liquid sample.

Calibration curves were constructed using the standard CAR solution in the range of 1–200 μM for both d- and l-CAR. The obtained equations were y = 0.0187x + 0.0008 (r² = 0.999) for d-CAR and y = 0.0191x + 0.0009 (r² = 0.999) for l-CAR. Both calibration curves showed good linearity, and RSD values (n = 5) for all points of the six concentrations were 0.94%–3.28%. The lowest point of the calibration curves (1 μM) was adopted as the lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) for both enantiomers. The LLOQ of the reported methods were 6.2 μM–1.2 mM,9,12–14) and the developed method achieved an equivalent or lower LLOQ, thus demonstrating a high sensitivity.

Precision of the method was evaluated by the intra- and inter-day assays of the meat, milk, and supplement samples. Because the d-forms were not observed in all the samples, the amounts of l-CAR were used for the evaluation. As shown in Table 2, the RSD values for all the samples (n = 5) were less than 5% for the intra-day precision and 15% for the inter-day precision, indicating that the developed method has good precision. The sample after derivatization remained stable for at least 1 week in a refrigerator at 4°C (approximately 93% remained).

For accuracy, the recovery rate was measured by adding two concentrations of a standard dl-CAR solution to the food and supplement samples. The accuracies (%) were 95.7–104.7 for l-CAR and 89.6–99.9 for d-CAR, which were in an acceptable range for quantification (Table 2).

Lipids and other compounds may have co-eluted and enhanced the ionization of the d-CAR. On the other hand, the ISMFs remained within the acceptable range from 98.9% to 102.6%.

3.4. Determination of CAR enantiomers in food samples

The validated system was applied to the determination of the CAR enantiomers in the food samples. CAR is known to be contained in animal-source foods, especially in edible meats.21–23) Therefore, ground meat, milk, yogurt, cheese, and Yakult were selected as the subjects for analysis. As for the milk and yogurt samples, four products from different suppliers were analyzed to investigate the difference between products. In addition, two rice koji-fermented foods (soy sauce and miso) were measured because a recent report found that a relatively high amount of CAR, likely almost l-isomers, was contained in Aspergillus oryzae-fermented foods.24,25)

CAR was extracted from the solid food samples with H2O and the supernatant and liquid samples were deproteinized with MeOH. An aliquot of the MeOH supernatant was dried, and the resulting residue was dissolved in a 50% MeCN solution for derivatization and LC-MS/MS analysis. The H2O extraction was employed in the present study to avoid racemization and production of CAR from acylcarnitine under severe conditions. The extraction procedures, repeated three times, demonstrated good reproducibility for all the samples (RSD < 10%). The chromatogram obtained from the beef sample is shown in Fig. 4 along with the results of the standard CAR and IS. A single peak was observed around 28 min, which was estimated to be l-CAR. In all the tested food samples, l-CAR was found, while the d-forms were not detected. Thus, the racemization and the production of d-CAR by bacteria are not expected to occur in the tested food samples.

The quantified values of l-CAR are summarized in Table 3. The limit of detection values for d-CAR were 0.04 mg/100 g for meat, 0.02 mg/100 g for the other solid foods, and 0.01 mg/100 mL for the liquid foods, and the LLOQs were 0.52 mg/100 g, 0.26 mg/100 g, and 0.06 mg/100 mL, respectively. The levels of l-CAR in the meat samples were highest in beef, followed by pork, and then chicken. As for the dairy products, milk and yogurt contained relatively high amounts of l-CAR, which would be secreted into milk from the cow’s body. No differences in contents were observed between the products for both milk and yogurt. Soy sauce and miso, which are plant-based foods, also contained small amounts of l-CAR. To date, CAR in food samples has been measured by achiral methods which cannot distinguish enantiomers. The reported amounts of CAR in beef, pork, and chicken were 87.5, 21.1, and 10.4 mg/100 g, respectively.26) Although quantitative values obtained in the present study (45.6, 12.8, and 3.8 mg/100 g, respectively) were lower than those in the literature, the relative order of the CAR contents was consistent with previous research. CAR is known to be mainly stored in muscle,27) and its level in the body is influenced by various factors such as age, breed, and body part.27–29) The differences in these factors may have contributed to the low CAR contents observed in the present study.

CAR in milk and yogurt has been reported to be 1.2–2.8 mg/100 mL and 1.9–2.6 mg/100 g, respectively.30–32) The levels in the present study were 1.1–1.3 mg/100 mL for milk and 1.1–1.2 mg/100 g for yogurt and were consistent with the results reported in previous studies, demonstrating that the developed method is applicable for food analysis. According to the nutrition labeling of milk and yogurt products, little variation was observed between the products in components such as protein and fat. This may be caused by the manufacturing method, and therefore, it is considered that the CAR contents between products also became equivalent.

A spike test was conducted to evaluate the ability of the method to detect d-CAR in real matrices. The dried residue of the MeOH supernatant of the pooled meat or milk sample was dissolved in a 5-μM d-CAR solution. After derivatization, the reaction mixtures were analyzed by the developed LC-MS/MS. A small peak of d-CAR was observed for both samples (Fig. S3).

3.5. Evaluation of CAR enantiomer contents in a supplement

The commercially available CAR supplement was analyzed by the developed method. It was labeled to contain 500 mg of l-CAR in three capsules, and the CAR was extracted from the three capsules with H2O. The LC-MS/MS result after derivatization is shown in Fig. 5 (0%). Only the l-forms were found in the sample, and their amount was determined to be 570.2 ± 17.9 mg/three capsules.

Fig. 5. Determination of 4-NPH-CAR in a supplement. The small chromatograms represent ×50 magnification. 4-NPH, 4-nitrophenylhydrazine; CAR, carnitine; d, dextro; l, levo.

To evaluate the ability of the present method to distinguish the CAR enantiomers, the standard dl-CAR solutions were added to the supplement samples at different levels. The spiked amounts were 0.5%, 1%, 2.5%, and 5% relative to l-CAR in the supplement, and the obtained chromatograms are shown in Fig. 5. When 2.5% and 5% amounts of the standard dl-CAR were added, the peak of the d-form was clearly observed, demonstrating that the present method can detect the contamination of 2.5% or more d-forms relative to the l-forms. The limit for the d-form contamination in the CAR preparations is set at 4% in the European Pharmacopoeia4) and the United States Pharmacopoeia.5) The developed method in the present study meets the criteria, and its application in purity testing is anticipated.

4. CONCLUSION

In the present study, an analytical method was developed for the determination of CAR enantiomers combining derivatization using 4-NPH with a chiral LC-MS/MS system. The derivatization process achieved the successful separation of dl-CAR with Rs values higher than 1.5, and the MS/MS detection with isotope-labeled CAR enabled a highly sensitive and selective analysis and precise determination. The system validation confirmed good linearity, acceptable precision and accuracy, and low matrix effects. Application of the method for food analysis revealed that l-CAR was contained in meat, dairy products, and koji-fermented foods. In contrast, d-CAR was not detected in the tested samples, suggesting that the racemization and the production of d-CAR by bacteria are unlikely to occur. The developed method was also applied to the analysis of CAR supplements and has been shown to detect the d-CAR contamination at the 2.5% level.

We demonstrated that the chiral LC separation could be changed and improved by derivatization of the analytes. This concept will also be effective for CAR-related compounds, such as acetylcarnitine and acylcarnitine, as well as various other chiral compounds. The present method is expected for further application to various foods, biological and pharmaceutical samples, and to be utilized for quality control of products and research on the biokinetics of CAR enantiomers.
