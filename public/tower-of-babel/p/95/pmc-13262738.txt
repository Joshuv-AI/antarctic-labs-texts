Benign Nodular Smooth Muscle Proliferation of the Lung Mimicking Malignancy
Authors: Tomislav Bečejac, Nikolina Tutić, Ana Mataić, Ivan Bunjevac
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe present an extremely rare and diagnostically challenging case of benign nodular smooth muscle proliferation (NSMP) in the lungs. A 41-year-old female patient presented with a cough and a right upper lung lobe lesion. Multislice computed tomography and positron emission tomography/computed tomography suggested malignancy despite relatively low metabolic activity. Fiberoptic bronchoscopy and transthoracic needle biopsy were inconclusive, so a right upper lobectomy with mediastinal lymphadenectomy was performed. Histopathology unveiled benign NSMP. This case underscores the diagnostic challenges of rare benign lung lesions mimicking malignancy and emphasizes a structured diagnostic and therapeutic approach. Clinical suspicion may arise in patients such as older adults presenting with well-defined pulmonary nodules, an inconclusive biopsy demonstrating smooth muscle proliferation, and low to moderate fluorodeoxyglucose uptake on positron emission tomography/computed tomography. When other diagnostic methods fail to provide a definitive diagnosis, surgical resection should be considered both as a diagnostic and therapeutic option. Histopathology remains the gold standard for differentiating benign from malignant lung lesions and enables definitive treatment in benign conditions, such as benign NSMP.Keywords: lung tumour, benign nodular smooth muscle proliferation, rare pulmonary lesion

INTRODUCTION

Smooth muscle tumours are classified as benign (leiomyoma, angioleiomyoma, genital-type leiomyomas), smooth muscle tumours of uncertain malignant potential (STUMP), malignant (leiomyosarcoma), and special related entities. They most commonly arise in the uterus but may occur elsewhere. Pulmonary involvement is generally sporadic, usually secondary to uterine metastases.1 Pulmonary nodular smooth muscle proliferation (NSMP) is particularly unusual, with only 2 reported cases.2 Due to the extreme rarity, knowledge regarding clinical presentation, patient and radiological characteristics, diagnostic pathways, and optimal management strategies remains limited. These lesions may mimic malignancy, complicating diagnostic and treatment decisions. Therefore, reporting additional cases is essential to improve recognition, refine the differential diagnosis, and guide management. We present a third case, initially suspected to be malignant, managed surgically, and ultimately diagnosed as a benign NSMP. This report provides clinical, radiological, and histopathological characteristics of pulmonary NSMP and proposes a structured diagnostic and therapeutic approach. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this case report and accompanying images.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 41-year-old non-smoking woman presented with a 1-week cough. Chest X-ray revealed a 3 cm lung mass in the right hemithorax. Multislice computed tomography showed a 23 × 25 × 25 mm well-circumscribed lobulated lesion in the right upper lung lobe. Positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) demonstrated a mild fluorodeoxyglucose uptake (SUVmax 3.03), and malignancy without dissemination could not be excluded (Figure 1). Fiberoptic bronchoscopy (FOB) was unremarkable and revealed no malignant elements. Transthoracic needle biopsy (TTB) showed a smooth muscle proliferation of uncertain aetiology. The multidisciplinary lung tumour board indicated a surgical biopsy. A right upper lobectomy with mediastinal lymphadenectomy was performed, as the tumour’s hilar location precluded wedge resection of the lung. An open surgical approach was selected due to a tumour hilar involvement. Postoperative recovery was uneventful. The patient was discharged from the hospital on the seventh postoperative day.

Histopathology demonstrated a 2.5 × 2.1 × 2 cm lesion with clear surgical margins, and nodular proliferation of bland spindle cells with smooth muscle differentiation (Smooth Muscle Actin-SMA+, desmin +, caldesmon +) (Figure 1). There was no atypia, necrosis, or mitotic activity. Focal entrapped alveolar spaces with scattered chronic inflammation were present, with no malignant features. Inflammatory myofibroblastic tumour (IMT) was excluded due to negative Anaplastic Lymphoma Kinase (ALK)/Receptor Tyrosine Kinase Gene 1 (ROS1) staining, lack of characteristic morphology, and a significant degree of inflammation. Benign metastasizing leiomyoma (BML) was excluded by immunohistochemistry and the absence of uterine pathology on gynaecological evaluation. All lymph nodes were negative.

At 1 and 6 months of follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic and without recurrence. The final diagnosis was benign pulmonary NSMP. No further treatment or follow-up was indicated.

DISCUSSION

Pulmonary NSMP is an extremely rare entity with only 2 prior cases described.2 Our findings are consistent with previous reports,2 confirming the rarity and diagnostic complexity. Distinguishing NSMP from leiomyoma, leiomyosarcoma, hamartoma, or IMT remains challenging.3 Unlike leiomyosarcoma, NSMP lacks nuclear atypia, necrosis, and mitotic activity. IMT, BML, and lung metastases were excluded through histopathological, immunohistochemical and clinical evaluation, including the absence of uterine lesions. A review of reported cases of pulmonary NSMP reveals several shared features with the present case (Figure 2, Table 1). Patients were predominantly older adults, with a slight female predominance. Clinically, symptoms were absent or non-specific, like a mild cough. Radiologically, lesions presented as small, well-defined pulmonary nodules, occasionally with ground-glass opacity, frequently raising suspicion for malignancy. Preoperative diagnosis remained challenging, as minimally invasive diagnostic procedures were either not performed or inconclusive. Consequently, surgical resection was required in all patients, serving both diagnostic and therapeutic purposes, with no reported recurrences. The potential role of hormonal factors remains uncertain. Slight female predominance raises the question of potential hormonal involvement, similar to other smooth muscle proliferative disorders such as BML, which is known to be hormone-sensitive. Currently, there is no direct evidence supporting hormonal receptor expression in pulmonary NSMP. Further studies are required to clarify whether hormonal factors play any role in its pathogenesis. FOB or TTB failed to provide a definitive diagnosis. The PET-CT scan was inconclusive. Thus, surgical resection sometimes remains the only option for both establishing a diagnosis and providing definitive treatment in uncertain cases. We propose a structured approach for suspected pulmonary NSMP: an initial clinical evaluation and high-resolution imaging followed by minimally invasive biopsy when feasible. Minimally invasive procedures, such as video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery or robot-assisted thoracoscopic surgery, are increasingly used4,5 and may reduce diagnostic delay in selected cases.

CONCLUSION

This case highlights the importance of considering benign NSMP in the differential diagnosis of lung nodules, even when imaging suggests malignancy. Definitive diagnosis requires histopathological confirmation and the exclusion of atypia, necrosis, and mitotic activity. Surgery remains the cornerstone in uncertain cases.
