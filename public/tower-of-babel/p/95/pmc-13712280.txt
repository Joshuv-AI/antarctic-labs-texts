Identification and characterization of a non-nucleotide RdRp allosteric inhibitor with pan-coronavirus protection
Authors: Qian Guo, Xiaomeng Yang, Zhihao Zhao, Jian-Piao Cai, Liang Wei, Kaiming Tang, Wan-Mui Chan, Allen Wing-Ho Chu, Yixin Huang, Tong-Yun Wang, Yubin Xie, Yulan Liu, Zi-Wei Ye, Tao Ni, Hin Chu, Jasper Fuk-Woo Chan, Kelvin Kai-Wang To, Kwok-Yung Yuen, Ying-Chih Chiang, Shuofeng Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) is a conserved and compelling target for pan-coronavirus antiviral development. To date, all US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved RdRp inhibitors are nucleotide inhibitors (NIs), which are prone to drug resistance and show limited clinical efficacy. Developing alternative non-nucleotide inhibitors (NNIs) with high target specificity, structural diversity, and metabolic stability could yield more effective antivirals. Here, we identify an allosteric RdRp NNI, BAY-850, via computational screening. BAY-850 exhibits potent, broad-spectrum antiviral activity against multiple SARS-CoV-2 variants and other human coronaviruses. Mechanistic studies show that BAY-850 binds directly to a previously unrecognized allosteric site on nsp12, thereby fine-tuning the catalytic motif F loop out-conformation the orthosteric site. Importantly, therapeutic administration of BAY-850 significantly reduces viral loads in the nasal turbinates and lungs of SARS-CoV-2-infected K18-hACE2 mice. These findings validate a novel allosteric site in RdRp and support BAY-850 as a start-point for broad-spectrum anti-coronavirus development.

Introduction

To date, seven coronaviruses have been identified in the human population, including human coronavirus (HCoV)-229E, -OC43, -NL63, and -HKU1, severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus (SARS-CoV), Middle East respiratory syndrome coronavirus (MERS-CoV), and severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus-2 (SARS-CoV-2), which cross-transmitted from animals (bat, bovine, mouse, or other animals) to human1. Moreover, coronaviruses have a wide host spectrum. For example, the SARS-CoV-2 spike could bind ACE2 (angiotensin-converting enzyme 2) orthologs from 44 domestic animals, pets, livestock, and animals in zoos and aquaria, and initiate viral entry2. These results indicate that the potential spillover events of other coronaviruses from animals to humans, especially bat-derived SARS-related coronaviruses (SARSr-CoVs), are increasing, which may cause more pandemics in the future. Therefore, it is imperative to develop universal coronavirus or pan-coronavirus vaccines or drugs against the next coronavirus pandemic.

Polymerase and protease are the main viral targets for developing SARS-CoV-2 antivirals. For the two clinically-prescribed polymerase inhibitors, remdesivir is administered intravenously daily for either 5 or 10 days due to the drug's poor hepatic stability and low plasma/serum stability. Molnupiravir requires a large daily dose (800 mg dosed twice daily; 1.6 g total) and comes with mutagenicity concerns. For the protease inhibitor Paxlovid, ‘COVID rebound’ is surprisingly common. Nirmatrelvir, the active antiviral component of Paxlovid, is a covalent inhibitor but suffers from off-target reactions and blunts antiviral adaptive immune responses3. Ensitrelvir (Xocova) do not require ritonavir boosting and are administered once daily. However, it still faces a rapid drug resistance and common adverse events including reduced blood high-density lipoprotein and increased blood triglycerides4. Of note, few of these drugs was originally designed for SARS-CoV-2 and/or coronaviruses, thus the development of novel inhibitors with higher specificity and durable efficacy are essential.

The catalytic core of the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) comprises the catalytic subunit nsp12 and the accessory subunits nsp7 and nsp8; this complex synthesizes all viral RNA5. RdRp's essential role in viral RNA synthesis, together with its highly conserved amino acid sequence and protein structure across coronaviruses, makes it an attractive target for pan-coronavirus therapeutics. There are two known classes of RdRp inhibitors: nucleotide analogue inhibitors (NIs) and non-nucleotide inhibitors (NNIs). The problem faced in the development of NIs (remdesivir, molnupiravir) is the high concentration of intracellular natural NTP, which increases the risk of drug toxicity. NNIs acting on allosteric sites exert antiviral activity by affecting the binding of the catalytically active site of RdRp to the substrate. NNIs do not need to undergo metabolic activation and neither to compete with intracellular NTP. Therefore, it is expected that NNIs harbor considerably more structural diversity, target specificity and metabolic stability. Recently, suramin was reported as a NNI of SARS-CoV-2 RdRp, and its mode of action to block the RdRp binding of both strands of the template–primer RNA substrate was supported by a cryo-EM structure6. Corilagin, a natural product, exerts its antiviral activity by preventing conformational changes of RdRp, which are obligatory for nucleotide incorporation into the growing viral RNA chain7. These studies demonstrate the feasibility to develop NNIs to combat SARS-CoV-2. However, the unfavorable off-target effects of either compound on cellular polymerases, helicases, or spike-ACE2 binding hinder their advancement into an IND-enabling stage.

In this study, we report the discovery of BAY-850, a NNI that targets a novel allosteric site on the SARS-CoV-2 RdRp. BAY-850 exhibited potent antiviral activity against SARS-CoV-2 and other pathogenic coronaviruses. Artificial selection of escape mutants followed by reverse genetics validation indicated that residue A95 in nsp12 is a key determinant of drug susceptibility. Molecular dynamics simulations revealed that both BAY-850 binding and the nsp12 A95V substitution allosterically remodel the RdRp catalytic motif F, hindering viral RNA incorporation. Importantly. BAY-850 showed favorable drug metabolism and pharmacokinetic (DMPK) properties with in vivo protection. These findings establish BAY-850 as a lead allosteric RdRp inhibitor for further antiviral development.

Results

Identification of BAY-850 as a RdRp NNI

We first mapped pockets distal to the RdRp catalytic core using computational tools. CavityPlus8 and ProteinLens9 highlighted two druggable allosteric pockets in nsp12—the NiRAN domain and the palm domain—which were selected for virtual screening against a bioactive library (MedChemExpress) of ∼7000 compounds (Fig. 1A). Based on the predicted binding affinity, the eleven top-scoring compounds were advanced to experimental validation (Fig. 1B), which was assessed by surface plasmon resonance (SPR) (Supporting Information Fig. S1A). Three compounds—Icatibant, Olcegepant, and BAY-850—exhibited stronger binding responses than that of suramin thus were prioritized for cell-based antiviral evaluation (Fig. S1B). A primer-elongation assay6 was exploited for validating the inhibitory effects of SARS-CoV-2 RdRp by the selected hits (Fig. S1C). Among these, BAY-850 showed the best polymerase inhibitory potency and antiviral efficacy was designated as a primary hit for further analysis (Fig. 1C). BAY-850 inhibited wild-type (WT) SARS-CoV-2 replication in VeroE6-TMPRSS2 and A549-ACE2-TMPRSS2 cells in a dose-dependent manner, with EC50 values of 1.68 ± 0.22 and 1.54 ± 0.19 μmol/L, respectively (Fig. 1D). BAY-850 showed negligible cytotoxicity in both cell lines after 48 h of exposure at antiviral concentrations (Fig. S1D). In A549-ACE2-TMPRSS2 cells infected at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.1, BAY-850 treatment (7.5 μmol/L) reduced viral RNA levels in culture supernatants by > 2 log10, which is comparable to that of 10 μmol/L Remdesivir (Fig. S1E). In A549-ACE2-TMPRSS2 cells infected with 1 MOI SARS-COV-2, DMSO-treated controls exhibited pronounced cytopathic effects (CPE) within 24 h, whereas BAY-850 provided robust CPE protection with minimal syncytia formation over 72 h (Fig. 1E). Consistently, immunofluorescence staining for the SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid (N) antigen showed that BAY-850 completely abolished viral protein expression in A549-ACE2-TMPRSS2 cells (Fig. 1F). BAY-850 was initially reported as a potent, isoform-selective inhibitor of the epigenetic regulator ATPase family AAA domain-containing protein 2 (ATAD2)10. To determine whether its antiviral effect depends on ATAD2 inhibition, we evaluated the potential proviral role of ATAD2. Two ATAD2-targeting siRNAs efficiently reduced ATAD2 expression in A549-ACE2-TMPRSS2 cells (Fig. S1G), whereas this knockdown did not lead to any virus suppression (Fig. S1H). Moreover, BAY-850 retained potent antiviral activity after ATAD2 silencing, with an EC50 of 0.81 ± 0.18 μmol/L (Fig. 1I). However, other validated ATAD2 inhibitors GSK8814 and AZ13824374 showed negligible RdRp binding, polymerase inhibitory potency, nor antiviral efficacy (Fig. S1I‒S1L). In an established SARS-CoV-2 mini-replicon system11, BAY-850 dose-dependently suppressed the replicon signal (Fig. 1J). Collectively, these results indicate that BAY-850's antiviral activity is independent of ATAD2 inhibition and support its role as a specific allosteric RdRp NNI.

Pan-coronavirus activity and model of action of BAY-850

To assess antiviral efficacy against SARS-CoV-2 variants of concern (VOCs), we treated A549-ACE2-TMPRSS2 cells with BAY-850 after infection with B.1.1.7 (Alpha), B.1.351 (Beta), B.1.617.2 (Delta), XBB.1.5, or JN.1 (Omicron). Consistent with its activity against WT virus, BAY-850 inhibited virus replication in a dose-dependent manner, with EC50 values ranging from 0.83 to 1.5 μmol/L (Fig. 2A). Across all VOCs, 7.5 μmol/L BAY-850 reduced viral RNA in culture supernatants for ∼3 log10, which is comparable to that of 10 μmol/L remdesivir (Fig. 2B). To evaluate its antiviral breadth against other human coronaviruses, we used Huh7 cells, which are permissive to both MERS-CoV and HCoV-229E infection. BAY-850 exhibited negligible cytotoxicity in Huh7 cells (Fig. S1D), and an EC50 value of 0.83 ± 0.08 μmol/L (MERS-CoV) and 1.45 ± 0.16 μmol/L (HCoV-229E), respectively (Fig. 2C). In contrast, BAY-850 showed no activity against influenza A/H1N1 virus, suggesting it's a specific coronaviruses inhibitor (Fig. S1F). Next, we performed an artificial drug escape resistance assay to investigate the compound's binding site on RdRp (Fig. 3A). This exercise is not only useful for target validation, but also meaningful for drug-resistance evaluation. Previous studies have reported that remdesivir-induced resistance typically emerges between passage 9 and 1312. However, drug resistance was not detectable after 10 passages of SARS-CoV-2 and under increasing pressure of BAY-850 (Fig. 3B). The antiviral resistance phenotype was eventually observed at passage 25 (P25), thus we performed plaque purification as well as reverse genetics validation. We found a 12.5-fold reduction in BAY-850 sensitivity when measuring the viral RNA level in cell lysates (Fig. 3C). In the cell culture supernatants, 7.5 μmol/L BAY-850 reduced the viral genome copy number by 3.5 log10 for the vehicle control strain, but only 1.4 log10 for that of escape strain (Fig. 3D). To assess the role of nsp12-A95 in the antiviral mechanism of BAY-850, we generated a recombinant SARS-CoV-2 virus carrying the nsp12-A95V mutation via site-directed mutagenesis, using a bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC)-based reverse genetics system13. Both the nsp12-A95V mutant and WT viruses were rescued and passaged in VeroE6-TMPRSS2 cells (Supporting Information Fig. S2A). Notably, the nsp12-A95V mutant exhibited attenuated replication compared to WT virus (Fig. 3F), indicating that this mutation in nsp12 severely impairs the viral replication cycle. As expected, BAY-850 demonstrated reduced efficacy against the nsp12-A95V recombinant SARS-CoV-2, with a 17-fold increase in EC50 (Fig. 3G). Lower concentrations of BAY-850 (0.94 to 3.75 μmol/L) failed to suppress mutant virus growth, while efficiently inhibiting WT virus (Fig. 3H). Furthermore, we purified WT and nsp12-A95V RdRp complex (nsp7-8-12) proteins for comparative binding affinity measurement (Fig. S2B). Utilizing the surface plasmon resonance (SPR) technology, BAY-850 bound to WT RdRp with a KD of 6.67 μmol/L, which was >2-fold higher affinity than that of A95V (KD = 15.6 μmol/L) (Fig. 3I). As a reference, Suramin showed equivalent binding affinities against WT (KD = 18.9 μmol/L) versus A95V protein (KD = 17.4 μmol/L) (Fig. 3J). Of note, BAY-850 exhibited ∼3-fold higher binding affinity than that of Suramin.

To further characterize nsp12 A95 is critical for BAY-850's allosteric inhibition on RdRp activity, a fluorescence-based polymerase assay was used. A95V mutation greatly abolished the RNA synthesis of RdRp (Fig. 3K and L). BAY-850 inhibited polymerase activity with an IC50 of 9.52 ± 0.98 μmol/L, which was >3-fold lower than that against A95V mutant RdRp (IC50 = 30.27 ± 1.48 μmol/L) (Fig. 3M). Moreover, Michaelis–Menton inhibitory kinetics was conducted to identify the mode of inhibition of BAY-850 (Fig. 3N). The inhibitory constant (Ki = 6.98 μmol/L) was similar to its binding affinity KD (6.67 μmol/L). The calculated Alpha value (0.9635) is close to 1. These results suggest BAY-850 as a non-competitive allosteric site inhibitor. Further characterization of the roles of RdRp-A95 during RTC machinery is warranted in the subsequent analysis.

Allosteric inhibition mechanism of BAY-850

We performed molecular docking analyses centered on residue A95 of nsp12. BAY-850 exhibited non-covalent binding within an allosteric pocket of the RdRp NiRAN domain, which is spatially distant from the RdRp catalytic core (Fig. 4A). Key interactions included hydrogen bonds (R10, Y87, N88, K91, V96, K98) and hydrophobic contacts (E83, E84, L89, A97, H99, D100, F101, F102). Most of these residues are highly conserved among emerging and human pathogenic coronaviruses, supporting BAY-850's broad-spectrum activity (Fig. S2C). According to the structure‒activity relationship (SAR) exploration, the essential elements for RdRp interaction mainly included several hydrogen bond donor and acceptor, which was not effectively interacted with its original host target ATAD2; whereas the irreplaceable core unit for ATAD2 inhibition was not required for RdRp binding (Fig. S2D). To elucidate BAY-850's allosteric mechanism, we hypothesized that its binding at the allosteric site could modulate the RdRp orthosteric (catalytic) site. We conducted 200-ns all-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to examine the effects upon BAY-850 binding, as well as the nsp12-A95V substitution. Stable root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) values throughout the simulation indicated minimal global conformational changes (Fig. S2E). Ligand trajectory analysis showed that BAY-850 binds more stably to WT RdRp than that of A95V mutant (Fig. 4B). The RdRp catalytic center consists of seven conserved motifs (i.e., A to G), with the catalytic cavity situated at their core14. We found that both BAY-850 binding and the A95V substitution enlarged this cavity (Fig. 4C), thereby disrupting the conformational dynamics necessary for RNA synthesis. We further analyzed dynamic changes in key amino acid pairs: N496-Y689 (involved in RNA template stabilization) and R555-D761 (NTP binding site)14,15. Both BAY-850 binding and the A95V substitution altered the N496-Y689 distance, while the A95V had marginal effect on the R555-D761 distance (Fig. 4D). No significant changes were observed for S501 and I847, which are critical for RNA duplex exit (Fig. S2F)16. Motif F, a loop essential for nucleotide binding and RNA elongation, was shifted by approximately 0.3 nm in both BAY-850-bound and A95V mutant forms compared to WT, likely resulting in an inactive conformation (Fig. 4E).

To assess the allosteric effects on motif F-mediated RNA synthesis, we modeled an RNA-bound RdRp complex and performed root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) analysis. As expected, the most pronounced RMSF differences between WT and BAY-850-bound or A95V mutant forms were observed in motif F (Fig. 4F). In addition, K545 within motif F, which typically rotates by ∼72° to engage the primer RNA, remained in its original orientation (120–135°) in the BAY-850-bound and A95V mutants, abolishing RNA elongation (Fig. 4G). Hydrogen bond formation frequencies between RNA recognition residues and the RNA duplex were also quantified (Fig. 4H). We found that both BAY-850 binding and the A95V mutation altered hydrogen bond patterns near motif F, decreasing frequencies at Q541/K545/K551/R555 and increasing them at N543/L544/R569, corroborating the abnormal conformation of motif F.

Based on these findings, we tried to quantify the native interaction between nsp12 and dsRNA by proximity ligation (PLA) technology. Notably, both BAY-850 treatment and the A95V mutation abolished the nsp12-dsRNA interactions upon WT SARS-CoV-2 infection, while BAY-850 showed negligible effects on A95V mutant virus (Fig. 4J‒L). Collectively, these results suggest that BAY-850 works as an allosteric RdRp inhibitor, primarily by inducing an out-of-conformation state in motif F at the RdRp orthosteric site (Fig. 4I).

DMPK and in vivo efficacy of BAY-850

Previous safety panel assays have ruled out the potential off-target effect of BAY-850 on other BD family members and critical kinase or GPCRs10. To access potential side-effects of ATAD2 inhibition, we monitored a panel of gene expression upon ATAD2 knock down or BAY-850 treatment, which defined the favorable safety window of BAY-850 in noncancerous cells (Supporting Information Fig. S3). To evaluate the preclinical value of BAY-850, we comprehensively documented its drug metabolism and pharmacokinetics (DMPK) profiles. In vitro solubility analysis demonstrated that BAY-850 possesses high kinetic and thermodynamic solubility (Fig. 5A). Based on these favorable DMPK properties, we next assessed the in vivo toxicity and therapeutic efficacy of BAY-850 against authentic SARS-CoV-2 Omicron in K18-hACE2 mice. Daily intraperitoneal administration of BAY-850 (5 mg/kg) to BALB/c mice for 14 days resulted in no mortality and negligible changes in body weight (Fig. 5D). Next, mice received their first i.p. dose of BAY-850 (5 mg/kg) were intranasally challenged with SARS-CoV-2 Omicron BA.2 (10,000 PFU), followed by daily treatment of the drug compound until sacrifice on 3dpi. For comparison, we included a NI Molnupiravir (200 mg/kg) and another NNI Suramin (10 mg/kg) for references. Both BAY-850 and Molnupiravir significantly reduced viral genome copies and infectious viral titers in the nasal turbinates and lungs, whereas Suramin showed marginal effect (Fig. 5F and G). Further, mice were challenged with SARS-CoV-2 Alpha (B.1.1.7) (200 PFU) for survival analysis (Fig. 5H). BAY-850 and Molnupiravir delayed the onset of death and significantly improved survival rates, whereas suramin was not (Fig. 5I). BAY-850 treatment provided generally better protection against weight loss, though not statically significant (Fig. 5J). Additionally, H&E staining was applied to detect infection-induced histopathological damage in the lung tissues. Compared with the vehicle group, BAY-850 treatment reduced the pathological changes including inflammatory infiltration, alveoli destruction, and the loss of epithelial integrity (Fig. 5K). Though BAY-850 exhibited some extent of in vivo antiviral activity, further hit-to-lead optimizations of BAY-850 are warranted for enhanced bioavailability and potency.

Discussion

Historically, therapies for viral diseases caused by human immunodeficiency virus, hepatitis B and C viruses rely largely on polymerase inhibitors17. Thus, it is not surprising that the main approach for the development of antivirals against SARS-CoV-2, as exampled by the clinically-approved remdesivir, molnupiravir and VV116, is based on targeting the RdRp as a central replication enzyme of the virus. The problem faced by all advanced NIs (remdesivir, molnupiravir, ribavirin, favipiravir) is the high concentration of intracellular natural NTP, which increases the risk of drug toxicity. Although efforts have been made to identify new NNIs6,7,18, they are essentially non-nucleotide orthosteric inhibitor that cannot overcome the drug resistance issue of existing NIs (Fig. S2G). In this study, we discovered BAY-850, a new allosteric NNIs whose binding triggers an inactive conformation of the RdRp orthosteric cavity thus destroy viral RNA elongation. The newly identified allosteric pocket of BAY-850 was found in the N-terminal NiRAN domain of nsp12. The NiRAN domain is essential for viral propagation and supports three functions linked to modification of the nsp9 N terminus: NMPylation, RNAylation, and deRNAylation/capping19. Several compounds have been reported to target the NiRAN active site, but they have shown limited in vitro antiviral efficacy20,21. When comparing the binding models of reported NiRAN active-site inhibitors (AT-527 and NCI-2) with that of BAY-850, we observed no overlapping residues; rather, they occupy distinct, opposing pockets, indicating a unique allosteric mechanism for BAY-850 in RdRp (Fig. S2H). BAY-850 fits into a cavity formed by a helix-loop-sheet motif (residues H82–K103) and forms an additional hydrogen bond with the N-terminal head-helix region (residues L8–R11). Four of the six residues involved in hydrogen bonding (R10, Y87, N88, and V96), as well as the principal hydrophobic packing contacts (E83, L89, A98, H99, D100, F101, and F102), are highly conserved across other pathogenic coronaviruses, indicating that BAY-850 is a pan-coronavirus inhibitor with distinct mode of action.

Michaelis–Menton inhibitory kinetics, MD simulations, and detectable native nsp12-dsRNA interaction by PLA revealed BAY-850 as an allosteric RdRp inhibitor. These features also provide preliminary proof-of-concept that allosterically inhibiting NSP12 can achieve antiviral effects. However, further chemical optimizations of BAY-850 based on the protein‒drug complex structures are essential in the future study to enhance its bioavailability and potency before proceeding to clinical studies. Skeleton-based optimization may focus on the pharmacophore moieties on the left side of BAY-850 and the four key nitrogen atoms (as indicated in the SMILES structure), as these are critical for RdRp targeting. Several strategies could be considered. For example, (i) based on trajectory analysis from MD simulations, we observed that the rightmost toluene group is suspended in the solvent and does not effectively interact with the protein surface. Therefore, replacing this ring structure with smaller hydrophobic groups containing polar substituents (e.g., –OH, –NH2) may help balance the molecular weight and enhance potential hydrogen bond formation. (ii) The benzofuran and chlorinated benzene moieties on the right side are irreplaceable for the original ATAD2 inhibition10, but do not stabilize the interaction with RdRp. Thus, we suggest replacing the benzofuran with a hydrogen bond-forming linear chain and substituting the chlorine atom with fluorine. These changes may abolish the original host-targeted ATAD2 effects, enhance RdRp selectivity, and maintain lipophilicity. (iii) Additional modifications could include substituting the ether group with hydrogen bond donors (–OH, –NH2) or hydrogen bond acceptors (–F) to improve non-covalent interactions with RdRp.

In summary, our study not only highlights the value of computational approaches in antiviral drug discovery but also suggests a live attenuated vaccine strategy by further engineering the established nsp12-A95V recombinant virus. While direct catalytic mutations in the viral RTC may induce compensatory changes in other genomic regions22, targeting substitutions at NSP12 allosteric sites appears to be a more technically feasible approach, especially when facing large-scale vaccine production and minimizing the risk of virulence reversion. Furthermore, BAY-850 may serve as a promising starting point for the development of next-generation RdRp allosteric inhibitors.

Experimental

Cells and viruses

The following cell lines—Vero E6-TMPRSS2, A549-ACE2-TMPRSS2, BEAS-2B-ACE2, Huh7 and A549—were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 1% penicillin/streptomycin (PS) and 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS). For virus receptor-engineered cell lines, Vero E6-TMPRSS2 was selected using 1 mg/mL G418, A549-ACE2-TMPRSS2 was maintained with 0.5 μg/mL puromycin and 300 μg/mL hygromycin, while BEAS-2B-ACE2 was selected with 1 μg/mL puromycin. Human Astrocyte was maintained in specific designed astrocyte medium (ScienCell, USA) containing astrocyte growth supplement and FBS. All cell lines were passaged at least twice per week, and their mycoplasma-free status was routinely checked and confirmed.

The following SARS-CoV-2 strains were used in this study: Wild type (GenBank: MT230904), B.1.1.7 (Alpha) (GenBank: OM212469), B.1.351 (Beta) (GISAID: EPI_ISL_2423556), B.1.617.2 (Delta) (GenBank: OM212471), Omicron BA.2 (GISAID: EPI_ISL_9845731), BA.5 (GISAID: EPI_ISL_13777658), XBB.1.5 (GISAID: EPI_ISL_15602393), and JN.1 (GISAID: EPI_ISL_18841631). MERS-CoV (EMC/2012) was kindly provided by Ron Fouchier (Erasmus Medical Center, Netherlands), and HCoV-229E was obtained from the Department of Microbiology, The University of Hong Kong. Virus culture and titration were performed as previously described23. Influenza A virus strain A/Hong Kong/415742/2009 (H1N1) pdm09 was cultured in embryonated chicken eggs as previously reported24. All experiments involving live viruses were carried out in Biosafety Level 2 or 3 facilities at the Department of Microbiology, University of Hong Kong.

Chemicals

All compounds were purchased from MedChemExpress unless otherwise specified. Stock solutions were prepared in DMSO and stored at −80 °C.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR

Cell culture supernatant or lysate from virus-infected samples was lysed using RLT buffer. Total RNA was extracted with the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The extracted RNA was subsequently used to quantify viral genome copies by quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) using the Takara One-Step TB Green® PrimeScript™ RT-qPCR Kit II (Takara, Japan). The primer sequences are listed in Supporting Information Table S1.

Cytotoxicity assay

To determine the CC50 of selected compounds, the indicated cell lines were treated with various concentrations of each compound for 48 h, and cell viability was assessed using the CellTiter-Glo® Luminescent Assay (Promega, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

siRNA knockdown of ATAD2

A549-ACE2-TMPRSS2 cells were pre-seeded in 48-well plates. The following day, two siRNAs targeting ATAD2 (Invitrogen) were transfected into the cells using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX reagent (Thermo Fisher, USA) once daily for two consecutive days. At 48 h after the initial siRNA transfection, cell lysates were collected for quantification of ATAD2 gene expression by qRT-PCR, or cell viability was assessed as described above to determine gene knockdown efficiency.

Cell-based antiviral assay

To evaluate the antiviral efficacy of selected drugs, cells pre-seeded in 96-well plates were infected with the indicated multiplicity of infection (MOI). Two hours post-infection, the infected medium was aspirated, cells were washed once with PBS, and then replaced with DMEM containing 1% PS, 1% FBS, and the drug at 2-fold serially diluted concentrations. For influenza virus infection, 2 μg/mL N-tosyl-l-phenylalanine chloromethyl ketone (TPCK) was added to the medium to facilitate efficient viral replication. At 48 h post-infection (hpi), cell culture supernatant and lysates were collected separately for subsequent viral load quantification.

IF staining and cellular imaging

To visualize the SARS-CoV-2 N antigen in infected cells, immunofluorescence (IF) staining was performed. Briefly, cultured cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature (RT) for 15 min and permeabilized with 0.02% Triton X-100 in PBS-T for 15 min. After blocking with 4% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at RT for 1 h, cells were incubated with an in-house SARS-CoV-2 N primary antibody (1:4000) overnight at 4 °C. Following primary antibody incubation, cells were washed with PBS-T and incubated with goat anti-rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor 488 secondary antibody (Thermo Fisher, USA) (1:1000) at RT for 1.5 h. Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33258 (Thermo Fisher, USA). Subcellular images were acquired using a GE IN Cell Analyzer 6500 HS equipped with a laser-based confocal imaging system.

Raise of escape mutant

The generation of BAY-850 escape mutant strain was conducted on a modified protocol as previously mentioned25. Briefly, A549-ACE2-TMPRSS2 cells were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well. The following day, confluent cells were inoculated with the SARS-CoV-2 wild-type strain at an MOI of 5 in the presence of either 5 μmol/L BAY-850 or 0.05% DMSO. After 24 h of incubation, 50% and 100% cytopathic effect (CPE) were observed in the BAY-850 and DMSO groups, respectively. Supernatants from both groups were collected as passage 1. For the subsequent 24 passages, 100 μL of supernatant from the previous passage was transferred to fresh cells in the presence of BAY-850 or DMSO. Supernatant from passage 25 was collected for single viral particle purification and Nanopore sequencing, as previously described25.

Plaque assay

To determine viral titers from collected samples, plaque assays were performed as we previously described but with slight modifications26. Briefly, Vero E6-TMPRSS2 cells were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well and incubated for 24 h. The following day, cells were inoculated with 10-fold serially diluted virus samples and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. After incubation, the virus supernatant was discarded and cells were washed twice with PBS. The cells were then overlaid with 1% agarose in DMEM and incubated for an additional 2–3 days at 37 °C. Plates were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at RT for 24 h. After removing the agarose overlay, plates were stained with 1% crystal violet and plaques were counted to quantify viral titers.

Reverse genetic construction

To validate whether the A95V mutation is essential for the antiviral activity of BAY-850, a recombinant SARS-CoV-2 carrying this single point mutation was generated using the pBAC-SARS-CoV-2 infectious molecular clone, as previously described13. λ-Red-mediated homologous recombination was employed to introduce the site-directed mutation into pBAC-SARS-CoV-2. BAC DNA, validated by Sanger sequencing, was transfected into Vero E6-TMPRSS2 cells pre-seeded in 6-well plates using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Cells were monitored daily for cytopathic effect (CPE) formation. Supernatant from virus-positive cells was collected at 72 h post-transfection. Plaque assays were performed to determine the titers of both wild-type and A95V mutant virus stocks.

Expression and purification of SARS-CoV-2 RdRp complex protein

The plasmid pRSFDuet-1(nsp8-nsp7) (nsp12), used for co-expressing the full SARS-CoV-2 RdRp complex in E. coli, was a gift from Marc Delarue (Addgene plasmid #165451)27. Site-directed mutagenesis (nsp12-A95V) was introduced into the full-length construct using overlapping PCR. All constructs were validated by sequencing. Protein expression and purification were performed following the optimized protocol as previously described but with slight modifications28. Briefly, E. coli (BL21) cells transformed with the constructs were grown in Lysogeny Broth (LB) medium to an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 0.6. After cooling, 0.05 mmol/L isopropyl-β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) was added, and cultures were incubated for an additional 20 h at 20 °C with shaking at 180 rpm. Bacterial pellets were harvested by centrifugation and washed with PBS prior to purification. Protein purification was performed at 4 °C using an ÄKTA Pure system (GE Healthcare, USA) with a series of chromatography steps, including a HisTrap nickel affinity column, a HiTrap Q HP anion exchange column, and Superdex 200 10/300 size-exclusion chromatography. Fractions containing RdRp were collected and concentrated for subsequent experiments. Remaining protein was flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. Protein purity was assessed by SDS-PAGE followed by Coomassie blue staining.

Cell-based SARS-CoV-2 replicon assay

A stable BHK-21 cell line harboring a non-cytopathic SARS-CoV-2 replicon was kindly provided by Dr. Tony. T. Wang in US FDA11. Briefly, replicon cells were maintained in culture medium supplemented with 200 μg/mL G418 for selection. For drug inhibition assays, replicon cells were seeded in 96-well plates without G418 at a density of 1.5 × 104 cells per well. The following day, cells were treated with various concentrations of the drug and incubated for an additional 5 days at 37 °C. NanoLuc activity was then quantified using the Nano-Glo Luciferase Assay System (Promega, USA), and cell viability was measured with the CellTiter-Glo kit to provide a normalized signal.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR)

To assess the binding affinity between selected compounds and RdRp, a Biacore™ T200 system (Cytiva, USA) was used to analyze the kinetic profiles of molecular interactions as previously mentioned but with slight modifications25,29. Briefly, purified RdRp with confirmed enzymatic activity was immobilized onto one channel of a Sensor Chip CM7 (Cytiva, USA) until a response of 20,000 RU was achieved. A paired channel without immobilization served as a blank control. Running buffer consisted of Biacore™ PBS-P+ containing 5% DMSO (Cytiva, USA). Compounds were injected at 2-fold serial dilutions, and binding responses were automatically monitored at 25 °C. For initial screening, RU signals for each compound were compared to suramin at the same concentration. For KD determination, sensorgrams were analyzed using a 1:1 binding model with DMSO solvent correction applied.

In vitro RdRp polymerase assay

The in-vitro RNA duplex elongation assay was conducted as mentioned before with slight modifications to detect the RdRp polymerase activity6. To prepare the template-primer RNA duplex, FAM-labeled primers (5′-FAM-GCUAUGUGAGAUUAAGUUAU-3′) (IDT, USA) were annealed with the poly-U template (5′-UUUUUUUUUUAUAACUUAAUCUCACAUAGC-3′) (IDT, USA) by heating to 94 °C for 5 min and then cooled to RT at a 1:1 ratio in annealing buffer (10 mmol/L Tris‒HCl, pH 8.0, 25 mmol/L NaCl and 2.5 mmol/L EDTA in DEPC water). The reaction buffer comprising 20 mmol/L Tris, pH 8.0, 10 mmol/L KCl, 6 mmol/L MgCl2, 0.01% Triton-X100, 1 mmol/L DTT supplement with RNase inhibitor in DEPC water was used for the activity assay. For reaction, the purified RdRp complex (1 μmol/L) mixed with 3 μmol/L template–primer RNA duplex and 1 mmol/L ATP (MCE, USA) was incubated at 37 °C water bath for 60 min. For a 20 μL reaction system, 40 μL quench buffer (94% formamide, 30 mmol/L EDTA in DEPC water) was added to stop the reaction and mixed with 2 × RNA loading (Beyotime, China) for additional denature at 95 °C for 5 min. The sample was loaded onto a 12% urea–PAGE denatured gel (Beyotime, China) and run at 90 V for 2 h. The gel was further imaged with SAPPHIRE FL imager (AZURE, USA) equipped with 488-wavelength channel. RdRp inhibition experiments were conducted as the same step with the exception that RdRp was incubated with compounds before adding the ATP on ice for 1 h.

The fluorescence-based activity assay was conducted using a SARS-CoV-2 RdRp Assay Kits (ProFoldin, USA) with slight modification to get the IC50 and Michaelis-Menton inhibition kinetics. Briefly, 25 μL reaction mixtures including 20.5 μL of H2O, 2.5 μL of 10x Buffer, 0.5 μL of 50 × NTPs in presence with 200 nmol/L RNA template and 200 nmol/L RdRp complex were incubated for 2 h at 37 °C in a black 384-well plate. Following the incubation, 65 μL of 1 × dye was added to the reaction mixture, and a microplate reader (SpectraMax, USA) was used to measure the fluorescence intensity at 535 nm (excitation wavelength at 485 nm). BAY-850 induced RdRp inhibition experiments were assessed as the same step with the exception that RdRp was incubated with compounds before adding the NTP at RT for 30 min. The set up of Michaelis–Menten kinetics curve of RNA substrates was conducted with the same assay condition. The initial velocity was determined by dividing the enzyme activity fluorescence by the reaction time. GraphPad prism 9.0.2 was used to plot initial velocity against substrate concentration and perform a simple linear regression with mixed-model inhibition function to determine the parameters.

Computational screening

SARS-CoV-2 RdRp structure was obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB ID: 6M71). Missing loops were modeled using the SWISS-MODEL server30. To identify potential allosteric binding sites for virtual screening, webtool CavityPlus and ProteinLens were used8,9. Virtual screening was conducted based on one bioactive compound library using Glide in SP model31.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation

System construction

Total eight systems were constructed: WT and A95V mutant RdRp in apo (PDB ID: 6M71) and RNA-bound forms (PDB ID: 7AAP), with or without BAY-850. The A95V mutation was introduced using the PDB Reader module of CHARMM-GUI32. Hydrogen atoms were added to all structures using the MolProbity33. Protonation states of ionizable residues were determined using PROPKA at pH 7.434. All systems were solved in rectangular water boxes using the TIP3P water model. The systems were neutralized and NaCl was added to achieve a physiological concentration of 0.09 mol/L. MD simulations were performed using GROMACS 2024.2 with the CHARMM36m force field35. Energy minimization used steepest descent with backbone (1000 kJ/(mol·nm2)) and side chain (200 kJ/(mol·nm2)) restraints. Equilibration was conducted for 2.5 ns at 310 K and 1 bar with reduced restraints (400 and 40 kJ/(mol·nm2), respectively). Production runs lasted 200 ns with 2 fs time steps. Temperature and pressure were controlled using V-rescale and C-rescale methods, respectively. Electrostatics were treated with PME (1.2 nm cutoff), van der Waals with force-switch (1.0‒1.2 nm), and bonds constrained with LINCS.

Trajectories analysis

Trajectories were analyzed with GROMACS utilities after least-squares alignment to protein Cα atoms. Ligand stability was quantified as the RMSD of BAY-850, taking the reference as the representative structure of the largest cluster. Global stability was quantified as the RMSD of protein Cα atoms. Cavity size was defined as the volume encompassed by seven catalytic motifs and calculated with Epock with a sphere radius of 8 Å36. Reported residue-pair distances were computed between the indicated residues' Cα atoms. The “shift distance” of motif F was computed as RMSD normalized to WT system in each system across all trajectory frames. Protein RMSF was calculated as the average position change of residues’ Cα atoms. RNA-RdRp rotation was defined as an angle with three landmarks: the ribose anomeric carbon of the RNA cytidine 10, the exocyclic amine nitrogen on its cytidine base, and the terminal ammonium nitrogen of K545. Protein-RNA hydrogen bond frequencies were calculated for each amino acid as the proportion of simulation frames in which the residue formed at least one hydrogen bond. Probability density distributions of the analyzed parameters were calculated by normalizing the frequency of occurrence across all trajectory frames. The represented trajectories were visualized using VMD37.

Proximity ligation assay (PLA)

PLA assay was performed to reflect the native interaction of nsp12-dsRNA according to the manufacturer's protocol (Sigma, USA). Briefly, the treated cells were fixed with 100% cold methanol for 5 min at RT followed by blocking with indicated solution for 1 h. Then, the cells were stained with primer antibody both anti-nsp12 (Abcam, UK) or anti-dsRNA (J2) (Cell signaling, USA) over night. Next, cells were incubated with PLA probe-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h, followed by ligation for 30 min and further fluorescent amplification for 100 min at 37 °C. Samples were washed and images were captured by LSM900 equipped with a × 40 Oil objective.

Drug metabolism and pharmacokinetics (DMPK)

In vitro solubility

The thermodynamic and kinetic solubility of BAY-850 were tested in 50 mmol/L phosphate buffer (PB) in pH 4.5 or 7.4. Briefly, compound was dissolved in indicated buffer and vortexed thoroughly for at least 2 min. Then, the solution shaken on an RT shaker at 800 rpm for 24 h. After centrifuging (4000 rpm) at 25 °C for 10 min, the supernatant was filtered and collected for concentration quantification by LC‒UV system.

Metabolic stability

The metabolic stability of BAY-850 in mouse and human liver microsomes was conducted as mentioned before25. Briefly, mouse and human liver microsomes extracted from CD-1 mouse or human were processed in 100 mmol/L potassium phosphate buffer. BAY-850 at 1 μmol/L in final incubating solution, with or without a NADPH co-factor, was added to initiate the reaction. Cold acetonitrile (ACN) containing 250 nmol/L tolbutamide and 250 nmol/L labetalol was added to end the reaction. Aliquots were taken at 0, 5, 15, 30, 45, and 60 min for concentration quantification by LC‒MS/MS analysis.

Mouse pharmacokinetic (PK)

The PK characteristics of BAY-850 were conducted on BALB/c mice by single intraperitoneal injection at 5 mg/kg dosage as described previously38. There were three 6 to 10 weeks male mice in each group. After drug injection, the blood samples were collected from saphenous vein at 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 24 h and placed in tubes containing EDTA-K2. After centrifuging (4000 rpm) at 4 °C for 10 min, the plasma samples were quickly frozen by dry ice and kept at −80 °C. Concentration of BAY-850 in plasma was quantified by LC‒MS/MS and PK parameters were calculated by i.p.-noncompartmental model 200.

Therapeutic efficacy of BAY-850 in K18-hACE2 mice

Male K18-hACE2 transgenic mice, ranging in age from 6 to 8 weeks, were housed and given regular food pellet and water. At infection day, the mice were intranasally infected with 10,000 PFU of Omicron (BA.2). Mice were further treated with indicated amount compounds once daily. Three days after infection, the mice were sacrificed for tissue (lungs and nasal turbinate) sampling. Infectious titer and viral genome number were determined using the established protocol39. To observe the survival rate, the mice were intranasally infected with 200 PFU of Alpha (B.1.1.7) and threated with indicated compounds once daily until death or 14 dpi. The body weight changes were monitored daily. Haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was used to assess the lung tissues pathology of infected mice as mentioned before26.
