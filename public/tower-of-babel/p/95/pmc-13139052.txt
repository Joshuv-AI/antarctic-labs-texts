The Role of Piezo 1 in the Study of Intervertebral Disc Degeneration: Phenotype, Mechanism and Treatment
Authors: Guangye Li, Chaoqun Feng, Haoyun Huang, Junwen Deng, Fei Yang, Rigao Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundIntervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD), a prevalent spinal disorder, is closely associated with abnormal mechanical stress. The nucleus pulposus (NP), annulus fibrosus (AF), and cartilaginous endplate (CEP) collectively respond to mechanical loading. The mechanosensitive Piezo 1 ion channel senses mechanical stress changes and converts the mechanical signals into chemical signals, and serves a pivotal role in IVDD pathogenesis.ObjectiveThis review summarizes the regional effects of Piezo 1 on mechanical stress‐induced IVDD and evaluates therapeutic strategies targeting Piezo 1 to maintain disc homeostasis.MethodsA systematic search of preclinical and clinical studies was conducted to delineate Piezo 1's phenotypic impacts, mechanistic pathways, and therapeutic potential in NP, AF, and CEP.ResultsIn the NP, activated Piezo 1 contributes to cellular senescence, apoptosis, ferroptosis, extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation and synthesis, oxidative stress, inflammation, and catabolic processes. Key regulatory targets involved include NLRP3, MAPK, p38, MMPs, ADAMTS, p65, Periostin, p53, p16, GRP78, CHOP, Cyt‐c, and Drp1. In the CEP, Piezo 1 mediates inflammation‐induced CEP degeneration through the CaMKII/Drp1 pathway and further participates in cellular senescence and apoptosis by activating Bax and caspase‐3 while inhibiting Bcl‐2. In the AF, Piezo 1 mediates apoptosis through the Ca2+/Calpain2/Caspase‐3 signaling pathway. Therapeutically, targeting Piezo 1 has demonstrated significant preclinical potential, including attenuating pathological alterations via pharmacological inhibition, selectively suppressing degenerative cascades through genetic/RNAi approaches, and modulating channel activity by nutritionally regulating membrane lipids.ConclusionPiezo 1 serves as a critical mechanotransducer in IVDD, exhibiting region‐specific effects on disc pathophysiology. Targeting Piezo 1 signaling not only offers mechanistic insights but also holds translational potential as a therapeutic strategy to improve IVDD, meriting further exploration in preclinical and clinical contexts.

Introduction

The intervertebral disc (IVD), the largest avascular structure within the human body, is positioned between adjacent vertebral bodies [1]. It effectively absorbs and distributes axial loads while facilitating a spectrum of spinal motions [2]. However, due to its limited regenerative capacity and constant exposure to mechanical and biochemical stress, the IVD is highly susceptible to structural and functional deterioration, ultimately leading to intervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD). IVDD has long been recognized as a major underlying cause of various spinal disorders. It is estimated that up to 75% of the general population experience low back pain at some point in their lives, with approximately 5% to 10% progressing to chronic conditions. In the United States alone, the direct and indirect economic burden associated with low back pain is estimated to approach 24 billion dollars annually, with IVDD recognized as a primary underlying cause [3, 4, 5, 6, 7].

IVDD significantly impairs quality of life and imposes a substantial socioeconomic burden. Current management strategies for IVDD are mainly non‐surgical approaches, aiming to alleviate symptoms through analgesic therapy, anti‐inflammatory agents, and physical therapy. However, these interventions primarily delay disease progression rather than address the underlying degeneration. When conservative measures fail to provide adequate relief, surgical options such as discectomy, spinal fusion, or artificial disc replacement may be considered to restore disc height, maintain spinal mobility, and alleviate pain [8, 9, 10]. Nevertheless, these surgical approaches are associated with significant risks, including adjacent segment degeneration, implant failure, and limited long‐term efficacy, underscoring the urgent need for novel, mechanism‐based therapies.

The onset and progression of IVDD are multifactorial, involving biomechanical stress, aging, genetic predisposition, and nutritional deficiencies [11, 12, 13]. Among these, abnormal mechanical stress resulting from an upright posture is considered one of the most critical contributing factors to IVDD. In recent years, studies have shown that mechanical loading can induce the expression of specific genes within the disc, ultimately leading to alterations in its structural properties [14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. This process, known as mechanotransduction, converts mechanical stimuli into biochemical signals that regulate physiological functions. Aberrant mechanical stress plays a pivotal role in modifying the disc microenvironment by influencing the dynamics of ion channels [21, 22].

Piezo 1 was first identified in 2010 and gained international recognition when its discovery contributed to the awarding of the Nobel Prize in 2021. Over the past decade, research on this protein has steadily increased, and its relevance has now extended to a variety of common clinical conditions, including hypertension, obesity, and musculoskeletal disorders such as osteoarthritis, IVDD, and neurological diseases. Current research has made steady progress in understanding the association between Piezo 1 and the IVD. Unfortunately, Piezo 1's mechanism in regulating mechanotransduction in nucleus pulposus (NP), annulus fibrosus (AF), and cartilaginous endplate (CEP) cells remains largely overlooked. In addition, its dynamic influence on ECM metabolism, as well as the key factors involved in mediating cellular aging, apoptosis, and inflammatory cascades in IVDD, remain underexplored and lack a systematic theoretical framework. Here, we synthesize current advances on Piezo 1‐mediated mechanotransduction within the NP, AF and CEP, delineate how channel dysregulation drives region‐specific degenerative cascades, and critically appraise pharmacological, genetic, and lipid‐based interventions designed to recalibrate Piezo 1 signaling. By explicitly addressing these three disc regions, our review aims to clarify the region‐specific roles of Piezo 1 and highlight its therapeutic potential, thereby providing valuable insights for the understanding and management of IVDD.

Methods

A systematic search of the PubMed database, Web of Science, and Embase was conducted for studies published between 1970 and 2025. Search terms combined Piezo 1 with intervertebral disc related keywords, including “intervertebral disc degeneration,” “nucleus pulposus,” “annulus fibrosus,” “cartilage endplate,” “mechanical stress,” and “intervertebral disc degeneration.”

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) Investigated Piezo 1 expression, activation, or downstream signaling in the intervertebral disc, specifically in NP, AF, or CEP regions; (2) Employed in vivo (animal models), in vitro (cell culture), or ex vivo disc models to explore Piezo 1‐mediated mechanisms; or (3) involved clinical specimens or patient‐based studies assessing Piezo 1 expression or potential therapeutic modulation in the context of IVDD.

Studies were excluded if the full text was not available, if they were duplicate records, or if they were not relevant to IVD pathology or Piezo 1. In total, the PubMed, Web of Science and Embase search yielded 253 records. After removing duplicates and screening titles/abstracts, 24 studies met all inclusion criteria and were included in the qualitative synthesis ultimately. The screening and selection process ensured that only studies directly relevant to Piezo 1's role in disc degeneration were summarized (Figure 1).

Development of Piezo 1 in IVDD

During the past decade, the research on Piezo 1 has demonstrated consistent progress, and its exploration in IVDD has exhibited a notable development trend over the recent years (Figure 2).

The development timeline of Piezo 1 in IVDD.

Piezo 1, a mechanosensitive ion channel originally known as FAM38A, was first identified by the Patapoutian team in 2010 and named after the Greek word “πίεση” (píesi), meaning “pressure” [23]. In 2012, Coste and colleagues pioneered the first recordings of mechanically activated cation currents, definitively establishing Piezo proteins as bona fide mechanosensitive ion channels [24]. By 2014, Piezo 1 was confirmed to mediate endothelial responses to shear stress. That same year, subsequent investigations further elucidated its roles in regulating ATP release in stretched epithelial tissues and responding to traction forces in neural stem cells [25, 26, 27]. In 2015, the full‐length molecular structure of Piezo 1 was resolved via cryo‐electron microscopy, revealing a trimeric, propeller‐like configuration essential for its mechanotransduction function [28]. In the same year, the discovery of Yoda1 marked the beginning of Piezo chemical pharmacology research [29]. In 2017, Piezo 1 was identified as a mechanosensor in dental mesenchymal stem cells, with its sensitivity to mechanical stimuli validated through low‐intensity pulsed ultrasound stimulation [30]. Between 2018 and 2022, systematic investigations significantly broadened the documented expression landscape of Piezo 1, confirming its presence in epithelial cells [31], gastric mucosa [32], neurons [33], glial cells [34], and immune cells [35]. After 2023, an increasing number of chemical modulators and their derivatives targeting Piezo 1 have been developed, enabling more comprehensive functional investigations [36, 37].

Until 2016, the first association between Piezo 1 and musculoskeletal degeneration was reported; research on its role in joint pathology has expanded substantially [38, 39]. By 2019, research had shifted toward the IVD, revealing that Piezo 1 is highly responsive to biomechanical stimuli in NP, and for the first time, elucidated the Piezo 1‐downstream mechanism that induces excessive apoptosis in NP cells [40]. In 2020, the linkage between Piezo 1‐mediated inflammation in NP cells and inflammasome activation was first elucidated [41]. From 2021 onward, extending from NP cells to AF and CEP, suggesting a broad and coordinated role for Piezo 1 across all major disc components [42]. In 2022, Piezo 1‐mediated downstream phenotypes—NP senescence, ECM homeostasis, and CEP inflammation—were elucidated for the first time [43, 44, 45]. In 2023, studies revealed that Piezo 1 mediates apoptosis in both AF and NP cells during IVDD, highlighting its role in mechanically induced cellular dysfunction across multiple disc regions [46, 47]. In 2024, its upregulation in degenerated CEP and association with cellular senescence and dysfunction drew increasing attention; for the first time, a potential link between Piezo 1 activity and ferroptosis in disc cells was proposed [48, 49, 50]. Most recently, in 2025, in vivo and in vitro studies have provided compelling evidence that Piezo 1 represents a promising therapeutic target for IVDD [51], including pharmacological inhibitors [52], gene editing [53], and nutritional and membrane‐lipid modulation [54]. These findings highlight the broad implications of Piezo 1 in the pathogenesis of IVDD across various disc components, suggesting its potential as a crucial player in IVDD. As research continues to uncover the intricate molecular mechanism involved, Piezo 1 emerges not only as a biomarker but also as a promising target for therapeutic intervention.

Structural Organization and Functional Domains of Piezo 1

Piezo 1 is a large trimeric transmembrane protein consisting of three subunits that form a “propeller” structure [55, 56]. The channel has a dome‐like conformation when embedded in the lipid bilayer of the cell, which is essential for its mechanosensitivity. Each subunit of the Piezo 1 structure contains a central ion‐conducting pore and two peripheral modules. A central ion‐conducting pore exists at the intersection of the three subunits, which consists of an outer helix, an inner helix, and C‐terminal extracellular domain [28] and allow Ca2+ influx in response to different types of external forces, such as fluid flow [57], pulling [58], and ultrasonic forces [59]. The peripheral mechanotransduction module consists of a beam‐like structure that acts as a mechanical tension transducer and an anchor domain capable of providing stability [60]. The propeller blade in the periphery can accommodate 38 transmembrane helices across the lipid bilayer extending outward and bending along the membrane, participating in sensing membrane curvature and tension (Figure 3). These structural features of Piezo 1 are crucial for its role in sensing mechanical stimuli and initiating cellular responses to mechanical forces.

Structure of the intervertebral disc, disc degeneration and schematic representation of the Piezo 1 channel (Bottom view). AF: annulus fibrosus; CEP: cartilaginous endplate; CTD: Iintracellular C‐terminal domain; NP: nucleus pulposus; PHs: peripheral helices. Piezo channels mediate mechanotransduction through a series of coordinated steps. Mechanical stimuli, including shear stress or membrane deformation, generate tension in the lipid bilayer, which is sensed by the Piezo channel's mechanosensitive domains. This tension triggers the opening of the central pore, allowing extracellular cations, particularly Ca2+, to flow into the cell. The influx of Ca2+ activates a range of downstream signaling pathways, including calpain activation [61] and ATP release [62], which in turn trigger various cellular processes. Through these signaling pathways, Piezo 1 activation triggers cellular events, emphasizing the critical role of Piezo channels in converting mechanical stimuli into biochemical signals that regulate key cellular functions.

Piezo 1 in IVDD Phenotypes and Mechanisms

In NP Region

Notochordal NP cells differentiate during embryogenesis in parallel with vertebral and intervertebral disc development, forming the gelatinous disc core while providing essential mechanical support in early disc formation [63, 64, 65].

The NP serves as the primary component responsible for maintaining the structural stability and biomechanical homeostasis of the IVD. It is mainly composed of NP cells and an ECM, which is rich in type II collagen and proteoglycans. Dysfunction of NP cells can disrupt the disc's mechanical equilibrium and structural integrity. Accumulating evidence suggests that IVDD often originates within the NP region, making it the most extensively studied tissue in the context of disc degeneration [44, 66, 67, 68]. Currently, the etiology of IVDD mediated by Piezo 1 in NP cells involves multiple factors, including ECM homeostasis, inflammation, senescence and regulated cell death (RCD) (Figure 4).

Piezo 1 mediates ECM homeostasis, inflammation, senescence, and RCD of NP cells.

ECM Homeostasis

The ECM is essential for the mechanical function and biomechanical microenvironment of NP cells, both in vivo and during in vitro culture. Previous studies have shown that the ECM tightly regulates NP cell behaviors—including proliferation, apoptosis, and differentiation—and that disruption of its homeostasis, namely an imbalance between anabolic and catabolic processes, is a hallmark of IVDD [69, 70]. Excessive compressive stress upregulates the mechanosensitive ion channel Piezo 1, leading to enhanced ECM degradation. Mounting evidence indicates that Piezo 1 modulates both ECM synthesis and breakdown, thereby driving the degenerative cascade in IVDD [49, 71, 72].

Under pathological mechanical overload or aberrant matrix stiffening, Piezo 1 undergoes conformational gating, permitting a sustained influx of Ca2+ into NP cells. The elevated intracellular calcium concentration activates calmodulin‐dependent kinase II (CaMKII) and stimulates the MAPK/ERK and PI3K/Akt pathways, culminating in nuclear translocation of NF‐κB and AP‐1 transcription factors [73]. These factors directly upregulate matrix‐degrading enzymes—matrix metalloproteinases (MMP‐3 and MMP‐13) and aggrecanases (ADAMTS‐4, ADAMTS‐5)—thereby accelerating the breakdown of proteoglycan and collagen II, which are principal components of the ECM [74, 75]. Simultaneously, Piezo 1‐induced Ca2+ signaling suppresses Sox9 and TGF‐β1‐mediated transcription of anabolic genes (COL2A1, ACAN), inhibiting de novo ECM synthesis. In addition to ERK1/2, p38 MAPK represents a critical regulator of ECM turnover [76]. Under mechanical overload and inflammatory stimulation, Piezo1‐dependent Ca2+ influx activates the p38 pathway, which upregulates MMP‐3, MMP‐13, and ADAMTS‐5, thereby promoting catabolic remodeling of the ECM. Concurrently, p38 MAPK suppresses Sox9‐dependent transcription of COL2A1 and ACAN, accelerating the loss of proteoglycans and collagen II [77, 78]. These findings indicate that p38 MAPK is an important mediator of Piezo1‐induced ECM remodeling and may act with ERK1/2 signaling, thereby reinforcing catabolic processes during IVDD. The expression of Piezo 1 and the ECM elasticity modulus increased in degenerative NP tissues. Bingjin Wang [42] conducted a comparative analysis of degenerative versus healthy lumbar NP cells and demonstrated that ECM rigidity positively correlates with Piezo 1 upregulation; this mechanosensitive response also triggered oxidative stress and ER stress in NP cells, whereas inhibition of Piezo 1 attenuated the senescence and apoptosis induced by ECM stiffening. Similarly, Ke and colleagues showed that a rigid ECM promotes Piezo 1‐mediated Ca2+ influx, which in turn activates ERK1/2 and drives phosphorylation of Drp1 at Ser616, culminating in mitochondrial fission and NP cell apoptosis [46]. Together, these findings establish ECM stiffness as a critical mechanical stimulus that activates Piezo 1 channels, regulates intracellular Ca2+ dynamics, and thereby contributes to the degenerative cascade in IVD.

Inflammation

Inflammation is recognized as a key driver of pathology. Pro‐inflammatory cytokines—including interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β), tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α), and interleukin‐6 (IL‐6)—act in concert to hasten IVDD by promoting ECM breakdown, inducing chemokine synthesis, recruiting immune cells, and triggering phenotypic shifts in disc cells [79]. The downstream signaling pathways activated by Piezo 1 play a critical role in modulating inflammatory responses. Elevated intracellular Ca2+ levels are a primary driving force for these downstream pro‐inflammatory pathways. This Ca2+ influx stimulates calcium‐dependent enzymes and transcription factors such as calmodulin, calcineurin, and calcium/calmodulin‐dependent kinase II, and modulates widespread inflammatory signaling pathways including nuclear factor‐κB (NF‐κB), mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK), and nuclear factor of activated T cells (NFAT) [73, 80, 81, 82]. NF‐κB is a member of the transcription factor family; excessive activation of Piezo 1 induces Ca2+ influx, which in turn activates NF‐κB signaling and drives the transcription of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, matrix metalloproteinases, and apoptosis‐related genes [68]. This NF‐κB–driven inflammatory cascade thus constitutes a critical link between Piezo 1 and the progression of IVDD. As a downstream effector of inflammation, MAPK/ERK contributes to NP cell degeneration by promoting ECM degradation, cellular senescence, and inflammatory cascades [83]. Recent evidence indicates that mechanotransduction through the mechanosensitive ion channel Piezo 1 regulates intracellular Ca2+ dynamics and activates MAPK/ERK signaling in response to mechanical stress [84]. However, it remains unclear whether Piezo 1‐mediated MAPK/ERK activation represents the sole link between abnormal mechanical loading and IVDD.

Under conditions of excessive mechanical stress, sustained activation of Piezo 1 leads to elevated cytokine levels, thereby fostering a pro‐inflammatory environment. Consistent with these mechanisms, Sheng Shi [43] observed upregulated Piezo 1 expression in degenerated human intervertebral discs; concomitantly, high‐intensity stress increased the expression of pro‐inflammatory cytokines—IL‐6, TNF‐α, and IL‐1β. Piezo 1‐mediated Ca2+ influx can activate the NF‐κB pathway, a central regulator of the inflammatory response. Moreover, emerging evidence suggests that Piezo 1 may interact with components of the NLRP3 inflammasome, affecting the maturation and secretion of pro‐inflammatory cytokines and thereby exacerbating IVDD. The NLRP3 inflammasome plays a detrimental role in metabolism and apoptosis in NP cells and contributes to IVD degeneration [85]. Mechanistically, inflammasome assembly facilitates caspase‐1 activation, which subsequently mediates proteolytic maturation and secretion of pro‐inflammatory cytokines IL‐1β and IL‐18 through pyroptotic pathways. Group from the Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University demonstrated the mechanism and the expression of piezo 1 and inflammasomes increased in a time‐dependent manner with mechanical stretching [41, 86, 87].

Senescence

As the primary tissue responsible for maintaining the structural stability and biomechanical balance of the IVD, dysfunction and loss of NP cells are considered hallmarks of IVDD [88]. Therefore, protecting NP cells from senescence and apoptosis through various mechanisms is crucial for delaying the progression of IVDD. Piezo 1 has been implicated in the degenerative process of NP cells within the IVD. In a study using disc tissue specimens obtained from surgical resection, Western blot and RT‐PCR analyses revealed that the expression level of Piezo 1 was significantly elevated in the degenerated disc group compared to the normal group [89]. Persistently activated Piezo 1 also elevates reactive oxygen species (ROS) and endoplasmic reticulum stress markers (GRP78 and CHOP), which promote NP cell apoptosis and senescence via the intrinsic (mitochondrial) pathway, further exacerbating IVDD [90, 91].

Recent research has underscored the crucial role of Piezo 1 in sensing extracellular mechanical cues, such as increased ECM stiffness and excessive compressive loading, and subsequently converting these physical signals into intracellular biochemical responses. Our previous discussion highlighted that the activation of Piezo 1 channels alters intracellular calcium concentrations, triggering signaling cascades associated with inflammation, oxidative stress, and endoplasmic reticulum stress. These responses ultimately influence NP cell fate, promoting cellular senescence and apoptosis, and accelerate IVDD. Cellular senescence is defined as an irreversible arrest of the cell cycle accompanied by characteristic phenotypic alterations, collectively referred to as the senescence‐associated secretory phenotype (SASP) [92]. This phenotype involves the upregulation of pro‐inflammatory cytokines such as IL‐1β, IL‐6, and IL‐8, which drive inflammatory cascades and exacerbate NP degeneration. Periostin, a matricellular protein from the fasciclin family, has been shown to mediate interactions between cells and the ECM [93, 94]. Notably, a study by Jinna Wu first demonstrated a self‐amplifying loop between NF‐κB and periostin, which enhances Piezo 1‐driven NP cell senescence under mechanical overload [44]. Importantly, senescent cells not only lose their anabolic capacity but also contribute to ECM breakdown, while increased ECM stiffness—another hallmark of IVDD—further intensifies oxidative stress‐induced senescence in NP cells, perpetuating a vicious cycle. Similarly, in vitro experiments by Sheng Shi demonstrated that mechanical stress upregulates Piezo 1 expression, which in turn elevates the levels of p53 and p16, two canonical cyclin‐dependent kinase inhibitors that serve as hallmarks of cellular senescence [43, 95]. Additionally, research by Wang revealed that Piezo 1 activation increases intracellular ROS and upregulates markers of ER stress such as GRP78 and CHOP, both of which are implicated in promoting senescence pathways via oxidative and ER stress mechanisms [42].

Regulated Cell Death

Throughout the progression of NP degeneration, multiple forms of RCD—including apoptosis, pyroptosis, and autophagy‐dependent cell death—have been observed to occur either independently or concurrently within the disc microenvironment [96]. Apoptosis, a tightly regulated form of programmed cell death, leads to a reduction in viable NP cell density and impairs the disc's capacity to maintain and remodel the ECM [97, 98, 99, 100]. Among the three major apoptotic pathways, which include the mitochondrial (intrinsic), death receptor (extrinsic), and ER stress pathways, the mitochondrial pathway is predominant due to its pivotal role in integrating diverse intracellular stress signals. Mechanistically, excessive mechanical loading induces Piezo 1 upregulation in NP cells, leading to abnormal Ca2+ influx that disrupts mitochondrial membrane integrity and triggers the release of cytochrome c. This, in turn, activates downstream caspases, including caspase‐1, 3, and 9, ultimately executing the apoptotic program [101]. Supporting this, Ke et al. [46] demonstrated that Piezo 1 activates the ERK1/2 signaling cascade and promotes phosphorylation of dynamin‐related protein 1 (Drp1) at Ser616, initiating mitochondrial fission as part of the intrinsic apoptotic response. Moreover, silencing Piezo 1 using short hairpin RNA (shRNA‐Piezo 1) has been shown to reduce mitochondrial depolarization and protect NP cells from mechanical stress‐induced apoptosis [40]. Notably, ECM stiffness—a known activator of Piezo 1—can also modulate apoptotic susceptibility, highlighting a mechanobiological feedback loop wherein stiffened ECM environments exacerbate Piezo 1‐mediated apoptotic signaling [27, 42].

Beyond apoptosis, recent studies have broadened the mechanistic understanding of Piezo 1's role in NP degeneration by implicating it in the regulation of ferroptosis. Pharmacological inhibition or conditional knockout of Piezo 1 significantly mitigated iron accumulation and ferroptosis, thereby slowing IVDD progression in preclinical models. Further expanding this view, another study demonstrated that mechanical stress‐induced Piezo 1 activation promotes Ca2+ influx, which triggers both ER stress and ferroptosis in NP cells [49]. In Piezo 1‐conditional knockout mice, these pathological changes were markedly attenuated. Moreover, the identification of the Selenium–SelK–GPX4 axis as a protective pathway that limits Piezo 1‐mediated Ca2+ overload provides a novel therapeutic target; this axis was shown to mitigate ER stress and ferroptosis, and to attenuate age‐related disc degeneration. These findings identify Piezo 1 as a critical upstream regulator of multiple cell death modalities in NP cells, encompassing senescence, apoptosis, and ferroptosis. These distinct yet interconnected pathological processes contribute to the complex pathogenesis of IVDD.

In CEP Region

The nutrition of the IVD relies on a pair of thin, translucent cartilage layers known as the CEPs, which cap the cranial and caudal surfaces of the NP and separate the IVD from the adjacent bony endplates of the vertebral bodies [102]. In humans, the CEP typically ranges from 0.1 to 1.6 mm in thickness and is composed predominantly of type II collagen, proteoglycans, and water [103]. The CEP contributes to IVD homeostasis through its regulation of nutrient and metabolite transport mechanisms [104, 105, 106]. Piezo 1 exhibits aberrant activation in response to mechanical loading alterations, particularly increased compressive stress and disrupted load distribution patterns. This pathophysiological response was consistently observed by Sun under experimentally applied axial impact loading to the rat spinal endplates [45]. This activation initiates a degenerative cascade that includes inflammation, chondrocyte dysfunction, and matrix degradation, thereby impairing nutrient diffusion and promoting disc degeneration.

Inflammation and Cartilage Calcification

Piezo 1 activation induced by axial impact to the CEP may contribute to degeneration by promoting inflammation and disrupting energy metabolism in CEP cells. Specifically, Piezo 1 promotes endplate inflammation by activating the expression of NLRP3 and IL‐1β, and this degeneration‐related mechanism occurs independently of whether structural damage to the endplate is present following impact [45]. Downstream of Piezo 1 activation, the transcription of catabolic MMPs and pro‐inflammatory genes such as IL‐1β and TNF‐α is also upregulated. Silencing Piezo 1 could reduce the activation of inflammation and delay the degenerative process. Peng's latest research identified the Ca2+/F‐actin/Yap signaling axis as a key mediator of Piezo 1‐driven CEP degeneration. Upregulation of Piezo 1 has been shown to promote inflammation and subsequently activate YAP through a non‐canonical F‐actin‐dependent pathway, thereby contributing to CEP calcification and accelerating the progression of IVDD [51]. A principal downstream outcome of Piezo1‐YAP signaling is the inhibition of SOX9. Activated YAP directly suppresses SOX9 activity, which disrupts chondrogenic programs by downregulating COL2A1 and other anabolic factors. It also induces the expression of MMP3 and MMP13, thereby promoting catabolic remodeling [51, 107]. CEP calcification is typically marked by decreased water content and elevated levels of proteoglycans, both of which compromise its nutrient diffusion properties. Critically, this Piezo 1‐driven calcification pathway recapitulates pathological changes observed in aging IVDs and scoliosis‐affected discs [108], suggesting shared mechanisms of nutrient deprivation and matrix remodeling across these conditions.

Apoptosis

Piezo 1 plays a role in mechanical conduction and apoptosis of cartilage. This statement is supported by the research of Chen [109], who showed that changes in mechanical stress in scoliosis patients can affect the apoptosis of CEP cells, and Piezo 1 plays a key role in this process through the Bcl‐2/Bax/Caspase‐3 signal pathway. In another study, both activation and inhibition of Piezo 1 were found to modulate CEP cell apoptosis under inflammatory conditions [48]. Li et al. [38, 52] obtained similar results in the study of articular chondrocytes. Their latest research indicates that the sensitization of Piezo 1 in articular chondrocytes may represent a pathogenic feedforward mechanism in osteoarthritis [110]. Given that both the CEP and articular cartilage (AC) are hyaline cartilage structures with similar ECM composition and mechanical loading functions, the shared involvement of Piezo 1 in mechanotransduction‐mediated apoptosis responses may reflect a common mechanobiological mechanism across load‐bearing cartilaginous tissues.

Senescence

Piezo 1 links mechanotransduction to mitochondrial dysfunction and plays a regulatory role in CEP cell senescence. Recent work demonstrates that Piezo 1 expression is markedly upregulated in degenerated CEP tissues and in CEP cells challenged with inflammatory stimuli such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS). Mechanistically, Piezo 1 activation under inflammatory stress triggers a deleterious Ca2+ dependent CaMKII‐Drp1 signaling axis that skews mitochondrial dynamics toward excessive fission and dysfunction, thereby promoting CEP cell senescence and ultimately exacerbating CEP degeneration [38, 48]. These findings suggest that Piezo 1 acts as a mechanosensitive integrator of mechanical and inflammatory stress, and serves as a critical upstream regulator of cellular senescence in cartilaginous endplate tissues.

Functionally and structurally, the CEP shares notable similarities with AC: both are avascular tissues originating from the mesoderm during embryonic development and composed of chondrocytes, fibro chondrocytes, and chondrocyte‐like cells [111, 112]. While CEP and AC are in distinct anatomical regions, they both facilitate the distribution and transmission of mechanical loads and depend on diffusion from surrounding vasculature and subchondral bone for nutrient exchange [105, 106, 113]. Given their physiological and developmental parallels, insights from AC research are highly relevant to CEP. Existing studies have clearly established Piezo 1 as a key mechanosensitive ion channel that mediates chondrocyte apoptosis and CEP degeneration. Increasing evidence has highlighted the pivotal role of Piezo 1 in mediating abnormal mechanical signals in AC cells [114, 115, 116]. Li et al. promoted that abnormal mechanical loading elevates Piezo 1 expression in chondrocytes, leading to excessive Ca2+ influx that activates endoplasmic reticulum stress via caspase‐12 and triggers the mitochondrial apoptotic cascade through upregulation of Bax and caspase‐3 and downregulation of Bcl‐2 [38, 117]. Beyond biomechanics, the osteoarthritis‐associated cytokine interleukin‐1α (IL‐1α) further amplifies this response: IL‐1α increases Piezo 1 transcription via a p38‐MAPK–HNF4–ATF2/CREBP1 axis, disrupts F‐actin organization, and renders chondrocytes hypersensitive to subsequent injurious loading, establishing a positive feedback loop that hastens cartilage degeneration [110]. From the AC to the CEP, a common pattern emerges: abnormal stress leads to Piezo 1 upregulation, excessive Ca2+ influx, and activation of apoptotic and mitochondrial fission pathways. These findings offer valuable insights, suggesting that chondrocytes in the AC, as part of the musculoskeletal system, may serve as a useful reference for understanding cellular responses in the CEP.

In AF Region

The AF is a highly organized fibrocartilaginous structure that forms the outer boundary of the IVD and is crucial for maintaining disc integrity under mechanical loading. Histologically, the AF is composed of approximately 15–25 concentric lamellae of collagen‐rich tissue, which are circumferentially aligned around the NP [13]. Each lamella primarily contains type I collagen fibers arranged in an alternating oblique orientation between adjacent layers, typically at 30 degrees to the horizontal plane. This arrangement confers the AF with substantial tensile strength and the ability to resist multidirectional mechanical stress [118]. Importantly, the AF displays marked regional heterogeneity, both in terms of composition and function. The outer AF is predominantly fibrous, characterized by densely packed type I collagen and elongated fibroblast‐like cells aligned parallel to the collagen fibers. In contrast, the inner AF exhibits a more cartilaginous phenotype, containing a mixture of type I and type II collagen as well as more rounded chondrocyte‐like cells embedded in a proteoglycan‐rich ECM. This gradient in matrix composition and cell phenotype reflects the functional transition between the mechanical boundary and the gelatinous core. Several studies have confirmed a positive correlation between abnormal mechanical loading and apoptosis in AF cells. However, research specifically addressing the functional role of Piezo 1 in the AF remains limited. To date, the most clearly elucidated evidence comes from a study conducted by Liu and colleagues [47]. Their findings demonstrated that under cyclic mechanical stretch, Piezo 1 in AF cells is activated by aberrant mechanical stress and they identified that Piezo 1 mediates AF cell apoptosis through the Ca2+/Calpain2/Caspase‐3 signaling pathway. In addition, abnormal shear stress is also an important biomechanical factor that contributes to IVDD and structural disruption of AF tissue [19]. Although direct evidence linking abnormal shear forces to apoptosis in AF cells is currently lacking, its pathological relevance should not be overlooked. These findings underscore the critical role of Piezo 1 in transducing aberrant mechanical signals into pro‐apoptotic cascades within AF cells, given the pivotal function of the AF in maintaining disc and spinal integrity, targeting Piezo 1‐mediated mechanotransduction presents a promising therapeutic avenue for mitigating AF degeneration and preserving intervertebral disc homeostasis (Figure 5).

Piezo 1‐mediated downstream pathways of CEP and AF cells.

Taken together, current evidence indicates that aberrant activation of Piezo 1 in IVD cells under conditions of mechanical overload and inflammatory stimulation elicits a spectrum of pathological responses. These include inflammation, oxidative stress, ECM degradation and impaired synthesis, mitochondrial dysfunction, cellular senescence, apoptosis, and ferroptosis. Notably, in the NP region, Piezo 1 uniquely contributes to ferroptosis by regulating intracellular iron homeostasis and lipid peroxidation, highlighting a region‐specific form of RCD. In the CEP, Piezo 1 activation contributes prominently to cartilage calcification, which impairs nutrient diffusion and exacerbates disc degeneration. In the AF, although current studies remain limited, the confirmed role of Piezo 1 in mediating mechanically induced apoptosis marks a promising starting point for future research into AF‐specific mechanotransduction pathways (Table 1).

Therapeutic Potential for Targeting Piezo 1 in IVDD

Recent findings suggest that manipulating Piezo 1‐mediated pathways can alleviate pathological cellular signaling cascades and nociceptive responses in degenerative conditions, thus highlighting its potential as a valuable therapeutic target for both tissue degeneration and associated pain.

Pharmacological Inhibitors

Emerging evidence suggests that pharmacological inhibitors hold promise as potential therapeutic candidates for a range of human diseases, including musculoskeletal disorders and cancers [120]. Grammostola mechanotoxin #4 (GsMTx4), a peptide inhibitor extracted from the venom of the tarantula Grammostola spatulata, was the first identified specific blocker of mechanosensitive ion channels, particularly Piezo 1 [121, 122]. Mechanistically, GsMTx4 functions primarily as a gating modifier rather than directly binding to the Piezo 1 channel's gating elements; it modifies local membrane tension, thus altering channel responsiveness to mechanical stimuli [123]. While GsMTx4's specificity includes a broader spectrum of mechanosensitive channels, Piezo 1 tends to be a primary target in tissues like the IVD where it is highly expressed. Due to its specificity, GsMTx4 has been extensively employed to explore Piezo 1's physiological and pathological roles, particularly within skeletal systems [124, 125, 126]. Li demonstrated that inhibition of Piezo 1 effectively alleviates IL‐1β–induced degenerative changes in cultured IVD, reversing these detrimental alterations [119]. Therefore, Piezo 1 inhibition provides not merely mechanistic insight but also a dual therapeutic approach, offering the potential for more effective management of IVDD. Notably, recent studies have underscored its therapeutic potential in mitigating IVDD. In degenerated human IVD, Piezo 1 overexpression disrupts extracellular matrix synthesis, elevates pro‐inflammatory cytokines (IL‐6, IL‐1β, and TNF‐α), and activates the NLRP3 inflammasome, promoting an imbalance favoring apoptosis over autophagy [41, 43]. Administration of GsMTx4 has been shown to partially reverse these detrimental effects, restoring cellular homeostasis and reducing inflammation. Additionally, GsMTx4 exerts protective effects on mechanically injured chondrocytes by specifically inhibiting the Piezo 1‐mediated Calcineurin/NFAT1 signaling cascade in osteoarthritis models, effectively attenuating inflammation and reducing chondrocyte apoptosis [52, 127]. Beyond skeletal disorders, GsMTx4 has demonstrated therapeutic potential in pulmonary diseases, neurological disorders, and oncology, including alleviation of pulmonary hypertension, reduction of neuroinflammation in intracerebral hemorrhage models, and inhibition of prostate and breast cancer cell proliferation [128, 129, 130].

In addition to GsMTx4, various other pharmacological agents have been identified that modulate Piezo 1 activity through distinct mechanisms. Non‐competitive inhibitors, such as gadolinium and ruthenium red, are commonly employed experimental tools to probe Piezo 1 function [131, 132]. Among competitive inhibitors, Dooku1—an analogue of the Piezo 1 agonist Yoda1—has attracted interest for its ability to antagonize Piezo 1 activation, particularly in cardiovascular research [133]. Natural compounds have also emerged as promising Piezo 1 modulators. Tubeimoside I, a triterpenoid saponin from Bolbostemmatis Rhizoma, has been shown to effectively suppress Yoda1‐induced Ca2+ influx in endothelial cells [134]. Salvianolic acid B, a key component of Salvia miltiorrhiza , modulates Piezo 1‐mediated calcium signaling and enhances MAPK/ERK1/2 activity, facilitating endothelial differentiation with implications for cardiovascular regeneration [37]. Additional compounds such as Escin and Jatrorrhizine are being investigated for their vascular protective roles via potential Piezo 1 inhibition [135, 136]. Continued efforts to optimize their specificity, bioavailability, and safety profiles will be critical for advancing Piezo 1‐targeted pharmacotherapies toward clinical application in IVDD and beyond.

Because Piezo1 supports endothelial and erythrocyte physiology, systemic inhibition may carry vascular and hematologic risks; disc‐confined dosing is therefore preferred in early trials. Initial clinical exploration should prioritize single‐dose, image‐guided intradiscal delivery and depot formulations to confine exposure, extend local residence, and mitigate off‐target liability.

Gene Therapy and RNA Interference Approaches

Given gene‐therapy and RNA‐interference can selectively suppress Piezo 1 expression—thereby interrupting the calcium‐dependent apoptotic and inflammatory cascades that drive NP, CEP and AF cells degeneration—these modalities constitute a rational strategy for restoring cellular homeostasis and slowing IVDD. In experimental models, key pathological features of IVDD—including cellular senescence, apoptosis, ECM loss, and increased expression of pro‐inflammatory cytokines—can be effectively mitigated by targeting Piezo 1. Gene silencing or editing of Piezo 1 has proven to be an efficient strategy for interrupting this degenerative cascade. Compared to pharmacological inhibition, genetic approaches offer greater specificity and precision in modulating Piezo 1‐mediated signaling. Gene‐silencing strategies targeting Piezo 1 in IVDD primarily employ RNA interference (RNAi) using small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) or shRNAs. Both exploit the endogenous RNAi machinery to degrade Piezo 1 mRNA but differ in delivery and duration [137]. Synthetic siRNAs allow rapid and straightforward transfection into disc cells, yet their effect is transient due to degradation or dilution. In contrast, shRNAs—typically delivered via plasmids or viral vectors—enable sustained intracellular expression of siRNA‐like sequences, resulting in more durable and potent gene silencing [40]. Targeting Piezo 1 via RNAi has been shown to significantly modulate inflammatory signaling in disc cell models. Sun et al. [41]. demonstrated that Piezo 1 silencing effectively degenerated discs inflammasome activation. These molecular changes collectively enhance disc cell viability and function. There is also compelling evidence that Piezo 1 knockdown mitigates oxidative stress and apoptosis induced by abnormal mechanical strain. By limiting pathological Ca2+ influx, Piezo 1 silencing reduces intracellular ROS accumulation and alleviates ER stress, as indicated by decreased GRP78 and CHOP expression in NP cells [42]. Functionally, this leads to improved cell survival, with reduced apoptosis and pyroptosis under injurious mechanical conditions. Beyond inflammation and cell death, Piezo 1‐targeted RNAi also preserves ECM integrity and protects CEP structure. Notably, Piezo 1 activation—whether pharmacologically via Yoda1 or through mechanical overloading—upregulates RUNX2 and type X collagen in CEP chondrocytes, promoting osteogenic differentiation. In contrast, genetic or pharmacologic inhibition of Piezo 1 prevents this shift, thereby maintaining endplate homeostasis [45, 51]. Li employed AggrecanCreERT2, an inducible recombinase targeting all IVD cells, to achieve conditional knockout (cKO) of Piezo 1, which effectively alleviated IVDD [119]. CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing represents an emerging strategy in the field of disc degeneration research. Studies have shown that CRISPR can be used not only to generate disease models but also to target key degenerative mediators in the IVD, such as matrix‐degrading enzymes and pro‐inflammatory cytokines [53]. Importantly, Piezo 1 plays a vital physiological role in maintaining nutrient diffusion and cellular metabolism in the healthy disc. Therefore, indiscriminate gene editing may risk disrupting essential homeostatic functions and could ultimately be counterproductive. To preserve essential mechanosensation, transient or regulatable gene‐silencing systems and disc‐restricted vectors will be crucial; preclinical packages should include large‐animal distribution, durability, and reversibility data alongside biomarker‐anchored efficacy.

Nutritional and Membrane‐Lipid Modulation

Most pharmacological strategies for modulating Piezo 1 focus on inhibiting excessive mechanotransduction. However, nutritional modulation has emerged as a compelling alternative. By altering membrane lipid composition through diet or supplementation, Piezo 1 activity may be indirectly regulated. As a trimeric mechanosensitive ion channel gated by membrane tension, Piezo 1 is highly responsive to the physical properties of the surrounding lipid bilayer, including curvature, thickness, and fluidity. Recent studies have demonstrated that incorporation of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) increases membrane fluidity and reduces bending stiffness, thereby influencing the channel's activation and inactivation kinetics. In particular, ω3 PUFA supplementation reduces chondrocyte responsiveness to both mechanical compression and Piezo 1 agonists, effectively dampening mechanotransduction [54]. Controlled experiments further support a causal relationship between specific lipid species and Piezo 1 activity. For example, Romero et al. reported that culturing cells with different fatty acids led to predictable alterations in Piezo 1 current properties. Additionally, in a rodent model of IVDD, long‐term dietary supplementation with ω3 PUFAs (EPA and DHA) significantly slowed disease progression [138, 139]. Although these protective effects were largely attributed to the anti‐inflammatory properties of ω3 PUFAs, the findings are consistent with a complementary mechanism involving membrane stabilization and Piezo 1 modulation. This dual action means dietary ω3 could address two major aspects of degeneration: inflammation and aberrant mechanical stress signaling. Together, these data support a tangible link between dietary lipid intake and disc health, and provide a rationale for further investigation into lipid‐based strategies for regulating Piezo 1 in IVDD. Given the modest and systemic nature of lipid remodeling, dietary ω‐3 PUFAs are best positioned as adjuncts to local Piezo 1‐directed interventions, with pragmatic surrogate endpoints to document incremental benefit.

Summary and Prospects

IVDD underpins prevalent spinal disorders and chronic back pain, imposing substantial personal and societal burdens. A decade of work now positions Piezo 1 as a central transducer linking aberrant mechanical load to the biochemical cascades that erode disc integrity. Overactivation of Piezo 1 in NP, AF and CEP cells fuels a multifaceted degeneration program encompassing inflammatory cytokine release, NLRP3 inflammasome priming, oxidative and endoplasmic‐reticulum stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, matrix catabolism, and cell‐fate shifts toward apoptosis or senescence. Within the intervertebral disc, mechanistic studies of Piezo 1 have predominantly focused on NP cells, followed by CEP cells, while data on AF remain comparatively limited. This discrepancy likely stems from a combination of scientific and technical challenges. NP cells are highly sensitive to mechanical and biochemical stimuli and are widely recognized as early contributors to IVDD, making them a preferred model for studying mechanotransduction. In contrast, the AF has a complex lamellar structure with pronounced regional heterogeneity in both cellular composition and biomechanical properties, which makes the isolation and long‐term culture of viable AF cells technically demanding. Additionally, AF cells have relatively low metabolic activity, and there is a lack of established in vitro models that can recapitulate the native anisotropic mechanical environment of the AF. These challenges have collectively hindered detailed investigations of Piezo 1 and other mechanosensitive pathways in AF tissue. Overcoming these barriers will be crucial for developing a more integrated understanding of Piezo 1‐mediated signaling across all regions of the IVD (Table 2).

Piezo1, as a mechanosensitive ion channel, plays a crucial role in maintaining the mechanical homeostasis of bones, joints, and muscles, and holds considerable potential for clinical translation. Therapeutic exploration has advanced along three complementary fronts. First, channel‐directed peptides and small molecules—exemplified by GsMTx4—attenuate Ca2+‐driven inflammatory and catabolic signaling in pre‐clinical IVDD models. Second, gene‐ and RNA‐based silencing affords precise, durable suppression of Piezo 1, reversing inflammatory and apoptotic markers and preserving extracellular matrix architecture in animal and cell studies. Third, nutritional modulation of membrane lipids, particularly ω‐3 polyunsaturated fatty acids, softens bilayer mechanics and shortens Piezo 1 open time, suggesting a non‐invasive route to temper pathological mechanotransduction. However, current research on IVDD is limited to preclinical studies involving in vitro, in vivo, and human tissue samples, which presents challenges for clinical application. With respect to mechanisms, obstacles include species‐specific differences, insufficiently elucidated pathways, and discrepancies between experimental models and actual disease processes. With respect to treatment, difficulties remain in disc‐targeted delivery, dosage determination, and safety concerns. With the progressive refinement of disease models, the integration of multi‐omics technologies, and rapid advances in biomaterials and drug delivery systems, the prospects for targeting Piezo1 in IVDD are promising.

In summary, while pre‐clinical data are encouraging, continued interdisciplinary efforts will determine whether modulating this channel can ultimately shift IVDD management from symptomatic relief toward true disease modification.
