Maternal mortality according to state abortion legislative climate following the US Supreme Court's Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization ruling
Authors: Emily Nuss, Mari Iwasaki, Lindsay Robbins, Peggy Ye, Misa Hayasaka, George Saade, Tetsuya Kawakita
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveWe aimed to evaluate how maternal mortality rates have evolved across US states following the Dobbs ruling, while accounting for the confounding influence of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID‐19) pandemic.Study designWe conducted a retrospective analysis of publicly available data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Wide‐ranging ONline Data for Epidemiologic Research (CDC WONDER) database from January 2018 to December 2024. This study period was divided into three intervals: (1) pre‐COVID (January 2018 to February 2020), (2) pandemic (March 2020 to September 2022), and (3) post‐Dobbs (October 2022 to December 2024). Using the Kaiser Family Foundation classification, we designated 13 states as abortion‐prohibited and 25 states plus the District of Columbia as abortion‐supportive, based on contemporaneous legislation. The remaining 12 states were excluded from the analysis. Our primary outcome was maternal mortality, defined as “the death of a woman while pregnant or within 42 days of termination of pregnancy.” To account for the clustering of outcomes by state, we employed generalized estimating equations with a Poisson distribution to compute difference‐in‐difference (DID) estimates with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs).ResultsAmong 19,023,203 births included in the analysis, 6,633,011 (34.9%) occurred in abortion‐prohibited states, and 12,390,192 (65.1%) occurred in abortion‐supportive states. In all study periods, abortion‐prohibited states exhibited higher maternal mortality rates than abortion‐supportive states. This disparity widened notably during the pandemic period (DID 8.7 [95% CI 2.6, 14.7] per 100,000 births). In contrast, the post‐Dobbs period was not associated with a further increase in this overall disparity (DID −0.7 [95% CI −6.1, 4.7] per 100,000 births). When each abortion‐prohibited state was compared individually to the pooled abortion‐supportive states, Texas and Louisiana showed a widening maternal mortality disparity in the post‐Dobbs period compared to the pre‐COVID period. Conversely, Alabama, Arkansas, Indiana, Kentucky, Mississippi, and South Dakota demonstrated a narrowing of this gap relative to abortion‐supportive states.ConclusionMaternal mortality rates remained consistently higher in abortion‐prohibited states compared with abortion‐supportive states throughout the study period. The COVID‐19 pandemic substantially magnified these preexisting differences, whereas the Dobbs ruling exerted variable effects across individual states. These findings underscore both the persistent inequities in women's health outcomes and the urgency of implementing targeted policies to address and mitigate such disparities nationwide.Keywords: abortion legislation, difference‐in‐difference, disparity, inequity

INTRODUCTION

In June 2022, the US Supreme Court's ruling in Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization overturned both Roe v. Wade and Planned Parenthood v. Casey. This effectively eliminated federal abortion protections and restored regulatory authority over abortion care to individual states [1]. In the immediate aftermath, abortion legislation diverged markedly across the country: 14 states completely banned abortion outside minimal exceptions, and four states restricted abortion to gestational ages less than 12 weeks [2]. In contrast, 17 states enacted laws to affirmatively protect abortion access by codifying abortion rights [3]. This fragmented landscape has amplified regional inequities in reproductive health care [2].

Abortion is a widely recognized safe procedure globally [4, 5, 6]. Prior to the Dobbs ruling, approximately 40% of individuals with unintended pregnancies in the United States elected to terminate the pregnancy [7]. Following the implementation of a restrictive abortion law, there was an increase in live births [8, 9]. Prior to the Dobbs ruling, multiple studies have shown that states with stringent abortion restrictions also report higher rates of maternal mortality [10, 11, 12]. Indeed, a decision analysis comparing restrictive and supportive legal frameworks suggested that restricting abortion access could lead to an additional 13,000 maternal deaths each year nationwide [13].

Although the national birth rate has continued to decline, emerging evidence shows that states with restrictive abortion laws have seen relative increases in live births and maternal mortality. However, data on the immediate impact of Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization remain limited. Prior investigations have predominantly assessed pre‐Dobbs conditions or examined single‐state scenarios, leaving critical gaps in understanding the ruling's broader consequences. Potential mechanisms linking restrictive abortion laws to higher maternal mortality include increased rates of pregnancies carried to term [8, 9], when the majority of serious complications occur, and reduced access to obstetric care due to the loss of maternity care providers [14], contributing to “maternity care deserts” [15]. In this study, we sought to evaluate the initial effects of the Dobbs ruling on maternal mortality rates, with particular attention to varying state‐level legislative approaches and the concurrent influence of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID‐19) pandemic.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This was a retrospective analysis using publicly available data from the CDC WONDER (Center for Disease Control and Prevention, Wide‐ranging ONline Data for Epidemiologic Research) database from January 2018 to December 2024 [16]. The CDC WONDER database comprises both the Natality online databases and multiple cause‐of‐death databases. Live birth counts were obtained from the Natality online databases, which track births in the 50 US states and the District of Columbia among US residents [17]. Mortality data were derived from the multiple cause‐of‐death databases, which are based on official death certificates for US residents [18]. Each death certificate includes one underlying cause of death, up to 20 additional causes, and key demographic variables. Data collection was completed in March 2025, at which time 2024 data remained provisional.

We restricted analyses to individuals aged 15–44 years because prior research suggests a false‐positive rate exceeding 80% in maternal mortality classification for individuals aged 45 years or older [19]. Although we relied on two distinct data sources, consistent inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied across both. This study was deemed exempt from Eastern Virginia Medical School Institutional Review Board (IRB) review.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the maternal mortality ratio (MMR) within 42 days postpartum, expressed per 100,000 live births. Consistent with World Health Organization guidelines, maternal death is defined as “the death of a woman while pregnant or within 42 days of termination of pregnancy, irrespective of the duration and the site of the pregnancy, from any cause related to or aggravated by the pregnancy or its management, but not from accidental or incidental causes” [20]. We identified that such deaths using the International Statistical Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD‐10) codes A34, O00–O95, and O98–O99 were considered [21].

Secondary outcomes included the MMR within 365 days postpartum (which captures late maternal deaths, ICD‐10 codes O96 and O97) per 100,000 live births, and all‐cause mortality among reproductive‐aged females. The inclusion of the 365‐day postpartum window accounts for the persistently elevated risk of pregnancy‐associated mortality during the first year postpartum [22]. All‐cause mortality in reproductive‐aged females was examined to control for potential misclassification in linking pregnancy status to death certificates [11].

Exposures

The exposures of interest were the study period (pre‐COVID, pandemic, and post‐Dobbs) and state‐level abortion legislation. The pre‐COVID period spanned from January 2018 to February 2020; the pandemic period was defined from March 2020 through September 2022, guided by the abrupt rise in COVID‐19 deaths and new hospital admissions [23]. According to CDC WONDER data, the number of COVID‐19‐related maternal deaths fell below 10 per month nationwide after September 2022, supporting the transition to the post‐Dobbs period (October 2022 to December 2024) [16]. This latter interval also accounts for the fact that most abortion restrictions were in place by late September 2022 following the Supreme Court's decision on June 24, 2022 [24].

States were classified as abortion‐prohibited if they enacted a total abortion ban from September 2022 to December 2024, and as abortion‐supportive if abortion remained legal beyond 22 weeks’ gestation throughout the study period [24]. Accordingly, abortion‐prohibited states included Alabama, Arkansas, Idaho, Indiana, Kentucky, Louisiana, Mississippi, Missouri, Oklahoma, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, and West Virginia, whereas abortion‐supportive states included 25 states (Alaska, California, Colorado, Connecticut, Delaware, District of Columbia, Hawaii, Illinois, Maine, Maryland, Massachusetts, Michigan, Minnesota, Montana, Nevada, New Hampshire, New Jersey, New Mexico, New York, Oregon, Pennsylvania, Rhode Island, Vermont, Virginia, Washington, and Wyoming) and the District of Columbia (Figure 1). North Dakota was excluded due to a unique legislative timeline in which a ban instituted in April 2023 was subsequently blocked in September 2024 [25]. An additional 11 states that imposed gestational limits ranging from 6 to 22 weeks were not included. The final analysis thus comprised 13 abortion‐prohibited states and 25 abortion‐supportive states, plus the District of Columbia.

Statistical methods

We initially evaluated parallel trends in monthly aggregated outcomes during the pre‐COVID period. Spline variables for each period (pre‐COVID, pandemic, and post‐Dobbs) were incorporated to account for potential nonlinear time effects. We employed generalized estimating equations (GEEs) with a Poisson regression, using an exchangeable correlation structure and robust standard errors [26]. From this model, we plotted predicted incidence over time. Parallel trends were deemed confirmed if, in the hypothetical absence of the pandemic and Dobbs, outcomes between abortion‐prohibited and abortion‐supportive states evolved similarly during the pre‐COVID timeframe (details are provided in Method S1).

After verifying parallel trends, data were aggregated according to each period (pre‐COVID, pandemic, and post‐Dobbs). Employing GEEs with a Poisson distribution and a log link function, we introduced interaction terms to capture the combined effects of abortion legislation and the pandemic or post‐Dobbs timeframe. We adjusted for Medicaid expansion status as a potential confounder, given its association with maternal health outcomes and health care access [27]. Detailed information is available in Method S2. To estimate predicted incidence, incidence rate differences, and DID (difference‐in‐difference) values, we conducted a post‐estimation analysis [28]. The pre‐COVID period served as the referent, given the recognized surge in maternal mortality during the pandemic and the relative rarity of COVID‐19‐associated maternal deaths in the post‐Dobbs period, which could otherwise bias comparisons. Additional details of these interaction analyses are presented in Method S3.

Secondary analyses

To assess the robustness of the state classification, we performed a leave‐one‐out sensitivity analysis, sequentially excluding each state to identify any disproportionate influence on the final results. We also conducted a separate analysis comparing each abortion‐prohibited state individually against the pooled group of abortion‐supportive states to elucidate state‐specific effects. Statistical significance was set at a two‐sided p value < 0.05. All analyses were carried out using Stata 18 (StataCorp).

Figure 5 displays the state‐by‐state DID estimates for maternal mortality within 42 days postpartum, comparing each abortion‐prohibited state with the abortion‐supportive reference. Most abortion‐prohibited states had positive DIDs during the pandemic, signifying a widening gap in maternal mortality relative to abortion‐supportive states. The largest increases were observed in Tennessee (19.7 [95% CI 14.7, 24.7]), Texas (13.2 [95% CI 8.2, 18.2]), Mississippi (10.7 [95% CI 5.7, 15.7]), and South Dakota (10.6 [95% CI 5.6, 15.5]). Kentucky, by contrast, exhibited a negative DID, indicating that the gap narrowed during the pandemic period. In the post‐Dobbs analysis, the direction and magnitude of DIDs varied across states. West Virginia (9.4 [95% CI 3.2, 15.6]), Texas (4.7 [95% CI 2.9, 6.4]), and Louisiana (1.8 [95% CI 0.1, 3.6]) showed notably widening disparities. Conversely, Alabama (−19.2 [95% CI −20.9, −17.5]), Arkansas (−11.3 [95% CI −19.4, −3.2]), Indiana (−3.9 [95% CI −7.3, −0.6]), Kentucky (−11.6 [95% CI −19.9, −3.3]), Mississippi (−2.5 [95% CI −4.2, −0.8]), and South Dakota (−5.7 [95% CI −10.3, −1.1]), demonstrated negative DIDs, reflecting a narrowing of the gap compared with supportive states. DISCUSSION

In abortion‐prohibited states, higher incidences of maternal mortality within 42 days, maternal mortality within 365 days, and all‐cause mortality among reproductive‐aged females were consistently observed compared to abortion‐supportive states. The pandemic significantly exacerbated these disparities. Disparities in maternal mortality rates and other secondary outcomes did not worsen in the post‐Dobbs period when compared to the pre‐COVID period. Despite these overall trends, our findings revealed substantial variability in maternal mortality outcomes among individual abortion‐prohibited states. Some states, such as Louisiana, South Dakota, and Texas, experienced marked increases in maternal mortality disparities relative to abortion‐supportive states during the pandemic or post‐Dobbs periods. Conversely, a few states—including Indiana and Missouri—showed evidence of a narrowing gap.

The results of our analysis are consistent with previous studies published before the Dobbs ruling, demonstrating that states that ultimately enacted restrictive abortion legislation experience higher rates of maternal mortality [11]. Harper et al. conducted a retrospective cohort study in 2023, also using CDC WONDER data, finding that each additional state abortion regulation correlated with an increase in maternal and infant mortality; maternal mortality was significantly lower in states with moderate legislation compared to those with restrictive legislation (−5.8 per 100,000 live births; 95% CI −9.9, −1.7) [11].

In this study, we found that the disparity in maternal mortality rates between abortion‐prohibited and abortion‐supportive states widened during the COVID‐19 pandemic. Structural disadvantages in restrictive states, such as reduced access to comprehensive reproductive health services and shortages of maternity care providers, could have already been present before the pandemic and, in some cases, were intensified by abortion restrictions implemented before Dobbs. During the COVID‐19 pandemic, health care services were limited to those deemed “essential,” and in several conservative states, attorneys general classified abortion as “nonessential,” contrary to recommendations from the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) that abortion is essential, time‐sensitive care [29, 30]. The pandemic likely magnified these vulnerabilities by straining health system capacity, disrupting prenatal and postpartum care, and delaying the management of complications, thereby contributing to the widening mortality gap.

Our study did not demonstrate a significant change in the maternal mortality ratio in the post‐Dobbs period among states that increased their abortion restrictions, although the overall maternal mortality rate remained consistently higher in abortion‐prohibited states. Addante et al. performed a retrospective study examining maternal mortality in the United States from 1995 to 2017 [31], reporting that among states that increased their abortion restrictions, maternal mortality rose at a steeper rate than in states that remained neutral or protective of abortion rights [31]. We suggest that the impact of restrictive abortion legislation had already manifested prior to the Dobbs ruling; consequently, the additional effect post‐Dobbs was not statistically significant at the population level.

Our study was based on the premise that abortion bans reduce abortion rates, increase live births, and thereby elevate maternal mortality risk. Nationally, abortion rates have increased since Dobbs, driven by expanded provision in protective neighboring states, increased availability of private abortion funds, and growth in medication abortion via telemedicine [32]. Prior reports have documented increases in live births, potentially representing a population at heightened risk for pregnancy‐related complications [8]. The absence of a statistically significant post‐Dobbs rise in maternal mortality rates may reflect the rarity of maternal death as an outcome, particularly in the early post‐ruling period, as well as partial mitigation by these protective strategies. Nonetheless, access to such mitigating resources is not equitably distributed; individuals from racial and ethnic minority groups and those with fewer financial resources may face greater barriers to traveling for care or navigating alternative abortion pathways, and are therefore more likely to experience increased birth rates and the associated risks of pregnancy and childbirth.

While overall disparities were consistent, substantial variability emerged among abortion‐prohibited states. Some, such as Louisiana, South Dakota, and Texas, exhibited notable increases in maternal mortality disparities, whereas states like Indiana and Missouri showed evidence of a narrowing gap. These patterns may reflect differences in health care infrastructure, socioeconomic conditions, and policy implementation timelines, underscoring the complexity of state‐level responses. For example, Alabama and Kentucky both implemented Medicaid postpartum coverage extensions to 12 months in 2022–2023, which may have improved access to care for high‐risk individuals despite abortion bans [33]. Future research should focus on local health systems, provider availability, and social determinants of health to clarify the factors driving this heterogeneous response.

Moreover, although our study primarily centered on the influence of restrictive abortion legislation on maternal mortality and related outcomes, it remains uncertain whether marginalized populations disproportionately suffered from the pandemic or the Dobbs ruling. Groups such as people of color, low‐income individuals, incarcerated populations, and rural communities often experience higher baseline health risks [9]. Future investigations should evaluate how post‐Dobbs legislative changes specifically affect these vulnerable populations.

Health care providers in abortion‐prohibited states must be aware of the higher risks of maternal mortality and all‐cause mortality in reproductive‐aged females compared to abortion‐supportive states. This reality calls for greater vigilance, comprehensive prenatal care, and improved access to postnatal care to mitigate these risks. Although our findings indicate that the Dobbs ruling was not associated with a statistically significant worsening of the maternal mortality gap, the pronounced exacerbation during the pandemic period suggests that individuals in abortion‐prohibited states were more vulnerable to this national emergency. Consequently, our findings underscore the importance of equitable access to health care services.

Strengths and limitations

One strength of this study is that we had access to comprehensive US birth and mortality data spanning a broad time range, permitting a national‐level examination. Although we ultimately classified only 13 states as abortion‐prohibited and 25 states (plus the District of Columbia) as abortion‐supportive based on legislative criteria, including state‐by‐state information allowed for robust comparisons and lends generalizability to the results. Another notable strength is the use of advanced statistical methods—including DID analysis—which accounts for both temporal events (e.g., the COVID‐19 pandemic and Dobbs ruling) and pre‐existing trends, offering insights that surpass simple before‐and‐after or single‐period comparisons. Finally, our assessment of state‐by‐state variability in maternal mortality outcomes provides a granular understanding of how local contexts and policy implementations can influence maternal health, thereby adding depth to the existing literature.

Owing to its retrospective nature, we cannot definitively claim causality between restrictive abortion legislation and maternal mortality. Although DID analysis partially accounts for time‐varying confounders under the parallel trends assumption, residual unmeasured confounders remain possible, including variations in chronic health conditions, race, ethnicity, and other social determinants across states. Additionally, this analysis cannot account for unique issues at the hospital level. For example, certain hospitals may have institutional bans on abortion care, which may be stricter than those at the state level. Further, our time‐based categorization—where births were allocated to the period in which they occurred—may have diluted potential associations, since pregnancies conceived before one period might end during the next. We additionally relied on provisional data from 2024, which may be subject to underreporting lags [28]. For this reason, our modeling strategy focused on predicted counts, potentially mitigating the limitations of provisional data. According to the National Vital Statistics System, provisional maternal mortality statistics have historically been highly concordant with final data, with 99% agreement in total counts and minimal variation in rates (<1%) once final data are released [34]. The relatively short post‐Dobbs window may not fully capture the long‐term health effects of legislative changes, given the time required for new policies to be enacted, implemented, and observable in public health metrics. An additional limitation is the reliance on birth and death certificate data, which are subject to potential misclassification and underreporting.

CONCLUSION

Maternal mortality rates remained consistently higher in abortion‐prohibited states compared to abortion‐supportive states. Although the COVID‐19 pandemic substantially magnified these disparities, the Dobbs ruling did not further widen the gap at the aggregate level. Nonetheless, considerable heterogeneity exists at the state level, underscoring the need for policy interventions tailored to local contexts. These findings highlight the ongoing inequities in women's health and pregnancy outcomes across the United States, underlining an urgent need for targeted public health and legislative strategies to address and alleviate these disparities.
