In vivo assessment of Berger’s space in primary angle closure disease using AS-OCT: a comparative study
Authors: Siyi Tan, Dongjun Li, Dianfeng Liu, Weiqi Wang, Wenjun He, Bingsong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo observe Berger’s space in vivo in eyes with primary angle closure disease (PACD) and to identify factors associated with its visibility.MethodsThis cross-sectional observational study was conducted at a tertiary center and included 81 participants (109 eyes) diagnosed with PACD. Anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT, CASIA2) images were used to evaluate the visibility of the Berger’s space, and ocular biometric parameters were measured. The visibility of the Berger’s space was compared among acute primary angle closure (APAC), fellow eyes of APAC (F-APAC), and chronic primary angle closure (glaucoma) (CPAC[G]). Additionally, ocular biometric parameters were compared between groups with visible and invisible Berger’s space, and generalized estimating equations (GEEs) were employed in the regression analysis to account for inter-eye correlations.ResultsThe APAC group exhibited the highest prevalence of visible Berger’s space at 36.67%, which was significantly greater than the rates of 9.38% in the F-APAC group and 17.02% in the CPAC(G) group (p = 0.028). The visible Berger’s space group was younger (p = 0.008) with narrower anterior chamber angle, and anteriorly shifted lens position (p = 0.031) compared to the invisible Berger’s space group. Factors associated with visible Berger’s space included acute angle closure (p = 0.017, odds ratio = 5.650, 95% CI: 1.325–24.084) and younger age (p = 0.017, odds ratio = 0.937, 95% CI: 0.889–0.989).ConclusionAcute angle closure and younger age were both associated with higher visibility of the Berger’s space. Eyes with visible Berger’s space are younger, have narrower anterior chamber angles and more anteriorly shifted lens positions.Keywords: Berger’s space, Primary angle closure disease, Biometric parameters

Introduction

Glaucoma is one of the leading causes of irreversible blindness worldwide. Primary open angle glaucoma (POAG) and primary angle closure glaucoma (PACG) are expected to affect 111.8 million persons worldwide in 2040 [1]. The prevalence of PACG is highest among individuals of Asian descent [2]. PACD is considered a spectrum of conditions with similar pathology, including primary angle closure suspect (PACS), primary angle closure (PAC), and the above-mentioned PACG [3]. Previous studies suggest that individuals with PACD exhibit various anatomical risk factors, including a smaller corneal curvature radius, shallow anterior chamber, thick iris, thick lens, anterior lens position, anterior ciliary body position, and shorter axial length (AL) [4–8].

Berger’s space is defined as the space between the posterior lens capsule (PLC) and the anterior hyaloid membrane (AHM), encircled by Wieger’s ligament [9–11]. Wieger’s ligament measures 8–9 mm in diameter and 1–2 mm in width in adults, with the outer border marked by Egger’s line [12, 13]. Under normal conditions, the PLC adheres tightly to the AHM, making the Berger’s space a potential chamber. Figure 1 shows the structure of the Berger’s space. The development of anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT) technology has greatly facilitated in vivo observation of this space. Previous studies have used AS-OCT to observe the Berger’s space and they have reported the prevalence of visible Berger’s space in general population range from 0.9% to 18.6% [9–11, 14]. These results suggest that Berger’s space visibility is low under physiological conditions, with age and AL being important factors influencing visibility [9–11, 14]. Kawasaki et al. [15] observed disruption of the PLC-AHM interface due to elevated intraocular pressure (IOP) in porcine eyes. A history of acute attack in angle closure glaucoma is accompanied by a sharp increase in IOP, thus we speculate that patients with PACD are accompanied by aqueous humor misdirection and the disruption of the PLC-AHM interface, which leads to an increase in the visibility of the Berger’s space. Therefore, in this study, we aim to use AS-OCT to investigate the visibility of the Berger’s space in PACD and the influencing factors, further exploring the relationship between anterior angle closure and Berger’s space.

Methods

This cross-sectional study was conducted at the Ophthalmology clinics of Beijing Tongren Hospital, Capital Medical University, China. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Tongren Hospital before patient enrollment, and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki were strictly adhered to throughout. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants in the study.

Participants

Participants diagnosed with PACD were recruited from the glaucoma clinics of the Ophthalmology Department at Beijing Tongren Hospital, Capital Medical University, China, between July 2023 and March 2024. These patients were categorized into three groups: (1) acute primary angle closure (APAC); (2) fellow eyes of APAC (F-APAC); (3) chronic primary angle closure (glaucoma) (CPAC[G]).

APAC was defined by the following criteria [16]: (1) the presence of at least one of the following symptoms: periocular pain or headache, nausea and/or vomiting, decreased vision, and a history of intermittent rainbow-colored halos around lights; (2) documentation of presenting IOP of at least 21 mmHg; (3) iridotrabecular contact of >180 degrees on gonioscopy; (4) slit-lamp biomicroscopic findings including ≥ 4 of the following: ciliary injection, corneal epithelial edema, fixed mid-dilated pupil, glaukomflecken, and shallow peripheral anterior chamber. The F-APAC group had no prior history or symptoms of acute angle closure.

CPAC(G) was defined by the following criteria [17]: (1) absence of symptoms or signs of acute attack, such as a mid-dilated pupil, segmental iris atrophy, and glaukomflecken; (2) elevated IOP (>21 mmHg); (3) gonioscopic confirmation of ≥ 3 cumulative clock-hours of peripheral anterior synechiae (PAS); (4) with or without characteristic optic nerve or visual field damage. When both eyes were eligible, the eye with more advanced glaucomatous optic nerve damage or visual field defects was classified as CPAC(G).

Participants were excluded if they had any of the following conditions: (1) open angles on gonioscopic examination prior to the use of anti-glaucoma medication; (2) secondary angle closure due to conditions such as iris neovascularization, lens intumescence or subluxation, tumors, or uveitis; (3) a history of previous intraocular surgery or laser treatment; (4) inability to tolerate ocular examination; (5) AL < 19 mm; (6) age < 18 years.

Examination

A complete demographic and medical history were recorded for each participant, including age, sex, and treatment history. Each eligible participant underwent a comprehensive ophthalmic examination, which included assessment of visual acuity, IOP measurement, slit-lamp, and fundus examination. A gonioscopic examination was performed on all participants by an experienced examiner (BW) in a dark room using a gonioscope (Ocular Instruments Inc, Bellevue, WA, USA). Visual field testing (Humphrey Field Analyzer, Carl Zeiss Meditec, Inc., Dublin, CA, USA) and OCT (Cirrus HD-OCT 5000, Carl Zeiss Meditec, Inc., Dublin, CA, USA) were performed by an experienced technician following standard protocols.

Anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT)

Images of the anterior segment of each participant’s eye were captured using CASIA2 (Tomey Corporation, Nagoya, Japan), and the relevant parameters were measured using the device’s built-in measurement program. The CASIA2 system, with an axial resolution of 10 μm and a scanning area of 16 mm × 13 mm (lateral × depth), captured images from the cornea to the posterior surface of the lens and a portion of the anterior vitreous, thus enabling complete visualization of the anatomical relationship between the PLC and the AHM. All participants were examined in a sitting position. The scans were conducted under dim room lighting conditions, with patients focusing on a central fixation target and the scan center aligned to the pupil. Images of the Berger’s space were acquired using a standard 16-line radial scan pattern, while the anterior chamber angle was imaged using a 360° rotational scan. The examiner was blinded to the participants’ clinical data. For patients with acute angle closure, the examinations were conducted within a period of two weeks following the onset. The acquired images were independently analyzed by two investigators (ST and DL) to determine the presence and grade the visibility of Berger’s space, and the inter-observer agreement was calculated. A visible Berger’s space was defined as the presence of a distinct, relatively hyporeflective (dark) space between the clearly identifiable hyperreflective bands of the PLC and the AHM. The invisible group was defined as a condition in which the hyperreflective bands of both the PLC and the AHM are clearly identifiable but fused or closely apposed, with no discernible hyporeflective space between them. Ocular biometry and data management

Central corneal thickness (CCT), anterior chamber depth (ACD), lens thickness (LT), and AL were measured by Optical biometry (IOL-Master 700, Carl Zeiss Meditec, Jena, Germany). This device offers excellent tissue penetration and precise measurements [18]. Lens position (LP) = ACD + 0.5×LT. Vitreous opacity and posterior vitreous detachment were examined using ocular ultrasound (ZS3, Zonare Medical Systems, Inc., Mountain View, CA, USA). Vitreous floaters were characterized by the presence of punctate, patchy, or flocculent echoes within the vitreous body, exhibiting varying intensities. Posterior vitreous detachment (PVD) was identified when floating bands of hyperechoic light were observed in the hypoechoic region of the vitreous body, which were not continuous with the echoes of the posterior wall of the eye. Additionally, there was a large amplitude of motion and no blood flow signal.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using R software version 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The normality of continuous data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Accordingly, normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation and analyzed using linear mixed-effects models (with a random intercept for subjects), while non-normally distributed data are presented as median (interquartile range) and were analyzed using the same models after rank transformation. Post-hoc comparisons employed Bonferroni correction. Categorical data are presented as frequencies (percentages) and were compared using Fisher’s exact test. Prior to regression analysis, all included variables were checked for multicollinearity. Binary logistic regression was conducted using generalized estimating equations (GEE), with variables entered individually into the model. Categorical independent variables used the smallest group as the reference, while continuous variables were included directly. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

In this study, we compared the proportion of visible Berger’s spaces among the subtypes of PACD and their relationship with ocular biometric parameters. The results revealed that the APAC group had a higher percentage of visible Berger’s spaces. Additionally, in eyes with visible Berger’s spaces, participants were younger, had a narrower anterior chamber angle, and more anteriorly shifted LP. Both acute angle closure and age were associated with Berger’s space visibility. To our knowledge, this is the first study to assess Berger’s space visibility in the subtypes of PACD.

The AHM forms the anterior surface of the vitreous body, extending from the pars plana and attaching tightly to the PLC via the Wieger’s ligament [19–21]. The space between the AHM and the PLC is defined as the posterior lens space, known as the Berger’s space [22, 23]. In normal ocular structures, the PLC is directly parallel to the AHM, meaning the PLC-AHM attachment is extremely tight under physiological conditions [24–26], with the Berger’s space typically appearing as a potential space in most individuals. When the Wieger’s ligament undergoes age-related changes or pathological degeneration, the adhesion between the PLC and AHM weakens, allowing aqueous humor infiltration, which triggers anterior vitreous detachment (AVD), making the Berger’s space visible [23, 27]. Previous studies have reported varying percentages of visible Berger’s space. Torii et al. [26] conducted an observational study on 32 patients with a mean age of 64.1 ± 9.3 years who underwent vitrectomy for fundus disease. During the procedure, 38 eyes were observed using a fiberoptic endoscope, with no spontaneous anterior detachment of the AHM noted. Based on this, they concluded that spontaneous visible Berger’s space is very low. Several subsequent studies have investigated the visibility of the Berger’s space using AS-OCT. Elekes et al. [9] evaluated the visibility of the Berger’s space in cataract patients, observing it in only 19 (18.6%) of the 102 patients included. Zhang et al. [14] examined 223 cataract patients, finding the Berger’s space visible in only 2 (0.9%) cases. In another study, Mori et al. [10] examined 624 eyes, identifying the Berger’s space in only 43 (6.9%) of them. A further subgroup analysis of 223 cataract eyes revealed the Berger’s space in 8.1% of cases. Lin et al. [11] also investigated cataract patients, finding the Berger’s space in only 4 of 119 eyes (3.4%). Our results showed visibility rates of 9.38% in F-APAC and 17.02% in CPAC(G). However, the visible rate was significantly higher in the APAC group, at 36.67%. Logistic regression results also indicated that APAC was a risk factor for visible Berger’s space.

A structurally and functionally intact AHM is essential for maintaining normal intraocular physiological function. During cataract surgery, the adhesion between the PLC and AHM is believed to stabilize the PLC and prevent perfusate from entering the Berger’s space. When AVD occurs, it allows the PLC to shift anteriorly, increasing the risk of intraoperative rupture of the PLC [24] and the retention of lens fragments beneath the PLC [21]. Kawasaki et al. [15] evaluated the effect of elevated IOP on the PLC-AHM interface in porcine eyes. Their results showed that higher IOP levels caused greater damage to the PLC-AHM interface. As the peak IOP increased, the aqueous humor first flowed into the Berger’s space, and as the peak IOP continued to rise, the AHM ruptured, allowing the aqueous to flow into the vitreous. The occurrence of AHM rupture is determined by the highest IOP level, with rupture occurring at the attachment site of the Wieger’s ligament [28]. Furthermore, clinical observations have indicated that the use of higher infusion pressure during cataract surgery increases the risk of AHM injury and elevates the postoperative visibility rate of Berger’s space, providing indirect evidence for a pressure-related mechanism. In a study by Elekes et al. [9] involving 83 patients with no preoperative visibility of Berger’s space, 50 cases (60%) showed Berger’s space after phacoemulsification; Lin et al. [11] reported a postoperative visibility rate of 27.7%, while Zhang et al. [14] observed visible Berger’s space in 19.7% of patients within one month after phacoemulsification.

Our results showed that PACD eyes with a visible Berger’s space were younger, had narrower anterior chamber angles, and more anteriorly shifted LP. Mori et al. [10] found that older age, longer AL, and greater corneal power were associated with visible Berger’s space. Elekes et al. [9] also reported that the visibility rate increased with age. Previous studies were conducted in normal or cataract populations to investigate factors influencing Berger’s space visibility under physiological conditions. Our study focused on PACD and found that acute angle closure was a risk factor for visible Berger’s space. Our result indicated that with the anterior chamber angle closed, the aqueous outflow pathway is blocked, preventing proper drainage of fluid from the eye. As a result of elevated intraocular pressure and aqueous humor accumulation, disruption or loosening at the PLC-AHM interface may permit aqueous to enter the retrolenticular space, rendering Berger’s space visible. As shown by our results, the LP in the eyes with visible Berger’s space was shifted anteriorly. Narrow anterior chamber angles is known risk factors for acute angle closure [29]. Contrary to previous findings, our results identified a significantly younger age in patients with a visible Berger’s space, pointing to a distinct underlying mechanism in our PACD cohort that may involve a greater susceptibility to aqueous misdirection in younger patients [30–32].

As mentioned previously, when the Berger’s space becomes visible, the Wieger’s ligament has ruptured. Bernal et al. [33] demonstrated that most posterior zonular fibers do not attach directly to the PLC but first anchor to the AHM near the ciliary body before inserting into the PLC. Some fibers insert near the Wieger’s ligament. Lv et al. [34] found that the vitreous zonule was significantly lower in APAC eyes compared to the fellow eyes. Salimi et al. [35] observed 806 PACD eyes and found that the percentage of zonulopathy was significantly higher compared to normal subjects. Qiao et al. [36] found that in PACG patients, the prevalence of zonulopathy was 46.2%, while in acute angle closure glaucoma, it was as high as 57.1%. In contrast, in the control group of patients with age-related cataracts, the percentage of zonulopathy was only 6.0%. Although an association between ligament damage and acute angle closure is suggested, our research cannot yet draw conclusions on the causal relationship between them. It remains undetermined whether the angle closure or the ligament damage occurs first, a key direction for future research.

In the current study, we also assessed the proportion of PVD and vitreous floaters in PACD. The results showed no significant differences in these two parameters among the APAC, F-APAC, and CPAC(G) groups. Also, no significant differences were found between the group with visible Berger’s space and the group without visible Berger’s space. Based on these results, we concluded that there was no association between AVD and PVD, nor between AVD and vitreous floaters. Schäffer et al. [37] found that AVD was diagnosed in 25.8% of 31 pseudophakic eyes, and these eyes also had PVD. They concluded that there was consistency in the attachment of the vitreous to the retina posteriorly and the lens anteriorly. Unfortunately, they did not compare the occurrence of AVD and PVD in these eyes prior to cataract surgery. In contrast, Elekes et al. [9] concluded that when vitreous liquefaction occurs and PVD forms, the vitreous constricts behind the lens, causing the anterior vitreous and the PLC to adhere more tightly.

The preoperative identification of a visible Berger’s space should be regarded as a significant risk factor for intraoperative and postoperative aqueous misdirection. This condition is frequently associated with zonular weakness and elevated vitreous pressure, which complicate both surgical management and postoperative IOP control. During cataract surgery in these eyes, surgeons should remain vigilant for signs of anatomical instability, such as poor anterior capsule followability during capsulorhexis, and posterior capsule wrinkling or anterior bulging during phacoemulsification. Additionally, indicators of elevated posterior segment pressure—including recurrent iris prolapse and persistent anterior chamber shallowing—warrant close monitoring.

There are several limitations to the current study. First, the sample size was small; however, it provided adequate power to detect significant differences between the subtypes of PACD. Second, we did not assess peak IOP or the duration of elevated IOP in the participants. Furthermore, in cases of severe lens opacity, AS-OCT may not be able to penetrate the lens sufficiently to accurately evaluate Berger’s space visibility. Finally, B-scan ultrasound, employed for PVD assessment, possesses insufficient sensitivity for reliable detection, potentially resulting in missed cases.

In conclusion, we observed a significant increase visibility of Berger’s space in eyes with acute angle closure. Furthermore, eyes with visible Berger’s space demonstrated a distinct profile characterized by younger age, a narrower anterior chamber angle, and an anteriorly positioned lens. This study confirms the hypothesis that PACD patients are accompanied by aqueous humor misdirection.
