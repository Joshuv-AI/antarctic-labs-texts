Older females with late-onset hypertension are at higher CKD risk than males
Authors: Nickolas Beauregard, Amanda J Vinson, Daniel I McIsaac, Celine Girard, Manish M Sood, Claire E Kendall, Ally Sweet, Ria Singla, Pouya Motazedian, Gregory L Hundemer, Greg Knoll, Shan Dhaliwal, Ann Bugeja
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundFemales develop hypertension later and are less likely to receive chronic kidney disease (CKD) prevention treatments. The long-term impact of these differences remains unclear. We aimed to investigate sex-based differences in the in CKD outcomes among older adults with hypertension.MethodsWe conducted a population-based retrospective cohort study of adults aged ≥66 years with new hypertension using linked health data from Ontario, Canada (1 January 2010, to 31 December 2021). The primary composite outcome was a 40% decline in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) or kidney failure. We used Cox proportional hazards models to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) and Fine–Gray models accounting for death as a competing risk (subdistribution HRs, sHRs).ResultsIncident hypertension developed in 121 490 individuals (57.7% female; mean age 73.1 years; mean eGFR 83.2 mL/min/1.73 m²; median follow-up 6.8 years), 17 343 (14.3%) experienced the composite kidney outcome. The crude incidence rate per 1000 person-years was higher for females compared with males (22.0 in females vs 21.1 in males; P < .001). Females had a higher adjusted risk of the primary outcome, after accounting for death {sHR 1.10 [95% confidence interval (CI) 1.07–1.14]}. Females had a higher crude incidence of eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m² than males (66.6 vs 59.4; P < .001), and a higher adjusted risk [sHR 1.15 (95% CI 1.13–1.18)]. For eGFR <45 mL/min/1.73 m², females had a higher crude incidence (19.2 vs 17.5; P < .001) and a higher adjusted risk [sHR 1.13 (95% CI 1.10–1.17)] compared with males.ConclusionOlder females with new onset hypertension were more likely to develop a 40% decline in eGFR or kidney failure compared with older males. This study will inform hypertension management practices in aging populations.

INTRODUCTION

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a leading cause of death worldwide [1, 2]. As hypertension is the second leading cause of CKD, understanding sex-related differences in the development of CKD among those with hypertension is needed to improve outcomes [1]. Despite the widespread prevalence of hypertension in females, they are underrepresented in contemporary clinical trials, limiting the ability to investigate sex disparities in outcomes [3–5].

New-onset hypertension is 4-fold higher among postmenopausal females than premenopausal females, while in age-matched males, the incidence is only 3-fold higher, potentially putting older females at greatest risk for CKD [6, 7]. Yet, there is limited literature regarding kidney outcomes in older adults with late-onset hypertension. Research on the relationship between hypertension and CKD has largely focused on younger populations with both incident and prevalent hypertension, or lack sex-specific analysis [8–13]. Additionally, many studies have defined CKD as an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) <60 mL/min/1.73 m2, using older eGFR equations, or consider kidney failure without addressing intermediate eGFR levels that can significantly impact cardiovascular morbidity and mortality [14]. Moreover, given that 25% of new-onset hypertension cases have been found to occur in older adults (≥66 years), the applicability of these findings to the older population remains uncertain [15]. Hypertension studies often underrepresent older adults, and the predominance of male cohorts creates a significant gap in the literature about sex differences in late-onset hypertension. This study evaluates the impact of new-onset hypertension on kidney outcomes in older, postmenopausal females compared with older males, using a contemporary population-based cohort. We hypothesized that, among older adults, females would have a higher risk of adverse kidney outcomes relative to males.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and setting

We conducted a population-based, retrospective cohort study on adults aged 66 years and older who received a new diagnosis of hypertension in Ontario, Canada. We examined data from 1 January 2010, to 31 December 2021. The data were sourced from the Institute for Clinical Evaluative Sciences (ICES, ices.on.ca), which manages healthcare data for over 14 million Ontario residents. Access to these data was authorized under section 45 of Ontario’s Personal Health Information Protection Act, which exempts it from requiring review by a research ethics board. We present findings in accordance with the REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely Collected Data (RECORD) guidelines [16].

Data sources

We linked and analyzed data from multiple ICES datasets using unique encoded identifiers. Vital statistics and demographic characteristics were obtained from the Ontario Registered Persons Database. Hospitalization, diagnosis and procedural information were sourced from the Canadian Institute for Health Information Discharge Abstract Database. Diagnostic data for emergency room and day surgery visits came from the National Ambulatory Care Reporting System. The Ontario Laboratories Information System contains laboratory information for Ontario residents. Health claims for outpatient and inpatient physician services were retrieved from the Ontario Health Insurance Plan database. Medication data were extracted from the Ontario Drug Benefits database [17]. We used validated codes wherever possible to ensure accuracy in baseline characteristics and outcomes [18–24]. Detailed covariates and outcome definitions used in the analyses are outlined in Supplementary data, Tables S2 and S3.

Exposure

Our exposure was biological sex (female or male) as information on gender was not available.

Patients

We identified Ontario residents aged 66 years and older with newly diagnosed hypertension between 1 January 2010, and 31 December 2017. New-onset hypertension was defined using the ICES-derived Ontario HYPER Dataset, a cumulative database containing all Ontario hypertension patients identified since 1991. Individuals are entered into the cohort if they meet a validated case definition with 94% specificity that incorporates both inpatient and outpatient claims. Eligible cases included individuals with: (i) a hospital admission with an International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) code for hypertension, or (ii) an Ontario Health Insurance Plan (OHIP) claim for hypertension followed within one year by either an additional OHIP outpatient billing claim or a hospital admission with a hypertension diagnosis [25]. The index diagnosis date was defined as the date of the second outpatient billing claim for hypertension or hospitalization with hypertension. We followed patients until a maximum follow-up date of 31 December 2021, with all patients having a minimum follow-up period of 90 days. We excluded patients who met any of the following criteria: <66 years of age, ineligible for OHIP (those without a valid health card) 1 year prior to the index date, non-residents of Ontario at the index date, death on or before the index date, baseline eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m², baseline albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR) >30 mg/g, dialysis or kidney transplant recipients, kidney donors, <90 days of follow-up, and those who had an OHIP billing by a nephrologist or urologist before the index date.

Outcomes

Outcomes were captured in the period of 2011–21, with a minimum follow-up of 90 days after the index date for each patient. The primary composite outcome was a 40% decline in eGFR from baseline or kidney failure, consistent with Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes recommendations for clinically meaningful kidney disease progression [14]. A 40% decline in eGFR was determined using one measurement taken at least 90 days after the index date [26]. Kidney failure was defined as the need for dialysis or kidney transplantation. Secondary outcomes were an eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m², eGFR <45 mL/min/1.73 m² and an ACR >30 mg/g, obtained a minimum of 90 days after the index date. Measurements of eGFR were derived from outpatient serum creatinine measurements (in µmol/L) for each patient using the race-free Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equation [27].

Statistical analysis

We used descriptive statistics by exposure status. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables are described with means and standard deviations (SDs) if normally distributed, or with medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) if they deviated from normality. Between-group differences were evaluated using absolute standardized differences, which quantify imbalance independent of sample size, with values >0.1 indicating substantive differences [28].

We examined the association between sex and time to occurrence of CKD-related outcomes using Cox proportional hazards regression models, with observation time starting from the index date. We evaluated the proportional hazards assumption for the exposure both statistically and graphically, using a log–log survival plot [29, 30]. Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard modelling was used to produce subdistribution hazard ratios (sHRs) that accounted for death as a competing risk for the primary and secondary outcomes [31]. Models were adjusted for age, non-hypertension Elixhauser comorbidities (present or absent) based on a 3-year lookback prior to the index date, baseline eGFR ascertained within 1 year closest to the index date, neighbourhood income quintile as a proxy for socioeconomic status, era of hypertension diagnosis (2010–13 or 2014–17), rural versus urban residence, having a family physician, number of hospitalizations in the previous year, number of healthcare encounters in the previous year and the use of medications known to lower blood pressure but prescribed for other indications, ascertained in the 120 days closest to the index date [18, 19, 32, 33]. We examined several potential effect modifiers of the association between sex and kidney outcomes a priori: period of hypertension diagnosis, diabetes status, prior cardiovascular disease (defined as previous heart failure, myocardial infarction or ischemic stroke), baseline eGFR and the use of blood pressure–lowering medications for indications other than hypertension. We tested consecutive effect modifiers in the model and report those where the P-value for the interaction term reached statistical significance. The cohort was then stratified by age and baseline antihypertensive use, then compared using Cox proportional hazards models and repeated using Fine and Gray subdistribution hazard modelling, adjusted for factors listed previously. We conducted exploratory analyses of eGFR outcomes, including an evaluation of eGFR slope between the index date and 2–5 years of follow-up. For each participant, the eGFR slope was defined as the change in eGFR from baseline to the first follow-up measurement within this interval, divided by the duration of follow-up. Differences in eGFR distributions were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, which was used to calculate P-values. Results are reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Statistical significance was determined using a two-sided P-value <.05. All analyses were performed with SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc).

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

Among the 1 059 026 patients with incident hypertension in Ontario, 121 490 (11.5%) were ≥66 years of age and had no kidney disease at baseline (Fig. 1). The cohort consisted of 70 057 (57.7%) females and 51 433 (42.3%) males, and the mean (SD) age of the cohort was 73.1 (6.2) years (Table 1). The mean (SD) eGFR was 83.2 (11.2) mL/min/1.73 m². Females were older and had fewer diagnoses of diabetes, coronary artery disease and previous myocardial infarction. Females were less likely to be prescribed an alpha-blocker, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor, beta-blocker or nitrates, but more likely to be prescribed thiazide diuretics. Females were less likely to be prescribed statins. There was no statistically significant difference between sexes in neighborhood income quintiles, year of hypertension diagnosis, rural residence, other comorbid conditions, number of blood pressure–lowering medications or prescriptions of other blood pressure–lowering medications at baseline. The vast majority of baseline ACR measurements are missing in 103 908 (83.5%) individuals.

Outcomes

The median (IQR) follow-up was 6.8 (5.0–9.0) years. For the kidney composite outcome, 17 343 (14.3%) developed a 40% decline in eGFR or kidney failure, with 10 423 (59.1%) of cases occurring in females (Table 2). Of those, 17 308 (10 416 females) developed a 40% decline in eGFR and 239 (101 females) progressed to kidney failure. The mean (SD) eGFR among those who experienced a 40% decline in eGFR was 39.4 (9.2) mL/min/1.73 m². Females had a higher crude incidence rate of the primary kidney composite compared with males (22.6 per 1000 person-years versus 21.1; P < .001). Females had a higher risk of the kidney composite [HR 1.07 (95% CI 1.03–1.10]; even after accounting for the competing risk of death [adjusted sHR 1.10 (95% CI 1.07–1.14)] (Fig. 2).

Stratified analyses

Older age and the use of blood pressure–lowering medications at baseline were statistically significant effect modifiers of the kidney composite outcome (P < .001). In stratified analyses adjusting for death, females had an elevated risk of the kidney composite compared with age-matched males across all age groups except for the oldest group [sHR for 66–75 years, 1.15 (95% CI 1.10–1.19]; sHR for 76–85 years, 1.16 (95% CI 1.10–1.23); sHR for ≥86 years, 1.12 (95% CI 0.98–1.29)]. After adjusting for death, females had a higher risk of the kidney composite compared with males when not using blood pressure–lowering medications for another indication at baseline [sHR 1.10 (95% CI 1.04–1.18)]. After adjusting for death, females had a higher risk of the kidney composite compared with males when using blood pressure–lowering medications for another indication at baseline [sHR 1.17 (95% CI 1.13–1.21)].

Additional analyses

The median eGFR slope in the whole cohort was –1.09 (IQR –3.99 to 0.69) mL/min/1.73 m², –1.10 (IQR –4.10 to 0.67) in females and –1.08 (IQR –3.85 to 0.72) in males (P < .01).

DISCUSSION

In this population-based study of older adults with new-onset hypertension, females had a higher risk of developing a 40% decline in eGFR or kidney failure compared with males, even after adjusting for death. This finding, largely driven by a 40% decline in eGFR, emphasizes the need to consider the competing risk of death in older adults, where non-kidney-related deaths may affect the relationship between hypertension and kidney disease. Among older individuals with incident hypertension, females were more likely than males to develop both moderate and advanced CKD, demonstrated by greater declines in eGFR and a higher risk of a ≥40% reduction in kidney function after accounting for the competing risk of death. Notably, this excess risk was most pronounced among females who were using antihypertensive medications at baseline (possibly for other indications), highlighting baseline antihypertensive use as an important effect modifier even after accounting for the competing risk of death. Nonetheless, progression to kidney failure requiring dialysis or transplantation remained uncommon. This finding aligns with prior evidence showing that CKD is more prevalent among women, particularly those with hypertension and older age [1]. However, our findings focus on CKD incidence following a new diagnosis of hypertension, whereas much of the existing literature has focused on CKD progression in prevalent hypertension. Large cohort studies and meta-analyses have similarly shown that women are more likely to have stage 3–4 CKD and men have a faster rate of eGFR decline and are more likely to progress to kidney failure [34, 35].

Our study is unique in that we investigated the association between sex and kidney outcomes in a population composed entirely of older adults with newly diagnosed hypertension. Moreover, we focused on a composite outcome that captured relative eGFR decline, rather than relying on fixed eGFR thresholds. In contrast, previous studies investigating sex differences in the development of CKD have either used an eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m² or end-stage kidney disease as the primary endpoint, without considering intermediate eGFR levels or percentage reductions in eGFR [8–11, 12, 36]. The choice of our composite outcome allowed us to investigate the effect of incident hypertension on kidney outcomes in individuals with a wide range of baseline eGFR. This approach is also more clinically relevant for older populations, where fixed eGFR thresholds may not adequately account for the natural decline in kidney function with aging, potentially leading to misclassification of kidney disease [37]. Furthermore, females and males had a similar risk of developing albuminuria. Despite nearly one-fifth of the cohort having diabetes, only 14% underwent ACR testing, suggesting that uptake of guideline-recommended kidney disease screening remains limited in routine practice.

Although our study highlights sex differences in kidney outcomes for late-onset hypertension, the causes remain unclear. Possible factors include disparities in the recognition and monitoring of kidney disease, differences in health-seeking behavior and variation in hypertension management between sexes. A large cohort study using health administrative data found that women with hypertension were less likely than men to receive a CKD diagnosis; however, this finding was based on diagnostic codes rather than measured serum creatinine values [38]. Another possible explanation is that women may be more susceptible to developing CKD after acquiring hypertension, possibly due to sex differences in vascular reactivity, hormonal influence and less aggressive blood pressure management, including medication prescriptions, adherence and long-term blood pressure control, which is unclear in older adults. Future studies should explore sex differences in hypertension management, including medication prescriptions, adherence and long-term blood pressure control.

Limitations

Our study's strengths include a large cohort from Ontario's health administrative data, focusing on older adults with a new diagnosis of hypertension and long-term follow-up. Our study also has limitations. Our findings are restricted to biological sex, and we are unable to comment on differences in outcomes related to gender as a social construct. The literature supports the influence of gender on hypertension outcomes; however, our study focused on older adults, a population in which most individuals have congruent sex and gender identities [6, 39–45]. Second, our study lacked blood pressure measurements, which affect interpretation of sex-specific prognostic differences, as higher blood pressure levels could attenuate the observed benefits in women with CKD. Although we used a validated case definition to identify newly diagnosed hypertension, we were unable to track the progression of hypertension over time. Because the cohort requires more than one outpatient claim or a combination of claims, individuals with a single outpatient diagnosis may have been missed, which could introduce selection bias; however, this definition has been validated with high positive predictive value in Ontario. Third, due to the observational nature of this study, our results reflect associations rather than causal relationships. While we adjusted for several potential confounders, there are other important variables that could influence the probability of developing CKD, which are not routinely collected as part of Ontario's health administrative data. For example, our datasets lacked information on race, body mass index measurements and smoking status. When interpreting sex differences in CKD progression, even careful covariate selection may include variables that act as mediators of kidney outcomes. Fourth, our study was limited by missing baseline urine ACR values for most participants, which may bias estimates given the prognostic role of proteinuria, although the large sample size and validated outcomes support the robustness of our findings. Finally, as eGFR measurements were taken non-randomly at the discretion of a treating physician, and healthcare utilization patterns may differ by sex, there is a potential for bias in the measurement of the outcomes. To address this, we controlled for several variables that could influence the measurement of the outcomes, including preexisting comorbidities and health services use in the year leading up to the index date.

CONCLUSION

In this population-based observational study of older adults with newly diagnosed hypertension, females had a higher risk of developing a 40% decline in eGFR or kidney failure compared with males after adjusting for all-cause death. Future studies should investigate the underlying mechanisms leading to sex differences in kidney outcomes after late-onset hypertension, including potential differences in hypertension management between older females and males. In conclusion, this study emphasizes hypertension management as in older populations, and in particular older females.
