Ergodicity and regime recoverability in finite Markov-modulated random walks
Authors: Arina Pambukyan, Abdul Khader Jilani Saudagar, Sachin Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We study a finite Markov-modulated random walk (MMRW) on a one-dimensional lattice with reflecting boundaries, where a hidden finite-state Markov environment \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_t$$\end{document} selects at each time step a regime-specific random walk kernel for the position process \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_t$$\end{document}. Treating the joint process \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Z_t=(X_t,E_t)$$\end{document} as a Markov chain on the product state space, we construct its transition kernel and derive a discrete-time Master Equation. We then state simple structural assumptions on the environment kernel and the regime-specific spatial kernels that guarantee irreducibility and aperiodicity of the joint chain; since the state space is finite, standard Markov chain theory implies existence and uniqueness of a stationary distribution and convergence in total variation. On top of this stochastic model we formulate supervised learning problems on simulated trajectories. Using sliding windows of increments \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta X_t$$\end{document}, we build summary-statistic feature vectors and study when standard classifiers can infer the hidden environment state from the observed path. For a two-regime MMRW, support vector machines, random forests and gradient boosting all recover the environment with test accuracy and macro F1 close to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.9$$\end{document}. We then consider short-horizon prediction of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta X_{t+1}$$\end{document} and compare three gradient-boosting predictors: a regime-agnostic baseline, a regime-aware model that uses environment probabilities estimated from Task A, and an oracle that observes the true environment. In the default single-run configuration, the regime-aware model yields only a modest downstream effect relative to the baseline, while the oracle quantifies the performance level that would be reachable under perfect regime information. Finally, we use the decoded environment sequence to estimate the environment transition matrix and its stationary distribution, and compare these to the corresponding quantities from the true environment, including a Frobenius-norm distance and spectral gaps. This links the probabilistic structure of the MMRW with the practical ability of standard machine-learning methods to recover latent regimes and coarse environment dynamics from finite data.

Introduction

Random walks are a canonical model for stochastic transport, and their behaviour becomes considerably richer when the step distribution is influenced by an evolving external regime. In a Markov-modulated random walk (MMRW), the transition kernel at each time step is selected by a finite-state Markov environment. This paper develops a finite, discrete-time Master-Equation formulation of such systems and identifies simple, simulator-matched sufficient conditions under which the joint chain \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(X_t, E_t)$$\end{document} is irreducible and aperiodic, and hence ergodic with convergence in total variation by standard finite-state Markov chain theory. We complement this probabilistic framework with numerical evidence illustrating mixing behaviour. On top of this, we formulate supervised learning tasks that use sliding-window features of the trajectory to infer the hidden environment, predict short-horizon increments, and examine how reliably standard classifiers can recover the latent regime structure from observed paths.

Background and related work

This work lies at the intersection of three research areas: random walks in random environments, Markov-modulated processes in applied probability, and hidden Markov models integrated with classical supervised learning techniques. In this section, we situate the finite Markov-modulated random walk (MMRW) within this broader context and establish the notation for finite Markov chains that will be used throughout the paper.

The primary focus is on a finite MMRW with reflecting boundaries. We examine it both as a Markov chain, for which an explicit master equation can be written, and as a controlled generator of trajectories, enabling the study of how latent regimes can be inferred and leveraged using standard learning procedures.

Random walks in random environments

Random walks in random environments (RWRE) model a particle moving on a lattice where the transition probabilities are random but fixed once realized. In one dimension this already produces behaviour that is far from the simple random walk on \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbb {Z}$$\end{document}: depending on the distribution of the local drift, the walk can be recurrent, transient with a positive speed, or transient with sublinear speed and trapping effects1. Systematic accounts and classifications of such regimes in one and higher dimensions can be found, for example, in2–4.

Most of this work takes place on infinite graphs and concentrates on quenched and annealed limit theorems, large deviations and scaling limits. In contrast, we fix a finite line with reflecting boundaries and a small number of environment states, and work directly with the joint chain \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Z_t=(X_t,E_t)$$\end{document}. This finite set-up avoids technical issues that arise in the infinite-volume case and creates a controlled setting for questions that are statistical in nature, such as how strongly different environment kernels must differ before they become distinguishable from short segments of the trajectory, and how that information translates into predictive gains.

Markov-modulated random walks and queues

Markov-modulated random walks are a discrete-time instance of Markov additive processes. An environment chain \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_t$$\end{document} evolves on a finite set and, conditional on \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_t$$\end{document}, a level process \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_t$$\end{document} behaves like a random walk with parameters that depend on the current environment. Models of this type appear in queueing theory, risk theory and telecommunications. Examples include Markov-modulated fluid queues and Markov-modulated risk processes where arrival rates or claim-size distributions depend on a latent regime5,6.

In much of the applied literature on Markov-modulated processes the level process lives on an unbounded state space and the main questions concern stability, stationary tails and hitting probabilities, often handled with matrix analytic techniques. Here the level process evolves on a finite one dimensional state space with reflecting boundaries, and we make use of the explicit block structure of the joint kernel. This permits a direct master equation for the joint law and a transparent ergodicity analysis, which then serve as the probabilistic foundation for the learning experiments.

Hidden Markov models and regime switching

If the environment \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_t$$\end{document} is unobserved and only the path of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_t$$\end{document} is available, the MMRW can be seen as a structured hidden Markov model (HMM). In a basic HMM a latent Markov chain is observed through conditionally independent emissions, and inference targets the hidden states and model parameters; see, for example, the classical tutorial7 and the monograph8. Regime switching models of this type are widely used for time series with latent regimes, including Hamilton type models for macroeconomic cycles9.

In the present formulation the environment \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_t$$\end{document} is latent and the observed process is the walker path \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_t$$\end{document} (equivalently its increments \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta X_t$$\end{document}). This yields a structured HMM in which the latent state is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_t$$\end{document} and the observation likelihood at time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t$$\end{document} is given by the regime-specific transition probability of the walk, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbb {P}(X_{t+1}=x' \mid X_t=x, E_t=e)$$\end{document}. In contrast to a basic HMM with conditionally independent emissions, here the emission likelihood depends on the current position through reflecting boundary effects; nevertheless, the likelihood can still be evaluated exactly because the regime-specific kernels are known in the simulator.

Classical HMM inference provides three standard tools. First, the forward recursion computes filtering posteriors \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbb {P}(E_t=e \mid X_0,\dots ,X_t)$$\end{document}, and combining forward and backward recursions yields smoothing posteriors \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbb {P}(E_t=e \mid X_0,\dots ,X_T)$$\end{document}; see7,8. Second, the Viterbi algorithm computes the most likely latent path (MAP decoding) \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\arg \max _{e_{0:T}} \mathbb {P}(e_{0:T}\mid X_{0:T})$$\end{document}10. Third, when the transition kernel \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q$$\end{document} and/or the regime-specific emission laws are unknown, they can be estimated by maximum likelihood using the EM algorithm for HMMs, commonly referred to as the Baum–Welch procedure7,8,11.

In this paper we do not treat HMM likelihood-based inference as the main methodology, because our goal is to use the MMRW as a controlled generator of labelled trajectories and to quantify regime recoverability under simple, interpretable window features and off-the-shelf classifiers. At the same time, to connect Task A to the canonical HMM literature and to provide a model-driven reference point, we include a likelihood-based forward recursion baseline restricted to the same local window information used by the classifiers (“5”).

Sliding window classification for stochastic sequences

A standard way to bring sequential data into a supervised learning framework is to cut the sequence into overlapping windows, extract features from each window, and train a classifier on the resulting feature vectors. This windowing strategy is widely used in time series classification, where raw sequences are mapped to summary statistics and then classified with standard models12,13.

The feature construction for the MMRW in “Learning on simulated MMRW” follows this pattern. Windows of increments are mapped to low order statistics, sign structure indicators and short run dependence measures. This keeps the learning layer close to classical tools, and makes it possible to relate the empirical recoverability of regimes back to concrete properties of the random walk kernels.

Lemma 4.5

(an aperiodic state) Under A3 the state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(x^\star ,e^\star )$$\end{document} has a positive self-transition probability\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {P}_{(x^\star ,e^\star )\rightarrow (x^\star ,e^\star )} = Q_{e^\star e^\star }\,P^{(e^\star )}_{x^\star x^\star } > 0.$$\end{document}In particular, the period of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(x^\star ,e^\star )$$\end{document} is equal to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1$$\end{document}.

Proposition 4.6

(aperiodicity) Under A1–A3 the joint chain \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Z_t$$\end{document} is aperiodic.

By Proposition 4.4 the chain is irreducible on the finite state space \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {Z}$$\end{document}. Learning on simulated MMRW

In this section we treat the Markov-modulated random walk as a data-generating mechanism and ask what can be recovered from a single long trajectory using standard supervised learning methods. The goals are: (i) to understand when the hidden environment \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_t$$\end{document} can be inferred from the observed path of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_t$$\end{document}; (ii) to assess whether such inferred environment information improves short-horizon prediction of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta X_{t+1}$$\end{document}; and (iii) to see how well the empirical environment dynamics can be reconstructed from decoded regimes and compared to the true environment chain. A separate direction, left for future work, is to treat the number of environment states \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K$$\end{document} itself as an unknown model order.

Task A can also be formulated as inference in a hidden Markov model in which the latent state is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_t$$\end{document} and observations are the walk transitions (equivalently, increments). Concretely, for each window starting at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_0$$\end{document} we use only the transition likelihoods generated by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\Delta X_{t_0},\ldots ,\Delta X_{t_0+w-1})$$\end{document} to obtain the filtering posterior at the end of the window and then propagate one step using \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q$$\end{document} to match the target label \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y^{(\textrm{env})}_{t_0}=E_{t_0+w}$$\end{document}. This produces an “oracle local” HMM benchmark that is directly comparable to the window-feature classifiers.

Future directions

Several extensions remain outside the current implementation and are left for future work.

Connection to MLE-based inference on graphs

Task A can be cast as likelihood-based inference in a Markovian generative model: given an observed trajectory \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(X_t)$$\end{document}, infer the latent regime process \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(E_t)$$\end{document} by maximizing the induced likelihood, or equivalently by computing posterior distributions under the model. This viewpoint links regime decoding to a broader class of MLE formulations for Markov chains and diffusion processes on graphs, including contagion source detection and related tracing tasks16,17. Within this perspective, our window-feature classifiers can be interpreted as scalable, discriminative approximations to a model-driven decoder: they trade the statistical efficiency of exact likelihood inference under correct specification for flexibility when the observation mechanism is only partially modeled and for the ability to operate with minimal analytic assumptions on the data-generating process.

Learning regime-specific kernels from data

The experiments in this paper use simulator-specified kernels \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{P^{(e)}\}$$\end{document}. In many applications these transition laws are unknown and must be estimated from observed trajectories. A natural extension is to treat the environment kernel \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q$$\end{document} and the regime-specific walk kernels \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{P^{(e)}\}$$\end{document} as parameters and learn them by maximum likelihood. This can be done either within a restricted parametric family (for example, learning drift and stay parameters in each regime) or via structured function approximators that enforce row-stochasticity and the support constraints of the underlying graph (for example, masked row-wise softmax parameterizations). Such kernel learning would move the framework from analyzing a known stochastic system to discovering latent regimes and their transition laws directly from path data, aligning the MMRW setting with current diffusion- and graph-inference pipelines8,11,18.

Model order and uncertainty quantification

In the present experiments the number of regimes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K$$\end{document} is fixed and known. An important extension is to treat \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K$$\end{document} as a model-order parameter, fit the Task A/Task B pipeline under competing values of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K$$\end{document}, and select among them using held-out predictive performance or information criteria. A complementary direction is to quantify uncertainty in downstream estimates derived from decoded regimes, such as empirical transition matrices, stationary distributions, and spectral gaps, and to study how these uncertainties depend on trajectory length and on separation between the regime-specific kernels \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{P^{(e)}\}$$\end{document}. This would connect the finite-sample inference questions more tightly to the ergodic theory of the joint chain.

Empirical summary and interpretation

In the default configuration used here (two regimes, window length \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w=16$$\end{document} and the feature set described above), the empirical results can be summarized as follows.

Task A: environment inference

The empirical distributions of basic window statistics (for example the mean of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta X_t$$\end{document} and the fraction of positive increments) split by true regime label show two clearly separated clusters for the chosen parameterization. This already suggests that the hidden environment is encoded in low-order summaries of the path.

Using the full feature vector, SVMs, random forests and gradient boosting all achieve high and very similar performance, with test macro-F1 close to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.9$$\end{document} and ROC-AUC above \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.9$$\end{document} (Tables 1 and 2). The normalized confusion matrix and ROC curve (Figs. 2a and 3) show that both regimes are detected symmetrically well, and the calibration curve (Fig. 2b) confirms that the probabilities produced by the reference SVM are well aligned with empirical frequencies. Task A therefore exhibits a clear recoverability regime: when the random-walk kernels \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P^{(0)}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P^{(1)}$$\end{document} differ sufficiently in drift and stay probability, simple window statistics and standard classifiers are enough to recover the hidden environment from a single trajectory.

Task B: next-increment prediction

For Task B we compare the three gradient-boosting strategies of Table 3. All models achieve only moderate accuracy, which reflects the intrinsic unpredictability of the next increment in a noisy Markov system. In the default single-run configuration, the regime-aware predictor yields only a modest change relative to the baseline. Appendix B shows that this effect is small and not uniformly stable across seeds. By contrast, the oracle predictor substantially outperforms both, especially in log-loss, which quantifies the potential gain from perfect regime identification and provides an upper reference point for the current feature set and model class.

Task C: environment dynamics

At an aggregate level, the decoded environment sequence allows us to reconstruct basic properties of the environment chain. The empirical transition matrices from true and decoded labels are close, and the stationary distributions differ by only a few percentage points in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ell ^1$$\end{document}-distance (Fig. 5b). The spectral gaps are of the same order as well, indicating comparable mixing behaviour. Together with the small Frobenius-norm distance \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left\Vert Q^{\textrm{true}}_{\textrm{emp}} - \hat{Q}_{\textrm{emp}} \right\Vert _{\textrm{F}}$$\end{document}, this suggests that the combination of ergodicity of the joint chain and high-quality regime inference is strong enough to recover coarse mixing characteristics of the hidden environment from finite data. At the same time, Appendix B shows that the decoded transition matrix is a biased plug-in estimate: even with high classification accuracy, misclassification systematically introduces a modest downward bias in estimated persistence in the default multi-seed analysis.

Interpretation

From the probabilistic side, the experiments support the idea that ergodic Markov-modulated random walks can have statistically visible environments: when the kernels \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{P^{(e)}\}$$\end{document} are sufficiently different, the latent chain \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(E_t)$$\end{document} leaves a clear footprint in the path of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(X_t)$$\end{document}. From the machine-learning side, the study illustrates how such hidden regimes can be exploited in practice. Task A quantifies when it is possible to infer a hidden regime from short time windows. Task B shows that inferred regimes are interpretable and that perfect regime information would be predictively useful, although the gain from predicted regimes alone is modest in the default configuration and not uniformly stable across seeds. Task C indicates that, once regimes are decoded with high accuracy, the empirical environment dynamics they induce are close to the true dynamics in the sense of transition probabilities, stationary behaviour and spectral gap.

Conclusion and future scope

This paper examined a finite Markov-modulated random walk (MMRW) on a one-dimensional lattice and developed a clear probabilistic description of the joint process \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Z_t = (X_t, E_t)$$\end{document}. By constructing the joint transition kernel and its Master Equation, and by identifying simple conditions that guarantee irreducibility and aperiodicity, we obtain ergodicity of the joint chain and a unique stationary distribution as a direct consequence of standard finite-state Markov chain theory. These results provide a clean theoretical setting in which long simulated trajectories can be treated as essentially stationary.

Building on this foundation, we explored how much of the hidden environment can be recovered from the observed path of the walker. The learning experiments showed three main findings: (i) standard classifiers, using only simple window-based features, can detect the latent regime with high accuracy; (ii) perfect regime information would substantially improve short-horizon prediction, whereas the gain from predicted regimes alone is modest in the default configuration and not uniformly stable across seeds; and (iii) decoded regimes preserve coarse features of the true environment dynamics, including transition probabilities, stationary behaviour, and mixing characteristics, although the resulting transition matrix should be interpreted as a biased plug-in estimate. Together, these observations highlight that the hidden environment leaves a clear statistical footprint when the regime-specific kernels differ sufficiently.

Several extensions follow naturally from this study. One direction is to allow for more environment states, asymmetric switching rules, longer spatial domains, or more general underlying graphs. It would also be interesting to develop explicit coupling arguments that yield sharp exponential convergence bounds in which the constants reflect both the environment-switching rate and the separation of the regime-specific kernels. A further step is to treat the number of environment states \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K$$\end{document} as an unknown quantity and investigate principled approaches to model-order selection. Finally, linking the empirical transition matrices obtained from decoded regimes with parametric or nonparametric estimators of the environment chain could offer a deeper understanding of when the underlying dynamics are statistically recoverable.
