Noradrenergic gene variation shaping vulnerability and resilience by affecting mental health-related characteristics: A systematic review
Authors: C.F.M. van Hooijdonk, S. Voulgaropoulou, L. Podrzaj, D. Wolvekamp, T.A.M.J. van Amelsvoort, N.K. Leibold
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Mental health problems are highly prevalent worldwide. Stress can precipitate both the onset and recurrence of mental health problems. While many individuals are exposed to similar stressors, there is a large heterogeneity in sensitivity to stress and the extent to which someone subsequently develops mental health issues. A key factor that contributes to an individual's capacity to cope with adversity, referred to as resilience, is genetic variation. Here, we systematically reviewed the effects of genetic variation in the genes encoding the norepinephrine (NE) transporter and receptors on resilience- and mental health-related features in a transdiagnostic approach (i.e., without restrictions regarding factors such as diagnosis or age). Our search yielded 49 studies for inclusion. The current evidence highlights that 1) genetic variations in the NE system (particularly in the ADRA2A and ADRA2B genes) exert influence on some cognitive processes, especially attention and emotional memory; 2) genetic variants in the alpha-2a receptor might play a role in some personality traits; 3) deletion in the ADRA2B and ADRA2C genes seems to be related with altered activity of the amygdala during emotional memory tasks; 4) the link between NE variants and response to methylphenidate are inconclusive. Altogether, these studies provide evidence for a genetic effect of the noradrenergic system in specific resilience- and mental health-related individual characteristics. However, the number of available studies specifically investigating noradrenergic genes were limited, emphasizing the need to perform more research on the noradrenergic system, while taking into account factors such as age, sex, environment, and the potential interactions with other genes.

Introduction

Across the globe, mental health issues are highly prevalent (World Health Organization Department of Mental Health and Substance Use, 2022). They are the leading cause of disability, involving enormous economic consequences (World Health Organization Department of Mental Health and Substance Use, 2022). Several factors, protective and risk-related, may impact people's chance of developing mental health issues.

A major risk factor for developing mental health problems is exposure to stress that arises from a wide range of adverse life events, such as childhood abuse. Previous research showed that exposure to (acute) adverse experiences early in life, such as sexual and physical abuse, increases the vulnerability to psychopathology in adulthood (Heim and Nemeroff, 2001). Moreover, chronic stress has been linked with several mental health issues (including anxiety (Sheth et al., 2017), depression (Sheth et al., 2017), attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) (Saccaro et al., 2021), and emotional dysregulation (Dvir et al., 2014).

Exposure to stress has been associated with changes in neurotransmitter, cognitive, and neuroendocrine systems (Bisagno and Cadet, 2014; McEwen et al., 2015), as well as changes in brain function and structure (Kaufman and Charney, 2001; McEwen, 2006). Nevertheless, not all individuals who face similar adverse life events develop a mental health disorder, suggesting that certain factors exist that protect these individuals and make them “resilient”. Resilience is often defined as the capacity to cope with adversity and maintain (relatively) normal functioning (Herrman et al., 2011). However, in addition to this definition of resilience, multiple others have been mentioned in the literature (Aburn et al., 2016; Kalisch et al., 2017). That being so, the exact definition of resilience and its operationalization are still debated. A very recent review defined resilience in three distinct ways: trait-based, process-based, and outcome-based (Kalisch et al., 2024). Briefly, in early resilience research it was assumed that resilience was related to a specific personality trait that allowed an individual to maintain mental health when encountering adversity (Block and Block, 1982; Kobasa, 1979). Hence, individuals possessing this beneficial trait were resilient, whereas individuals without this trait were not. However, later on it was observed that also internal, as well as, external factors played a role, such as behavior and social support from the environment (Werner, 1997). In addition, after being exposed to adversity, some individuals demonstrate personal growth and develop stronger competences (Tedeschi and Calhoun, 2004). Therefore, some research shifted to focusing on processes of individual successful adaptation to cope with adversity (Sapienza and Masten, 2011). This can involve processes at the biological level such as gene expression (Breen et al., 2015) and epigenetic modifications (Boks et al., 2015). Due to the wide variation in the timing and nature of biological changes among individuals, identifying and classifying these changes can be challenging. Consequently, recent research increasingly shifts the focus on resilience as an outcome (Kalisch et al., 2017). In this view, resilience is defined as maintaining mental health despite exposure to adversity. As many studies in the past did not apply this recent definition nor a uniform definition, the present systematic review focuses on characteristics related to mental health, which thus best aligns with the trait-based definition.

One biological factor that can influence traits are genetic variants that affect gene expression, subsequent protein activity, and functioning of neurotransmitter systems (Maul et al., 2020). A neurotransmitter system that plays a prominent role in shaping the response to stress is the noradrenergic system (Berridge et al., 2017). Norepinephrine (NE) is synthesized in the brainstem locus coeruleus (LC) by conversion of l-tyrosine to L-DOPA by the enzyme tyrosine hydroxylase (TH; Fig. 1). L-DOPA is then converted to dopamine by aromatic L-amino acid decarboxylase, which is subsequently decarboxylated to NE by dopamine beta-hydroxylase (DBH). The conversion by DBH takes place in synaptic vesicles, the transport of dopamine into this vesicle takes place via the vesicular monoamine transporter 2 (VMAT). After synthesis, NE can be further converted to epinephrine (E) by the enzyme phenylethanolamine N-methyltransferase (PMNT) or released from the presynaptic terminal into the synaptic cleft for signal transmission. Upon stimulation, such as by stress, NE neurons strongly increase their firing and release the neurotransmitter into the synaptic cleft (Benarroch, 2018). By binding to various receptors of the NE system throughout the brain and body, autonomic, behavioral, as well as emotional responses are triggered. After secretion, E or NE is either taken up into the presynaptic neuron by the norepinephrine transporter (NET) or metabolized by the enzymes catechol-O-methyltransferase (COMT) and monoamine oxidase-A (MAO-A) to terminate neurotransmission (Benarroch, 2018). Stress resilience is related to the activity of the LC (Grueschow et al., 2021), suggesting that the NE system may contribute to stress-related problems. In addition, chronic hyperactivity of the NE system is related to anxiety disorders (Charney, 2003).Fig. 1Systematic overview of the noradrenergic system. Tyrosine is converted to L-DOPA by TH. Also genetic variants in the NE system have been associated with various mental disorders. For instance, case-control studies have linked genetic variants in the genes coding for the adrenergic receptor type alpha-1A (ADRA1A) to generalized anxiety disorder (Zhang et al., 2017). Similarly, genetic variants related to the adrenergic receptor type alpha-2A (ADRA2A) have been associated with ADHD (Cho et al., 2008a; Schmitz et al., 2006), and variants in the gene encoding the adrenergic receptor type alpha-2C (ADRA2C) with substance use disorder (Rivero et al., 2016). Furthermore, genetic variation in the receptor type beta-2 (ADRB2) has been linked to generalized anxiety disorder (Zhang et al., 2017). With regard to NET, genetic variants have been associated with ADHD (Pazvantoglu et al., 2013), but also links with depression (Wang et al., 2015), panic disorder (Freitag et al., 2006), and generalized anxiety disorder (Chang et al., 2020) have been reported.

These categorical diagnoses, and those of other mental disorders, are currently based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM), in which the symptoms and criteria for classification of a specific disorder are described (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). While this manual has been valuable in establishing some consensus between clinicians, the conventional approach to categorize individuals into distinct disorders based on meeting a minimum number of specified symptoms also entails that individuals can have very heterogeneous clinical manifestations and individual traits, and still meet the criteria for the same disorder. Hence, this approach often results in individuals receiving the same treatment, even when their symptoms hardly overlap. In addition, symptoms are often not specific for a single mental disorder and co-morbidity with other DSM diagnoses is generally high among psychiatric patients (Newby et al., 2015). Instead of a disorder-specific treatment, patients may benefit more from (transdiagnostic) treatments tailored to their symptoms and molecular mechanisms associated with these symptoms.

Recognizing these limitations in the current approach to diagnose and treat mental disorders, a novel framework has been suggested changing the way mental health disorders are conceptualized (Newby et al., 2015). Moving away from a single diagnosis approach, transdiagnostic psychiatry seeks to cut across the traditional categorical diagnoses and identify shared (neuro)biological and emotional processes that contribute to mental health, related symptoms, and individual's traits. By identifying factors that underlie or influence mental health and intervening on them to reduce risks and/or increase resilience and mental well-being, this new approach holds promise to promote mental health and to prevent related issues.

As the dopaminergic and serotonergic systems have drawn most attention in this respect, the present systematic review aims to summarize studies that investigate how genetic variation in the NE system - a relatively understudied system - affects mental health-related characteristics that could shape vulnerability or resilience and explain potential individual differences. For this purpose, a transdiagnostic approach without any restrictions regarding factors such as patient population and age was chosen to understand the molecular mechanisms contributing to vulnerability and resilience. This knowledge is crucial to develop novel, personalized treatments to help those suffering from mental health issues to recover or to prevent recurrent mental health issues.

Methods

This systematic review was performed following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guideline (Liberati et al., 2009; Moher et al., 2009). The review protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; CRD42023448094).

Search strategy, selection criteria and data extraction

The literature search was performed in PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science from inception until July 14, 2023. The search strategy combined the following keywords: resilience or coping or adaptation or mental health, polymorphism, and noradrenergic system (see Appendix A.1 for the detailed search strategy). After the removal of duplicate publications in EndNote 20.6 (The EndNote Team, 2013), at least two investigators (CvH, NL, SV, DW, LP (authors), and/or MS (student assistant)) independently screened the titles and abstracts of the retrieved studies for eligibility in Rayyan (Ouzzani et al., 2016). If an abstract was not available, the full text of the article was retrieved. Disagreements between the investigators were solved by consensus. After the initial screening, two investigators (CvH, NL, SV, DW (authors), and/or MS (student assistant)) screened the full-text versions of the selected studies for inclusion, based on the specified inclusion and exclusion criteria. Further studies for assessment were identified by checking reference lists of relevant articles.

Inclusion criteria were original articles published in English, which assessed polymorphisms in the genes encoding a NE receptor or NET and resilience-/mental health-related characteristics in humans. We focused on these “indirect” trait-based characteristics of resilience instead of measures of resilience itself as there is currently no consensus about the definition of resilience. In addition, many study designs in this field of research do not address the specific resilience phenotype of the participants. Intervention studies were included when the intervention such as using a pharmacological compound targeted the NE system. Reviews, meta-analyses, case studies, case-control studies focusing only on a disorder, genome-wide association studies not mentioning NE system genes, conference proceedings, pre-prints, conference abstracts, commentaries, and articles without available full-text were excluded. Moreover, we excluded animal, in vitro and plant studies, as well as, studies that only investigated gene polymorphisms that are non-specifically linked to the NE system, namely polymorphisms in the genes encoding COMT, MAO-A, DBH, and TH.

Lastly, studies that reported data from participants with primary physical disorders that affect the NE system, such as those with adrenal gland tumors, were excluded. Likewise, articles that met an exclusion criterion or did not meet all inclusion criteria were excluded. Discrepancies between investigators were discussed with a third investigator until a consensus was reached. We extracted data for all included studies, into a table in Excel.

Outcomes

We investigated the association between NE gene polymorphisms and resilience-/mental health-related characteristics in humans. Polymorphisms of interest included polymorphisms in genes for NE receptors such as ADRA2A or alpha-2B adrenergic receptor (ADRA2B) or NET. Resilience- and mental health-related characteristics included traits that could influence resilience or mental health symptoms. We divided them into four categories: traits related to 1) cognition; 2) personality traits; 3) brain function (e.g., functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT)), and 4) pharmacological interventions. We did not include studies that used electroencephalography (EEG), diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), and structural magnetic resonance imaging, as these outcomes cannot be directly interpreted regarding functionality.

(Nor)epinephrine receptors

Adrenergic, or NE receptors, are a class of G protein-coupled receptors that mediate the effects of NE. There are two main types of NE receptors: alpha-adrenergic receptors and beta-adrenergic receptors (Strosberg, 1993; O’Donnell et al., 2012).

Alpha-adrenergic receptors are divided in alpha-1 and alpha-2 receptors (Hussain et al., 2023). Alpha-1 receptors are postsynaptic receptors and can be further categorized into alpha-1a, alpha-1b, and alpha-1d receptors. Alpha-1a and alpha-1b receptors are located in the amygdala, hippocampus, cerebellum, cortex, hypothalamus, midbrain, and spinal regions (Perez, 2020). Alpha-2 receptors can function both pre- and post-synaptically. Presynaptic alpha-2 receptors are autoreceptors on noradrenergic neurons, regulating NE release. These receptors are primarily located in the LC, amygdala, and hypothalamus (Hussain et al., 2023). The subtypes within this receptor group include alpha-2a, alpha-2b, and alpha-2c receptors (Hussain et al., 2023).

Beta-adrenergic receptors are subdivided into beta-1, beta-2, and beta-3 receptors. These receptor are widely distributed in the brain, with beta-1 and beta-2 receptors being most prevalent in the cerebral cortex and beta-3 in adipose tissue (Hussain et al., 2023).

In general, alpha-1 and beta-receptors have stimulatory effects, while alpha-2 receptors have inhibitory effects in the central nervous system (Gannon et al., 2015). Polymorphisms in the genes encoding NE receptors can lead to variations in receptor structure and function, and have been associated with various mental disorders (e.g., generalized anxiety disorder (Zhang et al., 2017)) and/or mental health symptoms (e.g., ADHD-related symptoms (Roman et al., 2003)). Such genetic variants also have the potential to affect resilience- and mental health-related characteristics, as described in the following sections.

Cognition

Cognition is a complex and multifaceted aspect of human behavior. It refers to processes related to perceiving, processing, storing, reasoning and judging information and it plays a crucial role in shaping how people interpret and respond to their environment (Shettleworth, 2010). Different types of cognitive processes include, among others, intelligent quotient (IQ), attention, memory, emotional processing, and executive functioning. The NE system has been shown to play a role in cognition (Borodovitsyna et al., 2017) and hence, genetic variation in the NE system may affect an individual's cognitive functions. Subsequently, polymorphisms in genes that encode for adrenergic receptors, particularly ADRA2A and ADRA2B (and NET, see section 3.2.1), have been associated with cognitive processes.

IQ

IQ refers to an individual's capacity to solve problem (Tsukahara and Engle, 2021). We now know that the brain NE system is heavily involved in several steps of various cognitive processes and prefrontal cortical function, including intelligence (Kim et al., 2010).

The effect of the ADRA2B rs1800544 genotype on IQ was assessed using the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for children (WISC) in children with ADHD by Kim et al. (2010). The rs1800544 (C−1291G) in the MspI site is located in the 5′-promoter region. No genotype effect was found.

IQ was also assessed in healthy individuals by Gibbs et al. (2010) and Naudts et al., 2012a, 2012b in relationship to a ADRA2B deletion polymorphism: 12Glu9, in which three glutamic acids from a glutamic acid repeat element are missing. IQ assessment focused on verbal IQ obtained by the National Adult Reading Test. No genotype effect was found.

These results are partly in contrast to a genetic association study that was performed by Bochdanovits et al. (2009). They assessed the association between cognitive ability and polymorphisms in the ADRB2 gene (rs1042713 and rs1042714). Two independent family-based Dutch cohorts (young cohort mean age = 12.4; adult cohort mean age = 36.7) and one large sample of 1063 unrelated Scottish individuals (i.e., the Lothian Birth Cohort of 1936 (Deary et al., 2007)), all without specific mental diagnoses, were genotyped for both single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). In the Dutch cohorts, the Dutch adaptations of the child and adult versions of the Wechsler Intelligence Scale were used to measure full scale, verbal and performance IQ (corrected for age and sex). Bochdanovits et al. (2009) found a family-based association between ADRB2 rs1042713 and increased performance IQ in the young, but not in the adult cohort. The association between ADRB2 rs1042714 and IQ was not assessed in this cohort, as it was not in Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (i.e., that the genetic variation did not remain constant from one generation to the next).

The Moray House test and the Matrix Reasoning from the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS; version III) were performed by the subjects of the Lothian Birth Cohort. Respectively, they measure cognitive ability with a focus on verbal reasoning and non-verbal reasoning. The rs1042713 and rs1042714 SNPs were both associated with lower scores on the Matrix Reasoning, as well as, the Moray House test, compared to the ancestral alleles. Hence, the effect of ADRB2 rs1042713 on IQ seems age-dependent, with higher performance in younger populations and lower performance in older populations.

Attention

The NE system is vital in maintenance and direction of attention in presence of distractors, filtering of irrelevant stimuli, and regulating arousal. All of these are vital functions of executive function and problem-solving modulated by the tonic and phasic LC neuronal signaling (Tsukahara and Engle, 2021). Activation of ADRA2A especially plays an important role in the function of the prefrontal cortex, a critical area associated with attention, working memory, response, and impulse control (Nestler et al., 2001). Among others, two genetic polymorphisms of interest within this gene are rs1800544 MspI and rs553668 (C1780T) in the DraI site (3′-non-coding region) (Kalisch et al., 2024).

Cho et al. (2008a) investigated these two polymorphisms and their possible association with attention in children with ADHD. More specifically, a computerized Continuous Performance Test (CPT) was used, in which for example visual stimuli with shapes are presented to a participant. Some are target stimuli to which the participant has to push a button, while this reaction has to be inhibited when a non-target shape is shown. Outcome parameters include omission errors, i.e., the number of failures to respond to the target stimuli indicating inattention; commission errors, meaning inappropriately responding to the non-target stimuli indicating impulsivity (or deficit in response inhibition); response times for correct responses, which is a measure of information processing and motor speed; response time variability, which is a measure of variability of attention over the course of the test, and is an indicator of (deficits in) vigilance or attentional regulation. Subjects homozygous for the C-allele (C/C genotype) of the rs553668 DraI polymorphism showed higher response time variability compared to subjects with C/T and T/T genotypes. Subjects homozygous for the G-allele (G/G genotype) at the rs1800544 MspI polymorphism showed a trend towards lower response time variability compared to subjects with C/C and C/G genotypes but failed to reach significance. No significant differences were observed for omission errors, commission errors, and response time profiles.

A few years later, the same research group evaluated potential gene-gene interactions between the rs553668 Dral polymorphism in the gene coding for ADRA2A and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF; rs11030101) on the CPT in children with ADHD (Cho et al., 2011). Significant main effects of ADRA2A rs553668 as well as interaction effects of this polymorphism and BDNF rs11030101 were found on commission errors and response time variability of the CPT, but only the latter finding on response time variability survived correction for multiple testing. Interestingly, children homozygous for the C allele (C/C genotype) for the ADRA2A polymorphism and homozygous for the A allele (A/A genotype) at BDNF rs11030101 had significantly higher scores on response time variability than children with other genotypes. No significant effects were found for omission errors and response time. The studies by this research group suggest that genetic variants in the NE system can affect attention-related characteristics.

Focusing on the main effects of rs1800544 MspI and rs553668 DraI, Park et al. (2013) reported no genotype differences in any CPT outcome parameter in children with ADHD.

Associations between polymorphisms in the ADRA2A gene (rs1800544 MspI and rs553668 DraI) and attentional performance in children with ADHD were also examined by Kim et al. but with a different computerized comprehensive attention test (Kim et al., 2013). The test consisted of four tasks: first, the visual selective attention task, which assesses the ability of a subject to respond to a visual stimulus (a circle); second, the auditory selective attention task, which measures the ability of a subject to respond to an auditory stimulus (the sound of a bell); third, the children's ability to respond to a specific characteristic of a visual stimulus, while ignoring other concurrently presented stimuli was assessed with the flanker interference task; lastly, with the sustained attention task the children's ability to retain attention and suppress impulsivity (i.e., response inhibition) was evaluated. Kim et al. (2013) reported a statistically significant association between ADRA2A rs553668 DraI genotype and performance on the auditory selective attention task. Children with ADHD with the T-allele of ADRA2A rs553668 had additively increased response time variability on this task compared to the C-allele (corrected for ADHD symptom severity, IQ, sex, and age). In addition, an association in the same direction was found in the sustained attention task but only at the uncorrected significance level. No association between ADRA2A rs553668 genotype and performance on the other tasks or between ADRA2A rs1800544 genotype and attentional performance were reported.

Choi et al. (2020) evaluated potential interactions between the ADRA2A rs1800544 MspI and rs553668 DraI polymorphism and blood lead (a neurotoxicant that has been associated with diagnosis and symptoms of ADHD). Cognitive functioning was assessed using the CPT. No significant interaction was found between any of the ADRA2A polymorphisms and blood lead level on CPT omission errors or response time variability, when adjusted for multiple comparison.

In the ADRA2B gene, De Quervain et al. (de Quervain et al., 2007) linked the 12Glu9 deletion variant to attention and concentration. Both were measured using the d2 cancellation test, which is a paper and pen test in which a certain symbol (e.g., a specific letter with two dashes) needs to be recognized and marked, while other surrounding distractors (such as other letters) need to be ignored. Using this test, no genotype effect was found on attention and concentration.

Memory

Memory allows us to store and recall past experiences. Thereby, memory is a crucial component for learning and daily functioning. Emotional events are more likely to be remembered than neutral ones. While this emotional memory can have an advantage in a future situation, as the previous experience can be used to make a decision, it can also contribute to mental issues when traumatic experiences are involved. Especially in these situations of heightened emotional arousal, enhanced memory consolidation is primarily mediated by the NE system of the brain (Sheth et al., 2017).

To better understand the involved mechanisms, De Quervain [60, see also previous attention section] linked the ADRA2B 12Glu9 deletion variant genotype to emotional memory and to working memory. Participants were presented with neutral, positive, and negative affective images, and after each presentation participants rated valence and arousal. A delayed memory task was done 10 min later, in which participants had to freely recall each picture. Individuals of the deletion variant (homo- and heterozygous were grouped) had a significantly better recall memory for emotional pictures, thus for both positive and negative affective images. There was no sex effect. In addition, no genotype effect was found on arousal ratings for emotional versus neutral pictures, indicating that the found difference in enhanced memory for emotional information in deletion carriers was not due to genotype-related differences in emotional arousal. To assess working memory the digit span task was used, in which a participant needs to repeat a presented sequence. If correctly repeated, the next shown sequence will be longer until the participants cannot correctly repeat the sequence anymore. No genotype differences were found regarding working memory. These results suggest that carriers of the ADRA2B deletion variant have a better recall memory for emotional pictures but genotype does not affect their working memory.

The same genetic variant was further investigated regarding its role in emotional memory and using a similar paradigm by Gibbs et al. [50, see also previous IQ section]. Neutral or negative-arousing pictures were shown, and after one week an unexpected, delayed recognition memory test was done. As the number of homozygous deletion carriers were low, these individuals were combined with homozygous individuals for analyses. Genotype did not significantly affect emotional memory, which is in contrast to the study by De Quervain described above. Possibly, methodological differences such as doing a recall 10 min or one week after the memory encoding play a role. However, there was a significant interaction between ADRA2B and COMT val158met genotype, with a trend of the homozygous COMT val/val genotype having a worse memory performance when not being carrier of the ADRA2B deletion variant. This suggests that the impaired memory performance in homozygous COMT val/val carriers is reduced by the ADRA2B deletion allele.

In a next study, the same research group [51, see also previous IQ section] investigated the contribution of the 12Glu9 deletion polymorphism in the gene coding for ADRA2B in two processes of recognition memory: recollection and familiarity. As in their previous study, neutral or aversive-arousing pictures were presented with a delayed memory task a week later. For analyses, homozygous and heterozygous deletion carriers were grouped due to the low number of homozygous individuals. Arousal ratings for images did not differ between genotypes. Similarly, no genotype differences were found for familiarity tested one week after the initial experiment. When using a parameter sensitivity analysis, with using hit rate (i.e., proportion of previously seen items correctly identified as ‘old’) and false alarm rate (i.e., proportion of items incorrectly identified as ‘old’) of trials in which participants were highly certain of their answer, a significant interaction of ADRA2B and COMT genotype was found on overall memory. In their previous study (Gibbs et al., 2010), also an interaction between these two genotypes on recognition memory for both aversive and neutral pictures was reported, which however was not the case in this study. Examining recollection and familiarity as separate processes may have resulted in a loss of power. Overall, these findings do not provide evidence for a role of ADRA2B genotype in memory recollection and familiarity.

Another slightly different focus was chosen by Mammarella et al. (2016) who investigated the effect of ADRA2B 12Glu9 deletion genotype on emotional working memory and willingness to implement behaviors to avoid negative information. For this purpose, they used a modified version of the Operation Working Memory Span (O-span) Test for emotional words. In this task, participants need to solve math equations, while trying to remember neutrally, positively, or negatively valence words that are semantically unrelated and had to be recalled later. The second task was an auditory comprehension task, in which a brief recorded story was read. The recording was made in two intonations, a positive and a negative one. During listening, participants could switch from the negative to the positive version at each sentence. Thereby, the willingness and flexibility to implement behaviors to avoid listening to negative information could be tested. Last, to assess memory for words from different affective intonations, a yes-no recognition task was used, in which a series of words previously heard during the listening task were mixed with new, not heard before words and participants had to indicate whether they heard the word before. As in the studies above, deletion carriers were combined into one group because of the low number of homozygotes. In the Operation Working Memory Span test, it was found that deletion carriers remembered more positive words than non-deletion carriers, while no difference was found for negative or neutral words. Analysis of the auditory comprehension task revealed that the deletion carrier group made significantly fewer shifts from the negatively intonated to the positive story, suggesting that this group has a tendency to not act to avoid negative information. In addition, an interaction between genotype and prosody was found. The valence of prosody during the auditory comprehension task affected memory. This is shown by the yes-no recognition task, in which deletion carriers recognized more words with a positive prosody than non-deletion carriers, while recognition was not affected in non-deletion carriers. In sum, carriers of the ADRA2B deletion variant were less willing to switch from negative to positive intonation but remembered more positive information, suggesting that the deletion variant is associated with greater cognitive-affective flexibility.

The group of Todd et al. (2013) explored the influence of the ADRA2B gene - and particularly the 12Glu9 deletion variant - on individual differences in emotional memory, perception, and susceptibility to traumatic memories. To test this, they conducted an attentional blink task to measure how deletion variant carriers and non-carriers perceive emotionally salient stimuli. During this task, rapid serial presentation of visual stimuli, i.e., positive, negative, or neutral words, was displayed. Reduction of the attentional blink for emotional (negative, positive) relative to neutral words, can be interpreted as a form of selective attention bias towards affective stimuli. The researchers found that carriers of the ADRA2B deletion variant demonstrated reduced attentional blink for negatively arousing words, indicating greater perceptual awareness of negative stimuli compared to non-carriers. This suggests that the ADRA2B gene may influence not only how emotions affect subsequent perceptual experience but also how perceptions are initially encoded. The study found that other genetic variations (in the genes encoding COMT and the 5-HTT) were associated with overall attentional-blink performance, while the primary influence on emotional modulation of the attentional blink was attributed to the ADRA2B gene. Overall, the findings highlighted that the ADRA2B gene contributed to heightened sensitivity to negative stimuli, influencing how individuals perceive and encode emotionally salient stimuli.

In line with the above, the same group focused on the same 12Glu9 deletion variant to investigate the relationship between ADRA2B and arousal at encoding as well as at subsequent memories (Todd et al., 2014). This time they used an emotional memory task, during which participants had to rate images for valence (negative - positive) and arousal (not at all arousing - very arousing). After one week, a memory task was conducted showing the previously shown and new images. Participants had to indicate how confident they were that they had seen the image before, as an index of recognition memory. The findings revealed a genotype effect with ADRA2B deletion variant carriers rating images as more arousing than non-carriers. In addition, carriers of the deletion variant showed a positive association between subjective arousal to negative images during encoding and memory accuracy one week later, indicating that the deletion variant was linked to enhanced memory for negative images. Interestingly, memory was enhanced for deletion carriers who rated images higher in arousal than those who rated them lower but worsened for non-carriers who rated images as higher in arousal than those who rated them lower. Moreover, main effects and gene × gene interactions between ADRA2B and some other genes were explored. Only an interaction between ADRA2B x KIBRA in overall memory performance (across all emotions) was found to be significant. The encoded KIBRA protein is believed to contribute to synaptic plasticity. Overall, this suggests that ADRA2B variants affect memory encoding and subsequent memory.

Using an attentional blink task (with negative and neutral words) as Todd et al. (2013), the group of Naudts et al. [52, see also previous IQ section] investigated if attentional bias for emotionally arousing information is associated with a polymorphism in the ADRA2B gene (12Glu9) and in the gene encoding 5-HTT (rs25531, 5-HTTLPR, serotonin-transporter-linked polymorphic region). No main effects of the individual genes were found. However, significant interaction between genes, suggested increased attentional bias for aversive compared to neutral information in participants carrying at least one copy of the short (S) allele of 5-HTTLPR, but only if they were ADRA2B deletion non-carriers. The same attentional bias for aversive information was also observed in participants possessing 5-HTTLPR long (L) allele homozygotes, only if they were ADRA2B deletion carriers. This indicates that when the 5-HTTLPR S-allele is present, which is considered a potential risk factor for depressive and anxiety disorders, the ADRA2B deletion variant moderates the negative attentional bias. Conversely, in the absence of the S-allele, the bias is intensified.

As previous studies provided some evidence for a role of the ADRA2B 12Glu9 deletion variant in enhanced emotional memory, Zoladz et al. (2014) examined the interaction of this polymorphism and stress on memory. To induce stress, participants had to put a hand in ice-cold water for 3 min, after which they had to study a list of words with varying emotional valence (negative, positive, neutral) and arousal (not and very arousing). Participants rated words on valence and arousal. A free recall test was done immediately afterwards, in which they had to write down as many words as they remembered within 5 min. Another free recall and recognition test was done 24 h later. In the recognition test, participants received words presented the previous day as well as new words and had to indicate for each word whether it was presented the day before (“old”) or new. Genotype did not affect word valence and arousal ratings. Regarding both immediate and delayed free recall, a significant genotype × menstrual stage × arousal interaction was reported. More specifically, when analyzing females only, the deletion variant was related to an increased recall of arousing words, when women were in the follicular phase of the menstrual cycle. In contrast, in female non-deletion carriers, recall of arousing words was enhanced when being in the luteal phase. In males, no genotype effect was found. Looking at stress effects revealed that female ADRA2B deletion carriers who underwent the stress induction task (and not the control task with warm water) showed better recognition memory for emotional and non-emotional words combined than other groups. This is not in line with previous work such as by De Quervain et al. who found specific effects for recalling emotional pictures in deletion carriers. However, previous studies used pictures instead of words and did not utilize stress induction. Overall, these findings imply that female carriers of the ADRA2B deletion polymorphism are susceptible to stress-induced increases in memory.

Emotional processing

Emotional processing refers to interacting processes to recognize, assess, evaluate and regulate emotions. Shoumin et al. (2014) investigated to which extent genetic variation in the NE system affects emotion regulation. Focus was on the 12Glu9 deletion variant in the ADRA2B gene and emotional regulation of attention, as attentional processes such as alertness and orientation can be affected by emotions (Cohen et al., 2011). To assess orientation and emotional regulation of attention, participants underwent a spatial cueing task, in which a visual cue was presented, and participants had to indicate as fast as possible whether a subsequently shown target was located on the left or right side. Trials were divided into valid and invalid ones. In valid trials, cues were congruent with target locations, whereas cues in invalid trials were incongruent with target positions. Thereby, valid trials directed attention to the “cued location”, as cue and target were at the same location, whereas invalid trials stimulated orientation to a location different from the target location (“uncued location”. Cues were facial pictures with neutral, negative (sad, fearful, angry) and happy expressions, and words (nouns, verbs, adjectives) with neutral, negative, and positive valences. No genotype effect was found on reaction times in valid and invalid trails. However, a significant interaction was found between genotype and orientation of attention. When cue and target were located at the same location, carriers homozygous for the deletion variant had a shorter delay in orientation to the correct location compared to individuals of other genotypes. While the emotional expression of the faces affected orientation of attention, no interaction with genotype was found. Using words instead of pictures with faces showed a marginal genotype effect on orientation, with a stronger orientation to the cued location in homozygous deletion allele carriers, particularly compared to heterozygous individuals. One week after testing, participants rated the pleasantness of the facial expressions and words. No genotype effect was found. Overall, these results indicate that genetic variation in the ADRA2B gene does not majorly affect emotional processing, but does have an effect on orientation of attention, with the deletion variant (presumably associated with higher NE levels) facilitating the orientation of attention.

Copying styles refer to thoughts and behaviors used to manage stressful or unpleasant situations and differ widely between individuals. As genetic factors could influence someone's coping style, Busjahn et al. (2002) examined the effects of four supposedly functional polymorphisms in the ADRB2 gene (C-47T, A46G, C79G, G491A) in twin pairs. Coping was measured using a questionnaire consisting of different types of reactions to different situations that adversely affect or disturb someone's emotional balance. Participants had to indicate how likely a certain reaction would be. Association analysis revealed significant associations between G491A SNP located in the coding region of the gene on coping factors defense, emotional coping and active coping. With regard to factor substitution only a marginal association was found. However, which genotype scored highest varied between these factors and no clear pattern could be derived from the data. Regarding the C79G SNP an association with factors defense and emotional coping was observed, also with varying results regarding which genotype or allele scored highest. Overall, these findings suggest that genetic variants in the ADRA2B, as well as, in the ADRB2 gene could play a role in some coping factors.

Executive functioning

The efficacy of executive functions varies between individuals. Waldman et al. (2006) investigated three ADRA2A SNPs (rs1800544 MspI, rs1800545 HhaI, rs553668 DraI) and their role in executive functions. The rs1800544 MspI and rs553668 Dral polymorphisms were associated with several measures of executive functioning. MspI was associated with the Tower of London task, variability in reaction time on the Stop Signal Task, and performance on Trail making Test Forms A and B; Dral was associated with performance on Trails A and B Forms, as well as the Trails B - A index. No significant associations were observed for the rs1800545 HhaI. Furthermore, no significant associations between any of these genotypes and the Stroop Color–Word Interference Test were found. These results suggest that some associations between performance on executive functioning tasks depend on genotype. However, as all executive functions measures were not normally distributed and as all measures showed significant skewness and kurtosis (except for the Stroop word condition), results should be considered preliminary.

Cummins et al. (2014) applied an entire test battery of five tasks to participants to link variation in the ADRA2A gene (rs1800544 MspI and rs602618) to performance. The first task was the Eriksen flanker task, in which participants are instructed to make directional responses (left or right) to a central target arrow while ignoring four flanking distractors; the second task was a choice response-time task in which participants were asked to make rapid motor responses to two target stimuli; in the third task, a stop-signal task participants had to make a fast motor response to a target stimulus but also had to inhibit their motor response, when the target stimulus was followed by another “stop” signaling stimulus; the fourth task consisted of a spatial competition task requiring participants to select target stimuli from a number of stimuli that also included competing distractors, while the size of stimulus arrays (target plus distractors) was manipulated to test attentional capacity; and the last task was an exogenous covert orienting task, requiring participants to select (validly, invalidly or neutrally) cued target stimuli from among competing distractors. Outcome measurements were reaction time for correct responses and the intra-individual coefficient of variation of the reaction time for correct responses. Analysis of the intra-individual coefficient of variation of the Eriksen flanker task, as well as the choice response-time and the stop-signal tasks showed a significant association with rs1800544, with each C-allele increasing the score. Likewise, each A-allele of rs602618 additively increased the score. For the Eriksen flanker task, both SNP associations withstand multiple testing correction, suggesting that the association between ADRA2A and the intra-individual coefficient of variation was mainly driven by associations with the flanker task.

In a cross-sectional multicenter study, 84 SNPs within 38 genes were associated with cognition by Kurita et al. (2016) in a large patient sample of more than 1300 cancer patients randomly divided into a discovery and a replication sample. Of the NE system, ADRA2A rs11195419 C/A and rs553668 G/C, ADRB1 rs180153, and ADRB2 rs1042713 G/A, rs1042714 C/G, and rs1042717 G/A were investigated (rs1800888 and rs1042719 were excluded from the analysis). The Mini-Mental State Examination, an observer-rated cognitive test battery, was used to assess cognition dysfunction in terms of orientation to time and place, registration of words, attention, calculation, word recall, language, and visual construction. Main analyses were done using a co-dominant model, in subsequent analyses dominant and recessive models were used. In the co-dominant model, no effect of NE receptors was found in the total discovery sample. Subanalyses in patients taking at least 400 mg morphine per day (n = 300) revealed a significant association with ADRA2A rs11195419, but this finding did not withstand multiple testing correction and could also not be confirmed in the replication sample. Similarly, in the dominant model, ADRA2A rs11195419 was only significantly associated with the score of the Mini-Mental State Examination in the subgroup of patients taking morphine. Again, multiple testing correction led to overall non-significance and replication in the second sample failed. In the recessive model, another SNP, namely ADRA2A rs553668 showed an association with examination scores, but again only in patients taking morphine in the discovery sample before correction for multiple testing. Therefore, this study does not show evidence for a role of these SNPs in the gene encoding ADRA2A in cognitive dysfunction.

In addition to variants in the gene coding for ADRA2A, also variants in the gene for ADRA2B were investigated. Based on previous research showing a role of the NE system in individual differences to interpret ambiguous information as either more positive or negative, or having a bias towards reward or punishment, Ehlers et al. (2019) aimed to determine the degree to which such a bias can be altered by training depending on ADRA2B 12Glu9 deletion genotype. The initial bias of an individual was measured using a bias probe task in which morphed faces with unambiguously emotional expressions ranging from happy (signaling reward) to angry (signaling threat) were shown. Participants had to make a forced choice whether the face was happy or angry. After repeated presentation of one expression, for example angry faces, ambiguous facial expressions tend to be perceived as the opposite expression, thus happy in response to repeated presentation of angry faces. Therefore, in a second phase, a two-back working memory task was used, in which all facial expressions were angry to shift participants’ biases towards more positive judgment. To ensure attention to the faces, participants had to push a button to indicate whether a shown face was the same as the one two faces back. The induced change in bias, referred to as bias flexibility, was assessed afterwards using the bias probe that was also used to assess baseline bias. No genotype effect was found on working memory performance in the two-back task. In addition, there was no statistical evidence for carriers of the deletion variant (homo- and heterozygous deletion carriers grouped) to shift their biases more flexibly than individuals without this variant. However, deletion carriers judged significantly more faces as happy for pictures towards the middle of the morph continuum, showing that they have a more positive bias than non-carriers. In a follow-up analysis, it appeared that the bias at baseline predicted the change in bias. More specifically, the higher the negative bias was at baseline, the more the bias changed towards positive judgments. Overall, these findings suggest that genetic variation in the NE system is associated with subjective perceptions of ambiguous stimuli as being more rewarding, but not with bias flexibility.

To sum up, these studies provide limited evidence for some effects of genetic variants in the NE system on cognition. However, effects are dependent on the investigated specific genetic variant and cognitive outcome measurement. ADRA2A variants affect some executive functions, while ADRA2B variants affect memory and attention. More specifically, the 12Glu9 deletion variant of ADRA2B has been associated with better recall memory for emotional pictures, but not emotional words, and with greater cognitive-affective flexibility as well as orientation of attention. IQ only seemed to be dependent on ADRB2 genotype when age was taken into account. In addition, some effects depended on interaction with other genes. This shows that more research on NE genes is needed, taking other NE receptor types such as the not yet investigated ADRA1A/C and factors such as age, sex, and potential gene-gene interactions into account.

Personality traits

Personality traits refer to the individual characteristics of a person, such as temperament, extroversion, and neuroticism. They can differ widely between subjects and may also shaped by intra-individual genetic variation.

Focusing on the ADRA1A and ADRA2A gene, Tsai et al. (2001) investigated the potential association between genotypes of the adrenergic receptors (rs numbers were not reported by the authors) and personality in healthy volunteers. No genotype differences were found on three dimensions of personality (i.e., harm avoidance, novelty seeking, and reward dependence) as assessed by the Tridimensional Personality Questionnaire (TPQ). Reward dependence relates to the aversion of isolation from others, worrying about the evaluation by others, and the preference for being in a group. Sub-analysis per sex also did not reveal any evidence for a genotype effect on personality.

The ADRA2A gene was also of interest to Maestu et al. (2008) who specifically focused on the rs1800544 C-1291T MspI polymorphism inf the promoter region of the ADRA2A gene. In an adolescent cohort, the Personality Item Pool Neuroticism, Extraversion, Openness (NEO) was used to obtain measurements of personality traits. This questionnaire measures the Big Five personality dimensions: neuroticism, extraversion, openness, agreeableness, and conscientiousness. No overall effect of genotype was found. However, when looking at subscales of the questionnaire, individuals homozygous of the G-allele scored significantly higher on depression and lower on morality and orderliness compared to other genotypes. Further, a significant interaction was found between genotype and sex on friendliness, gregariousness, and cheerfulness, with boys of one or two C-alleles scoring lower than girls of the same genotype, while boys of the G/G genotype scored higher than girls of the same genotype. This suggests that the genetic variant in the ADRA2A gene affects personality traits in male and female adolescents differently.

Comings et al. (2000a) tested if ADRA2A rs1800544 MspI could be considered a risk factor of behaviors of arousal, vigilance, impulsivity, irritability, and hostility in two independent groups of healthy individuals: university students as well as parents of twins (from the Minnesota Twin Family Study). Several questionnaires including the TCI were filled in by students, whereas parents of twins filled in the Multidimensional Personality Questionnaire. In the student sample, they found that the rs1800544 MspI polymorphism was associated with measures of indirect hostility, verbal hostility/aggression, irritability, and negativity. In the parent sample, the rs1800544 MspI polymorphism was associated with impulsivity only and hence not with aggression. Authors considered irritability and impulsivity being linked to similar behaviors and thus interpreted that the observed genotype effect on impulsivity in parents aligns with the one found on irritability in students. However, the genotype effect on aggression could not be found in parents. Because of this non-significant finding in measures of aggression in the parent sample, the authors concluded that the alpha-2a receptor might be associated with irritability rather than aggression.

Besides rs1800544, other SNPs occur in the ADRA2A gene. De Cerqueira et al. (de Cerqueira et al., 2011) assessed the association between three polymorphisms in the ADRA2A gene to personality dimensions in patients with ADHD. The three polymorphisms of interest were rs1800544 MspI, rs1800545 Hhal, and rs553668 DraI. Personality was assessed using the Temperament and Character Inventory (TCI; which is the newer version of the TPQ) that is subdivided into four temperament dimensions (i.e., novelty seeking, harm avoidance, reward dependance, and persistence) and three character dimensions (i.e., self-directedness, cooperativeness, and self-transcendence). As the less frequent alleles were rarely present in this study, homozygotes and heterozygotes carriers of the rare alleles were grouped and compared to noncarriers of the rare allele. Regarding the rs1800544 polymorphism, individuals with the G-allele had lower scores on harm avoidance. For the rs553668 polymorphism, individuals of the T-allele had less harm avoidance, less persistence, and more novelty seeking. No effect of rs1800545 was found on temperament or character. Subsequent haplotype analysis of G-G-T (rs1800544-rs1800545-rs553668 or MspI-HhaI-DraI) showed that carriers of this haplotype scored lower on harm avoidance and persistence, but higher on novelty seeking compared to individuals of other haplotypes. These results suggest that polymorphisms in the gene encoding ADRA2A are associated with personality traits that are often related to externalizing behavioral traits.

In addition to the ADRA1A and ADRA2A genes, the gene coding for ADRA2C has been investigated in the context of personality. The effects of the 322–325 deletion polymorphism in this gene on personality was studied in a sample of healthy individuals by Tsai et al. (2002). They found no effect of genotype on the TPQ score or the subscales of novelty seeking, harm avoidance, and reward dependence. Therefore, this study does not provide any evidence for a role of genetic variants in the α-2c receptor on personality traits.

Stein et al. (2004) investigated the impact of ADRB1 genotype on personality using the NEO-Personality Inventory-Revised. The study analyzed two ADRB1 SNPs: Ser49Gly and Arg389Gly. They found that the odds of having low extraversion (i.e., lack of warmth, lack of gregariousness, lack of activity, lack of excitement seeking, lack of positive emotions) were higher when the Gly49 allele was present. Linear regression analyses on the lower level facets of the domain showed that Ser49Gly was a predictor of facets e1 (lack of warmth), e2 (lack of gregariousness) and e3 (lack of assertiveness), but not e4 (lack of activity), e5 (lack of excitement seeking) and e6 (lack of positive emotions). Two additional assessments were conducted, the social interaction anxiety scale and the revised shyness scale. The Ser49Gly or Arg389Gly polymorphisms were not significantly correlated with the scores obtained with the social interaction anxiety scale. Furthermore, after adjusting for sex, no significant correlation between the ADRB1 polymorphisms and the revised shyness scale were found.

The association between polymorphisms in the NE system, including the ADRB1 rs1801252 SNP, and temperament was investigated by Yamano et al. (2008). Healthy female nursing students completed the reward dependence subsection of the TCI. Based on the mean reward dependence score of the entire sample, the group was divided into a high and low score group. There was no association between ADRB1 rs1801252 genotype and reward dependence.

Comings et al. (2000b) investigated the relative role of 59 candidate genes (related to, among others, NE, serotonin (5-HT), dopamine, and sex hormones) on temperament traits (novelty seeking, harm avoidance, reward dependence, and persistence) and character traits (cooperativeness, self-directedness, and self-transcendence) using the TCI with the aim to explore which genes predominantly contribute to these traits. NE-related genes (i.e., DBH TaqI, ADRA2A MspI, ADRA2B deletion/insertion, ADRA2C dinucleotide repeat, NET A1970G Mn/l, phenylethanolamine N-methyltransferase (PNMT) G-148A, ADRB1 Eco0109I, MAO-A FnuI, and COMT val158met) were reported to explain about 25% of the total variance of reward dependence, suggesting that the NE system plays a role in the tendency to respond to signals of social reward.

Overall, these studies suggest that genetic variants in the NE system could play a role in some personality traits. Some ADRA2A variants (i.e., rs1800544 and rs553668) seem to be associated with externalizing behavioral traits, however not all studies report this. Additionally, the rs1800544 ADRA2A SNP might affect personality traits differently in males and females, although others did not report an effect of sex on this genotype. Lastly, few studies investigated the effects of polymorphisms in the ADRA2C and ADRB1 genes. No consistent effects have been reported.

Brain function

Exposure to stress has been associated with changes in brain function and structure (Kaufman and Charney, 2001; McEwen, 2006). Multiple studies investigate the association between polymorphisms in NE receptor genes and brain function during rest or while performing a behavioral task, as indexed with fMRI and SPECT imaging.

One outcome parameter in the context of brain function is brain perfusion, which is closely linked to neuronal activity (Venkat et al., 2016). Brain perfusion can be determined using SPECT imaging. Kim et al. (2010) (see also section 3.1.1.) sought to examine whether the ADRA2A rs1800544 MspI polymorphism affects this parameter, as assessed with 99mTc-HMPAO SPECT, in children with ADHD. Carriers of the C-allele showed significant reduction in perfusion of the bilateral orbitofrontal regions compared to non-carriers, suggesting that this allele could be associated with pathophysiology. However, this effect was only present before multiple testing corrections, warranting more research.

Other studies used fMRI imaging to assess brain activation in, for instance, the amygdala while participants were in rest or performed a behavioral task (Rasch et al., 2009; Cousijn et al., 2010; Urner et al., 2011; Todd et al., 2015). The amygdala is an important brain region for the creation of emotional memories (LaBar and Cabeza, 2006). Rasch et al. (2009) investigated whether amygdala activity during encoding of emotional memories was dependent on the ADRA2B genotype, in particular the 12Glu9 deletion variant. While in a 3T MRI scanner, fMRI images were acquired while healthy participants performed a n-back and picture task. The 0- and 2-back versions of the n-back were used to assess working memory. Letters were shown on a screen and participants had to respectively indicate when the letter “x” was presented and when the presented letter matched the letter from two steps earlier. Working memory performance on the n-back task was similar for carriers and non-carriers of the ADRA2B 12Glu9 deletion variant. During the encoding phase of the picture task, participants were presented with pictures of scenes, and they needed to rate them according to valence (i.e., neutral, negative, or positive). Ten minutes after the picture task memory performance was assessed with a free-recall task. Heterozygous and homozygous carriers of the ADRA2B 12Glu9 deletion demonstrated higher recall performance of negative pictures than non-carriers, although not statistically significant. The groups did also not differ with regard to recall performance of positive and neutral images. Compared to participants without the deletion, carriers of the ADRA2B 12Glu9 deletion demonstrated higher activation of the right amygdala (left amygdala did not survive multiple corrections) while encoding images with negative versus neutral emotional valence. While encoding negative pictures, the functional connectivity between the right amygdala and other brain regions (i.e., left insula, left inferior frontal gyrus, and left postcentral gyrus) was significantly higher in participants with than without the deletion. This suggests that the communication between these regions is tightened. As the insula is thought to be involved in emotional memory, this might clarify why carriers of the ADRA2B 12Glu9 deletion variant seem to have a better recall memory for emotional pictures, as reported by De Quervain et al. (de Quervain et al., 2007).

Cosijn et al. (Cousijn et al., 2010) assessed the acute phasic activity of the amygdala to faces with emotional expressions in healthy participants with and without the 12Glu9 deletion. While in the fMRI scanner (3T), participants watched movie clips with non-arousing scenes with emotionally neutral content. Subsequently, they passively viewed photographs of faces with fearful or happy expressions. Carriers and non-carriers of the 12Glu9 deletion demonstrated a similar increase in amygdala response to faces. Additionally, continuous arterial spin labeling MRI was used to measure regional perfusion, as a measure of tonic amygdala activity. Again, no differences were reported between the groups.

When mood at the time of memory encoding or retrieval matches the valence of the emotional stimulus, valence-specific effects may be larger (Bower et al., 1981). Therefore, Urner et al. (2011) combined a negative mood induction procedure with an emotional-learning task during fMRI scanning in healthy carriers and non-carriers of the ADRA2B 12Glu9 deletion. While in the 1.5T scanner, negative mood was induced by showing the participants clips from a movie about the holocaust. Subsequently, they performed a memory task, during which they had to memorize 240 negative and positive pictures displaying humans and rate them according to valence. Urner et al. reported no difference in recognition memory accuracy between carriers and non-carriers. Nevertheless, deletion carriers demonstrated elevated activity in the left amygdala and left inferior frontal gyrus during successful memory formation, but not retrieval, relative to non-carriers. They found no significant interaction with emotional valence and thus report no evidence for mood-congruent effects.

As shown by some studies (Rasch et al. (2009)) but not all (Urner et al. (2011)) emotional events or stimuli tend to be remembered better and more vividly. This effect is called memory vividness and is partially due to the subjective experience of emotional events and stimuli as more perceptually vivid. As this emotionally enhanced vividness differs between individuals, Todd et al. (2015) investigated whether the 12Glu9 deletion variant in the ADRA2B gene (as described above) modulates brain activity related to emotionally enhanced vividness. Hetero- and homozygous deletion carriers were combined into one group and non-carriers were matched for sex and working memory performance. Using the visual noise magnitude estimation task, negative, positive, and neutral affective pictures were overlaid with different extents of noise. Participants had to rate each picture for the degree of noise, while brain activity was measured using a 3T fMRI scanner. After one week, participants rated the images on the degree of being emotionally arousing. Overall, the deletion carrier group had higher perceptual vividness for positive and negative pictures compared to neutral ones than the non-carriers. Looking at brain activity, emotionally enhanced vividness was associated with a stronger signal in the ventromedial prefrontal cortex in deletion carriers compared to non-carriers, an area that plays a key role in salience evaluation. Path analysis suggested that in non-deletion carriers emotionally enhanced vividness was most accurately represented by a simple model wherein the amygdala modulates the lateral occipital complex, whereas in deletion carriers the contribution of an additional ventromedial prefrontal cortex pathway played a key role. Regarding arousal ratings obtained one week later, no genotype effect was found.

Neumeister et al. (2006) examined the effect of a four amino acid deletion at codon 322–325 in the gene encoding the alpha-2c receptor on processing emotionally salient information in medication-free patients with major depression and healthy controls. Before a PET scan, during which participants viewed sad, happy, and fearful faces and had to indicate the gender of each face, the tracer 10 mCi of H215O was injected to assess neural responses. Patients with one or two deletion alleles had higher amygdala, and pregenual and subgenual anterior cingulate gyrus activity when viewing sad faces compared to patients without the deletion, as well as, to healthy individuals with the deletion allele. Moreover, in healthy deletion allele carriers (and patients in any genotype group), the amygdala was more activated in response to sad faces relative to happy faces, whereas activation was reduced when viewing fearful faces compared to happy faces. The findings of heightened responsiveness to sad faces in individuals that carry the deletion variant (patients and healthy), as opposed to noncarriers, suggests a genetic association between alpha-2C receptor function and the activation of brain regions crucial for emotional processing, independent of specific symptomatology.

In sum, brain perfusion might be affected by the ADRA2A rs1800544 polymorphism. However, before firm conclusions can be drawn, additional research is warranted, also with regard to not yet studied NE receptor subtypes such as ADRA1A/C. Additionally, ADRA2B and ADRA2C genotype seems to be related to the activity of the amygdala during emotional memory tasks (with higher activity in 12Glu9 deletion carriers), although this might depend on the specific task, the vividness of stimuli, as well as the environmental context. Therefore, the effect of genes on the brain might only become visible under distinct conditions.

Pharmacological interventions

Pharmacological interventions are a fundamental tool in research and are used to target specific biological systems to better understand the molecular mechanisms involved in mental health. Methylphenidate (MPH) is a pharmacological compound that inhibits the NET as well as the dopamine transporter, thereby increasing the concentration of these two neurotransmitters in the synaptic cleft. This increase, in turn, enhances neurotransmission, particularly in the prefrontal cortex (Verghese and Abdijadid, 2023).

The previously described study of Kim et al. (2013) (section 3.1.1) also examined the relationship between changes in attentional performance induced by MPH and polymorphisms in the ADRA2A gene (rs1800544 MspI and rs553668 DraI). MPH-naïve children with ADHD were treated with MPH for 12 weeks. Before and after the treatment period, attentional performance was examined as previously described (section 3.1.1). The MPH-induced change in mean response time variability on the flanker interference task significantly increased with increasing possession of the G-allele of ADRA2A rs1800544 MspI compared to the C-allele (corrected for ADHD symptom severity, IQ, sex, age, and MPH dose). At an uncorrected level, the G-allele of rs1800544 MspI also showed additive association with the sustained attention task.

Park et al. (2013) built on the previous studies examining the link between ADRA2A's rs1800544 MspI and rs553668 DraI and response to an 8-week MPH treatment regimen, however set out to achieve this by examining attentional performance using the CPT in children diagnosed with ADHD (see section 3.1.1. for baseline performance). Presence of a C-allele of the MspI polymorphism tended to reduce the response to MPH treatment when compared with the G-allele carriers. This was seen in the G/G group having a better improvement in both omission errors (inattention), commission errors (impulsivity), response times (information processing and motor response speed) and response-time variability (variability or consistency of attention) than the C-carrier group (C/G + C/C). Further, a significant association of the DraI T-allele and worse improvement in the mean response time variability as well as commission errors post-treatment was observed.

Unal et al. (2016) examined the effect of the ADRA2A promoter's rs1800544 MspI polymorphism on the treatment response to MPH, as quantified by two attention tasks, i.e., the CPT and Trail Making tests (TMT-A, B). The completion time for both TMT-A and -B, and the number of errors and corrections were significantly reduced after treatment. Treatment also reduced the CPT latency period and omission/commission error rates. However, for both findings no genotype effect was reported.

Overall, studies focused on assessing the effects of MPH depending on genetic variants in the ADRA2A gene reported inconsistent results. Studies used different methodologies, which may explain these divergent findings.

(Nor)epinephrine transporter

NET is a key protein involved in the regulation of extracellular NE reuptake in the central nervous system (Benarroch, 2018). It is primarily expressed in the presynaptic membranes of neurons of the NE system (Mandela and Ordway, 2006). Approximately 80% of NEs reuptake at nerve terminal synapses is mediated by NET (Craig, 2007) (Berridge et al., 2017), contributing to its deactivation by removing it from the synaptic cleft and storing it in presynaptic vesicles (Mandela and Ordway, 2006; Zhou, 2004).

NET is encoded by the SLC6A2 gene (NET gene) located on chromosome 16, locus 16q12.2. SLC6A2 contains numerous SNPs that have been associated with alterations in NET activity (Zhang et al., 2016). Several SNPs, such as the A-3081T, T-182C, and G1287A variants have been investigated as potential candidates that may affect susceptibility to various mental disorders (e.g., ADHD (Kim et al., 2006; Hohmann et al., 2015) and generalized anxiety disorder (Chang et al., 2020)) and/or mental health symptoms (e.g., ADHD-related symptoms (Bobb et al., 2005)). Such variants can also affect resilience- and mental health-related characteristics and influence treatment response to medication that targets NET (Hong et al., 2012), as described in the following sections.

Cognition

IQ

The effect of the intronic polymorphisms rs998424 and rs3785157 in the gene encoding NET on IQ in children with ADHD was examined by Bobb et al. (2005). A higher performance on the similarities subtest of the Full Scale Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children, Third Edition (WISC-III) was found in carriers of the rs998424 risk C-allele, which however was not significant when applying a more stringent alpha level of 0.01 given multiple testing.

In line with this, also in the study by Hohmann et al. (2015) no effect of the rs3785157 genotype was found on the IQ of children at an age of 11 years, measured by the Culture Fair Intelligence Test (CFIT) 20. In this longitudinal birth cohort study, also the promoter located SNP rs28386840 A/T of the NET gene was examined, but no significant association with IQ was found.

Likewise, in another study in children with ADHD (Cho et al., 2008b), no effect of the exonic silent restriction fragment length polymorphism rs5569 G1287A nor of the A-3081T polymorphism in the NET gene was reported on IQ using the WISC for children.

The same non-significant results were reported for these two polymorphisms in children with ADHD by Park et al. (2012) who assessed IQ using the WISC for children as other studies before.

Oh and Kim (2016) examined the effects of the two polymorphisms, and additionally rs2242446 T-182C, on IQ in drug-naive children with ADHD. As before, no genotype effect was found on verbal IQ, performance IQ or total IQ assessed by using the WISC for children.

Altogether, this limited evidence does not indicate a major role of genetic variants in the gene coding for NET in affecting IQ.

Attention

Using the CPT (as outlined before in section 3.1.1.), Cho et al. (2008b) investigated the effect of the NET rs5569 G1287A and the A-3081T polymorphisms on attention in children with ADHD. Only one trend was observed (short of significance), namely towards higher response time variability, a measure of vigilance or attentional regulation, in children with one or two T-alleles of the A-3081T polymorphism. This could indicate that this polymorphism could play a role in the sustained attention deficit commonly seen in ADHD, but more research is needed to confirm this finding.

Similarly, Kollins et al. (2008) applied this CPT in children with ADHD, siblings, and parents. Overall, the heritability of the test phenotypes (i.e., the outcome measures) were high and statistically significant. The CPT was also used by Song et al. (2011). The performance of children with ADHD was analyzed depending on the NET rs5569 G1287A genotype. No significant differences were found between the three genotypes regarding any outcome parameter. However, when grouping individuals based on the rare allele, it appeared that homozygous G/G carriers had significantly lower commission errors compared to those without G/G genotypes, indicating less impulsivity or a lower deficit in response inhibition. Other outcome parameters did not differ. This result indicates that the G-allele could play a protective role in the response inhibition deficit seen in ADHD.

Kim et al. (2013) aimed to assess attention using different computerized tests in children with ADHD (as outlined in 3.1.1.) depending on the NET rs5569 G1287A genotype. After controlling for ADHD symptoms, age, sex, and IQ, the NET G1287A A-allele was additively related to an increased mean reaction time variability in the auditory selective attention task as well as the sustained attention task. A trend in the same direction was found for the visual selective attention task, suggesting that the G1287A A-allele is related to an increased reaction time variability in various attention tasks, and thus worse attentional maintenance in children with ADHD.

The same genetic variant, NET G1287A, was investigated on its effect on the CPT in drug-naive ADHD children by Oh and Kim [100, see also previous section]. In addition, rs2242446 T-182C and rs28386840 A-3081T were assessed. No overall effect on genotype was found on CPT outcome measurements. However, separate analyses per sex, omission errors (i.e., failure to respond to the target stimuli as a measure of inattention) were lower in girls homozygous for the G-allele of the A1287A polymorphism. This indicates that the G-allele could have protective effects in females.

Also, Bobb et al. [96, see also previous IQ section] assessed attention in children with ADHD. Using a computerized task to test participants’ ability to divide, shift, direct, and sustain attention they determine the effects of the intronic polymorphisms rs998424 and rs3785157 in the NET gene on attention-related parameters. Participants had to react to visual and auditory stimuli and efficiently divide their attention when they needed to detect both a visual and auditory stimulus at the same time. Shifting selective attention was tested by changing the location of a stimulus to another one. No effect of rs998424 and rs3785157 was found, suggesting that these two polymorphisms do not play a major role in attention (in ADHD).

Hohmann et al. [95, see also previous IQ section] used a longitudinal birth cohort study to study the effects of rs3785157 and rs28386840 of the NET gene (the latter also being investigated in previous studies such as by Bobb et al. (2005)) on the CPT. A trend was found for rs3785157 being associated with the number of omission errors, with individuals of the T/C genotype making fewer errors than individuals of the other two genotypes. Additionally, this polymorphism had a significant effect on the sensitivity on the test, implying that carriers of the T/C genotype had a higher perceptual sensitivity compared to the C/C genotype.

In sum, while reports are not fully consistent on the effect of genetic variation on attention, there is some evidence for less impulsivity or better response inhibition in carriers homozygous for the G-allele of the rs5569 G1287A polymorphism. However, in one study this effect was only found in females, highlighting the importance to investigate sufficiently large samples to take sex into account.

Visual memory

Shang et al. (2015) investigated the relationship between genetic variation in the NET gene and visual memory in children with ADHD, siblings, and parents. In total, 21 SNPs with a minor allele frequency ≥0.10 were analyzed. Visual memory was assessed by means of the Pattern Recognition Memory (PRM) and Spatial Recognition Memory (SRM) tasks of the Cambridge Neuropsychological Test Automated Battery. These tasks include both memory and visual searches and put a high demand on effortful processing. The PRM consisted of a two-choice forced discrimination paradigm, in which the participant was asked to indicate which of the two shown geometrical figures was seen previously, after which feedback was provided. In the second task, the SRM, which was another two-choice forced discrimination paradigm, the participant saw empty boxes across the screen and had to memorize these. After showing five stimuli at different screen locations, the participant saw two boxes and had to indicate which of the two locations of these boxes was previously seen. Feedback was given after each trial. Outcome parameters in both tests were response latency of correct responses and the total number of correct responses. A significant association was found between rs36011 and both outcomes in both tasks. However, significance did not survive multiple testing correction. Furthermore, analysis of haplotypes showed evidence for overtransmission of rs36011 (T-allele)/rs1566652 (G-allele) in a haplotype composed of genetic variants located in intron 8 for both outcome parameters in both tasks. Overtransmission implies that this particular haplotype is transmitted from one generation to the next at a higher frequency than expected by change and suggests that the haplotype increases the risk of disease or a specific phenotype (Lewis and Knight, 2012). Statistical significance remained for this haplotype when being determined by permutation tests, except for the mean latency for correct responses in the SRM. The frequency of the haplotype was relatively high with 28.6%, potentially implying clinical relevance. Two additional overtransmitted haplotypes were rs3785152 (T-allele)/rs40147 (C-allele) in a haplotype composed of variants of intron 3 (rs36021, rs3785152, rs40147, rs1814269, rs1814270) for total correct responses in the PRM task, and rs1345429 (G-allele)/rs12920735 (A-allele)/rs5564 (T-allele)/rs47958 (C-allele)/rs3785157 (C-allele) in a haplotype composed of variants of intron 4, 5 and 7 (rs1345429, rs12920735, rs5564, rs47958, rs3785157), for the mean latency of correct responses in the PRM task and the total correct responses in the SRM task. However, none remained significant in the permutation test. Subanalyses in males only (considering the small number of females) led to similar findings. These results suggest that a haplotype rs36011 (T-allele)/rs1566652 (G-allele) of the NET gene could be associated with visual memory performance (in ADHD).

Overall, this study provides preliminary evidence for a role of the NE system in visual memory and shows the relevance of extending association studies with haplotype analyses.

Personality traits

Using the TCI, Samochowiec (Samochowiec et al., 2001) assessed the influence of the rs5569 G1287A in the NET gene on personality traits (i.e., harm avoidance, novelty seeking, reward dependency, and persistence) in healthy individuals. No effect of genotype was found on personality.

Similarly, Tsai et al. (2002) found no link between genotype of this rs5569 G1287A polymorphism and scores on the TPQ in healthy volunteers (see also section 3.1.2 for receptor variation association). Therefore, these studies do not provide any evidence for a role of the G1287A polymorphism of the NET gene in harm avoidance, novelty seeking, or reward dependence.

The previously described study by Cho et al. (2008b) (section 3.2.1) also related the NET rs5569 G1287A and the A-3081T polymorphism to personality in children with ADHD using the Junior Temperament and Character Inventory. Individuals with one or two T-alleles of the A-3081T polymorphism showed a trend (not statistical significance) to score lower on the cooperativeness profile compared to individuals homozygous for the A-allele.

Ham et al. (2005) also used the TCI to determine the relationship between personality and a variable number tandem repeat T-182C polymorphism in the NET gene in healthy subjects. A significant association was found with reward dependence, with carriers of the T/T scoring higher than individuals of the T/C genotype. Correcting for sex and age did not affect the result. However, it did not remain significant after correction for multiple testing. Comparing C-allele carriers (C/C + T/C) with individuals homozygous for the T-allele revealed that scores on reward dependence were lower in C-allele carriers. T/C carriers also scored lower than the group of C/C and T/T carriers, suggesting that being heterozygous is more related to reward dependence than a specific allele. These results should be interpreted with caution though, as the number of C-allele carriers was relatively low.

Likewise, Lee et al. (2008) associated this NET T-182C polymorphism to TCI scores in a healthy sample. Both a significant main effect for NET and an interaction between NET T-182C and the MAO-A variable number of tandem repeats polymorphism were found on novelty seeking scores, not on reward dependence as in the previously described study by Ham et al. (2005) (see paragraph above) Individuals homozygous for the NET T-allele and for the MAO-A high activity allele showed a higher novelty seeking score than carriers of the low activity MAO-A allele. However, in carriers with one or two NET C-alleles, the opposite pattern was observed, with the MAO-A low activity allele being associated with higher novelty seeking scores than carriers homozygous for the high activity allele. This suggests that NET T-182C and the MAO-A variable number of tandem repeats polymorphism interactively affect novelty seeking.

Andre et al. (2015) also studied the effects of variation in the gene encoding NET (i.e., rs2242446 T-182C) on TCI scores. At baseline, when patients with major depression were free of antidepressant medication for three months and currently did not use mood stabilizers and antipsychotics, there was no evidence for genotype differences in any of the four temperament domains (i.e., novelty seeking, harm avoidance, reward dependence, and persistence).

The previously described study of Yamano et al. (2008) (section 3.1.2.) also investigated, in healthy females, the association between NET rs2242446 in the 5’ flanking region and reward dependence, as measured with the TCI. They reported no association between NET rs2242446 genotype and reward dependence.

Using the NEO Five Factory Inventory, the study by Hahn et al. (2008) examined the association of different NET genotypes (i.e., rs28386840 A-3081T, rs2242446 T-182C, and rs5569 G155A) and personality (i.e., neuroticism, extroversion, openness, agreeableness, and conscientiousness). After multiple testing correction, the only observed association between A-3081T and conscientiousness did not remain significant. Hence, no significant association was observed between NET genotype and any score on the NEO, suggesting no major role of NET in personality.

Taken together, these studies do not provide robust evidence for a role of variants in the gene coding for NET in personality traits.

Brain function

Sigurdardottir et al. (2016) compared the availability of NET, as measured with (S, S)‐[18F]FMeNER‐D2 PET, between ADHD patients and healthy controls with different NET genotypes. They examined the functional polymorphism rs28386840 (the T-allele shows decreased promoter activity), as well as polymorphisms in the 3′ flanking region (i.e., rs15534 and rs40615) and the 5’ region (i.e., rs2242246) of the NET gene. Brain regions of interest were the thalamus, midbrain with pons (including the LC), putamen, and cerebellum. With regard to the functional rs28386840 SNP, NET binding was higher in the thalamus of ADHD patients than controls that were both homozygous for the major A-allele. No group differences were reported for minor T-allele carriers. However, controls homozygous for the A-allele had lower NET binding in the thalamus than controls with the T-allele. Thalamic NET binding was also higher in ADHD patients compared to controls that both carried the major T-allele of the rs2242446 SNP. Healthy controls with the rs15534 major C-allele had higher NET binding in the cerebellum compared to ADHD patients with the major C-allele. A similar pattern was observed for the rs40615 major T-allele. In short, these results suggest that some genetic variants in the NET gene are associated with altered NET binding in controls.

Focusing on brain perfusion using SPECT (tracer not specified), the previously described study by Park et al. (2012) (section 3.2.1.) assessed the effects of the rs5569 G1287A and the A−3081T polymorphisms in children with ADHD on this parameter before and after drug treatment. Before drug treatment, no genotype differences were found on brain perfusion (see section 3.2.4 for effects after treatment).

In sum, these limited number of studies suggest that some genetic variants in the NET gene might alter thalamic NET binding but not brain perfusion.

Pharmacological interventions

In addition to examining the relationship between changes in attentional performance induced by MPH and polymorphisms in the ADRA2A gene (section 3.1.4), Kim et al. (2013) also assessed the effects of the NET polymorphism rs5569 G1287A. Children with ADHD and naive for MPH received 12 weeks of MPH treatment, and underwent several neuropsychological tests to assess attention (as described in section 3.1.1). While a significant ADRA2A genotype effect was reported (section 3.1.4.), regarding the investigated variant in the NET gene only an association in the auditory selective attention task was found before multiple testing correction.

A different approach was chosen by Park et al. (2012) who investigated regional brain perfusion before (see section 3.2.3) and after treatment with MPH and the association with the rs5569 G1287A as well as the A−3081T polymorphism in children with ADHD. Brain perfusion was determined using SPECT. While there was no genotype difference at baseline, after 8 weeks of MPH treatment, carriers of the rs5569 G/G genotype showed hyperperfusion in the right inferior temporal gyrus and the left middle temporal gyrus compared to children with other genotypes. However, this effect was only found before multiple testing correction. There was no effect of A−3081T genotype.

Discussion

The capacity to which someone copes with adversity and manages to maintain (relatively) normal functioning is affected by individual characteristics such a cognition (Dobbin and Ross, 2018) and personality (Oshio et al., 2018) that interact with genetic variation (Herrman et al., 2011). The vast majority of studies on this topic to date have primarily focused on dopamine and 5-HT. As the NE system is involved in an individual's response to stress, the present review aimed to systematically summarize current evidence for a potential role of genetic variants in the NE system, particularly regarding genes encoding NE receptors and NET, in affecting individual characteristics that shape someone's resilience. For this purpose, a transdiagnostic approach without any restrictions regarding factors such as patient population and age was used. Here, we will integrate and summarize the main evidence per characteristic, directly followed by a general discussion for the respective characteristic.

Summary and discussion of findings

Cognition

Many aspects of cognition have been investigated with respect to genetic NE variation. Even though measures between different aspects of cognition often overlapped, making a clear distinction among them relatively hard, certain NE variants seemed to affect specific cognitive outcomes.

Altogether, limited studies were identified investigating the effect of genetic NE variation on IQ and findings do not indicate a major role of NE variants in affecting IQ. This is in contrast to a previous study that has reported a positive association between plasma concentration of NE and IQ (Jung et al., 2019). Similarly, positive associations have been reported between IQ and dopamine plasma levels (Jung et al., 2019). Genetic and epigenetic markers of dopaminergic neurotransmission (e.g., genes coding for dopamine receptor D2 and D4, COMT) have also been linked to IQ (Kaminski et al., 2018; Deyoung et al., 2011; Zmigrod and Robbins, 2021). In addition, there is evidence that the NE and dopamine systems can act in parallel but also in synergy to affect cognitive factors (for review see (Ranjbar-Slamloo and Fazlali, 2019)). Therefore, it would be interesting for future studies to further investigate how interactions between these two neurotransmitter systems and epigenetic control of gene expression could explain variation in IQ at the individual level.

In sum, certain NE variants seem to act as protective or risk factors of attentional problems. Electrophysiological findings in rodents are consilient with the modulatory role of NE in attentional processes (Zmigrod and Robbins, 2021; Robbins, 1984). For example, a study by Bari et al. (2020) has found that activation of NE neurons in the LC of mice improved attention, whereas inactivation of these NE neurons worsened distractibility.

Other variants, such as ADRA2A rs1800544 MspI, ADRA2B 12Glu9, NET rs998424 and rs3785157 did not show strong effects on attention. Similarly, even though a role of dopamine in regulating attention has been suggested (Nieoullon, 2002), a study by Parasuraman et al. (2005) failed to find association between the DBH gene and visuospatial attention. On the other hand, others have reported a link between higher attentional bias and low expression of the 5-HTTLPR (s-allele) (Fox et al., 2011).

Importantly, some studies reported gene × gene interactions (e.g., with BDNF) or sex dependent interactions (e.g., protective effect of homozygous G-alleles - NET rs5569 (G1287A) - only in female subjects). This highlights the importance of investigating sufficiently large samples and taking into account other factors that could potentially interact with these genes, like other genes, sex, and age.

Overall, many studies have investigated the effect of variants in the ADRA2B gene on emotional memory, but only one investigated the effect of NET on visual memory, thus the NET results should be considered exploratory. The findings are in line with results from pharmacological studies in healthy humans that demonstrated that selective manipulation of the NE system with pharmacological agents affects working and emotional memory (for a review see Chamberlain et al. (2006)). For instance, propranolol, a beta-blocker (i.e., an agent that blocks the beta-adrenergic receptors) impaired these aspects of memory (Cahill et al., 1994; Muller et al., 2005; Frcka and Lader, 1988), while reboxetine (i.e., a selective NE reuptake inhibitor) increased emotional (positive) memories (Cahill et al., 1994). Extensive research suggests that working memory performance not only depends on NE, but also on dopamine (Jay, 2003), acetyl-choline (Hasselmo and Sarter, 2011), and glutamate neurotransmission (Corlett et al., 2011). Therefore, while investigating the link between memory and genetic polymorphisms, it is important to consider the effect of other (confounding) variables, such as other genes, sex, hormones, and the environment.

More studies are needed to be able to draw concrete conclusions about the role of NE variants in emotional processing. Past research has shown that NE and noradrenergic receptors are highly associated with emotional processing (Pitman and Delahanty, 2005; Terbeck et al., 2016). In addition, previous work has suggested that genetic factors can exert important influences on coping styles (Busjahn et al., 1999). Thus, further research on genetic NE variants would be interesting to estimate the amount of variance that could potentially explain differences in emotional processing and coping across individuals.

In general, the impact of NE, dopaminergic and serotonergic neurotransmitters on executive functions has been widely reported (Logue and Gould, 2014). Besides the above-mentioned NE variations, Bomni et al. (Bombin et al., 2008) found that polymorphisms in dopamine receptor D3, but not D2 and COMT, were associated with worse performance in tasks measuring executive functions. In addition, another study found that the DBH gene was associated with increased working memory (Parasuraman et al., 2005). In line with this, implications of the 5-HTTLPR in executive processing have also been reported (Enge et al., 2011). Since all these neurotransmitter systems are highly interrelated and interact on a molecular level it is of substantial importance to evaluate their synergistic effects in different genetic combinations.

All in all, genetic variations in the NE system seem to exert influence on some cognitive processes, especially attention and emotional memory. There is more evidence for the involvement of NE receptors in cognition (particularly ADRA2A and ADRA2B), as more studies have investigated adrenergic receptors compared to NET. These findings align well with studies that have used pharmacological manipulations to target receptors of the NE system and have shown that targeting the NE system led to changes in attention and emotional memory (Reist et al., 2001; Smith and Nutt, 1996; De Martino et al., 2008). Other cognitive processes, such as learning, reward/effort valuation, and motor control might also be affected by the NE system (Breton-Provencher et al., 2021; Borderies et al., 2020; Voulgaropoulou et al., 2022). Here, we have not identified studies evaluating the role of genetic NE variants on these processes. Further research on different aspects of cognition could shed more light on the involvement of certain genetic NE variants in specific cognitive functions.

Overall, the included studies suggest that genetic variants in the NE system, in particular the alpha-2a receptor, might play a role in some personality traits, like harm avoidance. These findings are (partly) in line with results of earlier studies that found a positive association between biting and fighting behavior and noradrenergic activity in rodents and monkeys (for a review see Craig et al. (Craig and Halton, 2009)). Others reported a trend towards a positive association between aggressiveness and the concentration of NE's metabolite 3-methoxy-4-hydroxyphenylglycol (MHPG) in cerebrospinal fluid of patients with depression (Placidi et al., 2001). Moreover, beta-blockers might be effective in controlling aggression and reducing hostility in patients with psychiatric conditions (Molero et al., 2023).

Gene polymorphisms in the dopaminergic and serotonergic systems, as well as, those that are non-specifically linked to the NE system, such as SNPs in the genes encoding COMT and MAO-A, might also play a role in personality traits (Noble et al., 1998; Tommasi et al., 2021; Munafo et al., 2003) (for reviews see (Craig and Halton, 2009; Qiu et al., 2017)). For example, male members of a large Dutch family demonstrated abnormal violent behavior, which was linked to a point mutation in the MAO-A gene (Brunner et al., 1993). Additionally, a polymorphism in the dopamine D4 gene (i.e., a variable number of tandem repeats (VNTR) with 48-bp repeat unit) has been linked in humans with impulsivity (Tong and Barnes, 2013; Reiner and Spangler, 2011; Congdon et al., 2008). Novelty seeking in healthy Japanese males may also be affected by the C-824T polymorphism in the TH gene (Sadahiro et al., 2010).

Taken together, the results of multiple studies point toward a relation between personality traits and polymorphisms in the NE system, especially those related to the adrenergic receptors. Larger well-powered studies are necessary to disentangle the effect of specific polymorphisms, as well as the combination of multiple polymorphisms on distinct personality traits.

Our findings suggest that NE polymorphisms might play a role in some aspects of human brain functioning. Previous research also found evidence that the 5-HTTLPR S-allele is associated with hyperreactivity of the amygdala and the prefrontal cortex (Homberg and Lesch, 2011), which might contribute to hypervigilance and increased responses to stressful stimuli. Pezawas et al. (2005) reported a functional uncoupling between these brain regions in carriers with the 5-HTTLPR S-allele. This might explain the effect of environmental stimuli on behavior. One of the included studies reported higher functional connectivity between the right amygdala and other brain regions (i.e., left insula, left inferior frontal gyrus, and left postcentral gyrus) in participants with than without the 12Glu9 deletion in the ADRA2B gene. Additional research is needed to assess whether NE polymorphisms result in uncoupling between the amygdala and the prefrontal cortex.

Other genetic variants, such as those in the dopamine receptor D1 and D4 genes, have been associated with functional brain changes in patients with ADHD (for a review see (Yadav et al., 2021)). For instance, with resting-state fMRI, Qian et al. (2018) demonstrated that the absence of a 2-repeat allele of the dopamine receptor D4 gene is related to hyperconnectivity in the default mode network and sensorimotor network in ADHD children. Moreover, they found evidence for hypoconnectivity in the executive control network in these children compared to ADHD children with the 2-repeat allele.

In sum, due to practical reasons, it is often challenging to study the interactions between different brain regions, such as the amygdala and prefrontal cortex, in the living brain of humans. In addition, often one aspect of brain functioning, and few polymorphisms are assessed simultaneously. Therefore, the combined effect of multiple polymorphisms within the NE and other neurotransmitter systems on brain function still needs to be elucidated. Moreover, studies in nonhuman primates might provide additional insight in the effects of genetic polymorphisms in the NE system on brain function.

Methodological limitations

A major strength of this review is the rigorous approach used to systematically identify relevant articles for a transdiagnostic qualitative synthesis, including searching through reference lists of relevant articles. Additionally, we focused on the NE system, which is a relatively understudied neurotransmitter system compared to the dopaminergic and the 5-HT system. However, several limitations have to be kept in mind when interpreting the results. Records identified in the first search step are inevitably limited to studies that used specific keywords in their abstract and/or title. Importantly, some individual characteristics summarized in this review, such as IQ, could be secondary outcomes in some studies. As these studies often do not mention secondary outcomes in their title or abstract, they could be missed during the first step of the search. Therefore, a potential selection bias cannot be fully excluded. Second, the quality of studies is a potential source of bias, as sample sizes for genetic analyses were relatively small in some studies, warranting replication in larger samples. Third, the search of this systematic review focused on mental health-related characteristics to indirectly assess resilience. As outlined in the introduction, a very recent review (Kalisch et al., 2024) grouped resilience definitions as trait-based, process-based, and outcome-based. Importantly, it emphasizes that more recent research shifts its focus from traits and processes to resilience as an outcome. Since the majority of studies conducted to date have not used this recent definition, the current review is focused on trait-based resilience factors and does not provide an examination of exposure to adversity and the corresponding outcome. Thus, it should be noted that studies reported in this review do not cover all aspects of “resilience” and that results should not be overgeneralized. In addition, most of the investigated mental health-related characteristics discussed in the present review were only investigated by a small number of studies. These studies do not enclose all facets of, for example, cognition or personality. Additionally, due to the small number of studies on each characteristic, only a limited number of polymorphisms in a NE-related gene were covered and our study thus does not enclose all NE genes coding for NET and all of the receptor subtypes. Subsequently, due to high heterogeneity between the included studies (e.g., use of different ethnic groups and methodologies), we could not use a quantitative approach (i.e., a meta-analysis) to combine the data. Fourth, due to the lack of consensus with regard to the definition of resilience, we used indirect (i.e. resilience-/mental health-related characteristics) instead of direct measures of resilience. Furthermore, included studies did not comprehensively quantify the exposure to adverse life events, such as the corresponding time and load of the stress exposure. Lastly, many studies have investigated polymorphisms in genes that are non-specifically linked to the NE system, namely genes encoding COMT, MAO-A, DBH, and TH. Addressing these was beyond the scope of this review and is also hampered by the fact that many studies discuss e.g. COMT in the context of the dopaminergic instead of or in combination with the NE system, without examining a clear factor (such as plasma levels) that links the results to one of both systems.

Future directions

Our synthesis suggests that certain NE genetic variants affect individual resilience- and mental health-related characteristics. However, findings are not fully consistent, which also applies to results within a distinct characteristic. Larger studies that are well powered for genetic investigations are needed, including genome-wide association studies (GWAS). Generally, GWAS focus on categorical disorders [e.g. (Djurovic et al., 2010; Forstner et al., 2021; Gregersen et al., 2016; Ikeda et al., 2018; Legge et al., 2024; Lewis et al., 2010; Witt et al., 2017),]. Relatively few GWAS have specifically investigated transdiagnostic psychiatric phenotypes or individual features (such as risk-taking behavior as susceptibility marker for psychopathology (Karlsson Linner et al., 2019; Strawbridge et al., 2018), neuroticism (Neumann et al., 2022), or cognition (Davies et al., 2016; Soo et al., 2023)) but they have not reported associations with polymorphisms in the NE system. Further GWAS focusing on transdiagnostic phenotypes could enhance our understanding about the contribution of certain genes to specific symptoms, including those involved in NE signaling. Moreover, in this review, we focused on variation in the genes encoding the NET and NE receptors, as these are clearly linked to NE signaling. Enzymes involved in the metabolism of NE, such as COMT and MAO-A, are also involved in the breakdown of other neurotransmitters such as dopamine and 5-HT. If variants in these genes are investigated, it remains unclear whether the molecular effects are caused by NE or by other neurotransmitters. Therefore, more studies are needed that determine the role of genetic variation in COMT, MAO-A and other genes in direct relationship to NE and in interaction with the NE system. Furthermore, for many genetic variants examined it remains to be determined to which extent these are associated with any molecular functional consequences and whether the changes in, e.g., gene transcription efficacy are sufficiently robust to affect individual characteristics such as cognition. In addition, environmental stimuli like a stressful event can also influence gene expression, through epigenetic processes (Saccaro et al., 2021). Research has shown that environmental stress unfavorably affects gene transcription in neuroendocrine systems and the brain (Dvir et al., 2014; Bisagno and Cadet, 2014; McEwen et al., 2015). This can subsequently change how individuals react to physical and physiological stressors. It was beyond the scope of the current review to also provide an overview of studies focusing on epigenetics. However, future studies should examine the interaction of genetic variation and epigenetic changes in resilience to provide more comprehensive insights. Next, most studies also did not take into account that individual characteristics can vary between men and women, including neurochemical differences in neurotransmitter systems such as the NE (Bangasser et al., 2016), dopaminergic and 5-HT system (Cosgrove et al., 2007). Hence, studies comprehensively investigating sex effects are also needed. Successively, it is important that studies use a uniform definition of resilience, such as defining resilience as preserving good mental health when encountering adversity (thus defining resilience as outcome) as recently suggested (Kalisch et al., 2024), and measure this concept directly with validated measuring tools. When these two requirements are met, particularly prospective longitudinal studies could assess relevant outcome parameters before exposure to adversity, as well as soon after the end of exposure, to identify more robust predictors for resilience (Kalisch et al., 2017). In this approach, adversity should be quantified on an individual level and taken into account as much as possible (Kalisch et al., 2017, 2024). Recent efforts have been made into this direction (Ahrens et al., 2021). However, the large inter-individual variability of adversity occurrence in real life makes longitudinal studies time-consuming and hence relatively expensive. As alternative approach, it was proposed to measure an individual's exposure to stress and related mental health repeatedly throughout the study period (Kalisch et al., 2021). This could provide more comprehensive insights into the mechanisms underlying resilience. Last, when the focus is on resilience- and mental health-related traits, it is also important to identify (neuro)biological processes that contribute to these traits in a transdiagnostic approach rather than focusing on categorical disorders. The Research Domain Criteria (Rdoc) Framework suggests to examine biological, physiological, and behavioral factors and mechanisms that independently and in interaction affect risk and protective factors for mental health (Insel et al., 2010). Since its initiation, progress has been made, but much research remains to be done using this approach to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms underlying mental health for better diagnoses and treatments.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the current state of evidence supports a role for genetic variation in the NE system in affecting some resilience- and mental health-related individual characteristics. Although polymorphisms in the NE system are not the only genetic pathways that shape human cognition, personality traits, brain function, and responses to pharmacological interventions, it may have a critical role in shaping how vulnerable or resilient an individual is to stress, and the polymorphisms might explain potential individual differences. The molecular functional consequences of genetic variants need to be further determined to be able to utilize these insights in the clinic.
