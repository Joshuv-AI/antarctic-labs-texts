Differential effects of age and sex on tau pathology propagation in the htau mouse model: A neuropathological and proteomic study
Authors: Andreea‐Claudia Kosa, Lidia Lopez‐Gutierrez, Kunie Ando, Emilie Doeraene, Emmanuel Aydin, Hinde Lasri, Alain Wathelet‐Depauw, Karlien Pieters, David Van Morckhoven, Virginie Imbault, Christine Dubois, Xavier Bisteau, Basile Stamatopoulos, Mariana Igoillo‐Esteve, Jean‐Pierre Brion, Karelle Leroy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONAging is the main risk factor for Alzheimer's disease (AD), acting through still poorly understood mechanisms. AD is associated with the development of a tau pathology, a hallmark lesion propagating in the brain along neuroanatomically connected pathways. This study investigates changes in gene expression and patterns of tau pathology after induction of tau pathology propagation and the effects of age and sex on this propagation.METHODSYoung and old humanized htau mice were intracerebrally injected with pathological tau from human AD brain to induce tau pathology.RESULTSYoung and old htau male mice showed similar patterns of tau pathology propagation, whereas the density of tau pathology was increased in young compared to old female mice. Proteomic analysis demonstrated differential expression of proteins involved in endocytosis, autophagosome formation, and tau splicing.DISCUSSIONThe increased tau pathology formation in young female mice suggests that involvement of selected biological mechanisms could occur early in women during their midlife to explain their sensitivity to the development of tau pathology.Highlights Tau pathology induced by human PHF‐tau is mainly composed of 3R‐tau in htau mice.Splicing factors favoring 4R‐tau are downregulated in mice with tau pathology.Tau pathology propagation is increased in young females compared to old females.Proteins implicated in endocytosis and autophagy are modified when tau pathology propagates.Some protein expressions are similarly modified in young females and during normal aging.

INTRODUCTION

Age‐related disorders, especially Alzheimer's disease (AD), are becoming an increasing public health concern due to the global aging of the population. Indeed, AD prevalence increases sharply with age, especially after age 65: ∼5% at age 65–74, ∼13% at age 75–84 and > 33% of people above 84. 1 , 2 Aging is considered as the primary risk factor for AD with a higher proportion of women affected by the disease and this gender gap is predicted to increase in the future. 2 It was suggested that the sudden drop of sexual hormones during menopause could make the brain more vulnerable to AD, explaining the higher proportion of women affected by the disease. Nonetheless, hormonal replacement therapy users exhibited negligible or faster regional tau accumulation (depending on the moment they started the treatment) compared to non‐users 3 indicating that the sexual hormonal loss alone does not explain the higher proportion of women affected by the disease. However, an earlier age at the menopause and a late initiation of hormonal therapy are associated with an increased susceptibility to tau pathology, indicating the importance of better understanding the relationship between sex or age and the formation of tau pathology. 4

Another reason raised to explain the disparity between men and women concerned the fact that women live longer, but the prevalence of AD is higher in women than in men in the same range of age. 5

The transition from an asymptomatic state to clinically established AD with cognitive deficits involves the pathological formation of neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs). 6 Sex differences in the susceptibility to develop NFTs have been reported. Indeed, women had higher levels of NFTs density compared to men 7 , 8 , 9 and higher tau–positron emission tomography (PET) signals 9 already at the preclinical stage of the disease. 10 , 11 Moreover, tau pathology accumulation is accelerated in women during the evolution of the disease. 11 , 12

However, the underlying mechanisms linking aging and sex differences to AD pathogenesis remain largely unknown. 13 A preclinical mouse model would be of great interest not only to identify the mechanisms involved in tau pathology propagation but also to optimize menopausal treatment guidelines.

NFTs are intraneuronal lesions composed of abnormally hyperphosphorylated aggregated tau proteins forming paired helical filaments (PHF). Tau proteins are members of the microtubule‐associated protein tau (MAPT) family. While the brains of wild‐type (WT) adult mice mainly express 3 Tau isoforms (4R), the adult human brain presents six of them, all derived from a single gene by alternative mRNA splicing. The six tau isoforms differ from one another by the number of microtubule‐binding repetitive domains present in the C‐terminal of the protein (3R or 4R) and by the number of amino‐terminal inserts (0, 1, 2 N). The six tau isoforms expressed in the human adult brain are present in the composition of PHFs in AD.

During the evolution of the disease, tau pathology develops in specific brain areas that are synaptically connected, suggesting a transmission of tau pathology through a prion‐like mechanism. Mice expressing WT (non‐mutated) tau do not develop tau aggregates even in the presence of amyloid plaques. However, it is possible to induce the formation of NFTs in the mouse brain from WT tau by injecting intracerebrally abnormal tau proteins (PHF‐tau) collected from the AD brain. 14 , 15 , 16 The mechanism of NFT formation is based on the recruitment and seeding of endogenous WT tau proteins by PHF‐tau found in sarkosyl fractions extracted from AD brain.

In this study, we explore the role of aging in tau pathology spreading in htau mice intracerebrally injected with PHF‐tau extracted from AD brains, and we investigate the underlying molecular mechanisms involved.

METHODS

Human brain samples

Human brain tissue samples from a demented 60‐year‐old female, clinically diagnosed as having sporadic AD (NFT Braak stage VI and amyloid plaques Thal stage 4), and from a non‐demented 67‐year‐old male control subject, were taken at autopsy 24 h post mortem. Tissue samples were conserved at −80°C until processing. The AD subject exhibited advanced neuropathology (NFT Braak stage VI and amyloid plaques Thal stage 4), while the control brain showed no signs of NFTs or amyloid plaques. This study on post mortem brain tissue was performed in compliance with and following approval of the Ethical Committee of the Medical School of the Université Libre de Bruxelles (P2009/015).

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: The authors reviewed the literature using traditional sources such as PubMed. Previous studies have shown that aggregated tau can recruit and seed wild‐type (WT) tau protein following intracerebral injections of pathological tau. Pathological tau is transmitted through synaptically connected neurons and seeds WT tau to form highly phosphorylated and aggregated tau proteins, as in Alzheimer's disease (AD). However, even if the propagation of tau pathology could explain the sequential expansion of neurofibrillary tangles in AD, the cellular mechanism by which tau pathology propagates during aging remains unknown. Interpretation: In our mouse model expressing six human WT tau isoforms (as observed in human adult brain), tau pathology propagation was induced after the intracerebral injection of pathological tau (PHF‐tau) extracted from human AD brains, supporting a mechanism of prion‐like transmission of tau pathology. Discrepancies were observed between males and females during aging. Tau pathology propagation was increased in young females compared to old females, whereas propagation was similar in young and old males. Several differentially expressed proteins have been identified in our proteomic analysis and they are implied in cellular mechanisms such as endocytosis, autophagy or splicing. Future directions: Although we have identified proteins whose expression is linked to the formation of tau pathology and with some of them being specifically involved in this process in young females, their mechanisms of action on tau pathology propagation remain unknown. Moreover, future investigations should explore the contribution of sexual hormones to tau pathology propagation to better understand the implications for women when they are treated with hormonal therapy.

Preparation of human and murine sarkosyl‐insoluble PHF‐tau fraction

Sarkosyl fractionation of human frontal cortex and murine hippocampus was performed as previously described. 16 , 17 Briefly, 0.5 g of frozen frontal cortex from AD and control cases, or 130 mg of murine hippocampus, were homogenized in 10 volumes of ice‐cold PHF‐extraction buffer (10 mM Tris‐HCl (pH 7.4), 0.8 M NaCl, 1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid [EDTA], 10% sucrose). The homogenate was centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 20 min at 4°C. N‐lauroylsarcosine sodium salt (L‐5125; Sigma‐Aldrich) was added to the supernatant to reach a final concentration of 1% (w/v). The lysates from human brains were incubated at 4°C overnight, while murine hippocampal lysates were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with mild agitation, followed by an ultracentrifugation at 180,000 × g for 30 min at 4°C. The sarkosyl soluble supernatant was removed, and the sarkosyl‐insoluble pellet containing PHF was gently rinsed and re‐suspended in 0.25 mL of phosphate buffered saline (PBS) by vigorous pipetting. The sarkosyl‐insoluble fractions were then sonicated on ice at amplitude 60 for 40 pulses of 1 s with an interval of 10 s after every 10 pulses. The protein concentration was determined by Bradford protein assay (Bio‐Rad) and adjusted to 1 µg/µL. These sarkosyl fractions were aliquoted and kept at −20°C.

Antibodies

The total tau antibody (DAKO, A0024) is a rabbit polyclonal antibody raised to the C‐terminal part of human tau (amino acids 243‐441) but cross‐reacts with mouse and rat tau proteins. The mouse monoclonal antibodies AT8 (Thermo Fisher, MN1020), PHF1, and MC1 (kindly provided by Drs. P. Davies and S. Greenberg, New York) recognize tau phosphorylated at Ser 202 and Thr205 18 at Ser396 and Ser404 19 and a conformational epitope requiring both an N‐terminal fragment and a C‐terminal fragment, respectively. 20 Tau isoforms were analysed using specific antibodies for 3R (Millipore 05‐803, mouse monoclonal) and 4R isoforms (Cell Signaling 30328S, rabbit polyclonal). To evaluate the specificity of 3R and 4R antibodies, we used Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells transfected with plasmids coding for tau WT 0N3R or WT 0N4R coupled to enhanced green fluorescent protein (eGFP) as positive controls.

Negative staining and gold labeling of tau filaments by transmission electron microscopy

The sarkosyl‐insoluble material was ultrastructurally characterized by transmission electron microscopy. Samples were adsorbed onto formvar‐carbon–coated electron microscopy (EM) grids and negatively stained with potassium phosphotungstate as reported 16 , 17 before observation with a Zeiss EM 809T at 80 kV. The average length of sarkosyl‐insoluble filaments was measured on 20 filaments using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health [NIH]). For the detection of tau in filaments, we performed an immunolabeling on filaments adsorbed on formvar‐carbon‐coated EM grids using an anti‐tau antibody (total tau), followed by an anti‐rabbit polyclonal antibody coupled to 5 nm colloidal gold (Sigma). The length, the crossover (the half‐period), the thinner (minimal width), and the larger (maximal width) parts of the filaments were measured on 20 filaments illustrated in calibrated pictures using Image J software.

Western blotting

Proteins were quantified by Bradford assay (BioRad). Sarkosyl‐insoluble fractions were mixed with Laemmli buffer, denatured at 100°C for 5 min, loaded (5 µg/lane), and run in a 10% Tris‐glycine sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE) gel, and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. The nitrocellulose sheets were blocked in semi‐fat dry milk (10% (w/v) in Tris‐buffered saline) for 1 h at room temperature, and they were incubated overnight with anti‐phosphotau PHF‐1 antibodies as previously described 21 , 22 followed by anti‐mouse immunoglobulins conjugated to peroxidase. Finally, the membranes were incubated in Pico substrate (Pierce) and imaged in a Fusion SOLO 4S system equipped with a DARQ‐7 camera and the fusion‐capt software (Vilber‐Lourmat). Blot quantitation was performed with ImageJ software (NIH).

Biosensor FRET cells

The biosensor Fluorescence Resonance Energy Transfer (FRET) cellular assay (CRL‐3275) used cultured HEK293T cells expressing a tau repeat domain (RD) fused to cyan fluorescent protein (CFP) and a tau RD fused to yellow fluorescent protein (YFP). 23 This bio‐assay is based on the uptake of seeds into cells, which induces aggregation of tau‐CFP and tau‐YFP, producing a FRET signal. Cells were plated at a density of 400,000 cells per well in a six‐well plate. Eighteen hours later, cells were transfected with 2 ug/mL of sarkosyl insoluble fractions from control (CTL) or AD brains or from injected mice brains using jetPRIME transfection reagent (VWR) following the manufacturer's instructions. Cells were observed with a Zeiss Axioplan microscope 72 h after transfection and were then collected in 1× PBS buffer with 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Cellular FRET signals were measured as integrated FRET density by fluorescence‐activated cell sorting (FACS) (BD LSR Fortessa X‐20 Cell Analyzer), as reported. 17 , 24 The integrated FRET density was normalized to cells transfected with human AD sarkosyl fractions.

Mouse line

htau mice (Jackson, strain 005491) express the six human tau isoforms under the tau promoter in the absence of murine tau. 25 Briefly, this mouse line was generated by crossing 8c mice, expressing all human tau isoforms 26 with TauKO mice that do not express murine tau, due to the disruption of the mapt gene (encoding tau) with a cDNA for eGFP inserted into exon one. 27 Male and female heterozygous for the htau transgene were used in equal proportion in this study, and their numbers are specified in the figure legends. All mice were maintained on a C57BL/6J genetic background. Mice were genotyped by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using previously described primers for human tau (Jackson, Protocol 23169), murine tau (Jackson, Protocol 217593), and GFP 28 on genomic DNA. All mice were housed under a 12 h light/dark cycle, with food and water available ad libitum. All studies on animals were performed in compliance with and following approval of the Ethical Committee for the care and use of laboratory animals of the Medical School of the Université Libre de Bruxelles (protocol P711N).

Stereotaxic injection

Five‐month‐old (young) and 12‐month‐old (old) htau mice were deeply anesthetized with a solution of xylazine (2% v/v) (Rompun, Bayer) and ketamine (10% v/v) (Nimatek) in physiological saline solution by intraperitoneal injection (100 mL/10 g of body weight, final dose 10 mg/kg xylazine and 100 mg/kg ketamine). Unilateral stereotaxic injections were performed using a stereotaxic apparatus (Kopf instruments, Tujunga, CA) in the dentate gyrus of the hippocampus (Rostro‐caudal, −2.1 mm; Lateral, +1.5 mm; Depth, −2.0 mm). One microgram of sonicated sarkosyl‐insoluble material was injected in a volume of 1 µL at a speed of 0.1 µL/min with a pump (kdScientific), using a 200 mm diameter needle (ThermoFisher). The needle was gently removed 5 min after injection.

Behavioral tests

Open field

The open field test was used to measure anxiety to explore what could affect other behavioral tests, such as the Y maze or the Barnes maze. Mice naturally prefer to wander around a protective wall rather than in the center of the open field. We tracked movement trajectories for 10 min in a square gray box (44 cm × 44 cm) (Ugo Basile). A video tracking system (Any maze, Ugo Basile) was used to record the trajectory of mice and the time spent in the periphery versus the central zones of the arena. 29

Y maze

Short‐term spatial working memory was assessed in a spontaneous spatial preference Y‐maze test as previously described. 22 This test consists of three arms separated by 120 degrees. Each arm is 35 cm long, 10 cm high, and 5 cm wide. During the first phase, mice were placed in the “start” arm and were allowed to explore the “start” arm and the “other” arm for 10 min. Access to the third arm of the maze (“new” arm) was blocked by an opaque door. The mouse was then removed from the maze and returned to its cage for 60 min. In the test phase, the mouse was placed in the “start” arm of the maze and had access to all three arms. The mouse was allowed to explore for 5 min. The time spent in the new arm was measured for the first minute of the test phase.

Barnes maze

The Barnes maze test is used to study hippocampus‐dependent spatial learning as previously described. 30 , 31 Briefly, the Barnes maze is a circular platform (1 m diameter) with 20 holes equally spaced and located close to the edge of the platform. A black escape box is placed under one of the holes, and spatial cues are located at the four‐cardinal points above the platform. The mice were placed in the middle of the maze and allowed to explore. The session ended when the mice found the box or after 5 min had elapsed. The mice performed two trials per day with a 20 min inter‐trial interval for 5 consecutive days. The latency corresponding to the time spent finding the correct hole is measured after each trial.

All behavioral tests were performed 3 months after stereotaxic injection.

Histological staining and immunohistochemistry

Three months after the stereotaxic injection, mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation, and a sample number was assigned to allow blinded analysis for the age, gender, and the material injected in the mice. Brains were fixed in 10% formalin for 24 h before embedding in paraffin. Tissue sections (10 µm thick) and immunohistochemical labeling were performed using the avidin–biotin complex (ABC) method as previously described. 32 Briefly, tissue sections were treated with H2O2 to inhibit endogenous peroxidase and incubated with the blocking solution (10% normal horse serum in Tris‐buffered saline [TBS]). After an overnight incubation with the diluted primary antibody, the sections were sequentially incubated with horse anti‐mouse antibodies conjugated to biotin (Vector Laboratories), followed by the ABC complex (Vector Laboratories). The peroxidase activity was developed using diaminobenzidine (DAB) as chromogen. Slides were counterstained with hematoxylin. Formalin‐fixed sections were stained with the Gallyas silver‐staining method to identify tau aggregates. Tissue sections were examined with a Zeiss Axioplan microscope and digital images acquired using a DP23 camera (Evident Olympus).

Quantification of tau pathology

The density of tau positive profiles was estimated in the dentate gyrus and the Ammon's horn close to the injection point (Bregma coordinates between −1.58 mm and −2.3 mm), at a position more posterior to the injection point (Bregma coordinates between −2.8 mm and −3.52 mm) and in the entorhinal cortex (Bregma coordinates between −4.72 mm and −4.96 mm) of mice injected with AD fractions. The result was expressed as the number of positive profiles over the total area values of these brain areas as evaluated by ImageJ software on calibrated pictures. Five adjacent sections were used per brain sample for the analysis at the position of injection or posterior to the position of injection, and three adjacent sections for the evaluation in the entorhinal cortex.

The semiquantitative heat maps of young or old AD‐injected females were generated using AT8 labeling of three representative coronal sections at the position of injection (AP Bregma: −2.3 mm), posterior to the position of injection (AP Bregma: −3.08 mm), and entorhinal cortex (AP Bregma: −4.96 mm). After counting the number of AT8 positive profiles in individual brain areas for each mouse, the mean number of AT8 profiles was used to construct heat maps using Adobe Illustrator 2025 v29.2 to show the brain distribution of tau pathology.

Proteomic analysis

Hippocampal samples from young or old CTL or AD‐injected mice (n = 5 males and 5 females per group, dissected 3 months after stereotaxic injection) were analyzed by mass spectrometry. Samples were mechanically dissociated in 8 M Urea, 50 mM tris‐HCl, pH 8.5 with an ultra‐turrax. 100 mg of Lysing Matrix D beads (MPBio) were added, and samples were further extracted for 10 min in a TissueLyzer II (Qiagen) at 30 Hz. Samples were further sonicated (three cycles, 5 s on, 5 s off, amplitude 27%) before centrifugation (at 16,000 g, 10 min, room temperature [RT]), and supernatants were transferred to a new Eppendorf tube. Protein quantities were measured using a filter paper method. 33

A total of 50 µg of proteins were incubated first with TCEP (10 mM final, 646547; Merck) for 30 min at room temperature, then with CAA (55 mM final, 22790; Merck) for 30 min at room temperature, protected from light. After dilution eight times with TEAB 100 mM (18597; Merck), enzymatic digestion was carried out overnight with 1 µg of trypsin (V5117; Promega) and 1 µg of LysC (129‐02541; Wako) at 37°C. Peptides were then retained on hydrophilic–lipophilic balance solid phase extraction (HLB SPE) columns (Spin15‐ HLB.T3.50; Affinisep) and eluted with acetonitrile (ACN):H2O (65:35 v/v), 0.1% formic acid (FA). After evaporation, samples were reconstituted in H2O with 0.1% FA before injection.

Peptides were separated on a separation column (ChromXP C18 CL, 150 × 0.3 mm, 3 µm; Sciex) using a two‐step acetonitrile gradient (5%–25% ACN/0.1% FA in 65 min, then 25%–60% ACN/0.1% FA in 25 min) and were sprayed online in the mass spectrometer. SWATH acquisitions were performed using 71 windows of variable isolation width to cover a mass range of 400–1250 m/z. SWATH MS2 spectra were collected from 50 to 2000 m/z. The collision energy for each window was determined according to the calculation for a charge 2+ ion centered upon the window with a spread of 15. An accumulation time of 45 ms was used for all fragment‐ion scans in high‐sensitivity mode and for the survey scans in high‐resolution mode acquired at the beginning of each cycle, resulting in a duty cycle of ∼3.3 s.

Raw data files were converted to mzML format with MSConvert (Version: 3.0). The processing of mzML files was carried out with DIA‐NN 34 (version 1.8.2 beta 27) with search parameters set as follows: precursor false discovery rate (FDR) 1%; mass accuracy at MS1 and MS2 both set to 0; scan window set to 0; isotopologues and MBR turned on; protein inference at gene level; heuristic protein inference enabled; quantification strategy set to QuantUMS (high accuracy) 35 ; neural network classifier double‐pass mode; cross‐run normalization Retention time‐dependent. A universal library was used, and protein re‐annotation was performed. Spectra were searched against a prior in loco generated library containing 12,444 protein groups covered by 293,830 precursors, including common contaminants. 36 To generate the library, data‐dependent acquisition (DDA) and data‐independent acquisition (DIA) data acquired on TT5600 were used to generate one universal library using FragPipe (version 20.0) using a predefined workflow DIA_SpecLib_Quant. Specifically, decoys were first added to the FASTA, which contains human protein sequences (UP000000589, 54,739 reviewed and unreviewed entries, downloaded from UniProtKB on 05th October 2023). Then, MSFragger (version 3.8) 37 was used to search DDA and DIA raw data, with the following settings: precursor and fragment mass tolerance 30 ppm; strict trypsin with no more than five missed cleavages; peptide length 6–50; peptide mass 500–5000; C + 57.021464 as fixed modification; M + 15.9949, N‐term +42.0106, nQ ‐17.0265, nE ‐18.0106, DN +0.984016 and carbamylation +43.005814 as variable modifications; min matched fragments 4; max fragment charge 2. In the validation step, MSBooster 38 was implemented on both spectra and RT levels, and then Percolator and ProteinProphet integrated in Philosopher (version 5.0.0) were used for PSM validation and protein inference. Library generation was conducted using EasyPQP (IonQuant version 1.9.8) with RT calibration based on ciRTs and a Lowess fraction set to 0.04. Only fragment types b and y were included with a tolerance of 15 ppm, with a max delta_unimod.

Proteins showing a log2 fold change of [0.5] between groups and a p‐adjusted value less than 0.05 were considered differentially expressed. Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (STRING) is a database for predicted protein‐protein interactions at clusters, extracting results from many protein–protein interaction databases. It also uses the information from KEGG pathways and Reactome to provide the best annotations for the interactions of one protein. 39 Venn diagrams were created thanks to Heberle and colleagues, 40 graphics were generated using SRplot. 41

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the Prism program version 10.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Statistical comparisons were performed by Student t‐test or two‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with post‐test for multiple comparisons, as suitable, as described in the figure legends. Values of p < 0.05 were considered significant. Numbers of samples are indicated in the figure legends, and ranges represent means ± SEM.

RESULTS

Characterization of sarkosyl fractions from human brains

Prior to the stereotaxic injection of sarkosyl fractions in htau mice, we characterized the fractions extracted from non‐demented (CTL) or AD brains for the presence of phosphorylated tau filaments, and the seeding abilities of these fractions in FRET cells (Figure S1). The presence of tau filaments in the form of PHF in sonicated and non‐sonicated sarkosyl fractions was confirmed by a negative staining in electron microscopy (Figure S1A and S1B). The length of PHFs was significantly reduced in sonicated sarkosyl fractions compared to non‐sonicated fractions (Figure S1C). Western blot analysis showed that phosphorylated tau proteins were present in the AD‐derived sarkosyl fraction but not in CTL sarkosyl fractions (Figure S1D). The seeding ability of PHF‐tau proteins in the AD sonicated sarkosyl fraction was tested in the biosensor FRET assay cells (Figure S1E, S1F, and S1G). These experiments showed a significant FRET signal in cells treated with AD sarkosyl fraction (Figure S1F and S1G) but not in cells treated with CTL sarkosyl fractions (Figure S1E), indicating a seeding of recombinant tau by AD sarkosyl fraction in these cells.

Spatial learning impairments in young‐AD and old mice

Young (5‐month‐old) and old (12‐month‐old) htau male and female mice were stereotaxically injected in the dentate gyrus with CTL or AD‐derived sarkosyl fraction as described in Section 2. Cognitive performance of mice was studied 3 months post‐injection by several behavioral tests (Figure 1). Analysis of open field activity showed similar spontaneous exploratory activity in CTL and AD‐injected mice from both age groups (Figure 1B). To assess their preference for a dark environment, we have measured the time spent by the mice in the central brightly lit area of the field. Mice from all groups spent less time in the central zone (approximately 10% of the time used for the test lasting 10 min) than in the corners of the maze.

Short‐term spatial working memory was assessed by the Y‐maze as described in Section 2. Young mice injected with AD sarkosyl fraction showed a tendency to spend less time in the new arm compared to young mice injected with CTL sarkosyl fraction (Figure 1C), suggesting a reduced short‐term spatial memory in these animals. These results, however, were not statistically significant. No significant differences were observed between CTL or AD‐injected old mice or between young and old mice injected with AD sarkosyl fractions. A nonsignificant trend for reduced short‐term spatial memory was observed in old versus young mice injected with CTL sarkosyl fraction (Figure 1C). These changes are probably due to aging.

To study the hippocampus‐dependent spatial learning, we used the Barnes maze test. 30 , 31 As described in Section 2, mice were trained to find the escape box during 5 consecutive days, and we recorded the time spent by them to find the escape box (primary latency, Figure 1D). Compared to young mice, old mice injected with CTL sarkosyl fractions spent more time finding the escape box from day 2 until day 5, whereas no differences were observed between old mice injected with CTL or AD sarkosyl fractions. These results point to reduced hippocampus‐dependent spatial learning with aging, as expected. 42 , 43 Interestingly, from day 2 to 5, young mice injected with AD sarkosyl fractions spent significantly more time finding the escape box compared to young mice injected with CTL fraction, suggesting AD sarkosyl fraction‐mediated memory impairment in these mice. No significant differences were observed between AD‐injected male and female mice, independently of age. There is no significant difference in the number of errors during the learning phase between groups, even when separated by sex (Figure S2).

Tau pathology maturation in young and old mice

Young and old htau mice injected with CTL sarkosyl fractions did not develop tau pathology (Figure 2A and B). In the hippocampus, close to the injection point, AD‐injected young and old mice showed similar maturation of NFTs, evidenced by abnormal tau phosphorylation at Ser 202 and Thr205 (AT8 positivity), tau conformational changes (MC1 positivity), and tau aggregation (Gallyas positivity) (Figure 2C–2K). No significant differences were observed between AD‐injected males and females, regardless of age, in the injected and in the non‐injected side of the brain (Figure S3).

As the AD‐injected mice showed Gallyas‐positive aggregates, we extracted pathological tau from the injected mice's brains using the sarkosyl fractionation method. While insoluble tau was nearly undetectable in young mice injected with CTL sarkosyl fraction, it was significantly increased in AD‐injected mice (Figure 3A and B). Surprisingly, old AD‐injected mice showed less insoluble tau than young mice injected with this same sarkosyl fraction (Figure 3B). We next analyzed the type of tau isoforms (3R and/or 4R) present in sarkosyl fractions from young and old AD‐injected mice. These results showed that the tau pathology generated by the AD‐stereotaxic injection is mainly composed of 3R tau isoforms in both young and old htau mice (Figure 3C).

Electron microscopy analysis of sarkosyl fractions from AD‐injected mice unveiled the presence of tau‐positive twisted filaments (Figure 3D) detected by colloidal gold positivity with anti‐tau antibody (Figure 3E). The filaments from AD‐injected mice were similar between young and old mice and shared some structural characteristics with human PHFs. Indeed, the length, the half‐period, and the minimal width of the PHF filaments from non‐sonicated AD sarkosyl fractions were comparable to the filaments from AD‐injected mice (Figure 3F, H, and I). However, the maximal width of the filaments was significantly different between human and mouse samples (Figure 3J).

Tau pathology propagation in young and old mice

To determine the role of aging on tau pathology, we next examined the density of AT8 positive profiles in the hippocampus, close to the position of the injection (Figure 4A–4G), in a more posterior position in the hippocampus (Figure 4H–4N) and in the entorhinal cortex (Figure 4O–4U) of male and female, young and old mice injected with AD sarkosyl fractions. No significant differences were observed in the density of AT8‐positive profiles between young and old mice or males and females in the area close to the injection point (Figure 4A–4G). However, in the more posterior part of the hippocampus in the ipsilateral part of the brain, young female mice showed a significant increase in AT8‐positive profiles density compared to young male mice, but also compared to old female mice (Figure 4N). No significant differences were observed between old female mice and young or old male mice. Similar results were obtained in the entorhinal cortex between young and old females (Figure 4U). On the other hand, no significant differences have been observed in the number of AT8 positive profiles in the contralateral part of the brain at the site of injection or posterior to the site of injection (Figure S3C and S3D) between males and females or between young or old mice. The discrepancy in the formation of tau pathology between the ipsilateral and the contralateral parts of the brain could be explained by the low number of AT8‐positive profiles found in the contralateral part of the brain at the site of injection (approximately two positive profiles per hippocampus on average).

Seeding activity of sarkosyl fraction from AD‐injected mice

The seeding activity of sarkosyl fractions from AD‐injected mice was tested in FRET cells. As expected, human AD sarkosyl fraction showed a strong seeding activity in these cells, whereas human CTL sarkosyl fraction did not show any FRET signal (Figure 5A, B, and E). Proteomic signatures

In order to identify the molecular drivers of the tau pathology formation and the increased tau pathology observed in young female mice, we performed a proteomic analysis in hippocampal samples from AD‐ or CTL‐injected htau female and male mice. Proteins displaying a log2 fold change of [0.5] between groups and a p‐adjusted value < 0.05 were considered as differentially expressed (Figure 6A and B). AD‐injected young females showed 138 differentially expressed proteins compared to CTL‐injected young females (Figure 6A and Table 1), while only 55 proteins were differentially expressed in male mice under the same conditions (Figure 6B and Table 1). Almost all the differentially expressed proteins were downregulated. Indeed, only nine and four were upregulated in females and males, respectively, and none of them were common between males and females. Two of the upregulated proteins identified in AD vs. CTL‐injected young female mice (Q9CRC3 and Vmp1) were also upregulated upon tau pathology formation in old female but not in young male mice (Table 1).

When we compared the proteomic signatures of hippocampal samples from young mice developing tau pathology (orange circle in Figure 6A for females and Figure 6B for males, young AD‐ vs. CTL‐injected mice) and from mice with tau pathology during aging (green circle in Figure 6A for females and Figure 6B for males, AD‐injected mice young vs. old), we identified 18 and 13 common downregulated proteins in females and males, respectively, suggesting an important role of these proteins in the mechanisms leading to tau pathology formation (Table 1). To better understand the role/interaction that exists between the differentially expressed proteins and to identify specific mechanisms implied in females, the list of differentially expressed proteins in females with tau pathology (18 targets) is used to construct a network using STRING (Figure 6D). Among these 18 proteins, STRING identified 4 clusters involved in cytoskeletal organization and endosome trafficking (ARPC4, ARPC5L, Abl2, BRK1, Wasf1, Wasf3, WIPF3), in clathrin‐dependant endocytosis (Smap1 and Smap2), in transcription regulation/mRNA trafficking/splicing (KHSRP, FUBP1) and in RNA splicing (CELF4, CELF5, Mbnl2, Nova2) (Figure 6D). Interestingly, other differentially expressed proteins are implicated in cellular mechanisms, probably playing a role in tau pathology propagation, such as exocytosis/endocytosis/membrane repair and autophagosome formation for Pdcd6ip and VMP1, respectively. Among these differentially expressed proteins, 5 proteins (Nova2, Pdcd6ip, Smap2, Wasf3, Wbp2) are also downregulated in males (underlined in orange in Figure 6C).

Subsequently, we compared the differentially expressed proteins in young mice with tau pathology to those modified during normal aging to better understand why tau pathology is specifically increased in young females. This latter list of proteins was generated by comparing hippocampal samples of young versus old mice without tau pathology (CTL‐injected mice) to identify the mechanisms implicated during aging (blue circle in Figure 6A for females and 6B for males and Table 1). This analysis identified 30 and 9 downregulated proteins in females and males, respectively (Figure 6A and B). Among these differentially expressed proteins, 4 proteins (Col4a1, Col4a2, Lama5 and Ndufa12) are downregulated in males but also in females (written in bold in Table 1). Within these 30 proteins differentially expressed in females, STRING identified 3 clusters involved in amyloid degradation (ACE, Ttr), in extracellular matrix/intermediate filaments (Col4a1, Col4a2, Col18a1, Hspg2, Krt8, Lama5, Lamb2, Serpinh1, Vim), in mitochondria/ATP synthesis (Acaa2, Atp5p0, Auh, Hadahb, Ndufa12, Uqr10 Cbr4, Phb2), and in RNA splicing (Rplp0, SNRP, SNRPB, SNRPE). These results suggest that tau pathology formation in young females leads to cellular mechanisms modifications which are similar to events occurring during aging.

DISCUSSION

We have assessed the effect of aging on tau pathology propagation and associated proteomic signatures changes. As expected, AD sarkosyl fraction induces tau pathology formation in the somatic neuronal compartment in the htau mice model expressing 6 human tau isoforms, whereas the injection of CTL sarkosyl fractions has no effect, as previously described. 15 , 16 Moreover, sarkosyl fractions from injected mice are mainly composed of 3R isoforms forming PHFs with similar characteristics to human AD PHFs. The sarkosyl fractions from injected mice showed a migration pattern with several bands in Figure 3C that could be explained by the presence of different types of 3R isoforms (with 0N, 1N, or 2N inserts) or different states of tau phosphorylation. As an imbalance between tau isoforms (in favor of 3R isoforms) has been described in htau mice models, 25 it is expected that pathological tau recruits the main expressed isoforms in this mouse model. However, a modification of tau splicing could be responsible for this imbalance. Indeed, several splicing factors called Celf4, Celf5, Mbnl2, and Nova2 are downregulated in mice showing tau pathology. Celf4 decreases exon 2 inclusion and increases exon 10 inclusion. 44 Nova2 is involved in the inclusion of exon 10 45 whereas Mbnl2 regulates the inclusion of exon 2. 46 Sarkosyl insoluble fraction extracted from the brain of injected mice is mainly composed of 3R isoforms, confirming the decreased exon 10 inclusion due to the downregulation of these splicing factors. Dujardin et al. demonstrated that the 1N4R tau isoform is more potent in propagating in the brain compared to the 1N3R isoform. 47 The downregulation of these splicing factors could be a cellular mechanism to reduce tau pathology propagation by increasing 3R tau isoforms.

Even if we cannot completely exclude that behavioral tests could be affected by the surgery, we have observed that tau pathology formation and spreading induced by the injection of AD sarkosyl fraction leads to spatial learning impairments demonstrated by the Barnes test, in young mice when they are compared to young mice injected with CTL sarkosyl fraction. However, no significant changes have been observed with the Y maze, indicating that short‐term spatial memory is not affected by the formation of tau pathology. Spatial learning impairment was observed in old mice injected with CTL sarkosyl fraction and without tau pathology, as previously described. 42 The downregulation of SNRPs observed in young mice injected with AD sarkosyl fraction or in old mice injected with CTL fraction could explain the spatial learning impairments, as it was shown that snRNPs' splicing dysfunctions lead to cognitive deficits in mice. 48 In old mice injected with AD sarkosyl fraction, the absence of cumulative effects of tau pathology formation and aging on spatial learning impairments could be due to the limited ability of the Barnes test to discriminate subtle modifications in spatial learning.

At the site of injection, no significant changes in tau pathology formation have been observed, neither at the phosphorylation nor at the conformational/aggregation states between young and old mice injected with AD sarkosyl fractions in the ipsilateral or in the contralateral part of the brain. Concerning tau pathology propagation, we have quantified tau pathology in brain regions synaptically connected with the dentate gyrus, such as the Ammon's horn and the entorhinal cortex. Surprisingly, we have observed a significant increase in tau pathology formation in the more posterior part of the hippocampus and in the entorhinal cortex in young female mice, whereas no significant differences have been observed in these brain regions between young and old male mice. These results suggest that tau propagation mechanisms are exacerbated in the hippocampus of young females, whereas the seeding is not affected, as the formation of tau pathology is not modified at the site of injection or in FRET biosensor cells. The seeding activity in FRET biosensor cells is not underestimated due to a neuronal loss in old animals, as neuronal markers (MAP2 or Tubulin Beta 3) are not modified in the proteomic analysis.

As it is established that tau transgenic mice have insulin signaling pathway impairment which in turn can exacerbate tau hyperphosphorylation, 49 , 50 we have investigated the expression levels of proteins involved in the insulin pathway in our proteomic analysis. Modifications in the insulin pathway are probably not implicated in tau pathology propagation in our mice model, as there are no changes in IRS, Pi3K, AKT, or GSK3beta expression.

A previous study from Wegmann et al. has shown an increased propagation of human mutant tau during aging in WT mice injected with adeno‐associated virus expressing human mutant tau. 51 However, they have not observed a change in tau pathology formation. We cannot exclude that the absence of increased tau propagation during aging in our study is due to the difference in models used, implicating different types of tau isoforms or different cellular mechanisms of tau propagation between WT and mutant tau. Moreover, in Wegmann's study, tau spreading is based on the expression of human tau through a viral vector and not based on the recruitment of normal tau proteins by pathological tau proteins, as is the case in our study and as is the case in AD. In the publication of Nies et al., after the injection of human AD sarkosyl fraction in young and old WT mice brains, tau pathology developed mainly in neuropil threads, and very few are present in the somatic compartment of neurons. In this model, the aging affects tau pathology in the neuropil threads but not in the somatic compartment. 52 In our model, tau pathology developed mainly in the soma of neurons. Cellular mechanisms involved in neuronal processes could be different than those in the soma, explaining the discrepancy of the aging effect between the Nies study and ours. This discrepancy could also be due to the type of tau isoforms expressed in WT mice (4R only) compared to our model (htau mice expressing 3R/4R isoforms), closer to the human conditions.

Our proteomic analysis identified 18 and 13 proteins that are differentially expressed in the hippocampus of young female and male mice, respectively, when tau pathology developed. Interestingly, some of these proteins have been shown to be downregulated in AD (Dlat, Nova 2, Pdcd6ip, and Wasf1) 53 , 54 , 55 , 56 suggesting similar implications between mice models and AD human cases. Among the differentially expressed proteins, some downregulated proteins participate directly or indirectly in the cellular mechanisms involved in tau pathology propagation. Tau propagation occurs through different mechanisms, such as clathrin‐mediated endocytosis or autophagosomes. Smap1 and Smap2 are proteins involved in clathrin‐dependent endocytosis. 57 Vmp1, which is upregulated only in young females, converts LC3‐I into LC3‐II, leading to the formation of autophagosome 58 and LC3‐containing vesicles positive for clathrin promote vesicular trafficking of tau for degradation. 59 We hypothesize that the reduction of Smap1 and Smap2 and the increased Vmp1 expression are protective mechanisms induced by the cells in the hippocampus to reduce the uptake of tau, its propagation, but also to increase its degradation. However, Rose and colleagues have shown that tau fibrils accumulate in the lysosomes, disrupt the lysosomal membrane, recruit ESCRT machinery to try to repair membranes, and nucleate cytosolic tau. 60 ESCRT assembly at the injured membrane is dependent on Alix (Pdcd6ip), and the lack of Alix prevents repair of the injured membrane. 61 As Pdcd6ip (Alix) is downregulated in young male and female mice with tau pathology, we can hypothesize that the impairment in membrane repair due to Alix downregulation facilitates the recruitment of endogenous normal tau by tau fibrils (Figure 7B1). Moreover, it suggests that the increase in Vmp1 in young females, which was initially a protective mechanism, not only fails to degrade pathological tau proteins but could have an opposite effect by increasing the formation of autophagosomes, inducing the nucleation of normal cytosolic tau proteins into a pathological form, and could explain the increased tau pathology observed in young females (Figure 7B2). During aging, different cellular mechanisms are probably involved in tau pathology propagation (Figure 7A), as Pdcd6ip and Vmp1 are not differentially expressed as in young mice. Interestingly, we found some other downregulated proteins in both young females with tau pathology and during aging (ACE, ATP5P0, AUH, Ttr, and UQCR10), which have been reported to be downregulated in AD as well. 62 , 63 , 64 , 65 It has been shown that ACE and Ttr play a role in amyloid degradation. 66 , 67

Differentially expressed proteins in the hippocampus of young females injected with AD sarkosyl fraction, in old females during aging and hypothetical cellular mechanisms implied in young mice. (A) STRING network of 30 differentially expressed proteins in young female AD‐injected mice and in old females during normal aging. Clusters are identified by their functions and targets known as downregulated in AD are colored in yellow. (B) Hypothetical mechanisms implied in tau pathology propagation in young mice injected with AD sarkosyl fraction. Macroautophagy is a cellular mechanism allowing protein degradation through the fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes. Vmp1 is playing a key role in the early step of autophagosome formation. Previous studies have shown that tau fibrils are resistant to lysosomal degradation and induce membrane damage allowing to tau fibrils to recruit normal cytoplasmic tau. In this condition, ESCRT machinery, whose activity depends on Pdcd6ip (Alix) presence, is recruited to repair membrane damage. In young AD‐injected males (B1), the reduction of Pcd6ip expression could impair the function of ESCRT machinery to repair injured membrane leading to the increased recruitment of normal tau by tau fibrils. In young females (B2), a similar mechanism is probably occurring as Pdcd6ip is also downregulated in the hippocampus of young AD‐injected females. However, Vmp1 is specifically upregulated in young females and we hypothesize that it leads to the increased formation of autophagosomes and the consecutive recruitment of normal tau. AD, Alzheimer's disease.

Our old females are at a stage corresponding to the human pre‐menopause/menopause, as an important depletion of ovarian follicles has been described at the age of old females when tau pathology was induced. 68 It is particularly interesting as researchers have pinpointed menopause as a critical period for women, impacting their risk of AD later in life, but the implied mechanisms are still unknown. 69 This increased risk of women to develop AD during aging cannot simply be explained by the loss of hormones, as hormonal therapy in aged women increased tau accumulation in their brains. 3 We have shown that tau pathology propagation is more important in young females compared to old females. The preclinical phase of AD, during which tau pathology is starting but symptoms are not yet apparent, can start at midlife. Based on our results, we hypothesize that tau pathology onset probably starts earlier in life in women, explaining why women are more affected than men at the same age during aging. Further investigations will be necessary to decipher the role of hormones in tau pathology propagation in our young female model. However, in this study, our proteomic analysis allowed the identification of specific pathways affected in young females, opening new lines of research to better understand the female susceptibility to develop tau pathology. The role of these differentially expressed proteins on tau pathology propagation has not yet been deciphered and should be the center of interest in future experiments to better understand the mechanisms implied in tau pathology propagation.
