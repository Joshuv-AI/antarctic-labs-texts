Declining anthropogenic aerosols amplify Northern Hemisphere Hadley circulation weakening in the 21st century
Authors: S-Y Kim, S-W Son, Y Ming, D-C Hong, Xihui Gu, R J Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Hadley circulation plays a key role in transporting atmospheric heat from the tropics to the subtropics, shaping the climate in low latitudes. While its projected weakening has been largely attributed to rising greenhouse gas concentrations, the long-term impact of future anthropogenic aerosol concentrations remains underexplored. Here, we show that the projected changes in anthropogenic aerosol concentrations can account for about one-third of the Northern Hemisphere Hadley circulation weakening between 1980 and 2080, emerging as a major driver of tropical circulation change throughout the 21st century. This impact is linked to altered diabatic heating patterns in the northern tropics, driven by precipitation changes under reduced aerosol forcing. These results highlight a critical trade-off of aerosol mitigation: while improving air quality, it may amplify tropical atmospheric circulation changes driven by greenhouse gases.Subject terms: Atmospheric dynamics, Environmental impact

Introduction

The Hadley Circulation (HC) consists of ascending motion in the deep tropics, poleward flow in the upper troposphere, descending motion in the subtropics, and equatorward return flow near the surface. This thermally direct overturning circulation strongly influences tropical hydroclimate and maintains subtropical dry zones1–4. As global warming accelerates, climate model simulations consistently project a weakening of the Northern Hemisphere (NH) HC from the past to the future climate5–9. This weakening has primarily been attributed to changes in anthropogenic greenhouse gases and aerosols6,10–16.

While the weakening of the HC by increasing greenhouse gases is well documented, the response of the HC to changing aerosols is not well understood, as it is sensitive to the analysis period and models due to the complex spatiotemporal evolution of aerosol forcing10,15,17. Anthropogenic aerosols increased globally until the 1980s, particularly after the end of World War II, but have since followed divergent regional trajectories: declining in Europe and North America due to effective mitigation efforts but continuing to increase in East and South Asia18–23 (Supplementary Fig. 1). Past studies utilizing climate model simulations in different configurations have shown that the overall cooling effect of increasing anthropogenic aerosols has likely strengthened the HC in the NH, counteracting the weakening driven by greenhouse-gas-induced warming10,16,24. However, the impacts of anthropogenic aerosols over the past few decades are uncertain, as aerosol concentrations have declined in the Western Hemisphere but increased in the Eastern Hemisphere. While these zonally asymmetric aerosol forcings may have competed with each other, recent studies have suggested that changes in HC intensity are more sensitive to aerosol changes in the Western Hemisphere, thereby driving a likely weakening of the HC since the 1980s20,22,25. Despite these findings, the impact of time-varying aerosol forcings (Supplementary Fig. 1) on HC intensity change is not well quantified. The relative importance of anthropogenic aerosols compared to greenhouse gases and their underlying mechanism(s) also remains poorly understood.

In this work, we show the pivotal role of anthropogenic aerosol forcing in driving changes in the NH HC intensity. By examining large ensembles of single-forcing climate model simulations, we identify the mechanisms underlying aerosol-induced HC intensity changes and compare them with those driven by greenhouse gases. As the impacts of aerosols on atmospheric circulation changes are often obscure and model-dependent26–28, the robustness of the results is also tested by employing a multi-model framework and an additional suite of the latest large-ensemble simulations.

Results

Hadley circulation weakening due to anthropogenic forcings

The HC intensity changes, derived from the CESM1 large ensemble (CESM1 LENS) single-forcing experiments, are presented in Fig. 1. Under the all-forcing scenario (ALL experiment; see Methods), the NH HC intensity (Ψmax; see Methods and Supplementary Fig. 2) shows robust weakening from 1920 to 2080 at a rate of −0.62 × 109 kg s−1decade-1 (Table 1). This is consistent with a previous study6. The time of emergence of NH HC intensity change, determined by the year when the forced response exceeds the range of natural variability29–31, is estimated to be around 2020. The natural variability is defined as the one-standard-deviation range of Ψmax in the pre-industrial simulation (green lines in Fig. 1a).

The long-term change is less pronounced in the Southern Hemisphere (SH) HC intensity (Ψmin). From 1920 through 2080, Ψmin remains within the bounds of the internal variability (Fig. 1b). This result reinforces the finding that the HC weakening is more pronounced in the NH than in the SH6,12. This hemispheric asymmetry is consistent with prior studies and may be related to differences in aerosol distributions and stratospheric ozone24,27.

The intensity of the NH HC (hereafter referred to as HC unless otherwise specified) exhibits a distinct decadal variation (Fig. 1a). Specifically, the trend switches sign around 1980, transitioning from a strengthening phase prior to the 1970s to a weakening phase thereafter (Table 1). The recent weakening is consistent with observations5, although it is less clear in reanalyses32,33. As shown below, this transition is likely associated with changes in aerosol emissions in the NH (Supplementary Fig. 1; See also refs. 22,25,34,35).

The contribution of increasing greenhouse gas concentrations to the HC intensity change is quantified by taking a difference between ALL and fixed greenhouse gas concentrations (XGHG) experiments, denoted as GHG experiment (red in Fig. 1c; see “Methods”). The HC intensity trend in GHG experiment is statistically significant over the analysis period (1920–2080) but is smaller than the trend in ALL experiment. Even during the period of 1980–2080 when greenhouse-gas-induced HC weakening accelerates, the trend is only ~60% of that in ALL experiment (Table 1). The time of emergence in GHG experiment is also delayed by about 20 years compared to that in ALL experiment (compare crossing of green lines in Fig. 1c with Fig. 1a). This suggests that increasing greenhouse gases do not fully explain the HC weakening in ALL experiment, indicating a substantial influence of anthropogenic aerosols.

The contribution of anthropogenic aerosols is quantified by differencing ALL from fixed anthropogenic aerosols (XAER) experiments, denoted as AER experiment (blue in Fig. 1c; see “Methods”). While the aerosol-induced HC intensity change lies within the natural variability for the entire period (1920–2080), statistically significant trends are observed in two distinct sub-periods (Table 1): the earlier period (1920–1980) when anthropogenic aerosols increased globally (Supplementary Fig. 1), and the subsequent period (1980–2080) of anthropogenic aerosols decline, especially in the Western Hemisphere35. During 1920–1980, increasing aerosol concentrations strengthen the HC intensity by 0.47×109 kg s−1decade−1, which exceeds the trend in the ALL experiment. After 1980, declining aerosol concentrations weaken the HC by −0.35×109 kg s−1 decade−1, accounting for approximately 35% of the total HC weakening in the ALL experiment over 1980–2080 (Table 1). Since the latter weakening effect after 1980 outweighs the former strengthening effect before 1980, the net impact of anthropogenic aerosol forcing on HC intensity over the entire analysis period is an overall weakening (Table 1). The results are robust to the specific choice of transition year within the 1970–1980 period, as NH aerosol emissions are in a transition phase during this decade (Supplementary Fig. 1).

It is important to note that the aerosol effect is not always secondary. In certain periods, it acts as the dominant driver of HC intensity changes. For instance, during the earlier phase of aerosol decline (1980–2040), the aerosol-induced HC weakening (−0.56 × 109 kg s−1 decade−1) is more pronounced than the greenhouse-gas-induced weakening (−0.34 × 109 kg s−1 decade−1). This underscores the time-dependent nature of aerosol forcing and its critical role in shaping near-term tropical circulation changes.

The combined effects of greenhouse gases and aerosols are further evaluated by comparing the trend derived from the linear sum of the GHG and AER experiments (GHG + AER; purple in Fig. 1c) with that from the ALL experiment (black in Fig. 1a). The two time series are quantitatively similar, showing close agreement across the three investigated periods (Table 1). This result indicates that anthropogenic greenhouse gases and aerosols are the two primary factors governing the HC intensity changes from 1920 to 2080, consistent with ref. 35.

The above results, derived from a single model, are supported by multi-model analyses. The multi-model mean from the models participating in the CMIP6 Detection and Attribution Model Intercomparison Project (DAMIP; see Methods)36 shows qualitatively similar results, despite different experiment design and aerosol forcing (Table 1; See also Supplementary Fig. 3). In all-forcing experiments, HC intensity exhibits a slight strengthening during 1920–1980, followed by a weakening during 1980–2080. Approximately 26% of HC weakening is attributed to aerosol reductions: −1.13×109 kg s−1 decade−1 in all forcing experiments versus −0.29×109 kg s−1 decade−1 in aerosol-only experiments over 1980–2080.

The robustness of HC intensity changes is also tested by examining the latest CESM2 large ensemble (CESM2 LENS) single-forcing experiment (See “Methods”). This experiment differs from CESM1 LENS in that greenhouse gas or aerosol forcings are prescribed with all other forcings held fixed at 1850s levels. Despite this difference, as well as differences in aerosol emission scenarios, ensemble size, and integration length, qualitatively similar results are obtained (Supplementary Fig. 4). The anthropogenic aerosol-only experiment in CESM2 LENS shows a statistically significant HC strengthening of 0.43 × 109 kg s−1 decade−1 for 1920–1980, and a subsequent weakening of −0.48 × 109 kg s−1 decade-1 for 1980–2050. This result is consistent with the findings from both CESM1 LENS and CMIP6 DAMIP results (Table 1).

Physical processes of Hadley circulation weakening

While both increasing greenhouse gas concentrations and decreasing aerosol concentrations contribute to the HC weakening during 1980–2080, their underlying mechanisms may differ. The latitudinal-pressure structure of mass streamfunction (Ψ) indeed shows distinctively different responses of the HC to the two forcings (Supplementary Fig. 2). Greenhouse gas forcing leads to HC weakening centered in the subtropics (10°–30°N; Supplementary Fig. 2e), whereas aerosol forcing causes weakening primarily in the deep tropics within ~15° N (Supplementary Fig. 2f).

To investigate the physical processes associated with these changes, we employ the Kuo-Eliassen (KE) equation (See Eq. 2 in “Methods”). This equation provides a diagnostic framework that relates HC intensity changes to various physical processes in the atmosphere, such as the meridional gradient of diabatic heating (Q¯y), static stability (S2), eddy heat flux convergence (v′T′¯), eddy momentum flux convergence (u′v′¯), and zonal drag (X¯). Here, Q¯y is dominated by latent heating of tropical convection and precipitation32,33. Since precipitation is maximized near the Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ), climatological Q¯y is negative around the HC center. In a warming climate, negative Q¯y is anticipated to intensify as precipitation is projected to increase in the ITCZ and decrease in the subtropics37,38. On the other hand, S2, determined by the vertical profile of temperature change, is expected to increase in a warming climate because upper tropospheric warming is faster than lower tropospheric warming as the temperature profile in the tropics closely follows the moist adiabatic lapse rate. Other terms are relatively minor6. Eddy fluxes are affected by subtropical and mid-latitude weather systems, whereas X¯ is dependent on surface friction.

The Ψ changes reconstructed from the KE equation (δΨKE), defined as the sum of all individual terms in Eq. (4), reproduce the key features of the actual Ψ changes (δΨ; Supplementary Fig. 5). This agreement supports the validity of the KE-based diagnosis, despite the use of monthly-mean data to compute eddy flux terms. However, it should be noted that the KE equation does not represent a causal relationship. Moreover, individual terms in the right-hand side of Eq. (4) are not independent. Despite these limitations, the similarity between δΨ and δΨKE enables us to qualitatively evaluate the physical processes associated with the HC intensity changes.

For each experiment (ALL, GHG, and AER experiments), HC trends derived from the KE equation (δΨmax,KE) are computed for the period 1980–2080 relative to the 1941–1970 climatology (Fig. 2). In the ALL experiment (Fig. 2a), HC weakening is primarily driven by changes in S2 (red bar), as reported in previous studies6,11,39. The second largest term is Q¯y (green), which partially offsets the HC weakening driven by S2. The counteracting effects of S2 and Q¯y are also evident in the GHG experiment (Fig. 2b). As in the ALL experiment, the negative impacts of S2 and other terms overcome the positive impact of Q¯y, resulting in a net weakening of the HC. In the AER experiment, the change in S2 still acts to weaken the HC, although its effect is much weaker than in the GHG experiment due to the smaller aerosol-induced tropical warming (Supplementary Fig. 6). More importantly, unlike in the GHG experiment, the Q¯y effect is negative in the AER experiment during the aerosol-declining phase. This negative contribution from Q¯y, combined with enhanced stratification (S2), eddy influences (v′T′¯ and u′v′¯), and zonal drag (X¯), collectively accelerates the weakening of the HC (Fig. 2c).

Changes in S2 within the tropics are primarily determined by the thermodynamic response of the atmosphere to surface temperature changes. Greenhouse-gas-induced tropical warming is more pronounced in the upper troposphere than in the lower troposphere (Fig. S6e)6. This top-heavy warming pattern increases static stability and slows down the tropical overturning circulation6,11. A similar warming pattern emerges during the aerosol-declining period (1980–2080), where the warming is also amplified in the upper troposphere following the moist adiabat (Fig. S6f), albeit with a smaller magnitude. Specifically, the S2-induced HC weakening per 1-K tropical surface warming in response to aerosol reduction (−2.2×1010 kg s−1 decade−1 per 1-K) closely aligns with that induced by greenhouse gas increase (−2.5 × 1010 kg s−1 decade-1 per 1-K). This highlights that the same thermodynamic mechanism controls the HC weakening via S2 changes in both GHG and AER experiments.

The meridional gradient of diabatic heating, Qy, is another key component that modulates the HC intensity32. Latitudinal precipitation profiles, which determine Qy, respond differently to greenhouse gas and aerosol forcings (Fig. 3). In the GHG experiment, the maximum increase in precipitation occurs near the ITCZ, strengthening Qy. To achieve a balanced state, a thermally direct circulation develops, reducing Qy through adiabatic cooling in the deep tropics and warming in the subtropics (Fig. 2b). In contrast, aerosol-induced precipitation change shows a maximum at approximately 11°N (Fig. 3b). This reduces the Qy around the HC center and leads to HC weakening (Fig. 2c). Similar precipitation pattern changes, albeit weaker, are also observed in the CMIP6 DAMIP models (Supplementary Fig. 7), consistent with previous studies40–42. The precipitation peak at 11°N is associated with an anomalous cross-equatorial circulation (Supplementary Fig. 2f), driven by hemispherically asymmetric warming arising from aerosol forcing (Supplementary Fig. 6f)24,40,43,44.

Although smaller in magnitude, other terms also contribute to changes in HC intensity. Eddy momentum fluxes and zonal drag both contribute to the HC weakening (Fig. 2). Under global warming, enhanced stratification stabilizes the subtropical atmosphere, leading to a weakening of eddy activity and thus the HC7,45. The zonal drag, X¯, also contributes to HC weakening by modifying near-surface zonal winds and the associated vertical wind shear. For instance, the Pacific trade winds have strengthened over the past two decades in response to declining aerosol reduction46,47. This has likely led to an increase in surface friction, thereby weakening the HC.

Discussion

Climate models show broad agreement in the weakening of the Northern Hemisphere HC from the recent past to the future. While the HC weakening has often been attributed to increasing greenhouse gas concentrations and time-evolving aerosol concentrations15,16,24,27,48, the contribution of overall reduction in anthropogenic aerosols during the 21st century to HC intensity changes has not been fully determined. Here, we provide modeling evidence that the long-term decline in anthropogenic aerosol could account for approximately one-third of the HC weakening from 1980 until the end of the 21st century. Considering that the impact of aerosols on surface temperature change is much weaker than that of greenhouse gases (Supplementary Fig. 6), the significant impact of aerosol reductions to HC weakening is notable.

This result suggests that aerosol mitigation efforts, which are beneficial for air quality, could accelerate the tropical circulation changes induced by increasing greenhouse gas concentrations. This presents a complex challenge to global warming mitigation strategies. Due to the inherent spatial inhomogeneity of anthropogenic aerosols, i.e., a decline in the Western Hemisphere and a continued increase in the Eastern Hemisphere until recently, further studies are necessary with an emphasis on the relative role of regional aerosol forcing22,25. Such studies could utilize coordinated model experiments, such as the Regional Aerosol Model Intercomparison Project (RAMIP)23,49.

Given the substantial inter-model spread in projected HC intensity changes10,32, the underlying causes of these uncertainties and their relationship with aerosol forcing also require further analysis50,51.

Definition of Hadley circulation (HC) intensity

All analyses are conducted on the annual-mean v¯ from each ensemble member. The HC intensity is defined as the absolute value of 500-hPa mass stream function maximum (Ψmax) in the Northern Hemisphere and the minimum (Ψmin) in the Southern Hemisphere, between the ascending and descending branches in the tropics (red asterisks in Supplementary Fig. 2)27,52. The use of Ψmin accounts for the negative sign of Ψ in the Southern Hemisphere. The overall results are consistent when using the average of Ψ between 400-hPa and 700-hPa levels.

The change in ΨKE (i.e., δΨKE) is computed from 1980 to 2080, by decomposing L, ΨKE, and D into their values in the past period (1941–1970; denoted by past) and the deviation from the past, as in the literature6,33: L=Lpast + δL(t), ΨKE = ΨKE,past + δΨKE(t), and D = Dpast + δD(t).

Here, the small non-linear term, δLδΨKE, is neglected. This equation is solved numerically for each ensemble member using a Jacobi iteration method.

Physically, the first four terms on the right-hand side of Eq. (4) represent the contributions of diabatic heating (δΨQ), eddy heat flux (δΨh), eddy momentum flux (δΨm), and zonal drag changes (δΨX) to stream function change since 1980 (δΨKE) under fixed static stability. The last term, δΨS2, represents the change in stream function due to static stability change when the preceding four terms are constant in time.

While ref. 53 provides critical insights into the application of the KE equation, particularly regarding its limitations in predicting steady-state responses to perturbations, our analysis employs the KE framework as a diagnostic tool to quantitatively understand the contributing factors to long-term trends of Ψ changes from 1920–2080 relative to a 1941–1970 baseline, rather than inferring new steady states. Additionally, the Ψ changes reconstructed from the KE equation (ΨKE), defined as the sum of all individual rightmost terms in Eq. (4), successfully capture key features of the actual Ψ changes (Supplementary Fig. 5). This close agreement supports the validity of the KE-based diagnosis for understanding the long-term trends.

CESM1 LENS

A set of CESM154 large ensemble (LENS) simulations with a 1° spatial resolution35 is analyzed in this study. These simulations consist of 1) a 40-member ensemble simulation with all forcings (ALL), 2) a 20-member ensemble simulation with time-fixed aerosol concentrations (XAER), and 3) a 20-member ensemble simulation with time-fixed greenhouse gas concentrations (XGHG). Each ensemble member in XGHG and XAER experiments is initialized in the same manner in ALL experiment. ALL experiment is forced by historical forcings for the period 1920–2005 and by the CMIP5 RCP8.5 scenario for the period 2006–210055. Both XGHG and XAER experiments use the same forcing protocol, except that either greenhouse gas or aerosol concentrations are held fixed at the 1920s condition. The impacts of greenhouse gas and aerosol forcings on the HC intensity are quantified by computing the ensemble-mean differences between the two experiments, i.e., ALL-XAER (referred to as AER experiment) and ALL-XGHG (referred to as GHG experiment). AER experiment isolates the effect of aerosol forcing while the GHG experiment isolates that of greenhouse gas forcing. The temporal evolution of global-mean surface air temperature from 1920 to 2080 is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 8. ALL experiment shows a steady warming trend from the past to the future, with the warming intensity sensitive to the period. The warming before the year 1980 is much weaker than the one afterward, with a large variability. The warming trend in ALL experiment (0.24 K decade−1) is well captured by the linear sum of the GHG and AER experiments (GHG + AER; 0.23 K decade−1), supporting the use of these experiments to attribute forced responses. They are compared to an 1800-year pre-industrial control simulation (piControl), which is used to estimate the internal climate variability.

Although 40 members are available for ALL experiment, only the first 20 members are analyzed to make a fair comparison to XGHG and XAER experiments. The ensemble mean of Northern Hemisphere Hadley circulation intensity from all 40 ensemble members in ALL experiment is quantitatively the same as that from the first 20 members (Compare Fig. 1a to Supplementary Fig. 9).

CESM2 LENS

A set of CESM2 LENS simulations56 is also examined. They differ from CESM1 LENS simulations not only in the model but also in the experiment design. While CESM1 LENS uses an all-but-one framework (i.e., XGHG and XAER), CESM2 LENS uses a forcing-only framework. CESM2 single-forcing LENS simulations consist of 20 ensemble members of anthropogenic aerosol-only (AAER) experiments, and 15 ensemble members of greenhouse gas-only (GHG) experiments. To maintain consistency, the first 15 ensembles from both experiments are analyzed. Each experiment spans from 1920 to 2050, which is shorter than the period covered by CESM1 LENS, based on historical (1920–2014) and SSP3-7.0 (2015–2050) scenarios.

Although CESM2 LENS is the latest single-forcing large ensemble experiment, this study primarily analyzes CESM1-LENS. This is because it has a longer temporal coverage (extending to 2080) and a larger ensemble size (20 members), both of which are essential for robustly detecting long-term forced responses and identifying the underlying physical mechanisms.

CMIP6 DAMIP

In addition to CESM1 LENS, we use model output from the Detection and Attribution Model Intercomparison Project (DAMIP)36 of CMIP657. DAMIP provides single-forcing simulations that isolate the effects of individual forcing agents, including anthropogenic aerosols and greenhouse gases, in both historical and future climates. This dataset allows us to assess whether the HC response to aerosol forcings is consistent across a broader set of models. Specifically, four models which provide both the historical aerosol-only (hist-aer) and GHG-only (hist-GHG) simulations for 1850–2020, as well as their respective future scenarios (ssp245-aer and ssp245-GHG) for 2021–2100, are used. They are CanESM5, GISS-E2-1-G, MIROC6, and NorESM2-LM. Historical simulation for 1850–2014 and SSP2-4.5 scenario simulation for 2015–2100 are also examined to provide a more comprehensive comparison with CESM1 LENS. For consistency, only the first ensemble member from each model (r1i1p1f2 for GISS-E2-1-G and r1i1p1f1 for the others) is used for the common period 1920–2080 for a direct comparison among the simulations. Although one ensemble member from each model is utilized to avoid overpresentation of models with larger ensembles, the results do not change significantly when all available ensemble members are used.

It is important to note that the aerosol scenarios differ between the model experiments. DAMIP follows the SSP2-4.5 aerosol pathway, while the XAER experiment in CESM1 LENS and the aerosol-only experiment in CESM2 LENS are based on the RCP8.5 and SSP3-7.0 aerosol scenarios, respectively. As such, comparisons between CESM1 LENS, CESM2 LENS, and DAMIP should be interpreted qualitatively.
