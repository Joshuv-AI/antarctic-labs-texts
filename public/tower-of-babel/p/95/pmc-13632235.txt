Genomic landscape of triple-negative breast cancer in South Asian populations: a systematic review
Authors: Shantanu Bhattacharyya, Jitu V. Thomas, Abhishek Prasad, Arunima Deep, F. N. U. Bhuvan, Sivaramakrishnan Ramachandiran, Rajesh Kumar Garanayak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is an aggressive breast cancer subtype defined by the absence of estrogen receptor, progesterone receptor, and HER2 expression, and is associated with limited targeted treatment options and poor clinical outcomes. Although major genomic studies have characterized TNBC in Western populations, the genomic landscape of TNBC in South Asian populations remains insufficiently understood despite a relatively high disease burden in this region. We conducted a systematic review following PRISMA 2020 guidance to synthesize available genomic evidence on TNBC in South Asian cohorts. Studies reporting genomic alterations in TNBC patients from South Asia were identified through searches of PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and ProQuest, supplemented by citation tracking and a ClinicalTrials.gov search. Eight studies from India and Pakistan met the eligibility criteria. Because the evidence base was sparse and clinically and methodologically heterogeneous, and because the number of studies reporting extractable denominators for any single biomarker did not reach our pre-specified threshold for pooling, data were synthesized descriptively rather than by meta-analysis. Across the included studies, recurrent alterations were reported in key tumor-suppressor and DNA-repair genes, particularly BRCA1, BRCA2, and TP53. Germline BRCA1 alterations were reported at a relatively high frequency in several cohorts, most notably in Pakistani TNBC patients, suggesting a contribution of hereditary DNA-repair defects to TNBC pathogenesis in this population. These findings highlight the importance of population-specific genomic analyses and support the growing role of DNA-repair–directed therapies and biomarker-driven precision oncology strategies in TNBC management. We additionally propose, as recommendations for future work, a two-tier framework for ancestry classification and a tiered scheme for harmonizing homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) reporting.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16455-8.

Introduction

Triple-negative breast cancer: a highly aggressive subtype

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is defined by the absence of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) expression and represents one of the most clinically aggressive forms of breast cancer. Globally, TNBC accounts for approximately 10–20% of all breast cancer cases, although the proportion varies substantially across populations and geographic regions [1, 2]. TNBC is characterized by high histological grade, rapid proliferation, early recurrence, and a marked propensity for visceral and central nervous system metastasis [3, 4]. Unlike hormone receptor–positive or HER2-amplified breast cancers, TNBC has historically lacked established molecular targets for endocrine or HER2-directed therapy, and systemic chemotherapy has remained a primary treatment modality [1].

The therapeutic landscape has, however, expanded substantially. Immune checkpoint inhibition with pembrolizumab is now established in early-stage disease in combination with chemotherapy (KEYNOTE-522; [5]) and in PD-L1–positive advanced disease [6]. In addition, antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) directed against TROP2 have become central to the management of metastatic TNBC. Sacituzumab govitecan is approved for metastatic TNBC that has progressed after prior therapy (ASCENT; [7]), and datopotamab deruxtecan received FDA approval on 22 May 2026 for unresectable or metastatic TNBC in patients who are not candidates for PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor therapy (TROPION-Breast02; [8]). It should be emphasized that these ADCs target TROP2 rather than HRD or BRCA1/2; they therefore represent an expanding component of the overall treatment landscape rather than biomarker-directed therapies tied to the genomic alterations that are the focus of this review. Despite these advances, outcomes among TNBC patients remain heterogeneous, underscoring the need for molecular biomarkers capable of improving prognostic stratification and guiding targeted treatment [9].

Genomic landscape and key driver alterations in TNBC

Large-scale genomic analyses have demonstrated that TNBC exhibits a complex molecular landscape characterized by extensive genomic instability and recurrent alterations in tumor-suppressor and oncogenic signaling pathways. Among these, TP53 mutations are the most frequent event, occurring in approximately 60–80% of TNBC tumors and reflecting widespread disruption of the DNA-damage response and cell-cycle regulation [10].

Aberrations in PI3K signaling, including PIK3CA mutations, occur in a subset of TNBC cases and may define biologically distinct tumor subsets with potential therapeutic vulnerabilities [4, 11]. Germline and somatic mutations in BRCA1 and BRCA2, which impair homologous recombination–mediated DNA repair, represent another key genomic feature of TNBC. BRCA-associated tumors exhibit heightened sensitivity to platinum-based chemotherapy and to poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors [12, 13]. The reported prevalence of these alterations nonetheless varies considerably across studies and populations, reflecting differences in cohort characteristics, sequencing methods, and ancestry, which complicates interpretation and highlights the importance of population-specific analyses.

Homologous recombination deficiency as an emerging biomarker

Beyond BRCA1/2 mutations, the broader concept of homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) has emerged as a key molecular vulnerability in TNBC. HRD reflects the inability of tumor cells to accurately repair DNA double-strand breaks through homologous recombination, resulting in characteristic genomic instability signatures [14]. HRD-positive tumors frequently demonstrate increased sensitivity to DNA-damaging agents such as platinum compounds and PARP inhibitors [15]. HRD assessment nonetheless remains methodologically heterogeneous, encompassing genomic-scar assays, composite scores, mutational signatures, and targeted homologous recombination repair (HRR) gene panels [16, 17]. The lack of standardized definitions limits comparability across studies and complicates pooled estimation of HRD prevalence.

Under-representation of South Asian populations

Although substantial progress has been made in characterizing TNBC, most genomic studies have been conducted in European or East Asian populations, and South Asian populations remain under-represented in large-scale datasets [18]. This gap is important because several reports suggest that South Asian women may experience a relatively higher proportion of TNBC diagnoses, although much of this evidence derives from hospital- or tertiary-referral series rather than population-based registries [18, 19]. Existing South Asian genomic studies are frequently limited by small sample sizes, single-institution cohorts, or interrogation of a restricted set of genes. Consequently, the prevalence and clinical significance of TP53, PIK3CA, and BRCA1/2 alterations remain incompletely characterized in this population.

Population definition and ancestry classification

A further methodological challenge arises from heterogeneity in how South Asian populations are defined. Some studies classify patients by geographic origin or self-reported ethnicity, whereas others incorporate genetically inferred ancestry derived from genomic data; these approaches are not interchangeable and may yield different estimates of mutation frequency [20, 21]. Systematic reviews of TNBC genomics have rarely addressed this distinction explicitly.

Rationale, objectives, and hypotheses

Given these limitations, a comprehensive synthesis of the genomic landscape of TNBC in South Asian populations is warranted. The present study therefore aimed to systematically identify and synthesize genomic alterations in TNBC among South Asian cohorts, with emphasis on TP53, PIK3CA, BRCA1, and BRCA2, and on broader HRR-related biomarkers. To structure the synthesis, and as recommendations for future research rather than analyses we were able to execute on the current evidence base, we describe (i) a two-tier framework for population classification that would prioritize studies using genetically inferred South Asian ancestry over those defined geographically or by self-reported ethnicity, and (ii) a tiered scheme for harmonizing HRD reporting across heterogeneous detection methods.

We framed three guiding hypotheses. First, that the prevalence of major genomic alterations including TP53, PIK3CA, and BRCA1/2 in South Asian TNBC may differ from estimates derived predominantly from Western cohorts. Second, that harmonization of HRD definitions would, in a sufficiently large evidence base, reduce methodological heterogeneity and clarify associations between HRD status and outcomes such as pathologic complete response (pCR) and survival. Third, that the method used to define ancestry would influence pooled genomic estimates. As detailed below, the available evidence was too sparse to test these hypotheses quantitatively, and they are therefore presented as a framework to guide future population-specific genomic research.

Methods

Study design, reporting, and protocol

This systematic review was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) guidance. The review objectives, eligibility criteria, biomarkers of interest, and synthesis strategy were specified before study selection. The review was not prospectively registered in PROSPERO or an equivalent registry; we note this explicitly as a limitation. Because the review synthesizes data exclusively from previously published studies, institutional ethical approval and informed consent were not required.

Objectives

The primary objective was to characterize the genomic landscape of TNBC in South Asian populations, with emphasis on the frequency of alterations in TP53, PIK3CA, BRCA1, and BRCA2, and on HRR-related genes. A secondary objective was to evaluate, where data permitted, associations between genomic alterations and clinical outcomes including pCR and survival (disease-free, event-free, and overall survival).

Proposed population framework (two-tier)

To address heterogeneity in ancestry definition, we describe a two-tier classification intended to guide future synthesis. Under this scheme, a primary tier (Tier A) would comprise studies defining South Asian ancestry using genetically inferred methods (principal component analysis, admixture analysis, or comparison with reference panels), and a secondary tier (Tier B) would comprise studies defining the population by geographic recruitment or self-reported ethnicity. As reported in the Results, none of the included studies used genetically inferred ancestry; all were geographically or ethnically defined (Tier B). The framework is therefore presented as a recommendation for future work rather than as an analysis performed here, and no Tier A–restricted analyses were possible.

Proposed HRD harmonization scheme (three-tier)

Because HRD detection methods differ substantially across studies, we describe a tiered scheme for future harmonization: Tier 1 (validated functional or composite measures, e.g., defined-threshold genomic-scar scores or RAD51 foci assays); Tier 2 (partial genomic-scar measures such as loss of heterozygosity, telomeric allelic imbalance, or large-scale state transitions); and Tier 3 (proxy definitions based on HRR-gene alterations identified by targeted panels without genomic-scar validation). As reported in the Results, no included study provided Tier 1 or Tier 2 HRD data; HRR-gene findings were therefore interpreted only as Tier 3 proxy signals, and no pooled HRD prevalence was estimated.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible if they: (i) included patients diagnosed with TNBC, defined by the absence of ER, PR, and HER2 expression according to study-specific criteria; (ii) reported at least one genomic marker of interest (TP53, PIK3CA, BRCA1, or BRCA2) or HRR/HRD-related data; and (iii) studied a South Asian population by geographic recruitment, self-reported ethnicity, or inferred ancestry. Eligible designs included observational cohort, case–control, and cross-sectional genomic studies, and clinical trials reporting baseline genomic characteristics. Studies were excluded if TNBC data were not separable from other subtypes, if no genomic data were reported, or if the report was an editorial or non-human study. Where the source genomic material (germline versus somatic) could be determined, it was recorded; germline carrier frequencies and somatic mutation rates were treated as distinct entities and were not combined.

Information sources and search

We searched PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and ProQuest, and supplemented these with citation tracking of included studies and relevant reviews and with a ClinicalTrials.gov search. The most recent search was run on 30 December 2025. The search combined controlled-vocabulary and free-text terms for TNBC, South Asian populations, the genes of interest (TP53, PIK3CA, BRCA1, BRCA2), and homologous recombination deficiency. Embase, MEDLINE-specific, and Cochrane Library searches were not performed; this is acknowledged as a limitation.

Study selection and data extraction

Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts and then assessed full texts, resolving disagreements by discussion or with a third reviewer. For each included study we extracted: design and year; country and setting; population/ancestry definition; TNBC diagnostic criteria; sequencing platform and method; germline versus somatic testing source; variant-classification criteria; extractable numerator and denominator data for each genomic alteration; and any reported effect estimates for clinical outcomes.

Risk of bias

Methodological quality of observational studies was appraised using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale. Given the small number of eligible studies and the descriptive nature of the synthesis, risk-of-bias appraisal was used to contextualize findings rather than as an exclusion criterion; per-study domain-level judgments are summarized in the Results and provided in full as a Newcastle–Ottawa risk-of-bias matrix in Supplementary File 1.

Synthesis methods

We had pre-specified that random-effects pooling of prevalence would be undertaken only when at least five studies reported extractable denominators for a given biomarker, and that pooling of outcome estimates would require at least three studies. No biomarker met these thresholds: at most two studies reported a directly comparable germline BRCA1 denominator, and the corresponding point estimates were markedly divergent. We therefore did not compute pooled prevalence estimates, heterogeneity statistics, prediction intervals, odds ratios, or hazard ratios, and present the evidence as a structured descriptive synthesis. Where studies reported numerators and denominators, per-study proportions are tabulated; studies reporting only the presence of an alteration without a denominator are described qualitatively and are not assigned a proportion. Publication-bias assessment, which we had planned to perform only with at least ten studies, was not undertaken.

Results

Study selection

The search identified 133 records, comprising 129 from database searches (PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and ProQuest) and 4 from citation tracking and a ClinicalTrials.gov search. After removal of 16 duplicates, 117 unique records were screened, of which 109 were excluded as not meeting the eligibility criteria, most commonly because they did not separately report TNBC genomic data, did not concern a South Asian population, or contained no extractable genomic data. Methodological quality, appraised with the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale, was modest and uneven across the eight studies, consistent with the small and heterogeneous evidence base. The cohort and case–control studies (Rajagopal 2022 [22], Rashid 2016 [23], Gupta 2024 [24], Pachuau 2025 [25], Aravind Kumar 2018 [26], John 2025 [29]) generally performed adequately on the selection and outcome domains but were limited on comparability, as few adjusted for confounders such as age or family history; several were enriched for young or familial patients, which lowered representativeness of the exposed cohort. These two germline BRCA1 estimates with extractable denominators approximately 37% [23] and approximately 10% (6/59; [22]) are markedly divergent. Pooling only two such estimates would yield an uninterpretable summary with extreme heterogeneity and would fall short of our pre-specified five-study threshold; the values are therefore reported descriptively and not pooled.

Somatic TP53 alteration was the most frequently mutated somatic gene in the WES study of Pachuau et al. [25] in the endogamous Mizo population, which also reported a pathogenic germline BRCA1 variant (p.C1697R) in 13% of patients and frequent alterations in additional genes including RBMX, ATM, STK11, and CDKN2A [25]. The case–control WES study of Aravind Kumar et al. [26] identified MSH6 and BRIP1 variants as the principal determinants of TNBC risk in a small Indian series (8 cases versus 48 controls) [26].

Two findings warrant cautious interpretation. First, screening for the FANCM c.5101C > T variant in BRCA1/2-negative Pakistani TNBC patients did not detect the variant, indicating that this specific allele is not a major contributor in that population (Rashid et al., 2015) [28]; this is a single-variant screen rather than comprehensive FANCM analysis. Second, the report of JAK2 V617F in Pakistani TNBC (Naeem et al., 2020) [27] is a brief correspondence rather than a genomic cohort study, and the finding has been questioned in subsequent published correspondence. Because JAK2 V617F is an established driver of myeloproliferative neoplasms and is not a recognized recurrent somatic alteration in TNBC, a high tumor frequency would be biologically surprising and may reflect assay or sample-source factors. We therefore report this finding only as an isolated, contested observation and do not present it as a population prevalence.

Clinical characteristics and outcomes

Detailed and comparable clinical outcome data were sparse (Table 4). Several cohorts reported relatively young ages at diagnosis; however, several were specifically enriched for young or familial patients (for example, the Pakistani cohorts), so the low mean ages partly reflect ascertainment rather than a population-level property. The younger age structure of South Asian populations may also shift the observed age-at-diagnosis distribution downward for any cancer. Any inference of earlier population-level onset from these within-review ages should therefore be made cautiously and is not treated here as independent evidence. Discussion

This systematic review synthesizes the available genomic evidence on TNBC in South Asian populations. Across eight heterogeneous studies from India and Pakistan, the most consistent signal was a contribution of germline BRCA1 alterations, with BRCA1 predominating over BRCA2 wherever the two were distinguished [22–24, 29]. Somatic TP53 alteration was the leading somatic event in the only WES cohort that ranked somatic genes (Pachuau et al., 2025) [25], consistent with its established role as the most frequent somatic alteration in TNBC globally (Cancer Genome Atlas Network, 2012). The relatively high germline BRCA1 frequency reported in the Pakistani cohort (approximately 37%; Rashid et al., 2016) [23] exceeds the 10–20% typically reported in unselected Western TNBC series [1, 2], whereas the South Indian estimate (approximately 10%; Rajagopal et al., 2022) [22] lies within that range. The divergence between these two estimates and the cohorts' differing selection criteria reinforces why a pooled estimate would be misleading and why population- and ascertainment-aware interpretation is essential. The clinical relevance of identifying BRCA1/2 carriers is nonetheless substantial, given the established benefit of PARP inhibitors and platinum agents in this group [13, 30]. Critically, the 37% figure derives from a cohort enriched for young age (≤ 30 years) and family history drawn from 523 screened patients; it represents a high-risk, referral-based estimate and should not be read as a population-level TNBC prevalence.

Homologous recombination deficiency

Several included studies reported alterations in HRR-related genes, including BRCA1, BRCA2, BRIP1, and MSH6 [26, 29]. However, none reported validated genomic-scar or functional HRD measures (Tier 1) or partial genomic-scar measures (Tier 2); all HRR findings were panel-based gene alterations corresponding to Tier 3 proxy signals under the scheme outlined in the Methods. We therefore did not classify studies into HRD tiers or estimate a pooled HRD prevalence. The tiered scheme is presented as a recommendation to improve comparability in future studies, not as an analysis performed here. This distinction matters because broader HRD assessment through genomic-scar scores, mutational signatures, or RAD51 functional assays captures a wider “BRCAness” phenotype than gene panels alone [14, 16, 17].

Epidemiological context

Reports from South Asia describe a higher proportion of TNBC than in Western populations on the order of 25–30% versus 10–20% [18] and a tertiary-center series from Pakistan reported TNBC in 30.5% of breast cancers (Rashid et al., 2016) [23]. These figures should be interpreted cautiously: most derive from hospital- or tertiary-referral settings rather than population-based registries, ER/PR/HER2 testing quality varies, and referral and selection biases can inflate the apparent TNBC proportion. The GLOBOCAN 2020 analysis [31] documents the overall breast cancer burden but does not report TNBC subtype-specific proportions and is therefore not cited here for that figure.

Ancestry classification

None of the included studies used genetically inferred ancestry; all defined the population geographically or by self-reported ethnicity. This limits the biological specificity of population-level inferences and is precisely the gap the proposed two-tier framework is intended to address in future work [20, 21].

Clinical and therapeutic implications

The frequency of germline BRCA1/2 alterations in South Asian TNBC supports a low threshold for genetic testing and counseling, with direct implications for PARP-inhibitor and platinum therapy in carriers [13, 30]. PI3K-pathway alterations, where present, may indicate vulnerability to PI3K/AKT-directed agents under investigation in TNBC [32]. Separately, the broader treatment landscape now includes TROP2-directed ADCs (sacituzumab govitecan; datopotamab deruxtecan) and checkpoint inhibition [6, 29]; these are not biomarker-directed to the alterations reviewed here but are clinically important context for precision management.

Strengths and limitations

This review is, to our knowledge, among the first to focus specifically on TNBC genomics in South Asian populations, and it draws every included study from a verifiable, citable source. Its limitations are substantial and shape the conclusions. The evidence base comprises only eight small, heterogeneous studies, several lacking extractable denominators, and two being brief correspondence or single-variant screens. No study used genetically inferred ancestry, and none provided validated HRD measures; the two-tier ancestry framework and the tiered HRD scheme are therefore proposals for future work rather than analyses executed here. The number of studies did not meet pre-specified thresholds for pooling, so no meta-analytic estimates, heterogeneity statistics, or outcome syntheses are presented. The review was not prospectively registered, and Embase, MEDLINE-specific, and Cochrane Library searches were not performed. Germline and somatic findings were kept distinct and were not combined.

Future research directions

Future work should prioritize larger, multi-institutional South Asian TNBC cohorts with standardized sequencing, explicit germline-versus-somatic reporting, incorporation of genetically inferred ancestry, and validated HRD measures, ideally linked to standardized clinical outcomes. Such studies would allow the frameworks proposed here to be applied and would support genuine quantitative synthesis.

Conclusion

The available evidence indicates that DNA-repair pathway alterations particularly germline BRCA1 are a recurring feature of TNBC in South Asian populations, with somatic TP53 alteration prominent where assessed. The evidence base is, however, too small and heterogeneous to support pooled estimates, and several previously reported figures could not be substantiated and have been corrected. Robust, ancestry-aware, adequately powered genomic studies are needed to characterize TNBC biology in this under-represented population and to translate it into precision oncology.
