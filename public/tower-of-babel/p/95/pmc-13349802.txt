De novo antiglomerular basement membrane Goodpasture syndrome in a liver transplant recipient: balancing autoimmunity, allograft protection, and infection risk
Authors: Sanket Solanki, Saumya Sharma, Bisha Biju, Akansha Thakur, Rommel Sandhyav, Naveen Ganjoo, Sonal Asthana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Antiglomerular basement membrane (anti-GBM) disease causes rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis and diffuse alveolar hemorrhage (DAH) through autoantibodies directed against the α3 chain of type IV collagen. Evidence guiding adaptation of standard induction therapy (plasmapheresis, high-dose glucocorticoids, cyclophosphamide) for nonrenal solid organ transplant recipients remains scarce. A 55-year-old man, 9 years post-deceased donor liver transplantation for nonalcoholic steatohepatitis and maintained on low-dose tacrolimus, presented with pulmonary renal syndrome, severe metabolic acidosis, and high-titer anti-GBM antibodies. High-resolution computed tomography demonstrated bilateral ground-glass opacities consistent with DAH. He was treated with plasma exchange (seven sessions) and high-dose corticosteroids. Tacrolimus was discontinued, and maintenance immunosuppression was transitioned to mycophenolate mofetil with low-dose prednisone. Pulmonary hemorrhage resolved and anti-GBM titers declined to near the assay threshold; however, kidney function did not recover, and he remained dependent on hemodialysis. Four months later, he died of multidrug-resistant Acinetobacter pneumonia complicated by septic shock. To our knowledge, this represents the first reported case of de novo anti-GBM disease following liver transplantation. De novo Goodpasture syndrome can occur despite chronic immunosuppression in liver transplant recipients. Prompt recognition, individualized immunomodulation, and meticulous infection risk management are critical to controlling autoimmunity while preserving liver allograft function.

INTRODUCTION

Goodpasture syndrome, also termed antiglomerular basement membrane (anti-GBM) disease, is an autoimmune small-vessel vasculitis caused by immunoglobulin G (IgG) autoantibodies targeting the α3(IV) collagen NC1 domain within glomerular and alveolar basement membranes. It classically presents with rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis and diffuse alveolar hemorrhage (DAH) [1,2]. Standard management combines early therapeutic plasma exchange (PLEX), high-dose glucocorticoids, and cyclophosphamide, with outcomes strongly influenced by time to treatment initiation and by dialysis dependence at presentation [3,4]. In transplant recipients, both diagnosis and treatment are further complicated by coagulopathy, baseline immunosuppression, heightened infectious risk, and the need to preserve allograft function.

The transplant literature on anti-GBM disease is limited and largely focused on kidney allograft recipients transplanted for anti-GBM disease who subsequently experience recurrence. Contemporary cohorts report recurrence rates of approximately 2% to 4% when transplantation is deferred until antibody negativity; when recurrence occurs, graft loss is common [5]. In contrast, de novo anti-GBM disease following nonrenal solid organ transplantation remains essentially uncharacterized [6]. In a structured search of MEDLINE, Embase, and Web of Science (from inception through September 2025) using the terms “anti-GBM/Goodpasture” and “transplant/transplantation/allograft,” we identified no reported cases in liver allograft recipients. This case therefore addresses a practical and previously unexamined clinical question: how to adapt anti-GBM induction therapy in a coagulopathic, chronically immunosuppressed patient while simultaneously safeguarding a nonrenal allograft.

CASE REPORT

Written informed consent for publication including clinical details and images was obtained from the patient's next of kin.

A 55-year-old male with well-controlled type 2 diabetes mellitus and essential hypertension underwent deceased donor liver transplantation in 2015 for end-stage liver disease secondary to nonalcoholic steatohepatitis. His posttransplant course was uncomplicated, and he remained on stable long-term immunosuppression with low-dose tacrolimus (target trough 3–5 ng/mL). He maintained stable liver graft function until late 2022, after which he was lost to follow-up and discontinued routine immunosuppression monitoring. This lapse in care likely resulted in a period of relative underimmunosuppression, with tacrolimus underexposure potentially permitting the development of de novo anti-GBM autoimmunity.

In December 2024, he presented with a 2-week history of low-grade fever, dry cough, unintentional 5-kg weight loss, and fatigue. Physical examination revealed pallor, mild icterus, and bilateral basilar crackles; oxygen saturation was 97% on 2 L/min of nasal oxygen. Initial laboratory evaluation demonstrated normocytic anemia (hemoglobin 7.7 g/dL), acute kidney injury with a creatinine of 5.24 mg/dL (prior baseline 1.1 mg/dL), severe metabolic acidosis (pH 7.19, bicarbonate 9.9 mmol/L), and coagulopathy (international normalized ratio 1.8). Liver biochemical tests showed a modest cholestatic pattern, with aspartate aminotransferase 148 U/L (approximately 3.7× upper limit of normal), alanine aminotransferase 152 U/L (approximately 2.7× upper limit of normal), and total bilirubin 2.97 mg/dL (direct 2.93 mg/dL). Within 48 hours, he developed blood-tinged sputum and worsening dyspnea. High-resolution computed tomography of the chest revealed bilateral ground-glass opacities with interstitial septal thickening, findings consistent with DAH (Fig. 1).

Serologic testing demonstrated strong anti-GBM reactivity by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, reported as exceeding the assay’s upper limit of quantification (Table 1). Other autoimmune serologies, including perinuclear antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody / myeloperoxidase (p-ANCA/MPO), cytoplasmic antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody / proteinase 3 (c-ANCA/PR3), antinuclear antibody, and anti-double-stranded DNA, were negative. Urinalysis revealed hematuria and heavy proteinuria, with a protein-to-creatinine ratio of 5.89 mg/mg. Renal biopsy was deferred because of thrombocytopenia (platelet count 78,000/µL, declining to 56,000/µL), coagulopathy, and ultrasonographic evidence of bilaterally small, echogenic kidneys with poor corticomedullary differentiation, suggestive of chronic parenchymal disease.

Given the high clinical probability of anti-GBM pulmonary renal syndrome and the strongly positive serologic findings, treatment was initiated promptly on hospital day 3. Therapy consisted of seven PLEX sessions over 9 days using limited-volume exchanges with fresh frozen plasma replacement, together with intravenous methylprednisolone 500 mg daily for 3 days (with hydrocortisone 100 mg administered prior to PLEX), followed by an oral prednisone taper. Hemodialysis was subsequently initiated for uremia and refractory metabolic acidosis. Cyclophosphamide was withheld because of cytopenias and concerns regarding infection risk. Tacrolimus was discontinued, and maintenance immunosuppression was continued with mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) 500 mg twice daily and prednisone 10 mg/day to protect the liver allograft. Under this regimen, anti-GBM antibody levels declined to near the assay threshold and hemoptysis resolved; however, renal function did not recover, with urine output remaining below 100 mL/day and creatinine plateauing at 5 to 6 mg/dL. Liver enzymes normalized, and Doppler ultrasonography demonstrated no evidence of allograft rejection.

Four months later, the patient developed severe pneumonia due to multidrug-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii, complicated by acute respiratory distress syndrome. Despite maximal intensive care support, he died of septic shock in May 2025.

DISCUSSION

This case demonstrates that standard anti-GBM management principles can be adapted to a liver transplant recipient when therapy is carefully calibrated to bleeding risk, infectious vulnerability, and allograft protection. Three elements merit particular emphasis: (1) prompt diagnosis and treatment initiation, (2) transplant-adapted induction and immunosuppression modification, and (3) the development of a pragmatic clinical framework.

The patient presented with a classic pulmonary renal syndrome, characterized by hemoptysis with ground-glass opacities, rapidly progressive renal failure, and active urine sediment, together with strong anti-GBM serology, while ANCA and other autoantibodies were negative. In this setting, the absence of tissue confirmation (renal biopsy or bronchoscopy) should not delay therapy when the procedural risk is prohibitive. We therefore adopted a high-probability, treatment-first approach based on three converging factors: (1) a compatible clinical syndrome, (2) a strong anti-GBM serologic signal, and (3) a rapid clinicoradiologic response to antibody removal and corticosteroid therapy. Notably, the presence of bilaterally small, echogenic kidneys indicates chronic structural kidney disease that likely predated presentation; superimposed anti-GBM activity probably accelerated this process to end-stage renal disease, thereby explaining pulmonary remission with persistent dialysis dependence.

The Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) and American Society for Apheresis (ASFA) recommendations for induction therapy emphasize daily PLEX, high-dose glucocorticoids, and cyclophosphamide, with rituximab reserved as an alternative or adjunct in cases of cytopenias, infection risk, or inadequate response to alkylating agents [7,8]. In this coagulopathic, thrombocytopenic patient with suspected DAH, we prioritized limited-volume PLEXs with fresh frozen plasma replacement, thereby balancing hemorrhagic risk against the need for rapid antibody removal [9,10]. The observed decline in anti-GBM titers and cessation of hemoptysis support the clinical utility of this approach in similarly high-risk patients. Cyclophosphamide was withheld for safety reasons; had pulmonary control not been achieved within 48 to 72 hours, rituximab would have been favored as a cytotoxic-sparing alternative [11]. Discontinuation of tacrolimus resulted in MMF-prednisone–dominant maintenance immunosuppression for the liver allograft, and serial biochemical improvement together with a normal Doppler ultrasound supported the decision to withhold escalation of antirejection therapy during the autoimmune flare.

Most published anti-GBM reports involve kidney allograft recipients experiencing disease recurrence. However, clinical phenotypes under maintenance immunosuppression can be atypical, including seronegative relapse [12] or isolated pulmonary hemorrhage [13]. Early antibody removal and nondialysis-dependent presentation are associated with improved renal outcomes, yet recurrence frequently results in graft loss. Outside the context of kidney transplantation and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation [14], data are virtually absent. Our structured literature search identified no prior reports of de novo anti-GBM disease following liver transplantation. This case therefore fills a practical gap by describing a transplant-adapted strategy—plasma-supported PLEX, cytotoxic-sparing induction, and calcineurin inhibitor withdrawal with MMF-steroid maintenance [15]—that achieved rapid pulmonary control and preserved liver allograft function despite irreversible kidney injury. These findings reinforce the transplant principle of aggressively treating life-threatening autoimmunity while concurrently recalibrating immunosuppression to safeguard the allograft.

Practical framework is as follows: (1) do not delay therapy when the clinical syndrome is evident and anti-GBM antibody titers are high, particularly when invasive diagnostics are unsafe. Initiate PLEX and intravenous corticosteroids immediately, as early intervention is critical. (2) Tailor plasmapheresis to bleeding risk. Use fresh frozen plasma for volume replacement and consider limited-volume exchanges in the setting of coagulopathy or DAH to reduce hemorrhagic complications while maintaining effective autoantibody removal. (3) Choose adjunct immunosuppression on a case-by-case basis. Cyclophosphamide may be used when blood counts are adequate and infection risk is low; otherwise, cytotoxic therapy should be deferred, with planned escalation to rituximab if pulmonary hemorrhage remains uncontrolled at 48 to 72 hours or if anti-GBM titers fail to decline. This stepwise approach minimizes unnecessary toxicity while ensuring timely escalation when needed. (4) Rebalance transplant immunosuppression if end-stage renal disease develops. Withdraw calcineurin inhibitors to eliminate nephrotoxic exposure and maintain liver allograft protection with MMF plus prednisone, with close monitoring of liver enzymes and graft Doppler ultrasonography, and consideration of donor-specific antibody surveillance or biopsy when feasible. (5) Implement rigorous infection prevention. Provide Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia and cytomegalovirus prophylaxis, enforce catheter- and ventilator-associated infection precautions, and promptly taper intensified immunosuppression once autoimmune control is achieved to reduce ongoing infectious risk [16,17].

In this liver transplant recipient with probable de novo anti-GBM Goodpasture syndrome, early plasmapheresis combined with high-dose corticosteroids achieved rapid pulmonary stabilization, while transition to an MMF-prednisone regimen following calcineurin inhibitor withdrawal preserved liver allograft function. Renal recovery was not achieved, likely reflecting advanced chronic kidney damage at presentation. This case underscores that Goodpasture syndrome can develop despite chronic posttransplant immunosuppression, thereby broadening the differential diagnosis of pulmonary renal syndrome in nonrenal solid organ transplant recipients. It also offers an actionable management strategy for transplant teams confronting this rare, life-threatening autoimmune complication, emphasizing prompt therapy, individualized immunosuppressive adjustment, and vigilant infection prophylaxis.
