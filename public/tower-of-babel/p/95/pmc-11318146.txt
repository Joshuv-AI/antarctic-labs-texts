Material needs security and cardiovascular risk factors in rural South Africa
Authors: Rebekah J Walker, Caitlin Magro, Rabia Amjad, Laura C Hawks, Sandra Iregbu, Leonard E Egede
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe prevalence of cardiovascular disease is burgeoning in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). In sub-Saharan Africa, the prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors is increasing, though rates of CVD diagnosis and management remain low. Awareness of the influence of social determinants of health (SDOH) on cardiovascular outcomes is growing, however, most work focuses on high-income countries. Material needs security is a measure of SDOH that may be particularly relevant for LMICs. This study investigated the relationship between material needs security and cardiovascular risk in older adults living in South Africa.MethodsThe analysis included 5059 respondents age ≥ 40 in the Health and Aging in Africa survey, an observational cohort study administered in 2014 in Mpumalanga Province, South Africa. Linear regression models tested the association between material needs and eight cardiovascular risk factors (waist-to-hip ratio, body mass index, blood pressure, glucose, cholesterol, LDL, and triglycerides). Adjusted linear regression models controlled for sociodemographic confounders.ResultsThere were significant adjusted associations found between increased material needs security and four cardiovascular risk factors, including waist-to-hip ratio (β = 0.001; 95% CI [0.00002,0.002]), BMI (β = 0.19; 95%CI=[0.14,0.24]), glucose (β = 0.46; 95%CI=[0.02,0.90]), and triglycerides (β = 0.26; 95%CI=[0.02,0.49]).ConclusionIncreased material needs security was associated with significantly increased cardiovascular risk in older adults in rural South Africa. These findings can inform the approach to treatment and management of cardiovascular disease in South Africa and similar LMICs. Future investigations should evaluate the implementation and efficacy of interventions that recognize the role of material needs security in cardiovascular risk.Keywords: Material needs security, Cardiovascular disease, Non-communicable disease, South Africa

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the most common non-communicable disease (NCD)-related cause of death in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), accounting for 13% of deaths annually [1]. As the population ages, access to treatment for infectious diseases increases, and risk factors for NCDs rise, the region is experiencing an epidemiologic shift from primarily communicable disease profiles to chronic and NCD disease profiles [2–6]. However, diagnosis of CVD and treatment rates remain low [7–9]. Significant morbidity and mortality associated with CVD in SSA has been attributed to reduced access to healthcare, fewer available treatment options, an overall lack of public awareness, and changes in lifestyle [6, 10, 11]. The role of social determinants of health as emerging but understudied factors in the development and outcomes of NCDs in SSA is a growing area of research [12, 13].

Evidence highlights the role of social determinants of health, including poverty and material needs security, as factors that can exacerbate CVD risk [6, 14]. Material needs security is defined as access to basic needs necessary to maintain health and manage illness [15] Examples of material needs include access to housing, utilities, telecommunications, transportation, food, and medication [15]. Studies examining access to material needs and health outcomes found mixed results, especially between different regional and economic settings [16]. In the U.S., a high-income country, studies regarding material needs security and emergency department use indicated lower material needs security was associated with higher NCD risk [17, 18]. In contrast, diabetes was associated with higher material needs security in Lebanon [15] Similarly, data following the US housing crisis collected in 2013 did not indicate a relationship between hypertension and housing instability [19], though in India, housing insecurity was found to be an independent risk factor for hypertension development. [20]. Studies in Nigeria demonstrated higher rates of uncontrolled hypertension, a higher gradation of hypertension, and higher rates of obesity among individuals with low wealth compared to individuals with high wealth [21–23]. Other research in low- and middle-income countries indicated that high wealth and education level can serve as protective factors against cardiovascular risk factors like obesity [22] or are compounded by the effects of gender politics in the region [24]. In addition, the role of access to digital technologies and internet connectivity as material needs factors with potential effects on health outcomes are ongoing areas of study within SSA [25, 26]. Therefore, it is important to identify how material needs security may be affecting cardiovascular risk factors in different settings so that preventative interventions may be implemented [15].

South Africa is a middle-income country with the 6th largest population in Africa [27]. In South Africa, life expectancy, cardiovascular risk factors, and rates of NCDs are all simultaneously increasing [2, 4, 5]. Per the 2022 World Health Organization Noncommunicable Disease Progress Monitor, 51% of deaths in South Africa in 2022 were due to NCDs, the majority of which were related to CVD [28]. Studies indicate that 14.8% of South Africans will be over the age of 60 by 2050, increasing age related risk for NCDs [2, 3]. In addition, risk factors continue to increase, with as much as two-third of the population older than 50 having dyslipidemia, and tobacco use estimated at 37% among South African men and 8% among women [2, 3, 29, 30]. A study of hypertension care cascades performance among adults in a rural South African community found gaps in diagnosis, treatment, and control of hypertension, with the most gaps between those treated and those who achieved blood pressure control. [31] There are efforts within the South African government and health systems to reduce NCD risk factors, including programs to inform consumers of risks associated with tobacco, alcohol, and high salt diets [27, 28]. In addition, South Africa’s national strategic plan for preventing and controlling non-communicable diseases (NCDs) is a comprehensive framework that prioritizes prevention and control of NCDs, promotes health and wellness across the life course, ensures integrated services for people with NCDs, supports national capacity for high-quality research, and monitors NCD trends and determinants to evaluate progress [32]. This plan addresses risk factors and uses a comprehensive care cascade management system that promotes early detection, diagnosis, treatment, and control of NCDs to ensure an effective and efficient progression through different stages of care [32]. South African policies instituted to address the increasing burden of NCDs, including CVD, by targeting the food environment include legislation and regulations aimed towards reducing trans-fatty acids and salts in processed food, a ban on junk food advertisements during key television programs, taxation of sugar-sweetened beverages, and tax exemption for healthier food choices [32, 33]. However, government spending on health is limited and these programs have not been fully implemented [27, 28, 34] As a result, rates of NCD diagnosis and treatment remain low [27, 28, 34].

Within South Africa, there are areas of documented material needs insecurity which may affect NCD risk [35, 36]. Reports indicate limited access in some areas to electricity, water, and tarred roads [35, 36] Though significant efforts have been made to increase internet capabilities and connectivity in South Africa, access still remains low compared to other areas [25]. For example, in 2022, 21% of households still lacked at-home internet access. [37] Additionally, there are significant disparities in internet access and computer ownership based on region, race, sex, and socioeconomic class [25, 38, 39]. Rural communities, Black-led, and female-led households are more likely to lack internet access or rely solely on mobile devices for internet use at-home compared to urban residents, White-led, and male-led households [38]. Unemployment rates within South Africa are high and the remittance economy is often a primary source of income for many households [3]. Additionally, in the years since the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, food insecurity has drastically increased compared to pre-pandemic levels [40]. The impact of these factors on cardiovascular risk and the role of material needs security on cardiovascular disease in SSA are important questions as the South African population ages and changes.

The objective of this study was to evaluate the role of material needs security on cardiovascular risk. Material needs included household ownership of goods, including, but not limited to, refrigerators, ovens, cell phones, computers, and toilet facilities. Cardiovascular risk was defined by cardiovascular outcomes, including waist-to-hip ratio, biomarkers, body mass index, and blood pressure. It is hypothesized that material needs security will be associated with greater cardiovascular risk factors and CVD development.

Methods

Data source

The analysis was performed on “A Longitudinal Study of an INDEPTH Community in South Africa” (HAALSI) data [41, 42]. The data was collected with the Harvard Center for Population and Development Studies partnership and the University of the Witwatersrand Department of MRC/Wits Rural Public Health and Health Transitions Research Unit (Agincourt). Following approval by an ethical review board, local fieldworkers collected data using computer assisted personal interviews in the local language [41, 42]. The full HAALSI data was collected on 5,059 individuals aged ≥ 40 over three waves. Wave one was collected in 2013 with a response rate of 86% [41, 42]. Data for the analysis was obtained from “Harvard Dataverse” [43] and information regarding details of data collection by the research team is included in the description of the cohort [3]. The analysis was performed on 5,059 individuals who completed wave 1 of the study.

Outcomes

Eight cardiovascular (CVD) risk factors were analyzed as outcomes:

Waist-to-hip ratio: The calculation of the waist-to-hip ratio involved obtaining measurements for both the waist and hip. These measurements were taken while participants were in a standing position and recorded in centimeters [3]. The waist measurement was divided by the hip measurement to calculate the waist-to-hip ratio.

Biomarkers: Glucose, cholesterol, triglyceride, and LDL were included in the analysis as significant biomarkers of CVD risk factors. The biomarkers readings were taken in mmol/l. Blood droplets were collected by finger pricking for use in point of care measurement [3].

Body Mass Index: BMI was included as one of the CVD risk factors. BMI was calculated by HAALSI and included in the dataset [3].

Blood Pressure: Mean systolic blood pressure and mean diastolic blood pressure were included as an outcome. After 5 min of rest, three blood pressure readings were taken, with each reading 2 min apart. Mean systolic and diastolic blood pressure were calculated by averaging second and third readings, as stated in HAALSI [3].

Independent variable

Material needs security was calculated by counting the number of positive responses given to twenty-four questions regarding ownership of specific items. Questions asked about whether the household owned: refrigerator, washing machine, sewing machine, tube TV, flat screen TV, DVD/video player, satellite dish, radio, computer/laptop, stereo system, camera/video system, air conditioning, cell phone, phone, cement mixer/grinder, clock/watch, pressure cooker, sofa, bed, cot, table, electric fan, stove, toilet facilities. Since no individual indicated ownership of all 24 material needs security items, the continuous material needs security variable ranged from 0 to 19 with higher numbers indicating higher material needs security. A material needs quartile variable was then created based on the distribution of the continuous variable.

Covariates

Age (40–49, 50–59, 60–69, 70–79, 80+), sex (male, female), education (no formal education, some primary (1–7 years), some secondary (8–11 years), secondary or more (12 + years)), marital status (never married, separated/divorced, widowed, currently married) and employment status(employed part or full time, not working, homemaker) were included in the adjusted models as covariates.

Statistical analysis

Demographic characteristics were summarized with means and standard deviation for continuous variables and count and percentages for categorical variables. Each CVD risk factor was summarized with mean and standard deviation. First, unadjusted and adjusted linear regression was performed to investigate the association between each CVD risk factor and material needs security as a continuous variable. Each CVD risk factor outcome was run as a separate model. After unadjusted analyses, each model was adjusted for age, sex, education, marital status, and employment status. Second, unadjusted, and adjusted models were run using the material needs quartile variable as the primary independent variable. Again, each CVD risk factor outcome was run as a separate model. Following the model estimation, marginal means were obtained for each CVD risk factor by quartile of material needs security. Adjusted models were then run, with marginal means obtained for each CVD risk factor by quartile of material needs security adjusted for age, sex, education, marital status, and employment status. Significance of the results was based on p-value < 0.05. All analyses were conducted in SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) and Stata version 15 (Stata Corp, College Station, TX).

A significant association was identified between BMI and material needs security. BMI in the second, third, and fourth quartiles (27.09 (p < 0.0001), 28.14 (p < 0.0001), 28.98 (p < 0.0001), respectively) was significantly higher than that in the lowest quartile (25.42). Even after adjusting for covariates, these associations remained significant. The adjusted margin for the waist-to-hip ratio in the upper quartile (0.92) was significantly higher than that in the lowest quartile (0.91) (p < 0.01). Glucose and triglyceride levels also remained significantly elevated in the highest quartile compared to the lowest quartile of material needs security. Glucose level in the highest quartile (125.24 mg/dl) was significantly increased compared to the lowest quartile (118.38 mg/dl) (p < 0.01), and triglyceride level in the highest quartile (34.65 mg/dl) was significantly elevated compared to the lowest quartile (30.61 mg/dl) (p < 0.01). Additionally, BMI in the second, third, and fourth quartiles (27.13 (p < 0.0001), 27.18 (p < 0.0001), 28.31 (p < 0.0001), respectively) was significantly increased compared to the lowest quartile (26.13).

Discussion

Using a sample of adults aged ≥ 40 from rural South Africa, this study demonstrated an association between higher material needs security and significant increases in body mass index (BMI), glucose, triglycerides, and waist-to-hip ratio. There were no significant relationships with total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein, or blood pressure. For glucose, triglycerides, and waist-to-hip ratio, significant differences existed between the lowest and highest material needs security quartile. For BMI, significant differences existed between all quartiles compared to the lowest quartile. In line with our hypothesis and prior work in sub-Saharan Africa, this study suggests that increased material needs security is associated with increased cardiovascular risk in South Africa.

This manuscript adds to the current literature by offering greater insight into the role of material needs security on NCDs, especially in the setting of older adults in rural communities in SSA. In rural South Africa, studies have investigated NCDs and multimorbidity, particularly in the setting of ongoing HIV infection and HIV management, [2, 44, 45]; however, these studies did not investigate the role of material needs security in the development of NCD risk factors. Previous research on material needs security and diabetes conducted in Lebanon indicated a relationship between increased material needs security and development of diabetes [15]. Prior research in African countries also indicated high socioeconomic status is associated with greater prevalence of NCD risk factors, including hypertension, diabetes, and obesity [22, 23, 46]. By incorporating multiple risk factors in this analysis and investigating the pattern of differences across quartiles of material needs security, this study can assist in identifying areas for targeted future work.

First, BMI showed significant differences across all quartiles, however, BMI of 25 in quartile 1, 27 in quartile 2, and 28 in quartile 3 all remain in the overweight categorization of BMI. Average BMI for the highest quartile of material needs was 29, which is closer to an obese categorization. From a risk standpoint, obesity is a significant risk factor for both CVD and type 2 diabetes and triggers the development of insulin resistance, which is responsible for abnormal glucose metabolism. [47] Combined with the significant difference in waist-to-hip ratio and glucose levels between individuals at the lowest and highest quartiles, these findings suggest focusing on culturally relevant lifestyle changes that can improve BMI, waist-to-hip ratio, and glucose levels for individuals living with high material needs security may mitigate some of the increased CVD risk for this group. In rural South Africa, interventions to address obesity may be most effective if focused upon targeting sedentarism in leisure time. Lower socioeconomic communities typically have higher rates of physical activity compared to their higher socioeconomic counterparts due to increased physical labor associated with agricultural employment [48]. However, across socioeconomic classes, television time and sedentary leisure practices have increased in South Africa [48]. Physical activity and sport as recreation is particularly limited in rural South Africa due to lack of structural facilities for sport and limited amenities like accessible tapped water, which may make these communities at increased risk for obesity [48]. This is especially concerning for many women in rural South Africa, for whom cultural gender roles limit their ability to participate in sport [49]. Second, there was a statistically significant difference in triglycerides between the lowest and highest quartiles, again with the highest quartiles having higher levels. However, the levels were clinically low (35.5 compared to the average value of < 150 mg/dl in adults) and are not likely to increase cardiovascular risk. In addition, prior studies found low triglyceride levels in people of African descent, and that low triglyceride levels are poor markers of cardiovascular risk in people of African descent [50–54].

The findings of this study contrast with what exists in developed nations where high socioeconomic status correlates with lower cardiovascular risk [55–57]. South Africa is a middle-income country with a developing economy, possibly undergoing the 3rd stage of epidemiological transition, described as the age of “degenerative and man-made diseases” [58]. This stage is characterized by a change in lifestyle, especially among middle and high-income groups [58]. This means a preference for processed foods, often high in fat and simple sugars, low physical activity, and a sedentary lifestyle, translating to elevated blood pressure, blood sugar, lipids, and body weight [58]. Based on the UN Food and Agriculture Organization assessment of dietary consumption from the 1960s to 2001, rural South Africans consumed less fat and protein and more carbohydrates in accordance with a more traditional diet; whereas urban individuals demonstrated a greater nutrition transition associated with Westernized diets [59]. By 2013, diets in rural South Africa showed signs of the nutrition transition, as more women and children reported eating highly processed, high sugar, high fat, and high salt content meals [60]. Recent studies also found increasing presence of sugar-sweetened beverages and ultra-processed foods in both urban and rural communities throughout South Africa in the post-COVID era [61]. The findings of this study underline the need for interventions geared toward preventing cardiovascular diseases through health promotion, proactive health screenings, early detection, and treatment, and suggest a particularly important role of highlighting changes in BMI, waist-to-hip ratio, and glucose levels.

With the current shift in nutrition and other lifestyle behaviors that come with enhanced material needs security, there is a need to increase education on cardiovascular risk factors and lifestyle factors that promote cardiovascular health. This could be achieved through diverse communication strategies tailored for different cultural and demographic groups using the mass media and community agencies, community health workers, health care professionals, peers, and volunteers in different settings [62]. In addition to ongoing policy efforts, [32, 33] the fight against NCDs in South Africa can be further strengthened with more community and individual-level interventions. These interventions, such as health promotion through the mass media, worksites, and school health education programs, social marketing, the introduction of guidelines for treatment and follow-up for high-risk individuals, and training of healthcare providers, have shown to significantly improve population knowledge of CVD risk factors and influence positive lifestyle behaviors such as dietary practices, physical activity, smoking cessation, and alcohol use in low and middle-income countries [63–65]. The American Heart Association’s recommendation to create applications and online tools for people to evaluate their cardiovascular risks and access resources to improve their cardiovascular health [61] aligns with the 2nd South African National Strategic Plan by establishing and supporting the capacity of individuals to adopt healthy behaviors [32]. In this study, more than half of the sample owned smartphones and could access online resources, suggesting that this may be an important consideration for interventions delivered in South Africa. It is crucial that healthcare systems dedicate more resources to early identification, treatment, and control of CVDs by paying closer attention to the effects of material needs security.

The HAALSI dataset represents individuals over 40 in the Agincourt sub-district in the Mpumalanga province in rural South Africa. It offers a broad range of social, economic, and health backgrounds in this region, however, there are some limitations worth noting.

First, this is not a nationally representative sample and is limited in evaluating only adults in rural populations, excluding individuals younger than 40. Similarly, the Agincourt sub-district may be affected by demographic and cultural practices specific to the region and differ from other non-rural areas in South Africa or other LMICs. Second, this analysis is based on one time point and therefore is cross-sectional and cannot speak to causality. Finally, the last wave of data collected for HAALSI was in 2020. Therefore, this dataset does not account for the physical and social implications of the COVID-19 pandemic on LMICs or the ongoing effects of global economic changes.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated a significant relationship between increased material needs security and cardiovascular risk, specifically BMI, glucose, triglycerides, and waist-to-hip ratio. Increased awareness of CVD and its relationship to material needs security may promote the development of upstream preventative interventions, enabling low- and middle-income countries to prepare for and adapt to the evolving landscapes of NCDs.
