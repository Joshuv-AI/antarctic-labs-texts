Health-related quality of life and its associated factors among high school students with myopia in China
Authors: Xiaoyan Yang, Shitong Xie, Jing Wang, Chang Luo, Mengdi Li, Tianqi Hong, Jie Zhang, Wei Qi, Lihua Li, Jing Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the health-related quality of life (HRQoL) of Chinese high school students with myopia and to investigate its potential associate factors.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted in seven high schools across three representative regions in China. Students were invited to participate in an eye examination and complete an online survey. The HRQoL was measured by using the Child Health Utility Nine-Dimension (CHU-9D) and 25-items National Eye Institute Visual Functioning Questionnaire (NEI-VFQ-25). Sociodemographic characteristics and vision-related characteristics were also collected. Descriptive statistics were performed on respondents’ characteristics and one-way analysis of variance was used to compare HRQoL among subgroups. Multivariable linear regression analyses were performed to identify contributing factors to HRQoL.ResultsA total of 2,198 students with myopia (53.1% male, age was 16.7 ± 0.8 years) were included in this study. The utility value of CHU-9D was 0.851 ± 0.160 (mean ± SD), the score of NEI-VFQ-25 was 91.112 ± 9.619. Higher NEI-VFQ-25 scores correspond with higher CHU-9D utility values. Regression analyses showed that the CHU-9D utility values were higher in younger students, males, those who adopted normal sitting position, those who spent more than 10 h outdoor-activities per week, and those who wore frame glasses. The NEI-VFQ-25 scores were higher in older students, females, those who adopted normal sitting position, and those who did not wear glasses.ConclusionsMyopia has a non-negligible impact on the HRQoL among Chinese high school students. Age, sex, sitting posture, time of outdoor-activities, and glasses type were deemed as the associated factors of HRQoL.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24587-y.Keywords: Myopia, High school students, Health-related quality of life, NEI-VFQ-25, CHU-9D

Background

Myopia has emerged as a major public health issue in the world [1]. By 2050, it is projected that 49.8% of the global population will have myopia and 9.8% will have high myopia [2]. Studies have shown that East Asians have the highest prevalence of myopia, which is strongly correlated with age [3]. According to National Disease Control and Prevention Administration, the prevalence of myopia among children and adolescents in China was 51.9% in 2022 [4]. Myopia significantly increases the risk of severe vision loss, which can create a significant medical and financial burden for patients [5–8]. Myopia, especially high myopia, can also lead to associated comorbidities, such as retinal detachment, subretinal neovascularization, cataracts, and glaucoma [5, 9]. The impact of myopia and its comorbidities on daily activities, study/work, and social functioning can lead to a significant decline in health-related quality of life (HRQoL) [10].

One of the most commonly used methods for measuring HRQoL is generic preference-based measures (GPBMs), which usually covers the most important dimensions for describing health. The EQ-5D and Short Form Six-Dimension (SF-6D) for adults, and the EQ-5D-Y and Child Health Utility Nine-Dimension (CHU-9D) for children are the widely used GPBMs around the world. While the sensitivity of GPBMs in describing specific diseases such as vision-related disease is suboptimal due to the lack of relevant dimensions. The 25-items National Eye Institute Visual Functioning Questionnaire (NEI-VFQ-25) is regarded as a valuable tool for assessing patients with a diverse spectrum of eye diseases and vision impairments, and it has been validated in a broad range of impairments and diagnoses [11]. Therefore, combining GPBMs with disease-specific instruments like the NEI-VFQ-25 is essential for achieving a comprehensive evaluation of HRQoL.

In 2022, the myopia prevalence in Chinese students reached 81.2% in high schools, significantly higher than primary (36.7%) and middle schools (71.4%) [4]. This high prevalence, coupled with academic burden, highlight the urgency to understand HRQoL among high school students with myopia. Previous studies mainly focused on primary or middle school students, revealing that severe myopia correlates with poorer HRQoL, and increases mental health risks in both children and parents [12–14]. Few studies involving high school students have found that high school students have significantly poorer HRQoL compared to those in middle and primary school [15, 16]. While these studies were single-center studies lacking representativeness [15, 16], or only used generic measures, which have limited sensitivity in measuring vision-related dimensions [17, 18]. Among them, few studies explored the associate factors of HRQoL in adolescents with myopia, suggesting factors like glasses use and family environment influence HRQoL [15, 19].

The objective of this study was to evaluate the HRQoL of Chinese high school students with myopia using both generic and vision-specific HRQoL measures, CHU9D and NEI-VFQ-25, and to investigate potential associate factors that may influence HRQoL.

Methods

This study has been reviewed and approved by the Ethical Review Committee in Tianjin Eye Hospital (No. KY-2023025). Informed consent was obtained from all study respondents before the interview.

Study population

This cross-sectional study was conducted in three representative regions of North China, East China, and Northwest China between April and December 2023. The study encompassed students in high schools in the three regions that had consented to participate, following the agreement of local governments and school authorities. The inclusion criteria of study population were as follows: (1) enrolled in high school; (2) diagnosed with myopia; (3) clearly aware, with certain reading and comprehension skills; (4) voluntarily participated in this study; and (5) gave informed consent. According to the Clinical Practice Guideline of Integrative Chinese and Western Medicine for Myopia in Children [20], myopia is defined as the post-cycloplegic spherical equivalent (SE) (Note: SE = spherical power + 1/2 cylindrical power) ≤ −0.50 D, and can be categorized into preclinical myopia (−0.50 D < SE ≤ + 0.75 D, low myopia (−6.00 D < SE ≤ −0.50 D), and high myopia (SE ≤ −6.00 D).

Data collection

Data was collected via an online interview. Following the requisite endorsement by the agreement of local governments and school authorities, the QR code for the online interview will be transmitted to the parent group by the class teacher. The students will then complete the requisite tasks at home.

The questionnaire comprised three parts. The first part contained questions on socio-demographic characteristics such as sex, age, ethnicity, grade, place of residence, type of glasses worn, sitting position, and weekly time spent outdoors. The second part was vision-related characteristics, including naked-eye visual acuity and corrected visual acuity with their own glasses in the distance, refractive power which was measured and recorded by optometrists. The third part was the CHU-9D and NEI-VFQ-25.

Examinations of vision-related characteristics

All the students present on the screening day were invited to participate in an eye examination involved visual acuity measurements using the Snellen five-point visual acuity chart and noncycloplegic autorefraction (Tianle RM9000, Ningbo Ming Sing Optical R&D Co, Ltd, Zhejiang, China) for both eyes. A systematic review and meta-analysis had shown that for children over 12 years old, there is no significant difference between non-cycloplegic refraction and cycloplegic refraction [21]. Visual acuity tests start with uncorrected vision and then corrected vision with their own glasses. Students were asked to remove their glasses before the examination when examining the uncorrected visual acuity. For refractive tests, each eye was measured three times and the average was taken. All examinations were conducted first on the right eye and then on the left eye. During the examination process, 5% of respondents were randomly sampled for re-examination. If the re-test error rate exceeds 5%, the team leader would convene a meeting promptly to analyze the causes and implement corrective measures. Indicators exceeding permissible error ranges must undergo re-examination and correction. If the error rate exceeds 10%, all examination data collected that day would be considered invalid and require full re-examination. Otherwise, all acquired data would be excluded from statistical analysis.

Measures of HRQoL

The CHU-9D is a generic, pediatric preference-based instrument for measuring HRQoL of children aged 7–17 years old across 9 dimensions: worried, sad, pain, tired, annoyed, school, sleep, daily routine and activities. Each dimension has five levels of response, which generates 1,953,125 health states. The instrument requires self-response by the child based on a recall period of either today or last night. CHU-9D was developed based on qualitative interviews with 7–11 years old school children in Sheffield, UK, rather than adapting an existing adult instrument. As a result, it is considered to have better content validity [22]. The Chinese version of CHU-9D and the Chinese-specific scoring algorithm of CHU-9D were used in this study [23, 24]. The theoretical index score ranges from 0.0563 to 0.9998 (0 = being dead, 1 = full health).

The NEI-VFQ-25 is a questionnaire developed to measure self-reported vision-targeted health status aspects that are most important for patients with chronic eye disease. It was developed from an item reduction analysis of the 51-item National Eye Institute Visual Function Questionnaire (NEI-VFQ-51) [25, 26]. The NEI-VFQ-25 consists of a set of 25 vision-targeted questions and one single question on general health, generating 11 sub-scales: general health (GP), general vision (GV), ocular pain (OP), near activities (NA), distance activities (DA), social functioning (SF), mental health (MH), role difficulties (RD), dependency (DE), driving (DR), color vision (CV), peripheral vision (PV). The Chinese version of NEI-VFQ-25 was used in this study. The theoretical index score ranges from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better HRQoL. The driving sub-scale of NEI-VFQ-25 was removed due to its inappropriateness for adolescents and children to answer. Nevertheless, since the final score is calculated as the mean of all sub-scale scores, this removal did not affect the final score calculation.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were performed on respondents’ characteristics and the distribution of HRQoL. Frequencies and percentages were used for categorical variables, while means and standard deviations (SD) were reported for continuous variables. Unadjusted HRQoL differences between subgroups with different demographic characteristics were compared by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA).

A multivariable linear regression analysis was performed to identify factors contributing to HRQoL among Chinese high school students with myopia, providing adjusted estimates by controlling for other variables. The CHU-9D values and NEI-VFQ-25 scores were included as the dependent variable in the model, respectively. When using one measure score as the dependent variable, the other measure score was included in the model as an independent variable to analyze the relationship. Age, sex, grade, ethnic, sitting posture, time spent outdoors per week, degree of myopia and glasses type were included as the independent variables in the model.

All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using STATA software version 16.0.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of respondents

A total of 2,464 questionnaires from seven high schools (three schools from Tianjin, two schools from Shandong, and two schools from Xinjiang) were completed, of which 218 questionnaires were excluded because of duplicate responses, 34 questionnaires were excluded because of missing key parameters, 14 questionnaires were excluded because the respondents were not myopic patients, and the remaining 2,198 questionnaires were included in the final analysis. According to the 10 events per variable rule (10 EPV rule), considering that fewer than 25 variables were included in our study, a sample size of more than 250 would have been considered appropriate. However, to ensure greater representativeness of the sample and enhance the reliability of research findings, we ultimately included 2,198 respondents in our study. The flow chart of the sample inclusion for questionnaires data is illustrated in Appendix Fig. 1.

The social characteristics of the 2,198 participants who completed the survey are shown in Table 1. The mean (SD) age of the subjects was 16.7 (0.8) years, and the male to female ratio was almost 1:1. Due to convenience sampling, the largest number of respondents were from Tianjin (65.0%), followed by Shandong (24.9%), and the smallest number were from Xinjiang (10.1%). Only 60.7% of the respondents have a normal sitting posture. The degree of myopia was calculated using the refractive power. The naked-eye visual acuity was 0.44 ± 0.35 (mean ± SD) and the corrected visual acuity was − 0.04 ± 0.17 (mean ± SD). Data missing was observed in both the naked-eye visual acuity and the corrected visual acuity. Respondents with low myopia and high myopia accounted for 75.8% and 18.8%, respectively. Framed glasses are worn by 69.4% of the respondents, and 21.6% of the respondents do not wear glasses.

HRQoL of respondents

Table 2 showed the unadjusted statistics for CHU-9D utilities and NEI-VFQ-25 scores, categorized by respondent characteristics. Among the Chinese high school students with myopia, the value of CHU-9D was 0.851 ± 0.160 (mean ± SD), the score of NEI-VFQ-25 was 91.112 ± 9.619 (mean ± SD). The mean CHU-9D utility values in males (0.870) were significantly higher than in females (0.830) (P < 0.001), but this difference was not significant for the NEI-VFQ-25 scores. Although the differences were not all significant, HRQoL between grades showed a trend of sophomores being higher scores than freshmen and seniors. There were no significant differences in CHU-9D utility values between respondents with different degrees of myopia, but the mean NEI-VFQ-25 score for pre-myopia (94.145) was significantly higher than for low-myopia (91.043) and high-myopia (90.531) (P < 0.001). Participants who maintained a normal sitting position, spent more than 10 h outdoors per week, and did not wear glasses scored higher in both CHU-9D utility values and NEI-VFQ-25 scores compared to other groups.

The scores for the NEI-VFQ-25 increased with age (P = 0.020), whereas the utility values for the CHU-9D decreased with age (P = 0.007). Regarding sex, males had significantly lower NEI-VFQ-25 scores than females (P = 0.022), while CHU-9D utility values were significantly higher (P < 0.001). Both NEI-VFQ-25 scores and CHU-9D utility values were significantly higher for normal sitting posture. Compared to wearing frame glasses, wearing no glasses was associated with a significant increase in NEI-VFQ-25 scores (P < 0.001) but resulted in a significant decrease in CHU-9D utility value (P = 0.016). More time spent outdoors per week had a positive effect on CHU-9D utility scores, but no significant effect on NEI-VFQ-25 scores. The NEI-VFQ-25 scores and CHU-9D utility values demonstrated a significant positive correlation.

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluated HRQoL and its associate factors among high school students with myopia in China. We found that the majority of high school students with myopia in China had good HRQoL. Among the factors that affected HRQoL, sex, sitting posture, time spent outdoors per week, degree of myopia, and type of glasses showed significant correlation with CHU-9D utility values. Age, sex, sitting posture, and type of glasses were significantly correlated with NEI-VFQ-25 scores. To our knowledge, this study is the first multicenter cross-sectional study to assess HRQoL in a Chinese high school population with myopia using both the vision-specific measure NEI-VFQ-25 and the generic measure CHU-9D, and to explore the potential factors influencing HRQoL.

In this study, we found the HRQoL of Chinese high school students with myopia was at a favorable level. The CHU-9D value (0.851 ± 0.160) of the high school student with myopia in this study was found to be slightly higher than the utility (0.81 ± 0.15) of children aged 13–17 years in previous study [27]. Although the issue of sample representativeness may introduce some bias, the small difference is able to demonstrate that the HRQoL of sample populations of the two studies were at a consistent level. Based on previous studies using CHU-9D in Chinese adolescents, this study (0.851 ± 0.160) was similar to the utility value of the children with allergic rhinitis (0.84 ± 0.13) and the utility value of the patient after pediatric liver transplantation (0.85 ± 0.14) [28, 29]. Given the disparate demographic characteristics of the sample populations, further research is required to compare the impact between myopia and other diseases on HRQoL in the high school student population. This finding was consistent with previous myopic population studies. Li found that the mean utility values among myopic patients in mainland China were 0.96 ± 0.05 based on time trade-off and standard gamble [30]. Dou found that the HRQoL among myopic patients were 0.95 ± 0.06 based on time trade-off, 0.95 ± 0.10 based on standard gamble and 82.96 ± 11.62 based on NEI-VFQ-25 [31]. Despite the considerable utility value differences between previous studies and this study, utility values can exhibit systematic variations due to different measuring method, the study time point, and the demographic characteristics of the sample population, all these studies have demonstrated that HRQoL of the myopic population is at a well level.

Age, sex, sitting posture and glasses type were deemed as the most impacted factors of HRQoL in this study. Previous studies have demonstrated the impact of time spent outdoors on the development of myopia [32, 33]. Notably, time spent outdoors per week and degree of myopia did not significantly correlate with NEI-VFQ-25 scores in this study. It may be attributed to the fact that vision protection is primarily in the preventive phase. Since the sample population consisted entirely of myopic individuals, the impact of longer outdoor time on HRQoL was not significant. Wearing glasses with the appropriate prescription may negate the effect of the degree of myopia on HRQoL. Previous study has indicated that adolescents who do not use glasses have a lower HRQoL score than those who do use glasses [19]. However, in this study, wearing glasses can cause inconvenience, as evidenced by the decrease in NEI-VFQ-25 scores. Although it differed from the statistics in Table 2, the multivariable analysis provided more reliable results by controlling for other variables. It is possible that the higher utility value of wearing framed glasses compared to not wearing glasses is due to the fact that the sample population is all myopic. Wearing framed glasses can help mitigate the impact of myopia on their lives to a certain extent.

The study revealed difference between CHU-9D and NEI-VFQ-25 on measuring HRQoL of high school students with myopia. A significant difference was found in mean CHU-9D utility values between male and female respondents. However, there was no significant difference in mean NEI-VFQ-25 scores. Conversely, significant differences in mean NEI-VFQ-25 scores were found for different degrees of myopia, but not in CHU-9D utility values. This could be attributed to the fact that CHU-9D, as a generic measure, captures general health status and is less sensitive to vision-specific HRQoL. A study conducted in Singapore using PedsQL, a pediatric generic measure, found no significant effect of refractive error on the HRQoL of adolescents [34]. Another study on preschoolers with visual impairment in Singapore, which also used the PedsQL scale to measure the HRQoL, found that the presence or absence of visual impairment did not significantly affect HRQoL [18]. However, the NEI-VFQ-25 may have difficulty capturing the attributes with the highest number of problems, such as “tired”, “school work/homework”, and “worried”, resulting in a higher proportion of individuals scoring above 90. These findings suggest that relying solely on the generic or disease-specific instruments to measure vision-specific HRQoL in adolescents may be challenging.

It should be noted that this study is subject to certain limitations. Firstly, the NEI-VFQ-25 is not a preference-based measure, and the scoring algorithm is unique and not constructed based on the Chinese population, which may have an impact on the calculation of scores. Secondly, the CHU-9D questionnaire is only applicable to adolescents between the ages of 7 and 17. However, in order to ensure the number of samples in the senior year, some samples over the age of 17 were included in this study. Thirdly, the sample size was not sufficiently homogeneous across the grades, with a preponderance of samples in the first and second years of high school. This may have led to an overestimation or underestimation of HRQoL. Fourthly, this study only included high school students with myopia, without a non-myopic group as controls, which limits to confirm whether the identified influencing factors exert specific effects on myopia patients.

Conclusion

A non-negligible impairment of HRQoL is observed in myopic high school students in China. This study found that highly myopic high school students have significantly lower vision-specific HRQoL, and that HRQoL impairment due to the degree of myopia is more often influenced through the type of glasses worn. Additionally, factors influencing health utility and vision-specific HRQoL in myopic patients were identified, which can inform policy makers in implementing appropriate interventions. It is not recommended that the impairment of HRQoL in Chinese high school students be measured using a generic utility measurement; rather, a vision-specific measurement should be employed. Future studies should focus on the use of both generic and specific measurement to obtain more comprehensive results.
