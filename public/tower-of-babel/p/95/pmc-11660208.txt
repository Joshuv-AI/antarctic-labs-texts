Factors influencing inpatient bowel preparation: a scoping review
Authors: Marianne Marchildon, Jennifer Jackson, Janet Rankin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimInpatients undergoing colonoscopy experience a higher‐than‐average rate of inadequate bowel preparation (compared to outpatients) leading to canceled procedures, increased stress on the patient, increased time in hospital, and increased cost to the healthcare system. The aim of this scoping review was to identify research surrounding inpatient bowel preparation and to identify modifiable and non‐modifiable factors that influence the adequacy of bowel preparation in hospitalized patients undergoing colonoscopy and establish areas where nursing interventions may help improve overall bowel preparation rates.MethodsAn initial search of MEDLINE, CINAHL, Scopus, and Embase was undertaken to identify seed articles, followed by a structured search using keywords and subject headings. Studies conducted between 2000 and 2022 and published in English were included. A total of 37 full‐text studies were screened for inclusion, with 22 meeting inclusion criteria.ResultsAdvanced age, decreased mobility, constipation, extended length of stay, and multiple comorbidities were identified as non‐modifiable factors associated with inadequate bowel preparation. Narcotic use, failure to follow preparation instruction, and delayed time to colonoscopy were identified as modifiable factors associated with poor bowel preparation.ConclusionsEducational interventions and interprofessional programs, using a multifaceted approach, increase the odds of adequate bowel preparation, including nursing tip sheets, troubleshooting flowsheets, and bowel movement assessment scoring.Keywords: clinical gastroenterology in the elderly, endoscopy: colon, gastroenterology, screening and diagnosis

Introduction

Accurate identification of polyps, malignancy, inpatient bowel preparation (IBD), and lower gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding is only possible with clear visualization of the bowel mucosa during colonoscopy, which is accomplished when the bowel wall is clear of stool and residual fluids. 1 Detailed examination of the colonic mucosa and the therapeutic yield of colonoscopy is highly dependent on the level of bowel cleanliness. 2 , 3 When optimal bowel cleanliness is not obtained, GI abnormalities may be missed, and/or intervention may be impaired, resulting in negative repercussions for the patient undergoing procedure and increased cost to the healthcare system. 2 , 3 For inpatients, the burden of colonoscopy preparation is severe, with poor preparation linked to increased length of stay in hospital, increased costs of hospitalization, and increased stress and anxiety for the patient and healthcare staff. 4 , 5

Much is known regarding the necessity of adequate bowel preparation for optimal colonic examination. However, inpatient status remains a significant factor in inadequate bowel cleansing, with up to 30–50% of all inpatient colonoscopies resulting in an aborted procedure or suboptimal bowel scores 6 , 7 , 8 requiring additional bowel preparation and extended length of stay in hospital by at least 1 day and additional healthcare costs. 5 Both modifiable and non‐modifiable factors were attributed to poor bowel prep including comorbidities, social status, medication, health literacy, and patient and nursing education. 9 , 10 , 11

Significant research has been conducted in the outpatient settings, and although inpatients and outpatients share risk factors associated with inadequate bowel preparation, 12 findings from outpatient studies cannot be generalized to the inpatient population. 5 Outpatient risk factors are identified as being male, unmarried, having chronic illness, lower socioeconomic status, and taking tricyclic antidepressants. 8 In contrast, hospitalized patients are significantly older, acutely ill, have a lower level of physical activity, are often bedridden, have an increased prevalence of comorbidities, and present for colonoscopy for different reasons than outpatients. 5 , 12 Outpatients may also have more privacy at home and more control over their environment than inpatients. These differences mean that it is important to consolidate evidence about bowel preparation, specifically for inpatients.

Aim

The aim of this scoping review is to consolidate evidence about the modifiable and non‐modifiable factors that influence the adequacy of bowel preparation in the adult inpatient population undergoing colonoscopy. Identifying risk factors associated with inadequate bowel prep may help change practice in this area, including the role of nursing, in decreasing the number of canceled and rescheduled procedures, and minimize overall burden for patients and the healthcare system.

For the purpose of this review, “adequate bowel preparation” refered to patients presenting for colonoscopy with a level of bowel cleanliness that allows for clear visualization of bowel mucosa and where the procedure is not delayed or canceled due to residual stool in the bowel. The term “factors” refered to both modifiable and non‐modifiable variables associated with poor bowel preparation in the adult inpatient population undergoing colonoscopy. The lack of research into inpatient nursing care to facilitate colonoscopy preparation was the compelling rationale for conducting this review.

Methods

We utilized a scoping review methodology, 13 , 14 with the protocol developed in line with JBI standards, 15 available here. 16

Types of sources

This scoping review included both experimental and quasi‐experimental study designs including randomized controlled trials, non‐randomized controlled trials, before and after studies, and interrupted time‐series studies. Analytical observational studies including prospective and retrospective cohort studies, case–control studies, and analytical cross‐sectional studies were considered for inclusion. We considered descriptive observational study designs including case series, individual case reports, and descriptive cross‐sectional studies for inclusion. Qualitative studies that focused on qualitative data including, but not limited to, designs such as phenomenology, grounded theory, ethnography, and interpretive description were also considered. Opinion papers, gray literature, and industry guideline papers were excluded.

Search strategy

An initial limited search of Google Scholar was undertaken to identify seed articles. The index terms used to describe the articles were used to develop a full search strategy for MEDLINE (see Appendix I). The databases MEDLINE, CINAHL, Scopus, and Embase were searched using identified keywords and subject headings.

Keywords

The keywords for this review were bowel preparation, colonoscopy, colon cleansing, inpatient, and hospitalized patient. See Appendix I for implementation of these keywords in database searching.

Eligibility criteria

Participants

The participants in the studies included in this review were only adult patients admitted to hospital underdoing colonoscopy.

Concept

For the purpose of this review, the term adequate bowel preparation referenced patients presenting for colonoscopy with a level of bowel cleanliness that allow the endoscopist to clearly visualize the colonic mucosa, does not require additional ingestion of purgative solution, and does not result in the delay or cancellation of the procedure due to residual stool in the colon. Factors refers to both modifiable non‐modifiable variables associated with poor bowel preparation in the adult inpatient population undergoing colonoscopy. For this review, we scoped both patient and practitioner variables that can affect the adequacy of IBD. Literature failing to identify patient or practitioner factors (e.g. provision of educational material) associated with IBD were omitted from the review.

Context

This review considered literature within the context of the inpatient setting. Literature comparing inpatient and outpatient setting was considered if the knowledge presented highlighted specific inpatient differences or needs when compared to the outpatient setting; findings pertaining to the outpatient setting were excluded.

Inclusion/exclusion criteria

This scoping review considered all studies examining factors that influence the adequacy of bowel preparation in adult inpatients 18 years and older undergoing colonoscopy, admitted in emergency, and all other inpatient units. Studies with a focus on medically ordered purgatives and/or pre‐procedure diet regimes were excluded. Studies published between 2000 and 2022 and published in English were included.

Data extraction

Data were extracted from papers by one reviewer, with an independent spot check from a second reviewer. The data extraction tool was developed by the reviewers, including study participants, study aims, setting, and key results. These extraction categories were selected to accommodate a variety of research methodologies. Results

An initial search identified 1754 articles, a total of 37 full text studies were considered for inclusion, with 22 meeting inclusion criteria (Fig. 1). Research included in this review highlights the overwhelming proportion of inadequate bowel preparation in the inpatient population and several studies suggest that the rate of inadequate bowel preparation rates in inpatients is anywhere from 22% to 50%. 4 , 5 , 8 , 18 , 24

PRISMA diagram.

Non‐modifiable patient factors

Results of this review were mixed, regarding attributable factors associated with inadequate bower preparation; however, advanced age was noted to be an important and dominant factor in multiple studies. 4 , 12 , 19 , 25 , 27 , 28 McNabb‐Baltar et al. 4 found that the median age of inpatients with inadequate bowel preparation was 76 vs 67 in patients with adequate bowel preparation. Additionally, the odds of poor bowel preparation continue to increase with every 10‐year increase in age. 4 Chorev et al. 12 found that inpatients were significantly older than outpatients by an average of 6.2 years. Additionally, McNabb‐Baltar et al. 4 found that for every 10‐year increase in patient age, the odds of inadequate bowel preparation also increased. The finding of advanced age as an important predictor of inadequate bowel preparation in the inpatients could be attributed to the fact the elderly typically have a higher health burden, are frailer, and are less mobile. However, researchers did not explicitly link these elements of advanced age with inadequate bowel preparation; rather, older adults were at high risk for inadequate bowel cleansing.

Diabetes mellitus (DM) was identified as a non‐modifiable risk factor, due to its effects on gastric motility. 4 , 24 , 26 McNabb‐Baltar et al. 4 suggest that DM may contribute to inadequate bowel preparation due to its associated autonomic neuropathy and subsequent effects on gastric motility resulting in constipation. Gastroparesis, with associated nausea and vomiting, was also identified as a risk factor and may explain another mechanism whereby DM increases the risk of inadequate bowel preparation. 24 , 27 Overall, gastric motility disorders such as chronic constipation, pre‐procedural nausea and vomiting, and gastroparesis have been associated with decreased bowel preparation tolerability and efficacy. 5 , 24 , 27 , 28 Tricyclic antidepressant (TCA) use was also linked to slower GI transit time because of anticholinergic effects. 5 Ness et al. 7 suggested that people using TCAs may be a benefit from longer and more rigorous bowel preparation regimes.

An elevated comorbid score, as described by the American Society of Anesthesiologists, 29 has also been identified as a patient‐specific factor associated with inadequate bowel preparation. 4 , 5 , 27 Poola et al. 28 identified coronary artery disease, congestive heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, motility disorders, liver cirrhosis, and obesity as predictive risk factors for poor bowel preparation. Yadlapati et al. 5 found that chronically ill patients with an increased comorbid score are more likely to experience inadequate bowel preparation, which is likely linked to the presence of multiple chronic disease that impact tolerability and efficacy of oral purgative solutions. Additionally, decreased mobility or bedridden status has been attributed to constipation, higher comorbid score, and inadequate bowel preparation. 4 , 12 , 24

Several other non‐modifiable risk factors were reported in this review. One study specifically identified dementia as a risk factor for inadequate bowel preparation, 24 stating that patients with dementia are more likely to have poor bowel preparation secondary to difficulties swallowing, altered GI motility, decreased mobility, and difficulty with comprehension and adherence to preparation orders. Male sex has also been identified as affecting bowel preparation. 8 , 20 , 27 However, evidence to support this result is not conclusive and could be attributed to evidence that men may be less compliant with ingesting oral purgative solutions than women. 30 Finally, patient length of stay (LOS) in hospital was found to be an additional patient‐related factor. 26 , 27 Patients admitted to hospital develop symptoms of constipation within 2–3 days of admission and that constipation is associated with longer LOS. 31

Modifiable factors

Multiple modifiable risk factors were also identified. Chorev et al 12 found that the inpatient's ability to complete preparation instructions as ordered was an independent predictor of high‐quality bowel preparation. This result was supported by Fuccio et al., 26 who suggest that inpatients should ingest at minimum 75% of the prescribed oral purgative to protect against inadequate bowel preparation.

Once the patient has completed the bowel preparation regime, researchers in this review show that time to colonoscopy following completion of prep was associated with inadequate bowel preparation. 1 , 5 , 8 , 27 Specifically, patients who underwent colonoscopy in the morning fared better in terms of bowel preparation than those who underwent colonoscopy in the afternoon. 1 , 5 , 8 , 27 Mahmood et al. 8 found that the time between the start of bowel preparation and initiation should not exceed 14 h. Guo et al. 20 found that the time interval between the last dose of preparation and the initiation of procedure affected the quality of bowel preparation. Findings by Fuccio et al. 26 mirror the finding by Guo et al. 20 as a shorter interval was a protective‐setting factor against inadequate bowel preparation.

Like TCAs, opioids are known to slow gastric transit time. 5 The link between constipation and opioid use is well established, and up to 80% of patients who use opiates report constipation. 32 Nukui et al. 33 found that high‐dose and low‐dose opioid use was associated with inadequate bowel preparation. In their analysis, Agrawal et al. 24 found that inpatient narcotic use was associated with inadequate bowel preparation. Several other inpatient studies in this review support this finding 5 , 25 , 27 with Garber et al. 27 citing that if inpatient opioid use was avoided up to 3 days prior colonoscopy, the rates of inadequate bowel preparation would decrease by 5.6% (when combined with other modifiable factors, such as avoidance of a solid diet the day before a morning procedure). In contrast, Mahmood et al. 8 did not find statistical significance between inpatient narcotic use and rates of inadequate bowel preparation.

Educational interventions

Studies assessing the impact of patient education on rates of adequate bowel preparation have shown positive impacts on overall bowel cleanliness. 34 , 35 , 36 Six studies analyzed the effects educational interventions on in‐patients undergoing colonoscopy. 9 , 11 , 20 , 26 , 37 , 38 Four studies evaluated the effect of educational leaflets, booklets, and oral instructions on the quality of bowel preparation hospitalized patients. 9 , 26 , 37 , 38 One study analyzed the impact of an app on the quality of bowel preparation, 20 with another evaluating the effects of intensive education provided by nurses on the quality of IBD. 11

Printed material showed mixed outcomes in the reviewed studies. Providing patients with an educational leaflet prior to colonoscopy did not provide significant improvement in the quality of bowel preparation. 19 Four studies found that “educational interventions” for patients significantly improved overall bowel preparation scores for inpatients. 9 , 20 , 26 , 38 Ergen et al. 9 adapted the previously validating colonoscopy preparation booklet Getting Ready for Your Colonoscopy by Spiegel et al.. 36 In their adaptation of the booklet, outpatient references to screening colonoscopies and home medications were removed, and instructions to inpatient specific preparation with PEG included. 9 Rosenfeld et al. 38 provided patients with a 5‐min bedside counseling session with gastroenterology residents along with written educational materials left at the bedside (see Appendix II).

Fuccio et al. 26 found that a combination of written and oral instructions were protective factors against poor bowel preparation. Of interest, the mobile app intervention by Guo et al. 20 showed a significant increase patient compliance with preparation ingestion and willingness to participate in a follow‐up procedure. This app, which was specifically designed for inpatients, provided information describing the procedure; the importance of bowel preparation and diet compliance prior to procedure; an educational video demonstrating how to prepare the purgative; a description of adverse events from the purgative; and images rating bowel preparation as excellent, fair, good, and poor according to stool characteristics (see Appendix III).

Finally, Lee et al. 11 found increased adherence with preparation and diet instructions was greater following continuing education for nurses. Sessions were provided by expert colonoscopists to nurses and consisted of education explaining the importance bowel preparation; possible preparatory side effects; pre‐procedure diet and rationale; instruction for completion of purgative; and the importance of drinking additional water. 11 Brief weekly reviews of the educational material were also provided for 1 month. 11 Findings from this study also showed that patients' anxiety levels were lower, satisfaction levels were higher, and sleep quality was better in patients who received pre‐procedure education when compared to the control group. 11 Thus, intensive and ongoing nurse education had the best outcomes reported in this review.

Standardized electronic order sets and multifaceted approaches

A total of six studies evaluated the impact of electronic order sets linked to quality improvement programs on the adequacy of IBD. Sullivan et al. 23 created a standardized electronic health record (EHR)‐based order set with clear instructions and anticipatory guidance for provider oversight of colonoscopy bowel prep. The EHR order set delineated diet; preparation; interventions aimed at preventing nausea and vomiting and rescue medications; and stool assessment. Also, the miscellaneous orders directed the specialized nursing care for patients on anticoagulants and assessment (see Appendix IV). While analysis showed significant improvement in bowel scores in the right colon, Sullivan et al. 23 study found that overall bowel preparation scores did not show significant improvement.

Yadlapati et al. 5 also created an electronic bowel preparation order set including split‐dose preparation algorithm, automated orders for rescue medications, and nursing bowel preparation checks using the nursing bowel preparation tool. Unlike Sullivan et al. 23 Yadlapati et al. 5 showed significant improvement in overall bowel preparation scores and a decrease in overall canceled procedures due to inadequate bowel preparation. Argyropoulos et al. 17 also developed an electronic order set, the introduction of split‐dose bowel preparation with the addition of a dedicated GI nurse‐facilitator who consulted with the gastroenterology team regarding anticipatory procedures. Chart reviews were conducted by the nurse facilitator; if a risk for poor bowel preparation was identified, further consultation with the gastroenterology team occurred. 17 Combined, the interventions including electronic order sets with automated rescue medication, split‐dose bowel preparation, identification, and consultation of patient at perceived risk of inadequate bowel preparation showed significant improvement in the rate of acceptable bowel preparations. 17

In a patient‐centered approach to improving the quality of IBD, Gawron et al. 39 identified three areas for improvement: bowel preparation ordering procedures, nursing education, and patient education. To accomplish these aims, a standard bowel preparation order set was developed, accompanied by an educational patient leaflet with a self‐tracking section. These efforts were expected to standardize patient education regarding colonoscopy preparation. 39 A nursing tip sheet was also created to provide nursing staff with strategies for patients experiencing difficulty completing the bowel preparation. 39 Overall, the combined interventions of a standard bowel preparation order set, the development of a patient education leaflet, and the creation of a nursing tip sheet resulted in a significant reduction in the number of poor bowel preparations. 39

Gawron et al. 21 showed similar results with a comprehensive intervention to enhance IBD. Their analysis showed that the implementation of EHR bowel preparation order set with EHR alerts prompting nursing assessment, standardized nursing charting processes for progress tracking, a standardized education program for nursing and ordering providers, and print and video patient education materials significantly improved bowel preparation scores. Chambers et al. 18 surmised that the use of an electronic order set in addition to nursing, physician, and patient education on colonoscopy preparation led to a significant decrease in the number of patients with inadequate bowel preparation and failed procedures. Finally, with the implementation of a patient‐centered framework, Strauss et al. 22 demonstrated that a protocol that included standard split‐dose preparation instruction, a gastroenterology note template, and an education plan for patients, nurses, and physicians significantly increased the rate of adequate bowel preparation among inpatients.

Discussion

The articles in this review report that there are modifiable and non‐modifiable factors affecting inpatient bowel preparation, as well as educational and policy interventions. Researchers reported that multifaceted interventions produced the best outcomes, as education and policy change together were more effective than either measure independently. Multifaceted interventions addressed the broadest number of patient risk factors—hence, why they worked.

Age

The driving force behind inadequate bowel preparation in the inpatient population remains unclear; however, based on these data, inpatients are older. Age has been identified as an independent risk factor for inadequate bowel preparation. 4 However, it remains unclear whether age alone is a risk factor or if it is a risk factor due to the increased prevalence of comorbidities, increased level of immobility, and overall increased dependence on care for activities of daily living. What is known is that inpatients are older, sicker, and more dependent than outpatients and therefore are more difficult to prep for colonoscopy. 4 , 7 , 12 , 19 , 25 , 27 , 28

While age has been identified as a factor that can influence the adequacy of IBD, it is crucial for nurses and physicians to consider other patient factors in combination with age to accurately assess the risk of inadequate bowel preparation. Age alone should not be used to determine the level of risk for inadequate bowel preparation. Comorbidities and mobility status are other important factors that can significantly impact a patient's ability to successfully follow and complete bowel preparation instructions. By considering age in combination with other patient factors such as comorbidities and mobility status, nurses and physicians can more accurately assess the individual risk for inadequate bowel preparation. This comprehensive evaluation allows healthcare professionals to tailor their interventions and support patients accordingly.

Time to procedure

Timing of colonoscopy was identified as an important factor in the quality of bowel preparation. 1 , 26 This finding is in line with prior research, which demonstrates that bowel preparation scores are inversely related to the interval between the last dose of the bowel preparation agent and the start of colonoscopy. 40 Ideally, the interval between the last dose of the bowel preparation agent and the start of colonoscopy should be optimized to ensure better bowel preparation quality. However, the timing of the procedure is often dependent on endoscopy suite availability, making it a variable that may be difficult to control. Moreover, the timing of the procedure is also directly related to the patient's ability to complete the bowel preparation prescribed by the gastroenterologist. 26 Additionally, preparation ingestion of at least 75% is required to adequately cleanse the colon for colonoscopy, 26 a difficult task for hospitalized patients who may be elderly, frail, bedridden, and acutely ill, particularly if they are hospitalized. As such, morning colonoscopies or colonoscopies performed within 3–5 h of the last dose of bowel preparation may not be feasible for these patients, increasing their risk of inadequate bowel preparation. While the timing of the procedure is recognized as an important factor for bowel preparation quality, it can be difficult to modify due to constraints related to endoscopy suite availability. Patient populations may face challenges in completing the bowel preparation within the recommended time frame, further impacting the effectiveness of the preparation.

Education and policy

Several studies reported varied outcomes when evaluating the effectiveness of educational interventions in improving bowel preparation quality among inpatients. 12 , 19 , 38 One potential explanation for the decreased efficacy of education in the inpatient setting is the concept of patient activation. 38 Patient activation refers to an individual's understanding, competence, and willingness to participate in their own care decisions and processes. 41 In the case of bowel preparation, as the administration of the preparation is managed by the healthcare team, the level of patient activation may be lower, therefore negatively affecting bowel preparation quality. 5

Overall, interventions focusing on a multifaceted and patient‐centered approach to pre‐colonoscopy care have shown promise in enhancing the quality of IBD. Strauss et al. 22 emphasized the importance of educating everyone involved in pre‐colonoscopy care, including physicians, nurses, and patients regarding their role in achieving adequate bowel preparation. Russell et al. 39 stated that empowering patients with easy‐to‐follow instructions improves patients' overall experience during colonoscopy preparation. Standardizing bowel preparation orders to decrease practitioner variability and providing nurses with strategies to address preparation challenges, ensuring consistency in education and management of bowel preparation is also recommended. 39

Lee et al. 11 found that patients who received instructions from educated nurses demonstrated greater willingness to follow preparation instructions, experienced better overall bowel preparation, and showed lower levels of pre‐procedural anxiety. Empowering patients with clear instructions, standardizing orders, and providing nurses with strategies can contribute to better patient compliance and overall preparation quality.

Patient environment

The absence of literature examining the impact of inpatient environment relating to patient comfort and the lack of privacy during the bowel preparation process is surprising. Inpatient environments are known to be uncomfortable, loud, and often distressing to patients, which contribute to increased levels of anxiety and stress at a time when patients are particularly vulnerable. Yang et al. 42 noted that patients undergoing colonoscopy had higher levels of anxiety than the general population, with areas of anxiety linked to procedure preparation and embarrassment.

Although the built environment of the hospitals may be considered non‐modifiable, taking appropriate steps to increase patient privacy during bowel preparation could decrease pre‐procedural anxiety and embarrassment. Such steps could include ensuring the patient prepping for colonoscopy has a private bathroom or at least a semi‐private bathroom; pulling the curtains between patient beds; providing a commode at the bedside for patients who are less mobile; providing frequent peri‐care to immobile patients; and providing frequent reassurance and teaching to patients. The studies in this review did not include commentary on public vs private healthcare facilities or access to private spaces during bowel preparation. There may be opportunities to improve IBD by increasing access to private washrooms or other factors to promote privacy.

There may also be implications for diet related to the inpatient environment. As outpatients, people have more time for preparation and more choice around diet restrictions and personal preferences. 43 Inpatients usually have little choice around diet, which could make bowel preparation more difficult. Garber et al. 27 reported that when inpatient opiate use was avoided, the rates of inadequate bowel preparation would decrease, when combined with the avoidance of a solid diet the day before a procedure. Guo et al. 20 showed a significant increase patient compliance around bowel preparation and diet with their app that provided patients additional information about the procedure. Clinicians could collaborate with hospital staff to provide more options around inpatient pre‐procedure diet to offer patients more choices.

Future research

Inpatients remain at high risk for inadequate bowel preparation, and the costs associated with canceled and repeat procedures are considerable. Understanding patient factors associated with poor bowel preparation such as advanced age, diabetes, gastroparesis, TCA, and inpatient opioid use can help nurses and physicians identify patients at greater risks of poor bowel preparation who may require additional or extended preparation regimes.

Confusion surrounding the management of pre‐procedural nausea and vomiting, a common side effect of oral purgatives, could be improved with clear and concise standardized order sets that include appropriate interventions could improve the likelihood that patients complete the contents of the oral purgative. Adopting validated tools for assessment would provide nurses with validated tools to accurately assess patient stool and readiness for colonoscopy. Researchers can also examine the role of the built environment in hospitals in bowel preparation and potential benefits of private rooms and other factors that enhance patients' privacy during bowel preparation. There may also be opportunities to enhance dietary modifications for inpatients and researchers could explore how more food options could influence IBD.

Limitations

Limitation of this scoping review include the exclusion of preparation and diet‐specific research. Studies included in this review were conducted in high‐income countries, and therefore, it is difficult to assess the generalizability of the findings to lower income countries where access to timely care and resources may be limited. Additionally, the app and patient education developed by Guo et al. 20 was not developed in English, and therefore, its applicability and effectiveness may not be generalizable to English‐speaking countries.

Conclusion

Inpatients are at higher risk of inadequate bowel preparation, resulting in canceled procedures, increased length of stay, and increased patient and healthcare burden. The findings from this review identified age as an important independent predictor of inadequate bowel preparation in the inpatient population. Other risk factors included the presence of multiple comorbidities, polypharmacy, and decreased mobility are also important predictive factors of poor bowel preparation.

Time to procedure has also been identified as a strong predictor of inadequate bowel preparation. Ensuring optimal time to procedure would require increased collaboration among nurses, physicians, and multiple departments in a hospital. Educational interventions included in this review indicated that using a multifaceted approach to education that includes all team members involved in the process of colonoscopy preparation increase the odds of adequate bowel preparation in the inpatient population.

The scarcity of nursing research on the topic of IBD represents an opportunity for further development of strategies and practice guidelines/policy on the topic. The role of nursing in IBD needs to be further researched in order to identify the barriers to bowel preparation and to drive future strategies to improve IBD. Future research must also include the impact of the inpatient environment on the ability of the patient to adequately follow pre‐colonoscopy instructions and should include areas such as diet, privacy, and patient anxiety.
