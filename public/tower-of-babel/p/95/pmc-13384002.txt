Short-Term Effects of Comprehensive Periodontal Initial Therapy on Subgingival Microbiota in Nasopharyngeal Carcinoma Patients Undergoing Radiotherapy: A Pilot Randomised Controlled Trial
Authors: Shuying Lv, Zhikai Xu, Qingfei He, Yun Wu, Ling Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis pilot randomised controlled trial (RCT) compared the effects of supragingival scaling versus comprehensive periodontal initial therapy performed before radiotherapy on periodontal indices and the subgingival microbiota in patients with nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) and periodontitis. The study aimed to investigate the impact of periodontal initial therapy on periodontal health in patients undergoing radiotherapy, providing evidence for preradiotherapy oral health management.MethodsTwelve NPC patients with periodontitis were enrolled and randomly assigned to control or test groups. The control group received supragingival scaling before radiotherapy, the test group received comprehensive periodontal initial therapy. Clinical periodontal indices (probing depth [PD], attachment loss [CAL], bleeding index, bleeding on probing, and plaque index and subgingival plaque samples were collected from both groups before treatment and at the end of radiotherapy. The subgingival microbiota was analysed using 16S rRNA sequencing. Changes in indices and microbial community structure after radiotherapy were compared between groups.ResultsBetween-group differences in the changes of full-mouth mean PD and CAL were significant (P < .05). Microbial analysis revealed that 6 phyla (including Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes) dominated >90% of the microbiota, while 8 genera (including Fusobacterium, Prevotella, and Treponema) accounted for >70%. Alpha diversity decreased significantly in the test group postradiotherapy (P < .05), and beta diversity showed significant microbial structure changes (P < .05). Post-treatment, opportunistic pathogens (Haemophilus, Veillonella) were significantly higher in controls. Bacteria (Lactobacillus, Lautropia) increased in the test group but decreased in controls after treatment.ConclusionIn this pilot RCT, comprehensive periodontal initial therapy before radiotherapy was associated with favourable short-term changes in selected periodontal parameters and subgingival microbial profiles than supragingival scaling alone in NPC patients with periodontitis. Given the small sample size and potential microbiome-related confounders, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary and require confirmation in adequately powered multicentre trials.

Introduction

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is a highly invasive head and neck malignancy originating from the nasopharyngeal mucosal epithelium. Its case distribution shows significant geographical aggregation, with a notably high incidence in regions such as East Asia (especially Southern China), Malaysia, the Philippines, and other countries/areas.1,2 Although advancements in modern radiotherapy and chemotherapy have gradually improved the survival rate of NPC patients, treatment-related oral complications have become a key factor affecting their quality of life.

Radiotherapy-induced imbalance of the oral microecology, particularly changes in the structure and function of the subgingival microbiota, may be closely associated with the exacerbation of periodontal diseases. Studies have shown that periodontal tissues are highly sensitive to high-dose radiotherapy.3 Direct damage from radiotherapy to periodontal tissues reduces the self-repair and remodelling capacity of bone tissue and increases the risk of tissue infection and necrosis.4 Symptoms induced by radiotherapy, such as oral mucosal pain, dentin hypersensitivity, and salivary gland damage, further impede patients' oral hygiene practices, leading to plaque accumulation on tooth surfaces and promoting the worsening of periodontal diseases.5,6 Due to the impact of radiotherapy on the patient's periodontal tissues and oral microbiota, subsequent periodontal damage and even conditions like osteoradionecrosis can occur, severely affecting the patient's quality of life. Therefore, effective periodontal management before, during, and after head and neck radiotherapy is crucial.

Existing research predominantly focuses on the acute mucosal damage caused by radiotherapy, with limited understanding of the long-term effects on the subgingival microbiota-host interaction. There is a particular lack of knowledge regarding: (1) the succession patterns of the microbiota at different time points during radiotherapy; and (2) the quantitative relationship between key pathogenic bacteria and clinical parameters.

Traditional oral microbiological detection techniques, including bacterial culture, polymerase chain reaction (PCR), and DNA probes, can only perform qualitative or quantitative analysis for specific pathogenic bacteria and are limited in the number of bacterial species detectable in a single assay, presenting significant limitations for holistic microbiota studies.7, 8, 9 In recent years, molecular biology techniques based on the 16S rRNA gene have been widely applied in microbial community analysis. These methods offer the advantages of high throughput, high efficiency, and high accuracy,10 enabling comprehensive investigation into the composition, diversity, inter-species relationships, and potential functions of periodontal microbial communities.

It is well established that nonsurgical periodontal therapy alters the subgingival microbiota toward a composition consistent with periodontal health. Early culture-based studies first demonstrated this shift,11, 12, 13 and subsequent molecular analyses have confirmed consistent reductions in disease-associated taxa.14 More recently, high-throughput 16S rRNA sequencing and meta-analyses have further characterised therapy-induced microbial changes, including decreased alpha diversity and enrichment of health-associated genera such as Rothia and Actinomyces.15,16 While these effects are well documented in general periodontitis populations, patients with nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) undergoing radiotherapy pose a unique challenge due to radiation-induced salivary dysfunction, mucosal injury, impaired oral hygiene, and microbial perturbations. Although periodontal therapy has been recommended before head and neck radiotherapy,17,18 evidence from pilot randomised controlled trials examining short-term subgingival microbial changes associated with different preradiotherapy management strategies in this specific population remains limited.

Therefore, this pilot randomised controlled trial aimed to explore short-term changes in clinical periodontal parameters and subgingival microbial profiles associated with different preradiotherapy periodontal management strategies in NPC patients with periodontitis. By integrating clinical periodontal assessment with 16S rRNA high-throughput sequencing, the study was designed to provide preliminary clinical-microbiological evidence for structured periodontal management before radiotherapy in this specific patient population.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This pilot randomised controlled trial was conducted at the First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University. The study protocol was approved by the hospital’s Ethics Committee (Approval No. [2023]596). The trial was registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2500115187). All participants provided written informed consent.

A total of 12 patients with histopathologically confirmed nasopharyngeal carcinoma (T1-4N0-3M0 stage) and moderate-to-severe periodontitis according to the 2018 International Workshop for the Classification of Periodontal Diseases (clinical attachment loss ≥3 mm and probing depth ≥4 mm on at least 2 nonadjacent interproximal sites) were enrolled between March 2024 and October 2024. All patients received 30 to 33 sessions of intensity-modulated radiotherapy (total dose 69.96 Gy). Exclusion criteria were: diabetes or coronary heart disease; periodontal treatment within 1 year or antibiotic use within 3 months before enrolment; fewer than 16 natural teeth (excluding third molars); inability to cooperate with oral examinations. The final analysed subjects were aged 18 to 75 years, all with Karnofsky Performance Status score ≥60. No participant reported alcohol consumption.

The follow-up cohort comprised 6 subjects in the test group and 6 in the control group. No participants received systemic antibiotics during the D0–D1 observation period.

Clinical timeline and intervention

Timeline definition

D0 was defined as the baseline visit, occurring before any periodontal intervention and before the initiation of radiotherapy.

The assigned periodontal intervention was completed entirely before radiotherapy started.

D1 was defined as the end of the radiotherapy course (i.e., the day after the last radiotherapy session).

Intervention

Control group: supragingival scaling only. Supragingival scaling alone was selected as the comparator for 2 reasons. First, within the narrow preradiotherapy window (typically 1-2 weeks), comprehensive periodontal initial therapy may not be feasible for all patients due to time constraints and the risk of delaying radiotherapy. Second, in many clinical settings, supragingival scaling represents the minimum preradiotherapy oral hygiene management, whereas comprehensive therapy is not routinely implemented. Thus, the control intervention was a pragmatic comparator reflecting current practice, not definitive standard-of-care for stage II/III periodontitis. All participants were referred for complete periodontal management after the D1 assessment, ensuring that definitive treatment was not permanently withheld.

Test group: comprehensive periodontal initial therapy, including supragingival scaling, subgingival scaling, and root planing.

All periodontal interventions were performed by a single trained periodontist (blinded to group assignment only at the time of clinical examination; the treating clinician opened the sealed envelope immediately before treatment). Treatment was completed in 1 to 2 visits within 7 days before the first radiotherapy session. All patients received standardised oral hygiene instructions after the intervention. After the D1 assessment, all participants were referred for routine periodontal maintenance.

Clinical periodontal measurements

Clinical examinations were performed by a calibrated examiner (Kappa statistic assessed) using a William periodontal probe. The following parameters were recorded at D0 and D1: probing depth (PD), clinical attachment loss (CAL), bleeding index (BI), and plaque index (PI) for the Ramfjord index teeth (16, 21, 24, 36, 41, 44). All measurements were expressed as full-mouth mean values. The percentages of sites with PD ≥4 mm, CAL ≥4 mm and bleeding on probing (BOP) for all teeth were also calculated and are presented in the supplementary materials. Gingival recession was not recorded as a separate parameter; instead, CAL was directly calculated and recorded using the measured PD together with the position of the gingival margin (recession or hyperplasia).

Subgingival plaque sampling

Subgingival plaque samples were collected at D0 and D1 under strict sterile conditions. Before sampling, supragingival plaque was gently removed with sterile gauze, and the sampling area was isolated with cotton rolls. Sterile paper points were inserted into the gingival sulcus of the Ramfjord index teeth until mild resistance, left in place for 30 seconds, and then transferred to a sterile tube. For each patient, samples from the 6 teeth were pooled. In case of a missing Ramfjord tooth, the adjacent tooth of the same quadrant was substituted. Samples were immediately stored at −80°C until DNA extraction.

Microbiological analysis

DNA extraction and PCR amplification

Genomic DNA was extracted using the CTAB method. DNA purity and concentration were assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. The V3–V4 hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using primers 341F (5′-CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACNNGGGTATCTAAT-3′). PCR was performed in a 30 µL reaction mixture containing 15 µL of Phusion® High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix with GC Buffer (New England Biolabs), 1 µL of each primer (1 µM), 10 µL of diluted gDNA (1 ng/µL), and 2 µL of H₂O. The thermal cycling program was: 98 °C for 1 min; 30 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 50 °C for 30 s, 72 °C for 30 s; and a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. PCR products were purified by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis (1 × TAE) and recovered using a Universal DNA Purification Kit.

Library preparation and sequencing

Libraries were constructed using the NEB Next® Ultra DNA Library Prep Kit, qualified on an Agilent 5400 system, and quantified by Q-PCR. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina PE250 platform.

Bioinformatics analysis

Raw reads were imported into QIIME2 (version 2022.2). The DADA2 plugin was used for quality control, denoising, chimera removal, and merging of paired-end reads to generate Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs). After quality filtering, samples were rarefied to 20,000 reads per sample to normalise sequencing depth. Representative sequences were taxonomically annotated against the SILVA database (version 138, 99% similarity, trimmed to the V3–V4 region). Mitochondrial and chloroplast sequences were removed. Alpha diversity indices (observed ASVs, Chao1, Shannon, Faith’s phylogenetic diversity) were calculated, and beta diversity matrices (Bray-Curtis, unweighted UniFrac, weighted UniFrac) were computed. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was used for visualisation. Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) was applied to identify differentially abundant taxa between groups, with a logarithmic LDA score threshold of >2.0. Partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) was performed using the R package “mixOmics” to explore relationships between microbial communities and sample categories. Redundancy analysis (RDA) was applied using the R package “vegan” to assess associations between microbial communities and environmental factors. Co-occurrence networks were constructed based on Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients to evaluate inter-taxon associations.

Randomisation and blinding

A statistician generated a computer-based random sequence list. Sequentially numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes were prepared accordingly. A clinician who was not involved in the outcome assessments opened the envelope immediately before treatment to assign the intervention. All clinical periodontal examinations and subgingival sampling were performed by a separate examiner who was blinded to group allocation. The treating clinician could not be blinded due to the nature of the interventions, but the outcome assessor remained blinded throughout.

Statistical analysis

Because this was a pilot trial designed to explore preliminary signals in a difficult-to-recruit population, no formal sample size calculation was performed. Categorical data are presented as numbers (percentages). Continuous data were tested for normality; normally distributed data are expressed as mean ± SD, non-normally distributed data as median (interquartile range). For 2-group comparisons, independent samples t-test (normal, equal variance) or Mann–Whitney U test (non-normal) was used. For multiple comparisons, ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis test was used, with Bonferroni correction for posthoc tests. Spearman’s rank correlation was used for non-normal variables. For sequencing data, Kruskal–Wallis nonparametric ANOVA at the feature level was used to determine taxonomic differences. A 2-sided P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

From March 2024 to October 2024, a total of 12 patients with nasopharyngeal carcinoma and periodontitis who met the criteria were enrolled from the First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University. The patients' ages ranged from 28 to 72 years (mean 50.58 ± 12.09 years). The majority were male (83.33%), and none had a history of diabetes, coronary heart disease, or malignant tumours in other parts of the body.

Analysis of relative abundance of dominant taxa in subgingival microbiota

At the phylum level, Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Proteobacteria, Fusobacteriota, Actinobacteriota, and Spirochaetota were the dominant phyla in all groups before and after treatment, together accounting for more than 90% of the total microbiota. Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes together comprised approximately 50% of the total microbiota and represented a particularly dominant component among the dominant phyla. Notably, the ratio of Firmicutes to Bacteroidetes (F/B ratio) has been associated with health and disease in previous studies.

At the genus level, 8 genera dominated across all groups before and after treatment, accounting for approximately 70% of the total microbiota: Fusobacterium, Prevotella, Treponema, Streptococcus, Rothia, Actinomyces, Lactobacillus, and Haemophilus. Among these, Fusobacterium, Prevotella, and Treponema are Gram-negative anaerobes closely associated with periodontitis progression, and together they constituted up to 50% of the total microbiota.

After treatment, the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes (F/B) ratio did not differ significantly between groups (control group: from 0.83 to 1.46; test group: from 0.94 to 1.69; P > .05). However, the proportion of Firmicutes in the test group increased numerically from 17.82% to 43.72%, while the change in the control group was smaller. This corresponded to a numerically lower proportion of Bacteroidetes – a phylum that includes several known periodontal pathogens such as Porphyromonas gingivalis, Tannerella forsythia, Treponema denticola, Prevotella intermedia, and Prevotella nigrescens – in the test group.

Furthermore, the relative abundance of Fusobacterium nucleatum, Fusobacterium periodonticum, and Fusobacterium gonidiaformans (the latter 2 belonging to the Fusobacteriota phylum) decreased numerically in the test group (from 14.84% to 6.58%) but increased in the control group (from 10.31% to 16.62%) (Figure 3A). The results showed: No significant difference in the Shannon index was observed between the 2 groups before treatment (P > .05). The Shannon index in the test group decreased significantly after treatment compared to before treatment (P < .05). These results indicate a significant reduction in the diversity of the subgingival microbiota in patients who underwent periodontal initial therapy.

Beta diversity analysis

Based on Bray-Curtis, weighted UniFrac, and unweighted UniFrac distance matrices, this study evaluated the beta diversity of the subgingival microbiota through Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) (Figure 5).Fig. 5Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) and partial least squares-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) of subgingival microbiota beta diversity.Fig 5 dummy alt text

Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) and partial least squares-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) of subgingival microbiota beta diversity.

The results showed: In the PCoA based on Bray-Curtis and weighted UniFrac distances, the sample distributions before and after treatment were scattered across groups, without showing significant clustering. And, Analysis of Similarities (ANOSIM) and Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance (PERMANOVA) indicated no statistically significant differences between the groups (P > .05) (Figures 5A, 5C).

In the PCoA based on unweighted UniFrac distance, the post-treatment samples from the test group and the control group were clearly separated. ANOSIM (P = .035) and PERMANOVA (P = .048) further confirmed a significant difference in the community structure between the 2 groups (P < .05), suggesting a significant difference in the composition of the subgingival microbiota between the groups after treatment (Figure 5B).

Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) was performed using ASVs with an abundance greater than 10, the results showed tight clustering of samples within the same group and clear separation between different groups, indicating good performance of the grouping model (Figure 5D).

In summary, significant differences in microbial community structure were observed between the test group and the control group after treatment, suggesting that periodontal initial therapy may influence the microbiota structure by regulating rare species (to which unweighted UniFrac is sensitive).

These differences are likely associated with the different periodontal initial therapies received by the subjects in the 2 groups.

Discussion

This pilot randomised controlled trial compared supragingival scaling alone with comprehensive periodontal initial therapy before radiotherapy in patients with nasopharyngeal carcinoma and periodontitis. The main finding was that comprehensive periodontal initial therapy was associated with a more favourable short-term direction of change in selected periodontal parameters and a distinct subgingival microbial profile by the end of radiotherapy. These findings should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of treatment efficacy, but they provide preliminary clinical–microbiological evidence supporting further investigation of structured periodontal management before radiotherapy in this clinically specific population.

The novelty of the present study does not lie in demonstrating that nonsurgical periodontal therapy can alter the subgingival microbiota in general. This has been shown by early culture-based studies and later molecular analyses, which reported changes in subgingival recolonisation and reductions in disease-associated taxa after scaling and root planing.11, 12, 13, 14 More recent high-throughput sequencing studies and meta-analyses have further shown that periodontal interventions can modify microbial diversity and community composition, with short-term reductions in several periodontitis-associated genera.15,16 However, most of these data come from general periodontitis populations. Patients with NPC undergoing radiotherapy represent a different clinical context because radiotherapy-related salivary dysfunction, mucosal injury, impaired oral hygiene, and microbial perturbation may interact with pre-existing periodontal inflammation.17,18 Therefore, the present pilot trial extends established periodontal microbiology evidence to the specific setting of preradiotherapy oral management in NPC patients.

From the clinical perspective, the test group showed a more favourable direction of change in PD and CAL than the control group. This finding is consistent with previous studies suggesting that periodontal conditions may deteriorate after head and neck radiotherapy, including increases in probing depth, clinical attachment loss, and gingival recession.3,19,20 In the present study, the control group received supragingival scaling alone, whereas the test group received supragingival scaling, subgingival scaling, and root planing. The more comprehensive disruption of subgingival biofilm and removal of local deposits may partly explain the more favourable short-term periodontal pattern observed in the test group.

The absolute between-group difference in full-mouth mean PD was small and should not be interpreted as a definitive clinical improvement. However, the direction of PD and CAL changes was consistent with the supplementary affected-site analysis. Supplementary analysis of affected sites showed that sites with baseline PD ≥ 4 mm had a more favourable direction of change in the test group, with a statistically significant between-group difference (P < .05). Sites with baseline CAL ≥ 4 mm showed a similar numerical pattern (decrease in the test group vs. increase in the control group) but without a statistically significant between-group difference (P > .05; Supplementary Table 3). Full-mouth mean values may dilute changes at initially affected periodontal sites, and the supplementary analysis helps to address this limitation. Taken together, the full-mouth and affected-site findings suggest a consistent but preliminary periodontal signal.

The microbiological findings provide further support for differences between the 2 preradiotherapy management strategies. The dominant phyla and genera observed in this study were broadly consistent with the concept that the oral cavity contains a complex but structured microbial community, with a limited number of taxa accounting for a large proportion of the total community.21,22 After treatment, alpha diversity decreased in the test group, and beta diversity based on unweighted UniFrac distance showed post-treatment separation between groups. Because unweighted UniFrac is sensitive to the presence or absence of less abundant taxa, this finding may suggest that comprehensive periodontal initial therapy influenced the occurrence of specific subgingival community members during the radiotherapy period. This interpretation should remain cautious, but it is biologically plausible given the known effect of subgingival instrumentation on periodontal biofilm composition.

At the genus level, the control group showed higher post-treatment abundance of several taxa including Haemophilus and Veillonella, whereas the test group showed a different post-treatment microbial profile. Lautropia and Lactobacillus also showed opposite directions of change between groups. These genus-level findings should not be overinterpreted as direct evidence of beneficial or pathogenic function because 16S rRNA sequencing provides taxonomic rather than functional information, and the ecological role of individual genera may vary across oral niches and disease states.

Further analysis of intergroup differences revealed that the abundance changes of Lautropia and Lactobacillus showed opposite trends between the 2 groups. The abundance of Lautropia and Lactobacillus in the test group was higher after treatment compared to before treatment. Lactobacillus has been shown to modulate microbial balance and inhibit the growth of certain pathogens.23 Additionally, the abundance of Lautropia has been reported to be significantly higher in healthy periodontal sites compared with diseased sites, and significantly more abundant in gingivally healthy controls than in patients with gingivitis.24 These observations are consistent with a possible favorable ecological shift after comprehensive periodontal initial therapy, but they should remain interpreted cautiously because 16S rRNA sequencing is taxonomic rather than functional.

Importantly, the microbiological results should be interpreted together with the clinical periodontal outcomes rather than as isolated sequencing findings. The more favourable direction of PD and CAL changes in the test group, the consistent supplementary affected-site findings, and the between-group differences in microbial community structure together suggest a coherent short-term clinical–microbiological pattern. This integrated interpretation is more appropriate than attributing the findings solely to a definitive therapeutic effect, especially given the pilot design and the complex oral environment during radiotherapy.

The present findings also have practical implications for preradiotherapy oral management. In routine clinical practice, the interval between cancer diagnosis and radiotherapy is often limited, and comprehensive periodontal treatment may be difficult to complete without delaying oncologic care. The present study does not suggest that supragingival scaling alone is sufficient for managing stage II/III periodontitis outside this context. Rather, it highlights the potential value of early periodontal assessment, risk stratification, and feasible periodontal intervention before radiotherapy. For NPC patients with periodontitis, comprehensive periodontal initial therapy, when it can be completed within the available preparation window, may help reduce periodontal inflammatory burden and influence the subgingival microbial environment during radiotherapy.

Several limitations should be considered. This was a pilot randomised controlled trial with a small sample size in a clinically specific NPC radiotherapy population; therefore, the findings should be interpreted as preliminary. Gingival recession and xerostomia were not quantitatively assessed, which may have influenced the interpretation of CAL changes and microbiome profiles. In addition, full-mouth mean periodontal values may not fully capture site-specific changes at initially deeper periodontal sites. Future adequately powered multicentre studies with longer follow-up, separate recession measurements, xerostomia assessment, and prespecified site-level analyses are needed to confirm these findings.

Conclusion

In this pilot randomised controlled trial of NPC patients with periodontitis undergoing radiotherapy, comprehensive periodontal initial therapy before radiotherapy was associated with more favourable short-term periodontal changes and a distinct subgingival microbial profile compared with supragingival scaling alone. The consistency between the clinical findings, supplementary affected-site analysis, and microbiological changes suggests a coherent short-term clinical–microbiological pattern during the radiotherapy period. These preliminary findings support further investigation of structured periodontal assessment and feasible comprehensive periodontal intervention as part of preradiotherapy oral management.
