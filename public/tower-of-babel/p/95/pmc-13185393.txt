A medication literacy instrument for older chronic disease patients in China: development and validation
Authors: Boya Zhou, Zhigang Zhao, Mingfen Wu, Xixi Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMedication literacy is crucial for the safe and effective use of drugs among older adults with chronic conditions. However, a validated instrument tailored to older Chinese patients with multiple chronic diseases is lacking. This study aimed to develop and validate a medication literacy scale specific to this population.MethodsThis study utilized a cross-sectional design. We aimed to adhere to the COSMIN criteria where feasible.Items were initially derived from the Chinese Pharmaceutical Association’s Medication Behavior Risk KAP Questionnaire, then refined by an expert panel based on Nutbeam’s health literacy model and Pantuzza’s medication literacy components. A pilot survey of 114 older chronic disease patients (aged ≥ 60 years, taking ≥ 2 medications) was used to improve content validity and wording clarity. The main survey employed simple random sampling across primary, secondary, and tertiary hospitals in the Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei region. Participants completed an online self-administered questionnaire covering sociodemographics and medication literacy. Extended factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) were used to evaluate construct validity. The content validity index (CVL) is used as a quantitative evaluation index of the content validity of the scale. The item level content validity index (i-cvi) and the scale level content validity index (s-cvi) are used to evaluate the content validity of the items and the scale as a whole.ResultsThe exploratory factor analysis extracted 12 common factors with feature values > 1 and factor loadings > 0.5. The total cumulative variance contribution rate is 68.958%. After confirmatory factor analysis, the medication literacy instrument contains 41 items in nine dimensions. The total scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.918), while all subscales exceeded the threshold of 0.7. Confirmatory factor analysis revealed that the degree of fit of the model was good (χ 2/df = 2.437, IFI = 0.913, TLI = 0.901, CFI = 0.912, and RMSEA = 0.050). We classified participants into two groups: basic medication literacy (total score ≥ 156 or an average score ≥ 3.81) and low medication literacy (total score <156 or an average score <3.81).ConclusionsThe newly developed medication literacy scale exhibits strong psychometric properties and is suitable for assessing medication literacy among Chinese older patients with chronic diseases. It shows promise for use in clinical and public health settings to identify at-risk individuals and guide targeted interventions.Keywords: Medication literacy, Older adults, Chronic disease patients, China, Validity evaluation

Introduction

China is experiencing a rapid demographic shift towards an aging society. The proportion of the population aged 60 years and above reached 10% in 1999, formally marking China’s transition into an aging society [1]. By the end of 2023, this demographic had expanded to 297 million individuals, constituting 21.1% of the total population, which includes 217 million people aged 65 and above (15.4% of the population) [2]. Projections indicate that by 2035, the population aged 60 and above will reach approximately 420 million, accounting for over 30% of the total population, with the size and proportion of the older population expected to peak around 2050 [1].

This demographic aging is accompanied by a rising prevalence of chronic diseases, such as hypertension, among older adults, which adversely affects health and quality of life [3]. Currently, more than 190 million older individuals in China live with chronic conditions [1]. Pharmacotherapy is a cornerstone for long-term management of these diseases, aimed at controlling symptoms and slowing disease progression [4]. In this context, medication use can serve as a proxy indicator for both chronic disease burden and overall health status [5].

Improving health literacy is recognized as one of the most fundamental, economical, and effective strategies for enhancing population health outcomes [6]. Consequently, enhancing medication literacy—the application of health literacy skills within the medication use process—is critical for public health promotion.

Medication literacy can be considered the application of health literacy in the medication use process. Medication literacy reflects the specific literacy competencies required to act on medication-related information [7]. While several studies have assessed medication literacy in China, existing scales often pose challenges for older populations. The instruments used by Lin HW [8] and Qu J [9], for instance, employ scenario-based questions that demand advanced reading comprehension, making them more suitable for younger or middle-aged adults with potentially higher educational attainment. Although Shi S [10] developed a scale for hypertensive patients, its disease-specific nature limits its applicability to the broader older population with diverse chronic conditions. A medication literacy tool for older adults must be simple, intuitive, and capable of identifying specific literacy deficits across various domains—such as medication adherence, ability to understand drug instructions, and knowledge of antibacterial drugs. A comprehensive assessment is essential for identifying individual weaknesses and guiding targeted pharmacist interventions.

The Chinese Pharmaceutical Association (CPA) has developed the Medication Behavior Risk KAP Questionnaire for Chinese Residents, designed to assess medication-related risk behaviors in the Chinese population. Within China’s pharmaceutical research sector, this questionnaire is widely adopted for assessing public medication literacy. Over 24 studies have directly utilized it to evaluate medication-related risk behaviors in Chinese residents. As a generic instrument, it has been adapted in more than 12 published studies to suit specific populations—including pregnant women, pastoral communities, caregivers of minors, and university students, etc. —through validated cultural and contextual modifications.

Nutbeam D [11] proposed the concept models of health literacy, which distinguishes between three typologies of health literacy [12]: (1) Functional health literacy refers to the basic skills in reading and writing that are necessary to function effectively in everyday situations; (2) Interactive health literacy refers to more advanced cognitive and literacy skills which, together with social skills, can be used to actively participate in everyday situations, extract information and derive meaning from different forms of communication, and apply this to changing circumstance; and (3) Critical health literacy refers to more advanced cognitive skills which, together with social skills, can be applied to critically analyze information and use this to exert greater control over life events and situations. In 2022, Pantuzza [7] proposed a conceptual model of medication literacy, which likewise referenced Nutbeam’s framework and integrated 29 detailed operational components. Although the concept of medication literacy is gaining traction, the current evidence base is characterized by a scarcity of validated instruments, particularly for older chronic disease patients. This gap underscores the necessity of the present study. Our work aims to address this underexplored area by developing and validating a new scale—the Medication Literacy Instrument for Older Chronic Patients in China—thereby establishing a more solid foundation for future research. Here, “older adults” refers to individuals aged 60 years or older, while “chronic disease patients” refers to those diagnosed with chronic conditions who are currently using two or more medications to manage their chronic illnesses. This study established the items from the Medication Behavior Risk Knowledge-Attitude-Practice (KAP) Questionnaire for Chinese Residents as the initial item pool, with subsequent revisions informed by Nutbeam’s (2000) hierarchical health literacy framework and Pantuzza’s operationalized medication literacy components.

Materials and methods

We aimed to adhere to the COSMIN guidelines [13] where feasible. A self-administered questionnaire was developed, which relies on respondents’ self-assessment based on their knowledge and personal perceptions. This scale is constructed based on a reflective model, positing that “medication literacy” is a latent, comprehensive trait whose level is reflected by an individual’s performance on a series of specific medication-related knowledge items. This cross-sectional study was conducted in three phases.

Phase 1: item generation

The initial item pool was adapted from the Medication Behavior Risk Knowledge-Attitude-Practice (KAP) Questionnaire for Chinese Residents, which consists of 63 items. We conducted an extensive literature review, expert consultations, and focus group discussions to refine the instrument. The expert panel included specialists in pharmacy, health management, social sciences, and survey methodology. Literature searches were performed in PubMed, MEDLINE, Embase, CNKI, Wanfang, and VIP databases to identify medication-related issues commonly encountered by older adults. The revision process was informed by Nutbeam’s hierarchical health literacy framework and Pantuzza’s operationalized medication literacy components. The instrument was prepared in simplified Chinese and uses a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly agree, 2 = Agree, 3 = Neutral/Uncertain, 4 = Disagree, 5 = Strongly disagree). Reverse-scored items were recoded such that higher scores consistently reflect stronger trait expression.

Phase 2: content validity and pilot testing

A nationwide pre-survey was conducted involving 114 chronic disease patients aged 60 years or older, all of whom were taking two or more chronic disease medications. Trained pharmacists performed face-to-face interviews through home visits. The primary aim of this pilot study was to assess the comprehensibility and operational feasibility of the questionnaire.

Phase 3: psychometric validation

The refined questionnaire was subsequently evaluated for its psychometric properties, including structural validity, reliability, and other relevant measurement characteristics, in alignment with the COSMIN framework.

Sample and data collection

A simple random sampling method was employed to recruit participants from primary, secondary, and tertiary hospitals in the Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei region between November 2022 and August 2023. The sample size was determined based on the recommended principle of 5–10 times the number of questionnaire items [14]. Accounting for a potential 10% rate of invalid responses, the calculated sample size ranged from 270 to 539. To ensure the robustness of both exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), a total of 965 questionnaires were collected and randomly split into two groups: 392 for EFA and 573 for CFA.

Eligible participants met the following inclusion criteria: (1) aged 60 years or older; (2) diagnosed with one or more chronic conditions, including but not limited to hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary heart disease, dyslipidemia, asthma, hyperuricemia, stroke, gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), atrial fibrillation, malignant neoplasms, or other chronic illnesses; (3) currently taking two or more chronic disease medications; and (4) willing to participate and provide signed informed consent. Patients were excluded if they had severe physical or mental illnesses or cognitive impairments that affected communication ability. All participants were required to independently complete face-to-face interviews with trained pharmacists. Questionnaires with missing items were excluded from the analysis.

Data were collected through home visits by trained pharmacists, who administered the survey orally using the simplified Chinese version of the instrument. To ensure comprehension, the pharmacists used layman’s terms to explain the items and actively encouraged participants to ask questions during the survey, with detailed explanations provided for any queries. Respondents indicated their level of agreement on a 5-point Likert scale, and pharmacists recorded the responses electronically. To minimize comprehension bias resulting from varying educational backgrounds, pharmacists provided standardized explanations when participants had difficulty understanding any items.

Sociodemographic information was collected, including sex (male, female), age (60–64, 65–69, 70–74, 75–79, or ≥ 80 years), highest educational level (junior high school or below, high school/vocational secondary school/junior college, bachelor’s degree or above), current work status (retired, returned to work, farming or working), and personal monthly income (≤ 1,000 CNY, 1,000–2,999 CNY, 3,000–4,999 CNY, 5,000–9,999 CNY, ≥ 10,000 CNY).

Reliability evaluation

Reliability was assessed via Cronbach’s alpha, with a value above 0.70 indicating acceptable internal consistency [15].

Validity evaluation

Structural validity

The construct validity was evaluated via EFA and CFA. The Kaiser‒Meyer‒Olkin (KMO) test and Bartlett’s test of sphericity were used to determine the appropriateness of the sample for factor analysis [16]. When KMO > 0.6 and the significance is less than 0.05, the results of EFA are more reliable. The items are rotated via the maximum variance method. The criteria for exploratory analysis to determine how many factors to retain should include the following 4 aspects: (1) eigenvalues greater than 1; (2) a cumulative variance contribution rate of 60% or higher; (3) factor loadings of 0.50 or above; and (4) at least two items retained per factor. Factors not meeting these criteria were removed.

In the CFA, model fit was evaluated using multiple indices. A good fit was indicated by a normed chi-square (χ²/df) value ≤ 3 [17], a root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) ≤ 0.08, and comparative fit index (CFI), incremental fit index (IFI), and Tucker–Lewis index (TLI) values all above 0.90 [18].

Convergent validity was assessed by calculating the average variance extracted (AVE) and composite reliability (CR) for each subscale. Satisfactory convergent validity was defined as AVE > 0.4 and CR > 0.7 [19, 20]. Discriminant validity was evaluated using Pearson correlation analysis. Adequate discriminant validity was considered established if the square root of the AVE for each factor exceeded the maximum correlation coefficient between that factor and any other factor.

Content validity

Content validity was assessed by a panel of six clinical pharmacy experts. The content validity index (CVl) was employed as the quantitative measure, utilizing both the item-level content validity index (I-CVI) and the scale-level content validity index (S-CVI) to evaluate individual items and the overall scale, respectively. Experts were asked to rate the relevance of each item to its corresponding dimension using a 4-point scale: 1 = not relevant, 2 = weakly relevant, 3 = strongly relevant, and 4 = very relevant. The I-CVI for each item was calculated as the proportion of experts assigning a rating of 3 or 4. An I-CVI value of not less than 0.78 was considered indicative of good item-dimension correlation [21]. The Kappa statistic (K*), which corrects for chance agreement, was also calculated, with a value above 0.74 deemed excellent [22].The S-CVI is represented by the S-CVI/Ave, and when it is greater than 0.9, the overall content validity of the scale is good [23].

Preliminary cutoff scores

Preliminary cutoff scores were defined based on the critical value method assuming normality, which was confirmed by a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test (p > 0.05). The cutoff was calculated as µ + 0.253σ, identifying the top 40% as the “basic medication literacy” group. Results were rounded to the nearest integer.

Statistical analysis

Data collection was conducted using Wenjuanxing, and all responses were exported to Excel for management. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0 and AMOS 26.0. Reliability and validity analyses were carried out in SPSS, while structural validity was assessed using confirmatory factor analysis in AMOS. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Phase 1: instrument development and item refinement

Through five iterative rounds of expert consultations and focus group discussions conducted via Tencent Meeting (with session recording), the questionnaire was systematically refined. The revision process involved: (1) eliminating 30 original items; (2) incorporating 16 new items aligned with Pantuzza’s operationalized medication literacy components; and (3) finalizing a 49-item instrument, including reverse-scored items (items 27–30 and 44–49).

The finalized 49-item questionnaire covers 82.8% of components in Pantuzza’s conceptual framework. Several elements were excluded due to specific considerations: items related to”When and how to expect therapeutic effects,””Using technological devices (e.g., apps, mobile devices, textmessages), computing and the internet,”and “Monitoring medication effectiveness and safety (e.g., laborator texts)” were deemed excessively challenging for older chronic disease patients. Additionally, the component “instructions to follow if a dose is missed” was removed due to poor operability in the Chinese context, as most drug labels in China do not provide such information.

Scale development prioritized comprehensive coverage, mirroring Pantuzza’s detailed medication literacy framework [7]. Items concerning antimicrobial concepts, derived from the Chinese Pharmaceutical Association’s questionnaire, were retained in the final instrument. This decision was driven by the significant problem of antibiotic misuse prevalent across China, particularly in less developed regions. Older adults frequently self-medicate with antimicrobials based on personal experience, increasing individual risks of bacterial resistance. For instance, one study [24] identified irregular antibiotic use as a significant risk factor for drug-resistant infections in older patients with community-acquired pneumonia (CAP). Consequently, the expert panel unanimously endorsed retaining antimicrobial-related items as a critical dimension of the scale.

Phase 2: instrument refinement

Based on the experience and feedback obtained during the pre-survey, we refined both the presentation format and wording of the questionnaire. To ensure content validity, the expert panel determined that pharmacist-administered face-to-face interviews would be the optimal survey method. Reliability

The Medication Literacy instrument demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with an overall Cronbach’s α of 0.929.

Validity

Structural validity

Exploratory factor analysis

Prior to conducting exploratory factor analysis, the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure and Bartlett’s test of sphericity were performed on data from the EFA subgroup (n = 392). The KMO value was 0.887, and Bartlett’s test was statistically significant (χ² = 11,771.838, df = 1,176, p < 0.001), confirming that the data were appropriate for factor analysis.

Exploratory factor analysis extracted 12 common factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 and factor loadings exceeding 0.5. The total cumulative variance explained was 68.958%, indicating that the extracted common factors adequately represented the content of the original scale (Table 3). Based on the rotated component matrix and statistical criteria, several items were removed: factors 11 (item Q21) and 12 (item Q44), as well as items Q8, Q18, Q35, and Q37. Furthermore, item Q27 was reassigned to dimension 3 based on its content relevance. Following these adjustments, a total of 10 dimensions were retained(Table 4).

Content validity

Content validity was evaluated through a single round of consultation with six experts, each of whom independently completed an evaluation questionnaire. The results showed that the I-CVI ranged from 0.833 to 1.00, K*> 0.74, and the S‐CVI was 0.965, indicating good content validity of the instrument. No items were eliminated during this stage, as all experts unanimously agreed to retain the full set of 43 items.

Structural validity

Confirmatory factor analysis

The revised scale following exploratory factor analysis demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.929.

The 10 dimensions obtained from EFA were validated to determine the fit of the factor model with maximum likelihood. Data from 573 participants in the CFA group were used to assess the construct validity of the scale. The initial model showed the following fit indices: χ 2/df = 2.596, IFI = 0.895, TLI = 0.883, CFI = 0.895, and RMSEA = 0.053. These results indicated that the model did not meet the criteria for satisfactory fit and required further modification.

Discussion

This study developed and validated a comprehensive medication literacy scale tailored for older Chinese adults with chronic diseases. Grounded in the established Chinese Residents’ Medication Behavior Risk KAP Questionnaire and developed in accordance with COSMIN guidelines, the instrument was constructed through a systematic literature review focusing on medication risks in China’s older population. The scale underwent iterative refinement via multiple rounds of expert consultation and panel discussion, with continuous optimization of item clarity and operational feasibility. The final instrument employs comprehensive yet accessible items in an easily understandable format, enabling effective self-reported assessment of medication literacy in this population.

The research results indicate that the reliability, content validity, and construct validity of this scale have all yielded satisfactory results. In this study, the overall test–retest reliability was 0.918. Cronbach’s alpha values were all around 0.8 or higher, except for factor7(alpha = 0.78), thereby showing sufficient internal consistency. The results revealed that the I-CVI value of each item ranged from 0.833 to 1.00 and that the S‐CVI was 0.965, which indicates that the scale has good content validity. The exploratory factor analysis reclassified the scale into 12 dimensions, with a total cumulative variance contribution rate of 68.95%. The results of the CFA suggested that the initial model fit was less satisfactory and required further model corrections. The modified model showed that χ 2/df was 2.437, IFI = 0.913, TLI = 0.901, CFI = 0.912, and RMSEA = 0.050, which satisfied the requirements for model fitting. This scale evaluates the medication literacy of the target population in 9 dimensions, all reverse-scored items were recoded with higher scores indicating higher medication literacy. These dimensions encompassed 82.8% of domains in Pantuzza’s framework while integrating context-specific medication barriers among older Chinese adults with chronic diseases [25].

Against the backdrop of China’s aging population and high chronic disease prevalence, pharmacotherapy has become a cornerstone of geriatric care [26]. The growing older population and increasing health awareness have further amplified the demand for pharmaceutical services [27]. Assessing medication literacy represents the foundational step in guiding patients toward improved medication safety [28, 29]. The developed scale captures this construct through nine distinct dimensions:

Dimensions and their interpretations

Dimension 1: self-medication

This dimension evaluates patients’ beliefs and behaviors regarding self-initiated medication use. Since such practices are seldom voluntarily reported during clinical encounters, healthcare providers need to proactively inquire about them.

Dimension 2: ability to use medication labels

Proficiency in interpreting drug labels constitutes an essential skill for patients with chronic diseases to ensure safe and effective medication use.

Dimension 3: knowledge of medication therapy

This dimension covers critical details, including the distinction between dietary supplements and medications, safety profiles of over-the-counter drugs, the importance of vaccination, and drug-food interactions. Misconceptions in these areas may significantly compromise treatment outcomes.

Dimension 4: antibiotic use

Given the persistent prevalence of antimicrobial misuse in China—particularly in underserved regions—antibiotic-related literacy was retained as an independent dimension. This represents a key target for pharmacist-led interventions to mitigate associated health risks, especially among older adults.

Dimension 5: awareness of pharmacists’ roles

In China, pharmacists are often perceived merely as medication dispensers. This dimension highlights the importance of educating patients to seek pharmacists’ expertise for medication-related guidance.

Dimension 6: medication risk awareness

This dimension identifies risks associated with improper drug storage or administration, enabling pharmacists to provide individualized safety guidance.

Dimension 7: medication purchasing behaviors

As self-medication frequently involves unsupervised drug purchases, this dimension aligns with the “Five Rights” principle to ensure appropriate medication selection.

Dimension 8: perception of drug quality

Beliefs associating drug efficacy with price may lead to preferential use of costly medications. Pharmacists can address such misconceptions to optimize cost- effective treatment.

Dimension 9: understanding of administration routes

As a foundational aspect, this dimension may serve as a representative component in future streamlined versions of the scale.

Clinical implications and applications

The developed scale enables healthcare providers to identify specific medication literacy deficiencies in older chronic disease patients through both individual item analysis and dimensional performance evaluation. This facilitates targeted interventions and allows pharmacists to prioritize high-risk areas requiring immediate clinical attention. Furthermore, its coverage of knowledge, beliefs, and practice aligns with the KAP theoretical framework. This study also determined the preliminary cutoff values for medication literacy through a standard deviation-based critical value determination method, with an average score of 3.81 and a total score of 156. These reference values provide a practical benchmark for evaluating medication literacy at the group level and support comparative assessments across different patient populations.

While the medication literacy instrument demonstrated satisfactory comprehensibility and psychometric properties in this study, its applicability requires further validation in linguistically and culturally diverse populations. As the present investigation was conducted exclusively among older chronic disease patients in China, generalizability to other demographic or geographic contexts remains limited and should be approached with caution.

Limitations

This study has several methodological limitations that should be acknowledged. First, and most critically, representatives from the target population (older adults with chronic conditions) were not involved in either the development phase or in a dedicated content validity assessment to qualitatively evaluate the relevance of the items through methods such as cognitive interviews, as mandated by the COSMIN guidelines. Although we employed quantitative methods (e.g., the Content Validity Index) to assess content validity, supplemented by a extensive literature review, expert panel discussions, and a pre-test with 114 target participants to enhance the instrument’s comprehensiveness, clarity, and practicality, the lack of a formal qualitative methodology constitutes a methodological deviation from the COSMIN guidelines. Future research should prioritize incorporating these essential steps to strengthen the content validity and overall robustness of the scale. Test-retest reliability was not evaluated, restricting evidence for the scale’s temporal stability. This was primarily due to challenges in longitudinal follow-up within clinical settings. We hope to assess reliability over a 2-week interval in stable outpatient populations in the future to verify the effectiveness and reliability of this scale.

This research focuses on the selection of preliminary cutoff values for the scale using a ​​standard deviation-based critical value determination method​​ (e.g., µ + Kσ). This method is susceptible to the influence of sample size characteristics (e.g., small sample sizes or skewed distributions), which may introduce certain biases in cutoff estimation. Notably, ROC [30, 31] curve analysis is widely adopted for clinical scale cutoff optimization, as it determines diagnostic accuracy against an external gold standard. Future validation studies will apply ROC analysis to establish refined cutoffs, enhancing the scale’s clinical utility.

Conclusion

The medication literacy scale developed in this study demonstrates robust psychometric properties, establishing it as a valid and reliable instrument for assessing medication literacy among older chronic disease patients in China. This tool enables researchers and healthcare providers to identify specific deficiencies in medication literacy and deliver targeted patient education. Its implementation can support the development of tailored interventions to improve medication safety and adherence in this vulnerable population.
