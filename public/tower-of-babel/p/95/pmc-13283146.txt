Exploring the Linear and Non-linear Association Between BMI and Night-Time Blood Pressure Variability
Authors: Ionas Papasotiriou, Sotiria Spiliopoulou, Damianos Dragonas, Konstantinos Rizogiannis, Nefeli Tsoutsoura, Efstathios Manios
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe relation between obesity and blood pressure (BP) is well known; however, its relationship with night-time BP and BP variability (BPV) has not been adequately examined.AimTo examine the relationship between obesity and night-time BP/BPV.MethodsData from 6,767 adults (46.4% males), aged 56.0 ± 14.0, were collected during their visit to the Outpatient Hypertension Unit of a single institution and had valid ambulatory BP monitoring and anthropometric data. Body mass index (BMI) was estimated by weight and height measurements that took place at the office and categorized as normal weight [< 25 kg/m2; 1,719 (25.4%)], overweight [25–29.9 kg/m2; 2,976 (43.0%)] or obesity [≥ 30 kg/m2; 2,072 (30.6%)]. Standard deviation (SD) and coefficient of variation (CV) of BP were used as indices of BPV.ResultsAfter adjusting for confounders, BMI was significantly related to night-time systolic (b = 0.38, 95%CI: 0.31–0.46) and diastolic BP (b = 0.10 95%CI: 0.05–0.015). Similarly, after adjusting for confounders, obesity was related to SD (b = 0.25, 95%CI: 0.02–0.49) and CV (b = 0.23, 95%CI: 0.03–0.42) of night-time systolic BPV, while a similar pattern was found for diastolic BPV (p < 0.001). Restricted cubic spline models showed a significant joint effect of splines after adjusting for confounders, for all BPV indices (p < 0.05), but a trend for non-linearity found only for systolic BPV indices (χ2 = 3.74, p = 0.053 for SD and χ2 = 3.54, p = 0.060 for CV).ConclusionsIn conclusion, the relationship between obesity and both nighttime BP and BPV provides new insights into the possible effects of obesity on the cardiovascular system.Keywords: BMI, Obesity, Blood pressure, Blood pressure variability, Nighttime

Introduction

High blood pressure (BP) is responsible for a wide range of health problems, including asymptomatic damage to organs such as the carotid arteries, heart, brain and kidneys [1]. Research in recent decades has revealed that many dimensions of BP have a unique impact on the cardiovascular system and other affected organs [2, 3]. Focusing on blood pressure variability (BPV), chronobiology of BP, blood pressure fluctuations and differences between day and night, have changed our understanding about the composite and complex role of these dimensions, beyond mean systolic (SBP) and diastolic (DBP) blood pressure values [4–6].

Many studies have examined the nighttime patterns of BP and BPV, and some of them indicate that they possible possess an independent predicted ability and clinical importance [7–9]. Nighttime BP is normally lower than daytime BP, and disturbances in nighttime BP reduction can be a risk factor for cardiovascular damage [5, 10, 11]. Armitage et al. [12] examined ambulatory BP monitoring (ABPM) data from two cohorts of hypertensive patients and found that avoidance of nighttime BP measurements can lead to failure in diagnosing hypertension. In addition, results from the Second Australian National Blood Pressure study have shown that pre-treatment nighttime SBP was the best predictor of all ambulatory BP parameters and office BP, for predicting all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in older adults with hypertension [9].

Another meaningful dimension of BP is its variability, which represents the fluctuations of BP over time [13]. Both short- and long-term changes in BP have been observed in early studies on BP, while the development of ABPM during the last decades has allowed better capture and conceptualization of this phenomenon [13]. Currently, we know that several factors contribute to BPV throughout the day, some of which are related to daily activity, while others may represent cardiovascular regulation [2]. BPV has unique clinical importance as many studies have repeatedly shown its independent effect on organ damage and cardiovascular outcomes [14, 15]. In a meta-analysis by Stevens et al. [16], it was found that BPV is associated with cardiovascular outcomes and cardiovascular and all-cause mortality better than mean BP levels. Interestingly, the relationship between BPV and target organ damage has been found in both hypertensive and normotensive populations [3, 14, 17], indicating BPV’s effect on organ damage may begin long before the onset of hypertension.

As reported previously, BPV can be influenced by environmental and behavioral factors, as well as by the capacity of regulation mechanisms to maintain a steady BP [2, 18]. Although 24-hour and daytime BPV can be affected more by factors such as physical activity and other daily behavioral aspects, nighttime BPV is more likely to be affected by cardiovascular system regulation, and therefore, better depicts any physiological disturbances [13, 19]. However, despite the well-established relationship between obesity and BP, and the emerging role of nighttime BP and BPV on cardiovascular health, very little evidence currently exists for their relationship. Therefore, the aim of the present study was to explore the relationship of obesity with nighttime BP and BPV.

Methods

Study Design

The present study consists of a cross-sectional analysis of data from the Hypertension Unit of the Department of Clinical Therapeutics at Alexandra Hospital, Athens, Greece. According to the data analysis design, BP measurements were set as the dependent variable, while BMI and obesity were the main independent variables.

Participants

In total, 6,767 adults participated in this study, of whom 3,137 (46.4%) were males. Participants were recruited from their visit to the Outpatient Hypertension Unit of the hospital and had valid ABPM and anthropometric data. Participants were included in this analysis if (a) they were ≥18 years old, (b) they had available data for weight, height, antihypertensive treatment and all confounding variables such as age, sex, history of diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia and smoking, (c) had no history or clinical and laboratory evidence of coronary artery disease, heart failure, stroke and peripheral artery disease, (d) had no clinical signs or laboratory evidence of secondary causes of hypertension, (e) had at least 7 valid nighttime ABPM measurements.

Anthropometry

Participant’s weight and standing height were measured with professional equipment at the nearest 0.1kg and 0.1cm, respectively. For weight, participants were measured wearing the lightest clothes possible, while height measurements were recorded with the participant in an upright position, without shoes when possible. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg)/ height2 (m2), while National Institute of Health cut-off points were used to determine normal weight (< 25 kg/m2), overweight (25–29.9 kg/m2) and obesity (≥ 30 kg/m2), [20]. The underweight group was not included in this analysis because of the very low number of participants with BMI < 18.5 (n= 30).

Renal Function

In a subpopulation of the sample (n = 3,189) with available serum creatinine levels, renal function was estimated using the estimated Glomerular Filtration Rate (eGFR), which was calculated using the 2021 CKD-EPI creatinine Equation [21], according to the guidelines of the National Kidney Foundation and the American Society of Nephrology [22].

Blood Pressure Monitoring

Prior to implementing the ABPM, all participants underwent office BP measurements. BP was measured three times using a mercury sphygmomanometer or a validated blood pressure monitor. For each participant, the three pairs of SBP and DBP measurements obtained from the sphygmomanometer were averaged to calculate a single 'office' SBP and DBP value.

Participants’ BP monitoring was recorded with an ambulatory blood pressure monitor (Spacelabs 90217, Spacelabs Inc., Redmond, Wash, USA), which was attached to their nondominant arm on a usual working day for 24 hours, including sleep. Participants were instructed to follow their usual daily activities and sleep routine and keep their arm relaxed during the measurements. The monitor was set to automatically measure BP every 15 minutes throughout the 24-hour monitoring period. Since not all participants provided a sleeping record to define nighttime, we adhered to the recommendations by the European Society of Hypertension and set nighttime as a fixed time interval (01:00–06:00) [6]. For each participant, the mean 24-hour, daytime and nighttime SBP and DBP were calculated. Standard deviation (SD) and coefficient of variation (CV) of all valid nighttime measurements were used as indices of nighttime blood pressure variability (BPV), for both SBP and DBP.

Hypertension Status

Participants were considered hypertensive if they reported use of antihypertensive treatment, which was confirmed from their medical record, or if they found to have 24-hour ABPM > 130/80 mmHg. Participants with a positive for hypertension ABPM, but with no records of prior diagnosed hypertension or antihypertensive drug prescription, were considered treatment-naive patients. Participants with already diagnosed hypertension and under treatment, were further categorized into those with well-controlled BP if ABPM < 130/80, or into those with uncontrolled BP otherwise.

Statistical Analysis

Analysis of the data started with estimation of the descriptive statistics, which included the mean and the standard deviation for quantitative variables, and absolute and percentage frequencies for qualitative variables. To examine the relationship between BMI and obesity with nighttime systolic and diastolic BP, simple (model 1) and multivariate (model 2) linear regression models were performed. The multivariate models were adjusted for age, sex, smoking history and use of antihypertensive treatment, while BMI was examined both as a continuous and as a categorical variable. Similarly, the relationship of BMI and obesity with systolic and diastolic BPV was examined with simple (model 1) and multivariate (model 2) linear regression models, where the multivariate model was adjusted for age, sex, smoking history, use of antihypertensive treatment and mean nighttime systolic or diastolic BP. Subgroup analysis according to hypertension status was also performed for the relationship between obesity and nighttime BPV, where the models were adjusted for age, sex, smoking history and mean nighttime systolic or diastolic BP. The non-linear relationships were examined using Restricted Cubic Splines (RCS) models were knots set at the quartiles oof BMI in this population (25.0, 27.6 and 30.8). The Wald test was performed to assess the joint contribution of the splines to the model, while the Likelihood Ratio test was used to compare the fit of the linear and non-linear models. RCS models were fitted with and without adjustment for potential confounders, using the same variables for confounding as those reported in the linear models. All analysis performed on STATA (version 18.0) on Windows 11 environment, while statistical significance was set at the level of a = 0.05 for all tests.

BMI and Blood Pressure

Results from simple linear regression revealed that BMI, both as a continuous variable and as categorized to normal, overweight and obesity, was significantly associated with all measures of nighttime systolic and diastolic BP (table 2). According to coefficient of determination, BMI had the highest predicting ability for nighttime SBP (R2 = 0.015), while the same was found for its categorical variable (R2 = 0.013). After adjusting for age, sex, smoking history and antihypertensive treatment, BMI and overweight or obese status, were still significantly associated with all SBP measurements (p < 0.001). A similar pattern of relationships was also found between BMI/ obesity and nighttime DBP in both univariate and multivariate models (p < 0.001). The association between BMI and both nighttime SBP (b = 0.39, 95%CI: 0.28 – 0.51) and DBP (b = 0.10, 95%CI: 0.03 – 0.18) where still significant in the subpopulation of the study with available eGFR values (n = 3,189), after including eGFR in the multivariate model.

BMI and Blood Pressure Variability

Results from the linear regression of nighttime systolic and diastolic BPV on BMI are presented on table 3. The analysis for SBPV showed that in the univariate model BMI was significantly associated with SD (p < 0.001), but not with CV (p > 0.05). Similarly, in univariate analysis both overweight and obese participants had significantly higher expected mean values for SD (p < 0.05) than normal weight participants, but not for CV (p > 0.05). For DBPV, the univariate models revealed that BMI was significantly associated with both indices of BPV (p < 0.001), while the same was found for obesity (p < 0.001). However, being overweight was significantly associated with SD (p = 0.005), but not with CV (p = 0.771). On the other hand, multivariate analysis showed that BMI was significantly associated with both indices of nighttime systolic and diastolic BPV (p < 0.05). Overweight, was only associated with SD of nighttime DPBV, but not with any other BPV measure (p > 0.05). Obesity found to relate with all measures of nighttime BPV (p < 0.05, table 3).

Subgroup analysis for systolic BPV according to hypertension status is presented in Fig. 1. The results revealed that the relation between obesity and nighttime SBPV, either measured as SD or CV, was significant and consistent only in normotensive adults (p < 0.001). For treatment naive and adults under treatment, regardless of their BP control, the results showed no significant relationship between overweight or obesity and SBPV (p > 0.05).

Subgroup analysis according to hypertension status for the association between obesity and nighttime systolic blood pressure variability.

Subgroup analysis results for nighttime DBPV are presented in Fig. 2. These results showed that in normotensive participants, both overweight and obesity were significantly related with BPV measured as SD (p = 0.004, p < 0.001), while a significant relationship was also found with BPV measured as CV (p = 0.003, p < 0.001). However, these relationships were not statistically significant in treatment-naive adults or those under treatment with uncontrolled BP, either for SD or CV (p > 0.05). On the other hand, it was found that in patients under treatment with well-controlled hypertension, obesity, but not overweight, was associated with nighttime DBPV, for both SD and CV (p = 0.001).

Subgroup analysis according to hypertension status for the association between obesity and nighttime diastolic blood pressure variability

Among patients with hypertension that were under antihypertensive treatment, 732 (23.6%) were treated with angiotensin-converting-enzyme inhibitors, 1,125 (36.2%) with angiotensin II receptor blockers, 1,104 (35.6%) with diuretics, 994 (32.0%) with beta-blockers, 936 (30.2%) with calcium channel blockers and 410 (13.2%) with other classes of drugs. Many patients (n = 1,662) were using more than one class of antihypertensive drugs, and where further categorized to those receiving ≤ 2 classes of antihypertensive drugs (n = 2,301; 74.1) or > 2 (n = 804; 25.9). When these two groups of patients were compared, it was found that those taking ≤ 2 classes of antihypertensive drugs had significantly lower levels of SD of SBP than those taking > 2 classes (11.5 ± 3.7 mmHg vs 11.9 ± 3.9 mmHg, t = 2.869, p = 0.004), although no significant differences were found for SD of DBP (8.9 ± 2.6 mmHg vs 8.8 ± 2.6 mmHg, t =1.365, p = 0.172). A similar pattern was also found for CV, with those receiving ≤ 2 classes of antihypertensive drugs having lower levels for systolic (9.6 ± 3.1 vs 9.9 ± 3.2, t= 2.355, p =0.019) BPV, but no difference was found for diastolic BPV (13.1 ± 4.1 vs 13.2 ± 4.1, t = 0.599, p = 0.550).

In the subpopulation with available eGFR values, it was found for nighttime SBP that there was still a significant association between BMI and SD (b = 0.04, 95%: 0.01 – 0.06) or CV (b = 0.03, 95%CI: 0.01 – 0.05), after including eGFR as confounder in the multivariate model. For DBP, it was also found a significant association between BMI and SD (b = 0.05, 95%: 0.03 – 0.07) or CV (b = 0.09, 95%CI: 0.06 – 0.12) after including eGFR as confounder in the multivariate model.

Non-linear Relationships

Results from restricted cubic spline analysis are presented on Table 4, for both systolic and diastolic BPV. The results from univariate analysis showed a significant joint effect of BMI splines on SD of night-time SBP, SD of night-time DBP, and CV of night-time DBP (p < 0.05). However, the likelihood ratio test revealed that none of these models were significant for non-linearity (p < 0.05), although there was a trend for CV of night-time DBP (p = 0.067). After adjusting for confounders, the joint effect of BMI splines was significant for all BPV indices (p < 0.05), while a non-linearity trend was found for SD of night-time SBP (p = 0.053) and CV of night-time SBP (p = 0.060). The restricted cubic spline curves (Figs. 3 and 4), show a prominent U-shaped relation between BMI and CV of night-time SBP and a J-shaped relation between BMI and CV of night-time DBP.

Discussion

The main results of this study indicate that there is a relationship between BMI and weight status with both nighttime BP and its variability, with the latter being a newly introduced index to capture the effects of BP on organ damage. The relationship between BMI and nighttime BP variability was more profound for DBP variability, while in most cases, evidence of a dose-response relation was present. In addition, these results provide evidence that hypertension and antihypertensive treatment could act as modifiers to this relationship, where hypertension was found to mask it in most cases and antihypertensive treatment led to different results depending on whether BP was well controlled or not.

Ambulatory BP monitoring used in this study has been considered as one of the best methods to examine BP profile, and to capture the burden of BP on the cardiovascular system [21, 22]. This method allows for the exploration of blood pressure fluctuations during rest and sleep, with the patient following their daily routine [23]. ABPM has been also found to be a more accurate predictor of cardiovascular outcomes and asymptomatic target organ damage development [21]. This method allowed us to isolate nighttime BP measurements and examine their relationship with BMI and obesity. Our results showed that obesity was significantly related to both nighttime BP and its variability, while its relationship with nighttime BPV was independent of the effect of nighttime BP. These findings provide important information about the possible mechanism by which body weight can affect the cardiovascular system, since nighttime BP has been shown to better predict cardiovascular outcomes and mortality in many cases [8, 9].

Beyond the summarized BP values, its variability has also gained considerable attention and has been shown to have clinical importance. Some studies have shown that BP variability acts as an independent factor on cardiovascular system and relates with target organ damage and cardiovascular outcomes even after adjusting for the effect of BP [15, 16]. These insights into BP necessitate further investigation of the relationship between body weight and new BP dimensions. To date, the scientific evidence of this relationship is scarce, but the majority of studies support the existence of such a relationship [24, 25]. Although, in this study, we used two indices to capture nighttime BPV, BP’s standard deviation and coefficient of variation, our results indicate that BMI is mostly linearly related to SD, but non-linearly with CV, in both systolic and diastolic BP. The relationship between obesity and nighttime BP, as well as its independent relation with nighttime BPV, may be indicative of its effect on cardiovascular health and targeted organs, through BP and its variability, especially if we consider that several studies support the superiority of nighttime BP variability in predicting these outcomes [11, 26]

The subgroup analysis revealed that the relationship between obesity and nighttime BPV was consistent in normotensive adults for both systolic and diastolic BPV, although overweight was significantly related only to diastolic BPV. However, this relationship was not significant for systolic BPV in any group of participants with hypertension. Although any possible effect of BMI on systolic BPV may be masked by antihypertensive treatment, the non-significant relation among those with untreated hypertension is of particular interest. We can only hypothesize that in uncontrolled hypertension, the effect of BMI on BPV may be covered by other factors and mechanisms that predominantly influence BP fluctuations. On the contrary, we found that for diastolic BPV this relationship was present in hypertensive participants under treatment with controlled BP, but not in treatment-naive patients or those under treatment but with high BP. Therefore, these results indicate that the relationship between obesity and DBPV in well-controlled hypertensive adults follows a similar pattern to that of normotensive subjects, while untreated or uncontrolled hypertension seems to mask this relationship.

Subgroup analysis on treated hypertensive patients, showed that those receiving more classes of antihypertensive drugs had higher levels of systolic BPV. While this finding may seem contradictory, it could be explained by the fact that patients in need of more classes of drugs, are those with resistance to antihypertensive treatment and tend to have more uncontrollable hypertension, probably leading to more intense BP fluctuations throughout the day. These findings are also in accordance with evidence from another large cohort of patients, where BPV found to be higher in patients receiving multiple drugs than monotherapy [27].

Evidence of a non-linear relationship between BMI and blood pressure, using RCS models, has been previously reported in both adults [28] and children/adolescents [29]. However, as far as the authors know, no such evidence currently exists for the relation between BMI and night-time BPV. Here, we used RCS models that provide evidence for the existence of a non-linear relationship between BMI and CV of both systolic and diastolic BP. The RCS curves showed that the relationship between BMI and CV of night-time SBP is U-shaped, indicating that very low and very high values of BMI are related to higher values of systolic BPV. On the other hand, the relationship between BMI and CV of night-time DBP was J-shaped, with an almost flat curve, that raises abruptly at BMI values around 30 kg/m2.

Given that BP is often greatly influenced by obesity and body weight, it was expected to find a relation between BMI/ obesity and BPV. Accordingly, the mechanisms that explain the relation between BMI and BP variability are likely to be similar to the mechanisms that explain the relations between BMI and BP. We speculate that a key mechanism for this relationship is autonomic nervous system dysfunction, which is often found in obese individuals [30,331]. Specifically, obesity is characterized by a shift in dominance from the autonomic to the sympathetic nervous system, with a concomitant decrease in parasympathetic activity [30]. Adipose tissue also functions as an endocrine gland, secreting hormones and prohormones, such as leptin, and proinflammatory substances (e.g., cytokines), which affect endothelial function [32, 33]. This effect, combined with the increase in vascular stiffness that it can also cause, favors the increase in BP [34].

The evidence of this study provides more insights into the relation between BMI/ obesity and cardiovascular health. The burden of increased body weight on the cardiovascular system is well known, with a wide range of intermediate mechanisms being already identified [35, 36]. The effect of obesity on BP is a major mechanism by which obesity affects cardiometabolism [36]. For example, it has been shown that BP mediates the relation between BMI and target organ damage [37]. On the other hand, both night-time hypertension and night-time BPV, have been identified as independent risk factors for target organ damage [38, 39]. The effect of isolated night-time hypertension, without the co-existence of daytime hypertension, on clinical and subclinical damage on target organs [40], strengthens the argument about an independent role of nighttime BP patterns of cardiovascular health.

Although there are several methods to measure BP and BPV, ABPM has been suggested as one of the most accurate, which increases the validity of our results. However, this study is not free of limitations, with the major being its cross-sectional design which limits claims about the causative effect of obesity on BPV. Moreover, the use of BMI as an index of obesity also has some disadvantages, since it does not provide information about body composition. Also, obesity can cause obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), which is a major risk factor for nighttime hypertension [41, 42]. The lack of data on sleep quality, number of nighttime awakenings, and OSA is another important limitation of the study, as it may introduce bias due to unmeasured confounders. However, in the whole sample analysis, the association between BMI and nighttime BPV was adjusted for mean nighttime BP, to eliminate any possible effect from elevated BP during the night. Also, the subgroup analysis revealed that the association between BMI and BPV persisted among adults with either normotension or treated hypertension, further strengthening this assumption. The use of fixed narrow-time intervals to define night-time can be considered another limitation and there is a lot of criticism and discussion regarding this issue. To eliminate any possible bias about this practice, we followed the position statement of the European Society of Hypertension on ABPM [43]. It has been also supported by evidence that the prognostic value of BP changes is comparable between large-fixed intervals, narrow-fixed intervals or actual time intervals [44]. The availability of serum creatinine levels, and thereafter eGFR estimations, only in about half of the study’s sample constitutes another limitation. However, this happened since blood samples were introduced in later stages of the study, due to resources limitations. Therefore, selection bias or other missing-data bias is not expected to have affected the results

Conclusions

The results of the present study showed a positive relationship between obesity and both nighttime BP and nighttime BPV, providing new information about the relationship between obesity, the cardiovascular system and the relevant regulatory mechanisms. These findings are also of clinical importance, since they are indicative of possible new pathophysiological mechanisms, by which obesity relates with target organ damage and cardiovascular morbidity and mortality.
