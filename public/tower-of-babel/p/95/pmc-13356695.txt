Mineral and Bone Disease in CKD and Kidney Transplantation: Controversies, Gaps, and a Path Forward
Authors: Mehmet Kanbay, Ozgur Aktas, Sidar Copur, Tilman B Drueke, Ziad A Massy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe pathophysiology of chronic kidney disease (CKD)-related mineral and bone disorder (CKD-MBD) extends far beyond simple mineral imbalances. Evolving from the traditional understanding of secondary hyperparathyroidism, the conceptual framework has recently been updated to recognize 2 distinct but overlapping clinical syndromes: CKD-associated osteoporosis, which encompasses the significantly increased fracture risk and microarchitectural deterioration in this population; and CKD-associated cardiovascular disease, which accounts for vascular and structural cardiac abnormalities, including medial vascular calcification. Unlike traditional osteoporosis, the 2 clinical syndromes emerge from intricate interactions between declining kidney function and dysregulated mineral metabolism. The early rise in fibroblast growth factor–23 (FGF-23) levels; progressive phosphate retention; diminished vitamin D activation; secondary hyperparathyroidism; and uremic toxin accumulation, particularly uric acid and indoxyl sulfate, orchestrate profound disruptions in osteocyte, osteoblast, and osteoclast function. The complex interaction amplifies in the dialysis population, where protein-energy wasting affects most patients; and intensifies following kidney transplantation because of glucocorticoid, immunosuppressive, and anticoagulant treatments. Consequently, fracture rates in patients with CKD exceed those of age-matched controls by >4-fold, with patients on dialysis therapy facing a ≤ 8-fold increased risk. Available diagnostic and predictive tools need to be improved to adequately identify at-risk patients. The usefulness of additional, more recent biomarkers such as FGF-23, a-Klotho, and various bone turnover parameters remains a matter of debate. Bone biopsy, considered as the diagnostic gold-standard, remains impractical in routine practice. Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) cannot provide information on the type of renal osteodystrophy. Moreover, it demonstrates limitations in the presence of severe vascular calcifications. Trabecular bone scores (TBS) emerge as potential progress in noninvasive bone imaging, showing modest superiority over traditional densitometry in predicting fracture risks among secondary osteoporosis populations, yet large clinical trials with long-term follow-up are required. Therapeutic management spans from parathyroidectomy to pharmacological interventions with bisphosphonates, denosumab, teriparatide, romosozumab, whereas agents such as burosumab remain investigational, with no published clinical trials in this population. However, therapeutic nihilism persists, with most high-risk patients not receiving bone-targeted therapy despite accumulating safety data. Transforming care demands abandoning therapeutic nihilism, embracing personalized risk stratification, and conducting trials in populations historically excluded from bone health research.Keywords: burosumab, chronic kidney disease-mineral and bone disorder, fibroblast growth factor-23, osteoporosis

CKD-MBD is one of the most complex and clinically significant complications affecting the global CKD population of 673.7 million patients.1 CKD-MBD manifests as a systemic condition involving an abnormal metabolism of calcium, phosphate, parathyroid hormone (PTH), and vitamin D; disturbances in bone turnover, mineralization, volume linear growth, and strength; and vascular or soft tissue calcifications.2 Considering the global increase of CKD mortality rates by 41.5% between 1990 and 20173 and the steady increase in fracture rates with the progression of CKD,4,5 additional efforts are clearly needed to better understand and manage CKD-MBD.

CKD is an independent risk factor for osteoporosis,6 and the incidence rates of fractures are > 4 times higher in patients with CKD than in age-matched controls.4,7 Similarly, the incidence of fractures is greater in kidney transplant (KT) recipients than in the general public.8 Even though patients with CKD and KT experience a disproportionately high risk of fractures, CKD-MBD remains insufficiently investigated; notably, patients with CKD have been systematically excluded from pivotal trials of novel osteoporosis therapies.9 The scarcity of systemically reviewed evidence of diagnostic strategies and therapeutic approaches in such vulnerable populations warrants further investigation of bone disorders in CKD, end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), and KT populations. The 2025 Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) controversy conference proposed a paradigm shift in conceptual framework.10 The expert panel reached consensus that the original 2009 guideline framework and 2017 update may no longer optimally reflect current available evidence related to diagnosis and treatment in clinical practice. The committee proposed moving toward a framework of 2 clinical syndromes in adults: CKD-associated osteoporosis and CKD-associated cardiovascular disease, recognizing the distinct pathophysiological mechanisms and therapeutic approaches required for each syndrome.

This narrative review identified the relevant literature through searches of PubMed, Medline, Embase, and the Cochrane Library databases with no date restriction. The search strategy used Boolean operators combining key-terms, including “CKD-MBD;” “renal osteodystrophy;” “CKD-associated osteoporosis;” “kidney transplant bone disease;” “uremic toxins;” and specific pharmacological interventions such as “denosumab,” “romosozumab,” and related therapeutic terms. Priority was given to randomized controlled trials, systematic reviews and meta-analyses, and clinical guidelines, supplemented by prospective cohort studies and high-impact observational data. Mechanistic and preclinical evidence is presented as hypothesis-generating. This expert opinion narrative review aims to present a detailed overview of the current landscape of bone disease in CKD, ESKD, and after KT through evaluating unmet clinical needs, ongoing controversies, and emerging innovations.

Risk Factors: A Complex Interplay

CKD-MBD risk factors encompass both traditional osteoporosis-related and more specifically CKD-related risk factors involving complex, interacting pathophysiological mechanisms leading to various types of bone disease.11 Traditional risk factors remain relevant in CKD populations; these include advancing age, female sex (notably postmenopausal women), low body weight, diabetes mellitus, and previous fracture history, as well as smoking and excessive alcohol consumption, both of which independently impair osteoblast function, reduce bone mineral density (BMD), and amplify fragility fracture risk in the CKD population.12, 13, 14 However, the multiple metabolic and endocrine disturbances and the inflammatory state associated with CKD introduce numerous additional risk factors for altered bone metabolism, as illustrated in Figure 1.

It has been shown that bone formation rate is lower in patients with CKD stages 2 to 3, than in patients with CKD stages 4 to 5, which may in part be due to the skeletal action of uremic toxins, inducing a state of resistance to the action of PTH.15 Together with reports by others, this observation led us to postulate that the bone disease pattern in CKD changes along the decline in kidney function, switching from low bone turnover in early CKD stages to a state of high bone turnover in more advanced stages of CKD.15

CKD-specific mineral metabolism disturbances begin early in disease progression, with a rise in serum FGF-23 level being the first detectable biomarker, rising progressively with declining kidney function.16,17 The increase in FGF-23, alongside a-Klotho (Klotho) deficiency, precedes traditional bone marker abnormalities. Both of them contribute to the early dysregulation of bone turnover.17 Importantly, the action of FGF-23 is not limited to regulate phosphate metabolism; emerging evidence shows its involvement in iron metabolism, erythropoiesis, inflammation, insulin resistance, left ventricular hypertrophy, proteinuria, and acute kidney injury.16 Progressive phosphate retention and declining transformation of vitamin D to its active derivatives further contribute to the development of secondary hyperparathyroidism, which is observed in the majority of patients with advanced CKD.18

The chronic low-grade inflammatory state in CKD, driven by uremic toxins, oxidative stress, and gut dysbiosis, significantly contributes to altered bone metabolism.19 Proinflammatory cytokines, particularly tumor necrosis factor-a and interleukin-6, facilitate bone resorption via activation of the receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL)/RANK/osteoprotegerin signaling pathway while supprressing osteoblast function.19 Uremic toxins such as indoxyl sulfate activate the NLRP3 inflammasome in macrophages, leading to interleukin-1β maturation and perpetuating both skeletal damage and vascular calcification through shared inflammatory pathways.20

The accumulation of uremic toxins directly interferes with normal osteoclast and osteoblast function through multiple mechanisms. Uric acid has been proposed to be osteoprotective, because higher serum uric acid levels correlate with reduced osteoporosis and osteopenia prevalence.21 However, this is certainly not the case in patients with CKD. Uric acid decreases vitamin D synthesis, interfering with its protective role against osteoporosis, and is associated with higher FGF-23 and PTH levels.22 Chen et al.23 demonstrated that hyperuricemia suppresses 1a-hydroxylase activity, thereby reducing the conversion of 25-hydroxyvitamin D to calcitriol, whereas a large-scale population study revealed that higher serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D levels and vitamin D intake were inversely associated with hyperuricemia,24 suggesting a bidirectional interaction that interfere with vitamin D’s protective role against osteoporosis. Indoxyl sulfate, which is a uremic toxin derived from tryptophan metabolism by gut bacteria, induces skeletal resistance to PTH in cultured osteoblastic cells, though the mechanism of resistance is not fully elucidated.25 Indoxyl sulfate directly disrupts osteoclastogenesis. Using peripheral blood mononuclear cells and RAW 264.7 cell line, Mozar et al.26 have shown that indoxyl sulfate directly inhibits osteoclast differentiation and bone-resorbing activity in a dose-dependent manner, and its osteoclast inhibitory effects are enhanced in the presence of high inorganic phosphate concentration. Considering that hyperphosphatemia is a hallmark of advanced CKD, this synergistic toxicity provides a “double hit” to skeletal remodeling, contributing to the development of adynamic bone disease. The cellular uptake of indoxyl sulfate is mediated by organic anion transporters, as demonstrated by the fact that probenecid completely prevented its inhibitory effect on osteoclastogenesis.26 However, a preclinical study suggests a biphasic, concentration-and-time-dependent relationship between indoxyl sulfate and osteoclast differentiation mediated via the signaling axis linking aryl hydrocarbon receptor to nuclear factor of activated T-cells cytoplasmic 1 (NFATc1).27 Although short-term, low-dose indoxyl sulfate exposure paradoxically increases osteoclast differentiation by enhancing nuclear aryl hydrocarbon receptor– aryl hydrocarbon receptor nuclear translocator complex formation and NFATc1 expression, long-term or high-dose exposure decreases aryl hydrocarbon receptor nuclear translocator expression and shifts aryl hydrocarbon receptor function from a transcription factor to an E3 ubiquitin ligase that promotes NFATc1 degradation.27 This dual mechanism may explain the heterogeneity of bone turnover phenotypes observed across different CKD stages and uremic toxin burdens.

Chronic metabolic acidosis, present in a significant proportion of patients with kidney failure, contributes to bone mineral dissolution and impaired osteoblast function.28,29 In a cross-sectional study of 180 patients with CKD, Levy et al.30 established an up-to-date comprehensive skeletal phenotype for CKD-associated metabolic acidosis. Using high-resolution peripheral quantitative computed tomography (qCT) and quantitative backscatter electron microscopy, the study demonstrated that metabolic acidosis is strongly associated with decreased tissue mineral density, highly heterogenous calcium content, and marked, female-specific impairments in both cortical and trabecular microarchitecture.30 Future studies are needed to investigate the potential benefit of acidosis correction on bone structure and function. Emerging evidence reveals uric acid as a potentiator of CKD-MBD, with studies demonstrating a relationship between uric acid and vascular calcification; as well as associations with activated vitamin D, PTH, and FGF-23 levels.22,31 In addition, uric acid may be associated with newer CKD-MBD parameters, including activin A and microRNAs, suggesting complex interactions between traditional uremic toxins and mineral metabolism pathways.22,32

Sclerostin, a 22 kDa glycoprotein encoded by the SOST gene and secreted almost exclusively by osteocytes, has emerged as a key regulator of bone metabolism through its potent inhibition of the canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway.33 Upon binding to low-density lipoprotein-related receptors 5 and 6, Wnt ligand engagement and β-catenin nuclear translocation are prevented, which in turn results in suppression of osteoblast differentiation, a mechanism that is relevant for the pathogenesis of CKD-MBD.33 Notably, sclerostin is not only a biomarker but also a therapeutic target: romosozumab, a monoclonal antibody against sclerostin has been shown to increase bone formation while reducing fracture risk in patient with osteoporosis among the general population. This clinical application underscores the biological importance of Wnt pathway modulation but raises safety considerations in patients with CKD. Disruption of the Wnt signaling pathway appears to be an early event in CKD progression, with serum sclerostin levels becoming elevated by CKD stage 3, often preceding abnormalities in conventional biomarkers such as PTH, calcium, and phosphate.34, 35, 36 A cross-sectional study involving 56 patients with CKD stages 3b to 4 demonstrated that circulating sclerostin was independently able to predict low-turnover bone disease with a high degree of sensitivity for identifying adynamic bone, with no need to rely on invasive bone biopsies.37 In terms of cardiovascular outcomes, a study in 173 patients with CKD stages 3 to 5 showed that serum sclerostin independently associated with levels of FGF-23, phosphate, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), flow-mediated dilatation, and predicted both all-cause mortality and cardiovascular events over 26 months.38 In contrast, a meta-analysis of 9 studies, including 1788 patients, failed to confirm an association between sclerostin and mortality risk, though a high degree of heterogeneity limited definitive conclusions. The contrasting findings may be explained by the dual function of sclerostin: although it suppresses bone formation, its upregulation in calcified vascular tissue may serve as a protective, counter-regulatory mechanism limiting further ectopic mineralization.39 This raises concerns regarding the potential use of pharmacological sclerostin inhibition, which could inadvertently worsen vascular calcification in CKD.33 The complexity of sclerostin’s biology, particularly in early stages of CKD, where it seems to contribute to bone pathology yet may concurrently protect against vascular calcification, represents a significant gap in current understanding. Longitudinal clinical studies are needed spanning early stages of CKD through disease progression to shed more light on this issue.

Beyond established features of calcium-phosphate homeostasis, more recent data indicate that the intestinal microbiota is a critical determinant of the skeletal response to PTH. An experimental animal study demonstrated that intermittent PTH administration only exerts its anabolic effect on bone in the presence of an intact gut microbiota capable of generating the short-chain fatty acid, butyrate.40 Microbiota depletion led to reduced systemic butyrate levels and subsequently abolished the bone anabolic response to PTH, whereas restoration of butyrate reinstated the anabolic response.40 Mechanistically, butyrate enhances the differentiation of naïve CD4+ T cells into regulatory T cells in intestine and bone marrow. Regulatory T cells enable intermittent PTH to stimulate Wnt10b production by CD8+ T cells, a critical trigger for Wnt pathway activation and subsequent osteoblast proliferation and differentiation. The butyrate-dependent pathway operates via short-chain fatty acid receptor GPR43 (i.e., FFAR2) signaling in dendritic cells and GPR43-independent mechanisms in T cells.40,41 The clinical implications of this mechanism of action are particularly critical in CKD, where butyrate-producing bacterial populations markedly reduced and correlate inversely with disease severity.42 Such dysbiosis may contribute to the blunted bone response to PTH observed in advanced CKD, suggesting that microbiota-targeted strategies hold promise for bone anabolic therapy in patients with CKD at high risk of low bone turnover disease.

Polypharmacy can further reduce bone health of patients with CKD and proton pump inhibitor use is common in them; however, proton pump inhibitors impair intestinal calcium absorption and increases the risk of fragility fractures.43 Loop diuretics, prescribed for fluid volume and blood pressure management, known to promote renal calcium loss, also have been associated with increased fracture risk and bone loss at hip site.44 Further, observational studies of a high prevalence of subclinical vitamin K deficiency among patients with CKD revealed a link to increased morbidity and mortality rates.45,46 Vitamin K deficiency may promote vascular calcification through impaired activation of matrix GIa protein, a potent inhibitor of arterial calcification,47 and contributes to the uremic milieu, which represents the complex metabolic environment in CKD characterized by accumulation of uremic toxins, oxidative stress, and inflammation.48

Glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis pertains to be a major risk in people with CKD and KT recipients. The adverse effects are in essence due to an early, transient increase in bone resorption mediated by RANKL induction and osteoprotegerin suppression that drives osteoclastogenesis, followed by a sustained inhibition of bone formation that hinders the replication and differentiation of osteoblasts, and causing apoptosis of mature osteoblasts and osteocytes.49 Optimal prevention strategies remain a matter of debate. Bisphosphonates face restrictions in patients with kidney failure and alternative modalities lack robust clinical evidence.50,51 Moreover, sarcopenia and frailty, common consequences and common denominator of CKD, have been associated with alterations of calcium-phosphate metabolism leading to poorer CKD-MBD outcomes including an increase in bone fractures.52,53 Varying by the country, dialysis patients are at a 1- to 8-fold increase in fracture risk in comparison to the general population.54 They are subject to various risk factors, including protein-energy wasting, which affects 30% to 70% among them55; a wide range reflecting marked variation in diagnostic criteria applied (International Society of Renal Nutrition and Metabolism vs. subjective global assessment or Malnutrition Inflammation Score),56 heterogeneity in patient populations including but not limited to dialysis modality and comorbidity, and differences in study design across the literature,57 compromising bone quality via the malnutrition-inflammation complex syndrome.58

Post-KT risk factors present unique challenges to bone health, requiring specialized management. The frequent development of intimal and medial vascular calcification in ESKD favors worse outcomes in both early and later stages of KT.59,60 Younger age at KT may be associated with an increased risk of impaired bone health particularly in recipients with childhood-onset, congenital, hereditary, or early-acquired CKD, in whom transplantation at a young age may still reflect prolonged cumulative exposure to uremic toxins, accelerated cellular senescence, and increased cumulative glucocorticoid usage than older recipients whose CKD developed later in life.59 Prolonged use of unfractionated heparin inhibits osteoprotegerin activity and enhances osteoclastic bone resorption,61 and warfarin inhibits gamma-carboxylation of osteocalcin, contributing to progressive bone density decline in KT recipients and patients with ESKD.59,62

Diagnosis of Renal Osteodystrophy

Bone biopsy is the gold standard for the diagnosis of the different types of renal osteodystrophy and/or osteoporosis among patients with CKD; however, this method is challenging because of the invasive nature of the procedure and limited availability of bone histomorphometry experts. Therefore, nowadays bone biopsy in the evaluation of CKD-MBD is restricted to exceptional cases in which its result may directly impact clinical outcomes and treatment decisions.63

Laboratory Evaluation

Various biomarkers have been proposed for the prediction of fracture risk among patients with CKD, with variable prognostic value. Although serum calcium, phosphorus, PTH, and 25 hydroxy vitamin D levels are routinely monitored among patients with CKD within defined time periods, bone-specific alkaline phosphatase demonstrates superior diagnostic accuracy for fracture prediction compared with other biochemical parameters and BMD measurements.64 Additional markers, including serum FGF-23, Klotho, and sclerostin are under investigation for their potential prognostic value. A retrospective multicenter cross-sectional study in 492 patients on dialysis therapy revealed that cutoff values for 103 and 323 pg/ml for intact PTH effectively discriminated low from high turnover bone disease, as proven by bone histomorphometry.65 Similarly, the cutoff points of serum intact PTH proposed by KDIGO as 2 times to 9 times the upper limit of normal may be useful to discriminate to some extent patients with low bone turnover from those with high bone turnover. However, there is a quite large in-between gray zone where other tools are needed for a correct discrimination. bone-specific alkaline phosphatase, procollagen type 1 N-terminal pro-peptide and tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (i.e., TRAP-5b) are potential biomarkers for the diagnosis of bone formation and resorption, respectively, in patients with CKD because they are not eliminated via the kidney. They theoretically offer promise for the evaluation of CKD-associated bone disease irrespective of GFR.66 However, the diagnostic precision of these biomarkers is far from being perfect. Therefore, they are not routinely used in clinical practice. Beyond their role in bone metabolism, several CKD-MBD biomarkers have emerged as cardiovascular risk predictors, reflecting the intimate bone-vascular axis in CKD. In patients with mild-to-moderate degree CKD (eGFR: 30–90 ml/min per 1.73 m2), elevated FGF-23 and fetuin-A levels independently predict coronary artery disease as measured by Gensini score, even after adjustment for traditional cardiovascular risk factors.67 Similarly, in young adults without known CKD, elevated FGF-23 and fetuin-A levels were independently associated with coronary artery disease, suggesting their utility as novel cardiovascular biomarkers extends beyond patients with CKD to the general population.68,69 Finally, calcitonin, another hormonal regulator of mineral metabolism not routinely measured in the setting of CKD, was shown to independently associate with coronary artery disease in both CKD and non-CKD populations.70

Imaging Assessments

DXA is the most widely available and used radiological tool to assess BMD and to predict future osteoporotic fracture risk. However, there are major issues regarding the validity of DXA among patients with CKD, especially for CKD stages 4 and 5. A retrospective cohort study involving a total of 2754 patients with 384 incident osteoporotic fractures during a median follow-up period of 11.3 years revealed that DXA-assessed BMD provided satisfactory information on fracture risk even among patients with eGFR < 60 ml/min per 1.73 m2.71 It must be noted, however, that in this study only femoral neck BMD was measured (with its high cortical/spongious bone ratio relative to that of vertebral bone). The retrospective study design and low proportion of patients with ESKD were other limitations. Another study involving 485 patients undergoing hemodialysis revealed significant predictive power of total hip BMD on fracture risk only among female, but not male participants with low PTH levels according to gender-stratified receiver operating characteristic analysis.72 The major limitations of DXA among patients with CKD include, but are not limited to, the following: (a) vascular calcification and/or atherosclerosis of abdominal aorta may interfere with anterior-posterior image acquisition by DXA for vertebral BMD assessment; although this concern has been recently nuanced: a study by Jorgensen et al.73 specifically questioned the prevailing assumption that abdominal aortic vascular calcification has an important impact on the diagnosis of lumbar spine BMD in patients with CKD, finding that its actual effect on DXA-measured lumbar spine BMD was less pronounced than commonly assumed; (b) a history of osteoporotic fracture and/or vertebral calcifications may underestimate or overestimate BMD on DXA scan; (c) evaluation of vertebral BMD may be misleading because it is primarily composed of trabecular bone whereas patients with CKD with hyperparathyroidism predominately lose cortical bone, which is better evaluated by femoral neck or distal radius BMD assessment; and (d) CKD-MBD may be complicated by either low or high bone turnover disease, which often renders DXA results more difficult to interpret as regards the concomitant presence of osteoporosis. The validity of the FRAX tool for osteoporotic fracture risk prediction has been repeatedly studied among patients with CKD.74, 75, 76 In a study involving 320 patients with eGFR < 60 ml/min per 1.73 m2 and 1787 control participants FRAX was able to predict major osteoporotic fractures in those with reduced kidney function.74 However, the relatively low representation of patients with advanced stage CKD limits the generalizability of these results to the entire CKD population. High-resolution qCT may be more reliable, especially in advanced stage CKD, because it allows a more precise differentiation of altered skeletal microarchitecture and a better discrimination between changes in cortical and trabecular bone. This said, its superiority over that of DXA has not yet been definitively demonstrated.77,78

TBS, an emerging marker of osteoporosis, is a texture-based metric evaluating the gray-level variations at lumbar spine DXA scan, with high TBS levels indicating more favorable bone microstructure.79 The superiority of TBS over DXA may be particularly useful among patients with secondary osteoporosis, including those with CKD in whom the pathophysiology of fractures is more likely to be related to alterations of bone microstructure than global BMD alterations.80 A large-scale meta-analysis including 2446 patients with CKD not receiving dialysis, 663 patients on hemodialysis, and 530 patients with KT revealed lower TBS score in all groups than the controls. However, its predictive value for fracture risk was attenuated by adjustment: lower TBS predicted incident fractures in nondialysis patients (hazard ratio: 1.45/SD decrease, 95% confidence inteval: 1.05–2.00, P = 0.02); however, this association lost statistical significance after full adjustment for FRAX (hazard ratio: 1.26, 95% confidence inteval: 0.88–1.80, P = 0.21), whereas dialysis patients with prevalent fracture has significantly lower TBS values than those without.81 Clearly, there is a need for future large-scale clinical trials with longer follow-up periods to evaluate the value of TBS in osteoporotic fracture risk prediction among patients with CKD, and especially among those with CKD stages 4 and 5.

Bone-specific biomarkers, including bone-specific alkaline phosphatase and procollagen type I N propeptide, intact PTH, and DXA scoring have been reliably validated among patients with early-stage CKD with eGFR > 60 ml/min per 1.73 m2 and have received acceptable validation among patients with CKD with eGFR between 30 and 60 ml/min per 1.73 m2. However, neither methodology can effectively diagnose osteoporosis among patients with advanced stage CKD who are highly prone to developing both low and high turnover bone diseases. Therefore, combined use of bone-specific biomarkers with qCT may be used in clinical practice to improve diagnostic accuracy by providing data on both bone density and discrimination between low and high bone turnover disease. Bone biopsies should be reserved for patients with uncertain types of bone pathology based on biochemical and imaging measurements. In them, a correct diagnosis of the underlying bone disease, in particular bone turnover, is expected to guide more appropriately therapeutic approaches, knowing that in many patients with advanced stage CKD, antiresorptive therapies are contraindicated because they promote low bone turnover disease (Figure 2).

Treatment Efficacy, Safety, and Evaluation

Numerous therapeutic approaches are available targeting CKD-associated bone disease and the prevention of fractures. They have been progressively evolving based on a growing body of evidence. Like for the prevention of osteoporosis in the general population they include healthy food consumption with sufficient calcium and vitamin D intake; and a healthy lifestyle including smoking cessation, only moderate or no alcohol consumption, and regular physical exercise, including resistance exercise. Hyperphosphatemia, a core component of CKD-MBD, should be prevented with the ingestion of calcium salts, magnesium salts, iron salts, or established phosphate binders such as sevelamer and lanthanum carbonate. To avoid calcium and/or vitamin D deficiency, it may be necessary to supplement these nutrients to maintain normal calcium homeostasis while avoiding a positive calcium balance, which KDIGO recommends be prevented in advanced CKD. These general therapeutic measures will not be further discussed because they are part of clinical routine. Here, we focus on more specific treatment and prevention modalities in CKD. Parathyroidectomy

Phosphate retention and hypocalcemia in the context of reduced kidney function lead to secondary hyperparathyroidism at early stages of CKD. Prolonged stimulation of parathyroid function eventually leads to the transformation from diffuse parathyroid hyperplasia to nodular, autonomous parathyroid tissue and resistance to conventional pharmacotherapy. Parathyroidectomy in patients with severe secondary hyperparathyroidism may lead to an improvement of BMD along with a reduction of fracture risk, improvement in cardiovascular disease risk and reduction of all-cause mortality.82, 83, 84 However, surgical parathyroidectomy should be carefully indicated because patients with advanced stage CKD are prone to anesthesia and operation-related complications. Poor indications for parathyroid surgery may lead to poor postoperative outcomes with no improvement in bone disease. Indications for parathyroidectomy include, but are not limited to, symptomatic secondary hyperparathyroidism, calciphylaxis in association with high PTH levels refractory to conventional therapy, and PTH lowering treatment resistance.85

Bisphosphonates

This class of drugs are inorganic pyrophosphate derivatives with high affinity toward hydroxyapatite crystals. They are the most frequently administered antiresorptive agents in osteoporosis management in general population. Even though this therapeutic approach is valid for patients with CKD stage 1 to 3a, in absence of evident CKD-associated bone, the indication of bisphosphonates administration at CKD stages 4 and 5 is far from being established because they may have deleterious effects such as the induction of hypocalcemia, acute kidney injury via acute tubular necrosis, or focal segmental glomerulosclerosis.86 The use of bisphosphonates in patients with stage 4 and 5 CKD is off-label and should be limited to highly selected cases. Moreover, bisphosphonates may interfere with bone mineralization, and rare but serious adverse skeletal effects include osteonecrosis of the jaw and atypical femur fractures. Further, a large-scale cohort study has revealed a 14% higher risk for CKD stage progression with bisphosphonate therapy.87

Denosumab

Denosumab has emerged as a promising antiresorptive treatment option for patients with CKD after the negative experience with bisphosphonates in patients with eGFR < 30 ml/min per 1.73 m2. However, concerns regarding denosumab use in such patients include a higher risk for severe hypocalcemia88 and a potential rebound of bone resorption after denosumab discontinuation, all the more because antiresorptive agents such as the bisphosphonates are not an option for subsequent treatment.88 Moreover, the potential role of denosumab therapy in vascular calcification and cardiovascular event risk is unclear in the context of CKD.

Teriparatide

This compound is a recombinant human PTH, used as an anabolic agent in the management of osteoporosis in general population. Its efficacy and safety were comparable in patients with normal kidney function and those with mild-to-moderate CKD, as established in a large-scale cohort study of > 1621 patients in whom the BMD of lumbar spine and femoral neck was significantly improved.89 However, because people with more severe CKD were excluded from that study, it is impossible to extrapolate the results to them. It is noteworthy that a postmarketing observational study of 33 patients with advanced CKD receiving daily teriparatide therapy for ≤24 months showed beneficial effects including improvement in BMD and P1NP levels90; however, the small number of patients included does not allow for a definitive conclusion. The potentially beneficial effect of teriparatide on bone formation in patients with CKD is probably because of the modality of intermittent administration, in contrast to the deleterious effects of secondary hyperparathyroidism on bone turnover with permanently elevated PTH levels. There is a clear need for future large-scale prospective clinical trials to evaluate the role of teriparatide therapy in the management of CKD-associated osteoporosis, especially in patients with kidney failure.

Calcimimetics

The calcium-sensing receptor has become a key target in CKD-MBD. Type II agonists of the calcium-sensing receptor, namely cinacalcet and etelcalcetide, act as positive allosteric modulators to reduce the set-point for systemic calcium homeostasis, leading to a decline in the serum levels of calcium and PTH, the calcium × phosphorus product, and FGF-23.91 Both cinacalcet and etelcalcetide have been shown to allow an effective management of secondary hyperparathyroidism. Etelcalcetide certainly offers better adherence and a more favorable gastrointestinal adverse effect profile than cinacalcet, with its pharmacokinetic profile enabling thrice weekly i.v. administration.92 In a randomized head-to-head trial in hemodialysis patients with secondary hyperparathyroidism, etelcalcetide was noninferior and statistically superior to cinacalcet for reducing PTH over 26 weeks, although hypocalcemia was more frequent with etelcalcetide.93 A later systematic review and meta-analysis further supported the efficacy of etelcalcetide in lowering PTH, calcium, and phosphate levels in dialysis patients, while reinforcing the need for calcium monitoring because hypocalcemia risk.94 A meta-analysis evaluating 4 randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trials in 1184 patients with ESKD and poorly controlled secondary hyperparathyroidism (intact PTH ≥ 300 pg/ml) showed that cinacalcet therapy is linked to a statistically significant decline in the risk for parathyroidectomy, fractures, and cardiovascular disease–related hospitalizations.95 Similarly, beneficial effects of cinacalcet therapy on bone histomorphology were reported based on a multicenter clinical trial involving adult patients on dialysis with PTH levels ≥ 300 pg/ml in whom pretreatment and posttreatment bone biopsies were performed. Cinacalcet therapy led, as expected, to a decline in plasma PTH levels and to a significant improvement in bone histology.96 Thus, calcimimetic therapy is a valid therapeutic option for patients with CKD and secondary hyperparathyroidism in whom the administration of activated vitamin D, a sufficient calcium intake, and an unsuccessful control of hyperphosphatemia fail to achieve an adequate response. However, serum calcium levels should be carefully monitored both before and during calcimimetic therapy. Newer calcimimetics have since broadened this class. In a head-to-head phase 3 trial, the oral agent, evocalcet, provided noninferior control of PTH compared with cinacalcet while causing fewer gastrointestinal adverse events.97 Upacicalcet, an injectable agonist that binds a distinct site on the extracellular domain of the calcium-sensing receptor, offers a further optional with a comparatively low risk of hypocalcemia.98

Romosozumab

This monoclonal antibody targets sclerostin. It is both an antiresorptive and anabolic agent used in the management of osteoporosis. Reports on its use in patients with advanced-stage CKD is limited to a few clinical studies. Notably, in the general osteoporosis population, both the ARCH and BRIDGE trial reported a higher incidence of serious cardiovascular events with romosozumab than with alendronate or placebo.99,100 This finding led to regulatory contraindications in patients who have sustained a myocardial infarction or stroke within the preceding year, and warrants particular caution in CKD, where cardiovascular risk is already substantially elevated. Authors of a single-center cohort study involving a total of 13 patients with treatment-naïve osteoporosis undergoing hemodialysis therapy reported a 2.5% to 9% increase in BMD after 1 year of romosozumab therapy, with preservation of improved BMD after 1 additional year of denosumab therapy following romosozumab discontinuation.101 However, coronary artery and thoracic aorta calcification scores increased slightly from baseline to 12 months thereafter. Another single-center trial among 76 patients on hemodialysis treated with romosozumab for >1 year found an improvement of BMD scores at lumbar spine by 15.3% and femoral neck by 7.2%, and this was accompanied by an increase in circulating bone formation markers.102 Major limitations were the failure to assess vascular calcification, a major concern with romosozumab therapy, and the relatively short follow-up period as regards adverse cardiovascular events102

Stage-Specific, Turnover-Based Therapeutic Recommendations

Despite an expanding pharmacological options, CKD stage and bone turnover status remain the 2 most critical determinants of treatment selection, yet practical guidance integrating both dimensions is scarce. In early-stage CKD (eGFR > 30 ml/min per 1.73 m2), antiresorptive therapy with bisphosphonates or denosumab is generally acceptable provided bone turnover status is not severely suppressed, and the KDIGO 2025 Controversies Conference support their use when osteoporotic fracture risk is high and turnover state preserved, but cautions against these agents when turnover is low, because further suppression of an already low-turnover state promotes adynamic bone disease.10 When turnover state is uncertain, the expert consensus recommends prioritizing bone-specific alkaline phosphatase and P1NP to infer the likely turnover state before initiating antiresorptive therapy, because suppressing a preexisting low-turnover state risks promoting adynamic bone disease. In patients with advanced CKD (eGFR < 30 ml/min per 1.73 m2), anabolic agents such as teriparatide or romosozumab represent the preferred initial approach when low bone turnover is suspected, particularly in patients with adynamic bone disease or inappropriately low PTH, because antiresorptive agents may further reduce bone formation and worsen skeletal fragility. Conversely, in advanced CKD with high-turnover disease (elevated PTH and high bone-specific alkaline phosphatase) controlling the hyperparathyroid state through cinacalcet, active vitamin D analogues, or parathyroidectomy should precede any bone-specific pharmacotherapy, because persistent high turnover state undermines the efficacy of anabolic agents and exacerbates bone loss. A particular management dilemma in patients with advanced CKD involves denosumab discontinuation risk. Given that bisphosphonates are largely contraindicated in this group, the standard strategy of bisphosphonate consolidation after denosumab discontinuation is unavailable, leaving patients highly vulnerable to rebound vertebral fractures.

Concerns for KT Recipients

Kidney transplantation, the gold standard therapeutic approach for patients with ESKD providing a significant reduction in morbidity and mortality, has been associated with a considerable decline in BMD over the years along with an increase in fracture risk.103 First, persistent or tertiary hyperparathyroidism-associated hypercalcemia with increased renal tubular calcium reabsorption occurs in ≤59% and 45% of KT recipients at posttransplant months 3 and 12, respectively.104 Moreover, hypercalcemia may persist in ≤18% of recipients by month 12 and 6% even at posttransplant year 10.105 Second, hypophosphatemia associated with urinary phosphorus wasting linked to persistently high levels of FGF-23 and PTH levels and immunosuppressive agents is a frequent complication.106,107 Other contributors to bone disease in this patient population include vitamin D deficiency, corticosteroid and calcineurin inhibitor therapy, poor graft function, and use of anticoagulant drugs.108 The evaluation of short- and long-term alterations of bone structure and function after kidney transplantation has revealed a dramatical decline in BMD in the first 12 to 18 months, ≤9% at spine and hip with stabilization at posttransplant year 3 to 5, and thereafter a significant increase, ≤6%, at posttransplant years 6 to 10.109,110 The initial BMD decline predisposes to an increased fracture risk, as indicated by a 3-fold increase in hip fracture risk in the first 3 posttransplant years, compared with patients undergoing hemodialysis therapy.111 However, the declining exposure to glucocorticoid therapy over the last decade has led to a considerable decline in fracture risk.112 Another aspect of posttransplant osteoporosis to consider is the recognition of various underlying etiologies, including posttransplant high dose glucocorticoid therapy and persistent hyperparathyroidism as the primary causes in the early postsurgical period, whereas tertiary hyperparathyroidism and de novo osteoporosis become the major causes at later time periods. A proper diagnosis of posttransplant bone disease, including the assessment of fracture risk by performing DXA scan or qCT to determine T-scores and the measurement of bone turnover markers to differentiate low from high bone turnover will greatly contribute to patients' health and well-being.

A recent meta-analysis involving a total of 1066 KT recipients from 21 randomized clinical trials has evaluated the efficacy and safety of osteoporosis treatment options, including bisphosphonates, denosumab, and calcitonin. Denosumab was shown to result in superior improvements in lumbar spine BMD whereas bisphosphonates and calcitonin offer improvements in femoral neck BMD.113 However, the relatively low number of clinical trials with denosumab, the lack of studies analyzing the effects of anabolic agents such as teriparatide, abaloparatide, or romosozumab, the short follow-up periods ranging between 6 and 12 months, and lack of data on fracture incidence with strict focus on BMD measurements are among the major limitations of this meta-analysis, precluding any generalizability of the results. Nonetheless, the unique mechanism of action of denosumab via RANKL inhibition and its hepatic metabolism offer advantages over other therapeutics with variable kidney clearance, allowing a more stable plasma drug concentration. In contrast, infectious complications represent a potential concern of denosumab therapy. Although not yet demonstrated in large-scale clinical trials, they should be carefully monitored in upcoming clinical trials in these vulnerable patients population under immunosuppressive therapy.114 As regards the anabolic agent, teriparatide, a randomized controlled trial administering a once-daily dose to 24 adult kidney recipients early posttransplant failed to demonstrate a beneficial effect on femoral neck BMD, lumbar spine BMD, radial BMD, bone matrix mineralization status, or histomorphometricically assessed bone volume.115 However, the small sample size, and the inclusion of KT recipients without knowledge of osteoporosis status were considerable limitations. Moreover, the placebo group had a considerable decline in BMD, suggesting that the relatively stable BMD under teriparatide therapy might have been beneficial. A retrospective observational study in 18 KT recipients with low bone turnover, osteoporosis, and history of osteoporotic fracture revealed a beneficial effect of teriparatide therapy over 1 year on lumbar spine and femoral neck BMD measurements.116 Finally, recent data described a potential test, which may help stratifying hyperparathyroidism subtypes for tailored management. Indeed, oral calcium-mediated PTH suppression at M3 post-KT was linked to lower PTH levels at 1 year, suggesting that both parathyroid gland responsiveness and calcium bioavailability are associated with favorable outcomes in hyperparathyroidism.117

KT recipients are prone to develop osteoporosis, both in the presence of low and high turnover states, similar to patients with ESKD, in association with hypercalcemia, hypercalciuria, hypophosphatemia, vitamin D deficiency, persistent hyperparathyroidism, and immunosuppressive medications, including glucocorticoids. DXA or qCT for BMD assessment in combination with circulating bone turnover marker measurements appear to be valid diagnostic tools for the bone mineral disorder after transplantation, especially for osteoporosis. Denosumab and bisphosphonates appear to be effective and safe therapeutic options whereas reliable information on the effects of anabolic agents, including teriparatide or romosozumab remains relatively poor.

Unmet Needs, Awareness Gaps, and Controversies

Despite accumulating evidence and the generation of new guidelines, the management of renal osteodystrophy and CKD-associated osteoporosis faces underdiagnosis and significant awareness gaps, potentially compromising hard patient outcomes. The KDIGO 2024 Controversies Conference reinforced the evidence and practice gaps, highlighting a disconnect between available diagnostic capabilities and clinical implementation.118 A European survey showed that bone biopsies are performed only exceptionally in clinical practice despite being the gold standard for the diagnosis of renal osteodystrophy.86 The awareness and use of bone turnover biomarkers remain strikingly low in clinical practice. Only 25.5% of nephrologists consider FGF-23 and Klotho testing worthwhile for CKD-MBD monitoring, and 52.8% among them never check bone turnover markers.119 In hindsight, beyond routine monitoring of 25-hydroxyvitamin D (47.2% of nephrologists), most nephrologists rarely assess more recently developed biomarkers such as FGF-23, Klotho, P1NP, CTX, or TRAP-5b. This underuse could indicate a gap between available diagnostic tools and their implementation in clinical practice; however, it is still unclear whether their regular monitoring effectively leads to better patient outcomes.

Patient knowledge gaps exacerbate clinical outcomes. A survey involving 136 patients on hemodialysis revealed that 42% of them were unaware of high phosphate-containing foods, 46% remained unaware of the consequences of hyperphosphatemia, and 74% of patients wanted further information on CKD-MBD when only 28% of nephrologists had provided written materials.120 Medication adherence remains a major challenge among patients with CKD, particularly with phosphate binders. Data from the DOPPS study highlight the magnitude of this issue: 45% of patients reported skipping phosphate binders at least once in the past month, and nonadherence was associated with worse biochemical control of CKD-MBD, including elevated serum phosphorus and PTH levels.121

Therapeutic nihilism is a concerning limitation for CKD-MBD management, where the majority of high-risk patients with CKD with low bone density or prior fractures received no therapy because of perceived contraindications and lack of clinical trials on timing of osteoporosis medications.122 The nihilistic treatment approach persists despite emerging evidence supporting the safety and efficacy of bone-protective therapies across CKD stages when patients are appropriately selected and monitored.123

Diagnosis and management of CKD-MBD is rife with areas of controversy, partly explained by the heterogeneous nature of the condition and limited evidence-based consensus. Target levels of serum PTH epitomize this controversy and evidence gaps, with Japanese guidelines targeting 60 to 240 pg/ml versus KDIGO’s 2 to 9 times upper normal limit. However, by stimulating a randomized clinical trial using vast observational data from >2700 patients, a landmark target trial emulation by Platt et al.124 has challenged this permissive approach, demonstrating that a lower PTH target approach was associated with a 29% reduction in all-cause mortality and a 26% reduction in the composite outcome of cardiovascular disease and death, compared with a higher-target approach. This suggests that allowing PTH to rise progressively even within the widely followed present target ranges may compromise patient survival. Although the results of this target emulation are strongly suggestive, prospective randomized controlled trials are still needed to compare different PTH targets to definitively establish optimal therapeutic thresholds for different CKD stages.

Similarly, the optimal serum phosphate target in patients on dialysis remains undefined in the absence of definitive randomized controlled trials. Although aggressive phosphate binder administration is the current standard of care, the massive daily pill burden, and frequent gastrointestinal adverse effects pose adherence and safety issues.125 The ongoing PHOSPHATE trial (expected to report primary data in 2027) aims to definitively resolve this controversy by randomizing thousands of patients with ESKD to an intensive phosphate target (< 1.5 mmol/l) versus a liberalized target (2.0–2.5 mmol/l) to objectively assess cardiovascular events and overall mortality endpoints.126

Future Directions

The pathophysiology underlying renal osteodystrophy and effective treatment options are still ill-defined. There is a clear need for future preclinical and clinical studies. Burosumab, a relatively novel monoclonal antibody neutralizing FGF-23, with US Food and Drug Administration approval for use in X-linked hypophosphatemia, a fundamentally different disorder from CKD-MBD, has attracted speculative interest as a potential future hypothesis-generating target in CKD-MBD, given the pathophysiological role of FGF-23 excess in left ventricular hypertrophy, secondary hyperparathyroidism, vascular calcification, and other CKD-MBD–related complications. It must be emphasized, however, that there are currently no published clinical trials evaluating burosumab in the CKD-MBD population, and its use in this context cannot be recommended outside of investigational settings. Indeed, a critical safety concern largely unaddressed in preclinical models is that FGF-23 inhibition in nondialysis patients with CKD is expected to exacerbate hyperphosphatemia by removing one of the few phosphaturic mechanisms still operational in declining kidney function, potentially worsening vascular calcification and cardiovascular outcomes, which are the main complications this approach aims to mitigate. This concern must be prominently acknowledged rather than treated as a minor caveat. Burosumab belongs in a speculative, hypothesis-generating discussion with strong precautions. Future clinical trials are required to clarify this issue.

Inorganic Phosphate Transporters

Disturbed phosphorus homeostasis is an early event in CKD progression. It may contribute to bone pathology per se, even before the increase in serum PTH and FGF-23 levels, possibly via a dysregulation of PiT1 and PiT2 expression, the 2 high affinity sodium-dependent type III inorganic phosphate transporters. They are widely expressed in various tissues, including bone, myocardium, and smooth muscle cells, with variable changes in response to the decline in GFR.127 An experimental study in rats with mild CKD showed that PiT1 expression and the downstream ERK2 signaling pathway were downregulated in association with a low bone turnover state, predisposing toward adynamic bone disease.128 A discrepancy between tissue-specific expression of inorganic phosphate transporters in patients with CKD raises the possibility that altered phosphorus homeostasis in CKD is not simply a trigger of bone disease via endocrine disturbances or merely an innocent bystander but plays a fundamental part via alterations in phosphate transport activities in the pathogenesis of CKD-associated bone disease. This in turn may constitute a novel therapeutic target.

Another major aspect of CKD-MBD is the interrelationship with increased cardiovascular morbidity and mortality through vascular calcification, though it is unclear whether the connection is predominantly mediated by mineral and bone derangements or traditional cardiovascular risk factors. The i.v. administration of myo-inositol hexaphosphate (i.e., SNF472) has been shown to downregulate the formation and growth of hydroxyapatite crystals and therefore potentially vascular calcification. A randomized double-blind phase 1b clinical study in patients on hemodialysis demonstrated an effective inhibition of hydroxyapatite crystal formation without any major adverse events.129 A subsequent phase 2b, placebo-controlled, double-blind trial confirmed the beneficial effects of SNF472, attenuating coronary artery calcium and aortic valve calcification compared with standard-of-care, as assessed by computed tomography.130 However, another randomized controlled clinical trial in >202 patients failed to show a significant improvement in DXA scores or fracture risk; therefore, the previously found favorable outcomes in vascular calcifications were not replicated for bone outcomes.131 Here again, large scale clinical trials with longer follow-up time periods are needed to determine the efficacy and safety of SNF472 in patients on hemodialysis therapy.

Calciphylaxis represents a severe, distinct manifestation of CKD-related vascular calcification and warrants separate consideration. Current treatment remains multimodal and largely evidence-limited, including wound care, analgesia, infection control, optimization of calcium-phosphate-PTH balance, and off-label use of sodium thiosulfate. A recent meta-analysis did not show clear improvement in lesion healing or survival with sodium thiosulfate.132 SNF472 was subsequently evaluated in the phase 3 CALCIPHYX trial in patients on hemodialysis with ulcerated calciphylaxis lesions; although the trial did not meet its primary wound-healing or pain end points, numerically fewer calciphylaxis-related infections, hospitalizations, and deaths were observed in the treatment arm, supporting further investigation rather than routine clinical use.133

Beyond hydroxyapatite crystallization, several additional pathways relevant to the vascular and skeletal manifestations of CKD-MBD are under active investigation. Magnesium has attracted interest because lower serum magnesium is associated with greater cardiovascular risk and it inhibits calcification experimentally134; however, the randomized MAGiCAL-CKD trial did not demonstrate slowed progression of vascular calcification with oral magnesium supplementation, and a meta-analysis similarly found no significant reduction in vascular calcification scores, tempering earlier enthusiasm.135,136 Another emerging pathway linking skeletal, vascular, and renal components of CKD-MBD is the activin A/activin receptor type IIA axis.137,138 In experimental CKD models, circulating activin A and activin receptor type IIA signaling are increased, and treatment with an activin receptor type IIA ligand trap attenuated vascular disease, vascular calcification, and renal fibrosis, supporting this pathway as a mechanistic therapeutic target.137 Translational interest has therefore focused on activin receptor type IIA–IgG1 fusion protein trap such as sotatercept; in a phase 2 study of patients with ESKD, sotatercept showed a favorable safety profile, and was associated with trends toward dose-related slowing of vascular calcification, though effects on BMD and hard clinical outcomes remain insufficiently established.139 Collectively, these efforts underscore that the cardiovascular and skeletal components of CKD-MBD are tightly interlinked, and that an emphasis on bone metabolism should not obscure the accompanying vascular calcification syndrome.

Conclusion

CKD-MBD encompasses a complex disorder affecting millions of people worldwide; however, its management remains restricted by diagnostic limitations, therapeutic controversies, and implementation challenges (Table 2). An effective management of CKD-MBD demands the transition from therapeutic nihilism toward evidence-based approaches that take into account the systemic nature of CKD-MBD. Only through addressing unmet needs, resolving controversies, and embracing innovation can we improve outcomes for patients with CKD and KT recipients. Finally, both this review and the KDIGO 2025 framework on which it substantially draws are directed at adults; the diagnosis and management of CKD-MBD in children, in whom linear growth and ongoing skeletal development introduce distinct considerations, lie outside the present scope, and are governed by dedicated pediatric guidance.
