Printable grayscale nano-template for full-colour organic light-emitting diode near-eye displays with 3D achromatic metalenses
Authors: Youngsun Jeon, Cherry Park, Junhwa Seong, Junsuk Rho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Achromatic metalenses are essential for enabling compact, full-color imaging systems in augmented and virtual reality displays. Here, we present printable 3D achromatic metalenses using grayscale electron beam lithography (EBL) and dry etching-based height-encoded nano-template. By integrating nanoimprint lithography (NIL) from a nano-template without a soft mold, we realize high fidelity and throughput of 3D achromatic metalenses. The imprinted 3D meta-atoms provide controllable height diversity, introducing an additional degree of freedom for phase engineering even on a low-index resin platform. We design the metalens using a library-based phase-matching, where precomputed electromagnetic responses are used to select meta-atoms that best reproduce the hyperbolic phase simultaneously at RGB wavelengths. Our 3D achromatic metalens with a numerical aperture of 0.31 achieves diffraction-limited focusing at the same focal length across RGB wavelengths. The measured focusing efficiencies are 14.8%, 11.3%, and 12.3% at 450, 532, and 635 nm, respectively. As a proof of concept, we apply our 3D achromatic metalenses to a VR imaging platform, achieving a full-colour image. This work highlights grayscale EBL and NIL as a critical solution for achieving both aberration-free performance and scalable production, paving the way for next-generation compact and full-colour meta-optics.

Introduction

Two-dimensional (2D) metasurfaces composed of periodically arranged meta-atoms, which function as scatterers or waveguides, enable diffractive wavefront transformation at the wavelength scale. They exploit various degrees of freedom of light, such as phase, amplitude, and polarization, to encode multifunctional optical behaviors. These capabilities have been demonstrated in applications ranging from lenses1–11, holography12,13, waveguide14,15, structured light16,17, and augmented reality (AR)/ virtual reality (VR) devices18–25, highlighting their unprecedented potential as alternatives to conventional refractive optics with lightweight and ultracompact form factors. However, chromatic aberration arising from the dispersion of wavelength remains an inherent challenge. As chromatic aberration limits imaging quality, addressing this issue is critical for realizing practical full-colour optical systems. In parallel, there has been growing interest in computational imaging-assisted metalens for high-resolution real scene imaging26,27, further highlighting the increasing demand for high-performance metalens and underscoring the importance of achromatic metalenses as a promising solution for next-generation meta-optics28,29.

Over the past decade, significant advancements have been achieved in the development of achromatic metalenses. At the design level, dispersion-engineered meta-atoms have been introduced to mitigate chromatic aberration30–34. However, these approaches need high aspect ratio or intricate nanostructures, such as dual elements within a period, cross-shaped, or donut-shaped, to match high group delay. Due to the limitations of nanofabrication, variation in the vertical dimension (height) of each meta-atom has been neglected. To address these limitations, overlay fabrication-driven approaches have been adopted. Notable examples include multilayer35–37 and phase-compensated layers38,39. However, these methods require high-precision alignment, where fabrication defects can accumulate, leading to increased complexity and time.

As an approach to address these limitations, two-photon lithography (TPL) enables the direct writing of arbitrary three-dimensional nanostructures through polymerization, thereby offering unprecedented freedom in both topology design and phase modulation40,41. This allows the multifunctional metasurfaces42,43 and offers a promising pathway for addressing chromatic aberrations44–47. However, the relatively large voxel size of TPL, compared to the electron-beam size, imposes limitations on spatial resolution, making it challenging to fabricate devices in the visible. To date, RGB achromatic metalenses have primarily been realized through the use of high refractive index dielectric and complex-shaped nanostructures combined with advanced designs19,30,31. Furthermore, its inherently low throughput results in high costs, posing a significant barrier to commercialization. Therefore, it is timely to adopt one-step grayscale EBL to freely engineer the heights of meta-atom with high resolution.

Here, we introduce grayscale electron beam lithography (EBL) for scalable fabrication of 3D achromatic metalenses in the visible. Height-encoded nano-templates are fabricated by dry etching immediately after grayscale EBL. By optimizing the dose, development, and etching conditions, precisely controlled depths of holes are realized, enabling direct imprint from nano-template and independently controlled heights of meta-atoms. Unlike conventional nanoimprint lithography, our method does not need a soft mold, which significantly simplifies the replication by curing and easily detaching 3D achromatic metalenses, demonstrating the potential for time-efficient and scalable production. This fabrication strategy enables heights of meta-atom as an additional design degree of freedom, allowing millimeter-scale achromatic metalenses to be realized using simple isotropic geometries (cylindrical and square pillars) even in a low-index resin platform, without relying on high refractive index or complex-shaped nanostructures. By introducing height variations, effective refractive index differences are generated, enabling sufficient phase modulation and easily realization of RGB achromatic behavior. This demonstrates that the existing limitations can be effectively addressed through advanced nanofabrication. To validate our streamlined fabrication, we demonstrate its application as a compact and aberration-free VR platform.

Results

Optimization of height-encoded nano-template

The fabrication schematic of the height-encoded nano-template, based on grayscale EBL48–50 and dry etching, is illustrated in Fig. 1a. We utilize ZEP-520A51,52, a positive electron beam resist with high sensitivity and excellent etch resistance, serving as a grayscale etch mask. With a glass transition temperature of approximately 120 °C and the capability to achieve high-contrast patterning down to ~25 nm, making it suitable for high-resolution grayscale EBL52. After modulating the resist depth through the development, inductively coupled plasma dry etching is employed using CHF3 (30 sccm) and SF6 (90 sccm) under fixed RF power of 700 W and bias power of 50 W. During the dry etching, the input gases are dissociated into highly reactive F radicals in the plasma state. These F radicals readily react with the target materials to accelerate etching. In this process, etch inhibitors such as fluorocarbon polymers are generated as by-products, which passivate the sidewalls and promote anisotropic etching53. Under these conditions, the etch rates of Si and ZEP-520A are measured to be 8.2 nm/s and 3.6 nm/s, respectively (Supplementary Note 1). We exposed a 100 nm diameter circle pattern with a 450 nm period, varying doses at 400 μC/cm2 steps, followed by development. The etching time is fixed at 200 s. Under these conditions, the imprinted meta-atom, which begins to emerge only above the threshold dose, exhibited a linear increase in size with increasing dose, ranging from 200 nm in diameter and 600 nm in height to 350 nm in diameter and 900 nm in height (Fig. 1b). To analyze the effect of development time, we varied only the development time (3 min and 4 min), while keeping other conditions fixed. By comparing the results of nano-templates and meta-atoms fabricated under development times of 3 min (Fig. 1c) and 4 min (Supplementary Note 2), we set the development time to 3 min for subsequent fabrications. Furthermore, by sweeping both the exposed pattern size and dose of electron beam (Supplementary Note 3), we obtained a 3D meta-atom library with varying diameters and heights (Fig. 1d). While grayscale EBL inherently enables the realization of continuously increased size of 3D meta-atoms, we discretized with predefined dose steps for fabrication simplicity. To validate the streamlined fabrication, we designed a geometric dose map containing the diameter of the exposure pattern and dose information for each segment, in order to fabricate a ‘pizza’-shaped, 6-segmented circular pattern with target diameters and heights (Fig. 1e). Using the geometric dose map and streamlined fabrication, the height-encoded nano-template is fabricated (Fig. 1f). Design of 3D achromatic metalenses

To realize the 3D achromatic metalens under fabrication constraints, we developed a library-based phase matching framework constrained to fabrication-compatible nanostructures. The use of a grayscale-defined 3D structure library is motivated by the need for expanded phase-control freedom in RGB achromatic metalens design, since a simple single-height 2D pillar library provides substantially more limited phase coverage for multi-wavelength phase matching (Supplementary Note 4). These meta-atom geometries consist of isotropic cylindrical and square pillar structures, each varying in diameter and height. In simulation, all structures are implemented based on a nanoimprint resin with a refractive index of ~1.5 (Supplementary Note 5). Importantly, we exploit the rotational symmetry of the target hyperbolic phase and perform the optimization in a 1D radial domain, which is subsequently mapped to a full 2D aperture. This strategy enables scalable design for millimeter-scale apertures while significantly reducing computational cost compared to pixel-wise optimization. To further reduce fabrication complexity and dose-calibration burden, we employ a two-stage, usage-guided library reduction, retaining only the most frequently selected structures for final re-optimization. Phase and transmittance datasets were computed using rigorous coupled wave analysis (RCWA) simulations for all meta-atom structures under the three operating wavelengths (450, 532, and 635 nm). We demonstrated by finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) simulations that variations in the diameter and height of the meta-atoms significantly influence the resulting phase distributions, as shown in the simulated electric field distributions (Supplementary Note 6). In addition, simulation of fabrication-induced geometrical variations of the square meta-atom is considered (Supplementary Note 7). Each structure was characterized by its wavelength-dependent phase response and transmittance, and fabrication-compatible meta-atoms are indicated as black dots on the phase and transmittance maps in Fig. 2b. The optimized 1D radial indices are then discretized and mapped onto the full 2D aperture based on the radial coordinate, producing the complete structure distribution for the metalens. In this process, we employ a deterministic library-based phase-matching procedure: for each radial position, the ideal hyperbolic phase values at the target RGB wavelengths are compared with the precomputed RCWA phase responses of all candidate meta-atoms, and the geometry with the minimum total phase error is selected. While this study focuses on the NA = 0.31 and diameter of 1.2 mm, the same phase-driven optimization approach can be flexibly applied to other NA and lens diameters, enabling scalable and customizable achromatic metalens designs. As further supported by additional simulations with varying NA and enlarged aperture size in Supplementary Note 8. The initial full-library selection can yield an excessively diverse set of structures, which increases fabrication burden, particularly in grayscale EBL where each distinct geometry may require separate dose calibration. To reduce this complexity, we analyzed the usage frequency of all selected geometries in the initial lens layout and retained only the 20 most frequently selected meta-atoms. The corresponding usage-count distribution is shown in Supplementary Note 9. Here, these selected meta-atoms are then translated into corresponding exposure area and dose conditions, enabling their direct realization through grayscale EBL. A second-stage phase matching was then performed using only this reduced subset under the same multi-wavelength objective. This usage-guided reduction preserved the achromatic focusing performance while substantially decreasing the number of unique geometries and the associated calibration complexity. We note that restricting the design to the 20 most frequently selected meta-atoms has only a minor effect on performance. Compared with the full-library design, the focusing efficiency changed by only 0.24%, 0.10%, and 0.19% at 450, 532, and 635 nm, respectively. Figure 2c shows the meta-atoms in the reduced 3D meta-atom library and their corresponding phase matching at 450, 532, and 635 nm. Target and simulated phase profiles are compared, confirming accurate multi-wavelength behavior. These structures are directly translated into a geometric dose map, enabling direct implementation of the achromatic metalens through grayscale EBL. Based on this map, the desired meta-atom geometries can be realized by exposing each pixel with the corresponding dose, thereby achieving control over both diameter and height. The focusing efficiency was quantified as the encircled energy within a circular region of radius 3×FWHM of the focal-spot intensity profile. The resulting efficiencies were 0.254, 0.189, and 0.202 at 450, 532, and 635 nm, respectively. This methodology enables precise pixel-by-pixel design of RGB achromatic metalens using only a small number of unit meta-atoms, significantly reducing complexity while preserving optical performance. More importantly, this approach changes the fabrication strategy for propagation-phase achromatic metalenses by achieving RGB achromatic phase matching through grayscale-defined height variation using fabrication-compatible simple pillar structures, rather than relying on highly complex in-plane meta-atom geometries or high-index dielectric platforms.

Scalable fabrication of 3D achromatic metalenses

The fabrication schematic of 3D achromatic metalens is illustrated in Fig. 3a. ZEP-520A is spin-coated at 1000 rpm for 60 s, and pre-baked at 180 °C for 5 min. Graphic design system (GDS) files are generated by grouping meta-atoms with the same target diameter and height (Supplementary Note 10), and each GDS file is exposed under optimized dose through EBL (ELS-7800, Elionix). The sample is immersed in the developer (ZED-50, Zeon) for 3 min. Subsequently, the sample is annealed on a hotplate at 120 °C for 2 min to enhance the etch resistance of the mask. To ensure smooth release of the resin from the nano-template during nanoimprint, a vapor self-assembled monolayer coating (Trichloro-1H, 1H, 2H, 2H-Perfluorooctyl-silane, Sigma-Aldrich) is treated at 130 °C for 10 min. Unlike conventional master molds used in nanoimprint, which are positive structures, the fabricated nano-template is a negative structure, which is mechanically robust and ensures high repeatability over multiple imprint cycles (Supplementary Note 13).

Integration of 3D achromatic metalens with virtual-reality setup and imaging

To validate our streamlined fabrication, our 3D achromatic metalens is applied in compact full-colour VR devices, which creates an immersive experience and has significant attention as next-generation displays. Near-eye VR systems require compact, lightweight, achromatic, cost-effective, and scalable production. Recently, meta-optics has emerged as a promising alternative to overcome these limitations. Our 3D achromatic metalenses are imprinted onto a PET film, making them lighter and thinner than conventional meta-optics fabricated on glass substrates55,56, while also enabling scalable and cost-effective fabrication. A schematic of the VR setup integrated with 3D achromatic metalenses is shown in Fig. 5a. In our prototype, we obtained VR images from an OLED display by simply adjusting the distance between the 3D achromatic metalenses and the complementary metal oxide semiconductor (CMOS) sensor (Fig. 5b). As a proof of concept, we generated virtual images of target image using chromatic metalens and our 3D achromatic metalens (Fig. 5c). In the chromatic metalens, the image planes differ for each wavelength, making full-colour imaging impossible. In contrast, our 3D achromatic metalens enables full-colour VR imaging without chromatic blur across RGB wavelengths at a fixed image plane (Supplementary Note 20). Discussion

In this work, we successfully demonstrate the nanofabrication of a height-encoded printable nano-template by optimizing grayscale EBL and dry etching. The nano-structures imprinted from nano-template enable the realization of varying heights in a single step, which introduces an additional degree of freedom into the traditional metalens design method based on propagation phase. The metalens is designed using a library-based phase-matching framework based on a 3D meta-atom library spanning diameter and height, where precomputed phases are used to minimize the wrapped phase error across RGB wavelengths. To improve manufacturability, we further apply a two-stage, usage-guided subset selection to reduce the number of unique geometries required for calibration and fabrication. This approach relaxes practical constraints on aperture scaling and NA that often arise in dispersion-engineered achromatic metalenses.

As an application, the imprinted 3D achromatic metalens realizes full-colour VR systems. Recent research trends focus on utilizing diverse design methodologies to improve performance and functionalities. The 3D achromatic metalens realized through our optimized fabrication processes satisfies key requirements for the scalable production of VR systems, including aberration-free performance, light-weight form factor, and high-throughput. Compared to fabrication methods for conventional meta-optics, our method is simpler, cost-effective, and has the potential to be extended to large-area and scalable manufacturing. Therefore, our nanofabrication of 3D achromatic metalens provides a practical route toward future VR and meta-optics. This work highlights that advances in nanofabrication are important for enabling both high performance and scalable manufacturing for next-generation meta-optics.

Methods

Numerical simulation

We employ the in-house RCWA code, which is used to calculate phase and transmission in this study. The commercial FDTD solver from Lumerical Inc., ANSYS, is used to calculate the E-field distribution of different meta-atom across RGB wavelengths.

Optical measurement

To characterize the focusing and imaging performance of the metalens, a fiber-coupled RGB laser system was employed. Diode-pumped solid-state (DPSS) lasers with wavelengths of 450 nm (B), 532 nm (G), and 635 nm (R) were directed through a fiber-coupling module to generate clean Gaussian beam profiles. The output beams passed through a beam splitter and mirrors, allowing the three wavelengths to be aligned into a single optical path. The combined RGB beam was cropped using an iris to match the diameter of the metalens aperture. The collimated beam was then incident on the metalens. The diverging beam was collected by a commercial 50× objective lens (MXPLFLN, Olympus, NA = 0.5), followed by a tube lens, and imaged onto a CCD camera (INFINITY2-1RC, Lumenera). The imaging setup followed a similar configuration. The RGB beam was passed through the same objective lens and was directed toward a negative 1951 USAF resolution target (R3L1S4N, Thorlabs). The objective lens tightly focused the beam to ensure uniform illumination across the target area. The transmitted light through the target was then incident on the metalens. The resulting images were collected using an sCMOS camera (pco.panda 4.2 bi UV, Excelitas Technologies), with the metalens, tube lens, and camera mounted on a motorized linear stage for precise axial modulation. For the VR imaging demonstration, an OLED display panel (iPad Pro 3rd generation, Apple Inc.) was used as an image source. The emitted light from the display was directly imaged through the metalens, and the captured images were recorded using a 9-megapixel USB camera module (Arducam EK007, Arducam) equipped with an MT9N001 CMOS sensor.
