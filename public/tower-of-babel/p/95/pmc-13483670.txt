Prenatal diagnosis and estimated penetrance of 15q11.2 BP1–BP2 microdeletion syndrome in a Chinese cohort: a retrospective study and literature review
Authors: Yiqing Yu, Mengmeng Li, Jiazhen Chang, Hanzhe Zhang, Xiya Zhou, Yulin Jiang, Na Hao, Xueting Yang, Kaili Yin, Yan Lü, Qingwei Qi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe 15q11.2 BP1–BP2 microdeletion (Burnside-Butler syndrome) is a recurrent copy number variation associated with variable neurodevelopmental outcomes. Its incomplete penetrance and broad phenotypic spectrum challenge prenatal genetic counseling. This study aimed to characterize prenatal and postnatal clinical outcomes associated with the 15q11.2 BP1–BP2 microdeletion and to provide evidence to inform prenatal genetic counseling.ResultsAmong 5246 high-risk pregnancies undergoing invasive prenatal diagnosis at a single center between January 2022 and February 2026, 25 fetuses with 15q11.2 BP1–BP2 microdeletion were identified by chromosomal microarray analysis. Of these, 9 fetuses showed soft ultrasound markers, 3 had fetal growth restriction, and 3 had major structural anomalies. Five pregnancies were electively terminated, whereas 20 were continued. Postnatal follow-up ranging from 3 months to 3 years and 8 months revealed no developmental or growth abnormalities among live-born children (penetrance 0%). A literature review of prenatally diagnosed cases showed that 65 of 127 fetuses had abnormal prenatal ultrasound findings, while postnatal phenotypic abnormalities were reported in 12 of 77 live-born children. When the analysis was restricted to cases with standardized pediatric follow-up (n = 55), the penetrance decreased to 5.5%.ConclusionsOur data indicate that 15q11.2 BP1–BP2 microdeletion generally has favorable early postnatal outcomes. Given the low penetrance observed in prenatally ascertained cohorts and the mild effect size of this copy number variation, our findings suggest that the clinical implications of routinely reporting isolated 15q11.2 BP1–BP2 microdeletion in the prenatal setting may need to be interpreted with caution. Longer-term follow-up studies remain necessary to further clarify the neurodevelopmental impact of this microdeletion.Keywords: 15q11.2 BP1−BP2 microdeletion, Burnside-Butler syndrome, Prenatal diagnosis, Penetrance

Background

Chromosome 15 has five common breakpoints (BP) located at the proximal end of the long arm, which are usually referred to as BP1−BP5. These breakpoints facilitate non-allelic homologous recombination, leading to the occurrence of chromosome microdeletions and duplications [1]. As the first examples of genomic imprinting in humans, Prader-Willi syndrome (PWS) and Angelman syndrome (AS) are generally due to a chromosome 15q11–q13 deletion between BP3 to BP1 or BP2 depending on the parent-of-origin [2–5]. When the deletion fragments in PWS/AS patients involved the BP1–BP2 region, the severity of their cognitive function and neurobehavioral abnormalities was significantly higher than that of patients with normal BP1–BP2 fragments [6–8], thus suggesting that the BP1–BP2 region is a susceptible area for neuro-abnormalities.

The 15q11.2 BP1–BP2 microdeletion syndrome (Burnside-Butler syndrome, BBS) affects the ~ 500 kb region between breakpoints BP1 and BP2, containing four highly conserved and bidirectionally expressed non-imprinted OMIM genes: TUBGCP5, CYFIP1, NIPA2, and NIPA1 [9–11]. All these four genes are expressed in the central nervous system, involved in axonal growth and neural connectivity [12]. Large population-based cohort studies [13–15] have demonstrated that carriers from the general population show statistically significant but modest reductions in cognitive performance compared with non-carriers. In contrast, more severe neurodevelopmental and neuropsychiatric phenotypes reported in clinically ascertained cohorts are thought to be influenced by ascertainment bias.

Although the ClinGen database has rated it as a pathogenic copy number variation (CNV) in 2021 [16], its estimated penetrance of approximately 10.4% [11, 17], together with marked phenotypic heterogeneity, poses substantial challenges for prenatal genetic counseling. To date, most cohort studies have focused on postnatal populations enriched for neurodevelopmental disorders, whereas prenatal cohort data remain limited. In this study, we conducted a retrospective analysis of fetuses carrying the 15q11.2 BP1–BP2 microdeletion identified through prenatal diagnosis at a single center, and summarized published prenatal studies, aiming to provide empirical evidence to support informed prenatal diagnostic assessment, counseling, and postnatal follow-up.

Methods

Study population and samples

Between January 2022 and February 2026, a total of 5246 pregnant women with high-risk criteria, including advanced maternal age (≥ 35 years), sonographic abnormalities, abnormal prenatal screening results, or other pertinent clinical indications, underwent invasive prenatal diagnosis in Peking Union Medical College Hospital, Beijing, China. The genetic testing protocol consisted of chromosomal karyotype analysis and chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA). Comprehensive post-test counseling was provided, including interpretation of the genetic findings, discussion of their clinical significance, and guidance regarding potential implications for pregnancy management and follow-up. A total of 25 cases identified with a 15q11.2 BP1–BP2 microdeletion were retrospectively reviewed, yielding a detection rate of 0.48%. Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Peking Union Medical College Hospital [Approval No. K25C0553]. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment. The study was conducted in strict adherence to applicable ethical regulations, clinical practice norms, and the STROBE reporting framework.

G-banding chromosome karyotype analysis

15 mL of amniotic fluid or 5 mg chorionic villi was allocated for cell culture. Chorionic villi were mechanically minced to obtain a single-cell suspension, whereas amniotic fluid cells were pelleted by centrifugation. Cells from both sample types were maintained in a semi-open incubator and monitored microscopically to assess cellular proliferation and morphology. Once well-defined spindle-shaped fibroblast colonies exhibiting robust growth were observed, cultures were harvested and prepared for G-banding. Chromosomal analysis was performed at the 400-band resolution level. For each case, 20 karyotypes were counted and 15 cell colonies were analyzed. If any abnormalities were found, additional metaphases were counted.

CMA

10 mL of amniotic fluid or 10 mg chorionic villi was collected to extract the genomic DNA of the fetus by the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA). 2 mL of peripheral blood from the parents were collected for parental origin analysis. The extracted DNA was subsequently digested, ligated to adaptors, amplified, purified, and biotin-labeled. The biotin-labeled DNA was hybridized onto the Affymetrix CytoScan 750 K array (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and data analysis was conducted using Chromosome Analysis Suite (ChAS) version 4.3. Genomic coordinates were annotated based on the GRCh37/hg19 reference genome. Copy number variations (CNVs) larger than 200 kb or encompassing more than 50 markers were considered significant and reported. The classification and analysis of CNVs were based on international public databases and published literature, including OMIM database (Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man, http://www.omim.org/), DECIPHER database (Database of Chromosomal Imbalance and Phenotype in Humans using Ensembl Resources, https://decipher.sanger.ac.uk/), ClinVar database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/), UCSC (http://genome.ucsc.edu/), ClinGen dosage sensitivity database (ClinGen Dosage Sensitivity Map, https://www.clinicalgenome.org), and PubMed. CNVs were classified according to the relevant guidelines of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) [18] and ClinGen dosage sensitivity database.

Pregnancy outcomes and follow-up

Prenatal genetic testing and ultrasonographic findings, pregnancy outcomes, and postnatal conditions of the offspring were recorded. Retrospective follow-up of the infants was performed through telephone interviews and the Beijing Municipal Child Health Care System, with primary attention to growth retardation and developmental delay. Follow-up was conducted until February 2026.

Literature review of prenatal cases

The PubMed (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and Web of Science (http://www.webofscience.com) databases were systematically searched using the following keywords and subject terms: “15q11.2 BP1–BP2 microdeletion”, “15q11.2 microdeletion” and “Burnside-Butler Syndrome” in combination with “prenatal” and “prenatal diagnosis”. The last search was updated in December 2025. Only studies published in English were included. Data on fetuses with 15q11.2 BP1–BP2 microdeletion identified during prenatal diagnosis including clinical features and variant information were collected and sorted out.

Results

Subject information

In the present study, we retrospectively analyzed prenatal diagnosis results from 5246 pregnant women. A total of 25 cases detected with 15q11.2 BP1–BP2 microdeletion were enrolled. Among the 25 pregnant women, the mean age was 33.6 ± 3.79 years, with ages ranging from 26 to 39 years. Indications for invasive prenatal diagnosis included ultrasonographic soft markers in 9 cases (36.0%), ultrasonographic structural abnormalities in 3 cases (12.0%), fetal growth restriction in 3 cases (12.0%), advanced maternal age in 10 cases (40.0%), a history of adverse pregnancy outcomes in 5 cases (20.0%), and abnormal prenatal screening results in 4 cases (16.0%). Nine pregnancies had dual indications, consisting of either advanced maternal age combined with ultrasonographic abnormalities, or advanced maternal age combined with a history of adverse pregnancy outcomes, or ultrasonographic soft markers combined with fetal growth restriction (Table 1). The prenatal samples included 1 chorionic villus sample and 24 amniotic fluid samples.

Prenatal diagnosis results

As demonstrated in Table 1, 22 cases presented with an isolated 15q11.2 BP1–BP2 microdeletion, while three cases exhibited chromosomal aneuploidies in combination with the 15q11.2 BP1–BP2 microdeletion. As summarized in Table 1, the deletion sizes in these cases ranged from 312 to 855 kb, and all deletions encompassed the four OMIM genes TUBGCP5, CYFIP1, NIPA2, and NIPA1. Following genetic counseling, the parents of 14 fetuses declined parental origin testing. Among the remaining 11 families who underwent verification, the 15q11.2 BP1–BP2 microdeletion was maternally inherited in 3 cases, paternally inherited in 7 cases, and occurred de novo in 1 case. Clinical history and brief developmental assessments were obtained for the carrier parents during genetic counseling visits. Among the 10 parents carrying the microdeletion, one father (case 8) reported delayed speech during early childhood but had no subsequent neurodevelopmental abnormalities in adulthood. The remaining carrier parents did not report any history of neurodevelopmental disorders or related clinical manifestations.

Prenatal findings, pregnancy outcomes, and postnatal follow-up

Among the 25 fetuses identified with the 15q11.2 BP1–BP2 microdeletion in our high-risk prenatal cohort, prenatal ultrasonography detected soft markers, structural anomalies, or growth restriction in 13 cases. Two fetuses presented with more than one ultrasonographic finding. Among these, 9 fetuses presented with ultrasonographic soft markers. Two pregnancies were terminated due to trisomy 21, one pregnancy had not yet reached delivery at the time of analysis (February 2026) and remained under routine prenatal follow-up, and the remaining six showed normal outcomes. Fetal growth restriction was observed in three fetuses. Two of these demonstrated catch-up growth during the mid-to-late gestational period and had normal birth weights. In contrast, one fetus (case 18) continued to meet the diagnostic criteria for FGR on subsequent prenatal evaluations and had not yet been delivered at the time of analysis. Major structural anomalies were identified in three cases. One fetus with congenital diaphragmatic hernia continued the pregnancy and underwent ex-utero intrapartum treatment (EXIT) at delivery, whereas the pregnancies complicated by polycystic kidney disease and holoprosencephaly were electively terminated.

Following comprehensive genetic counseling, five pregnancies were electively terminated, including three cases with chromosomal aneuploidies (cases 21, 23, and 25) and two with severe structural anomalies (cases 22 and 24). Of the remaining 20 pregnancies that were continued, three had not yet reached delivery at the time of analysis, two resulted in preterm delivery, and the remaining fifteen resulted in full-term deliveries. Postnatal follow-up was conducted for all 17 fetuses, with ages ranging from 3 months to 3 years and 8 months at the time of assessment. No abnormalities were observed in the 17 children.

Discussion

In this study, we retrospectively analyzed 25 fetuses prenatally diagnosed with the 15q11.2 BP1–BP2 microdeletion, with a total of 17 fetuses followed postnatally. Our findings provide insights into the prenatal phenotypic spectrum, pregnancy outcomes, and postnatal development associated with this microdeletion, highlighting both the clinical challenges and the importance of genetic counseling.

Prenatal phenotypic spectrum and outcomes

In our prenatal cohort of 25 fetuses with the 15q11.2 BP1–BP2 microdeletion, approximately half presented with prenatal ultrasonographic findings, including soft markers, fetal growth restriction, and structural anomalies. This observation is consistent with the variable expressivity reported in previous studies [19–21], indicating that although the 15q11.2 BP1–BP2 microdeletion may be associated with both subtle and overt structural abnormalities, a substantial proportion of fetuses may exhibit no discernible prenatal anomalies. Notably, postnatal follow-up of all children revealed normal development, corresponding to an observed penetrance of 0% in our cohort. Although our cohort suggests reassuring postnatal outcomes for fetuses with 15q11.2 BP1–BP2 microdeletion, the single-center design and relatively small sample size limit the generalizability of these findings. This underscores the need to review and synthesize published prenatal cases to more comprehensively characterize the phenotypic spectrum and provide evidence-based guidance for genetic counseling.

Literature review

Following a comprehensive literature search and rigorous eligibility screening (Fig. 1), a total of 9 studies [19–27] were identified (Table 2), collectively reporting 137 prenatally diagnosed cases involving the 15q11.2 microdeletion. The microdeletion is typically identified by CMA, with reported prenatal detection rates ranging from 0.2% to 0.7% of all invasive diagnostic samples. In the aggregated literature, size of the deletions ranged from 310 kb to 7999 kb. A total of 61 cases underwent parental origin analysis, whereas 76 cases were not subjected to such testing for various reasons.

Excluding only one study that did not report prenatal ultrasound findings, a total of 127 cases were available for analysis. Approximately half of these fetuses (n = 65) showed abnormal ultrasound findings, including structural anomalies, soft markers, and fetal growth restriction. Congenital heart defects were the most frequently reported structural anomalies, while increased nuchal translucency represented the most common soft marker. No characteristic or syndrome-specific prenatal phenotype has been identified, underscoring the low predictive value of prenatal ultrasound for the neurodevelopmental risk associated with this microdeletion.

Excluding one study that did not report postnatal follow-up data, outcome information was available for 98 prenatally diagnosed cases. Of these, 21 pregnancies were electively terminated because of severe structural anomalies detected on prenatal ultrasound, whereas 77 pregnancies were carried to term after receiving specialized genetic counseling. Postnatal phenotypic abnormalities were reported in 12 of the 77 children who were followed after birth. The reported manifestations were heterogeneous and included growth retardation, neurodevelopmental delay, autism spectrum disorder and ataxia.

Combining our single-center cohort with previously published cases, a total of 162 fetuses carrying the 15q11.2 BP1–BP2 microdeletion were included. The overall prenatal detection rate remains low, ranging from 0.2% to 0.7%, consistent with the 0.48% observed in our cohort, highlighting the rarity of this microdeletion and the critical role of CMA in prenatal screening. Prenatal ultrasound data were available for 152 fetuses, of which approximately half showed ultrasonographic findings, including structural anomalies, soft markers, or fetal growth restriction. Postnatal follow-up data were available for 94 fetuses carried to term, of whom 12 exhibited phenotypic abnormalities, corresponding to an overall observed penetrance of approximately 12.8%. The reported abnormalities were heterogeneous, but most children demonstrated normal postnatal development, consistent with a low penetrance of clinically significant phenotypes among prenatally identified carriers.

Penetrance and genetic counseling

According to the Guideline for the application of chromosomal microarray analysis in prenatal diagnosis (2023, China) [28], susceptible CNVs associated with neurodevelopmental disorders may be stratified for reporting based on penetrance, with low-penetrance variants (recommended threshold < 10%) eligible for non-reporting. This guideline reflects a growing consensus that penetrance, rather than mere presence of a CNV, should guide clinical interpretation and risk communication in prenatal diagnosis.

Previous studies, using Bayesian models that incorporate background disease prevalence, suggested a penetrance of approximately 10% (95% confidence interval: 9.3–12.0%) [13, 17, 29, 30]. However, Goh et al. [31] proposed a revised penetrance framework designed to reduce inflation attributable to ascertainment bias and baseline risks in 2025. Under this model, the penetrance of the 15q11.2 BP1–BP2 microdeletion was recalculated as 1.3% (95% confidence interval: 1.0–1.6%), substantially lower than earlier estimates, suggesting that prior Bayesian-based approaches may overestimate individual-level risk, particularly for incompletely penetrant CNVs with marked phenotypic heterogeneity. By comparison, penetrance estimates derived from prenatally ascertained cohorts remain limited. In our single-center cohort, all children in follow-up demonstrated normal early development, corresponding to an observed penetrance of 0% during early childhood follow-up. When integrating our cohort with previously published prenatally diagnosed cases, the overall penetrance among pregnancies continued to term was 12.8%.

The discrepancy between the penetrance estimated from our literature-based prenatal cohort and those reported in large postnatal cohorts [13, 17, 29–31] can be primarily attributed to differences in case ascertainment and outcome assessment. All cases included in our analysis were identified prenatally, whereas most large-scale cohorts have been ascertained postnatally. Moreover, in many prenatal studies, postnatal outcomes were not explicitly assessed by pediatric specialists, and phenotypic abnormalities were often reported based on parental descriptions or obstetric follow-up alone, without standardized pediatric or neurodevelopmental evaluation. Accordingly, the accuracy and comparability of penetrance across studies may be limited.

In contrast, all postnatal follow-up data in our cohort were conducted within a structured child health surveillance system embedded in the Beijing pediatric healthcare network, ensuring standardized professional pediatric assessment. Among the published studies included in our review, only three [19, 21, 22] explicitly reported postnatal evaluations conducted within specialized pediatric systems. When restricting the analysis to these studies together with our cohort, phenotypic abnormalities were observed in 3 of 55 children, corresponding to a penetrance of 5.5% (Fig. 2).

Prenatal genetic counseling for the 15q11.2 BP1–BP2 microdeletion remains particularly challenging. Under the Guideline for the application of chromosomal microarray analysis in prenatal diagnosis (2023, China) [28], susceptible CNVs associated with neurodevelopmental disorders may be considered non-reportable when classified as low-penetrance variants (recommended threshold < 10%). Previous penetrance estimates [13, 17, 29, 30] for this microdeletion clustered around the 10% threshold, placing 15q11.2 BP1–BP2 in a borderline category for prenatal reporting. This challenge is further compounded by the fact that functional neurodevelopmental disorders, unlike structural birth defects, lack reliable prenatal diagnostic methods, limiting risk stratification during pregnancy.

In the present study, among fetuses with isolated 15q11.2 BP1–BP2 microdeletion who continued the pregnancy, all demonstrated normal early postnatal development, corresponding to a penetrance of 0%. When our cohort was combined with published prenatal cases [19, 21, 22] that underwent standardized pediatric follow-up, the overall penetrance was 5.5%, well below the 10% reporting threshold and closer to penetrance estimates obtained using baseline risk-adjusted models. Consistent with the low penetrance observed in prenatally ascertained cohorts, most carrier parents in our cohort appeared clinically unaffected based on clinical history obtained during genetic counseling. We therefore recognize that 15q11.2 BP1–BP2 microdeletion is a pathogenic CNV with a mild effect size. However, reporting this finding in a prenatal setting may disproportionately increase parental anxiety without providing actionable prognostic value or influencing pregnancy management decisions. Based on the currently available evidence, we suggest that the clinical implications of routinely reporting isolated 15q11.2 BP1–BP2 microdeletion in the prenatal setting may need to be interpreted with caution, while emphasizing careful exclusion of major structural abnormalities and comprehensive post-test counseling.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several notable strengths. First, all 17 cases in our single-center cohort underwent systematic postnatal follow-up within the Beijing Child Health Surveillance System, ensuring standardized pediatric assessment and reliable penetrance estimation. Second, we integrated published prenatal cases (n = 137) to provide a comprehensive overview of prenatal ultrasound findings and postnatal outcomes, supporting evidence-based prenatal counseling. Third, we performed stratified analysis based on follow-up quality, highlighting the influence of specialized pediatric assessment on penetrance estimates and offering clinically actionable insights.

Several limitations should also be noted. First, our cohort is relatively small and derived from a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, the duration of postnatal follow-up ranged from 3 months to 3 years and 8 months. Certain neurodevelopmental or neurobehavioral manifestations associated with the 15q11.2 BP1–BP2 microdeletion, such as learning difficulties or subtle behavioral traits, may become apparent only during later childhood or school age. Therefore, our findings mainly reflect early postnatal outcomes rather than long-term neurodevelopmental trajectories.

Conclusions

In this study, we report 25 fetuses prenatally identified 15q11.2 BP1–BP2 microdeletion from a single prenatal diagnostic center. By integrating these data with previously published prenatal cohorts with standardized pediatric follow-up, we found that the majority of fetuses exhibit normal early postnatal development, with an overall penetrance of 5.5%. Given these findings and current guideline recommendations, we suggest that the clinical implications of routinely reporting isolated 15q11.2 BP1–BP2 microdeletion in the prenatal setting may may need to be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, careful evaluation of major structural anomalies should be ensured, and routine postnatal developmental monitoring should be maintained. Longer-term follow-up studies remain necessary to further clarify the neurodevelopmental impact of this microdeletion.
