The Ralstonia solanacearum catalase-peroxidase KatG is crucial to survive environmental stresses and also plays a role in plant infection
Authors: Jordi Corral, Mercedes Rocafort, M Belén Ripa, Agustina Vandecaveye, Magdalena Pezzoni, Alicia Invernon, Núria S Coll, Marc Valls, Elena G Orellano
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThroughout their life cycle, pathogens are challenged by Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) and must deploy defence mechanisms against oxidative damage. Environmental stressors such as ultraviolet radiation and desiccation induce intracellular ROS production. Moreover, during pathogen colonisation, plant hosts initiate an oxidative burst, leading to the accumulation of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). Catalases mitigate ROS toxicity by catalysing the breakdown of H2O2 into water and oxygen. In this study, we explored the roles of the monofunctional catalase (KatE) and the bifunctional catalase-peroxidase (KatG) in the phytopathogen Ralstonia solanacearum during both environmental stress conditions and in planta colonisation stages. Our results demonstrate that both catalases are critical for surviving ROS, with KatG having a more preponderant role. Plant pathogenicity assays show that catalases are not required for virulence although they are important for bacterial fitness in the apoplast. In contrast, we prove that both catalases are vital for R. solanacearum response to oxidative stress in the external environment. KatG was indispensable for survival in soil, as well as for enduring ultraviolet radiation and desiccation exposure. Our findings reveal that the primary function of R. solanacearum catalases is to counteract ROS generated by environmental stressors rather than host-derived oxidative defences. This research identifies specific environmental stressors that these catalases combat during pathogen survival. Introduction

Ralstonia solanacearum is a Gram-negative β-proteobacterium that causes bacterial wilt in over 250 plant species, which results in significant yield losses for economically important crops such as tomato, eggplant, potato, tobacco, and banana1,2. This soil-borne pathogen has a complex life cycle, first it penetrates plant roots through natural wounds3subsequently colonising the root intercellular spaces or apoplast3 and moving to the xylem vessels4where bacterial multiplication together with exopolysaccharide production causes vascular obstruction and plant wilting5,6. At the end of infection, the bacterium is released from dead plant tissue back into the environment. R. solanacearum can survive for long periods of time in waterways or soil, which are important dissemination routes7. Survival in the environment depends on many environmental factors such as temperature, pH levels, salinity, soil moisture and, microbiota composition8–10. However, the molecular mechanisms used by the pathogen to be able to cope with these diverse environmental conditions are unknown.

Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS), such as hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), superoxide radicals (O2−), and hydroxyl radicals (OH−), are highly reactive molecules capable of producing severe oxidative damage to bacterial cells by targeting lipids, nucleic acids, and proteins11–13. Throughout their life cycle, bacterial pathogens like R. solanacearum are exposed to various sources of ROS that threaten their survival and adaptability. During plant colonisation, hosts trigger an early plant immune response, known as oxidative burst, when high ROS concentrations are produced with the aim of hampering pathogen growth14,15. In addition to host-derived ROS, environmental factors also contribute to oxidative stress. Soil conditions such as extreme pH16nutrient deprivation17desiccation8,18and ultraviolet radiation can lead to increased intracellular ROS production in bacteria19. Moreover, ROS can originate endogenously from microbial competition within the soil microbiome20. Antagonistic interactions among microbes, as well as metabolic activities like organic matter decomposition, can further elevate ROS levels in the environment21. Finally, exposure to xenobiotics, such as pesticides, antibiotics, and heavy metals, acts as an exogenous stress factor, also promoting oxidative stress22–24.

As a result of the constant ROS threat throughout their life cycle, bacteria have evolved a plethora of strategies to detoxify these reactive molecules, including scavenging enzymes such as catalases and peroxidases25,26. Inducible enzymes, including superoxide dismutases, catalases, and peroxidases, appear to be the primary components of the bacterial oxidative stress defence system27. Catalases are enzymes that convert hydrogen peroxide to oxygen and water and can be classified into three categories: monofunctional catalases, bifunctional catalases, and manganese-containing catalases28,29. The genome of R. solanacearum strain GMI1000 encodes a monofunctional catalase katE and two bifunctional catalases katGa and katGb, encoded in tandem, hereafter named katG30.

Previous work has shown that the monofunctional catalase KatE is important for the adaptive response of R. solanacearum to oxidative stress30. However, unlike other phytopathogenic bacteria, this enzyme did not contribute to virulence30. Our previous work on transcriptomic analysis of R. solanacearum during different life cycle stages revealed that catalase-encoding genes, particularly katG, are highly expressed in the soil, suggesting an involvement of these enzymes beyond host infection31. Hence, we studied the enzymatic activity of KatG in R. solanacearum and elucidated its role throughout the bacterial life cycle.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains, plasmids, plant material, and growth conditions

All R. solanacearum strains were routinely grown in rich B medium, Boucher’s minimal medium (MM)32 or CPG (Casamino acids-peptone-glucose) medium33 agar plates or broth and incubated at 28 °C with shaking at 180 rpm. When needed, rich B medium was supplemented with 0.5% glucose and 0.005% 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride with 1.5% agar or 0.7% agar34, and MM broth was supplemented with at 2% glycerol final concentrations.

The knockout mutants of ΔkatE (RSp1581), ΔkatG (RSc0775/RSc0776), and the corresponding complemented strains in R. solanacearum GMI1000 were previously constructed by double-joint PCR, as described by Tondo et al. (2020) and Pedro-Jové et al. (2023). R. solanacearum mutants were grown in rich B medium were supplemented with appropriate antibiotics: kanamycin (50 mg/liter), gentamicin (10 mg/liter), and/or tetracycline (5 mg/liter). In planta experiments were conducted using tomato (Solanum lycopersicum cv. Marmande) plants. Plants were routinely grown in a mixed soil of substrate 2 (Klasmann-Deilmann GmbH, Geeste, Germany), perlite, and vermiculite at a proportion of 30:1:1; during 4 weeks at 22 °C, a 60% of relative humidity and under long-day light regimen conditions (16 and 8 h of light-darkness, respectively). Before each assay, tomato plants were previously acclimated 2 days by transferring them to growth chambers at 27 °C under the same humidity and photoperiod conditions. All in planta experiments described below were performed using a completely randomized design to avoid bias from plant size or weight. Additionally, during incubations, plant racks inside were moved daily within the growth chambers to minimise spatial variability.

Exposure of R. solanacearum cultures to Methyl Viologen (MV) and subsequent assessment of catalase activity

To assess the response of R. solanacearum to the redox-cycling agent methyl viologen (MV), bacterial strains were grown in rich B medium supplemented with the appropriate antibiotics until reaching stationary phase. Saturated cultures were diluted 1:100 in fresh rich B medium and incubated at 28 °C with constant agitation (200 rpm) until early exponential growth phase or stationary growth phase (6.5–24 h, respectively). Cultures were then treated with MV at final concentrations of 0 mM (control), 0.5 and 1 mM for 20 min. Subsequently, cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and soluble protein extracts were prepared from the resulting bacterial pellets as following. Bacteria were washed and resuspended in 500 µL of ice-cold 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) containing 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) and then disrupted by intermittent sonication. Bacterial solutions were clarified by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. Protein concentrations in soluble cell extracts were determined by the Sedmak and Grossberg method35 with bovine serum albumin as standard. Catalase activity in cell extracts was monitored through the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide by following the decrease in absorbance at 240 nm36. The assays were performed at 25 °C in 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) containing 10 mM H2O2. To calculate the catalase specific activity an extinction coefficient of 43.6 M− 1 cm− 1 at 240 nm was used. One unit of catalase activity was defined as the amount of activity required to decompose 1 µmol of H2O2 per minute under the assay conditions.

The protein soluble extracts were also analysed by native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis followed by in-gel catalase activity staining.

Native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and catalase activity staining

Non-denaturing 8% (w/v) polyacrylamide gels were prepared in 50 mM glycine buffer (pH 9.5). Electrophoresis was carried out in 50 mM glycine buffer (pH 9.5) at 4 °C under a constant current of 10 mA. Once the tracking dye reached the bottom of the gel, electrophoresis was continued for an additional 30–60 min to achieve optimal band separation.

Immediately after electrophoresis under native conditions, gels were washed three times for 10 min each with distilled water, then incubated for 10 min in 0.9 mM H2O2. After gently rinsing the gels to remove excess H2O2, they were incubated with gentle agitation in a freshly prepared mixture of 2% (w/v) K3Fe(CN)6 and 2% (w/v) FeCl3. Catalase activity was visualized as clear, unstained bands against a coloured background formed by the precipitate, as described by Scandalios37.

Stress assays

To investigate the susceptibility of the different R. solanacearum mutant strains to different stressors overnight cultures adjusted in MQ water at an OD600 of 0.1 (108 CFU/ml) and were exposed to one of the following stressors: 2.5 mM H2O2 (oxidative stress), 0.0075% SDS (envelope stress), 2 µg/ml colistin (envelope stress), 60 mM KNO3 (nitrosative stress) and 20 mg/ml NaCl (osmotic stress). Then, after 2 h of incubation at 28 °C samples were washed to remove the stressors, and serial dilutions were plated in rich B medium plates supplemented with appropriate antibiotics. Each stress condition was tested with a minimum of three biological replicates, each consisting of four technical replicates. Susceptibility of the R. solanacearum mutant strains to oxidative stress was further evaluated with different methods. Method 1: Petri dishes were prepared with 10 ml of 1.5% (w/v) rich B agar medium with 5 ml of 0.7% (w/v) BG-agar medium containing 100 µl of a saturated culture of the strain to be tested. Sterile Whatman paper discs (Sigma Aldrich) were placed on the surface and hydrated with 5 µl of 100 mM H2O2. Cultures were incubated at 28 °C for 24 h, after which sensitivity was assessed by measuring the diameter of the inhibition zones generated. Method 2: R. solanacearum overnight cultures were inoculated into fresh rich B broth and grown to early exponential phase (6.5 h). Cultures were exposed to 1 mM or 2.5 mM H2O2 for 15 min at room temperature. Then, samples were washed, serially diluted and plated on rich B media supplemented with appropriate antibiotics. All experiments were performed with at least three biological replicates, each conducted in triplicate.

Quantification and structural analysis of biofilms

Quantitative analysis of R. solanacearum biofilm formation was carried out through crystal violet assay, following a previously described method38. Briefly, 96-well polystyrene microplates (Greiner, Kremsmünster, Austria) were filled with 95 µl of CPG broth plus 5 µl of each bacterial suspension (OD600: 0.1 / 108 CFU/ml) and incubated without shaking at 28 °C for 24 h. After incubation, OD600 was measured to quantify biomass growth. Then, liquid cultures were removed, and remaining biofilms were stained with 100 µl of 0.1% crystal violet. After 30 min incubating, wells were washed with MQ water three times, and the stained biofilms were solubilized with 100 µl of 70% ethanol and measured at OD580. Measurements were performed by using a V-1200 spectrophotometer (VWR, Radnor, PA) and biofilm quantification was normalised according to biomass absorbance (OD580/OD600). These experiments included at least three biological replicates, each with eight technical replicates.

For the analysis of the formation and biofilm structure of R. solanacearum strains were modified to express the green fluorescent protein (GFP) by electroporation with pRCG-Pps-GFP39. Saturated cultures of the GFP-labelled bacteria in rich B medium were adjusted to OD600 of 0.1 (108 CFU/ml) and diluted 1:20 in fresh CPG medium. Then, 300 µL of the bacterial suspensions were placed onto chamber-covered glass slides (nu155411, Lab-Tek, NUNC) that were statically incubated in a humidified PVC box at 28 °C. All microscopic observations were performed on a Zeiss LSM880 confocal laser scanning microscope (Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany) equipped with an argon laser and detector and filter sets for monitoring of GFP expression (excitation, 488 nm; emission, 517 nm). The images obtained were analysed with ImageJ software (https://imagej.nih.gov).

Bacterial virulence and bacterial fitness in the leaf apoplast assays

All in planta infection assays were performed following a previously described method40 with wild-type (WT), ΔkatE, ΔkatG and katG complemented strains. To determine virulence of R. solanacearum mutant strains, 4-week-old unwounded tomato plants were soil-soak inoculated by drenching each plant with 40 ml of bacterial culture, previously adjusted to 108 CFU/ml. Infection symptoms were recorded daily by using an established semi-quantitative disease index scale from 0 (no wilt) to 4 (death)40. Drenching assays were performed using 24 plants as technical replicates and three independent biological replicates using a complete randomised design. To determine bacterial fitness in the leaf apoplast, 4-week-old tomato leaves were vacuum-infiltrated during 60 s by submerging the aerial plant into 105 CFU/ml bacterial suspensions with 80 µl/l of Silwet L-77 and bacterial amounts were quantified at 0- and 3-days post inoculation (dpi). To determine bacterial fitness in the xylem, 4-week-old tomato plants were pin-inoculated at the site of the petiole using a sterile 0.3 × 13 mm needle (BD Microlance, Becton Dickinson) and two 5 µl droplets of 105 CFU/ml were inoculated. Bacterial multiplication was determined by sampling 3 cm stem above the inoculation site at 1 and 4 dpi. Experiments of bacterial fitness in the apoplast and xylem were performed in 4 technical replicates and 3 biological replicates, respectively.

Modified-pH soil agar experiments

To investigate the impact of pHs environment in R. solanacearum survival, soil agar medium was prepared as previously described with few modifications31by adjusting pH to neutral (7) or alkaline (8.5) final conditions. Bacterial cultures were adjusted to OD600 of 0.1 (108 CFU/ml), serially diluted and plated on the different soil extract agar plates and, as reference, in rich B media supplemented with appropriate antibiotics. After incubating during 48 h at 28 °C, bacterial colonies were counted. Experiments were performed in three biological replicates containing 4 technical replicates.

Desiccation resistance assays

R. solanacearum overnight cultures were washed twice, OD600 was adjusted to 0.1 in MQ water (108 CFU/ml) and spotted on sterile cellulose filters (0.45-µm pore size; Millipore). Each filter was dried by incubating for 4 h inside a sterile petri plate at 28 °C. After incubation, filters were recovered in 1 ml MQ-containing tubes and dilutions were plated on appropriated antibiotic-containing rich B media plates. Survival percentages were calculated by dividing the CFU obtained from each strain after desiccation by the CFU of the same strain at time zero, without desiccation treatment. Desiccation experiments included at least three biological replicates, each with four technical replicates.

UV light exposure assays (UV-A and UV-C)

To determine UV survival, R. solanacearum overnight cultures were collected, washed twice, and adjusted in MQ water at 108 CFU/ml final concentration. Then, a 24-well culture plate was filled with 1 ml of each adjusted culture and then irradiated with UV-C light (254 nm) at a dose of 20 using a Bio-Link BLX-254 cross-linker (Vilber Lourmat) or with UV-A (385 nm) at a dose of 1800 J/m2 using a Fluxen Raduv385. For each UV dose, samples were diluted and plated in appropriated antibiotic-containing rich B medium. The survival percentage was calculated as the number of CFU at each dose divided by the CFU at a dose of 0 J/m2. UV experiments were performed with 4 technical replicates in three biological replicates.

Statistical analyses and graphical representations

All graphs and statistical analysis were created with R version 4.4.3. Data were analysed in a two-tailed, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by the Tukey test for post hoc multiple-group comparisons. All assays were carried out at least in three independent experiments, each one with triplicated samples. Results were considered statistically significant when a p value of < 0.05 was obtained.

Results

Differential role of KatG and KatE on the R. solanacearum catalase activity at different growth stages

To investigate the enzymatic function of KatE and KatG during different growth stages, we explored the catalase activity of the mutants ΔkatE30 and ΔkatG31 during early exponential and stationary phase. First, we verified that mutant strains do not show altered growth kinetics by monitoring their growth kinetics spectrophotometrically at OD600 (Fig. S1A). Additionally, we obtained the catalase activity profile in non-denaturing polyacrylamide gels for the wild-type and the mutant strains generated (Fig. S1B). Next, we compared the catalase activity of the different mutants during exponential (Fig. 1A) and stationary growth (Fig. 1B). The results show that the catalase activity of the R. solanacearum WT strain was ~ 10 × higher during exponential growth than in stationary state based on the activity measured by a decrease in absorbance at 240 nm. Furthermore, in the absence of an exogenous oxidizing agent, KatE was the main catalase during early exponential growth, as the ΔkatG mutant showed a catalase activity comparable to that of WT strain (Fig. 1A). In contrast, during the stationary phase, single deletion of katE or katG did not affect significantly the catalase activity, but a decrease in activity was apparent in the ΔkatGE double mutant, suggesting redundant activity in these conditions (Fig. 1B). Catalase activity gels of WT, mutant and complemented strains enabled the identification of each enzyme and confirmed that the ΔkatGE mutant strain lacked the activity (Fig. 1C).

To investigate the protective ability of R. solanacearum catalase isoenzymes against oxidative stress, we used methylviologen (MV), MV simulates intracellular H2O2 stress in bacteria, generating superoxide (O2−) that is quickly converted into H2O2 and O2 by the action of superoxide dismutase (SOD)41. After treating bacterial cultures in early exponential growth with different concentrations of MV we observed an increase of intensity in the KatG catalase activity (+ 60 signal relative increase to control) indicating its induction by intracellular H2O2 (Fig. S1C).

The catalases KatG and KatE are important for survival against oxidative stress

Next, we investigated the importance of the catalase genes for R. solanacearum ability to survive under oxidative stress during different growth stages. We observed that during exponential growth, both ΔkatG and ΔkatE were more sensitive to exogenous H2O2 than WT strain, as measured by a statistically significant (p 0.01) increase in the halo diameter of ~ 5 and ~ 10 mm, respectively (Fig. 2A, B). Additionally, quantification of culturable bacteria after oxidative stress exposure showed that single mutation of the catalase genes causes an up to 600-fold reduction of CFUs (p < 0.01) (Fig. 2C). The double deletion mutant ΔkatGE exhibited an additive phenotype, as it displayed a complete lack of growth after exposure to 2.5 mM H2O2 (Fig. 2C). Remarkably, in line with the catalase activity measured during stationary phase (Fig. 1B), the catalase genes katG and katE were less important duribng this stage, as only the ΔkatGE mutant showed a significant (p < 0.01) decrease of CFUs after 1 or 2.5 mM H2O2 treatment (Fig. 2D).

ROS can be indirectly generated by other stressors, such as envelope42,43osmotic44and nitrosative stress23. Thus, we also tested whether the catalase genes facilitate survival against these stressors. Remarkably, the catalase mutants were not affected by envelope (SDS and colistin), osmotic (NaCl) or nitrosative (KNO3) stresses, highlighting their specific importance for direct oxidative (H2O2) stress survival (Fig. S2A).

Finally, given the importance of bacterial biofilm for protection against ROS45,46we investigated the biofilm characteristics of the different mutant strains. We observed that while the ΔkatE mutant had a slight decrease in biofilm formation and structure ~ 60% redction, p < 0.01)30, the ΔkatG mutant had an approximately a 50% enhanced biofilm production (Fig. 2E) that could be observed macroscopically (Fig. 2F). In addition to the increase biofilm production by the ΔkatG mutant, we observed that this mutant developed a more structured biofilm than the WT after 72 h growth (Fig. 2F).

The catalase KatG is important for bacterial fitness in the apoplast and xylem

Detoxification of ROS during host colonization is an important strategy used by plant pathogens to counteract oxidative bursts triggered by plant immunity47. Previous studies have demonstrated that KatE does not contribute to R. solanacearum virulence nor fitness in tomato30but the role of KatG remained unexplored. Therefore, to further characterize the importance of catalases during host-colonization, we investigated the role of ΔkatG and ΔkatGE double mutant during plant infection. Results showed that neither the ΔkatG nor the ΔkatGE double mutant affected virulence during tomato plant infection via soil-soaking. Ten days post inoculation, disease indexes of the knockout mutants were similar to those of the WT, with no statistically significant differences in wilting symptoms (Fig. 3A). However, when R. solanacearum mutant strains were inoculated through infiltration into tomato leaves, the ΔkatG and ΔkatGE mutants showed impaired fitness after three days postinoculation, with a two-log of reduction in bacterial growth (Fig. 3B), whereas the ΔkatE mutant exhibited the same growth kinetics as the WT strain. In contrast, when the ΔkatG and ΔkatGE mutants were introduced directly into the xylem through petiole inoculation we observed a non-statistically significant tendency of a reduced bacterial fitness after four days of infection (Fig. 3C). These results highlight the importance of KatG for optimal growth and fitness in different plant compartments. Reintroduction of katG into the ΔkatG mutant strain restored bacterial fitness in the apoplast, demonstrating clear genetic complementation of this phenotype (Fig. 3B).

The catalase KatG is important for survival to environmental stress

Previous research showed that the catalases katG and katE are highly expressed by R. solanacearum during soil survival and that KatG is key to survive in soil31. This previous knowledge drove us to investigate the molecular mechanism by which catalases promote soil survival. First, we investigated their importance to cope with different environmental stresses that promote ROS production, such as pH fluctuations16, desiccation8,18 or exposure to ultraviolet irradiation19. Culturability of the ΔkatG and ΔkatGE decreased in soil agar, a medium that resembles soil-like conditions31. Alkaline soils increase transition metal availability, which induces bacterial ROS production48,49. Thus, we investigated the role of catalases to survive exposure to an alkaline pH. At basic pH (8.5), culturability of the ΔkatG and ΔkatGE mutants was significantly reduced compared to neutral pH (2 and 3 logs, respectively), whereas the WT, ΔkatE and ΔkatG complemented strains were unaffected, showing similar growth rates in both pH conditions (Fig. 4A). Additionally, we investigated the importance of the catalases to cope with desiccation, a stress commonly encountered in soils with low moisture, which induces endogenous formation of ROS8,50. Only the mutants lacking katG (ΔkatG and ΔkatGE) were significantly more sensitive to desiccation, with a 0.5 and 1 log of growth reduction, respectively (p < 0.01) (Fig. 4B), whereas the phenotype was rescued in the complemented strain. Finally, we tested the ability of R. solanacearum mutants to survive to exposure by different UV lights (UV-A and UV-C) as some catalases are important to survive ultraviolet radiation11,51. UV-A reaches the Earth surface and water bodies, inducing oxidative stress through ROS production, leading to indirect damage, while UV-C is mostly absorbed by the atmosphere and causes direct damage to cells52. In line with previous results, only the ΔkatG and ΔkatGE mutants exhibited an approximately 3 log reduction in growth after UV-C exposure, and one log in case of UV-A exposure (Fig. 4C) a phenotype that was restored in the complemented strain. Together, these results highlight the specific role of the catalase-peroxidase KatG to cope against direct damage by UV-C radiation as well as indirect damage by UV-A exposure.

Discussion

Bacterial cells are exposed to fluctuating levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) throughout their life cycle, both in host-associated and external environments. In this study, we investigated the role of the primary ROS-detoxifying enzymes, particularly catalases, in the survival and adaptation of the β-proteobacterium R. solanacearum across diverse ecological niches.

We showed that KatE and KatG are the only active R. solanacearum catalases, as the double mutant ΔkatGE did not show any catalase activity (Fig. 1). We also observed that R. solanacearum catalase activity was higher during stationary phase than exponential, being KatE the main catalase during exponential growth (Fig. 1A), as opposite observed in other the phytopathogenic bacteria Xanthomonas citri53. During the stationary phase, KatE and KatG exhibit compensatory catalase activity, as only the double deletion results in a complete loss of activity (Fig. 1B). The predominance of catalase enzymatic activity during the exponential growth phase was unexpected, given that many bacterial species typically exhibit increased catalase production during the stationary phase54–56.

Consistent with a predominant activity during exponential growth, both KatE and KatG were essential for surviving H202 exposure in this phase. However, during the stationary phase, the sensitivity of the mutants to oxidative stress was mild and it was only observed at higher concentrations of H202 (Fig. 2). Intriguingly, the ΔkatG mutant exhibited greater sensitivity to oxidative stress during exponential growth than the ΔkatE mutant, although catalase activity assays revealed no difference between the ΔkatG deletion mutant and the WT (Fig. 1A). The gene katG is annotated to encode a bifunctional peroxide-catalase enzyme with the conserved peroxidase and catalase domains. Additionally, our previous work30identified that a protein with the same migration rate than KatG had peroxidase activity, suggesting that KatG has a double catalase-peroxidase role. The observation that the ΔkatG mutant exhibits catalase activity similar to WT but strong sensitivity to oxidative stress suggests that the peroxidase activity of KatG may be more relevant than its catalase activity for mitigating oxidative stress.This finding is consistent with observations reported in other bacterial species, such as Acinetobacter spp., where KatG primarily contributes to H202 detoxification through its peroxidase activity57. The fact that KatG but not KatE affects bacterial fitness in the leaf apoplast (see below) also suggests a major role for the KatG peroxidase, rather than its catalase activity during infection, as was reported in Xanthomonas campestris58.

Interestingly, R. solanacearum catalase mutants were not affected by envelope, osmotic or nitrosative stresses, highlighting their specific importance for direct oxidative stress survival (Fig. S2A), in contrast to previous observations in E. coli under response to osmotic59 or nitrosative60 stressors. In this context, the lack of effect in R. solanacearum mutants under these specific stresses suggests that the KatG role in detoxifying ROS is more relevant during direct oxidative stress conditions, rather than in mitigating damage linked to osmotic or envelope stress, which may involve different protective mechanisms. Interestingly, we observed that the ΔkatG mutant had increased production and a more structured biofilm than the WT strain. The reason for this increased biofilm production is unclear, nonetheless, we propose that it could be a compensatory response to an increase intracellular ROS12, as shown in other bacterial species such asAcinetobacter oleivorans61, Erwinia amylovora62, Xanthomonas citrii subsp citrii53 and Burkholderia glumae63. In the former case, biofilm formation clearly provides protection against ROS61. Here, we observed in R. solanacearum increased production and more structured biofilm in the ΔkatG mutant compared to the WT strain (Fig. 2E, F), suggesting a cellular response like the above-mentioned bacteria to protect cells from the intracellular ROS disbalance generated by the loss of katG. These findings contrast with the impaired biofilm development seen when the X. citri katG orthologue was disrupted53, highlighting the varied metabolic responses of different bacterial species to oxidative stress imbalances.

During early host-colonization, bacteria are exposed to an oxidative burst associated with the plant defence response. Many bacterial pathogens have been reported to rely on catalase enzymes to detoxify this oxidative stress56,62,64. For example, studies with catalase disruption mutants in P. syringae pv. tomato and Xanthomonas spp. demonstrated that catalases are key for bacterial virulence and fitness56,58,65. In contrast, in this study we have observed that deletion of individual catalase genes in R. solanacearum did not significantly affect its virulence in tomato (Fig. 3A)30 and only a minor bacterial fitness defect is observed in the ∆katG mutants in the leaf apoplast (Fig. 3B). These results indicate, that in R. solanacearum catalases can play a role in bacterial fitness in specific compartments inside the plant host, as in E. amylovora, where catalase mutants are important for survival in the leaf apoplast62.

Remarkably, the bacterial fitness defect observed in planta is minor, compared to the strong effect of catalase genes in soil survival, suggesting that the main role of catalases throughout the bacterial life cycle takes place in the soil (Fig. 4A). This result is in accordance with the negligible role of R. solanacearum KatE in virulence30, the strong up-regulation of catalase genes during R. solanacearum soil survival compared with host-colonization31 and results from P. syringae pv. syringae transcriptome showing little effect of oxidative stress during colonization in planta66. Curiously, the leaf apoplast is exposed to more environmental conditions than the xylem, which is a more confined microenvironment. Therefore, one can speculate that the fitness defect of the katG mutants in the leaf apoplast might not only be a result of the oxidative burst from the plan immune system, but also from the increased exposure to environmental stressors that induce bacterial ROS.

The predominant role of R. solanacearum catalases in soil, compared to in planta conditions, can be explained by two non-mutually exclusive hypotheses: either ROS is more important in soil than inside the plant, challenging the notion that this stress is major inside the host, or catalases have a preponderant role in soil and other redundant ROS-detoxifying systems are primarily active during plant infection. The first hypothesis is consistent with previous transcriptomic data reported for R. solanacearum, where the analysis of gene expression changes restricted to in planta conditions showed an upregulation of more than thirty oxidative stress-related genes67. However, when environmental stages outside the host were also considered, the expression of these genes was found to be even higher in the soil environment, surpassing their expression levels observed under in planta conditions31. Supporting the second hypothesis, other ROS-scavenging enzymes, such as those encoded by alkyl hydroperoxide reductase genes, like AhpC, were recently found to be highly induced when R. solanacearum grows in the xylem64,67 and deletion of the global ROS-responsive regulator oxyR decreased the virulence of R. solanacearum68. Furthermore, AhpC was demonstrated to contribute to the pathogenicity of R. solanacearum64, reinforcing the idea that the lack of virulence attenuation in the catalase-peroxidase mutants may be due to functional redundancy with other ROS-scavenging enzymes.

Additionally, during survival in the environment bacteria are exposed to environmental oxidizing agents such as ionizing (γ) or non-ionizing (UV) radiation, antibiotics, xenobiotics and osmotic stress / desiccation. Soil pHs have a strong impact on soil microbiome populations69–71. On one hand, acidic soils reduce the diversity of N2-fixing bacteria and promote soil-borne diseases by diminishing beneficial bacterial communities, while alkaline soils have low organic biomass and poor microbial activity49,69,72,73.

Our results suggest that under alkaline pH conditions, the catalase KatG, but not the monofunctional catalase KatE, plays a crucial role in survival within soil-agar mixtures (Fig. 4A). Previous reports show that R. solanacearum exhibits optimal growth at pH 6.5, with a noticeable decline above 8.174. High pH also alters the root microbiome composition, which is associated with transcriptomic changes in redox regulation, linking pH stress to oxidative response and microbial restructuring73. This suggests that alkaline soils, with conditions-promoting oxidative stress and complex microbial communities, might restrict R. solanacearum persistence48,49,74. In this study we observed that the catalase KatG is essential for R. solanacearum survival under the stressful conditions associated with alkaline pH.

Desiccation is another environmental stressor affecting microbial communities in soil69,71,75. Desiccation stress triggers ROS production, leading to damage at all levels of cellular organization18,75,76. Thus, in order to tolerate desiccation R. solanacearum does not only require to produce osmolytes, chaperones and proteins that stabilise cellular structures77,78, but it also requires the function of both catalases KatE and KatG to cope with the redox disbalance generated from the desiccation stress. Our results (Fig. 4B) align with previous studies in Acinetobacter baumannii, where catalases and regulators involved in the oxidative stress response were found to be crucial for survival under desiccation conditions79,80. Additionally, transcriptomic analyses and screens for desiccation-induced genes in Pseudomonas putida have revealed significant upregulation of numerous genes related to the oxidative stress response81.

Finally, ultraviolet (UV) radiation represents a pervasive and persistent environmental stressor for R. solanacearum in soil ecosystems. Beyond causing direct damage, UV radiation also generates ROS such as singlet oxygen, superoxide anions, and hydroxyl radicals. These molecules increase oxidative stress by harming essential cellular components, further reducing bacterial survival and adaptability. Atmospheric changes associated with climate change, such as ozone layer variation, have been proposed to affect the intensity of UV radiation at the Earth surface71,82. This escalation in UV exposure intensifies oxidative stress in soil microorganisms like Ralstonia, acting as a key selective pressure that shapes microbial community composition and diversity. In this work we demonstrate that ΔkatG and ΔkatGE mutants have decreased bacterial survival after exposure to UV-A and UV-C (Fig. 4C), highlighting the critical role of catalases, particularly KatG, in the ability of R. solanacearum to withstand UV exposure. Similar results have been described for X. citri subsp. citri, where KatG is essential for UV-A and UV-B resistance53 and as well as in B. glumae, where KatG is important for protection against visible light exposure63. These findings underline the importance of catalase enzymes, triggering efficient and specialized enzymatic responses in protection against UV damage and adaptation to challenging environmental conditions. Understanding how R. solanacearum copes with UV-induced ROS generation and oxidative damage is therefore crucial for comprehending its ecological fitness and pathogenic potential under changing environmental conditions.

Collectively, our results establish that catalases KatE and KatG play a crucial role in managing oxidative stress tolerance in R. solanacearum. These catalases are essential for coping with various stressors, including pH fluctuations (Fig. 4A), desiccation (Fig. 4B), and UV radiation (Fig. 4C, D), all of which are directly linked to environmental oxidative stress. The primary phenotype of mutants lacking KatG (ΔkatG and ΔkatGE) indicates that this enzyme plays a critical role in the detoxification of ROS under environmental conditions, which correlates with its high expression levels in soil31. In contrast, both catalases appear to play a minor role in in planta survival, as previously shown for KatE30in which alternative ROS-detoxifying mechanisms might be engaged64. While catalases are important for counteracting reactive oxygen species (ROS) in specific niches within the plant, we emphasize their predominant role during free-living conditions outside the host. In this context, promising studies have focused on novel treatments, such as nanoparticles, which induce ROS production against various disease-causing bacteria in plants, including R. solanacearum24. Our findings demonstrate that KatG is crucial for survival under diverse ROS-related stresses encountered in the external environment, thereby opening new avenues for the development of innovative ROS-based disease management strategies.
