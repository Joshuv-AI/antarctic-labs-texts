Rare Right Atrial Thrombus Deferred Atrial Septal Defect Closure and Implantable Cardioverter-Defibrillator Placement
Authors: Sachin Sapkota, Sandesh Murali, Kahtan Fadah, Edgar Ivan Usle Perez, Chalam Mulukutla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A 47-year-old man with nonischemic cardiomyopathy and atrial septal defect (ASD) was scheduled for percutaneous ASD closure and to be followed by implantable cardioverter-defibrillator placement. Preprocedural transesophageal echocardiography unexpectedly revealed a large right atrial thrombus, leading to cancellation of both procedures. The patient was managed conservatively with warfarin. This case illustrates the rarity of right atrial thrombus in patients without atrial arrhythmia or indwelling devices and emphasizes the critical role of preprocedural imaging in preventing potential embolic complications. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of a right atrial thrombus detected during ASD closure.

Case Summary

A 47-year-old man with nonischemic cardiomyopathy, heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (left ventricular ejection fraction 20%), and a secundum atrial septal defect (ASD) was evaluated for transcatheter ASD closure followed by implantable cardioverter-defibrillator placement. The patient reported persistent exertional intolerance but no chest pain, orthopnea, or syncope.

Initial transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) demonstrated a 6-mm ASD with no intracardiac thrombus and right chambers dilation (Supplemental Figure 1). Due to the concern of paradoxical embolism and ventricular dilation, the multidisciplinary discussion between the heart failure and electrophysiology teams discussed sequential ASD closure and implantable cardioverter-defibrillator implantation. At the time of the scheduled procedure several months later, repeat preprocedural TEE revealed a new, large, circular mobile echo dense mass in the right atrial appendage measuring approximately 3.8 × 3.2 cm, consistent with thrombus (Figure 1, Video 1). This was unexpected and rare, as prior imaging had shown no evidence of thrombus. In addition, the patient denied recent vascular procedures, central line placements, or other potential sources of an embolic nidus. Given the high risk of paradoxical right to left embolization during the procedure, both procedures were aborted. During a delayed follow-up visit 5 months later due to social issues, he remained compliant with anticoagulation. A repeat transthoracic echocardiogram for heart failure with reduced ejection fraction assessment did not demonstrate a right atrial thrombus (Supplemental Figure 2); however, the possibility cannot be fully excluded given the limitations of transthoracic echocardiogram. He was advised to continue anticoagulation and have a repeat TEE to assess thrombus resolution, after which anticoagulation would be discontinued, and the procedure reconsidered. Given the uncertain benefit in nonischemic cardiomyopathy and the patient's socioeconomic challenges, a wearable defibrillator was deferred. He was lost to follow-up due to complex social circumstances. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first published case in which a right atrial thrombus was unexpectedly identified during preprocedural imaging for transcatheter ASD closure.

Discussion

Right atrial thrombus is a rare clinical finding, especially in patients without risk factors such as atrial arrhythmia, intracardiac devices, or established venous thromboembolism. In our case, severe right-sided heart failure causing chamber dilation and low-flow states likely led to thrombus formation. The incidence is very low, with most studies reporting rates under 1% in patients undergoing precardioversion echocardiography for atrial fibrillation.1 Despite its rarity, the condition carries a significant risk of severe complications if the thrombus remains undetected.2 Detecting right atrial thrombus on preprocedural imaging is critical, as unrecognized thrombus—especially with an ASD—can lead to catastrophic paradoxical embolization. No specific guidelines are available for management of right atrial thrombi. Advanced therapies—thrombolysis and thrombectomy—may be used for unstable or mobile thrombi, or anticoagulation contraindication, but offer no clear benefit over anticoagulation alone for stable thrombi.3 Anticoagulation and serial echocardiography appear to be the most appropriate management strategy for a stable thrombus.

This case highlights the crucial role of repeat preprocedural imaging in detecting interval thrombus formation. In patients with interatrial shunts, right-sided thrombi pose unique procedural risks, necessitating careful timing of intervention and anticoagulation.
