How Can Community Paramedics Help in Addressing Mental Health for Vulnerable Australians?
Authors: Laura Hemming, Ruth Hardman, Louise Reynolds, Renee Sharples, Gina Agarwal, Evelien Spelten
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesOlder adults in rural communities often have difficulty accessing mental healthcare. Community paramedicine may offer a novel opportunity to address this need. This study aimed to explore whether a feasibility trial of a community paramedicine programme, CP@clinic, can influence mental health outcomes in rural adults.SettingThis study was conducted in a regional town in Victoria, Australia, across six clinic sites, offering a free, 3‐h drop‐in service with community paramedics for individuals seeking health assessments or advice.Participants205 clients (mean age 70 years) attended a community paramedic clinic.DesignThe mixed‐methods feasibility trial ran from 2022 to 2024. Data was recorded on chronic disease, quality of life, loneliness and mental health. Fourteen participants were interviewed to explore experiences and perceived benefits.Results47% of clients attending CP@clinic reported anxiety or depression. These participants experienced higher rates of social isolation, poverty, and health lifestyle risks. Participants reported that CP@clinic helped to improve their mental health both directly, through onward referrals to healthcare providers, and indirectly through improvements in mental health determinants across physical and social domains.ConclusionThis study found a clear overlap between mental, physical, and social health needs of CP@clinic consumers. There is a need for accessible community‐based services that address the mental health needs of older rural adults. CP@clinic may provide an opportunity to improve mental wellbeing in this population and should be further evaluated for this purpose.

Introduction

In Australia, around one third of adults live in rural, regional or remote areas [1] (defined as areas outside metropolitan cities). They experience several factors that place them at an increased risk of poor mental health [2, 3]. This includes higher levels of isolation and loneliness [4, 5], reduced opportunities for social participation and community engagement [6, 7] and poorer physical health, including chronic conditions such as arthritis, asthma and pulmonary disease [1, 3]. These challenges may be amplified in later life due to retirement, bereavement, declining mobility, and reduced access to informal support networks, all of which are more pronounced in rural contexts [8].

People living in rural, regional and remote areas (afterwards rural) also have lower average incomes and a higher risk of food and income insecurity [9, 10, 11, 12]. Older rural Australians may be particularly vulnerable to these stressors due to reliance on fixed incomes, rising living costs, and limited access to affordable food outlets and transport [13, 14]. These social determinants are known to be strong predictors of poor mental health [15, 16, 17], with substantial evidence linking financial strain, food insecurity, and social disadvantage to psychological distress, depression, and reduced quality of life among older adults [18, 19].

Moreover, rural people may face greater challenges in accessing healthcare. Common barriers to accessing mental healthcare in rural areas include limited resources, system complexity and navigation, technological limitations, distance to services, insufficient culturally‐sensitive practice and a lack of awareness [20]. These barriers are often intensified for older adults due to age‐related factors such as reduced mobility, sensory impairment, and increased reliance on others for transport [21]. Travelling long distances can be a particular challenge [21, 22] and telehealth is often not a suitable alternative due to lower digital literacy among older populations and persistent connectivity limitations in rural areas [23, 24, 25, 26].

One approach to addressing the healthcare needs of adults living in rural areas is a novel model of healthcare: community paramedicine [27]. Recently defined by an international panel of experts, a community paramedic: ‘provides person‐centred care in a diverse range of settings that address the needs of the community. Their practice may include provision of primary health care, health promotion, disease management, clinical assessment and needs based interventions. They should be integrated with interdisciplinary health care teams which aim to improve patient outcomes through education, advocacy, and health system navigation.’ [28]. Community paramedicine has been found to benefit both patients and the healthcare system [29, 30], especially for older populations [31].

One specific model of community paramedicine, CP@clinic (the Community Paramedicine at Clinic Program), has a strong evidence base and is focused on chronic disease prevention, management and health promotion. Developed by the McMaster Community Paramedicine Research Team at McMaster University, Ontario, Canada, the programme has been found to reduce chronic disease risk, improve quality of life [32, 33] and reduce the need for emergency healthcare [34].

Whilst CP@clinic has been found to be effective in managing chronic physical health conditions, less is known about its impacts on mental health. Given the bidirectional relationships between social isolation, physical health, poverty and mental health, it is reasonable to consider how the programme might directly or indirectly influence the mental health status of participants.

The current study explores the first trial of the CP@clinic model undertaken outside of Canada, in rural Victoria, Australia. The results of this feasibility trial are reported elsewhere [35]. This study aims to focus on the impact of CP@clinic on the mental health needs of rural individuals, and therefore poses two research questions: What are the social and mental health needs of CP@clinic clients? Materials and Methods

Research Design

This study utilised an embedded mixed methods study, whereby a qualitative study was nested in a feasibility trial of CP@clinic. Such an approach was considered beneficial to be able to understand the ‘what’ (using quantitative and qualitative data) as well as the ‘how’ (using qualitative data). This study adhered to the principles stated in the Declaration of Helsinki with ethical approval granted for all aspects of the study. The study is reported following the COREQ checklist (see File S1).

The CP@Clinic Program

This study was conducted in Mildura, a Local Government Area (LGA) in Victoria, Australia, a regional town with a population of approximately 57 000 located over 400 km from the nearest major city. Starting in August 2022, two paramedics were hired and completed formal training in the McMaster CP@clinic program. They engaged with local service providers to build connections and identified suitable venues for clinic operations. The first clinic began operating at a local community centre in August 2022, followed by the addition of five more sites over the next 6 months. Each clinic offered a free, 3‐h drop‐in service for individuals seeking health assessments or advice. Paramedics performed initial health risk assessments and, with guidance from the CP@clinic decision support, determined appropriate follow‐up actions, such as the need for onward referral, self‐management education and/or system navigation. The assessment process generated a summary of the client's risk factors, which were reviewed and discussed with the client and shared with their general practitioner (GP). Over time, community connectors—workers with lived experience—joined the clinics at various locations to enhance services, offering initiatives such as food assistance, community meals, and walking groups.

Participants and Recruitment

Eligible participants were anybody who had taken part in the CP@clinic program between August 2022 and September 2024. For qualitative interviews, the community paramedics approached clients who had attended several times and invited them to participate in an interview. Client details were then forwarded to the researchers to organise an interview time. Out of 16 clients invited to participate, 14 took part in an interview. No incentive or payment was offered for the interview. Purposive maximum variation sampling was used to select interview participants from this wider pool of participants to ensure a balanced representation of gender and clinic location. All interview participants provided written informed consent, including consent to publish quotes.

For quantitative data, assessment information for all CP@clinic attendees was entered into the CP@clinic database described below. A waiver of consent was obtained to extract deidentified demographic and health data from the CP@clinic database.

Data Collection

Quantitative—CP@Clinic Database

The CP@clinic database is an electronic medical record which incorporates evidence‐based health risk assessment tools and algorithms that offer decision support to guide paramedic actions. Community paramedics administered health risk assessment questions verbally to clients and entered this information into the database. Data collection occurred at sites across the Mildura LGA between August 2022 and September 2024.

The initial CP@clinic assessment covers demographics and other health related assessments including mental health. For the purposes of this study we focused on demographic information, quality of life (EQ‐5D‐5L; [36]), state of health (EQ‐VAS; [36]), loneliness (UCLA‐3; [37]), anxiety & depression (PHQ 4; [38]), food insecurity (single item question: In the past month, was there any day when you or anyone in your family went hungry because you did not have enough money for food?) [39] income insecurity (single item question: Do you ever have trouble making ends meet at the end of the month?) [39] and lifestyle (questions about alcohol consumption and smoking habits).

Qualitative

Qualitative interviews were undertaken with 14 clients between June and August 2023. Interviews were conducted face‐to‐face at 4 clinic locations by RH and ES. Interviews ranged in length from 8 to 60 min, with an average length of 15 min. Interviews were audio recorded and transcribed verbatim. A semi‐structured topic guide was used which explored participants' experiences, benefits of participation, and suggestions for improvement.

Analysis

Quantitative and qualitative data were analysed separately then integrated using an explanatory approach, whereby the qualitative data were used to explain the quantitative findings.

Quantitative

No participants were excluded from analysis, and missing data was not inferred. Descriptive statistics were conducted to describe the health status of the population. Following this, Pearson chi‐squared tests were conducted to determine significant differences between those experiencing anxiety and depression and those who weren't. All analyses were conducted in SPSS [40].

Qualitative

A ‘small q’ thematic analysis was conducted within a pragmatic research paradigm, appropriate for applied health services research aimed at informing service development and practice [41]. Small q refers to a structured, applied thematic analysis and is distinct from theory‐driven or interpretivist qualitative approaches [42]. Pragmatism supports the use of qualitative methods to complement and extend quantitative findings, with an emphasis on generating actionable insights rather than theory development [41, 43].

The analytic approach followed a structured and systematic approach, using clearly defined codes to support consistency and transparency in analysis [44, 45]. This approach enabled the identification of patterns in participants' accounts that were relevant to understanding experiences of CP@clinic and to contextualising quantitative findings.

LH conducted the qualitative analysis. LH is a university‐educated, white female in her 30s with considerable experience of qualitative research and analysis. Researcher reflexivity was considered throughout the analytic process, with the researcher's background and experience acknowledged as shaping analytic decisions [45].

All analyses were conducted using NVivo [46]. The procedures undertaken were systematic to ensure technical reliability and minimising researcher subjectivity [44, 45]. Consistent with a pragmatic orientation, findings were interpreted with a focus on their relevance for service delivery and real‐world application [41].

THE HOW: How Could CP@Clinic Improve the Mental Health of Clients?

Both quantitative and qualitative data highlighted the needs of CP@clinic clients in relation to social isolation and mental health. In interviews, clients spoke unprompted about how CP@clinic had helped to improve their mental health. This tended to happen in one of two ways: through directly addressing mental health concerns and indirectly—where mental health improvements came about due to improvements in determinants of mental health. Participants also spoke about how their mental health had improved as a result of other services provided by CP@clinic. Addressing physical health status and social determinants of health led to improved mental wellbeing.

Many participants spoke about the difficulties of experiencing comorbid physical and mental health issues. Discussion

Addressing Mental Health Through Community Paramedicine in Rural Australia

This study aimed to explore ‘the what’ and ‘the how’ of community paramedicine in rural Australia. In regards to ‘the what’—this study explored what the mental health needs are of CP@clinic clients. This study found that there was a strong overlap between physical, mental and social health in this population of middle‐aged and older people living in rural, regional and remote Australia. Those that had experienced anxiety or depression were more likely to have experienced issues with smoking and binge drinking, mobility issues and pain or discomfort, supporting previous research which has found an association in adults between mental health issues and smoking [47, 48], binge‐drinking [49], mobility issues [50] and pain [51, 52]. Nearly half of participants surveyed in this study experienced anxiety and depression; a figure that far exceeded the national estimate [53] and indeed the local prevalence rate in Mildura [54] of 10%.

In regards to ‘the how’—the study also sought to explore how CP@clinic contributes to the social and mental health of clients. CP@clinic was perceived by clients to improve their mental health both through receiving direct support for mental health concerns as well as indirectly through the improvement of physical health and social determinants of health. From a pragmatic perspective, these findings highlight the importance of addressing mental health not as an isolated clinical condition, but as embedded within physical health, social circumstances and access to support. The observed clustering of mental distress with poverty, food insecurity, loneliness and chronic illness highlights the limited utility of narrowly biomedical approaches for this population and instead supports the value of integrated, community‐based models of care.

Given the barriers that rural people face in accessing mental healthcare services [55, 56, 57], these findings indicate a need for accessible services that can directly cater to the mental health needs of those living in rural, regional and remote areas. Within a pragmatic service‐delivery framework, CP@clinic appears to reduce these barriers by offering low‐threshold, opportunistic access to support in familiar community settings.

Participants reported indirect mental health benefits through improvements in physical health, food security and social isolation. These findings contribute to a growing body of evidence demonstrating that non‐clinical, non‐biomedical interventions targeting social determinants of health can meaningfully influence mental wellbeing [58, 59]. Addressing physical health concerns and social isolation reduced sources of chronic stress and worry, thereby improving mental health without the need for formal psychological intervention. Whilst the link between general health anxiety and chronic illness is well‐established [60], previous interventions have targeted health anxiety separately from the chronic illness it is associated with [61]. There is scarce literature available that reports on models that aim to prevent chronic disease and the impacts this has on mental health. From a pragmatic standpoint, this suggests that mental health impact may be underestimated in evaluations that rely solely on symptom reduction, rather than considering broader improvements in daily functioning, security and social connection.

Food insecurity, poverty and loneliness were more common among participants who had experienced anxiety and/or depression, and addressing these factors also led to indirect mental health benefits. This is consistent with previous research which has found a relationship between mental health and food insecurity [15], poverty [62, 63] and loneliness [64, 65]. These findings echo those from CP@clinic clients in Canada, who commented on the ability of the programme to enhance connections both with peers and the community paramedics themselves [66, 67]. In this study, participants described in detail the mechanisms through which CP@clinic reduced loneliness, which consequently led to improvements in mental health. The impacts of loneliness have received much scholarly and community attention in recent years due to the COVID‐19 pandemic, with the WHO declaring loneliness a global public health concern recently [68]. A recent systematic review [69] found that interventions aiming to reduce loneliness in older adults (who comprised nearly two thirds of our sample) included animal therapy, psychotherapy, multicomponent, counselling, exercise, music therapy, occupational therapy, reminiscence therapy, social interventions and technological interventions, but most had only a small effect size. This suggests that formal interventions for loneliness may not be well received by older adults. Conversely, a multi‐purpose and non‐stigmatising service [55, 57] may encourage participation by individuals who would not otherwise seek formal help for loneliness, or poor mental health for that matter. By integrating social interaction into a broader, health focussed context, CP@clinic can allow clients to engage without the need to self‐identify as lonely or mentally unwell. This may be a more effective way of tackling this important social determinant of health for older populations.

Collectively, these findings position community paramedics as a uniquely placed workforce capable of addressing mental wellbeing through non‐clinical mechanisms. Their accessibility, trusted role, and capacity to navigate health and social systems enable them to respond flexibly to the intersecting needs of clients. Within a pragmatic model of care, community paramedicine offers a scalable approach to improving mental wellbeing by acting on social and structural determinants, rather than relying exclusively on specialist mental health services.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths. The embedded mixed methods design allowed for a comprehensive understanding of both the ‘what’ through quantitative data and the ‘why’ through [small q] qualitative insights. By integrating these methods, the study captured both the measurable health status and the contextual experiences of participants, providing a more holistic understanding of the CP@clinic program and its impacts on mental health. The use of a well‐established database system with real‐time decision support enhanced the quality and consistency of quantitative data collection.

However, some limitations should be acknowledged. The lack of a control group or pre‐post quantitative data limits the ability to attribute changes solely to the CP@clinic intervention. The reliance on self‐reported measures could have introduced response bias, particularly for sensitive topics like mental health and income insecurity. There were high levels of missing data, although this reflected the drop‐in nature of the service, whereby people might attend only once or choose not to complete the full assessment process. For future data analysis, it may be more informative to focus on participants who completed assessment and therefore engaged more fully with the CP@clinic intervention. Lastly, the context‐specific nature of the study, conducted within a single Local Government Area (Mildura), may limit the generalisability of findings to other regions.

The population in this study consisted predominantly of older adults; while this is a limitation, it can also be seen as a strength as it illustrated what the benefits of the programme are for this important population. Although we found that, consistent with previous research [70], younger age was associated with poorer mental health, this still remains a relatively older population with only 16 (7%) attendees aged less than 45 years. Middle‐aged adults (45–65 years) can be at high risk of chronic physical and mental health conditions, especially where there is poverty or social isolation [71]. There are generally fewer welfare or government supports in place for this group, which suggests an intervention such as CP@clinic may offer a rare but welcome opportunity to address chronic disease and mental health risks in this population [72].

Implications

Overall, this study has found that whilst the CP@clinic program is primarily focused on chronic disease management, it also provides both direct and indirect improvements to mental health.

This provides opportunities around the scope of community paramedics and additional interventions that could be delivered as part of CP@clinic. For instance, non‐clinical interventions such as the Distress Brief Intervention provide a framework for first‐line responders, including paramedics of all types, to provide a compassionate and empathetic response to those experiencing mental distress, and to safely refer them on to appropriate support. Such an intervention has been found to lead to significant improvements in distress of clients [73] and this has led to national rollout of the programme across Scotland in the UK. Formalising the community paramedic's ability to support those in mental distress would provide an evidence‐based mental health intervention for older adults living rurally [31].

Finally, these findings suggest there would be benefits from scaling up the CP@clinic programme across Australia. Currently, the programme is available only in four pilot sites in rural Victoria. Increasing the availability of this programme to all rural residents will not only improve access to populations that are often considered ‘hard to reach’, but will simultaneously address the workforce oversupply that currently exists for paramedics in Australia. This is further supported by the release of the final report ‘Unleashing the Potential of the Health Workforce’ highlighted the utilisation of community paramedics [p. 196] [74].
