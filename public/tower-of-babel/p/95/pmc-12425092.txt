Contribution of the combined oral contraceptive ethinylestradiol 20 µg/dienogest 2 mg, in the treatment of mild to moderate acne vulgaris and its perceived psychological effects in young university women
Authors: John J. Palacio-Cardona, Diana M. Caicedo-Borrero, Daniel Elías Cuartas, Carlos Calle-Madrid, Diana M. Aristizabal-Aristizabal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:In adult females, acne is usually a chronic condition, and its management remains challenging due to therapeutic failures or limited healthcare seeking, with repercussions on mental health. Oral contraceptives may provide an alternative to reduce acne and enhance psychological well-being.Objective:To identify the change in the severity of acne in young university women who used the combined oral contraceptive pills of ethinylestradiol 20 µg/dienogest 2 mg, and its perceived psychological effects on self-esteem, social relations, and noncontraceptive benefits.Methods:A prospective cohort was conducted between 2015 and 2016. Photographic monitoring of acne lesions was performed at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months after the start of the study. The photographs were evaluated by 2 specialists in dermatology, who established the degree of acne. Information was gathered regarding the perception of acne progression, the improvement of self-esteem, and noncontraceptive benefits. A descriptive analysis was performed.Results:In total, 86 women were included. At 6-month follow-up, the percentage of mild comedonal acne changed from 5.8 to 37.8%. At 12 months, at least 35% of the women had no acne lesions, and 43% were classified with mild comedonal acne. Results above 68% indicated that there was a favorable perception in the improvement of self-esteem, and the participants recognized that these changes improved their social relations (first month: 58.6% to sixth month: 90.5%).Limitations:A locally applied acne severity scale was employed, as it provides detailed lesion characterization but restricts comparability with other studies, and a lack of standardized scales for measuring perceived psychological and non-contraceptive benefits.Conclusion:Young women with acne who use oral contraceptives with dienogest and ethinyl estradiol obtain the additional benefit of reducing acne severity.

Introduction

Acne is a polymorphic skin disease; its clinical spectrum ranges from mild comedonal acne to severe inflammatory disease with nodules and cystic or conglobate acne. In adult females, acne is usually a chronic disease1 with inflammatory lesions (papules and pustules) located in the U zone of the face (mandibular line, perioral region, and side of the neck) in a degree of mild and moderate severity. Clinical studies have reported a prevalence of clinical lesions between 10 and 14% in adult females, while population surveys showed up to 54% of self-reported physiological states of the disease and an acne prevalence of 50.9% among women aged 20 to 29 years old. Furthermore, in studies with institutionalized population, up to 66% mild acne, 33% moderate acne, and 10% severe acne were identified.2,3

There are 2 main subtypes of acne that appear in adult females, 80% of cases are persistent acne into adulthood and 20% late-onset acne that begins after the age of 25 years.2 In adult females, increased sebum production, colonization by severe strains of Cutibacterium acnes, and hyperandrogenism have been reported as factors related to the presence of acne.4,5 Other studies sought to relate the severity of acne with increased androgen levels and have found that in adult women with acne, there has been an increase in serum levels of androgens such as dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate, androstenedione, dihydrotestosterone, and insulin-like growth factor 1, although the results are inconclusive.6,7

Acne treatment includes topical treatment, antibiotics, isotretinoin, and in some cases, where this fails or if acne is associated with hyperandrogenism, antiandrogenic medications such as spironolactone or oral contraceptives have been used. These studies have shown that in adult females, antibiotic therapy has failed by 82% and that 33% of isotretinoin treatments have relapsed.2,8,9 Additionally, less than 1% of adult women with acne seek medical treatment, and most of them do not continue. Within the alternative therapies, combined oral contraceptive pills (COCP) with nonandrogenic progestagens have been used, such as norgestrel, norethindrone10 o dienogest,11 the latter has shown evidence in reducing the acne lesion count in a group of females who used this contraceptive as a family planning regimen. Dienogest11 has significant antiandrogenic activity, which reduces the symptoms of acne vulgaris, hirsutism, seborrhea, alopecia, and skin and hair oils. However, the effect of dienogest with low doses of ethinyl estradiol on acne has not been studied in depth.12 This article seeks to demonstrate the additional effect of the routine use of dienogest as contraception in the improvement of the severity of acne, specifically, to identify the change in the severity of acne in young university women who use the combined oral contraceptive pills of ethinylestradiol 20 µg/dienogest 2 mg.

Methodology

This cohort study followed the STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines.13,14 (Supplementary Material https://links.lww.com/IJWD/A72, STROBE).

Study design

A prospective single-group cohort study was carried out between 2015 and 2016 within the Sexual and Reproductive Health Program at Universidad Santiago de Cali. The study enrolled university women who had been prescribed COCPs (20 µg ethinylestradiol/2 mg dienogest) by their physicians for contraceptive purposes and who were diagnosed with acne. The primary objectives were to track changes in acne severity and to assess psychological and noncontraceptive benefits.12

Population

Participants were recruited between February and May 2015. Inclusion criteria specified ages 18 to 30 years with mild to moderate acne. Women were excluded if they were overweight, had used other COCPs within 2 months before the start of this follow-up, or used systemic treatment for acne, had a history of atopy or severe acne conglobata, or had completed a pregnancy within 2 months before study enrollment. Out of 354 women evaluated for eligibility, 137 women fit the inclusion criteria, and a total of 120 were included in the study.12

Ethical considerations

The research protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Universidad Santiago de Cali, adhered to the principles of the Helsinki Declaration, and all participants signed an informed consent before beginning the study.

Follow-up schedule and outcome measures

During follow-up, from recruitment until 12 months of follow-up, 34.2% subjects were lost to follow-up; the main cause was voluntary retirement. This article included women with at least 2 photographs during follow-up for monitoring acne lesions. At each follow-up visit, continuous COCP of ethinylestradiol 20 µg/dienogest 2 mg intake was confirmed. In the assessment of the evolution in the severity of acne, each participant was visually assessed using photos of their face (one frontal, one left lateral, and one right lateral). The photos were taken at the beginning and at the 3rd, 6th, 9th, and 12th month follow-up. A digital camera, Canon EOS Rebel T3i, reflex type AF/AE with flash integrated, and CMOS image sensor with high sensitivity and high resolution was used. The images were captured with a Canon EF-S 18-55 mm lens, and the machine’s computerized autofocus was used. The photographic process of the study was carried out in the same place and in the same lighting conditions. The images did not receive postcapture treatment.

Two specialists in dermatology determined changes in the severity of acne, who independently and blindly evaluated the photos. To standardize the assessment, various scales were reviewed by specialists, and a consensus was generated to establish the diagnostic scale to be used. The group chose a classification system for acne that combined qualitative elements: mild, moderate, or severe comedonal acne; mild, moderate, or severe papulo-comedonal acne; mild, moderate, or severe papulo-postular acne; mild, moderate, or severe nodulo-cystic acne, and mild, moderate, or severe polymorphous acne. This scale used qualitative adjectives such as few, some, and numerous as a proxy measure of lesion count. Specialists were trained to use the classification.

Monthly, at each follow-up visit, information was gathered regarding the perception of acne progression, the improvement of self-esteem and social relationships, and noncontraceptive benefits of the medication in terms of clothing size, oil production, presence of colic, and the menstrual cycle.

Statistical methods

Descriptive statistics were generated for demographic characteristics of women. The concordance between the two dermatologists was evaluated with the kappa coefficient.15 Frequency distributions for each category of severity of acne were estimated for the 3rd, 6th, 9th, and 12th month follow-up. In addition, the change in the degree of severity between the start of follow-up and 6 and 12 months after was presented through absolute frequencies and graphically using a color scale. In the color chart (Fig. 1), the green area reflects the improvement in the degree of severity, that is, the group of people who changed from a high degree of severity to a lower one, while the red area shows the worsening of acne, the change from a minor to a higher degree of severity. As the color becomes more intense, it means that the change between the degrees of severity was greater (Supplementary Material Figure 1, https://links.lww.com/IJWD/A73. Guide to the colors of the heat maps). The frequencies of perceptions related to acne conditions and noncontraceptive benefits were estimated over the follow-up months. Analyses were performed using Stata 11.16

Women who had less than 2 photos of acne lesions were not included for this severity analysis. Table 1 shows the demographic and clinical characteristics of women. The median and interquartile range age of the study population was 20 (19-21) years old, 65% of them had a family history in first-degree relatives of acne and 79% of women did not use nonmedicated topical presentations for acne. The percentage of agreement between the 2 observers was almost perfect (≥95%) for all visits, according to the classification of Landis and Koch,17 this was statistically significant (Supplementary Material, Table 1, https://links.lww.com/IJWD/A75).

Variations of severity acne throughout the study

Table 2 shows the frequency of the severity degree of acne according to the dermatologists’ evaluations. At the start of follow-up, the most frequently occurring degrees of severity found among the study population were mild (D1:33,7%-D2:31,4%) and moderate (D1:33,7%-D2:30,24%) papulo-comedonal. At the third month, the relative frequency of moderate papulo-comedonal acne decreased, while the frequency of less severe presentations, such as mild papulo-comedonal acne and moderate and mild comedonal acne, increased up to 10 percentage points. In the sixth month, at least 10% of women had no acne lesions, and the relative frequency of mild comedonal acne increased 32 percentage points (5.8 to 37.8%). At the end of the follow-up, 35.5% of the women were classified as healthy (without acne lesions), 43% of the women were classified with mild comedonal acne, 6.3% were classified as moderate comedonal acne, 12.7% of the women were classified as papulo-comedonal acne, only 2.5% were classified as papulo-pustular acne and none of the women were classified as nodule-cystic acne. In addition, moderate degrees of acne decreased in the study population.

Another significant aspect to be observed in the same table is that the treatment produced important improvements in the self-esteem of the women. In fact, in month 1, 68.5% said so. By the sixth month, the percentage rose to 93.4% and by the last month the improvement reached 93.7%. A similar situation was evidenced with the perception of improved social relationships as a result of skin changes. Fifty-eight-point-six percent of the participants considered this in the first month of treatment. By the sixth month, it reached 90.5%, and in the last month a percentage of 91.1%. Both changes contributed to the fact that practically 100% of the participating women considered that it was a good decision to start treatment. Of the total sample, only one (1) person considered that she had not made the best decision in this regard.

Finally, Table 4 shows the noncontraceptive benefits of the treatment. The participants emphasize that the most significant were, in order: regularity of the cycle, reduction of menstrual cramps, and the production of oil on the scalp. Regarding the first benefit, in the month of initiation of treatment, 18% reported that their menstrual cycles became regular. By month 6, the percentage increased significantly to 91.7% and by month 12, 98.7% of the women reported that their cycle became regular. Regarding the second benefit, the decrease in menstrual cramps was noticeable from the first month of treatment (46 %), by the 6th month 50% indicated that the cramps had decreased and by the end (month 12) the change was not noticeable in a large percentage (22.8%) because the pain situation had already decreased significantly in the previous months.

Discussion

This study showed that continuous use of COCP of ethinylestradiol 20 µg/dienogest 2 mg reduced the severity of acne in adult women who use this contraceptive as a method of family planning. Most women (78%) completed the 12-month follow-up healthy or with little non-inflammatory comedone-type lesions. Acne is a multifactorial origin disease, and androgens play a major role in the pathogenesis of acne in adult women2; these have an effect on the sebaceous gland and epithelial proliferation at the level of gland ducts and follicles.18 Dienogest is a 19-nortestosterone-derived progestin, without estrogenic, antiestrogenic, or androgenic activity but with antiandrogenic potency. Its use in combination with ethinylestradiol reduces serum androgen levels, specifically, concentrations of total testosterone and free testosterone, leading to improvement of acne lesions.19

In our research, the women participants not only perceived improvement in acne conditions. They also showed improvement in interpersonal relationships and self-esteem. It implies that improvements in acne conditions have positive repercussions at the affective, interpersonal, and mental health levels. Both aspects make the participants consider that it is a good decision to have initiated the treatment and that it would demonstrate the therapeutic importance in the mental health conditions of the participants.

The results of this study on the improvement in the severity of acne are similar to those found by Palombo-Kinne et al,20 in a double-blind and randomized trial that compared the combination of ethinylestradiol (0.030 µg) with dianogest (2 mg) and ethinylestradiol with cyproterone acetate and its effect on the severity of acne measured by the Investigator Global Assessment scale. They found that 91% of women who received the combination with dienogest improved at least a degree of severity of acne at 6 months of follow-up. In the present study, which was carried out in a real-life context, with a combination of ethinylestradiol (0.020 µg) with dianogest (2 mg), similar results were found at 6 and 12 months of follow-up. Similar to this study, Costa et al.21 found a progressive decrease in the frequency of severe and moderate acne grades, with 29.6% of women having no acne at the end of follow-up. Meanwhile, Zimmermann et al,22 found that acne healing was observed more frequently in women who had mild acne. Other clinical trial made by Golbs et al23,24 found an improvement in the severity of acne, although with variable magnitude at 12 months of follow-up.

Other studies tested the combination of estradiol valerate and dienogest and reported improvement of acne lesions, although in a smaller amount than this study (53%).25 This combination used a stepped dose of dienogest. Additionally, the beneficial effects of dienogest on acne vulgaris are similar when combined with other progestogens, such as desogestrel,26–30 chlormadinone acetate,31–33 cyproterone acetate,20,34 drosperinone,33,34 and levonorgestrel.26,28,32 In the literature review, no study reported a null effect of the use of the combination studied in this work or of the dienogest.

Due to the demonstrated efficacy of COCPs after 6 months of treatment, recent (2024) guidelines from the American Academy of Dermatology recommend the use of COCPs (4 combinations approved by the Food and Drug Administration) for the treatment of moderate acne in women. This use should be guided by the patient’s comorbidity profile and preferences.35 Additionally, the Global Alliance to Improve Outcomes in Acne highlights that risks (pulmonary thromboembolism, myocardial infarction, stroke, and cancers) can be anticipated with a thorough medical history and that COCPs can offer added benefits to women who wish to use them or who use them for family planning, such as regulating menstrual cycles and treating hormonal disorders and pelvic pain.36

The scientific literature shows that acne has obvious medical and dermatological implications. In fact, visual appearance can affect people’s social, emotional, and psychological functioning. In fact, stressful events have been reported at several points in relation to acne. Adult patients with acne are affected by both common psychiatric conditions and a tendency to somatization.37 Its incidences are such that it even affects the quality of those who suffer from it.38,39 In this sense, acne possesses a significant burden in terms of social and psychological illness.40 Notably, a relationship has been shown to exist between acne and low self-esteem, loss of confidence, depression, anxiety, and even suicidal ideation.4,38,39,41–43 On the other hand, considers domain most affected by acne was symptoms and feelings. However, interpersonal relationships are the least affected. It means that, contrary to Boon-Bin Yap,38 interpersonal relationships are significant aspects that are deteriorated by the presence of acne.

The presence of acne has psychological implications for the formation of social and familial bonds. The quality of life of patients with acne and their families is lower compared with that of healthy individuals.44 Typically, the focus tends to be on relationships within one’s peer group. However, it is essential to consider familial relationships, as they form the foundation for the need to be accepted, recognized, and loved. Improvements in acne conditions can lead to fewer family conflicts, as acne reduction ceases to be a familial concern regarding how the affected family member is perceived. Economic concerns associated with the cost of specific treatments to improve acne conditions are also alleviated. This is particularly significant given that the socioeconomic context of the women participating in the study corresponds to middle- and lower-income strata.

Managing acne with hormones is one of the therapeutic alternatives for young adult women even without hormonal disturbances. These are highly effective, as this study demonstrates. Furthermore, they have the advantage of being long-term, have no potential to induce bacterial resistance,45 and can have low adverse effects. The study found that the most frequent adverse effect was small breakthrough bleeding (spotting) in 15 participating women.12

On the other hand, as mentioned, acne affects people’s quality of life. The fact that the women participating in the study recognize noncontraceptive benefits in the treatment, such as a decrease in clothing size, less production of oil on the scalp, and especially the regularity of the cycles and the decrease of cramps, all aspects that favor better conditions in their quality of life. This situation, when conceived favorably, is a factor that leads to continuing with the treatment in a consistent and systematic way.

Limitations and strengths

In relation to the scale of acne severity measurement, a locally used scaling was chosen because it describes in detail the characteristics of acne according to the lesions found, which limits the comparison with other studies. However, the clinical evaluation of acne has had multiple developments46,47 which has made it difficult to compare the studies carried out since there is no standardized scale.48 Additionally, in a clinical practice routine, a scale that makes a strict count of the lesions is less used. Instead, more qualitative and subjective scales are applied, like the ones used in this study. In future studies, the validation of the scale used would be necessary. The absence of a standardized scale for measuring perceived psychological and noncontraceptive benefits represents another limitation. However, this was partially mitigated by piloting the questions used before the start of fieldwork.

A strength of the study was the assessment of photographs by 2 dermatology experts who evaluated changes in the severity of the acne during the entire follow-up period, with a high agreement on the application of the acne classification scale. (Supplementary material Figure 2, https://links.lww.com/IJWD/A74). Including clinical experts in outcome measurement can lead to a more accurate and reliable evaluation, especially when contrasted with self-reported assessments, as seen in a cohort study published in 2021 that evaluated a similar combination of COCPs. Thus, the findings of this study offer relevant knowledge in this area. This article complements the evidence from Costa et al.21 regarding the perception of skin comfort benefits by also providing evidence on the perceived benefits of reduced acne severity due to COCP use on mental health, specifically improved self-esteem and interpersonal relationships.

Future research should explore the relationship between acne improvement and adherence to COCP use. This would help clarify whether the psychological and noncontraceptive benefits associated with the ethinyl estradiol and dienogest combination might encourage greater adherence to this contraceptive method.

Conclusion

The combination with very low ethinyl estradiol and dienogest improves the degree of severity of acne from early stages, 3 to 6 months of using the pill, and remains up to 12 months. The use of oral contraceptives could be an alternative to decrease the severity of acne in adult women in whom oral contraceptives are not contraindicated. Additionally, this reduction in acne leads to an increase in self-esteem in women, an improvement in social relationships, and positively impacts the quality of life. In contexts like that of the present study—characterized by economic limitations and persistent challenges in reducing unplanned pregnancies, especially among adolescents—the positive impact on acne and mental health could serve as an additional incentive to promote adherence to effective contraceptive methods.
