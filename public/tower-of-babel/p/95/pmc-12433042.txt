Identify malignant pulmonary nodules-associated circRNAs and develop a prediction model to estimate the probability of malignancy in pulmonary nodules
Authors: Keping Chen, Huidi Sun, Rui Zhang, Chuankun Yang, Guoqing Wang, Tong Lu, Zhimao Bai, Guoqiu Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe increasing incidence of asymptomatic pulmonary nodules (PNs) underscores the need for accurate malignancy estimation to guide early-stage lung cancer management. This study aimed to identify risk factors for malignant PNs and develop a risk prediction model.MethodsThis study enrolled 691 patients with PNs (444 training, 247 validation) and 62 healthy controls. Clinical, imaging, and serum data were collected. Transcriptome sequencing was performed to identify circular RNAs (circRNAs) associated with malignant PNs. Additionally, Tumor-associated antigens and tumor-associated autoantibodies (AAbs) were measured. Univariate logistic regression analysis was employed to identify risk factors for malignant PNs, followed by multivariate logistic regression to establish a risk prediction model. Finally, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of the risk prediction model.ResultsAmong five tumor-associated antigens, only neuron-specific enolase (NSE) levels were significantly higher in malignant versus benign PNs (P<0.001). AAb positivity rates and number of positive AAbs were elevated in malignant PNs. Transcriptome sequencing revealed hsa_circCFLAR_008 was upregulated in malignant PNs (P=0.04). The risk prediction model was established [area under the curve (AUC), 0.8241], and was validated with a concordance statistic (C-statistic) of 0.8344 in an independent cohort.ConclusionsA risk prediction model for malignant PNs was established. Hsa_circCFLAR_008 enhanced the diagnostic performance of the model and served as a novel biomarker for malignant PNs.

Highlight box

Key findings

• The study identified hsa_circCFLAR_008 as a differentially expressed circular RNA (circRNA) in malignant pulmonary nodules (PNs), which enhanced the diagnostic performance of the risk prediction model.

• The positivity rate and number of tumor-associated autoantibodies (AAbs) were significantly higher in malignant PNs compared to benign PNs.

• A risk prediction model incorporating clinical variables, tumor-associated antigens, AAbs, and circRNA was developed, demonstrating superior diagnostic performance compared to the Mayo Clinic model, with an area under the curve of 0.8241 in the training cohort and 0.8344 in the validation cohort.

What is known and what is new?

• The increasing incidence of asymptomatic PNs necessitates accurate malignancy estimation to guide early-stage lung cancer management. Traditional methods such as computed tomography scans and tumor-associated antigens have limitations in differentiating malignant from benign PNs. The Mayo Clinic model is widely used for malignancy probability estimation in PNs but may not be optimal for the Chinese population.

• This study is the first to integrate transcriptome sequencing to identify circRNAs associated with malignant PNs and develop a risk prediction model incorporating clinical variables, tumor-associated antigens, AAbs, and circRNA. The model exhibited strong discriminative capacity in both training and validation cohorts, highlighting its potential utility in PN risk estimation for the Chinese population.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The findings suggest that hsa_circCFLAR_008 could serve as a novel biomarker for malignant PNs, and the developed risk prediction model may improve the accuracy of malignancy estimation in PNs, potentially reducing unnecessary invasive procedures and facilitating early intervention for malignant nodules.

• Future large-scale, multicenter studies are warranted to further validate and optimize the risk prediction model. Clinical guidelines for PN management should consider incorporating the identified biomarkers and the risk prediction model to enhance the accuracy of malignancy estimation in PNs.

Introduction

Lung cancer is one of the most common cancers worldwide, with high morbidity and mortality (1). Early detection and treatment of lung cancer are crucial in reducing mortality and improving the overall survival of patients. Distinguishing malignant from benign pulmonary nodules (PNs) at early stages constitutes a critical clinical challenge. PNs are predominantly solitary or multiple small lung shadows with increased density, typically defined as ≤30 mm in diameter, which may signal early lung cancer. Radiographic methods, especially computed tomography (CT), have been widely used to screen for PNs, and an increasing number of patients have been detected (2). However, most of the PNs are benign and do not require treatment. For instance, tuberculosis and pneumonia often present in a similar clinical and radiological manner to malignant PNs (3-5). The key step in the management of patients with PNs is to estimate the probability of malignancy in the nodules according to the American College of Chest Physicians (ACCP) guidelines (6). Therefore, the diagnosis and treatment of patients with malignant nodules can be expedited, and diagnostic testing for patients with benign nodules may be minimized. Therefore, establishing definitive diagnostic criteria for differentiating malignant from benign PNs is critical in clinical practice. Based on the probability of malignancy, clinicians can make an appropriate management plan for patients with PNs. In this case, PNs with a high probability of malignancy should be evaluated aggressively and considered for surgical resection. Meanwhile, PNs with a low probability of malignancy should be monitored with surveillance imaging according to the clinical practice guidelines of ACCP (7).

Low-dose chest CT scan has a higher sensitivity for detecting PNs as compared to radiography. It can be used to outline the nodule’s characteristics including the location, shape, and margins, and assess the probability of malignancy in PNs. The widespread use of CT has identified smaller nodules and provided more information about the nodules. However, the lack of accuracy in CT scans usually leads to a high false-positive rate (8). At present, biopsy approaches, including surgical and minimally invasive pathological diagnoses, remain the gold standard for discriminating malignant PNs from benign PNs. Therefore, there is an urgent need to identify simple, specific, and noninvasive biomarkers that can be utilized for the diagnosis and monitoring of PNs. As the collection of blood samples is minimally invasive and the samples can be serial detected (9), tumor-associated antigens in the blood are widely used in cancer diagnosis and monitoring. Strikingly, multiple tumor markers such as pro-gastrin releasing peptide (Pro-GRP), neuron-specific enolase (NSE), carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cytokeratin 19 fragment 21-1 (CYFRA21-1), and squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) antigen can provide valuable information for diagnosis and monitoring of PNs (10). Moreover, the detection of tumor-associated autoantibodies (AAbs) in blood has been employed in lung cancer diagnosis (11). It has recently been shown that a panel of AAbs including antibodies against p53, PGP9.5, SOX2, GAGE7, GBU4-5, MAGE-A1, and CAGE demonstrate clinical value in the early diagnosis of lung cancer and could serve as a diagnostic biomarker (12,13). Due to their high stability, abundant expression, and high specificity, circular RNAs (circRNAs) have the potential to serve as promising biomarkers for certain human diseases (14). Previous studies have demonstrated that abnormal expression of circRNAs is associated with various diseases, including multiple types of tumors (15,16). CircRNAs can assist in the early diagnosis of tumors, monitoring of treatment efficacy, and prognosis evaluation. Additionally, circRNAs can serve as potential therapeutic agents for tumors. Therefore, it is necessary to investigate the role of circRNAs in estimating the probability of malignancy in PNs.

The Mayo Clinic model is widely adopted for malignancy probability estimation in PNs, demonstrating moderate to strong discriminatory performance across diverse nodule characteristics (7). The model is built from the clinical characteristics (e.g., age, smoking history) and radiographic features (e.g., nodule size, margin morphology) of patients with PNs. The accuracy and discrimination of prediction models depend on clinical characteristics and the prevalence of malignancy in the population from which they are derived (7). Therefore, it is necessary to determine whether the Mayo Clinic model is valid and can discriminate malignant nodules from benign PNs in the Chinese population.

This study aims to utilize transcriptome sequencing technology to detect circRNAs associated with malignant PNs. Then, we assessed the diagnostic value of the clinical and Imaging characteristics, respiratory tumor-associated antigen panel, AAbs panel, and circRNAs in discriminating malignant nodules from benign PNs. Furthermore, we develop an appropriate prediction model for estimating the probability of malignant PNs in the Chinese population with PNs. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-648/rc).

Methods

Sample size estimation

In this study, participants were required to have a definitive pathological diagnosis. As most PNs are benign and do not require surgical intervention, obtaining a definitive pathological diagnosis for benign PNs is challenging. Therefore, the number of patients with benign PNs was significantly lower than that of patients with malignant PNs in this study. To address this issue and ensure the robustness of our study design, we employed NCSS PASS 11 (version 11.0.7; NCSS, LLC, Kaysville, UT, USA) to calculate the required sample size based on a null hypothesis value, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.8, α of 0.05, β of 0.30, and a two-sided test. The minimum required sample size was 100 for malignant PNs and 50 for benign PNs. This approach ensured that our study was adequately powered to detect meaningful differences while acknowledging the inherent challenges in recruiting patients with benign PNs.

Study cohort

The study cohort flow chart was shown in Figure 1. The study population included patients with PNs ≤30 mm in diameter, who were recruited from the Zhongda Hospital between October 2019 and October 2022. The inclusion criteria were defined according to World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines. All patients underwent preoperative CT scans and subsequently underwent wedge resection, segmentectomy, or lobectomy via video-assisted thoracic surgery (VATS). Based on pathological diagnosis, the patients were categorized into two groups: those with malignant PNs and those with benign PNs. The control subjects with no nodules were enrolled after receiving routine examinations including the chest CT scan. Patients were excluded from the study if they lacked clinical data, CT scan reports, or definitive pathological diagnosis or had a history of cancer such as lung cancer and extra-thoracic cancer within 5 years or autoimmune diseases.

Ethical statement

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the ethics committee of Zhongda Hospital, Southeast University (No. 2022ZDSYLL121-P01) and informed consent was obtained from all individual participants.

Measurement of respiratory tumor-associated antigens

The respiratory tumor-associated antigen panel included CEA, NSE, CYFRA21-1, Pro-GRP, and SCC, which were assayed in both patients and control groups by using the Cobas 8000 analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland). Positivity thresholds for individual respiratory tumor-associated antigens were defined as: CEA ≥1.12 ng/mL, NSE ≥16.30 ng/mL, CYFRA21-1 ≥13.30 U/mL, Pro-GRP ≥68.3 pg/mL, and SCC ≥2.7 ng/mL.

Measurement of tumor-associated AAbs

The AAbs panel targeted the following antigens: p53, PGP9.5, SOX2, GAGE7, GBU4-5, MAGE-A1, and CAGE. All the antibodies were quantified by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, diluted serum samples were added into antigen-coated microwells, allowing AAbs in the serum to bind specifically to the immobilized antigens. After removing unbound components, enzyme-conjugated anti-human immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies were added to the microwells and incubated to form antigen-antibody complexes. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. AAb levels were determined relative to a standard curve. According to the manufacturer’s reference values, positivity thresholds for individual AAbs were defined as follows: MAGE-A1 ≥11.9 U/mL, SOX2 ≥10.3 U/mL, GAGE7 ≥14.4 U/mL, p53 ≥13.1 U/mL, CAGE ≥7.2 U/mL, PGP9.5 ≥11.1 U/mL, and GBU4-5 ≥7.0 U/mL.

Identification of circRNAs associated with malignant PNs

To identify circRNAs linked to the progression of PNs to lung cancer, serum samples were collected from three cohorts: patients with benign PNs, malignant PNs, or lung cancer (tumor diameter >30 mm), followed by transcriptome sequencing. Eight samples each from benign PNs, malignant PNs, and lung cancer were submitted to Guangzhou GENESEED Biotech Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China) for transcriptome sequencing. Total RNA was extracted from serum using the Trizol method. RNA quality was assessed, and sequencing libraries were prepared before sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Reads were aligned to the reference genome using STAR (Cold Spring Harbor, NY, USA) (17), and circRNAs were identified and quantified with DCC (Leipzig, Germany) (18). Gene expression was normalized using the trimmed mean of M-values (TMM) method. Differentially expressed circRNAs were defined as those with a fold change >1.5 and a false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted P value <0.05 using the edgeR package (19). Differentially expressed circRNAs were validated via quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR).

Total RNA was extracted from serum using the NP968 nucleic acid extraction system with manufacturer-provided reagents (Xi’an Tianlong Technology Co., Ltd., Xi’an, China). circRNA primers were designed and validated using the circbank database (http://www.circbank.cn/) (20), circBase database (http://www.circrna.org) (21), and circPrimer software (http://www.bioinf.com.cn). CircRNAs were detected on the Applied Biosystems 7500 platform (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The qPCR amplification condition was as follows: pre-denaturation at 95 ℃ for 30 s; 40 cycles of 95 ℃ for 5 s and 60 ℃ for 30 s; followed by a melt curve analysis using the default instrument program.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared with unequal variance t-test. Categorical variables were reported as frequency (percentage) and analyzed with the Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Non-normally distributed data were compared between groups using the Mann-Whitney U test. Univariable logistic regression analysis was conducted in the training cohort to identify variables associated with malignant PNs. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to develop a prediction model to estimate the probability of malignancy in PNs. The model’s diagnostic performance was validated in an independent cohort, with discrimination assessed by the concordance statistic (C-statistic). All analyses were performed with IBM SPSS Statistics (version 23.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study population

This study recruited 691 patients with PNs and 62 healthy controls. Pathological examination revealed that 592 patients had malignant PNs and 99 had benign PNs. The histological distribution of malignant PNs was as follows: invasive adenocarcinoma for 47.30% (280 cases), adenocarcinoma in situ accounted for 37.33% (221 cases), other types of adenocarcinoma for 8.45% (50 cases), SCC for 5.24% (31 cases), and other rare types of lung cancer for 1.68%. In this study, 377 malignant PNs and 67 benign PNs were designated as the training cohort, with the remaining cases forming the validation cohort.

Clinical characteristics of PN patients in the training cohort and healthy controls were summarized in Table 1. Malignant PNs were more prevalent in female patients. Age differed significantly among benign PNs, malignant PNs, and healthy controls (P<0.001), with healthy controls being younger than both PN groups. However, no significant age difference was observed between benign and malignant PN patients (P=0.55). When stratified into three age groups (<40; 40–60; >60 years), significant distributional differences were observed among the three cohorts (P<0.001), but no difference was found between benign and malignant PNs (P=0.45). Notably, PN prevalence increased with age, particularly in individuals over 60 years. The lack of healthy controls over 60 years in this study reflected the elevated incidence of PNs in this age demographic. Imaging characteristics of benign and malignant PNs were detailed in Table 2. Malignant PNs exhibited larger diameters (P=0.045), higher prevalence in upper lobes (P=0.002), and a greater proportion of pure ground-glass nodules (P=0.001). Contrary to expectations, spiculation was not associated with malignancy (P=0.42), which may reflect the complexity of PN radiological classification.

Clinical value of respiratory tumor-associated antigen for malignant PNs

Among the five tumor-associated antigens, CEA showed no significant differences. However, the other four tumor-associated antigen (NSE, SCC, CYFRA 21-1, and Pro-GRP) levels showed significant differences among malignant PNs, benign PNs, and healthy controls (P<0.001, P=0.001, P=0.01, and P<0.001, respectively). To further evaluate the clinical value of these tumor-associated antigens, this study analyzed their levels in malignant PNs and benign PNs. As shown in Table S1 and Figure 2, CYFRA 21-1, Pro-GRP, and SCC exhibited elevated levels in malignant PNs compared to benign PNs, though these differences had no statistical significance (P=0.68, P=0.06, and P=0.79, respectively). Notably, NSE was the only antigen with significantly higher levels in malignant PNs compared to benign PNs (P<0.001). Additionally, NSE levels in benign PNs were significantly higher than those in healthy controls (P<0.001, Figure 2B). These results suggested a stepwise increase in serum NSE levels from healthy controls to benign PNs and further to malignant PNs, highlighting its potential as a biomarker for estimating malignancy risk.

Clinical value of AAbs for malignant PNs

Four AAbs (p53, PGP9.5, SOX2, and GBU4-5) showed no significant differences among benign PNs, malignant PNs, and healthy controls (P=0.96, P=0.58, P=0.30, and P=0.30, respectively). In contrast, the other three AAbs (MAGE-A1, CAGE, and GAGE7) exhibited significant differences among the three groups (P<0.001, P<0.001, and P=0.008, respectively). However, MAGE-A1, CAGE, and GAGE7 levels did not differ significantly between malignant and benign PNs (P=0.58, P=0.98, and P=0.42, respectively; Table S2 and Figure 3).

According to the manufacturer-defined positivity thresholds for the AAbs assay, a sample was classified as AAbs positive if at least one AAbs exceeded its reference cutoff. Among 377 malignant and 67 benign PNs, 50.13% (189/377) of malignant PNs and 29.85% (20/67) of benign PNs were AAb-positive. The positive predictive value (PPV) for malignancy was 90.43% (189/209). Malignant PNs exhibited a significantly higher AAbs positivity rate than the benign PNs (χ2=8.333 and P=0.004). Furthermore, the number of positive AAbs per sample differed significantly between malignant and benign PNs (χ2=9.393 and P<0.001) (Figure 3H). Notably, samples with ≥3 positive AAbs were exclusively observed in malignant PNs, whereas benign PNs had ≤2 positive AAbs.

Differentially expressed circRNAs in PNs

Transcriptome sequencing identified 35 circRNAs with significant differential expression between benign and malignant PNs. Among these, three circRNAs (hsa_circCFLAR_008, hsa_circRFC1_002, and hsa_circNPAT_017) were also differentially expressed between malignant PNs and advanced lung cancer. Validation by qPCR in a cohort of 197 PN patients (165 malignant PNs and 32 benign PNs) showed detectable amplification for five circRNAs. However, only hsa_circCFLAR_008 showed a statistically significant difference between benign and malignant PNs (P=0.04) (Table S3 and Figure 4).

Development of a risk model for malignant PNs

Univariate logistic regression analysis identified potential risk factors for malignant PNs. Subsequently, a forward stepwise multivariate logistic regression was employed to construct a risk prediction model (designated as Model 1). The final model incorporated six variables: gender, nodule density, nodule location, NSE level, number of positive AAbs, and expression level of hsa_circCFLAR_008. The probability (P) of malignant PNs was calculated using the formula: P = 1/[1 + EXP(−1.648 − 1.114 × male + 1.173 × ground-glass + 0.732 × upper lobe + 0.162 × NSE + 0.779 × number of positive AAbs + 0.225 × hsa_circCFLAR_008)]. A cutoff value of P>0.8146 was defined as high malignant risk.

In the training cohort, serum NSE exhibited moderate discriminatory ability for malignant PNs with an AUC of 0.653 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.580–0.725] (Figure 5A). Similarly, hsa_circCFLAR_008 expression showed an AUC of 0.613 (95% CI: 0.556–0.670) (Figure 5B). Model 1 (incorporating NSE and hsa_circCFLAR_008) demonstrated significantly improved diagnostic performance compared to the Mayo Clinic model, with an AUC of 0.8241 (95% CI: 0.7727–0.8755), sensitivity of 80.05%, and specificity of 74.63% versus the Mayo model’s AUC of 0.6367 (95% CI: 0.5658–0.7076). Removing hsa_circCFLAR_008 reduced the model’s AUC to 0.7785 (95% CI: 0.7166–0.8403) (Model 2) (Table S4, Figure 5C). Validation in an independent cohort demonstrated comparable performance with a C-statistic of 0.8344 (95% CI: 0.7618–0.9071), closely aligning with the training cohort AUC of 0.8241. Collectively, Model 1 achieved clinically meaningful discrimination between malignant and benign PNs, with an AUC exceeding 0.82 in both training and validation cohorts, highlighting its potential utility in risk estimation.

Discussion

This study employed transcriptome sequencing to identify circRNAs associated with malignant PNs and evaluated the diagnostic potential of respiratory tumor-associated antigens, tumor-associated AAbs, and circRNAs for distinguishing malignant from benign PNs in a Chinese population. Our findings revealed that the positivity rate and number of AAbs were significantly higher in malignant PNs compared to benign PNs. Among five tumor-associated antigens, only NSE exhibited significantly elevated levels in malignant PNs. Additionally, hsa_circCFLAR_008 was identified as a differentially expressed circRNA in malignant PNs. Finally, we developed a multivariate risk prediction model incorporating six clinical variables: gender, nodule density, nodule location, NSE level, number of positive AAbs, and hsa_circCFLAR_008 level. Validation in an independent cohort revealed its superior diagnostic performance compared to the Mayo Clinic model, highlighting its potential in PN risk estimation.

The diagnostic potential of AAbs for early-stage lung cancer has been extensively validated (12,13). Luo et al. reported elevated AAbs in patients with lung cancer compared to those with benign lesions, suggesting AAbs as a supplementary diagnostic biomarker (22). However, their study lacked stratification by nodule size, and found no correlation between AAbs with pathological stages (22), limiting their utility in risk estimation of PNs. Similarly, Du et al. observed higher levels of PGP9.5, SOX2, GBU4-5, MAGE-A1, and CAGE in patients with lung cancer than in healthy controls (12). Notably, these studies focused on lung cancer rather than PNs ≤30 mm in diameter. Lung lesions with a diameter >30 mm were typically managed via invasive procedures (e.g., biopsy or resection), whereas PNs require non-invasive risk estimation to avoid unnecessary interventions. In contrast, our study specifically targeted PNs and found no significant differences in AAbs levels between malignant and benign PNs. However, the higher AAbs positivity rate and number of positive AAbs in malignant PNs suggested their limited but potential role in the risk prediction of PNs.

Consistent with previous study (23), we observed significant differences in serum levels of CEA, NSE, CYFRA21-1, Pro-GRP, and SCC among malignant PNs, benign PNs, and healthy controls. Notably, just NSE was significantly elevated in malignant PNs compared to benign PNs (P<0.001), whereas other markers showed no significant differential expression. While NSE is classically associated with small-cell lung cancer (SCLC) (24,25), its elevation in non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) has also been reported (26). In our cohort, 97.08% of malignant PNs were pathologically confirmed as early-stage NSCLC (e.g., adenocarcinoma, SCC), underscoring the clinical value of NSE in estimating malignancy risk in NSCLC-dominant populations.

As liquid biopsy biomarkers, circRNAs are increasingly being studied due to their high stability in peripheral blood and resistance to RNase degradation. Abnormal circRNA expression has been linked to various diseases, including cancers (27,28), neurodegenerative disorders (29), cardiovascular diseases (30), and immune disorders (31,32). Caspase-8 and FADD-like apoptosis regulator (CFLAR), a key negative regulator of apoptosis in mammalian cells, influences tumorigenesis by modulating cell proliferation and survival (33,34). As a circRNA derived from CFLAR, the identified hsa_circCFLAR_008 was differentially expressed in malignant PNs, which highlights its potential as a novel biomarker for malignant PNs. As novel biomarkers for PNs, the roles of the two plasma proteins LG3BP and C163A have also been investigated (35). The integrated classifier utilizing LG3BP and C163A has demonstrated remarkable sensitivity and negative predictive value, effectively reducing the need for invasive procedures in patients with low to moderate risk (35,36). Meanwhile, hsa_circCFLAR_008, though promising, may require further validation to establish their diagnostic accuracy and clinical utility. Both LG3BP/C163A and circRNA offer valuable tools for PN risk assessment. Future research should explore the combined use of these biomarkers to optimize diagnostic strategies and clinical outcomes.

The Mayo Clinic model, widely used for PNs >8 mm, demonstrates moderate-to-excellent diagnostic accuracy in Western populations (7,37). However, its predictive efficacy depends on clinical characteristics and malignancy prevalence in the source population (7). Therefore, the Mayo model is not suitable for risk assessment in the Chinese population, and our results also had confirmed this. So, we developed a risk prediction model incorporating imaging characteristics, tumor-associated antigens panel, AAbs panel, and circRNA. This model exhibited strong discriminative capacity in both training cohort and validation cohort. The ethnic-specific model met the urgent need for early-stage PN evaluation in Chinese population, particularly given the increasing detection rates of PNs. This study certainly had several limitations. First, this retrospective study was limited to the Chinese patients who were free of lung cancer, extra-thoracic cancer, and autoimmune disease within 5 years to avoid affecting the detection of tumor-associated antigens and AAbs. Second, the model was only internally validated. Third, the number of benign PNs was relatively small because a definite pathological diagnosis was required for all the patients in this study, although there are a large number of patients with benign PNs. Hence, future large-scale, multicenter studies are needed to optimize the risk prediction model for PNs.

Conclusions

This study successfully identified hsa_circCFLAR_008 as a novel biomarker for malignant PNs and developed a risk prediction model incorporating clinical variables, tumor-associated antigens, AAbs, and circRNA expression. The model demonstrated superior diagnostic performance compared to existing models, with an AUC of 0.8241 in the training cohort and 0.8344 in the validation cohort. These findings highlight the potential of hsa_circCFLAR_008 as a valuable biomarker for distinguishing malignant from benign PNs, particularly in the Chinese population. Future research should focus on validating this model in larger, multicenter cohorts to optimize its clinical utility.
