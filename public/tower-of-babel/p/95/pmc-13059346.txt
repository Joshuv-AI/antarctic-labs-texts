Bisphenol a and polycystic ovary syndrome in reproductive-age women: mechanistic exploration via network toxicology and molecular docking
Authors: Changsheng Peng, Caixia Wang, Jie Chen, Hui Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) is a prevalent endocrine and metabolic disorder affecting 6–20% of reproductive-age women worldwide, characterized by hyperandrogenism, ovulatory dysfunction, and polycystic ovarian morphology, often accompanied by insulin resistance and infertility. Emerging evidence suggests that environmental endocrine-disrupting chemicals, such as Bisphenol A (BPA), may contribute to PCOS pathogenesis. This study employs network toxicology and molecular docking to elucidate the mechanistic links between BPA exposure and PCOS. By integrating multi-database analyses, we identified 294 BPA-related targets and 67 shared targets with PCOS, including key genes (PPARG, HSP90AA1, IGF1R, and INSR) implicated in hormonal dysregulation, insulin resistance, and inflammation. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks and enrichment analyses revealed their involvement in critical pathways such as estrogen signaling, ovarian steroidogenesis, and PI3K-Akt signaling. Molecular docking confirmed stable binding between BPA and these targets, with IGF1R exhibiting the strongest affinity (− 8.0 kcal/mol). Validation using the GSE5090 dataset demonstrated significant upregulation of PPARG and HSP90AA1 in PCOS patients. Our findings suggest that BPA disrupts follicular development, exacerbates hyperandrogenism, and impairs metabolic homeostasis through multi-target interactions, providing novel insights into the environmental etiology of PCOS and potential therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

According to the 2023 report issued by the World Health Organization (WHO), approximately 17.5% of the global adult population, which is roughly equivalent to one-sixth, is affected by infertility [1]. Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is the most common cause of ovulatory disorder-related infertility and one of the most prevalent endocrine and metabolic disorders in women of reproductive age with a worldwide prevalence ranging from 6% to 20% [2, 3]. It is characterized by hyperandrogenism, ovulatory dysfunction, and polycystic ovarian morphology, often accompanied by complications such as insulin resistance, obesity, and infertility [4]. Women with PCOS are exposed to these metabolic alterations starting from a young age, but approximately 50% of PCOS patients are not diagnosed in a timely manner, which imposes a significant burden on their long-term health and leads to a considerable increase in healthcare-related costs [5]. The cost of infertility treatments associated with PCOS (e.g., in vitro fertilization) is substantial, with an estimated average annual global expenditure exceeding 5 billion USD. Additionally, patients with PCOS are at a threefold higher risk of developing anxiety or depression compared to the general population, which further compromises treatment adherence [6]. Although genetic factors and lifestyle are associated with PCOS, the potential role of environmental chemicals has garnered increasing attention in recent years, particularly endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs), which may contribute to PCOS pathogenesis through epigenetic or metabolic interference. It has been confirmed that a variety of natural and synthetic chemical substances have endocrine-disrupting activities including phytoestrogens, pharmaceutical and cosmetic ingredients such as parabens, triclosanol (TCS) and pesticides, plastic products such as bisphenol A (BPA) and its substitutes, plasticizers (phthalates), metals (heavy metals, trace elements), and industrial chemicals containing by-products [7–9].

BPA (2,2-bis (4-hydroxyphenol) propane), a primary component of plasticizers and epoxy resins, is widely found in daily products (e.g., optical, media, electrical and electronics, houseware and appliances, medical devices, dental materials, reusable and baby plastic bottles). Currently, it is one of the most mass-produced plastic monomers and plasticizers globally. Due to the hydrolysis of ester bonds linking BPA monomers in polycarbonate plastics, BPA can leach from food containers and composite materials. Humans are inevitably exposed to BPA through dietary intake, dermal contact, and other pathways, with drinking water being the primary route of exposure [10–12]. BPA has been detected in various human fluids and tissues, including serum, urine, amniotic fluid, follicular fluid, placental tissue, and umbilical cord blood. Notably, peripheral blood BPA levels are significantly higher in PCOS patients than in controls [13–15]. Although some countries/regions have imposed strict bans or restrictions on BPA in food-related applications, regulations in non-food sectors remain inadequate, potentially creating policy loopholes. Furthermore, global coordination is insufficient, with significant disparities in regulatory standards across countries, leading to potential “regulatory arbitrage” in international trade.

BPA is a classic estrogen receptor (ER) and androgen receptor (AR) modulator, capable of disrupting hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis function by mimicking / antagonizing sex hormone actions [16]. Animal studies indicate that BPA exposure can induce PCOS-like phenotypes [17]. Studies in adult rat models have demonstrated that neonatal exposure to BPA may lead to potential upregulation of gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) pulse generator activity in adulthood [18]. Additionally, BPA has been demonstrated to competitively displace sex steroids from sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG), resulting in elevated free testosterone concentrations [19]. Since PCOS is characterized by a proinflammatory state, chronic low-grade inflammation may further exacerbate ovarian dysfunction and enhance excessive theca cells androgen [20]. But human epidemiological findings remain inconsistent, and the underlying mechanisms are not fully elucidated. Studies focusing on single molecules or pathways struggle to explain the complexity of the BPA-PCOS association. By integrating multi-omics data, bioinformatics analysis, and molecular network modeling, key pathways, hub genes, and molecular interactions can be systematically identified, uncovering the cascade from molecular perturbations to phenotypic abnormalities following BPA exposure and addressing the fragmentation in mechanistic research.

This study leverages public databases to identify shared targets between BPA and PCOS, constructs protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks, and performs pathway enrichment analysis. Key nodes are further validated using GEO datasets and molecular docking. The findings aim to provide novel evidence for the environmental etiology of PCOS and establish a theoretical foundation for risk assessment and intervention strategies.

Methods

Identification of BPA and PCOS-related targets

PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) was utilized to obtain the SMILES (Simplified molecular input line entry system), 2D and 3D structure of BPA. To predict human target genes potentially influenced by Bisphenol A (BPA), we utilized several established databases, including Swiss Target Prediction (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/), ChEMBL database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/chembl/), super-PRED (https://prediction.charite.de/index.php/), PharmMapper database (https://lilab-ecust.cn/pharmmapper/index.html/) and Similarity Ensemble Approach (SEA) database (https://sea.bkslab.org/). PCOS-related genes were collected from the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/), MalaCards database (https://www.malacards.org/) and DISGENET database (https://disgenet.com/) using the keyword “PCOS, Polycystic Ovary Syndrome, Polycystic Ovarian Syndrome”. The retrieved gene sets for BPA and PCOS were then integrated, with duplicates removed and gene annotations standardized using UniProt ((https://www.uniprot.org/) to generate refined target lists.

Construction of Protein–Protein Interaction (PPI) network

Genes overlapping between BPA and PCOS targets were analyzed for protein-protein interactions (PPI) via the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/) with parameters tailored for “Homo sapiens”. A threshold of “Medium Confidence” with an interaction score > 0.4 was applied. The results obtained from STRING were then imported into Cytoscape software (version 3.9.1) for the evaluation of the PPI network’s topological properties.

Identification of hub genes from PPI Network

To identify central nodes in the interaction network, the MCODE plugin within Cytoscape was applied to detect key subnetworks. Additionally, the cytoHubba plugin was utilized to compute “Node Scores” using 9 algorithms (e.g., Degree, MCC, EPC, Betweenness). For each algorithm, the top 20 ranked genes were extracted, and genes common to all ranking methods were defined as hub genes.

Verification of key genes

Key genes were validated using publicly available gene expression data from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The dataset GSE5090 was retrieved by searching the keyword “Polycystic Ovary Syndrome”. GSE5090 is a microarray-based gene expression dataset consisting of 9 patients with PCOS and 8 non-PCOS control individuals.

Processed expression data were downloaded directly from the GEO database. Data preprocessing and normalization were performed according to the procedures described in the original dataset. No additional batch correction was applied, as all samples were generated under comparable experimental conditions.

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed for dimensionality reduction and visualization of the gene expression data. PCA was conducted using standard settings to summarize variance across samples.

Functional enrichment analysis for target genes

Enrichment analyses of potential or hub genes were conducted using the Metascape (https://metascape.org/gp/index.html/) to investigate Gene Ontology (GO) terms, encompassing biological processes (BP), molecular functions (MF), and cellular components (CC), and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways analysis. Significant enrichment of terms or pathways was determined based on an adjusted P-value threshold of < 0.05. Then these data were imported into the bioinformatics mapping website (http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/) to draw enrichment bubble charts.

Molecular docking validation

To achieve a more comprehensive understanding of the intermolecular interactions between BPA and the hub proteins identified in our study, we utilized the molecular docking method. The target protein preparation process included the removal of water molecules and natural ligands using PyMOL. Subsequently, the protein was processed in AutoDock Tools (v1.5.6) for hydrogenation, charge distribution, and refinement of non-polar hydrogen bonds. The docking simulation was performed using AutoDock v1.2.6, and the results were visualized and interpreted using PyMOL.

Statistical analysis

The data analysis was performed using R-Studio (R version 4.0.3). Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were employed to assess the statistical significance of differences between two sample groups, with a threshold of P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

It is a key raw material in the production of polycarbonate plastics and epoxy resins, widely used in both daily consumer products and industrial applications. It’s two-dimensional and three-dimensional structure showed BPA is composed of two phenol groups (hydroxyl, -OH) linked by a propane bridge (-C(CH3)2-) (Fig. 2B), forming a symmetrical structure. Verification of Key Genes

We identified multiple critical targets involved in the ovarian steroidogenesis pathway (Fig. 5A), and Mendelian randomization analysis confirmed the associations of IGF1R, PPARG, and HSP90AA1 with PCOS (Fig. 5B). PCA of the GSE5090 dataset revealed clear segregation between the control and PCOS groups. The first principal component (PC1) accounted for 21.25% of the total variance, while the second component (PC2) explained an additional 12.24% (Fig. 5C). Notably, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed as an exploratory dimensionality-reduction and visualization approach to assess global expression patterns within the dataset. Given the high-dimensional nature of transcriptomic data and the moderate sample size, PCA was used solely to summarize dominant sources of variance and to visualize overall sample distribution, rather than for formal classification or inferential analysis. Accordingly, PCA results were interpreted with caution to avoid overrepresentation of group separation.

To define a focused set of targets for downstream verification, key genes were prioritized using a predefined, multi-criteria strategy. Assessment of Binding Capacity Between BPA and PCOS Key Targets by Molecular Docking

Molecular docking analysis revealed the binding affinity between BPA and four key protein targets was low: PPARG (-7.0 kcal/mol), INSR (-7.3 kcal/mol), IGF1R (-8.0 kcal/mol), and HSP90AA1 (-6.2 kcal/mol). Docking scores below − 5.0 kcal/mol are generally considered indicative of potentially meaningful interactions. Molecular docking scores are typically reported as negative values, with larger absolute values reflecting stronger predicted binding affinity and more stable binding conformations. Our results support the structural feasibility of potential interactions between BPA and these targets.

Structural characterization demonstrated that the active pockets of all four proteins maintained stable conformations upon BPA binding (Fig. 7A-D), indicating that BPA binds to PPARG/ INSR/ IGF1R/ HSP90AA1 directly, which elucidates the toxicological mechanism of BPA induces PCOS.

Discussion

Bisphenol A (BPA) is a crucial organic chemical raw material used in the synthesis of polycarbonates and epoxy resins, providing materials with high strength, transparency, and chemical resistance. It is widely applied in food containers, electronic product casings, medical devices, thermal paper, cosmetics, and other fields. According to the Global BPA Market Report and Forecast 2021–2026, the global BPA market was valued at $10.92 billion in 2020. Driven by rising demand from the automotive sector, the market is projected to grow at a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 7.8% from 2021 to 2026, potentially reaching $30.62 billion by 2026. Manufacturing consumes more than 8 million tones of BPA globally on yearly basis with Asia (particularly China) being the primary consumer region [22, 23]. This widespread use makes human exposure to BPA unavoidable. Moreover, BPA’s persistence in water and its bioaccumulation potential raise concerns about its endocrine-disrupting effects. The rising prevalence of polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) among women of reproductive age has drawn attention to the impact of environmental endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs) on reproductive health [24–26]. Epidemiological and animal studies suggest that exposure to plasticizers and other endocrine-disrupting chemicals, especially during critical developmental periods such as puberty, may increase the risk of PCOS. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying this association remain unclear [27].

Against this backdrop, this study employs toxicological network analysis and molecular docking techniques to explore the influence of BPA on the molecular pathogenesis of PCOS. Our toxicological network analysis identified 294 potential BPA target genes, which are involved in key biological processes such as cellular responses to nitrogen compounds, hormonal stimuli, and exogenous stimuli, highlighting BPA’s endocrine-disrupting properties. Among the 67 overlapping targets shared by BPA and PCOS, critical factors such as IGF1R, INSR, TNF, and PPARG were implicated in insulin resistance, ovarian steroidogenesis, and inflammatory pathways. Molecular docking was further used as an in silico, supportive analysis to assess the structural feasibility of potential interactions between BPA and selected targets, with binding distances (e.g., bond lengths) reported as part of the docking output to describe predicted contact geometry.

Enrichment analysis, molecular docking, and public database mining identified four key genes (PPARG, HSP90AA1, IGF1R, and INSR) that play pivotal roles in PCOS pathogenesis. Molecular docking suggested potential structural compatibility between BPA and these proteins, with IGF1R exhibiting the lowest predicted binding energy. These genes were upregulated in PCOS patients and participated in shared biological processes and pathways.

Peroxisome Proliferator-Activated Receptor Gamma (PPARG), a member of the nuclear hormone receptor superfamily, regulates lipid metabolism, insulin sensitivity, inflammation, and cell differentiation. It is highly expressed in adipose tissue, the liver, and ovaries [28]. As a key regulator of adipogenesis, PPARG promotes the differentiation of preadipocytes into mature adipocytes and modulates lipid metabolism-related genes (e.g., lipoprotein lipase, fatty acid-binding proteins) [29]. By enhancing glucose uptake in adipocytes (via GLUT4) and suppressing hepatic gluconeogenesis, PPARG improves insulin resistance (IR). It also inhibits pro-inflammatory pathways (e.g., NF-κB pathway), reducing the release of cytokines like TNF-α and IL-6 [30]. Notably, PPARG is expressed in ovarian granulosa cells, influencing follicular development and steroidogenesis. Dysregulation of PPARG may disrupt granulosa cell function, leading to increased follicular atresia and anovulation [31]. Animal studies show that BPA-exposed female rats develop PCOS-like symptoms (hyperandrogenism, anovulation) with downregulated PPARG expression [32]. BPA inhibits PPARG-mediated adipocyte differentiation, causing adipose tissue dysfunction and ectopic lipid deposition, exacerbating IR. By suppressing PPARG, BPA enhances CYP17A1 (a key androgen-synthesizing enzyme) activity, leading to ovarian testosterone overproduction and reduced hepatic SHBG synthesis, thereby elevating free androgen levels. PPARG agonists (e.g., rosiglitazone) can partially reverse BPA-induced metabolic abnormalities. However, some studies report elevated PPARG expression in PCOS adipose tissue, possibly as a compensatory response [31]. The results in our study demonstrated that the molecular docking energy between BPA and PPARG is -7.0 kcal/mol, indicating a strong binding affinity, which suggests that BPA can significantly inhibit PPARγ activity.

Heat Shock Protein 90 Alpha Family Class A Member 1 (HSP90AA1), a molecular chaperone, facilitates the proper folding of nascent or stress-damaged proteins (e.g., hormone receptors, kinases). It stabilizes nuclear receptors such as the glucocorticoid receptor (GR), estrogen receptor (ER), and androgen receptor (AR), while also regulating key kinases like AKT and ERK. BPA exposure increases intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS), activating heat shock factor 1 (HSF1) and upregulating HSP90AA1 to counteract proteotoxic stress [33]. BPA enhances the HSP90AA1-AR interaction, prolonging AR half-life and amplifying testosterone’s stimulation of theca cells, thereby increasing CYP17A1 expression and androgen synthesis, this exacerbates hyperandrogenemia [34]. BPA also disrupts the HSP90AA1-ER complex, impairing estrogen-dependent follicular development. Clinical evidence confirms that serum BPA levels in PCOS women correlate positively with AR activity and free testosterone, while ovarian HSP90AA1 expression is elevated [32].

Although HSP90AA1 typically stabilizes AKT (a key insulin signaling molecule), BPA-induced HSP90AA1 dysfunction may promote AKT degradation, inhibiting GLUT4 translocation and worsening IR [35]. Additionally, BPA-HSP90AA1 interactions activate NF-κB, increasing pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6) and further impairing insulin sensitivity. BPA also overstabilizes anti-apoptotic proteins (e.g., Bcl-2), delaying granulosa cell apoptosis and contributing to follicular persistence or anovulation [36, 37]. In BPA-exposed PCOS model rats, ovarian HSP90AA1 and AR expression synchronously rise alongside elevated testosterone and anovulation. HSP90 inhibitors (e.g., 17-AAG) partially reverse these phenotypes [38]. Consistent with our findings, GEO database analyses reveal higher HSP90AA1 expression in PCOS patients.

Insulin Receptor (INSR), a transmembrane tyrosine kinase receptor, mediates insulin signaling and regulates glucose/ lipid metabolism, protein synthesis, and cell growth. In PCOS, ovarian INSR overactivation contributes to hyperandrogenemia and anovulation. In vitro studies show that BPA reduces INSR mRNA/protein levels in adipocytes (3T3-L1) and hepatocytes (HepG2), impairing insulin sensitivity [39]. BPA-exposed mice/rats exhibit decreased hepatic/muscular INSR expression, hyperglycemia, and IR [40]. Prenatal BPA exposure in rats reduces offspring muscle GLUT4 expression, suggesting long-term metabolic programming via PI3K/AKT pathway disruption [41]. INSR and Insulin-like Growth Factor Receptor (IGF1R), both tyrosine kinase receptors, coordinately regulate metabolism and growth. While INSR dominates glucose homeostasis (via PI3K/AKT), IGF1R drives proliferation (via MAPK). In ovarian theca cells, IGF1R and INSR jointly promote androgen synthesis. BPA may disrupt this balance, exacerbating PCOS.

HSP90AA1, PPARG, INSR, and IGF1R co-enrich in pathways such as AMPK, PI3K-Akt, Rap1, and FoxO signaling. Taken together, these findings highlight a potential multi-target, network-level relevance of BPA to PCOS-associated pathways and support future mechanistic studies to validate the predicted interactions and pathway perturbations.

Limitations and future directions

This study establishes an integrative, hypothesis-generating analytical framework that combines network toxicology, public transcriptomic data analysis, and molecular docking to explore potential molecular associations between BPA exposure and PCOS. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study is primarily based on public database analyses and in silico predictions, which, although valuable for systematic exploration, cannot fully capture the biological complexity of BPA exposure in vivo. In particular, BPA may exert tissue- and context-specific effects across endocrine and metabolic organs relevant to PCOS, such as the ovary, liver, and adipose tissue, and these complex multi-organ interactions are difficult to fully recapitulate using current computational models or single experimental systems.

Second, previous studies suggest that BPA may exhibit non-monotonic dose–response relationships, and the molecular mechanisms underlying certain low-dose effects remain incompletely understood. The present study does not address dose- or time-dependent dynamics and therefore cannot define exposure thresholds or temporal causality. Third, although molecular docking results support the structural feasibility of interactions between BPA and the selected targets, these predictions require further experimental validation to establish their functional relevance.

Accordingly, future studies should prioritize targeted in vitro and in vivo validation of the predicted targets and pathways identified in this study. Integrative multi-omics approaches, including transcriptomics, epigenomics, and metabolomics, may further refine key molecular nodes within BPA-associated networks. In addition, advanced experimental platforms, such as organoids or multi-organ microphysiological systems, may better model endocrine–metabolic tissue interactions relevant to PCOS. Finally, well-designed prospective cohort studies, particularly involving adolescent and young female populations, are needed to systematically evaluate the association between long-term BPA exposure and the development and progression of PCOS. More importantly, from the perspective of health protection and disease risk awareness, our findings suggest that the use of BPA-containing plastics and other materials with potential endocrine-disrupting properties should be approached with greater caution in daily life, especially among sensitive populations and during critical developmental windows, to mitigate potential long-term health risks.

Conclusion

This study adopts a hypothesis-generating, integrative analytical approach to explore potential molecular associations between Bisphenol A (BPA) exposure and Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS). Through multi-layer analyses combining network toxicology and molecular docking, PPARG, HSP90AA1, IGF1R, and INSR emerged as recurrently implicated targets, with IGF1R exhibiting a relatively low predicted docking energy, suggesting structural compatibility in silico. Overall, these findings support the possibility that BPA may be molecularly relevant to pathways involved in hormonal regulation, insulin signaling, and ovarian function, which are key features of PCOS. Collectively, this work provides a structured framework to inform future experimental validation and to guide subsequent mechanistic and translational studies in this field.
