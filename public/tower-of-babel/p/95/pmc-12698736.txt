Cancer-associated fibroblast miR-148a-5p/CALD1/collagen VI pathway promotes proliferation in Helicobacter pylori-positive gastric cancer
Authors: Qiuyu Jiang, Hao Zhang, Yu Cai, Fansheng Meng, Huibin Wu, Wenfeng Liu, Feng Zhang, He Chen, Zhixue Chen, Xizhong Shen, Ling Dong, Si Zhang, Ruyi Xue
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeHelicobacter pylori (Hp) markedly elevates the risk of gastric cancer (GC) through the induction of chronic inflammation, which facilitates the accumulation of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) within the immune microenvironment of GC. CAFs contribute to the progression of GC and adversely affect subsequent therapeutic outcomes for patients. However, there is a paucity of research concerning the impact of Hp on CAFs or the identification of potential targets for therapeutic intervention.MethodsWe analyzed public microRNA and transcriptome sequencing data to identify key microRNAs and signaling pathways in Hp + GC. We also used single-cell sequencing to explore cellular localization and interaction mechanisms. Molecular biology experiments, in vitro cell co-culture, and in vivo cell-derived xenograft (CDX) and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models validated our findings and assessed the pathway’s impact on GC proliferation and therapeutic potential.ResultsWe identified the “TLR/miR-148a-5p/CALD1/collagen VI” signaling pathway in Hp-stimulated cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) as a critical signaling pathway influencing the proliferation of Hp + GC. These CAFs contributed to GC cell proliferation by releasing substantial amounts of collagen VI, which interacted with tumoral SDC4 receptors. Administration of miR-148a-5p agomir in vivo effectively inhibited the proliferative effects and concurrently enhanced the efficacy of chemotherapy in Hp + GC mice models.ConclusionHp-stimulated CAFs played a significant role in promoting tumor proliferation in Hp + GC. Targeting its “TLR/miR-148a-5p/CALD1/collagen VI” pathway was a promising method to ease the collagen-rich microenvironment and inhibit the proliferation of GC cells. Furthermore, miR-148a-5p agomir might serve as a safer and more efficacious chemotherapeutic sensitizer for patients with Hp + GC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13402-025-01116-y.

Background

Gastric cancer (GC) ranks as the fifth most prevalent cancer and the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1]. The incidence rates of GC are closely associated with the high prevalence of Helicobacter pylori (Hp) infection, with the infection rate in adults reaching 43% in 2020 [2, 3]. Removing Hp is effective in preventing GC, but its effectiveness as a treatment for existing Hp-positive (Hp+) GC is uncertain. Since there are still lacking specific treatments for Hp + GC, elucidating the precise mechanism by which Hp promotes the development of GC is crucial for the advancement of effective therapeutic strategies.

The immune microenvironment of GC plays a crucial role in determining both the regression and prognosis of the disease, making it a focal point for identifying potential intervention targets. Current research on the influence of Hp on the immune microenvironment of GC predominantly emphasizes the role of cytotoxic cells. Evidence indicates that Hp infection status influences T cell heterogeneity, thereby modulating the tumor immune response [4]. Furthermore, Hp infection can upregulate the expression of squalene epoxidase (SQLE) via its virulence factor CagA protein, enhancing the stability of PD-L1. This process inhibits T cell activity and facilitates immune evasion in gastric cancer [5]. However, there is a paucity of comprehensive studies of Hp on mesenchymal stromal cells in GC.

However, the stroma represents a significant component of the tumor immune microenvironment and plays a crucial role in the development, progression, and therapeutic response of GC. The predominant element of stroma is cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), which are integral to the tumor microenvironment. CAFs facilitate a supportive milieu for cancer cell survival by secreting cytokines, proteins, and exosomes [6]. These fibroblasts establish intricate signaling networks through interactions with cancer cells, thereby facilitating their proliferation and metastasis to other organs and tissues [6]. The heterogeneity of CAFs adds complexity to their role in GC. Research has demonstrated that CAFs can enhance the migration and invasion of GC cells by upregulating MUC1, a mechanism mediated via the IL-11-STAT3/ERK signaling pathway [7]. Furthermore, CAFs contribute to tumor invasion by secreting HAPLN1, which results from the remodeling of the extracellular matrix [8]. In the treatment of GC, strategies aimed at CAFs are progressing rapidly. Nevertheless, the heterogeneity of CAFs poses a significant limitation to the application of these therapeutic approaches. Consequently, current research is primarily focused on identifying specific markers and categorizing CAFs into distinct subgroups [9]. Studies have shown that Hp, as a highly invasive pathogen, can not only colonize gastric epithelial cells but also invade the stroma of gastric cancer [10]. Such direct interactions have more immediate and critical impacts on stromal cells, especially CAFs, compared to indirect interactions with cytotoxic cells. However, this area of research remains largely uninvestigated. Dissecting the mechanisms by which Hp influences CAFs will facilitate our exploration of specific CAF subtypes in Hp + GC, thereby identifying potential therapeutic targets.

In this study, we demonstrated that Hp down-regulated miRNA miR-148a-5p and up-regulated downstream CALD1 expression in gastric cancer associated fibroblasts via toll-like receptor (TLR)-dependent pathway. CALD1 + CAFs subsequently promoted tumor proliferation through collagen VI-SDC4 interaction. Furthermore, intratumoral administration of miR-148a-5p agomirs might be a promising therapeutic treatment for improving chemotherapy efficacy in Hp + GC patients.

Methods

Data sources

The transcriptomic information and miRNA data from the TCGA-STAD and GSE54397 datasets were acquired individually from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) repositories. The TCGA-STAD dataset comprised a total of 407 samples, including 375 tumor samples and 32 normal samples. The GSE54397 dataset consisted of miRNA expression data from 32 samples, including 8 paired Hp + and 8 paired Hp- normal and tumor samples.

Fourty-two (21 Hp- and 21 Hp+) newly collected tumor tissues were obtained from gastric cancer patients undergoing surgical resection and diagnosed via pathological examination at Zhongshan Hospital (Shanghai, China). The status of Helicobacter pylori infection was confirmed by both the C13 breath test results and Helicobacter pylori immunohistochemical staining. This study was approved by the Zhongshan Hospital Research Ethics Committee (B2024-252), and all patients provided written informed consent.

Cell lines and cell culture

Human gastric cancer cell lines (AGS and HGC-27), primary gastric cancer-associated fibroblasts and HEK293T cells purchased from the Procell Co. Ltd. in Wuhan, China were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Invitrogen, 11875-093) or DMEM medium (Invitrogen, 11965-092) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, 1614007) and 1% streptomycin–penicillin (Invitrogen, 35050-061). All cell lines were confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination. CAFs from Hp negative (Hp-) and Hp + gastric cancer tissues were isolated from fresh surgical gastric cancer tissues. All CAFs used for in vitro H. pylori stimulation, genetic editing, miRNA modulation and animal experiments were commercially sourced to ensure experimental reproducibility. Primary CAFs isolated in-house were employed solely in Fig. 2, E and F. Hp-stimulated CAFs、 are Hp strain PMSS1 were purchased from Biobw, Beijing, China and was added to the medium at a MOI from 10 to 100 for 6 to 24 h. Antagonists used were listed as follows: Afimetoran (TLR, MCE, CAS No. 2171019-55-7, 1 µM); NOD1/2 antagonist-1 (NOD1/2, MCE, CAS No. 2704623-69-6, 5 µM [11]). Pathway inhibitors used were listed as follows: IMD-0354 (NFκB, MCE, CAS No. 978-62-1, 1 µM); p38 MAP Kinase Inhibitor III (MAPK, MCE, CAS No. 581098-48-8, 1 µM); Taselisib (PI3K, MCE, CAS No. 1282512-48-4, 10 nM). Other materials: Human collagen VI (Abcam, ab7538, 5 ng/ml).

Co-culture system

CAFs were pre-activated with HP (MOI = 100, 24 h) or not. For transwell co-culture, fibroblasts were seeded in the 6-well plates and tumor cells were in the 0.4 μm inserts (Corning, 3450) at a ratio of 1:3 [12].

Cell-derived xenograft (CDX) animal model

Animal experiments were conducted according to the Shanghai Medical Experimental Animal Care Commission Guidelines and approved by the Ethical Committee on Animal Experiments of the Animal Care Committee of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University. NOD-SCID mice were purchased from Gempharmatech Co., Ltd, Jiangsu, China. Gastric cancer associated fibroblasts were pre-activated with/without Hp (MOI = 100, 24 h) and was then mixed with HGC-27 cells at a ratio of 1.5: 1 [13] after washed for 3 times. Cells were subcutaneously inoculated into the flanks of 6-week-old male nude mice. Tumor size was measured biweekly using calipers and calculated until sacrificed. MiR-148a-5p agomirs were administered via intratumoral injection (5 nmol/ 50 µl PBS) every three days once the tumor volume reached 4 mm × 4 mm.

Patient-derived xenograft (PDX) animal model

Surgical tissues from Hp + GC patient were preserved in iced RPMI 1640 with 10% fetal bovine serum and were cut into 2 × 2 × 3 mm³ pieces. Before further steps, the tissues were kept in RPMI 1640 with penicillin and streptomycin. Six-week-old male NOD-SCID mice were selected for carrying patient tissues. In a sterile procedure, tumor tissues were planted subcutaneously into the mice’s backs, supplementing with penicillin and streptomycin. Once each xenografted tumor reached 1–2 cm³, we harvested and transplanted the tissues into new mice four times, following the same protocols. Tumor size was measured biweekly with calipers until the mice were sacrificed. 5-fluorouracil (5-FU; MCE, CAS No. 51-21-8, 30 mg/kg) and oxaliplatin (OXA; MCE, 61825-94-3, 5 mg/kg) were injected intraperitoneally biweekly [14]. MiR-148a-5p agomirs were administered via intratumoral injection (5 nmol/ 50 µl PBS) every three days once the tumor volume reached 4 mm × 4 mm.

Other materials and methods

Details of western blot, RNA extraction and quantitative reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT‐PCR), enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, immunohistochemistry, multiplexed immunofluorescence, CCK‐8 and colony formation assays, luciferase reporter assay and statistical analyses are described in the Supplementary materials and methods.

Results

MiR-148a-5p was down-regulated in Hp + GC and affected the expression of its target gene CALD1 in CAFs

In the GEO dataset GSE54397, the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between Hp + and Hp- GC tissues were screened, and then the DEGs between Hp + GC tissues and Hp + adjacent normal tissues were screened and intersected with the former. We obtained 5 differentially expressed miRNAs (miR-148a-5p, miR-3170, miR-551b-3p, miR-148a-3p and miR-150-5p) (Fig. 1A). Only miR-148a-5p was significantly correlated with the overall survival (OS) (HR = 0.54, p = 0.003), progression free interval (PFI) (HR = 0.58, p = 0.01) and disease-free survival (DSS) (HR = 0.43, p = 0.002) of GC patients in TCGA-STAD dataset (Fig. 1B, Fig. S1). Down-regulation of miR-148a-5p expression was verified in GSE54397 and Zhongshan cohort containing 21 Hp- and 21 Hp + GC samples (Fig. 1C, Fig. S2 A, B). To study the downstream target genes affected by the miRNA, 560 potential downstream genes were predicted by miRDB database. Then, the intersection was taken with the genes related to prognosis, DEGs between GC and adjacent normal tissues, and DEGs between Hp + GC tissues and Hp- GC tissues from TCGA-STAD dataset. Two genes (CALD1 and MAGEA1) were obtained (Fig. 1D), but only CALD1 was upregulated both in GC and Hp + GC. (Fig. S2 C, D). Therefore, CALD1 was selected as the downstream gene that miR-148a-5p might target in Hp + GC. The increased expression of CALD1 mRNA in Hp + and miR-148a-5p high-expressing GC was subsequently verified by Zhongshan cohort (Fig. 1E). The TCGA-STAD dataset verified that there was a significantly negative correlation between CALD1 and miR-148a-5p (Cor = 0.511, p < 0.001) (Fig. S2E). Prognosis analyses in the TCGA-STAD dataset also suggested that CALD1 was significantly negatively correlated with OS (HR = 1.63, p = 0.007), PFI (HR = 1.49, p = 0.038), and DSS (HR = 1.77, p = 0.015) of GC patients (Fig. S2F).

Using TISCH2 database, we found that CALD1 was mainly localized on CAFs in two single-cell datasets of GC (GSE134520 and GSE167297) (Fig. 1F, Fig. S3). We also found colocalization of CALD1 and the CAF fibroblast marker FAP in Hp + GC tissues (Fig. 1G). In order to validate the relationship between miR-148a-5p and CALD1, the binding sites were predicted by Targetscan database, and the mutant plasmids were next constructed (Fig. 1H). The transcriptional inhibition of miR-148a-5p on CALD1 was verified by dual luciferase reporter assay in both HEK293T cell lines and gastric cancer-associated fibroblasts (Fig. 1I). Furthermore, CALD1 + CAFs were verified infiltrated more in Hp + and miR-148a-5p low-expressing GC samples (Fig. 1J). These results suggested that miR-148a-5p/CALD1 axis in CAFs might be important in Hp + GC.

Hp-stimulated CAFs promoted GC cells proliferation via miR-148a-5p/CALD1 axis

Since CAFs infiltrated more in Hp + GC (Fig. 2A), we further investigate their impact on GC immune microenvironment. We applied cell-cell interaction (CCI) analyses in two GC single-cell datasets. CAFs had the strongest interactions with “Pit mucous cells” in GSE134520 and “Epithelial cells” in GSE167297 (Fig. 2B, Fig. S4A). Since both cell populations highly expressed the epithelial marker Epcam (Fig. S4B, C), we hypothesized that CAFs strongly interact with gastric cancer epithelial cells. In vitro, we found by colony formation and CCK-8 assays that the proliferative capacity of HGC-27 and AGS gastric cancer cells was enhanced, both by co-culturing with Hp-stimulated CAFs and with CAFs derived from Hp + GC patients (Fig. 2C-F). We constructed a subcutaneous cell derived xenograft (CDX) nude mice model containing CAFs and HGC-27 cells. The CAFs infiltration was confirmed by immunohistochemistry (IHC) on day 14 (Fig. 2G). Hp-stimulated CAFs promoted tumor growth, resulting in increased tumor volume, weight (Fig. 2H-J) and intratumoral KI67 positive cells (Fig. 2K). Meanwhile, CALD1 + CAFs infiltrated more in Hp-stimulated tumors (Fig. 2L). Therefore, Hp-stimulated CAFs can promote the proliferation of gastric cancer cells.

In the TCGA-STAD dataset, both low miR-148a-5p expression and high CALD1 expression were correlated with an increased T stage in patients. Additionally, elevated CALD1 expression was associated with a higher pathological stage (Tables 1 and 2). These findings suggest that the miR-148a-5p/CALD1 axis in CAFs may serve as a link between Hp infection and GC proliferation. Through in vitro colony formation and CCK-8 assays, as well as an in vivo CDX nude mice model, we confirmed that down-regulating miR-148a-5p in CAFs, rather than in tumor cells, more significantly promotes GC proliferation (Fig. 3A-E). Furthermore, the overexpression of CAF CALD1 was found to promote tumor cell proliferation, whereas the upregulation of CAF miR-148a-5p and the downregulation of CALD1 inhibited tumor cell proliferation. The knockdown of CAF CALD1 could rescue the impact of the miR-148a-5p antagomir on proliferation (Fig. 3F-I). These findings suggest that the miR-148a-5p/CALD1 axis in Hp-stimulated CAFs plays a critical role in the proliferation of GC cells.

Hp impaired miR-148a-5p/CALD1 axis in CAFs through activating TLR-dependent NFκB signaling pathway

In vitro stimulation of CAFs by Hp showed a time-dependent and MOI-dependent downregulation of miR-148a-5p and an up-regulation of CALD1 mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 4A, B). Meanwhile, withdrawing Hp after 24-hour infection could gradually recue the expression of miR-148a-5p and CALD1 (Fig S5A, B), which proved that Hp could directly affect the expression of both in CAFs. Meanwhile, agomir and antagomir of miR-148a-5p could down-regulate and up-regulate the mRNA and protein expression of CALD1, respectively (Fig. 4C, D). During Hp infection, pattern recognition receptor (PRR) played an important role. Among all PRRs, Toll-like receptor (TLR) family and NOD-like receptor (NLR) family (especially NOD1 and NOD2) are most important receptors in recognizing microbiota. To further discover if Hp infection affected the expression of miR-148a-5p through PRRs, we applied Toll-like receptor (TLR) antagonist and NOD1/2 antagonist to CAFs when stimulated with Hp (MOI = 100, 24 h). We discovered TLR was responsible for down-regulation of miR-148a-5p (Fig. 4E). According to KEGG PATHWAY Database, NFκB, MAPK and PI3K-Akt signaling pathways are classical TLR downstream pathways. While, only the inhibitors of NFκB could rescue the down-regulation of miR-148a-5p mediated by TLR (Fig. 4F). Meanwhile, NFκB signaling pathway was verified activated depending on TLR activation after Hp stimulation (Fig. 4G). These results indicated that Hp could directly down-regulate miR-148a-5p and subsequent up-regulate CALD1 in CAFs through activating TLR-dependent NFκB signaling pathway.

CALD1 + CAF-secreted collagen VI promoted GC cells proliferation by interacting with tumoral SDC4

In order to investigate how CALD1 + CAFs interact with GC cells, we first accessed their CAF phenotype. We found them highly expressed myofibroblastic CAF (myCAF) markers (ACTA2, TAGLN, MYLK) and desmoplastic CAF (dCAF) markers (COL1A1 and COL3A1) instead of inflammatory-like CAF (iCAF), normal myofibroblast (nCAF) or proliferating CAF (pCAF) markers (CXCL14, CXCL12, CXCL2; APOC1, CXCR4; BIRC5, TOP2A) [15] (Fig. S6A). We also found similar results in TCGA-STAD dataset, showing positive correlation between CALD1 and myCAF and dCAF markers (Fig. S6B). We then analyzed the differential gene expression between CALD1 high and low expression samples in TCGA-STAD. KEGG and GO enrichment pathway analyses were conducted. Top enriched pathways mainly included collagen/extracellular matrix-related pathways (Fig. S7A-D), indicating their possible role in ECM remodeling. Next, correlation analyses of CALD1 and other genes in single-cell datasets GSE13450 and GSE167297 showed strongest positive correlation (Cor >0.9) between COL6A2 and COL6A1among all (Fig. 5A red box). Their positive correlation (Cor = 0.617, p < 0.05; Cor = 0.785, p < 0.05) with CALD1 (Fig. 5B) and negative correlation (Cor = -0.348, p < 0.001; Cor = -0.407, p < 0.001) with miR-148a-5p (Fig. S8A) were also verified in the TCGA-STAD dataset. The survival prognosis analysis of the TCGA-STAD dataset al.so suggested that these two genes were associated with poor prognosis of patients (HR = 1.55, p = 0.048; HR = 1.43, p = 0.044, respectively) (Fig. S8B). Furthermore, analysis of the single-cell dataset GSE249874 (3 Hp + and 3 Hp- GCs) confirmed increased CAF abundance in Hp + GC, along with elevated expression of CALD1, COL6A1, COL6A2, and COL6A3 within CAFs, supporting our findings (Fig. S9).

Given that COL6A1 and COL6A2 are genes responsible for encoding the secreted protein collagen VI, we postulated that the upregulation of CALD1 in CAFs could enhance their secretion of collagen VI. We first verified the positive correlation between CALD1 and collagen VI using Hp + GC samples (Cor = 0.5638, p = 0.0129) (Fig. 5C, D). In vitro, CAFs overexpressing CALD1 showed increased mRNA expression of COL6A1 and COL6A2 and increased collagen VI at protein level. The secretion of collagen VI was also increased in supernatant. However, in CAFs treated with miR-148a-5p antagomir, the increased of COL6A1 and COL6A2 mRNA or collagen VI protein were rescued after CALD1 knockdown (Fig. 5E-G). Meanwhile, collagen VI accumulated more in tumors from Hp-stimulated group (Fig. 5H). The aforementioned findings collectively suggest that Hp can activate the TLR-NFκB signaling pathway, leading to the down-regulation of miR-148a-5p and the subsequent up-regulation of its target gene CALD1 in CAFs. This molecular cascade ultimately results in the increased secretion of collagen VI.

Details of CCI analyses in GSE134520 and GSE167297 demonstrated stronger “COL6A2-SDC4” and “COL6A1-SDC4” interaction among various COL6A1/2-receptors interaction (Fig. S10). Through in vitro rescue experiments, we determined that collagen VI facilitates the proliferation of gastric cancer cells dependent on SDC4 (Fig. 5I, J). Furthermore, the enhancement of proliferation by Hp-stimulated CAFs was contingent upon the release of collagen VI from the CAFs and tumoral SDC4 (Fig. 5K, L). Therefore, collagen VI released by Hp-stimulated CALD1 + CAFs promoted proliferation via tumoral SDC4.

Intratumoral administration of miR-148a-5p agomir enhanced the efficacy of chemotherapy in Hp + GC

To inhibit the effect of the Hp-mediated miR-148a-5p/CALD1/collagen VI pathway and thereby impede the proliferation of gastric cancer, we supplemented the deficient miR-148a-5p through intratumoral injection of agomir in CDX model containing Hp-stimulated CAFs. Our findings indicated that agomir injection effectively suppressed tumor proliferation, as evidenced by a reduction in tumor volume and weight as well as a decreased number of intratumoral KI67-positive cells (Fig. 6A-D). Simultaneously, there was a reduction in both collagen VI content and the number of CALD1 + CAFs within the tumor (Fig. 6D, E), suggesting a partial inhibition of the miR-148a-5p/CALD1/collagen VI pathway.

Platinum and fluoropyrimidine doublet are the first line chemotherapy for advanced GC with limiting efficacy [1]. Research has demonstrated that patients with Hp + GC exhibit improved responsiveness to chemotherapy. Our findings indicate that the application of miR-148a-5p agomir enhanced the efficacy of oxaliplatin (OXA) and 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) in both the Hp + CDX model and the Hp + GC PDX model, manifested by decreased tumor growth and an increased proportion of Tunel+/KI67 + cells (Fig. 6F-I).

In summary, our findings indicated that the miR-148a-5p/CALD1/collagen VI pathway, mediated by Hp-activated TLR-NFκB signaling pathway in CAFs, facilitated proliferation via tumoral SDC4 receptors. Furthermore, intratumoral injection of agomir restored miR-148a-5p levels, thereby offering potential therapeutic benefits in enhancing chemotherapy efficacy for Hp + GC patients (Fig. 7).

Discussion

Hp significantly contributes to gastric carcinogenesis by causing chronic inflammation, creating a distinct tumor microenvironment. While Hp has been extensively studied in the context of cytotoxic cells, its impact on CAFs remains poorly characterized. This study reveals novel mechanisms by which Hp modulates gastric CAFs: (1) Hp suppresses miR-148a-5p expression in CAFs through the TLR/NFκB signaling pathway. (2) Hp upregulates CALD1 expression in CAFs. (3) Hp induces the differentiation of CAFs into myCAF and dCAF subtypes. (4) Hp promotes CAF collagen VI secretion, which subsequently enhances tumor proliferation. These findings collectively demonstrate that Hp remodels the tumor-promoting stromal microenvironment through CAF reprogramming, providing new insights into Hp-driven gastric carcinogenesis. The TLR/NF-κB/miR-148a-5p/CALD1 pathway and collagen VI-mediated matrix remodeling represent potential therapeutic targets for Hp + GC.

Chemotherapy is a common treatment for GC, but the individual response rate varies greatly. For Hp + GC, we improve the curative effect by supplementing miR-148a-5p. Emerging evidence highlights the potential antitumor activity of miRNA-based therapies in advanced malignancies. In a Phase I clinical trial evaluating miR-34a mimic therapy, 1 out of 14 patients with advanced hepatocellular carcinoma achieved a partial response (PR), while 4 exhibited progressive disease (PD). Concurrently, another Phase I clinical trial investigating miR-221 inhibitor therapy reported stable disease (SD) in 8 of 16 patients with progressive cancers and 1 colon cancer patient who demonstrated a PR. Moreover, miRNAs may also enhance the sensitivity to chemotherapy by modulating signaling pathways associated with drug resistance. For instance, miR-34a [16], miR-505 [17], miR-362 [18], miR-20a [19], miR-198 [20] and miR106a [21] could inhibit cisplatin resistance-associated signaling pathways MAPK and PI3K/AKT。Our research first identified miR-148a-5p as a significant factor in tumor inhibition and sensitizer of chemotherapy. The simplicity of administration and favorable safety profile of its agomirs underscore its potential as a promising intervention target in Hp + GC.

TLRs play important roles during Hp infection [22–24]. We found that inhibition of both TLR and downstream NFκB could inhibit the decrease of CAF miR-148a-5p caused by Hp, which indicated that the decrease of miR-148a-5p was dependent on TLR-NFκB signal. Supporting our results, activation of various TLRs subsequent influences miRNA expression through downstream signaling, especially NFκB. For example, miR-146 increases in macrophages, monocytes, and T cells upon TLR2, TLR3 and TLR5 activation via NFκB [25]. Similarly, miR-155 is induced in mouse macrophages by TLR2, TLR3, TLR4 and TLR9 activations through NFκB and AP-1 [26, 27]. Additionally, miR-9 and miR-21 are upregulated in various cells following TLR-NFκB signaling [28–32]. Profoundly, NFκB can downregulate microRNAs via several distinct mechanisms. Directly, NFκB subunits like p50 can bind specific miRNA promoters (e.g., suppressing miR-532-3p in sarcopenia [33]). Indirectly, NFκB can modulate the expression of inflammatory cytokines such as IL1β, IL6 and IL12β [34], thus altering the environment for miRNA stability, processing, or target interaction. Therefore, intervention of the TLR-NFκB signalling pathway in CAF by TLR antagonists or NFκB inhibitors may become a potential way to intervene in Hp + GC.

Collagen is a vital component of the extracellular matrix, comprising approximately 30% of its total composition [35]. Our research indicates that collagen VI may function as a crucial effector molecule in the context of Hp-induced CAFs, which facilitate tumor progression. This conclusion is substantiated by two experimental validations: first, the genetic ablation of COL6A1 reduced the tumor-promoting effects of Hp-induced CAFs in rescue experiments; second, the exogenous addition of collagen VI demonstrated pro-tumorigenic effects by enhancing cancer cell proliferation. These findings are consistent with previous studies showing collagen VI suppresses apoptosis while promoting proliferation and metastasis in GC cells [36]. Moreover, growing evidence suggests that various collagen subtypes predominantly exhibit tumor-promoting roles in GC, with collagen I [37–40], V [41–43], X [44], and XI [45] acting as promoters, while collagen IV [46–48] serves as a suppressor. Notably, excessive collagen deposition within the tumor stroma forms physical barriers that hinder the delivery and penetration of therapeutic agents, thereby diminishing treatment efficacy and exacerbating clinical outcomes in patients with GC [49]. This highlights the critical need for therapeutic strategies aimed at modulating collagen in the management of GC. Our experimental data intriguingly demonstrate that the administration of miR-148a-5p significantly reduces intratumoral collagen VI levels in murine models. Consequently, we propose that the depletion of collagen VI may constitute a common mechanistic pathway through which targeting of both TLR-NFκB signaling and miR-148a-5p exert their therapeutic effects.

Conclusion

In general, Hp-stimulated CALD1 + CAFs are potent mediators in facilitating collagen accumulation and tumor proliferation. Targeting “TLR/miR-148a-5p/CALD1/collagen VI” pathway, especially upstream regulator miR-148a-5p, could potentially yield therapeutic benefits for patients with Hp + GC.
