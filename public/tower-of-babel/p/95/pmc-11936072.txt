Pediatric Erythema Leprosum Necroticans Presenting as Emergency
Authors: GHAZAL AHMED, NAMRATA CHHABRA, ANJU GEORGE CHIRAYATH, RITUL CHOUDHARY
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Lepromatous leprosy is considered rare in children owing to the immature immune system. Lepra reaction, although not uncommon, type-2 lepra reaction in childhood is scarcely reported. We report five such cases, all presented in our institutes' emergency department with severe type-2 lepra reaction as the presenting entity. They had multiple classical features of leprosy, like ulcero-necrotic lesions, deformity, neuritis, and high slit smear bacillary index, which are otherwise usual in adult leprosy and not seen in childhood leprosy. Childhood leprosy being an indicator of active transmission in the community, these cases are crucial from the rarity viewpoint but also a pointer of recrudescence in the post-elimination era, highlighting the need for more aggressive case detection and better active surveillance.

Introduction

Leprosy in children is extremely important from the clinicians' and public health perspectives.

It is an indicator of active community transmission.

Even in the post-elimination era, a few Indian states report many leprosy cases and childhood leprosy has been a growing concern [1, 2].

Childhood leprosy usually lies towards the paucibacillary end of the spectrum; multibacillary leprosy is scarce [2, 3].

Type-2 lepra reaction in the form of erythema leprosum necroticans is rarely seen [4].

These children land up in the emergency department (ED) due to systemic affection and painful presentation.

Clinical presentation in ED frequently hints at other skin and systemic illnesses, often not responding to antibiotics and the usual protocol of ED management.

However, associated skin findings help to decipher the diagnosis.

We intend to highlight the rarity of presentation, ongoing active transmission about one and a half decades after Indian national level elimination, familial contact transmission, and the possible need for more aggressive case detection, better active surveillance, and follow-up of cases and families treated for leprosy previously.

This report will also help ED physicians and pediatricians suspect lepra reactions in appropriate clinical scenarios.

Informed consents were obtained from the parents for all cases to publish the images and clinical information.

Case 1

A 10-year-old boy presented to the emergency department (ED) with multiple red, raised, painful lesions over the face and limbs with a fever (38.9° C) and joint pain.

A few lesions over the limbs had ulcerated.

There was no similar episode in the past or significant family history.

Examination revealed multiple discrete and tender enlarged neck lymph nodes, lips, and bilateral pinna were erythematous and swollen.

There were multiple ulcerated nodules over bilateral upper and lower extremities and a few bullous eruptions.

He had left-hand partial clawing deformity and right index finger dactylitis.

He had patchy anesthesia over both legs and hands in glove and stocking distribution with thickened and tender bilateral ulnar, common peroneal, and posterior tibial nerves.

Systemic examinations were otherwise within standard limits.

Case 2

A 14-year-old boy presented to ED with recurrent multiple red, raised, painful lesions over the face and limbs for four months with a history of fever and joint pain with each crop of lesions for a week.

A few lesions over limbs had ruptured to discharge pus.

The child had herpes zoster of T4 left dermatome two months back.

The patient was febrile (38.4° C); discrete, large, tender cervical, axillary, and inguinal lymph nodes and bilateral hands and feet edema were noted.

On cutaneous examination, multiple erythematous tender nodules were found over the face, and plaques with central ulceration and necrotic base were found over the extensor aspect of the upper and lower limbs.

Bilateral ulnar, lateral popliteal, and posterior tibial nerves were symmetrically thickened and tender.

There was a specific glove and stocking type of patchy sensory loss with right foot drop.

Systemic examinations did not reveal any abnormality.

Case 3

An 11-year-old girl presented to ED with recurrent multiple red, raised, painful lesions over the face and limbs for the last six months associated with high-grade fever and joint pain.

Lesions subside with treatment but reappear.

She suffered from borderline-tuberculoid leprosy and completed six months of pediatric multi-bacillary multi-drug therapy two years ago.

On presentation, she was febrile with edematous hands and feet and had cushingoid facies.

Multiple erythematous tender blanchable nodular lesions are present over the face and extremities-a few of the nodules were found to be ulcerated.

There were nodular lesions over the helices of the ears.

Bilateral ulnar, lateral popliteal, and posterior tibial nerves were symmetrically thickened but non-tender.

Sensations and sensory systems were intact.

Motor examination revealed the weakness of the last interossei of both hands but no visible deformity.

Systemic examination did not reveal any abnormality.

Case 4

A 7-year-old boy presented to the pediatric outpatient department with a fever and painful skin eruptions, for which he was started on antibiotics without any relief.

On examination, he had erythematous, tender nodules.

Few of them were bullous, and few were ulcerated.

The patient was febrile and had a toxic look.

He had patchy anesthesia over his right leg and left forearm.

Thickened ulnar and common peroneal (right>left for both) were present.

His father died due to an unknown cause and had clawing of fingers and similar episodes of skin eruptions like the son's, indicating that the father was the probable source of infection.

No other abnormalities were noted.

Case 5

A 17-year-old boy presented to Dermatology for a recurrent painful skin eruption associated with an on-and-off fever for nearly two years; the current episode lasted one week.

On examination, he was febrile and had multiple erythematous, tender skin nodules predominantly over the upper limbs.

Few of these nodules had ulcerated with an overlying crust.

There were a few thin scars over the upper limb.

He had clawing of the left little finger and weakness of the right, patchy anesthesia over gloves, and stocking distribution.

Ulnar nerves were thickened (left>right).

The World Health Organization's multi-bacillary multi-drug therapy (MDT) adjusted for their respective age and weights was started.

Systemic steroids in tapering dosage to control the lepra reaction were also administered.

Supportive management, such as analgesics and antipyretics, antibiotic coverage for ulcerated and infected cases, and family counseling were also provided.

Entire patients recovered well from acute illness within 2-5 days.

Discussion

This case series highlights the need to keep lepra reaction as a differential in pediatric ED in leprosy-endemic countries.

Due to the acute episodes' systemic affection and the erythema nodosum leprosum’s (ENL) painful nature, these children usually turn up in the ED.

They are usually started on empirical antibiotics with no improvement.

Early attention to the dermatological findings would quickly help diagnose the case, leading to starting the systemic steroids at the earliest, causing early remission from the lepra reaction and MDT to treat leprosy.

Analysis of pediatric leprosy cases in the post-elimination era failed to find any case of pediatric lepromatous leprosy (LL) and only one type-2 lepra reaction in the case of borderline lepromatous leprosy (BLL) [1, 2].

Even in the pre-elimination era, pediatric LL was not expected. Studies found only one case of ENL out of 138 pediatric leprosy cases over ten years (2001-2014) and one case out of 60 pediatric leprosy cases over 18 years (1997-2014) [5, 6].

Ghunawat et al. found that only 1.7% of total cases had type-2 reactions and developed it after starting MDT [6].

In our case series, out of the five cases, four (80%) were LL, and one (20%) was BLL under 15 years of age that presented for ENL necroticans over the last five years (2019-2023).

Interestingly, all children developed type-2 reactions before starting the MDT except one, in whom ENL occurred after completing two years of treatment.

Lepra reactions are rare in children as they lack a mature immune system.

It usually occurs in type-1 lepra reaction, as most pediatric cases are towards the tuberculoid pole of the leprosy spectrum [7].

Necroticans type of ENL in type-2 lepra reaction has not yet been reported.

In a study on severe ENL, none were less than 18 years of age [8].

All of our cases were under 18 years of age and presented with severe type-2 reactions manifesting all kinds of ENL lesions, including nodules, bullous, ulcerative, and necrotic.

An international multicentric study on ENL recorded the youngest patient, who belonged to India, to be 12 years old [9].

The youngest among our cases was a 7-year-old boy.

The other rare features were ulcero-necrotic lesions, deformity, and neuritis.

Although all these features are known in leprosy and ENL, they are almost always found in adults [1, 6, 8, 10].

Although Aggarwal et al. reported two cases of ENL in children, none were as severe as ours [11].

Similarly, SSS positivity is low in children [6].

One out of 18 children had LL in the pre-elimination era and was found to be SSS positive with a bacillary index (BI) of 3.5, and none had LL in the post-elimination period [1].

Four children had a BI of 6+, while one had 5+and a morphological index varying from 10% to 40%.

The present cases are, however, more than just a rarity, as childhood leprosy indicates active disease transmission in the population.

Although India achieved elimination back in 2005, a recent study indicated that there is an increase in the proportion of childhood leprosy (<15 years) as compared to the pre-elimination era from 7.9% to 11.2% [1].

Few other studies showed a decreasing trend in childhood leprosy [2, 6].
