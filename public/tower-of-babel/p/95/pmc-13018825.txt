Amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA) in Alzheimer’s immunotherapy: a framework and challenges for global surveillance strategies
Authors: Na Hu, Xiyue Yang, Feng Feng, Bing Zhang, Yaou Liu, Hui Gao, Qin Chen, Cun-Jin Zhang, Xiaochun Chen, Fuhua Yan, Sven Haller, Su Lui
Published: 2026

Background

Amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA) represent a spectrum of MRI-detectable complications linked to anti-amyloid monoclonal antibody therapies for Alzheimer’s disease (AD).1 2 The pathophysiology arises from the interplay between amyloid-β (Aβ) clearance and vascular inflammation (figure 1).1 These antibodies mediate parenchymal amyloid clearance with coincident destabilisation of cerebrovascular integrity, particularly in patients with pre-existing cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA). This process mobilises Aβ fragments that overwhelm perivascular drainage, triggering an inflammatory cascade and blood-brain barrier (BBB) disruption. The resultant manifestations are vasogenic oedema/sulcal effusion (ARIA-E) and microhaemorrhages/superficial siderosis (ARIA-H). Their frequent co-occurrence underscores a shared mechanism of Aβ-mediated vascular injury.3

Proposed pathophysiology of amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA).1 Anti-amyloid immunotherapy promotes clearance of parenchymal amyloid-β (Aβ), increasing levels of soluble Aβ (sAβ). The accumulation of sAβ in perivascular spaces impairs vascular clearance, triggering perivascular inflammation and blood-brain barrier disruption. This cascade leads to vasogenic oedema and/or sulcal effusion (ARIA-E) through increased vascular permeability and microhaemorrhages and/or superficial siderosis (ARIA-H) due to vascular fragility. APOE, apolipoprotein E; CAA, cerebral amyloid angiopathy.

The risk of ARIA is influenced by genetic, therapeutic and clinical factors. The apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 allele, particularly homozygosity, remains the strongest genetic predictor due to its association with vascular amyloid burden and BBB impairment.4 5 A clear gene-dose effect was observed in pivotal trials, with ARIA-E incidence rising from 5.4%–14.8% in non-carriers to 10.9%–23.8% in heterozygotes and 32.6%–41.1% in homozygotes and ARIA-H from 11.9%–18.9% to 14%–30.8% and 39%–54.2%.6 This pronounced risk gradient makes pretreatment APOE genotyping a cornerstone of individualised risk assessment, directly informing shared decision-making, monitoring intensity and reimbursement policies. Therapeutic variables (eg, antibody target epitope and dose) and clinical factors (eg, advanced age, pre-existing microhaemorrhages, CAA and anticoagulant use) also substantially influence ARIA incidence.1 2 7 Emerging evidence further identifies systemic inflammation and hypertension as modifiable risk elements, underscoring the need for dynamic and personalised risk stratification.7 The multiplicity of these influences makes comprehensive risk assessment a clinical necessity.

Managing ARIA requires a global imaging surveillance strategy, but implementing it faces pronounced obstacles. Current strategies, derived from clinical trials and real-world evidence, emphasise risk stratification, monitoring protocols and adaptive dosing.4 5 8 9 Central to this paradigm are two pillars: baseline MRI to establish eligibility and APOE-genotype-guided serial MRI follow-up. Management is dictated by ARIA severity: mild, asymptomatic cases may continue with enhanced monitoring, while moderate-to-severe radiographic or any symptomatic cases typically necessitate dose suspension or permanent discontinuation.5 10 Symptomatic ARIA requires rapid imaging evaluation to exclude mimics, with corticosteroids considered in severe presentations. Although ARIA risk declines with prolonged therapy and most cases are transient, the potential for recurrence demands sustained vigilance.11 Thus, proactive imaging management is indispensable for the safe deployment of anti-amyloid immunotherapies.

ARIA presents distinct MRI features essential for its detection and monitoring (figure 2). ARIA-E manifests as cortical, subcortical and/or sulcal hyperintensities on T2-weighted fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences, indicative of vasogenic oedema or sulcal effusion.2 Crucially, the diagnosis requires the exclusion of alternative causes of cerebral oedema, such as acute infarcts, infections or other inflammatory conditions, as well as subarachnoid haemorrhage. Conversely, ARIA-H is identified by hypointense foci on T2* gradient recalled echo (GRE) or the more sensitive susceptibility weighted imaging (SWI), corresponding to microhaemorrhages or superficial siderosis.2 The differential diagnosis for ARIA-H includes other causes of cerebral microhemorrhages, notably CAA and hypertensive microangiopathy. The accurate recognition of these imaging hallmarks is, therefore, foundational to all subsequent management decisions.

Current landscape of national ARIA imaging guidelines

Guidelines for monitoring anti-amyloid therapies exhibit both shared principles and regional adaptations. In the USA, regulatory bodies mandate a baseline MRI prior to therapy initiation, followed by frequent follow-up scans within the first year.4 5 Professional societies further recommend standardised 3T MRI protocols and centralised radiologist training to improve diagnostic consistency.12 Reimbursement policies also support this intensive imaging schedule, reinforcing its integration into clinical practice.13 In the UK, a distinct paradigm has been pioneered, implementing APOE genotype-guided eligibility criteria to optimise patient safety and resource allocation.14 15 This approach has been aligned with the subsequent European Medicines Agency regulatory decision.16

Similarly, practical recommendations are emerging globally. In North America, the Canadian Consortium for Neurodegeneration in Aging (CCNA) Imaging Workgroup has provided structured imaging recommendations.17 In Europe, the French Federation of Memory Clinics has issued detailed appropriate use recommendations.18 In Asia, Korea has published guidelines for the use of lecanemab,8 19 while Japanese societies have collaboratively established practical radiological guidelines for anti-amyloid treatment monitoring.20

Collectively, these initiatives reflect a shared foundation in risk mitigation, yet reveal divergent implementation pathways shaped by regional healthcare infrastructures, resource availability and regulatory perspectives.

Addressing healthcare disparities: the Chinese model for ARIA surveillance

China, having approved lecanemab as the third country globally, faces the formidable obstacle of delivering anti-amyloid immunotherapy within a context of vast need and uneven resources. With an estimated 40 million individuals living with mild cognitive impairment and 10 million with AD among those aged 60 years or above, the demand is immense.21 This is compounded by notable healthcare disparities, including a concentration of advanced MRI systems in urban centres and a nationwide shortage of specialists trained in ARIA recognition.

To ensure equitable and safe delivery of therapy, an innovative and scalable ‘Hub-and-Spoke’ telemedicine framework has been developed within China’s hierarchical medical system. Under this model, tertiary academic hubs (eg, West China Hospital) perform baseline and monitoring scans on-site to assure quality, while delegating routine infusions to local spoke hospitals or infusion centres. These hubs also provide remote specialist consultations to spoke sites and maintain 24/7 emergency MRI access for suspected ARIA cases. This system is reinforced by standardised ‘ARIA Alert’ clinical protocols, which trigger immediate neurological consultation and expedited MRI for any symptomatic patient.

By strategically integrating centralised expertise with decentralised care delivery, this approach presents a feasible strategy for implementing complex anti-amyloid therapies across heterogeneous healthcare settings. It may serve as an instructive model for other developing nations embarking on similar treatment pathways.

Imaging management of ARIA: integrating APOE genotyping and standardised protocols

The imaging management of ARIA has evolved into an individualised paradigm, strategically guided by genetic risk assessment and executed through standardised imaging protocols. This integrated approach guarantees that the considerable benefits of anti-amyloid therapies are balanced against the risk of ARIA in a patient-specific manner.

The central role of APOE genotyping

Central to this paradigm is APOE genotyping, which has transitioned from a simple risk marker to a foundation of clinical decision-making. Initially recommended for risk discussions by the US Appropriate Use Recommendations (AUR),4 5 10 it is now mandated by regulatory agencies14 15 and expert panels18 19 to guide pretreatment shared decision-making. This shift is strongly supported by real-world evidence, underscoring the heightened susceptibility of APOE ε4 carriers.22

Genotyping results directly dictate clinical management. For instance, lecanemab is contraindicated in APOE ε4 homozygotes in Europe due to their unfavourable risk-benefit profile.14 16 18 For eligible patients, a paradigm shift towards genotype-guided dosing is emerging, with a slow titration protocol shown to dramatically reduce ARIA incidence.23 Furthermore, monitoring intensity is stratified by genotype, with guidelines recommending intensified MRI schedules for APOE ε4 carriers versus potential de-escalation for non-carriers.4 5 10 18 This stratification represents a fundamental advance, moving the central clinical question from whether a patient is eligible for therapy to how the entire management pathway should be tailored based on genetic risk.

Standardised MRI protocols for consistent monitoring

To effectively implement this risk-adapted protocol, consistent and high-quality MRI is indispensable. Several expert panels from North America, Europe and Asia have consequently put forth harmonised imaging recommendations.45 8 12 17,20 24 To facilitate comparability over time, it is ideal to use the same scanner vendor, field strength and scanner for each examination. However, harmonising MRI acquisition parameters across different scanner vendors and magnetic field strengths presents a critical yet unresolved issue for protocol standardisation. Disparities in image contrast, resolution and sensitivity between systems from manufacturers such as GE, Siemens and Philips can lead to inconsistencies in ARIA detection and quantification. A minimum field strength of 1.5T is required, with 3T preferred for its superior sensitivity in detecting microhaemorrhages.4 5 8 12 17 24 Acknowledging practical constraints, the American Society of Neuroradiology (ASNR) has collaborated with manufacturers to develop standardised, harmonised ARIA protocols accessible online (https://www.asnr.org/education-resources/alzheimers-webinar-series), serving as a valuable global reference.

Standardised MRI sequences form the foundation of ARIA monitoring. National guidelines share a consensus on core MRI sequences, typically including T2* GRE, T2-FLAIR and diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI).12 However, recommendations for SWI vary, reflecting an evolving consensus. While some guidelines recognise it as an equivalent alternative to T2* GRE,4 5 8 19 others advise its use as a complementary adjunct.17 Finally, a few have moved to explicitly endorse SWI as a suitable substitute.20 This divergence in guidance reflects the dual nature of SWI: a tool of superior sensitivity that simultaneously introduces complex clinical trade-offs. The performance of both T2* GRE and SWI for microhaemorrhage detection is influenced by specific hardware and software implementations, further complicating cross-platform consistency.

SWI’s key advantage lies in its superior sensitivity for microhaemorrhage detection over T2* GRE, driving its adoption by several Asian radiologist panels.20 24 However, this enhanced sensitivity introduces two major clinical concerns: selection bias during patient screening18 and potentially unnecessary treatment interruptions, with simulation studies indicating a >50% increase in discontinuation rates when replacing T2* GRE with SWI.25 To mitigate these risks, we recommend consistent postbaseline SWI use and propose a dual-pathway strategy involving concurrent acquisition of both sequences. This maintains trial protocol compatibility while accumulating real-world evidence for SWI-specific diagnostic thresholds. Accordingly, we propose prospective, multicentre studies with industry partnerships, standardising key parameters (eg, field strength, slice thickness, GRE echo time and SWI reconstruction) to establish evidence-based thresholds for harmonising ARIA-H grading across sequences.

While this foundational work is underway, a practical framework for the interim is required. Pending the establishment of these validated thresholds, evaluation by a multidisciplinary team remains crucial when interpreting ARIA-H burden, especially during sequence transitions. Emerging technologies such as accelerated MRI protocols26 and artificial intelligence (AI)-powered microhaemorrhage quantification tools27 are expected to facilitate this integration. Ultimately, the development of these cross-platform diagnostic criteria will be vital for harmonising ARIA-H grading between SWI and T2* GRE, ensuring that sequence transitions do not adversely influence clinical management while preserving patient access to treatment.

Protocol implementation across clinical scenarios

To translate standardised protocols into clinical practice, the ASNR recommends individualised MRI examinations to three distinct patient scenarios, each with proposed order codes to enhance operability:12

Technical consistency is critical for detecting subtle changes. Key parameters include a slice thickness ≤5 mm, consistent imaging planes and a T2* GRE echo time ≥20 ms (adjusted for field strength). The use of 3D T2-FLAIR is recommended over 2D to achieve superior visualisation with fewer artefacts, and a total acquisition time is ideally kept within 30 min.12 17

Notably, a marked protocol-practice gap persists in emergency settings, where CT is first-line despite its poor sensitivity for ARIA. Recent guidelines thus stress that expedited MRI is fundamental when clinical suspicion remains following a negative CT.8 10 18 To bridge this gap, leading Chinese tertiary centres have implemented a structured ‘ARIA Alert’ clinical pathway. Under this framework, patients and caregivers receive comprehensive education at treatment initiation to recognise neuroemergencies and are directed to seek immediate care at designated centres. On arrival, the protocol is activated: an emergency CT is performed to rule out macrohaemorrhage or mass effect. Any haemorrhage identified warrants immediate immunotherapy suspension and neurology consultation. Crucially, if CT is non-diagnostic yet clinical concern for ARIA persists, an urgent MRI is mandated. This integrated emergency pathway not only reinforces ARIA as a critical differential in acute neurology but also provides a transferable model for safeguarding patients in real-world practice.

Image analysis and reporting of ARIA

Accurate classification of ARIA severity through MRI is essential for clinical decision-making. The current standard relies on qualitative visual assessment using the ARIA radiographic severity grading system (table 1),2 which is incorporated into US prescribing labels for lecanemab and donanemab and recommended by the Radiological Society of North America (RSNA) expert consensus.12 This system quantifies ARIA-E by the count and size of new brain oedematous lesions and/or sulcal effusions and ARIA-H by the number of new microhaemorrhages and/or superficial siderosis. Nevertheless, longitudinal comparison of lesions is time-consuming and error-prone. The inherent subjectivity of visual assessment is underscored by high reported inter-reader variability and substantial initial miss rates for ARIA-E, reaching 42% in phase II studies28 and 31% in phase III trials.29 Lesion quantification reproducibility is further hindered by variations in MRI sequence parameters, image quality and reader expertise, especially under high clinical workloads. These limitations collectively highlight an urgent need for more objective, efficient and reproducible analytical tools.

Computer-assisted diagnostic tools are emerging to address these challenges. Subtraction techniques applied to FLAIR images help to enhance ARIA-E detection,30 while AI algorithms improve microhaemorrhages31 and oedema32 identifications. Recently, dedicated AI-based software tools have been developed for integrated ARIA detection and quantitation. Notably, one such tool demonstrates considerably improved performance: sensitivity for ARIA-E rose to 87% with AI assistance (vs 71% without) and for ARIA-H to 79% (vs 69%), while specificity remained above 80% for both.33 While such tools show clear potential for improving diagnostic sensitivity and consistency, their generalisability across imaging centres and reader populations still requires further multicentre validation. The development and standardisation of objective ARIA assessment tools are crucial for mitigating diagnostic variability. Future AI models may further enable precise progression monitoring and prognosis assessment, pending validation and integration within robust regulatory frameworks. Moreover, AI-driven imaging techniques may increase patient compliance by reducing acquisition time.

To complement these technological advances and further standardise interpretation, structured reporting templates are strongly recommended. Education and dissemination

As ARIA represents a relatively new entity in real-world clinical practice, comprehensive education and knowledge dissemination would contribute to timely, accurate and consistent imaging services. Effective coordination among multidisciplinary healthcare providers, patients and radiologists is critical for optimising patient care and safety.12 17 24 To address this need, an integrated framework has been proposed to standardise imaging diagnosis, reporting and communication.17 Centralised patient registry programmes, such as the US Alzheimer’s Network for Treatment and Diagnostics (ALZ-NET; www.alz-net.org) and the UK Yellow Card system (https://yellowcard.mhra.gov.uk), enhance patient identification through systematic collection and sharing of real-world clinical data. These platforms also serve as educational hubs, empowering patients and caregivers with evidence-based insights and practical caregiving strategies on AD therapeutics.

Academic associations are actively promoting ARIA education for radiologists and technicians through authoritative white papers.2 4 5 8 12 17 Notably, educational portals have been initiated or sustained by organizations such as the ASNR (https://www.asnr.org/education-resources/alzheimers-webinar-series) and the European Society of Neuroradiology (https://ariaeducation.eu), offering continuous resources such as real-world cases, recommended literature, webinars, industry updates, and standardized MRI protocols. Beyond this, the CCNA advocates specialised radiologist training through accredited continuing medical education programmes.17 To support longitudinal tracking of MRI findings, patients should be encouraged to retain personal copies of all baseline and follow-up scans for each monitoring visit, in addition to participating in data-sharing platforms.12 This practice not only enhances patient engagement and satisfaction but also boosts healthcare system efficiency, particularly for patients transitioning between imaging centres during long-term treatment.

Global harmonisation and disparities in ARIA imaging management guidelines

While foundational principles of ARIA management, such as the importance of baseline MRI, APOE-guided risk stratification and serial monitoring, are universally acknowledged, their translation into national imaging guidelines reveals substantial variation. These differences reflect divergent regional priorities, resource constraints and risk-benefit assessments, presenting both an obstacle and an opportunity for global harmonisation.

Recent 2025 national guidelines exemplify this spectrum of approaches. The Japanese guidelines establish a meticulous framework emphasising facility readiness and radiologist certification, ensuring systemic quality control.20 The Korean guidance delves deeply into multidisciplinary workflow integration and the ethical nuances of patient selection and management.19 Moreover, the French recommendations adopt a more conservative posture than the US AUR, particularly regarding the strictness of MRI-based eligibility criteria and the frequency of monitoring scans.18 Interestingly, these disparities are further compounded by population-specific observations. Subgroup analyses of clinical trials and real-world observations indicate that some Asian cohorts may exhibit a distinct, often milder, ARIA profile.34 This suggests that racial and ethnic factors, alongside healthcare infrastructures, contribute to regional discrepancies in risk stratification and monitoring intensity.

Consequently, the current global landscape is characterised by national adaptations and an ongoing technological transition. Achieving true harmonisation will require future guideline iterations to not only reconcile differing risk tolerances and healthcare systems but also to incorporate standardised recommendations for advanced sequences (eg, SWI), accelerated protocols and AI tools. The ultimate goal is to forge a cohesive, globally applicable framework that ensures both patient safety and practical feasibility across all clinical settings.

Future directions

The evolving management of ARIA presents several prioritised research and clinical pathways that directly address the challenges outlined in preceding sections. Future efforts should focus on translating current evidence into refined, equitable and precise surveillance strategies.

A primary direction involves the validation and standardisation of emerging biomarkers. While APOE genotyping is established, quantitative plasma APOE ε4 assays offer a rapid, accessible alternative.35 The integration of such genomic insights with neuroimaging markers (eg, microhemorrhages and superficial siderosis),36 37 cerebrospinal fluid proteomics (eg, CHIT1 and DDAH1),38 and plasma biomarkers (eg, phosphorylated tau181/217 and Aβ42/40)39 40 will be crucial for constructing dynamic, multimodal risk profiles. This integrated approach is essential for personalising monitoring schedules and pre-emptively identifying ARIA-susceptible individuals.

Resolving key methodological and technical uncertainties is also important for reliable ARIA surveillance. First, inter-reader variability and lesion quantification reproducibility must be improved through the development and multicentre validation of AI-based automated detection tools. However, the transition of these promising AI tools into clinical practice requires rigorous evaluation beyond initial performance metrics. Key steps include robust validation across diverse patient populations and imaging protocols to ensure generalisability, transparent reporting of limitations (eg, susceptibility to training data bias and image quality variations) and navigation of the regulatory pathways to establish their clinical utility and safety for informed decision-making. Second, prospective studies directly comparing SWI with T2* GRE are needed to establish evidence-based diagnostic cut-offs and resolve the current clinical paradox. Third, harmonising MRI acquisition parameters across vendors and field strengths will ensure consistent image interpretation globally, a goal that requires active international collaboration.

Beyond these methodological priorities, several pathophysiological and technical questions warrant focused investigation. The synergistic combination of amyloid positron emission tomography with advanced MRI techniques, such as dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI and emerging methods like vascular-water-exchange MRI, may offer valuable insights into BBB integrity disrupted by amyloid-mediated immunoreaction, providing a direct window into ARIA pathophysiology. Techniques including arterial spin labelling and CO2 challenge MRI should be leveraged to probe cerebral perfusion and cerebrovascular reserve in patients undergoing antiamyloid therapy. Additionally, the relationship between ARIA and cerebral small vessel disease (cSVD) remains poorly defined. Key unanswered questions include whether underlying cSVD burden predisposes to ARIA, influences dosing strategies or could be modulated to mitigate ARIA risk.

Finally, building robust, inclusive real-world data ecosystems is imperative for global equity. Expanding international registries, such as ALZ-NET and the Yellow Card system, will capture long-term outcomes and safety data across diverse populations and healthcare settings. Collaborating with established international organisations and study groups will be instrumental in driving protocol harmonisation across clinical sites, thereby accelerating the development of globally applicable evidence. Parallel efforts must focus on standardising biomarker assays and imaging protocols across ethnic groups to ensure that safety and efficacy of immunotherapies are accurately assessed and equitably delivered worldwide.

Conclusions

ARIA presents a critical challenge in the administration of antiamyloid immunotherapies for AD, necessitating a standardised and integrative imaging management framework for global surveillance. This framework must pivot on rigorous image acquisition protocols, centralised interpretation standards and structured reporting systems to ensure both patient safety and therapeutic efficacy. However, significant hurdles persist, including global disparities in imaging infrastructure, variability in radiologic expertise for ARIA detection and the logistical burden of serial MRI monitoring, particularly in resource-limited settings such as China. Future efforts must prioritise the development of centralised imaging hubs, the implementation of AI-assisted tools for image analysis and quantification and the establishment of international registries to harmonise real-world imaging data. Ultimately, a cohesive imaging management paradigm, underpinned by continuous education and global collaboration, is indispensable. Only through such a coordinated, technology-enhanced approach can we achieve equitable and effective ARIA surveillance, thereby supporting the safe and widespread use of Alzheimer’s immunotherapy worldwide.
