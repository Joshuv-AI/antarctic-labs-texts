Pilot study of dual-arm absolute quantification of miR-130a and miR-365a in plasma and urine reveals 3p/5p strand-ratio shifts in prostate cancer
Authors: Mirabela Romanescu, Alexandra Ioana Bendelic, Paula Diana Ciordas, Vlad Dema, Tiberiu Răzvan Bardan, Alin Adrian Cumpanas, Aimee Rodica Chis, Catalin Marian, Ioan Ovidiu Sirbu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProstate cancer (PCa) is one of the most common malignancies in men worldwide, yet early detection and accurate risk stratification remain challenging. MicroRNAs (miRNAs), key post-transcriptional regulators, have emerged as promising minimally invasive PCa biomarkers. Accumulating evidence suggests that the relative abundance of the 3p and 5p miRNA mature strands is not fixed but dynamically shaped by the cellular context. Therefore, dual-arm profiling followed by analysis of 3p/5p ratios may distinguish disease states and stages more effectively than evaluating the canonical strand alone. In this pilot, single centre case–control study, we used RT-qPCR with synthetic RNA-based calibration curves to quantify the expression levels and strand ratios of hsa-miR-130a and hsa-miR-365a in plasma and urine of PCa patients and assessed their diagnostic value and association with the disease. Our results revealed concordant downregulation of hsa-miR-130a-3p in PCa in both fluids, accompanied by significant alterations in 3p/5p ratios, suggesting dysregulation of miRNA processing or strand selection. Similarly, only hsa-miR-365a-3p was significantly reduced in patient urine, whereas hsa-miR-365a-5p showed strong correlations with clinical markers such as prostate specific antigen (PSA) and PSA density. These findings, supported by public expression data, highlight the tissue- and fluid-specific selection of miRNA strands, highlight the potential of dual-arm profiling for a more nuanced, non-invasive PCa diagnostics and warrant validation in a larger, multi-centre cohort.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42960-8.Subject terms: Biomarkers, Cancer, Oncology

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is the second most diagnosed malignancy in men and the fifth leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide1. Contrary to common belief2, PCa is typically asymptomatic, particularly in its early stages or when the tumour arises in the prostate’s peripheral zone3,4. Most of the time, when lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) are present5, they are not necessarily indicators of PCa but rather signs of aging or benign prostatic hyperplasia6. Together with the lack of symptoms, the reluctance of PCa patients to seek medical attention contributes to delays in presentation and diagnosis7.

Since the discovery of prostate specific antigen (PSA)8, PCa diagnostic routine has remained largely unchanged, with abnormal blood PSA results prompting the standard 12-core transrectal ultrasound-guided biopsy (TRUSGB)9. However, this approach has limitations since the standard systematic biopsy (SB) does not adequately sample and diagnose clinically significant cancers (CSC) in the anterior prostate zone10,11. At the same time, interobserver variability in Gleason grading and the challenge of discerning aggressive from indolent disease12,13 increases the risk of overdiagnosis and overtreatment14. Additionally, prostate biopsy procedures carry the risk of complications, including sepsis and urinary retention15. Various PSA derivatives have been investigated as alternatives to total PSA (tPSA), yet their clinical uptake remains limited, given the cost and only marginal improvements over tPSA16 in identifying CSC9,16,17. The advent of multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (mpMRI) prompted the adoption of the Prostate Imaging-Reporting and Data System (PI-RADS) and the transition to targeted (TB) and perilesional biopsies (PB)18,19. While TB and PB enhance CSC detection and reduce the number of biopsy cores, excluding SB may result in omitting certain CSC20. With PCa incidence projected to double by 204021, there is a pressing need for more effective, specific, and non-invasive diagnostic biomarkers.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are short non-coding RNAs that modulate gene expression at the post-transcriptional level and regulate key biological processes, including cell proliferation, differentiation and apoptosis, development, and metabolism22. Most miRNA biogenesis begins in the nucleus, where the transcribed primary miRNA (pri-miR) is cleaved by the Drosha-DGCR8 complex to produce a precursor miRNA (pre-miR), which is further processed in the cytoplasm to a mature double-strand miRNA by the endoribonuclease Dicer23,24. Typically, only the guide strand of the duplex is selectively loaded onto the miRNA-induced silencing complex (miRISC) to silence complementary mRNA targets, whereas the passenger strand is degraded25. However, emerging evidence suggests that under certain conditions, passenger strands can also be incorporated into miRISC and play regulatory roles, a phenomenon called arm switching26,27. This process may involve alterations to the seed sequence (nucleotides 2–8 from the 5’ end of the miRNA), the production of isomiRs22 through alternative cleavage (during Drosha and Dicer processing) or the addition of non-template nucleotides, particularly via uridylation28–30.

The balance between the 3p and 5p arms is tissue- and context-dependent27,31,32, and can shift in pathological conditions due to changes in the cellular environment, inflammation, and aging33–38. Notably, studies integrating both miR-3p and miR-5p reveal significant strand expression biases between tumour and adjacent normal tissue39–41, suggesting that, along with the corresponding miRNA expression landscape, arm switching may hold diagnostic or prognostic relevance in cancers42.

miRNAs are routinely released into the extracellular milieu and biological fluids, where they circulate in extracellular vesicles (EVs) or bound to proteins (especially AGO2)43, and function as intercellular signalling molecules that modulate gene expression and influence biological processes in the recipient cells44. While initially considered mere by-products of cellular injury, accumulating evidence shows that the secretion of extracellular miRNAs is a regulated process45. miRNAs are remarkably stable even under harsh storage and handling conditions46: they are resistant to boiling, RNase digestion, exposure to varying pH levels, and repeated freeze–thaw cycles47–49. Moreover, most of them remain stable at room temperature for at least 48 h and can be stored at − 20 °C and -80 °C for extended periods without significant degradation48,50,51.

Extracellular miRNAs are frequently dysregulated in human malignancies, where they function as oncogenes (oncomiRs) or tumour suppressors49,52,53. Their exceptional stability in biological fluids makes them ideal, minimally invasive biomarkers for cancer screening, diagnosis and prognosis54,55. In PCa, multiple circulating miRNAs from plasma, plasma-derived EVs, and urine have already been proposed as promising biomarkers56–59. However, relatively few studies have explicitly quantified and analysed both 3p and 5p strands of a miRNA duplex. This bias could stem from the biological differences between the two arms (e.g., abundance, stability, secretion), and the experimental design (e.g., quantification techniques, normalization methods, and data analysis)27,60,61. As a result, with few exceptions62,63, miRNA passenger strands are often overlooked, potentially obscuring clinically valuable information.

Here, we undertook a pilot, case–control study to profile the expression of the 3p and 5p arms of hsa-miR-130a and hsa-miR-365a (hereafter referred to as miR-130a and miR-365a), two miRNAs with documented dysregulation profiles in PCa. We used reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) with synthetic standard curves to quantify the absolute miRNA copy number in plasma and urine samples from age-matched PCa patients and healthy controls. Then, we assessed how arm-specific miRNA expression data correlate with clinical variables, including age, PSA, PSA density (PSAD), PCa stage and prostate volume. Finally, we analysed miRNA-seq expression data from isomiRdb to examine tissue-specific patterns of miRNA arms usage across various tissues, highlighting the context-dependent regulation of these miRNAs. Given the pilot nature of our endeavour, one important aim was to test technical feasibility and generate data that will guide sample-size calculations for a forthcoming multi-centric validation study.

Results

Demographic and clinical profile of study participants reveals predominance of intermediate-risk prostate cancer cases

We recruited 41 participants, including 21 men with biopsy-confirmed PCa and 20 age-matched controls; their demographic and clinical profiles are summarized in Fig. 1a and b. Age and prostate volume did not differ significantly between the two groups (P > 0.05). In the patient group, the mean plasma tPSA was 9.97 ± 6.62 ng/mL, with a mean PSAD (PSA to prostate volume ratio) of 0.3 ± 0.16 ng/mL2 (Supplementary Table S1).

We used two complementary PCa grading metrics: PI-RADS (derived from mpMRI to assess the risk of CSC and the benefit from image-guided biopsy) and Gleason scores (derived from biopsy histopathology evaluation to stratify tumour aggressiveness) (Fig. 1c). Except for two patients with an equivocal/intermediate risk (PI-RADS = 3), all patients were deemed biopsy-indicative, with PI-RADS scores ≥ 4. Standard curves from synthetic RNA dilution series enable quantification of target miRNAs by RT-qPCR

Standard/calibration curves were generated for the tenfold dilution series of the four synthetic RNA oligonucleotides to enable absolute quantification (copies/μL) of the target miRNAs (Fig. 2). Every dilution underwent the entire workflow as the experimental samples. The standard curves were generated by plotting the log10 of each known concentration in the dilution series (x-axis) against their corresponding Ct value (y-axis).

Differential expression and arm ratio shifts of miR-130a and miR-365a in plasma and urine distinguish prostate cancer patients from controls

The absolute concentration (copies/μL) of the four miRNA strands (miR-130a-3p, miR-130a-5p, miR-365a-3p, miR-365a-5p) in plasma and urine specimens of patients and controls (Fig. 3) was determined by interpolating the adjusted Ct values on the standard curves. Both the expression level and the calculated ratios (plasma/urine or 3p/5p) are presented on a log10 scale, meaning that a log10 ratio of 1 equals a tenfold increase. Complete statistics, together with adjusted P values determined with Benjamini–Hochberg method are presented in Supplementary Table S3 and Supplementary Table S4.

(a) Absolute miRNA concentration in plasma samples from patients versus controls; (b) Absolute miRNA concentration in urine samples from patients versus controls; (c) Ratio of miRNA expression between plasma and urine samples from patients versus controls; (d) Ratio of 3p (guide) and 5p (passenger) miRNA arm expression in patients versus controls.

For miR-130a, the guide arm is consistently less abundant in patients vs. controls both in plasma (P < 0.0001) and urine (P < 0.01). By contrast, the expression of the passenger strand depends on the biological fluid examined: downregulated in patients’ plasma but upregulated in urine (P < 0.0001). Comparing the two fluids within each cohort, the guide strand is more concentrated in plasma than urine, a phenomenon more pronounced in controls (P < 0.0001). The same plasma-dominant pattern is observed for the passenger strand in controls, but not in patients, suggesting disease-related redistribution. For miR-365a, we found a similar decrease of the 3p arm in patients v. controls both in plasma (P < 0.05) and urine (P < 0.01), while the 5p strand remained largely undetected. No significant differences were observed in miRNA expression levels between the different grading groups (Supplementary Table S5).

Examining plasma-to-urine ratios, we observed significantly higher miR-130a guide strand concentrations in plasma across both cohorts (Supplementary Table S4). The passenger strand of miR-130a was also more abundant in plasma than in urine among controls but not among patients. For miR-365a-3p, we detected higher concentrations in urine than in plasma in both patients and controls. Significant differences in plasma/urine expression ratios between patients and controls were observed for the two arms of miR-130a (P < 0.01) but not for miR-365a (P > 0.05).

Finally, strand selection is altered in PCa: for miR-130a, the 3p/5p ratio differs markedly between patients and controls in both plasma (P = 0.0501) and urine (P < 0.0001), suggesting a potential shift in arm preference during miR-130 processing. For miR-365a, the circulating arm ratio is significantly lower in patients (P < 0.01) compared to controls, whereas the urine ratio could not be evaluated due to the less robust expression. Together, this data reinforces the idea that altered strand/arm selection is a feature of miRNA dysregulation and may have potential as biomarker.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was used to evaluate the diagnostic performance of individual miRNAs and their corresponding 3p/5p ratios in distinguishing PCa patients from controls (Fig. 4). In plasma, miR-130a-3p and miR-130a-5p showed exceptional discriminative ability (area under curve, AUC = 0.9952, and 0.9286, respectively), while the miR-130a 3p/5p ratio and miR-365a-3p have modest performances (AUC = 0.6857 and 0.7214, respectively). In urine, the miR-130a 3p/5p ratio demonstrated the best diagnostic accuracy (AUC = 0.9333), outperforming either strand alone: miR-130a-5p remains a good marker (AUC = 0.8857), while miR-130a-3p and miR-365a-3p had fair discriminative value (AUC ≈ 0.7000). For comparison, conventional clinical variables such as age and prostate volume show poor discriminative value in this cohort (Supplementary Table S6). Diagnostic performance of ROC analysis was estimated by 10 × repeated fivefold stratified cross-validation. Each iteration used 80% of samples for model fitting and 20% for evaluation; the process generated 50 independent out-of-fold ROC curves. For the best-performing miRNAs (e.g., miR-130a-3p in plasma and miR-130a 3p/5p ratio in urine), the mean cross-validated AUC remained consistently high, with narrow 95% confidence interval and standard deviation (Supplementary Table S6).

Discussion

Since miRNAs were first proposed as solid-tumour biomarkers38, and Mitchell et al. demonstrated their stability and diagnostic value in blood49, the research interest in circulating miRNA has rapidly expanded52,53. Here, we used RT-qPCR with calibration curves to compare the expression of the 3p and 5p arms of miR-130a and miR-365 in plasma and urine from PCa patients and age-matched controls. For miR-130a, both strands are reduced in patient plasma, while in urine, the 3p arm is downregulated, and the 5p arm is upregulated. For miR-365a-3p, the 3p strand is lower in both plasma and urine from patients. Correlation analysis revealed (both positive and negative) associations between miRNA and clinical variables. Furthermore, isomiRdb mining showed marked tissue-specific differences in the 3p/5p balance.

Both miR-130a and miR-365a are important context-dependent regulators, acting either as oncomiRs or tumour-suppressors. Mir-130a-3p shows a dual behaviour: down-regulated in colorectal and breast cancer but upregulated in cervical and gastric tumours64–67. MiR-130a-5p typically acts as a tumour suppressor, being reduced in non–small cell lung, hepatocellular and gastric cancer68–70. Similarly, miR-365a-3p and miR-365a-5p are aberrantly expressed in various malignancies, with functions in regulating proliferation and apoptosis71–73.

Reduced expression of miR-130a-3p in biofluids and tissues of PCa patients has been previously reported74–77. We previously confirmed this downregulation in plasma (fold change = − 1.47, P < 0.001) and formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue (fold change = − 1.62, P < 0.001)78 and extend it, in the present study, to urine. The miR-130a-3p is silenced in PCa tissue and cell lines by promoter hypermethylation and H3K27me3 deposition76; restoring its expression curbs AR/MAPK signalling, suppresses DEPDC1 and SEC23B76,77 s and re-sensitizes drug-resistant PCa cells to paclitaxel through caspase-8 activation79. The data on miR-130a-5p remains scarce, because most PCa studies did not distinguish between the two strands (or they implicitly examined the guide strand). The reports on miR-365a are inconsistent, irrespective of the PCa tissues and biofluids analysed. We previously found miR-365a-3p upregulated in plasma (fold change = 1.670, P < 0.05) but downregulated in FFPE tissues (fold change = − 1.412, P < 0.01)78. In the present study, we found lower levels in both plasma and urine. Other studies reported higher miR-365a-3p serum levels80, increased tissue expression alongside downregulation of its predicted sponge, LINC0064181, reduced levels in castration-resistant PCa82, and decreased 5p abundance in urinary exosomes83. Lower circulating levels of certain microRNAs in cancers may result from either increased uptake or reduced secretion. OncomiRs can be avidly retained/taken up by tumour cells, often stabilized by Argonaute binding, to promote pro-tumorigenic pathways, thereby depleting their circulating levels84. In contrast, tumour-suppressive miRNAs may be selectively sequestered, degraded, or preferentially packaged into exosomes for disposal, limiting their secretion into circulation and reducing their systemic anti-tumour effects85,86.

According to the American Urological Association, PSA is the first-line screening test for PCa; however, its traditional cutoff value (4 ng/mL) is now considered unreliable and potentially misleading87. The prostate size is inversely associated with the likelihood of detecting PCa on biopsy88, yet positively correlated with the risk of BPH and LUTSs3. In this context, by normalizing PSA to prostate volume, PSAD serves as a valuable adjunct and helps distinguish cancer from benign hypertrophy87,89. In our study, prostate volume did not differ significantly between patients and controls; additionally, no significant correlations were observed between age, prostate volume, and serum PSA levels.

Lower tissue levels of miR-103a-3p are associated with higher Gleason scores and pathological features such as lympho-vascular/perineural invasion and extra prostatic extension75. In our study, plasma miR-130a-3p showed a positive correlation with prostate volume (rho = 0.41 and P < 0.1), while urinary miR-130-3p negatively correlates with PSA and Gleason scores, confirming its association with PCa aggressiveness74,76. Similarly, plasma miR-130a-5p negatively correlates with the PI-RADS score, although this association did not reach statistical significance. The plasma 3p/5p ratio declines with age, consistent with age-related and frailty-related drops in circulating miR-130a-3p90. The link between miR-365a strand expression and PCa clinical variables remains largely unexplored. Lyu et al. found that adding a panel of miRNAs to PSA and prostate volume significantly improved diagnostic accuracy. However, while miR-365a-3p performed well in the discovery phase, it was ultimately not included in the final five-parameter diagnostic model80. In our study, plasma miR-365a-3p positively correlates with prostate volume, whereas the urinary levels show a positive association with both PI-RADS and Gleason primary score. In contrast, the plasma miR-365a-5p arm is negatively associated with the PI-RADS score, but showed strong positive correlations with age, plasma PSA, and PSAD. These strand-specific (and sometimes opposing) correlations suggest that the two arms of miR-365a capture complementary facets of tumour biology, a possibility that warrants deeper investigation.

Our ROC analysis demonstrated that plasma miR-130a-3p and miR-130a-5p have exceptional discriminative power, with AUC values of 0.9952 and 0.9286, respectively. By contrast, the 3p/5p ratio showed limited diagnostic accuracy in plasma. In urine, individual miRNAs performed modestly; however, the miR-130a 3p/5p ratio achieved an AUC of 0.9333, highlighting its potential utility as a non-invasive urinary biomarker. Overall, these finding compare favourably with, and in some cases exceed the diagnostic performance of other biomarkers reported in prior studies. For instance, serum exosomal miR-141 distinguished metastatic from localized PCa with an AUC of 0.869491, while PCa antigen 3 (PCA3) urine test yielded a pooled AUC of 0.75 across 46 clinical studies, increasing slightly to 0.80 in the context of initial biopsies92. The SelectMDx test demonstrated only modest diagnostic potential in high grade PCa (AUC = 0.63), although its combination with MRI improved performance to an AUC of 0.7593.

MicroRNA biogenesis from precursor hairpins is inherently directional. Mutations in Dicer’s RNase IIIA domain abolishes all 3p-derived miRNAs while only partly reducing 5p products, whereas a reciprocal RNase IIIB mutation selectively eliminates 5p-derived miRNAs (including tumour-suppressor let-7 family members) but spares many 3p strands94. Perturbations at the 3′ end of pre-miR can likewise skew Dicer processing and strand selection. Terminal nucleotidyl transferases (TENTs), particularly TUT4 (and to a lesser extent TUT7), add non-templated (NT) nucleotides to pre-miRNAs, altering Dicer’s footprint and shifting the cut site to favour one strand. For example, uridylation of pre-miR-324 by TUT4/7 repositions Dicer and generates a shortened miR-324 duplex from which the 3p strand is preferentially loaded over the 5p strand, thereby impairing tumour cell proliferation28. Removing TUT4 from human cells dramatically reduces miRNA uridylation levels and alters abundance of specific miRNAs, whereas loss of TUT7 alone has minimal effect. By contrast, eliminating 3′ adenylation (catalysed by TENT2/PAPD4) causes only marginal changes in miRNA steady-state levels, indicating that 3′ uridyl tails, not adenyl tails, are the predominant and functionally relevant NT additions on miRNAs95. Functionally, TUT4/7-mediated uridylation exerts pro-tumorigenic effects. Knockout of TUT4/7 in the metastatic prostate DU145 cells nearly abolishes 3′ uridylated isomiRs and removes the U-tails, which in turn causes a significant defect in cancer cell proliferation and migration96. LIN28A/B activity correlates with TUT4–DIS3L2 uridylation and is overexpressed in PCa, particularly in aggressive or stem-like cell lines (e.g. LNCaP, DU145, PC3) and tumors97,98. Consequently, in LIN28-positive cancers, TUT4/7-mediated oligouridylation of let-7 precursors prevents Dicer processing, resulting in the accumulation of uridylated, non-functional pre-miRNAs.

The preferential uridylation of the 3′ arm of miRNA precursors and the resulting bias in strand maturation suggests that the 3p/5p miRNA ratio might serve as a proxy indicator of TUT4/7 activity in cancer cells. For certain miRNAs, an elevated arm ratio in tumour samples could reflect enhanced 3′ uridylation of the pre-miR that either favours the 3p strand (as with miR-324) or selectively impedes 5p biogenesis (as with let-7). However, caution is warranted in using miRNA arm ratios as a biomarker of uridylation, as many confounding factors independent of TUT4/7 can influence miRNA arm dominance. These include intrinsic Drosha and Dicer processing preferences based on primary sequence and secondary structure, duplex end thermodynamics affecting strand loading into Argonaute, and additional 3′ modifications such as adenylation or trimming that affect strand stability. Additionally, tumour specimens often contain mixed cell populations and extracellular miRNA sources (e.g. exosomal miRNAs from surrounding stromal cells), which can confound the observed arm ratios and obscure tumour-intrinsic TUT4/7 activity.

As a pilot investigation, our study is constrained by size and single centre recruitment, which limit the statistical power and the ability to stratify by tumour grade. The expression of the passenger strand of miR-365a—especially in urine—was often near the RT-qPCR detection limit, should be treated as exploratory, and require validation in larger cohorts. As plasma and urine differ in matrix composition and pre-analytical handling, cross-biofluid absolute comparisons should be viewed as semi-quantitative and interpreted cautiously. Because TaqMan microRNA assays may detect a limited spectrum of isomiRs within a given arm, the reported arm and fluid ratios should be interpreted as ratios of the assay-detected isoform pool. Finally, the cross-sectional design precludes any conclusions about temporal or therapy-related changes.

Conclusion

Early PCa research focused almost exclusively on the dominant (guide) strand, and dismissed the potential contributions of the passenger strand, regarded as a non-functional by-product of miRNA biogenesis. The perspective has since shifted, and disease-associated arm switching is now becoming increasingly documented, as both strands are seen as biologically and clinically important. In this respect, although limited by sample size and single-centre recruitment, our pilot study expands the understanding of miRNA-strand-specific dysregulation in PCa.

Our findings underscore the value of analysing both the guide (3p) and passenger (5p) strands of miR-130a and miR-365a in PCa. The consistent downregulation of miR-130a-3p and the disturbed 3p/5p ratios in both plasma and urine point to altered miRNA processing or strand selection in PCA. ROC analysis further highlighted the strong diagnostic performance of plasma miR-130a-3p and miR-130a-5p, as well as urine miR-130a arm ratio. Urinary miR-365a-3p is reduced while plasma miR-365a-5p closely tracks with PSA and PSAD, underscoring its clinical relevance. By focusing on plasma and urine, our analysis supports the use of strand-resolved miRNA profiling as a tool for non-invasive PCa diagnostics. Public tissue datasets further confirm that miRNA arm balance is highly tissue- and fluid-specific.

We speculate that the LIN28-TUT4/7 uridylation pathway biases miRNA processing (down-regulation of tumour suppressive let-7) to favour oncogenic outcomes in PCa, with the 3p/5p miRNA ratio emerging as a potential readout of this dysregulation. A dual-arm strategy (measuring both guide and passenger strand) opens a promising avenue for a more nuanced and precise miRNA-based diagnostics. Adequately powered, multi-centre prospective cohorts and mechanistic investigations are needed to validate our findings, clarify the clinical utility of strand-resolved miRNA profiling in PCa and to unravel the drivers of miRNA arm selection in cancer.

Materials and methods

Study rationale and design

The present case–control study was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki Code of Ethics and has been evaluated and approved by the Local Ethics Commission for Scientific Research of the “Pius Brinzeu” Timis County Emergency Clinical Hospital (no. 338/19.10.2022) and the Scientific Research Ethics Committee of “Victor Babes” University of Medicine and Pharmacy (no. 53/10.11.2022). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants involved in this study. All experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. RT-qPCR experiments adhered to the MIQE guidelines99; we tailored our project taking into consideration the guidelines for reporting non-randomised pilot studies100.

In this study we profiled the expression of both 3’- and 5’-arm of miR-130a and miR-365a in matched plasma and urine samples. For both miRNAs, the 3p arm represents the guide strand, while the 5p arm corresponds to the passenger strand. Absolute concentrations were quantified by RT-qPCR using standard curve calibration of target miRNAs. Total RNA was purified by phenol/chloroform method, then reverse-transcribed and amplified with dedicated TaqMan primers and probes. Statistical analysis was carried out in RStudio with specialized biostatistics packages. An overview of the study design and workflow is provided in Fig. 7.

Overview of the experimental workflow and analytical pipeline.

Study participants and clinical variables

This study includes PCa patients who were admitted or referred to the Urology Department of “Pius Brinzeu” Timis County Emergency Clinical Hospital, as well as age-matched healthy controls. The inclusion criteria consisted of (1) diagnosis of prostate cancer (PCa), adenocarcinoma histological type, and (2) signed informed consent. PCa diagnostic was confirmed by a board-certified urologist using the plasma total PSA (tPSA), digital rectal examination (DRE), transrectal ultrasound-guided prostate biopsy (TRUGPB), and multiparametric prostate MRI (mpMRI). A case was classified as a positive diagnosis when all three of the following criteria were met: PSA > 4 ng/μL, Gleason grade group > 1, and PI-RADS > 3. Exclusion criteria were (1) benign prostate diseases, (2) cognitive impairment, and (3) inability to provide an informed consent.

We collected the following numerical variables in a de-identified form: age, serum tPSA concentration, and prostate volume. Categorical variables recorded included the Gleason pattern, Gleason score, Gleason Grade Group, PI-RADS category, and DRE findings. Considering that one in eight men are diagnosed with prostate cancer during their lifetime, we calculated that 40 individuals should be enrolled in our pilot study to reach a confidence level of 80% at a confidence interval of 5%.

Sample collection and primary processing

Peripheral blood and midstream urine samples were obtained from PCa patients and age-matched controls. Venous blood was collected into EDTA-coated vacutainers, kept refrigerated at 4 °C for a maximum of 2 h, and then centrifuged for 10 min at 1500 × g and 4 °C to separate the plasma. Supernatants displaying signs of haemolysis, hyperlipidaemia, hyperbilirubinemia, or visible turbidity were discarded to avoid downstream interferences. The eligible plasma samples were aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C until further use. Urine was collected in 50 mL tubes prefilled with an equivalent volume (1:1, v/v) of RNAlater™ Stabilization Solution (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), resulting in a twofold dilution of the original sample. Urine:RNALater mixes were stored at room temperature for a maximum of seven days until downstream processing. Specimens with signs of haematuria, bilirubinuria, or turbidity were likewise discarded.

Isolation of miRNA-enriched total RNA

Plasma specimens were thawed on ice, while urine:RNAlater mixtures were processed directly at room temperature. For both biofluids, a fixed volume of 250 μL was centrifuged for 5 min at 16,000 × g and 4 °C to eliminate precipitates and cell debris contaminants. RNA was then purified from 200 μL of the supernatant using miRNeasy Serum/Plasma kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. For external normalization, a synthetic Caenorhabditis elegans miR-39 control (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) was spiked in at the beginning of the extraction. The purified RNA was eluted in nuclease-free water (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and stored at − 80 °C until further use.

cDNA synthesis and real-time qPCR

Reverse transcription and quantitative PCR were performed separately for the 3’- and 5’-arm of both target miRNAs and the spike-in control cel-miR-39-5p. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using the TaqMan™ MicroRNA Reverse Transcription Kit along with specific stem-loop primers provided in the inventoried TaqMan™ MicroRNA Assays, following the manufacturer’s protocol (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA). cDNA samples were stored at − 20 °C until further use. Each cDNA target was then amplified in triplicate on a 7900HT Fast Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) using the TaqMan™ Universal Master Mix II (no UNG) and dedicated TaqMan probes/primers sets provided in TaqMan™ MicroRNA Assays.

Generation of standard curves

The four synthetic miRNA lyophilized oligonucleotides (Eurogentec, Kaneka Corporation, Seraing, Belgium) were dissolved in nuclease-free water to generate stock solutions at a concentration of 1014 copies/μL. Each stock was then serially diluted tenfold to produce ten working standard solutions spanning 1010 to 101 copies/μL. All solutions were stored at − 80 °C until further use.

The working solutions were then subjected to the same purification, reverse transcription, and amplification protocol as plasma and urine samples. Carrier RNA from bacteriophage MS2 (Sigma-Aldrich, Merck Group, St. Louis, Missouri, USA) was used to mimic the RNA background in biological fluids and augment the recovery of low-abundance species. For this, 0.5 μL of carrier RNA corresponding to a final concentration of 2 ng/μL was added to each dilution. The final volume was adjusted to 200 μL with nuclease-free water. Of note, the standards are not fully matrix-matched to RNAlater-stabilized urine.

isomiRdb data analysis

To complement the experimental data, miRNA expression data from 47 human tissues were retrieved from the isomiRdb database (Chair for Clinical Bioinformatics, Saarland University, Saarbrücken, Germany). This database was compiled from raw miRNA-seq datasets submitted to the Sequence Read Archive (SRA), The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), and miRMaster. The raw expression data were presented in three formats: read counts, reads per million (RPM), and log10-transformed values expressed as log10(RPM + 1). To calculate the arm ratio in each tissue, we used a linear scale by dividing the RPM of the 3p arm by the RPM of the 5p arm.

Data analysis and statistics

We obtained two raw RT-qPCR data sets (one for plasma and one for urine) consisting of three technical Ct replicates for the five microRNAs: four targets (hsa-miR-130a-3p/5p and hsa-miR-365a-3p/5p) and the spike-in normalizer cel-miR-39-5p. To correct for sample-to-sample variability in RNA purification and RT-qPCR efficiency, we computed a correction factor (CF) from the spike-in signal within each matrix. CF was defined as the deviation of the sample’s mean cel-miR-39-5p Ct from the mean cel-miR-39-5p Ct across all samples of the same biofluid type (Eq. 1). Separate CFs were derived for plasma and urine to account for matrix-specific differences in recovery. An adjusted Ct for each target miRNA in each sample was then obtained by subtracting the CF from the target’s mean raw Ct (Eq. 2).

Adjusted Ct values were converted to copy numbers (absolute miRNA concentration, copies/µL) with the standard-curve equation parameters (the y-intercept and the slope) (Eq. 3).

Because urine was collected in RNAlater at 1:1 (v/v), urine concentrations were back-calculated to the original (undiluted) urine by applying a two-fold dilution correction prior to any cross-biofluid comparisons. Differences in miRNA expression between the patient and control groups were expressed as fold change (FC) and calculated using the base-10 logarithm of the absolute miRNA concentrations for each group (Eq. 4).

The same log10 copy numbers were used to compare plasma vs. urine and 3p (guide) vs. 5p (passenger) strands. A positive log10(3p/5p) ratio indicates that the 3p strand is more abundant; a negative value indicates 5p predominance. Strand ratios and any ratio-based statistical analyses were computed only for samples for which both strands were detectable.

The discriminative ability of each predictor to differentiate between cases and controls was evaluated using ROC analysis. For each predictor, the optimal classification threshold was determined based on the point closest to the top-left corner of the ROC space, maximizing both sensitivity and specificity. To assess model performance and ensure generalizability ROC analysis was complemented by a repeated cross-validation procedure. Specifically, a 10 × repeated fivefold cross-validation was performed for each predictor using stratified random sampling to preserve class proportions. In each iteration, the dataset was randomly partitioned into five folds; four folds were used for model evaluation and one-fold for testing. This process was repeated ten times with different random partitions, resulting in 50 distinct train/test splits per predictor.

All data analyses and visualizations were performed in R (RStudio v2023.06.0 + 421). Descriptive and inferential statistics analyses were performed using the default arguments of R functions. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For inferential analysis, two-tailed heteroscedastic tests were employed. The parametric Welch two-sample t-test was applied to normally distributed data, while the non-parametric Wilcoxon Rank Sum two-sample test was used for data that did not meet the normality assumption. Associations between variables were examined using Spearman correlation, employing pairwise complete observations and a confidence level of 0.95. Significance was set at P < 0.05 for all tests. To account for multiple comparisons across all variables analysed, P values were adjusted (FDR-corrected) using the Benjamini–Hochberg method.
