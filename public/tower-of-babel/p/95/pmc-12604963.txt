An exploratory study on the effects of dermal suctioning prior to underwater treadmill exercise on post-exercise oxidative stress in healthy dogs
Authors: Takuma MIYATA, Saya KAWAI, Seri SEKI, Hiroki SHIBUTANI, Tomonari SHIBUTANI, Katsumi ISHIOKA
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOxidative stress has attracted attention as an indicator of exercise load. Minimizing the impact on the body is essential during underwater treadmill exercise. Here, we conducted an exploratory study of the effects of dermal suction, which has been reported to improve blood flow in healthy dogs, prior to underwater treadmill exercise. Five healthy beagles (two neutered males and three spayed females) underwent dermal suctioning with Medicell for PETS over the entire body for 30 min. Underwater treadmill exercise was performed (water temperature: 32–33°C, water level: hip joint, load: 1.5 km/hr for 20 min). Blood samples were collected immediately before; immediately after; and 1 hr, 1 day, and 3 days after the underwater treadmill exercise; the diacron-reactive oxygen metabolites (d-ROMs), indicating the oxidation level, and biological antioxidant potential (BAP), indicating antioxidant capacity, were measured to determine the BAP/d-ROMs ratio as the potential antioxidant capacity. The results showed that dermal suction significantly decreased d-ROMs 3 days after underwater treadmill exercise, and the BAP/d-ROMs ratio was significantly higher immediately after exercise and 1 and 3 days after dermal suctioning. The parasympathetic nervous system is activated by dermal suctioning, resulting in muscle relaxation and mild psychological change. This study showed that dermal suction tended to decrease oxidative stress after exercise. Future studies should consider effects on the nervous system and exercise intensity indices and should be conducted in larger canine cohorts.Keywords: dermal suction, dog, fascia, oxidative stress, underwater treadmill

INTRODUCTION

Physiotherapy is administered to improve motor function in dogs and cats. An underwater treadmill is used for exercise therapy to leverage the characteristics of water. The buoyancy force of water pushes the body upward and acts in the opposite direction of gravity, enabling exercise with a reduced load on the body. The water level set at the height of the dog’s greater trochanter has been reported to reduce weight-bearing by approximately 40% of body weight [14]. Hydrostatic pressure is the pressure applied to the body’s surface that reduces swelling and edema in joints and has a beneficial effect on the cardiovascular system. Hydrostatic pressure varies with depth and has different effects on cardiopulmonary function, depending on the water level. Viscous resistance is the resistive force of motion in water caused by friction between fluid molecules, which requires propulsion to move forward. Therefore, performing underwater treadmill exercises at the water level of the mid-femur region requires more muscular activity than at shallower water levels, placing a greater load on the biceps femoris muscle, which is involved in propulsive force [21]. Previous studies have also suggested that water level significantly affects joint kinematics during exercise [6]. Hydrotherapy, including underwater treadmill exercise, is expected to become increasingly popular. Thus, more research is needed to understand its effects on animals with medical conditions and to ensure it is performed safely. Accordingly, this study was initially conducted as basic research in healthy dogs.

To safely implement exercise therapy, understanding the exercise load and setting an appropriate intensity are essential to avoid significant bodily damage. Exercise intensity is often determined by measuring lactate as an indicator of exercise intensity; however, recently, oxidative stress has been measured and reported in dogs [19]. Oxidative stress is an imbalance between oxidative and antioxidant reactions in an organism, leading to an inclination toward an oxidative state and causing oxidative damage, which is associated with aging and various diseases [13]. Oxidative stress markers included the diacron-reactive oxygen metabolite (d-ROMs) test, which evaluates the degree of oxidation, and the biological antioxidant potential (BAP) test, which assesses the antioxidant potential. These parameters were measured using blood samples. In the d-ROMs test, reactive oxygen metabolites in biological samples produce alkoxyl and peroxyl radicals via the Fenton reaction in the presence of iron released from plasma proteins by an acidic buffer solution. These radicals can oxidize alkyl-substituted aromatic amines, producing pink derivatives that can be photoquantified at 505 nm [3]. In the BAP test, adding a plasma sample to a colored solution obtained by mixing a ferric chloride solution and a thiocyanate derivative solution caused decolorization; the intensity, photometrically measured at 505 nm, was proportional to the ferric ion-reducing capacity of the plasma [5]. The reference ranges for plasma d-ROMs and BAP levels in healthy Labrador Retrievers are 67.1–91.5 U. CARR and 2,069–2,554 µmol/L, respectively [19], which are comparable to the reference ranges in humans [9, 20], with a similar range for BAP but approximately 1/3 in d-ROMs. A previous study in dogs reported that d-ROMs values increased significantly 1 hr after exercise, and BAP values decreased significantly immediately after 20 min of light running on land, suggesting that oxidative stress occurs [19].

In humans, coupling therapy based on traditional Chinese medicine has been used to vacuum-localize areas of the skin for various benefits, including pain reduction, increased blood circulation, improved flexibility, and healing [1, 2]. Additionally, coupling therapy promotes blood circulation, activates the parasympathetic nervous system, and improves the general condition of the body by enhancing systemic blood flow. Similar effects have been reported with massage [8] and dermal suction [7, 15, 17] using specially designed instruments. Although massage does not require special equipment, it does necessitate an understanding of fascial properties and tissue responses to pressure. In contrast, dermal suction using a dedicated device requires no such specialized knowledge and is simpler and potentially more effective. However, to the best of our knowledge, no studies have examined the effects of dermal suction on oxidative stress in dogs. Therefore, this exploratory study investigated the feasibility of performing dermal suction on healthy dogs prior to underwater treadmill exercise to reduce exercise-induced physical stress.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Experimental animals

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nippon Veterinary and Life Science University (2023S-3, S2023S-3). The breeding and management of laboratory animals were conducted in accordance with the Manual on Breeding Facilities, Care and Storage of Laboratory Animals, and Animal Experiments at the Nippon Veterinary and Life Science University. Five healthy adult beagles (two neutered males and three spayed females) with an average age of 4.3 years (3.9–5.7 years) were included in the study. No power analysis or other sample size justification was performed. Food and water were provided ad libitum before the experiment. Food portions were measured for each feeding and divided into two meals per day. Body weight was measured monthly to ensure appropriate weight maintenance.

Experimental protocol

First, the rectal temperature (RT), pulse rate (PR), and respiratory rate (RR) were measured immediately before the underwater treadmill exercise, and 1 mL of blood was drawn from the lateral saphenous vein to measure oxidative stress. Subsequently, the subjects performed walking exercises on an underwater treadmill (TP-130pro, Tokyo Menics, Saitama, Japan) for 20 min, as described in a previous study [19]. RT, PR, and RR were measured immediately and 1 hr after the exercise, and blood samples were collected. Towel drying was performed immediately after the exercise measurements and blood collection, and the animals were allowed to rest. Blood samples were also collected 1 and 3 days after the underwater treadmill exercise (without dermal suctioning; control group). Approximately 1 month later, the same procedure was performed on the same subject. After the measurements and blood sampling were completed just prior to the underwater treadmill exercise, dermal suctioning was performed using a 1.0-cm-diameter probe attached to the Medicell PETS device (MJ Co., Okayama, Japan), maintaining a suction pressure of 15–20 kPa. The probe was moved for approximately 10 min from the neck to the back, waist, and base of the tail, and for 5 min each over the entire forelimbs and hindlimbs, totaling 30 min. The subjects then exercised on the underwater treadmill for 20 min, and their RT, PR, and RR were measured; blood samples were drawn at the same time as in the group without dermal suctioning (with dermal suctioning; Medicell group).

Underwater treadmill

The water temperature was set at 32–33°C [18], and the water level was adjusted to the subject’s hip joint position. The walking speed was adjusted with a dial to match the animal’s slow walking speed of 1.5 km/hr. Both groups, with and without dermal suctioning, performed 20 min of continuous water walking, during which time the veterinarian determined that the animal was at level 4 during the water training using the canine perceptual motor criteria; the animal was stopped at that point and allowed to rest.

RT, PR, and RR

The RT was measured from the anus using a rectal thermometer (Thermoflex for animals; ASTEC, Ibaraki, Japan). PR was measured by palpating the internal femoral artery for 15 sec and then converted to the corresponding 1-min value. RR was visually counted for 15 sec three times and converted to the corresponding 1-min values.

Oxidative stress markers

After collection, blood was aliquoted into heparin collection tubes and immediately centrifuged at 6,200 rpm for 5 min to separate the plasma. After centrifugation, 100 μL of plasma from each sample was dispensed into Eppendorf tubes and stored frozen at −30°C until oxidative stress markers were measured. Oxidative stress markers were measured for d-ROMs and BAP using a free-radical analyzer (FREE Carpe Diem, Wismar, Tokyo, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The ratio was calculated from the measured d-ROMs and BAP values and evaluated for potential antioxidant capacity.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using EZR (Jichi Medical University, Tochigi, Japan) [11], an application of the R software. EZR is a modified R Commander plug-in designed to add statistical functions frequently used in biostatistics. For each of the following parameters: RT, PR, RR, d-ROMs, BAP, and BAP/d-ROMs ratio, the Holm method was used for repeated-measures analysis of variance for within-group comparison between the control and Medicell groups, and a paired t-test was used for between-group comparison. All results are expressed as mean ± SE, and P<0.05 was considered significantly different.

RESULTS

No subject discontinued the 20-min underwater treadmill exercise or showed adverse reactions, such as skin redness or fever, attributable to dermal suctioning.

No significant difference in RT was observed between groups; however, the MEDICEL group showed a pattern of variability opposite to that of the control group 1 hr after underwater treadmill exercise (Fig. 1). PR exhibited similar variability, with no significant differences in either within-group or between-group comparisons (Fig. 2). For RR, one animal in the MEDICEL group continued panting despite a rest period immediately before the underwater treadmill exercise. This value was included in the RR analysis and contributed to group differences, but neither within-group nor between-group comparisons reached statistical significance, and both showed similar variability (Fig. 3). d-ROMs were significantly decreased in the MEDICEL group 3 days after underwater treadmill exercise. In contrast, d-ROMs in the control group slightly exceeded the reference range (67.1–91.5 U. CARR) at the same time point (Fig. 4). BAP levels were generally below the reference value (2,069–2,554 µmol/L), both immediately before and 3 days after underwater treadmill exercise (Fig. 5). The Medicell group showed a significant increase in potential antioxidant capacity compared with the control group immediately after underwater treadmill exercise and after 1 and 3 days (Fig. 6).

DISCUSSION

RT after underwater treadmill exercise showed a pattern opposite to that of the control group 1 hr after exercise, likely as a result of dermal suctioning. This may be explained by the fact that dermal suctioning before exercise increased subcutaneous blood flow [15] and continued to enhance systemic circulation up to 1 hr post-exercise. Although a previous study [15] reported a significant increase in RT at 20 min after dermal suctioning, the present study suggests that the effects of dermal suction may persist beyond the immediate post-treatment period. Homeostatic regulation of core temperature is an important function of the central nervous system, which responds to environmental temperature changes and energy homeostasis, and also influences the autonomic nervous system, including immune responses and various behavioral states [16]. Therefore, we believe that dermal suction may help stabilize RT and consequently reduce physiological stress on the organism.

No significant changes were observed in the PR or RR with or without dermal suctioning, similar to a previous study [15], suggesting that the dermal suctioning procedure is not stressful for animals. The significant post-exercise decrease in d-ROMs and the significant post-exercise increase in potential antioxidant capacity with dermal suctioning are consistent with reports that dermal suctioning acted on the parasympathetic nervous system, resulting in muscle relaxation and mild psychological changes [12]. These results are also consistent with reports on the sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous systems. A previous study reported that dermal suction restored the balance between the sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous systems [4]. Therefore, further studies are needed to investigate the effects on autonomic function and clarify the mechanisms by which dermal suction influences antioxidant capacity. In addition to its antioxidant capacity, it is clinically effective in restoring homeostasis by increasing parasympathetic nervous system activity and treating various stress-related health problems [10]. Therefore, dermal suction may have a relaxing effect.

In contrast, in the present study, the exercise duration was the same as that in a previous study in dogs [19]. However, the results differed from the post-exercise d-ROMs and BAP variations observed in the control group. Differences in exercise intensity between the present study and previous studies [19] could not be compared. This, along with the limited data available for estimating maximal oxygen consumption (VO2max), was the reason we could not examine this issue. The difference in exercise environment between land and water may also have affected the results; thus, future studies should examine VO2max in this context. There is limited research on the appropriate intensity of physiotherapy exercise for dogs and cats. Therefore, it is necessary to first investigate the appropriate exercise intensity for physical therapy in these animals. Subsequently, the effects of dermal suction on post-exercise oxidation levels and antioxidant capacity under conditions of appropriate exercise intensity should also be evaluated. The underwater treadmill exercise selected for physiotherapy in this study is commonly performed on animals with impaired motor function and is expected to impose a different burden on them compared to healthy dogs. Therefore, post-exercise care should be carefully considered. Dermal suction prior to underwater treadmill exercise may influence oxidative stress, as indicated by this study. Further research is needed, incorporating additional factors such as parasympathetic nervous system activity and VO2max, as well as a larger sample size.

An exploratory study was conducted on the effect of dermal suction on the oxidative stress response after underwater treadmill exercise in healthy dogs. Results showed that dermal suction tended to lower post-exercise oxidative stress. Future studies should consider effects on the nervous system and exercise intensity indices such as VO2max, and additional studies should be conducted in a larger cohort of dogs.
