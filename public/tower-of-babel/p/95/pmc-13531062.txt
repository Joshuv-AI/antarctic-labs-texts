Subacute Stent Thrombosis Associated With Prasugrel Refractoriness in a Patient With Chronic ITP
Authors: Masahiro Takeo, Hiroki Okabe, Keisuke Hidaka, Yutaro Naka, Koshi Setoyama, Masaharu Kataoka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo our knowledge, there have been no reports of stent thrombosis due to prasugrel nonresponse in patients with immune thrombocytopenia (ITP).Case SummaryA 66-year-old man with chronic ITP presented with an inferior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction. The patient underwent drug-eluting stent implantation. On the sixth day, he developed recurrent chest pain. Coronary angiography revealed subacute stent thrombosis. P2Y12 reactive unit (PRU) revealed prasugrel nonresponse (PRU 274), prompting a switch to ticagrelor, which improved PRU (PRU 72). No further stent thrombosis events were observed thereafter.DiscussionThis case demonstrates that stent thrombosis can occur in patients unresponsive to prasugrel and that PRU measurement may be useful even in ITP patients.Take-Home MessagesIn ITP patients, it is necessary to carefully balance the risk of thrombosis with the risk of bleeding. Prasugrel nonresponse may contribute to stent thrombosis, and PRU may be useful for identifying prasugrel nonresponse in patients with ITP.Key words: acute coronary syndrome, antiplatelet, chest pain, stents, thrombosis

History of Presentation

A 66-year-old man presented with sudden chest pain and was transported by ambulance. No heart murmur or leg edema was noted. A blood sample showed elevated troponin and platelets of 30,000/μL. Electrocardiography showed ST-segment elevations in II, III, and aVF (Figure 1A). Echocardiography showed wall-motion abnormalities in the inferior wall region, consistent with the electrocardiographic changes. Emergency coronary angiography (CAG) demonstrated a total occlusion lesion in the right coronary artery (RCA) #2 (Figure 1B). After giving aspirin 200 mg and prasugrel 20 mg, a drug-eluting stent was placed at the same site. After RCA stent placement, reduced blood flow was observed, considered to be due to distal embolization. Balloon angioplasty (5.0 × 6 mm, 14 atm) was performed, and nicorandil and nitroprusside were administered into the coronary artery, but blood flow remained at TIMI flow grade 2. An intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) was therefore inserted, and the procedure was completed with TIMI flow grade 3 (Figure 1C). The patient was managed in the intensive care unit. Aspirin (100 mg) and prasugrel (3.75 mg) were used as antiplatelet agents. Additionally, continuous intravenous calcium heparin was administered during IABP insertion. The thrombopoietin receptor agonist previously used for immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) was discontinued, and steroid therapy was initiated. Chest pain disappeared postoperatively, and cardiac enzyme levels peaked (maximum creatinine kinase: 1,490 U/L) on the second day of percutaneous coronary intervention. On the fifth day after percutaneous coronary intervention, the IABP was removed, continuous intravenous heparin administration was discontinued, and the patient was transferred to the general ward. However, on the morning of the sixth day, a sudden chest pain flare-up was observed. Electrocardiography revealed ST-segment elevation in the inferior wall area. An emergency CAG was performed on suspicion of stent thrombosis. A reoccluded lesion was observed at the proximal edge of the stent implanted in the RCA (Figure 2A). The guidewire passed smoothly through the lesion. Intravascular ultrasound revealed thrombus formation within the stent and distally beyond the stent placement site (Figures 2B and 2C). Thrombus aspiration was performed using a thrombus aspiration catheter, and a small amount of the thrombus was retrieved. Due to the presence of a large amount of residual thrombus, long-term dilation was performed using a perfusion balloon. We inserted an IABP again and administered continuous intravenous heparin.

Past Medical History

The patient had been undergoing treatment for ITP for approximately 40 years. Before the acute myocardial infarction, treatment consisted of steroids (7.5 mg) and a thrombopoietin receptor agonist (eltrombopag; 25 mg). His platelets were controlled at about 30,000/μL. The patient was managed in a stable outpatient setting with no major bleeding or thrombotic complications. The patient was administered ursodeoxycholic acid to treat liver function abnormalities.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnoses of stent thrombosis were failure to respond to antiplatelet agents, potential thrombophilia, metal allergy, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies, thrombophilia associated with ITP or ITP medications, acute coronary syndrome, in-stent protrusions, and no flow after stent placement.

Investigations

The aspirin reaction unit was 390, indicating an adequate response. However, the P2Y12 reactive unit (PRU) was 274, suggesting that the patient may not have had an effective response to P2Y12.

Platelet aggregation function was assessed using ADP and collagen-induced aggregation, confirming adequate inhibition under aspirin and prasugrel therapy. The immature platelet fraction was 12.6%. Due to ITP, the immature platelet ratio was high, and the possibility that this could cause thrombosis cannot be ruled out. Potential thrombophilic predisposition was investigated using blood samples. Antiphospholipid antibody syndrome, collagen disease, and vasculitis were ruled out from the physical findings and blood test results. Patch tests for metals and drugs used in the stents were also performed, all of which were negative.

Thrombopoietin receptor agonists, which have been reported to cause stent thrombosis, were discontinued after catheter treatment, and the platelet counts were managed with steroids alone. The reported half-life of eltrombopag at a dose of 25 mg/d repeated administration is 39.7 ± 3.2 hours. Based on this pharmacokinetic profile, the effect of eltrombopag was likely diminished at the time of onset, suggesting a limited contribution to thrombosis. However, there have been reports of thrombosis associated with steroid use, and the possibility of thrombosis due to steroid administration cannot be completely ruled out.

HIT antibodies were measured using a latex turbidimetric immunoassay and showed 1.0 U/mL (positive at ≥1.0 U/mL) after the onset of stent thrombosis, but a repeat test 2 weeks later showed a negative result. Considering the platelet count trend, the 4 Ts score, and the overall clinical presentation, the likelihood of HIT-related thrombosis was considered low.

Management

Stent thrombosis due to inadequate antiplatelet effect was strongly suspected, and the patient was switched from prasugrel to ticagrelor.

Outcome and Follow-Up

CAG was performed 1 week after stent thrombosis onset (day 13). Although stenosis was observed proximally to the right coronary stent placement site due to residual thrombus, TIMI flow grade 3 was confirmed. The IABP was removed, and anticoagulant therapy was continued. CAG performed 2 weeks after IABP removal showed progression of stenosis at the same site. The PRU at this point was 72 (day 20), indicating an effective response to P2Y12 inhibitor therapy. The cause is considered unlikely to be attributable to antiplatelet agents. The patient showed no chest symptoms, but due to the high degree of stenosis and the high risk of reocclusion, a drug-eluting stent was placed at the same site (day 35). Subsequently, the patient experienced no chest pain and was nearing discharge. However, against the background of pancytopenia due to ITP medication and increased susceptibility to infection from steroids, the patient developed Pneumocystis pneumonia (day 47). Despite antifungal therapy, the patient died due to respiratory failure (day 71).

Discussion

We reported a case of subacute stent thrombosis associated with prasugrel refractoriness in a patient with chronic ITP. To our knowledge, this is the first such report.

The main cause of thrombocytopenia in ITP is that platelets opsonized by antiplatelet autoantibodies targeting mainly platelet membrane glycoproteins GPIIb/IIIa (CD41/CD61) and GPⅠb/Ⅸ (CD42) are trapped and phagocytosed via Fc receptors on reticulospinal cells such as spleen.1 Major bleeding complications of ITP reportedly occur in approximately 5% of patients over their lifetime, particularly in older patients and those with low platelet counts.2 Studies have shown that patients with acute myocardial infarction complicated by ITP have more bleeding complications and longer hospital stays than patients without ITP; however, in-hospital mortality is not significantly different.3 On the other hand, there is also a risk of thrombosis. There are reports of thrombosis attributed to increased immature platelets, inflammatory cytokines, and endothelial damage caused by autoantibodies, indicating a higher thrombosis risk than in non-ITP patients.4 There are also case reports of stent thrombosis associated with TPO antibody positivity and intravenous immunoglobulin administration.5,6 Numerous reports exist of hypercoagulable states due to steroid use.7

Antiplatelet therapy after stent placement for acute coronary syndrome involves the use of cyclooxygenase inhibitors and P2Y12 receptor inhibitors. Thienopyridines are prodrugs oxidized by cytochrome P-450, a drug-metabolizing enzyme in the liver, to produce active metabolites that irreversibly bind to the P2Y12 receptor on platelets, thereby exerting antiplatelet effects. In Japan, clopidogrel (300 mg) and prasugrel (20 mg) are typically used as loading doses, followed by maintenance doses of clopidogrel 75 mg/d and prasugrel 3.75 mg/d. Notably, the 2 drugs differ in their metabolic pathways: clopidogrel is primarily activated via CYP2C19, whereas prasugrel is metabolized mainly through CYP3A4 and CYP2B6. In Japan, the prasugrel maintenance dose is 3.75 mg/d, which is lower than those in Western countries.8 In Asian patients, compared with Caucasian patients, clopidogrel may not have sufficient antiplatelet activity due to genetic polymorphisms in CYP2C19. There have been several reports of stent thrombosis with suspected prasugrel refractoriness; however, none have been reported in patients with ITP.9 Nonthienopyridine agents exert their antiplatelet effects by directly and reversibly acting on the platelet P2Y12 receptor. Ticagrelor is administered at 180 mg twice daily for acute myocardial infarction. PRU is often used to evaluate the antiplatelet effect of P2Y12 receptor inhibitors. Although there have been reports of optimal PRU after catheterization,10 there are few reports on PRU measurement in patients with ITP receiving antiplatelet drugs.

In this case, prasugrel resistance was suspected, and PRU was measured. Switching from prasugrel to ticagrelor resulted in favorable PRU values. Potential mechanisms of prasugrel nonresponse included abnormalities in the prasugrel metabolic pathway and an increase in platelets unaffected by antiplatelet drugs due to accelerated platelet turnover. This case suggests the diagnostic utility of PRU measurement in ITP patients; however, the optimal PRU threshold for ITP patients remains unclear. It should be noted that PRU values may be underestimated in ITP patients due to their inherently low platelet counts. Further investigation is needed to determine the optimal PRU threshold in ITP patients undergoing coronary artery treatment.

Conclusions

We report a case of subacute stent thrombosis associated with prasugrel nonresponse in a patient with chronic ITP. Case reports of prasugrel nonresponse in ITP patients are rare, and PRU measurement may be useful for diagnosis in ITP patients.
