Midodrine to facilitate cardioprotective therapy in hypotensive HER2-positive breast cancer patients with cancer-therapy–related cardiac dysfunction—multicenter case experience
Authors: Efstratios Koutroumpakis, Cezar Iliescu, Anita Deswal, Charles Porter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHER2-targeted therapies significantly improve outcomes in HER2-positive breast cancer but are associated with cancer therapy–related cardiac dysfunction (CTRCD), which may necessitate interruption of oncologic treatment. Hypotension, which is not uncommon in patients with cancer, may limit the use of guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) required for left ventricular (LV) recovery, and no approved strategy exists to safely augment blood pressure in this setting.Case presentationWe report two cases of hypotensive breast cancer patients who developed CTRCD during HER2-targeted therapy and were unable to tolerate GDMT due to symptomatic hypotension. In both cases, initiation and titration of midodrine enabled stabilization of blood pressure, facilitation of cardioprotective therapy with beta-blockers and/or sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors, and subsequent recovery of LV systolic function. This strategy allowed continuation or reinitiation of life-prolonging HER2-directed therapy without worsening heart failure or significant adverse effects.Discussion and conclusionsMidodrine may serve as a practical hemodynamic adjunct to enable guideline-directed cardioprotective therapy in selected hypotensive patients with HER2 therapy–related cardiomyopathy. These cases illustrate the feasibility of blood pressure augmentation as a strategy to facilitate cardiac recovery while preserving access to essential oncologic therapy. They do not establish efficacy or safety, and prospective studies with systematic assessment and predefined endpoints are warranted to define patient selection and long-term outcomes.Keywords: Midodrine, Cardio-Oncology, HER2-positive breast cancer, Hypotension, Cancer-therapy–related cardiac dysfunction, Guideline-directed medical therapy, Permissive cardiotoxicity

Background

Human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-targeted therapies for HER2-positive breast cancer carry a well-recognized risk of cancer-therapy–related cardiac dysfunction (CTRCD), which may necessitate interruption of oncologic therapy and jeopardize cancer control. Left ventricular systolic dysfunction related to HER2 inhibition is often reversible, but recovery frequently depends on timely initiation and titration of cardioprotective guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT). Cancer patients, predisposed to hypotension due to cachexia, autonomic dysfunction, volume depletion, or polypharmacy, are particularly intolerant of the blood pressure (BP)–lowering effects of GDMT, including β-blockers, renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) inhibitors, and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists. As a result, clinicians are often forced to use subtherapeutic “mini-doses” of cardioprotective medications or to interrupt cancer therapy altogether when adequate GDMT cannot be tolerated. No approved therapy exists specifically to raise systemic BP to facilitate GDMT for CTRCD and enable continuation or resumption of essential cancer treatment.

Midodrine, a selective peripheral α1-adrenergic receptor (AR) agonist, increases systemic vascular resistance and BP without direct stimulation of cardiac β-adrenergic receptors, thereby avoiding antagonism of β-blocker therapy. Its active metabolite, desglymidodrine, has a predictable onset of action (approximately 30 min) and a short half-life of 3–4 h, allowing flexible dosing and titration around other blood pressure–lowering therapies. Unlike agents that increase sympathetic tone or intravascular volume, midodrine exerts its effect through peripheral vasoconstriction, making it an attractive option in patients with heart failure or CTRCD who are vulnerable to volume overload or arrhythmias.

Preclinical and translational data further suggest that α1-AR signaling may promote adaptive hypertrophic responses and anti-apoptotic pathways in cardiomyocytes, contrasting with the maladaptive effects of chronic β1-AR overstimulation [1, 2]. In our prior retrospective cohort of 85 cancer patients with heart failure treated with midodrine, continuation of midodrine therapy was not associated with worsening cardiac biomarkers or echocardiographic parameters, and supine hypertension occurred in only 6% of patients [3]. Notably, continued midodrine use—particularly when initiated for orthostatic hypotension—was associated with a lower observed risk of mortality compared with patients who discontinued midodrine or received it for other causes of hypotension, although these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the retrospective, non-randomized design [3]. Collectively, these data provide reassurance regarding the cardiovascular safety of midodrine in a high-risk oncology population and support further investigation of its role as a hemodynamic adjunct to facilitate cardioprotective therapy.

Accordingly, we present two cases of HER2-positive breast cancer patients with CTRCD and symptomatic hypotension in whom midodrine enabled beneficial GDMT and continuation of life-prolonging oncologic therapy (Table 1). These cases illustrate a pragmatic cardio-oncology approach in which hemodynamic stabilization with midodrine enabled cardioprotective therapy and preservation of life-prolonging oncologic treatment, consistent with principles of close multidisciplinary collaboration in high-need patients. Case presentation

Case 1

A 47-year-old woman diagnosed with ER-positive, PR-positive, HER2-negative right breast cancer with three positive axillary nodes underwent right mastectomy followed by adjuvant doxorubicin (240 mg/m²), paclitaxel, radiation, and tamoxifen. No prior history of cardiovascular risk factors or disease. She later developed HER2-positive metastatic leptomeningeal carcinomatosis, which responded to intrathecal methotrexate and whole-brain radiotherapy, achieving a brief complete response before progression required capecitabine. With further progression, fam-trastuzumab deruxtecan (fTD) was initiated with baseline LVEF 60%.

After 8 cycles, LVEF declined to 30%, prompting interruption. Systolic blood pressure at that point was 100–110 mmHg and heart rate 80s bpm. Metoprolol succinate 12.5 mg/d was started and advanced to 25 mg/day. LVEF recovered to 60% allowing cycle 9 fTD therapy after a 10 week pause. Progressive hypotension to the 70-mmHg systolic range despite reduction of metoprolol to 12.5 mg/d which led to its discontinuation after cycle 12, while fTD was continued. After cycle 30, LVEF was 55%. Following cycle 33, treatment intervals were extended to every 6 weeks due to fatigue and hypotension; LVEF had fallen to 46% with systolic BPs 77–90 mmHg.

The cardio-oncology team initiated midodrine 2.5 mg BID–TID and metoprolol succinate 12.5 mg daily while continuing fTD. Midodrine was titrated to 7.5 mg BID and metoprolol to 25 mg daily, improving BP to 110–120/60–80 mmHg with symptom improvement. After cycle 38 of uninterrupted fTD, LVEF improved to 54%. The patient’s quality of life and hope for sustained successful treatment improved sufficiently for her to rescind her DNR status. This outcome underscores the clinical imperative driving the stepwise management approach in this case: the primary goal was recovery of LVEF sufficient to permit continuation of fTD, not maximization of guideline-directed heart failure therapy. Given the patient’s extreme fragility, persistent neurologic dysfunction from leptomeningeal disease, prior DNR status, and proximity to hospice transition, beta-blocker monotherapy with low-dose metoprolol succinate was judged the most appropriate and acceptable initial cardioprotective strategy. The decision not to initiate sacubitril/valsartan or an SGLT2 inhibitor at that time reflected both the hemodynamic risks of additional agents and the explicit preferences of the patient and oncology team. With midodrine providing hemodynamic support, metoprolol could be reintroduced and titrated, enabling the LVEF recovery that allowed fTD to continue uninterrupted. After cycle 40, LVEF reached 57% on midodrine 7.5 mg BID and metoprolol 25 mg/d, with systolic BP 91–103 mmHg over the preceding 6 weeks.

Case 2

A 55-year-old woman diagnosed with ER-negative, PR-negative, HER2-positive left-sided invasive ductal carcinoma (stage IIA, T2N0M0) received six cycles of neoadjuvant dose-dense doxorubicin and cyclophosphamide (ddAC) (240 mg/m² total doxorubicin dose) followed by four of the planned six cycles of paclitaxel with trastuzumab. Six months later, bilateral mastectomy revealed residual invasive carcinoma, and she was placed on adjuvant trastuzumab. Another six months later, she developed neurologic symptoms; brain MRI showed a solitary metastasis. Craniotomy confirmed metastatic breast carcinoma (ER–, PR–, HER2 3+), treated with whole-brain radiotherapy and resumption of trastuzumab every 3 weeks with palliative intent.

She remained clinically stable on trastuzumab with echocardiographic surveillance every 6 months. She did not have any prior history of cardiovascular risk factors or disease. After approximately 10 years, LVEF declined to 42% (prior 51%). She reported recurrent syncope, hypotension, and exertional dyspnea. Vital signs showed BP 90s/60s mmHg. ECG demonstrated normal sinus rhythm; echocardiography showed normal filling pressures; treadmill nuclear stress testing revealed normal perfusion.

Due to low BP, fragility as well as patient’s and oncologist’s preferences, the treating team elected to use a single cardioprotective agent with the most favorable hemodynamic profile. Empagliflozin was selected for its relatively lower effect on blood pressure compared to beta-blockers or RAAS inhibitors; however, given that the patient’s baseline systolic pressures were consistently at or below 90 mmHg, which was below the exclusion threshold used in landmark EMPEROR-Reduced trial eligibility criteria (symptomatic hypotension or SBP < 100 mmHg), midodrine was initiated first to establish hemodynamic stability before empagliflozin was added. Midodrine was initiated and titrated to 7.5 mg TID, along with empagliflozin at 10 mg daily. Trastuzumab was temporarily held for 7 months. BP stabilized to 100–110/60–70 mmHg without supine hypertension. LVEF improved to 49% at 6 months and 51% at 9 months. Trastuzumab was successfully restarted and for the last 9 months, she remains clinically stable on midodrine and empagliflozin with preserved systolic function (LVEF 51–53%), continued HER2-targeted therapy, and no evidence of cancer progression.

Discussion

These cases illustrate the use of midodrine as a hemodynamic adjunct to facilitate cardioprotective therapy in hypotensive patients with HER2-therapy–related cardiomyopathy. The sole aim of midodrine in both cases was to stabilize blood pressure sufficiently to permit initiation or continuation of guideline-directed cardioprotective agents with subsequent LVEF recovery observed likely attributed to those agents—beta-blocker and/or SGLT2 inhibitor therapy. HER2-targeted agents, particularly trastuzumab and antibody–drug conjugates, are associated with potentially reversible myocardial dysfunction, and recovery of left ventricular function is often the critical determinant enabling continuation of oncologic therapy. However, the GDMT drug classes most effective for promoting recovery—β-blockers, RAAS inhibitors, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, and SGLT2 inhibitors—frequently induce hypotension, creating a therapeutic impasse in patients with preexisting or treatment-related low blood pressure.

In clinical practice, this hemodynamic limitation often forces clinicians to use subtherapeutic doses of cardioprotective medications or to discontinue cancer therapy altogether, decisions that may adversely affect both cardiac recovery and cancer outcomes. In the presented cases, midodrine improved systemic BP sufficiently to permit GDMT and support LVEF recovery while allowing continuation or reinitiation of life-prolonging HER2-targeted therapy. This approach reflects the principles of close multidisciplinary cardio-oncologic collaboration, in which hemodynamic barriers to cardioprotective therapy are addressed proactively to maintain access to life-prolonging oncologic treatment. Midodrine’s use as a hemodynamic bridge is consistent with the broader concept of permissive cardiotoxicity [4], which acknowledges that temporary cardiac risk may be accepted in selected high-need patients under close multidisciplinary monitoring when there is no acceptable alternative treatment path.

The two cases illustrate how midodrine can be integrated into distinct institutional approaches tailored to individual patient profiles and clinical histories. In Case 1, the patient had previously achieved LVEF recovery on metoprolol succinate but was unable to continue it due to symptomatic hypotension; midodrine was therefore used to restore the hemodynamic conditions under which beta-blocker therapy could be reintroduced and titrated. In Case 2, the patient had not previously received beta-blocker therapy, and the treating team elected to use a single cardioprotective agent with the most favorable hemodynamic profile. Empagliflozin was selected, and even though it has relatively lower effect on blood pressure compared to beta-blockers or RAAS inhibitors, the patient’s baseline systolic pressures were still below the exclusion threshold used in the landmark EMPEROR-Reduced trial (symptomatic hypotension or SBP < 100 mmHg at screening). Therefore, midodrine was initiated first to establish hemodynamic stability before empagliflozin was added (Figure). Together, these cases demonstrate that the specific cardioprotective strategy can be individualized based on prior treatment history, patient preference, and institutional experience, while midodrine serves a consistent role as the hemodynamic enabler in both.

Among available pharmacologic options for hypotension, midodrine has several properties that make it particularly suitable for this application. Fludrocortisone increases blood pressure primarily through sodium and water retention and may exacerbate volume overload or heart failure, while droxidopa, a norepinephrine precursor, increases sympathetic tone and may carry arrhythmogenic risk. In contrast, midodrine selectively activates peripheral α1-adrenergic receptors, increasing vascular tone without increasing heart rate or myocardial oxygen demand and without interfering with β-blocker–mediated cardioprotection. This pharmacologic profile allows midodrine to serve as a hemodynamic “bridge,” stabilizing blood pressure so that disease-modifying heart failure therapies can be safely administered.

From a safety perspective, preclinical data suggest that α1-adrenergic signaling is not detrimental to the myocardium and may support cardiomyocyte survival under stress. Experimental studies demonstrate that α1-AR activation can promote adaptive hypertrophic responses [1, 2], whereas α1-AR blockade has been associated with increased heart failure risk, as observed in the doxazosin arm of the ALLHAT trial [5]. These observations support the biological safety of midodrine in the setting of cardiomyopathy, including CTRCD, and provide reassurance that peripheral α1-AR agonism is unlikely to antagonize the cardioprotective mechanisms of beta-blocker therapy. These preclinical findings are hypothesis-generating and have not been validated in clinical studies of midodrine; they are cited here to support safety, not to attribute direct cardioprotective efficacy to midodrine.

Our prior observational experience in cancer patients with heart failure further supports the safety and tolerability of midodrine, demonstrating no signal for worsening cardiac function and a low incidence of supine hypertension [3]. Importantly, that study also identified a favorable survival trend among patients who were able to continue midodrine therapy, with early separation of mortality curves and a lower observed risk of death in univariate analyses. While these findings cannot be interpreted as evidence of a survival benefit due to the inherent limitations of retrospective analyses and potential confounding, they are nonetheless reassuring in that no signal of increased mortality was observed. Extending these findings to patients with HER2-positive breast cancer and CTRCD suggests the feasibility of midodrine as a hemodynamic adjunct in a vulnerable population for whom hypotension frequently limits access to cardioprotective therapy. These observations are anecdotal and do not establish the efficacy or safety of midodrine; they are reported to raise awareness of a potential management strategy for experienced cardio-oncologists encountering this specific clinical triad when no other viable options exist. The feasibility of beta-blocker monotherapy for LVEF recovery in non-ischemic cardiomyopathy is well-established. Groote et al. demonstrated EF improvement from approximately 30% to 40% over three months in stable heart failure patients on background ACE/ARB therapy, with greater recovery in non-ischemic etiologies [6]. CTRCD, a form of non-ischemic cardiomyopathy, shares this biology of reversibility, supporting the rationale for stepwise beta-blocker initiation as in Case 1. The broader evidence on midodrine in non-cancer heart failure is limited and conflicting. Three retrospective observational studies published in 2024 reported divergent results: Scoma et al. found midodrine at discharge was associated with greater GDMT initiation or up-titration, though without a survival benefit and with more frequent heart failure rehospitalizations [7]; while Wu et al. in a larger propensity-matched cohort reported associations with increased risks of respiratory failure, ICU admissions, hospitalizations, and mortality, with a paradoxical reduction in emergency room visits [8]. A 2025 systematic review concluded that high-quality evidence supporting midodrine in heart failure remains insufficient [9]. All three studies carry significant methodological limitations inherent to retrospective designs. Critically, none enrolled cancer patients, who differ substantially from the general heart failure population in the cause and clinical context of hypotension. Several limitations of this report merit explicit acknowledgment. First, the small sample size precludes conclusions about efficacy or safety of midodrine in this setting. Second, concomitant use of cardioprotective agents makes it impossible to attribute LVEF recovery to any single intervention; midodrine’s contribution was hemodynamic facilitation, not direct cardioprotection. Third, the absence of comprehensive cardiac biomarkers (GLS, troponin, natriuretic peptides) reflects the deliberate, pragmatic approach of the treating teams: in patients with advanced metastatic breast cancer approaching critical oncologic decision points, the clinical priority was recovery of LVEF sufficient to continue anti-HER2 therapy, and additional testing was judged unlikely to alter the core management strategy. Fourth, the two cases represent different institutions with different clinical approaches, limiting direct comparability. These cases are best interpreted as hypothesis-generating observations warranting prospective investigation with systematic assessment and predefined endpoints.

Conclusion

Hypotension frequently limits access to GDMT in patients with HER2-targeted therapy–related LV dysfunction. These cases illustrate the potential feasibility of midodrine as a hemodynamic adjunct to enable cardioprotective therapy in selected patients with this clinical triad. They do not establish efficacy or safety, and prospective studies with systematic cardiac phenotyping and predefined endpoints are needed to define the role of midodrine in this setting.
