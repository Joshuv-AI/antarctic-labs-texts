Recurrent Case of Microcystic Adnexal Carcinoma Arising From the External Auditory Canal: A Case Report
Authors: Amna Enam, Hira Zaidi, Maryam Tanveer, Iqbal A. Muhammad Khyani, Sakarie Mustafe Hidig
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMicrocystic adnexal carcinoma is a local, slow‐growing, aggressive, and cutaneous neoplasm which is extremely rare in the external auditory canal. Although benign, it invades locally and recurs frequently. Complete surgical excision and long‐term follow‐up are necessary. Misdiagnosis can be prevented with a deep biopsy. Due to possible hearing issues, radiotherapy should be eschewed.

Introduction

Micorcyctic adnexal carcinoma (MAC) was described by Goldstein et al. [1] as a relatively uncommon cutaneous neoplasm. This locally aggressive malignant adnexal tumor has high potential for local recurrence. Although this tumor looks benign, it is notorious for aggressive local invasion and frequent recurrence even after proper treatment. Head and neck areas are commonly affected, and the upper lip is the most common site [1, 2], but microcystic adnexal neoplasm (MAC) originating solely from the external auditory canal (EAC) is relatively rare.

Tumors in the EAC can affect hearing, balance, facial movement, and dental occlusal function due to their proximity to important surrounding organs. In this article, we present a case of a MAC that originated in the EAC. This case has been written according to the CARE guidelines for clinical case reporting.

Case Presentation

History and Examination

A 38‐year‐old male from Saudi Arabia, an electrician by profession, came to the outpatient department of otorhinolaryngology with a presenting complaint of a growth in the left ear for the last 3 years, as shown in Figure 1. This similar type of growth also started growing behind the left ear 2 years ago, as shown in Figure 2. According to the patient, the growth was initially small, which progressed with time and obliterated the auditory canal, leading to decreased hearing and also causing earache. The patient didn't have any associated symptoms of bleeding, vertigo, tinnitus, or facial weakness.

Upon further inquiry, the patient said that he had a similar growth in the left ear 1 year before, which was excised via surgery, but unfortunately, the patient didn't know the diagnosis. He was addicted to Naswar and cigarettes, with the habit of smoking roughly 15 cigarettes per day. The patient didn't mention the use of any drugs. On physical examination, a warty, non‐tender growth that didn't bleed on probe test was seen obliterating the external auditory canal. The pinna was normal, while the tympanic membrane couldn't be seen on examination. Another non‐tender, firm, irregularly margined growth approximately 4 × 3 cm in size was observed on the tip of the mastoid behind the same ear. To examine the hearing, Rennie's and Weber's tests were done. Rennie's test was found positive for the right ear and negative for the left ear. In Weber's test, hearing was lateralized towards the left ear. On further examination, it was found that all cranial nerves were intact and no palpable lymph nodes were present.

Investigations and Treatment

To rule out malignancy, a punch biopsy was done, which showed moderate dysplastic squamous epithelium with no convincing invasion. A repeat biopsy showed superficial fragments of squamous and basaloid cell proliferation suggestive of seborrheic keratosis. An HRCT of the temporal bone showed a growth obliterating the external auditory canal lateral to the tympanic membrane, as shown in Figure 3. Both the tympanic membranes and the ear ossicles were preserved.

Temporal bone CT revealed a mass in the cartilaginous portion of the external auditory canal without obvious invasion into the bony portion.

Surgical incision was performed under general anesthesia, combining both periaural and post‐auricular approaches, as shown in Figure 4. Due to the complex anatomy of the auditory canal and febrile nature of the tumor, it was resected in multiple bits and pieces, as can be seen in Figure 5. After surgery, with the patient's consent, the excised mass was sent for histopathological evaluation, which revealed that it was a microcystic adnexal Carcinoma (MAC). Conclusion and Follow‐Up

No postoperative complications were observed. The EAC underwent normal epithelialization, and there were no signs of local recurrence. Radiotherapy was not considered due to the possible risk of hearing loss, and not enough data are available to prove its effectiveness. Long‐term follow‐up of such cases is required as they are highly recurrent.

Discussion

MAC was first defined in 1982 by Goldstein. Only 6 cases of MAC with involvement of the EAC have been reported worldwide; one is secondary to carcinoma of the nasopharynx [3, 4].

MAC, a slowly growing tumor, is first seen as a flesh‐colored to yellow asymptomatic mass, invading the local structures and deep tissues in its course, but lymph node metastasis has not been reported. Its poorly defined margins and potential to invade local perineural structures with a high risk of recurrence demand a microscopic surgical excision rather than a standard surgical excision [5]. Wide resection and Mohs microsurgery are commonly used, and radiotherapy is used as an adjuvant therapy.

MAC most commonly occurs in the thirties to seventies, but the age range is much wider. Its occurrence has also been reported in a patient in the nineties. The youngest reported was a 10‐year‐old girl with MAC on her arm, which has been present there for the past 6 years. The risk of incidence is equal among men and women [6].

Histologically, it is seen as the strands and nests of small and medium‐sized keratinocytes with highly eosinophilic cytoplasm, depending upon the level of keratinization. The basaloid cells are present near the periphery. It has also been seen that large clusters and nests of squamous cells are confined more superficially, while small cell clusters, cysts, nests, and strands are seen more deeply [2].

Chi et al. [7] reported a case of MAC in the EAC of a 46‐year‐old female with no history of smoking and exposure to radiation. She had a similar mass 2 years back, which she excised herself.

A case of MAC in the EAC of a 49‐year‐old female with invasion of the parotid duct and facial nerve has been reported, which was treated with surgical excision and radiotherapy [8]. Another case of MAC in a 22‐year‐old male with no radiation history in the external auditory canal reaching the middle ear was reported [9].
