Biomarkers of Injury Severity in Shoulder Instability and Posttraumatic Osteoarthritis
Authors: Patrick W. Rooney, Reilly Corken, Mark D. Wishman, Cory Call, Maria Bozoghlian, Carter Lane, Mitchell C. Coleman, James V. Nepola, Brendan M. Patterson, Joseph W. Galvin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract» There is a poor understanding of the significance of biomarkers in the blood, synovial fluid, and tissue in patients with shoulder instability and posttraumatic osteoarthritis (PTOA).» Identification of biomarkers associated with injury severity in shoulder instability may provide prognostic information to help guide surgical treatment algorithms.» Current evidence suggests that the biomarkers of periostin, tumor necrosis factor-alpha, IL1B, and cartilage oligomeric matrix protein are associated with injury severity in patients with recurrent anterior shoulder instability.» There is limited information on genetic, transcriptomic, and protein biomarkers which may indicate individuals who are at more risk for development of shoulder instability.» An improved understanding of the local biomolecular tissue response to first-time and recurrent anterior shoulder instability may contribute to the development of novel therapeutic strategies for gene and protein targets to halt the early cascade of PTOA.

Introduction

Shoulder labral tear with anterior instability is a common diagnosis with an incidence in the US general population of 0.08 per thousand-person years1. The burden of disease is even higher in contact athletic populations and the military2,3. Traumatic injury to the static and dynamic stabilizers results in shoulder instability and leads to subluxation, dislocation, and apprehension with associated pain.

Delayed or nonoperative management risks chronic labral tears, attritional bone loss of the glenoid, and/or humerus and can be associated with worse outcomes and development of early posttraumatic osteoarthritis (PTOA)4-9. Recurrent instability is a known risk factor for the development of attritional bone loss and PTOA. This highlights the importance of improving understanding of the biologic changes in the glenohumeral joint following first-time and repeat shoulder dislocation events.

Biomarkers have emerged as valuable tools for understanding the pathophysiology of shoulder pathology, particularly in rotator cuff disease. The genome-wide association study by Tashjian et al. identified significant differences in 2 single-nucleotide polymorphisms in patients with full-thickness rotator cuff tears compared with genetically matched population controls, representing one of the initial attempts to integrate genetics into the understanding of shoulder pathology10. Notably, the volume of research on biomarkers related to rotator cuff tears far exceeds that focused on shoulder instability and glenohumeral osteoarthritis11-13.

The purpose of this review was to summarize the current state of biomarkers in patients with anterior shoulder instability and PTOA.

Biomarkers Associated with Risk of Instability

While structural causes of symptomatic recurrent shoulder instability are well described, the role of biomolecular factors and an understanding of the composition and variations in the shoulder capsule and its laxity remain poorly defined. Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (EDS) is a well described connective tissue disorder related to defects in collagen formation14. Patients with EDS demonstrate systemic laxity affecting several joints including the shoulder. These patients are at an increased risk for recurrent dislocations and reoperation following surgical management of instability15. Recent proteomic research in patients with hypermobile EDS found significant reductions in C1QA, C3, C8A, C8B, and C9. Cytokine profiling revealed alterations in immune cell mediators in patients with EDS, supporting a model of dysregulated inflammatory response16.

Relaxin protein circulation in the peripheral blood has been identified as significantly associated with patients who develop shoulder instability. In a prospective case-control analysis, Owens et al. identified that preinjury serum relaxin concentrations were associated with risk of subsequent shoulder instability in young athletes. Subjects were 2.18 times more likely to have acute shoulder instability during the follow-up period for every 1 pg/mL increase in serum relaxin concentration at baseline17. Relaxin is a hormone that affects collagen cross-linking and tissue compliance18. Relaxin may play a role in shoulder instability by compromising the static stabilizers of the glenohumeral joint and predispose patients to further instability. These findings align with previous studies implicating hormonal influences in joint laxity and support the consideration of sex-specific biological factors in assessing instability risk19.

These biomarkers represent targets of hyperlaxity that have the potential to be linked to a quantitative laxity index in the evaluation of first-time dislocators.

Biomarkers of Anterior Shoulder Instability Disease Severity

A number of studies have investigated biomarkers related to the injury severity in anterior shoulder instability. These investigations identify biomarkers in the serum, synovial fluid, and tissues of subjects undergoing arthroscopic and open stabilization procedures. Common pathology associated with recurrent instability events include soft tissue Bankart lesions, Hill-Sachs lesions, and attritional glenoid bone loss (GBL)20. This section will review the studies which have examined the glenohumeral joint microenvironment in patients following anterior shoulder instability events.

Tissue/Capsular Biomarkers

Few studies have examined tissue-specific biomarkers in recurrent shoulder instability and PTOA. Belangero et al. evaluated the glenohumeral capsule of 18 patients with traumatic anterior shoulder instability. They found that the expression of COL5A1 was lower in the anteroinferior region than in the macroscopically unaffected anterosuperior region of the glenohumeral capsule, while no difference was found in in the remaining expression of collagen genes21. Type V collagen accounts for 2% to 5% of the total amount of collagen in most tissues. This type of collagen intercalates with type I collagen, which is the predominant protein in the capsule, to form heterotypic fibrils22. In EDS cases, decreased COL5A1 expression has been described in approximately 25 to 30% of the patients with mutations of this gene. In these patients, formation of abnormal collagen fibrils that are wide and irregular has been observed23. COL5A1 being downregulated in the anteroinferior capsule of anterior shoulder instability patients is interesting as it is important for the integrity and structure of larger collagen fibrils and is also downregulated in patients with EDS.

Other studies have identified that capsular biopsy specimens in patients with recurrent anterior shoulder instability demonstrate evidence of synovial inflammation. Gotoh et al. found that in patients with shoulder instability, mRNA levels of IL-1β are higher in the synovium of the glenohumeral joint compared with the subacromial bursa24. Similarly, Muneshige et al. found that patients with recurrent dislocations undergoing arthroscopic Bankart repair demonstrated higher synovial mRNA levels of tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), IL1B, pan-macrophage (CD68), and M1 macrophage (CD80) compared with those undergoing arthroscopic rotator cuff repair25. IL-1β and TNF-α mediate cartilage catabolism by inducing MMPs and ADAMTS in the synovium of patients with osteoarthritis26-28. These findings suggest that increased levels of M1 macrophages in the glenohumeral capsule may lead to increasing inflammatory cytokine production and a chronic cycle of inflammation in the joint following recurrent instability.

The capsule in instability patients may play a key role in an acute and chronic inflammatory response leading to PTOA. However, the glenoid and humeral head cartilage are also important tissues. Aleem et al. examined the articular cartilage from the anteroinferior glenoid of patients undergoing shoulder stabilization surgery and total shoulder arthroplasty. Digital quantitative PCR was used to assess the relative expression of 57 genes (36 genes from OA risk allele studies, 21 genes from differential expression studies) in acute instability, chronic instability, and osteoarthritis. The authors found that cartilage in the OA group displayed higher expression of CCL3, CHST11, GPR22, PRKAR2B, and PTGS2 than cartilage in the group with acute or chronic instability. They concluded that glenoid cartilage has an inflammatory and catabolic phenotype in shoulders with OA but an anabolic phenotype in shoulders with instability29. It is important to improve our understanding of the “cross-talk” between the inflammatory state of the glenohumeral capsule and the glenoid articular cartilage in patients with shoulder acute and chronic shoulder instability.

Synovial Fluid Biomarkers—Periostin

Not only is it important to understand capsular biomarkers in shoulder instability patients, it is also key to investigate the synovial fluid. Shedding of proteins in the synovial fluid following shoulder instability events may help identify key biomarkers following injury.

Recently, the proteome of the synovial fluid in young patients with anterior shoulder instability was evaluated using label-free quantitative tandem mass spectrometry. Periostin (POSTN), transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGF-b1), and type VI collagen (COL6A3) were significantly elevated in anterior shoulder instability patients. POSTN was present in anterior shoulder instability patients and not present in any of the posterior instability patients. It had a log-fold change of 4.7 (adjusted p value = 0.03). POSTN is a proinflammatory catabolic protein whose abundance in synovial fluid moderately correlated (r = 0.7) with the severity of joint injury and the size of the Hill Sachs impaction fracture on the posterolateral humeral head30 (Table I).

In the setting of anterior cruciate ligament rupture, early expression of periostin has been implicated as a proinflammatory precursor leading to PTOA31,32. This finding has also been demonstrated in vitro and in vivo29. Therefore, POSTN is a relevant protein target that is present in the synovial fluid and highly expressed in the glenoid articular cartilage of patients with recurrent anterior shoulder instability.

The number of dislocation events leads to changes in the synovial microenvironment. A study by Jacobs et al. demonstrated patients with multiple prior shoulder dislocation events exhibit elevated levels of inflammatory biomarkers TNF-α, IL-2, IL-4, IL-8, and IL-13 at the time of surgical stabilization compared with those with a single dislocation event33. These findings suggest that repeated dislocation events drive heightened inflammatory and tissue remodeling response.

Serum Biomarkers Associated with Glenoid and Humeral Head Bone Loss

Several studies have investigated whether serum biomarkers are significantly associated with injury severity in anterior shoulder instability (Fig. 1). Yu et al. determined whether preoperative biomarkers of cartilage turnover and inflammation are associated with specific shoulder lesions in shoulder instability. Thirty-three patients undergoing surgical treatment for shoulder instability were assessed for the presence or absence of associated shoulder lesions. Biomarkers including cartilage oligomeric matrix protein (COMP), C-reactive protein (HS-CRP), interleukin-8 (IL-8), and macrophage inflammatory protein-1β (MIP-1b) were collected at the time of surgery. The authors reported that patients with Hill-Sachs lesions had a 31% increase in COMP plasma levels (p = 0.046)34. At the cellular level, COMP stabilizes type II collagen molecules and is released upon the breakdown of cartilage35.

A recent study sought to identify clinically relevant peripheral blood biomarkers in shoulder instability patients. The authors hypothesized that the severity of GBL would be associated with the expression level of genes in the blood and capsular tissue of patients with anterior shoulder instability. Blood and anterior capsular tissue specimens obtained during surgery were compared between patients with significant GBL (≥10%) and patients without significant GBL (<10%). There were 9 genes that were identified as significantly differentially expressed in the blood, and 5 of these (IFIT1, IFIT3, IFI44, PRKCB, and OAS2) were confirmed using nonparametric tests. The authors developed a model using the 5 genes to predict the severity of GBL, and this had an accuracy of 88%. There was a higher expression level of the gene PRKCB (p value = 1 × 10−4) in the peripheral blood of subjects with ≥10% GBL compared with subjects with <10% GBL. The protein kinase C β (PRKCB) gene encodes the PKCβ enzyme, a member of the PKC family of serine/threonine kinases36. This gene is overexpressed in Ewing sarcoma. Surdez et al. found that the transcriptional activation of PRKCB was directly regulated by the chimeric fusion oncogene EWSR1-FLI1, which drives the growth of the malignant bone tumor37. These findings suggest that increased expression of PKCβ is associated with bone turnover and may represent a serum marker for GBL in shoulder instability (Appendix A).

Biomarkers of Glenohumeral Osteoarthritis and PTOA

The development of PTOA of the glenohumeral joint is a long-term consequence of untreated shoulder instability events8,9. Therefore, understanding biomarkers associated with the development of glenohumeral osteoarthritis could identify patients at risk of PTOA and drive clinical decision-making.

Casagrande et al. evaluated humeral head cartilage in 26 patients with glenohumeral arthritis using RT-qPCR and found patients with later-stage glenohumeral arthritis had differentially expressed, increased transcript levels for multiple proteins including connexin 43 (p = 0.03)38. The finding of connexin 43 upregulation is of interest as it is the most abundant connexin in musculoskeletal tissue. These proteins form hexameric hemichannels and gap junctions along the cell membrane allowing for transfer of solutes between cells and are responsive to mechanosensory stimuli. In pathologic states, it is hypothesized these gap junctions propagate proinflammatory mediators and cell death signaling molecules potentially leading to death of chondrocytes and resulting in the development of glenohumeral osteoarthritis39.

An additional study by Wanner et al. evaluated synovial fluid biomarkers via mass spectrometry and cytokine analysis in 16 patients with and without glenohumeral arthritis. They found 31 and 38 proteins were differentially expressed in the patients with early-stage and late-stage OA, respectively. In addition, cytokine analysis showed proinflammatory cytokines IL-6, IL-8, and IL-18 were all significantly elevated in the patients with early-stage and late-stage OA compared with nonarthritic controls40. These findings show potential biomarkers for monitoring progression of disease as well as a potential target for selective blockade of proinflammatory cytokines.

Of course, the development of serum-based biomarkers for identifying those at risk of developing PTOA is often the end goal of biomarker science due to ease of accessibility and serial sampling. Cartilage turnover biomarkers may offer additional insight into the biological activity associated with glenohumeral instability, particularly in young patients with minimal radiographic joint damage. In a study by Owens et al., serum levels of type II collagen cleavage product (C2C), a marker of cartilage degradation, and procollagen II C-propeptide, a marker of cartilage synthesis, were measured in patients with a history of anterior shoulder instability and compared with healthy controls. The study found that C2C concentrations were significantly elevated in the instability cohort, suggesting increased cartilage catabolism in response to repetitive subluxation or dislocation events41. These findings suggest that biochemical changes in cartilage metabolism may precede radiographic osteoarthritic changes.

Summary

The identification of biomarkers such as relaxin, periostin, COMP, and inflammatory biomarkers, among others, reflects a growing appreciation for the biological complexity of the glenohumeral joint in patients with shoulder instability. There is a poor understanding of the normal injury response and the aberrant response following instability. Findings of lower expression of collagen genes in capsular tissue, such as COL5A1, along with high levels of mRNA of TNF-α, IL1B, CD 68, and CD 80 in the synovial fluid of patients with instability are a promising research avenue to further investigate biomarkers present in the joint environment in patients with this pathology. Furthermore, elevated levels of periostin and TGFβ-igh3 found in patients with instability and Hills-Sachs lesions or GBL suggest a role of these biomarkers in patients with recurrent shoulder instability. Serum biomarkers have also been investigated in patients with Hills-Sachs lesions and GBL, finding higher serum levels of COMP, HS-CRP, IL-8, as well as higher expression of PRKCB. These molecules may serve not only as diagnostic or prognostic tools but also as indicators of ongoing joint degeneration or therapeutic response. Relaxin and biomarkers of EDS represent targets of hyperlaxity that have the potential to be linked to a quantitative laxity index in the evaluation of first-time dislocators and those with recurrent instability. Further research is needed to validate these biomarkers in larger, longitudinal cohorts and to explore their utility in guiding surgical decision-making, particularly in borderline cases.
