Autoantibodies directed against the angiotensin II type 1 receptor and the endothelin-1 type A receptor in patients with systemic sclerosis
Authors: Katherine E van der Wouden, Saad Ahmed, Wieke M van Oostveen, Eva M Hoekstra, Sophie I E Liem, Tom W J Huizinga, René E M Toes, Alexandre E Voskuyl, Gabriela Riemekasten, Cynthia M Fehres, Madelon Vonk, Jeska K de Vries-Bouwstra
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate the clinical applicability of autoantibodies (AAbs) measured by ELISA against the angiotensin II type 1 receptor (AT1R) and endothelin-1 type A receptor (ETAR) in systemic sclerosis (SSc) patients.MethodsSerum samples from n=279 SSc patients from the Leiden Systemic Sclerosis cohort, n=42 patients with primary Raynaud’s phenomenon, n=24 patients with rheumatoid arthritis and n=20 healthy controls were tested for anti-AT1R- and anti-ETAR AAbs. Levels were compared between groups with Mann-Whitney U tests or Kruskal-Wallis tests. Risk ratios and Kaplan-Meier analyses were used to determine associations between AAbs and disease manifestations or all-cause mortality. Analyses were repeated in an independent cohort with n=310 SSc patients from the Radboud University Medical Center.ResultsAAbs against AT1R and ETAR could be detected by ELISA in the sera of all groups tested. Levels were slightly higher in the SSc group compared with the pooled non-SSc group (p=0.043). No associations could be found between anti-AT1R AAbs or anti-ETAR AAbs and disease manifestations or all-cause mortality. In the Radboud cohort, patients with diffuse cutaneous SSc (p=0.001) and interstitial lung disease (p=0.007) had higher median anti-ETAR AAb levels. Patients who died during follow-up had lower levels of anti-AT1R- (p=0.005) and anti-ETAR AAbs (p=0.020).ConclusionsWe confirm positive ELISAs for anti-AT1R AAbs and anti-ETAR AAbs in the sera of several patient groups and healthy controls. Previously described associations with disease manifestations and all-cause mortality could not be confirmed in our cohorts. Based on the current study, the determination of these AAbs is of limited predictive value in clinical practice.

Introduction

Systemic sclerosis (SSc) is a rare rheumatic disease, characterised by autoimmunity, microvascular damage and fibrosis of the skin and internal organs.1 2 The exact mechanism driving the disease remains insufficiently understood, but vasculopathy, reflected by endothelial cell damage, plays a major role in the pathogenesis of SSc.3

Clinically, this vasculopathy can present itself by the presence of Raynaud’s phenomenon (RP), which is a hallmark symptom and often the first sign of disease, preceding other manifestations and occurring in approximately 90% of all SSc patients.1 3 Other vascular complications, such as pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) and scleroderma renal crisis (SRC), have an important impact on survival.4 Despite its rarity, SSc has the highest mortality rate of all rheumatic diseases.2 However, since the introduction of ACE inhibitors, the survival of SSc patients with SRC has increased dramatically.5 Furthermore, bosentan, an oral endothelin receptor antagonist (ERA), has been shown to prevent recurrent digital ulcers (DUs), reduce the proportion of patients with multiple DU and improve the clinical status and survival of patients with PAH in SSc.6 7 These observations indicate that the pathways involving angiotensin II and endothelin-1 are relevant clinical targets in SSc.

In 2011, the presence of autoantibodies (AAbs) against the angiotensin II type 1 receptor (AT1R) and endothelin-1 type A receptor (ETAR) in the sera of SSc patients was reported.8 This study stated that high levels of these AAbs were associated with more severe disease manifestations and predicted mortality related to SSc measured in three different SSc cohorts comprising 478 SSc patients and 683 controls. Additionally, an activating effect of SSc-IgG in an AT1R-dependent and ETAR-dependent manner was described. In recent years, several studies have been published using the same ELISA-based detection technology describing the presence of these vascular AAbs in association with SSc severity and SSc-related manifestations.913 Together with the finding that AT1R and ETAR are present on cells comprising blood vessels, such as endothelial cells, pericytes and smooth muscle cells,14 15 these data could imply that the activation of these receptors by receptor-specific AAbs could play a part in the pathogenesis of SSc. This might indicate a direct link between autoimmunity and vasculopathy in SSc. However, recently two studies failed to find an association between anti-AT1R AAbs or anti-ETAR AAbs and clinical manifestations in SSc.16 17

Given the conflicting literature, together with the important implications of unravelling a key player in the pathophysiology of SSc, the potential of these AAbs serving as a biomarker in daily practice, and the relatively low costs of the drugs needed to block these receptors, we sought to replicate the presence and levels of anti-AT1R AAbs and anti-ETAR AAbs and their clinical associations in two well characterised, longitudinal SSc cohorts.

Methods

Patients from the Leiden University Medical Center

SSc patients from the Leiden University Medical Center (LUMC) were retrieved from the prospective, observational cohort study (the Leiden Combined Care in Systemic Sclerosis (CCISS) cohort) between 1 January 2009 and 31 December 2019. Patients with RP, a suspicion of SSc or other connective tissue diseases (CTDs) can be referred to the outpatient clinic of the LUMC, where patients are included in the CCISS cohort and undergo a complete diagnostic work-up by different medical specialists and paramedics, of which the results are systematically recorded in a research database. Subsequently, patients are discussed in a multidisciplinary team meeting, in which a diagnosis is made, and a treatment proposal is done. If patients are diagnosed with SSc or another CTD, patients are followed up in the LUMC annually. Serum samples are obtained at the moment of inclusion (corresponding to the first visit) and annually thereafter.18

The SSc patients included for the current study had a clinical diagnosis of SSc and met the 2013 American College of Rheumatology (ACR)/European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) classification criteria for SSc.19 Preferably, patients who were not taking angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) or ERAs at the time of serum sampling were selected.

Age-matched and sex-matched control serum samples were obtained from patients with primary RP (PRP), patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and healthy controls. Patients with PRP were recruited from the CCISS cohort, as described above. PRP was defined as the presence of RP without abnormalities in nailfold capillaroscopy, the absence of antinuclear antibodies in serum and the absence of other characteristics of very early diagnosis of systemic sclerosis (VEDOSS) or SSc. Serum samples of patients with RA were obtained from the Leiden Early Arthritis Clinic (CME no. B19.008), in which patients with confirmed arthritis with an onset of less than 2 years prior to the visit were included.20 Healthy donor serum was obtained from the Rheumatic Diseases biobank in the LUMC (CME no. B14.011).

Vascular AAbs assay and measurements

All samples were sent to CellTrend GmbH in Luckenwalde, Germany, where the measurements were conducted using a commercially available solid-phase assay (CellTrend, 12000, 12100). The ELISAs were developed to test for the presence of anti-ETAR IgG AAbs or anti-AT1R IgG AAbs. As described previously, 96-well polystyrene microtiter plates were coated with membrane extracts from Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells that overexpress either human AT1R or ETAR, with 1 mM calcium chloride being added to each buffer to maintain receptor conformation. Duplicate samples of a 1:100 serum dilution and standards were incubated at 4°C for 2 hours. Plates were washed, after which the plates were incubated for 1 hour with a 1:20 000 dilution of horseradish peroxidase-labelled goat anti-human IgG (Jackson, West Grove, Pennsylvania, USA).8 The ELISAs for anti-AT1R- and anti-ETAR AAbs were conducted twice in the same sample of 29 patients from the Leiden cohort to determine test–retest reliability.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to describe patient characteristics at time of sampling. Continuous variables were either presented as a mean with SD or median with IQR, according to their distribution. Test–retest reliability was calculated using an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Correlations between anti-AT1R AAb and anti-ETAR AAb levels were determined using a Spearman’s rank order correlation test. Comparisons between two groups were performed with Mann-Whitney U tests, comparisons between several groups were performed with Kruskal-Wallis tests. AAb status was determined using the cut-off values previously set (9.23 units for anti-AT1R AAbs and 10.4 units for anti-ETAR AAbs).8 We also calculated a novel cut-off by using the mean plus two times the SD of the AAb levels of our HCs, to account for possible differences between populations. Associations between AAb status and outcomes were determined by calculating risk ratios (RRs), and for associations between AAb status and mortality, Kaplan-Meier analyses were performed.

To confirm our findings, we performed the same analyses in an independent cohort from the Radboudumc. The Nijmegen Systemic Sclerosis cohort consists of 1258 patients clinically diagnosed with SSc. This database was established in 1989 and is still open for inclusion. All patients were enrolled in the Radboudumc in Nijmegen after informed consent at the first visit after the moment of diagnosis with SSc and followed up at least annually. All patients have provided informed consent for the use of their pseudonymised data and bio samples for (inter-)national research. For the current study, patients fulfilling the 2013 ACR/EULAR classification criteria for SSc (n=310) were included between March 2019 and June 2019, of whom serum samples had been tested for the presence of anti-ETAR IgG AAbs and anti-AT1R IgG AAbs in cooperation with CellTrend. This sample has been described earlier.21 22

Finally, both cohorts were joined, and a multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted to explore if all-cause mortality, the presence of interstitial lung disease (ILD) and the presence of PAH are associated with the level of anti-AT1R AAb and anti-ETAR AAb levels when adjusted for relevant covariates. All statistical analyses were performed in Stata (V.16.1, StataCorp). Figures were made using GraphPad Prism (V.9.3.1). A p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Antibodies against anti-AT1R and anti-ETAR and SSc

The median (IQR) serum unit levels of anti-AT1R AAbs and anti-ETAR AAbs in the SSc patients from the Leiden cohort were 15 (10.5–24.6) and 12.9 (10.0–16.6), respectively.

The ICC between the anti-AT1R AAbs levels of the 29 SSc patients measured twice showed moderate reliability (0.544, 95% CI 0.234 to 0.755, p=0.0008), and relatively weak reliability for anti-ETAR AAbs (0.296, 95% CI −0.066 to –0.592, p=0.0532) (figure 1A,B, respectively).

(C) Correlation between anti-AT1R AAb and anti-ETAR AAb levels. AT1R, angiotensin II type 1 receptor; ETAR: endothelin-1 type A receptor.

A strong correlation between anti-AT1R AAbs and anti-ETAR AAbs was observed (R=0.81, p<0.001) (figure 1C).

Using previously defined cut-offs,8 81% (n=226) of the SSc patients were positive for anti-AT1R AAbs (cut-off: 9.23 units) and 73% (n=203) were positive for anti-ETAR AAbs (cut-off: 10.4 units). For RA patients, 83% (n=20) of the patients were positive for anti-AT1R AAbs and 71% (n=17) were positive for anti-ETAR AAbs. For PRP patients, 81% (n=34) were positive for both anti-AT1R- and anti-ETAR AAbs. In the HCs, similar seropositive percentages were found, as 85% (n=17) were positive for anti-AT1R AAbs and 75% (n=15) were positive for anti-ETAR AAbs.

The levels of anti-AT1R AAbs or anti-ETAR AAbs did not differ significantly between patients with SSc, patients with PRP, patients with RA and the healthy controls (p=0.20 and p=0.81, respectively) (figure 2A,B). When pooling the patients with PRP, RA and the healthy controls into one non-SSc group, and comparing this to the SSc patients, patients with SSc showed significantly higher levels of anti-AT1R AAbs (p=0.043), but not for anti-ETAR AAbs (p=0.348) (figure 2C,D).

The population-based cut-off (mean level in units plus two times the SD of HCs) resulted in a cut-off of 52.29 units for anti-AT1R AAbs and a cut-off of 32.92 units for anti-ETAR AAbs. Using this cut-off, 9% (n=26) of the SSc patients were positive for anti-AT1R AAbs, and 8% (n=21) were positive for anti-ETAR. For RA patients, 4% (n=1) of the patients were positive for anti-AT1R AAbs and anti-ETAR AAbs. For PRP patients, 10% (n=4) were positive for both anti-AT1R- and anti-ETAR AAbs. In the HCs, 10% (n=2) were positive for anti-AT1R AAbs and 5% (n=1) were positive for anti-ETAR AAbs.

Vascular AAb levels above the cut-off and SSc-related adverse outcomes

The original study analysing the possible association between seropositivity and SSc-related disease manifestations used a specific cut-off for each different disease manifestation.8 Using these ‘manifestation specific’ cut-offs for both anti-AT1R- and anti-ETAR AAbs, RRs were determined for the association with these disease manifestations. Unfortunately, the pooled vascular manifestations (SRC, DU and PAH combined) were not included in the study.8 Therefore, we used the lowest cut-off stated previously, as this would allow inclusion of most subjects that would potentially be positive for these AAbs. For anti-AT1R AAbs, this was the cut-off for SRC (15.77 units) and for anti-ETAR AAbs, this was the cut-off for PAH (15.74 units). The RR for the association between anti-AT1R AAbs and the pooled vascular manifestations was 0.67 (95% CI 0.38 to 1.20), and 0.60 (95% CI 0.31 to 1.22) for anti-ETAR AAbs. The RR for the association between anti-AT1R AAbs and ILD was 1.05 (95% CI 0.78 to 1.41) and 0.96 (95% CI 0.68 to 1.38) for anti-ETAR and ILD. The RRs for the association between anti-AT1R AAbs or anti-ETAR AAbs and dcSSc were 1.10 (95% CI 0.74 to 1.58) and 1.14 (95% CI 0.75 to 1.73), respectively. For all other clinical outcomes analysed (all-cause mortality, SRC, DU and PAH), the RRs were lower than one and also not statistically significant (figure 3). The RR for SRC and anti-ETAR AAbs could not be calculated since no patients with an SRC had levels above the cut-off.

Next, we compared the levels of anti-AT1R- and anti-ETAR AAbs between patients with or without one of the defined clinical outcomes. Patients with an SRC in their history had significantly lower anti-AT1R AAb and anti-ETAR AAb levels compared with patients without an SRC (median=9.0 units vs median=15 units, p=0.024 and median=9.5 units vs median=13 units, p=0.012, respectively). Patients with PAH had significantly lower anti-ETAR AAb levels (median=8.4 units) than patients without PAH (median=13 units, p=0.048). Patients who had a vascular manifestation showed significantly lower median AAb levels for anti-AT1R AAbs (median=12 units) and anti-ETAR AAbs (median=11 units) compared with the median anti-AT1R AAb (median=15 units, p=0.034) and anti-ETAR AAb levels (median=13 units, p=0.004) of patients without any vascular manifestations. To control if the use of ARBs or ERAs had an influence on these results, a sensitivity analysis was performed by comparing the levels of anti-AT1R AAbs and anti-ETAR AAbs between patients with and without the respective clinical outcomes, taking only the patients into account not taking these drugs. This analysis did not change the results (data not shown).

Using the cut-offs of 52.29 units for anti-AT1R AAbs and 32.92 units for anti-ETAR AAbs based on the mean plus two times the SD of the AAb levels of our HCs, we analysed the number of SSc patients testing positive for each clinical outcome. Due to small numbers of patients with an event, statistical testing could only be performed for dcSSc and ILD, which showed no significant difference in patients above and below the cut-offs for anti-AT1R- or anti-ETAR AAbs (online supplemental table 1).

Vascular AAb levels and mortality

Next, we analysed the association between both anti-AT1R- and anti-ETAR AAb levels above the general predetermined cut-off8 and all-cause mortality using Kaplan-Meier analyses. Out of all 279 patients followed, 29 patients died during the observation period, of which 2 patients had elevated anti-AT1R AAb levels, 2 patients had elevated anti-ETAR AAb levels and 3 patients had both elevated anti-AT1R AAb and anti-ETAR AAb levels. We found no statistically significant association between anti-AT1R AAb positivity and all-cause mortality (HR 1.76, 95% CI 0.78 to 4.0) or anti-ETAR AAb positivity and all-cause mortality (HR 1.81, 95% CI 0.80 to 4.1) (figure 5).

Reproduction of results in the Nijmegen SSc cohort

To confirm our findings, we set out to replicate our analysis in a second, independent SSc cohort. From the Nijmegen SSc cohort, 310 patients were included. The mean age was 58.8 years, 67% were female, 32% had dcSSc and median disease duration was 10 years. At the time of sample collection, 13% of the patients had PAH, 6% had at least one DU and 3% had an SRC in their history (online supplemental table 2).

In the Nijmegen SSc cohort, the median levels (IQR) for anti-AT1R AAbs and anti-ETAR AAbs were 7.7 units (6.1–10.4) and 6.7 units (4.1–11.6), respectively. In this cohort, 35% (109) of the patients were positive for anti-AT1R AAbs (cut-off: 9.23 units) and 27% (85) were positive for anti-ETAR AAbs (cut-off: 10.4 units).

Again, a strong correlation between anti-AT1R AAb and anti-ETAR AAb levels was observed (R=0.80, p<0.001) (online supplemental figure 1).

RRs using the cut-offs determined before8 could not be determined for the Nijmegen cohort, as there were too few cases exceeding the predetermined cut-offs for ILD, PAH, SRC, DU and all-cause mortality.

In contrast to the observation made in the Leiden cohort, the Nijmegen SSc cohort, statistically significant differences between AAb levels were found for both anti-AT1R AAbs or anti-ETAR AAbs and all-cause mortality, with median levels being higher in the patient groups who did not die (anti-AT1R AAb: median=6.5 units vs median=7.8 units, p=0.005; anti-ETAR AAb: median=4.9 units vs median=7.0 units, p=0.020). Furthermore, patients with dcSSc showed significantly higher median anti-ETAR AAb levels compared with SSc patients with a different disease subset (median=8.1 units vs median=5.9 units, p=0.001), and patients with ILD showed significantly higher median anti-ETAR AAb levels compared with SSc patients without ILD (median=7.6 units vs median=6.1 units, p=0.007). There was no significant difference between median levels in other outcome groups (online supplemental figure 2).

Kaplan-Meier analyses could not be performed for the Nijmegen cohort due to the limited number of cases exceeding the cut-off for mortality.

Pooled multivariable logistic regression analysis

Lastly, the Leiden and Nijmegen cohorts were joined, and multivariable analyses were performed to rule out possible confounding by other clinical characteristics, adjusting for age, sex, dcSSc, ILD and PAH. We did not observe an independent association between anti-AT1R AAb positivity and all-cause mortality (OR 1.00, 95% CI 0.97 to 1.02) or anti-ETAR AAb positivity and all-cause mortality (OR 0.97, 95% CI 0.94 to 1.00). Furthermore, no independent associations were found between anti-AT1R AAb positivity or anti-ETAR AAb positivity and ILD or PAH (table 2).

Discussion

In this study, we aimed to identify the presence and levels of anti-AT1R- and anti-ETAR AAbs in the sera of SSc patients measured by ELISA, and to evaluate their potential roles as biomarkers for more severe disease and increased mortality risk in clinical practice.

We found that anti-AT1R- and anti-ETAR AAbs could be detected in SSc patient samples by ELISA, but as shown before, the presence of these AAbs is not specific for SSc, as AAbs against anti-AT1R and anti-ETAR were also found in patients with RA, patients with PRP and HCs in similar levels. Importantly, the levels in SSc patients frequently did not exceed the cut-offs previously established for the association with clinical outcomes. Consequently, all RRs calculated for the associations between anti-AT1R- or anti-ETAR AAbs and SSc-related outcomes (dcSSc, ILD, SRC, SU, PAH, all-cause mortality and the composite outcome of all vascular manifestations) were not statistically significant. It is, however, important to acknowledge that mean levels of these AAbs can differ from population to population, and cut-offs derived from one cohort might not be applicable to patients from different populations.

To evaluate associations between clinical disease manifestations and levels of anti-AT1R- and anti-ETAR AAbs, we compared levels in the groups with and without the disease manifestation in both the Leiden and the Nijmegen cohort. In this analysis, we do observe significant differences in AAb levels between patients. Interestingly, we observe higher median levels in patients without SRC and patients without PAH as compared with the patients who did exhibit these disease manifestations in the Leiden cohort, which is in the opposite direction of the associations reported in the previous study.8 In the Nijmegen cohort, we do observe higher median levels of anti-ETAR AAbs in patients with ILD and patients with dcSSc (online supplemental figure 2). In addition, patients in the Nijmegen cohort who did not die during follow-up had higher levels of anti-AT1R AAbs and/or anti-ETAR AAbs compared with those who died. Altogether, our results are not in line with earlier studies, in which correlations and/or associations were found between higher anti-AT1R- and anti-ETAR AAbs determined by ELISA and SSc-specific disease manifestations, such as DU, PAH, oesophageal and skin manifestations, and all-cause mortality.9 10 23

Several possible explanations could account for these, apparently, contrasting observations. The reliability of the assay performed for our patients and controls could be called into question and could have changed during the years. However, the ELISA for all samples was performed at CellTrend GmbH (Luckenwalde, Germany), using the same test as applied before.8 Furthermore, as mentioned before, the cut-off determined in a specific population might not be applicable in a different population. However, when applying the cut-off based on HC samples from the same population and tested in the same batch, we could still not confirm the previously described clinical associations. In the original publication, clinical associations were determined by comparing SSc patients to the high number of controls, rather than comparing subgroups of SSc patients, such as those with and without PAH. As a result, more associations with SSc-related symptoms were identified. Here, we compared SSc patients with a limited size of healthy controls (n=20). However, in both our cohorts, median levels of anti-AT1R AAbs and anti-ETAR AAbs do not differ between groups with and without specific disease manifestations in the vast majority of cases. This, together with the fact that the use of cut-offs derived from other cohorts does not appear to be applicable to these AAbs, importantly limits their applicability in clinical practice.

The finding that anti-ETAR AAb levels were higher in patients with dcSSc and ILD in the Nijmegen cohort, but lower in those who died during follow-up in the Nijmegen cohort, was a counterintuitive result. This discrepancy could be explained by death not being a current disease state, but occurring during follow-up, making it possible that levels could have fluctuated over time. Furthermore, we only found this association in the Nijmegen cohort, and not in the Leiden cohort, and only for anti-ETAR AAbs, and not for anti-AT1R AAbs, suggesting that it may represent a chance finding rather than a robust association. These contrasting findings also highlight the volatility of these markers, showing the limited applicability for them as a biomarker or a marker for prediction of disease outcomes.

Finally, the patient selection could also be an explanation of the differences observed in the different studies. The LUMC and the Radboudumc are both academic hospitals specialised in SSc and other CTDs, to which patients with SSc are referred from all over the Netherlands, allowing a wide range of patients with varying symptoms to be seen annually. In the selection from the LUMC cohort, 88% of patients were not taking ARBs or ERAs at the time of serum sampling. A sensitivity analysis was conducted on the small subgroup of patients that were taking these drugs, indicating that this aspect did not impact the results. It is notable that the two SSc cohorts from the LUMC and the Radboudumc showed contrasting results in AAb levels; a large number of all patients in the Leiden cohort had levels above the suggested cut-offs for either anti-AT1R AAbs or anti-ETAR AAbs, whereas these numbers were notably lower in the Nijmegen cohort. This is striking, given that the Nijmegen cohort contained a relatively large number of patients with PAH, which led us to expect higher anti-AT1R AAbs and anti-ETAR AAb levels in that cohort. This difference, however, might also be explained by differences in handling and/or storage of the samples, which cannot be accounted for, but again limit clinical applicability of the ELISA under study. The relatively low anti-AT1R AAbs and anti-ETAR AAb levels measured in the current study, compared with previous research, along with the observed differences between samples taken from the Leiden cohort and the Nijmegen cohort, highlight the challenges of using the results of this ELISA for diagnostic and predictive purposes in clinical practice.

Previous studies have shown that serum from SSc patients contains factors that stimulate endothelial cells in an AT1R-dependent and ETAR-dependent manner.8 11 This is in line with clinical observations, where clear therapeutic effects are observed from treatment with oral ERAs and ACE-inhibitors.5 24 25 However, it is unclear whether a specific AT1R-binding or ETAR-binding antibody is responsible for these observed effects.

The difficulties in defining and capturing functional antibodies targeting G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) have been acknowledged. In contrast to the SSc-specific antinuclear antibodies, antibodies targeting GPCRs are described to be highly prevalent in healthy donors and might have functional relevance for healthy cell and tissue homeostasis.26 Binding of these antibodies to its natural target, the GPCR, could exert different effects, ranging from agonistic to neutralising, and even antagonistic effects, depending on local circumstances, including the conformational structure of the receptor.27 Given the complex antibody-receptor interaction, it is not surprising that the presence and levels of the AAbs do not correlate directly to disease outcome in clinical practice.

Moreover, a recent study by Bankamp et al23 also evaluated the association between vascular AAbs and SSc-specific disease manifestations, but measured anti-AT1R activation by means of a luminometric bioassay. The results presented by these authors indicated that more than half of the SSc patients tested had functional AAbs against AT1R, but they found no associations between anti-AT1R AAbs measured by luminometric method and any clinical manifestations of SSc. These authors reported a strong correlation between anti-topoisomerase I-AAb, anti-AT1R-AAb and anti-ETAR AAb reactivity measured by ELISA, which they argued could be due to contaminations of nuclear and cytoplasmic antigens against the plasma membrane fractions used for the coating of the anti-AT1R AAb and anti-ETAR AAb ELISA. In line with these findings, studies in our research group could not detect functional anti-AT1R AAb and anti-ETAR AAb responses in plasma and purified IgG of SSc patients using AT1R- and ETAR-overexpressing CHO cells.28 How previously reported associations between levels of AT1R AAbs or ETAR AAbs and different vascular manifestations should be explained remains to be explored.2931

In conclusion, we set out to evaluate the clinical applicability of anti-AT1R AAbs and anti-ETAR AAbs measured by ELISA for risk stratification in SSc. We took advantage of two large, clinically well-defined cohorts of SSc patients from the Netherlands.

Although we confirm a positive ELISA for anti-AT1R and anti-ETAR in the sera of patients SSc, this result is not specific for SSc, as this was observed in similar levels in other patient groups and healthy controls. Detected levels can differ between different SSc populations, complicating its use in clinical practice. Previously reported associations between higher levels of anti-AT1R AAbs or anti-ETAR AAbs and clinical disease outcomes or all-cause mortality could not be unequivocally confirmed in our hands. Based on the current study, we conclude that the determination of anti-AT1R AAbs or anti-ETAR AAbs by ELISA is of limited diagnostic and predictive value in clinical practice for SSc patients.
