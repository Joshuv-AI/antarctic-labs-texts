A novel scalable method for the production of rennet-treated milk-derived extracellular vesicles for improved curcumin oral delivery
Authors: Emily Schifano, Francesco Vari, Luca Buccini, Mariana Karimova, Kuanysh Syman, Diana Varnadyan, Daniela Uccelletti, Simone Dinarelli, Maurizio Zuccotti, Andrea Alfieri, Simona Sennato, Francesco Mura, Marco Rossi, Luciana Dini, Stefano Tacconi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExtracellular vesicles (EVs) are lipid bilayer-enclosed nanoparticles secreted by all cell types and are gaining increasing attention as natural, biocompatible drug delivery nanovehicles. Among the various sources, bovine milk-derived EVs (MEVs) represent an abundant, cost-effective, and eco-friendly alternative, with demonstrated potential in delivering chemotherapeutics, bioactive natural compounds, miRNAs, and other therapeutic agents. However, the effective and scalable production of MEVs still requires protocol optimization for contaminant removal, especially casein micelles and fat globules, which may be co-isolated and compromise EV quality. This study aimed to identify an efficient and scalable strategy for MEV production suitable for oral drug delivery applications.ResultsThree casein removal methods, acetic acid precipitation, ultracentrifugation, and commercial rennet, each combined with ultrafiltration/size exclusion chromatography (UF/SEC) for EV isolation were compared. Rennet treatment outperformed the other methods, yielding MEVs with superior purity, integrity, and minimal aggregation. MEVs with high quality and purity from whey derived from rennet-treated bovine milk successfully isolated by tangential flow filtration (TFF), a method more suitable for large-scale production. These vesicles were subsequently employed for the passive loading of curcumin, a hydrophobic compound with known bioactive properties but poor intrinsic bioavailability. The resulting curcumin-loaded MEVs (CurMEVs) were evaluated for their ability to traverse the intestinal barrier using in vitro and in vivo models. In addition, a prolongevity effect was observed in Caenorhabditis elegans animals supplemented with CurMEVs. Results confirmed that MEVs enhance curcumin stability and bioavailability.ConclusionsOur findings support the use of rennet combined with SEC or TFF to produce MEVs as scalable, safe, and effective delivery systems, offering a promising platform for future nutraceutical and pharmaceutical applications. In parallel, the study highlights the added value of converting dairy industry by-products, such as whey, into a sustainable source of nanocarriers.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-025-03724-0.

Introduction

Extracellular vesicles (EVs), membrane-enclosed nanoparticles known for their crucial role in intercellular communication, are now attracting increasing interest as promising vehicles for drug delivery. Indeed, due to their nature, EVs inherently possess properties suitable for drug delivery: (i) carry biological material (i.e., proteins, lipids and nucleic acids); (ii) are internalized by target cells; (iii) cross biological barriers; (iv) are biocompatible; iv) can be artificially loaded with exogenous bioactive compounds [1, 2] (v) are secreted physiologically by all cell types and present in almost all biological fluids, such as blood [3], saliva [4], urine [5] and milk [6]. Despite their versatility, the use of EVs for nano-delivery presents some concerns, such as the choice of the starting biological material, the use of the most appropriate isolation protocol and the scalability of their production [7].

Bovine milk-derived extracellular vesicles (MEVs) hold significant promise for targeted nano-delivery due to their natural ability to encapsulate and protect bioactive compounds, thereby enhancing their stability and bioavailability. Aiming to develop efficient and cost-effective nanodrug carriers for use as food supplements, we have focused on the development and validation of a scalable system based on these natural bio/eco-friendly nanocarriers. MEVs represent a suitable low-cost natural source of EVs with great potential as natural nanocarriers. Indeed, several evidence have shown their effectiveness for the delivery of chemoprotective or chemotherapeutic drugs [8], bioactive natural compounds [9–13], miRNA [14, 15], and therapeutic agents [16]. MEVs are particularly well-suited for loading hydrophobic molecules, which, due to their chemical nature, are often unstable and poorly bioavailable in their free form. These molecules can be efficiently incorporated into biological membranes through simple passive loading, without compromising the integrity of EVs. An example is the encapsulation of curcumin in bovine MEVs for oral administration since, despite its advantageous bioactive properties, in its free form it is poorly bioavailable and stable [8, 9, 17]. Indeed, the use of MEVs as carriers for curcumin enhances its solubility, stability, and bioavailability [8, 9, 17].

Furthermore, loading curcumin into natural nanocarriers such as MEVs could allow for a prolonged release of the molecule at low doses, preventing potential toxic effects associated with its overdose or long-term intake [18].

To develop effective nanocarriers and maximize their quality and purity, the removal of contaminants (i.e., fat globules and casein), which can be co-isolated with EVs, and the choice of the most appropriate isolation/concentration approach are crucial steps in the production of MEVs. Casein micelles, which are large (typically 100–500 nm), can be mistaken for EVs in certain preparative techniques, especially in methods that rely on size or density-based separation. In most of the studies conducted on bovine MEVs, the main tested methods for casein removal and whey production from skimmed milk are based on isoelectric precipitation with acids [19, 20], EDTA-induced dissociation [21, 22], and ultracentrifugation [23]. Regardless of the approach used, the whey preparation procedures must not affect the morphology, quality and molecular profile of isolated EVs. For example, a study conducted by Rahman et al. showed that acid-based casein removal from bovine milk induced a partial degradation of EV-surface proteins [24], while the use of high-speed centrifugation does not allow the effective removal of casein [25] and alters the quality of MEVs promoting their aggregation [26].

Without sustainable practices, milk whey is considered the most important environmental pollutant in the dairy industry because of its high pollutant load [27] and its disposal as wastewater which are associated with serious environmental risks. The use of whey as a source of EVs could allow the environmentally friendly reuse of this waste material; however, to date, no common commercial enzyme mixture (i.e., rennet) for casein removal has been tested for MEV production. In this scenario, with the goal of transitioning to large-scale MEV production for drug delivery, this study evaluates three distinct methods for casein removal, i.e., acid-acetic precipitation, ultracentrifugation, and commercial rennet, each paired with different EV isolation techniques, including ultrafiltration/size exclusion chromatography (UF/SEC) and tangential flow filtration (TFF). Given that rennet treatment provided the highest purity, yield, integrity, and minimal aggregation of EVs, we utilized these nano-vesicles for curcumin delivery across the intestinal barrier in both in vitro and in vivo models.

Methods

Raw bovine milk supply and whey preparation

Raw bovine milk was sourced from local farmers. For all the preparations 200 mL of raw bovine milk was used. The initial processing involved defatting the milk through two sequential centrifugation steps: first at 2000 g x 20 min, followed by a second centrifugation at 3000 g x 30 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was then treated for casein removal to obtain whey, using three different methods:

Rennet Treatment (REN): The supernatant was treated with rennet (purchased from a local grocery store) and incubated at 37 °C for 30–40 min, allowing coagulation to occur.

Rennet Treatment (REN): The supernatant was treated with rennet (purchased from a local grocery store) and incubated at 37 °C for 30–40 min, allowing coagulation to occur.

Isoelectric Precipitation (AA): Acetic acid was added to adjust the pH to 4.6–4.8 at 37 °C, and the mixture was centrifuged at 2000 g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove the clotted casein.

Isoelectric Precipitation (AA): Acetic acid was added to adjust the pH to 4.6–4.8 at 37 °C, and the mixture was centrifuged at 2000 g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove the clotted casein.

Ultracentrifugation (UC): Finally, the supernatant was subjected to ultracentrifugation at 40,000 g for 1 h at 4 °C (50.2 Ti fixed-angle rotor; Beckman Coulter) to obtain the whey (interphase) and remove casein (precipitate).

Ultracentrifugation (UC): Finally, the supernatant was subjected to ultracentrifugation at 40,000 g for 1 h at 4 °C (50.2 Ti fixed-angle rotor; Beckman Coulter) to obtain the whey (interphase) and remove casein (precipitate).

From all three preparations, milk whey was recovered and filtered with Whatman filter (0.38 μm) and centrifuged at 3000 g x 30 min to remove the remaining casein. The obtained supernatant was filtered with 0.22 μm PES filter units (polyethersulfone membrane filter units, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA) and further processed for EV isolation.

MEV isolation

For EV isolation by size exclusion chromatography (SEC), 200 mL of milk whey was loaded into Amicon Ultra-15 Centrifugal Filter Units with an Ultracel-10 membrane (MWCO = 10 kDa; Merck Millipore, MA) and concentrated to 1.5 mL by repeated centrifugation at 4000 × g. The concentrated whey was then fractionated by SEC, following the method described in [28]. Briefly, the sample was loaded onto a 10 mL CL-2B Sepharose column (GE Healthcare, UK). EV-enriched fractions (MEVs) were collected from fractions 8 to 10 (0.5 mL per fraction) and pooled together to a final volume of 1.5 mL. For EV isolation by tangential flow filtration (TFF), 200 mL of whey was concentrated using TFF-EVs filters (50 nm pore size; HansaBiomed Life Science, EE) to a final volume of 1.5 mL. The isolated EVs were either used directly for further analysis (TFF-MEVs) or subjected to an additional SEC step for further purification (TFF + SEC-MEVs). In all cases, protein concentration of EVs was measured by Bradford assay (Bio-Rad Laboratories, CA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Western blot analysis

For Western Blot analysis, the pooled EV-enriched fractions were concentrated with Amicon Ultra-4 Centrifugal Filter Units with an Ultracel-10 membrane (MWCO = 10 kDa; Merck Millipore, MA, USA) by repeated centrifugation at 4000 × g. A total of 10 µg of EV proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) using a 10–12% polyacrylamide gel (SureCast™ Acrylamide Solution, ThermoFisher Scientific, MA). Proteins were then transferred onto PVDF membranes. After transfer, the membranes were blocked for 1 h at RT with 5% nonfat dry milk or 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TTBS). Following blocking, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (Table S1). After washing with TTBS, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (Table S1), diluted in TTBS containing 5% nonfat dry milk and/or 3% BSA. Immunoreactive bands were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents (Immobilon Crescendo Western HRP substrate; Merck Millipore, DE). Band densities were quantified by densitometry using Image Lab™ Version 6.0.1 2017 (Bio-Rad Laboratories, CA).

Dynamic light scattering (DLS)

The hydrodynamic size of EVs have been determined by Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) by using a NanoZetaSizer apparatus (Malvern Instruments Ltd, Worcestershire, UK) equipped with a 5 mW HeNe laser and backscatter detection. Samples were allowed to equilibrate for 2 min at 20 °C for 5 min in the thermosted cell of the instruments, then measurements were carried out in triplicate. The size of the observed EVs populations was determined by Z-average size and polydispersity index (PDI) by cumulant method [29]. To get the size distribution of samples data has been analyzed in the framework of the Mie theory by using the NNLS algorithm [30].

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM)

After isolation, EVs were fixed with 0.1% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 30 min at room temperature (RT). The fixed EVs were then stained with 2% uranyl acetate for 7 min at RT. Following staining, the sample was loaded onto formvar-carbon-coated grids and examined using a Zeiss Auriga Scanning Electron Microscope (Zeiss, DE) equipped with the STEM module, operating at 20 kV. For cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM), samples were prepared using a Vitrobot Mark IV instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA) under controlled conditions. After glow-discharging carbon/copper grids (C-Flat 1.2/1.3 Cu 300 or Lacey Carbon TH Cu 200), 4 µL of EV sample (diluted 1:10 in filtered PBS) was loaded on the grid and blotted once for 3 to 6 s at 4 °C with 95% relative humidity. Grids were then rapidly vitrified by plunging into liquid ethane. Imaging was performed using a 200 kV Glacios cryo-transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA), equipped with a Falcon 3EC direct electron detector. Data were collected in low-dose mode (30 e⁻/Å²) across various magnifications (73,000× to 150,000×) and at a defocus value of −3.0 μm, using the EPU software (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA).

Thin layer chromatography (TLC)

EV lipid profile was analyzed by TLC as reported in [31]. In brief, lipids were extracted from concentrated EV samples (150 µL) using the Bligh and Dyer procedure. Equal amounts of protein from each sample were loaded onto silica gel plates and separated by TLC. For lipid separation, different mobile phases were used depending on the lipid type: (i) for neutral lipids, hexane/ethyl ether/acetic acid (70/30/1; v/v/v); (ii) for phospholipids, chloroform/methanol/water (65/25/4; v/v/v); for sphingolipids, toluene/methanol (70/30; v/v). The plates were then sprayed uniformly with 10% cupric sulfate in 8% aqueous phosphoric acid, dried for 10 min at RT, and heated at 145 °C for 10 min to visualize the lipid spots. Quantification of the lipid spots was done via densitometry using a ChemiDoc MP imager under white light epi-illumination with a 590/110 filter. The spots were first visualized through coloration and carbonization, then measured using Image Lab Version 6.1 (Bio-Rad Laboratories, CA). The lipid content of each species was expressed as a percentage of the total lipid content in the sample.

Atomic force microscopy (AFM)

The AFM measurements were performed with an home-designed microscope described in detail elsewhere [32]. The images were acquired in contact mode by using MSCT probes (Veeco) with nominal elastic constant of 0,03 N/m, a speed of 3 s/row on square areas of 10 micrometers with 512 points per line. The actual elastic constants were calibrated on each day of experiment by the thermal noise method, in order to ensure a probe force below 1nN throughout all the measurements. The specimens were prepared by drop casting 15µL of water-based solutions containing the vesicles fixed with PFA 0,1%, onto freshly cleaved mica and letting them completely air dry at room temperature before the AFM characterization.

Curcumin preparation and loading efficiency

Curcumin (Sigma-Aldrich, MO) was dissolved in DMSO and added to MEV-enriched fractions (1.5 mL) at the concentration of 1 mg/mL. After incubation at RT for 10, 30, 60, 180–360 min, insoluble curcumin was precipitated by centrifugation at 5000 g x 15 min for two times. To further remove any residual unbound curcumin, the supernatant was processed with an additional SEC fractionation. The curcumin-loaded MEVs (CurMEVs) were isolated by collecting the post-SEC fractions 8–10 (1.5 mL). A summary of the protocol is reported in Fig. 2A.

Solubility, stability, and resistance to pH variation

Curcumin solubility was determined by measuring the absorbance spectrum of free curcumin vs. CurMEVs with NanoDrop 2000c (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA). Curcumin stability in CurMEVs was assessed following the method described in [10], with minor modifications. In brief, an equal amount of curcumin and CurMEVs were diluted in the same volume of PBS (1mL) and incubated in the dark at 37 °C. At time points 0, 30, 60, 90, 120, 150, and 180 min, an aliquot from each sample was collected, and the absorbance at 420 nm was measured. Curcumin concentration was determined as described in Sect. 2.8. The initial curcumin concentration for both free curcumin and curcumin-loaded MEVs was standardized to 1. Curcumin stability in CurMEVs was also evaluated at different pH conditions 2.7 (acid), 6.7 (neutral), and 8.5 (basic) over the time. Specifically, pH in 1 mL was adjusted with hydrochloric acid (HCl; Sigma-Aldrich, MO) or sodium hydroxide (NaOH; Sigma-Aldrich, MO) solution and measured by a pH meter (HANNA Instruments, ITA). Samples were stored in the dark at 37 °C and, at 0, 30, 60, 90, 120, 150, and 180 min, stability was measured spectrophotometrically as previously described.

Cell culture

All cells were maintained at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Human intestinal cell line Caco-2 cells (ATCC) and HT29-MTX were cultivated in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM, Sigma-Aldrich, MO) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS, Sigma-Aldrich, MO), 105 units/L penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (1% P/S; Sigma-Aldrich, MO) in T75 flasks. Cells were subcultured as soon as they reached 80 to 90% confluence (every 2 to 3 days). Raji B cells (ATCC) were cultured in suspension Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 Medium supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS, and 1% P/S. Raji B cells were maintained at a cell density of 1 million cells/cm3.

Cell viability

Caco-2 cells were seeded at the density of 20 000 cells/well in a 96-well plate and allowed to adhere. The day after, cells were treated with 4 µg/mL of MEVs and CurMEVs, and with free Curcumin at the same concentration of the encapsulated form determined by LE value. Cell viability was measured by 3-[4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl]−2,5 diphenyl tetrazolium bromide (MTT, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA) assay as reported in [31] after 24 and 48 h of treatment.

Internalization in Caco-2 cells and in vitro digestion

Internalization of MEVs, CurMEVs and free Cur in Caco-2 monoculture was visualized after 30 min and 1 h of treatments. Cells were grown on 11 mm glass coverslips up to confluence. After, cells were treated with MEVs, Cur-MEVs and free Cur for 30 min and 1 h, washed with DPBS and fixed with 4% formalin. After additional washes with DPBS, cells were mounted on glass-slides with DAPI mounting medium (SouthernBiotech, AL, USA) for nuclei staining and then observed with Axio Observer fluorescence microscope (Zeiss) equipped with Apotome 3. To evaluate the effects of gastric and intestinal digestion on CurMEVs, an in vitro digestion assay was performed as described by [33] with minor modifications. CurMEVs were diluted with an equal volume of DPBS and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C with 18.5% w/v HCl (pH 2.0) and 80 mg/mL pepsin in 0.1 M HCl (Sigma-Aldrich, MO) for the simulation of gastric phase. To mimic the intestinal phase a mix of 4 mg/mL pancreatin (Sigma-Aldrich, MO) and 24 mg/mL bile extract in 0.1 M NaHCO3 (Sigma-Aldrich, MO) was added followed by another incubation for 1 h at 37 °C. To inactivate the enzymes the samples were cooled down at 4 °C. The internalization of CurMEVs in Caco-2 cells after the digestion was performed and the results were observed as described above.

Tri-culture model of intestinal barrier and transepithelial transport assay

Caco-2 and HT29 cells were seeded in ratio 9:1 on the apical chamber of 8 μm pore-size polycarbonate-Transwell inserts (Corning, NY). The cells were cultured in 24-well Transwell plates at a final density of 5 × 105 cells per insert. The culture medium (500 µL in the apical and 1mL in the basolateral compartments) was replaced every 2 days. At day 14 of differentiation Raji B cells were seeded in the basolateral chamber at the density of 5 × 104 cells per insert (final ratio Caco2/HT29/Raji 9:1:1) and cultured up to day 24. For transepithelial transport analysis, free curcumin (Cur), CurMEVs, and empty MEVs were added to the upper chamber of the Transwell system. At different time points (0, 30, 60, and 360 min), absorbance at 420 nm was measured in the lower chamber to determine the amount of curcumin transported across the epithelial barrier (Fig. 4D).

Scanning electron microscopy

Cells were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 mol/L cacodylate buffer (pH 7.4) for 1 h at ice temperature, followed by postfixation with 1% OsO4 in the same buffer for 1 h on melting ice. After fixation, cells were dehydrated through a series of ethanol concentrations (50%, 70%, 95%, and 100%), then chemically dried using a mixture of ethanol and Hexamethyldisilazane (HMDS) (Sigma-Aldrich, MO) in ratios of 2:1, 1:1, and 1:2, and finally in pure HMDS, which was allowed to evaporate overnight. The specimens were then mounted onto aluminum stubs and coated with a 3 nm layer of chromium using a Quorum Q150 T sputter coater (Quorum Technologies, UK). Samples were observed under a Zeiss Auriga Scanning Electron Microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

Alcian blue staining

Mucus production was visualized by alcian blue staining. In brief, the culture media were removed, and the cells in the insert were washed twice with PBS. Cells were treated with Methacarn solution (60% Methanol, 30% chloroform, 10% acetic acid) for 1 h at 4 °C and then stained with 10 mg/mL of alcian blue in glacial acetic acid. Subsequently, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature. The insert membrane with cells was cut out of the plastic insert holder, mounted onto a glass slide using a drop of DPBS: glycerol (1:1) and visualized by optical microscopy.

C. elegans strains and growth conditions

The wild-type Caenorhabditis elegans strain N2 was utilized in all experiments and maintained on nematode growth medium (NGM). Worms were fed with heat-killed Escherichia coli OP50, supplemented with MEV and CurMEV (10 µg/mL), and with free Cur at the same concentration of the encapsulated form determined by LE value. Untreated populations served as controls. Heat-killed OP50 cells were prepared as described in Schifano et al. [34].

C. elegans lifespan analysis and biodistribution

For lifespan assays, synchronized N2 adults were allowed to lay embryos for 2 h on NGM plates containing heat-killed E. coli OP50 and various treatments before being removed. Lifespan started when the progeny reached reproductive maturity (t0). Worms were daily transferred to fresh plates with newly prepared bacterial lawns and observed. Nematodes were considered deceased when they failed to respond to gentle stimulation with a platinum wire. A minimum of 80 worms per condition were assessed in each experiment. For biodistribution analysis, 15 worms per condition at different timepoints (2,3,4 and 7 days) were anesthetized with sodium azide (20 mmol L − 1) (Sigma-Aldrich, MO) and observed by Axio Observer fluorescence microscope (Zeiss) equipped with Apotome 3. The experiments were repeated three times.

Pumping rate and body bending measurements

Pharyngeal pumping activity and locomotion ability were examined using a Zeiss Axiovert 25 microscope as described in [35]. The assessment was conducted on 10-day-old adult worms raised on different treatments from the embryonic stage. Approximately 10 worms per condition were analyzed. All aging-related experiments were performed in triplicate.

Smurf assay

Intestinal barrier integrity was evaluated using the Smurf Assay. Control and treated worms were incubated in M9 buffer containing 5% food-grade Brilliant Blue FCF (Sigma-Aldrich, MO) at 20 °C for 3 h. Excess dye was removed through repeated washes with M9 buffer. Worms were then mounted on 3% agarose pads containing 20 mM sodium azide and observed using a Zeiss Axiovert 25 microscope. The diameter of the intestinal lumen (µm) was measured in 10 worms per treatment using Zeiss ZEN Microscopy Software 2011. The experiments were repeated three times and 15 worms per group were used in each experiment.

Statistical analysis

For C. elegans experiments, statistical analyses were conducted using Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA followed by a Bonferroni post-hoc test (GraphPad Prism 9.0, GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, United States). Differences with p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant and denoted as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001. All experiments were conducted at least in triplicate. Data are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Fluorescence images were analyzed using ImageJ software, quantifying mean fluorescence intensity as the ratio of pixels per unit area of the worm.

Results

Elimination of casein with commercial rennet allows to obtain high-quality MEVs in comparison to the isoelectric precipitation and ultracentrifugation

After defatting raw bovine milk, we compared three different approaches for casein removal: commercial rennet treatment (REN), acetic acid precipitation (AA), and high-speed ultracentrifugation (UC) (Fig. 1A). The clarified whey from each method was then processed using ultrafiltration/size-exclusion chromatography (UF/SEC) for MEV isolation (Fig. 1A), as SEC is a well-established method for EV isolation from milk [36]. To assess the quality of the isolated MEVs, we performed western blot analysis on the three preparations (Fig. 1B-C). Ponceau red staining revealed that casein was not completely removed in the EV fraction obtained from UC-treated whey, unlike the AA and REN treatments (Fig. 1B). The expression of EV-associated markers (TSG101, CD81, CD9, and Alix) was analyzed, and as shown in Fig. 1C and Supplementary Fig. 1A, most of these markers were detected in all three preparations. In the AA-treated whey, a reduction in the tetraspanin CD81 was observed, while UC-MEVs exhibited a decrease in the endosomal markers TSG101 and Alix (Fig. 1C). Further, a morphological characterization of MEVs was carried out using high-resolution microscopy. TEM micrographs showed the presence of round shaped EVs, with diameters ranging from 50 to 200 nm in all preparations (Fig. 1D, left). However, MEVs isolated from AA- and UC-treated whey showed strong aggregation compared to REN-MEVs (Fig. 1D, left). The same morphological features were observed by AFM analysis (Fig. 1D, right). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis revealed that AA-MEVs and REN-MEVs had similar size distributions (from 80 to 400 nm), with mean Z-average diameters of 163.5 ± 1.7 nm and 176.8 ± 1.0 nm, respectively (Fig. 1E; Supplementary Table 2). In contrast, UC-MEVs exhibited a broader diameter range (from 40 to 600 nm) and a smaller mean diameter of 123.9 ± 0.4 nm (Fig. 1E; Supplementary Table 2).

Given the potential of EVs as nanoparticles for drug delivery, studying their lipid composition is essential to predict their ability to interact with target cells and release their cargo [37, 38]. To evaluate the impact of the isolation procedure on MEV lipid composition, we analyzed the lipid profiles of all preparations by TLC (Fig. 1F, Supplementary Fig. 1B). AA-MEVs and REN-MEVs exhibited comparable lipid compositions, with an enrichment of phosphatidylserine (PS) in REN-MEVs. Conversely, UC-MEVs showed an increase in phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) and phosphatidylcholine (PC), along with a decrease in cholesterol (CHOL) and ceramide (CER) compared to the other two methods (Fig. 1F, Supplementary Fig. 1B ).

Based on these findings, we conclude that, compared to acid precipitation and ultracentrifugation, rennet treatment for casein removal is an economical and effective method for obtaining MEVs with biochemical and physical properties that are more suitable for drug delivery applications.

Curcumin loading in REN-MEVs

Once defined as the best procedure for casein removal, we reproduced the same experimental approaches reported in [10] to test curcumin loading, stability and solubility. This was crucial to define the applicability of REN-MEVs as carriers of bioactive compounds. Because of the hydrophobicity of curcumin, the simple passive loading approach was preferentially used. REN-MEVs were incubated at room temperature with curcumin 1 mg/mL for different time points (10, 30, 60, 180 and 360 min.) and, after removing the insoluble curcumin by precipitation, REN-MEVs loaded with curcumin (CurMEVs) were cleaned from the unbound compound with an additional step of SEC (Fig. 2A). Because of the intrinsic optical properties of curcumin, we first determined the loading efficiency (LE) by measuring the absorbance at 420 nm before and after the second step of SEC. As shown in Fig. 2B, the maximum LE (28.11%) was observed after 180 min (3 h) of incubation with curcumin and the loading procedure didn’t affect the size and integrity of MEVs, as revealed from DLS (Fig. 2C), TEM and cryo-EM analysis (Fig. 2D). Of note, CurMEVs had a more irregular and jagged border, probably due to the presence of hydrophobic curcumin within the lipid bilayer (Fig. 2D). Interestingly, the cryo-EM analysis clearly revealed the presence of molecules adhering to the surface of the CurMEVs, which was not observed in the unloaded MEV (Fig. 2D right side).

Curcumin loaded in MEVs is more soluble, stable and less toxic than the free form, and it can cross the intestinal barrier

As already reported in [9, 10], curcumin loaded in MEVs is more stable and soluble than the free compound. By using the same experimental approaches, we tested curcumin solubility and stability (Fig. 3A-B). Measuring the absorbance spectra, it was evident that the CurMEVs had a reduced absorption peak at 420 nm in respect to free curcumin, suggesting a higher solubility of the compound when loaded into MEVs (Fig. 3A). Moreover, in line with previous findings [10], curcumin loaded in MEVs demonstrated superior stability over time, as evidenced by a drastic reduction in absorbance of the free compound after just 30 min (Fig. 3B).

Stability, solubility and toxic effects of curcumin-loaded MEVs. Storage stability is a key parameter for nano-formulated carriers. Therefore, we analyzed the structural integrity of CurMEVs using TEM after storage at −80 °C for 7 days, 15 days, and 4 months (Fig. 3C). TEM micrographs showed that the formulation remained stable up to 15 days of storage at −80 °C, while after 4 months, although CurMEVs were still detectable, their morphology was compromised (Fig. 3C).

Given the potential of CurMEVs as a food supplement for oral administration, we further tested the stability of the nanoformulation under pH conditions typical of the gastrointestinal tract and evaluated its potential toxicity in Caco-2 cells in vitro (Fig. 3D-E). As shown in Fig. 3D, the preparation remained stable at both acidic (pH 2.7) and basic (pH 8.5) conditions, as evidenced by both the preservation of EV morphology (Fig. 3D top) and the unchanged amount of curcumin released over time (Fig. 3D bottom). Absorbance measurements indicated that the amount of free, insoluble curcumin remained unchanged across time points and pH conditions, suggesting that CurMEVs possess excellent resistance and stability in these environments. Additionally, no significant changes in cell viability were observed in Caco-2 cells treated with CurMEVs for 24 and 48 h compared to control cells (Fig. 3E), whereas free curcumin reduced cell viability by more than 50% relative to CTR cells. After, we utilized the autofluorescence properties of curcumin to analyze the internalization of free curcumin and CurMEVs in Caco-2 cells after 30 min and 1 h of treatment. As shown in Fig. 4A, CurMEVs were internalized to a much greater extent than the free compound at both time points, emphasizing the effectiveness of MEVs as a carrier for enhanced curcumin delivery into recipient cells. Since orally administered nanocarriers must resist degradation during gastrointestinal digestion, we also evaluated the effects of in vitro digestion protocol on CurMEVs integrity and their rate of internalization in recipients Caco-2 cells. No differences in uptake by Caco-2 cells (Fig. 4F) or morphological alterations (Fig. 4G) were observed in digested CurMEVs compared to undigested ones, suggesting that the digestion process does not affect their structural and functional integrity. Notably, as clearly shown in Fig. 4F, the curcumin fluorescence signal remained as punctate intracellular dots even after the digestion process, suggesting that CurMEVs still internalized via an endocytic route despite the exposure to the gastric and intestinal phases. The ability of CurMEVs to cross the intestinal barrier (IB) has been previously demonstrated using in vitro models [9, 10]. Here, we tested this capability using the same experimental approach. As a model of the IB, we used a triple co-culture system of Caco-2, HT-29, and Raji B cells grown on a transwell filter. This model has been previously reported as a more physiological, functional, and reproducible in vitro representation of the IB [39, 40]. First, we evaluated the reliability of the protocol and the development of the intestinal epithelium after 21 days of differentiation by observing microvilli formation using SEM microscopy (Fig. 4B) and mucus production through Alcian Blue staining (Fig. 4C). Next, we assessed the ability of CurMEVs compared to free Cur to cross the intestinal epithelium by adding the loaded MEVs and free curcumin to the culture medium in the apical chamber (Fig. 4D). At different time points (0, 30, 60, and 360 min), we quantified the amount of curcumin in the bottom chamber culture medium by measuring the absorbance at 420 nm (Fig. 4D). As shown in Fig. 4E, when curcumin was delivered via MEVs, it crossed the intestinal epithelium more efficiently than the free form at all time points. The highest concentration of curcumin in the bottom chamber was observed 60 min after treatment with CurMEVs, showing a 7-fold increase compared to the free form.

Biodistribution and effects on C. elegans physiology and aging

To investigate in vivo biodistribution, C. elegans model was used, supplemented with MEVs, Cur, or CurMEVs for 2, 3, 4, and 7 days from hatching. The biodistribution of Cur fluorescence in C. elegans changed over the course of the treatment (Fig. 5A). Initially, fluorescence was predominantly localized in the pharyngeal region, where it remained accumulated throughout the treatment. As the treatment progressed, fluorescence gradually extended to the distal intestine. Higher-magnification images revealed that in worms treated with free Cur, fluorescence remained confined to the basal region of intestinal cells. In contrast, in worms treated with CurMEVs, fluorescence was distributed throughout the entire intestinal epithelium along the whole intestine. Non-specific fluorescence signal was not observed in untreated worms or in those treated with MEVs, as Cur was not present. Notably, the median lifespan of wild-type worms fed with OP50 and supplemented with Cur or CurMEVs from embryo hatching was significantly extended compared to untreated controls (Fig. 5B). Specifically, 50% worm viability was observed on days 13 and 14 in Cur- and CurMEVs-supplemented worms, whereas in untreated animals, it was recorded on day 12. In contrast, MEVs supplementation resulted in a shorter lifespan, with a median survival recorded on day 9. Interestingly, the lifespan extension was more pronounced in CurMEVs-treated worms compared to those supplemented with free Cur, suggesting that the improved biodistribution of Cur via MEVs may enhance its beneficial effects. To determine whether the pro-longevity effect was associated with delayed aging, aging markers such as locomotion and pharyngeal pumping were analyzed (Fig. 5C and D). Accordingly, after Cur supplementation, locomotion on day 10 of adulthood increased by approximately 30% compared to the untreated population, whereas MEVs supplementation reduced locomotion by about 20% (Fig. 5C). Conversely, the number of pharyngeal contractions, which measure food intake, increased by approximately 15% only in response to CurMEVs supplementation (Fig. 5D). These results suggest that the beneficial effects observed, attributed to the presence of Cur, are not only maintained in worms treated with Cur-loaded vesicles but are also enhanced. This indicates that curcumin not only exerts its beneficial effects but also becomes more widely distributed and bioavailable. Since aging is also associated with increased intestinal permeability, a gut epithelial cell damage and leakage were assessed using a blue dye-based Smurf assay (Fig. 5E). The test confirmed that untreated worms exhibited significant gut distension with high dye leakage, a phenomenon also observed in MEVs-treated worms, although to a lesser extent. Conversely, 10-day-old nematodes treated with Cur or CurMEVs showed reduced intestinal permeability, with minimal or even no leakage. Notably, as visible in the images in Fig. 5.E and highlighted in histograms in Fig. 5F, the beneficial effect of curcumin is also evident in the measurements of intestinal lumen diameter. On day 10 of adulthood, a reduction of 40% and 50% was observed following treatment with Cur and CurMEVs, respectively, from embryo hatching. Intestinal lumen dilation is also considered an aging marker, as it tends to increase with age.

Application of a tangential flow filtration (TFF)-based isolation protocol for MEVs scalability

After confirming the effectiveness of using rennet for MEV production and their potential as carriers of bioactive compounds, it is crucial to adapt alternative isolation protocols for large-scale production. One promising approach is the application of Tangential Flow Filtration (TFF), which has become increasingly popular for large-scale EV isolation due to its ability to efficiently concentrate, purify, and process high-volume samples while preserving EV integrity [41, 42]. TFF operates by flowing the sample tangentially across a membrane, enabling separation of components based on size or molecular weight. We applied to TFF alone or in combination with SEC (TFF + SEC) to isolate MEVs from the milk whey obtained from rennet treatment. Following isolation, we examined the morphology of the MEVs using TEM (Fig. 6A). Both methods successfully concentrated EVs with high yield, and the MEVs displayed a typical round, cup-shaped morphology. However, MEVs isolated using TFF + SEC appeared more aggregated than those isolated with TFF alone (Fig. 6A). We further assessed the size distribution of the isolated MEVs using DLS. TFF-derived MEVs exhibited a broader size range, and a larger mean diameter (219 ± 0.35 nm) compared to those isolated with TFF + Sect. (189 ± 4 nm), likely due to the increased aggregation in the TFF-derived samples (Fig. 6B). Additionally, the PDI for TFF-derived MEVs was higher than that of the TFF + SEC samples, indicating greater aggregation in the TFF-isolated MEVs (Supplementary Table 3). In addition, the protein pattern shown by Ponceau Red in Fig. 6C showed that, using TFF alone, the remaining casein was not completely removed compared to TFF + SEC. Interestingly, the expression of surface marker CD81 but not of intraluminal markers (i.e., TSG101 and Alix) was reduced in TFF + SEC compared to TFF alone (Fig. 6D and Supplementary Fig. 2).

Discussion

The development of efficient and scalable strategies for the isolation of milk-derived extracellular vesicles (MEVs) is a prerequisite for their therapeutic exploitation. While several protocols have been proposed, comparative studies analyzing both the structural integrity and functional potential of MEVs across different isolation workflows remain limited. Our findings contribute to filling this gap by systematically evaluating three commonly employed casein removal strategies (rennet treatment (REN), acetic acid precipitation (AA), and ultracentrifugation (UC)) and exploring their downstream implications on MEV quality, lipid composition, and drug-loading efficiency. Consistent with prior works [19, 36, 43–45], our results confirm that ultracentrifugation-based protocol result in incomplete casein depletion, leading to co-isolation of casein aggregates and non-EV proteins. This contamination not only compromises EVs purity but may also interfere with downstream applications such as cargo profiling, EV uptake, and drug delivery. In contrast, REN treatment resulted in virtually casein-free MEVs, suggesting that enzymatic treatment offers a more targeted and effective approach than physical or chemical precipitation. This aligns with recent findings by Cetinkaya et al. [46], which emphasized that chymosin treatment is more effective in casein removal from human milk compared to sodium citrate and acetic acid treatment. An equally important consideration is the choice of EV isolation method, as it can significantly impact not only the integrity of EVs but also their biological function, and consequently, their effectiveness as nanocarriers for drug-delivery. EV isolation methods, including ultracentrifugation, polymer-based precipitation, size-exclusion, and immunoaffinity capture, leverage specific EV properties such as density, size, and surface markers, each with distinct advantages and limitations [47, 48]. While ultracentrifugation remains a widely used technique, it is not universally suited for isolating MEVs across different milk types [46]. As the gold-standard method for EV isolation from complex biological fluids, such as plasma [28], urine [49], saliva [50], and milk [45], we employed SEC combined with UF for MEV isolation. In particular, the integration of REN treatment prior to SEC enabled the efficient removal of casein, resulting in high-quality MEV preparations with minimal contamination. These findings are consistent with the work of Cetinkaya et al. [46], who demonstrated that the combination of chymosin treatment and qEV SEC column effectively eliminates micellar and sub-micellar casein in isolated MEVs. Furthermore, the observed differences in EV marker expression across the different approaches used indicate that the choice of protocol can influence not only the subtype of EVs recovered but also their biochemical integrity, potentially affecting their functionality in downstream applications. For instance, reduced levels of CD81 in AA-derived MEVs confirmed the degradation effects reported by [51], where acidic conditions were shown to disrupt CD81-tetraspanin structure. Since tetraspanins contribute to EV trafficking and uptake [52, 53], their partial loss could impact MEV bioactivity. These findings challenge the assumption that all isolation methods are functionally equivalent and highlight the need to preserve protein integrity during processing, particularly when EVs are intended for delivery applications. A less explored but increasingly important variable, particularly for drug-delivery applications, is the lipid composition of EVs. While lipidomics is not routinely employed in EV characterization, recent studies have begun to emphasize its relevance for EV stability and membrane fusion [38, 54]. Our data showed that UC and AA treatments reduced the levels of cholesterol and ceramide, essential lipids for maintaining membrane rigidity and resisting physical stress during systemic circulation. This reduction could impair EV performance by increasing susceptibility to degradation or aggregation, effects that have been linked to reduced therapeutic efficacy in prior nano-delivery studies [55, 56]. Conversely, the enrichment of PS in REN-derived MEVs may offer functional advantages. PS has been implicated in enhancing EV uptake via phospholipid receptors, as demonstrated by Wei et al. [57][, and may regulate immune evasion and longer circulation time. Moreover, PS plays a stabilizing role in lipid bilayers as has been associated with reduced EV aggregation [54]. The increased PS enrichment, more homogeneous morphology, and reduced aggregation rate of REN-MEVs observed in our study are consistent with this evidence and aligns with prior observations that EV uniformity correlates with improved internalization and targeting [55, 56, 58].

Importantly, the applicability of a new EV-based nanoformulation lies not only in its therapeutic efficacy but also in the feasibility of its large-scale production. Although various isolation techniques, such as ultracentrifugation, density gradients, immunoaffinity, and microfluidics, have been explored for MEV isolation [6], they often exhibit low throughput and limited suitability for scale-up production. Tangential flow filtration (TFF) has gained recognition for its efficiency in concentrating, purifying, and processing high-volume samples while maintaining EV integrity, making it particularly suitable for industrial-scale production [41, 42]. As shown by our findings, applying TFF-based separation from REN-treated bovine whey, the quantity, purity, and quality of the MEVs remain unchanged, classifying TFF as an alternative isolation method for a scale-up potential production.

After determining that REN-based casein removal was the most effective method for obtaining MEVs with optimal physical and biochemical properties, we evaluated their potential for drug delivery by loading them with curcumin, a common hydrophobic compound already used as cargo for MEVs [10, 17, 59]. Curcumin, a polyphenolic compound derived from turmeric, is well-known for its potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects [13]. Despite its therapeutic potential, its clinical application has been limited due to its poor water solubility, low bioavailability, and rapid degradation in the gastrointestinal tract [13]. In agreement with [9, 10], we confirm that loading curcumin into MEVs significantly enhances its solubility, leading to improved bioavailability and increased stability over time. By improving solubility and stability, curcumin-loaded MEVs can maintain or even enhance the compound’s biological activity. Moreover, MEVs retain their stability across varying pH levels and exposure to in vitro gastro-intestinal digestion, essential parameters to take into account especially for oral administration of the nanoformulation. Notably, CurMEVs maintain their dot-like intracellular distribution post-digestion, indicative of endocytic uptake and preserved vesicle integrity, as free curcumin typically yields a diffuse intracellular signal. In line with this, several studies have demonstrated that MEVs from various sources, including bovine, buffalo, pig, and human milk, exhibit remarkable stability under gastrointestinal digestion and more ability in crossing the epithelial barriers [12]. By using both an in vitro IB model and testing in vivo biodistribution in Caenorhabditis elegans, it is clear that MEV-encapsulated Cur exhibits significantly greater diffusion in gastro-intestinal tract compared to the free form. Despite the differences in terms of cellular systems used, our results perfectly align with previous evidence where, using a Caco-2 monoculture IB system, encapsulated Cur in milk-derived EVs crossed more the epithelium compared to the free form of the compound [10]. On note, multiple studies have demonstrated that MEVs are absorbed by intestinal epithelial cells, which facilitates their entry into the bloodstream [60–63]. Once in circulation, these EVs quickly reach and accumulate in various organs, including the liver, kidneys, spleen, lungs, and heart, after 6 h of oral administration [63]. This improved diffusion of CurMEVs likely contributed to the observed extension of lifespan in treated worms. The enhanced longevity in CurMEVs-treated worms aligns with previous studies showing that improved curcumin delivery, particularly via nanoparticle or vesicle formulations, enhances its therapeutic effects, including lifespan extension and anti-aging benefits [64–66].

Taken together, this study reinforces the notion that MEV isolation protocols significantly shape vesicle characteristics and, consequently, their therapeutic utility. Enzymatic casein removal via REN, when combined with scalable TFF processing, offers a reproducible and industrially viable platform to produce functionally competent MEVs. Future studies should explore how these findings translate across different drug cargo and disease models, and whether REN-MEVs maintain their performance in more complex in vivo systems.

Conclusions

In this study, we compared three casein removal methods, rennet treatment, acetic acid precipitation, and ultracentrifugation, to identify the most effective approach for the isolation of high-quality MEVs suitable for drug delivery applications. Our results demonstrate that REN treatment allows us to obtain MEVs with superior purity, structural integrity, and biochemical properties compared to AA and UC methods. REN-treated whey resulted in complete casein removal, minimizing contamination, and preserving the characteristics of MEVs. Importantly, in combination with TFF, REN treatment allowed scalable MEV isolation without compromising the quality of the vesicles, supporting its applicability in industrial-scale production. We also demonstrated that REN-treated MEVs can effectively encapsulate and stabilize curcumin, significantly improving its bioavailability and biological activity. In vitro and in vivo models confirmed that curcumin-loaded MEVs exhibit enhanced gastrointestinal diffusion and had pro-longevity effects. This work not only confirms previous findings on the suitability of milk-derived EVs for drug delivery but also introduces an innovative, cost-effective, and scalable strategy using commercial rennet for the isolation of MEVs from whey, a by-product of the dairy industry, offering a dual advantage in both therapeutic development and waste valorization. These findings highlight the potential of REN-MEVs as robust, scalable, and effective nanocarriers for oral drug delivery and pave the way for further studies on milk-derived vesicle-based therapeutics.
