Synthesis of BODIPY‐Linked N‐Heterocyclic Carbene Metal Complexes for Cancer Chemotheranostics and Catalysis Applications
Authors: Shraddha SP Yadav, Ananda Guha Majumdar, Manish Ramesh Shingole, Seemita Banerjee, Mahesh Subramanian, Rohit Singh Chauhan, Soumyaditya Mula
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFluorescent N‐heterocyclic carbene (NHC) metal complexes are useful for various chemical and biological applications. In this study, we developed a simple strategy to synthesize BODIPY‐linked NHC metal complexes involving Ag, Cu, Ni, and Pd. The synthesis began with the preparation of BODIPY‐imidazolium salt as a precursor ligand. This ligand was used to obtain BODIPY‐imidazolium silver carbene complex, which subsequently facilitated the syntheses of BODIPY‐linked NHC transition metal complexes (Cu, Ni, and Pd) via the transmetalation method. This approach offers a versatile pathway for the synthesis of various other fluorescent carbene metal complexes. The applications of these BODIPY‐linked NHC metal complexes were also explored. Chemotherapeutic anti‐cancer activity of BODIPY‐imidazolium silver carbene complex showed high cancer cell killing efficacy as compared to its precursor ligand. This outcome confirmed that the attachment of the silver atom is essential to enhance the cytotoxic activities. Additionally, the fluorescence property of the BODIPY moiety is highly useful for cellular imaging, which establishes the silver complex as a potential chemotheranostic agent for cancer management. Furthermore, BODIPY‐imidazolium copper carbene complex was explored as a non‐noble metal‐based catalyst for ammonia borane (AB) hydrolysis to generate hydrogen. This catalytic system was further applied for chemoselective reduction of the aromatic formyl groups in the presence of other reducible functional groups. Overall, this investigation presents a simple and efficient design strategy for the synthesis of different BODIPY‐linked metal carbene complexes with potential applications in catalysis, cellular imaging, and cancer therapy.

Introduction

N‐Heterocyclic carbenes (NHC) are well‐known electron‐donating ligands that are capable of forming complexes with transition metals as well as main group elements, providing arrays of materials useful for various applications.[ 1 ] NHC metal complexes are generally synthesized by using heterocyclic scaffolds such as imidazole, thiazole, triazole, benzimidazole, acyclic diamine, etc.[ 1 ] Besides their easy and inexpensive synthesis, NHCs offer ample opportunity to functionalize at the nitrogen atom or carbon backbone of corresponding NHC precursors, enabling to tune their electronic and steric properties.[ 2 ] Leveraging these functionalization flexibilities, organic dyes were previously attached with NHC complexes for their applications in cellular bioimaging,[ 3 ] cancer therapy,[ 3 ] sensing,[ 3 ] photocatalysis,[ 3 ] etc.

In the past few decades, the boron‐dipyrromethene (BODIPY) class of dyes has been explored for countless applications.[ 4 ] Superior photophysical and electrochemical properties are the main attractive features of this class of dyes.[ 5 ] BODIPY dyes were also linked with various ligands capable of complexation with transition metals and main group elements to develop a variety of organometallic compounds. These complexes are widely used for photocatalysis, energy harvesting, photodynamic therapy, cellular imaging, etc.[ 6 ] Attachment of different ligands with the BODIPY moiety is sometimes challenging, and also different ligands are required for complexation with different metal ions, which makes the synthetic methodology tedious. In this respect, N‐heterocyclic carbenes (NHC) act as a universal ligand for various transition metals as well as main group metals. But reports on BODIPY‐linked NHC metal complexes are still limited. In the recent past, a few reports were published on syntheses of BODIPY‐linked NHC metal complexes and their applications in catalysis, photodynamic and antimicrobial therapy, and chemodosimetry.[ 7 ] In this work, our aim was to develop a new BODIPY‐linked NHC ligand that will be useful for synthesizing different metal carbene complexes. This synthetic strategy was anticipated to provide an array of compounds with outstanding properties of both NHCs and BODIPY, useful for various applications. So we developed the synthetic protocol for the synthesis of BODIPY‐imidazole‐based NHC ligand and its corresponding NHC metal carbene complexes with silver, copper, nickel, and palladium.

The medicinal applications of silver‐attached compounds are well documented in the literature.[ 8 ] For instance, silver compounds have been used as antimicrobial agents for centuries.[ 9 ] Also, silver complexes have demonstrated significant anticancer activities with improved pharmacological properties.[ 10 ] But, pharmaceutical research on organometallic NHC‐silver complexes is not well explored so far.

Recent studies have shown that NHC‐silver complexes exhibit notable anti‐cancer activity[ 11 ] with higher efficacy, reduced side effects, and lower resistance development compared to established chemotherapeutic agents like cisplatin, carboplatin, and oxaliplatin.[ 12 ] To the best of our knowledge, BODIPY‐linked NHC‐silver complexes have not yet been used as chemotherapeutic agents for killing cancers. Fluorescence properties of BODIPY dyes can also be used for cellular imaging, simultaneously to check the accumulation as well as localization of the complex in the tumour cells. Thus, we explored the cellular imaging as well as chemotherapeutic anticancer activities of the synthesized BODIPY‐NHC silver complex. Also, anticipating the potential catalytic abilities of BODIPY‐NHC transition metal complexes,[ 12 ] their catalytic activities in ammonia borane (AB) hydrolysis to generate hydrogen were investigated towards chemoselective hydrogenation of organic functional groups. All these results are discussed below.

Result and Discussion

Synthesis and Photophysical Properties

First, BODIPY‐imidazolium salt (4) was designed as a versatile precursor for the synthesis of different NHC–metal complexes. The ligand 4 was synthesized in four steps starting from nitro‐BODIPY dye (1), which was synthesized as per the reported protocol (Scheme 1).[ 13 ] Nitro‐BODIPY dye (1) was reduced using a Pd‐catalyzed hydrogenation reaction to furnish anilino‐BODIPY dye (2).[ 14 ] Reaction of chloroacetyl chloride with dye 2 gave chloroacetyl amido‐BODIPY (3).[ 15 ] Finally, BODIPY 3 was refluxed with 1‐methyl imidazole to furnish BODIPY‐imidazolium salt (4) in good yield (Scheme 1).

Synthesis of BODIPY‐imidazolium salt (4). Reaction conditions: a) 10% Pd/C, DCM/EtOH (1:1), 25 °C, 12 h; b) chloroacetyl chloride, DCM, 0–5 °C, 30 min; c) 1‐methylimidazole, CH3CN, reflux, 18 h.

All the compounds were characterized by 1H and 13C NMR spectroscopy as well as by mass spectrometry. In the 1H NMR spectrum of BODIPY 4, signals for both BODIPY as well as the imidazolium moiety were seen (Figures 1 and S9). For example, aryl protons of the BODIPY core resonated as doublets at 7.82 and 7.31 ppm with two proton integration each. Methyl proton signals were observed as singlets at 2.47 and 1.36 ppm for C3/C5 and C1/C7 protons, respectively. Methyl and methylene protons of the ethyl groups were resonated at 0.99 (triplet) and 2.35 ppm (quartet), respectively. All the aryl protons of the imidazolium moiety appeared as singlets at 9.01, 7.67 and 7.64 ppm which are attributed to the protons at the C2 (NCHN), C5, and C4 positions of the imidazole ring, respectively. The characteristic methyl and methylene protons attached to the imidazolium moiety were resonated as singlets at 3.99 and 5.25 ppm, respectively. The 13C NMR spectrum showed 6 aliphatic peaks and 12 aromatic signals with characteristic amide carbonyl resonance at 163.7 ppm and methylene signal at 50.9 ppm (Figure S10). Another signature peak for C2 (NCHN) of imidazolium ring was observed at 137.9 ppm, confirming the presence imidazole moiety in dye 4.[ 16 ] The imidazolium salt is highly colored, strongly fluorescent and highly stabile in both air and moisture, remaining stable in both the solid state and in solution.

1H NMR spectrum of BODIPY 4 (red) and 5 (black).

The BODIPY‐imidazolium salt (4) was utilized to synthesize the NHC‐silver metal complex. One common approach for synthesizing NHC silver complexes involves the reaction of imidazolium salts with a silver source like Ag2O, resulting in strong silver─carbon bonds.[ 17 ] A similar approach was taken in our case, and the reaction of BODIPY‐imidazolium salt (4) with Ag2O in dichloromethane at room temperature furnished BODIPY‐imidazolium silver complex (5) in a very good yield (70%) (Scheme 2). The conversion was confirmed by the NMR spectroscopy. The 1H NMR spectrum of complex (5) showed an upfield shift in C‐4 and C‐5 proton signals of the imidazole rings (Figures 1 and S11). Additionally, the absence of a downfield NCHN signal in the 1H NMR spectrum confirmed the successful formation of BODIPY‐imidazolium silver complex (5). In the 13C NMR spectrum, the carbene carbon (NCHN) resonance observed in 4 at 137.9 ppm shifted downfield to 182.2 ppm in 5, which is a typical signature for carbene–silver complexes (Figure S12).[ 18 ] Further, the molecular ion peak of complex 5 in the HRMS spectrum confirmed its formation.

Synthesis of BODIPY‐imidazolium metal complexes. Reaction conditions: a) Ag2O, dry DCM, 25 °C, 10 h; b) CuCl, dry DCM, 25 °C, 8 h; c) [NiBr2(DME)], dry DCM, 25 °C, 8 h; d) [Pd Cl2(CH3CN)2], dry DCM, 25 °C, 8 h.

Next, BODIPY‐imidazolium silver carbene complex (5) was exploited to synthesize BODIPY‐linked NHC‐transition metal complexes via the transmetalation method, where the easily labile silver atom of 5 was replaced by different transition metals.[ 2c ] First, copper complex (6) was synthesized by stirring 5 with CuCl in dry CH2Cl2 at room temperature as an orange solid.[ 19 ] In the 1H NMR spectrum of 6, characteristic signals for both BODIPY as well as C‐4 and C‐5 protons of the imidazolium moiety were observed, while the signal for carbene proton (NCHN) was absent as expected (Figure S13). Further, mass spectrometric analyses confirmed the successful formation of the mono BODIPY‐imidazolium copper carbene complex (6). The coordination of BODIPY‐NHC ligands around the silver and copper center in 5 and 6, respectively, suggests linear geometry, which is characteristic of d10 coinage metal complexes in the + 1 oxidation state. Such linear arrangements arise due to the preference for minimal steric hindrance and electronic repulsion in two‐coordinate systems. Similar structural motifs are well‐documented for Ag(I) and Cu(I) complexes bearing N‐heterocyclic carbene ligands.[ 19 ]

After the successful formation of complex 6, we were interested in exploring whether two equivalents of the carbene ligand could be transferred to [NiBr2(DME)] to synthesize a BODIPY‐linked nickel carbene complex via the transmetalation method. Interestingly, the reaction between [NiBr2(DME)] and BODIPY‐imidazolium silver complex (5) in dry CH2Cl2 at room temperature resulted in BODIPY‐imidazolium nickel carbine complex (7) after appropriate treatment, yielding the desired products in good yields as an orange solid. 1H and 13C NMR showed all the characteristic peaks for BODIPY as well as imidazolium moieties as expected (Figures S15, S16).[ 19 ] Further, mass spectrometric analyses confirmed the successful formation of complex 7. The well‐resolved and sharp multiplicities observed in the NMR spectrum (1H and 13C) of complex 7 are indicative of its diamagnetic behaviour, which in turn suggests the presence of a low‐spin Ni(II) center. Such an electronic configuration is typically associated with a square planar geometry, implying a coordination number of four around the nickel metal center.,[ 19 , 20 ] Finally, BODIPY‐imidazolium palladium carbene complex (8) was synthesized via transmetalation of 5 using [PdCl2(CH3CN)2] as the source of Pd.[ 21 ] The chemical structure was confirmed by 1H and 13C NMR spectroscopy as well as by mass spectrometric analyses (Figures S17, S18). A square planar geometry is commonly expected for Pd(II) complexes. In the present study, the coordination environment around the palladium center involves two carbene carbon atoms from N‐heterocyclic carbene (NHC) ligands and two chloride ions, resulting in a stable and electronically favorable configuration.[ 21 ]

In another approach, BODIPY‐imidazolium salt (4) was also exploited to synthesize NHC‐transition metal complexes via direct method. For these, mixture of BODIPY‐imidazolium salt (4) and base (K2CO3) was refluxed with different transition metals like copper, nickel and palladium separately.[ 16 ] Unfortunately, no NHC metal complex was obtained in any of the reactions. Probably, the BODIPY‐imidazolium salt (4) degraded in the stringent reaction condition.

Spectroscopic properties of the ligand, BODIPY‐imidazolium salt (4) and BODIPY‐imidazolium metal carbene complexes 5–8 were evaluated in DCM (Table S2). The ligand 4 showed typical absorption spectra of BODIPY dye with maximum absorbance (λ abs) at 523 nm. It has a green‐yellow fluorescence (Φfl = 0.28) with emission maximum (λem) at 535 nm (Figure S1). Complexation of the ligand 4 with silver, copper, nickel, and palladium did not change the spectroscopic properties much. Both the absorption and fluorescence spectra of BODIPY‐imidazolium metal carbene complexes 5–8 were similar, with λabs at ∼523 nm and λem at ∼535 nm (Figures S2 and S3). Fluorescence quantum yields also remained mostly unaffected after metalation of BODIPY‐imidazolium salt (4). BODIPY moiety in all the complexes (5–8) is not electronically connected and placed far apart from the metal center, thus the fluorescence properties of the BODIPY moiety remained unaltered. This will be advantageous in various optoelectronic and theranostic applications as discussed vide infra.

Cellular Imaging and Chemotherapeutic Activity of Silver Complex 5

To evaluate the cancer cell killing efficacy of the BODIPY‐imidazolium silver carbene complex (5) as compared to its precursor ligand BODIPY‐imidazolium salt (4), their uptake by the cancer cells was checked first. For this, both 4 and 5 were incubated with pancreatic cancer cells (MIA PaCa‐2) for 30 min, and then fluorescence imaging was done using excitation and emission wavelengths of 524 and 536 nm, respectively. Live cell imaging revealed a concentration‐dependent uptake of both 4 and 5 (Figure 2a). Comparing with the nuclei staining dye Hoechst 33 342, it was confirmed that both 4 and 5 are localized in the cytoplasm of the cells (3rd column of Figure 2a).

Evaluation of cellular uptake and cytotoxicity of ligand 4 and silver complex 5: a) Live cell imaging and quantitative measurement of fluorescence intensity to measure cellular uptake of 4 and 5 in MIA PaCa‐2 cells; b) clonogenic assay of MIA PaCa‐2, U2‐OS, A549, and DLD‐1 cells treated with different concentrations of 4 and 5.

Next, cytotoxicity profiles of 4 and 5 were evaluated by clonogenic[ 22 ] and MTT[ 22 ] assays in multiple cancer cell lines of pancreatic (MIA PaCa‐2), bone (U2‐OS), colorectal (DLD‐1), and lung (A549) origin (Figure 2b and S4). Results of long‐term cytotoxicity were evaluated by clonogenic assay, which revealed that BODIPY‐imidazolium silver carbene complex (5) had significantly higher cytotoxicity compared to its precursor 4 in all the cell lines (Figure 2b). For further quantitative measurement of short‐term cytotoxicity, the MTT assay was performed with 4 and 5. Results of the MTT assay recapitulated the results of the clonogenic assay, with 5 displaying significantly higher cytotoxicity compared to 4 (Figure S4). Determination of IC50 values revealed that while 4 had IC50 of >25 µM against all tested cell lines, in that respect 5 had much lower IC50 values of 7.5, 9.2, 7.5, and 7.2 µM, respectively, against MIA PaCa‐2, U2‐OS, DLD‐1, and A549 cells, respectively (Figure S4b). It is important to mention here that the IC50 value of silver complex 5 is either comparable to or better than that of other well‐known anticancer molecules (Table S3). Taken together, all the results reveal that BODIPY‐imidazolium silver carbene complex (5) is efficiently taken up by cancer cells and its fluorescence is useful for cellular imaging.[ 22 ] Also, complex 5 shows significantly higher cytotoxic potential compared to its precursor ligand 4, i.e., attachment of silver enhances the chemotherapeutic efficacy of 5. The cytotoxicity of silver complex 5 under photoirradiation was also evaluated, but no significant increase in cell killing was observed compared to the unirradiated conditions (data not shown). Thus, BODIPY‐imidazolium silver carbene complex (5) is very promising as a potential chemotheranostic agent for cancer management.

In order to characterize the mechanism of action of BODIPY‐imidazolium silver carbene complex (5), we performed flow cytometric analysis of the Sub‐G1 population in MIA PaCa‐2 pancreatic cancer cells in response to treatment with silver complex 5. We treated cells with 5, 10, or 20 µM concentrations of 5 for 24 or 48 h, followed by measurement of apoptotic population through flow cytometry. Silver complex 5 was found to induce apoptosis in MIA PaCa‐2 cells in a time‐ as well as concentration‐dependent manner. Additionally, the characteristic distribution of cells suggested induction of a robust cell cycle arrest in G1 and S phases of the cell cycle (Figures 3a and S5).

Induction of apoptosis and DNA damage in MIA PaCa‐2 cells by silver complex 5. a) Sub‐G1 analysis of MIA PaCa‐2 cells treated with 5, 10, or 20 µM complex 5 for 24 or 48 h. b) γ‐H2AX and 53BP1 foci in MIA PaCa‐2 cells treated with 5, 10, or 20 µM complex 5 for 8 or 24 h. Cells were subjected to treatments followed by immunofluorescent detection of γ‐H2AX and 53BP1 foci.

Induction of DNA damage in cells is reported to trigger G1 and S‐phase arrest in mammalian cells.[ 22 ] Additionally, reports from multiple groups have suggested the capability of silver complexes to participate in diverse interactions with DNA.[ 22 ] These indicators prompted us to investigate the induction of DNA damage as an underlying mechanism of the cytotoxicity of silver complex 5. We treated MIA PaCa‐2 cells with 5, 10, or 20 µM concentrations of complex 5 for 8 or 24 h, followed by evaluation of DNA damage markers γ‐H2AX and 53BP1. γ‐H2AX and 53BP1 are considered to be faithful indicators of DNA damage.[ 22 ] Our results indicated that treatment of complex 5 results in a time‐ and concentration‐dependent elevation in γ‐H2AX as well as 53BP1 levels in MIA PaCa‐2 cells, indicating robust induction of DNA damage in these cells (Figures 3b and S6). The induction of DNA damage by complex 5 further provided a mechanistic basis of silver complex 5‐mediated induction of apoptosis and G1/S arrest. Taken together, our results suggest that silver complex 5 induces DNA damage in pancreatic cancer cells, followed by cell cycle arrest and apoptosis, leading to cell death.

Catalysis Studies With Transition Metal Complexes 6–8

Hydrogen (H2) is a clean energy carrier as well as a sustainable and environmentally friendly reagent for various chemical processes.[ 23 ] But storing and handling of pressurized hydrogen is a real challenge, which can be solved by on‐site generation of hydrogen from solid and liquid sources. In this context, ammonia borane (AB) has emerged as a good hydrogen‐storing material due to its high hydrogen content (19.6 wt%), good solubility in water, low molecular weight, nontoxicity, and excellent stability in ambient conditions.[ 24 ] Theoretically, AB can produce 3 equiv of hydrogen during hydrolysis (Equation 1). The catalytic activity of BODIPY‐imidazolium Cu/Ni/Pd carbene complexes (6–8) was tested for AB hydrolysis at 30 °C to generate hydrogen. The amount of evolved hydrogen was measured at different time intervals by the water displacement method using a standard hydrolysis setup as described in earlier studies.[ 26 ] In a typical experiment, water (50 mL) and catalyst (8 µmol) were taken in the reaction flux and equilibrated at the reaction temperature (30 °C). Then AB (2.5 mmol) was added to the reaction flux and the generated hydrogen was collected in a burette by the water displacement method.

Figure 4 illustrates the hydrogen evolution profile from the AB solution over time in the presence of BODIPY‐imidazolium Cu/Ni/Pd carbene complex catalysts (6–8). All three catalysts are capable of hydrolyzing AB to generate hydrogen. But Cu‐complex (6) showed higher catalytic activity compared to Ni‐complex (7) at 30 °C. Notably, the activity of Cu‐complex (6) is comparable to that of noble metal‐based Pd‐complex (8), suggesting its potential as a viable substitute for noble metal‐based catalysts. The turnover frequency of the catalyst is defined as the number of moles of hydrogen produced per mole of catalyst per unit time (min−1). For the Cu‐complex 6, the turnover frequency was calculated at the point of 50% conversion and found to be 289 min−1, which is comparable/higher than that of many noble metal‐based catalysts (Table S1).

Hydrogen generation from hydrolysis of ammonia borane (2.5 mmol, 50 mL) over BODIPY‐imidazolium Cu/Ni/Pd carbene complex catalysts (6–8) at 30 °C.

After thoroughly understanding the AB hydrolysis process catalyzed by the BODIPY‐imidazolium copper carbene catalyst (6), we proceeded to evaluate its efficacy as a tandem catalytic system, where AB would serve as the hydrogen donor. The objective was to achieve the one‐pot hydrogenation of various organic functional groups such as nitro, formyl, ketone, cyano, etc (Table 1). While noble metal‐based catalysts are well known for facilitating these reductions, we focused on the Cu‐complex because of its high catalytic activity. The experiments commenced with the reduction of organic aldehydes. Initially, the reduction of 3,4‐dimethoxy benzaldehyde (9a) was performed using only AB (without copper catalyst, 6) in methanol at room temperature. The reduction of 9a was very slow, furnishing ∼ 60% of the hydrogenated product 10a in 24 h (Figure S19).[ 26 ] Whereas, when the reduction of 3,4‐dimethoxy benzaldehyde (9a) was performed using 1 mol% copper catalyst and AB in methanol at room temperature, the reaction was very fast, completed almost instantly, as monitored by TLC (Table 1). 1H NMR analyses of the reaction mixture showed the complete disappearance of the formyl peak at 9.85 ppm and appearance of benzylic proton signal at 4.63 ppm, confirming the quantitative conversion (>99%) of the aldehyde to the corresponding alcohol (10a) (Figure S20). Importantly, only 1 mol% of the Cu‐catalyst was sufficient for the quantitative reduction of the formyl group. Thus, BODIPY‐imidazolium copper carbene catalyst (6) enhanced the reaction rate of the reduction of the formyl group immensely. Using the similar reaction condition, another formyl compound, 4‐bromobenzaldehyde (9b) was also reduced quantitatively (>99%) to its corresponding benzyl alcohol (10b) instantly, confirming the high catalytic efficiency of the Cu‐complex (Table 1). In both cases (9a/b), the reactions were so rapid and clean that photoirradiation had no noticeable effect on enhancing the reaction rate.

After the reduction of the aldehyde, we proceeded to investigate the reduction of other functional groups such as cyno, nitro and ketone. For this, reduction of o‐nitrotoluene, m‐nitrotoluene, p‐nitroanisole, 2‐methoxy acetophenone, and benzonitrile was attempted separately under the similar optimized condition. However, no reduced products were obtained in any of the reactions, indicating. the catalytic system is ineffective to reduce cyno, nitro and ketone groups. Subsequently, the reduction of multifunctional organic compounds was examined. For this, 4‐cynobenzaldehyde (9c) was selected as reactant. As expected under the similar optimized condition, cyno group remained unaffected, while only formyl group was reduced to alcohol, yielding 4‐cynobenzylalcohol (10c) as the sole product with quantitative yield (>99%) (Table 1). Compound 10c was characterized by 1H NMR and mass analysis. The disappearance of the formyl peak at 10.09 ppm and appearance of benzylic proton peak at 4.78 ppm confirmed the reduction of the formyl group. Additionally, the absence of any peak corresponding to benzylic amine group confirmed that the cyano group remained unreacted (Figure 5). Even with an increased catalyst amount (2–3 mol%), no reduction of the cyno group was observed, further demonstrating the high chemoselectivity of copper catalyst (6). Similar observation was found for reduction of 4‐nitrobenzaldehyde (9d) where only the formyl group was reduced leaving the nitro group unaffected (Table 1), even after increasing the catalyst amount and extending the reaction time. The product 4‐nitrobenzyl alcohol (10d) was confirmed by 1H NMR and mass spectra analysis, showing corresponding molecular ion peak of compound 10d (Figure S21). These results clearly demonstrate, the high chemoselectivity of the BODIPY‐imidazolium copper carbene catalyst (6) which specifically reduces formyl group.

1H NMR of 4‐cyno benzaldehyde (9c) and 4‐cynobenzylalcohol (10c).

Formyl groups are generally easier to reduce than nitro, cyano, and ketone groups due to their more electrophilic nature. Also, formyl carbonyl groups are less sterically crowded than those of ketones. Thus, aldehydes can be readily reduced by mild reducing agents, whereas other groups (nitro, cyano, and ketone) require stronger reducing agents as well as more aggressive conditions. Also, due to the soft nucleophilic nature of the borane, it preferentially reacts with the formyl carbonyl group due to its greater reactivity. Thus, the soft nucleophilic nature of the borane and the higher reactivity of the formyl group exhibit remarkable chemoselectivity for formyl group reduction. The probable mechanism of reduction of the formyl group is shown in Scheme S1. The reduction of aldehyde 9a by ammonia borane without BODIPY‐imidazolium copper carbene catalyst (6) was also checked, which took a long time (24 h) for the conversion of 60% 9a to 10a as discussed vide supra. But, in the presence of the BODIPY‐imidazolium copper carbene catalyst (6), the reaction rate increases immensely, and the corresponding alcohols (10a‐d) were obtained almost instantly with quantitative yields. Thus, the mild reaction conditions, rapid reaction kinetics, and excellent conversion yields make this new method highly effective for the practical synthesis of alcohols from aldehydes.

Conclusions

Thus, we developed a new BODIPY‐linked NHC ligand, BODIPY‐imidazolium salt (4), which is used to synthesize four BODIPY‐imidazolium metal carbene complexes containing silver, copper, nickel, and palladium (5–8). The synthetic strategy showed that various BODIPY‐linked carbene complexes can be synthesized using the same ligand 4. All four BODIPY‐imidazolium metal carbene complexes (5–8) possess properties of both NHCs and BODIPY moieties. Chemotherapeutic anti‐cancer activities of BODIPY‐imidazolium silver carbene complex (5) were evaluated, which showed high cell killing efficacy as compared to its precursor ligand 4. This confirmed the importance of the imidazolium silver complex in showing cytotoxic activities. Detailed mechanistic studies show that silver complex 5 induces DNA damage, followed by cell cycle arrest and apoptosis, leading to cell death. Additionally, the cellular imaging ability of the dye 5 established it as a potential chemotheranostic agent for cancer management in the future. Further, the catalytic activity of BODIPY‐imidazolium copper carbene complex (6) was shown in hydrogen generation by ammonia borane (AB) hydrolysis and chemoselective reduction of the aromatic formyl groups in the presence of other reducible functional groups. Conclusively, the catalyst's effectiveness under mild conditions, along with its rapid reaction kinetics and excellent conversion yields, highlights its potential for practical applications in the chemo‐selective synthesis of alcohols from aldehydes. As a whole, this investigation shows a simple design strategy for the synthesis of different BODIPY‐linked metal carbene complexes useful for catalysis, cellular imaging, as well as cancer chemotherapy.

Experimental Section

Synthesis of Anilino‐BODIPY (2)[ 5 ]

A solution of nitro‐BODIPY (1) (100 mg, 0.25 mmol) in DCM/EtOH (1:1; 30 mL) was degassed properly, 10% Pd/C (10 mg, 10 mol%) was added in to it and the mixture was stirred under H2 at 25 °C for 12 h. Then the reaction mixture was passed through a Celite pad, the filtrate was evaporated and the crude mixture was purified by column chromatography (silica gel, EtOAc/petroleum ether (20:80)) to furnish pure anilino‐BODIPY 2 (78 mg, 85%). 1H NMR (500 MHz, CDCl3) δ: 7.00 (d, J = 8.5 Hz, 2H), 6.77 (d, J = 8.5 Hz, 2H), 2.52 (s, 6H), 2.31 (q, J = 7.5 Hz, 4H), 1.39 (s, 6H), 0.98 (t, J = 7.5 Hz, 6H); 13C{1H}(125 MHz, CDCl3) δ: 153.8, 139.6, 138.3, 132.8, 130.9, 129.7, 118.9, 29.7, 17.0, 14.6, 12.5, 11.9, 0.9 ppm.

Synthesis of Chloroacetyl Amido‐BODIPY (3)[ 15 ]

To an ice‐cold (0–5 °C) solution of anilino‐BODIPY (2) (100 mg, 0.25 mmol) in dry dichloromethane (15 mL), chloroacetyl chloride (34 mg, 0.30 mmol) was added dropwise with constant stirring. After the addition, the temperature of the reaction mixture was allowed to increase up to room temperature, and stirring was continued for 30 min. After that, the reaction was quenched with water, and the organic residues were extracted with dichloromethane. The organic layer was dried over sodium sulfate (Na2SO4), filtered, and the filtrate was reduced under vacuum to obtain the crude product. The crude product was subsequently purified by column chromatography (silica gel, EtOAc/petroleum ether (20:80)) to get pure 3 (80 mg, 67%). 1H NMR (500 MHz, CDCl3) δ: 8.38 (s, 1H), 7.72 (d, J = 10 Hz, 2H), 7.28 (d, J = 10 Hz, 2H), 4.24 (s, 2H), 2.53 (s, 6H), 2.30 (q, J = 7.5 Hz, 4H), 1.31 (s, 6H), 0.97 (t, J = 7.5 Hz, 6H); 13C{1H}(125 MHz, CDCl3) δ: 163.8, 153.9, 139.2, 138.2, 137.3, 132.9, 132.5, 130.8, 129.2, 120.1, 42.9, 17.0, 14.6, 12.5, 11.9 ppm.

Synthesis of BODIPY‐Imidazolium Salt (4)

General Procedure for Catalytic Hydrogenation

To a methanol (7 mL) solution of the organic compound (0.1 mmol) in a Schlenk tube, copper catalyst (6) (1 mol%) and AB (0.5 mg) were added simultaneously. The resulting solution was stirred at room temperature, and reactions were monitored with TLC. After completion of the reaction, the solvent was concentrated under reduced pressure to get the corresponding products, which were analyzed by 1H NMR.
