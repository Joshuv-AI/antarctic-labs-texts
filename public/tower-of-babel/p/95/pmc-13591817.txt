Dental school caries and periodontitis risk analysis skillset: survey of carryover to practice
Authors: David C. Johnsen, Carlos Garaicoa-Pazmino, Aditi Jain, Leonardo Marchini, Kim Vo, Sara Miller, Jack Rossi, Wei Shi, Derek Smith
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundScarce evidence is available in how clinicians process the management of high-risk individuals in real-world scenarios. The purpose of the present study is to discern the degree to which graduates apply explicit risk analysis skillsets in practice over time, and how graduates prioritize risk factors for caries and periodontitis.MethodsThe importance of risk factors for caries and periodontitis and management of low/high risk private practice patients were surveyed among dental graduates within the last ten years. Surveys were sent from August 2025 through October 2025. Data patterns were observed with statistics as support.ResultsA total of 73 surveys were obtained. For 75% of respondents, risk level influenced treatment planning. For caries-affected patients, respondents ranked risk from highest to lowest with all being considered worthy: oral hygiene, current disease level, patient compliance, sugared beverages, xerostomia, prognosis, no treatment/monitor conditions for up to 5 years, dental literacy, fluoride exposure, social barriers, and general health. For periodontitis, respondents’ rankings were similar with smoking and diabetes also ranking high. Most respondents (71%) stated that they adapted ideas from dental school to practice and were more likely to score low risk patients with a more optimistic prognosis than high risk patients.ConclusionsDental graduates take risk factors into account in making patient decisions in practice. Similarities in the thought process was noted in determining risk for caries- and periodontitis-affected patients. Oral hygiene, current disease level, and patient compliance are risk factors with consistent high ratings for both caries and periodontitis, indicating the importance of engaging the patient to improve health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09804-5.

Background

An essential routine activity for general dentists is to assess patient risk for caries or periodontitis as a foundation for treatment planning and for long-term follow‐up to maximize sustained patient health [1, 2]. While the pathogenesis and treatment of dental caries and periodontitis are distinctly different, the thought processes in conducting risk assessment are essentially identical.

It remains unclear how dental graduates apply caries and periodontal risk-assessment concepts in clinical practice. One complication is a “substantial variation among selected models in assessing risk levels” for caries [3]. Moreover, it is unknown whether graduates continue to practice according to what they were taught in school. Everyday clinical practice can differ significantly from the environment at dental schools.

An on-going exploratory model combining analyses for caries and periodontitis has been reported and implemented for predoctoral students for over seven years [2, 4]. The thought process for the student is essentially identical in seminars for risk analyses for caries and periodontitis; the exceptions are that periodontitis analysis prioritizes medical conditions (e.g., diabetes, smoking and obesity) whereas caries analysis includes relevant factors such as high sugar diet, xerostomia and fluoride exposure. The remaining factors are similarly assessed: current disease level, oral hygiene, patient compliance, prognosis, and social factors (e.g., domestic stability, finances, transportation, disability, etc.). A first step in the risk assessment for either caries or periodontitis, is for the practitioner to identify major risk factors. The second step in risk assessment for caries or periodontitis is to determine the capacity of the patient to subscribe to professional recommendations [1, 5]. Success in this assessment is a basis for projecting risk and prognosis. A third step is to assign risk and prognosis, considering both immediate risk and potential disease progression [6]. The final step is the disease (caries or periodontitis) management that occurs at three different levels: managing the person, treating the disease, and mitigating the disease consequences.

A question arises whether mitigating the disease consequences without managing the person can bring sustained, long-term health. This risk assessment model leads the student to try to manage the person before attempting to treat the disease or mitigate disease consequences. A recent study [2] including a student sample of patients seeking advanced periodontal care and routine specialty procedures (e.g. dental implants, restorations in the esthetics zone), the authors were surprised to see how sharply the students discerned between low- and high-risk individuals, based on diet control among patients affected with dental caries and the influence of systemic/environmental factors (e.g., diabetes, smoking, obesity) upon the disease severity among periodontitis-affected patients.

A conundrum for faculty, student, and dental education institutions is the dual mission to bring oral health to its patients and at the same time train students with an array of preventive and reparative procedures for practice [7]. A key question among clinicians is: “Does treatment bring/stabilize health?.” For healthy people—those who know about prevention, avoid sugar and tobacco, attend regular check-ups, and enjoy domestic stability, financial security, and transportation—the answer can be “Yes!.” Occasional disease recurrences can be alleviated with treatment, and in this situation, dentists help patients to maintain their oral health.

For higher risk groups with recurring disease, there is a lack of proven interventions to bring sustained oral health [8, 9]. Preventive and reparative procedures for higher risk individuals with recurring disease tend to mitigate rather than eliminate or even control disease [1, 2, 7]. A high-risk person who is unable to provide enough self-caring with circumstances largely beyond their control, or who is unwilling to provide enough self-caring, the challenge for the student/dentist to provide curing increases greatly.

Scarce evidence is available in how clinicians process the management of high-risk individuals in real-world scenarios. Moreover, it remains unknown if the clinician’s confidence changes when dealing with low- and high-risk individuals and whether their decision-making process follows a specific pattern (e.g. structured, intuitive). Thus, a hypothesis is that graduates will take part of what they learned in school and part from practice.

The lack of a risk assessment tool to better consolidate the analyses for caries and periodontitis remains as an important research gap within dentistry. A plan for refining current critical thinking skillsets gathered in dental school will depend on input regarding current carryover to practice. Thus, the purpose of the present survey is to determine the degree to which graduates take critical thinking skillsets from dental school into practice.

Methods

The purposes of the project are to (1) discern the degree to which graduates apply concepts in risk analysis for caries and periodontitis into practice; (2) discern the level of importance graduates place on specific risk factors; (3) discern similarities and differences graduates place on trying to manage patients at high risk for recurring disease compared to patients at low risk for recurring disease; and (4) glean perceptions to improve critical thinking learning guidance for risk analysis in dental school.

This is a cross-sectional study using an anonymous survey. The present study met the definition of human subjects research; thus, it was approved by the University of Iowa Institutional Review Board (IRB) (IRB #202508026). Since a written consent form is not required per federal regulations due to the nature of the study, an IRB-approved information sheet was electronically provided to potential subjects. By completing the survey questions, this indicated consent. The present survey was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and fully adhered to the Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies (CROSS) guidelines (Appendix A in Supplementary Material).

Recruitment of eligible participants

Potential respondents were identified from lists of graduates from the University of Iowa College of Dentistry (UI-COD) for the previous ten years. Eligible participants must be UI-COD graduates with a DDS degree within the previous ten years. Logistical tasks included: draft a cover letter for potential participants, collaborate with biostatistics colleagues, perform a pilot survey with recent graduates, acquire a list of DDS graduates from the last ten years from the University of Iowa Alumni Office, convert the survey to Qualtrics, distribute the survey via email from the Office of External Relations, retrieve/analyze results, develop a report addressing project purposes and share the report with appropriate constituencies. Potential respondents were identified from a list of e-mail addresses provided by the University of Iowa Alumni Office. Two separate emailing waves were conducted between August 18, 2025 - September 1, 2025, and between October 1, 2025 - October 29, 2025. For the initial mailing, potential respondents received wording on the purpose of the survey and that the survey was entirely anonymous. A reminder e-mail was sent to all in the survey thanking those who had responded and requesting non-respondents to participate. This process was done for two e-mail waves.

Questionnaire/data collection

Questions for the survey (Appendix B in Supplementary Material) were developed to try to get a sense of practices used by alumni regarding risk for caries and periodontitis. A pilot survey was conducted with ten faculty dentists. Reasons for the survey were driven by the lack of interventions to bring sustained health to groups of high-risk people with recurring disease – both caries and periodontitis [7]. Questions followed the format of a previous questionnaire gaining insights on use of critical thinking skillsets from dental school used into practice [10]. Since risk assessment is a critical thinking skill, the format was viewed to have transferability. In addition to being of value in understanding what practices alumni were using and whether teaching was influencing practice patterns, we were hoping to use results to affect teaching materials and practices. Teaching methods could either be reinforced or changed based on results. The questions were in four categories: (1) Use of risk in making treatment decisions, (2) Importance of an array of individual risk factors, (3) Carryover of risk practices from school to practice, and (4) Optimism gaining sustained health for people of higher risk and lower risk. A question on whether analysis was done more intuitively defined as “using a natural understanding and/or common sense rather the formal proofs” or in more structured fashion defined as “a definitive, organized pattern with a clear relationship between their components” were used in a previous survey [10].

Methodology

For relevance of survey questions, five referees (teaching faculty experienced with surveys and with experience in peer reviewed publication) were asked to rate each item on a four-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (not relevant) to 4 (very relevant). A content validity index (CVI) was obtained by dividing the number of referees who choose options 3 and 4 into the total number of referees. The accepted CVI is 0.80 [11]. A low CVI indicates that certain items should be eliminated or revised to establish sufficient content validity. The two questions on confidence in a patient gaining minimum disease gained a score of 0.8; all other questions tested gained a score of 1.0. The CVI was judged adequate to proceed. Between the pilot survey and the CVI one question was deleted, and the rest remained with the original wording.

Questions related to application of critical thinking skillsets in practice captured responses on a 5-level Likert scale. The format was considered validated in a previous study with application of the format extended to risk assessment in the current project. Validity of the current approach will be viewed over time with added scrutiny [10]. On this numeric scale, a score of 1 (not much) to 5 (a great deal) indicated the most negative and positive response, respectively, to the extent of which the graduate’s experience in University of Iowa College of Dentistry help them to implement or develop critical thinking skills.

Statistical analysis

Results were recorded electronically for statistical analysis with R (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria). A descriptive analysis reporting the frequencies of responses to each survey question was performed. Count and frequencies were calculated for categorical responses. Likert type responses regarding how important a feature is, were categorized with the lower three levels of importance being collapsed (1,2,3) and the upper two levels being collapsed (4,5) into a single binary variable. Chi-square test was used in subsequent bivariate comparisons. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value of < 0.05.

Results

There were 594 total email addresses, 53 bounced back for a total of 541 successful emails, which targeted the same alumni who were emailed multiple times. Of 541 surveys sent out over the two e-mailing sequences, 73 surveys were returned for a return rate of 13%. The sample size and percent of responses by year of graduation are shown in Table 1. The responses ranged from 4 to 11 for respective years with a median of 7 responses. Of surveys received, most questions were answered and resulting in 1533 possible responses, there were 10 non-responses for a 99% rate of responses to individual questions. Analysis of data from the two mailings were similar with no differences in data patterns.

Importance of risk assessment

For the first purpose, “Discern the degree to which graduates apply concepts in risk analysis for caries and periodontitis into practice”, graduates placed a high level of importance on using results of risk assessment in treatment planning. As depicted in Table 2, responses were nearly identical for responses to the two questions, “What level of importance do you place on establishing a risk level for caries or periodontitis for your patients?” and “To what extent does your risk assessment influence your selection of procedures?”. For both questions, the “4” response was the highest (39.7%, n = 29 for caries and 42.5%, n = 31 for periodontitis) with the combined “4” and “5” making up 68.5% (n = 50) and 74.0% (n = 54) of responses, respectively. No respondent rated either question with a “1”.

Risk assessment based on caries and periodontitis

The second purpose is, “Discern the level of importance graduates place on specific risk factors”, responses to questions on caries risk (Fig. 1A). For three factors surveyed, “sugared drinks” received the highest rating with 82.0% (n = 60) giving a 4 or 5, followed by “Xerostomia” with 80.0% (n = 58) giving a 4 or 5 rating, with a drop to “fluoride exposure” with 53.0% (n = 39) rated either a 4 or 5. A statistically significant difference was noted when comparing the combined ratings (4,5 vs. 1,2,3) for sugared drinks and xerostomia against fluoride exposure (Chi-square = 35.2; p < 0.01).

For responses to questions on periodontitis risk (Fig. 1B), smoking received the highest response with 92.0% (n = 67) rating a 4 or 5, followed by diabetes with 76.0% (n = 55) giving a 4 or 5 followed by a large drop to obesity with only 16.0% (n = 12) giving a 4 or 5. A statistically significant difference was noted when comparing combined ratings (4,5 vs. 1,2,3) for smoking and diabetes against obesity (Chi-square = 127.8; p < 0.01).

For scores for either caries or periodontitis (Table 3 and Fig. 1C), “current disease level” had 97.0% (n = 71) giving a 4 or 5, followed “oral hygiene” response with 96.0% (n = 70) giving a 4 or 5, followed by “patient compliance” with 88.0% (n = 64) giving a 4 or 5, followed by “your prognosis” with 80.0% (n = 58) giving a 4 or 5. A drop was seen for the next four factors, with “social barriers” with 48.0% (n = 35), “nothing done for five years” with 57.0% (n = 42) giving a 4 or 5, followed by “general health” with 45.0% (n = 33) giving a 4 or 5 and “dental literacy” with 44.0% (n = 32) giving a 4 or 5. A statistically significant difference was noted when comparing combined ratings (4,5 vs. 1,2,3) for disease level, compliance and your prognosis versus health, social barriers, and dental literacy (Chi-square = 83.3; p < 0.01).

Translation of risk assessment into clinical practice

For the third purpose, “Discern the degree of adaptation of risk analysis into practice”, the question “Which describes the way you make decisions on risk assessment?” resulted in choices of “Intuitive” and “Structured” thought processes. “Intuitive” having 65.8% (n = 48) of responses and “Structured” having 34.2% (n = 25) of responses. For the question, “Which of the following is the best description of your practice activities for risk analysis?”, results were more strongly toward using ideas from the UI-COD and followed by formulation of their own thought processes, less for “I use the thought processes largely as offered in the University of Iowa College of Dentistry” and ultimately, followed as a distant third, “I do not use ideas from the exercises in the College” (Table 4).

Provider’s confidence to treat high risk patients

For the fourth purpose, “Discern similarities and differences graduates place on trying to manage patients at high risk for recurring disease compared to patients at low risk for recurring disease”, results were starkly different as depicted in Fig. 2. For the question, “How confident are you a person at low risk/minimal disease will remain with minimal disease?” responses were mostly 3, 4, or 5; there was not a 1 score. For the question, “How confident are you that a person at high risk with recurring disease will convert to sustained health (minimum disease)?”, 15% (n = 11) of respondents gave a 4 or 5 compared to over 89% of respondents (n = 65) who gave a 4 or 5 to the question on low-risk patients. The most common score was a 2 for high-risk people.

Results for the fourth purpose were further analyzed by comparing responses for each individual respondent; data were collected for each respondent as (1) low risk patients were scored with a more optimistic outcome than high risk patients, (2) low risk and high risk patients were scored the same, and (3) low risk patients were scored with a less optimistic prognosis than high risk patients. By a wide margin, individual respondents were more likely to score low risk patients with a more optimistic prognosis than high-risk patients: 84.9% (n = 62) respondents gave a higher score to low-risk patients compared to high-risk patients; 11.0% (n = 8) gave the same score to both high- and low-risk patients, and 2.7% (n = 2) gave a higher score to high-risk patients compared to low-risk patients. Only one participant (1.3%) provided no response to this question. The most common set of responses was the assignment of a 4 for the low-risk scenario to a 2 for the high-risk scenario.

Discussion

Given the relatively low response rate, the similarity between data for the first and second mailings, and the high quality of responses, these results should be interpreted with caution for generalizability. Nevertheless, they provide a valuable preliminary view of practice patterns in risk analysis. Regarding whether graduates use a more intuitive or structured approach, results are consistent with a previous survey leaning toward a more intuitive decision-making approach [10].

Interpretations are possible from the data starting with the first objective which is discern the degree to which graduates apply concepts in risk analysis for caries and periodontitis into practice. Risk assessment is an important step for most dentists but moderately influences the selection of procedures/treatment. We hypothesize for future studies that graduates see disease mitigation/management as more directly attributed to patient habits than background factors – with some overlap in these two considerations. The gap between factors about patient habits – oral hygiene, smoking, sweet consumption, compliance, etc. – and background factors – general health, social barriers, dental literacy, fluoride exposure etc. – was unexpected, given the connection between upstream determinants and habits.

A second purpose is to discern the level of importance graduates place on specific risk factors. A break/gap for factors on personal habits is worthy of further study; the comment refers to responses in personal habits judged as more important and factors on background judged as less important. Interestingly, most dentists surveyed use concepts from dental school and applied into their practice. Results from the present study are highly similar to those from a previous survey of graduates with the largest response being “I use ideas from the College and adapt to practice” [10]. As with the previous survey, more than 95% respondents reported that they did use ideas from the dental school, indicating the impact of explicit critical thinking exercises following a designated thought process.

The third purpose of the project is to discern similarities and differences graduates place on trying to manage patients at high risk for recurring disease compared to patients at low risk for recurring disease. Most dentists surveyed did not feel confident in bringing sustained health to high-risk patients with recurring disease. Graduates appear skeptical that such patients will change, focusing instead on factors that patients can control. One interpretation of these data is graduates view the diseases of caries and periodontitis as dynamic conditions more than inert dentitions – a human more than a typodont. Beyond the scope of the survey is the connection between social (upstream) determinants and health. Factors that connect habits and upstream determinants were not included, for example access to hygiene aids or healthy foods for low income or people in remote locations.

There are implications for the educator. Graduates have figured out that high risk people have a lower chance for sustained health. A question is raised on whether to differentiate between high risk and low risk patients in our counseling and who owns the problem. We are not aware of how widespread an escalating approach is to guide high-risk patients as previously described [7]. It seems worthwhile to explore other places in patient care where the thought process from two different disciplines are essentially identical, bringing these closer together for the benefit of the patient [12].

At the moment, we do not have successful interventions to bring sustained health to high-risk people with recurring disease [7]. An implication for the educator and the practitioner is that high-risk patients will take more time and energy to get them on the path to sustained health. Such an intervention would include societal level factors – socioeconomic status, access to care, dental insurance, family structure – and would be multidisciplinary [13].

Another factor is that there is limited reimbursement for preventive care. If we reimburse, is there an expectation that some health benefit will come from the money spent? Interpretation: spending more time and money beyond a standard level on people with high risk and recurring disease may not bring health, but only more treatment and cost. One approach a student or practitioner can take is, “I will continue to see you as my patient and provide treatment, but unless habits change, more disease will occur, and more treatment will be called for.”

The fourth purpose of the project is to glean perceptions to improve critical thinking learning guidance for risk analysis in dental school. The theoretical learning model based on emulation has held up for over a decade with peer reviewed publications, but without a follow-up into practice for any critical thinking skillsets until recently [10]. Results may suggest a positive overall influence of critical thinking skills gained from the dental school experience and taken into practice. For purpose #1, “Discern the degree to which graduates apply explicit critical thinking skillsets in practice,” results may suggest a positive impact of dental school experiences.

Caries and periodontitis are chronic conditions. If we frame caries and periodontitis only as acute, then treatment requires little involvement of the patient—patients simply go to the dentist to get something “fixed.” If we frame caries and periodontitis as chronic, treatment is unlikely to bring health without full engagement/self-caring of the patient [7]. Much of dentistry involves treatment with limited sustained engagement of the person/patient. Results may suggest that dentists view caries and periodontitis as diseases of a person and not just the dentition. The importance of oral hygiene, patient compliance, smoking and sweets are interpreted to support perceived importance by graduates for engagement with the patient.

With extensive scientific evidence on the concepts of risk analysis, this project adds to the literature on the use of risk assessment in practice and thus, it was considered exploratory. The number of questions on the survey was kept to a minimum with survey-weary dentists. Groupings of questions and individual questions are considered successful in gaining a foothold on practice habits. With current results, follow up questions could be considered. For example, specifics on the way risk analyses are included into treatment plans and the modes of communication with high-risk patients compared with low-risk patients. The stark results are encouraging for continued inquires for graduates. Similarities in the thinking for analyzing risk for caries and periodontitis is seen as reinforced with this project [7]. Graduates completed the parts of the survey where there was no distinction between factors contributing to caries and periodontitis risk.

Limitations

Addressing the complexity of management for high-risk patients in a short survey of graduates is an inherent challenge. Moreover, the survey low response rate limits generalizability. While surveys were anonymous, respondents could assume there is a “correct” answer with a bias in that direction. Caution is called for in over-interpreting results including respondents doing what they say they do. A consideration is that each cohort of alumni may have been taught by different faculty members, reflecting turnover in the educator population over time. The survey is limited to dentists. Inclusion of allied personnel could change the outlook and impact on attitudes. For example, a dental hygienist or dental therapist may be able to spend more time with a high-risk patient and have greater impact. General health is consistently at the bottom on both caries and periodontitis. Why this is important for the educator: Even with efforts of interprofessional collaboration, this could imply dentists still see oral health as separate which is understandable given other risk factors and our specialization of oral health. If high risk patients have problems relating to general health, a call is for continued emphasis on interprofessional education.

Conclusions

Dental graduates take risk factors into account in making patient decisions in practice. Similarities in the thought process were noted in determining risk for caries- and periodontitis-affected patients among surveyed practitioners. Oral hygiene, current disease level, and patient compliance are risk factors with consistent high ratings for both caries and periodontitis, indicating the importance of engaging the patient to improve health.
