Genomic insights into the medieval cultivation history of influential high-yielding Asian rice
Authors: Ngoc Hai Trieu Phong, Yoshihiro Kawahara, Masahiko Kumagai, Takeshi Itoh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh-yielding rice landraces that contributed substantially to historical population expansions were widely cultivated across Asia. These landraces are generally believed to have been Champa rice, which originated in present-day Vietnam. Historical records indicate that Champa rice was disseminated to southern China in the eleventh century and subsequently transmitted to medieval western Japan. Contrary to the recent view that Champa rice was derived from the Aus group, our extensive molecular phylogenetic and population genomic analyses indicate that currently available Vietnamese and Japanese Champa rice candidates are associated with the Indica group. Furthermore, although the Japanese landraces were expected to be phylogenetically closer to Vietnamese Indica landraces, they are distinct from the Southeast Asian Indica subpopulation and instead cluster within the Chinese Indica subpopulation. In addition, although the examined Vietnamese landraces show evidence of hybridization between Indica and Japonica, their Indica-derived components appear to be more closely related to Southeast Asian Indica. These findings suggest that historically influential high-yielding rice in Asia did not originate from a single source; rather, a broader diversity of landraces than previously recognized contributed to improvements in rice productivity through complex dissemination processes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53634-w.Keywords: Champa rice, Red rice, Population growth, High-yielding rice, Population genomicsSubject terms: Evolution, Genetics, Plant sciences

Introduction

The rise and fall of human civilizations have been closely coordinated with regional demographic changes. Since human populations must be sustained by sufficient agricultural production, population growth has generally required substantial increases in cereal crop yields. The Green Revolution represents a recent example: drastic increases in cereal production supported rapid global population expansion during the twentieth century1. Between 1960 and 2000, yields in developing countries increased by 208% for wheat, 109% for rice, and 157% for maize1,2, while the world population doubled from 3 to 6 billion during the same period1,3. Among historical cases in which agricultural innovation drove civilizational development, Champa rice (Lúa Chiêm Thành in Vietnamese, Touboshi in Japanese, and Zhànchéngdào in Chinese; Supplementary Note 1) stands out as a particularly important cereal crop. It likely contributed substantially to population growth in Asia during the medieval period4, yet was later almost lost and forgotten in the modern world. In this study, we show genomic evidence elucidating the spread of this once-influential high-yielding cereal crop across East Asia.

The region of present-day China has been highly populous throughout human history. After fluctuating between approximately 37 and 60 million for a millennium up to the eleventh century, the population experienced its first major demographic expansion beginning in the late eleventh century under the Song (Sung) dynasty4,5. During this period, the population is estimated to have increased from 55 million in 960 to 100 million by 12806–8. The dynasty flourished across southeast Eurasia, including the fertile lower Yangtze region. A high-yielding rice variety known as Champa rice was widely cultivated in that area because of several favorable agronomic traits, such as early maturation and drought tolerance4,9. Champa rice is thought to have been introduced from the Champa kingdom to Fujian (Fukien) in southern China, although the precise timing of its arrival remains uncertain9 (Supplementary Fig. 1). The Champa kingdom, located in present-day central and south Vietnam between the second and seventeenth centuries, maintained a maritime trade network that facilitated commercial and cultural exchanges with both eastern and western Asia; trade routes connecting Champa to Fujian formed part of this network10–12. Historical records indicate that, following an imperial edict issued by Emperor Zhenzong, 30,000 Chinese bushels of Champa rice seeds were disseminated to the lower Yangtze and lower Huai regions in 1011–10124,13 (Supplementary Fig. 1). These lines of evidence suggest that the adoption of Champa rice contributed significantly to enhanced agricultural productivity and food security during a period of rapid population growth in southern China4,13.

Champa rice was sufficiently influential that it is believed to have been subsequently transmitted eastward to Japan. While rice cultivation in modern Japan is largely restricted to a limited group of Japonica varieties, an ancient DNA data analysis suggested that a much broader diversity of rice types, including Indica, was utilized in earlier periods14. Among the most significant introductions of novel rice varieties was Champa rice, which coincided with the emergence of double-cropping practices in medieval Japan15,16. Champa rice is thought to have been introduced to Japan via the Song dynasty, likely between the twelfth and thirteenth centuries (Supplementary Fig. 1), where it was commonly referred to as red rice (akagome or akamai)15,17. Although the precise timing and routes of this introduction remain debated, written records indicate the presence of this red rice by 1308 in Tanba and by 1397 in Sanuki, both located in western Japan15,18 (Supplementary Fig. 2). Subsequently, Champa rice appears to have become widespread across western Japan between approximately 1300 and 145015. During a broadly overlapping period, Japan’s population grew substantially, from an estimated 7.5 million around 1000 to 15.4 million by 150019, which was generally attributed to improvements in agricultural productivity alongside other social and environmental factors15. Champa rice cultivated in Japan was characterized by early maturation, red awns, and notable resistance to blight, insects, flooding, and drought16, and these traits are consistent with historical descriptions of Champa rice in China.

In addition to the routes by which Champa rice spread, its origin also remains unclear. Asian cultivated rice comprises diverse varietal groups with distinct genetic, morphological, and physiological characteristics; in 1928, large-scale fertility experiments identified two major varietal groups, Indica and Japonica20, and this dichotomous classification was widely adopted21. On the basis of this framework, a microsatellite marker study indicated that Japanese Champa rice, known as Touboshi, belongs to the Indica group22. However, another varietal group, Aus, was also recognized as either a minor ecotype of Indica or an intermediate form between Indica and Japonica23,24. A later genome-wide study in 2005 indicated that Aus constitutes a genetically distinct major lineage separate from both Indica and Japonica25. Based on this refined classification, Champa rice was suggested to belong to the Aus group, with the hilly regions of eastern India, Bangladesh, and Myanmar proposed as its geographic origin9.

In this study, we aim to elucidate the process by which this high-yielding rice spread from Southeast Asia to Japan. Given the availability of numerous genome-wide sequences for rice landraces26–29, we first curated passport data for genetic stocks with available sequence data and selected candidate Champa rice landraces from Japan and Vietnam. We then conducted large-scale comparative genomic analyses using molecular phylogenomic and population genomic approaches to classify the varietal affiliations of modern Champa rice landraces. We found that the Japanese candidate Champa rice landraces clustered within Chinese Indica, whereas the Vietnamese candidate Champa rice landraces exhibited close affinity with Southeast Asian Indica. Based on these analyses, the potential geographic origins of these landraces are inferred and discussed.

Results

Exploring potential Champa rice landraces in Japan and Vietnam

Champa rice in medieval Japan was cultivated across multiple regions and was known by various names, such as akagome, Taitougome, and Touboshi (Supplementary Note 1)15,17,30. For example, in the Japanese-Portuguese dictionary Vocabvlario da Lingoa de Iapam com adeclaração em Portugues, published in 1603, both akagome (“acagome”) and Touboshi ("Tǒboxi") were recorded as red rice (“arroz vermelho” in Portuguese) (Supplementary Note 2). Since this dictionary was compiled by missionaries for everyday communications, the inclusion of these terms indicates that this rice variety was familiar and important to ordinary Japanese people at that time. In Japanese, akagome and akamai are general terms for red rice; aka means red, and gome and mai are rice. Accessions named akamai from four distinct geographical locations were available in the Rice Core Collection of Japanese Landraces maintained by the Genebank of Japan. In addition, Karahoushi was included in our analysis, as this name may represent a misreading or variant transcription of Touboshi (Supplementary Note 1). Consequently, six Japanese accessions, five of which were sampled from western Japan, were selected as potential candidates of Japanese Champa rice landraces (Supplementary Fig. 2; Supplementary Note 3). Genome-wide sequencing data for these accessions were retrieved from the international nucleotide sequence databases28 (Supplementary Table 1).

Although a large number of Vietnamese rice accessions are preserved in the National Plant Genebank of Vietnam (https://en.prc.org.vn/?lang=en_US), detailed information identifying landraces related to Champa rice was not readily available. For this reason, we manually examined the passport data for Vietnamese rice accessions and selected candidates based on the following criteria: (1) suggestive names, such as Chiem (Champa) or do (red); (2) collected sites located within the historical territory of the Champa kingdom; or (3) characteristic phenotypes, including early maturation. For example, three accessions with names containing padai, a term meaning “rice” in the Cham language31, were included in our dataset. Based on these criteria, a total of 25 Vietnamese accessions derived from two studies were selected for analysis26,29 (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2).

To investigate the origins and spread of these candidate Champa rice accessions, genome sequence data from all accessions in the World Rice Core Collection of the Japanese Genebank, together with several selected accessions from the 3000 Rice Genomes Project. were incorporated into the analysis26,27. In total, genome sequencing data from 112 accessions were aligned to the Nipponbare reference genome. Variant-calling using GATK identified 9,200,204 variations, including 7,799,238 single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and 1,400,966 insertions/deletions (indels) (Supplementary Table 3). To minimize potential bias in downstream analyses, sites containing missing alleles were removed, resulting in a dataset of 3,349,449 variants: 2,897,923 SNPs and 451,526 indels (Supplementary Table 3). Subsequent linkage disequilibrium pruning yielded 397,390 sites suitable for population structure analysis. For molecular phylogenetic analyses, homozygous SNP sites were further selected, retaining 898,127 SNPs without indels.

Molecular phylogenetic network analysis

A bifurcating phylogenetic tree was reconstructed using the maximum likelihood (ML) method (Supplementary Fig. 3). Although Japanese Champa rice accessions were expected to be closely related to Southeast Asian rice, some accessions (Touboshi and akamai) from Japan were placed within the Chinese Indica group. In contrast, several Vietnamese Champa rice accessions formed a distinct cluster that was not closely related to the Japanese accessions. Notably, contrary to a previous report suggesting the Aus origin for Champa rice9, the candidate Champa rice accessions from both Japan and Vietnam were clearly separated from the Aus group in our ML tree. In addition, some akamai and Vietnamese accessions clustered within the Japonica group, indicating that these accessions are unlikely to represent Champa rice. Accordingly, subsequent analyses focused exclusively on non-Japonica accessions.

Although the ML tree was reconstructed under the assumption that the molecular phylogeny of the examined accessions could be represented by a single bifurcating tree, hybridization events are known to be common among rice groups, as evidenced by the frequent occurrence of intermediate landraces and cultivars26. To assess the influence of hybridization on phylogenetic reconstruction, we performed a maximum parsimony (MP) analysis to calculate the retention index, which quantifies the degree of homoplasy32. The resulting retention index of the most parsimonious tree (tree length = 9,206,666) was relatively low (0.592), suggesting extensive historical exchange of genomic regions among the analyzed accessions.

To visualize the relationship among the analyzed accessions, including potential hybridization events33, a phylogenetic network was reconstructed (Fig. 1). The Indica, Aus, and Japonica groups were clearly resolved. Within Indica, two distinct subgroups corresponding to Southeast Asia and China were observed, and four Japanese Champa rice accessions (Akamai JRC21, Akamai JRC43, Touboshi and Karahoushi) were positioned within the Chinese Indica subgroup, suggesting a close genetic relationship. Notably, contrary to our initial expectations, a substantial number of Vietnamese accessions exhibited network patterns consistent with hybrid origins involving multiple varietal groups. One putative parental lineage appears to be Japonica, whereas the identity of the other contributing group could not be clearly resolved within the network.

Population structure analysis

The structure of Asian rice subpopulations, including candidate Champa rice landraces, was further investigated using principal component analysis (PCA). Because the proportions of explained variance indicated that the fourth and subsequent principal components contributed minimally (Supplementary Fig. 4), we focused our analyses on the first three principal components. The cumulative variance explained by these principal components accounts for 49.8% of the total variance in the dataset, which is comparable to that reported in a previous study27. When the four groups (Indica, Japonica, Aus, and admixed accessions) assigned by the original data sources were plotted using PC1 and PC2, substantial overlap was observed among these categories (Supplementary Fig. 5), suggesting that the original classifications did not appropriately reflect the genetic structure of the dataset. Group identities were therefore relabeled based on clustering patterns observed in the phylogenetic network (Fig. 2). This relabeling resulted in well-separated Indica, Japonica and Aus groups, with admixed accessions positioned between Indica and Japonica, which suggests hybridization between these two groups. Notably, four Japanese candidate Champa rice accessions (dark green circles) clustered within the Indica group, in agreement with the phylogenetic analyses described above. Intriguingly, several Vietnamese accessions (light blue triangles) were also located between Indica and Japonica, but appeared to form a distinct cluster. A PCA based on PC1 and PC3 showed a broadly similar pattern; however, the Vietnamese accessions were not clearly separable from other admixed accessions in this projection (Supplementary Fig. 1).

To further examine the population structure of the 112 accessions, ADMIXTURE analyses were performed using K values ranging from 2 to 9 (Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8). A cross-validation test identified K = 3 as having the lowest error rate and K = 4 as the second lowest (Supplementary Fig. 7), and these two models were therefore examined in detail (Fig. 3a). In both cases, Aus, Indica, and Japonica exhibited distinct allele components, although low levels of gene flows were sporadically observed among groups. Four Japanese candidate Champa rice accessions were consistently assigned to the Indica group, although their relative affinity to the Chinese versus Southeast Asian Indica subgroups could not be clearly resolved. A close relationship between Japanese and Chinese accessions was also apparent in the analyses with K = 5, K = 6 and K = 7 (Supplementary Fig. 8). A notable difference between the K = 3 and K = 4 models was observed for several Vietnamese accessions: under K = 3, these accessions appeared as admixed between Indica and Japonica, whereas under K = 4, they formed a distinct allele component (Fig. 3a). The former scenario is consistent with the phylogenetic network analysis. Overall, these population structure analyses support the hypothesis that the Vietnamese candidate Champa rice accessions are unlikely to be related to Aus but were instead derived from Indica and Japonica lineages, although the timing of the hybridization events remains uncertain. To obtain a clearer view of the population structure of the Vietnamese candidate Champa rice, admixed accessions and Japonica-type Vietnamese accessions were discarded, resulting in a reduced dataset of 67 accessions for ADMIXTURE analysis (Fig. 3b; Supplementary Figs. 9 and 10). For both K = 3 and K = 4, the resulting population structure patterns were essentially consistent with those obtained from the full dataset.

Subpopulation differentiation measured by Fst

Whereas the inferred cultivation history of the Japanese Champa rice accessions appeared relatively straightforward, multiple evolutionary scenarios were plausible for the Vietnamese accessions. Our phylogenetic network analysis suggested that the Vietnamese admixed landraces arose through multiple hybridization events involving Japonica and one of three possible donor groups: Chinese Indica, Southeast Asian Indica, or Aus (Fig. 1). To identify the group most closely related to the Vietnamese accessions, we evaluated subpopulation differentiation among five groups (Chinese Indica, Southeast Asian Indica, Aus, Japonica, and Vietnamese admixed group) using the 67 accessions selected in the previous section. Window-based pairwise Fst values were calculated across the genomes between the Vietnamese admixed group and each of the other four groups (Fig. 4). The mean and standard deviation of Fst values were as follows: Chinese Indica, 0.232 ± 0.095; Southeast Asian Indica, 0.215 ± 0.095; Japonica, 0.259 ± 0.110; and Aus, 0.319 ± 0.107 (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 11). Differences among the mean Fst values were statistically significant for all comparisons (Steel–Dwass test, p < 0.001). These results indicate that the Vietnamese admixed accessions exhibit the lowest genetic differentiation from Southeast Asian Indica, which implies their closer genetic relationship to this subpopulation than to Chinese Indica, Japonica, or Aus.

Since Fst values varied substantially across chromosomes (Fig. 4), we examined them separately for each chromosome and compared the Vietnam-China (VC) and Vietnam-Southeast Asia (VSEA) pairs (Table 1). In nine chromosomes (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 8, 10, 11 and 12), Fst values were significantly lower for VSEA than VC (Mann–Whitney U test, p < 0.001); however, this pattern did not hold for the remaining three chromosomes. For example, a pronounced reduction of Fst was observed around the central region of chromosome 6 for the VC comparison, resulting in a significantly lower Fst value for VC than for VSEA on this chromosome (Mann–Whitney U test, p < 0.001). Such chromosomal region-specific patterns likely reflect a complex history of hybridization and introgression among rice subpopulations, potentially associated with artificial selection for agronomic traits.

Polymorphisms in genes related to pericarp color

Because the red coloration is a distinctive characteristic of Japanese Champa rice, as reflected in the name akamai (red rice), polymorphisms in a gene associated with grain coat color were examined. The Rc gene is a major determinant of red pericarp pigmentation; a non-functional mutant allele, rc, characterized by a 14-nucleotide deletion in exon 6, is thought to have been selected during rice domestication34,35. The frequencies of Rc or rc alleles were quantified across six rice groups (Table 2). Consistent with previous reports, Rc was predominant in Aus, whereas 90% of Japonica accessions carried the rc allele. Both Rc and rc alleles were observed among Chinese and Southeast Asian Indica accessions, in agreement with the prevailing view that the rc allele was introgressed from Japonica into Indica as white pericarp rice became favored34,35. All four Indica-type Japanese Champa rice accessions carried the functional Rc allele, which aligned with their red pericarp phenotype. Approximately half of the Vietnamese accessions possessed the rc allele; and this pattern is compatible with their inferred Indica ancestry and a history of hybridization with Japonica. Moreover, an MP tree was reconstructed using 17 homozygous SNP sites without missing genotypes, identified within the Rc locus and its 100-bp flanking regions, from the 67 selected accessions (Supplementary Fig. 12). The resulting tree showed that, in addition to the previously reported introgression of rc from Japonica into Indica35, several candidate Vietnamese accessions were placed either within the rc cluster or in intermediate positions between the Rc and rc clusters, further supporting a complex hybridization history for the examined Vietnamese accessions.

In the genome-wide phylogenetic analyses (Supplementary Fig. 3; Fig. 1), two Chinese accessions, WRC09 (Ryou Suisan Koumai) and WRC19 (Deng Pao Zhai), were found to be closely related to the Japanese Champa rice accessions. Whereas WRC19 carried the non-functional rc allele, WRC09 retained the functional Rc allele. In line with these genotypes, passport data from the Genebank indicate that the hulled rice color of WRC19 is white, while that of WRC09 is red-brown, as reflected in its landrace name (Supplementary Notes 3 and 4).

Discussion

Because the Champa kingdom was located in present-day Vietnam, molecular phylogenetic and population genomic analyses were expected to place modern Champa rice accessions within the Southeast Asian Indica group. However, contrary to this expectation, none of the examined Japanese Champa rice candidates were closely associated with the Vietnamese or Southeast Asian accessions; instead, four were classified within the Chinese Indica group (Figs. 1, 2 and 3). Two scenarios may account for this observation. As previously noted by Ho4, indigenous early-ripening varieties other than Champa rice existed in southern China. Thus, shortly after its introduction and widespread adoption, substantial portions of the Champa rice genome may have been replaced through intensive hybridization with local Chinese varieties prior to its dissemination within China and onward to Japan. Alternatively, Chinese Indica rice unrelated to Champa rice may have been introduced directly into Japan during the period of active maritime trade between Japan and the Song and Ming dynasties (10th–sixteenth centuries)16,36, and subsequently came to be misidentified as “Champa rice” in Japan. Taken together, these scenarios suggest that, contrary to the prevailing view that Japanese Champa rice was derived from present-day Vietnam15–17, the red rice varieties (akamai and Touboshi) that contributed to enhanced rice productivity in medieval Japan were likely derived from the Chinese Indica group. Nevertheless, it remains possible that Vietnam-derived rice varieties in Japan were not captured in our dataset because of limited sampling. A more comprehensive survey of additional Champa rice landraces will be necessary to clarify the historical dissemination routes of this influential crop.

Several Vietnamese Champa rice candidates were unexpectedly positioned as intermediates between Indica and Japonica (Figs. 1, 2 and 3). Whereas they formed a distinct cluster in the PCA (Fig. 2), both the phylogenetic network reconstruction and the ADMIXTURE analysis (K = 3) indicated mixed ancestry components consistent with hybridization between Japonica and Indica (Figs. 1 and 3). While this admixture could, in principle, reflect a single historical hybridization event predating the dissemination of Champa rice in southern China, the paraphyletic pattern observed in the phylogenetic network instead suggests multiple hybridization events among the examined accessions. A similar complex pattern was detected at the Rc locus (Supplementary Fig. 12), further supporting the multiple independent hybridization events. Accordingly, the hybrid Vietnamese accessions analyzed here may represent relatively recent recombinants, although explicit temporal inference would be required to confirm this. Notably, although Indica rice predominates in modern Vietnam37, seven of the 55 Vietnamese accessions included in the 3000 Rice Genomes Project were classified as Japonica26; several Vietnamese accessions in our dataset were also identified as Japonica (Figs. 1, 2 and 3), supporting the occurrence of admixture between Indica and Japonica in Vietnam. To further investigate the origin of the Indica-derived genomic portion in these hybrid rice accessions, Fst analysis was conducted (Fig. 4; Table 1). The degree of subpopulation differentiation indicated the closest affinity between the Vietnamese accessions and the Southeast Asian Indica subpopulation. These results support the interpretation that the candidate Vietnamese Champa rice accessions were shaped by multiple and complex hybridization events between Southeast Asian Indica and Japonica, although the timing and directionality of these admixture events remain to be determined.

Although the phenotypes of Champa rice were previously reported to align with those of Aus9, our results indicate that the examined Champa rice candidates are not closely associated with the Aus group. Instead, they are included within or show close affinity to the Indica group, which is consistent with a previous preliminary phylogenetic analysis of Taiwanese Champa rice38. Additionally, the Japanese red rice varieties (akamai) showed relatively close affinity to the Chinese red rice variety Ryou Suisan Koumai (WRC09), which carries the functional Rc allele (Fig. 1; Supplementary Fig. 12). Although the precise route and process of Champa rice dissemination within China were not investigated in this study, our results raise the possibility that Indica varieties similar to those identified in Vietnam and Japan contributed substantially to the historical demographic expansion during the Song dynasty.

Despite our extensive search of large datasets for candidate Champa rice (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2), no clear ancestral lineages were identified in our molecular phylogenetic analysis (Fig. 1). In addition, because Champa rice was disseminated over 1000 years ago, this long time span may have substantially weakened signals of its genetic ancestry in modern accessions. Whereas candidate Vietnamese Champa rice accessions were placed between Indica and Japonica (Figs. 1 and 2), suggesting potential hybrid origins (Fig. 3, K = 3), an alternative possibility is that they were derived from a distinct lineage (Fig. 3, K = 4). Therefore, to more fully capture the historical diversity of Champa rice, the inclusion of additional rice accessions from diverse subpopulations may facilitate further identification of candidate Champa rice. For example, weedy rice, which is often characterized by red pericarp, is known to be derived from multiple sources39,40, and descendants of Champa rice may be present among modern weedy rice accessions. Another consideration is that, while we identified more than 3 million SNPs and indels in this study, a substantial number of sites with missing genotypes were excluded for simplicity, potentially introducing bias into the dataset. Although genotype imputation is an alternative approach, it may introduce different biases; therefore, higher sequencing coverage would be beneficial if new sample collections are undertaken.

Human population growth has historically depended on agricultural productivity, and enhancing cereal crops production remains a critical global challenge, as the world population is projected to exceed 10 billion by the mid-2080s41. Asia currently accounts for the largest share of the global population, and rice is one of the most important cereal crops in this region. In 2023, Asia produced approximately 717 million of the 800 million tons of paddy rice harvested worldwide42. Ensuring sufficient cereal production therefore requires continued improvement of rice cultivars across multiple agronomic traits. Champa rice has attracted considerable attention due to its historical importance and distinctive characteristics, including early maturation and resistance to biotic and abiotic stresses9. Given that Asian rice varietal groups exhibit extensive phenotypic diversity adapted to specific environmental conditions, the exploration of previously undiscovered or underutilized genetic resources, which are potentially comparable or superior to Champa rice, will play an important role in future rice breeding and improvement efforts in Asia.

Methods

Sequence dataset

Genome sequence data for a total of 112 Asian rice landraces and varieties were downloaded from the International Nucleotide Sequence Database Collaboration (Supplementary Table 1)43. Japanese Champa rice candidates were selected from the Rice Core Collection of Japanese Landraces28, and included Touboshi (JRC42), Karahoushi (JRC44), and four accessions of akamai (JRC21, JRC40, JRC41 and JRC43), based on their historical names, which are indicative of Champa rice. Vietnamese Champa rice candidates comprised 19 accessions from Higgins et al. (2021) and six accessions from the 3000 Rice Genome Project26,29. Selection of Vietnamese landraces was based on the following criteria: (i) variety name containing suggestive terms such as Chiem (Champa); (ii) collection sites located within the historical territory of the Champa kingdom; or (iii) documented phenotypic characteristics associated with Champa rice, including early maturation (Supplementary Table 2). To provide broader phylogenetic and population context, 11 accessions from the 3000 Rice Genomes Project and 69 accessions from the World Rice Core Collection, representing major Asian rice subpopulations, were also incorporated into the dataset (Supplementary Table 1)26,27. The rice reference genome (IRGSP-1.0) was downloaded from the Rice Annotation Project Database44,45.

SNP calling

Low-quality bases and adapter sequences were removed from the raw reads using Trimmomatic (ver. 0.39) with the following options: LEADING:20 TRAILING:20 SLIDINGWINDOW:10:20 MINLEN:5046. The trimmed reads were aligned to the reference genome using BWA-MEM (ver. 0.7) with the option ‘-M’, and the resulting alignments were sorted with Samtools (ver. 1.11)47,48. Alignments were further processed using Picard tools (ver. 2.26), in which reads were cleaned and merged using FastqToSam and MergeBamAlignment (http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard). Artifactual duplicates were identified and removed using Picard MarkDuplicates with the following option: REMOVE_DUPLICATES = true. Genome-wide variant discovery was performed using GATK (ver. 4.2) HaplotypeCaller in genomic variant call format (gVCF) mode with the following options: –filter-expression "QD < 5.0 || FS > 50.0 || SOR > 3.0 || MQ < 50.0 || MQRankSum < −2.5 || ReadPosRankSum < −1.0 || ReadPosRankSum > 3.5" –filter-name “FILTER”49. The resulting gVCF files were imported into GenomicsDB using GATK GenomicsDBImport, followed by joint-genotyping and variant-filtering with GATK GenotypeGVCFs to generate a final multi-sample variant call format (VCF) file. VCF files were further processed and manipulated using VCFtools (ver. 0.1.7) and BCFtools (ver. 1.22)48,50.

Molecular phylogenetic analysis

The multi-sample VCF file was converted to Phylip and FASTA format files using vcf2phylip (10.5281/zenodo.2540861). Heterozygous SNPs and indels were excluded, resulting in a dataset of 898,127 homozygous SNP sites, which was used for subsequent phylogenetic analyses. Maximum likelihood (ML) tree reconstruction was performed using the RAxML-NG package (ver. 1.2)51. The optimal substitution model was determined using ModelTest-NG (ver. 0.1.7), and GTR + G4 was selected as the best-fitting model based on all three information criteria (BIC, AIC, and AICc). The ML tree was inferred with 100 bootstrap replicates to assess the branching patterns. Maximum parsimony (MP) analysis was conducted using MEGA1252. The most parsimonious tree was obtained using the subtree-pruning-regrafting algorithm with search level 1, in which the initial trees were generated by random addition of sequences (10 replicates)32. The retention index was calculated from this MP analysis. To visualize reticulate evolutionary relationships potentially arising from hybridization, a phylogenetic network was reconstructed using the Neighbor-Net algorithm implemented in SplitsTree (ver. 6.4)33,53. Bifurcating phylogenetic trees were visualized using the TreeExplorer function in MEGA1252.

Principal component analysis

To reduce the effects of linkage disequilibrium (LD), all single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were pruned using PLINK (ver. 1.9) with the option ‘–indep-pairwise 10 kb 1 0.5’, corresponding to a window size of 10 kb, a step size of one SNP, and an LD threshold of 0.554. The resulting LD-pruned sites were used for principal component analysis (PCA) implemented in PLINK54. Three output files (bed, bim, and fam) were generated and retained for downstream analyses. PCA results were visualized using the tidyverse package in R55.

Population structure analysis

Population structure was inferred using ADMIXTURE (ver. 1.3) with the option ‘–seed = 1’56. The LD-pruned PLINK files generated in the PCA step were used as input. ADMIXTURE produces two output files: the Q matrix, which represents ancestry proportions for each individual, and the P matrix, which contains estimated allele frequencies for each inferred population. Cross-validation tests were performed to evaluate model support for different numbers (K) of ancestral populations. ADMIXTURE results were visualized using R (https://www.R-project.org/).

Population differentiation analysis

Genetic differentiation among rice groups was evaluated using Fst statistics. Weir and Cockerham’s Fst values were calculated using VCFtools (ver. 0.1.7)50,57. For window-based analyses, Fst was computed in sliding windows of 100 kb with a step size of 10 kb across the genome. Statistical significance of differences in genome-wide Fst distributions among rice groups was assessed using the Steel–Dwass test implemented in the pSDCFlig function of the NSM3 package (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/NSM3/index.html) in R. Differences in chromosomal Fst values between the Vietnam–Chinese Indica and Vietnam–Southeast Asian Indica comparisons were evaluated using the Mann–Whitney U test implemented as wilcox.test in R.
