Same‐day fluorescence confocal microscopy of MRI‐targeted prostate biopsies: A feasibility study
Authors: Salim K Younis, Mohamad Watfa, Lin Wang, Karim Daher, Nicolas A Soputro, Abdulrahman Al‐Bayati, Samarpit Rai, Rui M Bernardino, Ruben Olivares, Riccardo Autorino, Christopher J Weight, Jihad Kaouk, Jane K Nguyen, Zeyad R Schwen

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo determine the diagnostic performance of ex vivo fluorescence confocal microscopy (FCM) for prostate cancer detection in magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)‐targeted biopsy specimens using final histopathology as the reference standard, and to quantify workflow component times relevant to same‐day implementation.Patients and MethodsIn this single‐centre prospective observational study, men undergoing MRI‐targeted transperineal prostate biopsy were enrolled. Introduction

Prostate biopsy remains the cornerstone for the diagnosis and risk stratification of prostate cancer [1]. Despite advances in multiparametric MRI and MRI‐targeted biopsy techniques, standard histopathological results are not available at the point of care and are typically reported days after biopsy [1, 2]. This delay prolongs patient uncertainty and limits opportunities to accelerate diagnostic decision‐making after biopsy.

Fluorescence confocal microscopy (FCM) is a digital optical imaging technique that enables rapid visualisation of unfixed tissue with near histological resolution by rendering images that mimic conventional haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, while preserving specimens for downstream histopathology and immunohistochemistry [3, 4, 5]. Ex vivo FCM has been widely adopted in dermatopathology and has more recently been applied to urological specimens, including prostate biopsies [4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. Prior prostate biopsy studies have demonstrated substantial agreement with conventional histopathology for cancer detection, while also highlighting more modest agreement for grading and classification of clinically significant disease, particularly near the International Society of Urological Pathology (ISUP) Grade Group (GG) 1 vs GG 2 threshold [4, 7, 9, 10].

Although these investigations support the diagnostic potential of FCM, the technology is still in the early phases of clinical validation. In addition, many studies emphasise feasibility or concordance without detailed reporting of workflow component timings, reader agreement, or performance specifically in MRI‐targeted biopsy specimens [5, 6, 7, 9, 10]. Defining diagnostic performance and the time required for specimen preparation, scanning, and interpretation is important to inform future prospective studies evaluating live, in‐office implementation for prostate biopsies.

The aim of this study was therefore to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of ex vivo FCM for detection of prostate cancer in MRI‐targeted biopsy specimens using final histopathology as the reference standard, and to quantify workflow component times relevant to potential same‐day implementation. These data are intended to support future prospective studies integrating live, point‐of‐care interpretation during biopsy.

Patients and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This was a single‐centre prospective observational study evaluating the diagnostic performance and workflow feasibility of ex vivo FCM for in‐office assessment of MRI‐targeted prostate biopsy specimens. The study was conducted at a tertiary academic urology centre, with biopsy procedures performed in both the outpatient clinic and operating room settings according to institutional practice. Ex vivo FCM imaging was performed on‐site immediately after tissue acquisition. Institutional Review Board approval was obtained prior to study initiation.

Objectives

Primary Objectives

(I) to determine diagnostic performance of ex vivo FCM for prostate cancer detection in MRI‐targeted biopsy specimens using final histopathology as the reference standard; and (II) to quantify workflow feasibility for in‐office implementation by measuring specimen preparation, scanning, and interpretation times.

Secondary Objective

To evaluate interobserver agreement between readers for prostate cancer detection and classification of clinically significant cancer (CSC), defined as ISUP GG ≥2 [1].

Patient Cohort

Men undergoing prostate biopsy for suspected or known prostate cancer were eligible for inclusion. Indications for biopsy included elevated PSA levels, abnormal DRE, MRI‐detected lesions, or active surveillance of previously diagnosed prostate cancer. The present analysis focused on MRI‐targeted biopsy specimens. In cases where multiple target lesions were sampled during a single biopsy session, each lesion was treated as an independent target for diagnostic performance analysis. Patients with prior prostate cancer treatment, including radiotherapy or focal therapy, were not excluded to reflect real‐world clinical practice.

Biopsy Acquisition

All biopsies were performed using a transperineal approach under MRI‐targeted guidance according to standard institutional protocols (Fig. 1A). Targeted biopsy cores were obtained from MRI‐visible lesions at the discretion of the treating urologist (Fig. 1B). Immediately after acquisition, targeted biopsy specimens were transferred for ex vivo FCM imaging prior to routine histopathological processing.

The FCM Workflow

Fresh biopsy cores were immersed in an acridine orange‐based solution for 10 s per specimen according to a standardised protocol (Fig. 1C). The specimens were then transferred to the scanning window, measuring 4.8 cm in length and 3.6 cm in width, for imaging. FCM imaging was performed using a Histolog® Scanner (SamanTree Medical, Lausanne, Switzerland), which uses non‐ionising, low‐power optical radiation with a wavelength of 488 nm to provide fast imaging of the surface of the excised specimen, generating pseudo‐H&E‐like digital images (Fig. 1D).

Each biopsy specimen was scanned in its entirety, producing high‐resolution images suitable for pathological interpretation (Fig. 1E). Specimen preparation time and scanning time were recorded contemporaneously during the biopsy session to assess workflow feasibility. Following imaging, biopsy cores were immediately placed in formalin and processed according to standard histopathology protocols. Confocal imaging did not alter tissue orientation or compromise downstream histological assessment.

Reference Standard Histopathology and Digital H&E Correlation

Conventional histopathological evaluation of biopsy specimens served as the reference standard. Formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded sections were stained with H&E and reviewed by experienced genitourinary pathologists according to contemporary reporting standards. Immunohistochemical staining was performed when deemed necessary by the reporting pathologist.

For illustrative and translational correlation purposes, a subset of targeted biopsy specimens underwent whole‐slide digital scanning of corresponding H&E sections derived from the same region of the same biopsy core imaged by FCM (Fig. 2). These paired images were used to enable direct, spatially matched comparison of architectural patterns across imaging modalities and ISUP GGs, facilitating cross‐modality interpretability. This qualitative pairing was performed for visualisation and educational purposes only and was not incorporated into quantitative diagnostic performance analyses.

Reader Study

The FCM images were independently reviewed by two board‐certified genitourinary pathologists who were blinded to clinical information and final histopathological results. Both readers had limited prior experience interpreting prostate FCM images. Prior to the study, both readers completed an ~2‐h, standardised manufacturer‐provided training module focused on digital viewer navigation and general multi‐tissue image interpretation, concluding with a 1:1 expert review session. Because this module was not exclusively prostate‐specific, each reader subsequently reviewed approximately five representative prostate biopsy cases to familiarise themselves with targeted tissue features. Additionally, the readers were already familiar with the platform's visual interface, having previously reviewed ~36 FCM specimens from a different urological domain (bladder tumours).

Each reader independently reviewed the same set of MRI‐targeted transperineal biopsy specimens in a separate reading session conducted after image acquisition, and readers had no access to corresponding histopathology during FCM interpretation. For the purposes of this study, ‘positive’ was defined as the presence of prostatic adenocarcinoma on final histopathology. ‘Negative’ was defined as no diagnostic evidence of prostatic adenocarcinoma, including benign tissue and atypical small acinar proliferation (ASAP). For the purposes of binary cancer detection analysis, specimens without definitive prostate adenocarcinoma, including those reported as ASAP, were classified as negative, as ASAP is not diagnostic of malignancy on histopathology. For positive specimens, readers additionally assessed whether findings were consistent with CSC.

Image interpretation was performed consecutively, in a separate session, to approximate routine diagnostic conditions. Interpretation time per specimen was recorded during the reading session to assess feasibility. Interobserver agreement was evaluated based on independent assessments of the same specimens.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were: (i) per‐target diagnostic performance of FCM for prostate cancer detection using final histopathology of the corresponding targeted specimen as the reference standard, and (ii) workflow feasibility for in‐office implementation, quantified by specimen preparation time, scanning time, and interpretation time. The secondary outcome was interobserver agreement between readers for prostate cancer detection and classification of CSC.

Exploratory analyses included concordance between FCM interpretation and histopathology for ISUP GG categorisation and descriptive characterisation of discordant targeted biopsy specimens. Discordance was assessed at the targeted biopsy specimen level across three independent assessments: FCM interpretation by Reader 1, FCM interpretation by Reader 2, and final histopathology. A specimen was classified as discordant if any disagreement was present among these three assessments for cancer detection, classification of CSC, or both. Discordant specimens were categorised as cancer‐discordant only, CSC‐discordant only, or discordant for both, and clinical, imaging, and pathological characteristics were summarised descriptively to identify patterns associated with disagreement. Additionally, a post hoc subgroup analysis was performed excluding targeted specimens from patients with a history of prior prostate cancer treatment to evaluate diagnostic performance in a strictly treatment‐naïve cohort.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise patient, MRI, and biopsy characteristics. Continuous clinical variables were reported as median with interquartile range (IQR), and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Workflow time variables, including specimen preparation, scanning, and reading times, were summarised using the mean (SD).

Diagnostic performance was assessed using sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative PV (NPV), and overall accuracy, with corresponding 95% CIs calculated using the Wilson score method for binomial proportions. Interobserver agreement was evaluated using Cohen's κ statistics, with 95% CIs derived from the asymptotic normal approximation based on the standard error of κ. Cohen's κ values were interpreted according to the Landis and Koch criteria as follows: <0.00, poor; 0.00–0.20, slight; 0.21–0.40, fair; 0.41–0.60, moderate; 0.61–0.80, substantial; and 0.81–1.00, almost perfect agreement [11]. All analyses were performed at the target‐lesion level using R software (version 4.4.2; The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Patient and Biopsy Characteristics

A total of 44 patients undergoing MRI‐targeted prostate biopsy were included in the study. All biopsy procedures were completed successfully without any perioperative complications. The median (IQR) patient age was 68.5 (61–73) years, and the median (IQR) PSA level was 5.3 (4.1–9.6) ng/mL. In total, 50 MRI‐targeted biopsy specimens, which included 139 cores from 44 patients, were imaged using FCM and included in the final analysis. Final histopathology identified prostate cancer in 33 of 50 targeted biopsy specimens (66%), of which 17 specimens (52%) met criteria for CSC. Benign histology was observed in 14 targets (28%), while ASAP was identified in three targets (6%). Workflow Feasibility

Ex vivo FCM staining, specimen preparation, and scanning were successfully completed for all targeted biopsy specimens. The mean (SD) specimen preparation time was 2.20 (1.24) min. Scanning time was standardised by device protocol at 50 s (0.83 min) per specimen. Image interpretation was performed in a separate reading session following acquisition, with a mean (SD) interpretation time of 1.7 (0.83) min. Taken together, specimen preparation, scanning, and interpretation indicate that a preliminary FCM assessment can be completed in <5 min per targeted biopsy specimen, based on recorded component timing metrics. The CSC‐only discordance was enriched in intermediate‐risk disease: seven of 11 (64%) CSC‐discordant‐only targets were ISUP GG 2, whereas cancer‐discordant‐only targets more commonly mapped to GG 1 (three of five [60%]) or Negative (two of five [40%]). No discordant cases involved GG ≥3 disease (Table 5). Discordance also occurred more frequently among Prostate Imaging‐Reporting and Data System (PI‐RADS) 3 and 4 targets. Notably, of the five targets with ‘Not Assigned’ PI‐RADS scores (representing patients with prior focal or radiation therapy where a formal score was omitted), two were discordant (one CSC‐discordant only, and one discordant for both cancer and CSC). Conversely, discordance was entirely absent among the small subset of PI‐RADS 5 targets (n = 12) for both cancer and CSC detection in this cohort (Table 5).

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the diagnostic performance and workflow feasibility of ex vivo FCM for prostate cancer detection in MRI‐targeted prostate biopsy specimens. Our findings demonstrate that FCM provides rapid, high‐quality visualisation of targeted biopsy tissue with reproducible diagnostic performance, while highlighting known limitations in grading and classification of clinically significant disease.

The diagnostic performance observed in this cohort is consistent with prior studies evaluating FCM in prostate biopsy specimens, which have reported high concordance with conventional histopathology for cancer detection but more variable agreement for grading and CSC classification (Table S2) [4, 5, 6, 7, 9]. Difficulty at the GG 1 vs GG 2 threshold has been repeatedly described, especially in the setting of limited tumour volume or borderline architectural features [7, 9, 10]. Notably, Reader 1 demonstrated higher specificity whereas Reader 2 showed higher sensitivity, reflecting differing diagnostic thresholds during early FCM adoption. This pattern is consistent with prior reports in emerging digital pathology platforms, where limited reader experience and the absence of standardised interpretive criteria may contribute to threshold variability [4, 7]. From a clinical perspective, this divergence suggests that structured training, atlas‐based guidance, and potential decision‐support tools may help harmonise interpretation and optimise the sensitivity–specificity balance in future implementations [12, 13]. The predominance of CSC‐only discordance in GG 2 targets observed in the present study is therefore biologically and diagnostically plausible rather than unexpected. Representative paired FCM and corresponding H&E images illustrating this architectural overlap at the GG 1–GG 2 threshold are shown in Fig. 2.

Beyond overall diagnostic performance, our exploratory discordance analysis provides hypothesis‐generating insight into the biological and imaging contexts in which FCM disagreement is most likely to occur. Discordance clustered in intermediate‐risk scenarios, particularly among PI‐RADS 3 lesions and GG 2 targets. Furthermore, we observed that targets from previously treated glands (which lacked formal PI‐RADS scores) accounted for a notable proportion of discordance. This suggests that treatment‐related tissue changes, such as fibrosis or radiation atypia, may complicate FCM interpretation just as they do conventional MRI. While higher‐grade disease and PI‐RADS 5 targets remained uniformly concordant, these findings must be interpreted with caution due to the small sample sizes within the discordance sub‐categories. This pattern suggests that disagreement reflects inherent biological and sampling complexity at diagnostic thresholds rather than systematic limitations of FCM interpretation. The absence of discordance in GG ≥3 disease further supports the reliability of FCM for identifying clinically consequential cancer, while reinforcing its role as a rapid detection adjunct rather than a definitive replacement for conventional histopathology [4, 12, 13].

From a workflow perspective, specimen preparation and scanning were completed during the biopsy session, and interpretation times measured in a separate consecutive reading session demonstrate that review is rapid. While live interpretation was not performed during the biopsy procedure in this study, the combined preparation, scanning, and interpretation times suggest that incorporating FCM into routine practice and support the feasibility of future real‐time, point‐of‐care interpretation during biopsy with minimal incremental procedure time. This approach is aligned with efforts to streamline diagnostic pathways after prostate biopsy [1, 2].

An additional theoretical application of FCM in the targeted biopsy setting is assessment of sampling adequacy. Targeted biopsy cores may occasionally contain predominantly extraprostatic tissue or lack representative glands despite apparent targeting, particularly in smaller or equivocal lesions [1, 2]. Rapid visualisation of biopsy content represents a compelling future research direction, as it could hypothetically allow immediate identification of non‐representative samples and prompt repeat sampling with minor trajectory adjustment during the same session. The discordance patterns observed in this study, which clustered in borderline biological and sampling‐sensitive scenarios, are consistent with this hypothesis. This application remains speculative and is unsupported by direct efficacy data in the present study.

Accordingly, future prospective trials should assess whether deployment of real‐time FCM feedback during targeted biopsy reduces the rate of non‐diagnostic or non‐representative cores, improves lesion‐level cancer detection, and decreases the need for repeat biopsy. Relevant endpoints may include per‐lesion cancer detection rates, number of cores required per target, and procedural efficiency, when immediate adequacy feedback is available [9, 10, 12].

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single tertiary centre and included a modest number of patients and MRI‐targeted biopsy specimens, limiting statistical power for subgroup analyses. Notably, the small number of CSC cases (n = 17) results in wide CIs for CSC‐specific performance metrics; thus, these estimates should be interpreted with caution. Second, because analysis was performed at the lesion level, some patients contributed more than one target specimen, introducing potential intra‐patient correlation. Third, while ASAP represents a clinically actionable finding that typically prompts repeat biopsy, such specimens were classified as negative for the binary purpose of cancer detection in our analysis. Future studies evaluating FCM may benefit from specifically investigating its ability to differentiate ASAP from benign tissue. Furthermore, while no specimens were excluded due to technical scanning failures, thereby minimising selection bias, the readers were aware they were participating in a validation study. Although they remained strictly blinded to all clinical data and histopathological outcomes, this awareness introduces a potential Hawthorne effect that could influence reader vigilance compared to routine clinical practice. Finally, interpretation was performed offline rather than during live biopsy sessions, and concordance was evaluated as an exploratory outcome. Nevertheless, workflow timing metrics were recorded prospectively, and image reading was conducted consecutively to approximate real‐world diagnostic conditions in an MRI‐targeted biopsy setting.

Despite these limitations, our findings support FCM as a feasible and accurate digital biopsy adjunct for prostate cancer detection in MRI‐targeted biopsy specimens. With further standardisation, training, and prospective validation incorporating live interpretation, FCM may complement existing biopsy workflows and contribute to more efficient and patient‐centred prostate cancer diagnostics [3, 12].

Conclusion

Ex vivo FCM enables rapid assessment of MRI‐targeted prostate biopsy specimens with high diagnostic accuracy and reproducible interobserver performance for prostate cancer detection. Although image interpretation in this study was performed offline, measured specimen preparation, scanning, and interpretation times support feasibility within minutes per target, compatible with same‐day or in‐office workflows. Limitations persisted for grading, with discordance concentrated at the GG 1 vs GG 2 threshold and in sampling‐sensitive scenarios. These findings support FCM as an adjunct to conventional histopathology and provide a rationale for prospective implementation trials to test whether live FCM feedback during targeted biopsy can reduce non‐representative cores and improve lesion‐level cancer detection.
