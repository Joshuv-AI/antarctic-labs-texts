Ambulance services for road traffic injury (RTI) victims in Bangladesh: a cross-sectional study on the ground realities and the way forward
Authors: Zarin Tasnim, Samiun Nazrin Bente Kamal Tune, Bushra Zarin Islam, Nahitun Naher, Syed Masud Ahmed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSwift and safe transport of road traffic injury patients for life-saving interventions requires a functioning ambulance system which is largely lacking in the low- and middle-income countries. This study explored the current situation of ambulance-based prehospital care services in Bangladesh to inform the development of an evidence-based national ambulance system.MethodsA cross-sectional study conducted in five selected districts of Bangladesh, employing qualitative and quantitative approaches, elicited data on the 29 ambulances and the knowledge and skills of their operators. Findings were validated through 32 key informant interviews with relevant stakeholders on their experiences and insights towards developing a national ambulance system in the country.ResultsMost of the ambulances were ‘makeshift ambulances’ (converted microbus) and commonly lacked the necessary supplies and equipment, including a Global Positioning System tracker or location identifier system. Around 20 drivers had 10+years of work experience, but none were trained in first-aid. Some drivers developed a few life-saving skills like removing foreign bodies from the airway or immobilising fractured limbs, etc from work experiences. The public sector ambulances were mostly used for transporting referral patients and, quite frequently, for transporting staff. Public ambulances had government-determined fixed rates, whereas private ambulances did not. The responsible authority/operator for the public sector ambulances was ambiguous and not coordinated by any local/central authority.ConclusionThe ‘ambulance’ service in the country is rudimentary, fragmented and poorly resourced, lacking any central/regional level coordination. A recommendation is made to develop a national ambulance service, integrating public and private sectors and operating under a central/regional authority.

Introduction

Globally, road traffic injuries (RTIs) are the most common cause of death between 5 and 29 years of life (estimated 1.19 million deaths annually), especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC).1 Though possessing only around 60% of the world’s vehicles, LMICs account for 93% of all road fatalities worldwide.1 Factors accounting for the high RTI prevalence in these countries include poor roads and vehicles, inadequate traffic enforcement, pedestrians with poor awareness and near-absence of prehospital care, including first-responder care and ambulance care.2 As a result, about 80% of injury deaths in these countries occur in prehospital settings.3 The WHO describes the safe and rapid transfer of the injured as a vital component of prehospital emergency care.4 Evidence suggests that the implementation of prehospital care can reduce the risk of death in injured patients by 25% in the LMICs.5 Less than 1% of the population in many LMICs has access to formal emergency medical transportation services, such as ambulances.6 Transfer of the injured, either from the scene of an injury to a facility or between facilities, has two essential components: transporting the victim and care during transport. Well-equipped ambulances with trained staff are crucial to ensure emergency care during transport to a healthcare facility.

The RTIs disproportionately affect the poor in countries like Bangladesh, with consequences for the household’s economic well-being.7 RTIs are the second leading cause of injury deaths (18%) in Bangladesh.8 Compared with the ownership of vehicles, it has the highest fatality rate in the world: 100 deaths per 10 000 motor vehicles.9 Road crash fatalities and injuries predominantly affect the working-age population in Bangladesh. Also, the RTIs ranked ninth among the top causes of inpatient admission in all government hospitals for persons above five.10 The levels of mortality and morbidity are quite high, and the economic loss from RTIs is estimated to be around 2–3% of the gross domestic product.11

Bangladesh has a National Road Safety Strategic Action Plan with a vision of achieving a 50% reduction in road crash fatalities.12 However, there are significant gaps in the country’s pre-crash and post-crash response services.12 First-responder care is rarely available, and the availability12–14 and quality of ambulance services13 14 are significant concerns. RTI victims are rarely transferred from the injury spot to the health facility on time15; even if they are, the prehospital care required during this time is hardly ensured,12 15 given the poor state of the ambulances, including the absence of trained technicians/paramedics.13 14

The Government of Bangladesh is keen to improve road safety management, including emergency care, to minimise loss from RTIs.12 16 17 There is a clear dearth of literature regarding the situation of ambulance-based prehospital services in the country. This study aimed to explore the current situation of ambulance-based prehospital services, identify the gaps and recommend measures to inform the development of an evidence-based national ambulance system in Bangladesh.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional study design was adopted for this study combining (1) knowledge and skill assessment of the ambulance operators plus on-site physical assessment of the ‘ambulance’ vehicles, and; (2) key informant interviews (KIIs) with stakeholders to explore their perceptions and experiences, including their insights regarding the challenges of building a national ambulance service in the country. The Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research was followed as standard reporting protocol for this study.18

Study settings and sample

The study was conducted in five districts, for example, Feni, Jessore, Jhinaidah, Manikganj and Dhaka. The districts were selected based on their proximity to highways prone to road traffic accident, and the location of healthcare facilities where RTI victims usually receive care. The assessment was carried out at two tiers of public sector health facilities, for example, the district hospitals and the Upazila (subdistrict) Health Complexes (UpHC), except in Dhaka. In Dhaka district, the assessment was carried out in the selected secondary level health facility (Mohanagar General Hospital), a tertiary facility (ie, the Dhaka Medical College Hospital, public sector) in the south city corporation area, and some selected facilities in the private sector including the National Medical College Hospital. Dhaka Medical College Hospital provided an urban context of a large city and was also the home to the prime tertiary public hospital of the country. For qualitative data, interviews were conducted with central and district-level key informants (KIs), using ‘purposive’ and ‘snowball’ sampling techniques.

Study participants

The ambulance drivers, their assistants and ambulance owners in the public and private sectors were purposively selected for vehicle assessment in the selected study sites. The participants for the vehicle assessment were selected based on the experience of transporting the RTI victims from the accident spot to the hospital. On the other hand, the local level authorities at selected districts, such as the civil surgeon (CS), director and superintendent of the tertiary hospitals and district hospitals, Upazila Health and Family Welfare Officer from the UpHC, fire brigade authorities and local authorities (district/Upazila level) for the private ambulance services were purposively selected for KIIs. However, some additional respondents, including the representative from private clinic owners’ associations/managing directors/owners of different private hospitals/clinics/volunteer organisations that provided ambulance services, high officials of the local and Dhaka office of the Bangladesh Road Transport Authority (BRTA), and Presidents of the Bangladesh Medical Association and Swadhinata Chikitsak Parishad were selected through snowball sampling. The relevant authority, organisation and facility representatives with the most comprehensive knowledge about the local and national level ambulance system were selected as study participants. Hence, the vehicle assessment of 29 ambulances from the public and private sectors was performed, which was supplemented by 32 KIIs.

Tools development

We developed a checklist for quantitative assessment of the vehicles based on WHO prehospital trauma care guidelines that were most relevant for this study.19 Apart from the ‘respondents’ socio-demographic profile, the checklist also collected information on the ambulance’s essential features, ‘respondents’ knowledge and skill on managing trauma patients during transport and the availability of life-saving equipment and other resources.19 Characteristics of the ambulance such as type of vehicle and ownership, physical infrastructure, manufacturer, function, link to the call centre were noted.19 The knowledge and skills related to assessment of the scene and scene management, physical assessment of the patient (eg, airway, breathing, consciousness, bleeding and injury), patient resuscitation and management of wound, burn, injuries, fracture, etc were elicited.19 Checks were also done for essential equipment and supplies for emergency response, including fire extinguisher tools for immobilising the patient, oxygen cylinder with administration equipment, suction device, etc, and findings recorded.19

For collecting qualitative data, we used a KII guideline. The KII guideline included information on the ambulance service delivery system, ambulance workforce, ambulance dispatch system, ambulance-based equipment and medicines, financing and governance of the ambulance system, access, coverage, responsiveness, quality of ambulance service and recommendations. The expert panel reviewed the instrument and supplies checklists; subsequently, pilot testing was done to ensure the validity and reliability of the tools.

Data collection

For vehicle assessment, data originated through interviews, observation and cross-checking of the basic amenities available in the ‘ambulances’. The data collection team included researchers with medical backgrounds for vehicle assessment and anthropologists to conduct the KIIs. The data collection team was supervised by a field coordinator and monitored by a quality control expert. The in-person face-to-face interviews and on-site observations were conducted in March and November 2020; the pause in between was due to the outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic. Apart from ambulances in the selected facilities, some private ambulances were also included for the assessment.

Data management and analysis

Ambulance-related data were entered in Excel and then transferred to Stata V.13.0 for analysis. Five per cent of the data were re-entered to check for any missing values. A codebook was developed by the research team and used for this purpose. For analysis of qualitative data, verbatim transcriptions were prepared in Bengali. We used a standard template to prepare a transcript and maintain consistency of information. Qualitative data transcripts were read, re-read and checked with voice recordings for accuracy. In the second stage, the Bangla transcripts were translated into English. In the third stage, four researchers developed a list of a priori codes using the guidelines and the code list was used to code the transcripts; inductive codes that emerged were analysed. Given the qualitative nature of the data collected, we used the framework method of analysis.20 We used a matrix to populate and organise data according to the theme subthemes below (table 1).

Results

A total of 29 respondents participated in the ambulance vehicle assessment. Most of the ‘respondents’ were male of 22–45 years of age; of the drivers, only 5 came from the fire brigade. 19 drivers did not complete secondary education; 20 had more than 10 years of working experience. A total of 32 KIIs were conducted with respondents from the relevant public (n=21) and private institutions (n=11) in the four selected study areas for qualitative exploration. Their age ranged between 27 and 67 years, and four were female. There was wide variation in education, for example, from no formal education in the case of the private ambulance association representative to a postgraduate medical degree for the high government officials/physicians of different facilities. Their total work experience ranged from 1.5 to 34 years, and experience in the current workplace ranged from 1 month to 23 years.

Ambulance basic features, equipment and supplies

Of the 29 vehicles, 4 were from the fire brigade, 9 were from public sector facilities and 16 were private ambulances. All the vehicles had functional warning lights and sirens. 93% of the vehicles were microbuses, 75% were ‘makeshift’ type, that is, converted type ‘ambulance’ and 89% were involved in transporting patients only. 88% of these vehicles were linked to the National Emergency Hotline number 999. The ambulances had no Global Positioning System tracker or location identifier system.

Except for the Fire Service Civil Defence (FSCD) ambulances and intensive care unit type ambulances in Dhaka, the makeshift ambulances hardly had the necessary supplies and equipment. Oxygen and stretchers were available in all the ambulances checked; other commonly available items included torch (75%), nasal cannula and tube (72%), plastic bag (55%) and towel and portable water (51%). Most ambulances did not have basic supplies (eg, gauze, bandage, arterial tourniquet, sterile gloves, cleaning solutions) or equipment (eg, torch, immobiliser, blood pressure machines, needles). There was no medicine available in any of the ambulances.

In the first-aid box, medicines are kept for different treatments… primarily a bandage, a splinter… using it we tie a bandage so that they cannot move it, and then we tie a fixed bandage what we provide for primary care…. Life-saving means if any patient has breathing problem then we provide CPR. Providing CPR we drop them to the hospital and our responsibility is done there … (Representative, FSCD)

Public ambulances only contains some basic equipment like stretcher, oxygen cylinder, mask and associated tubing… no medicines are there…actually, no need to have any medicines there is no certified medical personnel is there to prescribe these to patients. (District Hospital Authority)

The available workforce in the ambulances, including their knowledge and skills

Except for the Department of FSCD (https://fireservice.gov.bd/), other facilities do not have any organograms, nor could they specify the number of staff available for ambulance services. Most public facilities had ambulance drivers according to the number of available vehicles, for example, Dhaka Medical College Hospital had eight drivers for their eight functional ambulances. The UpHC/DHs usually had one or two ambulances. In some cases, only one driver was available for two ambulances. No helper or trained medical/primary care staff was available for any ambulance except for the Department of FSCD ambulances. The Department of FSCD had a structured ambulance service workforce organogram that included a team leader, one driver, one trained nursing assistant and firefighters. Private facilities do not have any structured organogram and drivers were the main workforce for providing ambulance services; however, a few ambulances had helpers who had no training in emergency care service. To quote:

Number of drivers is one here, although we have two ambulances…We do not have any organogram for ambulance workforce. (District Hospital Authority)

Most of the ambulance drivers were not trained in first-aid or life-saving skills; however, few learnt some skills through ‘learning by doing’ work experience. In the quantitative assessment, most respondents had no knowledge regarding scene survey, scene management, patient assessment and management of emergency conditions like airway, breathing, circulation, shock, hypothermia, wound and burn. Respondents had some knowledge on recognising life-threatening injuries, removal of foreign bodies from the airway, immobilise patients, etc. They had adequate knowledge regarding calls for help, evaluating the extent of bleeding, controlling external haemorrhage through direct pressure and arranging patient transport and oxygen administration. One of the ambulance drivers recounted:

Training means no formal training…We have learnt some basic things from hospital staff just by observing. Dressing, fixing bandage, transfusing saline and fixing oxygen line etc. …I do not have training. But at hospitals we have nurses, we have ward boys…we have learnt from them. (Representative, Ambulance Drivers Association)

The absence of a trained workforce for the operation of the ambulances, in both public and private sectors, was a major gap mentioned repeatedly by the KIs. There was no specific training on medical/first-aid care/primary care provided to the drivers (who sometimes happen to be the only person managing and transporting the victim) from any public or private institute. Although Fire Service drivers were not trained in primary care, the FSCD authority has had nursing assistants with basic 2 weeks’ training in primary care. The drivers of the private ambulances were mostly self-learners. Moreover, ambulance drivers had no specific skill requirement apart from the regular driving license.

Driving license is the only requirement for the recruitment of ambulance drivers in public facilities…any eighth grade completed person having a driving license can be recruited as an ambulance driver… (Upazila Health Complex Authority)

Private ambulance drivers don’t need any educational qualifications…only the driving license is enough. (Ambulance Owner Association Representative)

Availability of ambulances when in need, services provided and cost charged

Respondents found it difficult to accurately report the total number of functional ambulances (both public and private) in the study areas and had varied opinions. The number of functional ambulances in public facilities at UpHCs and DHs appeared to range between 8 (in Jhenaidah district) to a maximum of 16 (in Jashore district). The number of ambulances by the Fire Service department ranged between 1 (in Feni district) to a maximum of 20 (in Dhaka district). According to private ambulance association authorities, the maximum number of ambulances reported was in Dhaka (n=3000), whereas Jhenaidah district had the lowest number of ambulances (about 30–40).

I will not say that the total number of ambulances is sufficient… the total number of ambulances must be less against the population ratio in Dhaka city. (Hospital Authority _ Dhaka)

Respondents mentioned that any type of transport available near the injury spot is used by the injured to reach the hospital for emergency medical services (EMSs). Some commonly used vehicles included rickshaw vans, rickshaws, three-wheelers, private cars, microbuses (rented) and private/charitable local ambulances.

Victims are taken to the hospital by whatever transport is available in the injury area… It can be a van, CNG or microbus. (District Hospital Authority)

The public facility ambulances were mostly used to transport referred patients from one hospital to another, within and outside the districts; some were also used for pick-up and drop-off services of the hospital staff. Findings reveal that Fire Service ambulances played a vital role in providing necessary emergency care to RTI patients apart from their regular services. Most private ambulances used to provide care to all types of RTI cases.

The main role of our ambulance is to send the discharged patients to their home…also our staff use these as regular office transport. (Medical College Hospital Authority)

All public ambulances, including Fire Service ambulances, followed the service charge rates fixed by the government (eg, 9 Bangladeshi Taka (BDT) per km). There were no such fixed rates for the private ambulances. The service rate varied based on the RTI victim’s need (eg, arranging any oxygen cylinder or first-aid, which may not be available in the ambulances) and distance. The service charge across study areas also varied, both within and outside the area. For the private ambulance, the range of service charges varied between BDT 500 and 13 000 within the city area; outside the city, the charges varied from a minimum of BDT 1000 to a maximum of 50 000 based on distance and ambulance type.

Governance

According to the KIs, there is no common platform for the ambulance system in Bangladesh. However, some respondents assumed that the DGHS of the MoHFW might be the authority at the central level. At the local level, they assumed that the system could be under the authority of the district CS. The FSCD ambulances were operated by the Department under the Ministry of Home Affairs. The private ambulances are mostly organised at the regional or local levels without any central authority.

I think ambulances are organized at the facility level… we don’t have any higher authority to control or monitor the ambulances, we do it from our hospital. (District Hospital Authority)

Private ambulances are organized at facility level…we buy, manage, regulate and control our ambulances…no authority from govt. or private sector is involved here. (Ambulance Owners Association Authority)

Most of the KIs had no idea about the ambulance registration process; a few perceived that the ambulances are registered by BRTA and that no additional criteria are applied for registration. According to the BRTA authorities, the serial number of ambulances usually starts at 71, whereas the makeshift ambulances have the same serial number as the microbuses. According to the BRTA respondent, ambulances used to be registered under institution/organisation ownership and not individuals as per government rules. Public ambulances were financed by the government, and private ambulances by the owners and/or relevant organisations; some ambulances were financed by the ambulance driver/owners’ association. The financing of the charitable or other social welfare-based ambulance services varied; some were partially financed, some fully subsidised and influential local people and elites also supported a few.

Public ambulances are financed by govt…these are under govt. facilities, so there must be a fund for the servicing and repairing or regular use like other services we do have… (District Hospital Authority)

Private ambulance financing or any management is done by their own facility or by the individual driver/owner… It mainly depends upon the amount of users’ payment… (Private Clinic Owner Association Representative)

According to the respondents, no nationwide standard operational procedure or standard indicators/benchmarks are available to monitor the quality of ambulance-based emergency services.

No …we do not have any indicator or benchmark for validating the standard of these ambulances. (District Health Authority).

Discussion

Ambulance services are vital in providing prehospital treatment and care in the ‘golden hour’. Swift and safe transport of patients from the emergency scene to the appropriate healthcare facility for further life-saving interventions requires a comprehensive, structured system which is largely lacking in the LMICs. This study was done to explore the current status of ambulance-based emergency care services available in the selected districts of Bangladesh to inform the design of an improved system in the country. We applied a cross-sectional, qualitative and quantitative approach for the assessment of the situation. Findings reveal a poor state of the ambulance services in the country. The services are fragmented, rudimentary, poorly resourced (both regarding human resources for health and logistics and equipment) and uncoordinated.

The WHO emphasises the importance of EMS systems which are usually the first point of contact with the healthcare systems.21 Many high-income countries have well-developed emergency ambulance systems with essential equipment and staff trained to provide on-the-spot care and support during transportation to health facilities as needed.22 The situation is different in many LMICs like Bangladesh, where there is a lack of effective and efficient ambulance services.23

Our study reveals the near absence of essential equipment and supplies in most ambulances (mostly ‘makeshift’) assessed. Similar observations are made in another study, which identified the lack of necessary equipment for trauma response as a major barrier in sub-Saharan Africa and Pakistan; hospital ambulances lacked essential equipment such as burn kits and spine immobilisation boards.24 25 In contrast, a study in India reported that around 68% of ambulances met the standards.26

In our study, no public or private ambulances had any trained emergency care provider in the ambulances except the FSCD ambulances; most ambulance drivers did not know emergency primary care management. A similar observation was made in a review where 61% (19/31) of the articles identified a lack of skilled personnel as a critical barrier.27

In our study, respondents were uncertain about the number of ambulances available in the selected districts. For example, the number of private ambulances in Dhaka city was mentioned to be around 3000. Uncertainty prevails when, in another study, the number of available ambulances in Dhaka city was estimated to be 2.8 per 100 000 inhabitants.28

Interestingly, the public sector ambulances were found to be used not for transferring victims from the injury spots to the nearest health facility but for transporting referral patients and, quite frequently, for transporting staff. This is not uncommon for ambulances in the LMICs where most ambulances are used for transport and not as emergency care vehicles.29

We found that public ambulances had fixed fare rates, and the fares varied based on distance and type of ambulance for private ambulances. At the public facility, service costs increased when referred to tertiary facilities with major injuries, and in private facilities, care costs were unregulated and much higher. Another study from Dhaka reported that only 11% of emergency patients used an ambulance to get to the hospitals, and transport costs were paid out of pocket.28

We found that the ambulance system was running without any central (or regional) authority or system. There was ambiguity regarding the responsible operator/authority for the public sector ambulance system in the country, and private ambulances run under their system of governance. This fragmented system is also a major problem reported in Nepal.30 Overall, there is still a substantial need for evidence to understand the strengths, weaknesses and priority areas for emergency ambulance service improvement in low-resource settings31 like Bangladesh.

Strengths and limitations

This study was conducted in selected districts close to the accident-prone highways with the purposively selected participants. Therefore, the findings cannot be generalised to the whole country. In addition, the vehicle assessment survey was conducted on a relatively small sample. Thus, we recommend a large-scale survey for the nationwide ambulance service assessment, including cost-effectiveness analysis of the use of ambulances in prehospital first responder care for RTI patients.

Despite the limitations, our study provided insights regarding the current situation and gaps in ambulance-based emergency care for road traffic accident victims and contributed to the development of an evidence-based, comprehensive and regulated national ambulance service in the country. Besides, this study applied both quantitative and qualitative methods to strengthen the validity and reliability of the findings. The quantitative data for ambulance assessment were collected through both face-to-face interviews and observation checklists and helped triangulation of findings to strengthen evidence.

Conclusions and recommendations

The current ambulance system in Bangladesh is characterised by a lack of trained human resources as first responders (ambulance drivers and assistants), including shortage of basic emergency supplies (gauze, bandage, etc) and essential equipment like blood pressure machine. Besides, there is poor coordination and governance in the system, compromising the quality of emergency care during transport. Findings suggest further investigation using large-scale data for some comprehensive reform of the existing system, such as ensuring the availability of essential equipment and basic supplies resources for resuscitation and first-aid as per the international norm and for the registration of a vehicle as an ambulance. Next, training and developing human resources (including drivers, bystanders and the highway police) in life-saving emergency care services is essential for managing RTI victims in the ‘golden’ hour. A recommendation is made to develop a national ambulance service in the country, integrating public and private sectors under a central/regional authority (eg, division) having a common organogram and a universal standard operating procedure. This will help reduce RTI-related morbidity and mortality and contribute to achieving universal health coverage by 2030.
