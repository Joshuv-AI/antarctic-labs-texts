Metabolizable and bioavailable energy of soybean gums and soap stocks determined in precision-fed roosters, restrict-fed broiler chickens, and ad libitum-fed broiler chickens
Authors: S.C. Wells, S.W. Hufford, R.L. Drysdale, L.S. Parisi, K. Gaffield, M.T. Kidd, B.W. Parsons
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The objective was to determine metabolizable and bioavailable energy content of soybean gums and soap stocks using precision-fed roosters and both restrict-fed and ad libitum-fed broiler chickens. In Experiment 1, nitrogen-corrected true metabolizable energy (TMEn) of gums and soap stocks relative to refined soybean oil was determined using precision-fed adult Single Comb White Leghorn roosters. Diet 1 was 85% corn and 15% Solka Floc. Diets 2 through 7 were the same as diet 1, except 7.5 or 15% soybean oil, gums, or soap stocks were added in place of Solka Floc. In Experiment 2, TMEn of soybean meal (SBM) with 4% inclusion of gums and soap stocks was measured in precision-fed roosters. In Experiments 1 and 2, roosters were fasted for 26 h, precision-fed 25 g of diets, and excreta were quantitatively collected for 48 h. In Experiment 3, relative bioavailable energy (RBV) of gums and soap stocks was determined in restrict-fed broilers. Energy-deficient diets containing 0, 5, or 10% soybean oil, gums, or soap stocks were fed from 10 to 21 d-of-age. In Experiment 4, nitrogen-corrected apparent metabolizable energy (AMEn) was determined in SBM with 4% inclusion of gums and soap stocks using ad libitum-fed broilers from 19 to 22 d-of-age. In Experiment 1, TMEn increased with lipid inclusion (P < 0.05). The TMEn of gums and soap stocks relative to soybean oil was 53 and 45% (as-fed basis) and 79 and 81% (DM basis), respectively. In Experiment 2, the addition of 4% gums and soap stocks increased TMEn of SBM (P < 0.05). In Experiment 3, RBV of gums and soap stocks ranged from 40 to 51% (as-fed basis) and 73 to 77% (DM basis) relative to soybean oil in broilers. In Experiment 4, the addition of 4% gums and soap stocks increased AMEn of SBM (P < 0.05) in broilers. In conclusion, soybean gums and soap stocks are viable lipid energy sources in poultry diets, particularly when moisture content is reduced.

Introduction

Soybean meal (SBM) is the most used protein source in poultry diets in the United States. Its widespread use is largely due to its high CP content (typically 45 to 48%, as-fed) and a well-balanced amino acid profile that complements cereal grains like corn (Summers and Fisher, 1961; Pettigrew et al., 2008; Dei, 2011; Cromwell, 2012; Aguirre et al., 2024). Since soybeans are processed for both oil and protein, SBM has become a reliable and consistent feed ingredient, with poultry accounting for more than 61% of all SBM consumed in the U.S. (American Soybean Association, 2024). As the volume of SBM production has increased over the past decade, there has been increasing interest in exploring potential uses for co-products generated during soybean oil refining. Among these co-products are soybean gums and soap stocks, which may be referred to as “soybean add-backs” when reintroduced into SBM during processing. This practice is especially common when oil refineries are located adjacent to soybean crushing facilities (Gerde et al., 2020). Gums are produced from crude soybean oil during the degumming step, where phosphoric acid and water are added to crude soybean oil, causing precipitation of phospholipids (Daun, 2004; Erickson, 1995a, 1995b). Soap stocks, however, are produced in the next sequential step of oil refining, when sodium hydroxide is added to neutralize free fatty acids. This step results in the recovery of a mix of free fatty acids, mono- and diglycerides, and other residual compounds (Erickson, 1995a, 1995c; Vieira et al., 2015).

As is common with many co-products, the nutrient composition of gums and soap stocks can be variable due to differences in the levels of impurities that were removed from crude soybean oil during the refining process (Gaffield et al., 2024b). Soybean gums and soap stocks contain roughly 47% and 30% crude fat on an as-fed basis, respectively, which makes them a potentially valuable energy source in poultry diets (Gaffield et al., 2024b). Furthermore, lipid-rich ingredients can also exhibit extra-caloric or extra-metabolic effects in the diet where they can reduce digesta passage rate, thereby increasing digestibility of other feedstuffs in the diet (Horani and Sell, 1977; Wiseman et al., 1986). Although these effects can be useful in practical diets, this poses additional challenges when evaluating lipid-based feedstuffs, as the determined metabolizable energy (ME) content of the feedstuff itself can exceed the gross energy content of the ingredient depending on the bioassay used. Two methods commonly used to determine ME values of lipid-based feedstuffs are the restricted-growth broiler chick assay (Squibb, 1971) and the precision-fed rooster assay (Aardsma et al., 2017).

The objective of this study was to determine the available energy content in soybean gums and soap stocks and the effects of these products on the metabolizable energy of SBM using precision-fed roosters, restrict-fed broiler chickens, and ad libitum-fed broiler chickens.

Materials and methods

The protocols used in conducting these experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Arkansas Division of Agriculture at Fayetteville, AR, U.S. (numbers 24015 and 24043).

Test lipids

The lipids evaluated herein were refined soybean oil, soybean gums, and soybean soap stocks. Refined soybean oil was used as the reference standard for expression of relative energy availability in Experiments 1 and 3. This sample was a food-grade product. The gums and soap stocks used in the present study were obtained from a commercial soybean crushing plant (Bunge Global, Inc., Emporia, KS, U.S). These 2 samples were crude products that had not undergone additional purification or acidulation. Immediately upon receipt, samples were stored frozen at -20°C to preserve quality. The same samples of soybean gums and soap stocks were utilized for all 4 experiments herein. The conventional SBM utilized in Experiments 2 and 4, to which additional gums and soap stocks were added, was obtained from a commercial solvent-extracted soybean crushing plant.

Experimental design

Four experiments were conducted to determine the available energy content in soybean gums and soap stocks to assess the potential effects of these co-products on the ME content in SBM.

Experiment 1 was conducted to determine the nitrogen-correct true ME (TMEn) of soybean gums and soap stocks relative to refined soybean oil. Adult Single Comb White Leghorn roosters with intact ceca were used in a completely randomized design. Adult roosters were individually housed in cages with raised wire floors and nipple drinkers in an environmentally controlled room with a 16 h light: 8 h dark cycle. A total of 42 roosters were utilized in this experiment, where there were 7 treatments with 6 replicates pens of 1 individually caged rooster per treatment. Roosters were 52 wk-of-age and weighed approximately 2,200 g. Prior to the initiation of the experimental period, roosters had unrestricted access to a nutritionally complete mash feed via a trough feeder. Roosters were fasted for 26 h prior to being precision-fed 25 g of feed. Excreta were collected for 48 h post feeding and were subsequently frozen prior to being lyophilized, ground, and analyzed for gross energy and nitrogen.

There were 7 dietary treatments in Experiment 1. Diet 1 consisted of 85% corn and 15% Solka Floc, which was used as an inert filler. Dietary treatments 2 through 7 were the same as diet 1, except that 2 increasing levels of 7.5 and 15% of each test lipid source were added in place of Solka Floc. The 3 lipid sources evaluated were refined soybean oil, soybean gums, and soybean soap stocks. The levels of test ingredients fed were selected based on previous studies by Aardsma and Parsons (2017); Aardsma et al. (2017), and von Schaumburg et al. (2019). The TMEn of the diets was calculated as described below.(1)TMEn of diet (kcal/kg) = [(GE consumed - (GE excreted by fed birds + 8.22 × nitrogen retained by fed birds) + (GE excreted by fasted birds + 8.22 × nitrogen retained by fasted birds)) / feed intake (g)] × 1000where: GE consumed (kcal) = diet intake (g) × gross energy of diet (kcal/g); GE excreted by fed or fasted birds (kcal) = excreta voided (g) × gross energy of excreta (kcal/g); 8.22 = gross energy (kcal) of uric acid per g of nitrogen (Hill and Anderson, 1958); nitrogen retained by fed or fasted birds (g) = diet intake (g) × diet nitrogen (%) – excreta voided (g) × excreta nitrogen (%).

The TMEn of the test lipids was then calculated using the following equation:(2)TMEn of test ingredient (kcal/kg) = [TMEn of test diet (kcal/kg) – TMEn of diet 1 (kcal/kg)] / [test ingredient inclusion rate (%) / 100]

The TMEn of test ingredients was also calculated using the slope-ratio method described by Aardsma and Parsons (2017), where the TMEn of the diet was regressed on supplemental lipid level. Multiple linear regression was conducted using the model below.(3)Y = a0 + b1x1 + b2x2 + b3x3where: Y = TMEn of diet (kcal/kg); a0 = y-intercept; b1 = slope of refined soybean oil; x1 = soybean oil inclusion rate (%); b2 = slope of soybean gums; x2 = soybean gums inclusion rate (%); b3 = slope of soybean soap stocks; x3 = soybean soap stocks inclusion rate (%).

Experiment 2 was conducted to determine the TMEn of soybean meal with additional gums and soap stocks. Adult Single Comb White Leghorn roosters with intact ceca were used in a completely randomized design. All housing and procedures were the same as Experiment 1. A total of 24 roosters were utilized in this experiment, where there were 4 treatments with 6 replicates pens of 1 individually caged rooster per treatment The 4 treatments consisted of: 1) conventional soybean meal, 2) as 1 + 4% gums, 3) as 1 + 4% soap stocks, and 4) as 1 + 2% gums and 2% soap stocks. The TMEn of soybean meals were calculated using equation [1]. The levels of gums and soap stocks were selected to represent estimated levels of these products that are blended into SBM at commercial solvent-extraction soybean crushing plants (Gaffield et al., 2024a)

In Experiment 3, male Cobb 500 broiler chickens were used to determine relative bioavailable energy (RBV) of soybean oil, gums, and soap stocks. A total of 210 broiler chickens were utilized in this experiment, where there were 7 treatments with 6 replicates pens of 5 broiler chickens per treatment. Broiler chickens were obtained from a commercial hatchery and were housed in battery cages with raised wire floors in an environmentally controlled room (cages were 24 inches by 24 inches). The lighting schedule was as follows: 24 h of light on d0, 23L:1D from 1 to 4 d-of-age, 20L:4D from 5 to 15 d-of-age, and 18L:6D from d16 until the end of the experiment. Chickens were provided a nutritionally complete crumbled corn-soybean meal starter diet ad libitum until 9 d-of-age. On d9, birds were fasted overnight prior to the start of the experimental phase. On d10, birds were individually weighed and allotted to treatment groups in such a way as to equalize initial BW across treatments. Each cage of 5 birds was equipped with a single trough feeder and a nipple drinker. From 10 to 21 d-of-age, birds were restrict-fed the dietary treatments to equalize feed intake among pens and to ensure energy would be the limiting factor for weight gain (Squibb, 1971). During the experimental phase, feed was added every morning at 0800 h and feeders were shaken 3 times per d to ensure complete consumption of feed. Mortality was accounted for before each feeding. Feed allotment for the first feeding (study day 0, 10 d-of-age) was determined by multiplying published daily intakes (Cobb-Vantress, 2022) of birds of equivalent weight by 0.7 to achieve a 30% feed restriction as described by Aardsma et al. (2017). Each subsequent day’s allotment was calculated as the next published day’s intake multiplied by 0.7 (Table 1). In Experiment 4, male Cobb 500 broiler chickens were used to determine nitrogen-corrected apparent metabolizable energy (AMEn) in SBM with or without additional gums and soap stocks. A total of 150 broiler chickens were utilized in this experiment, where there were 5 treatments with 6 replicates pens of 5 broiler chickens per treatment. Broiler chickens were obtained from a commercial hatchery and were housed in battery cages with raised wire floors in an environmentally controlled room (cages were 24 inches by 24 inches) as described previously for Experiment 3. Chickens were provided a nutritionally complete crumbled corn-soybean meal starter diet ad libitum until 18 d-of-age. On d18, birds were fasted overnight prior to the start of the experimental phase. On d19, birds were individually weighed and allotted to treatment groups in such a way as to equalize initial BW across treatments. Each cage of 5 birds was equipped with a single trough feeder and a nipple drinker. From 19 to 22 d-of-age, birds were provided dietary treatments ad libitum. From d20 to 22, excreta were collected on trays placed beneath each cage. On d22, feed intake and BW were recorded and excreta were collected. Excreta were stored frozen prior to being lyophilized, ground, and analyzed for gross energy, nitrogen, and titanium.

Dietary treatments in Experiment 4 were mash corn-soybean meal-based diets (Table 3). Diet 1 was a nutritionally complete corn-soybean meal-based diet which served as the reference diet. Diets 2 through 5 were the same as diet 1, except that 30% of the test ingredients were added in place of the complete reference diet. The test ingredients were the same samples as Experiment 2: 1) conventional soybean meal, 2) as 1 + 4% gums, 3) as 1 + 4% soap stocks, and 4) as 1 + 2% gums and 2% soap stocks. All diets contained 0.5% titanium dioxide, which was used as an indigestible marker. Chemical analysis

Samples were analyzed for gross energy in an adiabatic bomb calorimeter (Model 6200, Parr Instruments, Moline, IL, U.S), nitrogen via combustion (AOAC International, 2007; method 990.03) and DM (AOAC International, 2007; method 930.15) at the Central Analytical Lab, University of Arkansas, Division of Agriculture, Fayetteville, AR, U.S. Additional analyses were conducted at the Agricultural Experiment Station Chemical Laboratories, University of Missouri, Columbia, MO, U.S. to measure titanium (Meyers et al., 2004), fatty acid profile (AOAC International, 2007; methods 996.06, 965.49, 969.33; AOCS, 2013; method Ce2-66), iodine value (AOCS, 2013; recommended practice Ja 14-91), insolubles (AOCS, 2013; method Ca 3a-46), unsaponifiables (AOCS, 2013; method Ca6a-40), and peroxide value (AOAC International, 2007; method 965.33).

Calculations and statistical analyses

Data were analyzed using the GLM procedure of SAS (SAS Institute Inc., 2011). All data were initially analyzed using a 1-way ANOVA for a completely randomized design. Differences among means were evaluated using Fisher’s least significant difference test (Carmer and Walker, 1985). Multiple linear regression analysis was then conducted in Experiments 1 and 3 to determine TMEn and bioavailable energy of gums and soap stocks relative to refined soybean oil using the slope-ratio method (Littell et al., 1995; 1997). In Experiment 1, TMEn of diets was regressed on supplemental lipid intake for soybean oil, gums, and soap stocks. In Experiment 3, BW gain was regressed on supplemental lipid intake for soybean oil, gums, and soap stocks. The relative TMEn and RBV of the test lipids were calculated by dividing the regression coefficient (i.e., slope) of the test lipids by the regression coefficient (i.e., slope) of soybean oil. Slope contrasts were performed to assess differences among relative TMEn and RBV values obtained from multiple linear regression. There were 6 individually caged roosters per treatment in Experiments 1 and 2, in which 1 individually caged rooster was the experimental unit. There were 6 pens of 5 broiler chickens per treatment in Experiments 3 and 4, in which the pen was the experimental unit. Statistical significance was considered at P < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Composition of lipid sources

The analyzed composition of the lipids used in all experiments are presented in Table 4. The fatty acid (FA) profile of the soybean oil herein was in good agreement with the NRC (1994). No information is available for the FA profiles of crude soybean gums and soap stocks in either the poultry (1994) or swine (2012) NRC; however, the values observed in the present study were similar to Gaffield et al. (2024b) and Lipstein et al. (1965). The moisture levels of soybean gums and soap stocks in the present study were 34.5 and 44.7%, respectively, and the crude fat levels were 66.4 and 48.3%, respectively. These were within the ranges reported in Gaffield et al. (2024b) for moisture and crude fat content of the 24 gum and 12 soap stocks samples that were evaluated by those authors. All 3 lipid sources in this study (Table 4) had a high proportion of linoleic, oleic, palmitic, and linolenic acids compared with other FA. The peroxide values for the gums and soap stocks were considerably higher compared with refined soybean oil despite samples being stored frozen immediately upon receipt from the crushing plant. The AMEn of the control soybean meal in Experiment 4 was estimated at 2,345 kcal/kg as-fed, which is consistent with previously reported values for typical SBM in the literature (Potter and Matterson, 1960; NRC, 1994; Aguirre et al., 2024). Results obtained from precision-fed roosters in Experiment 2 and ad libitum-fed broiler chickens in Experiment 4 were in good agreement. Both the precision-fed rooster assay and the ad libitum-fed broiler assay demonstrated increased ME content of SBM when additional gums or soap stocks were incorporated into the control SBM. The magnitude of increases, however, were greater in ad libitum-fed broilers than in precision-fed roosters. For example, the mean increase in AMEn was 264 kcal/kg DM in Experiment 4, which was greater than the mean increase in TMEn of 79 kcal/kg DM in Experiment 2. The cause for the discrepancy in magnitude of the responses observed among bioassays is likely due to differences in feeding regimen rather than age, since the estimates obtained for the relative TMEn and RBV of gums and soap stocks in Experiments 1 and 3 were similar. In ad libitum-fed broilers, it is possible that there is a greater opportunity for alterations in digesta passage rate from increased dietary lipids, which could thereby increase the ME of the control SBM that the gums and soap stocks were added to (Horani and Sell, 1977).

When evaluating the data obtained from Experiments 1 through 4 herein, the relative TMEn and RBV of gums and soap stocks and their ability to increase the ME of SBM was dependent upon expression of values on an as-fed vs. DM basis. It is common for crude gums and soap stocks to contain high levels of moisture, where the mean moisture content of gums and soap stocks in the survey conducted by Gaffield et al. (2024b) was 18 and 29%, respectively. The water content in these crude ingredients dilutes the energy of these feedstuffs while also reducing the shelf life as they can be prone to microbial growth and oxidative rancidity. It is important to note, however, that facilities that both crush oilseeds and refine seed oils can add crude gums and soap stocks back to the SBM prior to the toasting process, thereby removing the added moisture in the SBM from the gums and soap stocks (Gaffield et al., 2024a). Therefore, the evaluation of the available energy content of crude gums and soap stocks herein on a DM basis is likely more indicative of the captured value of these products on a commercial level. The high relative TMEn and RBV of crude gums and soap stocks relative to soybean oil ranging from 73 to 81% on a DM basis (Table 5, Table 7), combined with the increased TMEn and AMEn of SBM with added gums and soap stocks (Table 6, Table 9) demonstrates that these products can be blended into SBM at crushing plants to not only provide a use for gums and soap socks, but also increase the energy content of SBM. Although there has been relatively little research conducted in poultry with crude gums and soap stocks, studies that have evaluated acidulated forms of these co-products have reported similar or slightly lower nutrient utilization compared with other lipid-based products (Thacker et al., 1994; Irandoust et al., 2012; Kerr et al., 2024).

In summary, the present study provides information on the use of crude gums and soap stocks as a dietary energy source for poultry, an area in which there is a paucity of information, and integrates both digestibility-based and bioavailability-based approaches to provide a robust view of energy availability in the feedstuffs evaluated. Soybean oil consistently delivered the highest ME values of the 3 soybean co-products evaluated; however, soybean-derived lipid sources, crude gums and soap stocks, still provided considerable energy contributions for poultry when corrected for moisture content. The relative TMEn and RBV of soybean gums and soap stocks ranged from 73 to 81% on a DM basis. Furthermore, the addition of 4% gums and soap stocks to SBM increased the TMEn and AMEn of SBM on a DM basis in both precision-fed roosters and ad libitum-fed broiler chickens. Overall, moisture content was a critical factor influencing the energy value of gums and soap stocks. Data herein indicates that if the moisture content of SBM is adequately controlled, both gums and soap stocks can be blended into SBM at soybean crushing plants as viable energy sources to increase the ME of SBM for poultry, while also providing a use for the co-products obtained from soybean oil refinement.
