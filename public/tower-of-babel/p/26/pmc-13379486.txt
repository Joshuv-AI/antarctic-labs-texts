Prevalence and management of residual respiratory events in OSA patients treated with CPAP: a prospective observational clinical experience
Authors: S. Hondeghem, T. Mettay, C. Espana-Dominguez, A. V. Bruyneel, Marie Bruyneel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeResidual respiratory events (RREs) are common in continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) users. They can lead to poor CPAP adherence and early termination. The management of RREs has not been standardized yet. The purpose of this study was to test a structured management algorithm for RREs detected by CPAP devices in OSA patients.MethodsThis was a prospective observational clinical study that aimed to test a specific algorithm in patients exhibiting RREs. This study also included a case-control comparison. Cases were OSA patients (AHI ≥ 15) exhibiting RREs (AHIflow >10) during the first 3 months of CPAP use, reported by telemonitoring. The control group were similar CPAP patients with no RREs. All underwent in-lab polysomnography (PSG) with manual titration to set the fixed CPAP pressure on the Resmed® CPAP device. In case of RRE, a specific algorithm was followed to manage and resolve these events, applied only after leak correction.ResultsA total of 19/273 patients exhibited RREs. Compared to matched controls, cases had higher RREs on titration PSG and used more often naso-buccal masks (p < 0.05). Among the 19 RRE patients, predominant obstructive AHIflow was observed in 15 patients, which generally resolved rapidly with an increase in CPAP pressure, allowing an AHIflow ≤10 to be obtained. Predominant central AHIflow and Cheyne-Stokes respiration (CSR) was observed in four patients. Individualized management was successful and one was moved to auto-servo ventilation.ConclusionsOnce leaks are controlled, occurrence of RREs in compliant CPAP users is uncommon. A structured algorithm helped clarify the likely mechanism of device-detected RREs and guided individualized management during the first months of CPAP therapy. The most common cause of RRE was insufficient CPAP pressure, but cases of COMISA and of “idiopathic” central sleep apnea (CSA)/CSR also occurred that required specific management.

Introduction

Residual respiratory events (RREs) are common in continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) users, occurring in 10% to 25% of people with moderate-to-severe obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) despite careful CPAP titration [1]. Some factors have been identified as responsible for high intra-subject RRE variability including age, OSA severity at diagnosis, heart failure, opioids, and alcohol consumption [2].

RREs can be both obstructive and central and can be detected via today’s CPAP devices either by downloading data from a SD card or via telemonitoring (TMg). Device-detected RREs are of major clinical importance. For example, it has become clear that in patients who exhibit treatment-emergent central sleep apnea (TE-CSA), CPAP adherence is poor and early termination is common, in comparison to those with classic OSA [3]. Furthermore, TE-CSA is associated with comorbid conditions that can benefit from identification and treatment (e.g., atrial fibrillation). When correctly identified and adequately managed, patient symptoms and quality of life can be improved [4].

The routine use of telemonitoring (TMg) of CPAP-treated OSA patients has changed management of our patients. Recorded CPAP data review allows to objectively document compliance, treatment efficacy (e.g., presence of RREs, apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) flow), and potential causes of side effects (e.g., leaks). TMg can be used on asynchronous (store-and-forward) and the synchronous (real-time) models [5]. The asynchronous model is generally used to prepare for medical (or technical) consultations, while the synchronous TMg model is generally used to monitor CPAP-recorded data over several days following treatment adaptation in the event of a problem being detected.

The management of device-detected RREs has not been standardized yet. Researchers and scientific societies have proposed different algorithms to help clinicians manage these events [6–8]. The first step is always to eliminate leaks, as CPAP algorithms for event detection are not reliable for measuring RREs in cases involving significant leaks [9]. The retained cut-off point at which RREs need attention and require treatment adaptation is AHIflow>10.

The purpose of this study was to test a management algorithm for device-detected RREs in CPAP-treated OSA patients. Secondary aims were to assess the prevalence of RREs and to compare RRE patient characteristics to those of patients who were not exhibiting RREs.

Materials and methods

Setting

The study was performed in the pneumology department of the CHU Brugmann, a tertiary university hospital located in Brussels.

Patients

Consecutive adult patients diagnosed with moderate-to-severe obstructive sleep apnea syndrome (OSAS) (AHI ≥15) assessed by in-lab attended polysomnography (PSG) and treated with CPAP were prospectively included. All patients underwent in-lab PSG with manual titration to set the fixed CPAP pressure for further therapy [10]. The morning after manual titration, a fixed CPAP pressure level was prescribed to eliminate obstructive respiratory events, snoring and oxygen desaturation, even during supine rapid eye movement (REM) sleep. All patients were treated with the Resmed® CPAP device (S10, S11) and telemonitored via Airview by ResMed® which is a secure, cloud-based patient management system designed for HCW to remotely monitor respiratory therapy.

Exclusion criteria included predominant central sleep apnea syndrome, language barriers, cognitive or psychiatric disorders making comprehension of information regarding CPAP therapy and the study’s informed consent difficult.

Design

This was a prospective observational clinical study that aimed to test a specific algorithm in patients exhibiting RREs. This study also included a case-control comparison. Cases were CPAP patients exhibiting an RREs (AHIflow >10) during the first 3 months of CPAP use, reported by TMg printout. The control group consisted of similar patients who do not exhibit RREs. Cases and controls were issued from the same patient’s cohort and recruited during the same period. An individual one-to-one matching was performed for age (± 5 years), body mass index (BMI (± 5 kg/m2), gender, and OSA category (moderate, AHI ≥ 15 and ≤ 29, severe, AHI ≥ 30).

Usual care follow-up

The TMg report was printed before the first medical visit after CPAP initiation, planned 3 months after CPAP treatment initiation. No remote control of CPAP efficacy/use was performed in between.

Intervention in case of RRE detected by CPAP device

In case of device-detected RREs (AHIflow >10 on CPAP data extraction report), a specific algorithm was followed to manage these events (Fig. 1). These device-detected RREs were not systematically confirmed by PSG or respiratory polygraphy (RP).Fig. 1Algorithm applied to manage respiratory residual events in CPAP users. Algorithm applied to manage respiratory residual events in CPAP users. OAHI obstructive apnea-hypopnea index, CAHI central apnea-hypopnea index, TMg telemonitoring, CPAP continuous positive airway pressure, CSR Cheyne-Stokes respiration, P percentile

The algorithm was developed based on existing guidance [6–8], with the novel feature of suppressing leaks before intervention. It was designed for use with compliant patients only, in order to minimize bias related to leaks and adherence.

In this case, a remote control of TMg data was performed 2 weeks after intervention. In some patients, 2 interventions were needed until the problem was resolved or the cause of the RREs was correctly identified and another therapy was tried. RREs were considered as resolved when AHIflow ≤10 was achieved.

Data collection

Demographics, lifestyle habits, medical history, detailed data from diagnostic and titration PSG, and the results of four questionnaires (Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS), Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS), Insomnia Severity Index (ISI), Functional outcomes of sleep questionnaire (FOSQ)) fulfilled during diagnostic PSG and medical consultation at 3 months were collected.

Ethical approval

All included patients provided written informed consent to participate in the study. The study protocol was approved by the Brugmann University Hospital ethics committee (B0772021000123).

Data treatment and statistics

The RRE group and the control group were described using descriptive statistics for sociodemographic characteristics, PSG diagnosis, titration PSG parameters, and questionnaire scores (ESS, FSS, ISI, and FOSQ). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. To address the study objectives, the two groups were compared after 3 months of follow-up for questionnaire outcomes and CPAP data. Initial CPAP parameters (initial pressure and initial mask) were also reported.

Given the matched case-control design, all comparisons between groups were conducted using paired statistical procedures. For categorical variables, Chi2 test was used, or the Fisher exact test when appropriate. For continuous variables, the normality of within-pair differences was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. When the assumptions for parametric testing were met, a paired t-test was applied; otherwise, the non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen's d. A p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics.

Discussion

This study shows that RREs are rather unusual in clinical practice when leaks have been previously controlled. In this cohort, a structured algorithm helped clarify the likely mechanism of device-detected RREs (Resmed® CPAP devices) and guided individualized management during the first 5 months of CPAP therapy.

Of note, the presence of RREs was not associated with lower compliance nor with lack of symptom improvement under CPAP.

However, 4/19 patients exhibited central AHIflow or CSR under CPAP that required particular attention, as these can be associated with serious medical events.

When RREs occur, leak management should be the priority [9]. Significant leaks can have a major impact on the data collected by TMg, leading to faulty detection of RREs. On the CPAP circuit, there are two types of leaks: intentional leaks (i.e., mask exhalation port, essential for carbon dioxide removal) and unintentional leaks (i.e., mask seal defects, mouth breathing). Unintentional leaks can alter the capacity of the CPAP algorithm to detect RREs by producing a variation of flow that the CPAP interprets as a respiratory event [12]. This contributes to a significant degree of imprecision in the estimation of AHIflow by CPAP machines when compared to AHIPSG, as reported in the meta-analysis from Iftikhar et al. [13].

Each CPAP manufacturer uses their own calculation method to detect and report RREs. For example, the Resmed® Airsense10 CPAP device uses the forced oscillation technique to detect obstructive and central events [14]. The Resmed Airsense10 oscillates the flow when it falls; if oscillations dissipate, it means that the upper airways are open, reflecting a central event. When reporting RREs, some CPAP brands exclude certain events from their AHIflow calculations, during ramp or in case of major leaks, for example. According to the retrospective study conducted by Vidal et al. in 13,764 patients, alerts related to high AHIflow can be reduced by up to 50% if central hypopnea is excluded and by up to 20% in cases where significant leak periods or substantial pressure increase periods are excluded [15]. Consequently, the AHIflow obtained by TMg is heavily influenced by these calculation methods, with patients affected by AHIflow>10 varying from 4.7% to 12.3%. Significant leaks can lead to an overestimation of up to 5 events per hour, compromising the reliability of telemonitoring [16].

The low prevalence of RREs in the present study was related to leak correction occurring before the application of the RREs algorithm.

The use of naso-buccal masks was more common in the RRE group and may be related to leaks, mouth breathing or patients who are more difficult to treat [2]. Because fixed pressure levels were similar between groups, the higher prevalence of naso-buccal mask use in the RRE group likely reflects underlying patient complexity (e.g., mouth breathing or difficult upper-airway mechanics) rather than a greater need for higher pressure.

Generally, as expected, obstructive RREs were very easily resolved by CPAP pressure increase [10], and TMg allowed us to remotely control treatment adequacy [1]. We have previously shown that pressure modification is the only adaptation that significantly increases CPAP compliance during the first 6 months in telemonitored CPAP patients [17].

Regarding central RREs, treatment-emergent central apnea (TE-CSA) is a dynamic process that varies over time with a variable prevalence, between 0.5% and 20% [4, 18]. In the large cohort reported by Liu et al., 3.5% of patients exhibited residual central sleep apneas at weeks 1 and 13 of CPAP treatment, 19.7% of patients exhibited emergent apneas, 25.2% exhibited persistent apneas, and up to 55% had transient apneas [4]. The presence of TE-CSA remains generally a major challenge in the management of OSA because it negatively influences treatment adherence and favors CPAP discontinuation (Hazard Ratio 1.27-1.72) [4]. However, the present study found no association between central RREs and lower compliance. As presented here, close monitoring and individualized management can lead to a rapid understanding and resolution of TE-CSA. Indeed, we faced a clear misclassification of obstructive RRE as central RRE in one patient and as CSR in another. We know from manufacturers that false positive cases of CSA/CSR can be detected by CPAP. This is related to the fact that, in theory, mixed apneas can look like CSA or CSR [19].

When CSR is detected by CPAP, medical work-up is mandatory as CSR is associated with heart failure episodes, arrhythmias, serious cardiac events, and poor prognosis [7, 20]. The first step is to confirm CSR. The gold standard for the diagnosis of CSR is fully attended PSG according to AASM criteria [21]. RP may detect a periodic breathing pattern suggestive of CSR, but lacks EEG and comprehensive sleep staging, limiting its ability to definitively characterize central events and cyclic breathing [22].

In the case of CSR detected by CPAP device, RP is an acceptable first screening option and should include a microphone to facilitate the distinction between obstructive and central hypopneas [23]. If CSR is confirmed, biological, cardiac, and neurological work-up is required, as well a careful review of current medications, to exclude the major causes of CSR, namely heart failure, arrhythmias, renal failure, stroke, baclofen, ticagrelor, and opioids [7]. In this study, two patients exhibited CSR, and one had to move to another ventilation mode to suppress this breathing pattern, probably triggered by diastolic dysfunction [24].

A common problem associated with OSA is chronic insomnia. Co-occurrence of these sleep disorders is called COMISA and is very common, affecting up to 67% of patients that share similar sleep complaints [25]. Frequent awakenings in insomniacs lead to breathing instability and predisposes to further upper airway obstruction, particularly marked in OSA patients. As a consequence, a temporary (or very variable) increase in AHIflow can be observed on TMg in COMISA CPAP users [26]. This was observed in one of our patients exhibiting central RRE. This condition should be carefully worked up by clinicians in case of AHIflow from unknown origin.

Finally, RREs were significantly associated with more difficult and less effective CPAP titration. As naso-buccal masks, depression, and low effective pressure are predictors of poor adherence, intensive early intervention through TMg support strategies (e.g., a dedicated algorithm) implemented early after CPAP initiation therapy could be a crucial element to improve patient care [27].

Limitations

These results are limited to a single-center experience. We did not test the structured management algorithm of device-detected RREs against a comparator, we have only reported on our clinical experience following a relatively brief period of observation after CPAP initiation. These device-detected RREs were not systematically confirmed by PSG or RP. Furthermore, the algorithm was only tested on RREs detected by Resmed® CPAP devices (S10 and S11). As other manufacturers use different algorithms for RRE detection, this hampers generalizability. Moreover, this algorithm is applicable to fixed-pressure CPAP users, and an important proportion of OSA patients now use APAP in high-income countries. The small number of RREs, and especially of CSA and CSR cases, reduces the generalizability of the application of our original algorithm. However, the case-control design enabled the correct characterization of patients with these RREs. Nevertheless, the small number of cases and controls included in the study may not provide sufficient statistical power to detect certain differences between groups.

Conclusion

Once leaks are controlled, occurrence of RREs in compliant CPAP users are rather uncommon at the beginning of treatment. In this cohort, a structured algorithm helped clarify the likely mechanism of device-detected RREs (Resmed® CPAP devices) and guided individualized management during the first months of CPAP therapy.

The most common cause of RRE was insufficient CPAP pressure, but cases of COMISA and of “idiopathic” CSA/CSR also occurred that required specific management. However, these case-based clinical observations were conducted on a limited number of patients, particularly with regard to central events. Further studies are needed in this specific subgroup before these findings can be generalized to broader CPAP-treated populations.
