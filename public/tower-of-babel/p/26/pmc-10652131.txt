A dual-process of targeted and unbiased Nanopore sequencing enables accurate and rapid diagnosis of lower respiratory infections
Authors: Yifan Guo, Zhenzhong Li, Lijuan Li, Shan Li, Lingxiao Sun, Xinfang Yang, Yan Dai, Ju Gu, Lan Yang, Xue Liu, Binghuai Lu, Jiajing Han, Kang Chang, Li Gu, Yuyao Yin, Shijun Sun, Chendi Jing, Hongbin Chen, Manjiao Liu, Hui Xu, Ryon Liu, Yong Ren, Hao Guo, Hui Wang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundNanopore metagenomics has been used for infectious disease diagnosis for bacterial pathogens. However, this technology currently lacks comprehensive performance studies in clinical settings for simultaneous detection of bacteria, fungi, and viruses.MethodsWe developed a dual-process of Nanopore sequencing for one sample, with unbiased metagenomics in Meta process and target enrichment in Panel process (Nanopore Meta-Panel process, NanoMP) and prospectively enrolled 450 respiratory specimens from multiple centers. The filter system of pathogen detection was established with machine learning and receiver operator characteristic (ROC) curve to optimize the detection accuracy based on orthogonal test of 21 species. Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes were identified based on the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD) and single-nucleotide polymorphism matrix.FindingsOur approach showed high sensitivity in Meta process, with 82.9%, 88.7%, and 75.0% for bacteria, fungi (except Aspergillus), and Mycobacterium tuberculosis groups, respectively. Moreover, target amplification improved the sensitivity of virus (>80.0% vs. 39.4%) and Aspergillus (81.8% vs. 42.3%) groups in Panel process compared with Meta process. Overall, NanoMP achieved 80.2% sensitivity and 98.8% specificity compared with the composite reference standard, and we were able to accurately detect AMR genes including blaKPC-2, blaOXA-23 and mecA and distinguish their parent organisms in patients with mixed infections.InterpretationWe combined metagenomic and enriched Nanopore sequencing for one sample in parallel. Our NanoMP approach simultaneously covered bacteria, viruses and fungi in respiratory specimens and demonstrated good diagnostic performance in real clinical settings.FundingNational Key Research and Development Program of China and National Natural Science Foundation of China.Keywords: Nanopore sequencing, Metagenomics, Target amplification, Lower respiratory infections, Machine learning

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed with the terms “lower respiratory infections, Nanopore sequencing, and machine learning,” or “lower respiratory infections and Nanopore sequencing,” or “Nanopore sequencing and machine learning” for articles published up to September 30, 2022 with no language restrictions. We identified 13 references and only 8 of them were relevant to this study, mostly utilizing the 16S/ITS sequencing or nasopharyngeal samples other than lower respiratory specimens for investigation. The Nanopore platform was only used in four studies, but only the performance of bacterial lower respiratory tract infection (LRTI) diagnosis was well evaluated. The number of fungi detection in Nanopore platform was few and especially focus on Candida spp. or Aspergillus spp. Only one study revealed a good performance of RNA virus detection with enrichment in Nanopore sequencing. Notably, we found no reports dissecting the diagnostic accuracy of Nanopore sequencing in real clinical practices that simultaneously cover various pathogens, including bacteria, fungi, and viruses. Currently, the performance of Nanopore sequencing for diagnosing infection in the respiratory systems, based on the filter system with machine learning and ROC curve, remains unclear.

Added value of this study

This study illustrated the diagnostic performance of LRTIs with Nanopore sequencing in real clinical settings. We developed a NanoMP approach with dual barcodes and performed target amplification and metagenomic sequencing with human genome removal simultaneously. The microbial filtering system included multidimensional markers, which generated from sequencing process, using the machine learning and ROC curve to improve the detection accuracy. Our findings demonstrate that several pathogens with target amplification improve detection sensitivity. Additionally, our findings confirm AMR genes subtypes and parent species could be identify based on long read length.

Implications of all the available evidence

These results have implications for the rapid diagnosis of lower respiratory tract infections. The study confirms that targeted amplification can improve the detection sensitivity of specific pathogens, compensating for the shortcomings of metagenomic processes (the removal of host genes may affect the detection of certain pathogens). Our results demonstrate that multiple indicators and methods can be included to filter out excessive false-positive microorganisms in sequencing results. This work suggests that parallel targeted amplification and metagenomic sequencing can better cover the pathogen spectrum of lower respiratory tract infections without prolonging experimental time, maybe more suitable for clinical application. In the future, more pathogens can be included in the targeted amplification spectrum, and host transcriptome indicators can be incorporated into machine learning to achieve rapid and accurate diagnosis.

Introduction

Lower respiratory tract infections (LRTIs) include community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP), and ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP), which cause more than 25% of deaths from pneumonia-associated hospitalizations.1 However, the identification of causative pathogens, such as bacterial, fungal, viral, and atypical pathogens, remains challenging owing to the limitations of the available detection methods.

Recently, clinical metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) has rapidly moved from research to clinical laboratories.2,3 Owing to rapid library preparation, real-time data acquisition, and analysis, Nanopore sequencing has a short turnaround time and long read length, which are suitable advantages for clinical use, especially in antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes detection.4 Nevertheless, most studies had focused on bacterial and fungal detection.4, 5, 6 and insufficient performance for viral identification had been reported,7 especially few large cohorts study covered bacterial, fungal and virus detection simultaneously. Many confounding factors, such as contamination and large amount of human DNA background, challenge the clinical application of these detection methods. Therefore, various studies had also reported inconsistent detection performance owing to the different filter systems used.5,8 Thus, the establishment of a cut-off value should be comprehensively considered based on various markers.

In order to meet the clinical application requirements, a concept that combines unbiased pathogen detection (Meta process) and specific pathogen target amplification (Panel process) was proposed (Nanopore Meta-Panel process, NanoMP) based on dual-barcode system. Considering the high prevalence of HSV1 (Human alphaherpesvirus 1), EBV (Human gammaherpesvirus 4), and CMV (Human betaherpesvirus 5) in healthy people,9, 10, 11 we included these ubiquitous DNA viruses, as well as Aspergillus fumigatus and RNA viruses, such as rhinovirus (Rhv) for methodology assessment in the panel process.

To evaluate the performance of the NanoMP approach in real clinical applications, we conducted a prospective study with a largest cohort of 450 samples from 418 patients to date. The NanoMP approach was established based on dual-barcode system to demultiplexing, which is a process that covers all pathogens with human DNA removal and a panel process to improve specific pathogens using target amplification. The new filter system was set up based on machine learning and receiver operator characteristic (ROC) curve, which includes several quality control variables to explore the diagnostic value. Moreover, several AMR genes and its subtype were also identified.

Methods

NanoMP approach

Our in-house Nanopore sequencing pipeline (NanoMP) consisted of two processes. The Meta process included human DNA removal using saponin-based differential lysis, microorganism DNA extraction, and library preparation to cover all pathogen spectra, whereas the Panel process included DNA/RNA extraction, target amplification and library preparation (Fig. 1). Detailed information on nucleic acid extraction, library preparation, sequencing and bioinformatic analysis for the Meta and Panel processes were shown in the Supplementary Methods. Metagenomic sequencing data with human genome removed were deposited as National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) sequence read archive (SRA) under Bioproject PRJNA818777. The sequencing information of 1 h and all sequencing result were shown in Supplemental Tables S1 and S2.

Schematic workflow of this study. A total of 450 sputum/BALF samples from 418 patients with suspected pulmonary infection. Several microbiological tests were performed, including culture, qPCR, ELISA, GeneXpert MTB/RIF, and histology. All samples were divided to two processes as follows: 1) in the meta process, human genome removal, pathogen cell wall lysis, DNA extraction, dual-barcode linkage and library preparation were performed; and 2) in the panel process, DNA/RNA extraction, target amplification with several species, such as EBV, CMV, HSV1, Aspergillus fumigatus, and Rhv, single barcode linkage, and library preparation were conducted. These two processes were mixed, and sequencing. The detail bioinformatics process was presented in Supplemental Method. Final species detection was performed based on filter system compared with gold standard. The pathogens were reported rely on clinical judgment. BALF, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid; ELISA, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay; qPCR, quantitative polymerase chain reaction; EBV, Epstein–Barr virus, human gammaherpesvirus 4; CMV, human cytomegalovirus, human betaherpesvirus 5; HSV1, human alphaherpesvirus 1; Rhv, Rhinovirus.

Dual-barcode design for Nanopore sequencing

Given to the SQK-PBK004 kit only contains 12 barcodes and to meet the need of double processes (17 samples per flow cell, including 6 samples in parallel for double process, and 5 negative controls), we designed a new dual-barcode system for the Meta process (Fig. 1). Based on the normal strategy of barcode linkage in commercial kits and as shown in the Panel process (Barcode A), we extended and added a barcode (Barcode B) based on the flanking region (FLK) to produce dual barcodes to discriminate the differences between the Meta and Panel processes.

Sample collection

The 450 remaining specimens of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) (n = 348) and sputum (n = 102) samples were obtained from the clinical laboratories of the Peking University People's Hospital, China–Japan Friendship Hospital, and Beijing Chao-yang Hospital, and collected from January 28 to June 28, 2021. This study was only concerned with whether it is LRTIs, without taking gender into account. The demographic characteristics, clinical laboratory findings, radiography and pathology results, clinical treatment, and outcomes of the 418 patients were extracted from the patients' medical records.

This study was approved by the Peking University People's Hospital Institutional Review Board (approval number 2019PHB010-01). All samples were collected with participant consent.

Limit of detection

To evaluate the limit of detection (LoD) for this assay, we spiked with serial tenfold dilutions (10–104 CFU/mL or 10-104 Copies/mL) of Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Staphylococcus aureus, Aspergillus fumigatus, CMV, and Rhv using uninfected normal respiratory flora (NRF) BALF samples, which was negative of these spiked pathogens that confirmed by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR). Moreover, the commensal background was determined by 16S qPCR as previously described4 and divided into high (Ct = 25) and low backgrounds (Ct = 28). Each concentration of the mixed microorganisms was tested in decuplicate in different commensal backgrounds. LoD was defined as the lowest concentration at which spiked-in pathogens were detected ≥8 mock samples (Supplemental Table S3).

Computational algorithm for pathogen identification

We developed a pathogen identification algorithm applicable for Nanopore datasets to optimize and assess performance accuracy. Based on the alignment and pathogens detected by culture and/or qPCR testing, a total list of 21 species, contained Gram positive/negative bacteria, fungi, DNA virus, RNA virus, Mycoplasma, which represent higher frequency identification and typical pathogens in clinical (whenever possible, microbial validation should be performed using the gold standard method, such as an IVD kit or a rigorously validated in-house assay), were selected for methodological evaluation compared with gold standard, which is clinical culture, qPCR, and pathogen validation assays. The detailed information of pathogen validation assays, threshold establishment based on machine learning model with XGBoost and ROC analysis (Fig. 2), were presented in the Supplemental Method.

The workflow of the filter system establishment. For bacteria and virus groups in the Meta process, the candidate features were selected using LASSO. The datasets with the selected features divided into train/test datasets, and tuned the hyper-parameters of XGBoost models with stratified cross-validation. ROC curve was to assess the performance of final models with the optimal hyper-parameters. For fungi (except Aspergillus), MTB, and Aspergillus groups in the Meta process; HSV1, EBV, CMV, Rhinovirus and Aspergillus fumigatus in the Panel process, the best metrics were picked based on Spearman's correlation coefficient, then the optimal thresholds were set to the maximum Youden's index, as determined from the ROC curves. LASSO, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator; MTB, Mycobacterium tuberculosis; HSV1, human alphaherpesvirus 1; EBV, Epstein–Barr virus, human gammaherpesvirus 4; CMV, human cytomegalovirus, human betaherpesvirus 5.

Clinical judgement

All species detected by Nanopore platforms were searched in Pubmed and CAP-CHINA (https://www.chinapneumonia.cn/pathogens) to determine whether the organisms cause pneumonia. Sequencing results were divided into four categories (Defined, Probable, Possible, and Unlikely) and the criteria are presented in the Supplemental Method. Detailed information for each patient was provided in Supplementary Table S4. The composite reference standard included results from all microbiological tests (including culture and other tests), pathological examinations, and clinical adjudications. Definite and probable results were considered positive for clinical diagnosis, whereas possible and unlikely results were considered negative for clinical diagnosis. No matter which pathogen was positive in one sample, the sample was considered consistent with the clinical diagnosis.

Antimicrobial resistance gene identification

A single nucleotide polymorphism matrix (SNPM) database was prepared (see Supplemental Method) with sequences of the homology model from the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD, v3.1.2).

Reads were mapped against CARD sequences using BLAST (v2.10.1+) to determine antimicrobial resistance (AMR) gene families. The most frequent AMR gene subtype among all alignment records was selected for each read and cross-referenced to REF in the SNPM. Further calls for the AMR subtype were performed using minimap2 and Samtools to obtain allelic variants for each read according to the SNPM. The BLAST scoring matrix was then limited to these alleles to calculate the scores for the different subtypes in a site-specific manner. Reads sharing a specific REF were attributed to the subtype with the highest median score if scores in all subtypes differed significantly (p < 0.05); otherwise, they were assigned to the most frequent subtype of the highest score. AMR genes were annotated using additional information from CARD.

Statistical analysis

Sample size was calculated using PASS software (v15.0; NCSS Corporation, USA). Student t-test, Mann–Whitney U test and the chi-square test/correction for continuity of chi-square test/Fisher's exact test were used to compare continuous and categorical data, respectively. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed in SPSS software (v25.0; IBM Corporation, USA) and Scipy (v1.7.3, Python 3.7).

Role of the funding source

The funders did not play any role in the study design, data collection, management, analysis, interpretation, review, approval of the manuscript, or the decision to submit the manuscript for publication.

Results

In this prospective study, 450 samples (348 BALF and 102 sputum samples) from 418 patients were collected and enrolled. They were divided into LRTI (n = 249) and non-LRTI (n = 169) groups based on the criteria of CAP,12 HAP13 and suspected infection according to a clinical specialist's opinion. As shown in Table 1, the incidence rates of a history of aspiration, invasive mechanical ventilation, and immunosuppression status were higher in the LRTIs group. A higher prevalence of fever, sputum, and dyspnea was detected in patients with LRTIs. The non-LRTI group has a higher prevalence of malignancy but a lower number of patients with hematological diseases (Table 1).

Limit of detection

The limit of detection (LoD) was determined using uninfected NRF BALF samples in decuplicate spiked microorganisms (P. aeruginosa, S. aureus, A. fumigatus, CMV, Rhv) with serial tenfold dilutions at known cell densities. The background commensal levels were potential result the different LoDs for bacteria. The Panel process improved the LoDs for A. fumigatus and CMV compared with the Meta process (Supplemental Table S3).

NanoMP characteristics

Each sequencing run contained six samples and five negative controls (host removal [NC1], DNA extraction [NC2], and library preparation [NC3] controls in the Meta process; DNA/RNA extraction [NC2], and library preparation [NC3] controls in the Panel process), and the entire process lasted approximately 6.5–7.0 h (Fig. 1), including nucleic acid extraction (2 h, daul processes), library preparation (3.5–4.0 h, daul processes), sequencing and analysis (∼1 h). The overview of sequencing information was shown in Supplementary Figure S1.

Establishment of filter system and detection performance of the meta process

The total datasets of species-level result, 972 in bacteria group and 270 in virus group were included to train and test XGBoost classifiers (Supplemental Figure S2). For bacteria group, the area under the curve (AUC) score of XGBoost classifiers was 0.915 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.894–0.916) in train datasets; while 0.923 (95% CI, 0.897–0.931) in test datasets. For viral group, AUC was 0.817 (95% CI, 0.772–0.811) in train datasets, while 0.793 (95% CI, 0.726–0.805) in test datasets (Fig. 2).

Based on final models for bacteria and virus groups, the AUC scores of both groups were 0.916 and 0.809, respectively (Fig. 2). Compared with clinical and orthogonal test results, the sensitivity and specificity of the XGBoost classifiers were 84.8% and 82.9% for the bacteria group, and 81.0% and 67.2% for the virus group, respectively (Fig. 2).

For fungi (except Aspergillus), MTB, and Aspergillus groups, the Reads and Ratio_run were selected using correlation analysis, and the establishment of the cutoff value was based on ROC, with AUC values of 0.94, 0.87, and 0.71, respectively (Fig. 2, Supplementary Figure S3, Supplemental Table S5).

To compare the performance between the different methods for bacteria and virus groups, we performed the ROC analysis based on the above method in the bacteria and virus groups. The Reads and Ratio_run was carried to downstream analysis, with a 71.6% sensitivity and 69.3% specificity for the bacteria group, and 48.6% sensitivity and 77.1% specificity for the virus group. High sensitivity for the bacteria group and high specificity for both groups were observed with XGBoost classifiers (Fig. 2, Supplemental Figure S4, Supplemental Table S5).

Based on XGBoost classifiers, the sensitivity was >80% for both groups, demonstrating that machine learning could be used to establish the threshold value. When the missed detection organisms were added, excellent performance for the bacteria group was observed, with a sensitivity and specificity of 82.9% and 82.9%, respectively (Fig. 3a). However, more missed detections were observed in the virus group, with a sensitivity of 39.4% (Fig. 3a). For the ROC curve analysis, the sensitivity and specificity were 88.7% and 97.1% for the fungi (except Aspergillus), 75.0% and 99.8% for the MTB, and 42.3% sensitivity for the Aspergillus groups (Fig. 3b). The enhancement of specific pathogens detection in panel process

Based on the correlation analysis and ROC curve of the best metrics of EBV, CMV, HSV1, A. fumigatus, and Rhv in the Panel process, the AUC score was 0.86, 0.86, 0.94, 0.91, and 0.99, respectively (Fig. 2). The sensitivity and specificity were 92.1% and 91.3% for HSV1, 84.0% and 79.9% for EBV, 81.5% and 86.0% for CMV, 81.8% and 99.8% for A. fumigatus, and 100% and 97.5% for Rhv, respectively (Fig. 3c, Supplemental Figure S5, Table 2).

Performance of Nanopore sequencing based on clinical judgement

Of the enrolled 450 samples, 149 (33.1%) were culture-positive (Fig. 4a), and the positive result increased to 195 (43.3%) samples based on culture and other microbiological tests (Fig. 4b). The Nanopore platform identified 224 (49.8%) samples with Definite or Probable pathogens (Supplementary Table S4). Overall, compared with the culture results, the Nanopore sequencing had 89.9% clinical sensitivity and 71.8% clinical specificity. In addition, 45 samples were positive for other microbiological tests, indicating a clinical sensitivity of 91.8% and a clinical specificity of 83.9% compared with all microbiological tests. Compared with the composite reference standard, the Nanopore platform had 80.2% clinical sensitivity and 98.8% clinical specificity (Fig. 4b).

Antimicrobial resistance gene detection

Nanopore sequencing has been used to identify antibiotic resistance genes in previous studies.4,14, 15, 16 Therefore, we constructed a SNPM database to detect AMR genes and their subtypes, which could also be tracked to the organisms relying on the long-read length (Fig. 4c). Among the cultivated pathogens, P. aeruginosa, A. baumannii, K. pneumoniae, and S. aureus, which showed a higher frequency identification in culture results and carried a heavy burden to patients (Fig. 4a), were selected to analyze AMR genes. As shown in Fig. 4d, we identified 16 samples with blaOXA (15 blaOXA-50 like and 1 blaOXA) in P. aeruginosa, 15 samples with blaOXA (12 blaOXA-66, 12 blaOXA-23, and 4 blaOXA) in A. baumannii, four samples with mecA in S. aureus, and five samples with blaKPC-2 in K. pneumoniae (Supplementary Table S6). In the clinical antibiotic susceptibility testing, the agreement was 78.6%, 83.3%, 62.5%, and 100% for carbapenem-resistant P. aeruginosa, A. baumannii, K. pneumoniae, and methicillin-resistant S. aureus, respectively. Overall, these data demonstrated the good performance of Nanopore sequencing in identifying and tracking the host of resistance genes in clinical settings.

Discussion

In this prospective study, 450 respiratory tract samples were used to investigate the utility of the Nanopore platform for detecting infectious pathogens. We established a dual-barcoding NanoMP approach to cover DNA/RNA pathogens, including host DNA depletion in the Meta process and target pathogen amplification in the Panel process. The comprehensive identification filter system was developed using machine learning and ROC curve, which was used in clinical samples. Clinical detection performance and AMR genes identification based on SNPM database was also finalized based on clinical results.

Our previous work demonstrated that fewer reads were generated in the Nanopore platform than in the Illumina platform in lung biopsy tissue samples,17 leading to a weak detection performance. Another study reported that viral enrichment with spiked primers could enhance viral detection in the Nanopore platform.7 To overcome this limitation, target amplification and unbiased detection were used. Considering the high prevalence of some viruses, fungi, and RNA viruses, EBV, CMV, HSV1, A. fumigatus, and Rhv were selected to validate the feasibility of the method. In the Meta process, the bacteria and fungi (except Aspergillus) groups performed well, based on human genome removal in wet experiments (sensitivity: 82.9% and 88.7%, respectively). However, host depletion was probably the reason that the virus performed poorly (sensitivity: 39.4%). In addition, centrifugation performed in the wet experiment could have removed some viruses owing to their fragility, small size, and light weight. Moreover, for the Aspergillus group, with a lower sensitivity of 42.3%, cell walls might not have been degraded during DNA extraction owing to their thickness. In contrast, the pathogens in the panel list were significantly enriched after PCR amplification. For RNA viruses, a good sensitivity (100.0%) was observed after target amplification. These results indicated that our proposed method of target amplification significantly improved detection sensitivity. However, the small sample size of respiratory RNA virus may be influenced by the high rate of wear mask during COVID-19 pandemic (the sample collection time was from January 28 to June 28, 2021). In the future, the panel list can be expanded to include more pathogens or RNA viruses that are difficult to detect.

In mNGS, establishing the cutoff value was critical for distinguishing legitimate pathogens. Reads per million ratio metric, environmental markers, the influence of amplification cycle in library preparation, and the abundance of and multiple/unique mapping reads were considered to have developed the filter system in several studies.5,8,18, 19, 20 However, the method of establishing cutoff values in different studies was chaotic. More attention should be paid to the development of universal methods. In this study, all markers generated in the sequencing process were collected and input to XGBoost, a machine learning method, to determine their importance. The sensitivity and specificity of all taxa were >80%, this finding demonstrates that our model could be a promising tool that could be extended to other studies in the future. Moreover, previous works focused on bacteria only6 or bacteria and fungi5 with Nanopore platform, but none of these studies included all types of pathogens, especially viruses. Hence, a multicenter, large cohort study with 450 samples aiming to identify bacteria, viruses, fungi and their AMR genes was more likely to reflect the real clinical performance of Nanopore sequencing.

Regarding antimicrobial resistance gene identification, three samples that produced carbapenemases in P. aeruginosa, A. baumannii, and K. pneumoniae were mismatched with their clinical results. However, the tracked host reads were higher in groups with antimicrobial resistance genes, than in those without (Supplemental Figure S6a). We also distinguished different AMR genes and their parent organisms in patients with mixed infections (Supplemental Table S6). Furthermore, the correlation of the reads between organisms and their associated AMR genes identified was strongly positive based on spearman's coefficient and also needed further investigation (Supplemental Figure S6b, c).

Due to the extended duration of our research, we opted to maintain consistency by using the same basecalling version (Guppy v3.2.10) as we did at the outset of our study, even though it has since been updated to Guppy v6.3.8. Consequently, we compared the impact of results with 17 samples using these two versions (Supplemental Table S7). We discovered that variations in the basecalling version had no influence on pathogen detection and final interpretation. This further underscores the robustness and stability of our methodology.

This study had some limitations. First, only a few pathogens were included in the panel list to confirm performance; therefore, more microorganisms that were difficult to detect clinically should be considered in future studies. Second, most of species lack gold standard confirmed by IVD kits, orthogonal testing was not performed for all pathogens and contained only 21 species. Moreover, only acquired AMR genes were considered in our AMR analysis result, excluding chromosomal point mutation. Finally, potential pathogen identification did not identify the causative agent of the diseases diagnosed, and pathogens were frequently detected in respiratory samples from people without respiratory symptoms.

In summary, we demonstrated the utility of Nanopore sequencing to extend the scope of conventional clinical diagnostic methods with LRTIs. Furthermore, we had successfully established a NanoMP approach, could elevate the sensitivity of specific pathogens. A universal method to set up a cutoff value based on machine learning had the potential to be applied to other sample types in in-house mNGS platforms. In addition, we confirmed the good performance of Nanopore sequencing for clinical patients with LRTIs and identified some key AMR genes that tracked organisms based on long read length.
