ZRANB1 depletion inhibits neuroblastoma progression by destabilizing MYCN through EZH2-mediated deubiquitination
Authors: Zhichao Wang, Long Cheng, Guanghui Li, Huiyan Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeuroblastoma (NB) is a common malignant solid tumor in children, notably aggressive in cases with MYCN amplification. Unraveling the mechanisms behind NB progression is crucial for developing targeted therapies. ZRANB1, a deubiquitinating enzyme, has emerged as a significant player in many cancers, but its specific function in NB still to be fully elucidated.MethodsBioinformatic analyses of transcriptomic datasets (GSE85047 and GSE80149) were performed to identify potential MYCN-dependent pro-survival genes. The biological functions of ZRANB1 in NB cells were assessed by CCK-8, colony formation, migration, invasion, and tumor sphere formation assays. Co-IP, Western blot, CHX chase, and ubiquitination assays were performed to assess the regulatory relationship among ZRANB1, EZH2, and MYCN. Catalytically inactive ZRANB1 mutant and in vitro pull-down assays were performed to validate the requirement of deubiquitinase activity and direct interactions. We evaluated the contribution of the ZRANB1-EZH2-MYCN axis to tumor growth using cell-based assays and mouse xenograft models.ResultsZARNB1 was substantially higher in MYCN-amplified BE(2)M17 cell lines. Functional studies demonstrated that ZRANB1 promoted proliferation, migration, and tumorigenicity. Mechanistically, although ZRANB1 did not directly bind MYCN, it formed a ternary complex with EZH2 and MYCN, thereby stabilizing MYCN via EZH2-mediated regulation. Deubiquitinase activity was required for ZRANB1-mediated MYCN stabilization, and direct ZRANB1-EZH2 interaction was confirmed by in vitro pull-down assays.ConclusionsThis study identifies ZRANB1 as an upstream deubiquitinase that stabilizes EZH2, thereby indirectly maintaining MYCN stability in MYCN-amplified neuroblastoma. These findings establish a ZRANB1-EZH2-MYCN regulatory axis and highlight ZRANB1 as a promising therapeutic target in MYCN-amplified NB.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10565-026-10186-z.

Introduction

Neuroblastoma (NB), a neural crest-derived tumor, is the most common extracranial solid tumor in children (Quintás et al. 2020). NB exhibits highly variable biological and clinical behaviors, from spontaneous regression to aggressive, treatment-resistant metastases involving multiple organs. These variations are partially due to specific genetic alterations (Qiu & Matthay 2022). The current treatment strategies for NB involve a combination of approaches tailored to the patient's specific risk category, including surgical resection, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, stem cell transplantation, and clinical trials (Chung et al. 2021). Due to the genetic, morphological, and clinical heterogeneity properties that NB exhibited (Zafar et al. 2021), those treatments for NB are still limited by significant side effects, development of drug resistance, incomplete tumor resection, and long-term health sequelae, underscoring the necessity for continued investigation to establish safer and more efficacious therapies for NB.

Approximately 20–25% of NB cases harbor MYCN amplification, which is strongly associated with aggressive disease. Despite advances in treatment, the prognosis for outcomes for MYCN-amplified high-risk NB patients remains unfavorable (Zhao et al. 2023). Abnormal MYCN amplification caused by genomic MYCN amplification was widely known as a key driver of high-risk NB and among the most robust predictors of poor outcome (Bansal et al. 2022; Otte et al. 2020). In the meanwhile, it was reported that the presence of aberrant MYCN expression was evident at diagnosis and was absent in the later stages of tumorigenesis in NB without MYCN amplification. This early elevation suggests a fundamental role of MYCN in the initial phases of these cancers, leading to distinct cellular dysregulation (Bhavsar 2023; Braoudaki et al. 2021). In the meanwhile, low expression of MYCN protein was reported to correlate with better prognosis, indicating the stability of MYCN is supposed to be a predictor for tumor progression in NB (Yang et al. 2022). Given the critical role of MYCN in the pathogenesis of high-risk NB, there has been significant interest in understanding the mechanisms underlying MYCN regulation and function.

MYCN, a MYC family oncogene encoding a bHLH domain-containing protein, lacks a suitable small-molecule binding pocket and enzyme activity, rendering it undruggable by conventional inhibitors (Wolpaw et al. 2021; Yang et al. 2021). Therefore, MYCN-amplified NB therapies that direct target to MYCN are still limited. Indirect strategies that mediate MYCN transcription, translation and protein stability gain a lot of attentions in MYCN amplification NB. MYCN protein levels are tightly regulated by ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation (Borankova et al. 2023; Diebold et al. 2023; Sasada et al. 2019). Ubiquitin–proteasome system was reported to involve with cellular protein homeostasis, and dysregulation of this system can trigger the stabilization of oncogenic proteins, contributing to tumor progression (Han et al. 2023; Park et al. 2020). Li et al. discovered thatYM155 suppress NB cell growth and proliferation through inhibiting MYCN degradation by downregulating USP7 deubiquitinase activity (Li et al. 2023). HUWE1, an E3 ubiquitin ligase, promotes MYCN ubiquitination and subsequent degradation, thus inactivating Delta-like 1 (DLL1)-NOTCH1 signaling to downregulate cellular function in glioblastoma (Yuan et al. 2022).

ZRANB1 (Zinc Finger RANBP2-type Containing 1) is a deubiquitinating enzyme that participates in diverse cellular functions, including modulation of protein stability, and has been reported to correlate with prognosis in multiple cancer types (Afonina & Beyaert 2016; Zhao et al. 2020). Recent studies have demonstrated that ZRANB1 stabilizes oncogenic proteins by preventing their ubiquitination and degradation (Li et al. 2021; Shen et al. 2022). Notably, ZRANB1 has been shown to directly deubiquitinate and stabilize EZH2 in triple-negative breast cancer, with ZRANB1 levels correlating with EZH2 expression and unfavorable patient outcomes (Zhang et al. 2018). Best known for its role in transcriptional silencing via histone methylation, EZH2 (Enhancer of Zeste Homolog 2) is a core subunit of the Polycomb Repressive Complex 2. However, accumulating evidence indicates that EZH2 also exerts methyltransferase-independent functions, such as regulating protein stability (Duan et al. 2020; Huang et al. 2024). In neuroblastoma, EZH2 is upregulated and predicts poor survival (Mrunalini et al. 2026), but whether it contributes to tumor progression through non-canonical mechanisms remains to be fully elucidated.

Although the ZRANB1-EZH2 regulatory axis has been established in breast cancer, whether ZRANB1 regulates EZH2 stability in neuroblastoma, and whether this regulation impacts MYCN-amplified neuroblastoma progression, remains unknown. In the present study, we establish that in MYCN-amplified neuroblastoma, ZRANB1 stabilizes EZH2 through deubiquitination, which in turn maintains MYCN protein stability and promotes tumor progression. This finding not only extends the ZRANB1-EZH2 regulatory axis to a new tumor type but also provides a rationale for targeting ZRANB1 as a therapeutic strategy for MYCN-amplified neuroblastoma.

Method

Detailed methods and resources are provided in the Supplemental Experimental Procedures.

Bioinformatics analysis

Gene expression data were gained from the GEO datasets GSE85047 and GSE80149. GEO2R analysis was performed to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs). DEGs were considered significant if they met the criteria of |log2 fold change|> 1 and adjusted p-value < 0.05. Expression patterns of DEGs were visualized using the R package "pheatmap" to visualize the expression patterns of DEGs.

Gene expression data from the TARGET-Asgharzadeh cohort (Tumor Neuroblastoma TARGET—Asgharzadeh—249—custom—huex10t) were obtained from the R2 Genomics Analysis and Visualization Platform (https://r2.amc.nl). ZRANB1 expression levels were analyzed for correlation with ADRN core transcription factors, including PHOX2B, HAND1, GATA2, and ISL1. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to assess the strength of associations. Patients were dichotomized into high and low expression groups by median values of ZRANB1, PHOX2B, HAND1, GATA2, and ISL1. Kaplan–Meier curves were generated for overall survival (OS), with statistical significance assessed by the log-rank test.

Cell lines and culture

BE(2)-M17 and IMR-32 (MYCN-amplified) and SH-SY5Y and SK-N-AS (non-amplified) were obtained from ATCC (Manassas, VA, USA). Cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin in 5% CO2 at room temperature.

Cell transfection

Transfections of BE(2)M17 and SH-SY5Y cells with shRNA or overexpression plasmids were performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific). For knockdown experiments, cells received shRNA targeting ZRANB1, EZH2, MYCN, or control non-targeting shRNA (sh-NC). For overexpression experiments, cells were transduced with plasmids encoding ZRANB1, EZH2, or an empty vector control. Transfection efficiency was verified by qPCR and Western blot 48 h post-transfection.

The catalytically inactive ZRANB1 mutant (CA-ZRANB1, C443S) was generated by site-directed mutagenesis, substituting cysteine 443 with serine. All constructs were verified by DNA sequencing. For transfection, cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells/well 24 h before the experiment. Plasmid DNA (2 µg) was mixed with 4 µL P3000 Reagent and 5 µL Lipofectamine 3000 in 100 µL Opti-MEM, incubated for 15 min at room temperature, and added dropwise to each well. Cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection for downstream assays.

Cycloheximide (CHX) chase assay

CHX (10 µg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich) was added to block protein synthesis. Cells were harvested at 0, 1, 2, 4, and 6 h (MYCN stability) or 0, 4, and 8 h (CA-ZRANB1 validation). MYCN and GAPDH protein levels were detected by western blot as described. MYCN protein half-life was determined by plotting the relative protein levels against time.

MG132 treatment

To examine the effect of proteasome inhibition on MYCN stability, proteasome inhibitor MG132 (10 µM, Sigma-Aldrich) treatment was conducted to prevent protein destabilization. Following treatment, cells were harvested and lysed for further analysis. MYCN protein levels and ubiquitination status were detected with western blot analysis as described.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

IP lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors was used to prepare cell lysates. After pre-clearing with Protein A/G agarose beads, lysates were incubated overnight with specific antibodies: anti-MYCN (ab227822, 1:30, Abcam, UK), anti-ZRANB1 (ab82132, 1:50, Abcam, UK), or anti-EZH2 (ab307646, 1:30, Abcam, UK). Protein A/G beads were added for 2 h at 4 °C to pull down immune complexes. Beads were washed, and immunoprecipitated proteins were eluted and analyzed by WB.

Immunoblotting (IB) for ubiquitination levels

MYCN ubiquitination was examined by immunoprecipitation followed by ubiquitin blotting. Cells were harvested in RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors and 10 mM N-ethylmaleimide. After clearing using Protein A/G beads, lysates were incubated overnight at 4 °C with an anti-MYCN antibody (ab227822, 1:1000, Abcam, UK). Immune complexes were pulled down with Protein A/G agarose beads, washed, and eluted using SDS sample buffer. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, and blocked with 5% skimmed milk. Membranes were probed overnight with an anti-ubiquitin antibody (ab134953, 1:1000, Abcam, UK), followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (#5125, 1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA). Signals were visualized with ECL substrate using a ChemiDoc XRS + System. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software.

Xenograft tumor model

BE(2)M17 cells (5 × 106) with ZRANB1 overexpression or knockdown were implanted subcutaneously into the flanks of Balb/c nude mice (4–6 weeks old, male, Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME, USA). For intraperitoneal injection, cells were injected into the peritoneal cavity of Balb/c nude mice. After 28 days of tumor growth, mice were sacrificed. Tumors were collected, weighed, and fixed in formalin for subsequent immunohistochemistry.

Statistical analysis

All data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Differences between groups were analyzed using Student's t-test or one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test for multiple comparisons. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To further characterize ZRANB1 expression in the context of NB heterogeneity, we analyzed its correlation with ADRN and MES lineage markers in the TARGET-Asgharzadeh cohort. Notably, ZRANB1 expression was significantly correlated with ADRN core transcription factors including PHOX2B, HAND1, GATA2, and ISL1, but not with MES markers (Figure S1A). For subsequent experiments, BE(2)M17 cells were used as the MYCN-amplified model for their higher expression levels, facilitating efficient knockdown. SH-SY5Y cells, with minimal ZRANB1 and MYCN expression, served as the non-amplified model. This pairing highlights the differences in ZRANB1 and MYCN expression and stability between the two NB subtypes.

ZRANB1 maintains MYCN stability by preventing ubiquitination

To investigate how ZRANB1 affects MYCN stability, we performed CHX chase assays and MG132 treatment following ZRANB1 knockdown or overexpression. qPCR analysis revealed that MYCN mRNA levels were unaffected by ZRANB1 modulation, indicating that ZRANB1 does not regulate MYCN expression at the transcriptional level (Fig. 3A). To determine whether ZRANB1 affects MYCN protein expression through synthesis or degradation pathways, BE(2)M17 and SH-SY5Y cells were treated with cycloheximide (CHX). Western blot and CHX chase assays demonstrated that ZRANB1 knockdown accelerated MYCN protein degradation, while overexpression of ZRANB1 maintained MYCN stability (Fig. 3B). Additionally, treated with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 abrogated MYCN reduction upon ZRANB1 knockdown in BE(2)-M17 cells (Fig. 3C), suggesting that ZRANB1 may regulate MYCN protein levels through ubiquitin–proteasome pathways. To further determine whether ZRANB1's deubiquitinase activity is required for MYCN stabilization, we generated the catalytically inactive mutant CA-ZRANB1 (C443S). Western blot revealed that WT-ZRANB1, but not CA-ZRANB1, significantly increased MYCN expression in BE(2)-M17 cells (Figure S2A). CHX chase assays revealed that WT-ZRANB1 delayed MYCN degradation, while Vector and CA-ZRANB1 exhibited rapid degradation (Figure S2B). Ubiquitination assays confirmed that WT-ZRANB1 reduced MYCN ubiquitination, whereas CA-ZRANB1 showed levels comparable to Vector (Figure S2C). Cellular phenotype assays further demonstrated that WT-ZRANB1 promoted proliferation and migration, while CA-ZRANB1 had no effect (Figure S2D). Collectively, these results demonstrate that ZRANB1 stabilizes MYCN by preventing its ubiquitination and degradation, and that this function is strictly dependent on its deubiquitinase activity.

ZRANB1 regulates MYCN stability through EZH2

To explore how ZRANB1 interacts with and regulates the ubiquitination of MYCN, Co-IP analysis was performed. ZRANB1 does not directly bind to MYCN (Fig. 4A), but directly associates with EZH2 (Fig. 4B), suggesting that ZRANB1 may exert its regulatory effects on MYCN through EZH2. Further Co-IP analysis demonstrated that ZRANB1, MYCN, and EZH2 form a complex, indicating that ZRANB1 stabilizes MYCN indirectly via EZH2 (Fig. 4C). Western blot analysis showed that EZH2 knockdown reduced MYCN expression, while EZH2 overexpression increased MYCN levels; co-knockdown of ZRANB1 and EZH2 led to more pronounced reduction of MYCN, whereas co-overexpression resulted in greater MYCN elevation (Fig. 4D). Ubiquitination assays demonstrated that silencing EZH2 increased MYCN ubiquitination, and co-knockdown of ZRANB1 and EZH2 further enhanced this effect (Fig. 4E). To directly demonstrate ZRANB1-EZH2 interaction and ternary complex formation, we performed in vitro pull-down assays. His-ZRANB1, GST-EZH2, and FLAG-MYCN were in vitro translated, mixed, and pulled down with nickel beads. ZRANB1 promotes tumor progression in vivo

To assess ZRANB1 function in tumor growth in vivo, the xenograft tumor model was applied. Mice bearing BE(2)-M17 xenografts with ZRANB1 overexpression showed significantly larger and heavier tumors, whereas ZRANB1 knockdown reduced tumor volume and weight (Fig. 6A). Further immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis was conducted on the excised tumors to examine the levels of ZRANB1, MYCN, and the proliferation marker Ki-67. The ZRANB1 overexpression group exhibited significantly higher levels of ZRANB1, MYCN, and Ki-67 compared to the control group, indicating enhanced tumor cell proliferation. In contrast, tumors from the ZRANB1 knockdown group showed significantly lower expression levels of these markers, suggesting reduced tumor cell proliferation and growth (Fig. 6B). To further confirm the regulation of MYCN by ZRANB1 in vivo, we examined ZRANB1 and MYCN protein levels in tumor tissues. Discussion

Since its identification in 1983 as an amplified oncogene in NB, MYCN, a member of the MYC transcription factor family, has been one of the most extensively studied genes in this disease (Liu et al. 2020a, b). Recognized for its role in oncogenesis and its impact on disease prognosis, MYCN remains a primary target in NB therapy. Directly targeting MYCN is challenging due to its "undruggable" nature (Wolpaw et al. 2021). Alternative therapeutic strategies have concentrated on disrupting MYCN-driven tumorigenesis by targeting MYCN transcription, translation, protein stability, and synthetic lethality (Bartolucci et al. 2022; Liu et al. 2020a, b). Our gene expression analysis using GEO datasets GSE85047 and GSE80149 revealed a significant upregulation of ZRANB1 and USP4 in MYCN-amplified (AM) NB samples. This was confirmed by higher ZRANB1, USP4, and MYCN expression in MYCN-amplified versus non-amplified cell lines. The upregulation of ZRANB1 in MYCN-amplified NB cells suggests that ZRANB1 may correlated with oncogenic activity of MYCN in NB.

ZRANB1 has been widely studied in the context of tumor biology. In triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), ZRANB1 has been reported to promotes therapy resistance by stabilizing the MRN complex through deubiquitinating NBS1, thus maintaining DNA damage repair and contributing to resistance against PARP inhibitors and radiation (Mei et al. 2023). Additional evidence indicates that high ZRANB1 phosphorylation, associated with its deubiquitinase activity, predicts poor prognosis in hepatocellular carcinoma (Feng et al. 2019). Additionally, ZRANB1 has been also proposed to promotes HCC progression by stabilizing SP1 through deubiquitination, which in turn upregulates LOXL2 expression, thus enhancing tumor growth and metastasis. In colorectal cancer (CRC), ZRANB1 has been demonstrated that contributes to CRC progression by promoting tumorigenesis, stabilizing oncogenic factors through activating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling (Miao et al. 2022). Collectively, these studies indicate that ZRANB1 functions as a deubiquitinase that promotes cancer progression through distinct substrates in different tumor types; however, its role in neuroblastoma remains unexplored.

Here, we demonstrate ZRANB1 expression in MYCN-amplified neuroblastoma cells. ZRANB1 knockdown significantly suppressed proliferation, migration, and sphere formation in BE(2)-M17 and SH-SY5Y cells, whereas overexpression enhanced these phenotypes. Mechanistically, ZRANB1 stabilizes MYCN through dual mechanisms: direct deubiquitination and indirect stabilization via EZH2, establishing a ZRANB1-EZH2-MYCN regulatory axis. Notably, ZRANB1 expression positively correlates with ADRN (Adrenergic) lineage markers, suggesting that this axis predominantly operates in the ADRN subtype. These findings extend the pro-tumorigenic role of ZRANB1 to neuroblastoma, uncover a dual mechanism by which ZRANB1 regulates MYCN stability, providing a basis for targeting ZRANB1 therapeutically in MYCN-amplified neuroblastoma.

To determine how ZRANB1 controls MYCN stability, we carried out CHX chase assays and MG132 treatment following ZRANB1 knockdown or overexpression. qPCR analysis revealed that ZRANB1 did not affect MYCN mRNA levels, indicating that ZRANB1 regulates MYCN post-translationally. Consistently, Western blot and CHX chase assays demonstrated that ZRANB1 knockdown accelerated MYCN protein degradation, while ZRANB1 overexpression prolonged MYCN half-life. The proteasome inhibitor MG132 prevented the decrease in MYCN caused by ZRANB1 depletion, suggesting that ZRANB1 acts via the ubiquitin–proteasome system. Direct evidence from ubiquitination assays revealed that ZRANB1 knockdown increased MYCN ubiquitination, further supporting that ZRANB1 protects MYCN from proteasomal degradation. Collectively, these results indicate that ZRANB1 preserves MYCN stability by inhibiting its ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation, thereby fueling MYCN-driven tumorigenesis in NB.

EZH2 silences tumor suppressor gene transcription via catalyzing histone H3 lysine 27 trimethylation and is highly expressed in multiple malignancies, representing an important therapeutic target (Liu & Yang 2023; Zeng et al. 2022). For example, EZH2 is stabilized by SASH1-mediated inhibition of TRAF6 ubiquitination, facilitating hemangioma cell growth, motility, and invasiveness (Yu et al. 2022). EZH2 acts as a degradation inhibitor of TRAF6-mediated K63-linked ubiquitination through stabilization by SKP2, thereby facilitating prostate cancer progression (Lu et al. 2017). In hepatocellular carcinoma, EZH2 drives tumorigenesis through epigenetic regulation (Wang et al. 2023). In NB, EZH2 was found to directly bind to MYC and stabilize MYCN independent of its methyltransferase function (Wang et al. 2022). ZRANB1 directly binds and deubiquitinates EZH2, leading to its stabilization in breast cancer (Zhang et al. 2018). Nevertheless, the ability of ZRANB1 to regulate MYCN through EZH2 and the functional importance of this cascade in MYCN-amplified NB remain elusive.

Herein, we uncovered that ZRANB1 regulates MYCN stability through dual mechanisms. First, ZRANB1 directly binds and deubiquitinates MYCN, thereby preventing its proteasomal degradation. Second, ZRANB1 indirectly stabilizes MYCN by forming a ternary complex with EZH2. Co-IP experiments revealed that ZRANB1 associates with EZH2 rather than directly binding MYCN, and that ZRANB1, EZH2, and MYCN coexist in the same complex. Functionally, ZRANB1 knockdown reduced both EZH2 and MYCN protein levels, whereas EZH2 overexpression partially rescued MYCN expression upon ZRANB1 depletion, indicating that EZH2 serves as a critical mediator of ZRANB1-dependent MYCN stabilization. Moreover, ubiquitination assays demonstrated that ZRANB1 stabilizes EZH2 via deubiquitination, leading to reduced MYCN ubiquitination and degradation. Collectively, these findings establish that ZRANB1 maintains MYCN stability through both direct deubiquitination and indirect EZH2-mediated regulation.

Mechanistically, we uncovered that ZRANB1 stabilizes MYCN via two distinct yet cooperative pathways. First, ZRANB1 directly deubiquitinates MYCN, removing its ubiquitin chains and thereby shielding it from proteasomal degradation (Fig. 3). Second—and more strikingly—ZRANB1 does not bind MYCN directly; instead, it engages EZH2 to form a ZRANB1-EZH2-MYCN ternary complex. Within this complex, ZRANB1 deubiquitinates and stabilizes EZH2, which in turn protects MYCN from ubiquitin-mediated degradation. Rescue experiments confirmed that EZH2 acts as an essential mediator, as its overexpression restored MYCN levels upon ZRANB1 depletion. Collectively, these data reveal ZRANB1 as a central upstream mediator that controls MYCN stability through both direct and EZH2-mediated deubiquitination, revealing a previously unrecognized regulatory layer in MYCN-driven neuroblastoma.

Notably, we found that ZRANB1 significantly correlates with ADRN core transcription factors, including PHOX2B, HAND1, and GATA2, positioning ZRANB1 within the ADRN transcriptional regulatory network. Neuroblastoma can be classified into ADRN and MES subtypes, with MYCN amplification predominantly occurring in the ADRN subtype; accordingly, ZRANB1 showed correlation with MYCN in ADRN-enriched samples, while the signal was diluted in samples with MES cell admixture. Furthermore, ADRN and MES cells exhibit differential therapeutic sensitivity: MES cells are intrinsically resistant to ALK inhibitors (Westerhout et al. 2022), show delayed clearance of MES markers after chemotherapy, whereas treatment-responsive tumors display an ADRN phenotype (Gelineau et al. 2025). Based on these findings, the association between high ZRANB1 expression and better prognosis observed in the same TARGET-Asgharzadeh cohort can be explained by ZRANB1, as an ADRN marker, reflecting the treatment sensitivity of the ADRN subtype rather than contradicting its pro-tumorigenic function.

Both in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrate that ZRANB1 cooperates with EZH2 to stabilize MYCN and promote neuroblastoma cell growth. Specifically, ZRANB1 depletion suppressed tumor growth in xenograft models, an effect associated with reduced MYCN protein levels and decreased proliferation. Thus, ZRANB1 may be a viable target for treating MYCN-amplified neuroblastoma.

MYCN is a key driver in many cancers. Due to its "undruggable" nature, the strategy of indirectly targeting tumor cells by manipulating MYCN-related interaction molecules has rapidly developed and become a research hotspot (Borgenvik et al. 2020; Hagemann et al. 2023; Tucker et al. 2019; Xu et al. 2023). We discovered a previously unknown mechanism by which ZRANB1 stabilizes MYCN through its interaction with EZH2, forming a ZRANB1-EZH2-MYCN regulatory axis. This discovery provides new opportunities for treating MYCN-amplified neuroblastoma by targeting this axis to reduce MYCN stability.

Several questions remain for future investigation. First, the precise molecular details of how ZRANB1 and EZH2 coordinate to regulate MYCN stability warrant further exploration. Second, whether this regulatory axis operates in other MYCN-driven cancers remains to be determined. Third, while our study was primarily conducted using established cell lines that cover the ADRN and MES subtypes, these models cannot fully recapitulate the heterogeneity of primary tumors. Future studies utilizing patient-derived primary cells and PDX models are needed to validate the translational relevance of the ZRANB1-EZH2-MYCN axis. Additionally, the other 24 differentially expressed deubiquitinases identified in our screen warrant further investigation as potential regulators of MYCN stability or neuroblastoma progression. Addressing these questions will help establish the broader therapeutic relevance of targeting ZRANB1-EZH2-MYCN signaling.

In conclusion, our findings establish ZRANB1 as a critical regulator of MYCN stability through its interaction with EZH2, thereby promoting neuroblastoma progression. These results underscore the therapeutic potential of targeting the ZRANB1-EZH2-MYCN axis in MYCN-amplified neuroblastoma and provide a basis for future mechanistic and translational studies.
