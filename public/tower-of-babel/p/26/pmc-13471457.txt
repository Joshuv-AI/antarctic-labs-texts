Increasing burden of Clostridioides difficile infection in Spain: nationwide trends in hospitalisations, 2016–2023
Authors: Yolanda Ortega-López, Jesús Molina-Cabrillana, Marco Antonio Sempere-Alcocer, Rosa López-Gigosos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClostridioides difficile infection (CDI) is a major cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. In Spain, the epidemiology of Clostridioides difficile infection remains insufficiently characterised due to the limited availability of epidemiological data.AimTo provide a comprehensive national description of the incidence, temporal trends and epidemiological characteristics of CDI in Spain from 2016 to 2023.MethodsA nationwide retrospective observational study was conducted, using the Spanish Hospital Discharge Database (RAE-CMBD). Annual incidence rates were calculated per 10,000 hospital admissions at national and autonomous community (AC) levels, by sex and age group. Temporal trends were assessed using Kendall’s tau. CDI cases were classified according to timing of onset as present on admission or hospital-onset.ResultsA total of 81,487 CDI -related hospitalisations were identified. National incidence increased steadily from 15.6 to 31.4 cases per 10,000 admissions between 2016 and 2023, showing a significant upward trend. The highest burden was observed among patients aged ≥ 75 years. Overall, 72% of CDI cases were present at hospital admission, while 28% were hospital-onset. Hospital-onset CDI was associated with higher in-hospital mortality compared with cases present on admission (13.8% vs 10.7%).ConclusionThe incidence of Clostridioides difficile infection diagnosed in Spanish hospitals showed a substantial increase over the study period. Despite the study’s limitations, these results reinforce the importance of antimicrobial stewardship and infection prevention programmes across all levels of healthcare settings to reduce the impact of CDI.

Background

Clostridioides difficile is the causative agent of Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI). Following ingestion, spores may colonise the intestine. Disruption of the gut microbiota—most commonly due to antibiotic exposure—can promote bacterial overgrowth and toxin A and B production, leading to mucosal inflammation and epithelial damage [1]. This pathogen is responsible for a wide spectrum of clinical manifestations, ranging from asymptomatic colonisation to mild diarrhoea and severe, potentially life-threatening complications, including pseudomembranous colitis, toxic megacolon, sepsis and death [2, 3].

CDI is a major cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide [4, 5]. CDI is responsible for nearly 500,000 infections annually and approximately 30,000 deaths in the United States, with an incidence of 116.1 cases per 100,000 persons in 2022, with community-associated CDI occurring more frequently than healthcare-associated CDI (62.1 vs 54.0 per 100,000 persons) [1, 3, 6]. In Europe, estimates from the healthcare-associated infection point prevalence survey suggest around 152,657 cases per year (95% CI: 83,404–325,432), with an incidence density of 2.58 cases per 10,000 patient-days in 2020 (95% CI: 2.42–2.75; median: 2.05) and an overall increasing trend [7, 8]

Recent antibiotic exposure, advanced age and hospitalisation—often associated with increased antibiotic use—are the most important risk factors for CDI [2, 9, 10]. Other recognised risk factors include chemotherapy, solid organ and haematopoietic stem cell transplantation, and long-term treatment with proton pump inhibitors [11]

CDI represents a particularly complex condition due to its clinical, social and economic impact [3, 12], substantially impairing patients’ quality of life. The increasing incidence and severity of recurrent disease contribute significantly to the overall burden of CDI and pose a major public health challenge [8, 9, 13].

From an epidemiological perspective, CDI cases are classified as healthcare-associated, community-associated or of indeterminate origin, according to the timing and setting of symptom onset [14]. CDI remains one of the most relevant healthcare-associated infections, particularly those with hospital onset, where exposure to high-risk antibiotics and transmission of C. difficile are more likely to occur [2, 7, 15]. It is the leading cause of hospital-acquired diarrhoea, accounting for approximately 30% of cases, while its contribution to diarrhoeal disease in non-hospitalised patients is increasingly recognised [3, 16].

In the United States, population-based surveillance systems have shown a declining trend in healthcare-associated CDI in recent years, whereas community-associated CDI continues to increase [3, 6]. This pattern has not yet been observed in European countries [8], likely reflecting differences in surveillance systems, which are predominantly hospital-based.

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is recognised as one of the ten leading global public health threats [17], and is closely linked to CDI, as both are driven by similar underlying factors, including antibiotic use and the transmission of microorganisms [18]. Consequently, CDI, like AMR, requires targeted prevention and control programmes [19].

Since January 2016, a European Union-wide CDI surveillance network has been in place, using a common protocol to monitor hospital-based CDI incidence and generate comparable data to support prevention and control strategies [14]. Spain’s participation in this network has been minimal, resulting in a lack of nationally representative and comparable European data [8]. Available information is largely limited to point prevalence surveys conducted at European [7] and national level [20] which showed an increase in CDI prevalence among hospitalised patients from 14.1% in 2012 to 35.9% in 2019 (cases per 10,000 hospitalised patients).

Although a national healthcare-associated infection surveillance project including hospital-based CDI surveillance has existed since 2015, it has not yet been fully implemented, and no recent national incidence estimates are available. Previous studies have focused on estimating the economic burden of CDI in Spain and its association with prolonged hospital stay [21, 22]; in 2013, an estimated 7,601 episodes of C. difficile-associated diarrhoea were reported, corresponding to an incidence of 17.1 episodes per 10,000 hospital discharges [22].

In this context, this study aims to provide an updated and comprehensive overview of the burden of CDI within the Spanish public healthcare system. Specifically, we describe recent trends in overall CDI incidence, including hospital-onset and community-onset cases, and explore their association with antimicrobial consumption, with the goal of informing targeted prevention and control programmes.

Methods

Study design and data sources

We conducted a retrospective, hospital-based observational study using anonymised individual-level data from the Spanish National Registry of Specialized Healthcare Activity (RAE-CMBD), a nationwide administrative database covering all hospital discharges in Spain [23] over eight years (2016–2023). Data on CDI cases were obtained from the General Subdirectorate for Health Information, Ministry of Health, through an official request for all CDI episodes recorded in public acute care hospitals between January 2016 and December 2023.

Hospitalisation denominators, stratified by age group, sex and autonomous community (AC), were obtained by the interactive RAE-CMBD statistical portal of the Ministry of Health, Management Intelligence Area.

Aggregate data on hospital and community antimicrobial consumption were extracted from the publicly available maps published by the Spanish National Antibiotic Resistance Plan (PRAN) for each autonomous community and year, expressed in defined daily doses (DDD) per 1,000 inhabitants per day.

Study population

The study population comprised all admissions to public acute care hospitals within the Spanish National Health System between 2016 and 2023. CDI episodes were defined as hospitalisations with at least one recorded CDI diagnosis. Analyses were conducted across the 17 AC and the two autonomous cities of Spain.

Variables

CDI cases were identified using ICD-10 diagnostic codes extracted from both primary and secondary diagnosis fields in the RAE-CMBD database. For the period 2016–2019, the code A04.7 (Enterocolitis due to Clostridium difficile) was used. From 2020 onwards, when updated codes were introduced, CDI cases were identified using A04.71 (Enterocolitis due to Clostridium difficile, recurrent) and A04.72 (Enterocolitis due to Clostridium difficile, not specified as recurrent). A hospitalisation was considered a CDI case if any of these codes were recorded in any of the up to 20 diagnosis fields available per episode.

Each diagnosis field includes a present-on-admission (POA) indicator. Diagnoses with POA marked as “yes” were considered present at the time of hospital admission, whereas those marked as “no” were considered to have occurred during hospitalisation.

All variables mandatorily collected in the RAE-CMBD for each hospitalisation episode were available [23], Variables used in the analysis included date of birth, sex, date and time of admission, type of contact and visit, date and time of discharge, intensive care unit (ICU) admission, length of ICU stay, primary diagnosis and corresponding POA indicator, secondary diagnoses and their POA indicators, AC, and discharge destination.

For the extraction of total hospitalisation denominators for the study period, aggregated data were obtained by year, age group, sex and AC from the RAE-CMBD statistical portal.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analysis

Continuous variables were summarised using means and standard deviations or medians and interquartile ranges, as appropriate. CDI cases and cumulative incidence were calculated for each study year and described at the national level, by autonomous community, and by age group.

Absolute and relative frequencies were calculated for CDI cases present on admission and for hospital-onset CDI (HA-CDI), using the present-on-admission (POA) indicator corresponding to each diagnosis coded as A04.7, A04.71 or A04.72. Absolute and relative frequencies of recurrent CDI and all-cause mortality were also calculated.

Trend analysis

Temporal trends in the number of CDI cases and cumulative incidence were assessed using Kendall’s rank correlation. Kendall’s correlation was used because the variables did not meet the assumption of normality. Additionally, these were time series with potential outliers and a non-linear trend. This non-parametric approach is robust to outliers and suitable for detecting monotonic changes over time, which was the primary objective of the trend analysis rather than estimating effect sizes.

Antimicrobial consumption

The association between CDI incidence and the consumption of four antimicrobial classes (aggregated across community and hospital settings) was assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. P-values were adjusted using the false discovery rate (FDR) method to reduce the risk of type I error due to multiple comparisons.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.2). Data tables were managed using Microsoft Office Standard 2019 (xlsx files). Figures were generated using the ggplot2 (v4.0.0) and ggpubr (v0.6.0) packages, along with other base R functions.

Results

Overview/descriptive epidemiology

Between 2016 and 2023, a total of 81,487 hospitalisations with a diagnosis of Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI) were identified nationwide. In 37.5% of cases, CDI was recorded as the primary diagnosis at admission, corresponding to a national rate of 8.5 CDI cases per 10,000 hospital admissions for any cause. The cumulative incidence increased steadily over the study period, from 15.63 cases per 10,000 admissions in 2016 to 31.35 cases per 10,000 admissions in 2023 (Fig. 1). CDI by timing of onset (present on admission vs hospital-onset)

Community-associated CDI (present on admission)

We were only able to classify CDI cases into two categories according to the place of onset: CDI present on admission (community-associated in our study) and hospital-onset (HA-CDI). The community-associated (CA-CDI) category also includes all community-onset healthcare-associated CDI cases, which could not be specifically identified due to the lack of information on recent healthcare exposure.

Overall, 72% of all Clostridioides difficile infections (CDI) were present at the time of hospital admission (CA-CDI in our study). Among these cases, 54.7% occurred in patients aged ≥ 75 years, a proportion that remained stable throughout the study period (minimum 52.1% in 2017, maximum 55.9% in 2022) (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Relative frequency of community-associated and hospital-onset Clostridioides difficile infection by autonomous community, Spain, 2016–2023. Hospital-onset CDI (acquired during hospitalisation)

Hospital-onset CDI (HA-CDI), acquired during the hospital stay, accounted for 28% of all CDI cases. The relative contribution of HA-CDI varied across AC, but remained consistently lower than that of CA-CDI in all regions (Fig. 3). Among HA-CDI cases, the majority (45%) occurred in patients aged ≥ 75 years, with an increasing proportion over the study years (minimum 42.5% in 2016; maximum 48.5% in 2023). Men had a higher risk of acquiring HA-CDI compared with women (52.5% vs. 47.5%), and this difference was statistically significant. Observed mortality was higher among HA-CDI cases (13.8%) than among patients with CA-CDI (10.7%). A statistically significant association was found between CDI type and discharge disposition (χ2(6) = 720.45; p < 0.001), although the effect size was small (Cramer’s V = 0.10).

Missing data

The present-on-admission (POA) indicator was missing in 4% of records.

Recurrence and mortality

Recurrence

Between 2020 and 2023, 15.47% of CDI cases were identified as recurrent infections. Among these, recurrent CDI was recorded as the primary reason for hospital admission in 51.83% of cases. Older women were the most affected group, with women aged ≥ 75 years accounting for 49.3% of all recurrent CDI episodes. Recurrence is often not adequately coded; therefore, many of these cases may have been missed.

Mortality

Overall, 11.4% of patients with a diagnosis of Clostridioides difficile infection died during hospitalisation, irrespective of the underlying cause of death.

Association with antimicrobial consumption

Aggregate data on antimicrobial consumption at hospital and community level were available at autonomous community level for four antimicrobial classes: beta-lactam antibacterials, penicillins (J01C); other beta-lactam antibacterials (J01D); quinolone antibacterials (J01M); and macrolides, lincosamides and streptogramins (J01F).

A statistically significant negative association was observed between CDI incidence and the consumption of three antimicrobial classes. These results are reported descriptively; however, they should be interpreted with caution, as the analysis was based on aggregated consumption data and did not allow adjustment by setting (hospital vs. community) or patient age. The objective of this analysis is exploratory, with an ecological study design; therefore, it cannot be used to support causal inference.

Discussión

In the context of Spain’s limited participation in the European CDI surveillance network [8], and the absence of an operational national surveillance system providing up-to-date incidence data, this study was undertaken to address an important epidemiological gap.

We used nationwide administrative data (RAE-CMBD), which provide valuable population coverage and enable large-scale analyses; however, it entails inherent limitations. These include potential misclassification due to coding practices and lack of detailed information on key variables such as disease severity, microbiological methods, prior healthcare exposure or differentiation between first episodes and repeated hospitalisations of the same patient. Consequently, findings should be interpreted in light of these constraints.

Our aim was not only to characterise the recent burden and trends of Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI) in Spain, but also to provide a robust baseline that may inform the future development of targeted prevention and control programmes.

The assignment of a CDI diagnostic code in the RAE-CMBD requires explicit documentation of CDI in the clinical record, supported by a prior microbiological diagnosis. Despite this, a proportion of cases identified as CDI are likely to represent asymptomatic colonization. The sensitivity of the RAE-CMBD for identifying CDI cases is unknown; therefore, under-ascertainment is likely. Another limitation of the RAE-CMBD database is that it does not include information on microbiological diagnostic activity, and annual data regarding the number of CDI diagnostic tests performed were not available. Since previous studies have demonstrated that reported CDI incidence is strongly influenced by testing density and diagnostic methodology [24, 25], part of the increase observed over time may reflect changes in diagnostic practices. In addition, although this could not be directly assessed in the present study, increased clinical awareness of CDI may also have contributed to greater testing and case detection over the study period.

Our results are in line with previous Spanish studies based on CMBD data [26]. The most recent national estimates, published in 2013, reported 7,601 CDI cases per year and an incidence of 17.1 episodes per 10,000 hospital discharges, already showing an increasing trend at that time [22]. Our analysis confirms and extends these observations, demonstrating a sustained rise in both the number of cases and incidence rates, which approximately doubled by the final year of the study (2023). In contrast, a more recently published study on CDI epidemiology in Spain reported substantially different incidence estimates; however, the denominators used were not clearly specified, limiting the comparability of those results with both earlier Spanish studies and our findings [27].

Older adults, particularly those aged ≥ 65 years and especially ≥ 75 years, were disproportionately affected by CDI across all AC. In contrast, very few CDI cases were reported among children younger than 1 year; diagnoses in this group should be interpreted with caution, as asymptomatic colonisation is common and clinical infection is rare due to age-related biological factors. Consequently, current guidelines do not recommend C. difficile testing in children under 12 months of age [28].

The introduction of more sensitive C. difficile diagnostic assays in the late 2000s, particularly nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs), has likely contributed to the observed increase in CDI incidence [29]. In Spain, diagnostic recommendations and testing algorithms were published by the Spanish Society of Infectious Diseases and Clinical Microbiology in 2015. In 2020, national guidelines recommended combining GDH, toxin detection, and NAATs to optimize diagnostic accuracy and reduce underdiagnosis [30]; therefore, a progressive shift towards multistep diagnostic algorithms in Spain, particularly in large provincial hospitals, is to be expected. As a future line of research, a multicenter study could be considered to evaluate the heterogeneity in diagnostic strategies used in Spanish hospitals. A retrospective study conducted in a Spanish hospital over a decade showed a significant increase in CDI incidence following the implementation of a two-step laboratory diagnostic algorithm, although this was accompanied by higher testing rates and sample positivity [31]. Therefore, the sustained year-on-year rise in CDI incidence, including in the most recent years, suggests that additional drivers beyond diagnostic practices are contributing to the continued increase [3]. Additional unmeasured factors may also contribute to these findings, including population ageing, stable patterns of antimicrobial use over time, increased clinical awareness of CDI, and broader determinants such as lifestyle-related changes in gut microbiota or One Health–related factors. However, these variables were not captured in our analysis and their potential influence should be interpreted with caution.

The analysis of CDI incidence trends by AC and age group provides, to our knowledge, the first national-level epidemiological overview of CDI in Spain. This regionalised assessment offers relevant insights to inform the design and implementation of targeted prevention and control strategies.

By comparison, surveillance data from the United States reported a CDI incidence of 116.1 cases per 100,000 population in 2022, with community-associated CDI exceeding healthcare-associated CDI incidence (62.1 vs 54.0 cases per 100,000). Furthermore, an estimated 69,800 first CDI recurrences were reported in 2017, underscoring the considerable burden of recurrent disease [1].

In both the United States and European countries, surveillance systems indicate that most CDI cases are classified as healthcare-associated, accounting for approximately 60% of cases, while around one third are classified as community-associated or of unknown association (CA/UA-CDI) [1, 8]. In Spain, data from national point prevalence surveys have suggested that up to two thirds of CDI cases are of nosocomial onset [20]. In contrast, in our study, only 28% of CDI episodes were identified as hospital-onset, while 72% were already present at the time of admission and were therefore classified as community-associated or of unknown association. The data used in this study were not derived from standardized CDI surveillance protocols; therefore, classification into cases present on admission and hospital-onset limits comparability with European or international surveillance data. Nevertheless, the finding that CDI constituted the primary reason for hospital admission in 37.5% of cases underscores the substantial clinical impact of the infection, irrespective of its place of acquisition.

Although current surveillance systems predominantly classify CDI as a healthcare-associated infection, increasing evidence suggests that sources and reservoirs of C. difficile outside hospital settings play a relevant role in transmission [32]. This observation is consistent with reports indicating a rising incidence of community-associated CDI [3]. In this context, future surveillance strategies would benefit from population-based approaches, which are more sensitive for detecting infections associated with community and non-hospital healthcare settings. To better understand transmission dynamics outside hospitals and their contribution to the marked increase in CDI incidence observed in recent years, consideration should also be given to surveillance of asymptomatic colonisation.

Regardless of the acquisition setting, our findings underscore the substantial burden of CDI on patients and the Spanish healthcare system. Although the database included variables potentially related to disease severity (e.g. intensive care unit admission, length of hospital stay and comorbidities), these were not analysed, as the temporal sequence of events could not be reliably established. Given that major CDI risk factors are well described [2], this study focused on incidence patterns rather than individual-level severity determinants.

The widespread and increasing use of broad-spectrum antibiotics in both healthcare and community settings remains a key driver of CDI. The observed negative correlation between antimicrobial consumption and CDI incidence is likely explained by the ecological design of the study and the use of aggregated data. Differences in case mix, healthcare structure, and diagnostic practices across regions, as well as the inability to distinguish between community and hospital antibiotic use, may have influenced these findings. In addition, temporal changes in diagnostic methods and antimicrobial stewardship interventions could have contributed to this counterintuitive association. The lack of sufficiently disaggregated antimicrobial consumption data precluded assessment of the association between antibiotic use and CDI incidence in Spain, representing an important limitation and underscoring the need to strengthen national antimicrobial surveillance systems.

CDI prevention relies on two key components, antimicrobial stewardship programmes (ASP/PROA) and infection prevention and control programmes. The coordinated implementation and strengthening of both strategies are essential to curb the growing burden of this infection [33].

CDI incidence is widely regarded as an indicator of healthcare system performance, reflecting both infection prevention measures and antimicrobial stewardship effectiveness [2]. As prior antibiotic exposure is the main risk factor for CDI, incidence rates may also serve as an indirect measure of stewardship impact. Current surveillance classifications based on acquisition setting (community- or healthcare-associated) may, however, be artificial and of limited utility, as they risk obscuring the central role of healthcare interventions across all levels of care in CDI development.

Several countries have successfully reduced hospital-onset CDI through dedicated surveillance and prevention programmes [3, 9, 15]. In contrast, Spain currently lacks a national CDI-specific prevention strategy. Establishing a population-based national surveillance system, strengthening participation in European surveillance networks, and implementing targeted prevention programmes should therefore be prioritised.

Conclusion

This study provides a description of the national incidence of Clostridioides difficile infection, its temporal trends, and its distribution across autonomous communities, based on diagnoses recorded in Spanish hospitals between 2016 and 2023. CDI incidence increased steadily in most regions, with the highest burden observed among older adults and a substantial proportion of cases identified at hospital admission. These findings should be interpreted considering the limitations of the RAE-CMBD database, which lacks information on microbiological diagnostic activity, prior healthcare exposure and the diagnostic methods used. The use of more sensitive techniques, such as NAAT, and increased clinical awareness during the study period may have contributed to improved case detection and the observed increase in incidence. These results highlight the need for a population-based national surveillance system and reinforce the importance of antimicrobial stewardship and infection prevention programmes across healthcare settings to reduce the impact of CDI.
