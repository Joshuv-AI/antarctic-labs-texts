Introduction

Extraskeletal Ewing sarcoma (EES), a rare soft tissue cancer, was formerly a subset of the ES family of tumors (ESFT), a group of small spherical malignant cells [1].

In terms of their molecular lineage and initial neural tissue, these cells are similar to one another. Peripheral primitive neuroectodermal tumor (pPNET), Askin's tumor of the chest wall, and Ewing sarcoma of bone (ESB), which is regarded as the second most prevalent form of primary bone tumors in children of that age group, are also included in ESFT along with EES [2,3]. Comparing EES to ESB, EES has a lower frequency, a delayed onset, and an improved overall 5-year survival [4].

EES typically begins in the upper extremities, gluteal area, or proximal thigh [5]. EES is a rapidly growing tumor, resulting in compression symptoms such as localized pain [5]. Imaging investigations are typically the first step in EES diagnosis. Histology and immunohistochemistry are crucial in the diagnosis of EES [1]. Radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and/or surgical excision are all possible treatment options for EES [2,6]. Here we report a case of Extraskeletal Ewing's Sarcoma of the soft tissue with bone and lung metastasis in a 12-year-old male patient. The case report has been reported in line with SCARE Criteria [7].

Case presentation

A 12-year-old male adolescent, from a rural area in northern Ethiopia, presented with swelling over the proximal left thigh of 7 months duration that rapidly increase in size over the last two months associated with pain and limping. For which the family applied herbal medication on the swelling. He developed a dry cough, fast breathing, left-side chest pain, and high-grade fever one week before admission. Otherwise, he had no self or family history of a similar illness, previous surgical intervention or history of allergy.

Upon examination, he was febrile at 38.7 degrees centigrade and he was tachypneic with a respiratory rate of 38 breaths per minute. He had about 20 × 15 cm firm, tender, and erythematous proximal left thigh swelling that is fixed to the structures with an overlying scar (Fig. 1). Other systematic physical examinations are non-revealing.

Clinical photography at presentation showing Left proximal thigh huge mass with overlying scar.

After admission, the patient was investigated with total blood count, LDH, chest X-ray, thigh, and pelvic X-ray, and thigh MRI. His blood count revealed severe anemia of hemoglobin 5.8 g/dL and elevated LDH (>1200 U/L). His chest X-ray showed bilateral asymmetric multiple nodules predominantly in the hilar area (Fig. 2) and the thigh and pelvic X-ray showed soft tissue swelling, iliac bone irregularity, and ipsilateral femur bone ill-defined lateral cortical outline (Fig. 3). MRI of the pelvis and thigh showed a large relatively well-defined soft tissue mass located deep within the anterior compartment of the left thigh. Proximally the mass extended to the pelvic side wall along the iliopsoas tendon. It showed a heterogenous signal with foci of layering T1-hyperintense signal and solid restriction component. The post-contrast study showed predominant peripheral enhancement with papillary projections and suggestive necrosis. There was a proximal medial cortical breach and marrow infiltration with enhancement. The neurovascular status looked uninvolved (Fig. 4A–F).

Left hip AP X-rays showing soft tissue swelling, iliac bone irregularity and ipsilateral femur ill-defined cortical outline.

A–F Thigh and pelvic MRI slices illustrating large heterogenous signal soft tissue mass over the anterior thigh extending to the pelvis along the iliopsoas tendon with peripheral enhancement and cortical breach with bone marrow infiltration.

Subsequently, incisional biopsy was done and the histology study showed small round cells having finely stippled chromatin, inconspicuous nucleoli, scant eosinophilic and clear cytoplasm, indistinct cell border forming loose clusters admixed with extensive necrosis, ghosty cells and fibrin (Fig. 5A–F).

Histopathology slides. (A) Necrotic area with close up view. (B) Extensive necrosis left upper and lower with focal viable cells (C) low power view of viable focus (D) Nests of neoplastic cells separated by delicate collagenous septae (E) high power view (400× magnification) with monotonous neoplastic cells with moderate clear cytoplasm (F) high power view (600× magnification) showing neoplastic cells with marked clear cytoplasm.

With final diagnosis of Extraskeletal Ewing Sarcoma (EES), two cycles of chemotherapy were given. While was admitted he developed pain and swelling of the thigh and leg of same extremity for which Venous Doppler was done and showed acute proximal DVT (Left femoral vein). He started DVT treatment but went against medical advice from the PICU.

Discussion

Ewing Sarcoma (ES) is an aggressive tumor that is a member of the Ewing Sarcoma Family of Tumors (ESFT) primarily affects bone tissue. ESFT consists of classic Ewing Sarcoma of the bone (ESB), Extra skeletal Ewing's Sarcoma (EES), Peripheral Primitive neuroectodermal tumor (PPNET), and Askin tumor of the chest wall. These group of tumors are small round cell and share neural histology in common [2,8]. EES is a rare tumor with an incidence ranging from 0.1 to 0.4 per a million people [1,2]. EES patients presented with rapidly increasing swelling mainly over the soft tissue of the proximal thigh, pelvis, paravertebral region, chest wall, upper arm and shoulder [2,8] associated with mild pain [1,2,8]. The manifestation of the disease is dependent on the primary location of the tumor in the body and the affected tissue by distance metastasis, that occurred in about 25 % of EES at evaluation [2]. Age at presentation ranges 10–30 years with male to female equivalent ratio [1,2,8]. Our case is a 12 year old male patient with left proximal thigh EES with metastasis to the ipsilateral femur, pelvic ring bones and to the lung after he presented with a fast growing swelling over his left proximal thigh over 7 months period associated with high grade fever, dry cough and fast breathing of a week duration.

Even though the imaging features of EES are non-specific, imaging is crucial for the diagnosis, the preoperative assessment, the staging, and the evaluation of treatment outcomes [2,8]. It also helps in determining the easily accessible biopsy site [8].

Plain radiograph in correlation with a clinical feature used in ruling out some tumors that have a similar presentation with EES. Despite the fact that Malignant Fibrous Histiocytoma may have a similar clinical presentation to EES, it often occurs in older age groups (after the 5th decade of life) and may show calcification on plain X-rays [9]. It has not been reported that EES exhibits pretreatment tumor calcification [10]. Liposarcomas are other tumors that likely present in a similar fashion with EES but they are unlikely to present before 3rd decades of life and will have fat when well differentiated, unlike EES [11]. The Ap thigh and pelvic X-ray taken in our case showed left proximal thigh swelling and increased density, ipsilateral pelvic bones cortical irregularity, and ipsilateral proximal femur ill-defined cortical outline with no matrix calcification (Fig. 3).

Low to intermediate signal intensity on T1-weighted images, high signal intensity on T2-weighted sequences, and post-contrast enhancement are among the MRI characteristics of EES [12]. Our case MRI finding also goes with these features but it is hyperintense on T1 signal. This may happen due to the hemorrhagic features of the EES [8].

Due to the fact that EES is a malignant tumor, it requires extensive metastatic workup, particularly to the lung, bone, and brain. PET/CT is crucial in determining whether metastatic disease is present [12]. The PA chest X-ray taken at the time of presentation, in our case, showed multiple, bilateral, asymmetric nodular masses mainly in the hilar region suggestive of metastasis (Fig. 2).

Due to the lack of unique clinical and radiological features of EES, the histopathological study is mandatory for the definitive diagnosis of EES among competitive differential diagnoses mainly rhabdomyosarcoma and lymphoma [1]. However, in our case immunohistology was not done due to lack of the service in our country and financial limitations of the family for the work up abroad.

Since there hasn't been a sufficient study on EES, management has largely been based on the soft tissue sarcoma protocol; nevertheless, recent trials have shown that improved outcomes can be achieved by implementing the National Comprehensive Cancer Network's (NCCN) recommendations on ES management and developing a personalized approach for each individual case of EES based on presentation [13]. Current literatures support the treatment of EES is based on ES treatment protocol and is a multimodal approach [14]. The mainstay of the management of localized EES is surgery with full resection [2]. Resection margins have a key role in the local management of malignant tumors. Wider resection margins are needed when chemotherapy is ineffective. Postoperative radiation is advised for achieving better local control whenever wide negative margins are not possible, for instance, due to vascular or nerve structures [6]. Radiation therapy is the only option for treatment if the tumor cannot be excised [15,16]. Chemotherapy is still an option for treating metastatic disease with the objective of extending progression-free survival [6]. The prognosis in such cases is very poor [6]. Patients, like our case, with bone and lung metastasis have worst prognosis irrespective of local irradiation and chemotherapy treatment [16].

Given our case is EES with distant metastasis to the bone and lung, we started to manage the case with chemotherapy of an Ewing sarcoma treatment protocol (took two cycles) consisting of Vincristine, Actinomycin D, and cyclophosphamide (VAC)-in specialized referral hospital. He would have been benefited from the provision of radiation therapy [16] but radiotherapy treatment is not available in our referral hospital. Meanwhile, the patient started to develop diffuse left lower extremity swelling for which venous Doppler study was done, and was diagnosed to have femoral vein acute venous thrombosis and started management for this but the family took him home against medical advice.

Histologic response to preoperative chemotherapy and metastasis at time of diagnosis [17,18], elevated white blood cell count (>11 × 1019/L), low hemoglobin (≤10 g/dL) and an increased LDH levels (>2 × N U/L) at the time of presentation are associated with poor prognosis in EES patients [19,20].

Similarly, our case has metastasis to bones, and lungs at time of presentation. He also has low hemoglobin level (5.8 g/dL), elevated LDH (>1200 which is >4 × N U/L) all these are a poor prognosticator of the final outcomes of the patient. The histologic response for the chemotherapy and the final patient outcome were not possible to know as the patient discontinued the treatment early in the course of the management.

Limitation of this case report

In the diagnosis of this rare case, we didn't include a histochemistry analysis to differentiate the top competitive diagnosis due to lack of the service in our country. In addition, the final outcome of the case is not known as the patient discontinued the treatment early in the course of the management.

Conclusion

EES is a rare tumor but it should be in the differential diagnosis of adolescent patients presented with soft tissue mass/swelling. The diagnosis of EES is done with constellations of evidences from clinical presentation, imaging and histopathology analysis. Though metastasis from EES is not common, the metastatic work up should not be undermined in the evaluation of patients with EES. The Treatment of EES is based on the Ewing's Sarcoma treatment protocol with neoadjuvant chemotherapy, surgical excision and adjuvant chemotherapy and or radiation therapy based on the stage of the disease.
