Chronic mesenteric ischemia, renovascular hypertension and critical limb threatening ischemia treated with thoraco-visceral and lower extremity bypass: Major open surgical techniques will remain essential
Authors: Ioannis Tsouknidas, Panagiotis Volteas, Angela Kokkosis, Jonathan Price, Apostolos Tassiopoulos, David Landau
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
A 68-year-old female with extensive medical and surgical history, including open and endovascular procedures, presented with chronic limb-threatening ischemia, hypertension, and chronic mesenteric ischemia. A computed tomographic angiogram showed significant paravisceral aortic atherosclerosis involving the celiac, superior mesenteric, and renal arteries. She underwent an open descending aorta to superior mesenteric artery, left renal artery, and left common femoral artery bypass with a “trifurcated” ringed polytetrafluoroethylene graft. Nine months after surgery, she was progressing with significant improvement of her symptoms. Open surgical approaches in aortoiliac disease are reserved for patients with unfavorable anatomy or previous failed endovascular attempts. Detailed preoperative planning and careful patient selection are imperative for optimal outcomes.

Aortoiliac occlusive disease (AIOD) is the result of chronic atherosclerosis involving the aorta and the iliac arteries.1 Symptoms vary according to the level of stenosis and the vessels involved. Patients can present with intermittent claudication, rest pain, tissue loss, intestinal angina, hypertension, and impotence.2 Noninvasive studies, such as ankle-brachial index (ABI), pulse-volume recording, arterial duplex scanning, and computerized tomographic angiogram (CTA) are typically performed for diagnosis and surgical planning.3 Indications for surgical treatment include disabling claudication, rest pain, tissue loss, mesenteric angina, and renovascular hypertension. Open surgery has typically shown superior primary patency but with higher perioperative morbidity and mortality rates, which are lower with endovascular approaches in the majority of cases. Open surgical procedures are therefore undertaken in selective patients with unfavorable anatomy, complicated extensive disease not amenable for endovascular treatment, or unsuccessful previous endovascular attempts. Open surgical options include aorto-femoral bypass, aortoiliac endarterectomy, iliofemoral bypass, and extra-anatomic bypass.4 Extra-anatomic bypass is usually reserved for patients with multiple previous abdominal surgeries, intra-abdominal infection, history of radiation, or high-risk patients.3 Here, we present a challenging and unusual case of a patient with extensive AIOD that underwent open descending aorta to superior mesenteric artery (SMA), left renal artery, and left common femoral artery bypass.

The electronic medical records of the patient were reviewed retrospectively and were summarized. The patient provided consent for the surgical procedure and the publication.

Case report

Case presentation

A 68-year-old female presented to our clinic with complaints of a new left foot wound and short distance bilateral lower extremity claudication. She also endorsed decreased appetite, postprandial epigastric pain, and an unintentional >30 lb weight loss during the prior year. She had worsening kidney function associated with labile hypertension on four antihypertensive medications, including a diuretic. She had an extensive medical history of severe peripheral arterial disease, carotid artery disease, and chronic kidney disease, and she was a former heavy smoker. Her past surgical history included a left aorto-iliac stent graft and left-to-right femoral-femoral bypass, video assisted thoracoscopic surgery, left thoracotomy, left lower lobe resection for lung adenocarcinoma, and abdominal wall reconstruction for hernia repair. On exam, she had no palpable femoral, popliteal, or pedal pulses. She had two punctate wounds involving her left hallux that were painful to touch. ABI showed biphasic pedal waveforms bilaterally (left, 0.59; right, 0.48). Toe-brachial index was zero, with flat line waveform. A CTA demonstrated significant paravisceral aortic atherosclerosis with near occlusion of the orifices of the celiac artery, SMA, and both renal arteries due to densely calcified intra-aortic plaque (Fig 1, Fig 2, Fig 3; Supplementary Videos 1-3, online only). The inferior mesenteric artery was patent. Bilateral common iliac arteries were occluded. Bilateral internal iliac arteries were occluded at the orifice, with distal reconstitution on both sides. The distal aorto-iliac artery stent and femoral-femoral bypass were occluded. There was significant collateralization to both common femoral arteries, which were patent. A preoperative duplex showed heavily calcified suprarenal aorta with posterior shadowing, significant stenosis in the celiac artery and SMA (peak systolic velocity 728 cm/s), and post stenotic turbulence in the bilateral renal arteries, indicative of inflow disease. After significant discussion with the patient, she agreed to proceed with open surgical treatment. Operation

The patient was positioned in a modified right lateral decubitus position with the hips anteriorly rotated and secured. The left lung was selectively isolated during intubation. A left thoracotomy was performed at the seventh intercostal space. The lung was found to be densely adherent to the surrounding tissues. Extensive lysis of adhesions was required to free the lung and adequately expose the distal descending thoracic aorta. Once achieved, the thoracotomy incision was extended to a left paramedian laparotomy. The peritoneum was intentionally entered, and the diaphragm was divided circumferentially. A medial visceral rotation was then performed, exposing the visceral segment of the aorta, left renal artery, and proximal portions of the celiac artery and SMA. The left common femoral artery and the proximal portion of the femoral-femoral bypass were exposed through a separate oblique groin incision. Systematic heparin was administered.

While the exposure was being gained, a separate team prepared a trifurcated prosthetic graft on the back table by suturing a 7-mm polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) Gore ringed bypass graft end to side, just above the bifurcation point of a 16- × 8-mm bifurcated Gore-Tex PTFE graft (Gore) (Fig 4). Once completed, the distal thoracic aorta was clamped, and an end to side anastomosis was completed from the beveled common portion of the graft to the aorta with CV-5 suture (Gore-Tex). The aorta and graft limbs were flushed, and the descending thoracic aorta was unclamped. Then the SMA bypass anastomosis was performed followed by the left renal, using the 8-mm graft limbs (Fig 5). The ringed 7-mm limb was tunneled through the retroperitoneum and under the inguinal ligament to enter the groin incision. It was anastomosed in end-to-side fashion to the previous hood of the left-to-right femoral-femoral bypass. Strong palpable pulses were felt in the graft limbs and all three recipient vessels. Postoperative course

Postoperatively the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit. She was extubated successfully on postoperative day (POD) 1. She was weaned from all antihypertensive drips and was restarted on her home medications. She was downgraded to the stepdown unit on POD 3. Her inpatient course was somewhat prolonged due to persistent oxygen requirements secondary to atelectasis and a pleural effusion. A CTA performed for work-up of her respiratory status demonstrated patency of the bypass (Fig 6). Oxygen requirements were ultimately weaned, and the patient was discharged home on POD 10. At a 20-day postoperative outpatient visit, her appetite was significantly improved, and she was able to eat full meals. Her blood pressure was less labile. She was ambulating slowly, and her claudication had improved. All three bypasses were patent on duplex study. At her 4-month postoperative visit, she had a good appetite, was tolerating three standard meals per day, and had gained 5 lbs. Her blood pressure was well-controlled with three medications. Her left hallux wound had completely healed. ABIs showed significant improvement on the left side (right, 0.47; left, 1.07). At 9-month postoperative follow-up visit, she had good appetite and was able to walk for long distances. Discussion

Here, we describe the case of a patient with extensive AIOD that underwent open extra-anatomic bypass to alleviate critical limb threatening ischemia, chronic mesenteric ischemia, and renovascular hypertension. Although the WIfI score of 3 (1, 2, 0) predicted a low risk for amputation at 1 year5 and her hypertension could potentially have been managed medically, her mesenteric ischemia demanded treatment. An open surgical approach was chosen in our case due to previously failed endovascular treatments and an inability to overcome the nearly occluded visceral segment as it related to stenting the SMA or renals. Stenting the iliacs to treat her tissue loss would have been futile without addressing the nearly occluded aorta. It seemed most appropriate therefore to do what was done. We elected not to perform a visceral segment aortic endarterectomy for two primary reasons: first, we were concerned that the quality of the densely calcified aorta might lead to significant hemorrhage. Second, such a procedure would not have addressed the lower extremity ischemia without a simultaneous bypass to the leg, which would add significant intraoperative ischemia time, whereas it would be technically challenging to originate a bypass from the same level as the endarterectomy. A new femorofemoral bypass was not performed, because the patient had unilateral tissue loss. If the patient develops significant symptoms on the right side in the future, we would redo a femorofemoral bypass, understanding that it would be challenging, but no more complicated if it was done at the same time with the extra-anatomic bypass that we did. In our case, close monitoring and treatment by our multi-disciplinary team (physicians, nurses, physical therapists, respiratory therapists) were all instrumental in achieving a successful outcome.

Conclusion

In the era of widespread endovascular means to treat occlusive disease, open surgical approaches can still provide immediate, satisfying results in patients with aortoiliac and aorto-visceral atherosclerotic disease deemed inappropriate for endovascular approaches. Detailed preoperative planning, familiarity with the techniques, and careful selection to patients are imperative for good outcomes.
