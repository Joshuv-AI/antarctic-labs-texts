A promising application of kidney-specific cell-free DNA methylation markers in real-time monitoring sepsis-induced acute kidney injury
Authors: Ruilian You, Xiangming Quan, Peng Xia, Chao Zhang, Anlei Liu, Hanshu Liu, Ling Yang, Huadong Zhu, Limeng Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSepsis-induced acute kidney injury (SI-AKI) is a common clinical syndrome that is associated with high mortality and morbidity. Effective timely detection may improve the outcome of SI-AKI. Kidney-derived cell-free DNA (cfDNA) may provide new insight into understanding and identifying SI-AKI. Plasma cfDNA from 82 healthy individuals, 7 patients with sepsis non-acute kidney injury (SN-AKI), and 9 patients with SI-AKI was subjected to genomic methylation sequencing. We deconstructed the relative contribution of cfDNA from different cell types based on cell-specific methylation markers and focused on exploring the association between kidney-derived cfDNA and SI-AKI.Based on the deconvolution of the cfDNA methylome: SI-AKI patients displayed the elevated cfDNA concentrations with an increased contribution of kidney epithelial cells (kidney-Ep) DNA; kidney-Ep derived cfDNA achieved high accuracy in distinguishing SI-AKI from SN-AKI (AUC = 0.92, 95% CI 0.7801–1); the higher kidney-ep cfDNA concentrations tended to correlate with more advanced stages of SI-AKI; strikingly, SN-AKI patients with potential kidney damage unmet by SI-AKI criteria showed higher levels of kidney-Ep derived cfDNA than healthy individuals. The autonomous screening of kidney-Ep (n = 24) and kidney endothelial (kidney-Endo, n = 12) specific methylation markers indicated the unique identity of kidney-Ep/kidney-Endo compared with other cell types, and its targeted assessment reproduced the main findings of the deconvolution of the cfDNA methylome. Our study first demonstrates that kidney-Ep- and kidney-Endo-specific methylation markers can serve as a novel marker for SI-AKI emergence, supporting further exploration of the utility of kidney-specific cfDNA methylation markers in the study of SI-AKI.KEYWORDS: Sepsis-induced acute kidney injury, cell-free DNA, methylation

Introduction

Sepsis-induced acute kidney injury (SI-AKI) is a common clinical syndrome with an incidence of up to 60% in septic shock patients [1,2]. The prognosis for SI-AKI patients is worse than that for patients with either syndrome alone, accompanied by prolonged hospitalization, increased mortality, a greater rate of long-term disability, and decreased quality of life [1,3,4]. SI-AKI is characterized by a rapid decline in kidney function, injury to kidney tubular epithelial cells, and dysfunction of kidney microvascular endothelial cells together with multi-organ dysfunction syndromes [5]. However, the pathophysiology of SI-AKI has not been fully elucidated.

The current diagnosis of SI-AKI relies mainly on the serum creatinine concentration and urine volume, which may be considered as key limitations due to kidney damage without changes in function or injury not met by the SI-AKI criteria [6–9]. In the interim, general supportive care is the only accessible treatment for SI-AKI due to the heterogeneity of critically ill patients and their underlying conditions, making specialized medications unsuitable for this condition [10,11]. It has been suggested that combining damage and functional biomarkers will increase the sensitivity of SI-AKI stage, survival discrimination, and recovery assessment [12–14]. A real-time, specific, and highly sensitive biomarker that reflects kidney damage is still needed.

Circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA) is a fragment of DNA (typically 167 bp on average) released mainly from apoptotic or necrotic cells to blood, with a 15–60 min half-life in circulation [15,16]. Increased cfDNA levels have been reported in septic patients, which indicates excessive cell death in the immune system and affects solid tissues [17–19]. The size of cfDNA could be a crucial indicator associated with epigenetic activities and specific pathological processes [20,21]. Additionally, as in the case of kidney allograft injury, cfDNA biomarkers have displayed the potential to identify disease before clinical manifestations or histological alterations appear [22].

Liquid biopsies based on cell-specific methylation markers and cfDNA have displayed potential as a universal method for detecting organ damage under different physiological conditions [23,24]. Netanel Loyfer et al. recently reported a DNA methylation atlas of healthy human cell types for identifying genomic loci with methylated or unmethylated uniquely in specific cell types and developed methods based on the methylation patterns of such loci in plasma cfDNA, providing a more sensitive and specific indicator of the tissue origins of cfDNA in all individuals [25]. Moreover, the correlation between target organ-associated cell-derived cfDNA and the degree of target organ damage has been continuously demonstrated in lung epithelium-derived cfDNA for lung injury and vascular endothelium-derived cfDNA for sepsis [26,27]. As each organ tissue has a unique DNA methylation pattern, injured organ tissues can be traced by plasma cfDNA methylation patterns. Therefore, detecting plasma cfDNA in septic patients and tracing kidney-associated cell-derived cfDNA based on cell-specific methylation patterns might be useful for monitoring and assessing SI-AKI in real time.

Herein, we extracted plasma cfDNA from sepsis patients with or without SI-AKI and performed bisulfite-free method of genomic methylation sequencing (GM-seq) via the ten-eleven-translocation (TET) approach [28]. We deconstructed the contribution of cfDNA from different cell types based on the reference methylation atlas and evaluated the association between the proportion/concentration of kidney epithelial cells (kidney-Ep) derived cfDNA and SI-AKI. To meet the need for accurate assessment of SI-AKI, we further constructed specific methylation markers of kidney-Ep and kidney endothelial cells (kidney-Endo) for targeted assessment of these markers and explored whether these markers can be applied in SI-AKI [23]. We also explored the pathophysiology of SI-AKI through difference in the cfDNA methylome and fragmentome between the two groups.

Materials and methods

Patient inclusion and sample collection

We recruited participants into three groups: healthy individuals and patients with SI-AKI and sepsis-non acute kidney injury (SN-AKI). The inclusion criteria for the patients were as follows: 1) willing to participate in the study; 2) aged between 18 and 80 years; 3) diagnosed with sepsis according to the sepsis 3.0 [29], while the definition and staging classification of AKI were established according to the KDIGO criteria [6]. The exclusion criteria were patients who were expected to die within 24 hours, pregnant, or lactating females. Patients older than 60 years were classified as the aged group, and patients aged between 18 and 60 years were classified as the adult group. SI-AKI was defined as meeting the sepsis and AKI criteria but excluding injury that primarily developed as the indirect consequence of sepsis or sepsis therapies (for example, AKI caused by antimicrobial agent-induced nephrotoxicity or abdominal compartment syndrome) [30–32]. Patients with sepsis but without AKI were classified as having SN-AKI. Healthy individuals were defined as those without a history of any disease within one year.

Sample collection and DNA extraction

10 ml peripheral blood samples were collected and placed in Streck® cell-free DNA blood collection tubes at 6–37°C for up to 5 days before plasma separation. Then, the blood samples were centrifuged at 1608 × g for 10 minutes to separate the plasma from the peripheral blood cells, followed by centrifugation at 1608 × g for 10 minutes to pellet any remaining cells. According to the manufacturer’s instructions, cfDNA was isolated from 4 mL plasma aliquots using the QIAamp Circulating Nucleic Acid Kit (Qiagen, Germany), and the DNA concentration was quantified using the Qubit® dsDNA HS (High Sensitivity) and Qubit dsDNA BR (Broad Range) Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher, USA). Then, the cfDNA was washed in 50 μl of AE buffer and stored at −80°C.

Construction of gm-seq libraries

We adapted a modified approach called GM-seq (Additional file 2: Figure S1A) [28]. Based on GM-seq method, we utilized the Hieff NGS® Ultima Pro DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Yeason, cat. on. 12201ES96) for cfDNA end repair, A-tailing, and adaptor ligation. Following the purification of magnetic beads, 5-methylcytosine (5mC) and 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC) were converted by EM conversion module(TET2) and proteinase K (New England Biolabs, USA) to 5-acylcytosine (5fC) or 5-carboxyl cytosine (5caC), respectively, which were subsequently reduced to dihydrouracil (DHU) by pyridine borane (Alfa Aesar, USA). The obtained sequencing library was further sequenced through barcode and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification. Each sample was sequenced in PE100 mode and 200 G (62.2× mean depth, range: 54.2–86.7×) by DNBSEQ-T7(MGI, China).

Moreover, to assess the successful conversion rate of TET oxidation enzymes in specifically oxidizing 5mC and 5hmC to 5caC, 0.5% (by mass) of positive controls (synthetically methylated DNA fragments) and 1% (by mass) of negative controls (synthetically unmethylated DNA fragments) were added to the conversion system. The conversion rate of 5mC was 95.36% with a false-positive rate (conversion rate of unmodified C) of 0.35% in this study (Additional file 2: Figure S1B)

Gm-seq data mapping and preprocessing

We filtered the raw reads and analyzed them by fastp software, and then filtered them by Sentieon pipeline to obtain clean reads. The clean reads were aligned with the reference genome (hg19), sorted and tagged with repetitive sequences to obtain the comparison result BAM file [28]. The alignment results of the reference human genome, negative control, and positive control from bam were obtained by SAMtools software and quality control analysis of the alignment information of bam was performed. AsTair software was used to detect methylation sites and obtain methylation signal-related data [33]. The libraries exhibited a high mapping rate and unique deduplicated mapping rate of 94.05% and 86.58%, respectively (Additional file 2: Figure S1C).

Fragmentomic analysis

We sorted the bam files using SAMtools (version 1.3.1) and converted them to bed format to extract the length and coordinate information of sequencing fragments at the whole-genome level. Fragments longer than 10 kb or read pairs with both reads aligned with a mapping quality (MAPQ) less than 30 were removed from the sequencing data. The remaining fragments were calculated for their distribution in different length intervals. In addition, the hg19 genome was divided into windows 1 Mb in length, and the blacklisted regions were filtered out from the sequences, and the proportion of short cfDNA fragments (between 30 bp and 167 bp) in all windows across the entire genome was calculated.

Tissue deconvolution of cfDNA based on reference methylomes

The contribution of each cell type to the cfDNA concentration was calculated via the UXM fragment-level deconvolution algorithm with default parameters, based on a reference methylation atlas that assigns unmethylated fragments’ percentage to each marked region (a total of 978 regions) for 40 cell types, thereby estimating the relative contributions of each cell type (Additional file 2: Figure S2) [25]. The relative contribution of each cell type to the 40 cell types was outputted and summed to 1.

Targeted assessment of kidney epithelial and endothelial methylomic markers

Building upon the original dataset (GEO accession No. GSE186458) from Netanel Loyfer et al., we conducted additional targeted assessment of kidney-Ep and kidney-Endo specific methylomic markers [25]. The 5 kidney-associated cell types were divided into 2 groups: kidney-Ep (composed of tubular epithelial cells, glomerular epithelial cells and glomerular podocytes) and kidney-Endo (composed of tubular endothelial cells and glomerular endothelial cells). Methylation markers with cell specificity were subsequently calculated by using the wgbstools (version 0.2.2) with default parameters and subsequently screened with the criterion that the methylation level of differentially methylated region (DMR) was less than 0.3 in target cells and greater than 0.6 in other cells [34]. In the end, 24 and 12 cell-specific markers were selected for kidney-Ep and kidney-Endo, respectively. Unsupervised agglomerative hierarchical clustering of kidney-Ep and kidney-Endo samples and additional 38 other cell types was performed using the R package factoextra based on the methylation level of these markers. The Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) dataset was used to show the expression patterns of genes that reside immediately adjacent to these markers [35]. Nevertheless, we encountered challenges in obtaining cell-specific markers solely for glomerular/tubular epithelial cells and glomerular pedunculated cells, as their methylation patterns exhibited high similarity.

Then, we used GM-seq data to extract inclusion region metrics pertaining to these markers, thereby emulating targeted analysis. We amalgamated all methylation markers unique to kidney-Ep/Endo into a single comprehensive region. Subsequently, within this designated region, we quantified the fragments exhibiting kidney-Ep/Endo specific methylation patterns, then divided them by the total number of fragments considered as the fraction of kidney-Ep/Endo in total cfDNA, respectively. We further multiplied the fraction by the total concentration of cfDNA and defined the result as the concentration of cfDNA derived from the kidney-Ep or kindey-Endo.

DMR analysis

De novo differential methylation analysis of the sequencing data was performed using metilene [36]. The cyclic binary segmentation algorithm was used to identify all regions with large differences in average methylation levels, followed by two-dimensional Kolmogorov‒Smirnov tests to determine the significance of the differences. DMRs were annotated with gene information using the R package Genomation [37]. In the analysis of DMRs between groups, only data with a CpG coverage depth greater than 5 were included for further analysis. The filtering criteria for DMRs required an adjusted p- value less than 0.05 for significance and a methylation-level difference threshold of 10%, with at least 3 CpG sites per DMR. Additionally, DMRs located on sex chromosomes were excluded to reduce the effect of gender. We subsequently conducted Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses of the genes with DMRs via ShinyGO [38].

Statistical analysis

The data are presented as percentages, means ± standard deviations (for normally distributed data), or medians and quartiles (for skewed data). The values contained in () mentioned in the main text are medians. To compare differences between groups, a t-test was used for continuous variables, while the Mann‒Whitney U test was used for skewed data. For variables that do not have a bivariate normal distribution, Spearman correlation analysis will be used. The R package compareGroups was used to organize data into tables [39]. The R package FactoMineR and factoextra were used for principal component analysis of DMRs [40]. The row and column clustering on the heatmap of the DMRs data was based on the complete linkage algorithm in hierarchical agglomerative clustering, using Euclidean distance as the distance measure between data points. p < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

Higher plasma cfDNA levels in SI-AKI patients

We included 9 SI-AKI patients, 7 SN-AKI patients, and 82 healthy controls. There were no significant differences in gender composition or age between the sepsis patients with or without AKI (Additional file 1: Table S1). Compared with that in healthy controls, the plasma cfDNA concentration in septic patients was significantly higher, with the highest level of cfDNA occurring in SI-AKI patients (Figure 1a). When grouped and compared based on the compounder factors, the concentration of cfDNA was increased in septic patients with coagulation dysfunction (p = 0.022), but there was no significant difference in the grouped by different age, blood pressure, or septic shock (Figure 1b-e). Furthermore, there was no significant correlation between cfDNA concentration and the sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) score (Figure 1f).

Modified human cfDNA fragmentation pattern in septic patients

The GM-seq technique selectively induces base changes only at modified cytosines while preserving the majority of the DNA, enabling a comprehensive investigation of cfDNA fragmentation [41]. We first divided the human genome (hg19) into windows 1 Mb in length and calculated the ratio of short cfDNA fragments (lengths between 30 bp and 167 bp) in each group. We first observed the compatibility of the cfDNA fragmentation patterns generated by GM-seq and whole-genome sequencing, which revealed a peak fragment size of 167 base pairs (bp) and numerous short fragments with lengths ranging from 30 to 167 bp (Figure 2a). The septic patients had considerably more 30 to 167 bp short fragments compared with healthy controls (mainly 147 to 167 bp in length) (Figure 2b), which is consistent with the findings of previously published study [19].

The ratio of short cfDNA fragments was significantly higher in SI-AKI, mainly contributed by fragments (30-147 bp) without significant difference between SN-AKI and healthy controls (Figure 2c). Meanwhile, there was no significant difference in the ratio of short cfDNA fragment grouped by septic shock status, hypertension status, or SOFA score (Additional file 2: Figure S3A-C). However, we observed a significant difference in the ratio of short cfDNA fragments (mainly contributed by 30–147 bp) between groups with or without coagulation dysfunction. Furthermore, there was a positive correlation with prothrombin time (PT) (R = 0.56, p = 0.027) but not with activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) or fibrinogen (Fbg) (Figure 2d, Additional file 2: Figure S3D).

Cell type origins of cfDNA based on reference methylomes in SI-AKI patients

To determine the contribution of cfDNA from different cell types, we applied the UXM fragment-level deconvolution algorithm to infer the relative contribution of each cell type based on a reference atlas with the methylomes of 40 major cell types [25]. The results showed that blood cells (including granulocytes, megakaryocytes, and monocyte-macrophages) accounted for the vast majority of the total DNA extracted from cfDNA, while solid organ-associated cell types accounted for a smaller proportion (excluding hepatocytes) (Figure 3, Additional file 1: Table S2). The relative contributions of megakaryocytes and Mono+Macro cells showed a significant gradual decreasing trend from healthy controls to patients with SN-AKI to patients with SI-AKI (Figure 3, Additional file 1: Table S2). The relative contribution of hepatocyte-derived cfDNA was significantly higher in SI-AKI patients (6.99% [6.23%; 10.3%]) than in healthy controls (3.62% [2.49%; 5.37%], p = 0.0034) or patients with SN-AKI (2.65% [1.52%; 5.03%], p = 0.042), which coincides with the higher percentage of concurrent liver injury in SI-AKI patients than that in SN-AKI patients (55.6% vs 14.3%, Figure 3, Additional file 1: Table S1-S2).

Kidney epithelial-derived cfDNA independently reflects the progression of SI-AKI

Then, we explored the association between kidney-Ep cfDNA and SI-AKI incidence. The relative contribution of kidney-Ep cfDNA was significantly higher in SI-AKI (1.34% [1.16%; 1.58%]) than in SN-AKI (0.83% [0.70%; 0.98%], p = 0.0033) and healthy controls (0.84% [0.49%; 1.06%], p = 0.00042) (Figure 4a, Additional file 1: Table S2) and was significantly positively correlated with the cfDNA concentration (R = 0.87, p < 2.2e-16) (Figure 4b). Then, we multiplied the relative contribution by the total concentration of cfDNA and defined the result as the concentration of cfDNA derived from kidney-Ep. The concentration of kidney-Ep cfDNA was more than ten times greater in SI-AKI (0.368 [0.128;1.002] ng/µl) patients than healthy controls (0.004 [0.002;0.007] ng/µl) and SN-AKI (0.009 [0.006;0.027] ng/µl), accompanying with a significant difference between SN-AKI and healthy controls (p = 0.024) (Figure 4c). According to the receiver operating characteristic curve, the concentration of kidney-Ep cfDNA achieved high accuracy in distinguishing SI-AKI from SN-AKI, with an AUC of 0.9206 (95% CI 0.7801–1), as well as the total cfDNA concentration (AUC = 0.8889, 95% CI 0.7134–1; Figure 4d). More severe SI-AKI stages tended to accompany higher kidney-Ep cfDNA concentration at the same time of diagnosis (Figure 4e).

Targeted assessment of kidney epithelial and endothelial methylomic markers

We used the dataset provided by Netanel Loyfer et al. and further constructed specific methylation markers of kidney-Ep and kidney-Endo for targeted assessment to develop direct identification of DNA methylation-based liquid biopsies [23,25]. We selected 24 and 12 cell type-specific markers that were uniquely unmethylated in kidney-Ep and kidney-Endo cells, respectively, compared with other cell types (Figure 6a; additional details are shown in Additional file 1: Table S3, Table S4 and Additional file 2: Figure S4). We performed unsupervised hierarchical clustering on the methylomes of these markers and discovered that samples from each of the two cell types were densely clustered within themselves (Additional file 2: Figure S5). Some genes that were next to loci unmethylated in the kidney were highly expressed in the kidney and a few other cell types (e.g., SCIN, expressed in the kidney, small intestine, esophagus, and salivary gland); however, others were expressed in both the kidney and multiple tissues (Additional file 2: Figure S6).

The fraction of molecules from these markers that contained the kidney-Ep/Endo specific methylation patterns was subsequently calculated. Although there were no significant differences in the fraction of kidney-Ep cfDNA between any two groups (Figure 6b), the fraction of kidney-Ep cfDNA was correlated with the total cfDNA concentration in septic patients (R = 0.58, p = 0.024; Figure 6c). The concentration of kidney-Ep cfDNA was calculated by multiplying its fraction by the concentration of total cfDNA; the concentration was significantly higher in SI-AKI (0.599 [0.288;1.550] ng/µl) than in SN-AKI (0.026 [0.017;0.076] ng/µl) or in healthy controls (0.014 [0.010;0.019] ng/µl) (Figure 6d).

Moreover, the fraction of kidney-Endo cfDNA showed no significant difference between SI-AKI and SN-AKI, and was not correlated to the concentration of total cfDNA (Figure 6e-f). However, SI-AKI displayed the higher concentration of kidney-Endo cfDNA than that in healthy controls or SN-AKI (Figure 6g). Notably, the fraction of kidney-Endo cfDNA was positively correlated with the fraction of kidney-Ep cfDNA in septic patients (R = 0.71, p = 0.0019, Figure 6h).

Furthermore, the concentrations of kidney-Ep cfDNA, kidney-Endo cfDNA, and kidney cfDNA (kidney-Ep + kidney-Endo) were able to distinguish SI-AKI from SN-AKI (receiver operating characteristic [ROC] curve, AUC = 0.9048, 0.9365, 0.9206, respectively), as well as total cfDNA (AUC = 0.8889, 95% CI 0.7134–1) (Figure 6i). There were no correlations between the kidney-Ep/kidney-Endo cfDNA and the sepsis-related serum creatinine level, urine volume, incidence of septic shock, hypertension, or age, but only for coagulation disorders (Additional file 2: Figure S7A-C).

In addition, for kidney-Ep cfDNA, we further explored the differences between the two algorithms in our study. The number of markers screened was different (UXM fragment-level deconvolution (n = 25), targeted assessment approach (n = 24) for kidney-Ep cfDNA, and shared with the other seven markers (Additional file 2: Figure S8A). The values calculated by the targeted assessment approach were significantly higher (2.58-fold times, median) than those via UXM fragment-level deconvolution (Additional file 2: Figure S8B), which is consistent with previous report [27].

To explore the potential crucial pathways involved in SI-AKI, we analyzed the overall methylated proportion of all the CpG sites. There was no difference between SI-AKI and SN-AKI (Figure 7a), with a total of 1962 DMRs, involving 249 hypermethylated DMRs (hyperDMR) and 1323 hypomethylated DMRs (hypoDMR), mainly on chromosomes 1, 16, 17, and 19 (Figure 7b, Additional file 1: Table S5). Then, based on the methylation rate of DMRs in each sample, unsupervised clustering analysis confirmed that the results were consistent with the actual grouping, which was consistent with the results of principal component analysis (Figure 7c-d).

Discussion

Identification of effective biomarkers to assess the severity, duration, and trajectory of individual SI-AKI cases more quickly and accurately is an urgent and unmet clinical need [42]. In this study, we first validate that kidney-derived cfDNA is a promising marker in identifying and monitoring SI-AKI, and potentially reflects kidney damage without changes in function and injury unmet by the SI-AKI criteria.

Our study showed that the increase in cfDNA concentration with an increase in solid organ-associated cell-derived cfDNA in septic patients is generally consistent with the presence of multiple organ injuries, resulting in increased cfDNA being released into the bloodstream. These findings were supported by a previous study in which a higher cfDNA concentration was found to be an indicator of excessive cell death [19]. However, we did not find a correlation between high SOFA score, septic shock, or high cfDNA concentration, which may be caused by the small sample size of this study [18,19]. A further advanced search is needed for additional validation since the observed trends were not statistically significant.

An increased inflammatory response and abnormal coagulation function are observed during sepsis, and these factors are crucial in the ensuing microthrombosis that results in renal injury [43,44]. We found that in patients with sepsis, coagulation dysfunction was associated with higher cfDNA concentrations and a greater proportion of short cfDNA fragments. In particular, it should be noted that the longer the prothrombin time, the higher the proportion of short cfDNA fragments (30–147 bp). Short cfDNA fragments are considered indicators of specific pathological processes, such as the oxidative stress induced by hypotension [21,45–47]. An important observation from our study was that sepsis (mainly contributed by 147-167 bp) and coagulation dysfunction (mainly contributed by 30–147 bp) differed in the distribution of differential short cfDNA fragments but not as the name as the result between SI-AKI and coagulation dysfunction, which may be caused by the co-occurrence of SI-AKI in most patients with coagulation dysfunction (5/6, 83.3%). We have reason to assume that the onset of sepsis causes an increase in the proportion of short cfDNA fragments (mainly 147-167 bp), and the oxidative stress associated with the onset of coagulation dysfunction further exacerbates the breakdown of cfDNA (mainly 30–147 bp).

Our study first validated the association between kidney-derived cfDNA and SI-AKI incidence. The UXM fragment-level deconvolution algorithm based on a reference methylation atlas was used to decipher the cellular origins of cfDNA in this study [25]. We found that the relative contribution of kidney-ep cfDNA was higher in SI-AKI than in SN-AKI and in healthy controls and was positively correlated with the cfDNA concentration, indicating that one of the main causes of the increase in total cfDNA concentration during SI-AKI is the massive release of kidney-ep DNA into the bloodstream. To minimize the effect of heterogeneity toward septic patients, we defined the relative contribution multiplied by the total cfDNA concentration obtained as the absolute concentration of kidney-ep cfDNA, which was specifically elevated in SI-AKI and achieved high accuracy in distinguishing SI-AKI from SN-AKI, and found the concentration of kidney-ep cfDNA tend to increase with more severe stages of SI-AKI. Notably, the concentration of kidney-ep cfDNA was significantly higher in SN-AKI than in healthy controls, suggesting that kidney damage occurring in septic patients (kidney damage without change in function or injury unmet by SI-AKI criteria) is expected to be detected via kidney-ep cfDNA. Furthermore, after excluding the influence of confounding factors, our results showed that coagulation disorder alone can cause elevated kidney-Ep cfDNA concentrations except SI-AKI, which may due to the co-occurrence of SI-AKI in most patients coagulation dysfunction in our study. Given that hypoDMR genes were enriched in the pathway of complement activation and coagulation cascades in our study and that coagulation indicators have shown to predict the occurrence of SI-AKI, we tentatively believe that the occurrence of coagulopathy does not interfere with the detection of SI-AKI by kidney-Ep cfDNA, which needs to be confirmed by further studies [48]. Therefore, it is reasonable to assume that kidney-Ep cfDNA can serve as a promising biomarker for SI-AKI assessment and is potentially associated with the degree of kidney damage in real time.

Since kidney-Endo is involved in kidney injury and adaptive repair, to meet the needs of accurate assessment of SI-AKI, we further constructed the specific methylation markers for kidney-Ep and kidney-Endo based on the data of the reference atlas [5,25,49]. The kidney-Ep and kidney-Endo markers we selected each had a locus that displayed a kidney-specific methylation pattern, and these loci were methylated elsewhere and unmethylated in kidney-Ep/kidney-Endo cells, and unsupervised clustering of its methylomes grouped together the methylomes of kidney-Ep/kidney-Endo from the same cell types, suggesting a unique identity of kidney-Ep/kidney-Endo compared with other cell types. We also observed that the expression of most genes next to these markers was not restricted to the kidney, which may be caused by the majority of these markers being located in introns or intergenic regions. Given the complicated relationship between DNA methylation and gene expression, further studies are needed to explore the significance of these markers for the kidney [50,51].

Compared with deconvolution based on the cfDNA methylome, targeted assessment of kidney-Ep and kidney-Endo specific methylomic markers allows for extreme depth analysis to reduce costs and can also be turned into a streamlined qPCR procedure that does not require sequencing, making it more suitable for clinical promotion [23,52,53]. The association between kidney-Ep cfDNA and SI-AKI detected by deconvolution based on the cfDNA methylome essentially reproduced by the targeted assessment of kidney-Ep and kidney-Endo specific methylomic markers. Interestingly, there was a significant positive correlation between kidney-Ep methylomic markers and kidney-Endo methylomic markers, indicating the emergence of SI-AKI with increased turnover/death events in both cell types, which is consistent with the currently proposed mechanism for the emergence of SI-AKI involving kidney-Ep and kidney-Endo. Given that the combination of markers from multiple relevant cell types could improve the accuracy of detection, the concentrations of kidney-derived cfDNA supplemented with the sum of kidney-Ep and kidney-Endo cfDNA, which also accurately separated SI-AKI from SN-AKI in our study, provided a window for a comprehensive understanding of the underlying dynamics of kidney injury [26]. Since kidney-Ep and kidney-Endo damage is an early event in SI-AKI, we propose to monitor the kidney-Ep and kidney-Endo cfDNA methylomic biomarker to aid in early diagnosis.

However, with respect to creatinine and urine volume, we could not find any correlation between kidney-Ep/kidney-Endo cfDNA and these parameters, possibly because of the small sample size. On the other hand, creatinine and urine volume are considered as kidney functional indicators, and differ from kidney-Ep/kidney-Endo cfDNA, which is potentially representative of kidney damage. What’s more, precise damage assessment, recovery monitoring, and early identification of SI-AKI can be considerably improved by the quantitative characterization of kidney-Ep and kidney-Endo [13,42]. Combined with rapid increase and decrease in the serum creatinine concentration and urine volume and the necessity of timely monitoring, it is reasonable to assume that kidney-associated cell-derived cfDNA has potential for identifying and monitoring SI-AKI. More research is undoubtedly required to quantify the association between changes in kidney function and kidney-Ep/kidney-Endo cfDNA.

The liver and kidneys, frequently implicated in dysfunction among sepsis patients, pose an unresolved question regarding the extent to influence the outcomes of this investigation. The level of circulating cfDNA, determined by a balance between DNA release and DNA clearance processes, currently, there are at least three potential principal mechanisms for cfDNA clearance, hepatic and splenic reticuloendothelial uptake, kidney filtration, and direct enzymatic breakdown by nucleases [54]. Animal studies have illuminated that the liver shoulders the lion’s share (approximately 70–90%) of cfDNA clearance responsibilities, overshadowing the kidney system’s contribution, which seems relatively minor compared to other clearance modalities [54,55]. Therefore, cfDNA is still a reliable and timely biomarker for kidney damage [55]. When liver or kidney disorder or with acute damage, which might significantly perturb the dynamics of cfDNA generation, breakdown, and elimination, consequently altering the cfDNA concentration and profile. Given the incomplete understanding of the quantitative link between the severity of hepatic and kidney dysfunction and cfDNA clearance efficacy, further scientific inquiry is imperative. Notably, considering the substantial contribution of liver-derived cfDNA in total cfDNA, concomitant severe liver damage in SI-AKI patients can potentially lead to a surge in liver-derived cfDNA, thereby diminishing the relative contribution and possibly rendering the kidney-specific cfDNA undetectable via the deconvolution of the cfDNA methylome. Our study has devised a method for targeted assessment of kidney-Ep and kidney – Endo specific methylomic markers, which not only mitigates the interference from disproportionate increases in non-kidney cfDNA due to extensive tissue damage but also heightens the sensitivity for detecting kideny cfDNA signatures while economizing on detection costs. Nonetheless, we acknowledge the necessity for expanded research in these domains to solidify our findings and enhance their applicability.

Our study has several limitations. First, the relatively low sample size of septic patients and the limited cell type samples of publicly available genome-wide methylation data (reference methylation atlas), which do not fully cover all organ tissues and cells of the human body. Second, the mechanism for increased tissue-specific cfDNA remains incompletely understood; it may be due to a disturbance of the local clearance of debris from dying cells, an accelerated rate of cell death in the tissue of origin, or an enhanced turnover rate. Regardless, there seems to be a strong correlation between elevated kidney-derived cfDNA and clinical SI-AKI. Third, kidney-specific (kidney-Ep and kidney-Endo) methylomic markers were not tested in a panel for similar clinical applications. Admittedly, to optimize specificity and sensitivity and to comprehend the association between cfDNA dynamics and clinical phenotypic prediction, additional research will be necessary before kidney-specific cfDNA biomarkers are clinically applied for SI-AKI patients.

Conclusions

Our findings provide insight into how kidney-derived cfDNA can identify SI-AKI and preliminarily display the feasibility of performing targeted assessment of kidney epithelial and endothelial specific methylation markers to develop DNA methylation-based liquid biopsies for further clinical SI-AKI decision-making.
