Development and characterization of starch films functionalized by Oxalis triangularis anthocyanins and Chenpi essential oil Pickering emulsion for beef preservation
Authors: Gang Liu, Tingting Liu, Changxing Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this work, we prepared an antibacterial film using potato starch (PS) incorporated with Oxalis triangularis anthocyanin (OA) and Chenpi essential oil Pickering emulsion (PE) stabilized by PS and nano titanium oxide (NT) and applied them for preservation of beef. The PE-PS-NT100 emulsion showed excellent stability with a 209.6 nm particle size and a 43.84 mV zeta potential and was used for film development. The introduction of OA and PE in active films significantly reduced light transmittance, 35.52% and 15.39% increase in elongation at break and tensile strength, respectively; 54.74% and 22.43% reduction in water permeability and absorption capacity, respectively; improved thermal stability and hydrophobicity. The composite film with 0.9 mL of CEO (PS@OA@PE0.9) showed significant antimicrobial activity against E. coli and S. aureus, could prolong the shelf life of beef by up to 12 days, and hence was promising for food preservation.Keywords: Beef preservation, Chenpi essential oil, Oxalis triangularis, Pickering emulsion, TiO2 nanoparticle, Antibacterial film

Introduction

Beef, as one of important food sources, is valued for its delicious taste and rich nutrients such as vitamins, amino acids and high-quality proteins, which are essential to the human body (Ma, Chen, et al., 2025). However, beef is susceptible to physicochemical oxidation and microbial contamination when stored and distributed not well. Petroleum-based materials (e.g., polyethylene and polypropylene) have been extensively applied in food packaging because of good mechanical strength, gas-selective barrier property and moisture resistance. However, these packaging materials are not biodegradable and can release harmful microplastics. Recently, natural polysaccharide–based materials including chitosan, starch, pectin and its derivatives have obtained considerable attention because of outstanding biocompatibility, water solubility and film-forming ability. Potato (Solanum tuberosum L.), as one of the most important food crops, plays a central role in global food security because of its easy cultivation and high nutritional value. Potato starch (PS) shows tremendous promise in film fabrication because of good gel-forming ability, strong barrier property, low gelatinization temperature and cost-effectiveness (Wu, Zhu, Zhang, et al., 2025). However, PS film exhibits inherent deficiencies: poor barrier property and insufficient mechanical strength. Nowadays, natural active ingredients have been added into packaging films to enhance mechanical properties and prolong food shelf life. Anthocyanins, a large subgroup of polyphenols, can enhance the mechanical performance and barrier property of films via matrix hydrogen bonding and prolong food shelf life due to their potent antimicrobial and antioxidant activities (Jiang et al., 2023). O. triangularis, belonging to Oxalidaceae family, exhibits remarkable reproductive capacity and vigorous growth in humid and low regions. The leaves of O. triangularis taste sour and exotic and have intensely purple color with high content of anthocyanins. O. triangularis anthocyanins have been proven having strong antioxidant ability and showing great potential for active packaging development (Jiang et al., 2023). Titanium dioxide (TiO2), one kind of metal oxide nanoparticles, showes many advantages including high stability, low toxicity, ultraviolet absorption, low cost, antibacterial activity, and biocompatibility and has been used to developing composite films with high performances (Zhang & Rhim, 2022). Chenpi, is the dried pericarp of mature fruit from Citrus reticulata and its cultivated varieties. It is a well-known medicinal herb with wide range of pharmacological activities such as alleviation of cough and phlegm, regulating of digestion and therapeutic benefit for cardiovascular disease. The Chenpi essential oil (CEO), mainly composed of terpenes and terpenes, shows a broad spectrum of antimicrobial activity and has garnered increasing interest in antimicrobial packaging film fabrication (Zhao,Yan, et al., 2022). However, Introduction of CEO into films simply is often unsatisfactory because CEO is not adequately stabilized and prone to phase separation from the film matrices. It has been demonstrated that Pickering emulsion encapsulation technique can improve the bioactivity and bioactivity of essential oils and Pickering emulsion-formulated essential oils can also improve the film performances. However, according to our knowledge, there is no study on the synergistic improvement of the properties of PS film by OA and CEO Pickering emulsion.

In this study, CEO Pickering emulsion (PE) was firstly prepared using PS and nano TiO2 and their stability was examined by measuring the particle size and zeta potential. The active composite films including PS, PS@NT, PS@NT@OA and PS@OA@PE was then developed through blending PS with NT, OA and PE. The film structure was initially characterized using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and X-ray diffraction spectra (XRD) and its physical properties were then characterized by testing water absorption ability, mechanical property, water vapor permeability, water wettability, light transmittance and thermal stability. After determining the antibacterial capacity, the films were applied to beef preservation, and their effectiveness in preserving beef was assessed by determining pH and total volatile basic nitrogen (TVB-N). Our research furnished theoretical understandings for application of the PS@OA@PE films in beef preservation.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

The O. triangularis leaves were picked in a park in Huaian, Jiangsu Province, China and the anthocyanins (OA) extraction solution (12.48 ± 0.94 mg/g) were prepared using our previous reported method (Jiang et al., 2023). Chenpi essential oil (CEO) (85% purity) was obtained from Jiayun Plant Fragrance Oil Co. Ltd. (Nanchang, China). Potato starch (PS) (98% starch and 0.25% ash) was provided by Tianjin Honglu Food Co. Ltd. (Tianjin, China). Nano titanium oxide (NT) (purity >99%, particle size about 20–30 nm) was purchased from Hubei Institute of material protection (Wuhan, China). Fresh beef was purchased from a local market (Huaian, China). All other chemical reagents were of analytical grade.

Preparation and characterization of CEO Pickering emulsion (PE)

The NT stabilized Pickering emulsion (PE-NT100) was prepared by mixing 100 mg of NT, 0.9 mL of CEO and 9.1 mL of distilled water and homogenized at 30000 rpm for 5 min at 20 °C using an HR-6B homogenizer (Huxi Industrial Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The PS stabilized emulsion (PE-PS-NT0) was prepared by homogenizing 10 mL of the mixture containing 0.9 mL of CEO and 5 mL of gelatinized starch solution (prepared using 500 mg PS) under the same conditions. The PS and NT stabilized Pickering emulsions (PE-PS-NT50 and PE-PS-NT100) were prepared by homogenizing 10 mL of the mixture containing 0.9 mL of CEO, 5 mL of gelatinized starch solution (prepared using 500 mg PS), and different amounts of NT (50 mg and 100 mg). The zeta potential and particle size of emulsions were determined by a particle size analyzer (Nano Brook, 90Plus Brookhaven Instruments, USA). Samples were 100-fold diluted with distilled water and assessed at 25 °C (Zou, et al., 2025b).

Composite film fabrication

Films were developed according to Jiang et al. (2023) with some adjustments. 4.5 g of PS was added to 150 mL of deionized water and gelatinized at 90 °C for 0.5 h to afford PS solution. Glycerol (2 g) was incorporated to the PS solution and stirred at 1000 rpm for 5 min to generate film-forming solution of PS film. When the solution was cooled below 50 °C, 10 mL of OA and PE containing 500 mg PS, 100 mg of NT and different amounts of CEO (0.3, 0.6 and 0.9 mL) were added to the film-forming solution and stirred at the same conditions. The film-forming solution was poured onto an acrylic plate (24 cm × 24 cm × 5 cm) and completely dried at 25 °C, affording active films. Depending on the contents of NT, OA and PE added, the resulting films were designated as PS (without NT, OA or CEO), PS@NT (with 100 mg of NT), PS@NT@OA (with 100 mg of NT and 10 mL of OA), PS@OA@PE0.3 (with 10 mL of OA and PE containing 0.3 mL of CEO), PS@OA@PE0.6 (with 10 mL of OA and PE containing 0.6 mL of CEO), and PS@OA@PE0.9 (with 10 mL of OA and PE containing 0.9 mL of CEO). Before testing, these films were preconditioned for 48 h at 4 °C in a ziplock bag.

Structural characteristics of active composite films

The surface (150 ×) and cross-section (1500 ×) of the films were recorded via a scanning electron microscope (Phenom Pro, Phenom World, Netherlands). Before microscopic imaging, film samples were subjected to gold sputter coating at a 10 kV accelerating voltage. The functional groups of films were identified via an FT-IR spectrophotometer (Nicolet-Model 5700 FT-IR Spectrometer). The scan was carried out with 32 scanning periods from 4000 to 400 cm−1. The crystallinity characteristic of film samples was assessed in the range of 5°–80° (2θ) at 5°/min using an X-ray diffractometer (D8, Bruker Co., Ltd., Germany) (Jiang et al., 2023).

Water absorption capacity (WAC)

WAC of the films was determined according to the methodology of Maciel, Assis, Rios, and Pertuzatti (2025) with some modifications. 2 g pieces of film samples were dried at 50 °C for 4 h in a drying oven. Following removing residual moisture, the film was soaked in 10 mL of purified water for 2 h, then removed and surface-dried with filter paper. The WAC was computed using the mass changes of samples before and after water immersion.

Transmittance of composite films

A Shimadzu, UV-3600 spectrophotometer (Tokyo, Japan) was applied to assess the transparency and ultraviolet shielding ability of composite films. Films (1 cm × 3 cm) were vertically placed in a quartz cuvette, with scanning performed at 200–700 nm wavelengths (Zou, Wang, et al., 2025).

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

TGA characterization of the films was carried out on a TG 209 thermogravimetric analyzer (Netzsch, Germany). Approximately 5-mg film sample was heated at 10 °C/min (20–700 °C) under 20 mL/min nitrogen flow (Zou, Wang, et al., 2025).

Antimicrobial activity

Aantimicrobial efficacy was assessed based on a previously reported method with slight revisions (Ni et al., 2025). Each bacterial strain (E. coli and S. aureus) was inoculated (1 mL into 9 mL LB medium) and incubated at 37 °C for 8–12 h to exponential phase, respectively. The suspension was serially diluted in LB medium to ∼107 CFU/mL. 30 mg of film was blended with 10 mL bacterial solution and incubated with shaking (170 rpm) at 37 °C for 12 h to get the culture solution. After 10-fold dilution with sterile saline, 100 μL of culture suspension was spread onto PCA agar and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h.

Beef preservation experiment

Sample preparation

Fresh beef was divided into pieces (30 mm × 30 mm × 20 mm). The meat samples were randomly divided into 6 groups (3 pieces per groups), wrapped with PS, PS@NT, PS@NT@OA and PS@OA@PE films, respectively and stored at 4 °C.

pH

Refer to the method of Wang, Li, et al. (2024) with minor revisions, a 5 g beef sample was added to 50 mL of pure water and put in a glass homogenizer for homogenizing. After being left to stand for half an hour, the pH of the homogenized solution was determined using a PHS-3C meter (Shanghai Yidian Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

TVB-N measurement

The total volatile basic nitrogen (TVB-N) contents of beef were assessed using the method described by Lin et al. (2025). Briefly, 10 g meat sample was mixed with 50 mL distilled water, homogenized using a glass homogenizer and filtered through filter paper. 1 g of magnesium oxide was added to the distillation tubes containing 10 mL of the filtrate and 75 mL deionized water, followed by the determination of TVB-N via a Kjeldahl nitrogen analyzer (K1100, Haineng Future Technology, Hainan, China). Result was expressed as mg N/100 g meat.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± SD; one-way ANOVA and Duncan's test were carried out in GraphPad Prism 9.5.0 (p < 0.05 for significance).

Results

Characterization of CEO Pickering emulsion

Fig. 1 shows appearance and zeta potential and particle size of Pickering emulsions after 15-day storage at 20 °C. Oil-water two-phase separation was observed in the CEO Pickering emulsion (PE-NT100 and PE-PS-NT0) stabilized solely by PS or NT, indicating a poor emulsification effect of PS or NT. When PS and NT were simultaneously introduced, the composite Pickering emulsions (PE-PS-NT50 and PE-PS-NT100) showed a uniform appearance, indicating excellent emulsification performances. The Zeta potential is one of the key parameters for Pickering emulsions because it is a direct indicator of the surface charge characteristics and the stability of the system. The absolute zeta potential more than 30 mV indicats strong electrostatic repulsion formed between particles, which is beneficial for adsorbing particles at the oil-water interface and formation of a stable dispersed system (Li et al., 2026). As shown in Fig. 1A, all emulsions showed a positive Zeta potential value, indicating the droplet surface was predominantly positively charged, likely due to the influence of positively charged groups in the system. The Pickering emulsion (PE-NT100 and PE-PS-NT0) stabilized solely by PS or NT showed Zeta potential values of 26.77 ± 0.86 mV and 29.62 ± 2.36 mV, respectively, indicating a moderate repulsion. The PE-PS-NT50 and PE-PS-NT100 showed higher Zeta potential values (39.91 ± 0.94 mV and 43.84 ± 2.74 mV, respectively) than PE-NT100 and PE-PS-NT0 indicating the charge repulsion between the droplets was greater and the droplets were more stable. The average particle size of stabilized solely by PS or NT or both of them was present in Fig. 1B. The particle size of PE-NT100, PE-PS-NT0, PE-PS-NT50 and PE-PS-NT100 were (837.53 ± 14.84) nm, (589.61 ± 20.76) nm, (379.42 ± 1.22) nm and (209.6 ± 34.86) nm, respectively. The large standard deviation of the particle size of PE-PS-NT100 might be due to the high dilution (100 folds), which decreased the PS and NT concentrations and further made particles unstable. The larger particle size of both PE-NT100 and PE-PS-NT0 led to phase separation of the two emulsion system after 15 days of storage. Our findings suggested that PE could be effectively stabilized by simultaneous introduction of PS and NT.

Structural properties of active composite films

Chemical structures analysis

When investigating composite films, FT-IR spectroscopy is a commonly applied technique for assaying molecular-level variations in their constituent chemical groups (Wu et al., 2024). As presented in Fig. 2A, the peaks observed in 3309–3334 cm−1 and 2926–2928 cm−1 were attributed to the O—H and C—H stretching vibrations, respectively (Tan, Cui, Hu, Li, & Wu, 2025). The band near 1025 cm−1 was attributed to C—O bond stretching vibrations of the C—O—C groups in PS. The absorption band at 1412 cm−1 was associated with the C—O—H stretching vibration of glycerol in composite films (Abdillah, Lee, & Charles, 2024). When compared to the PS film, the PS@NT film did not show any new peaks, suggesting that the introduction of NT didn't change the chemical structure of the PS film. The broad peak at 3309 cm−1 shifted to higher wave numbers (3325 cm−1), suggesting reduced free O—H stretching due to binding interactions between NT and PS. This behavior might be explained by the hydrogen bonding interactions established between NT and the PS matrix (Wang et al., 2025). No new peaks or obvious wavelength shifts were observed in PS@OA@PE films after PE addition, confirming no chemical reaction occurred between PE, PS and OA. The bands at 3220 cm−1 became flattened and moved to a higher wavenumber (3309, 3325 and 3332 cm−1) with the increased concentration of PE addition, suggesting decreased free O—H stretching vibrations in PS, OA and PE. Wang et al. (2025) also reported adding clove essential oil pickering emulsions stabilized by cellulose nanocrystals into pectin-konjac glucomannan led to hydrogen bonding interaction in glucomannan and pectin-konjac and resulted in an obvious shift in the O—H tensile vibration bands.

Crystal structure analysis

The crystal structure information of the PS, PS@NT, PS@NT@OA and PS@OA@PE films was investigated via XRD spectra. As depicted in Fig. 2B, the PS film had some weak peaks at 2θ = 5.6°, 14.1°, 17.1°, 19.8°, 22.2° and 24.4°, which were characteristic peak of B and V-type crystalline structures (Gao et al., 2021). Notably, the addition of nano-TiO₂ led to the appearance of new diffraction bands at 25.6°, 30°, 47°, 54° and 63° in the XRD pattern of the PS@NT film, which were identified as the characteristic peaks of nano-TiO₂ and confirmed the successful loading of nano-TiO₂ in the PS matrix (Xie, Liu, Zhang, & Liu, 2023). The PS@NT film showed a lower peak at 2θ = 17.2°, suggesting a decreased crystallinity. The decreased crystallinity might be due to the H-bond interactions between NT, PS and glycerol. For the PS@NT@OA film no obvious changes were observed in the positions and intensities of the diffraction pattern peaks, suggesting the crystal integrities of the PS@NT@OA film were not affected after incorporation of 10 mL OA and indicating good compatibility between OA, NT and PS. The PS@NT@PE0.3 film showed a peak shape similar with the PS@NT@OA film, indicating 0.3 mL of CEO addition was favorably compatible with the matrix of the composite film. When the CEO content was increased from 0.6 to 0.9 mL, the peaks of the PS@NT@PE films became flatter, broader and less intense. The peak at 19.6° was observed to disappear in the PS@OA@PE0.9 film, suggesting an amorphous transition occurred in it. This result might be attributed to the hydrogen bond formed between PS and CEO, which rearranged or disrupted the network structure between PS and glycerol and led to the crystallinity reduction of the PS@OA@PE0.9 film. The findings suggested the crystallinity structure of the PS@OA@PE films could be affected by filler compounds. Our results were consistent with those of Hasheminya and Dehghannya (2025), who reported that the crystallinity of the polyvinyl alcohol-Plantago major mucilage composite films was reduced after being introduced with ZnO nanoparticle stabled Satureja sahendica essential oil Pickering emulsion, resulting from amorphous complexes formed by hydrogen bonding between film components.

Surface and cross-sectional appearances

The surface and cross-sectional images of PS, PS@NT, PS@NT@OA and PS@OA@PE films are given in Fig. 2C and Fig. 2D. The PS film displayed a relatively homogeneous and smooth surface with minor starch agglomerates on the surface, while its cross-section showed discernible cracks or pores. The surface of the PS@NT and PS@NT@OA films showed intensified roughness with obvious protrusions, bumps and irregularities on it. The surface images of the PS@NT and PS@NT@OA films indicated a partial degree of phase separation between NT and the PS (Torche et al., 2025). The result was due to the excess NT agglomerated together because of its high surface energy, destroying the structure integrity of the composite films (Lan et al., 2021). Similarly, Nguyen and Lee (2023) reported that the addition of higher amount incorporation of NT had negative effects on film structure integrity. However, Zou et al. (2025c) found that the cross-section of the Chlorella vulgaris nanocellulose/corn starch films became smoother without obvious wrinkles, indicating the Chlorella vulgaris nanocellulose were well dispersed in the corn starch matrix. Compared to the PS@NT@OA film, the PS@OA@PE films showed more uniform surface with fewer micro-pores in cross-sections, indicating that PE stabilized by PS and NT could improve the continuity of the PS@OA@PE films. This improvement was mainly attributed to CEO and NT cross-linking, which strengthen the interaction among PS, CEO, and NT formed a homogeneous and dense network.

Physical characterization of films

Appearance and color

The film's optical performance is key to consumer acceptability of packaging food (Wang et al., 2025). The optical characteristics include L (black-white), a (green-red), and b (blue-yellow) and color difference (∆E). The effect of OA, NT and CEO on the appearance and color of the potato starch-based films was assessed. As depicted in Fig. 3, the PS film showed a transparent appearance with the highest lightness (90.73 ± 0.72) and the smallest a value (−0.13 ± 0.06). No difference was observed in ∆E for the PS and PS@NT films. The result showed the introduction of NT did not alter the optical characteristics of the PS film. The PS@NT@OA film showed a purple color with a lower lightness (59.25 ± 0.37) and b values (−11.11 ± 0.10) and a higher a value (44.12 ± 0.02) than the PS@NT film. The a value of the PS@NT@PE films was significantly increased with increased content of the CEO added. The L and b values were the lowest when the amount of CEO was 0.3 mL and kept unchanged with the increase in the amount of CEO from 0.6 mL to 0.9 mL. Our results indicated that incorporation of OA and CEO significantly affected the color parameters of the PS@NT@PE films.

Mechanical properties

The mechanical properties including tensile strength (TS) and elongation at break (EAB) are essential indexes of food packaging films, which determine the range of external stresses that the films can withstand during food transportation and shelf life (Zou, Wang, et al., 2025). As shown in Fig. 4A, the PS film had a TS of 13.46 ± 0.25 MPa, which was consistent with the findings of Mathew, Sudheep, and Thomas (2025), indicating its limited structural integrity and the film's relative brittleness. After incorporation of 100 mg NT, the PS@NT film showed increased TS (14.17 ± 0.32 MPa), which was caused by the formation of hydrogen bonds (Liu, Xiong, Chen, Lin, & Tu, 2015). The TS of the PS@NT@OA film (16.35 ± 0.49 MPa) was slightly enhanced compared to the PS@NT film. This improvement in TS aligned with findings of Zhang et al. (2019), where anthocyanin addition could increase the TS of the composite films by exerting cross-linking effects on the film matrix. The PS@OA@PE0.3 film exhibited enhanced TS (18.24 ± 0.93 MPa) compared to the PS@NT@OA film. This improvement in TS might be more uniform stress distribution and dispersion of CEO within the starch matrix after being incorporated with PE (Zhang, Zhang, Cao, & Jiang, 2021). However, beyond 0.3 mL loading amounts, a decline in TS was observed (13.41 ± 0.68 and 11.86 ± 0.21 MPa for the PS@OA@PE0.6 and PS@OA@PE0.9 films, respectively). The reduction in TS could be due to excess CEO disrupting the formation of matrix networks, causing inhomogeneity and attenuating component interactions in composite films (Yang et al., 2025).

Tensile strength (TS) and elongation at break (EAB) (A), water contact angle (B), and water vapor transmission rate and water absorption capacity (C) of films.

EAB of active films is also presented in Fig. 4A. The PS@NT film exhibited higher EAB (130.83 ± 0.64%) compared to the PS film (120.85 ± 0.20%). The increased EAB of the PS@NT film might be due to the formation of micropores in the interface (Xie et al., 2023). The PS@NT@OA film had higher EAB value (136.09 ± 1.93%) than the PS@NT film (130.83 ± 0.64%). This indicated that the incorporation of anthocyanins, which could act as plasticizers, could improve flexibility of the PS@NT film. As CEO content was increased from 0 to 0.3 mL, the EAB of the PS@@OA@PE film were observed to be increased from 136.09 ± 1.93% to 139.45 ± 2.27%, indicating enhanced flexibility and ductility, primarily because CEO could create flexible interfacial regions that increase molecular chain mobility and decrease stress concentration. However, further elevation of CEO content from 0.6 to 0.9 mL led to a reduction in EAB. This decline might be resulted from phase separation or aggregation of emulsion components at higher concentrations. Our results suggested that PE addition enhanced the mechanical performance of the PS-based active films through plasticization and nano-reinforcement and showed considerable application potential in food preservation packaging.

WCA (water contact angle)

Wetting behavior of a material is very important for food packaging because it directly decides the preservation effect of this material (He et al., 2025). WCA is a primary indicator and used to classify the wetting behavior of a material is hydrophilic (< 90°) or hydrophobic (> 90°). The WCA of PS, PS@NT, PS@NT@OA and PS@OA@PE films are depicted in Fig. 4B. PS showed 41.97 ± 1.74° of WCA, indicative of hydrophilic properties. The WCA value of the PS@NT film was increased to 80.17 ± 2.76° after incorporation of NT. When OA was added, a notable decrease in WCA was observed for the PS@NT@OA film (33.27 ± 2.19°), indicating increased effect of OA on surface hydrophilicity. The increased hydrophilic effect of OA was because of rich glycosidic and hydroxyl groups in anthocyanins. The PS@OA@PE films exhibited significantly higher WCA than the PS@NT@OA film, which was probably resulted from the hydrophobic property of CEO within PE (Zhang et al., 2026). With increased addition of CEO in Pickering emulsion, WCA of the PS@OA@PE films progressively increased, reaching 95.81 ± 1.39° at 0.9 mL of CEO, suggesting a significant improvement in surface hydrophobicity. This result was primarily ascribed to the CEO in PE, which was hydrophobic and homogeneously dispersed throughout the film matrix, diminished the interaction between water molecules on the surface of the composite films (Wu et al., 2025). Our results indicated that incorporation of PE was able to increase the moisture resistance of PS-based films and endowing them with suitability for food packaging applications under humid-heat conditions.

WVTR

WVTR reflects the efficiency of moisture transfer from one side of the film to the other, which directly affects the quality of the packaged food and is an important parameter for the barrier performance of packaging materials (Ni et al., 2025). Fig. 4C shows the WVTR of the PS, PS@NT, PS@NT@OA, and PS@OA@PE films. The WVTR of the PS and PS@NT films were 828.55 ± 26.19 and 643.43 ± 9.13 g m−2 d−1, respectively. It was clear that WVTR of PS film was decreased after introducing NT due to well-dispersed TiO2 nanoparticles and strong interfacial adhesion, which created a tortuous path for water molecule transformation. No marked variations in WVTR were found between the PS@NT and PS@NT@OA films, indicating the incorporation of OA could not decrease the WVTR of the film. PE addition significantly decreased the WVTR of PS@OA@PE films (p < 0.05). This is because of the addition of CEO interfered with water molecule migration from the composite films. The CEO existing in the composite films increased the curvature of the internal transmission path of water molecules across the film, which hindered the penetration of water molecules and consequently led to a decrease in the WVTR of the composite films (Wu et al., 2025). Among the PS@OA@PE films, the PS@OA@PE0.9 film has the highest WVTR (559.65 ± 33.95 ×g m−2 d−1), which might be resulted from the aggregation of unstable Pickering emulsions in the film when added in larger amounts (Huang et al., 2025). Similarly, Zhao, Guan, Zhou, Lao, and Cai (2022) discovered that the WVTR of anthocyanidin/chitosan active films became improved when a higher concentration of the cinnamon perilla essential oil Pickering emulsion was added. Our findings indicated that an appropriate content of PE introduced favored enhancing the films' water vapor barrier properties.

Water absorption capacity (WAC)

The WAC values of the PS, PS@NT, PS@NT@OA and PS@OA@PE films are given in Fig. 4C. The PS film showed a WAC value of 69.1 ± 0.9%. The WAC of the PS@NT film (66.3 ± 0.6%) became decreased after incorporation with NT. The result was due to the redispersion of TiO2 consumed the space where water could be combined and partial blocking water-accessible pores. The WAC of PS@NT@OA film was significantly lower than that of PS@NT film, due to the formation of intermolecular bonds between the anthocyanins in OA and PS and glycerol reduced ability of films to absorb water. The WAC of the PS@OA@PE films (53.6–57.5%) was markedly less than that of the PS@NT@OA film. The WAC reduction was mainly due to emulsion hydrophobic constituents occupying starch chain micropores and interstitial spaces. Notably, the PS@OA@PE films showed the decreased WAC with the increased CEO addition amounts. Similar results were reported in previous studies by Liu, Qu, Cao, and Wang (2025) that incorporating Toona sinensis essential oil greatly decreased the water absorption rates of the Toona sinensis essential oil-chitosan composite films. It was worth noting that the water absorption rate of the hydrophobic film (PS@OA@PE0.9) was 53.6 ± 0.4%. Tang et al. (2025) found that the ZCS/SA composite film with 3% of TEON showed a WCA of 105.29° and about 60% of swelling rate. Zou, Zhao, et al. (2025b) also found that the SL/CS composite film with 10% of SL oil showed 91.79° of WCA and more than 70% of water absorption rate. However, Ban et al. (2026) prepared a hydrophilic composite film with a WCA of 77.5°, showing a lower water absorption rate (about 22%). The discrepancies in water absorption capacity might be ascribed to differences in film formulations and variations in test conditions.

Optical and UV blocking properties

UV radiation can induce various spoilage reactions in foods, including lipid oxidation, antioxidant degradation, off-flavor formation, protein degradation, and pigment loss. Films with effective UV light barrier properties are vitally important for restraining food deterioration (Zou, Wang, et al., 2025). The UV–visible light transmittance of composite films is shown in Fig. 5A. The PS film had the highest UV light transmittance which could be resulted form low level content of UV-absorbing groups and uniform network structures in it. The PS@NT film had lower light transmittance than the PS film due to NT's large specific surface area and high refractive index (Ning et al., 2024). The PS@NT@OA film exhibited an enhanced UV light barrier property due to the abundance of aromatic rings in anthocyanin structures (Amaregouda, Kamanna, & Gasti, 2022). The transmittance of the PS@OA@PE films became less than that of PS@NT@OA film and decreased significantly when the concentration of CEO increased. In the ultraviolet region (200 to 300 nm), the PS@OA@PE films showed a transmittance value no more than 1%, indicating good UV-shielding capability. This effect was attributed to the synergistic UV-absorbing capacity of NT and CEO. Similar results have been obtained by Danmatam, Pearce, and Pattavarakorn (2023) who found that the percentage of transmittance of carboxymethyl cellulose/oolong tea extract films was lower than the pure carboxymethyl cellulose film and reduced with the increased content of oolong tea extracts.

Thermal property

The thermal stability of active films was determined using derivative thermogravimetry (DTG) and thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) techniques. TGA and DTG analysis revealed three distinct weight loss stages for the PS, PS@NT, PS@NT@OA, PS@OA@PE films from 20 °C to 700 °C (Fig. 5B). The initial stage (50 °C–175 °C) was linked to adsorbed water evaporation and volatile CEO volatilization (Wu, Yue, et al., 2025). The second phase, at 175 °C–250 °C, was mainly resulted from thermal degradation of OA and CEO and decomposition of PS molecules. The third stage (250–350 °C) arose from PS backbone thermal degradation via carbonization and chain scission. The PS@NT film showed the highest residual mass when the sample was heated to 300 °C, suggesting NT introduction could effectively delay degradation of PS-based films. It has bee demonstrated that TiO2 nanoparticles improved the thermal stability of the composite films not only because the nanoparticles were thermally stable and could endure high temperatures but also they could interact with starch chains, fill the gaps between the chains, produce a cohesive coating that enhances the film's heat stability (Farokhi, Milani, & Amiri, 2025). The PS@NT@OA film at 250 °C suffered the lowest weight loss of 27.6%, indicating had the lowest thermal stability, attributing to high heat temperatures accelerated the structural destruction of anthocyanin molecules. The PS@OA@PE films had less thermal decomposition than PS film at 400–700 °C, indicating superior thermal stability. The enhanced thermal stability was presumably resulted from the interaction between the multiphase system and the higher compatibility between the components. The thermal stability of the PS@OA@PE0.9 film was higher than that of the PS@OA@PE0.3 and PS@OA@PE0.6 films at 400 °C–700 °C. Bhatia et al. (2025) reported that the thermal stability of the collagen/K-carrageenan films was enhanced when the Origanum majorana essential oil were introduced. However, Sharma et al. (2023) found that the addition of CEO to the films negatively affected the thermal stability when compared to the control film. These founds indicated that an appropriate addition amount of CEO was necessary for composite films with enhanced thermal stability due to an optimal balance of synergistic interactions between the emulsion constituents and the starch matrix, which ensured homogeneous distribution and effective inhibition of thermal degradation (Wu, Yue, et al., 2025). Compared with the DTG peak of the PS film at 319.4 °C, no significant peak shifts were observed in PS@NT@OA and PS@OA@PE films (Fig. 5C). While the peak of the PS@NT film shifted to a higher temperature (337.2 °C) after adding NT, indicating that the thermal stability of the PS@NT film was increased.

Antimicrobial activity

Gram-negative E. coli and Gram-positive S. aureus are key pathogens responsible for causing food deterioration and food-borne illnesses. Therefore, it is necessary to incorporate antibacterial substances into packaging films for the purpose of extending food shelf life and safeguarding food safety (Liu et al., 2024). As shown in Fig. 6, the plates treated with the PS film showed the highest E. coli and S. aureus colony counts, indicating less significant inhibitory effect of the PS film on the two microorganisms. The PS@NT film significantly enhance antibacterial activity against both bacterial species, due to the fact that NT could produce and activate reactive radicals, which could directly carry out lipid peroxidation, destroy the cell membrane and lead to the death of bacterial cells (Salarbashi, Tafaghodi, & Bazzaz, 2018). The PS@NT@OA film showed enhanced antimicrobial activity than the PS@NT film due to anthocyanins presented in films could hinder the extracellular enzyme secretion, block bacterial growth substrate delivery and disrupt bacterial cell wall integrity (Jeyaraj, Vidana Gamage, Cintrat, & Choo, 2023). The PS@NT@OA film had less antibacterial capacity against S. aureus than E. coli due to differences in physiology, metabolism and cell wall structures of the two bacterial species (Zhang et al., 2019). The antimicrobial activity of the PS@OA@PE films was significantly increased after the introduction of CEO. Even at a lower addition amount 3% (w/w), the PS@OA@PE0.3 film had markedly higher antibacterial capacity against E. coli and S. aureus. The CEO in Pickering emulsion could significantly suppress bacterial colony growth dose-dependently through inhibiting critical enzymatic activities, disrupting metabolic pathways and destroying bacterial cell membrane integrity. The PS@OA@PE films exhibited excellent antimicrobial activities and were greatly possible to be active packaging materials to prolong the food shelf life. In film packaged beef, the microorganisms responsible for spoilage usually depend on the storage temperature, oxygen availability and packaging atmosphere. Under refrigerated conditions, Pseudomonas, a kind of aerobic psychrotrophs, often acts as important spoilage bacteria (Wang, Xu, Zhang, Yan, et al., 2026). Ferreira et al. (2022) have found that the Melaleuca alternifolia oil Pickering emulsion has a strong antimicrobial effect against S. aureus, E. coli and P. aeruginosa. Our study on P. aeruginosa inhibition effect of the PS@OA@PE film is in progress.

Application in beef preservation

Appearance and pH

As shown in Fig. 7 A, the surface of the beef packaged in PS film became dark on the 8th day, and turned brown on the 12th day, while the samples packaged with the PS@OA@PE0.6 and PS@OA@PE0.9 films began to be dark on the 12th day. The pH variation in beef samples is an indicator of beef quality (Ma et al., 2025). Effects of different active films on pH of stored beef were further studied using the PS film as controls. As shown in Fig. 7B, the pH value of the beef on day 0 was 5.07 ± 0.1. The low pH value was resulted from the release of inorganic phosphate and lactic acid during the glycogen catabolism and anaerobic glycolysis reactions in packaged beef (Wu, Zhang, et al., 2025). The pH of PS film-packaged beef rose slowly to 5.19 ± 0.09 by day 4, and then increased rapidly. This rapid pH rise was caused by the accumulation of ammonia and biogenic amines by via microbial metabolic activity and endogenous enzymatic degradation of proteins in beef during storage (Ma, Chen, et al., 2025). The pH of beef treated with PS@NT, PS@NT@OA and PS@OA@PE films on day 4 was ranged from 5.21 ± 0.1 to 5.28 ± 0.04, showing no difference with that of the PS film treated beef. The pH of the PS@NT film treated sample was 6.69 ± 0.18 on day 8, indicating an increased preservation effect of the PS@NT film due to the appearance of NT. The pH of beef in the PS@NT@OA film group was 6.32 ± 0.13 on the 8 day, lower than that of the PS@NT film, indicating the addition of OA could enhance preservation effect of the PS@NT film. The PS@OA@PE film treated beef showed a lower pH than the PS@NT@OA film, suggesting the PS@OA@PE film could decrease pH and quality deterioration of beef. Notably, higher amount of PE incorporation in the PS@OA@PE films further inhibited the quality deterioration of beef. In general, beef could be considered spoiled when its pH exceeded 6.7. The pH of control group was increased to 6.76 ± 0.12 the 8 day, exceeding the spoilage threshold and indicating significant quality deterioration. By contrast, the pH value of the PS@OA@PE film packaging samples was maintained within the sub-fresh meat range, suggesting delayed spoilage and better freshness maintenance.

Changes of appearance (A), pH (B) and total volatile basic nitrogen level (C) of composite film treated beef during storage at 4 °C.

TVB-N

TVB-N reflects the decomposition degree of nitrogenous substances such as nucleic acids and proteins by enzymes and microorganisms and is widely used for assessing meat freshness (Qiao, Tang, & Dong, 2017). Fig. 7C shows the variation of the TVB-N values of beef packaged with different composite films. The TVB-N content of fresh beef in the beginning was 6.33 ± 0.30 mg/100 g. The TVB-N content of beef treated with PS@NT, PS@NT@OA and PS@OA@PE films at 4th day was ranged from 7.39 ± 0.21 to 8.11 ± 0.18 mg/100 g, showing no difference (p > 0.05) with that of the PS film treated beef. The TVB-N values of the PS-treated beef showed a rapid increase from the 4th day (7.67 ± 0.65 mg/100 g) to the 12th day (20.4 ± 0.40 mg/100 g). This increase was due to the degradation of protein compounds by the spoilage bacteria and endogenous proteolytic enzymes, leading to the accumulation of volatile ammonia, trimethylamine and dimethylamine. At the 8th and 12th days, the TVB-N content of the PS@NT, PS@NT@OA and PS@OA@PE film packaged beef was always less than that of PS film packaged beef (Fig. 7C). According to the Chinese standard (GB 2707–2016), the upper limit of TVB-N value in meat is 15 mg/100 g. The TVB-N content of the PS, PS@NT and PS@NT@OA film packaged beef on the 12 day was 20.40 ± 0.40 mg/100 g, 18.10 ± 0.03 mg/100 g and 17.11 ± 0.28 mg/100 g, respectively, indicating the protein degradation occurred in the composite film-packaged meat. The TVB-N content were 14.47 ± 0.49 mg/100 g, 12.32 ± 0.08 mg/100 g and 11.75 ± 0.84 mg/100 g, respectively for the PS@OA@PE0.3, PS@OA@PE0.6 and PS@OA@PE0.9 film treated beef, which did not exceed 15 mg/100 g and was defined as fresh meat. Our findings suggested that the PS@OA@PE films could slow down oxidative deamination of non-protein nitrogen compounds and protein degradation caused by bacterial populations. The inhibition of TVB-N production was consistent with the findings of Ma, Lv, et al. (2025) who found that the pectin/chitosan based films functionalized by phenethyl isothiocyanate nanoemulsion markedly decreased the TVB-N content of beef during the storage period. In summary, the PS@OA@PE films showed a strong capacity to preserve beef freshness and had great potential applications as packaging material in meat preservation fields.

Conclusion

In conclusion, Pickering emulsion (PE) was firstly prepared using potato starch and TiO2 nanoparticles and the particle size and Zeta potential of the PE-PS-NT100 Pickering emulsion was determined to be 209.6 ± 34.86 nm and 43.84 ± 2.74 mV, indicating excellent stability. New active films composed of PS, OA and PS/NT stabilized CEO Pickering emulsion (PE) were then successfully developed for application in beef preservation by using the solution casting method. The structural properties of the composite films were confirmed by a combination of FT-IR, XRD, SEM and TGA and some hydrogen bond interaction between PS, OA, NT and CEO occurred. The addition of OA, NT and CEO significantly enhanced the films' thermal, mechanical, UV light and water barrier properties and improved antibacterial capability against E. coli and S. aerous. When applied to beef preservation, the PS@OA@PE films containing 0.3 mL–0.9 mL CEO effectively reduced pH and TVBN content, and prolonged the shelf life by 12 days. As a result, the use of OA and PS/NT-stabilized PE was a favorable approach for enhancing the antibacterial capability of the PS-based film, and these films showed the potential as packaging materials to package beef for preservation.
