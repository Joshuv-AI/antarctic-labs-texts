Polyphenol mediated zinc-oxygen synergistic hydrogel remodels senescent microenvironment for periodontal tissue regeneration
Authors: Chengxinyue Ye, Jin Liu, Jinhui Ran, Shoushan Hu, Lu Yang, Jialiang Zhao, Xinyi Fang, Zhenzhen Zhang, Xin Xiong, Xueman Zhou, Yating Yi, Xiaolong Zeng, Xiong Lu, Jun Wang, Chaoming Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Senescent mesenchymal stem cells residing in an inflammatory, dysbiotic, and hypoxic microenvironment pose a barrier to periodontal regeneration. Here we introduce a hydrocaffeic acid (HCA)-mediated silk fibroin hydrogel incorporating Mn/HCA-modified calcium peroxide (Mn-hCaO₂) and HCA-modified zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 (hZIF8) to rejuvenate this environment. The strategy imbues the hydrogel with enhanced adhesion and adaptability in periodontal pockets. The Mn-HCA complex acts catalytically to reduce oxidative stress and correct hypoxia. Simultaneously, Zn²⁺ and HCA restore microbial balance and mitigate inflammation. This multifunctional hydrogel targets the senescent periodontal niche by normalizing microbiota homeostasis and promoting a regenerative immune profile. Concurrently, it directly alleviates telomere shortening, DNA damage and oxidative stress in stem cells, thereby rejuvenating cellular function. By specifically addressing hypoxia and zinc deficiency, this polyphenol-mediated synergistic strategy offers a pathway to rejuvenate the senescent periodontal microenvironment, thereby overcoming tissue regeneration barriers and offering a translatable pathway for treating periodontitis and other aging-associated inflammatory diseases.

Introduction

Periodontitis (PD) is an inflammatory disease with a multifactorial etiology, leading to irreversible damage to periodontal support tissue1. The stress-induced premature senescence of mesenchymal stem cells, characterized by cell cycle arrest and diminished self-renewal and differentiation capacity, may pose a significant barrier to periodontal regeneration2,3. In the course of PD, virulent microbial pathogens and the ensuing inflammatory cascade can induce premature senescence of stem cells by causing stress-induced mitochondrial dysfunction and DNA damage4,5. Senescent cells emit various intercellular signals into the niche that aggregate local inflammation and impair tissue function6. The senescent niche, which houses the senescent stem cells, drives a vicious circle of periodontal destruction7,8. Owing to the imbalance between limited oxygen availability and soaring energy demand, PD lesions are frequently characterized by hypoxic conditions, which can lead to a cascade of biological responses that hinder the regenerative process9. Oxygen deprivation can interrupt energy metabolism and stimulate the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which results in oxidative DNA damage, subsequently halting cell cycle progression10. Countering these effects, oxygen supplementation can contribute to the improvement of bone metabolism and facilitate the regeneration of bone defects11. Oxygen concentration also plays an indispensable role in modulating the balance of microbiota by impacting the composition and function of microbial communities. Coupled with aberrant oxygen levels, zinc deficiency is also a risk factor in intractable inflammation and failure of tissue repair in PD12,13. Zinc is an essential element in many biological processes, including cell growth, immune function and antioxidant defense reaction14. Low zinc bioavailability can be found in various pathological conditions linked to premature aging, while short-term zinc supplementation has been shown to effectively counteract the adverse effects of zinc deficiency in the elderly15. Current anti-aging therapies primarily focus on eliminating senescent cells or antagonizing their cellular responses, with relatively limited exploration of how these or related strategies might apply to the complex and diverse periodontal senescent microenvironment16–18. Reshaping the senescent periodontal niche is a novel approach to rejuvenating stem cells and enhancing tissue regeneration7,19,20.

Hydrogels with biocompatible structures that mimic the extracellular matrix of periodontal cells are widely utilized in periodontal tissue regeneration and functional reconstruction21. Currently, most hydrogel development focuses on locally sustained and controlled release of drugs that exhibit antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, or pro-regenerative properties22–24. However, significant challenges remain, as no current hydrogels can effectively modulate the senescent periodontal microenvironment. To remain anchored in this challenging environment, a hydrogel should possess strong wet adhesion and appropriate mechanical properties, ensuring long-term efficacy in the oral cavity. To modulate the senescent periodontal microenvironment, it should simultaneously target multiple aging-related processes, such as hypoxia and zinc ion deficiency. Traditional oxygenating materials like CaO2 have shortcomings, including insufficient oxygen release duration and excessive H2O2 residue, potentially exacerbating oxidative damage to cells. Zeolite imidazolate framework-8 (ZIF-8) is a biocompatible zinc ion donor that has been widely used thanks to its antibacterial activity alongside promotion of angiogenesis and bone regeneration25,26. However, the hydrophobic characteristics of ZIF-8 make it difficult to achieve uniform dispersion within a hydrogel network.

Polyphenols, such as tannic acids (TA), epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG) and hydrocaffeic acid (HCA), exhibit immunomodulatory effects due to their unique phenolic hydroxyl groups, which can be beneficial for inflammatory diseases27. Moreover, polyphenols are metabolized by local microbiota, then serve to regulate their homeostasis, suggesting potential in PD treatment28. Additionally, the hydrophilicity of polyphenol can alter the wettability of a material’s surface through modification. Similar to mussel adhesive proteins, polyphenol-modified hydrogels exhibit strengthened tissue adhesion while retaining the biological properties inherent to polyphenols. Therefore, the construction of polyphenol-mediated hydrogels with anti-aging properties could effectively address the challenges in the senescent periodontal microenvironment.

In this study, we engineered an injectable polyphenol-mediated oxygenating hydrogel based on HCA-mediated manganese-modified CaO2 nanoparticles (Mn-hCaO2), HCA-mediated ZIF8 nanoparticles (hZIF8), and silk fibroin methacrylate (SMA) for targeted modulation of the senescent periodontal microenvironment. The abundance of phenolic hydroxyl groups in HCA enhances the antioxidant, adhesive and mechanical properties of the hydrogel. HCA modification can not only improve the dispersibility and stability of nanoparticles in the hydrogel matrix, but also form a cascade enzyme-like catalytic unit together with Mn. This modification offers a simple and safe strategy for constructing an H2O2-eliminating oxygenating system. hZIF8 can release a sustained supply of Zn2+ to ameliorate zinc ion deficiency. By specifically addressing both hypoxia and zinc ion deficiency, the hydrogel restores the essential micronutrients and oxygen levels, thereby rejuvenating the senescent periodontal microenvironment and enabling senescent cells to re-enter the cell cycle. In this manner, the polyphenol-mediated hydrogel simultaneously targets multiple aging-related processes, such as telomere shortening, DNA damage, cellular antioxidant systems, and oral microbiota homeostasis, which promotes robust periodontal tissue regeneration.

Results

Design strategy

CaO2 and ZIF8 were modified with HCA. After modification with HCA, Mnn+ was anchored onto the surface of CaO2 through the chelation effect of phenolic hydroxyl groups (Fig. 1A). CaO2 reacts with water to release oxygen, alleviating hypoxia, but its hydrolysis produces cytotoxic H2O2. Fortunately, the Mnn+-HCA shell can catalyze this H2O2 into O2 via catalase (CAT)-like activity. Therefore, the Mnn+-HCA chelate complex constitutes a cascade enzyme-like catalytic system, which not only reduces the risk of H2O2-induced oxidative stress, but also further promotes the production of O2 (Fig. 1B). In addition, after modification by HCA, hZIF8 can be uniformly dispersed in SMA hydrogel matrix with Mn-hCaO2. The hydrogel can be injected into the periodontal pocket and polymerized using UV light irradiation (395 nm) (Fig. 1C). The abundance of phenolic hydroxyl groups enhances the wet adhesive and mechanical properties of the hydrogel, benefitting its application in a dynamic periodontal pocket. The pathological periodontal niche sustains a vicious cycle of hypoxia, microbiota dysbiosis, and pro-inflammatory immune patterns, collectively driving stem cell senescence and impairing tissue regeneration (Fig. 1D). The Mn-hCaO₂/hZIF8/SMA hydrogel disrupts this circuit via synergistic actions of HCA, Zn²⁺ and O₂ release, which mitigate senescence through hypoxia alleviation, oxidative damage protection, telomere maintenance and enhanced DNA repair. Characterization of Mn-hCaO2/hZIF8/SMA hydrogel

The spheroid morphology of the Mn-hCaO₂ nanoparticles (NPs), which preserved the characteristic shape of the CaO₂ core, was primarily characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Supplementary Fig. 1A, B) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Fig. 2A, B). The successful incorporation of Mn and HCA was confirmed by the uniform distribution of Ca, O, C and Mn in elemental mapping and the presence of C═O and C─O bonds in the high-resolution O 1s spectrum (Fig. 2C). The crystal phase of Mn-hCaO2 was in good agreement with CaO2, which indicated that the structural integrity of CaO2 was not affected (Supplementary Fig. 1C). The zeta potential decreased in hCaO2 owing to the negative charge of HCA and it slightly increased in Mn-CaO2 when Mn2+ was chelated to the electronegative hydroxyl and carboxyl groups of HCA (Fig. 2D). The concentration of Mn²⁺ was tuned to maximize the oxygen-release performance of the composite (Supplementary Fig. 1D). The oxygen-generating kinetics of Mn-hCaO2 was measured by a dissolved oxygen (DO) meter when dispersed in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) at 37 °C, mimicking the in vivo environment (Fig. 2E). The oxygen release curves of CaO2 and hCaO2 both reached peaks at 24 h with a maximum DO concentrations of 14.75 mg L−1 and 15.92 mg L−1, respectively. Mn-hCaO2 reached a DO concentration of 20.72 mg L−1 at the second hour, and the DO concentration was maintained higher than 10 mg L−1 to the 60th hour. This sustained concentration is possible because during the oxygen generation reaction of CaO2, a large amount of H2O2 is produced as an intermediate product. Mn-hCaO2 rapidly decomposed H2O2 and the H2O2 concentration approached 0 mg L−1 from the 12th hour (Fig. 2F). H2O2 rapidly decomposed into oxygen due to the strong catalytic activity of Mnn+-HCA shell of the Mn-hCaO2, which also boosted the production of oxygen. Considering the excessive oxidative stress in the PD microenvironment, the capacity of the NPs to handle external H2O2 and •OH was also tested (Fig. 2G, H). The hZIF8 was found to form a typical dodecahedral crystal structure similar to that of ZIF8 with a shrink size of around 80 nm, as shown in SEM (Supplementary Fig. 1E, F) and TEM images (Fig. 2I, J). The successful incorporation of HCA was confirmed by the presence of O element on the surface and a decline in zeta potential (Supplementary Fig. 1G). The crystal phase of ZIF8 was maintained after HCA modification (Supplementary Fig. 1H). The hZIF8 had good dispersity in aqueous solution, as was demonstrated by the Tyndall effect (Supplementary Fig. 1I).

The Mn-hCaO2/hZIF8/SMA hydrogel exhibited good plasticity, tissue adhesion and compression resistance, meeting the requirements of the dynamic and wet conditions of the oral cavity. The compositional ratios of the hydrogel were systematically optimized for functional performance and structural stability. The proportion of hZIF8 to Mn-hCaO₂ was specifically determined through mechanical testing to enhance compressive strength (Supplementary Fig. 2A, B). At the micro level, the hydrogel displayed porous reticular structures with an average pore size of 300 μm and a uniform distribution of C, N, O, Ca and Zn elements on the surface of the hydrogel (Supplementary Fig. 2C–F). The hydrogel is injectable and can adapt to irregular shapes (Supplementary Fig. 2G). The Mn-hCaO₂/hZIF8/SMA pre-gel solution exhibited pronounced shear-thinning behavior with low viscosity, facilitating injection (Fig. 2K). Photo-gelation occurred within 12 s under UV irradiation (395 nm, 10 W) (Fig. 2L). Frequency sweep measurements (0.1–10 Hz) confirmed the structural stability of the hydrogel (Supplementary Fig. 2H).

HCA-modified NPs not only endowed the hydrogel with good tissue adhesiveness, but also improved its mechanical properties. The hydrogel is capable of adhering to both soft and hard oral tissues, including alveolar bone, cementum, enamel, and gingiva, demonstrating its suitability for the challenging oral environment (Fig. 2M). We compared the Mn-hCaO2/hZIF8/SMA hydrogel to pure SMA hydrogel by testing its in situ adhesion to porcine skin, and the results showed the adhesion strength of the hydrogel with HCA-modified NPs was around 15 kPa, three times that of pure SMA hydrogel (Fig. 2N). The compressive modulus of Mn-hCaO2/hZIF8/SMA hydrogel increased to around 150 kPa, compared to 67 kPa for pure SMA hydrogel, and was able to withstand 80% compressive deformation (Fig. 2O). To mimic the repetitive forces of mastication, cyclic compression to 60% compressive deformation was applied (Fig. 2P). The compression stress remained after 30 cycles, demonstrating the resistance of the material to cyclic compression. These improvements in the modified hydrogel’s mechanical properties could be attributed to the nano-reinforcement effect of Mn-hCaO2 and hZIF8.

The swelling and degradation properties of a hydrogel material are also crucial for its suitability in tissue regeneration applications. Incorporating hZIF8 and Mn-hCaO₂ increased the swelling capacity of the SMA hydrogel, exhibiting a 155% equilibrium swelling ratio within 10 h. This enables conformal adaptation to complex periodontal defect geometries, promoting defect filling and retention (Supplementary Fig. 2I). In vitro, the pure and modified hydrogels both exhibited over 90% degradation within 30 days in artificial saliva at 37 °C, which indicated that ZIF8 and Mn-hCaO₂ incorporation did not have significant effects on the degradation rate (Supplementary Fig. 2J). In vivo studies confirmed near complete degradation by day 30 (Supplementary Fig. 2K). The hydrogel degradation is mediated by hydrolysis of methacrylate-derived ester bonds under physiological conditions and aligns with the typical timeline required for new bone regeneration.

Mn-hCaO2/hZIF8/SMA hydrogel suppresses stem cell senescence and modulates macrophage polarization in vitro

An inflammatory microenvironment induced by microbial virulence was established by applying liposaccharide (LPS) stimuli to human-derived periodontal ligament stem cells (hPDLSCs). The proportion of senescent cells, labeled by a senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) assay, significantly increased in proportion after 48 h of inflammatory stimuli (Fig. 3A). Expression of cellular senescence-related genes P16 (Fig. 3B), P21 (Fig. 3C) and P53 (Supplementary Fig. 3A) also increased dramatically, indicating that the hPDLSCs entered a senescent state. Intracellular ROS labeling with a DCFH-DA probe showed increased intracellular ROS levels in the hPDLSCs, suggesting a stress-induced mechanism of cellular senescence (Supplementary Fig. 3B, C). Interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), two key inflammation-related cytokines of SASP, were significantly increased in mRNA expression (Fig. 3D, E). Treatment with Mn-hCaO2 significantly suppressed senescence, as demonstrated by a reduction in senescence-related biomarkers and intracellular ROS together with downregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Similarly, hZIF8 supplementation also suppressed these indicators effectively. The combination of both Mn-hCaO2 and hZIF8 produced the optimum anti-senescent ability in this pro-inflammatory microenvironment. The free radical scavenging ability of Mn-hCaO₂/hZIF8/SMA hydrogel was further confirmed using 2,2-Diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) assay, which revealed that phenolic hydroxyl groups of HCA neutralized radical species through electron transfer (Supplementary Fig. 3D). Arrested proliferation and differentiation are characteristic of aging hPDLSCs. Initially, we confirmed that light exposure did not impair hPDLSC proliferation using a Cell Count Kit-8 assay (Supplementary Fig. 3E). Under inflammatory challenge, hPDLSC proliferation was significantly suppressed after 1, 3, and 5 days, whereas the LPS+Mn-hCaO₂/hZIF8/SMA group exhibited enhanced proliferation from day 1, suggesting synergistic roles of Mn-hCaO₂ and hZIF8 in promoting cell viability (Fig. 3F). To assess the osteo-inducive property of the hydrogel, a 14-day osteogenic induction was performed in hPDLSCs in an inflammatory microenvironment. The LPS+Mn-hCaO2/hZIF8/SMA group showed the highest relative mRNA expression of osteoblast markers, including alkaline phosphatase (ALP) (Supplementary Fig. 3F), osteocalcin (OCN) (Fig. 3G) and specificity protein 7 (SP7) (Fig. 3H). The SMA matrix itself could elevate osteogenic activity to some extent by providing a suitable mechanical environment for cell adhesion and stretching; the incorporation of hZIF8 and Mn-hCaO2 NPs further promoted the ALP activity of hPDLSCs. The results of ALP staining were consistent with the mRNA expression results (Fig. 3I). Taken together, these findings demonstrate that Mn-hCaO2/hZIF8/SMA hydrogel can effectively restore the proliferation and osteogenic differentiation capability of hPDLSCs compromised by inflammatory stimuli.

Macrophages are a group of pioneering immune cells important to the host response against microbial infection. Different macrophage subtypes can secrete cytokines and chemokines that either promote bone resorption or regeneration, and thus play crucial modulatory roles in the periodontal niche29,30. THP-1 cells were induced to generate M0 macrophages, which were then stimulated with LPS for polarization assessment. After 48 h of inflammatory stimuli, the macrophages presented an inclination towards M1 polarization with upregulated expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-1β and TNF-α, as well as the M1 surface marker CD80 (Fig. 3J). The LPS+Mn-hCaO2/SMA group and LPS+Mn-hCaO2/hZIF8/SMA hydrogel successfully reversed this effect and inclined the macrophages toward M2 polarization, characterized by upregulated expression of M2-related markers including arginase-1 (ARG-1), Mer tyrosine kinase (MerTK) and CD206 (Fig. 3K). These results revealed that the Mn-hCaO2/hZIF8/SMA hydrogel can modulate the M1/M2 ratio, which in turn can produce highly significant changes in the cytokine network in the niche and steer the environment towards a pro-regenerative state.

Gene ontology (GO) analysis of the DEGs between the Mn-hCaO2/hZIF8/SMA and SMA groups revealed that the Mn-hCaO2/hZIF8/SMA hydrogel had a significant impact on several biological processes, including cell cycle, DNA repair, hypoxic response, cellular zinc ion homeostasis, telomere maintenance, aging, cell junctions, collagen biosynthesis, NF-kappaB transcription factor activity, and antimicrobial humoral immune responses mediated by antimicrobial peptides (Fig. 4E).

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) and heatmap analysis confirmed the Mn-hCaO2/hZIF8/SMA hydrogel’s positive regulation of cellular zinc homeostasis, DNA repair, cell cycle, telomere maintenance and antibacterial activity (Fig. 4F, G). ROS-induced DNA damage and telomere dysfunction constitute one of the molecular hallmarks of aging and can amplify inflammatory reactions. The Mn-hCaO2/hZIF8/SMA hydrogel battled intracellular ROS, endonuclear telomere shortening and DNA damage via multiple mechanisms (Fig. 4H). The release of zinc from the hydrogel upregulated metallothionein (MT), forming a non-enzymatic system that protects stem cells from oxidative damage31. Additionally, the polyphenol component of the hydrogel upregulated Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (KEAP1), which initiates a robust cytoprotective system mediated by antioxidant response elements (AREs)32. Sirtuins, important antioxidant targets, were also regulated by the Mn-hCaO2/hZIF8/SMA hydrogel, modulating aging-associated signaling pathways33. Regarding telomere maintenance, the Mn-hCaO2/hZIF8/SMA hydrogel upregulated several key genes: ataxia-telangiectasia-mutated (ATM), which facilitates the recruitment of telomerase; protection of telomere 1 (POT1) and tankyrase 2 (TNKS2), which are essential for telomerase activation and telomere extension34,35. The Mn-hCaO2/hZIF8/SMA hydrogel also altered the ROS-accumulating and hypoxic environment. These processes collectively promote the re-entry of senescent stem cells into the cell cycle. Taken together, these findings suggest that the Mn-hCaO2/hZIF8/SMA hydrogel revitalizes senescent stem cells by interfering with telomere shortening, DNA damage, and cellular antioxidant systems, attributed to the polyphenol-mediated synergistic effect of hZIF8 and Mn-hCaO2.

Discussion

This study introduced an injectable polyphenol-mediated zinc-oxygen synergistic hydrogel capable of ameliorating hPDLSCs senescence in both direct as well as indirect niche-guided manners.

To date, one of the most perplexing aspects of PD is the difficulty in achieving the complete regeneration of alveolar bone height and density, despite the alveolar bone being one of the most actively remodeled sites in the human body. Recent studies have demonstrated the accumulation of senescent cells and the secretion of inflammatory factors in PD, establishing a link between cellular senescence and the progression of the disease41. Current anti-aging agents can be categorized as senescent cell-clearing agents, known as “senolytics”, or SASP inhibitors, sometimes called “senomorphics” 42. However, few strategies have been developed to achieve a combined effect on both fronts. The Mn-hCaO2/hZIF8/SMA hydrogel exerts multi-faceted, synergistic anti-aging effects by targeting multiple characteristics of the microenvironment. The reestablishment of microbial and immune balance in the periodontal niche along with the tissue affinity of the hydrogel structure created a favorable environment for active stem cell implantation and reprogramming. The alleviation of hypoxic and zinc deficient conditions then directly inhibited hPDLSC senescence by restoring DNA damage repair, modulating telomere homeostasis and reinforcing antioxidant defense, as well as curbing the expression of inflammatory factors. In this manner, the senescent periodontal niche was rejuvenated, resulting in robust tissue regeneration.

Current oxygenating materials face significant challenges that limit their biomedical application. First, efforts to augment oxygen generation frequently yield cytotoxic H2O2 byproducts, with acidic inflammatory microenvironments arising from glycolytic metabolism and lipid peroxidation further amplifying this detrimental cascade. To address this limitation, we constituted Mn-HCA enzyme-mimetic catalytic units. The Mn-based catalysis converts detrimental H2O2 into O2 through efficient disproportionation, simultaneously maximizing oxygen yield while suppressing cytotoxic radical formation. HCA, as a typical polyphenol compound, mitigates oxidative stress by directly scavenging free radicals through hydrogen atom transfer or electron donation from phenolic hydroxyl groups, while concomitantly upregulating endogenous antioxidant enzymes (e.g., SOD) via activation of the anti-inflammatory signaling pathway (e.g., Nrf2 and Sirt). These units concurrently enhance oxygen release and scavenge both endogenous and environmental H₂O₂, thereby significantly improving biosafety. Second, current oxygen-releasing materials exhibit inadequate mechanical integration characterized by poor tissue adhesion and compressive resistance in periodontal contexts. To overcome this limitation, we implemented surface-functionalized polyphenols on CaO₂ and ZIF8 NPs. This strategy constructed dense, dynamic chemical bond networks between polymer chains, substantially enhancing the compressive resistance of our Mn-hCaO₂/hZIF8/SMA hydrogel. The polyphenols enabled robust interfacial adhesion to both soft and mineralized tissues through dynamic hydrogen bonding with soft tissues and metal-coordination chelation with calcium ions in hard tissues. Consequently, our hydrogel demonstrates superior mechanical properties, rendering it more suitable for periodontal applications. Furthermore, existing CaO₂-based materials suffer from single-functionality and limited regenerative efficacy. Our study has shown that the Mn-HCA component in our hydrogel contributes to microbiotic homeostasis restoration and cellular senescence attenuation through telomere maintenance and oxidative injury defense. Concurrently, zinc supplied by hZIF8 further promotes tissue regeneration by combating oxidative stress while enhancing the proliferation and osteogenic differentiation of stem cells. In conclusion, this integrated platform delivers multifunctional therapeutic effects, including pro-osteogenic, anti-inflammatory, anti-senescence, and microbiota-regulating capabilities, thus enabling targeted treatment within complex inflammatory microenvironments.

Long-term stability and off-target effects remain central challenges in the design of microbiome-modulating biomaterials. To address these issues, we developed a hydrogel-based delivery strategy enabling sustained release of oxygen, zinc, and polyphenol, aimed at remodeling the dysbiotic microenvironment in PD. Oxygen supplementation enables selective targeting of aerobic pathogens without introducing exogenous microbes or broad-spectrum antimicrobials. Some polyphenol extracts have been shown to enrich gut probiotics including Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus, while their application within the oral microbiota has remained unexplored43,44. Here, we demonstrated that HCA contributes to favorable rebalancing of the periodontal microbial community by enriching probiotics. Together, these strategies minimize ecological disruption and support a gradual transition toward a stable and healthy microbiome. Microbiome off-target effects, or unintended consequences that occur in the microbial communities of non-targeted regions elsewhere in the body, can pose a concern in strategies that shift microbial balance45. In the present study, the confined application within periodontal pockets, ensured by tissue adhesion, was designed to minimize leakage into the oral cavity or gastrointestinal tract. Furthermore, the small applied volume and localized action also diminish risks of off-target impacts on non-targeted microbial communities. These findings highlight the potential of an injectable polyphenol-mediated oxygenating hydrogel system to restore ecological balance in situ. Nevertheless, our study is limited by its inability to fully recapitulate the impact of dietary variations on oral microbiome dynamics in humans, which may influence treatment outcomes. Future studies under clinically relevant conditions are warranted to validate this strategy as a precise and sustainable therapeutic intervention for PD.

In this study, the positive outcomes from the combined supply of oxygen and zinc exceeded the additive effects of each one alone, which indicates synergy between oxygen and zinc ions in alleviating hPDLSC senescence and boosting periodontal regeneration. The oxygen level, as a pivotal environmental component of the niche, can govern fundamental processes in tissue regeneration46,47. Consistent with prior findings, we found that oxygen supply is critical for reversing cellular senescence, facilitating cell cycle re-entry, and modulating periodontal microbiotic homeostasis. Transcriptomic sequencing of hPDLSCs further revealed that oxygenation significantly upregulated DNA repair processes. The DNA repair pathways, including nucleotide excision repair (NER), base excision repair (BER) and high-fidelity homologous recombination (HR), are highly reliant on aerobic ATP production48. In inflammatory conditions, energy deficiency and upregulation of HIF-1α cause conversion to a transcriptional program that suppresses HR while promoting error-prone repair mechanisms like non-homologous end joining (NHEJ), increasing cells’ susceptibility to senescence49. Thus, oxygen availability directly influences DNA repair pathway choice and senescence progression. However, oxygen supplementation can be a double-edged sword: excess oxygen may induce ROS generation and cause oxidative organelle damage. Zinc counteracts this risk by serving as an essential cofactor for multiple enzymes and transcription factors and contributing to the regulation of antioxidant defenses by supporting the activity of antioxidant enzymes15,50. Meanwhile, zinc is an essential cofactor required for the structural integrity and catalytic function of telomerase51. Our data also reflected that zinc supply can benefit telomere stability by influencing telomerase-related pathways. When supplied together, oxygen and zinc exhibit complementary functions: oxygen enables efficient zinc-dependent metabolic and synthetic reactions, while zinc mitigates potential oxidative stress. Besides these direct anti-senescent activities, our study also indicated that oxygen and zinc synergistically restore microbiotic homeostasis and promote immune modulation, establishing a pro-regenerative niche for stem cells. These mechanisms underlie the rejuvenating strategy demonstrated here for PD treatment. One limitation of the study is the inapplicability of classical synergy models in our multifunctional system owing to the functional inseparability of the HCA-modified components and composition-dependent mechanical changes of the hydrogels. Future work using dose-response analyses with defined elemental stimuli would facilitate quantitative synergy assessment of oxygen and zinc.

To conclude, the Mn-hCaO2/hZIF8/SMA hydrogel developed here achieved the combined effects of microbiotic restoration, immunomodulation and stem cell rejuvenation. The reestablishment of microbial and immune balance in the periodontal niche along with the hydrogel structure’s affinity for both soft and hard tissues in the moist oral cavity created a favorable environment for active stem cell implantation and differentiation. The hydrogel further actively modulated telomere homeostasis and the intracellular anti-oxidative system, eliminating stress-induced premature senescence. In summary, this study demonstrated an anti-senescent strategy for treating PD.

Materials

Lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP) and hydrocaffeic acid (HCA) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, USA. Zn(NO3)2·6H2O, MnCl2·4H2O, NH3·H2O and H2O2 were purchased from Kelong, China. CaCl2, 2-methylimidazole (2-MIM), N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF), glycidyl methacrylate (GMA), poly(ethylene glycol) diacrylate (PEGDA) and other chemical reagents were all purchased from Aladdin, China.

Preparation of CaO2, hCaO2 and Mn-hCaO2 NPs

Typically, 5 g of CaCl₂ was dissolved in 25 mL of deionized (DI) water. The mixture was then poured into 300 mL of ethanol under vigorous stirring. After 10 min, the pH was adjusted to 10 using NH₃·H₂O to create an alkaline environment. Subsequently, 25 mL of 30% H₂O₂ was added dropwise to initiate the reaction. Stirring was continued for 30 min. The product was collected by centrifugation at 5000 × g for 5 min, washed twice with ethanol, and dried at 60 °C to obtain CaO₂ nanoparticles. For hCaO₂, the same steps were followed with the inclusion of 0.05 g HCA in the initial solution. Mn-hCaO₂ was prepared based on the hCaO₂ procedure with an additional step. After the addition of H₂O₂ and 10 min of reaction, 30 mg of MnCl₂·4H₂O dissolved in 10 mL of DI water was added dropwise to the mixture. Stirring continued for another 30 min. The resulting product was centrifuged at 5000 × g for 5 min, washed twice with ethanol, and dried at 60 °C, yielding Mn-hCaO₂ nanoparticles.

Preparation of ZIF8 and hZIF8 NPs

ZIF8 and hZIF8 nanoparticles were synthesized via a one-pot method, with hZIF8 being derived from the basic ZIF8 procedure through the addition of HCA. In a typical synthesis, 4.516 g 2-MIM and 1 mL DMF were dissolved in 17 mL DI water. Separately, 0.232 g Zn (NO3)2·6H2O (Zn: 2-MIM = 1:70 in molar ratio) was dissolved in 2 mL DI water and added dropwise to the vigorously stirring 2-MIM solution. After the 3-h reaction, the product was collected by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 8 min, washed twice with DI water, and dried at 60 °C to obtain ZIF8 nanoparticles. For the preparation of hZIF8 nanoparticles, the 2-MIM/DMF mixture was initially dissolved in 15 mL of DI water. One minute after the addition of the zinc precursor, 5 mg of HCA dissolved in 2 mL of DI water was added dropwise to the reaction mixture. After the 3-h reaction, the light pink hZIF8 product was collected following the same centrifugation, washing, and drying steps.

Characterizations of NPs

The morphology of the nanoparticles (CaO2, hCaO2, Mn-hCaO2, ZIF8 and hZIF8) were observed by SEM (Gemini 300, Zeiss, Germany). The microstructure and elemental composition of the nanomaterials were obtained using transmission electron microscopy (TEM; Tecnai-F30, FEI, USA) equipped with energy dispersive spectrometer (super-X detector, FEI, USA). The crystal phases of the nanoparticles were assessed by X-ray diffraction (Rigaku Ultima IV, Rigaku, Japan). The chemical states of the nanoparticles were assessed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (ESCALAB 250Xi, Thermo Scientific, USA). The Zeta potential of the nanoparticles was assessed by a particle size & zeta potential analyzer (ZETA-AIZER, Malvern, UK).

Oxygen releasing profiles of Mn-hCaO2 NPs

For the oxygen releasing curve, 20 mg CaO2, hCaO2 or Mn-hCaO2 were dispersed with 10 mL PBS placed in a sealed container at 37 °C, respectively. The dissolved oxygen (DO) concentration of each sample was measured with a dissolved oxygen analyzer (JPBJ608, INESA, China) at the scheduled time (0, 0.5, 2, 5, 12, 24, 36, 48, 60, 78 and 96 h). The oxygen releasing curve was obtained with the DO concentration versus time. At the same scheduled time, the H2O2 concentration of the solution was measured with a H2O2 content assay kit (BC3595, Solarbio, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions at the scheduled time. Absorbance at 415 nm was acquired using a microplate reader (Varioskan LUX, Thermo Scientific, USA). The H2O2 concentration of the samples was calculated referring to a standard curve. The DO and H2O2 concentration in PBS were also tested as control under same conditions.

Catalytic activity of Mn-hCaO2 NPs

First, 20 mg CaO2, hCaO2 or Mn-hCaO2 were dispersed with 10 mL PBS placed in a sealed container at 37 °C until the oxygen release was completed, respectively. Then, the samples were placed in an anerobic chamber filled with N2 gas and 120 μL 30% H2O2 solution was added into each sample. The catalytic activity was evaluated with the DO concentration of each sample with a dissolved oxygen analyzer (JPBJ608, INESA, China) at the scheduled time (2, 15, 30, 60, 90, 150 min).

Antioxidant activity of Mn-hCaO2 NPs

The antioxidant acidities were evaluated with a tetramethylbenzidine (TMB)-based colorimetric method. 20 mg of CaO2, hCaO2 or Mn-hCaO2 was dissolved in 2.85 mL DI water and 0.15 mL 10 g L−1 TMB working solution (T9230, Solarbio, China), respectively. Then, the samples were incubated for 4 h at 37 °C in dark environment. The radical scavenging capability was evaluated with the absorbance change at 652 nm using a microplate reader (Varioskan LUX, Thermo Scientific, USA).

Preparation of Mn-hCaO2/hZIF8/SMA hydrogel

The preparation of the SMA matrix was based on the method first described by Kim et al.52. To degum the silk fibers, silk cocoons were boiled in a 0.25 wt% Na2CO3 solution for 30 min and washed with DI water, which was repeated for twice. The degummed silk fibers were dried at 60 °C overnight. Afterwards, 20 g degummed silk fibers were dissolved in 200 mL of 9.3 M LiBr solution in a 70 °C water bath for the extraction of SF. For the synthesis of SMA, after the SF was extracted with the LiBr solution, the mixture was transferred to a 50 °C water bath. Then, 16 mL GMA was added dropwise to start the methacryloyl modification reaction. The stirring continued for 3 h and then 200 mL DI water was added to end the reaction. After that, the mixture was transferred into a dialysis bag (molecular weight cutoff of 14000) for 7 days. The deionized water was refreshed every 8 h. SMA was stored under dry and photophobic conditions. A 15% (w/v) SMA solution was first prepared by dissolving 0.45 g of SMA in 3 mL of deionized water. Subsequently, 50 μL PEGDA, 8 mg LAP, 20 mg Mn-hCaO2 NPs and 10 mg hZIF8 NPs were added and thoroughly mixed. This solution was injected into the desired mold or locations and exposed to 395 nm light (10 W) for 15 s to induce gelation. The detailed composition and concentrations of each hydrogel were provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Characterization of hydrogels

The morphology and elemental composition of different hydrogels were examined with SEM equipped with an energy dispersive spectrometer (Gemini 300, Zeiss, Germany). For sample preparation, the hydrogels were freeze-dried at −70 °C and sectioned to expose the internal structures. Then, the hydrogel sections were treated with gold sputtering by a sputter coater (JFC-1200, JEOL, Japan) before SEM observation.

Rheology test of hydrogels

The dynamic rheological tests of the hydrogels were characterized using a rheometer (MC2302, Anton Paar, Austria) at room temperature. The viscosity of the initial solution was measured with changed rotate speed (rad/s). To track the crosslinking process, the storage modulus G’ (Pa) and loss modulus G” (Pa) changed with time was measured under 10% strain. Then, the hydrogels were prepared in a cylindrical shape with a height of 15 mm and a diameter of 1 mm. Strain amplitude sweeps (0.01–100%) were first performed to determine the linear viscoelasticity region. Dynamic frequency sweeps were performed at angular velocities ranging from 0.1 to 10 Hz at 1.0% strain amplitude.

Compression test of hydrogels

The compressive tests were carried out with a universal test machine (INSTRON 5567, Instron, USA) equipped with a 100 N load cell. The hydrogels were prepared in a cylindrical shape with a height of 11 mm and a diameter of 15 mm. For compressive test, the hydrogels were compressed at a loading rate of 5 mm/min and maximum strain was 80%. For cyclic compressive test, the hydrogels were compressed to a fixed strain of 60% at a loading rate of 5 mm/min and then relaxed for recovery. A total of 30 cycles were conducted. Isolation, culture and stimulation of hPDLSCs

The hPDLSCs were isolated from healthy periodontal ligament tissues of human premolars obtained following therapeutic extraction for orthodontic reasons. All donor teeth were free of dental diseases, and the patients provided informed consent and had no history of systemic diseases. Periodontal ligament tissue was gently scraped from the middle third of the tooth root using a sterile scalpel and then digested with 3 g L−1 type I collagenase (Gibco, USA) in minimum essential medium α (α-MEM) (Gibco, USA) at 37 °C for 30 min. Digestion was stopped by adding an equal volume of complete α-MEM medium, followed by another centrifugation step. The pellet was resuspended in complete α‑MEM culture medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (Gemini, USA), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, USA) and 1% glutamine (Gibco, USA). The cells were maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator, with medium changes every 2 days. Cells were passaged upon reaching 80% confluence using 0.25% trypsin, and passages 3–5 were used for subsequent experiments. For cell-hydrogel co-culture experiments, the cells were seeded with a density of 5 × 103 well−1 for 96-well plate or 5 × 104 well−1 for 24-well plate and co-cultured with respective hydrogels (1 mm height, diameter in line with the well). Except the normal group, 1 mg L−1 LPS (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) stimuli was added to the cultural medium after cell attachment to mimic an inflammatory microenvironment.

Cellular senescence and intracellular ROS labeling of hPDLSCs

After 48 h of LPS stimulation, cellular senescence of hPDLSCs were evaluated with β-Galactosidase staining using a Senescence-associated β-Galactosidase Staining Kit (C0602, Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Intracellular ROS of hPDLSCs was labeled by DCFH-DA probe (2′,7′-dichloro fluorescein diacetate) using a ROS assay kit (S0033S, Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

DPPH free radical scavenging assay of the hydrogels

The free radical scavenging of the hydrogels was tested with a DPPH free radical scavenging assay (SD9360, Solarbio, China). The hydrogels were prepared in a cylindrical shape with a height of 9 mm and a diameter of 2 mm.Then, the hydrogel was added to 3 mL DPPH solution prepared at a concentration of 0.04 g L−1 with methanol. These samples were then placed in an oven at 37 °C in the dark for 120 min. Subsequently, the DPPH solution was extracted and its absorbance was measured using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer at a wavelength of 516 nm.

Proliferation of hPDLSCs

Cell proliferation on 1st, 3rd and 5th day was evaluated by CCK8 assay (K1018, APExBIO, USA). After washing with PBS, each well was incubated with a mixture of CCK8 reagent and α-MEM (1:10) at 37 °C for 2 h. Cell proliferation was evaluated by the absorbance at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Varioskan LUX, Thermo Scientific, USA).

Osteogenic differentiation of hPDLSCs

The osteogenic differentiation medium was prepared with 10 mM β-sodium glycerophosphate (Sigma, USA), 50 mg L−1 L-ascorbic acid (Sigma, USA) and 100 nM dexamethasone (Sigma, USA). After 14 days of induction, cells were fixed with 4% PFA and ALP staining was performed with a BCIP/NBT Alkaline Phosphatase Color Development Kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol (C3206, Beyotime, China).

Macrophages polarization

Human myeloid leukemia mononuclear cells, THP-1, were generously offered by the State Key Laboratory of Oral Diseases, West China Hospital of Stomatology. The THP-1 were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) Medium 1640 (Gibco, USA) containing 10 % FBS (Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, USA) in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C. The cells were seeded with a density of 5 × 104 well−1 for 24-well plate and co-cultured with respective hydrogels (1 mm height, diameter in line with the well). 100 μg L−1 Phorbol 12-Myristate13-Acetate (PMA, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was applied to induce THP-1 to form macrophages. Except the normal group, 1 mg L−1 LPS (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) stimuli was added to the cultural medium after cell attachment to mimic an inflammatory microenvironment. Macrophages polarization was examined on the 48 h after stimulation.

Transcriptome sequencing

hPDLSCs treated with different group of hydrogels were collected at the 48th hour after 1 mg L−1 LPS stimuli. The experiment was conducted with 4 independent replicates. The cell samples were treated with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) and frozen in liquid nitrogen. RNA sequencing was performed using Illumina HiSeq X10 (Illumina). Bioinformatic analysis was performed using the OmicStudio tools (https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool).

Animal experiment

A total of 60 eight-weeks-old male Sprague Dawley rats were used for construction of ligament-induced PD model. Sex was not considered as a biological variable in this study. Only male rats were used, primarily to maintain consistency with the established protocols of prior foundational work in this model, while also reducing animal use and focusing resources on the core therapeutic question. The rats were randomly divided into six groups, including (1) normal control, (2) PD, (3) PD + SMA, (4) PD+hZIF8/SMA, (5) PD+Mn-hCaO2/SMA and (6) PD+Mn-hCaO2/hZIF8/SMA. The investigators were blinded to the group allocation during data collection and analysis. For PD model construction, the rats were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (30 mg kg−1). The 3-0# non-absorbable surgical suture was ligated to the dental cervix of the maxillary first molar and then pressed into the gingival sulcus for 4 weeks until experimental PD was formed. For hydrogel application, 20 μL premixed precursor solution was injected into the periodontal pocket and crosslinked with blue-violet light (395 nm, 10 W) for 15 s. Samples were collected 4 weeks after treatment. The maxilla was collected and fixed with 4% PFA for observation and histology. Fresh alveolar bone tissue surrounding the first molar was collected in liquid nitrogen for RNA extraction. Liver, kidney, spleen and cervical lymph node tissues were collected and fixed with 4% PFA for biotoxicity analysis.

Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) analysis

Harvested maxilla samples were scanned by a micro-CT scanner (μCT 50, SCANCO, Switzerland). Scanning parameters was set as 70 kV for voltage, 200 μA for current and 10 μm for voxel size. Micro-CT reconstruction and quantitative analysis was performed with the equipped analyzing system. Alveolar bone height (ABC-CEJ) was measured by the distance from the top of alveolar ridge to the cementum-enamel junction. BMD and bone volume to tissue volume (BV/TV) of the furcation area were also analyzed.

Realtime quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA of alveolar bone tissue or cultured cells was extracted with RNAiso reagent (9108, Takara, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The total RNA concentration was measured with a micro-spectrophotometer (NanoDrop One C, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Then, the total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using PrimeScript RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (RR047A, Takara, Japan). RT-qPCR was performed with QuantStudio™ 6 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with SYBR Premix Ex Taq II (RR420L, Takara, Japan). Relative gene quantitation was calculated with the 2−ΔΔCT formula. The primers are listed in Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Table 3.

Histology

Harvested maxilla samples were fixed with 4% PFA and decalcified in 14% EDTA (pH 7.4) for 8 weeks. The tissues were dehydrated and embedded in paraffin and sectioned by 5-μm thickness with a microtome (HistoCore Biocut R, Leica, Germany). H&E staining (C0105, Beyotime, China), Masson’s trichrome staining (G1340, Solarbio, China) was conducted using paraffin sections according to the manufacturers’ instructions. For immunohistochemical staining, after antigen retrieval with pepsin at 37 °C and peroxidase blocking (MXB, China), sections were incubated with primary antibody at 4 °C overnight. Detection was performed using the EnVision two‑step system (GK500705, Dako, Denmark), followed by 3,3’-Diaminobenzidine (DAB) development (SW1040, Solarbio, China). Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and mounted. Immunofluorescence was performed on frozen sections. The tissues were imbedded in optimal cutting temperature compound (OCT, Sakura, USA) and sectioned by 5-μm thickness with or a cryostat (CM 3050, Leica, Germany). After rewarming and washing, sections were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 2 h and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. Following PBS washes, sections were incubated with fluorophore‑conjugated secondary antibody for 2 h. Nuclei were counterstained with 1 mg L−1 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (28718-90-3, Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Sections were dehydrated and mounted with an anti‑fade mounting medium (Solarbio, China). Details for used antibodies in immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence were provided in Supplementary Table 4. β-Galactosidase staining was performed on frozen sections using a Senescence β-Galactosidase Staining Kit (C0602, Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Images were acquired with an optimal microscope (Ni-E, Nikon, Japan) or a laser scanning confocal microscope (N-STORM Ti2 A1+, Nikon, Japan).

GSH/GSSG assay

Fresh alveolar bone tissue surrounding the first molar was collected and grounded in liquid nitrogen. Then, GSH/GSSG ratio was measured by a commercial GSH and GSSG Assay Kit (Beyotime, S0053, China) based on the enzymatic recycling method with 5,5’-dithiobis(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB).

16 s ribosomal RNA sequencing

Subgingival biofilm samples were collected after 7 days after hydrogel application. Silk sutures were pressed to gingival sulcus 24 h in prior for accumulating the microorganisms. The genomic DNA was extracted and the V4 region hypervariable regions of the 16S rDNA were amplified with PCR. The PCR products were quantified with PicoGreen dsDNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen, USA) and then sequenced by NovaSeq PE250 platform (Novogene, China). Data possession was performed based on QIIME2 platform. Bioinformatic analysis was performed using the Novomagic platform (https://magic.novogene.com).

In vitro antibacterial assay

Porphyromonas gingivalis (ATCC 33277) was routinely cultured under anaerobic conditions (85% N₂, 10% H₂, 5% CO₂) at 37 °C in brain heart infusion (BHI) broth supplemented with 5 mg L−1 hemin and 1 mg L−1 vitamin K. The bacteria were suspended with a density 1 × 108 CFU mL−1 and co-cultured with different groups of respective hydrogels (1 mm height, diameter in line with the 24-well plate). After 24 h of anaerobic incubation, the bacteria were harvested by centrifugation at 6000 × g for 10 min and washed twice with PBS. Bacterial viability was confirmed by CFU measured with optical density at 600 nm. For SEM imaging, bacterial pellets were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde over night at 4 °C. Samples were then dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (30%, 50%, 70%, 90%, and 100%, 10 min each). Then they were mounted on silicon wafers, sputter-coated with gold, and observed under a scanning electron microscope.

Statistical analysis

All results were analyzed by a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with a Tukey’s post hoc test. The data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) with n ≥ 3. Differences were considered statistically significant for P < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, USA) and Origin 2021 (OriginLab, USA).
