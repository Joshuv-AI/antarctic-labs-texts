Family history of haematological malignancy and prognosis across non‐Hodgkin lymphoma subtypes
Authors: George A Cholack, Raphael Mwangi, Prokop Vodička, Thomas M Habermann, Verena Hinder, Dennis P Robinson, Andrew L Feldman, Thomas E Witzig, Jose C Villasboas, Stephen M Ansell, Brian K Link, Yucai Wang, Carrie A Thompson, Susan L Slager, Matthew Maurer, Anne J Novak, James R Cerhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryFamily history of haematological malignancy (FHHM) reflects inherited susceptibility to non‐Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL), but its prognostic significance remains poorly understood. We used a cohort study of 1994 NHL patients to evaluate associations between FHHM and clinical outcomes across lymphoma subtypes. We estimated associations, adjusted for sex and lymphoma‐specific prognostic index, of self‐reported FHHM in a first‐degree relative with overall survival (OS), event‐free survival (EFS) and lymphoma‐specific survival (LSS) using multivariable Cox regression models and with failure to achieve EFS at 24 months (EFS24) using logistic regression. FHHM prevalence was 12.8%, and the median follow‐up among survivors was 12.0 years. FHHM was not associated with OS. In subtype‐specific analyses, FHHM was associated with inferior LSS (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.98, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.11–3.53) in follicular lymphoma (FL); this association and the association with EFS24 failure strengthened among those treated with front‐line immunochemotherapy (EFS24 failure odds ratio = 2.57, 95% CI 1.08–5.99; LSS HR = 2.68, 95% CI 1.09–6.58). In mantle cell lymphoma, FHHM was associated with inferior EFS (HR = 1.70, 95% CI 1.02–2.85). Interaction testing did not demonstrate significant heterogeneity of FHHM effects by lymphoma subtype. FHHM may be associated with adverse outcomes in select NHL groups, particularly FL. These findings warrant further investigation of germline and environmental factors potentially contributing to more aggressive FLs.Keywords: family history, non‐Hodgkin lymphoma, prognosis, survival

INTRODUCTION

Non‐Hodgkin lymphomas (NHL) are a heterogeneous group of B‐ and T‐cell malignancies with diverse molecular features. 1 Prognosis varies across subtypes and is influenced by tumour biology, host factors and treatment response. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 While many of these factors have been incorporated into evolving risk stratification tools, most do not include germline and environmental factors. Family history of haematological malignancy (FHHM) is often routinely collected in clinical practice, of great interest to patients, and may offer additional insight into NHL prognostication and risk stratification at diagnosis.

FHHM is known to reflect inherited susceptibility to NHL and shared early environments that can influence lymphomagenesis. 7 , 8 , 9 Several case–control studies have shown that individuals with first‐degree relatives with lymphoproliferative disorders, multiple myeloma or leukaemia are at increased risk for developing various subtypes of lymphoma 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 ; these risk estimates were largely unaffected after adjustment for a variety of putative lymphoma risk factors. 9 , 14 Furthermore, multiple susceptibility loci have been identified from genome‐wide association studies (GWASs) for various NHLs, including diffuse large B‐cell (DLBCL), follicular (FL), marginal zone (MZL) and certain T‐cell (TCL) lymphomas. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21

In contrast, there have been fewer studies investigating the prognostic impact of FHHM 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 or germline genetics 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 across common NHL subtypes. Baecklund et al. 22 demonstrated that patients with indolent lymphomas with a first‐degree relative with the disease tended to have concordant overall survival (OS) relative to their first‐degree relative with lymphoma, supporting a role for inherited factors. Additionally, Anderson et al. 23 illustrated that TCL/anaplastic lymphoma patients with a family history of NHL had inferior OS at 5 and 10 years compared to patients lacking such a family history, although this finding was only based on five patients with a family history of NHL. Apart from these findings, the cohort studies evaluating FHHM as a prognostic factor for OS 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 and lymphoma‐specific survival (LSS) 24 , 25 have been null. Genome‐wide 30 , 34 and candidate gene association studies 26 , 31 , 32 , 33 of prognosis have implicated a variety of biological pathways, including immune function, drug metabolism, DNA repair and angiogenesis, but largely do not overlap susceptibility loci. 35 The existing literature exploring associations of FHHM and NHL prognosis is limited in several ways, including that the majority of patients were diagnosed before 2000, which was prior to contemporary NHL classification and treatment advances over the past two decades. 1 To address these limitations, we analysed a rituximab‐era, prospective cohort study, which included front‐line treatment data and adjustment for contemporary disease‐specific prognostic indices. Additionally, this is the first study to evaluate associations between FHHM and a broader range of clinically relevant end‐points, including event‐free survival (EFS), event‐free survival at 24 months (EFS24), OS and LSS across common NHL subtypes (DLBCL, FL, MZL, mantle cell lymphoma [MCL] and T cell). We hypothesized that NHL patients with an FHHM would have similar prognosis to those without an FHHM, regardless of NHL subtype.

METHODS

Study population

For this prospective cohort study, we utilized Mayo Clinic cases enrolled in the University of Iowa/Mayo Clinic SPORE (Specialized Programs of Research Excellence) Molecular Epidemiology Resource from 2002 to 2015, as previously described. 36 Briefly, consecutive NHL patients, aged 18 years and older and who were residents of Minnesota, Iowa or Wisconsin at the time of diagnosis were prospectively approached within 9 months of diagnosis for enrolment into this cohort study. Pathology was reviewed by a haematopathologist and classified according to the World Health Organization criteria. 37 All participants provided written informed consent, and the cohort protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the Mayo Clinic.

Exposure assessment and treatment characteristics

FHHM status was assessed upon enrolment when patients completed a risk‐factor questionnaire. Participants were asked to report any first‐degree relative with a cancer, with a request to specifically include Hodgkin lymphoma (HL), NHL, multiple myeloma, and any other cancer (Figure S1). To harmonize with InterLymph, 38 we defined FHHM as having a first‐degree relative with HL, NHL, multiple myeloma or leukaemia. Patients with missing FHHM were excluded. Proportions of patients completing this questionnaire at various time points post‐enrolment are reported in Table S1. All clinical and treatment data abstraction adhered to standardized protocols. 36 We report the frequencies of patients who received first‐line chemotherapy, with details by lymphoma subtype described in the Supplemental Methods.

Clinical end‐points and follow‐up

The primary end‐points were OS and EFS. We defined OS as time from NHL diagnosis to death from any cause. We defined EFS as time from NHL diagnosis to disease relapse, re‐treatment and/or initiation of second‐line therapy or death from any cause. 36 Secondary end‐points were LSS (time from diagnosis to death from lymphoma or lymphoma treatment‐related complications) and failure to achieve 24 months of event‐free survival (EFS24; an end‐point associated with aggressive disease and inferior outcomes). 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 Patients were contacted every 6 months for the first 3 years and then annually thereafter to ascertain disease progression, new treatments used, disease transformation and any new cancer diagnoses and all reported events were validated against medical records. 36 Through 2024, only 1% of the cohort has been lost to follow up.

Statistical analysis

The chi‐squared test and two‐sided Wilcoxon rank sum tests were used to determine associations between FHHM with categorical and continuous variables respectively. Cochran–Armitage trend tests were performed to assess linear trends across levels of ordinal variables (age and body mass index [BMI] groups). We used multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analysis to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association of FHHM with OS, EFS and LSS. The proportional hazard assumption for the covariates was tested by Schoenfeld residuals. If the assumption was violated, then the final models included estimates stratified by the violating covariate. We used logistic regression to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CI for the association with failure to achieve EFS24. All models were adjusted for sex and a lymphoma‐specific prognostic index: International Prognostic Index (IPI) 44 for DLBCL, Follicular Lymphoma International Prognostic Index (FLIPI) 45 for FL, mucosa‐associated lymphoid tissue International Prognostic Index 46 for MZL, Simplified Mantle Cell Lymphoma International Prognostic Index (sMIPI) 47 for MCL and IPI 44 for TCL. We performed likelihood ratio tests comparing nested regression models with and without FHHM‐subtype interaction terms. In a second set of models, we further adjusted for BMI and smoking status, as these factors have been associated with inferior OS and LSS in NHL patients. 48 We did not include age as a covariate in the models, as it was determined a priori to be collinear with the prognostic indices; however, we did perform a sensitivity analysis by including age (continuous variable) in the models to evaluate any possible effect. All reported p‐values were two‐sided, and differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using R version 4.2.2. ChatGPT was used to proofread the manuscript.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

The cohort consisted of 1994 NHL patients, including 650 DLBCL, 679 FL, 291 MZL, 178 MCL and 196 TCL patients. Baseline characteristics, stratified by NHL subtype, are presented in Table 1. The proportion of males ranged from 43.0% for MZL to 77.0% for MCL. The median age at diagnosis ranged from 58 (TCL) to 66 years (MCL). Most patients in each NHL subtype had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status <2. At a median follow‐up of 12.0 years (among survivors), 1202 (60%) participants experienced an event, and 807 (40%) participants had died. The median time to questionnaire completion for the entire cohort was 29 days, and 81.5% of patients had completed the questionnaire by 90 days. Additionally, the median time to questionnaire completion as well as the proportion of patients completing the questionnaire by various time points (14, 30, 60, 90, 180 and 180+ days) did not significantly differ between those with and without a FHHM (Table S1).

Baseline characteristics, stratified by FHHM

Baseline characteristics of the cohort stratified by FHHM status are presented in Table 2, with details by NHL subtype in Table S2. Briefly, 256 (12.8%) patients reported an FHHM, with this proportion ranging from 9.7% for TCL to 14% for FL. Collectively, NHL patients with an FHHM (median age 65 years) were significantly older at diagnosis than those without an FHHM (median age 62 years; p = 0.001). The proportion of each NHL subtype did not substantively vary between those with and without an FHHM. The distributions of smoking status, BMI group and ECOG performance status did not significantly differ between NHL patients with and without a FHHM. The distributions of those receiving front‐line chemotherapy did not significantly vary within each lymphoma subtype between patients with and without a FHHM (Table S2).

Survival outcomes

There were no significant associations between FHHM and OS for any NHL subtype (Table 3). The associations of FHHM with OS in MZL (HR 1.56, 95% CI 0.89–2.75) and in TCL (HR 0.59, 95% CI 0.28–1.24) showed effect estimates of notable magnitude but had wide CIs. For EFS, FHHM was associated with inferior EFS in MCL patients (HR 1.70, 95% CI 1.02–2.85; Table 3). FHHM was also associated with inferior EFS in MZL (HR 1.38, 95% CI 0.90–2.13), although this association did not reach statistical significance. In FL, FHHM was associated with inferior LSS (HR = 1.98, 95% CI 1.11–3.53; Table 3). FHHM was also associated with inferior LSS in MZL (HR 1.95, 95% CI 0.53–7.14), MCL (HR 1.41, 95% CI 0.68–2.95) and with superior LSS in TCL (HR 0.45, 95% CI 0.14–1.45), although none of these associations were statistically significant, likely in part due to a small number of events. Finally, FHHM was associated with failure to achieve EFS24 in FL (OR 1.55, 95% CI 0.97–2.44, p = 0.07; Table 4) and in MCL (OR 1.97, 95% CI 0.73–5.28), although neither association was statistically significant. Further adjustment for BMI and smoking status did not significantly alter these results (data not reported). A sensitivity analysis for these analyses by further adjusting the models for age did not significantly change the results (data not reported). We performed likelihood ratio tests comparing nested regression models with and without FHHM–subtype interaction terms, and there was no statistically significant interaction between FHHM and subtype for association with OS, EFS, LSS, or EFS24 failure (data not reported).

Given treatment heterogeneity across these subtypes, we conducted a post hoc analysis restricting to patients receiving front‐line chemotherapy to assess the associations of FHHM with EFS24 failure, LSS and EFS (Table 5). In FL, the associations of FHHM with inferior LSS and EFS24 failure strengthened and were significant in the immunochemotherapy (IC) treated subset (LSS HR 2.68, 95% CI 1.09–6.58; EFS24 failure OR 2.57, 95% CI 1.08–5.99). For DLBCL, MCL, MZL and TCL, the chemotherapy‐restricted estimates for LSS were consistent with the direction of effect estimates from the primary analysis. This was also true for the associations of FHHM with EFS24 failure in DLBCL, MZL and TCL. However, in MCL, the magnitude of the association of FHHM with failure to achieve EFS24 greatly attenuated in the chemotherapy‐treated group (OR 1.15, 95% CI 0.32–3.68). When restricted to chemotherapy‐treated patients, the effect of the association of FHHM with EFS in MCL was attenuated (HR 1.41, 95% CI 0.77–2.58) and not significant, while the remaining subtype‐specific estimates were concordant with the primary analysis. Given the findings for FHHM with inferior LSS and EFS24 failure among IC‐treated FL patients, we performed an additional post hoc analysis to explore these associations in FL patients who were observed (Table S3). Similarly, FHHM was associated with inferior LSS and higher EFS24 failure, but the effect estimates were smaller in magnitude and had wide CIs, suggesting limited power.

DISCUSSION

There were four key findings of this study. First, the prevalence of NHL patients with a self‐reported FHHM was 12.8%. Second, FHHM was not significantly associated with OS in any NHL subtype. Although formal interaction testing did not demonstrate statistically significant heterogeneity of FHHM effects by subtype, subtype‐specific analyses were undertaken to explore potential differences in direction and magnitude of associations. Third, in subtype‐specific analyses, FHHM was associated with a greater likelihood of EFS24 failure (borderline statistically significant) and inferior LSS in FL, with both findings strengthening among those treated with front‐line IC. Lastly, a subgroup analysis of MCL patients was notable for an inferior association of FHHM with EFS while weak and statistically non‐significant associations were observed with EFS24 failure and LSS.

The overall prevalence of FHHM in this cohort was 12.8%, which is slightly higher compared to other studies. In a large case–control study, 9 9.1% of participants were reported to have an FHHM (same definition as our study). The slightly lower prevalence is consistent with a lower median age of their cases. Other studies have reported family history prevalence ranging from 0.9% to 5%. 22 , 23 , 25 These differences are likely due to varying family history definitions (e.g. any lymphoma in Anderson et al., 23 NHL in Ji et al. 24 and any haematological malignancy in Simard et al. 25 ), NHL classification and source of family history.

The lack of significant associations of FHHM with OS across NHL subtypes is clinically important, as patients often seek to know how their family history may impact their own prognosis. These results were concordant with the majority of the results from other studies, 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 although our TCL results differed from Anderson et al. 23 who demonstrated that TCL/anaplastic lymphoma patients with a family history of NHL had inferior OS at 5 and 10 years. This could be due to different types of TCL included, enrolment period and sample size differences. These results will require replication in larger, well‐annotated TCL cohorts. Additionally, the direction and magnitude of associations between FHHM and inferior OS, EFS and LSS in MZL were noteworthy, although these findings are preliminary given a limited number of events. Given that prior studies 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 were unable to separately evaluate MZL patients, replication in larger MZL cohorts is needed.

FL patients with FHHM were more likely to fail to achieve EFS24, and to have significantly inferior LSS, independent of FLIPI and sex. These novel findings strengthened when restricted to front‐line IC‐treated patients, who are generally patients with higher tumour burden and more aggressive disease. Of the studies 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 examining associations of FHHM with prognosis, only Simard et al. 25 separately analysed FL patients and found no difference in OS or LSS between those with and without an FHHM. This discrepancy may be due to their smaller sample size and/or enrolment timeframe (e.g. 1999–2002). Notably, none of these studies 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 assessed FHHM in relation to EFS24 failure, a prognostic marker of poor outcome for OS in IC‐treated FL patients. 39 , 49 Progression of disease within 24 months has been consistently validated as a critical predictor of long‐term outcomes in both IC‐ and non‐IC‐treated patients across numerous retrospective studies, registries and clinical trial settings. 39 , 50 , 51 , 52 , 53 , 54 Ghesquieres et al. 34 recently identified two loci associated with inferior EFS in GWAS among IC‐treated FL. Additionally, in a candidate gene study, Charbonneau et al. 26 showed that FL patients receiving rituximab and carrying specific variants in CFH (rs3766404) and CFHR5 (rs6694672) had inferior EFS. Finally, it is important to note that the majority of patients in our cohort predate the widespread adoption of bendamustine–rituximab as a front‐line regimen for untreated FL, which underscores how our findings largely reflect outcomes for regimens excluding bendamustine. 55 , 56 Albeit requiring validation in larger genomically annotated cohorts, our findings suggest that FHHM may be a potentially novel and accessible marker of patients with FL who may benefit from more intensive monitoring or earlier consideration of novel therapies.

MCL patients with an FHHM demonstrated significantly inferior EFS independent of sMIPI and sex. Similar, but insignificant, trends were observed for FHHM with inferior EFS24 and LSS. None of the previously described studies 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 analysed EFS nor EFS24 in patients with MCL. Our EFS and EFS24 failure findings may have clinical relevance, as it has been shown by Wang et al. 43 that MCL patients who fail EFS24 tend to have an inferior overall prognosis, supporting further evaluation of these associations in larger cohorts. While there have been no GWASs in MCL investigating susceptibility loci or germline loci associated with survival, a whole‐exome candidate wide sequencing study 57 showed that pathogenic variants in ATM and TP53 were associated with increased risk of developing MCL. Additionally, a candidate gene study 29 in MCL found that inherited variation in genes coding for tumour necrosis factor and NF‐κB (nuclear factor kappa‐light‐chain‐enhancer of activated B cells) was associated with OS. The results of these studies 29 , 57 provide early but incomplete evidence for a role of shared genetics influencing risk of developing MCL and tumour biology that may contribute to more aggressive disease and poor outcomes, which requires further study.

This study had several strengths. To our knowledge, this is the largest analysis of a prospective cohort study to evaluate associations of FHHM with NHL prognosis in the rituximab era that included treatment data and adjustment for NHL subtype‐specific prognostic indices. Other strengths included expert pathology review and classification according to the World Health Organization criteria, 37 validated clinical event and outcome data, well‐validated survival outcomes not included in other studies (e.g. EFS24 failure) and a long duration of follow‐up. Limitations of this study include the potential for residual confounding; self‐reported FHHM status and potential misclassification; not accounting for which first‐degree relative constituted the positive FHHM for an indexed patient; lack of data for the age of onset of haematological malignancy in the first‐degree relative (due to missing data); and the potential for false positives due to multiple hypothesis testing. All of our findings will require independent replication. Furthermore, we did not have the ability to report on individual effects of family history of concordant NHL subtype on outcomes. Lastly, the TCL results are more challenging to interpret given the heterogeneity of diseases within this group. All of our results will require and benefit from independent replication in larger cohorts to achieve greater statistical power, particularly for rarer subtypes such as MCL and TCL.

In summary, FHHM was not associated with OS across any examined NHL subtype. Subtype‐specific analyses revealed associations between FHHM and inferior EFS in MCL and FL. Additionally, FL‐specific analyses revealed associations of FHHM with a higher likelihood of EFS24 failure and inferior LSS, particularly among patients treated with front‐line IC. Genetic or environmental factors associated with FHHM may underlie pathways influencing disease aggressiveness and early clinical failure in FL. Large prospective studies in well‐annotated cohorts are warranted to confirm these findings.
