Machine learning based prognostic model for oral squamous cell carcinoma using SEER data and external validation
Authors: Yangxiao Zhang, Hongsheng Liu, Yixuan Liao, Zhenxing Su, Luwen Song, Zhenghao Ma, Zhi qiang Pan, Yunqi Chen, Mingxun Xia, Jiancheng Li, Lina Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryOral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) is characterized by an insidious onset, pronounced aggressiveness, and a substantial impact on patient survival. Current prognostication relies heavily on the TNM staging system, which often lacks precision. To address this, we developed and validated a machine learning (ML)-based prognostic nomogram. Analyzing 8,927 patients from the SEER database, we employed ML algorithms (LASSO, XGBoost, random forest (RF), support vector machine (SVM)) to identify key determinants, including age, race, sex, grade, and TNM stage, and constructed a Cox-based risk model. Crucially, benchmarking analysis demonstrated that our model achieved a C-index of 0.714, significantly outperforming the traditional TNM staging system (C-index: 0.665, p < 0.001). These findings indicate that integrating demographic and pathological features with ML techniques yields superior risk stratification compared to anatomical staging alone. This nomogram offers a precise tool for personalized survival assessment and clinical decision-making.

Introduction

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) is the predominant subtype of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, with over 500,000 new cases and more than 280,000 deaths reported globally each year.1,2,3 OSCC arises from the mucosal epithelium of the oral cavity and exhibits highly malignant and locally invasive behavior, often accompanied by early cervical lymph node metastasis. These features markedly impair patients’ quality of life, with a 5-year overall survival (OS) rate ranging from 40% to 60%.4,5 Because high rates of recurrence and metastasis in advanced stages substantially contribute to treatment failure, developing efficient and reliable methods for accurate prognostic prediction is critical for optimizing individualized treatment strategies and enhancing patient survival.

Prognostic assessment of OSCC has traditionally relied on the TNM staging system; however, TNM classification primarily reflects tumor burden and local invasion, without fully incorporating the biological characteristics of tumor cells or individual patient variability, which may limit the accuracy of treatment and prognostic evaluation. To address this, researchers have developed various predictive models to augment traditional staging. For instance, clinical nomograms have been constructed using demographic and surgical factors; yet, they often rely on single-center cohorts with limited sample sizes.6,7 Molecular models based on gene signatures or biomarkers offer robust accuracy,8,9 but their reliance on expensive and time-consuming testing restricts routine clinical deployment. Additionally, while radiomics-based models have emerged as non-invasive tools,10 most lack strict independent external validation.

In contrast, risk prediction models built using large-scale clinical datasets and advanced machine learning (ML) techniques offer more precise estimation of survival outcomes and prognostic risk, demonstrating high predictive accuracy and potential clinical applicability.11,12 Additionally, such models can be applied to assess tumor metastasis and recurrence, evaluate the efficacy of adjuvant therapies, and predict various survival outcomes.13,14,15,16 Nevertheless, ML-based prognostic models derived from the SEER database with external validation using local clinical data remain scarce. Given the substantial differences in survival across demographic groups, constructing models that account for such variability and ensure equitable predictions is particularly important. In this study, we analyzed clinical data from patients with OSCC to identify key prognostic factors and applied ML methodologies to develop and validate a risk prediction model, providing a robust framework for individualized survival assessment and clinical decision-making.

Results

Feature selection

Key clinical features associated with OS in patients with OSCC were identified using machine-learning approaches—XGBoost, support vector machine (SVM), and random forest (RF)—and are listed in Table 1. To synthesize the feature importance across different algorithms, the mean SHAP value for each variable was calculated. Based on our predefined threshold (mean SHAP >0.01), seven robust variables met the criteria: age, Stage_T, Stage_N, grade, race, sex, and Stage_M. Conversely, specific distant metastases, including lung, bone, and liver, exhibited negligible predictive contributions (all mean SHAP <0.002) and were therefore systematically excluded by the ML screening. Simultaneously, LASSO regression initially retained 9 predictors: sex, age, race, grade, Stage_T, Stage_N, Stage_M, liver metastasis, and lung metastasis. By taking the strict intersection of these LASSO-retained variables and the threshold-filtered ML features, liver and lung metastasis were eliminated. Construction of a risk prediction model

Based on the modeling cohort, the selected clinical features were incorporated into a nomogram to establish an OS risk-prediction model for patients with OSCC. Internal evaluation and internal validation of risk prediction models

Using the C-index, time-dependent area under the ROC curve (5-year AUC), 5-year calibration curve (CC), and 5-year decision curve analysis (DCA), the model underwent internal assessment and validation. The C-index values for internal evaluation and validation were 0.718 and 0.685, respectively. The 5-year AUC values were 0.745 and 0.743, indicating satisfactory discriminatory ability (Figure 3). The 5-year DCA curves showed relatively high net benefit for both internal evaluation (18%–92%) and internal validation (18%–90%) (Figure 4). External validation of risk prediction models

From an initial pool of 198 patients treated at the First Affiliated Hospital of Bengbu Medical University (2018–2022), 173 were retained for external validation following rigorous data curation. Follow-up was concluded in December 2023. While the external validation cohort shared comparable age and sex distributions with the SEER training cohort, a focused comparative analysis revealed several notable baseline differences and inherent cohort biases (Table 2). Demographically, the external cohort represented an exclusively Asian population, contrasting with the predominantly White population (84.4%) in the SEER dataset. Clinicopathologically, the external cohort exhibited a slightly more advanced disease profile at baseline, characterized by a higher proportion of T2 (37.0% vs. 24.1%), N2 (23.1% vs. 17.8%), and M1 (5.2% vs. 1.6%) classifications, as well as a higher incidence of Grade IV tumors (6.9% vs. 0.4%). Furthermore, a follow-up bias was observed, as the overall mortality rate was lower in the external cohort (40.5% vs. 61.2%), reflecting its more recent and relatively shorter follow-up period compared to the mature SEER database. Performance comparison between nomogram and machine learning models

To further benchmark our modeling strategy against complex algorithms, the predictive performance of the proposed nomogram was directly compared with three trained ML models (XGBoost, RF, and SVM) across training, internal validation, and external validation cohorts. The detailed comparisons of the C-index and 5-year AUC are summarized in the Supplemental Materials (Table S2). Additionally, the corresponding 5-year ROC curves for these ML models across all datasets are provided in the Supplemental Materials (Figure S3). While the ML models exhibited competitive performance metrics during the internal evaluation, the traditional Cox-based nomogram demonstrated superior robustness and generalizability in the independent external validation cohort. These findings confirm that our parsimonious nomogram not only achieves predictive accuracy comparable to or exceeding that of complex nonlinear algorithms in external validation but also provides an intuitive and interpretable scoring system highly suitable for routine clinical application.

Discussion

In recent years, ML has made significant advances in medicine by efficiently uncovering underlying patterns in large volumes of clinical data. In this study, by taking the overlap of features selected across four distinct ML methods (LASSO, XGBoost, SVM, and RF), our pipeline identified seven core predictors of OSCC mortality: age, sex, race, tumor grade, Stage_T, Stage_N, and Stage_M.

To interpret the inner workings of our models and evaluate their clinical plausibility, we employed SHAP analysis, which revealed a hierarchy of variable importance prioritizing patient age, primary tumor T stage (Stage_T), and lymph node N stage (Stage_N). The identification of advanced age and male sex as high-risk factors is highly consistent with large-scale epidemiological studies.17,18,19,20,21 Similarly, the profound impact of higher T/N classifications on local recurrence and distant metastasis is universally documented.22,23,24 Following these, tumor differentiation (grade) and race provided moderate independent contributions, with the latter serving as a recognized proxy for unmeasured socioeconomic disparities and healthcare access.25,26,27 Given that these individual predictors are well-established in clinical oncology, their selection primarily serves to validate the clinical plausibility of our ML pipeline. Rather than identifying previously unknown individual risk factors, the core contribution of this study lies in its rigorous methodological framework and clinical integration. Our approach features the integration of large-scale, population-based data with independent cross-regional external validation, systematic ML feature selection, and direct performance benchmarking against both the traditional TNM staging system and pure ML models. By capturing complex, nonlinear interactions and collinearity among these routinely available clinical parameters, our proposed approach not only encompasses clinically recognized factors but also transcends the predictive limitations of the traditional anatomical TNM staging system alone.

To bridge the gap between complex algorithmic outputs and practical clinical utility, we developed a risk nomogram based on our ML framework. To rigorously justify this modeling strategy, we conducted a parallel performance comparison between the traditional Cox nomogram and advanced ML algorithms (XGBoost, RF, and SVM). While complex ML models exhibited marginally superior discriminative ability during initial training, they demonstrated a notable performance drop in the external validation cohort, indicative of overfitting to the source SEER population. In contrast, the Cox nomogram maintained robust and stable predictive accuracy across all cohorts. Therefore, in this specific clinical context, the nomogram not only ensures model simplicity and facilitates straightforward visual interpretation but also provides superior generalizability and a significantly lower risk of overfitting compared to “black-box” models. While nomograms have been successfully applied in prognostic assessments for various head and neck malignancies,28,29,30,31 the OSCC risk nomogram developed in this study specifically translates mathematical interactions into intuitive, quantitative predictions of 2-year and 5-year OS. To explicitly define its clinical application scenario, this nomogram is primarily designed for pre-treatment prognostic assessment immediately following initial diagnosis. All incorporated predictors, including demographic factors, tumor grade derived from the initial biopsy, and clinical TNM stage evaluated via physical examination and imaging, are readily obtainable prior to any therapeutic intervention. This pre-treatment utility is critical, as it empowers clinicians to identify high-risk patients early and tailor the initial comprehensive treatment strategy. Based on the predicted 5-year OS probabilities, patients were stratified into three distinct risk categories: low risk (>60%), intermediate risk (20%–60%), and high risk (<20%). Furthermore, DCA corroborated the clinical utility of the model, demonstrating a substantial net benefit across these threshold probabilities. According to NCCN guidelines for head and neck cancers, standard surgical resection remains the cornerstone of treatment, with adjuvant radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy guided by pathological risk factors. Utilizing our nomogram’s risk stratification, tailored management strategies can be conceptually proposed: low-risk patients may receive standard surgery with routine guideline-based follow-up; intermediate-risk patients are recommended to undergo adjuvant therapy and closer, more frequent monitoring; and high-risk patients should promptly undergo multidisciplinary team (MDT) evaluation to consider neoadjuvant therapy, immunotherapy, or clinical trial enrollment, alongside highly intensive surveillance. Ultimately, this risk-adapted approach integrates quantitative survival prediction with established care pathways. As demonstrated by our benchmarking analysis, this integrated model significantly outperforms the traditional TNM staging system alone, validating the clinical value of combining demographic and pathological factors to support data-driven, individualized management. However, it should be noted that while these proposed surveillance strategies offer a logical framework for personalized care, they are currently data-derived hypotheses that warrant further prospective clinical trials for rigorous validation.

In summary, this study utilized the SEER database alongside clinical data from local patients with OSCC and applied ML approaches, including LASSO, XGBoost, RF, and SVM, to identify seven key risk factors: age, race, sex, grade, Stage_T, Stage_N, and Stage_M. Based on these features, we developed and validated a robust predictive nomogram for mortality risk in patients with OSCC. Crucially, comprehensive benchmarking demonstrated that our proposed model not only exhibited consistent stability across parallel ML algorithms but also significantly outperformed the traditional TNM staging system in both internal and external validation cohorts. These findings highlight its superior accuracy and strong clinical utility, offering a refined, data-driven tool for personalized risk stratification and patient management.

Limitations of the study

This study has several inherent limitations. First, as a retrospective analysis, it is subject to potential biases and missing data. Important clinical confounders, such as smoking, alcohol use, oral hygiene, family history, and specific socioeconomic characteristics, were not recorded in the SEER database. The ML algorithms objectively retained “race” as a critical mathematical proxy to capture these unmeasured systemic disparities. However, a model utilizing US-specific racial categories poses challenges for global generalizability. When applied to our mono-ethnic Chinese external validation cohort where “race” was uniformly encoded as Asian and thus provided zero predictive variance, the model still maintained robust discrimination. While this indicates that the remaining core clinicopathological variables are overwhelmingly strong, generalizable prognostic drivers, we explicitly acknowledge the geographical limitations of the “race” variable. Therefore, for future clinical applications in different regions, we recommend substituting “race” with more specific local variables (such as a socioeconomic index) or performing local model calibration to ensure optimal performance. Second, patient age was analyzed as categorical rather than a true continuous variable, which inherently risks some loss of statistical information. This categorization was necessitated by the SEER database, which originally provides age exclusively in pre-categorized 5-year intervals. To avoid data sparsity and overfitting while reflecting distinct clinical phenotypes (early-onset, middle-aged, elderly), we aggregated these into three broader groups. Third, although our external validation cohort demonstrated the robustness of the core clinicopathological variables, the data were derived from a single center in China. This single-center retrospective design may inherently reflect specific local institutional practices (e.g., surgical expertise and follow-up protocols), which could restrict the broader applicability of the model to diverse global healthcare settings. Therefore, future research must prioritize large-scale, multicenter, prospective clinical trials that integrate genomic, radiomic, and more granular socioeconomic data. Developing and externally validating such multimodal prognostic models across diverse populations will be essential to further enhance predictive accuracy, fairness, and clinical utility. These limitations must be carefully weighed when interpreting the results or applying the proposed nomogram in clinical practice.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human participants

This retrospective study involved two independent cohorts of patients diagnosed with oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC). The training cohort data were extracted from the public Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database. Because the SEER database provides de-identified and publicly available patient data, the requirement for ethical approval and informed consent was waived.

The independent external validation cohort consisted of 198 patients with histologically confirmed OSCC treated at the First Affiliated Hospital of Bengbu Medical University (Bengbu, Anhui Province, China) between 2018 and 2019. Given the retrospective nature of this study involving no clinical interventions, the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the First Affiliated Hospital of Bengbu Medical University approved the protocol for the external validation cohort (Approval No. 2020LK075) and waived the requirement for informed consent. Because this analysis was based entirely on existing medical records, no new human participants were recruited, and no new biological samples were collected. Demographic and clinical characteristics, including age, sex, race, tumor grade, and TNM stage, were collected for all participants. The sample sizes and detailed characteristics of the populations are described in Table 2.

Influence of sex on the results

In this study, both male and female patients were included to explicitly evaluate the association of sex with overall survival. Subsequent comparative analyses revealed that sex significantly influenced survival outcomes, with male patients generally exhibiting a higher risk of mortality compared to female patients (detailed statistical comparisons are presented in the results section and Table S1). Consequently, sex was confirmed as a core independent predictor through our machine learning screening and was incorporated into the final prognostic nomogram to ensure accurate, sex-adjusted risk stratification.

Method details

Data sources and study design

Clinical data for OSCC patients were obtained from the SEER database (2000–2022) using SEER∗Stat software (version 8.4.5), including sex, age, race, tumor stage, pathological grade, metastasis status (lung, bone, liver), tissue differentiation, survival status, and survival time. Follow-up data in SEER are updated annually, and complete survival information was available through December 2022. The SEER program is a population-based cancer registry that collects representative data on cancer incidence, treatment, and survival across different regions and racial groups in the United States, covering multiple state registries, making it suitable for developing widely applicable prognostic models. For external validation, clinical information from 198 patients with histologically confirmed OSCC treated at the First Affiliated Hospital of Bengbu Medical University (Bengbu, Anhui Province, China) between 2018 and 2019 was collected, including sex, age, tumor differentiation, TNM stage, and metastatic status. Follow-up was completed by December 2023. This single-center, tertiary hospital cohort represents routine clinical practice outside the SEER environment and allows evaluation of the model’s generalizability across different healthcare systems and populations. Inclusion criteria were: (1) histologically confirmed OSCC diagnosed in SEER between January 2000 and December 2022, or at the First Affiliated Hospital of Bengbu Medical University between January 2018 and December 2019; (2) complete clinical information and survival outcome data. Exclusion criteria included missing variables exceeding 10%, absence of pathological confirmation, or incomplete survival records.

Data collation and splitting

Comprehensive data quality checks were performed prior to analysis, including verification of internal consistency and follow-up completeness. Variables with minimal missingness were processed using simple imputation (mean substitution for continuous variables and mode replacement for categorical variables). Missing-data patterns were compared across demographic groups (sex, race, age), and the proportions were similar, indicating a low likelihood of systematic bias. After data cleaning and imputation, a total of 8,927 OSCC cases with valid clinical information were retained, and all variables were prepared for subsequent modeling. The SEER dataset was randomly split into a training cohort (n = 6,250) and an internal validation cohort (n = 2,677) at a 7:3 ratio. A comparability assessment was subsequently performed to evaluate the baseline balance between the two cohorts, which is summarized alongside the external validation cohort in Table 2. Detailed associations between these baseline characteristics and overall survival status across the cohorts are provided in the Supplemental Materials (Table S1).

Outcomes and feature selection

The main evaluation metric was overall survival (OS), defined as the interval from diagnosis to death from any cause or the last follow-up. Since OS is based on registry follow-up and hospital records, it does not involve subjective assessment, and therefore, assessor characteristics and blinding were not applicable. The selection of candidate predictors was informed by established evidence, clinical rationale, and the availability of variables across both the SEER dataset and the external validation cohort. To ensure the model’s practical utility for pre-treatment decision-making, all clinicopathological variables extracted for modeling including tumor grade and TNM stage were strictly defined as baseline parameters available at the time of initial diagnosis, prior to any therapeutic intervention. It should be noted that in the SEER database, patient age is originally recorded as categorical data in 5-year intervals rather than continuous numerical values. To avoid data sparsity and model overfitting while preserving clinical interpretability, we aggregated these intervals into three distinct age groups (<30, 30-59, and 60-89 years). This grouping strategy conceptually reflects early-onset, middle-aged, and elderly OSCC cohorts, each possessing distinct baseline physiological and prognostic characteristics.

To identify the most robust prognostic determinants and mitigate the bias of single-method selection, we employed a comprehensive screening strategy integrating linear and non-linear approaches within the training cohort. First, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was used. LASSO serves as a penalized technique suitable for high-dimensional data, shrinking non-informative coefficients to zero; the tuning parameter (λ) was optimized via 10-fold cross-validation. Simultaneously, three machine learning algorithms were applied to capture complex nonlinear interactions: Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), an ensemble boosting framework that enables efficient modeling of nonlinear effects and higher-order interactions in large clinical datasets; Random Survival Forest (RSF), constructed through bootstrap aggregation of numerous decision trees to provide robust performance with strong resistance to overfitting; and Support Vector Machine (SVM), which relies on maximizing classification margins, making it well-suited for handling high-dimensional clinical variables. For these models, Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) values were calculated to quantify the specific contribution of each variable. To ensure a rigorous and transparent step-by-step feature selection process, we calculated the average SHAP value across the three machine learning models for each candidate variable. A predefined threshold of mean SHAP > 0.01 was established to objectively filter out negligible contributors. The final candidate predictors were exclusively determined based on the intersection of two strict criteria: (1) variables retained by the optimal LASSO regression, and (2) variables meeting this mean SHAP threshold across the machine learning algorithms.

Prognostic model construction

The key variables identified through the multi-algorithm selection process were subsequently included in a multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis to construct the prognostic nomogram for predicting 2-year and 5-year overall survival. Specifically, the exact nomogram points were generated by proportionally scaling the regression coefficients (β) derived from this multivariate Cox model. The predictor variable with the highest absolute regression coefficient was assigned a maximum of 100 points, and the points for all other variables were calculated as fractions of this maximum value using the rms package in R.

In this study, we selected 2-year and 5-year overall survival (OS) probabilities as the primary predictive endpoints based on both clinical and methodological considerations. Clinically, the first 24 months following initial treatment represent the absolute peak period for local recurrence and distant metastasis in OSCC patients. Thus, the 2-year OS rate serves as a critical milestone for evaluating initial treatment response and guiding early, intensive follow-up interventions. Meanwhile, the 5-year OS rate remains the universally recognized gold standard in oncology for assessing long-term therapeutic efficacy and potential clinical cure. Methodologically, selecting these two highly representative and adequately spaced time points avoids the visual clutter in the nomogram that typically arises from including too many intervals (e.g., 1, 3, and 5-year). Furthermore, our cohorts possess sufficient follow-up durations to ensure robust and reliable survival estimates at these specific landmarks, allowing for a focused and dynamic assessment of prognostic risk trajectories. To address the comparative performance of the linear nomogram against complex non-linear models and to evaluate the robustness of our model, we parallelly established full prognostic models using XGBoost, RSF, and SVM based on the same training dataset. The predictive performance of these three machine learning models was evaluated using the concordance index (C-index) and the 5-year area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). These metrics were calculated across the training, internal validation, and external validation cohorts, and were directly compared with the nomogram to verify its generalizability and clinical utility.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical and comparative analysis

Model performance was assessed using the concordance index (C-index), the time-dependent area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (5-year AUC), 5-year calibration plots, and 5-year decision curve analysis (DCA), which together evaluate discrimination, calibration accuracy, and clinical utility at specific prognostic time points. For benchmarking against the traditional AJCC TNM staging system, a conventional Cox proportional hazards model incorporating solely the TNM variables (Stage_T, Stage_N, and Stage_M) was constructed. The C-indices of the multi-factor nomogram and this TNM-only model were directly compared across the training, internal validation, and external validation cohorts. To match the feature input requirements of the established SEER-based model, the “Race” variable for all patients in this mono-ethnic Chinese external validation cohort was uniformly encoded as “Asian or Pacific Islander.”

SPSS 25.0 was applied for data organization and for conducting chi-square tests (or Fisher’s exact tests where appropriate) to evaluate between-group differences. R version 4.5.1 was used to generate the survival prediction model and to produce the C-index, ROC-based AUC, calibration plots, and decision curve analyses. Because the predictive C-indices from the proposed nomogram and the TNM-only model represent correlated data derived from the same patients, asymptotic Z-tests based on U-statistics were performed using the compareC R package to determine the statistical significance of their differences. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Survival predictive metrics were reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Comprehensive statistical details, including the exact value of n (representing the number of patients), can be found in the results section, Tables, and corresponding figure legends. A two-sided p value <0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. Corresponding time-dependent ROC curves, machine learning algorithms, and LASSO regressions were computed using relevant R packages (timeROC, xgboost, randomForestSRC, survivalsvm, glmnet, etc.) as detailed in the key resources table and Data S1.
