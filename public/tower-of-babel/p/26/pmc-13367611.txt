Disparities in cancer screening and preventive care access among LGBTQ+ populations: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Manas Pustake, Veeravenkata Garikiparthy, Mohammed Arfat Ganiyani, Oboseh John Ogedegbe, Swarada Joshi, Atulya Aman Khosla, Saif Syed, Avi Harisingani, Nishant Tiwari, Sakditad Saowapa, Stevenson Ongsyping, Jesus Gomez, Javier Corral, Rohan Garje
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Cancer screening is vital for early detection and improved survival, yet disparities persist among marginalized groups. Sexual and gender minority (LGBTQ+) populations face barriers to preventive care, including discrimination, limited access to affirming providers, and socioeconomic inequities. This study evaluates differences in mammogram, cervical, and colorectal cancer screening between LGBTQ+ and non-LGBTQ+ adults using nationally representative data.Materials and Methods:We conducted a cross-sectional analysis using the latest Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System. Of 278,519 respondents, participants were categorized as LGBTQ+ (n = 14,603) or non-LGBTQ+ (n = 258,942) after excluding missing data. Descriptive statistics and Chi-square tests assessed demographic and screening differences. Logistic regression models estimated associations between sexual orientation and screening uptake, adjusting for age, race/ethnicity, education, and income. Statistical significance was set at α =0.05.Results:LGBTQ+ respondents were younger and reported lower education and income compared to non-LGBTQ+ adults. Significant disparities were found in mammogram screening (χ2 = 21.85, P < 0.001). Bisexual individuals (odds ratio [OR] =5.37, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 2.24–12.88, P < 0.001) and those identifying with “other” sexual orientations (OR = 2.96, 95% CI: 1.09–8.00, P = 0.033) were less likely to receive mammograms compared to heterosexual individuals. After adjustment, no significant differences were observed for cervical (P = 0.462) or colorectal screening (P = 0.709). Sexual orientation predicted only mammogram utilization.Conclusions:Disparities in cancer screening persist, particularly in mammogram use among bisexual and other LGBTQ+ individuals. These findings highlight the need for targeted public health interventions, culturally competent care, and inclusive data collection to reduce structural barriers and improve screening equity for LGBTQ+ populations.

Introduction

Cancer remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality globally, with preventive screenings playing a pivotal role in early detection and improved survival rates.[1] Despite advances in public health initiatives aimed at broadening access to cancer screenings, disparities persist across sociodemographic groups. Among these, sexual and gender minority populations, which include lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer, and other identities (LGBTQ+), face unique and systemic barriers to equitable healthcare.[23] Research over the past decade has increasingly highlighted how structural inequities, including stigma, discrimination, and a lack of culturally competent care, contribute to poorer health outcomes in LGBTQ+ communities.[4] These challenges are particularly pronounced in preventive care, where disparities in cancer screening rates underscore broader systemic failures in addressing the needs of marginalized populations.

The recognition of LGBTQ+ health disparities has grown alongside advocacy for inclusive healthcare policies, yet significant gaps remain in understanding the mechanisms driving these inequities. Studies suggest that LGBTQ+ individuals are less likely to engage in routine cancer screenings compared to their heterosexual and cisgender counterparts.[2] For instance, lesbian and bisexual women report lower rates of mammography and cervical cancer screenings, while gay and bisexual men are less likely to undergo colorectal cancer screenings despite elevated risks for certain cancers.[56]

To address these knowledge gaps, this study leverages the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS), a nationally representative dataset administered by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC).[7] As the largest ongoing telephone health survey in the United States, the BRFSS collects data on health-related risk behaviors, chronic conditions, and preventive practices, including cancer screenings. Critically, recent iterations of the BRFSS have incorporated questions on sexual orientation and gender identity, enabling researchers to disaggregate data for LGBTQ+ populations, a methodological advancement that addresses longstanding limitations in health disparity research. By analyzing BRFSS data, this study examines disparities in three key cancer screenings: mammography for breast cancer, Pap smear tests for cervical cancer, and colonoscopy for colorectal cancer.

Existing literature on LGBTQ+ cancer screenings has been constrained by methodological challenges, including reliance on small, nonrepresentative samples or regional datasets.[8910] The present study advances the field by utilizing a robust, nationally representative sample to compare cancer screening rates between LGBTQ+ and non-LGBTQ+ individuals while accounting for key sociodemographic variables, such as age, race, education, and income. This approach not only strengthens the validity of the findings but also helps identify subgroups within the LGBTQ+ population that may benefit from targeted, culturally responsive interventions.

The theoretical underpinnings of this research are drawn from the minority stress model, which posits that chronic stressors related to stigmatized identities, such as discrimination and internalized homophobia, contribute to health disparities.[11] Applied to preventive care, this model suggests that LGBTQ+ individuals may avoid screenings due to anticipatory anxiety about biased treatment or a lack of trust in healthcare systems.

This study is guided by two primary research questions: First, do significant disparities exist in mammogram, cervical cancer, and colonoscopy screening rates between LGBTQ+ and non-LGBTQ+ populations in the United States? Second, how do sociodemographic factors, such as race, education, and income, interplay the disparities in these populations? To answer these questions, a cross-sectional analysis of BRFSS data was conducted. This study design provides a snapshot of current disparities, offering a foundation for longitudinal studies to explore causal relationships.

The implications of this research extend beyond academic discourse. Findings can guide policy efforts to reduce barriers to care, including training providers in LGBTQ+ cultural competency and advocating for inclusive insurance coverage. By highlighting disparities in preventive care, the study emphasizes the urgency of incorporating LGBTQ+ health into broader public health initiatives, a key step toward equity. This research also offers actionable insights to bridge gaps between LGBTQ+ health needs and healthcare delivery, ultimately helping patients by improving access to timely, appropriate cancer screening.

Materials and Methods

Study design and Data Source

We conducted a cross-sectional study to examine disparities in cancer screening and access to preventive care among LGBTQ+ populations. Data are drawn from the BRFSS, a nationwide survey administered by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC).[7]

The BRFSS is conducted annually across all 50 U.S. states and U.S. territories. It consists of telephone-based surveys that collect self-reported information on health behaviors, medical conditions, and the utilization of preventive health services, including colonoscopy, cervical cancer screening, and mammography. Overall, the BRFSS provides comprehensive health-related data on risk behaviors, chronic conditions, and preventive care practices, including cancer screening, among U.S. residents.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were first run to examine the general characteristics of the study population, including the prevalence of cancer screenings across both LGBTQ+ and non-LGBTQ+ groups. The following steps were taken to analyze the data:

Descriptive Statistics: Frequencies and percentages for categorical variables (e.g., cancer screening rates, age, gender, race) were calculated for both the LGBTQ+ and non-LGBTQ+ populations. Mean and standard deviation were reported for continuous variables.

To assess the relationship between sexual orientation (LGBTQ+ vs. non-LGBTQ+) and categorical outcomes such as the receipt of cancer screenings (colonoscopy, cervical, mammogram), a Chi-square test was used, if the expected values were <5, Fisher’s test was used. These tests evaluate whether there is a significant association between LGBTQ+ status and the likelihood of receiving preventive cancer screenings.

P values were calculated for each test to assess statistical significance. A threshold of P < 0.05 was considered indicative of a statistically significant difference between the LGBTQ+ and non-LGBTQ+ populations.

Regression analysis: To assess further the relationship between sexual orientation (LGBTQ+ vs. non-LGBTQ+) and categorical outcomes such as the receipt of cancer screenings (colonoscopy, cervical, mammogram), logistic regression was employed. Multivariate models were computed to adjust for confounders if significant on univariate analysis.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted using publicly available, de-identified data from the BRFSS. Since the data are anonymous and do not involve human subjects directly, institutional review board approval was not required. However, the study adheres to ethical standards of data handling and reporting, ensuring the privacy and confidentiality of the data.

Discussion

This study contributes to the growing body of evidence documenting disparities in cancer screening among LGBTQ+ populations. Consistent with previous research, our findings demonstrate that screening behaviors are shaped by sexual orientation. The observed differences, particularly the lower likelihood of mammogram screening among bisexual individuals, underscore persistent inequities in preventive healthcare engagement across subgroups within the LGBTQ+ community.

Recent studies have emphasized similar challenges. Kratzer et al. noted that reliance on localized or limited datasets can obscure broader patterns in cancer screening across diverse LGBTQ+ populations, calling for more comprehensive national surveillance to inform targeted interventions.[6]

In alignment with these concerns, Hara et al. documented significant disparities in cancer screening engagement among LGBTQ+ individuals, showing that screening rates remain lower than those of non-LGBTQ+ counterparts.[9] They further note that the lack of data disaggregation by ethnicity and socioeconomic status hinders nuanced policy design. Similarly, Morris et al. emphasized the role of medical education in mitigating bias and improving provider competence regarding LGBTQ+ health, a critical determinant of preventive screening behaviors.[10]

The racial and ethnic composition in this dataset, approximately 73–74% White non-Hispanic across LGBTQ+ and non-LGBTQ+ groups, closely aligns with national estimates from the FSS Claims Data and NHIS.[1213] Notably, a higher proportion of Hispanic and multiracial individuals appears within the LGBTQ+ group (10.1% and 4.9%, respectively). This mirrors findings by Meyer (2013) and Buchmueller et al. (2007).[1415]

Consistent with prior evidence, this study finds lower educational attainment among LGBTQ+ respondents, particularly bisexual individuals (14.5% with no high school diploma; 35.7% with college degrees, compared to 43.4% among heterosexual respondents). Hsieh and Ruther and Lombardi et al. (2020) similarly identified systemic educational inequalities linked to stigma and discrimination in academic settings.[1617] Parallel trends emerge in income distribution: LGBTQ+ adults are disproportionately represented in lower income brackets. This pattern echoes reports from Gates, and Badgett et al.[1819]

Our findings indicate that bisexual women are significantly less likely to have undergone mammogram screening (odds ratio = 5.37, P < 0.001) compared with heterosexual women, while those identifying as “other/something else” also show reduced odds. This aligns with Buchmueller and Carpenter et al, who first identified lower screening rates among lesbian and bisexual women.[20] Subsequent study by Lewis et al. reinforced these findings, linking reduced engagement to provider bias, inadequate insurance coverage, and stigmatizing healthcare environments.[21] Breast cancer screening disparities are of particular concern among lesbian and bisexual women, who have been shown to have higher lifetime risk of breast cancer, partly due to lower parity rates, less breastfeeding, and higher prevalence of certain behavioral risk factors. These well-documented risk differentials underscore the importance of ensuring routine mammography access and culturally informed counseling for sexual minority women.

For cervical and colorectal cancer screenings, no statistically significant differences were observed across sexual orientation groups. These findings correspond with mixed evidence in the literature. Solazzo et al. reported lower Pap testing among sexual minority women, possibly due to misconceptions about screening relevance. However, more recent national datasets, including NHIS and BRFSS, demonstrate convergence in screening rates when controlling for socioeconomic and healthcare access factors.[22]

Similarly, colorectal cancer screening patterns here (χ2 = 1.39, P = 0.709) align with Sabatino et al., who observed that age, insurance coverage, and routine primary care engagement, rather than sexual orientation, are stronger predictors of screening uptake.[23]

Screening recommendations must also account for the impact of gender-affirming hormone therapy on cancer risk. Long-term estrogen therapy in transwomen increases breast tissue development and may elevate breast cancer risk, whereas testosterone therapy in transmen does not eliminate the need for screening if breast or cervical tissue remains. These clinical nuances underscore the importance of individualized, anatomy-based screening approaches.

Overall, these results reinforce the growing consensus that structural determinants, such as income, education, and insurance, mediate most observed disparities in cancer screening across LGBTQ+ populations. Bisexual individuals, in particular, continue to experience disproportionate barriers to care and poorer outcomes, consistent with national surveillance data.

These disparities can be understood within established frameworks, like the Minority Stress Model (Meyer, 2003) and Intersectionality Theory (Crenshaw, 1989), which emphasize that overlapping systems of oppression (e.g. gender, sexuality, socioeconomic status) collectively shape health behaviors and outcomes.[2425] Addressing these inequities thus requires multifaceted interventions, expanding provider education, ensuring inclusive healthcare policies, and dismantling procedural biases in screening recommendations.

Strengths and limitations

This study’s limitations warrant careful consideration. Although BRFSS includes optional items capturing gender identity, these data do not fully represent transgender and gender diverse respondents, limiting our ability to examine disparities unique to this population. This gap is particularly important because transgender individuals have distinct cancer screening needs. For example, transwomen using long-term estrogen therapy have increased risk for breast cancer and require age- and risk-appropriate mammography. Transmen who have undergone chest masculinization surgery may still require screening depending on residual breast tissue. Additionally, although transwomen do not have a cervix, individuals who have undergone vaginoplasty may still develop HPV-associated neoplasia in neo-vaginal tissue. Transwomen who have undergone genital surgery also retain a prostate and should continue age-appropriate prostate cancer screening. The inability of BRFSS to fully capture these aspects highlights the urgent need for inclusive data systems to accurately assess cancer risk and screening behaviors among transgender populations.

Conclusions

Efforts to improve cancer screening equity must address multilevel barriers faced by LGBTQ+ individuals. Although not directly evidenced in our study, at the patient level, financial instability, lack of insurance coverage, and prior negative experiences with the healthcare system might contribute to medical mistrust and reduced engagement with preventive care. At the provider level, insufficient training in LGBTQ+ cultural competency can lead to inappropriate assumptions, inadequate counseling, or missed opportunities for screening. At the institutional level, exclusionary practices, including medical intake forms that omit sexual orientation and gender identity, limited availability of gender-affirming services, and underrepresentation of LGBTQ+ people in clinical trials, further perpetuate disparities. Addressing these intersecting barriers is essential for achieving equitable preventive cancer care.
