International trends in cancer mortality revisited: Japan’s site-specific progress and remaining challenges until 2024
Authors: Kota Katanoda, Hirokazu Tanaka, Yuri Ito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study examined recent international trends in age-standardized mortality for all cancers combined and major cancer types, comparing Japan with selected countries (Australia, Canada, the Republic of Korea (Korea), the United Kingdom, and the United States of America) using the latest available national data (from 1980 through the early 2020s). Overall cancer mortality in Japan continued to decline at a pace similar to that of other high-income countries. Marked reductions were observed for stomach and liver cancers in Japan, which historically had high mortality. These declines narrowed international differences, and mortality from female liver cancer in Japan has now fallen below levels in Western countries. Stomach cancer mortality rates have been continuously decreasing in Japan, while the pace was slower than that of Korea. In contrast to those decreasing cancers, Japan showed persistently high mortality for colorectal, pancreatic, and cervical cancers. Mortality from male lung cancer and female breast cancer in Japan declined only slowly or continued to rise, resulting in levels approaching those in Western countries. The decline in liver cancer is considered an effect of measures against hepatitis B and C, and could serve as an international model. The decrease in stomach cancer is also one of Japan’s progressive movements, but comparisons with Korea indicate challenges remain in secondary prevention. Strengthened primary and secondary prevention are urgently needed for colorectal, lung, female breast, and cervical cancers, which have not shown a clear decreasing tendency in Japan.

Introduction

Cancer mortality is an essential outcome indicator for cancer control. In Japan, age-standardized rates (ASRs) of overall cancer mortality have continued to decline, largely attributable to decreases in stomach, liver, and male lung cancer [1]. In the international context, the overall decline in cancer mortality in Japan is similar to that observed in other countries; however, Japan has been lagging behind for several cancer types—such as colorectal, lung, female breast, and cervical cancers—for which primary prevention, such as tobacco/infection control, and secondary prevention, such as screening, have been established [2]. Since this last report using the data up to 2016, no updated analyses have been conducted. This study aimed to descriptively compare recent trends in ASRs of mortality for all cancers combined and for specific cancer types between Japan and selected countries using the latest available national data until the early 2020s.

Materials and methods

Data on ASRs of all-cancer and site-specific cancer mortality in each country were obtained from the Global Cancer Observatory database, provided by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) [3]. ASRs were adjusted to the world standard population (Segi) [4]. We compared the status in Japan with that in Australia, Canada, the Republic of Korea (Korea), the United Kingdom (UK), and the United States of America (US). These countries were selected from the North America, Western Europe, and Western Pacific regions in terms of population size and long-term national data availability as well as consistency with our previous report [2]. For Western Europe, we also selected four other countries for additional comparison (France, Germany, Italy, and Sweden). We analyzed all cancer and nine major site-specific cancers: stomach (International Classification of Diseases version 9 and 10 (ICD-9, 10, respectively) code: 151 and C16), colorectum (including the anus) (153–154 and C18-C21), liver (155.0 and C22), pancreas (157 and C25), lung (162 and C33-C34), female breast (174 and C50), prostate (185 and C61), cervix uteri (180 and C53), and corpus uteri (182 and C54). For all cancers (140–208 and C00-C97), non-melanoma skin cancer (173 and C44) was excluded according to the definition of the IARC database. ASRs were calculated for two age groups: all ages and ages under 75 years old. We examined the latter because many prefectural cancer control plans in Japan have chosen the ASR in those under 75 years as a numerical goal. The first year of the observation period was set at 1980. We set this starting year because causes of deaths other than cancer, such as neonatal and communicable disease mortality, could have affected the earlier period, as in our previous report [2]. The last year of the observation period in each country was dependent on the available data in the database (Australia: 2023, Canada, Korea, and US: 2022, Japan and UK: 2021). For Japan, the most recent data (2022–2024) were obtained from the Vital Statistics, Japan [5, 6].

As a supplementary analysis, we calculated annual percent changes (APCs) of the most recent 10 years by applying a log-linear regression model to each of the cancer mortality trends (Joinpoint Regression Program, Version 5.2.0.0. April, 2024) [7].

Results

Figure 1 presents the trends in ASRs of mortality for all cancers combined and by cancer type. The decline in all-cancer mortality in Japan was comparable to that observed in other countries for both males and females. Korea has shown a particularly steep decline in recent years compared to other countries.

The trends in the ASR of all-cancers and site-specific cancer mortality by sex and country for all ages.

Mortality from stomach and liver cancer remains higher in Japan and Korea than in Western countries; however, larger declines in these two East Asian countries have narrowed the gaps. Korea has experienced more rapid declines than Japan for both sexes, and since the 2010s, mortality has been consistently lower in Korea. For female liver cancer, Japan’s mortality has fallen below that of Western countries.

For colorectal cancer (including the anus), while mortality was higher in Western countries than in Japan and Korea in the 1980s, subsequent decreases in the former and increases in the latter resulted in convergence. In recent years, mortality has begun to decline in Korea, whereas Japan has shown no clear decrease, resulting in Japan having the highest levels among the countries compared.

Pancreatic cancer mortality continues to rise sharply in Japan for both sexes relative to other countries, making Japan’s high mortality particularly notable in recent years.

Male lung cancer followed a pattern similar to colorectal cancer: Japan and Korea had lower mortality than other countries in the 1980s, but mortality increased until the 1990s before declining more slowly than in Western countries. Consequently, Japan and Korea have recently exceeded these countries. For female lung cancer, Japan and Korea remain lower than Western countries, and Japan has recently surpassed Korea.

For female breast cancer, mortality in Western countries remains higher than in Japan and Korea but has continued to decline steadily, approaching the increasing levels observed in Japan.

Cervical cancer mortality was higher in Western countries than in Japan and Korea in the 1980s. However, sharp declines in these countries from the 1980s to 1990s reversed this pattern; with rapid declines in Korea since the 2000s, Japan has continued to exhibit the highest mortality.

The mortality of cancer of the corpus uteri has increased across all countries since the 2000s, with Japan now reaching levels similar to those in Western countries.

Prostate cancer mortality has shown gradual declines in all countries, and Western countries have consistently maintained higher levels than Japan and South Korea.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that Japan’s ASRs of mortality from all cancers combined continue to decline similarly to other countries. Notably, stomach and liver cancer—formerly major contributors to Japan’s high mortality—have shown sustained declines, narrowing gaps with Western countries and or even reversing the pattern, as observed for female liver cancer. In contrast, Japan continues to exhibit the highest mortality levels for colorectal, pancreatic, and cervical cancers. For male lung cancer and female breast cancers, slow declines or continued increases in Japan have resulted in mortality approaching the levels seen in Western countries. Many of these cancers have well-established primary and secondary prevention strategies [8–10], highlighting the need to strengthen preventive measures to replicate the mortality reductions achieved for stomach and liver cancers.

Compared with our 2021 report [2], overall patterns remained unchanged, although several noteworthy changes were identified. For stomach cancer, Japan and Korea had previously shown similar mortality levels; however, the present analysis reveals more clearly that Japan now exceeds Korea. Although Japan has expanded primary prevention through insurance-covered Helicobacter pylori eradication [11], Korea has emphasized secondary prevention through endoscopic screening [12, 13]. Secondary prevention is expected to yield more immediate reductions in mortality. In Korea, the cancer screening program are directly managed by the National Health Insurance Service through two channels—designated screening facilities and contract-based hospitals for workplaces [12]. Employers are mandated to provide screenings for employees and to offer one day of leave for this purpose [12, 13]. Screening data from the two routes are centrally integrated by the Korea Central Cancer Registry. In contrast, inadequate quality assurance in workplace-based cancer screening remains a challenge in Japan [14], indicating a need for improved quality control and data integration.

Another major change after the previous report is that female liver cancer mortality in Japan has fallen below that in Western countries. Japan’s viral hepatitis control program has systematically implemented national screening of hepatitis B and C virus (HBV, HCV) for residents 40 years old or older and HBV screening for pregnant women with follow-up observations of positive cases [15]. Modeling studies suggest that these measures, along with the introduction of direct-acting antivirals, are considered to have contributed to the decline in hepatocellular carcinoma mortality [16, 17]. Achieving the WHO goal of eliminating HCV by 2030 is considered feasible [18], making this a notable progress movement in Japan’s disease control efforts.

The convergence of female breast cancer mortality between Japan and Western countries is also important. These Western countries are considered to have achieved mortality reductions through secondary prevention and treatments for metastatic breast cancer [19, 20]. Japan urgently needs to strengthen and expand its screening programs and effective treatment strategies.

Japan’s relative position for cervical cancer has further deteriorated, with an increasing gap compared to Korea. Although proactive HPV vaccination recommendations resumed in all municipalities in 2022, vaccination coverage remains only 20%–30% [21]. Reducing mortality requires a combination of primary prevention through vaccination and secondary prevention through screening [22].

For pancreatic cancer, Japan’s markedly high mortality has become even more apparent. Japan bears a high burden of pancreatic cancer within the Western Pacific Region in terms of age-adjusted mortality, incidence, and disability-adjusted life year [23]. Early detection is difficult and prognosis is still poor globally. [24] Although there is no single dominant risk factor; tobacco smoking and type 2 diabetes mellitus account for 27% of the burden in males and 7% in females [25]. Steady implementation of primary prevention—such as diabetes management and smoking cessation—is essential.

In summary, Japan’s ASRs of cancer mortality continue to decline similarly to other countries, with especially pronounced reductions in stomach and liver cancer. In contrast, notable declines have not been observed for colorectal, lung, female breast, and cervical cancers. These cancer types have established primary and secondary prevention measures, underscoring the importance of strengthening these strategies.
