Museomics reveals uncultured symbionts with biosynthetic potential in nudibranchs
Authors: Dafne Porcel Sanchis, Marta Pola, J Pamela Engelberts, Oleanna Guerra-Font, Lauren Messer, Inés Alberola-Mora, Lucía Escobar Sáez, Néstor Pérez Gómez, Álvaro Portolés Campo, Juan Valero-Tebar, Luis María Naya Garmendia, Jesús Carlos Preciado Barahona, Rosario Gil García, Vicente Arnau, Simon J McIlroy, Mária Džunková
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMuseum specimens are widely used for PCR-based pathogen detection, yet their potential for metagenomic discovery of beneficial microbes remains underexplored, largely due to difficulties in distinguishing true symbionts from contaminants. Here, we use metagenomics of museum specimens to uncover symbioses in endangered or difficult-to-collect animals, such as nudibranchs. To date, Doriopsilla is the only nudibranch demonstrated to harbor an uncultured symbiont involved in chemical defense, leaving it unclear whether comparable associations occur in other nudibranchs. We hypothesized that bona fide symbionts should belong to abundant, uncultured lineages consistently present across individuals of the same host taxon collected across space and time.ResultsUsing ethanol-preserved specimens archived for up to 30 years, we doubled the number of available nudibranch microbiome datasets and found that dominant uncultured symbionts are rare, with most nudibranchs likely relying on alternative chemical defense mechanisms. An exception were Polycera and Felimare that contained two previously unknown symbionts, Candidatus Polyceribacter and Candidatus Felimaribacter, from distinct uncultured orders that are globally rare in marine metagenomes. These symbionts encode diverse biosynthetic gene clusters exhibiting strain- and species-level microdiversity consistent with metabolites previously reported from their hosts. Their restricted host distribution, phylogenetic distinctiveness, and phylogenetic similarity to symbionts of sponges or corals that are not nudibranch prey, support long-term evolutionary specialization and functional convergence. Fine-scale diversification further suggests host-driven microbial adaptation following symbiosis establishment.ConclusionsOverall, this study establishes museomics as a robust framework for symbiosis research and advances understanding of the evolutionary and chemical ecology of host–microbe interactions in rare marine invertebrates. Introduction

Archival specimens in natural history museums and research collections harbor vast genetic resources that can be documented by DNA sequencing, occasionally enabling the study of microbes preserved within museum specimens [1, 2]. Past use of museomics has largely focused on resolving the origin and spread of historic or ongoing diseases such as Lyme disease, leprosy, and malaria [3–5]. Nevertheless, beneficial symbiotic bacteria are generally overlooked by museomics despite their recognized roles in host health and ecology [6]. To our knowledge, only a few studies have examined well-established host–symbiont pairs in preserved specimens, such as Photobacterium in Siphamia fish [7], Wolbachia in Drosophila [8], or the gut microbiome of century-old snails [9]. These studies relied on amplicon sequencing, which captures temporal shifts in microbial diversity and can indicate the presence of potentially novel microbial taxa based on phylogenetic divergence, but does not permit inference of their metabolic potential or symbiotic functions [10]. Genome-resolved metagenomics overcomes these limitations; however, its application to museum specimens remains challenging due to foreign microbial contamination introduced during handling of specimens not originally collected for microbiome research, which may hinder distinguishing obligate symbionts from transient bacteria [11].

Obligate symbionts are often consistently present across individuals of the same host species and exhibit reduced genomes, as observed in many insects [12, 13], although environmental conditions can influence symbiont specificity in some systems, such as chemosynthetic worms [14]. Additionally, obligate symbionts often belong to uncultured microbial lineages, reflecting their adaptation to host-specific microniches and reliance on host-derived nutrients, which impede their isolation in pure culture [15, 16].

Metagenomic analysis of bacterial symbionts in museum specimens would be particularly useful for soft bodied marine animals, such as nudibranchs, which unlike sponges and corals are difficult to sample due to their small size and environmentally driven variability in localization [17, 18]. Of the ~ 3000 described nudibranch species, only ~ 5% have been analysed for chemical defense molecules, revealing a wide variety of terpenoids, alkaloids, macrolides, and peptides [19–23]. Zoologists distinguish aeolid and dorid nudibranchs partly by their chemical defence strategies: aeolids sequester cnidocysts from cnidarian prey in their cerata [24], whereas dorids are thought to produce or acquire their defence chemicals directly from food [25]. Although bacterial cells have been observed in nudibranch tissues more than a decade ago [26], their identities and roles remained unresolved. The subsequent studies relied only on 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing with limited phylogenetic resolution and minimal replication [27–33]. Metagenomics has been applied only to Chromodoris nudibranchs via a co-assembly of four individuals, without recovering metagenome assembled genomes (MAGs) [34, 35]. A previous function-driven single-cell genomics study of the dorid nudibranch Doriopsilla fulva (MacFarland, 1905) [36] revealed that its mantle microbiome is dominated by a single uncultured species, Candidatus Doriopsillibacter californiensis from uncultured order Candidatus Tethybacterales, consistently abundant across individuals and actively producing a previously unknown betalactone likely involved in host chemical defense [37]. Taken together, previous studies have generated microbiome data for only 23 nudibranch species (15 genera), representing ~ 0.6% of all known nudibranch species, and have confirmed a symbiotic role only for Ca. Doriopsillibacter.

Here we leverage ethanol-preserved museum specimens of nudibranchs from university collections dating back up to 30 years to explore their microbial symbionts, reducing the need for extensive sampling of valuable nudibranch populations. In this study, we hypothesized that bacterial symbionts involved in chemical defence are consistently present across conspecific nudibranch specimens, independent of sampling location and time, and are more likely to belong to lineage-specific uncultured taxonomic lineages, reflecting probable host dependence, as previously observed in several symbiont-dependent animals [38, 39]. Although bacteria isolated from nudibranchs can produce bioactive compounds [40–45], these are culturable free-living taxa, in which antimicrobial activity is widespread, as in other complex microbiomes such as human skin [46, 47], and may reflect museum contamination [48]. For this reason, we prioritized uncultured taxa as more likely sources of previously uncharacterized metabolites of pharmaceutical interest. Through genome-resolved metagenomics of a small portion of mantle (not damaging museum specimens), we identified previously unknown symbionts encoding biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) significantly different from those already described in public databases.

Methods

DNA extraction

Mantle samples (~ 1 mm3) from 54 specimens collected across multiple locations between 1992 and 2022 were analyzed (Fig. 1a). The collection included at least two individuals from each of the 14 genera, representing 22 nudibranch species (Supplementary Table S1, Supplementary Fig. S1). Samples were homogenized in 1 ml sterilized 0.9% NaCl using a Potter–Elvehjem homogenizer and filtered through 5 µm syringe filters (Fisher Scientific, Ref: 17,104,381) to remove host cells for microbiome analysis, while 200 µl of unfiltered homogenate was used for nudibranch DNA extraction. DNA was extracted by alkaline lysis: 200 µl of sample was mixed with 140 µl lysis buffer (700 µl 0.43 g/10 ml KOH, 430 µl 0.8 g/10 ml DTT, 370 µl water, pH 12) and incubated 10 min at room temperature. Heat shock lysis was applied at −80 °C for ≥ 10 min followed by 55 °C for 5 min, then neutralized with 140 µl Stop buffer (5 g Tris–HCl/10 ml, pH 4). Proteins were digested with 1 µl Proteinase K (20 mg/ml) for 30 min at 55 °C. DNA was purified using Clean NGS magnetic beads (LabClinic, Ref: CNGS-0050), resuspended in 20 µl water, and stored at − 20 °C for PCR or metagenomic sequencing.

Identification of nudibranch species

Specimens were identified by Sanger sequencing (Applied Biosystems, ref: ABI3730XL) of COI amplicons generated by PCR using KAPA2G Fast HotStart ReadyMix (Merck, ref: KK5601) and primers 5’-GGT CAA CAA ATC ATA AAG ATA TTG G-3’ and 5’-TAA ACT TCA GGG TGA CCA AAA AAT CA-3′ [49]. PCR conditions were 95 °C for 3 min, 38 cycles of 95 °C for 60 s, 40 °C for 60 s, 72 °C for 90 s, followed by 72 °C for 5 min [50]. Reactions (25 µl) were purified with Clean NGS beads and resuspended in 10 µl water. Each sample was sequenced in two separate BigDye Terminator v3.1 (Thermo Fisher, ref: 4,337,454) reactions using the same primers. Chromatograms were analyzed with MEGA v.11 [51] to trim ends and retain the central 600–660 bp of the COI amplicon.

Microbial diversity overview

The V3 and V4 regions of 16S rDNA were amplified using KAPA2G Fast HotStart ReadyMix, 1 μl of extracted DNA and universal primers with Illumina adapters (5’-TCG TCG GCA GCG TCA GAT GTG TAT AAG AGA CAG CCT ACG GGN GGC WGC AG-3’ and 5’-GTC TCG TGG GCT CGG AGA TGT GTA TAA GAG ACA GGA CTA CHV GGG TAT CTA ATC C-3’), using the following PCR conditions: 95 ºC for 3 min, 28 cycles of 95 ºC 30 s, 55ºC for 30 s, and 72 ºC for 30 s, followed by 72 ºC for 5 min [52]. Reactions (25 µl) were purified with Clean NGS beads and sequenced on Illumina MiSeq platform.

Read quality was assessed with FastQC v0.11.9 [53] and MultiQC v1.13 [54]. Based on the resulting profiles, adapters and primers were removed using Fastp v0.23.299 [55] (-detect_adapter_for_pe, -disable_quality_filtering and -disable_length_filtering parameters) and Cutadapt v3.5100 [56] (–Linked adapters, –discard- untrimmed, –overlap 10, –minimum-length 1 parameters). Sequence truncation (‘truncLen’ parameter 228 bp and 219 bp; max expected error of 2 and 5 in R1 and R2 reads, respectively) was performed with DADA2 v1.22.0101 [57]. Reads R1 and R2 were merged with DADA2 core sample inference algorithm with the pool inference behavior to obtain amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). ASVs that passed chimera removal with DADA2 were classified by the IDTAXA classifier of the DECIPHER v2.22108 [58] package with a 50% confidence using the SBDI Sativa curated 16S rDNA GTDB database [59] enriched with Ca. Tethybacterales order and mitochondrial and chloroplast sequences, as described in our previous study [37]. Chloroplast and mitochondrial sequences were removed as the first step in the ASVs analysis. ASVs were clustered into OTUs at 97% sequence identity using a workflow adapted from the pipeline available at JCSzamosi/Cluster_ASVs.Rmd (GitHub), which relies on the DECIPHER v2.21.0 [58] and DADA2 v1.22.0101 [57] packages.

We aimed to identify nudibranch symbionts shared across all specimens from the same nudibranch species/genus. To gain meaningful biological insights into the ecological distribution of closely related bacterial groups that cannot be classified using the conventional 16S rDNA databases, we clustered the ASVs, which may capture sequencing artefacts rather than true strain- or species-level diversity, into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at a 97% similarity threshold commonly used in core microbiome studies [60]. As 97% clustering does not reliably reflect genus-level boundaries [61], interpretation was enhanced by a phylogenetic tree was constructed from ASVs comprising ≥ 1/3 of the microbiome and related full-length 16S rDNA sequences using Silva v1.2.11 [62] with RAxML [63], and visualized in iTOL v6 [64]. The core microbiome, defined as OTUs with > 1% abundance in ≥ 50% of specimens (or both if only two were available), was analyzed at genus and species level using Microbiome R v1.18.0110 [65] and visualized with ComplexHeatmap v2.12.0112 [66]. The 1% core microbiome threshold was set based on previous nudibranch data [40] and also to increase the likelihood of detecting genuine symbionts even in samples where the majority of microbial DNA may derive from environmental contaminants. Environmental bacteria were defined as taxa frequently identified as contaminants in low-biomass microbiome studies [67, 68]. To distinguish putative symbionts from potential museum- or seawater contaminants, we applied a combination of criteria: (i) forming part of the core microbiome of a nudibranch species/genus, (ii) host-specificity, and (iii) phylogenetic placement within uncultured host-associated lineages rather than typical laboratory or free-living taxa. Host specificity was defined as a criterion requiring that taxa identified as part of the core microbiome of a given nudibranch species or genus could not comprise > 1/3 of the core microbiome in other taxonomically unrelated nudibranch specimens within our collection.

Finally, ASVs representing ≥ 1/3 of the microbiome in at least one sample and unclassified at the order level were analyzed using the Integrated Microbial NGS platform [69] IMNGS (https://www.imngs.org, August 2023) with a 97% identity threshold via the Parallel Similarity Query to assess their occurrence in other environments and hosts.

Metagenome assembled genomes

For shotgun metagenomics, we selected samples in which 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing revealed taxa unclassified on order level, comprising at least one third of the microbiome. Libraries were prepared from the same extracted DNA using the xGen™ DNA EZ Library Prep Kit (IDT, ref. 10,009,821) following the low-input (< 100 pg) protocol and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq platform.

Adapter sequences were identified with FastQC v0.11.9 [55] and MultiQC v1.13 [54] and removed using Fastp v0.23.299 [55] as described above. Paired-end reads were assembled with SPAdes v3.15 –meta option [70] and mapped back to assemblies using BBMap v39.01 [71] (k = 13). Subsequently, binning was performed using MetaBAT2 v2.15 [72] (-m 1500), and bin quality was assessed with CheckM2 v1.1.0 [73]. Bins with < 50% completeness were discarded in accordance with MIMAG standards [74].

Taxonomic assignment was performed with GTDB-Tk v2.4.1 [75] with –classify_wf settings using GTDB releases R220 and R226 [72], to assess classification consistency across database updates and to evaluate potential phylogenetic ambiguities in lineages with limited representative genomes. Average nucleotide identity (ANI) of the obtained MAGs was calculated with FastANI v1.33 [76] to assess species-level relatedness. Phylogenetic reconstruction was performed using the GTDB-Tk de_novo_wf workflow based on 120 conserved single-copy genes identified with Prodigal v2.6.3 [77], aligned with hmmalign v3 [78] and inferred by maximum likelihood using FastTree2 [79]. The analysis included MAGs from this study, closely related GTDB genomes at the highest shared taxonomic rank (hereafter “closest GTDB relatives”), and Ca. Doriopsillibacter from our previous work, with Actinomycetota used as the outgroup.

MAG abundance was estimated by mapping raw metagenomic reads to reconstructed MAGs using CoverM v0.7.0 [80], and to 21 nudibranch genome assemblies from the GoaT database [81] (Supplementary Table S2) to assess host-derived reads.

To compare metagenomic and 16S rDNA results, 16S sequences were extracted from MAGs with Barrnap v0.9 [82] (bacterial SSU profiles) and used for the construction of the 16S-based phylogenetic tree described above.

The presence of MAGs from this study was assessed across 707,499 public metagenomes using Sandpiper [83] (assessed in September 2025; based on GTDB R226).

Functional annotation of MAGs and closest GTDB relatives was performed with Prokka [84], followed by KEGG assignment using BlastKOALA v2.2 and KEGG Mapper [85]. Metabolic features were also confirmed with ANVIO [86]. To explore the potential roles of obtained MAGs in nudibranch health, we analysed their metabolic potential and compared it with their closest GTDB relatives and Ca. Doriopsillibacter [37], focusing on nutrient cycling and biosynthesis of B-vitamins and amino acids.

Metagenomic contigs > 1.5 kbp were screened for BGCs with AntiSMASH v7.0 [87] and v8.0 [88] using medium strictness and all search features. Longest BGC-containing contigs were compared across samples by blastn to manually reconstruct split BGCs for comparative visualization, after which these regions were reanalysed in AntiSMASH for consistency. To revise BGC coverage, metagenomic reads from relevant samples were mapped to the BGCs using bwa mem v0.7.17 [89] with default parameters, retaining reads with mapping quality 60. Amino acid sequence similarity (AASS) of core biosynthetic genes was analyzed by blastp [71] with the most similar BGCs from AntiSMASH ClusterBlast and closest GTDB relatives. These genomes were also analysed by AntiSMASH with identical parameters to detect additional BGCs absent in our MAGs.

Mobile genetic elements were assessed with geNomad v1.8.0 [90] using default settings, and their potential hosts were inferred from k4freq genomic distances of unbinned contigs > 5 kbp to the MAGs, as described previously [91].

Results

Largely transient microbiomes with few dominant, host-specific symbionts

The 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing generated 5.5 million reads (Supplementary Table S3) yielding 9,604 high-quality ASVs. Only 45.4% of ASVs were classified at the genus level, representing 37.8% of total microbial abundance, highlighting the high prevalence of previously unknown bacteria in nudibranchs (Supplementary Table S4). The ASVs were clustered into 4,116 OTUs (Supplementary Table S5). The most abundant phyla were Pseudomonadota (22.34%), Bacillota (3.41%), Actinomycetota (2.43%), and Bacteroidota (1.68%). Notably, in more than half of the nudibranch species, over one third of the microbial community belonged to lineages unclassified at the order level (Fig. 1b), which suggested presence of yet uncultured symbionts.

The core microbiome (OTUs with > 1% abundance in ≥ 50% of specimens of the same taxonomic group) was defined in 19 of 22 nudibranch species, comprising 1–8 core OTUs (Fig. 1c; Supplementary Fig. S2; Supplementary Table S6). From the total of 4,116 detected OTUs, only 51 were classified as core OTUs (Supplementary Table S6), indicating that 98.76% of all detected OTUs, accounting for 54.34% ± 27.47% of microbial abundance per sample, likely represent transient bacteria acquired from seawater or introduced during museum preservation and handling. From the 51 core OTUs, 17 represented taxa derived from museum handling (e.g., Corynebacterium, Cutibacterium, Staphylococcus, Micrococcus) [67, 68, 92–97], while 12 belonged to bacterial genera with cultured representatives from marine ecosystems (e.g., Vibrio, Endozoicomonas, Poseidonibacter [32, 98–105]). Together these 29 OTUs with cultured representatives at the order level accounted for 20.38 ± 21.70% (median 11.05%, range 0–93%) across all specimens, indicating a lack of host specificity. Combined with their affiliation to well-characterized museum- or laboratory-associated contaminants and widespread seawater lineages, this suggests that these taxa are unlikely to represent genuine symbionts. Nevertheless, the lack of host specificity was also observed among some core OTUs unclassified at the order level, likely because they represent yet-uncharacterized marine free-living microbes or broadly distributed opportunistic pathogens. One example is OTU_840 and its close relative OTU_809 (Supplementary Fig. S3), which formed part of the core microbiome of the aeolid nudibranch Aeolidiella sanguinea (11.9% and 87.03% relative abundance, respectively) but were also detected in four non-related specimens: Trapania maculata (3.07%), Polycera faeroensis (78.63%), and Polycera kernowensis (8.62% and 18.31%). BLASTn analysis indicated affiliation with Mycoplasma species (92% sequence similarity), which are widespread pathogenic bacteria. After discarding core OTUs that were not host-specific or that belonged to likely contaminating culturable lineages, only 12 host species retained core OTUs considered potential symbionts: Armina tigrina, Dendrodoris grandiflora, Dendrodoris limbata, Felimare cantabrica, Felimare picta, Felimare tricolor, Felimida purpurea, Polycera faeroensis, Polycera kernowensis, Polycera quadrilineata, Trapania maculata, and the non-nudibranch control Aplysia fasciata. None of the core OTUs were related to Ca. D. californiensis, the dominant symbiont previously described in Doriopsilla fulva37, indicating that nudibranch symbionts span phylogenetically distinct lineages.

From the nudibranch species harboring core OTUs considered potential symbionts, only Polycera and Felimare contained target OTUs at proportions > 33% (Fig. 1c), which was sufficient for metagenomic sequencing and recovery of high-quality MAGs. In Polycera kernowensis, the dominant core OTU_97 comprised 61.99 ± 37.51% of the microbiome and reached up to 47.41% in other Polycera species, indicating a genus-specific symbiont. Likewise, Felimare specimens were dominated by OTU_199 (59.22 ± 31.36%), with F. tricolor also harboring OTU_175 (3.57% and 49.63%). Shotgun metagenomic sequencing of P. kernowensis (n = 3), P. quadrilineata (n = 1), F. cantabrica (n = 2), and F. tricolor (n = 1) (Fig. 2a–d) yielded 24.7 ± 8.4 million reads per sample, with assemblies averaging 690 ± 195 Mbp (Supplementary Table S7). Binning recovered 11 MAGs (2 medium, 9 high quality) with 90.35 ± 14.71% completeness and 0.38 ± 0.66% contamination (Supplementary Table S8). The recovered MAGs corresponded to the most prevalent 16S rDNA amplicons in these samples (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Polycera metagenomes contain Ca. Polyceribacter and Ca. Itsasoibacter

Each Polycera metagenome contained a dominant MAG (9.18 ± 7.35% of reads), which was assigned to genus and order JABSOH01 (Alphaproteobacteria, Fig. 2e, Supplementary Table S8), corresponding to core OTU_97 and representing a single species (ANI 99.77%, Supplementary Table S9) with consistent genome sizes (1.88 ± 0.26 Mbp) and high genome quality (91.97 ± 9.64% completeness, 0.06 ± 0.04% contamination). Given the genome completeness ≥ 95% of three MAGs (Pkern03.2, Pkern13.1.2.3, Pkern14.2, Supplementary Table S8), we propose the new taxon Candidatus Polyceribacter euskerae within the family Ca. Polyceribacteraceae and order Ca. Polyceribacterales, reflecting its association with Polycera and its collection from the Basque Country (Euskadi). For its closest GTDB relative from abyssal sinking particles we propose the name Ca. Polyceribacter profundus (Supplementary Note S1). Database surveys indicate that Ca. Polyceribacter is globally rare: 16S rDNA approach (ASVs searched in IMNGS) detected it in only 199 marine samples at low abundance (0.19 ± 1.2%), except in a Paraplanocera flatworm microbiome where it dominated (34%, Supplementary Table S10, Supplementary Fig. S4), and similarly, only 76 marine metagenomes (Sandpiper database) contained Ca. Polyceribacter, typically < 1%, with the highest in the soft coral Eleutherobia (7.64%) (Supplementary Table S11).

Recent GTDB releases revealed instability in lineages previously assigned to the JABSOH01 order. Comparative phylogenomic analyses in this study resolved these inconsistencies, supporting the exclusion of JABAAW01, JAHZMA01, JAHZLG01, and JABAAW01 genomes from Vazella and Haliclona sponges [106] from the Ca. Polyceribacterales order (Fig. 2e). Similarly, Acropora-associated MAGs from our previous study, formerly assigned to the JABSOH01 order108, can define new order Candidatus Acroporibacterales and family Ca. Acroporibacteraceae, with Ca. Acroporibacter fitzroyensis as the type species (Supplementary Note S1).

P. kernowensis metagenomes contained additionally three MAGs representing two closely related lineages (99.96% ANI, Supplementary Table S9) of the GTDB placeholder genus DT-68 (family Metamycoplasmataceae, Bacillota, Fig. 2e, Supplementary Table S8), comprising 2.85 ± 1.2% of reads (Fig. 2f). This lineage was detected in 193 marine invertebrate metagenomes (max. abundance 74.15%, Supplementary Table S11). Consistently, in our amplicon survey the OTU_840–OTU_809 cluster corresponding to DT-68 (Supplementary Fig. S3) occurred across multiple nudibranch genera, indicating a broad host range rather than host-specific symbiosis. Given the high genome quality of two MAGs (Pkern13.4 and Pkern14.3; > 96% completeness, 0.3% contamination; Supplementary Table S8), we propose the name Candidatus Itsasoibacter, derived from the Basque word “itsasoa” (sea), with Ca. Itsasoibacter basquensis and Ca. Itsasoibacter euskerae types species associated with Polycera nudibranchs (Supplementary Note S1).

Felimare metagenomes contain Ca. Felimaribacter and an unclassified MAG

The most dominant MAG in each Felimare metagenome (up to 2.68 ± 1.75% of reads) corresponded to the core OTU_199 (Fig. 2f, Supplementary Fig. S3). These high quality MAGs (> 97% completeness, < 0.1% contamination) represented two species (ANI 93.22%, Supplementary Table S9), associated with F. tricolor and F. cantabrica, respectively, and showed consistent genome sizes across specimens (1.74 ± 0.10 Mbp; Supplementary Table S8).

GTDB releases showed unstable classification of these MAGs, assigned in R220 to a new family within the rare order QNFE01 and in R226 to the genus CAKMZV01 from the octocoral Leptogorgia [107]. However, phylogenetic analysis of these closest GTDB relatives and ANI values < 80% indicate that these Felimare MAGs form a separate order (Fig. 2e, Supplementary Table S9). Based on three high-quality MAGs, we propose the new order Ca. Felimaribacterales, family Ca. Felimaribacteraceae, genus Ca. Felimaribacter, which includes two species: Ca. Felimaribacter euskerae from F. cantabrica and Ca. Felimaribacter basquensis from F. tricolor (Supplementary Note S1). Ca. Felimaribacter is extremely rare globally, with closely related 16S rRNA gene sequences detected in only ten marine datasets at very low abundance (0.003 ± 0.005%; Supplementary Table S10).

An additional MAG (Ftric20.4, genome completeness 83.11%, contamination 1.05%), corresponding to OTU_175, was recovered from a single F. tricolor metagenome, where it comprised 0.11–0.92% of reads and was absent from F. cantabrica (Fig. 2f; Supplementary Fig. S3). Its phylogenetic placement remains unresolved due to the scarcity of close relatives JAAOII01 and G963455285, each represented by a single genome from deep-sea sediments near sponges [108] and the polychaete Protula [109] (Fig. 2e). These lineages are globally rare, detected in only 35 metagenomes of diverse sponges and molluscs at low abundance (1.79 ± 3.08% across 39 samples; Supplementary Table S11), and absent from public 16S rDNA amplicon datasets.

Distinct metabolic adaptations define symbiotic roles of nudibranch bacteria

The combination of ecological observations and analysis of genomic features, provides support for a host-associated lifestyle in nudibranch symbionts. For example, Ca. Polyceribacter and Ftric20.4 possess eukaryotic-like proteins such as ankyrin repeats, which may facilitate host–microbe interactions [110, 111], while fibronectin-binding proteins in Ca. Felimaribacter may contribute to host attachment.

Unlike related symbionts of sponges and corals, nudibranch-associated bacteria showed limited nitrogen metabolism: Ca. Polyceribacter, Ca. Felimaribacter, and Ca. Doriopsillibacter were restricted to ammonia assimilation into glutamate (Fig. 3; Supplementary Fig. S5), whereas Ca. Itsasoibacter and MAG Ftric20.4 lacked this pathway entirely.

Taurine, a widespread metabolite in marine animals including sea slugs [112, 113], may mediate host–symbiont interactions: Ca. Polyceribacter, Ca. Felimaribacter, and Ftric20.4 can import and catabolize host-derived taurine to sulfite/sulfide for amino acid biosynthesis (e.g., cysteine), whereas Ca. Doriopsillibacter enters the pathway at sulfite. In turn, they may supply sulfur compounds to their hosts, as reported for marine sponges [113] (Fig. 3, Supplementary Fig. S6). In addition, Ca. Felimaribacter, Ca. Doriopsillibacter, and Ftric20.4 encoded the SOX system for thiosulfate oxidation, potentially enabling energy generation and synthesis of sulfur-containing compounds.

B-vitamins and essential amino acids are central to nudibranch metabolism but are often not synthesized by animals. Ca. Felimaribacter and Ca. Polyceribacter encoded pathways for several essential vitamins and amino acids (e.g., biotin, cobalamin, lysine, methionine; Supplementary Fig. S7), indicating a nutritional role for the host. In contrast, Ftric20.4 was auxotrophic for multiple amino acids, and Ca. Itsasoibacter lacked most vitamin, amino acid, nitrogen, sulfur, and fatty acid biosynthesis pathways, which together with its small genome (0.79 ± 0.27 Mbp) and low GC content (30.66%), supports a host-dependent, likely parasitic lifestyle. Ca. Itsasoibacter does not rely specifically on Ca. Polyceribacter for these nutrients, as their co-occurrence in all Polycera metagenomes was not mirrored in public datasets.

The terpene precursor cluster P.terprec (20.8 kbp) was the most widespread among the four Ca. P. euskerae MAGs and 13 closest GTDB relatives (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Fig. S8). It had conserved gene order within Ca. Polyceribacter species, but was divergent across related families (AASS of the core biosynthetic gene 29–62%).

The ectoine cluster P.ecto (10.4 kbp, 10 genes) showed a unique gene architecture compared with public databases (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Fig. S9). It was conserved across all Ca. P. euskerae MAGs, while among 13 closest GTDB relatives, only 5 JABAAW01 genomes contained an ectoine cluster, but with distinct gene organization and low AASS (40.8 ± 2.9%).

The RiPP-like (ribosomally synthesized and post-translationally modified peptide-like natural products) cluster P.ripp (10.8 kbp, 11 genes) was present in P. kernowensis MAGs but absent in P. quadrilineata, reflecting strain-level variation (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Fig. S10). Among closest GTDB relatives, RiPP-like clusters were found only in two Ca. Acroporibacter genomes (39–42% AASS) and one Ca. Polyceribacter genome (79% AASS), all with distinct structures.

The most variable gene organization in Ca. Polyceribacter had the BGC P.hpksa (up to 28.4 kbp, 35 genes), which combined arylpolyene and HR-T2PKS (highly reducing type II polyketide synthase) genes (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Fig. S11). It contained three β-ketoacyl synthases, sharing 24–34% AASS among them, and 11 accessory biosynthetic genes. This P.hpksa cluster was absent in the closest GTDB relatives, and antiSMASH analysis indicated that it represents a previously uncharacterized BGC. It was detected in Ca. P. euskerae MAG from P. quadrilineata, whereas in P. kernowensis metagenomes it occurred in unbinned fractions. Elevated coverage (3 ×) and a high geNomad plasmid score (0.91, Supplementary Table S12), along with the presence of a mobilization nuclease/relaxase, indicated that P.hpksa in P. kernowensis is located on a multicopy plasmid. k4freq distances further supported its association with Ca. P. euskerae (0.021 vs. 0.044 ± 0.015 for unrelated contigs, Supplementary Table S13).

Among the 13 closest GTDB relatives, Ca. P. euskerae exhibited the highest overall BGC count, lacking only two BGC types detected in related genomes: a distinct Type III PKS present in three Ca. Acroporibacter genomes and a crocagin BGC identified in the JAHZMA01 genome (Supplementary Fig. S12).

Ca. Felimaribacter harbors two previously unknown BGCs

The three Ca. Felimaribacter MAGs harbored two BGCs (Fig. 4a). The terpene precursor cluster F.t erprec (20.9 kbp) exhibited a unique structure, with only 33–47% AASS of the core polyprenyl synthase shared with known clusters (Supplementary Fig. S13). Even more distinctive was the RiPP-like cluster adjacent to T3PKS genes, F.ripppks (up to 41 kbp), which showed pronounced species-level variation: the two species shared a 20.5-kbp core region, whereas sequences flanking this region were detectable by read mapping, suggesting alternative genomic locations and the potential production of distinct bioactive compounds (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Fig. S14). AntiSMASH searches indicated that no previously known species harbored a similar RiPP–T3PKS adjacency. Likewise, the five closest GTDB relatives either contained RiPP-like and T3PKS BGCs on separate contigs with distinct organization (CAKMZV01, 68–74% AASS to F.ripppks core genes) or lacked this cluster entirely (QNFE01). These lineages encoded phosphonate BGCs absent in Ca. Felimaribacter (Supplementary Fig. S15), further highlighting phylogenetic divergence inferred from whole-genome sequence data.

The Ftric20.4 MAG encoded a simple phosphonate (Supplementary Fig. S16) and a terpene-precursor BGC (Supplementary Fig. S17).

Discussion

Museum collections provide a valuable source of genetic resources for analyzing multiple individuals of the same nudibranch species across sites and time points, as these animals are scarce and difficult to collect, and only a few species, such as Berghia stephanieae, can be maintained in aquaria for replicated studies [114]. Broad comparative screening using museum specimens also minimizes unnecessary collection of wild populations, which is particularly relevant given that many nudibranch species do not appear to harbor stable uncultured symbionts of biotechnological interest. In species lacking such symbionts, analyses of freshly collected individuals would primarily recover transient seawater-associated microbes rather than specialized host-dependent symbionts. However, a limitation of ethanol-preserved museum material is the inability to perform activity-based single-cell assays, such as in situ expression of biosynthetic gene clusters demonstrated previously for Ca. Doriopsillibacter [37] or Raman-based single-cell screening [115]. Therefore, museum-based surveys represent an efficient strategy for prioritizing candidate taxa before more resource-intensive fresh specimen collection and functional validation.

Accordingly, in this study we focused on detecting previously unknown BGCs in core symbionts from uncultured orders. Our survey identified dominant host-specific uncultured symbionts only in Felimare and Polycera, where each symbiont belongs to a different order distinct from the Ca. Tethybacterales order previously identified in Doriopsilla37. Earlier 16S-based studies reported either highly diverse microbiomes or other dominant taxa unrelated to these uncultured symbionts [27–32, 35], such as Mycoplasmatales in B. stephanieae, where this culturable order has been linked to stress-induced pathogenicity35. In our dataset, several uncultured taxa also formed part of the core microbiomes of multiple nudibranch species, which could initially suggest broader symbiotic associations. However, these lineages are also present in diverse, phylogenetically and ecologically distant marine animals, arguing against strict host specificity. One example is the OTU_840–OTU_809 cluster corresponding to Ca. Itsasoibacter, which was detected across multiple marine animal hosts yet lacked identifiable BGCs, suggesting a more generalist or potentially pathogenic lifestyle rather than a specialized defensive symbiosis. Together, these results indicate that dominant uncultured symbionts occur in only a subset of nudibranch species and are not broadly shared across taxa.

Where do nudibranchs acquire the uncultured bacterial symbionts involved in their chemical defence? Although several nudibranchs contain defence compounds similar to those of their prey [19, 116], it is now clear that bacteria are not universally responsible for nudibranch chemical defence. For example, laboratory studies showed that Berghia does not acquire bacteria from its anemone prey Exaiptasia [33], but instead incorporates dinoflagellates transported by nematocysts [117]. Similarly, the sacoglossan sea slug Elysia rufescens acquires defensive kahalalide molecules from the algal symbiont Ca. Endobryopsis kahalalidefaciens without acquiring the bacterium itself [118]. Nudibranchs primarily feed on invertebrates including sponges, bryozoans, and cnidarians, with prey preferences remaining largely conserved over evolutionary timescales [119, 120]; accordingly, P. kernowensis and P. quadrilineata likely feed on Bugula bryozoans [121–123], whereas F. cantabrica and F. tricolor probably consume Dysidea sponges [124, 125]. Comparisons with publicly available sequence datasets, in our case by IMNGS [69] and sandpiper [83], therefore provide valuable clues to potential microbiome transfer.

The bryozoan Bugula harbors the bacterial symbiont Endobugula sertula (Cellvibrionaceae), which produces bryostatins, pharmaceutically relevant polyketides synthesized through polyketide synthases (PKS) [126–129]. Bryostatin-like compounds have also been reported from the nudibranch Polycera altra, where their presence has been putatively attributed to sequestration from Bugula prey [123]. Endobugula is not strictly bryozoan-associated, as it has been isolated from seawater [130] and detected in metagenomes from the coral Eleutherobia and the alga Pyropia haitanensis [131], where, interestingly, traces of Ca. Polyceribacter are also present [129]. In contrast, Endobugula was not detected in Polycera metagenomes. This pattern suggests that Polycera nudibranch either selectively acquires Ca. Polyceribacter horizontally from their Bugula prey or established this association earlier and now maintains it via vertical transmission. With Ca. Polyceribacter now identified as a potential source of PKS biosynthetic capacity, chemical characterization will be necessary to determine whether its metabolites include bryostatin-like compounds [132].

There are no reports of secondary metabolite transfer from Dysidea sponges to Felimare nudibranchs, although prey-derived sesquiterpenes from Dysidea have been detected in another chromodorid nudibranch, Hypselodoris [133]. Felimare metagenomes lacked the dominant cyanobacterial symbionts of Dysidea [134–136], paralleling the absence of Endobugula from Bugula in Polycera metagenomes. Publicly available Dysidea datasets do not contain Ca. Felimaribacter, leaving the source of horizontal transmission unresolved and raising the possibility of an earlier acquisition followed by a specialized, Felimare-specific association. Consistently, other Chromodorididae nudibranchs grazing on Dysidea or related sponges, analysed previously (Chromodoris, Ceratosoma, Goniobranchus [40], Doriprismatica [31], Mexichromis [28]) or here (Felimida), lacked Ca. Felimaribacter. If Felimare contains sesquiterpenes, they could be derived either from terpene BGCs in Ca. Felimaribacter or alternatively produced by the unique F.ripppks (PKS-based), as a recent study on Acremonium showed that a compound previously classified as a sesquiterpene was actually polyketide-derived [137].

Ca. Polyceribacter, Ca. Felimaribacter, and Ca. Doriopsillibacter belong to three distinct bacterial orders within the Alpha- and Gammaproteobacteria and colonize hosts from three different nudibranch families (Discodoridae, Chromodorididae, and Dendrodorididae). Their closest phylogenetic relatives are symbionts of corals Acropora [110] and Leptogorgia [107], and sponges Vazella, Haliclona [106], Ianthella [138], Cymbastela, Crella, Crambe, Scopalina, Tethya [139], Cinachyrella, Iophon, and Tsitsikamma [140], which are not the direct prey of these nudibranchs. This pattern suggests that symbioses in nudibranchs arise through independent acquisition events, allowing ecologically compatible microbes from divergent lineages to establish long-term evolutionary specialization. Each nudibranch symbiont characterized so far also encodes distinct classes of BGCs, suggesting that these independently acquired symbioses may support host defense through diverse biosynthetic strategies rather than through selection for a single conserved metabolite. At the same time, the absence of dominant microbial partners in most nudibranch species indicates that such specialized symbioses are not universal and that alternative chemical defense strategies are commonly employed.

Once established, these symbiotic associations appear to be shaped by host evolution. MAGs from F. cantabrica and F. tricolor correspond to two distinct Ca. Felimaribacter species (Ca. F. euskerae and Ca. F. basquensis), whereas MAGs from P. kernowensis and P. quadrilineata represent two strains of Ca. P. euskerae, indicating host-associated diversification within these bacterial genera. Notably, both Ca. Polyceribacter strains carried an arylpolyene/HR-T2PKS BGC (P.hpksa), which differed in structure and genomic location, being plasmid-encoded in P. kernowensis and chromosomally integrated in P. quadrilineata. This reflects rapid adaptation to host-specific selective pressures, as exemplified by plasmid–chromosome BGC mobility in selvamycin-producing Pseudonocardia symbionts of fungus-growing ants (Apterostigma) [141]. The enrichment of diverse BGCs in Ca. Felimaribacter and Ca. Polyceribacter, in contrast to other MAGs that either lacked BGCs (Ca. Itsasoibacter) or encoded only ubiquitous pathways such as terpene precursors and phosphonates (MAG 20.4) [142–144], highlights their specific functional importance for nudibranch hosts and indicates that the remaining bacteria likely represent transient microbes or opportunistic pathogens rather than stable mutualists.

Conclusions

Our study establishes museomics as an effective approach for uncovering bacterial symbionts in rare or difficult-to-collect marine invertebrates. Metagenomic analysis of ethanol-preserved nudibranchs revealed that stable associations with dominant uncultured symbionts are uncommon, suggesting that alternative mechanisms such as dietary sequestration of bioactive compounds and de novo biosynthesis by the host are more likely to underlie chemical defence than symbiont-produced metabolites. Nevertheless, we identified two previously unknown lineages, Ca. Polyceribacter and Ca. Felimaribacter that define the uncultured orders Ca. Polyceribacterales and Ca. Felimaribacterales, and that encode unique biosynthetic gene clusters possibly involved in chemical defense of Polycera and Felimare nudibranchs. The restricted distribution of nudibranch symbionts to certain host taxa, their phylogenetic proximity to symbionts of sponges that are not prey, and host-specific species- or strain-level diversification support long-term evolutionary specialization rather than transient prey-derived acquisition. Together with Ca. Doriopsillibacter, these symbionts demonstrate that some nudibranchs have independently established specialized partnerships with distinct microbial lineages, each contributing unique metabolic potential. More broadly, this work highlights the evolutionary and chemical heterogeneity of nudibranch symbioses and underscores the value of museum collections as an underexplored resource for revealing hidden microbial partnerships.
