A novel prognostic biomarker for colorectal cancer: the ratio of red cell distribution width to lymphocytes
Authors: Jiahao Huang, Zongxuan Huang, Guangquan Qin, Huage Zhong, Jialiang Gan, Yun Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Our previous study revealed that an elevated ratio of red cell distribution width to lymphocyte ratio (RLR) might be linked to unfavorable poorer outcomes among colorectal cancer (CRC) patients. Nevertheless, additional research is necessary to authenticate these findings and ascertain the clinical relevance of RLR in the context of CRC. A total of 1,541 patients diagnosed with CRC were retrospectively included in this study. The association between clinical-pathological data and overall survival (OS) was examined using Kaplan-Meier curves. A machine learning approach was employed to identify prognostic indicators, construct a risk model, determine independent factors influencing OS, and assess the model’s accuracy. In CRC patients, RLR exhibited the highest average area under the curve, sensitivity, and specificity, as well, as the highest youden, except for CEA. Subsequently, the three indicators, preoca199, preoCEA, and preoca125, were normalized for CRC patients. Using the weight matrix in the SVM classification model, the 24 indicators were ranked, identifying the top 5 important indicators. The final models included CEA (HR = 1.0032, P = 0.0025), RLR (HR = 1.018, P = 0.0157), T-stage (HR = 1.4456, P < 0.001), N-stage (HR = 1.2092, P = 0.0962), and Stage (HR = 1.7024, P = 0.0012). Notably, OS was significantly prolonged in the group with low levels of CEA and low RLR. RLR could be a novel prognostic marker for OS in patients with CRC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47529-z.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks as the third most prevalent cancer worldwide, constituting approximately 10% of all cancer cases. CRC incidence rates exhibit variations across the world, with the highest occurrences observed in developed countries such as the United States, Canada, and Australia1,2. Conversely, the lowest rates are found in developing countries in Africa and Asia3. The 5-year survival rate for individuals diagnosed with localized CRC stands at around 90%, whereas for those with metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC), it hovers around 14%4. Early detection and treatment of CRC are crucial for improving outcomes and enhancing survival rates.

Prognostic typing of CRC employs an approach to determine the prognosis of the cancer and guide treatment decisions. Molecular markers, including microsatellite instability (MSI), CpG island methylator phenotype, and mutations in genes such as KRAS and BRAF, are utilized in prognostic typing5. Patients with MSI-high tumors may benefit from immunotherapy, while those with mutations in the KRAS or BRAF genes might require distinct treatment strategies6. Besides assisting in treatment decisions, prognostic typing can also aid in predicting the likelihood of recurrence and survival rates for CRC patients7. By comprehending the tumor’s molecular characteristics, doctors can develop personalized treatment plans tailored to the individual patient’s requirements. However, additional biomarkers are needed to predict the clinical course of CRC and further enhance prognosis, similar to other cancer types.

In recent years, a growing body of evidence suggests a strong link between the systemic inflammatory response and the different stages of carcinogenesis8. Elevated levels of cytokines in the tumoral microenvironment have been shown to stimulate angiogenesis and tumor proliferation, potentially increasing the metastatic potential of the tumor9. Numerous studies have established that increased inflammation is associated with a poorer prognosis10. Several biomarkers, such as the prognostic nutritional index (PNI), systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), and neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), which serve as indicators of systemic inflammatory and immune status, have demonstrated their predictive value for overall survival (OS) and tumor recurrence in various cancers, including CRC11.

In patients with CRC, higher levels of lymphocytes have been consistently linked to improved outcomes and higher survival rates12. Research suggests that patients with elevated lymphocyte levels tend to have better survival rates and respond more favorably to treatment13. Conversely, patients with lower lymphocyte levels may require more aggressive treatment options14. Ongoing investigations are currently exploring the potential of lymphocyte levels as a biomarker for predicting response to immunotherapy and guiding treatment decisions. Therefore, monitoring lymphocyte levels in CRC patients can prove to be a valuable tool in predicting disease prognosis and determining the most appropriate course of treatment.

Red blood cell distribution width (RDW) referred to in this study is red blood cell distribution width-coefficient of variation (RDW-CV), with a clinical reference range of 11.5%-14.5%.RDW measures the variation in the size of red blood cells in a blood sample. Elevated RDW levels have been associated with various health conditions, including anemia, heart disease, and cancer15. Studies have shown a significant correlation between high RDW levels and poor prognosis in some cancers16. Several studies have suggested a potential association between elevated RDW levels and inferior outcomes in CRC. The RDW to lymphocyte ratio (RLR) has recently emerged as a novel biomarker for predicting survival in various cancers, including CRC16,17. RLR is calculated by dividing the RDW value by the lymphocyte count. Our previous study18 indicated that a higher RLR might be associated with poorer outcomes in CRC patients. However, further research is necessary to validate these findings and determine the clinical utility of RLR in the context of CRC.

Materials and methods

Patients and study design

A total of 1,541 patients with CRC who underwent potential curative resection for CRC at the First Affiliated Hospital and Affiliated Tumor Hospital of Guangxi Medical University (Nanning, Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region, China), between September 2004 and May 2019 were eligible for inclusion with the aim of identification of prognostic clinical variables, see Fig. 1 All.

The workflow diagram.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) patients with a confirmed histological diagnosis of CRC; (b) patients who underwent resection of the primary tumor; and (c) availability of preoperative blood samples. To ensure the baseline status reflected the preoperative condition without therapy-induced interference, blood samples for RLR calculation were collected during routine preoperative examinations, strictly within 7 days prior to surgery.

Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (a) familial adenomatous polyposis or hereditary colon cancer; (b) clinically evident acute infections (defined as fever > 38 °C, active pneumonia, severe urinary tract infection, or white blood cell count > 10 × 109/L) diagnosed preoperatively or on the day of blood collection19; or (c) concurrent malignancies; and (d) presence of chronic conditions known to significantly alter red blood cell indices or immune status, including severe chronic kidney disease (eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2), decompensated liver cirrhosis, active autoimmune diseases, hematological disorders (e.g., thalassemia, leukemia), or recent blood transfusion within 3 months. Baseline clinicopathological parameters, including general demographic information; medical history (past, personal, and family history); preoperative and postoperative routine blood tests; serologic markers; inflammation-related indicators, degree of histologic differentiation, pathologic TNM stage, and preoperative and postoperative adjuvant chemoradiotherapy, were collected from the patient’s medical records. Machine learning models and Cox regression analysis were employed to identify the RLR as a potentially valuable prognostic nutritional predictor. Subgroup analyses based on different clinicopathological parameters were conducted to explore the utility of RLR ratios in specific subgroups.

Demographic data of the patients, including age, sex, TNM stage, and pathological characteristics, were extracted from the patient’s medical files. Laboratory data, such as neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, monocyte count, RDW, red blood cell (RBC) count, and platelet count, were obtained from the hospital information management system.

The NLR value was calculated by dividing the neutrophil count by the lymphocyte count, while the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) was calculated using the formula “platelet (P) × neutrophil (N)/lymphocyte (L)”. The RLR value was calculated by dividing the RDW (i.e., RDW-CV) value by the lymphocyte count. The study protocol was approved by the medical ethics committee of the two affiliated hospitals of Guangxi Medical University. All the procedures were performed in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki(2023-E441-01, LW2023129).

Classification model and regression model

In this study, we utilized the LIBSVM (Library for Support Vector Machines) package, developed by Professor Chih-Jen Lin of Taiwan University, to construct support vector classification (SVC) and support vector regression (SVR) models20. These models were employed for the prediction of Akzheimer’s disease classification and clinical Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score21. LIBSVM is an open-source library based on an SVM and is renowned for its simplicity, user-friendliness, speed, efficiency, and minimal parameter adjustments required for SVM19. It offers support for both binary and multiple classifications, as well as regression tasks.

To evaluate the effectiveness of our proposed methods, we conducted experiments using support vector machine classifiers with linear or radial basis function (RBF) kernels on the same dataset22 These classifiers were employed to differentiate between prognoses for various groups.

LASSO, COX regression model

Lasso is a data dimensionality reduction method that can be applied to both linear and non-linear cases. It selects relevant variables from the sample data using a penalty19 based approach. By shrinking the original coefficient, small coefficients are directly compressed to zero. Consequently, variables associated with these coefficients are considered non-significant and subsequently discarded.

The COX regression model, also known as the proportional hazards regression model (proportional hazards model, the Cox model), was introduced by the British statistician D.R. Cox in 1972. This semi-parametric regression model enables the analysis of survival outcomes and survival times, allowing for the simultaneous assessment of the impact of multiple factors on survival. It can effectively handle data with censored survival time without requiring an assumption about the underlying survival distribution. Since its inception, this model has been extensively utilized in medical follow-up research and remains the most commonly used method for multifactor analysis in survival analysis. In the context of COX regression, the term “Exp (coef)” refers to the risk hazard ration (HR), while “Lower95” and “Upper95” are the upper and lower confidence intervals of the HR, respectively.

Statistical analysis

The date of CRC diagnosis was designated as the diagnosis date, and overall survival (OS) was calculated from the diagnosis date until the date of death, with censoring at the last follow-up date for survivors. The association between clinical and histopathological parameters with OS were evaluated using Kaplan–Meier curves and compared using the log-rank test. The chi-square (Χ2) test was used to examine the relationship between the NLR and RLR groups with clinicopathological parameters. To assess the potential prognositic factors for OS, including sex, age, TNM stage, NLR, RLR and RDW, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were conducted. The hazard ratios (HRs) obtained from the Cox analysis were reported as relative risks, accompanied by their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All analyses were performed using the R statistical software package (version R-4.3).To account for potential inter-center heterogeneity, we first compared baseline characteristics between the two centers based on data in Tables 1 and 2, and then performed a multivariate Cox regression analysis adjusted for the “center” factor to confirm the independent prognostic value of RLR.

Results

Table 1 presents the demographic and clinicopathological data of the 1,541 patients included in the study (detailed baseline data for all patients are shown in Table S1). Strict adherence to the exclusion criteria yielded a highly homogeneous cohort: the prevalence of congestive heart failure, chronic kidney disease, and chronic liver disease was 0% in the final study population. Furthermore, all included patients maintained baseline hemoglobin levels ≥ 100 g/L, ensuring that severe anemia did not confound the RDW measurements.

The study population was pooled from two clinical centers, comprising 1,305 patients from the first center and 236 from the second. Significant differences in T-stage distribution were observed between the two centers (P < 0.001), reflecting the different patient referral patterns of the institutions. However, since T-stage and N-stage were included as covariates in the subsequent multivariate analysis, the potential confounding effect of center-specific case mix was largely accounted for by these clinical variables. To minimize inter-center heterogeneity, we focused on the aggregated data of the entire cohort rather than comparing individual center characteristics. Consequently, the combined inflammatory indicators for the total population showed a median NLR of 8.05 (IQR: 6.25–10.89) and a median RLR of 2.13 (IQR: 1.56–3.04).

Regarding the T stage in the overall cohort, there were 6 patients with Tis, 31 with T1, 230 with T2, 494 with T3, and 777 with T4, with 3 cases marked as NA. In terms of the N stage, 887 patients had N0, 431 had N1, and 223 had N2. The stage distribution was as follows: 200 had stage I, 694 had stage II, 643 had stage III, and 4 had stage NA. Notably, no Stage IV patients were included in the final analysis. Among the patients, 1,237 (80.3%) were alive, while 304 (19.7%) had died during the entire follow-up period. The median follow-up time was 37 months. Given the strict uniform exclusion criteria and consistent laboratory protocols adopted by both centers, the pooled data demonstrated high consistency in key demographic and clinicopathological features, supporting the validity of the combined analysis.To determine the optimal cut-off values for the biomarkers, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed (Fig. 2; Table 3). The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated, CEA with an AUC of 0.63 (sensitivity 68.1%, specificity 53.3%), RLR with an AUC of 0.553 (sensitivity 49.3%, specificity 62.3%), NLR with an AUC of 0.552 (sensitivity 39.8%, specificity 70.5%), lymphocyte with an AUC of 0.547 (sensitivity 58.9%, specificity 52.4%), and RDW with an AUC of 0.544 (sensitivity 39.8%, specificity 67.9%). RLR exhibited the highest average AUC, sensitivity, specificity, and Youden index among the prognostic factors, except for CEA.

A total of 1,305 patients with complete screening data were included in the study. To address the issue of imbalanced data, the dataset was resampled with the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE), resulting in a total of 2,162 samples. Subsequently, the three indicators (preCA199, preCEA, and preCA125), were normalized for the 2,162 patients. An SVM classification model was then constructed using 24 indicators as input to classify the patients. The performance of the model yielded an accuracy (ACC) = 83.53494624, sensitivity (SEN) of 73.29998018, specificity (SPE) of 93.71437762; and an AUC of 91.32876105 (Fig. 3A). To further understand the importance of the indicators, a weight matrix in the SVM classification model as utilized to rank the 24 indicators. Based on this ranking, the top five important indicators were identified (Fig. 3B). Subsequently, these top five indicators were combined in pairs, resulting in a total of 10 combinations. Classification was performed using each combination of indicators, and the corresponding results were obtained (Fig. 3C).

Our objective was to develop a model to explore the value of RLR (specific biomarker), compared to conventional tumor markers in predicting OS in CRC patients. Initially, we included all enrolled populations in our study. Using LASSO regression (Fig. 4A-B), we screened five biomarkers and clinicopathological parameters that exhibited the great contribution to OS. These five markers were subsequently included in multivariate Cox regression analysis to confirm their independent prognostic significance. The final model comprised CEA (HR = 1.0032, P = 0.0025), RLR (HR = 1.018, P = 0.0157), T-stage (HR = 1.4456, P < 0.001), N-stage (HR = 1.2092, P = 0.0962), and Stage (HR = 1.7024, P = 0.0012) (Fig. 4C).

Furthermore, we built a prognostic model based on these five indicators and calculated a prognostic risk score for each sample. The samples were then sorted from low to high according to their risk score and visualized in a scatter plot (Fig. 4D), Notably, the scatter plot demonstrated a higher concentration of patients with short OS in the high-risk score range (Fig. 4E). The difference in prognostic OS between the high- and low-risk groups was statistically significant, with the high-risk group exhibiting a worse prognosis (Fig. 4F).

To validate the effectiveness of our model(Baseline data for the population of discovery and validation sets are shown in Table 2), we performed PCA (Fig. 4G) and tSNE (Fig. 4H) analysis using the prognostic risk score. These analyses effectively distinguished between the high-risk and low-risk groups. Finally, we evaluated the diagnostic efficacy of our model by analyzing the risk scores for each sample using a time-based ROC curve, which demonstrated an AUC of 0.723 for 5-year diagnostic efficacy (Fig. 4I).

Prognostic nomograms were developed to predict one-year and two-year outcomes, incorporating these three markers (Fig. 5A). The red line in the graph serves as a guide for physicians, illustrating that by inputting the corresponding patient data, they can obtain survival rates at 1, 3, and 5 years. The nomogram’s predictive accuracy was validated using bootstrap sampling within the model population. The one-year OS (green line), three-year OS (blue line), and five-year OS (red line) in the graph closely overlapped with the dotted line, indicating a high degree of concordance (Fig. 5B).

The result demonstrated that patients with elevated CEA levels, increased RLR expression, advanced T-stage, and higher clinical stage had poorer prognoses. Furthermore, CEA, RLR, T-stage and clinical stage were identified as independent prognostic factors for OS in CRC patients.

Discussion

Predicting postoperative survival in CRC patients is crucial for effective patient care1. Various factors, including lymphocyte levels, have been identified as valuable prognostic indicators2,5,6. Studies have consistently shown that patients with higher lymphocyte levels tend to have better survival rates10. Consequently, monitoring lymphocyte levels can be useful in predicting the prognosis of CRC patients following surgery.

However, this study aimed to investigate the prognostic significance of inflammatory markers and clinical features in CRC patients. Our findings revealed that OS was associated with the T-stage, CEA, RLR, and clinical stages. Notably, RLR emerged as an independent prognostic factor for OS.

Recent research has emphasized the role of inflammation in tumorigenesis and its association with the tumor microenvironment11. Patients with CRC have been observed to have significantly higher NLR and PLR levels compared to 1,232 healthy participants23. Furthermore, in a retrospective analysis of 1,383 postoperative CRC patients, low NLR, PLR, and SII were associated with improved OS and disease-free survival (DFS)24. Additional analyses, including one by Proctor et al., involving 12,118 patients, and 1,413 of them with CRC diagnosis, demonstrated that NLR serves as a significant surrogate marker for OS and cancer-specific survival (CSS)25. Although the enrolled patient characteristics and optimal cutoff values for NLR may vary across patients, numerous studies have demonstrated the utility of NLR as a valuable predictor of survival in CRC patients.

Besides lymphocyte levels, other biomarkers have also been explored as potential prognostic indicators after CRC surgery26 For instance, red blood cell distribution width (RDW) has been associated with various health conditions, including cancer. Some studies have suggested that elevated RDW levels might correlate with poorer outcomes in CRC patients. Nevertheless, the relationship between RDW and CRC remains incompletely understood, warranting further investigation.

The RDW to lymphocyte ratio (RLR) has been proposed as a potential predictor of survival in various cancers, including CRC18,27. Some studies have suggested that higher RLR may be associated with poorer outcomes in CRC patients. However, further research is required to validate these findings and determine the clinical utility of RLR in predicting survival after CRC surgery.

In a study by Ma et al., RLR was higher in CRC patients than the healthy population, suggesting its potential as a biomarker28. Other studies retrospectively examined 273 Renal Cell Carcinoma patients and showed that high RLR (≥ 8.6) was associated with shorter survival29. Furthermore, the study revealed that RLR is an independent prognostic factor for OS. Consistent with these findings, our study also demonstrated that patients with low RLR had longer OS durations compared to those with high RLR. Although RLR showed only a marginally higher AUC than NLR (0.553 vs. 0.552) and lymphocyte count (0.547), its independent prognostic significance in multivariate analysis—despite adjustment for CEA and TNM stage—confirms its incremental value where NRI calculation was not feasible. Biologically, RLR uniquely integrates erythropoietic stress (RDW) with immune competence, offering a more comprehensive assessment of host-tumor interaction than NLR, which reflects inflammation alone.

One of the notable strengths of our study lies in the utilization of machine learning approaches, which adds significant value to our research30. By employing machine learning algorithms, we were able to analyze and interpret complex data patterns, identify relevant biomarkers, and develop predictive models for survival outcomes in CRC patients. This approach enhances the robustness and precision of our findings and lays the groundwork for personalized medicine and improved patient care.

Although the long recruitment period introduces potential temporal heterogeneity, the consistent single-center protocols and the biological stability of fundamental hematological indices (RDW, lymphocytes) mitigate this concern. Moreover, the extended timeframe enhances the generalizability of RLR across different eras of CRC management.

We acknowledge the heterogeneity between colon and rectal cancers (e.g., neoadjuvant therapy usage). Due to data anonymization in this retrospective study, subsite-specific stratification was not feasible. However, existing evidence suggests systemic inflammatory markers (RDW, lymphocytes) generally exhibit consistent prognostic value across both subsites. Thus, RLR likely reflects a pan-CRC host response. Nevertheless, the lack of site-specific validation remains a limitation, warranting future prospective stratified studies.

It is worth noting that this study has several strengths, particularly the utilization of machine learning approaches, which significantly enhances the robustness and precision of our predictive models. However, several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, our two-center, single-region cohort necessitates multi-ethnic validation to account for genetic and environmental variations affecting RLR baselines. Second, the long enrollment period (2004–2019) may introduce temporal heterogeneity. However, core treatment principles (fluoropyrimidine/oxaliplatin-based chemotherapy) remained consistent throughout, and the exclusion of stage IV patients minimized confounding from evolving targeted therapies. Additionally, since RLR is derived from CBC—a standardized assay unaffected by technological shifts—the relative prognostic value of RLR likely remains stable despite minor evolutions in clinical practice. Third, we hypothesize RLR reflects a ‘dual-hit’ mechanism: combining inflammation-driven ineffective erythropoiesis (high RDW) with compromised immune surveillance (low lymphocytes), signifying a hostile tumor microenvironment, though molecular verification is needed. Fourth, restricted to pre-treatment data, the dynamic prognostic value of postoperative RLR remains unexplored.

Future studies should therefore prioritize: (1) multi-center validation across diverse populations; (2) longitudinal monitoring during therapy; and (3) integration with molecular markers (e.g., MSI, KRAS) to refine personalized CRC management. “CRC is an inflammation-associated tumor closely linked to inflammatory processes. RLR, as an immunoinflammatory indicator, holds the potential to serve as a novel biomarker for CRC. Our previous and current studies, which are the first of their kind in the literature, have shown that patients with CRC with low RLR values exhibit longer survival. Furthermore, RLR has been identified as an independent prognostic factor for OS.

RLR may have advantages such as being a multi-testable, cost-effective, and easily implementable marker in daily clinical practice for CRC patients. However, more research is needed to fully elucidate the relationship between these biomarkers and survival outcomes, as well as to determine the optimal integration of these markers into clinical practice.
