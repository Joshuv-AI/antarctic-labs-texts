Meflin/ISLR is a marker of adipose stem and progenitor cells in mice and humans that suppresses white adipose tissue remodeling and fibrosis
Authors: Toshikazu Ishihara, Katsuhiro Kato, Kotaro Matsumoto, Miyako Tanaka, Akitoshi Hara, Yukihiro Shiraki, Hidenori Morisaki, Yuya Urano, Ryota Ando, Kisuke Ito, Shinji Mii, Nobutoshi Esaki, Kazuhiro Furuhashi, Mikito Takefuji, Takayoshi Suganami, Toyoaki Murohara, Atsushi Enomoto
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIdentifying specific markers of adipose stem and progenitor cells (ASPCs) in vivo is crucial for understanding the biology of white adipose tissues (WAT). PDGFRα‐positive perivascular stromal cells represent the best candidates for ASPCs. This cell lineage differentiates into myofibroblasts that contribute to the impairment of WAT function. However, ASPC marker protein(s) that are functionally crucial for maintaining WAT homeostasis are unknown. We previously identified Meflin as a marker of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) in bone marrow and tissue‐resident perivascular fibroblasts in various tissues. We also demonstrated that Meflin maintains the undifferentiated status of MSCs/fibroblasts. Here, we show that Meflin is expressed in WAT ASPCs. A lineage‐tracing experiment showed that Meflin+ ASPCs proliferate in the WAT of obese mice induced by a high‐fat diet (HFD), while some of them differentiate into myofibroblasts or mature adipocytes. Meflin knockout mice fed an HFD exhibited a significant fibrotic response as well as increases in adipocyte cell size and the number of crown‐like structures in WAT, accompanied by impaired glucose tolerance. These data suggested that Meflin expressed by ASPCs may have a role in reducing disease progression associated with WAT dysfunction.Keywords: adipose stem and progenitor cells, adipose stem cells, fibrosis, glucose tolerance, immunoglobulin superfamily containing leucine‐rich repeat, Islr, Meflin, mesenchymal stem cell, metabolic syndrome

INTRODUCTION

There is accumulating evidence that mesenchymal stromal/stem cells (MSCs) or mesenchymal progenitors with multilineage differentiation potential are present throughout the body, including white adipose tissues (WAT) (Marcelin et al., 2022; Pittenger et al., 1999; Zuk et al., 2001; Zuk et al., 2002). These cells can be isolated from the normal WAT by adherence to plastic and expanded in culture. The isolates are called adipose tissue‐derived stem cells (ADSCs) or stromal vascular fraction cells (Mazini et al., 2019). Those isolated and ex vivo expanded ADSCs have a number of tissue‐protective, regenerative, and immunomodulatory effects when administered to animal models of various types of diseases (Czerwiec et al., 2023). These findings have led to numerous clinical trials to test for possible therapeutic effects following the administration of autologous or allogeneic ADSCs to patients with chronic wounds, autoimmune diseases, and ischemic diseases (Czerwiec et al., 2023; Mazini et al., 2019; Shimizu et al., 2022).

The identity and spatial localization of the in situ counterpart of ADSCs in WAT have yet to be determined (Si et al., 2019). Researchers have given these cells different names based on the isolation methods, including adipocyte progenitor cells (APCs), white fat progenitor cells, or adipose stem and progenitor cells (ASPCs; the preferred term here). A common feature of ASPCs is that they can yield mature adipocytes in vivo (de novo adipogenesis) following cell transplantation and in lineage‐tracing studies in mice. Pioneering studies have shown that these cells are negative for endothelial and hematopoietic cell markers such as CD45 and platelet and endothelial cell adhesion molecule 1 (CD31), whereas positive for integrin subunit beta 1 (CD29), CD34, Sca‐1, and CD24 (Marcelin et al., 2022; Rodeheffer et al., 2008; Sakers et al., 2022). They are also positive for platelet‐derived growth factor receptor β (PDGFRβ), a marker of mural cells including pericytes in capillaries and vascular smooth muscle cells (SMCs) in larger vessels (Marcelin et al., 2022; Sakers et al., 2022; Tang et al., 2008; Vishvanath et al., 2016). Consistent with this, another study showed that perivascular cells positive for melanoma cell adhesion molecule (MCAM, CD146), chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan 4 (CSPG4/NG2) and PDGFRβ in various fetal and adult tissues can give rise to MSCs in vitro that undergo tri‐lineage differentiation into adipocytes, chondrocytes, and osteocytes (Corselli et al., 2012; Crisan et al., 2008; Crisan et al., 2009). However, lineage‐tracing studies have challenged potential contributions from those mural cells in vivo (Guimarães‐Camboa et al., 2017). Thus, it is inconclusive and debatable which cell type(s) correspond to ASPCs and the in situ counterpart of ADSCs in WAT.

PDGFRα was initially identified as a marker of mesenchymal progenitors or fibro/adipogenic progenitors of skeletal muscle (Joe et al., 2010; Uezumi et al., 2010). It is also expressed by perivascular cells and defines ASPCs (Berry & Rodeheffer, 2013). PDGFRα+ ASPCs are localized adjacent to capillary blood vessels or in the tunica adventitia of vessel walls and are thus also called adventitial fibroblasts or perivascular fibroblasts (Berry & Rodeheffer, 2013; Cattaneo et al., 2020). Interestingly, recent parallel lineage‐tracing studies have employed Cre alleles selectively labeling several vascular wall cell types and demonstrated that PDGFRα+ fibroblasts, which may or may not express PDGFRβ, but not Tbx18+ mural cells, make a significant contribution to de novo adipogenesis that occurs in various WAT depots (Cattaneo et al., 2020; Han et al., 2021). Thus, PDGFRα+ ASPCs that reside within the vascular niche contribute significantly to adipocyte generation. PDGFRα activation by its cognate ligands in ASPCs induces fibrosis and lipodystrophy of WAT by converting them into fibroblasts that produce excess amounts of extracellular matrix and block their differentiation into adipocytes. Those data suggest that PDGFRα and its ligands are pro‐fibrotic factors that promote fibrosis at the expense of generating adipocytes (Marcelin et al., 2019; Sun et al., 2017).

We previously identified Meflin, a glycosyl‐phosphatidylinositol‐anchored membrane protein. It is encoded by the immunoglobulin superfamily containing the leucine‐rich repeat (Islr) gene. It is a specific marker of bone marrow‐derived MSCs (BM‐MSCs) or fibroblasts that reside adjacent to the vasculature in the WAT, BM, skeletal muscle, brain, skin, heart, kidney, pancreas, and lung (Maeda et al., 2016). Meflin upregulates the activities of bone morphogenetic protein 7 (BMP7) and downregulates lysyl oxidase (Lox) by interacting with them, and it suppresses fibrosis and tissue stiffening (Hara et al., 2019; Iida et al., 2022; Kobayashi et al., 2021). Further analysis of Meflin knockout (KO) mice revealed that Meflin inhibits the differentiation of MSCs into osteoblasts and chondroblasts in embryonic development (Maeda et al., 2016). This finding was consistent with observations made by culturing BM‐MSCs, in which Meflin depletion accelerated osteogenic and chondrogenic differentiation (Maeda et al., 2016). Meflin KO mice were subjected to several models of cancer and cardiac, pulmonary, and renal fibrotic diseases. Those studies revealed that Meflin is essential for repair or regeneration following acute tissue injuries and chronic sustained injuries and that it inhibits the differentiation of fibroblasts into α‐smooth muscle actin (α‐SMA)‐positive myofibroblasts that have a central role in fibrosis and tissue stiffening (Ando et al., 2024; Hara et al., 2019; Kobayashi et al., 2021; Minatoguchi et al., 2022; Miyai et al., 2020; Miyai et al., 2022; Mizutani et al., 2019; Nakahara et al., 2021).

Our previous study showed that most Meflin+ BM‐MSCs are positive for PDGFRα, implying that Meflin might be a marker of ASPCs in WAT (Maeda et al., 2016). This hypothesis was partly supported by previous lineage‐tracing studies using Meflin reporter mice (Hara et al., 2021; Kuwano et al., 2021). In the present study, we examined the expression of Meflin in ASPCs and its changes in the hypertrophic visceral WAT of mice fed a high‐fat diet (HFD). We also used lineage‐tracing analysis to investigate the contribution of Meflin+ cells in de novo adipogenesis and fibrogenesis. Finally, we addressed the role of Meflin in the inflammation and fibrosis of WAT in HFD‐fed mice and examined how Meflin was functionally involved in glucose metabolism by performing oral glucose tolerance and insulin tolerance tests.

RESULTS

Expression of Meflin in ASPCs from human and mouse WAT

We examined publicly available datasets of single‐cell transcriptomic analyses of human and mouse subcutaneous and visceral WAT (GSE176067 and GSE176171). Thus, we examined which cell type(s) expressed the ISLR (human) and Islr (mouse) genes that encode human and mouse Meflin, respectively (Emont et al., 2022). The data showed that Meflin expression was highly and specifically enriched in PDGFRA + and CD34 + ASPCs but not other cell types, including pericytes and SMCs that were positive for MCAM (CD146) or CSPG4 (NG2) in both human and mouse WAT (Figure 1a, b). Interestingly, the distributions of cells highly positive for ISLR and PDGFRA were not entirely identical within the ASPC cluster of human WAT, with some cells being highly enriched in ISLR expression but weakly positive for PDGFRA and vice versa, suggesting that ASPCs were transcriptionally heterogeneous. The expressions of ISLR/Islr and PDGFRA/Pdgfra in the ASPC clusters were not necessarily correlated with those of genes encoding THY1 (CD90), 5′‐nucleotidase ecto (NT5E, CD73) and endoglin (ENG, CD105), which are well‐established markers of ADSCs cultured and expanded ex vivo (Supporting Figure S1). This observation supports the notion that the gene expression profile of ADSCs does not necessarily reflect that of their in situ counterparts. Neither ISLR/Islr nor PDGFRA/Pdgfra was expressed in other constituents of WAT, including ADIPOQ/Adipoq‐positive mature adipocytes, immune cells, mesothelial cells, and endothelial cells (Figures 1 and S2). Taken together, the analysis suggested that Meflin (ISLR/Islr) is expressed by a subset(s) of human and mouse PDGFRα+ ASPCs.

Upregulation of Meflin expression in WAT of obese mice induced by HFD

We sought to examine the changes in Meflin expression and the number of Meflin+ ASPCs that accompanied the hypertrophic change of visceral WAT. Thus, we fed 10‐week‐old male C57BL/6 mice with a standard chow diet (SD) or HFD for 16 weeks, followed by quantitative PCR (qPCR) and in situ hybridization (ISH) for quantification of Meflin expression in the epididymal fat pads that represent visceral WAT (Figure 2a). qPCR analysis showed a significant increase in Meflin expression in the epididymal fat pads. That conclusion was confirmed by ISH which detected cells positive for Meflin in tissue sections obtained from the epididymal fat pads (Figure 2b–d). Co‐ISH for Islr and Pdgfra, the most established marker of ASPCs (Berry & Rodeheffer, 2013; Cattaneo et al., 2020; Sakers et al., 2022), revealed that the majority of cells that increased following HFD were double‐positive for Islr and Pdgfra (Figure 2d). The number of cells that expressed only Islr or Pdgfra was also increased, although it did not reach statistical significance (Figure 2d). These data showed that Meflin+PDGFRα+ cells in the epididymal fat pads have the potential to increase in response to hypertrophic induction by HFD.

Morphology of Meflin+ cells and their lineage cells in the hypertrophic WAT: Analysis of Meflin reporter mice

Next, we used a Meflin reporter mouse (Ando et al., 2024; Hara et al., 2021; Kobayashi et al., 2021; Kobayashi et al., 2022; Mizutani et al., 2019) that we had previously generated to reveal the morphology of Meflin+ cells and their subsequent lineage. In that way, we traced their fate in the hypertrophic epididymal fat pads. We intraperitoneally (i.p.) administered tamoxifen into Meflin‐CreERT2 mice that were crossed with Rosa26‐LSL (LoxP‐stop‐LoxP)‐tdTomato mice (Meflin‐CreERT2; LSL‐tdTomato) (Mizutani et al., 2019) for five consecutive days to label Meflin+ cells with tdTomato expression. Subsequently, they were fed an SD or HFD for 16 weeks (Figure 3a). We first visualized cells that actively expressed Meflin and examined their perivascular localization by subjecting Meflin‐CreERT2; LSL‐tdTomato mice to in vivo vascular staining by intravenous injection of an anti‐CD31 antibody and isolectin B4 (Hirata et al., 2018; Minatoguchi et al., 2022) 1 day after the last dose of tamoxifen (Week 0), followed by isolating the epididymal fat pads and tissue clearing (Figure 3a, b). Consistent with our previous findings with the pancreas, BM, kidney, and skin (Ando et al., 2024; Maeda et al., 2016; Minatoguchi et al., 2022), we found that most Meflin+ cells were localized in close association with the vasculature, extending their long and thin cytoplasmic protrusions along the capillaries (Figure 3b). This was in accord with previous studies that showed that most PDGFRα+ ASPCs are localized close to the vasculature, some of which have a morphology that is not distinguishable from that of pericytes (Cattaneo et al., 2020; Han et al., 2021).

We next undertook an immunofluorescence (IF) analysis of tdTomato+ cells with an anti‐red fluorescent protein (RFP) antibody. Consistent with a previous study using another Meflin‐CreERT2 mouse line (Kuwano et al., 2021), the data showed that the number of tdTomato+ cells was increased in the hypertrophic epididymal fat pads in HFD‐fed mice compared to SD‐fed mice at week 16 (Figure 3c, d). This was consistent with the results obtained with ISH. Notably, most of the tdTomato+ cells proliferating in the hypertrophic epididymal fat pads exhibited a spindle‐shaped morphology. A minor fraction was positive for CD36, a marker of mature adipocytes (Figure 3c).

Meflin+ cells give rise to mature adipocytes and myofibroblasts in hypertrophic epididymal fat pads

We further evaluated the fate of tdTomato+ Meflin lineage cells in the hypertrophic epididymal fat pads induced by HFD. Thus, we stained the tissues obtained at week 16 for the ASPC marker PDGFRα and the myofibroblast marker α‐SMA by IF (Figure 4a). The expression of those markers and cell morphology showed that the number of α‐SMA+tdTomato+ myofibroblasts, but neither mature adipocytes nor PDGFRα+tdTomato+ cells, was significantly increased by HFD. They comprised approximately 45% of the tdTomato+ population (Figure 4b, c). The data suggested that Meflin+ cells predominantly give rise to myofibroblasts, but less often yield mature adipocytes, supporting our notion that Meflin is a marker of at least a subset of ASPCs (Figure 4d).

Meflin deficiency increased adipocyte cell size and the progression of fibrosis and crown‐like structure (CLS) formation in hypertrophic WAT

We previously found that Meflin deficiency accelerated the differentiation of BM‐MSC into osteoblasts and chondroblasts in the neonatal period (Hara et al., 2019; Maeda et al., 2016). Therefore, we aimed to examine the role of Meflin in de novo adipogenesis in epididymal fat pads. Toward that end, we fed 10‐week‐old male Meflin KO mice (Maeda et al., 2016) and wild‐type littermates an HFD for 16 weeks. We then determined the size of adipocytes in the epididymal fat pads (Figure 5a, b). The result showed that the mean diameter of the adipocytes in Meflin KO mice was significantly larger than that in wild‐type mice, suggesting that Meflin deficiency may promote the hypertrophic changes that occurred in ASPC‐derived adipocytes. In addition, the hypertrophic epididymal fat pads developed in Meflin KO mice exhibited accelerated fibrosis in some regions compared to those in wild‐type mice, as assessed by Masson's Trichrome staining (Figure 5c, d). These data suggested that Meflin suppressed the growth of adipocytes and subsequent fibrosis in the stroma of hypertrophic WAT.

(c), (d) The epididymal fat tissues of wild‐type (WT) and Meflin KO mice (KO) fed either SD or HFD were subjected to Masson's Trichrome staining (c), followed by measurement of fibrotic areas (arrows) and quantification (d).

In the hypertrophic epididymal fat pads developed in Meflin KO mice, some adipocytes were surrounded by a fibrotic interstitium (Figure 5c). This development was reminiscent of the CLS, a marker of macrophage infiltration and a hallmark of a chronic fibroinflammatory reactions observed in hypertrophic WAT or various disease conditions associated with obesity and metabolic syndrome such as metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatohepatitis (Cinti et al., 2005; Ichioka et al., 2011; Murano et al., 2008; Tanaka et al., 2014; Tanaka et al., 2018; Wueest et al., 2010). Staining the hypertrophic epididymal fat pad tissue for the macrophage marker F4/80 clearly showed a significant increase in CLS in Meflin KO mice (Figure 6a, b). Thus, Meflin deficiency may accelerate WAT inflammation and the associated fibrosis that may impair the function of WAT.

Meflin deficiency leads to the development of insulin resistance

In these studies, we observed accelerated hypertrophic growth of adipocytes, fibrotic development, and formation of CLS in the WAT of Meflin KO mice fed an HFD. Consequently, we hypothesized that Meflin KO mice might develop insulin resistance linked to adipocyte dysfunction. First, we discovered that Meflin KO mice exhibited no significant body weight loss but a significant increase in the total weight of the inguinal and epididymal fat pads compared with wild‐type littermates when fed an HFD (Figure 7a–c). Intriguingly, the total weights of brown adipose tissues (BAT) isolated from the interscapular region were comparable between wild‐type and Meflin KO mice (Figure 7c).

Next, wild‐type and Meflin KO mice were fed either an SD or an HFD for 16 weeks, Then, they were subjected to fasting for 18 or 1 h. This was followed by a glucose tolerance test (intraperitoneal glucose tolerance: IPGTT) and an intraperitoneal insulin tolerance test (IPITT) to examine whether the increased volume of WAT led to insulin resistance in Meflin KO mice (Figure 7d). We first found that plasma insulin concentrations measured in the IPGTT test were comparable in wild‐type and Meflin KO mice (Figure 7e). Interestingly, we found significantly elevated blood glucose concentrations after the administration of glucose (2 g/kg) or insulin (0.5 U/kg) in Meflin KO mice compared with those in wild‐type littermates (Figure 7f, g). Those data suggested that Meflin KO mice had the characteristics of insulin resistance.

DISCUSSION

Metabolic diseases and syndromes associated with obesity are increasing worldwide. There is an urgent need for greater understanding of the mechanisms underlying those diseases as well as for the development of therapeutics (Saltiel & Olefsky, 2017). Evidence suggests that the impairment of WAT function is central to the etiology of those diseases (Rosen & Spiegelman, 2014). However, it has proven to be difficult to identify intrinsic factors that maintain WAT homeostasis and suppress the development and progression of hypertrophic growth, inflammation, and fibrosis within WAT. In the present study, we found that Meflin is expressed explicitly in at least some subset(s) of ASPCs. Moreover, Meflin+ cells have the potential to increase in number, retaining the expression of PDGFRα and giving rise to mature adipocytes and myofibroblasts, resulting in a condition of excessive fat accumulation in WAT. We also found that Meflin participates in suppressing the hypertrophic growth of adipocytes, the development of fibrosis, CLS formation, and the development of insulin resistance. These observations are consistent with the previously described functions of Meflin in other organs, suggesting that it may have protective roles in disease progression (Ando et al., 2024; Hara et al., 2019; Kobayashi et al., 2021; Miyai et al., 2022; Mizutani et al., 2019; Nakahara et al., 2021). However, we could not provide any further insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying Meflin's prevention of adipocytes' increase in cell size, WAT fibrosis, and the progression of insulin resistance. The role of Meflin in controlling inflammation in WAT was also not investigated in the present study. We previously reported that the number of Meflin+ fibroblasts was positively correlated with that of CD4+ T cells in the stroma and favorable response to immune checkpoint inhibitors in lung and urothelial cancers (Barrett & Puré, 2020; Miyai et al., 2022; Owaki et al., 2024; Shiraki et al., 2022), implying that the immune regulatory function of Meflin+ fibroblasts may be different from that of conventional α‐SMA+ (Meflin mRNA expression: weak or negative) myofibroblasts. Therefore, the differential roles of the heterogeneous fibroblasts in WAT inflammation will be the subject of further investigation.

A previous study by another research group showed that Meflin may have a role in aggravating insulin resistance by mediating proteasome‐dependent degradation of insulin receptor subunits Insrα and Insrβ (Zhang et al., 2023). The reason for this discrepancy is worthy of further investigation. One possible explanation may be that the study by Zhang et al. examined the phenotype of a conditional Meflin KO mouse line generated by crossing Meflin floxed (Islr fl/fl) mice with a transgenic mouse line that constitutively expressed Cre recombinase under the promoter of the adiponectin gene (Adipoq‐Cre). Under those conditions, they observed a specific depletion of Meflin in mature adipocytes (Zhang et al., 2023). However, our analysis of single‐cell transcriptomic data sets of human and mouse WAT showed that Adipoq is predominantly expressed in adipocytes but not in most of the ASPCs that are positive for Meflin and PDGFRα (Supporting Figure S2), making it difficult to unambiguously interpret the phenotype observed in Adipoq‐Cre; Islr fl/fl mice. We used conventional global Meflin KO mice, whose phenotype may be accompanied by compensation mechanisms or reciprocal upregulation of molecular pathways that affect insulin sensitivity. Thus, the precise role of Meflin in ASPCs, WAT homeostasis, and glucose metabolism should be further investigated in detail through examining inducible conditional KO of Meflin in ASPCs of adult mice.

The observations obtained here are consistent with our hypothesis that Meflin is preferentially expressed in MSCs and undifferentiated fibroblasts. Those cells are not distinguishable from one another by morphology or in situ location. In fact, they share common features and immunological markers across tissues and organs (Miyai et al., 2020). There has been increasing attention focused on the identification of bona fide undifferentiated or naïve fibroblasts that are capable of differentiating into specialized fibroblasts, including myofibroblasts and cancer‐associated fibroblasts. A recent study performed a comprehensive single‐cell transcriptomic analysis across multiple steady‐state mouse tissues. It identified several genes, including peptidase inhibitor 16 (Pi16), collagen type XV alpha 1 chain (Col15a1), and dermatopontin (Dpt) as markers of undifferentiated fibroblasts. Those cells serve as sources that can develop into specialized fibroblasts and were termed “universal fibroblasts” (Buechler et al., 2021). Interestingly, ASPCs of human and mouse WAT express these universal fibroblast markers to various extents (Supporting Figure S2), suggesting the existence of common physiological features between MSCs, fibroblasts, and ASPCs. It will be worth further exploring how those universal fibroblast markers, PDGFRα and Meflin, are functionally involved in regulating ASPC function and WAT homeostasis. Identification of stimuli and factors that maintain the expression of PDGFRα and Meflin in undifferentiated ASPCs or downregulate that in mature adipocytes will be another subject of our future work.

In summary, we report that Meflin is expressed explicitly in ASPCs that are positive for PDGFRα and localized in the perivascular space. There, it may have a role in suppressing the hypertrophy of adipocytes, fibrosis, and CLS formation of WAT and maintaining glucose homeostasis in HFD‐fed mice. Thus, Meflin is a functional marker of ASPCs, the role of which seems to be different from that of another ASPC marker, PDGFRα.

EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES

Animals

All animal experiments were carried out according to protocols approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Nagoya University Graduate School of Medicine, Japan (approval number M220014‐006). The generation of Meflin KO mice and their phenotype were described previously (Maeda et al., 2016). Genomic DNA was extracted from mouse tails and was used for PCR genotyping. Sequences of the primers were as follows: PCR1 forward, 5′‐GCTGCATTTGAGCTGAGCCTCTGG‐3′; PCR1 reverse, 5′‐AACCCCTTCCTCCTACATAGTTGG‐3′; PCR2 forward, 5′‐TGAGGTTAGCCTGGGGACTTCAC‐3′; PCR2 reverse, 5′‐GGCTAGAACTCTCAAAGTAGGTCAGG‐3′. The generation of Meflin‐CreERT2 knock‐in mice was described previously (Mizutani et al., 2019). To conduct lineage tracing experiments, Meflin‐CreERT2 mice were crossed with Rosa26‐LoxP‐stop‐LoxP (LSL)‐tdTomato mice (JAX Stock Number: 007909) to generate Meflin‐CreERT2; Rosa26‐LSL‐tdTomato mice. They were maintained in a temperature‐, humidity‐ and light‐controlled room (12 h light/dark cycles), and allowed free access to water and standard diet (SD) (CE‐2; 343.1 kcal per 100 g, 12.6% energy as fat; CLEA Japan, Tokyo, Japan). We used male 9–10‐week‐old mice with a C57BL/6J background in all experiments in the present study unless otherwise noted.

Metabolic syndrome mouse model

To make a metabolic syndrome mouse model, 10‐week‐old male animals were given a HFD (D12492; 556 kcal per 100 g, 60% energy as fat; Research Diets, New Brunswick, NJ) for 16 weeks and then harvested. We compared metabolic syndrome model mice to control mice fed a SD (CE‐2). Mouse body weights were measured every week, and when they were harvested, weights of the inguinal fat tissue, epididymal fat tissue, and BAT were calculated.

qPCR

Total RNA was purified from the whole epididymal fat tissue using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Cat. no. 74104; QIAGEN) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Purified RNA samples were reverse‐transcribed using ReverTra Ace (Cat. no. TRT‐101; Toyobo) with oligo dT and random primers. qPCR of the generated cDNAs was performed with a TaqMan Gene Expression Master Mix (Cat. no. 4369016; Applied Biosystems) using a StepOnePlus thermal cycler (Applied Biosystems). Applied Biosystems synthesizes customized TaqMan probes and primers for the mouse Meflin (Islr) coding sequence (Mm01700423_m1). Cycling conditions were as follows: 50°C for 2 min, 95°C for 10 min, 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s, and 60°C for 1 min. The data were analyzed using the 2‐ΔΔCt method and normalized to actb (Mm02619580_g1).

ISH

ISH analyses were performed using formalin‐fixed and paraffin‐embedded and mouse tissue samples with RNAscope technology (RNAscope Multiplex Fluorescent Reagent Kit v2; Advanced Cell Diagnostics) and custom‐designed probes according to the manufacturer's instructions as described previously (Hara et al., 2019; Mizutani et al., 2019). Briefly, tissue sections were baked in a dry oven (HybEZ II Hybridization System; Advanced Cell Diagnostics) at 60°C for 1 h, deparaffinized, and incubated with an H2O2 solution for 10 min at room temperature (RT). Slides were boiled in target‐retrieval solution for 15 min, incubated with protease solution for 30 min at 40°C, incubated with the relevant probes for 3 h at 40°C, and then successively incubated with Amp1 to −3 reagents. Staining was visualized with (C1: Opal570, #:op_001003, Marlborough), and (C2: Opal690, #:op_001006, AKOYA), followed by incubation with for 4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI) for nuclear staining and mounting in PermaFluor Aqueous Mounting Medium (TA‐030‐FM, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The RNAscope probes used were as follows: mouse Meflin (Islr) (NM_012043.4, region 763–1690; catalogue number 450041) and mouse Pdgfra‐C2 (NM_011058.2, region 223–1161; catalogue number 480661‐C2).

Assessment of ISH staining

Cells that had three or more dots or clusters of ISH signals were considered positive. We analyzed three high‐power fields (HPF) in each slide. We counted cells double positive for Islr and Pdgfra and single positive for either Islr or Pdgfra, then compared the number of those cells between the SD and HFD groups.

Tissue clearing and three‐dimensional (3D) imaging

For 3D imaging of mouse tissues, Meflin‐CreERT2 mice were crossed with Rosa26‐LSL‐tdTomato mice. Vasculature staining, preparations of CUBIC‐L and CUBIC‐R solutions, and tissue clearing were performed as previously described with some modifications (Ando et al., 2024; Hirata et al., 2018; Matsumoto et al., 2019; Minatoguchi et al., 2022; Tainaka et al., 2014; Takahashi et al., 2020;). Briefly, a mixture of 20 μL anti‐CD31 antibody (clone MEC 13.3; 102,516, BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA), 20 μL isolectin B4 (1000 μg/mL; I32450, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 160 μL D‐PBS (−) were injected intravenously. After 1 h, deeply anesthetized mice were perfused with cold PBS and then with 4% PFA in PBS. Isolated epididymal fat tissue were immersed in 4% PFA overnight at 4°C. The tissues were immersed in 1/2 CUBIC‐L solution (1:1 mixture of water and CUBIC‐L) at RT overnight, followed by CUBIC‐L solution exchanged every 2 days with gentle shaking at RT for 1 week. Afterward, the sample was gently shaken in 0.05% NaN3/D‐PBS(−) solution containing with 2 ng/mL DAPI at 37°C for 3 days. The decolorized and delipidated samples were washed three times with 0.05% NaN3/PBS at RT overnight under gentle shaking. The samples were then immersed in the 1/2 CUBIC‐R solution at RT for 20 h and then in the CUBIC‐R solution for 2 days. The surface of the sample was made transparent, so it was observed under light sheet microscopy (Zeiss Lightsheet 7, Carl Zeiss AG, Germany) with a focus on the surface layer. Z‐stack images with a total thickness of 469.11 μm and a step size of 2.79 μm were acquired with a 5× objective lens, 1.5× digital zoom, and 3 × 3 tiling. 3D‐rendered images were visualized, captured, and analyzed using Imaris software 9.2 (Bitplane, Zurich, Switzerland).

Lineage tracing experiments

10‐week‐old Meflin‐CreERT2; Rosa26‐ LSL‐tdTomato mice were administered 2 mg tamoxifen (T5648‐5G, Sigma‐Aldrich) by intraperitoneal injection five times every day. Tissues were harvested 3 days or 16 weeks after the fifth injection. We examined more than 10 tdTomato‐positive cells per slide, followed by counting the numbers of α‐SMA‐ and/or PDGFRα‐positive cells. Adipocytes in tdTomato‐positive cells were identified based on their morphology.

Histological analysis

The epididymal fat pad was fixed with neutral buffered formalin and embedded in paraffin. Four‐micron‐thick sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin, Masson's Trichrome, or subjected to IHC or IF. For the analysis of the size of adipocytes, ~100 cells were counted per section using image analysis software (ImageJ ver. 1.59, National Institute of Health).

Masson's trichrome staining

Masson's trichrome staining was performed using standard protocols as described previously (Ando et al., 2024). Briefly, deparaffinized and rehydrated tissue sections were placed in a solution with 10% trichloroacetic acid (203‐04952, Fujifilm Wako Chemicals) and 10% potassium dichromate (164‐03651, Fujifilm Wako Chemicals) for 30 min. Next, the sections were washed under running tap water and stained in Weigert's iron hematoxylin for 2–3 min. Then, washed sections were placed in 2.5% phosphomolybdic acid (P1910, Tokyo Chemical Industry, Tokyo, Japan) and 2.5% phosphotungstic acid (040116‐14, Alfa Aesar, Heysham, UK) for 45 s and stained with 0.75% Orange G (152‐02252, Fujifilm Wako Chemicals). The sections were washed with 1% acetic acid (017‐00256, Fujifilm Wako Chemicals) and further stained in a solution with Ponceau‐S (P3504, Sigma‐Aldrich), Fuchsin acid (061‐01332, Fujifilm Wako Chemicals), and Azophloxin (11,640, Sigma‐Aldrich) for 30 min. After another wash with 1% acetic acid, the sections were placed in 2.5% phosphotungstic acid for 10 min. Finally, the washed sections were stained in aniline blue (000‐04982, Kishida Chemical, Osaka, Japan) for 10 min and washed again quickly with 1% acetic acid. The fibrotic area was measured by image analysis software (Image J).

IF

Harvested adipose tissues were fixed in ice‐cold 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, 163‐18435, Wako) in PBS at 4°C overnight, followed by transfer through increasing concentrations of sucrose (5%, 10%, 15%, and 20% in PBS) for 6–12 h each. Tissues were placed in an embedding solution (8% gelatin [G1890, Sigma‐Aldrich], 1.5% polyvinylpyrrolidone [P5288, Sigma‐Aldrich], 15% sucrose [196‐00015, Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical] in PBS) and frozen using ice water and stored at −20°C. Frozen samples were sectioned to 20 μm thickness using a cryostat (CM3050S, Leica). Sections were rehydrated in PBS. The sections were incubated with blocking solutions (10% normal donkey serum [IHR‐8135, ImmunoBioScience], 0.1% Triton X‐100 in PBS) for 60 min at RT, followed by incubation overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies diluted in the blocking solutions. Following three washes with buffer (0.1% Triton X‐100 in PBS), the sections were treated with secondary antibodies in blocking solution overnight at 4°C. Sections were washed three times with wash buffer and counterstained with DAPI for nuclear staining, followed by mounting in PermaFluor Aqueous Mounting Medium. The primary and secondary antibodies used for IF were as follows: chicken polyclonal anti‐RFP (600‐901‐379, Rockland Immunochemicals, 1:500), rabbit monoclonal anti‐α‐SMA (#19245, Cell Signaling Technologies, 1:300), goat polyclonal anti‐CD36 (AF2519, R&D SYSTEMS, 1:200), goat polyclonal anti‐Pdgfra (AF1062, R&D SYSTEMS, 1:300), Cy3‐conjugated donkey anti‐chicken IgY (703‐165‐155, Jackson ImmunoResearch, 1:500), Alexa Fluor 488‐conjugated donkey anti‐rabbit IgG (A21206, Invitrogen, 1:500), Alexa Fluor 488‐conjugated donkey anti‐goat IgG (A11055, Invitrogen, 1:500), CF647 Donkey Anti‐Goat IgG (H + L), Highly Cross‐Adsorbed (#20048, Biotium, 1:500), and Alexa Fluor 647‐conjugated donkey anti‐rabbit IgG (A31573, Invitrogen, 1:500).

IHC

For IHC on formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded tissues, sections were deparaffinized with xylene and rehydrated with PBS, followed by immersion in an antigen retrieval buffer (HistoVT One, pH 7, Nacalai Tesque) for 30 min at 98°C. The sections were washed three times in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) and incubated in blocking buffer (X0909, Dako) for 30 min, followed by incubation with anti‐RFP rabbit polyclonal antibody (1:1000, 600–401‐379, Rockland Immunochemicals), which also detects the tdTomato fluorescent protein, or anti‐F4/80 rabbit polyclonal antibody (1:1000, 28463‐1‐AP, Proteintech) diluted with antibody diluent buffer (1% BSA, 0.1% NaN3 in PBS) overnight at 4°C. The sections were washed three times with PBS and immersed in 0.5% H2O2 in methanol for 15 min to inactivate endogenous peroxidase. The sections were washed three times in PBS and incubated in EnVision+ System HRP Labeled Polymer Anti‐Rabbit (K4003, Dako) for 30 min at RT. The sections were washed in PBS, incubated with Liquid DAB+ Substrate Chromogen System (K3468, Dako) for 5 min at RT, dehydrated in ethanol, and cleared in xylene, followed by mounting with xylene‐based mounting media Entellan (100869, Merck Millipore).

For the analysis of CLS, we examined more than 16 high‐magnification fields per slide, followed by quantification of the number of CLS.

Glucose and insulin tolerance tests

A glucose tolerance test was performed after an 18‐h fast. Blood glucose concentrations were measured with a blood glucose test meter (Glutest Neo Super, SANWA KAGAKU KENKYUSHO CO., LTD.) at 0, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min after intraperitoneal injection of glucose (2 g/kg body weight). For the insulin tolerance test, insulin (0.5 U/kg body weight of Humulin R‐Insulin; Eli Lilly, Indianapolis, IN) was injected i.p. after a 1‐h fast. Blood glucose concentrations were measured 0, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min after insulin injection. For the IPGTT and IPITT data, one‐way ANOVA and Tukey–Kramer tests were used at the 120‐min point (p <.05), and the area under the curve was analyzed with one‐way ANOVA and Tukey–Kramer tests (p <.05).

Serum analysis

Serum insulin concentrations were measured using a commercially available rat/mouse insulin enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay kit (M1108, Morinaga Institute of Biological Science, Inc.).

Statistical analysis

Data were graphed using Graph Pad Prism version 6. Data are presented as the mean ± s.e.m., and p <.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis was performed using analysis of variance followed by the one‐way analysis of variance (one‐way ANOVA) and the Tukey–Kramer test. An unpaired t‐test was used to compare the two groups. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics ver. 27.0 (IBM Corp.).
