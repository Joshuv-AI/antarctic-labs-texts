Epidemiological analysis of a cholera outbreak in pastoral communities in the Somali region of Ethiopia
Authors: Mussie Abdosh Hassen, Nasir Warfa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCholera is an acute diarrhea disease caused by infection of the intestine with the toxigenic bacterium Vibrio cholerae serogroup O1 or O139. It is a waterborne disease of global health importance with an estimated 3 to 5 million cases and over 100,000 deaths annually.MethodsOur key objective was to analyze the epidemiology of a cholera outbreak in the Somali region of Ethiopia, which took place in 2017, using an electronic surveillance cholera database. We investigated 7 cholera-affected-districts. In total, 4115 cases of cholera were reported, of which 3846 (93.5%) records were eligible.ResultsThree thousand eight hundred forty six cases and 112 deaths from 11 Cholera Treatment Centres (CTC) were reported, with attack risk (AR) of 103 per 10,000 populations, and a case fatality rate (CFR) of 2.95 %. The CFR was 3.1 in females and 2.8 in males. People with travel history to a cholera affected area were five times more likely to die from cholera (OR 5.00; 95% CI 3.23-7.87). People with severe dehydration were seven times more likely to die (OR 7.80; 95%CI 2.44-47.60). The age-specific CFR was highest (4.9%) in the 2-4 years’ age group, and lowest (1.7%) in the 15-44 age range. ConclusionsPastoral communities ought to be provided access to clean and safe drinkable water. There must be availability of anti-cholera vaccines to tackle the burden of the disease, for example, the OCV recommended by the Global Task Force on Cholera Control.Keywords: Cholera, Climate change, Gender equity, Epidemic management, Public hazards, Somali region of Ethiopia

Background

Cholera is characterized by acute watery diarrhea (AWD) with or without vomiting, and severe dehydration, often resulting in death if left untreated [1, 2]. One in ten infected people present with severe illness characterized by profuse watery diarrhea, vomiting, and leg cramps. Rapid loss of body fluids leads to dehydration, shock, and organ failure; and death can happen within hours [3]. The Case Fatality Rate (CFR) from untreated cholera can be as high as 30–50% but prompt administration of rehydration therapy can reduce the CFR to as low as 1% [1, 2]. Vibrio cholerae is usually found in water or food sources that have been contaminated by feces from an individual with the cholera disease. Cholera is most likely to be found and spread in places with poor sanitation and inadequate hygiene practices [4, 5]. Cholera remains an extremely important global public health challenge in countries where access to safe drinking water cannot be guaranteed [3, 6].

In 2017, an outbreak of cholera occurred in the Somali Regional State (SRS) and in other parts of Ethiopia. This 2017 outbreak was worsened by drought-induced displacement and the arrival of refugees from drought-affected border towns of Somalia [5]. With the highest number of cases and deaths, the Somali region was the most affected state. During this period, more than 38,715 cases leading to over 797 deaths (CFR 2.1%) had been reported in the country. Of these, 34,069 (88%) cases and 749 (94%) of the deaths occurred in the Somali region. In addition to El Niño-induced drought, the wide geographical land mass of the region and the pastoral-cum-nomadic lifestyles of the people further exposed the community to epidemic prone diseases as well as other humanitarian crisis including internal conflicts, protracted security situations and political instability [7], all of which are fertile grounds for disease outbreaks.

The World Health Organization (WHO) maintains an annual record of reported cholera cases and deaths and publishes annual statistics in the Weekly Epidemiological Record [8]. The WHO estimate that only 5–10% of the cases occurring annually are officially reported. This low reporting is due to a combination of factors including limited capacity of epidemiological surveillance systems and laboratories in low-income countries. Also, there is fear of socio-economic damages for reporting highly infectious diseases [6]. Since the change of administration in 2018, the government of Ethiopia has allowed greater academic and scientific freedom and is proactive in the advancement of effective public health strategies and knowledge that contribute to communicable and non-communicable disease prevention and disease control in the country. It is in this context we report epidemiological findings from the 2017 cholera outbreak in the Somali region, and the lessons learned. Specific objectives are to assess the incidence and CFRs by time, place, and persons in seven districts of Dollo zone (County), one of 11 zones (Counties) of the Somali region affected by the 2017 outbreak.

Method

Study area and data source

This manuscript is based on secondary surveillance data obtained during the 2017 cholera outbreak of seven districts in Dollo Zone in the Somali region of Ethiopia. After review by the ethical board of Somali Region Health Bureau (SRHB, Jigiga, Ethiopia), an ethical approval was granted in 2019. On 27 May 2020, a second ethical approval was received from the Institutional Ethics Review Board (IRB) of the Institute of Tropical Medicine (ITM), Antwerp, Belgium. The source of the data used for this study was the electronic surveillance cholera line list database of the SRHB Public Health Emergency Core Process. Following the recommendations of the ethics committees, all personal identifiable data were completely anonymised. Data was obtained soon after ethical approvals granted on 27.05.2020.

Cholera case identification and inclusion criteria

A case of cholera is suspected when (1) cholera symptoms are found in an area where the disease is not known to be present, and a patient aged five years or older develops severe dehydration or dies from acute watery diarrhoea (AWD). (2) When the symptoms are found in an area where there is a cholera epidemic and a patient aged five years or older develops acute watery diarrhoea, with or without vomiting, or dies from acute watery diarrhoea. Suspected case is confirmed when Vibrio cholerae O1 or O139 has been isolated from stool. In addition, we expanded our inclusion criteria to include children between 2 and 5 to also show how the cholera impacted on this age group. All individuals residing in Dollo districts who were reported to have cholera and who had the required data record completed were included. Individuals that did not fulfil the inclusion criteria were excluded [3].

Study design and source of data

Retrospective analysis of secondary surveillance data of cholera cases was conducted using a cholera outbreak line-list database obtained from the Somali Regional Health Bureau’s Public Health Emergency Management Core Process. In the Dollo zone, a total of eleven cholera treatment centers (CTCs) were established and were active in its seven districts, reporting epidemiological data back to the Somali Regional Health Bureau (SRHB). The Database consisted of detailed information of all reported cases that came to one of the treatment centres. All individual records in the database with the required information were analyzed and used for this study. The total cases of cholera reported from 11 CTCs to the SRHB was 4115, of which 3846 (93.5%) were eligible. See Fig. 1.

Statistical analysis

We used R (version 3.6.1) to conduct statistical analyses using Fisher’s Exact and Pearson’s Chi Square tests. We analysed the cholera outbreak in terms of time, place, and person. Attack Rates (AR) and Case Fatality Rate (CFR) were calculated by zone districts. We assessed risk factors using bivariate and multivariate regression models. Variables included in the regression model include age, sex, occupation, water sources, date of admissions, date of onset of symptoms, date of discharges, reported week, district zones, water sources, contact history, occupation, travel history, length of stay in CTCs, dehydration status, and laboratory results.

Results

Dollo zone in 2017 was 373,795, of whom 214,183 (57.3%) were males and 159,612 (42.7%) were females. 35,137 (9.4%) were urban inhabitants. 200,354 (53.6%) and 138,304 (37.0%) were rural and semi-rural pastoralists, respectively. Most of the population were aged from the fifteen to forty-five age group. Just over 20% were in the five to fourteen age group, and around 15% were aged group forty-five or over.

A total of 3846 cases of cholera from seven districts of Dollo zone that met the inclusion criteria were included in the study. Figure 2 shows a chart of cholera cases reported over an 18-week period (See Fig. 2). The data shows that the initial outbreak started with a low to moderate number of cases per week in the first five weeks followed by a rapid rise of cases starting from week 6 until week 15. The outbreak was characterised by two mini peaks (weeks 6 and 15) and one main peak (week 12), mirroring an unusual stop-start peak patterns, which suggest semi-controlled person-to-person transmissions (See Fig. 2).

From Table 4, bivariate analysis showed that people who had a travel history to a cholera impacted area were five times more likely to die from cholera (OR 5.00; 95% CI 3.23–7.87), when compared with non-travellers. People with severe dehydration were 7.8 times more likely to die than people with mild to moderate dehydration (OR 7.80; 95% CI 2.44–47.60). People with moderate dehydration were 1.6 times at risk of dying from cholera than those with mild dehydration. Deaths due to cholera was 4.76 times more for people who were using water provided through water trucking (OR4.76;955CI 1.48–29.14), when compared with other water sources (for example, tap water).

People who were living in Warder district were 4.25 times more likely to die from cholera (OR4.25; 95% CI 2.13–9.72) than persons living in other districts, followed by people living in Danot district with odds ratio of 2.42 times (OR2.42; 95% CI 1.10–5.91). The odds ratios for the age groups were: for 2–4 years (OR3.06; 95% CI 1.80–5.24), for 5–14 years (OR2.10; 95% CI 1.32–3.70); for 45 + years (OR2, 95%CI 1.10–3.59). The groups were more likely to die from cholera than the reference 15–44 age group.

In the final multivariate model, death due to cholera was associated with being in state of severe dehydration (aOR5.04; 95% CI 1.48–31.71); having a history of travel to the infection area (aOR10.50; 95% CI, 5.50–20.50). Interestingly, people were more likely to die from the use of water delivered through water trucking vehicles than water sources from tap water, borehole, shallow water, and water pool (aOR,4.21; 95% CI2.65-6.82.82). Patients from the Bookh district (aOR7.24; 95%CI 2.77–20.00.77.00), people from Galadi (aOR3.28; 95% CI 1.25–9.22) and those from Danot (2.07; 95% CI 1.07–6.32) were more likely to die of cholera than other districts.

Discussion

This study aimed to analyse the epidemiology (magnitude, spatial distributions, time, place, and person) of cholera cases and deaths which occurred in the Somali region of Ethiopia in 2017. During the cholera outbreak, many regional states of Ethiopia were affected with 38,715 cases and 797 deaths. However, pastoral communities in the Somali region had the highest number of cases (34,069: 88%) and deaths (749: 94%). The Somali Region State attack rates (AR) was 59.3/10,000 with a CFR of 2.1%. In 2016, the CFR in Africa was reported to be 1.8% [26, 27], and was higher than the WHO recommended benchmark (or acceptable) rate of < 1% [12]. The CFR of 2.1% found in the Somali region was more than double the recommended WHO rate of < 1%.

There are several other crucial findings from this study which have implications for public health, disease prevention and disease control. Warder was one of the districts worst affected by the outbreak but people from this town were least likely to die from cholera. Warder’s odds ratio was reduced in the final multivariate model. This could be partly because the most affected cholera age-group (15–44) were in Warder, and this strongest group was the least group to die from the outbreak. Or it may be the case that Warder (capital town for Dollo) had speedier access to resources and expertise, and thus was more effective in case fatality management than the other districts.

Moreover, the study findings support the importance of providing timely treatment for children under the age of 5 as this age-group had the highest case fatality rate for the 2017 cholera in the Somali Region of Ethiopia. From our findings, people obtaining water from “water trucking” were more likely to suffer from cholera and more likely to die from it than people using other sources of water, for example, tap water and water from Wells. One may then ask why people drinking water delivered through trucks were more at risk than people who were drinking water from the Wells, from which the trucks obtain the water in the first place. One possible explanation can be that if the tanks on the water trucks are not cleaned between the first and subsequent loads, then there is an opportunity for Vibrio-cholerae to multiple after each loading time.

We recommend, particularly during times of infectious disease outbreaks, that local governments must work with water trucking companies to minimize the risk of spreading water-borne diseases through contaminated water tanks. We drink water to stay alive. We do not expect to die from drinking water. Having access to clean and safe drinkable water is a basic human right. Like many other states in Ethiopia, most people in the Somali region do not have access to clean and treated water, making them specifically susceptible to recurrent water-borne diseases. The disease burden can be reduced by evaluating and addressing cholera risks in countries that rely on water trucks for drinking water. On the other hand, there must be strong mobilization activities on awareness creation through community engagement for behavioral change and improved sanitation practices and use of oral cholera vaccines (OCV) so as to meet the global strategy for cholera control that was developed by the Global Task Force on Cholera Control (GTFCC).

We found major gender inequalities: women were more likely to be exposed to and die from the disease. In the context of living in pastoral-cum-nomadic settings, it is common for women (more than men) to engage in everyday household chores and are likely to have faced greater transmission risks through increased person-to-person contacts. Findings of the present study showed that people with a history of travel to and from an infected area were at a greater risk of being infected with Vibrio cholerae and were more likely to die from cholera. This could be an indication of lapses in the measures that were taken to control movements between people in the infected districts. There could be other explanations too. For example, this is one of the remotest counties in the region, far away from the regional capital city (Jigjiga) where the emergency response was being coordinated. Also, despite being amongst the largest and remotest counties, healthcare infrastructures are underdeveloped in this zone, with limited health centres scattered over a massive geographical area. We are not entirely sure why Warder and Danod were worst hit within Dollo. Since Warder is the capital town of Dollo, it is possible that movement of people was greater in Warder than other districts.

From our findings, people with dehydration symptoms were 7.8 times more likely to die than people with mild to moderate dehydration. In emergency treatment situations, non-trained healthcare workers can easily mix-up moderate and severe cases. For instance, severe dehydration can be mistaken for a moderate situation. If severe cases are diagnosed as moderate, that may lead to more deaths, not less. We recommend the introduction of strategies targeting disease prevention and disease control in locations that are vulnerable to droughts, floods and disease outbreaks. Cholera specialist training should be provided to healthcare and extension workers so then they are able to identify and treat moderate and severe cases, precisely and promptly.

From a global perspective, one possible explanation as to why the Somali region had a higher CFR rate than the acceptable WHO recommendation is that the cholera outbreak occurred when these pastoral communities were experiencing severe drought, resulting in extreme water and food shortages [9], and pushing people to drink unsafe and contaminated water. We recently obtained a large data set from the World Bank Metrology Centre to assess the implications of the local climate change variability for the Somali Region of Ethiopia in the past decades [10]. We carried out trend analysis covering average precipitation and temperature records from 1950s. The rainfall experienced in the years of 1974, 1999, 2009 and 2017 fell below the average precipitation levels, leading to prolonged dry spells and ultimately contributing to the onset of long and frequent drought conditions in subsequent years. Our intention is to carry out a new retroactive analysis to see if the regions’ cholera outbreaks in the past several decades can be attributed to the times when they were either sharp declines in rainfall trends or when the periods of maximum rainfalls were taking place, with both situations being known risk factors for cholera outbreaks [10].

Limitation and future research

In terms of strengths, this manuscript makes significant contributions in the field of public health since it is reporting the findings of a major public health issue (cholera) from one of the remotest communities of the world. The paper brings scientific evidence and attention to the need to protect neglected pastoral communities from cholera outbreaks. The first challenge was the distance between where the affected community lived and where the cholera treatment centres were set up, which delayed the timely arrival of the patients in the centres. It is possible that there were more people who were unable to reach the treatment centres and who died of cholera than reported in this paper. We had no way to include cases that have not reached the treatment centres in the study. Also, there is a possibility that some patients were assessed to have moderate dehydration when they could have severe dehydration. Indicators for assessing toilet ownership of individuals were not included in the line list, though the Somali Regional Health Bureau report indicated that there was less than 5% toilet coverage in the region, at the time when the cholera disease broke out. We reported that women had higher death rates than men, this finding should be interpreted with caution as we did not perform specific age-group gender analysis. Here, we reported crude age data for all the seven districts of Dollo zone because the disease affected this group in similar ways. Finally, despite the limitations noted above (which are common to studies of cross-sectional nature), our findings illustrate the potential to conduct more comparative and longitudinal studies in the future, for example, carrying out more in-depth analysis into the effects of climate change on cholera outbreaks.

Conclusion

The 2017 outbreak was primarily exacerbated by lack of access to clean water, poor sanitation practices and history of travel among highly mobile pastoral communities living on nearby international border locations, with the Dollo County having the longest international borders with Somalia. The possibility of future epidemics (like the 2017 cholera outbreak) taking longer time to control is most likely unless the risk factors we discussed above, and the recommendations notes here, are taking into considerations. The effective prevention and control of cholera requires timely implementation of integrated and comprehensive preventive and disease control approaches that involve activities both inside and outside of the health sector. We urge federal governments, regional Health Bureaus, UN agencies, and local NGOs to provide enhanced cross-agency collaborations so then more people can be saved at the earliest possible time, with specific attention given to improving early warning, response and preparedness systems. There must be an improved surveillance system that protects mobile pastoral populations who live in remote places of the world from infectious diseases.
