Peripheral hepatojejunostomy for unresectable malignant biliary obstruction: is it still a valid strategy?
Authors: Mohammad H. Fard-Aghaie, C. H. Stephan, S. Imdahl, G. A. Stavrou, J. Laengle, K. C. Wagner, T. Hackert, K. J. Oldhafer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposePeripheral hepatojejunostomy (HJ) is rarely performed today but may offer durable internal biliary drainage in patients with hilar or intrahepatic malignancy deemed unresectable at laparotomy. Abandoning resection leaves the question of how best to secure biliary continuity. In this retrospective study, we evaluated whether HJ remains a valid option by detailing indications, technique, and short-term outcomes in patients in whom curative resection was abandoned intraoperatively.MethodsWe retrospectively analysed 20 consecutive patients treated at our centre between 2010 and 2024. In all cases, planned curative resection was abandoned intraoperatively, and a HJ was constructed to drain segmental intrahepatic ducts. Data on preoperative biliary interventions, operative technique, perioperative outcomes, and follow-up were collected.ResultsAmong 20 patients (median age 71 years, IQR 62–77), 10 (50%) underwent bilateral HJ. There were no intraoperative deaths. Ninety-day mortality was 15%; overall morbidity was 50%, with major complications (Clavien–Dindo ≥ III) in 20%. Median overall survival after HJ was 8 months (IQR 4–20).ConclusionPeripheral HJ remains a selective option for internal biliary drainage when curative resection is abandoned intraoperatively. In selected patients, it can provide durable palliation while reducing reliance on long-term external drainage and repeated biliary interventions. This technique may warrant reconsideration in selected contemporary practice settings.

Introduction

Malignant biliary obstruction at the level of the liver hilum is most commonly caused by hilar cholangiocarcinoma [1], with other contributors including advanced gallbladder carcinoma, centrally located intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma or metastases. These tumours often present with obstructive jaundice and distressing symptoms such as pruritus and cholangitis [2]. Definitive cure is only possible if the tumour can be completely resected; however, a large proportion of patients are unresectable at diagnosis due to locally advanced disease or metastases [3]. Despite advances in imaging and surgical techniques, only about one-third of patients with hilar cholangiocarcinoma undergo successful curative resection [4], meaning the majority will require palliative biliary drainage. Indeed, over 70% of patients present with jaundice [4], and without intervention the resulting cholestasis causes progressive liver dysfunction and poor quality of life. Palliative options include non-surgical biliary drainage (endoscopic or percutaneous) or surgical bypass.

Surgical bilioenteric anastomosis has long been used to bypass obstructed bile ducts. The technique of peripheral hepatojejunostomy, created by resecting a wedge of liver parenchyma to expose small bile duct radicles, was first described by Longmire and Sanford in 1948 [5]. In this approach, a slice of the liver edge (typically the left lateral segment) is removed to open multiple biliary ducts, which are then anastomosed to a Roux-en-Y jejunal loop [6]. An alternative approach introduced by Couinaud in 1957 accesses a left segmental duct via the umbilical fissure (the classic Hepp–Couinaud “round ligament” approach) [7]. These innovations provided internal biliary drainage decades before modern stenting, but at the cost of a laparotomy in patients often debilitated by malignancy.

Over the past few decades, management of malignant hilar obstruction has shifted toward less invasive techniques. Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) with stent placement and percutaneous transhepatic drainage (PTD) are now first-line palliative treatments. These interventions avoid an open operation and can often be performed even in high-risk patients [8]. Nevertheless, they have important limitations. Complete drainage of both hepatic lobes via stents may require multiple devices; for example, a Bismuth type IV hilar tumour might necessitate two or more stents to drain separated left and right ductal systems. Each indwelling stent carries a risk of occlusion from sludge or tumour ingrowth and often necessitates repeat procedures for exchange. In complex hilar strictures, achieving adequate endoscopic drainage can be technically impossible, or large tumours may physically prevent stent placement or quickly occlude a stent [8]. External percutaneous drains are an alternative when internal stents fail, but having an external biliary catheter for life is uncomfortable and adversely affects quality of life [9] .

Given these considerations, surgical biliary drainage may still have a place in contemporary practice. A common context is the intraoperative recognition of unresectability during an exploration undertaken with curative intent. In selected patients, completing a peripheral HJ during the same anaesthesia can establish internal biliary continuity as an alternative to postoperative external drainage or staged interventions [10]. Another scenario is failure of minimally invasive approaches – patients who have persistent or recurrent cholestasis despite multiple ERCP/PTD attempts, or who are predicted to require frequent re-interventions for stent maintenance. In select cases, surgical biliary bypass has even been used as a temporizing measure to bridge patients to a definitive therapy such as liver transplantation or delayed resection after relieving cholestasis, although this is rare in modern practice [10].

In this context, we offer a descriptive account of peripheral HJ performed after intraoperative abandonment of curative resection. Our single-centre experience, set against the background of modern endoscopic and percutaneous practice, summarises indications, technique, and short-term outcomes to help contextualise when this operation may remain appropriate.

Materials and methods

Study design and patients

We conducted a retrospective review of all patients at the Asklepios Hospital Barmbek who underwent an exploratory laparotomy for a suspected hilar malignancy and received a peripheral HJ for palliative biliary decompression from 2010 to 2024. In all included cases, curative resection was abandoned intraoperatively and a peripheral hepatojejunostomy was performed during the same operation. Patients who proceeded to successful curative resection, or those who did not receive an HJ (e.g., closed without bypass or managed with only an external drain), were excluded.

Data collection

Relevant clinical and perioperative data were obtained from a prospectively maintained hepatobiliary surgery database and review of hospital records. Collected variables included patient demographics (age and sex), tumour diagnosis and any preoperative biliary interventions. Specifically, we noted whether each patient had undergone endoscopic stenting or PTD prior to surgery. The effectiveness of preoperative drainage was assessed using laboratory values, particularly total bilirubin. Intraoperative findings were documented: reasons for unresectability and the HJ procedure performed (unilateral or bilateral anastomoses). Postoperative outcomes were tracked, including length of hospital stay, need for intensive care, and any complications.

Surgical technique

All patients underwent a Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy to intrahepatic bile ducts at the time of laparotomy. After a modified Makuuchi or Chevron incision, a thorough exploration of the abdomen was performed to confirm the extent of disease. Once the tumour was deemed unresectable, attention was turned to biliary bypass. Approximately 40 cm distal to the ligament of Treitz, a Roux limb of proximal jejunum was created and brought into the upper abdomen as the conduit for biliary drainage. We aimed to establish bilateral drainage whenever feasible. To do so, a hepaticojejunostomy was constructed on the left lateral sector (exposing ducts of segments II–III) and another on segment V, using the same Roux limb for both.

To expose the intrahepatic ducts, a thin wedge of superficial liver parenchyma (approximately 5 cm in width and a few centimetres deep) was resected from the inferior edge of the left lateral sector. The liver parenchyma was divided using a scalpel. In this way, multiple small biliary orifices were exposed. Electrocautery was minimized on this cut surface to avoid thermal injury to the opened ducts; haemostasis was achieved by manual pressure and fine sutures for any bleeding vessels. An enterotomy was made on the Roux limb sized to slightly smaller than the liver cut surface, and a single-layer end-to-side hepatojejunostomy was fashioned. The jejunal mucosa was sutured directly to the liver capsule using continuous or interrupted 4 − 0 absorbable sutures, effectively intubating the exposed biliary radicles with the jejunum.

If the right lobe was also to be drained, the Roux limb was then passed to the right side. A similar wedge resection of the liver surface was performed over segment V to expose peripheral bile ducts of the right lobe, and a second hepatojejunostomy was created in the same manner. In cases where one lobe was either atrophic or inaccessible (for instance, a contralateral lobe completely destroyed by tumour), a unilateral HJ was performed. No trans-anastomotic stents were used in any of the HJ anastomoses. After completing the biliary-enteric anastomoses, the Roux limb was anastomosed to the side of the jejunum (jejunojejunostomy) and drains were placed.

Outcome measures and follow-up

The primary outcomes assessed were: (1) success of biliary decompression (2), postoperative morbidity and mortality (3), long-term HJ patency, and (4) overall survival. Biliary decompression was evaluated by comparing preoperative and postoperative total bilirubin levels. Postoperative complications were graded by the Clavien–Dindo classification system (grades I–V) [11]. For a comprehensive assessment of morbidity, we also calculated the Comprehensive Complication Index (CCI) for each patient, which aggregates all postoperative complications into a 0–100 score [12]. Bile leakage from the HJ was specifically monitored and classified according to International Study Group of Liver Surgery (ISGLS) criteria [13]. Postoperative mortality was defined as any death occurring within 90 days of surgery.

All patients were followed through outpatient records, hospital documentation, and correspondence with referring physicians to determine their clinical course after surgery. Follow-up was not based on a predefined prospective surveillance protocol. We noted any episodes of recurrent cholangitis or biliary obstruction during follow-up and recorded any interventions (endoscopic or percutaneous) needed to address these issues after the initial hospital discharge. Absence of biliary re-intervention was taken as evidence of sustained HJ patency during follow-up. Overall survival was calculated from the date of HJ surgery to the date of death or last known follow-up.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the patient cohort. Continuous variables (e.g., bilirubin levels) are reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), given the non-normal distribution in this small series. Categorical variables are described as counts and percentages. No hypothesis testing or comparative statistics were performed, as this was a single-cohort descriptive study without a control group. All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software, and results are interpreted primarily in a descriptive context.

Results

Patient characteristics

Between 2010 and 2024, 20 patients at our institution met the inclusion criteria for this study (out of 2705 hepatobiliary operations performed in that period). These patients underwent a peripheral HJ for unresectable malignant hilar obstruction. The median age was 71 years (IQR 62–77), and 65% of the patients were male. The underlying diagnoses were predominantly perihilar cholangiocarcinoma (Klatskin tumours), along with colorectal liver metastases and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma. All patients presented preoperatively with obstructive jaundice, and many had symptoms of cholestasis such as intense pruritus or episodic cholangitis.

Prior to surgical referral, a majority of patients (85%) had undergone attempts at endoscopic and/or percutaneous biliary drainage. In several cases, stent placement was anatomically challenging or incomplete (for example, only one lobe drained).

A bilateral peripheral HJ was accomplished in 10 of 20 patients (50%). In the remaining 10 patients, a unilateral HJ was performed. Of these, 5 patients had only a left-sided anastomosis (segment III) and 5 patients had only a right-sided anastomosis (segment V). In all cases, we targeted the lobe that was expected to provide the most functional liver drainage.

No biliary endoprostheses (stents) were used across any of the HJ sites. There were no intraoperative complications such as uncontrolled haemorrhage or injury to adjacent organs during the HJ construction.

Postoperative outcomes

All patients survived the immediate postoperative period. There were no intraoperative or perianesthetic deaths. Within 90 days after surgery, however, 3 patients (15%) died due to progressive advanced malignancy.

Postoperative morbidity was moderate. Overall, 10 of 20 patients (50%) experienced one or more complications of any grade. The majority of these were minor complications (Clavien–Dindo grade I–II), occurring in 6 patients (30%). Major complications (Clavien-Dindo grade ≥ III) occurred in 4 patients (20%). One patient developed a bile leak (Grade A) which was managed conservatively. The median length of postoperative hospital stay was 12 days (IQR 10–20). Patients with complications tended to have longer stays. Ten patients (50%) required ICU observation beyond the first 24 h postoperatively. The median Comprehensive Complication Index for the cohort was 10.5 (IQR 0 to 24.7), consistent with an overall moderate complication burden.

Compared with the median preoperative bilirubin level of 1.5 mg/dl (IQR 0.9-3.0), the median postoperative bilirubin level decreased to 0.7 mg/dl (IQR 0.6–1.2). In practical terms, most patients were discharged with only mild residual cholestasis.

Follow-up, patency, and survival

Three patients were lost to follow-up. The HJ provided durable biliary drainage for the great majority of our cohort. Only two patients required any form of biliary re-intervention during follow-up.

By the last follow-up, 16 of 18 patients (89%) had died from progression of their underlying malignancy. The median overall survival after HJ surgery was 8 months (IQR 4–20).

Discussion

In our series, peripheral hepatojejunostomy provided internal biliary continuity and sustained improvement in jaundice in many patients. Morbidity and 90-day mortality were not negligible, underscoring the importance of careful selection and centre expertise.

In the palliative setting of advanced biliary malignancy with limited life expectancy, treatment strategies should follow a principle of minimizing procedural burden and risk, favoring the least invasive and safest effective approach whenever feasible.

Accordingly, we consider peripheral HJ a selective option—complementary to endoscopic and percutaneous approaches—rather than a replacement. Its potential to lessen dependence on external devices and repeated interventions may be relevant when resection is abandoned at laparotomy.

One important consideration is the surgical technique itself. Two principal approaches to intrahepatic cholangiojejunostomy have been described historically: Longmire’s original technique from 1948 and the round ligament (Hepp–Couinaud) approach, first described in 1957 [7]. While the round ligament technique can be less destructive to liver parenchyma, it typically drains only the left lateral section and can be technically challenging in the presence of significant hilar fibrosis or tumour bulk. In practice, various modifications and hybrids of these techniques have been reported, but none has emerged as a clearly superior gold standard [14]. Our centre favours the bilateral wedge resection method because it offers the opportunity to drain both lobes and has been associated with higher rates of successful cholestasis relief than single-sided anastomoses in the literature. Indeed, Schlitt et al. reported that among their patients, those who received bilateral peripheral HJ had a 93% rate of significant bilirubin improvement, compared to 64% in those with only a left-sided HJ [10]. We advocate for bilateral drainage whenever anatomy and patient condition permit, as long as the added operative time is justified by the goal of more complete biliary decompression.

Our results can be contextualized alongside historical surgical outcomes and the outcomes of modern non-surgical palliation. In the landmark series by Schlitt et al., 56 patients underwent peripheral HJ for unresectable hilar tumours with a 30-day mortality of 9% and median survival ~ 6 months [10]. In our contemporary series, we observed a 0% 30-day mortality and a 90-day mortality of 15%, with a median survival of ~ 8 months. The slightly lower early mortality in our cohort likely reflects improvements in patient selection and perioperative care over the past two decades. Additionally, advances in surgical technique, anaesthesia, and critical care have likely contributed to safer outcomes. We also routinely performed dual anastomoses when feasible, which may have improved the success of biliary drainage and reduced cholangitis compared to some historical practices where single anastomosis was more common.

When comparing peripheral HJ to endoscopic or percutaneous palliation, there are clear trade-offs. On one hand, endoscopic stenting and PTD avoid a laparotomy and have a lower immediate procedural risk for most patients [8]. Current multidisciplinary guidelines generally recommend these minimally invasive approaches as first-line palliation for malignant hilar obstruction. On the other hand, their effectiveness can be limited by the complexity of hilar tumours. As discussed, achieving adequate drainage of both the left and right ductal systems often requires multiple stents or catheters [1]. Each stent carries a finite lifespan due to occlusion from sludge, bacterial biofilm, or tumour ingrowth. Consequently, patients with stents frequently need repeat interventions – exchanging stents every 3–4 months or managing cholangitis episodes. Additionally, large or rapidly progressive tumours can physically preclude successful stenting; for example, a bulky tumour may obstruct guidewire passage or immediately compress a stent. In such cases, patients may be left with external drains as a last resort, which significantly impairs their comfort and lifestyle.

An additional important consideration is the impact of surgical intervention on the timely initiation of systemic therapy. In the modern era, systemic treatment—including chemo-immunotherapy—plays a central role in the management of advanced biliary malignancies. Surgical bypass may delay the start of these therapies and, in selected cases, could offset its potential palliative benefit. This aspect should therefore be carefully weighed in multidisciplinary decision-making.

Peripheral HJ offers some distinct advantages in these contexts. It provides a one-time, internal bypass of the obstruction that, if successful, can spare the patient from the cycle of recurrent interventions. By directly anastomosing the biliary tree to the intestine, a wide drainage outlet is created that is not prone to clogging as stents are. In our series, once the HJ had healed, the majority of patients did not require any further biliary procedures – illustrating the durability of the solution. This long-term patency must be weighed against the initial risk of surgery. Even with modern improvements, an open HJ entails a hospitalization and potential complications that would be avoided with a purely endoscopic approach. In our study, serious complications were relatively infrequent but infections, bleeding, or liver failure remain concerns whenever an operation is done on a patient with advanced malignancy. Some of this risk is inherent to the patient’s condition rather than the procedure itself; for instance, patients with malignant obstruction often have cholestatic liver injury, malnutrition, and immunosuppression, which predispose them to complications whether they undergo surgery or not. Indeed, an exploratory laparotomy alone in such patients carries a non-negligible risk. Thus, the decision to proceed with a surgical bypass must carefully consider the patient’s overall condition, life expectancy, and preferences.

In the current era, we propose that peripheral HJ be reserved for specific indications where its benefits outweigh its risks.

It should also be acknowledged that, in order to avoid non-therapeutic laparotomy, staging laparoscopy may be a valuable adjunct in selected patients with equivocal preoperative findings. However, certain determinants of unresectability, such as complex vascular involvement, may only become apparent during open exploration.

In such intraoperative scenarios, the decision whether to proceed with a surgical bypass or to terminate the procedure and transition to non-surgical management must be individualized.

One indication is the scenario in which unresectability is discovered during surgery performed with curative intent. In these cases, the patient is already under anaesthesia and has been deemed fit for surgery, and an HJ can be added with relatively low incremental risk. Performing the bypass at that moment saves the patient from a second procedure and provides immediate relief of jaundice. Another possible indication is failure of less invasive drainage methods. If a patient experiences recurrent cholangitis or persistently high bilirubin despite optimal ERCP/PTD attempts, and if their performance status is still acceptable, a surgical solution may offer more definitive relief. This is particularly pertinent for younger patients or those expected to live long enough that the cumulative risk of repeated procedures becomes significant. In these situations, a multidisciplinary discussion is essential. Input from gastroenterologists, interventional radiologists, and hepatobiliary surgeons should be obtained to weigh the risks and benefits of attempting a surgical bypass versus continuing with non-surgical means.

There are several limitations to our study. First, the retrospective nature and relatively small sample size limit the strength of our conclusions. We did not have a concurrent control group of patients managed with stenting alone for direct comparison. Thus, our analysis is primarily descriptive, demonstrating feasibility and outcomes of HJ without definitively proving superiority over modern non-surgical management – a question that would require a prospective trial. Due to the retrospective design, minor episodes of cholangitis or interventions performed outside our institution may have been underreported. Second, there is an inherent selection bias: our patients were those whom the treating surgeons believed might benefit from a surgical bypass – either because they were already in the operating room or because other methods had failed. Patients with very advanced disease or poor performance status were likely managed non-surgically and never referred for surgery, and were therefore not captured in our series. Therefore, our outcomes apply to a somewhat filtered patient population, and results might differ in a broader unselected population. Third, while we qualitatively noted improvement in symptoms, we did not formally measure quality of life outcomes. Relief of pruritus, improved nutrition, and performance status are critical benefits of successful biliary drainage, and future studies should incorporate patient-reported outcome measures to better quantify the palliative impact.

Accordingly, peripheral hepatojejunostomy should be regarded as a highly selective option rather than a standard approach, reserved for situations in which less invasive strategies are not feasible or have proven insufficient. The prolonged inclusion period in the present series reflects the rarity and highly selective contemporary use of this procedure.

Despite these limitations, this series supports a restrained take-home message: peripheral hepatojejunostomy retains a selective role within multidisciplinary care for malignant hilar obstruction. Endoscopic and percutaneous approaches should remain the first line options given their low upfront risk and wide availability. In selected patients—particularly when resection is abandoned and anatomy or intraoperative circumstances favour immediate restoration of internal biliary continuity—completion of peripheral HJ can offer durable decompression with an acceptable short-term safety profile and may reduce reliance on external devices and repeated exchanges. Maintaining proficiency in this technique broadens the therapeutic repertoire for a small but important subset of patients.

Conclusion

Peripheral HJ retains a selective role within multidisciplinary management of malignant hilar biliary obstruction. The published evidence remains limited and heterogeneous and often combining different procedures or small case reports, which constrains formal synthesis. Our series contributes focused, contemporary data on a clearly defined intraoperative scenario.

In our experience, completion of a peripheral HJ after abandonment of curative resection was associated with sustained relief of jaundice in many patients and an acceptable short-term safety profile. Within a multidisciplinary framework, this operation may be considered in carefully selected candidates when, at laparotomy, single-session restoration of internal biliary continuity is judged to be pragmatically advantageous compared with staged endoluminal or percutaneous strategies and repeated device exchanges.

Maintaining familiarity with peripheral HJ preserves the full spectrum of options—from least invasive to more durable surgical bypass—needed to achieve biliary decompression and support quality of life in advanced hilar malignancy. From Longmire’s early descriptions to present-day practice, its value has evolved rather than disappeared. In high-volume centres, and for a small but important subset of patients, this historically grounded technique merits reconsideration as part of the modern surgical toolbox.
