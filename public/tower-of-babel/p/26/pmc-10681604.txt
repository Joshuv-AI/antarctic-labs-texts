Effectiveness of Huangqi Guizhi Wuwu decoction combined with rehabilitation training for shoulder hand syndrome after stroke: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Zhenyu Shi, Shuxuan Wang, Fuping Wu, Zhenhang Liu, Yaqun Wang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Background:Shoulder hand syndrome (SHS) is a common complication of stroke. This meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of Huangqi Guizhi Wuwu decoction (HGWD) combined with rehabilitation training in managing it, as its efficacy remains inconclusive.Methods:Seven databases, including PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Library, SinoMed, Chinese National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wanfang Data, and VIP database were searched in this study. The search deadline was April 30, 2023. Randomized controlled trials that included either standalone rehabilitation training or HGWD combined with rehabilitation training were included, and data were independently extracted by 2 reviewers who assessed the risk of bias.Results:Thirteen studies involving 1270 patients were included in this study. Meta-analysis showed that the combined treatment was significantly more effective than standalone rehabilitation therapy (odds ratio = 4.49; 95%CI: 2.98–6.76; Z = 7.17; P < .00001). Compared with the control group, the intervention group had a lower visual analog scale score (mean difference [MD] = −2.80, 95%CI (−3.15, −2.45), Z = 15.84, P < .00001). In addition, the Fugl-Meyer assessment scale score improved (MD = 9.69, 95%CI (7.60, 11.78), Z = 9.08, P < .00001). The SHS score in the intervention group decreased more compared to the control group (standard mean difference = −2.27, 95%CI (−3.19, −1.34), Z = 4.79, P < .00001). Serum biomarkers related to SHS decreased, including serum substance P (MD = −7.52, 95%CI (−8.55, −6.48), Z = 14, P < .00001) and bradykinin (MD = −1.81, 95%CI (−2.68, −0.95), Z = 4.1, P < .00001). Although there was no statistical difference in joint mobility score (MD = −4.19, 95%CI (−8.16, −0.22), Z = 4.79, P = .28), sensitivity analysis after excluding one study still suggested that the joint mobility score of the combined treatment group was higher than that of the standalone rehabilitation treatment group.Conclusion:The results of this study indicate that HGWD combined with rehabilitation training may be more effective in treating SHS after stroke compared to standalone rehabilitation therapy.

1. Introduction

SHS, which is also referred to as type I CRPS or reflex sympathetic dystrophy, is characterized by pain and discomfort in the shoulder and hand, is a common complication after stroke.[1] The disease typically occurs between 1 and 3 months after stroke, with an incidence rate ranging from 12.5% to 70.0%.[2] Research suggests that age, gender, smoking, nerve injury, shoulder subluxation, and prolonged immobilization of the affected limb are all risk factors for developing SHS after stroke.[3] Patients with SHS typically experience swelling in the affected upper limb and shoulder joint, finger pain, redness and warmth of the skin, limited joint movement, shoulder dislocation, stiff skin, muscle atrophy or spasms, which significantly affect the functional recovery of the paralyzed upper limb and reduce the patient quality of life.[4] Treatment strategies for SHS usually include rehabilitation therapy, medication, psychological therapy, and regional anesthesia techniques.[5] However, no specific treatment seems to generate lasting impacts upon SHS, especially among individuals who suffer from hemiplegia.[6] Therefore, comprehensive and multidisciplinary interventions are believed to be more effective.[7–9]

HGWD coupled with rehabilitation training is widely used to treat SHS.[2] SHS belongs to the category of “joint and muscle pain” in traditional Chinese medicine, where causative elements like wind and cold, dampness invade the body, leading to obstruction of the meridians, disharmony between stagnation of blood and qi, and imbalances between the yin and yang energies.[10] HGWD is a classic formula recorded in Zhang Zhongjing Jin Gui Yao Lue Table 1 displays information regarding the Han Dynasty era. HGWD consists of Huangqi, Guizhi, Shaoyao, Dazao, and Shengjiang, each and every one of which have the effects of tonifying qi, dispelling wind, cold and dampness, nourishing meridians, promoting blood circulation, etc. Generally speaking, HGWD is used to treat “blood stasis” characterized by pain and numbness, muscle discomfort, and muscle atrophy, which is consistent with the characteristics of SHS.[11] Correct posture during rehabilitation training can improve venous and lymphatic reflux to a certain extent and prevent limb spasm; Centriolar entanglement can stimulate muscle contraction, restore the function of muscle pump of the affected limb, promote venous return, improve the vasodilation and contraction ability of the affected limb blood vessels, and thus effectively increase the range of joint motion. The active and passive movement of the patient has a positive effect on maintaining the normal muscle tension and effectively preventing the adhesion of the shoulder and elbow joint. A few latest research studies have demonstrated that HGWD molecular mechanisms in mice involves raising the pain threshold, thereby relieving discomfort,[12] reducing inflammatory factors (such as IL-1, IL-6, and COX-2) through immune regulation to exert anti-inflammatory effects,[13] and inhibit the production of inducible nitric oxide synthase,[14] inducible nitric oxide synthase plays a crucial part in alleviating symptoms of SHS, and HGWD has been shown to reduce its production. However, there is a lack of studies to summarize the effectiveness of HGWD combined with rehabilitation training in the treatment of SHS. Therefore, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis, primarily highlighting the unique advantages of combined HGWD versus rehabilitation training alone in alleviating SHS, to provide an overview of the available evidence on this topic.

2.6. Data screening and extraction

The researchers followed the Cochrane Collaboration Systematic Review Manual Version 5.0. Initially, 2 researchers used EndNote X20 bibliographic management software to screen the literature based on the PRISMA flow chart. They excluded duplicates and clearly unqualified literature according to the predefined criteria and then reviewed the full text of the remaining articles. In the second round of screening, 2 researchers independently assessed whether the articles met the inclusion criteria. Disagreements were resolved by consulting a third researcher for studies that were difficult to determine. Relevant information, such as the author name, publication year, country, sample size, patient age, course of the disease, and details of the control and interventions, were extracted using Excel 2013 software. Outcome variables, and treatment duration. For studies that did not report relevant data, we attempted to obtain missing information by email or other means of communication with the authors.

2.7. Quality of literatures

The Cochrane tool for assessing bias risk was employed to evaluate the included studies. The 2 researchers evaluated the literature performance on evaluation criteria and made judgments of low, unclear, and high risk, respectively, and then cross-checked them. If there were any discrepancies, we would discuss them. If consensus could not be reached, we would discuss it with a third party researcher.

2.8. Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis in this study was conducted using RevMan 5.3 software. For binary variables, statistical analysis was done using relative risk. For continuous variables, weighted mean difference was used when the measurement tool and unit of the indicator were the same, and standardized mean difference was used when they were different. Both were presented as effect sizes and a 95% confidence interval (CI). Heterogeneity was assessed using the Q test and I² value. If P > .1 or I2≤50%, it indicated low heterogeneity, and a fixed-effect model was used for meta-analysis. If P < .1 or I² > 50%, it indicated heterogeneity among the studies, and a random-effects model was used for analysis. The source of heterogeneity was explored using sensitivity analysis and subgroup analysis. For subgroup analysis, participants were categorized into 3 groups based on the duration of treatment: ≤30 days group, 3060 days group, and 6090 days group. If there were no fewer than 10 studies included in the result measurement, a funnel plot was used to evaluate publication bias.

3.2. Risk of bias

The methodological quality graph (Fig. 2) shows that 4 trials[17,19,22,26] used random number methods. The remaining 9 trials[15,16,18,20,21,23–25,27] used “random allocation” but did not describe the method of generating randomized sequences. None of the trials mentioned whether blinding was used or reported participant losses, thus reducing the risk of attrition bias.

Risk of bias graph, summarizing the assessment of methodological quality across included studies.

3.3. Results of meta-analysis for combination therapy vs rehabilitation treatment alone

This analysis comprised 9 experiments with 471 participants to assess clinical effectiveness. The fixed-effect model was used due to non-significant heterogeneity (P = .91, I² = 0%). The findings indicated that the group receiving combined treatment had a higher clinical efficacy rate than the standalone rehabilitation treatment group (odds ratio = 4.49, 95%CI: 2.98 to 6.76, Z = 7.17, P < .00001) (Fig. 3). For visual analog scale (VAS) scores, 12 experiments[15–25,27] including 586 patients were evaluated using the random-effects model due to significant heterogeneity (I² = 97%). The analysis showed that the combined treatment group had considerably lower VAS scores than the standalone rehabilitation treatment group (mean difference [MD] = −2.80, 95%CI: −3.15 to −2.45, Z = 15.84, P < .00001) (Fig. 4). Concerning Fugl-Meyer assessment scale (FMA) scores, 12 experiments[15–24,26,27] involving 590 patients were analyzed using the random-effects model due to significant heterogeneity (P < .00001, I² = 91%). The results indicated that the combined treatment group had notably higher FMA scores than the standalone rehabilitation treatment group (MD = 9.69, 95%CI: 7.60–11.78, Z = 9.08, P < .00001) (Fig. 5). The SHS scores were analyzed in 5 experiments[17,19,22,23,26] with 221 participants using the random-effects model due to significant heterogeneity (P < .00001, I² = 93%). The analysis revealed that the combined treatment group had significantly lower SHS scores than the standalone rehabilitation treatment group (standardized mean difference = −2.27, 95%CI: −3.19 to −1.34, Z = 4.79, P < .00001) (Fig. 6). Joint mobility scores were analyzed in 4 experiments[18,20,24,27] involving 175 patients using the random-effects model due to significant heterogeneity (P < .00001, I² = 94%). The results showed that the combined treatment group had significantly lower joint mobility scores than the standalone rehabilitation treatment group (MD = −4.19, 95%CI: −8.16 to −0.22, Z = 4.79, P = .28) (Fig. 7). Finally, serum markers serum substance P and bradykinin (BK) were analyzed in 2 experiments[19,22] including 86 patients each using fixed-effect and random-effects models, respectively. The analysis showed that the combined treatment group had significantly lower levels of serum substance P (MD = −7.52, 95%CI: −8.55 to −6.48, P < .00001) and BK (MD = −1.81, 95%CI: −2.68 to −0.95, P < .0001) compared to the standalone rehabilitation treatment group, although heterogeneity was significant for BK analysis (Figs. 8 and 9).

3.4. Sensitivity analysis

We conducted a sensitivity analysis on various indicators including VAS, FMA, SHS scores, and joint mobility scores. The results showed that after excluding the study by Liang,[18] A notable contrast was observed in the MD of joint mobility scores between the combined treatment group and the standalone rehabilitation treatment group (Fig. 10), and the heterogeneity of the meta-analysis decreased significantly (P = 1, I² = 0%) indicating that the study had a significant effect on the heterogeneity of the meta-analysis. This finding underscores the need for further analysis of this study and caution when incorporating its conclusions into the overall meta-analysis. For the other indicators, the meta-analysis remained relatively stable even after excluding individual studies, suggesting that the combined use of HGWD and rehabilitation training has a consistent positive effect on the outcome indicators of patients with SHS after stroke.

3.6. Subgroup analysis

A subgroup analysis was performed on the outcomes of VAS and FMA scores, categorized by treatment duration (Figs. 13 and 14). The analysis showed that the heterogeneity of all 3 subgroups did not decrease significantly, suggesting that treatment duration may not be the source of heterogeneity. Notably, in the subgroup with a treatment duration of 30 days or less, no significant statistical difference was observed in the FMA scores between the combined treatment group and the standalone rehabilitation treatment group.

4. Discussion

SHS is a stroke complication that has a considerable impact on patients’ quality of life. In China, the combination of HGWD and rehabilitation training is widely used to treat SHS after stroke. Previous studies have suggested its potential effectiveness, but a systematic evaluation of the evidence was lacking.[15–27] This meta-analysis included 13 randomized controlled trials with 1270 patients. The results showed that the combination therapy was significantly more effective than standalone rehabilitation therapy, as indicated by significant differences in efficiency, FMA scores, VAS scores, joint mobility scores, and serum biomarkers (serum substance P and BK). These findings suggest that HGWD combined with rehabilitation training may be a promising approach for treating SHS after stroke and provides clinicians with additional evidence-based options for patient care.

It is worth noting that treatment duration may be an important factor affecting intervention effectiveness. Subgroup analysis shows that the longer the duration of combination therapy, the more effective it seems to be. In terms of VAS scores, combination therapy within 90 days (MD = −3.44) is superior to within 60 days (MD = −2.83) and within 30 days (MD = −2.93). FMA also has a similar upward trend. These findings suggest that with the extension of treatment time, combination therapy may have cumulative benefits. However, unfortunately, subgroup analysis did not reduce heterogeneity, and the amount of research incorporated in the subset analysis was fairly limited and of substandard quality. Therefore, this finding should be interpreted with caution. When conducting sensitivity analysis for joint mobility scores, after excluding Liang et al’s literature,[18] the heterogeneity of the meta-analysis decreased significantly. We believe that experimental design or implementation bias may be one of the reasons for its instability.

This study still has certain limitations: the inclusion of literature is not comprehensive enough to obtain supplements and gray literature, which may lead to publication bias; it is unclear whether the included literature implemented blinding for patients and operators, and the experimental design did not follow the allocation hiding principle, which may result in implementation bias; the treatment duration of the study was inconsistent, leading to deviation in treatment effectiveness; all included studies were conducted in China, so it is necessary to further research outside of China to improve the universality and applicability of the results.

In conclusion, HGWD combined with rehabilitation training has a significant effect on improving clinical symptoms and daily life abilities of SHS after stroke, and it is more effective than solely using rehabilitation therapy. Therefore, in practical applications, we recommend that clinicians prioritize the use of HGWD combined with rehabilitation training to help patients recover their health. In addition, we suggest that Subsequent research should focus on ensuring greater consistency in experimental design, implementation, and reporting quality, and try to avoid bias as much as possible. To improve the universality and reliability of the conclusions drawn, cross-national collaborative research should be increased to provide more accurate and scientific guidance for clinical practice.
