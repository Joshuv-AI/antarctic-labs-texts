Relationship of prognostic nutritional index with clinical outcomes and survival in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis
Authors: Nurcan Guler, Aylin Ozgen Alpaydin, Kemal Can Tertemiz, Can Sevinc, Hulya Ellidokuz, Gokçen Omeroğlu Simsek, Eyup Sabri Ucan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIdiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a chronic and progressive lung disease. Nutritional indices have been reported as significant prognostic factors in IPF. This study aimed to evaluate the relationship between the prognostic nutritional index (PNI) and clinical findings, functional parameters, prognosis, and mortality in patients with IPF followed in our clinic.MethodsThis retrospective study reviewed the medical records of patients diagnosed with IPF between 2014 and 2021. The GAP (Gender, Age, Physiology) score, pulmonary function tests, and laboratory results were recorded at the time of diagnosis and at the initiation of antifibrotic treatment. PNI was calculated using serum albumin and lymphocyte counts according to a previously defined formula. Patients were followed from the start of antifibrotic therapy to their final visit within the sixth month. All baseline data were reassessed at the six-month follow-up. Survival time was defined as the period from the initiation of follow-up to the date of death. For patients who were still alive, survival was calculated up to the end of the final data collection, which concluded in December 2024.ResultsA total of 120 patients (86 males; mean age: 69.2 ± 8.5 years) diagnosed with IPF were included. The most common symptoms were dyspnea (87.5%) and cough (82.5%). Forty-eight patients died during the study period. The modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) dyspnea scores and GAP scores were significantly lower in survivors (p = 0.016 and p < 0.001, respectively), whereas PNI levels were significantly higher in this group (p = 0.004). Baseline PNI levels showed weak negative correlations with both the mMRC score (r = − 0.308, p < 0.001) and the GAP score (r = − 0.358, p < 0.001). PNI levels at baseline and at 6 months were significantly higher in patients with stable parenchymal findings than in those with progressive fibrosis (p < 0.001).ConclusionsThese findings suggest that PNI, which reflects both nutritional and inflammatory status, may serve as a useful prognostic marker for predicting disease progression and mortality in patients with IPF.Keywords: Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, Prognostic nutritional index, Mortality

Background

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a chronic, progressive interstitial lung disease of unknown aetiology, characterized by irreversible fibrotic remodelling, progressive loss of lung function, and symptoms such as persistent cough and exertional dyspnea, ultimately leading to respiratory failure or death from associated comorbidities [1–3]. Although the disease consistently demonstrates progressive behaviour over time, its clinical course and rate of progression remain heterogeneous and difficult to predict [2]. Despite recent advances in the understanding and management of IPF, the disease continues to be associated with poor prognosis, as current antifibrotic therapies merely slow disease progression, and supportive or palliative approaches provide only symptomatic benefit [4]. In recent years, evidence has increasingly shown the effectiveness of pirfenidone and nintedanib in slowing functional decline in IPF; however, limitations such as tolerance, side effects, and their inability to reverse established fibrosis are also highlighted in the literature [5]. Furthermore, bibliometric analyses highlight the increasing scientific advances in antifibrotic therapies for interstitial lung diseases in recent years. These efforts reflect growing interest in identifying factors influencing clinical outcomes in IPF, the development of treatment strategies, and expanding indications for antifibrotic therapies in non-IPF progressive fibrotic lung diseases [6].

Increasing evidence suggests that systemic inflammation and nutritional status are important determinants of prognosis in IPF. Several haematological indices reflecting systemic inflammation, such as neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), have been investigated. Prior studies have demonstrated that marked increases in NLR and PLR over 12 months are associated with unfavourable outcomes, and that these markers are inversely correlated with forced vital capacity (FVC) and six-minute walk distance (6MWD) [7, 8]. Immune dysregulation in IPF involves aberrant activation of innate immune cells—including macrophages, neutrophils, fibrocytes, and myeloid-derived suppressor cells—which contribute to persistent inflammation and tissue injury through the release of cytokines, proteases, and reactive oxygen species [9].

Nutritional status has also emerged as a significant prognostic factor in IPF. Malnutrition, weight loss, and low body mass index (BMI), as well as composite indices such as the Advanced Lung Cancer Inflammation Index (ALI), Controlling Nutritional Status (CONUT) score, and Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index (GNRI), have all been linked to disease progression and mortality. Notably, GNRI has also been shown to predict both antifibrotic treatment tolerability and mortality risk in IPF patients [10–12].

The prognostic nutritional index (PNI), which incorporates serum albumin concentration as a marker of nutritional status and peripheral lymphocyte count as an indicator of immune competence [13–15], has demonstrated prognostic significance in several malignancies, including lung cancer [16, 17]. However, evidence regarding the association between PNI and outcomes in IPF remains limited.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the association between the prognostic nutritional index (PNI) and clinical characteristics, functional parameters, and prognosis in IPF patients receiving antifibrotic therapy in our clinic.

Methods

Study design and patients

In this retrospective study, we evaluated adult patients diagnosed with IPF (ICD code J84 and subcategories) between 2014 and 2021, with a minimum follow-up duration of three years at the Pulmonary Diseases Clinic of Dokuz Eylül University Faculty of Medicine. All patients were diagnosed according to current international guidelines [18], through multidisciplinary discussions involving pulmonologists, radiologists, and pathologists, and the diagnoses were confirmed in line with the most recent ATS/ERS/JRS/ALAT Clinical Practice Guidelines [1]. Patients with usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP), probable UIP, or possible UIP patterns who were eligible for and prescribed antifibrotic therapy were included in the study.

Exclusion criteria consisted of infection, corticosteroid and/or other immunosuppressive drugs use, systemic inflammation, comorbidities (malignancy, congestive heart failure, tuberculosis, sarcoidosis, lymphoma). Demographic and clinical data, including age, sex, smoking history, and pulmonary function test results, were retrieved from the hospital’s electronic medical records.

The study protocol was approved by the local ethics committee (Approval number: 2022/17–23).

Pulmonary function tests

Spirometric measurements were performed by the same technician using a Jaeger Lab Manager V452i device in accordance with ATS criteria [19, 20]. The parameters assessed included forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV₁), forced vital capacity (FVC), the FEV₁/FVC ratio, and diffusion capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO).

GAP score

The GAP (Gender, Age, Physiology) score, a validated prognostic model for estimating mortality in IPF, was calculated for all patients. The score incorporates gender, age, FVC, and DLCO [21]. The total score ranges from 0 to 8, with Stage I defined as 0–3 points, Stage II as 4–5 points, and Stage III as 6–8 points (Table 1).

Disease progression

Functional disease progression was defined according to current ATS/ERS criteria for progressive pulmonary fibrosis (PPF) and included a decline of ≥ 5% in FVC and/or a decline of ≥ 10% in DLCO (corrected for Hb) during a follow-up [1].

Acute exacerbation of IPF was diagnosed according to ATS/ERS criteria and defined as an acute worsening of respiratory symptoms accompanied by new bilateral ground-glass opacities and/or consolidation on high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT), not explained by cardiac failure or fluid overload. All HRCT images and acute exacerbation diagnoses were evaluated centrally through multidisciplinary discussion involving experienced pulmonologists and a dedicated thoracic radiologist.

Antifibrotic treatment tolerability

Discontinuation of antifibrotic therapy was defined as permanent cessation of pirfenidone or nintedanib due to adverse effects, as recorded in clinical follow-up.

Study protocol and follow-up

The GAP index, pulmonary function test results, and relevant laboratory parameters were documented at the time of the diagnosis and initiation of antifibrotic therapy. The follow-up period extended from the start of treatment to the sixth-month visit, with last data collection concluding in December 2024. All baseline data were reassessed at the six-month follow-up.

Survival time was defined as the period from the initiation of follow up to the date of death. For patients who were still alive, survival was calculated up to the end of final data collection also concluding in December 2024.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 30.0. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data. A 95% confidence interval was adopted, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Variables with normal distribution were reported as mean ± standard deviation, while those with non-normal distribution were presented as median values along with minimum and maximum ranges. Group comparisons were conducted using the independent samples t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, depending on the distribution of the variables. Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical variables. Correlations between variables were assessed using Pearson’s, Spearman’s, or Kendall’s correlation analyses, based on the data type and distribution.

Multivariate analyses were conducted using a logistic regression model. Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan–Meier method and a Cox proportional hazards regression model.

Antifibrotic treatment was discontinued in 21 patients due to gastrointestinal and cutaneous side effects. 12 patients were using pirfenidone and 9 patients were using nintedanib. No significant difference was observed in PNI values between patients whose antifibrotic treatment was discontinued and those whose treatment was continued (p = 0.378).

During the study period, 48 patients (40.0%) died, 85.4% due to respiratory causes such as respiratory failure, acute IPF exacerbations, and pulmonary infections. There were no significant differences between survivors and non-survivors in terms of age or gender. However, digital clubbing was more frequent among non-survivors (p = 0.042), and their modified mMRC dyspnea scores were significantly higher (p = 0.016). Pulmonary functions in terms of FVC% (p < 0.001) and DLCO % (p = 0.017) were also lower in this group. Similarly, the composite index of GAP score was significantly higher in non-survivors (p < 0.001). Mortality rates increased with GAP stage: 19.6% in stage I, 52.7% in stage II, and 62.7% in stage III (p < 0.001). PNI was also significantly lower in non-survivors (p = 0.004).

According to PPF physiological criteria, no statistically significant difference was observed in PNI assessment between patients who were stable or whose progressed during the follow-up period (p = 0.646). Radiological follow-up showed disease progression on thorax CT in 47 (39.1%) patients, of whom 63.8% died. The mortality rate was significantly higher in patients with radiological progression than in those who remained stable with radiological findings (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2).

Discussion

Increasing evidence indicates that nutritional status and immune function are closely linked to disease progression, with malnutrition and immune dysregulation contributing to persistent inflammation, impaired tissue repair, and worse clinical outcomes in IPF patients [23–25]. PNI, which integrates serum albumin as a marker of nutritional status and peripheral lymphocyte count as an indicator of immune competence, may reflect the combined impact of nutritional and immunological deficits on disease trajectory. In this retrospective cohort of 120 patients receiving antifibrotic therapy, we found that lower pulmonary functions, higher GAP scores, and poorer nutritional status, as assessed by PNI, were associated with increased mortality. These findings underscore the central role of pulmonary function decline in determining prognosis, while highlighting the potential relevance of nutritional and systemic inflammatory status in the overall clinical course of IPF. Additionally, patients with radiological progression on thorax CT exhibited a significant relationship with baseline and follow-up PNI values.

The prognostic nutritional index (PNI) was originally developed to assess preoperative immune-nutritional status and surgical risk in patients with gastrointestinal malignancies [22]. Derived from serum albumin concentration and peripheral lymphocyte count, PNI serves as an effective indicator of nutritional and immunological status in cancer patients. Most studies on PNI in respiratory diseases have been conducted in oncology settings, particularly among patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), where lower PNI has consistently been associated with poorer prognosis, including those receiving immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy [26–30]. Beyond malignancies, PNI has also been evaluated in chronic respiratory conditions. For instance, combining PNI with the COPD Assessment Test (CAT) improved prediction of exacerbations in elderly patients with COPD [31], and in nontuberculous mycobacterial lung disease (NTM-LD), lower PNI values were independently associated with mortality [32].

Data regarding the prognostic role of PNI in IPF remain limited. A study from China involving 264 IPF patients compared three nutritional indices; Nutrition Risk Index (NRI), CONUT score, and PNI, and found that PNI was less sensitive in detecting malnutrition. Nonetheless, poor nutritional status, particularly as assessed by NRI, was significantly associated with both all-cause and IPF-related mortality [33]. Although direct comparative analyses between PNI and other nutritional indices are outside the scope of this study, PNI offers practical advantages compared to other indices due to its simplicity and calculation using routine laboratory parameters. In our cohort, the relationship between PNI and clinical parameters was investigated. Baseline PNI showed an inverse correlation with mMRC and GAP scores, indicating that increasing age and disease severity were associated with poorer immunological and nutritional status. This relationship also remained evident at the 6-month follow-up for functional parameters, including FVC%, DLCO %, and mMRC. Furthermore, lower PNI was significantly associated with higher mortality (HR = 0.893, 95% CI: 0.821–0.972, p = 0.008), suggesting that nutritional and immunological deficits may influence disease prognosis. Consistent with our findings, a study of 112 patients with Sjögren’s syndrome-associated interstitial lung disease (SjS-ILD) demonstrated that a PNI < 47.7 was strongly associated with increased mortality in multivariate analysis (HR 9.40, 95% CI 1.54–57.21) [34]. Taken together, these findings support the notion that nutritional and immunological deficiencies may play a significant role in determining prognosis in interstitial lung diseases.

Functional parameters used to monitor disease progression during follow-up (FVC% and DLCO %) were not statistically different at the 6th month of antifibrotic therapy. Previous studies have demonstrated that antifibrotic therapy reduces the annual rate of FVC decline [35, 36]. In our cohort, all patients were receiving antifibrotic treatment, which may have contributed to the stabilization of functional parameters observed during follow-up.

Clinical, functional, and radiological parameters remain central in IPF follow-up. In our study, digital clubbing was more frequent among non-survivors, reflecting more advanced disease. In a prospective study of 34 patients with IPF, both GAP score and mMRC score were shown to predict mortality [37]. Consistent with this, we observed weak negative correlations between PNI and both mMRC and GAP scores, suggesting that poorer nutritional and immunological status may worsen dyspnea and contribute to higher mortality risk. Furthermore, patients with progressive changes on thoracic CT had lower PNI values and higher mortality, supporting the potential role of PNI as a prognostic biomarker in IPF. Similarly, a study evaluating the prognostic value of malnutrition in IPF using quantitative CT analysis reported that greater fibrosis extent was associated with poorer nutritional indices, including lower PNI and NRI scores [33].

In the meta-analysis evaluating 8408 IPF patients, FVC% (HR = 0.98; 95% CI: 0.97, 0.99; P < 0.001), DLCO % pred) (HR = 0.98; 95% CI: 0.97, 0.99; P < 0.001), GAP score (HR = 1.70; 95% CI: 1.20, 2.40; P = 0.003) was a risk factor associated with mortality in patients with IPF [38]. In another study, 119 IPF patients, multivariate Cox analysis identified RDW, low NLR, low BMI, and reduced DLCO as independent predictors of mortality [39]. In our study, we found that lower FVC%, lower DLCO %, lower PNI and higher mMRC score were significantly associated with mortality in univariate analysis, while FVC% (HR = 0.937, 95% CI: 0.903–0.973, p = 0.001) and mMRC score (HR = 2.245, 95% CI: 1.237–4.074, p = 0.008) was an independent predictor of mortality in multivariate analysis.

Previous studies have demonstrated that poor nutritional status and low body mass index are associated with reduced tolerability of antifibrotic therapy and higher discontinuation rates due to adverse effects [35, 40]. Malnutrition and systemic inflammation may increase susceptibility to gastrointestinal and cutaneous toxicity [41, 42]. However, in our study, no significant difference in PNI values was observed between patients who discontinued antifibrotic treatment and those who continued therapy. The relationship between nutritional status, as assessed by PNI, and antifibrotic treatment tolerability warrants further investigation in larger prospective cohorts.

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective and single-center design, along with a relatively small sample size, may limit the generalizability of our findings. Despite these limitations, our results underscore the importance of integrating nutritional and immunological assessments with functional and radiological monitoring to enhance risk stratification and prognosis prediction in IPF. Prospective, multicenter studies with longer follow-up are warranted to validate these findings and clarify the role of PNI in guiding nutritional interventions and treatment decisions in IPF.

Conclusions

Collectively, our results suggest that integrating functional, nutritional, immunological, and radiological assessments may enhance risk stratification and support more tailored management strategies in patients with IPF.
