Predicting down syndrome: a comparative evaluation of nasal bone length in mid-trimester pregnancy
Authors: Mustafa Koçar, Hasan Süt, Coşkun Ümit, Erdal Şeker, Gülşah Aynaoğlu Yıldız
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of fetal nasal bone length (NBL), the biparietal diameter to nasal bone length ratio (BPD/NBL), and nasal bone length multiples of the median (NBL-MoM) values for Down syndrome (DS) screening between 17 and 25 weeks of gestation in a population-specific cohort.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on fetuses between 17 and 25 gestational weeks. Normative data for NBL and NBL-MoM were generated using gestational age–specific reference medians, and regression equations were established. The NBL, BPD/NBL ratio, and NBL-MoM values were compared between DS and euploid groups. NBL-MoM values were calculated using raw (non-transformed) measurements divided by the expected gestational age–specific median, and euploid status was confirmed by normal postnatal outcomes. Sensitivity, specificity, and likelihood ratios were calculated for various cutoff points.ResultsNBL showed a significant linear correlation with gestational week (R² = 0.31, p < 0.001), while BPD/NBL was independent of gestational age. In DS cases, absence of the nasal bone was observed in 40% of fetuses, compared to 0.03% in controls (sensitivity: 40%, specificity: 100%, LR⁺: 1228). An NBL < 2.5 mm yielded a sensitivity of 49% and specificity of 99.9% (LR⁺: 746). Using the 5th percentile as a cutoff, sensitivity and specificity reached 86% and 98.9%, respectively (LR⁺: 75, LR⁻: 0.15). ABPD/NBL ratio ≥ 9 yielded 93% sensitivity and 97.5% specificity (LR⁺: 37). NBL-MoM values < 0.65 yielded 97% sensitivity and 98% specificity.ConclusionOur findings support the use of population-specific reference ranges for fetal NBL, BPD/NBL ratio, and NBL-MoM values in the second trimester to improve the screening accuracy for Down syndrome. These sonographic markers—particularly NBL < 5th percentile, BPD/NBL ≥ 9, and NBL-MoM < 0.65—may provide valuable, non-invasive indicators to support clinical assessment.Trial registrationThis study was retrospectively registered and authorized by the local Ethics Committee of Ankara University (approval number: 2025/696; date: 03 September 2025).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-08918-4.

BACKGROUND

Down syndrome (DS) is the most common genetic abnormality observed in newborns [1]. Invasive diagnostic procedures such as chorionic villus sampling (CVS) and amniocentesis are recommended for high-risk populations, particularly women over the age of 35 or those with positive screening results. However, these procedures carry a procedure-related fetal loss risk of approximately 0.5% to 1% [2]. Therefore, before resorting to invasive testing, ultrasound-based screening programs or cell-free DNA (cfDNA) tests may be preferred. Although cfDNA testing is available in many countries, its widespread use is limited due to high costs [3].

Prenatal sonography has played an important role in the detection of fetuses with Down syndrome by identifying various sonographic markers. Currently, one of the most widely used and effective approaches for trisomy 21 screening involves the combination of maternal age, fetal nuchal translucency (NT), and maternal serum levels of free β-human chorionic gonadotropin (β-hCG) and pregnancy-associated plasma protein-A (PAPP-A) measured between 11 and 14 weeks of gestation. This strategy has been reported to achieve detection rates approaching 90%, with a false-positive rate around 5% in some studies [4]. It has also been estimated that incorporating the assessment of the fetal nasal bone (presence or absence) into the first-trimester combined screening can increase the detection rate up to 97% [5]. Recent studies have indicated that the nasal bone is absent in approximately 64% of fetuses with Down syndrome during the first trimester, compared to around 4.8% in euploid fetuses. These findings suggest that nasal bone absence may serve as a useful adjunctive sonographic marker in early screening [6, 7].

In the first trimester, evaluating the nasal bone may be challenging due to technical and developmental factors, as illustrated by data showing that 8.7% of nasal bones were initially absent or unclear. However, follow-up scans demonstrated the presence of nasal bones in over 71% of these cases, indicating that early non-visualization does not necessarily reflect a true absence [8]. In contrast, nasal bone assessment during the second trimester is generally more feasible and reliable, making it a practical component of mid-trimester Down syndrome screening protocols [9]. Moreover, hypoplastic nasal bone is not only associated with increased risk for trisomy 21, but may also be linked to a broader spectrum of genetic abnormalities detectable through chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA), underscoring its relevance in comprehensive prenatal evaluation [10, 11].

Second-trimester reference ranges for nasal bone length (NBL) have been reported for various populations, including Caucasian, African-American, South American, Chinese, Japanese, Korean, and Iranian fetuses [12]. These studies indicate that ethnicity may influence the effectiveness of nasal bone screening for trisomy 21. However, establishing a definitive cutoff value for nasal bone hypoplasia remains challenging due to inter-ethnic variability [13, 14].

Similarly, population-specific nomograms have also been reported in Turkish cohorts. Yanık et al. [15] established normal second-trimester NBL reference ranges, while Göynümer et al. [16] demonstrated gestational age–specific distributions in a large Turkish population. These findings emphasize that locally derived standards may improve diagnostic precision and reduce false-positive rates compared with universally applied thresholds. However, most previous Turkish studies primarily focused on normative measurements rather than evaluating the diagnostic performance of combined indices such as BPD/NBL ratio or NBL-MoM -based approaches.

Given these findings, there is a growing interest in refining nasal bone measurements and related sonographic indices during the second trimester to improve the accuracy and clinical utility of prenatal screening. Accordingly, this study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of fetal nasal bone length (NBL), the biparietal diameter to nasal bone length ratio (BPD/NBL), and nasal bone length multiples of the median (NBL-MoM) values for Down syndrome (DS) screening between 17 and 25 weeks of gestation in a population-specific cohort.

Methods

This retrospective study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The medical records of pregnant women who underwent second-trimester ultrasound screening between 2011 and 2022 were reviewed. Written informed consent had been obtained from all participants. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ankara University Ethics Committee (approval number: 2025/696; date: 03 September 2025).

All cases with a confirmed genetic diagnosis of Down syndrome during the second trimester between September 2011 and September 2022 constituted our study group. A total of 35 cases of Down syndrome were identified in this group. The control group included cases that underwent routine mid-trimester (17–25 weeks) ultrasound evaluation as part of clinical follow-up, delivered at our clinic, and had no structural or chromosomal abnormalities. Euploid status was confirmed by normal postnatal outcomes and the absence of phenotypic abnormalities at birth. This group included a total of 3070 cases. To ensure randomization, cases were randomly selected in equal distribution across each month within the study period.

Gestational age was determined according to the last menstrual period (LMP) and confirmed with ultrasonographic measurements during the first and second trimesters. Crown-rump length (CRL) in the first trimester and biparietal diameter (BPD) in the second trimester were used as reference. When the discrepancy between the LMP and ultrasonographic dating was more than 7 days in the first trimester or 14 days in the second trimester, ultrasound measurements were considered definitive; otherwise, the LMP was used.

BPD and nasal bone lenght (NBL) measurements were recorded for all cases in both groups. Nasal bone measurements were performed in a true midsagittal section of the fetal head that visualized the nasal bone, lips, maxilla, and mandible, with the insonation angle positioned at approximately 45° or 135° relative to the nasal bone.

For the control group, the median, 2.5th, 5th, 95th, and 97.5th percentiles of NBL measurements were calculated. BPD/NBL ratios were also computed. To calculate multiples of the median, each nasal bone length measurement was expressed as a nasal bone length multiple of the median (NBL-MoM), defined as the observed NBL divided by the expected gestational age–specific median derived from the control group. Log10 transformation was applied only for regression modelling and smoothing of the median curve. NBL-MoM calculations were performed using raw (non-transformed) measurements.

Initially, we assessed whether the absence of the fetal nasal bone could predict DS. Subsequently, to evaluate hypoplasia independently of absence, additional analyses were performed in fetuses with a detectable nasal bone (NBL > 0). Then, among cases with detectable nasal bones, we evaluated the diagnostic performance of different cut-off values: NBL < 2.5 mm, < 2.5th and <5th percentiles, BPD/NBL ratios > 7.6, 8.5, 9, 10, 11, and 12, and NBL-MoM values < 0.65, < 0.75, < 0.76, and < 0.80.

Statistical analysis

Data were summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median (minimum–maximum), as appropriate. Group comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test or Welch’s t-test according to the homogeneity of variances. Categorical variables, including the presence or absence of the nasal bone and maternal chronic diseases, were compared using Fisher’s exact test. The diagnostic value of clinical measurements to differentiate DS cases from controls was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Optimal cut-off values for NBL, BPD/NBL, and NBL-MoM were determined using Youden’s index.

For ROC analyses, fetuses in whom the nasal bone could not be visualized were assigned an NBL value of 0.5 mm, representing the minimal measurable length, to allow inclusion in ratio-based calculations. Additional stratified ROC analyses were conducted in the detectable nasal bone subgroup to avoid potential bias from combining absent and hypoplastic cases.

Previously reported cut-offs from the literature were also considered. Diagnostic performance (sensitivity, specificity, and positive and negative likelihood ratios with 95% confidence intervals) was calculated for both binary nasal bone presence and the evaluated cut-offs. Additionally, univariate linear regression models were used to analyze the relationship between gestational age and NBL, BPD/NBL, and NBL-MoM.

All analyses were conducted using R statistical software (version 4.1.2). The ‘reportROC’ package was used for diagnostic performance evaluation. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

In our study, we established a nomogram for nasal bone (NBL) measurements between 17 and 25 weeks of gestation in our own population. In addition to the high sensitivity and specificity of absent and hypoplastic nasal bone in predicting Down syndrome (DS), we found that the 5th percentile of NBL, a NBL-MoM value of < 0.65, and a BPD/NBL ratio of 9 were significantly effective in predicting DS within our population. A particular strength of our study is that it enables accurate identification of healthy fetuses in our population with a low false-positive rate, which may help reduce maternal anxiety when used as part of DS screening. A further strength of our study is the comprehensive methodological approach, including the evaluation of absolute measurements, percentile-based thresholds, biometric ratios, and NBL-MoM -based indices within the same cohort, which enhances the robustness and reproducibility of our results.

While cfDNA testing stands out as the most sensitive approach for first-trimester aneuploidy screening, its integration into routine practice remains challenging in our country, largely due to economic constraints—a limitation likewise observed in numerous other healthcare systems [3, 17]. A further cost-efficient approach entails assessing nuchal translucency (NT) in fetuses with a crown-rump length (CRL) ranging from 45 to 84 mm, serving as a practical first-trimester screening method [4]. Observing the nasal bone during screening has been shown to enhance the diagnostic performance of the test [5]. In contrast, when the nasal bone is not visualized, the resulting increase in variability often renders counseling families considerably more difficult. In the first trimester, visualizing the fetal nasal bone often presents considerable challenges. Even under the expertise of seasoned sonographers, more than 71% of cases initially assessed as absent or inconclusive during successive short-interval examinations were later confirmed to have an identifiable nasal bone. This finding highlights that the inability to detect the nasal bone in early gestation does not necessarily indicate its true absence [8]. In contrast, assessment of the nasal bone during the second trimester is generally more feasible and reliable, making it a practical component of mid-trimester screening protocols for Down syndrome [9].

When an isolated absent or hypoplastic nasal bone is identified in the first trimester, current recommendations favor conducting a detailed follow-up assessment in the second trimester rather than proceeding directly to invasive diagnostic testing [17]. When screening for trisomy 21, relying solely on maternal age or second-trimester serum biochemistry yields detection rates of approximately 30% and 65%, respectively, each associated with a 5% false-positive rate [18]. Given the realities in our country, where many pregnant women are unable to attend routine visits during the first trimester, there is a clear need for an effective screening strategy applicable beyond early pregnancy. Consequently, exploring more reliable screening options after 14–15 weeks of gestation represents a reasonable approach. In the second trimester, nasal bone hypoplasia has been shown to identify cases of trisomy 21 more effectively than a simple assessment of nasal bone presence or absence [19]. Therefore, conducting a comprehensive screening around the 16th week of gestation and beyond—one that includes assessment of absent or hypoplastic nasal bone together with other soft markers—and subsequently referring the patient for invasive testing when indicated may represent a more practical strategy [17]. Numerous studies have advocated a similar methodological approach [20–22]. Implementing this strategy in low-resource settings is expected to reduce both overall costs and the number of invasive procedures performed.

Another study reported that in a population with a high prevalence of first-trimester screening—approximately 90%—the majority underwent either first-trimester combined screening (69%) or cfDNA testing (24%) as their primary screening modality [23]. In a cohort of pregnancies at or beyond 18 weeks’ gestation with hypoplastic nasal bone (NB) as the primary ultrasound indication, 127 diagnostic amniocenteses were performed. Overall, a major chromosomal abnormality was identified in 22 cases (17%), of which 16 were trisomy 21. Among the 47 cases with hypoplastic NB accompanied by an additional soft marker or structural anomaly (non-isolated hypoplastic NB), chromosomal abnormalities were detected in 14 (30%). Notably, three of these involved pathogenic CNVs that would have been missed by cfDNA screening [24].

This population-based study, focusing on fetuses undergoing prenatal diagnosis for hypoplastic NB detected in the second trimester, reinforces the strong association between this marker and trisomy 21. It also demonstrates an elevated risk of atypical chromosomal abnormalities when other ultrasound anomalies are present. While no abnormalities other than trisomy 21 were found among our cases of isolated hypoplastic NB, the upper limit of the confidence interval reached 5%. These findings provide valuable data to inform decisions about the need for diagnostic testing following a low-risk cfDNA result [24].

Bromley et al. [25] observed the absence of the nasal bone in 6 out of 16 cases of Down syndrome between 15 and 20 weeks’ gestation, whereas this finding was present in only 0.5% of euploid fetuses. Similarly, other retrospective studies have reported an absence of the nasal bone in approximately 40–45% of Down syndrome cases identified during the second and third trimesters [18, 26]. A study from China reported an absence of the nasal bone in only 0.13% of euploid fetuses following first-trimester screening, and identified a strong association between this finding and aneuploidy [27]. Consistent with the existing literature, our study identified absent nasal bone in 40% of Down syndrome cases, while this finding was present in only 0.03% of euploid fetuses—significantly lower than the previously reported rates of 0.5% or 0.13%. This discrepancy may be attributable to the widespread implementation of comprehensive first-trimester screening in our population, as well as the inherently low prevalence of absent nasal bone in our region. These observations represent a noteworthy finding that warrants further investigation.

Meta-analyses conducted by Moreno-Cid et al. [28] and Agathokleous et al. [29] reported positive likelihood ratios (PLR) ranging from 40.08 to 66.75 and negative likelihood ratios (NLR) of 0.71 for absent nasal bone. In contrast, for nasal bone hypoplasia, PLR values ranged between 15.15 and 23.27, with NLR values of 0.46 to 0.47. These findings underscore the ethnic variation and diagnostic heterogeneity present across studies, highlighting the necessity for each population to establish its own reference standards for nasal bone assessment—thereby providing the rationale for conducting our study. Similar population-specific nomograms have also been reported in Turkish cohorts. Yanık et al. [15] and Göynümer et al. [16] demonstrated gestational age–specific nasal bone distributions and emphasized the importance of locally derived thresholds. Our findings are consistent with these reports and further support the use of population-based reference standards.

To account for gestational age and enhance diagnostic reliability, researchers have adopted various measurement strategies. Notably, definitions of nasal bone hypoplasia differ across the literature: some rely on absolute nasal bone length thresholds (e.g., < 2.5 mm) [30], while others utilize percentiles [31], NBL-MoM [32], or ratios such as BPD/NBL [25]. This variability underscores the lack of a universal standard and the importance of contextualizing diagnostic criteria.

In a prospective study involving 1,046 high-risk pregnancies, nasal bone hypoplasia—defined as either absent or measuring < 2.5 mm—was identified in 61.8% of Down syndrome cases and in only 1.2% of euploid cases. The presence of hypoplasia increased the prior risk nearly 50-fold, whereas the detection of a normal nasal bone reduced the risk by half [30].

In our study, the mean and median nasal bone lengths in the 21 Down syndrome cases were 4.1 ± 1.18 mm and 4.4 mm (range: 2–6), respectively, compared to 6.76 ± 0.85 mm and 6.7 mm (range: 2–10.7) in the control group, demonstrating a statistically significant difference. Using a threshold of < 2.5 mm yielded a sensitivity of 49% and a specificity of 99.9%. This cut-off increased the risk of Down syndrome by 746-fold, while measurements above this threshold reduced the risk to 0.52 times the baseline. Given the low sensitivity observed in our population, the 2.5 mm cut-off was deemed insufficient.

Bunduki et al. [31] defined nasal bone hypoplasia as a measurement below the 5th percentile and found this in 59.1% of 22 Down syndrome cases, compared to 5.1% among 1,600 euploid fetuses. Shi et al. [10] employed a < 2.5 percentile threshold using the MCA method and identified chromosomal anomalies in 9% of cases. In another study utilizing the same 2.5 percentile cut-off, 12.7% of fetuses were found to have aneuploidy [17], while two separate studies from India reported even higher rates of 21% and 26.9%, respectively [33, 34]. In our cohort, the 2.5th and 5th percentile thresholds demonstrated sensitivities of 86% and 94% and specificities of 98.9% and 96.4%, respectively, yielding PLRs of 75 and 27, and NLRs of 0.15 and 0.06. The 5th percentile cut-off proved to be more effective for our population.

Bromley et al. [25] recommended using a BPD/NBL ratio greater than 10 for the detection of Down syndrome, noting that this criterion was present in 81% of Down syndrome fetuses compared to 11% of euploid fetuses. In our study, BPD/NBL ratios remained stable throughout gestation. Among Down syndrome cases, the mean and median ratios were 12.83 and 10.77, respectively, whereas these values were 7.3 and 7.25 in the control group. Using cut-off values of 8.5 and 9 yielded sensitivities of 96.6% and 93% and specificities of 93% and 97.5%, respectively, with corresponding PLR and NLR estimates indicating strong diagnostic performance.

Odibo et al. [32] compared various cut-off points, including NBL-MoM thresholds of < 0.75, < 0.5, and < 0.25 as well as a BPD/NBL ratio > 11. While NBL-MoM < 0.75 and BPD/NBL > 11 demonstrated comparable sensitivities (49% and 61%, respectively), NBL-MoM < 0.75 exhibited a significantly higher specificity (92% vs. 84%), making it a more effective parameter. In our cohort, the mean and median NBL-MoM values were 0.50 and 0.54 for Down syndrome cases, compared to 0.82 and 0.81 among controls. Applying cut-offs of < 0.65, < 0.75, and < 0.80 yielded sensitivities of 97%, 98%, and 97%, alongside specificities of 80%, 100%, and 54%, respectively. The < 0.65 threshold demonstrated statistically significant performance, underscoring its diagnostic utility. Importantly, to minimize potential bias arising from combining fetuses with absent and hypoplastic nasal bones, we additionally performed stratified analyses restricted to cases with a detectable nasal bone, and similar diagnostic performance was observed. This confirms that the predictive value of hypoplasia is independent of complete nasal bone absence.

This retrospective study, conducted over an 11-year period and encompassing 3,070 cases, allowed us to establish gestational age–specific reference standards for nasal bone length in our population.

Limitations

The retrospective single-center design imposes certain limitations and may restrict generalizability. Because many pregnancies in our setting undergo early first-trimester screening, some affected cases may have been diagnosed and terminated before the second trimester, potentially introducing selection bias. In addition, referrals from other centers may have contributed to cohort heterogeneity.

Genetic analyses were not performed in all cases from which nasal bone percentiles were derived, and genetic confirmation was not universally available for fetuses identified with absent or hypoplastic nasal bone. Similarly, euploid status in the control group was inferred from normal postnatal outcomes rather than systematic karyotyping. Although this approach is widely accepted in prenatal screening studies, a small risk of misclassification cannot be entirely excluded.

Because ethnicity was not formally recorded, potential ethnic variation in nasal bone measurements could not be directly evaluated. Given that nasal bone measurements may vary across ethnic groups, external validation in diverse populations is warranted.

Prospective multicenter studies conducted between 17 and 25 weeks’ gestation are required to externally validate these findings. While it remains premature to define precise detection rates for combined strategies incorporating maternal age, biochemical markers, and ultrasonographic parameters, our results suggest that second-trimester nasal bone assessment may serve as a useful adjunctive marker in Down syndrome risk stratification.

Conclusion

Considering our findings, assessment of the fetal nasal bone between 17 and 25 weeks of gestation may contribute to mid-trimester risk stratification for Down syndrome. Given the known influence of population-specific variability, locally derived reference ranges may improve screening performance. In our cohort, the 5th percentile NBL threshold, a BPD/NBL ratio ≥ 9, and an NBL-MoM cut-off < 0.65 demonstrated strong discriminatory performance. However, prospective multicenter validation studies are warranted before broad clinical implementation.
