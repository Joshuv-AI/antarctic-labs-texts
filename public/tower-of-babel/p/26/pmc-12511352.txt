Comprehensive images and serum biomarkers for biliary atresia and other cholestasis in pediatrics
Authors: Shuyi Liu, Rui Zhang, Yongshang Yu, Chunyixue Geng, Hongbin Ma, Yuyun Liu, Wenhua Qin, Yue Zhang, Qiqiao Zhang, Wenjing Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiliary atresia (BA) and other pediatric cholestatic diseases are rare but serious conditions that require early and accurate diagnosis. Ultrasound has become the primary diagnostic method for BA, particularly for evaluating gallbladder contractility through fasting and postprandial comparisons. However, there is currently a lack of publicly available datasets containing paired fasting-postprandial gallbladder ultrasound images, other key sonographic features, and relevant serum biochemical indicators. The dataset consisted of 2,759 ultrasound images obtained from 1,019 infants diagnosed with cholestatic diseases. This included 377 BA cases (664 fasting and 328 post-prandial images) and 642 non-BA cases (1,004 fasting and 580 post-prandial images). Additionally, 183 images documenting abnormal ultrasonographic findings such as triangular cord sign, dilated hepatic artery, and hilar hepatic cysts, and relevant hepatic biochemical indicators were also collected. The study implemented nnU-Net to perform automated gallbladder segmentation across both fasting and postprandial ultrasound examinations. The dataset represents a valuable resource for enhancing the diagnostic accuracy of BA and facilitating the development of clinical decision-support system.Subject terms: Gastrointestinal diseases, Diagnostic markers

Background & Summary

Biliary atresia (BA) and other cholestatic childhood diseases are rare diseases that affect the function and/or anatomical structure of intrahepatic and extrahepatic biliary tree, characterized by persistent cholestatic jaundice during neonatal period, among which BA is the most common one1–3. BA is a serious hepatobiliary disease and can easily lead to be severe fibro-obliteration in infancy4. If effective treatment is not performed timely, cholestasis can develop rapidly inevitably, and liver transplantation is required for the treatment on end-stage cirrhosis, even leads to death by age 2 years1,4. Despite the low incidence rate ranging from 1 in 5,000 to 19,000 live births, BA is the most common diseases for pediatric liver transplantation in infants under one year3. Kasai portojejunostomy is currently the main surgery for BA patients to prevent bile duct occlusion and prolonged the survival of native liver5. Therefore, early and accurate diagnosis is crucial for BA. To differentiate BA from other cholestasis diseases, several biomarkers for BA diagnosis have been identified and characterized in the recent past. The primary indicators include total bilirubin (TBIL), direct bilirubin (DBIL) and gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT) levels, and other relevant hepatic biomarkers6–8. However, the accuracy of using a single diagnostic method is limited, making it necessary to incorporate imaging techniques to support diagnosis. To date, ultrasound is the most commonly non-invasive examination for BA, offering high diagnostic efficacy, particularly in cases where there is poor or absent gallbladder contraction. The typical ultrasound indicators for identifying BA include the triangular cord sign in the hepatic hilum, dilated hepatic artery, hilar hepatic cysts, and various gallbladder abnormalities. Gallbladder abnormalities may present as the absence of the gallbladder, reduced size, abnormal shape or wall structure, and a lack of gallbladder contraction9–11. However, only experienced physicians can make the correct diagnosis among the diseases. In most general hospitals that do not specialize in women’s and children’s healthcare, the lack of relevant diagnostic and treatment experience often leads to delayed diagnosis in infants with BA. Therefore, the early and accurate identification of BA in infants with more convenient tools still poses great challenges.

Deep learning (DL), a crucial component of artificial intelligence (AI), is an increasingly disruptive technology that is gradually being applied to ultrasound imaging. Its use in analyzing imaging data enables enhanced lesion detection, segmentation, and classification12–14. Thus, it can help reduce human errors in this labor-intensive process and enhance the reproducibility of results. Additionally, DL algorithms in ultrasound have demonstrated superior performance compared to traditional methods. In recent diagnostic tasks for abdominal diseases, DL algorithms have even outperformed human experts15–17. As is known, the successful development of DL models relies on a large sample size and data diversity. Existing ultrasound image datasets for cholestatic diseases remain limited, particularly those encompassing both fasting and postprandial states along with other diagnostic sonographic features. In practice, ultrasound-based gallbladder identification, segmentation and measurement in cholestatic infants presents significant diagnostic challenges, regardless of whether the images are taken in a fasted or post-prandial state. Children with BA often exhibit gallbladder non-contraction, poor filling, or non-visualization on ultrasound images. These findings can be attributed to gallbladder atony, underdevelopment, and varying degrees of fibrosis in the gallbladder wall11. Also, in some cases of cholestatic hepatitis, the gallbladder may also appear small, empty, or poorly filled due to hepatocellular damage and reduced bile production and flow. It is indisputable that accurate detection and segmentation of the gallbladder remains crucial for the effectiveness of diagnostic models.

Herein, the study presented a substantial clinical dataset comprising 2,759 real-world ultrasound images and corresponding biochemical profiles, including DBIL, TBIL, GGT, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and total bile acids (TBA), from 1,019 pediatric patients with cholestatic diseases. The imaging data included: (i) 377 BA patients (664 fasting gallbladder images, 328 post-prandial gallbladder images, and 160 other images of abnormal ultrasonographic findings showing triangular cord sign, dilated hepatic artery, and hilar hepatic cysts); and (ii) 642 non-BA patients (1,004 fasting gallbladder images, 580 post-prandial gallbladder images, and 23 other images). Ultrasonographic assessment of the remaining cases revealed no other abnormal sonographic features beyond the gallbladder images. A detailed description of the dataset’s design, image acquisition, gallbladder nnU-Net segmentation pipeline, and image processing was provided. Subsequently, the study developed a BA diagnostic model through three data modalities: (i) ultrasonographic morphology, (ii) serum biochemical marker profiles, and (iii) incorporating demographic variables, ultrasonographic characteristics, and serum biochemical profiles. Specifically, the study employed nnU-Net for automated gallbladder segmentation in both fasting and postprandial states. This dataset is expected to serve as a valuable resource for improving the accuracy of BA diagnosis and supporting decision-making tools.

Methods

Subject characteristics and data acquisition

The study received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Guangzhou Women and Children’s Medical Center (GWCMC), denoted by approval number [2024] No. 002B01. The institutional ethics committee approved a waiver of informed consent for this retrospective study, as all data utilized in this study were fully anonymized. All personally identifiable information was systematically removed from both imaging data and electronic health records, ensuring complete de-identification. This protocol adhered to international ethical standards and regulatory requirements, safeguarding participant privacy while enabling secondary use of existing clinical data for research purposes. The retrospective study collected ultrasound images from the institutional Picture Archiving and Communication System, with corresponding demographic and laboratory data extracted from electronic hospital records. From 2016 to 2022, cholestasis patients were recruited from GWCMC. The inclusion criteria specified infants under five months of age who were diagnosed with cholestasis according to the diagnostic standards established by the North American Society for Pediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology & Nutrition in 200418. A diagnosis of cholestasis was made in infants presenting with a serum TBIL exceeding 87.5 µmol/L, a serum DBIL level DBIL/TBIL ratio above 20%, or in those with a TBIL below 87.5 µmol/L coupled with a DBIL over 17.1 µmol/L. The exclusion criteria included: (1) No intraoperative cholangiography and liver biopsy data were unavailable for children diagnosed with BA. (2) No fasting gallbladder ultrasound images. (3) No gallbladder detection during ultrasonography, and (4) Substandard image quality of the ultrasound images. Notably, the non-BA cases in this study represented transient cholestatic conditions, including physiological jaundice, infections, and other self-limiting causes. The serum biochemical indicators, including DBIL, TBIL, GGT, ALT, AST, ALP and TBA were obtained at the time of admission before any treatment was administered. Fasting gallbladder ultrasound images were obtained after the infants had fasted for more than 4 hours, while postprandial gallbladder ultrasound images were taken 30 minutes to 1 hour after feeding. It should be noted that all infants have fasting gallbladder ultrasound images, but postprandial gallbladder ultrasound images may be absent in some cases. In total, 1019 patients with cholestasis were identified.

The ultrasonographic evaluation parameters included standardized measurements of fasting gallbladder length (long-axis) and width (short-axis) in millimeters (mm), followed by postprandial measurements after feeding. Other findings such as the triangular cord sign, hepatic artery dilation, and hilar hepatic cysts were also evaluated. The thickness of the triangular cord sign was measured at its typical location anterior or adjacent to the portal vein bifurcation. Gallbladder contraction rate was calculated as [(fasting maximal cross-sectional area - postprandial maximal cross-sectional area)/fasting maximal cross-sectional area] × 100%, and length-to-width ratio was derived from fasting gallbladder. If the postprandial gallbladder cross-sectional area was larger than the fasting one, it was defined as absent contraction.

Data anonymization and renaming

The ultrasound images and associated data were de-identified by removing all personal information to ensure patient anonymity and protect individual privacy. For each patient, the ultrasound images were organized into one separate subfolders. The dataset organized gallbladder images into Fasting/Post-prandial subfolders with sequentially numbered files, while other findings were systematically renamed based on sonographic features (e.g., TC.jpg, Dilated hepatic artery.jpg, Cyst.jpg) (Fig. 1).

An overview of the folder structure for the dataset images. Folders named with numbers represent individual patients, with a total of 1,019 patients and 2,759 images.

Labeling and ROI annotation

In the dataset, the enrolled ultrasound images were designated as representations of the gallbladder. Experienced radiologists meticulously delineated the gallbladder wall contour to demarcate the region of interest (ROI) (Fig. 2). This annotation was performed using the Labelme tool, ensuring precise and standardized marking for subsequent analysis.

nnU-Net segmentation algorithm

The gallbladder segmentation was performed using nnU-Net, and nnU-Net Algorithm overview was as followed (Fig. 3): nnU-Net (“no-new-Net”) is a deep learning-based medical image segmentation method that uniquely automates the configuration of all critical steps, including preprocessing, network architecture, training processes, and post-processing. It features four core characteristics: fully automated configuration, universality, efficiency, and broad applicability. The algorithm automatically adapts to data characteristics, eliminating manual parameter tuning to achieve fully automated configuration. It is universally applicable to any type of medical image and segmentation task while achieving high-performance segmentation with minimal computational resources. In international medical image segmentation competitions, it matches or surpasses state-of-the-art specialized methods.

Image processing

The nnU-Net image processing pipeline primarily consists of data preprocessing, image augmentation, network training, model selection, and post-processing. During data preprocessing, redundant blank areas were first cropped to retain only non-zero regions, thereby reducing computational load. Subsequently, key dataset characteristics were extracted, encompassing image dimensions, spacing, modalities, class ratios, etc. Intensity normalization follows: per-image z-score normalization (subtracting mean and dividing by standard deviation) was performed while ignoring zero-value regions. In the image enhancement phase, dynamic application of various techniques were used to enhance generalization. Random affine transformations were applied for rotation, scaling, and elastic deformation. Intensity variations were introduced using Gaussian noise and brightness adjustments. Finally, training patches were randomly cropped from the augmented images to reduce boundary artifacts.

Experimental detail

In terms of network auto-configuration, automatically determined the optimal network patch size and topology, including network depth-number of downsampling layers-and the number of feature maps per layer, based on data dimensions and GPU memory capacity. Generally, larger patch sizes were prioritized to capture contextual information. When the spacing of input images approaches isotropy, isotropic kernels (3 × 3 × 3) and strides (2 × 2 × 2) were used for feature extraction. Additionally, for large datasets that cannot be effectively processed by full-resolution 3D U-Nets, a “Cascade” structure was enabled: the first low-resolution 3D U-Net processed the downsampled global information, and the second full-resolution 3D U-Net refined the output of the previous network.

For network architecture of U-Net, the base architecture followed the classical U-Net and 3D U-Net designs, featuring an encoder-decoder structure with skip connections. Each scale level employed two convolution blocks comprising a convolutional layer, instance normalization, and Leaky ReLU activation. Downsampling was performed using stride convolutions, while upsampling was implemented with transposed convolutions. In order to stabilize training, deep supervision was applied to model. The model defaulted to three configurations: a 2D U-Net for slice-level images, a full-resolution 3D U-Net for complete 3D volumes, and a 3D Cascade U-Net that first performed coarse segmentation with a low-resolution network and refined results with a full-resolution network. The optimal configuration or ensemble model was selected via cross-validation for final predictions (Table 1).

Data Records

The dataset is available at the Figshare Repository and the website19. The raw medical images have been stored in jpg format. Simultaneously, the uploaded Excel database contained participant data, including: (1) demographics (ID, sex, age); (2) laboratory values (TBIL, DBIL, GGT, ALT, AST, ALP, TBA); and (3) ultrasonographic measurements (fasting/postprandial gallbladder dimensions, visualization of triangular cord sign, dilated hepatic artery, hilar hepatic cyst). These materials have been thoughtfully organized and uploaded into a single zip file named “Raw Data”, which includes a folder of “Raw Images” containing a total of 2,759 raw US images, and an Excel file titled “List Data”. The configuration file (dataset.json) and the cross-validation results (5-fold) with detailed segmentation performance metrics were provided to ensure full reproducibility of the segmentation pipeline and facilitate benchmarking against future studies.

Technical Validation

Upon completion of training, the system employed cross-validation to select either a single optimal model or combined models (2D U-Net, 3D U-Net, 3D Cascade) for ensemble predictions to achieve peak performance. Post-processing was automatically executed based on validation results: the Largest Connected Component strategy was first applied to eliminate false-positive regions, followed by a soft voting mechanism to further enhance segmentation accuracy.

Accurate gallbladder delineation is essential in BA assessment. In Fig. 4, it presented the gallbladder segmentation results using nnU-Net with the ROI annotated by expert radiologists. As demonstrated in Table 1, nnU-Net achieved superior segmentation performance across multiple metrics: a mean Dice of 0.797, mean relative volume error of 0.266, mean sensitivity of 0.808, mean specificity of 0.998, mean Hausdorff_95 of 27.580, mean pixel accuracy of 0.996 and mean Intersection over Union of 0.701. BA diagnostic model development: Table 2 presented the predictor selection for BA diagnosis followed a two-stage analytical protocol. Initial screening was performed using univariate logistic regression with a significance threshold of P < 0.05 for all candidate variables: demographic factors (sex, age), hepatic biochemical markers (TBI, DBIL, GGT, ALT, AST), and ultrasonographic parameters (gallbladder contraction rate, triangular cord sign, fasting gallbladder length and width) were selected. Subsequent multivariate logistic regression analysis identified four independent predictors that retained statistical significance (P < 0.05): age [OR: 0.991, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.983–0.999], GGT (OR: 1.003, 95% CI: 1.002–1.004), gallbladder contraction rate (OR: 0.120, 95% CI: 0.057–0.254), and triangular cord sign visualization (OR: 8.635, 95% CI: 4.091–18.223). Candidate variables (P < 0.05) for receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve construction were identified through univariate logistic regression screening. The overall combined model for BA diagnosis included four variables (age, GGT, gallbladder contraction rate and triangular cord sign visualization) identified through multivariate analysis. A logistic regression model was then applied to transform the linear predictor values into diagnostic probabilities using SPSS (version 26.0). Subsequently, the predicted probabilities were used to construct ROC curve for diagnostic performance evaluation. Figure 5 presented the ROC curves evaluating the diagnostic performance of: the combined predictive model (Fig. 5a), sonographic features (Fig. 5b), and individual hepatic biochemical markers (Fig. 5c) for BA. Additionally, Table 3 summarized the diagnostic parameters (AUC [95% CI], P value, cutoff, sensitivity, and specificity) for individual predictors and the overall combined BA prediction model. For BA diagnosis, GGT exhibited the best diagnostic performance among single markers (AUC 0.845), whereas the overall combined model achieved maximal diagnostic accuracy (AUC 0.894). As the proportion of cases with missing data did not exceed 50% for any variables, listwise deletion was applied in the logistic regression analysis. Given the greater clinical impact of false-negative BA diagnoses, the study prioritized sensitivity (>95%) when determining cutoff value. Study limitations

This study has several limitations. Firstly, due to the retrospective nature of the study, it may not be all ultrasound images relevant to BA could be comprehensively provided, despite our best efforts to retrospectively review all stored ultrasound images and reports. Secondly, potential center-based selection bias could hindered proper representation of the large amount of non-BA cholestasis cases, which represent the majority of neonatal cholestasis in clinical practice. Future prospective multicenter studies are warranted to validate these findings.
