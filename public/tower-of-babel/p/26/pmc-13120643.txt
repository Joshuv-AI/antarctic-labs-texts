An Appraisal of Labor Augmentation: An Update on Current Evidence and Practice
Authors: Victor N Chilaka, Mariam Al Baloushi, Huda Saleh, Zeena Al Mansoor, Badreldeen Ahmed, Justin C Konje
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAugmentation of labor remains the cornerstone of intrapartum care, typically employed to expedite delivery by enhancing the frequency, strength, and duration of uterine contractions. The decision to augment depends on the stage of labor and the woman’s obstetric history. While up to half of all women who enter spontaneous or induced labor may require augmentation, its use is not without risk. Although augmentation in nulliparous women during the first stage of labor is generally uncontroversial, its application in multiparous women, during the second stage of labor, in breech presentation, or in women who have had a previous cesarean section remains contentious. Emerging debate also surrounds whether oxytocin should be routinely discontinued once active labor is established. In this review, we appraise contemporary evidence on the diagnosis of labor dystocia, indications, and methods for labor augmentation, outcomes, complications, and areas of clinical uncertainty. Current international guidelines are compared, and evidence-based recommendations are provided. We conclude by highlighting unresolved areas that warrant further research to inform future policy and clinical practice.

Introduction

Labor augmentation, defined as the intervention to enhance the efficiency of uterine contractions in women who are already in spontaneous labor, has been the cornerstone of intrapartum care. The goal is to reduce the duration of labor and minimize maternal and fetal risks associated with prolonged labor, including chorioamnionitis, postpartum hemorrhage (PPH), and operative delivery.1,2 The methods of labor augmentation include intravenous administration of synthetic oxytocin and artificial rupture of membranes (amniotomy), either alone or in combination.2,3

The rationale for augmentation is rooted in the physiological understanding of labor progression, based on the Friedman and Zhang curves.4,5 The Friedman curve, developed in the 1950s, was based on a relatively small cohort and characterizes labor as having a latent phase (up to 4 cm dilation) followed by a rapid linear active phase (4–10 cm), with a distinct deceleration phase near full dilation, giving the curve a sigmoid shape. Slow progress or arrest is diagnosed in this model when cervical dilation does not meet the expected rate or if there is no change in dilation over 2 hours in the active phase.5,6 This often leads to earlier diagnosis of labor dystocia and, consequently, higher rates of labor augmentation or cesarean section (CS) for arrest disorders.5–7

In contrast, the Zhang curve, derived from a much larger and more contemporary cohort, demonstrates that labor progression is more gradual, especially before 6 cm dilation, and lacks a clear deceleration phase. On the basis of this model, the active phase is considered to begin at 6 cm, not 4 cm, and the expected rate of dilation is slower and more variable.5 Using the Zhang curve, slow progress is diagnosed only when dilation is persistently slower than the fifth percentile for the population, and longer periods without cervical change are tolerated before diagnosing arrest—especially before 6 cm.5,8 This reduces the likelihood of prematurely diagnosing labor arrest and is associated with lower intervention rates without increasing adverse outcomes.5,9

Augmentation protocols have become increasingly scrutinized, particularly with concerns about over-medicalization of labor and unnecessary intervention. Complications such as uterine tachysystole, fetal hypoxia, and uterine rupture, particularly in women with previous uterine scars, require exercising caution, guided by continuous monitoring during augmentation.10,11

Recent large-scale trials and population-based studies have called into question some long-standing practices in the induction and/or augmentation of labor. The ARRIVE trial concluded that inducing labor at 39 weeks in low-risk nulliparous women did not result in a significantly lower frequency of a composite adverse perinatal outcome; however, it did result in a significantly lower frequency of CS.12 While this study primarily focused on induction, it stimulated a strong debate about the implications of oxytocin use in early labor and its potential for unnecessary augmentation in low-risk populations.3 Similarly, the World Health Organization Labour Care Guide is now promoting a less interventionist approach, urging a redefinition of labor dystocia and supporting physiological labor progression.13,14

Controversies persist regarding the appropriateness of augmentation in specific clinical scenarios, including in multiparous women, in the second stage, and its use in women with prior CSs and breech presentations. These debated areas reflect the current uncertainty in applying rigid augmentation protocols to highly individualized labor patterns.15,16

This review aims to appraise the current evidence on labor augmentation, including in areas of clinical controversy, and provide practice recommendations. By addressing both the indications and limitations of augmentation strategies, we aim to support a more judicious and patient-centered approach to intrapartum care.

Search strategy and evidence synthesis

A literature search was undertaken using PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library. Searches covered the period from January 2000 to March 2025 and used combinations of keywords and the MeSH terms “labor augmentation,” “oxytocin,” “labor dystocia,” “trial of labor after cesarean section,” “breech presentation,” and “second stage of labor.” Randomized controlled trials, systematic reviews, large observational studies, and international guidelines were prioritized. Evidence was synthesized narratively and anchored to contemporary guideline recommendations. The strength of evidence underlying key practice statements is indicated where relevant.

Diagnosis and assessment of labor

Accurate assessment of labor progression is fundamental to the decision-making process regarding augmentation. Labor dystocia is defined as abnormally slow or protracted labor progression characterized by slow cervical dilation and/or delayed fetal descent and is the leading indication for primary CS in contemporary obstetric practice.7,9 The diagnosis of labor dystocia is stage-specific and should be premised on current evidence-based criteria that reflect contemporary labor patterns.

The phases of labor

Labor is classically divided into three phases/stages: first, second, and third. The first stage is further divided into the latent and active phases.

First stage: the latent phase

The latent phase, defined as the interval between the onset of labor and the beginning of the active dilatation phase, is the period during which uterine contractions may be regular and painful but unaccompanied by little or no cervical dilatation. It is characterized by intense biochemical and electromechanical activity despite the lack of (or negligible) progressive cervical dilatation. This period is functionally the time when the cervix and surrounding tissues undergo preparation for active labor.17,18 While there is a general agreement on what constitutes the active, second, and third stages of labor, there is no consensus on the latent phase.

Precisely when the transition from the latent to the active phase of labor occurs is not well defined, especially given wide individual variation among parturients. This variation is reflected in differences in when active labor is presumed to start across various guidelines (at 3, 4, 5, or 6 cm dilatation).11,19–21 An assumption can be made from these findings that the changeover likely occurs somewhere between 3 and 6 cm, depending on several factors, including the individual herself, parity, and the degree of prelabor cervical maturation.22 Determining this critical point can only be done retrospectively by pinpointing when on the labor curve the rate of cervical dilatation began to accelerate.21

The latent phase involves cervical effacement and dilation to 4 to 6 cm, often with variable contractions. The active phase was previously considered to begin at around 4 cm dilation, but recent guidelines suggest that 6 cm may better define active labor, especially in nulliparous women. A study by Peisner and Rosen23 found that < 50% of nulliparas had gone into active labor at 4-cm dilatation, approximately 74% had done so by 5 cm, and yet more than 10% were still in the latent phase at this stage. It would therefore seem arbitrary to designate a specific cervical dilatation as the endpoint of the latent phase of labor. Having said that, most clinical guidelines regard 6 cm as the critical dilatation for transition. The time it takes most women to reach this point varies. It is therefore essential to adopt a uniform standard for clinical care, and this has been accepted as 20 hours for nulliparous women and 14 hours for multiparous women, based on the 95th percentile from the data of Friedman and more recent studies.19,21,24–26 With difficulties defining the length of the latent phase, it follows that there will be some inconsistency in diagnosing a prolonged latent phase. Still, it is generally defined as exceeding the 95th percentile of the limits from Friedman’s data and other studies (i.e., > 20 hours in primigravida and > 14 hours in multigravida).22

Second stage: descent and expulsion phase

The second stage is defined as the period from full cervical dilatation to delivery of the baby. In the second stage, dystocia is defined as: (a) protracted second stage if it exceeds 3 hours in nulliparous women with an epidural (or 2 hours without an epidural) or 2 hours in multiparous women with an epidural (or 1 hour without an epidural); or (b) arrest of descent if there is no descent after 1 hour of adequate pushing.29

These thresholds are somewhat arbitrary, and the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists suggests that longer durations may be appropriate in the absence of maternal or fetal compromise, particularly if progress is being made.9 Furthermore, distinctions between the passive second stage (complete dilatation before active pushing begins) and the active second stage should be recognized.

Indications for labor augmentation: prolonged latent phase, protracted active phase, and secondary arrest of dilatation

Augmentation of labor aims to correct labor dystocia secondary to inadequate uterine contractions. Contractions are inadequate either in terms of frequency (i.e., < 4 contractions every 10 minutes or objectively/subjectively weak) or not achieving satisfactory cervical dilatation (of at least 0.5 cm/h in the active phase) and descent of the presenting part. Augmentation is initiated only when the passage (pelvis) and passenger (fetus), by virtue of their dimensions/shape and size/presentation, respectively, are not considered contraindications to vaginal delivery.30Table 1 summarizes these indications.

The prolonged latent phase is > 20 hours in primigravidas and > 14 hours in multigravidas. This diagnosis, however, is best individualized, as discussed earlier, especially since the timing of the transition from latent to active is imprecise.

The prolonged active phase is failure of the cervix to dilate by at least 2 cm over a period of 4 hours in those with a cervical dilatation of at least 4 to 6 cm (Table 1) and with ruptured membranes (i.e., in active labor). It is the most common indication for augmentation and is further divided into secondary arrest or protracted active phase.

The prolonged second stage is > 2 hours of active pushing in a nulligravida without an epidural and 3 hours with an epidural, and more than 1 hour and 2 hours in a multipara without or with an epidural, respectively.31

Augmentation regimens: high-dose versus low-dose oxytocin

Two regimens are used for labor augmentation: high- and low-dose oxytocin regimens. These regimens are based on the initial and incremental doses of oxytocin. The high-dose regimen is one with an initial oxytocin dose of ≥ 100 milliunits (mU) administered within the first 40 minutes, followed by incremental rates of ≥ 600 mU in the first 2 hours,7,46 or a starting dose of ≥ 4 mU/min, with increases of ≥ 4 to 6 mU/min at intervals of 15 to 40 minutes.32–34 The low-dose regimen is one in which the starting dose is < 100 mU in the first 40 minutes, with subsequent increments of < 600 mU in the first 2 hours,7 or < 4 mU/min, with subsequent increments of < 4 mU/min every 15 to 40 minutes.9,35,36 The rationale for using the high dose is that it may shorten labor and potentially increase the spontaneous delivery rate, whereas the rationale for the low dose is the minimization of tachysystole and its consequences on both the fetus and mother. Several randomized, retrospective, and prospective studies have generated variable results with these regimens. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses of these studies concluded that the high-dose regimen was associated with a significant decrease (by approximately 1.5–3.5 hours)36–38 in the duration of labor, an increase in spontaneous vaginal delivery rates,36,37 and a decrease in CS rates.36,37 While Kenyon et al.36 did not report on any difference in tachysystole, Wei et al.37 and Aboshama et al.38 reported an increase in uterine hyperstimulation. Notably, none of the studies showed a negative effect on maternal morbidity for either regimen. A potential confounder that may not have been considered in these studies was parity. In the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality systematic review by Myers et al.39, it was shown that the reduction in CS rates was greater among nulliparous women. Regarding neonatal outcomes/morbidity, there were no significant differences in rates of low Apgar scores, cord blood acidemia, neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission, or serious neonatal morbidity or mortality between high-dose and low-dose regimens.34–38 A more recent systematic review and meta-analysis by Logue et al.40 not only confirmed these lack of differences in neonatal outcomes but showed a decreased risk of chorioamnionitis in the high-dose group. Some studies have reported a higher incidence of non-reassuring fetal heart rate patterns and adverse perinatal outcomes with high-dose regimens, particularly in resource-limited settings; however, these findings have not been consistent in larger, higher-quality studies from other settings.32,34,41

It is therefore not surprising that the current consensus is that high-dose regimens do not confer significant benefits for major maternal or neonatal outcomes compared with low-dose regimens, with some nuanced differences in secondary outcomes. Overall, the safety profile of the high-dose regimen is similar to that of the low-dose regimen when used with appropriate monitoring. The potential reduction in the risk of chorioamnionitis may, however, confer some advantage in using this regimen, except that it requires more intense monitoring and therefore is more costly. The increased risk of tachysystole with high-dose regimens underscores the importance of careful titration and fetal monitoring.34,36–38,42 The recent clinical guideline from the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists states that either high- or low-dose oxytocin regimens are reasonable for labor augmentation as there are no clinically significant differences in major maternal or neonatal outcomes.9 The choice of regimen may be individualized based on institutional protocols and patient-specific factors. On balance, we believe that it may be prudent to use the high-dose regimen in nulliparous women and the low-dose regimen in multiparous women. In settings with limited resources, it would be advisable to use the low-dose regimen as it requires less intense monitoring (i.e., is cheaper to monitor).

Specific conditions in which augmentation is undertaken

Augmentation in the latent phase

A latent phase that is more than 20 hours in a primigravida or 14 hours in a multigravida is considered prolonged. How best should this be treated? Opinions vary, with some recommending supportive care, reassurance, hydration, rest, and analgesics (aimed at reducing anxiety and general fatigue; e.g., morphine sulfate) in the hope that active labor will ensue.22 This approach seems to be safe for the fetus and mother, although there have been concerns raised about the possibility of occasional maternal or fetal deoxygenation.43–45 For most women, however, having contractions for 14 to 20 hours and being told they are in the latent phase and must await the onset of active labor is frustrating and demoralizing. It is on this basis that augmentation with oxytocin has been advocated by some.22,46 Studies have reported success rates of approximately 85% with augmentation.17,21

In a randomized trial of the management of nulliparous women with a prolonged latent phase after therapeutic rest with zolpidem (a non-benzodiazepine narcotic), Bräne et al.47 found that prompt initiation of oxytocin did not significantly alter the CS rate or the patients’ perception of their experience in labor compared with a group managed more conservatively. In another study, Sargunam et al.46 randomized women to receive labor augmentation or expectant management for 24 hours. The length of labor was shorter in the augmented group. Still, there were no differences between the groups in the rates of CS or instrumental deliveries or in measures of immediate neonatal outcome.

Does a long latent phase predispose to problems in later labor or beyond? Some studies showed that the risk of CS or subsequent dysfunctional labor in the active phase was not increased and that a very long latent phase contributed minimally to the risk of an abnormal long-term outcome.21,24 More recent data, however, suggest that a prolonged latent phase may predispose to several complications, including an increase in the rate of CS for dystocia. In a study that compared labors with a latent phase > 18 hours to controls with a shorter latent phase, the study group was more likely to experience amniotomy, oxytocin, instrumental delivery, and emergency CS,48 suggesting that a prolonged latent phase is associated with maternal morbidity. In fact, several observational studies have reached similar conclusions.21,43,49 It is therefore logical to conclude from these studies that a prolonged latent phase may be a harbinger of further complications of labor, hence a reason to consider augmentation. Despite this, it is essential to state that approximately 10% of women with a prolonged latent phase are indeed in false labor, and their contractions eventually abate spontaneously, thus not needing any intervention.22

While oxytocin is effective in reducing labor duration, it can be associated with fetal and maternal complications that could potentially be minimized by discontinuing it when there is, for example, tachysystole or fetal hypoxia.50–52 If the assumption that once a woman requiring oxytocin during the latent phase enters the active phase and natural oxytocin takes over from synthetic oxytocin,53 could discontinuation of oxytocin then reduce exposure duration and therefore reduce the risk of complications, especially those related to tachysystole, without compromising the chances of vaginal delivery?

Two meta-analyses, one in 2018 (Boie et al.54) and one in 2019 (Hernández-Martínez et al.55), and a series of studies suggested that once a woman is in active labor, the labor will continue even if oxytocin stimulation is stopped, resulting in a lower risk of CS for fetal indications secondary to uterine hyperstimulation.54–58 Boie et al.59 in a double-blind randomized controlled trial, concluded that, in a setting where monitoring of the fetal condition and the uterine contractions could be guaranteed, routine discontinuation of oxytocin stimulation could lead to a small increased risk of CS but a significantly reduced risk of uterine hyperstimulation and abnormal fetal heart rate. In a multicenter, open-label, randomized trial, Girault et al.60 concluded that discontinuing oxytocin when the active phase was reached did not clinically or significantly reduce neonatal morbidity compared with continuous oxytocin. This did not also have a significant effect on neonatal morbidity, mode of delivery, or PPH. However, participants in the discontinuous oxytocin group did experience a longer active first phase or second stage. Interestingly, both the 2018 Boie et al.54 study and the 2021 Boie et al.59 study reported that the reduction in uterine tachysystole in the discontinuous oxytocin group in their trial, although consistent with previous published studies, did not translate into a reduction in neonatal acidosis.

A further meta-analysis by Jiang et al.61—which combined the later study of Boie et al.59—while demonstrating an overall reduction in CS rate with discontinuation of oxytocin, observed that this was not the case in the women who had been in active labor. Two subsequent randomized trials undertaken by El-Hakem et al.62 and Girault et al.63 generated more data that were combined into a more comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis (15 randomized trials comprising 5734 patients) by Whitley et al.64 This meta-analysis concluded that discontinuation of oxytocin was indeed associated with a 20% reduction in CS (relative risk (RR): 0.80; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.66–0.97), tachysystole (RR: 0.45; 95% CI: 0.34–0.60), and non-reassuring fetal heart rate tracing (RR: 0.64; 95% CI: 0.49–0.82). The duration of the active phase and second stage of labor were increased by 30.4 minutes and 6.3 minutes, respectively. Discontinuation did not have an impact on neonatal morbidity (Apgar score at 5 minutes < 7 and NICU admission). Reassuringly, the delay in the first and second stages of labor did not increase the risk of chorioamnionitis and PPH (although this was assessed in only two of the 15 studies). Furthermore, and reassuringly, more than two-thirds of the women did not require reinitiation of oxytocin.

Despite these conclusions, the authors highlighted the limitations of their data, which included heterogeneity of the patients, variability in the definition of active labor (5 cm or 6 cm), exclusion of medical conditions and women with a body mass index of > 30 kg/m2 in some of the studies, and failure to report on the duration of discontinuation when the oxytocin was restarted in others. A caveat to their conclusions was that the finding of beneficial effect is dependent on the trustworthiness of the included studies.64 Interestingly, another very recent systematic review and meta-analysis of 15 randomized controlled trials by Jafar et al.65 confirmed the reduction in tachysystole and non-reassuring fetal rate tracings and longer active phase of labor but no overall reduction in the CS rate. These findings are contrary to those by Whitley et al.64 The differences could be that the CS rate in the Jafar et al.65 study was mainly in those who had been in active labor whereas Whitley et al.64 included studies that analyzed intention to treat data. That the data from these various studies show diversity in results suggest the impact of potential individual variation (variables) in the initiation and maintenance of labor. In some of the studies—for example, multiparous women, despite comparable rates of uterine tachysystole between the groups of discontinuous and continuous oxytocin—discontinuation of oxytocin was associated with a statistically significant increase in neonatal morbidity, albeit not clinically significant. This highlights the need for individualized care and consideration of patient-specific factors (such as age, parity, body mass index, and comorbidities). These observations support the importance of personalized approaches to managing labor—something that is already being implemented—including oxytocin flow rate adjustments based on uterine tachysystole or abnormal fetal heart rate patterns. Crucially, findings from previous studies have shown that during the active phase of labor, 30% to 40% of women whose oxytocin administration was discontinued required a restart of oxytocin.54,59,60,66

To conclude, the management of a prolonged latent phase must include the option of augmenting uterine activity with oxytocin or providing a sedative-induced period of maternal rest. Both are equally effective in advancing labor to the active phase of dilatation. A very long latent phase may, however, be a harbinger of other labor dysfunctions.22 While the data for discontinuing oxytocin with the establishment of active labor are convincing, more research is required to generate robust data. In the meantime, we advocate individualization based on patient factors and proper counseling.

Augmentation in women with a previous CS

Augmentation of labor in women with a prior CS with oxytocin increases the risk of uterine rupture with reported rates as high as 1.0% to 2.0% in large cohort studies compared to rates of 0.4% to 0.7% in those going into spontaneous labor.67–69 This risk is dose- and duration-dependent.

Evidence emphasizes that the decision to augment labor in women with a prior CS should be individualized, with careful patient selection and shared decision-making.10 Augmentation with oxytocin is considered acceptable in appropriately selected women for trial of labor after CS, provided close maternal and fetal monitoring is in place and that hospital protocols for rapid response to uterine rupture are established. In these women, it may be acceptable to allow prolonged latent phases to avoid unnecessary oxytocin use and reduce the risk of uterine rupture.15

In summary, for women with one prior lower segment CS, the best practice may be to use oxytocin augmentation with careful titration and monitoring. While there is an increasing number of women with two previous CSs being allowed to labor and deliver vaginally, we do not recommend augmentation in this group as the risk of uterine rupture would be considerably higher.

Augmentation in multiparous women

Multiparous women generally have higher rates of successful vaginal delivery and lower rates of labor dystocia. Labor is often rapid, and delays should prompt careful reassessment to exclude fetopelvic disproportion before assuming true dystocia.

The risks associated with augmentation in this group are generally lower than in nulliparous women. Still, prolonged or high-dose oxytocin administration seems to be associated with increased risk of PPH.70,71 The risk of PPH rises with both the duration of labor and the maximum dose of oxytocin, particularly at doses ≥ 20 mU/min and labor durations ≥ 16 hours.70 However, some studies have found no clinically significant association between oxytocin augmentation and PPH when controlling for labor pattern and confounders, suggesting that the risk may be more closely related to labor dystocia and duration rather than to oxytocin itself.72

For augmentation of labor in multiparous women, there is enough evidence to support that, where the indication is protracted or arrested labor, extending the duration of oxytocin augmentation (up to 4 hours with adequate uterine activity or 6 hours with inadequate activity) is associated with high rates of vaginal delivery and does not increase adverse neonatal outcomes.9 Multiparous women have a high likelihood of vaginal delivery even after prolonged augmentation, and CS should not be performed solely for slow but progressive labor if maternal and fetal status are reassuring.9

In summary, for multiparous women with protracted labor, oxytocin augmentation can be safely continued for at least 4 hours if there is no cervical change, provided maternal and fetal status remain reassuring. In these women, we recommend using the low-dose oxytocin regimen. A CS should not be performed solely for slow progress within this window.

Augmentation in breech presentation

Current evidence does not directly address the question of augmentation in breech presentation. This is partly because vaginal breech delivery is uncommon, although there is now an accepted opinion that, in well-selected cases with available expertise, vaginal breech delivery should be allowed. Should these women be induced if they fail to go into spontaneous labor or be augmented if labor progress is slow? Although augmentation has always been controversial due to concerns about increased risk of fetal compromise and entrapment, there is some evidence for its use in those with appropriate indications. In a guideline written by the Guidelines Committee of the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists,73 review of evidence on augmentation of labor in women as part of the induction of labor for breech presentation showed outcomes similar to those of induction in vertex presentation. They therefore concluded that “in properly selected and carefully managed cases of breech presentation, induction of labor seems a safe and reasonable option.73” In another more recent study, Gaillard et al.74 also concluded that “induction and augmentation of labor for breech presentation does not seem to increase neonatal mortality or severe neonatal morbidity compared with planned cesarean delivery.” While there are studies on the induction of labor in women with breech presentation, no studies have focused only on the augmentation of women in spontaneous labor with breech presentation.74

In a large retrospective study (PREMODA) of more than 8000 singleton breech presentations, Goffinet et al.75 reported an augmentation rate of 74.1% and, interestingly, this was also associated with slow cervical dilatation. The authors concluded that augmentation should be avoided as adequate progress may be the best evidence for adequate fetopelvic proportions. However, they stated that its use should not be excluded in those with an epidural and a low contraction frequency. Two guidelines, while not endorsing augmentation in this group, have advised cautious use. The Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists recommend in their green-top guideline that, “augmentation of slow progress with oxytocin should only be considered if the contraction frequency is low in the presence of an epidural analgesia.73” The Society of Obstetricians and Gynecologists of Canada guideline states that “oxytocin augmentation is acceptable to correct weak uterine contractions if labor progress is poor despite contractions, while cesarean section is recommended.76” Based on the above studies and recommendations, we would recommend that augmentation should be considered cautiously in spontaneous breech labors where fetopelvic disproportion has been excluded, the woman has an epidural, and the slow progress is directly related to inadequate uterine contractions.

Augmentation in the second stage of labor

In the second stage of labor, augmentation may increase the risk of adverse neonatal outcomes, including low Apgar scores and the need for neonatal resuscitation, especially when labor is prolonged or oxytocin is used at high doses.71 Additionally, excessive uterine activity from oxytocin can lead to fetal heart rate abnormalities and, rarely, uterine hyperstimulation or rupture.60,71

Augmenting labor with oxytocin in the second stage is controversial, further compounded by the lack of agreement on when to initiate pushing in the second stage (delayed versus immediate pushing). Recent evidence indicates that delayed pushing does not improve vaginal delivery rates and is associated with a longer second stage and increased perinatal morbidity.60 Early pushing is therefore recommended once full cervical dilatation is achieved. Augmentation with oxytocin in the second stage may be considered for inadequate contractions but should be balanced against the risks of uterine hyperstimulation and fetal compromise.77

Artificial rupture of fetal membranes (ARM) as a method of augmentation

Early amniotomy alone as an augmentation process seems to improve the outcome for both mother and the neonate without increasing the rate of infectious morbidity. It shortens labor and reduces CS and maternal morbidity, with no significant increase in intra-amniotic infection when performed within the first 12 hours of induction or at the start of oxytocin.75 Prolonged labor after early amniotomy is likely to increase the risk of chorioamnionitis and maternal morbidity. Balancing the benefits of early ARM and chorioamnionitis is therefore critical and combining this with oxytocin has been shown to result in maximum benefit. The optimal sequence is thus early ARM in conjunction with oxytocin augmentation, individualized as clinically indicated and not delayed solely to reduce infection risk.9,78,79

Overview of comparisons between guidelines

There are several guidelines on labor care and management. As highlighted in the discussions above, although there are in general agreements in most aspects, there are minor differences. These differences include the definition of the onset of active labor, the indications for augmentation, oxytocin titration/incremental steps, and when to stop oxytocin following the establishment of active labor. Other areas where there is variation in the recommendations include augmentation in women with prior CS and those with breech presentation in labor. Outcome of augmentation of labor

Overall, labor augmentation is effective in increasing the likelihood of vaginal delivery in cases of protracted or arrested labor, especially when adequate time is allowed for cervical changes. In nulliparous women, extending oxytocin augmentation to 8 hours can reduce the cesarean delivery rate from 35.5% to 18%, compared to a 4-hour limit, without adversely affecting neonatal outcomes.9,34,80 In multiparous women, vaginal delivery rates after 2 hours of oxytocin augmentation for active-phase arrest are as high as 91% and remain favorable (88%) even after 4 hours of augmentation, supporting a more liberal approach to duration before diagnosing labor arrest.9 Once effective uterine contractions are achieved, cervical dilation proceeds rapidly in both nulliparas and multiparas, with 95% progressing by 1 cm/h within 2 hours after 5 cm dilation.81

Observational data indicate that vaginal delivery rates after oxytocin augmentation in women undergoing a trial of labor after prior cesarean delivery are approximately 74%, with no increase in maternal deaths, uterine rupture, or hysterectomy in well-monitored settings.82 The timing of augmentation appears to affect outcomes. A French study showed that early augmentation of labor was associated with an increased CS rate, but this had no negative impact on neonatal outcomes.60 Maternal satisfaction scores are lower when augmentation is performed in the second stage, likely reflecting increased fatigue and higher intervention rates.83

Complications of labor augmentation

While augmentation of labor can be a valuable intervention to expedite labor, it is not without risk, especially when oxytocin is not carefully titrated and monitored. When used in complex clinical scenarios, it can lead to significant maternal and fetal complications.

Uterine tachysystole and hyperstimulation

Uterine tachysystole, defined as more than five contractions in 10 minutes averaged over 30 minutes, can lead to fetal compromise due to reduced uteroplacental perfusion.84 When it occurs, prompt recognition and timely intervention will minimize maternal and fetal risks. Continuous electronic monitoring of uterine activity and fetal heart rate is essential throughout oxytocin administration to detect tachysystole and associated fetal heart rate abnormalities. The first-line intervention is the immediate discontinuation of the oxytocin infusion, as the drug’s short half-life allows for rapid cessation of uterine stimulation and restoration of normal contraction patterns.84–86 Maternal repositioning, typically to the lateral position, is recommended to optimize uterine blood flow and fetal oxygenation. Supplemental oxygen may be administered to the mother if signs of fetal compromise are present. However, the evidence for benefit is limited.84–86

If tachysystole persists or is accompanied by non-reassuring fetal heart rate patterns, additional supportive measures may be necessary, including bolus intravenous fluids and, in select instances, the administration of acute tocolytics such as beta-adrenergic agonists (e.g., terbutaline, 0.5 mg administered subcutaneously) or oxytocin receptor antagonists (e.g., atosiban). Atosiban is given as an initial intravenous bolus injection of 6.75 mg over 1 minute (i.e., 0.9 mL of a 7.5 mg/mL solution) followed by a continuous high-loading infusion dose of 18 mg/h for 3 hours. Systematic reviews of randomized trials on these agents have shown a reduction in uterine activity and improved fetal heart rate tracings; however, the evidence for clinically meaningful improvements in neonatal outcomes is limited, and the decision to use tocolytics should be individualized.84,87

Uterine rupture

Uterine rupture is a rare but catastrophic complication of labor augmentation, particularly in women with a prior CS. In women without a prior uterine scar, the absolute risk remains very low but is still increased by labor augmentation with oxytocin.88 In women with one previous CS, the rate of uterine rupture is approximately 0.4% to 0.7% in those who labor spontaneously (with no augmentation), but this rises to 1% to 2% with oxytocin augmentation.89–91 Compared to spontaneous labor, oxytocin augmentation and prostaglandin use are associated with increased risk of uterine rupture. The RR of uterine rupture with oxytocin augmentation compared to spontaneous labor is approximately 2.2 (95% CI: 1.5–3.3), indicating more than a two-fold increase in risk.92 When compared to spontaneous labor, the RR for prostaglandin induction is even higher, with estimates around 6.0 (95% CI: 2.0–18.0), and an overall rate of 2.2% to 2.9%, reflecting a more substantial increase in the risk.69,92

Early recognition is critical, with signs such as abnormal fetal heart rate patterns (most commonly prolonged or recurrent late decelerations or bradycardia), loss of fetal station, abdominal pain, vaginal bleeding, and maternal hemodynamic instability.88,91 If uterine rupture is suspected, immediate cessation of oxytocin and rapid evaluation are essential, followed by emergency laparotomy and delivery if rupture is confirmed or highly suspected, with simultaneous maternal resuscitation and neonatal support.88,91

PPH

Prolonged second-stage labor, regardless of augmentation, is associated with increased risks of chorioamnionitis, uterine atony, and severe perineal lacerations, but not with significant increases in adverse neonatal outcomes such as low Apgar scores, NICU admission, or neonatal sepsis.9 Multiple cohort studies and meta-analyses have concluded that oxytocin augmentation is associated with a modest but statistically significant increase in the risk of PPH. A recent meta-analysis found a pooled odds ratio (OR) of 1.27 (95% CI: 1.05–1.53) for PPH among those receiving oxytocin augmentation compared with those who did not. The risk is further elevated with higher oxytocin doses and longer augmentation durations (≥ 4.5 hours), as well as independently by prolonged labor (≥ 16 hours).93 However, a large U.S. cohort study using advanced statistical modeling found no clinically significant association between oxytocin augmentation and PPH after controlling for labor pattern and confounders (adjusted OR: 1.02; 95% CI: 0.82–1.24).70,72

Operative vaginal delivery and CS

Some systematic reviews and large cohort studies report that labor augmentation is associated with increased rates of operative vaginal delivery and emergency CS.57 However, the magnitude of risk varies by population and clinical context. One meta-analysis found a reduced likelihood of operative vaginal birth with elective induction at 39 weeks (OR: 0.87; 95% CI: 0.79–0.97).94 A study in a low-resource setting reported increased operative delivery rates with oxytocin augmentation.71 When augmentation is performed in the second stage of labor, outcomes are less favorable than when it is performed in the active phase. It is associated with longer labor duration, an increased rate of operative delivery, and a higher frequency of perineotomies and perineal trauma.83

Neonatal complications

Augmentation may increase the risks of adverse neonatal outcomes, including low 5-minute Apgar scores (< 7), need for assisted ventilation, NICU admission, and neonatal death before discharge. In a large cohort, oxytocin augmentation was associated with an increased risk of bag-and-mask ventilation (adjusted RR: 2.1; 95% CI: 1.8–2.5), low Apgar score (adjusted RR: 1.65; 95% CI: 1.49–1.86), and neonatal death (adjusted RR: 1.93; 95% CI: 1.46–2.56).71 Other reported neonatal complications include bacterial sepsis, intracranial hemorrhage, hyperbilirubinemia, and neonatal seizures.71,94,95 Neonatal acid-base status may be suboptimal, with higher umbilical cord base deficits and lower pH as reported by Svärdby et al.83, although Apgar scores were not consistently affected.

Conclusion

Augmentation of labor, while commonplace, is not benign. It requires precise diagnosis of the indication, appropriate timing, and vigilant monitoring to balance the benefits and potential risks. As obstetric practice continues to evolve, there is a growing need to tailor augmentation strategies to the individual clinical scenario, avoiding routine protocols in favor of personalized, evidence-informed care. With greater adherence to updated guidelines and the generation of more robust data to inform decision-making in controversial areas, it is possible to optimize outcomes for both mother and baby while maintaining safety at the heart of intrapartum practice. Future research and policy directions

Despite significant advances in labor augmentation and management, several areas require further research to refine practice and reduce interhospital variation. The optimal oxytocin dose titration and maximum duration need clarification. Also, the safety of stopping oxytocin in established labor (e.g., a follow-up to CONDISOX trial59) requires more thorough examination, especially with respect to the potential of increasing morbidity (e.g., chorioamnionitis if labor is prolonged), and exploring the potential risks of chorioamnionitis with prolonging labor in the discontinuation group. Randomized blinded studies taking into consideration the various factors that may affect labor would generate robust data upon which firm practice recommendations can be made. It is also essential to develop standardized and universal definitions of second-stage dystocia. More studies should be undertaken to determine whether augmentation should be routinely offered to women undergoing planned vaginal breech delivery. Research should also elucidate the role of biomarkers or real-time algorithms in predicting labor progression/success of augmentation. The use of digital tools and artificial intelligence in cardiotocography interpretation and labor diagnostics is inevitable in the near future and requires a careful, phased introduction.
