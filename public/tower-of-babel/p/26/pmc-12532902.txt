Fine-needle aspiration cytology for the diagnosis of ewing’s sarcoma in bone: a case report
Authors: Yumeng Cai, Jiangang Gu, Xin Wei, Jun Wang, Jiuyuan Fang, Hongxi Zhang, Ke Lv, Zhilan Meng, Zhiyong Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEwing’s sarcoma (ES) is an aggressive small round cell tumor traditionally diagnosed through open biopsy. We present a systematically evaluated case suggesting that standardized ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC), when combined with immunohistochemical (IHC) and molecular analysis, may provide diagnostic reliability approaching that of open biopsy. A 26-year-old female presented with an insidiously developing left popliteal fossa mass. Ultrasound-guided FNAC demonstrated characteristic small round blue cells, with IHC showing diffuse positivity for CD99, FLI-1, and Bcl-2. Subsequent fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) analysis identified the EWSR1 gene rearrangement. The patient exhibited significant radiographic response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy after two cycles, as evidenced by MRI. Definitive surgical resection specimens similarly demonstrated EWSR1 rearrangement by FISH, corroborating the initial diagnosis. Following four adjuvant chemotherapy cycles, the patient achieved disease-free status at the last follow-up. This case highlights the potential utility of optimized FNAC specimen triage (incorporating smears, liquid-based cytology, and cell blocks) for rare tumors, enabling comprehensive ancillary testing while maintaining diagnostic accuracy and supporting timely therapeutic decision-making.Keywords: Fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC), Ewing’s sarcoma (ES), Cell block, EWSR1, Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)

Introduction

Ewing sarcoma (ES) is an aggressive small round cell tumor (SRCT) predominantly affecting children and young adults [1]. While histopathological confirmation through open biopsy or core needle biopsy (CNB) is currently considered the reference standard for SRCT diagnosis [2], the characteristic cytomorphologic features are well described [3, 4]. However, the diagnostic application of fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) has been limited by sampling constraints [5, 6]. Only limited cases of ES diagnosed primarily by FNAC with subsequent treatment guidance have been reported [6, 7]. We present a case of ES in a 26-year-old female where ultrasound-guided FNAC enabled diagnostic confirmation and permitted timely therapeutic intervention.

Case presentation

A 26-year-old woman presented to our orthopedic department with a gradually enlarging left popliteal fossa mass over 10 months. The patient had a previous medical history of bilateral ovarian borderline serous cystadenoma. Color Doppler ultrasound revealed a cystic-solid mass (6.7 × 6.2 × 4.3 cm) located in the muscular layer of the superolateral left popliteal fossa. Subsequently, ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration biopsy (FNAB) was performed, and specimens prepared for both Thinprep Cytologic Test (TCT) and cell block.

In TCT preparations, t he cellularity was moderate, with scattered tumor cells present, some of which were arranged in small clusters (Fig. 1a). The cells were small to medium in size and exhibited round to oval shapes. The cytoplasm was eosinophilic, with moderate to scant amounts, and displayed poorly defined cell borders. The nuclei were markedly enlarged, predominantly round to oval, although some demonstrated irregular contours, including eccentric or reniform nuclei. Partial nuclear hyperchromasia with coarse granular chromatin was noted, and prominent nucleoli were observed in some cells. Mitotic figures were identifiable, and the nuclear-to-cytoplasmic (N/C) ratio was increased (Fig. 1a). No other cell types were seen in the background.

Compared to the TCT preparations, the cell block sections demonstrated significantly higher tumor cellularity, with neoplastic cells arranged in poorly cohesive sheets. Occasional tumor cells formed rosette-like structures (Fig. 1b). Small foci of necrosis were identified. The background showed abundant thin-walled blood vessels surrounded by tumor cells. Under high-power magnification, the cell block sections demonstrated an admixture of two distinct cell populations. The first population comprised smaller cells with hyperchromatic nuclei showing chromatin condensation and scant cytoplasm, occasionally appearing as bare nuclei. The second population consisted of larger, paler cells with more abundant cytoplasm. These cells exhibited granular nuclear chromatin and displayed single or multiple discernible nucleoli (Fig. 1b).

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining of the cell block was positive for BCL-2 (Fig. 2a), FLI-1 (Fig. 2b), CD99 (Fig. 2c), SMARCA4, and INI-1. Fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) was performed on a section from the cell block using a break-apart probe for EWSR1 gene rearrangement. FISH showed split signals in approximately 30% of tumor cells, confirming the EWSR1 rearrangement (Fig. 2d). Therefore, based on the patient’s age, cytomorphological features, and ancillary test results, a diagnosis of ES was established.

a Cell block stained with BCL-2 showing diffuse nuclear staining. b FLI-1 staining with diffuse nuclear staining. c CD99 staining with diffuse membranous staining. d FISH from cell block using break-apart probe for EWSR1 gene rearrangement shows split signals in tumor nuclei

The patient underwent two cycles of neoadjuvant chemotherapy with a VDC/IE regimen (vincristine, doxorubicin, cyclophosphamide alternating with ifosfamide and etoposide). Comparative MRI before and after chemotherapy demonstrated a reduction in lesion volume at the left distal femur, indicating a favorable treatment response (Fig. 3). Subsequently, the patient underwent wide resection of the left distal femoral tumor with prosthetic knee reconstruction. Histopathological examination of the surgical specimen confirmed diffuse infiltration of tumor cells within the medullary cavity (Fig. 4a), arranged around small blood vessels, with nuclear shrinkage and a high N/C ratio (Fig. 4b). EWSR1 FISH analysis also demonstrated split signals in 30% of tumor cells, corroborating the initial diagnosis. The patient completed four additional cycles of adjuvant chemotherapy and remains disease-free at follow-up, with no evidence of tumor progression on imaging studies.

Discussion

This case illustrates a potential minimally invasive diagnosis approach for ES using ultrasound-guided FNAC in conjunction with IHC and FISH analysis. While FISH analysis revealed EWSR1 gene break-apart signals without partner gene identification, the composite assessment of patient's age, clinical presentation, pathological characteristics, and treatment response remained consistent with classical ES. To our knowledge, this represents one of the few reported cases where ES was diagnosed and managed through FNAC with ancillary techniques, thereby circumventing open biopsy in this specific case [7–9]. Although histopathological examination remains standard for ES diagnosis, our case suggests that FNAC, when integrated with ancillary techniques, may achieve comparable diagnostic reliability in selected cases.

The cytomorphologic features observed in our case aligned with classic cytologic features of ES [3, 10]. The TCT preparations demonstrated characteristic small round blue tumor cells with increased N/C ratios, scant cytoplasm, and coarse chromatin - features that were essential for establishing the initial diagnostic consideration of ES. Of particular significance was the identification of the dual cell population in cell block sections: the smaller hyperchromatic cells representing typical ES morphology, while the larger cells with open chromatin likely reflect degenerative changes or regional heterogeneity, a phenomenon documented in most of ES cases [10]. Furthermore, the superior architectural preservation in cell block sections enabled clear identification of diagnostically critical features, including pseudorosette formation, necrotic foci, and perivascular tumor infiltration, while simultaneously providing optimal material for ancillary studies.

The differential diagnosis of SRCT in bone and soft tissue includes undifferentiated small round cell sarcomas (ES and Ewing-like sarcoma), lymphoma, small cell carcinoma, and other sarcomas (such as rhabdomyosarcoma and desmoplastic small round cell tumor) [11]. Lymphomas typically lack cohesive cell clusters and “rosette-like” structures, while small cell carcinomas often exhibit more prominent nuclear molding and necrosis. Positive expression of BCL-2, SMARCA4 and INI-1 helped exclude other sarcomas such as rhabdomyosarcoma or desmoplastic small round cell tumor. It is noteworthy that the molecular characteristics of ES require EWSR1 gene rearrangement with ETS family genes (typically FLI1 or ERG), whereas rearrangement with non-ETS family genes should be diagnosed as Ewing-like sarcoma. The latter typically occurs in older patients with a wide age range, demonstrates more diverse and atypical pathological features, shows poorer response to conventional ES chemotherapy regimens, and carries higher risks of recurrence and metastasis [12]. In our diagnostic process, we carefully considered the patient’s age. This 26-year-old female patient falls within the characteristic age range for classical ES. Furthermore, her excellent response to standard VDC/IE chemotherapy also supports the diagnosis of classical ES.

FNAC has been historically underutilized in ES diagnosis due to concerns about sampling adequacy and diagnostic certainty. However, emerging evidence suggests that advances in IHC and molecular techniques may enhance its diagnostic utility [13]. In this case, the combination of FNAC with cell block preparation and ancillary techniques (including IHC and FISH) provided a minimally invasive diagnostic approach. When supported by characteristic clinical and imaging findings, FNAC may represent a potential first-line diagnostic option in selected cases. The FNAC-based diagnostic approach in our case allowed prompt initiation of neoadjuvant chemotherapy, which appeared to contribute to the observed treatment response.

We present a representative case of ES diagnosed through FNAC combined with IHC and molecular testing, thereby avoiding open biopsy in this instance. Our findings suggest that for rare tumors, when FNA yields sufficient material, systematic sample allocation incorporating cell block preparation alongside conventional smears and liquid-based cytology (e.g., TCT)—may facilitate comprehensive ancillary testing (IHC and molecular studies), potentially enabling accurate diagnosis. This approach appears to reduce patient trauma while potentially streamlining therapeutic decision-making in select cases.

However, several important limitations should be acknowledged. First, as a single-case report, our findings require validation in larger prospective studies to assess generalizability. Second, the diagnostic success may reflect selection bias, given the favorable anatomical characteristics (superficial, large size) of the popliteal mass in this case. Third, while FISH confirmed EWSR1 rearrangement, the unidentified partner gene introduces residual diagnostic uncertainty. Finally, longer-term follow-up data would be valuable to fully evaluate the clinical outcomes associated with this diagnostic pathway.

Notwithstanding these limitations, our case provides preliminary evidence that a meticulously implemented FNAC-based diagnostic protocol (incorporating cytomorphology, IHC, and molecular testing) can achieve diagnostic accuracy comparable to traditional biopsy in carefully selected ES cases, while reducing invasive procedural risks and expediting therapeutic intervention.
