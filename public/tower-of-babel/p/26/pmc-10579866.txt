A rare, likely pathogenic variant causing Leber's hereditary optic neuropathy in three-generation females of an African-American family
Authors: Yanjun Chen, Elizabeth R Kellom
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeWe report a rare, likely pathogenic variant gene causing Leber's hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON) in three-generation female members of an African-American family.ObservationsThe granddaughter and mother presented with a subacute, painless visual loss in both eyes at age 10 and 42 years to legal blindness. The maternal grandmother presented with a gradual onset of moderate visual loss at age 60. The mother and grandmother reported a history of bariatric surgery and subsequent vitamin deficiencies. All three patients shared similar Optical Coherent Tomography (OCT) findings of profound thinning of ganglion cell complex (GCC) and relatively preserved peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer thickness (pRNFL). Nuclear and mitochondrial DNA sequencing identified a 14596A > T likely pathogenic variant, p.(Ile26Met), in the MT-ND6 gene, with 100% homoplasmy in the granddaughter and mother and 65% heteroplasmy in the grandmother. The mother and grandmother were treated with idebenone in addition to vitamin supplements, with a slight improvement in their vision.Conclusions and ImportanceOur patients’ presentation stresses the importance of including LHON in the differential diagnosis in females presenting with unexplained bilateral, painless, severe visual loss. The OCT finding of profound GCC thinning with relatively preserved pRNFL thickness can be a red flag for LHON. A collaboration with genetic specialists to utilize expanded gene sequencing may greatly enhance our ability to identify rare pathogenic variants.Keywords: Leber's hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON), 14596A>T variant, Females, Nutritional deficiency

Introduction

Leber's hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON) is a maternally inherited hereditary optic neuropathy due to pathogenic variants in the mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) in Complex I, leading to defects in the electron transport chain with resulting degeneration and apoptosis of retinal ganglion cells and optic nerves. Patients usually present with bilateral, painless, profound visual loss during their teenage years and young adulthood, with males being affected four to five times more frequently than females. Three common mtDNA pathogenic variants (11778G > A, 14484T > C, and 3460G > A) account for 90–95% of LHON.1 In this report, we presented a rare, likely pathogenic variant causing LHON, presenting with bilateral visual loss among three-generation female members in an African American family.

Findings

Case 1. The granddaughter of the family first presented at age ten years with profound, painless visual loss in both eyes for five months. The visual loss was “fast-paced but not overnight.” She has also had mild intermittent tremors and “neuropathy” with numbness and tingling in her hands and feet, starting around the time of the visual loss. The patient was a product of full-term pregnancy and uneventful delivery; she met all developmental milestones. She was the only child and lived with her mother. The family history was unremarkable except for a maternal uncle with undefined “vision issues.” The eye examination showed visual acuity of 4/200 and 20/600 and an Ishihara color plate of 0/11 and 1/11 in the right and left eye, respectively. The visual fields were full on confrontation. The pupil size was 7 mm in the dark and 6 mm in the light; there was no relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD) on the swinging flashlight test. The slit lamp and dilated fundus exam showed diffuse disc pallor in both eyes and were otherwise unremarkable (Fig. 1, top). Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT) noted a peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer thickness (pRNFL) of 84 and 88 μm, and ganglion cell complex (GCC) thickness of 51 and 54 μm in the right and left eye, respectively (Fig. 2, left). An MRI of the brain and orbit (performed a few months following the visual loss) showed no contrast enhancement of the optic nerves or T2 hyperintensity subcortical white matter lesions. Extensive systemic inflammatory and infectious workups were unremarkable, including complete blood count with differential, complete metabolic panel, folic acid, B12, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, c-reactive protein, antinuclear antibody (ANA), lupus anticoagulant, angiotensin converting enzyme (ACE), antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody, SSA/SSB, serum and urine protein electrophoresis, Bartonella Hanselae antibody, rapid plasma reagin (RPR), Borrelia Burgdorferi antibody, anti-aquaporin-4 antibody, and heavy metal (arsenic, cadmium, lead, and mercury). The spinal fluid analysis showed RBC 0, WBC 0, protein 18.1 mg/dl, glucose 52 mg/dl, negative ACE, ANA, venereal disease research lab (VDRL), microbiology (including acid-fast bacterium), and cytology. Cancer-associated retinopathy/optic neuropathy autoantibody test (Oregon Health Science University) reported positive autoantibodies against 24-kDa (HSP27), 36-kDa (GADPH), 40-kDa (aldolase), and 62-kDa protein. A chest CT showed no evidence of sarcoidosis. The patient then received intravenous immunoglobulin treatment elsewhere for presumed autoimmune optic neuropathy without significant improvement of vision.

Case 2. Seven years later, the mother, then a 42-year-old African American female, presented with a progressive visual decline over a few months. Her past medical history was significant for a gastric-banding bariatric surgery fifteen months prior. She had been taking multivitamins, vitamin D, and B12 supplements following the bariatric surgery. She has had intermittent tremors since childhood. The patient worked as an office clerk and reported no history of tobacco or heavy alcohol use, travel overseas, or exposure to heavy metals or chemicals. Other than her daughter (Case 1) and her brother (unclear history), no other known case of visual loss in the extended family. On the eye examination, she had a visual acuity of 20/300 and 20/250 and an Ishihara color plate of 1/11 and 1/11 in the right and left eye. The pupil size was 4 mm in the dark and 3 mm in light in both eyes without RAPD. A dilated fundus exam noted a temporal pallor of the optic disc in both eyes (Fig. 1, bottom). The automated perimetry testing showed paracentral arcuate defects in both eyes (Fig. 3, Case 2, top). OCT measured a peripapillary RNFL thickness of 125 and 138 μm, and GCC thickness of 62 and 62 μm in the right and left eye, respectively (Fig. 2, middle). An MRI of the head and orbit was unremarkable for compressive or inflammatory optic neuropathy. The extensive laboratory workup (similar panel as in Case 1) noted a low folate level of 4.4 (≥7.0 ng/ml) and B2 of 2 (5–50 nmol/L). Vitamin A, B1, B6, and B12 levels were within normal limits. She was also found to have an M-spike of 0.11 g/dl on serum protein electrophoresis and was diagnosed with low-grade IgG monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance by hematology. The etiology of the visual loss was felt to be due to nutritional deficiency. The patient was started with folate and B2 supplement with normalization of the levels on repeated testing. The visual field defects deteriorated further six months following the presentation, then improved in the 18-month follow-up (Fig. 3, Case 2, middle and bottom). The visual acuity remained unchanged.

Automated perimetry testing for Case 2 and 3. The patient in Case 2 first presented with paracentral arcuate defects, with an interval progression of visual field defects in the first six months, then showed an improvement in 18-month follow-up. The patient in Case 3 presented with patchy defects involving the center, which improved in one year.

Case 3. Six months following the mother's visit, the grandmother, an African American female at age 60, visited the clinic with a 4-month history of “increasing fuzziness” in her vision. The grandmother endorsed a European heritage without further details, as she was adopted shortly after birth. Similarly, the grandmother had a prior history of gastric bypass surgery seventeen years ago; she was taking regular vitamin supplements, including B12, vitamin D, and calcium. She also endorsed a history of mild intermittent tremors since childhood and “neuropathy” with numbness and tingling in her hands and feet. Her eye exam noted visual acuity of 20/40 and 20/50 and Ishihara plate of 9/11 and 9/11 in the right and left eye, respectively. The pupil size was 5 mm in the dark and 4 mm in light without RAPD. The slit lamp exam noted mild nuclear sclerosis and cortical cataract in both eyes. The dilated fundus exam noted a mild temporal pallor of the optic disc and mild macular drusen in both eyes. The automated perimetry testing showed patchy defects that involved the central field in both eyes but with high fixation loss and false negatives (Fig. 3, Case 3, top). OCT measured a peripapillary RNFL thickness of 80 and 80 μm, and GCC of 54 and 57 μm in the right and left eye, respectively (Fig. 2, right). An MRI of the head and orbit was unrevealing for compressive or inflammatory lesions on the optic nerves; there were a few punctate nonspecific T2 hyperintensity subcortical white matter lesions, suggesting chronic small vessel ischemic changes. The laboratory workup noted a low B2 of 5 nmol/l (5–50), B6 of 16.6 nmol/l (20.0–125.0), and B12 of 274 pg/ml (213–816). She was treated with B2, B6, and B12 supplements with normalization of the levels. The 1-year follow-up exam showed improvements in her vision, with visual acuity of 20/40 and 20/30, Ishihara plate of 10/11 and 10/11 in the right and left eye, and less prominent visual field defects on automated perimetry testing (Fig. 3, Case 3, bottom).

Upon the grandmother's presentation, hereditary optic neuropathy was suspected, given a similar presentation in three generations within the family (Fig. 4). Also, a thorough review of family history revealed vision problems in the mother's (Case 2) only brother when he started having trouble with color perception in early adolescence and was visually disabled at age 40+. For Case 2, a multi-gene optic atrophy panel, including 31 nuclear genes and full mtDNA sequencing, was conducted at Blueprint Genetics, a Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA)-certified laboratory. Results identified a likely pathogenic variant in the MT-ND6 gene, consistent with a diagnosis of LHON. The genetic analysis did not reveal other pathogenic variants in genes responsible for optic atrophy/optic neuropathy, such as OPA1, OPA3, POLG, WFS1, UCHL1, NR2F1, MFN2, DNM1L, ATAD3A, or AFG3L2. Targeted testing was then performed for Case 1 and 3. The granddaughter and mother were both 100% homoplasmic in saliva for the 14596A > T variant, p.(Ile26Met), in the MT-ND6 gene, and the grandmother had 65% heteroplasmy in the blood (collected due to dry mouth). The mother and grandmother were both treated with idebenone (300 mg three times daily) in addition to vitamin supplements. The granddaughter was not treated with idebenone due to the delayed diagnosis.

Discussion

In this cohort of three cases of LHON among a three-generation African American family, we identified a rare mitochondrial variant, 14596A > T, p.(Ile26Met), in the MT-ND6 gene. With the more powerful and rapid gene sequencing technology, more than eighteen allelic variants have been added to the three primary pathogenic mtDNA pathogenic variants.2 The 14596A > T variant has been identified in thirteen individuals from a kindred of a 7-generation LHON-dystonia Dutch family.3 A muscle biopsy noted respiratory chain defects, suggesting the pathophysiologic role of the variant in the development of optic neuropathy and dystonia. All three patients in our cohort reported extra-ocular symptoms, intermittent tremors, and the granddaughter and grandmother reported neuropathic symptoms of numbness and tingling of extremities. The association between these extra-ocular neurologic presentations with the pathologic gene has yet to be elucidated.

The clinical presentation in our cohort shared a few unique features. First, the mother and grandmother both had a history of bariatric surgery with resulting nutritional deficiency, which likely aggravated the underlying mitochondrial dysfunction and expedited the manifestation of the disease.4,5 After being treated with nutritional supplements and idebenone, they both noticed an improvement in vision subjectively as well as on automated perimetry testing, emphasizing the benefit of correcting environmental factors and supplementing idebenone in the visual outcome in LHON.6 Also, the varying mtDNA pathogenic variant burden could have contributed to the varying degrees of visual loss in our patients. However, it remains puzzling why the visual loss showed a progressively earlier onset and increased severity in successive generations, with the granddaughter presenting with the most severe visual loss at the earliest age without any environmental triggers. Anticipation is generally regarded as a feature of trinucleotide repeat expansion where there is a clear molecular mechanism for the increased severity of phenotype; it has not been reported in LHON.1 Therefore, the phenotypical variation demonstrated in our cohort may be coincidental rather than a reflection of anticipation. Second, the cohort showed an unusual female preponderance. LHON is generally regarded as affecting males (80–90% of cases), with a manifestation of optic neuropathy in 50% and 10–15% of male and female carriers, respectively.5,6 The apparent female predominance in our cohort could have been attributed to the lack of male relatives in the family. It is of note that the only male in the family, the mother's brother, also had vision loss, thus could potentially share a similar genetic variant. Unfortunately, we were unable to obtain genetic testing on him to confirm. The female preponderance shown in our cohort emphasizes the importance of considering LHON as a potential diagnosis in females presenting with unexplained bilateral, severe, painless visual loss. Another clinical feature that is worthwhile to mention is the OCT findings of profound GCC thinning but relatively preserved RNFL thickness in all three cases, as reported by Asanad et al.7 Therefore, profound GCC thinning with “normal” RNFL thickness may serve as a red flag for LHON. None of our patients demonstrated circumpapillary telangiectasia, a disc appearance characteristic for LHON,8 though all patients presented months following the onset of the visual loss when they'd likely developed optic atrophy. Lastly, LHON is rarely reported among African-Americans9,10 despite diverse ethnic and geographic constituents, including northern European, Italian, Asian, and Brazilian populations.2,11 The apparent racial disparity of LHON could be related to access to health care or genetic predisposition. Further evidence is required to elucidate such a matter.

Conclusions

Although male-dominant, LHON can affect females with resulting bilateral, painless, severe visual loss, often present later and aggravated by nutritional or toxic factors that stress the mitochondrial function. The OCT finding of profound GCC thinning with relatively preserved pRNFL thickness in the setting of unexplained visual loss should raise concern for mitochondrial optic neuropathy. With the rapid advance in genetic medicine, a collaboration with genetic counselors may greatly enhance our ability to identify rare pathogenic variants.
