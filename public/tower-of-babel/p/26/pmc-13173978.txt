Malaria at the margins: climatic extremes, migration, and urban informal settlement risk in South Africa
Authors: Sailas Hussein Matamanda, Walter Musakwa, Armstrong Dzomba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalaria remains a significant public health issue in sub‑Saharan Africa, influenced by climate variability, population movement and urbanisation. In South Africa, efforts to eliminate malaria operate within a dynamic environment where climatic fluctuations and population movements complicate risk patterns. However, existing approaches to analyzing climate-malaria relationships, often rely on seasonal averages, ignoring the potential influence of rare climatic extremes and migration flows. This study investigates how climatic extremes, climatic variability, and migration flows interact to shape malaria risk and inform adaptive surveillance and control strategies.MethodsClimate data (monthly minimum/maximum temperature, rainfall and relative humidity) for Limpopo, Mpumalanga and KwaZulu‑Natal (2000–2024) were obtained from the South African Weather Service. Laboratory‑confirmed malaria cases were sourced from provincial surveillance reports, and in‑migration data into Thembelihle and selected parts of Soweto (2017–2024) were obtained from the Soweto and Thembelihle Health and Demographic Surveillance System (SaT‑HDSS). Seasonal climate trajectories were evaluated using Ordinary Least Squares regressions. Climatic seasonality was assessed using diagnostic metrics, humidity plateaus, rainfall extremes and thermal season‑length indices. Shock‑year dynamics (KwaZulu‑Natal 2022 floods; Limpopo 2017 Cyclone Dineo) were analysed using monthly anomalies against 2000–2010 climatological envelopes. Pearson correlations examined same‑year and lag‑1 climate-malaria associations. Climate-malaria-migration co‑movement was assessed using min–max‑normalised rainfall, humidity, temperature, incidence and migration data (0–1). Malaria tail events were defined as years at or above the provincial 90th percentile. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), Version 30; p < 0.05 was considered significant.ResultsClimate trends across the three endemic provinces showed consistent minimum-temperature warming, declining warm-season humidity, and rainfall patterns increasingly dominated by short-duration extremes. Province-specific seasonality diagnostics revealed that KwaZulu-Natal experienced frequent multi-month humidity plateaus, Limpopo showed pronounced lagged hydrological effects following extreme rainfall events, and Mpumalanga displayed prolonged warm-season durations in recent years. Shock-year analyses (KwaZulu-Natal 2022 floods; Limpopo 2017 Cyclone Dineo) demonstrated compound climatic anomalies, combinations of intense rainfall, sustained humidity, and sub-seasonal cooling that extended environmental suitability beyond expected seasonal boundaries.Same-year humidity was positively associated with malaria incidence in KwaZulu-Natal and Mpumalanga, whereas lag-1 rainfall showed a significant relationship with malaria in Limpopo, reflecting hydrological carry-over effects. Following the April–May 2022 floods, migration into Thembelihle increased markedly, and a co-movement analysis indicated temporal alignment between malaria tail years, elevated rainfall, higher minimum temperatures, and increased migration during climatologically disrupted periods.ConclusionsClimatic extremes and post‑shock mobility reorganise malaria risk in South Africa. Tail‑aware, lag‑sensitive and mobility‑integrated surveillance is essential for adaptive elimination planning.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-026-05907-y.Keywords: Malaria, Climate variability, Surveillance, Elimination strategies, South Africa

Background

Malaria continues to be a critical public health concern globally, despite evidence of decreasing incidence and mortality. Its burden is concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa and parts of Asia, underpinned by complex interactions between parasite biology, vector ecology, human behaviour, and health system capacity [1, 2]. Global reports continue to underscore uneven progress in malaria control. While many countries have achieved substantial gains, recent reversals have emerged due to entrenched obstacles including drug and insecticide resistance, climate variability, funding constraints, and operational gaps in surveillance [3, 4]. These realities compel a renewed focus on context-specific drivers of risk and on how shifting environmental systems reshape transmission dynamics.

Recently, a considerable literature has shown that climate variability, particularly changes in temperature, rainfall, and humidity, directly alters malaria transmission potential. Warmer conditions can accelerate parasite development within mosquitoes and shorten gonotrophic cycles, while rainfall and humidity modulate vector survival and the availability of aquatic breeding sites [5, 6]. In diverse settings, climatic anomalies have been tied to increased caseloads. In the Western Ugandan highlands, severe flooding elevated village-level risk by approximately 30% [7]. In the southern regions where malaria occurs in the Americas, both minimum and maximum temperatures and humidity have been implicated in case dynamics [8]. These findings echo long-standing research from the East African highlands, where climate variability has precipitated epidemics [9].

Even as climate opens and closes windows of transmission, programmatic and biological constraints sustain vulnerability. The withdrawal or weakening of key strategies, particularly indoor residual spraying (IRS) and long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs), has contributed to resurgences in several regions. Additionally, the spread of antimalarial drug resistance has exacerbated this issue [11, 12]. Historical and contemporary experiences in Africa and South Asia illustrate how resistance to chloroquine and other therapies can undo gains when pharmacological efficacy erodes and case management falters [10–12]. These headwinds are not independent of climate pressures. Shocks that expand vector niches or amplify transmission can expose weaknesses in coverage, logistics, and clinical suspicion, especially in settings where malaria is not routinely anticipated.

Urbanization adds a further layer to the risk landscape. As cities in sub-Saharan Africa grow rapidly, unplanned development and inequities concentrate exposure among populations with limited access to prevention and healthcare [4, 13]. Informal settlements, often located close to wetlands, marshes, and riparian vegetation, provide ideal conditions for Anopheles breeding. Poor drainage and sanitation create lasting pools of stagnant water that serve as larval habitats [14, 15]. Evidence from Dar es Salaam and other African cities shows that micro-environmental features, urban agriculture, vegetation density, and small water bodies, can create intra-urban hotspots where parasite rates cluster spatially [13, 14]. In such contexts, high population density increases human-vector contact, while lower socioeconomic status correlates with reduced access to timely diagnosis, protective tools, and definitive treatment [16, 17]. The urban malaria problem is therefore not simply a matter of imported infections; it reflects how city form, services, and livelihoods modulate receptivity and vulnerability.

South Africa offers a crucial test bed for these intersecting dynamics. The national malaria program operates within a formal elimination strategy and vector control framework that targets transmission in the endemic provinces, while strengthening surveillance and response systems nationwide [4, 18]. Within the endemic belt, local ecological and climatic factors significantly influence malaria risk. In KwaZulu Natal, precipitation and vegetation indices are associated with transmission, showing the highest risk in the northeast [19]. Additionally, the onset of rainfall in Limpopo has been connected to a "malaria season," during which sustained increases in cases are observed over the following months [20]. Detailed analyses in Mpumalanga further demonstrate the influence of daily climate variables on both Anopheles abundance and case dynamics [21, 22]. These findings align with climate-informed modelling efforts that identify temperature ranges of 20–30 °C and specific rainfall patterns as conducive to transmission and potential resurgence [23]

At the same time, South Africa’s recent experience with climatic shock events highlights the need for event-aware malaria preparedness beyond typical seasonal expectations. The April–May 2022 floods in KwaZulu Natal caused extensive humanitarian and infrastructural disruptions [24]. Additionally, earlier cyclonic events, such as Cyclone Dineo in 2017, established conditions that were conducive to vector proliferation and transmission. Such episodes raise practical questions: how do short bursts of extreme rainfall or sustained humidity anomalies reorganize risk in receiving geographies, and how quickly can surveillance and control adapt? More broadly, climate shocks may interact with mobility in ways that are not yet captured in routine systems.

A parallel concern resides in non-endemic urban provinces, most notably Gauteng, where odyssean malaria has been documented. These locally acquired infections are generally considered to result from mosquitoes inadvertently translocated via road, rail, air, or freight, often from endemic regions [25]. Clinically, such cases are consequential because malaria is not routinely suspected in non-endemic urban settings, leading to delayed diagnosis and severe presentations [25]. High volumes of inter-provincial traffic amplify the probability of vector introduction, while urban environmental conditions such as drainage, housing quality, and access to healthcare, shape receptivity differently from endemic areas.

Across the literature, a common limitation persists: reliance on seasonal averages or annual aggregates that may obscure the influence of rare climatic extremes and lagged hydrological effects on transmission [19, 21]. While the ecological roles of rainfall, temperature, and humidity are well established [5, 6], it remains unclear whether extreme months and their lags play a decisive role in structuring interannual variability across South Africa’s endemic belt. Similarly, integrated research that connects shock-timed migration flows into urban informal settlements with malaria preparedness in non-endemic areas is limited [4, 13]. Existing approaches have not fully examined whether and how climatic extremes and human mobility interact to influence malaria risk, nor have they translated such insights into operational triggers for elimination strategies.

To address this gap, the present study examines the joint influence of climate variability and human mobility on malaria risk within South Africa’s endemic belt and at urban receivers. Specifically, we pose the following research question: How do climatic extremes and variability influence malaria transmission dynamics and interact with human mobility patterns, particularly migration into urban informal settlements?

Methods

Study area

This study focuses on the three provinces in South Africa where malaria is endemic: Limpopo, KwaZulu-Natal, and Mpumalanga (Fig. 1). These regions represent the core of the country’s residual malaria burden and share ecological and socio-economic characteristics that influence transmission dynamics. Limpopo Province, located in the northern region of South Africa, has a population of 6572720 according to the 2022 national census [26]. Unemployment rates range between 40 and 50%, and the province comprises five districts. Malaria transmission is particularly high in the Vhembe district [27].

Map of South Africa’s malaria endemic belt

KwaZulu-Natal is situated along the eastern seaboard. Home to an estimated 12.4 million people, it ranks as the second most populous province in South Africa. [28]. It also has the largest child population in the country and a high proportion of rural inhabitants, many of whom rely heavily on social grants [29]. The province includes multiple districts with malaria risk primarily confined to the uMkhanyakude District [18].

Mpumalanga, in the northeast of South Africa, experiences malaria transmission mainly in the Ehlanzeni district, which borders Mozambique and Eswatini [30, 31]. The province has an estimated population of 5 143 324 [26]. It also has high youth unemployment, with only 12% of women and 38% of men employed [32].

The study also has a secondary emphasis on urban informal settlements in non-endemic provinces, particularly Soweto and Thembelihle in Gauteng (black dot) (Fig. 1), which serve as urban receivers for migration-driven risk. Thembelihle is in the southern region of Johannesburg (26.3368° S latitude, 27.8634° E), in Soweto.

Data sources

The data pertaining to the average daily maximum and minimum temperatures measured in degrees Celsius, total monthly rainfall recorded in millimetres, and relative humidity expressed as a percentage were acquired for KwaZulu-Natal, Limpopo, and Mpumalanga, the endemic provinces, during 2000–2024. This information, sourced from the South African Weather Service (SAWS), was collected from 12 weather stations, with four stations located in each province.

Migration data were provided by the Soweto and Thembelihle Health and Demographic Surveillance System (SaT-HDSS). The dataset comprised anonymized micro-records of in-migration into selected suburbs in Soweto, and Thembelihle from the endemic provinces between 2017 and 2024, aggregated by year, origin province, and migration reason. Migration data are collected through routine household surveillance rounds conducted by SaT-HDSS fieldworkers, who update demographic events, including in-migration, directly from households.

Annual malaria case counts for Limpopo, Mpumalanga, and KwaZulu-Natal for the period 2000–2024 were obtained from publicly available provincial surveillance reports published by South Africa’s Department of Health. These reports document laboratory-confirmed malaria cases, including those verified through microscopy and/or rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs). The provincial malaria case counts include all confirmed malaria infections irrespective of migration status.

Approach

The study’s data was analysed using IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), Version 30. Monthly rainfall was summed to annual totals (mm·year⁻1) to reflect water availability/surface pooling potential; monthly relative humidity was averaged to annual means (%·year⁻1) to represent sustained ambient moisture supportive of anopheline survival. Where months were missing, annual aggregates were computed over available months; no interpolation, smoothing, or detrending was applied to preserve signals carried from extreme months.

Seasonal trajectories

To assess the seasonal evolution and long-term directional tendencies of climatic conditions relevant to malaria receptivity, we constructed seasonal climate trajectories for Limpopo, Mpumalanga, and KwaZulu-Natal using monthly maximum temperature, minimum temperature, relative humidity, and rainfall for 2000–2024. Climate variables were aggregated into four standard climatological seasons: summer (Dec-Feb), autumn (Mar-May), winter (Jun-Aug), and spring (Sep-Nov) [33, 34], with seasonal means calculated for temperatures and humidity and seasonal totals for rainfall. For each climate variable and season, we generated seasonal time-series curves for the three provinces, displaying annual values relative to a common long-run seasonal mean red. To quantify directional trends, we fitted Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regressions of seasonal climate values against year for each province-season combination and evaluated trend significance using two-sided t-tests on slope coefficients [35, 36]. The resulting p-values were also calculated providing a representation of statistically significant seasonal climate trends.

To characterise climatic seasonality relevant to malaria receptivity in KwaZulu-Natal, Limpopo, and Mpumalanga, we developed three diagnostic metrics: humidity plateaus, rainfall extremes, and thermal season-length indices, using monthly temperature, humidity, and rainfall observations for 2000–2024 [21, 23]. A baseline climatology (2000–2010) was first established by computing month-specific percentiles for each province: the 75th percentile for relative humidity (RH₇₅) [37], the 90th percentile for rainfall (R₉₀) [38], and the 25th and 75th percentiles for mean temperature (T₂₅, T₇₅) [39], the latter derived from monthly minimum and maximum temperatures. These thresholds allowed anomalous conditions to be detected relative to each month’s expected climate rather than annual averages. Humidity plateaus were then identified by evaluating each month each year against its RH₇₅ threshold and mapping runs of consecutive above-baseline months. These percentile thresholds were selected because they mark meaningful departures from normal climatic conditions that are known to influence malaria transmission [40]. Higher-than-usual humidity and rainfall create favourable environments for mosquito survival and breeding, while mid-range temperature conditions support parasite development within the vector [41]. Using these percentiles, therefore, allows us to identify periods when climate conditions shift into ranges associated with increased malaria receptivity. For each year, we extracted diagnostic indicators including the longest uninterrupted humidity run, the number of plateau events lasting ≥ 2 months, and the presence of persistent multi-season plateaus lasting ≥ 3 months. These metrics were summarised using province-specific time-series diagrams to visualise temporal variation in humidity persistence. The malaria time series was constructed from these annual provincial totals by arranging them sequentially from 2000 to 2024, as the surveillance system reports malaria incidence at the annual rather than monthly scale.

Robust trend detection and non-parametric validation of seasonal climate trajectories

To strengthen inference and address potential sensitivity of Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) trend estimation to non-normality, heteroscedasticity, and episodic extremes, seasonal climate trajectories were additionally evaluated using non-parametric trend detection techniques. The Mann–Kendall (MK) test was applied to each seasonal time series of minimum temperature, maximum temperature, rainfall, and relative humidity for each province to assess the presence of monotonic trends independent of distributional assumptions. Statistical significance was evaluated at α = 0.05 and α = 0.01.

Where significant trends were detected, the magnitude of change was estimated using the Theil-Sen slope estimator, which provides a robust median-based estimate of trend magnitude and is less sensitive to outliers than OLS. The MK and Theil-Sen analyses were conducted on the same seasonal aggregates used for OLS trajectory construction, ensuring direct comparability between parametric and non-parametric results.

Climatic extremes and shock years

To characterise climatic anomalies during major shock years, we constructed monthly time-series diagrams for rainfall (mm), relative humidity (%), and mean temperature (Tmax+Tmin)/2 using province-level datasets for KwaZulu-Natal and Limpopo covering 2000–2024. For each province, we developed a baseline climatology for the period 2000–2010 and computed month-specific interquartile ranges (25th-75th percentile bands) together with monthly means to provide a robust reference envelope against which anomalous conditions could be assessed. These baseline distributions enabled the identification of deviations associated with extreme climatic events, such as floods, cyclones, and anomalously wet or humid seasons, by visually contrasting observed monthly values with the expected climatological range. The resulting plots offered a fine-scale depiction of climatic disruptions temporally aligned with documented malaria outbreak years and other known environmental shocks.

Migration and correlation analysis

In-migration entries into selected suburbs in Soweto and Thembelihle from the endemic provinces were aggregated by year, province of origin, and reason for migration for the period 2017–2024. A clustered bar chart was used to visualize temporal patterns. The analysis was descriptive in nature, and no inferential statistical tests were applied. The primary objective was to highlight absolute changes in migration flows and detect potential spikes following major climatic events. Migration events were aggregated by calendar month and year based on recorded visit dates, with all migration reasons pooled to describe overall mobility patterns.

Annual-to-annual associations between annual malaria counts and climatic variables (rainfall, temperature and relative humidity), were assessed using Pearson product moment correlation coefficients for each province over the period 2000–2024. Same-year correlations were computed for all variables to capture contemporaneous relationships, while lag-1 correlations were calculated for rainfall and minimum temperature to explore delayed effects related to habitat persistence and nocturnal thermal conditions. A p value < 0.05 was considered significant. This was done using the student t-test.

Climate-malaria-migration co-movement analysis (2000–2024)

To examine annual co-movement between climatic conditions, malaria incidence, and population mobility, we constructed province-specific normalised time series for KwaZulu-Natal, Limpopo, and Mpumalanga covering the period 2000–2024. Annual rainfall, mean relative humidity, mean maximum temperature, mean minimum temperature, annual malaria cases, and annual in-migration counts were used in the analysis. Migration data were available from 2017 onward and were incorporated into the series without extrapolation for earlier years.

Normalisation was performed independently for each province to preserve region-specific variability while enabling direct visual comparison across variables with different units and ranges. The transformed series, therefore, represent relative rank and magnitude rather than absolute values.

Malaria tail events were defined as years in which annual malaria incidence reached or exceeded the provincial 90th percentile of the full 200–2024 distribution. These years were flagged and visually highlighted on the co-movement plots to identify periods of unusually high transmission relative to each province’s historical baseline.

The resulting co-movement visualisations (Figs. 9a-c) were designed as exploratory, descriptive tools to assess directional alignment and timing, rather than to imply causality or point-wise agreement across variables. The climate, malaria, and migration series exhibit substantial interannual variability, particularly for variables influenced by episodic extremes (e.g., rainfall) or by limited temporal coverage (e.g., migration). As a result, the normalised annual series may appear ‘noisy’ when viewed across the full period. Interpretation, therefore, focused on identifying multi-variable alignment during key periods, particularly malaria tail years and documented climate shock years, rather than uniform co-movement across all years.

Results

Seasonal climate trajectories with OLS trendlines and significance

The seasonal climate trajectories reveal quantitative and visual evidence of long-term climatic shifts across the three provinces. Seasonal mean maximum temperatures show consistent upward trends across all provinces, with several statistically significant (p < 0.05) trends, particularly in Mpumalanga and Limpopo, where warming is evident across all four seasons (Fig. S1). KwaZulu-Natal exhibits warming as well, though the significance varies by season. The trajectory lines for Limpopo are consistently positioned above the long-run mean, indicating a sustained warm shift across decades. Mpumalanga’s lines rise markedly after 2010, indicating acceleration in warming intensity. Minimum temperatures display strong and significant increases in all three provinces, especially Mpumalanga, where every season shows a significant warming trend (p < 0.01) (Fig. 2). Limpopo similarly shows robust warming across seasons (many p < 0.01). KwaZulu-Natal experiences warming in most seasons, with some seasonal significance, especially in summer and spring. Minimum temperature warming is especially pronounced, with trajectories drifting steadily above the long-run mean after 2010 in all provinces (Fig. 2). Relative humidity trajectories show declining tendencies in many seasons, particularly in Mpumalanga (winter p < 0.05) and KwaZulu-Natal (summer and winter p < 0.05). Declines are modest but consistent in KwaZulu-Natal and intermittently significant, while Limpopo shows mostly nonsignificant downward drifts. The trajectory lines often sit below the long-run mean in warm seasons, indicating a progressive drying signal. Rainfall patterns show high interannual variability and predominantly non-significant linear trends (most seasonal p-values > 0.10) (Fig. S1). Some weak tendencies exist: KwaZulu-Natal shows slightly declining autumn and winter rainfall, Limpopo displays mildly declining winter totals, and Mpumalanga shows isolated seasonal fluctuations. However, none of these trends reach statistical significance. The rainfall panels highlight the dominance of episodic extremes rather than sustained linear changes.

Seasonal climate trajectories (minimum temperature and relative humidity) for Limpopo, Mpumalanga, and KwaZulu-Natal (2000–2024)

Non-parametric trend analysis of seasonal climate variables (2000–2024)

Mann–Kendall testing indicates statistically significant monotonic declines in relative humidity in KwaZulu-Natal during summer, autumn, and winter (τ = − 0.31 to − 0.48, p ≤ 0.033), whereas no significant monotonic trend is detected in spring (Table S1). Maximum temperature shows no statistically significant monotonic trend, with only weak, non-significant warming observed in spring.

In Limpopo, relative humidity displays strong and statistically significant monotonic declines across all seasons (τ = − 0.40 to − 0.61, p < 0.01). Maximum temperature shows significant monotonic increases during summer and winter (τ ≈ 0.33–0.35, p < 0.05). Minimum temperature exhibits statistically significant positive monotonic trends across all seasons (τ = 0.42–0.67, p < 0.01), with Theil-Sen slopes indicating consistent warming.

In Mpumalanga, relative humidity demonstrates a significant monotonic decline in winter (τ = −0.29, p = 0.046). Minimum temperature shows statistically significant monotonic trends across all seasons (τ = 0.43–0.61, p < 0.01), with Theil-Sen slope estimates indicating persistent seasonal change. Rainfall shows no statistically significant monotonic trends across provinces or seasons (all p > 0.10), with slope estimates varying in sign and magnitude.

The MK and Theil-Sen results confirm that the seasonal warming and drying patterns identified using OLS are monotonic and statistically robust. Strong agreement in trend direction and seasonal structure is observed for minimum temperature and relative humidity across provinces. The absence of significant rainfall trends under both OLS and nonparametric testing reinforces the dominance of interannual variability over sustained change.

Seasonality metrics: humidity plateaus, rainfall extremes, and thermal season length indices

Seasonality diagnostics showed that each province exhibits a distinct profile of humidity persistence, climatic extremes, and thermal-season duration. These metrics contextualise and illustrate the intra-annual structure of climatic anomalies. KwaZulu-Natal displayed frequent multi-month humidity plateaus, including multiple years with ≥ 3-month sequences, indicating prolonged periods of elevated moisture (e.g., 2000–2001 with 4–5 month runs) (Fig. 3). Rainfall extremes occurred across multiple seasons, including recurrent autumn and summer extremes, consistent with storm- and cyclone-linked rainfall shocks, such as those associated with Cyclone Dineo (Fig. S2). KwaZulu-Natal’s thermal indices showed occasional long cool-season runs (e.g., 8 months in 2011) but also years with extended warm seasons (e.g., 7 months in 2019), reflecting high interannual thermal variability. In Limpopo humidity plateaus were comparatively rare, though isolated years (2000, 2003, 2006) exhibited long humid sequences (Fig. S2). Rainfall extremes were generally infrequent, with only sporadic seasonal exceedances. Warm-season lengths fluctuated strongly, with some years showing very prolonged warm periods (e.g., 12 months in 2011–2012) and others displaying notable cool-season persistence (e.g., 3–4-month cool spells in early 2000 s). Mpumalanga showed occasional ≥ 3-month humidity plateaus (e.g., 2000) but generally shorter runs thereafter (Fig. S2). Although rainfall extremes were irregular, several years featured multi-season anomalies (e.g., 2009 and 2013). Thermal indices showed the most dramatic pattern of the three provinces: from 2016 onward, Mpumalanga exhibited very long warm-season runs, including 12-month warm seasons in 2016, 2022, and 2023, indicating pronounced warming and near-continuous thermal suitability for vectors (Fig. 3). These results show that temperature trends are robust and consistently positive across all provinces, regardless of estimator, indicating strong warming signals. Humidity trends show significant declines in several seasons, but significance varies by estimator, implying that humidity variability is more affected by extremes. Rainfall trends are weak and estimator-sensitive, especially in KwaZulu-Natal, where outlier seasons influence OLS. Mpumalanga’s thermal environment is shifting most dramatically, with several years of continuous warm-season conditions, while KwaZulu-Natal maintains persistent humidity plateaus and repeated rainfall extremes, conditions favourable to malaria transmission.

Climatic extremes and shock-year dynamics

Figure 4 illustrates monthly climatic anomalies during two major shock years, KwaZulu-Natal’s 2022 flood season and Limpopo’s 2017 Cyclone Dineo year, compared against the 2000–2010 provincial climatology. In KwaZulu-Natal, the 2022 flood window (February-April) shows a pronounced rainfall surge, with monthly totals rising well above both the long-term mean and the historical interquartile range (IQR). This rainfall pulse coincided with a sustained elevation in relative humidity, which remained persistently high through April, even as temperatures dropped sharply below the climatological envelope. The combination of extreme precipitation and prolonged high humidity created a multi-month period of anomalously favourable environmental conditions for malaria transmission despite cooler temperatures.

In Limpopo, Cyclone Dineo in February 2017 produced a distinct early-season rainfall peak, exceeding the upper IQR boundary and aligning with a sharp humidity rise. Although rainfall declined rapidly in subsequent months, relative humidity remained elevated for an extended period from February to May, indicating a sustained moisture signal despite the absence of continued heavy rainfall. Temperature anomalies in 2017 initially tracked above the long-term mean but dipped below the climatological range through winter before recovering toward the end of the year. This configuration reflects a rapid-onset climatic shock followed by several months of moisture retention, conditions conducive to delayed or lagged malaria responses.

Across both provinces, the shock years demonstrate a clear deviation from seasonal norms, with rainfall spikes and humidity plateaus extending beyond the historical variability indicated by the IQR shading. These anomalies provide empirical support for the statistical findings that climatic extremes, not average seasonal conditions, drive malaria-relevant environmental windows. The KwaZulu-Natal 2022 and Limpopo 2017 profiles each reveal distinct but convergent patterns of short-term climatic disruption followed by sustained humidity anomalies, reinforcing their role as catalytic periods for malaria incidence spikes observed in surveillance data.

KwaZulu-Natal 2022 flood season

Figure 5 presents the monthly post-shock climatic trajectory for KwaZulu-Natal in 2022, contextualised against the 2000–2010 historical climatology. The province experienced a marked climatic disruption during the autumn flood window, with rainfall far exceeding both the long-term monthly mean and the IQR. April recorded an extreme precipitation peak exceeding 250 mm, an order-of-magnitude departure from typical autumn values, indicating a concentrated hydrological shock rather than a gradual seasonal anomaly.

KwaZulu-Natal 2022 post-shock climate anomalies relative to the 2000–2010 climatology. Monthly rainfall (mm; A), relative humidity (%; B), and mean temperature (°C; C) for KwaZulu-Natal during 2022 are shown against the historical 2000–2010 baseline. The shaded vertical band highlights the autumn flood window (March–May), during which rainfall strongly exceeded the historical range, accompanied by sustained elevated humidity and sub-seasonal cooling. These patterns indicate a compound climatic disturbance characterised by extreme precipitation, prolonged atmospheric moisture, and suppressed temperatures, extending malaria-relevant environmental suitability beyond typical seasonal expectations

Relative humidity mirrored this pattern, remaining persistently elevated throughout the flood period. Monthly humidity values exceeded the long-term mean for March–May and remained above or near the upper IQR boundary, signalling a sustained moisture regime despite the rapid decline in rainfall after May. This extended humidity plateau is climatologically significant, as it prolongs the environmental suitability window for Anopheles vector survival beyond the rainfall peak.

Temperature behaved differently, showing a strong negative anomaly following the flood period. From April through August, mean temperatures dropped below both the long-term mean and the IQR, reaching a pronounced mid-winter minimum in June. The combined pattern, extreme rainfall, multi-month elevated humidity, and sub-seasonal cooling, demonstrates that the 2022 shock produced a compound disturbance rather than a single-variable anomaly. Importantly, the co-occurrence of high moisture and declining temperatures after April aligns with the lagged malaria increases observed later in the year, consistent with known vector-climate relationships.

The post-shock climatic signature in KwaZulu-Natal reveals a rapid-onset hydrological extreme followed by prolonged atmospheric moisture retention and temperature suppression. This post-shock climatic signature in KwaZulu-Natal was inferred from the anomaly plots, which show (i) a rapid-onset hydrological extreme, evidenced by February-April rainfall far exceeding the long-term mean and upper IQR; (ii) prolonged atmospheric moisture retention, indicated by sustained above-average humidity through March–May despite the decline in rainfall; and (iii) temperature suppression, with April-August temperatures falling below both the climatological mean and IQR. This compound anomaly underscores how climatic shocks reshape seasonal conditions in ways that extend malaria-relevant environmental windows beyond what seasonal averages would predict.

Limpopo 2017 cyclone season

Figure 6 displays the monthly climatic progression in Limpopo during 2017, the year marked by Cyclone Dineo, and compares it with the 2000–2010 provincial climatology. The cyclone produced a pronounced rainfall shock in February, with monthly precipitation exceeding 280 mm, well above both the historical mean and the IQR. This exceptional early-season peak reflects a sudden hydrometeorological disturbance rather than a gradual accumulation of seasonal rainfall. Although rainfall diminished sharply after February, the province continued to register intermittent above-average precipitation episodes later in the year, indicating a disrupted seasonal pattern rather than a single isolated spike.

Malaria incidence (Annual, 2000–2024)

Annual laboratory-confirmed malaria cases show pronounced interannual variability across all three endemic provinces (Fig. 7). All three provinces show a clear decline in malaria cases during the early 2000 s, reflecting the national-scale upscaling of vector control and case management interventions. KwaZulu-Natal exhibits the most dramatic early reduction, dropping precipitously from more than 40,000 cases in 2000 to fewer than 3000 by 2003, after which incidence stabilises at historically low levels (Table S2). This sustained suppression is consistent with KwaZulu-Natal’s longstanding, high-coverage IRS programme and effective treatment policies, which have historically produced rapid reductions in malaria transmission. These strategies were maintained through adaptive insecticide management, including the reintroduction of DDT after 2000 to counter emerging pyrethroid resistance [44].

Limpopo and Mpumalanga have higher intrinsic receptivity because local ecological conditions can support mosquito survival and parasite development, and their proximity to high-transmission areas in Mozambique and Zimbabwe increases the likelihood that infected individuals or vectors will cross the border. KwaZulu-Natal, while maintaining the lowest incidence levels, shows a slight uptick in 2022 that corresponds with its major flood event.

Migration into Thembelihle and selected parts of Soweto shows an apparent post-2022 increase from KwaZulu-Natal, peaking in 2023 with 106 recorded entries, compared with 61 in 2022 and 20 in 2021 (Fig. S3). This also means the counts should be interpreted as surveillance-captured events rather than population-scaled migration rates. This pattern remains consistent with displacement-driven mobility following the April–May 2022 flood disaster. In contrast, Limpopo exhibits sustained inflows with higher counts during 2022–2023 (47 and 49, respectively), while Mpumalanga remains low overall but rises to 24 entries in 2023. These results indicate that migration into Thembelihle and selected parts of Soweto is episodic rather than uniform, with KwaZulu-Natal dominating the recent spike and Limpopo contributing steady inflows across the period. These findings align with qualitative evidence from migration reasons, which emphasise family reunification and housing relocation during post-shock periods.

Discussion

Extremes, mobility, and risk

Malaria risk across Limpopo, Mpumalanga, and KwaZulu-Natal is reorganized by short-lived climatic extremes and episodic post-shock mobility, rather than by gradual shifts in seasonal means. Three lines of evidence converge. First, contemporaneous humidity plateaus, or multi-week periods with relative humidity above month-specific baselines, align with incidence increases. The humidity-malaria correlations within the same year are strongest and most significant in Mpumalanga and KwaZulu Natal, while they are weak in Limpopo (Table 1). This consistent with evidence that relative humidity materially increases adult Anopheles survivorship and shapes mosquito population dynamics [40]. Second, rainfall pulses propagate lagged risk through hydrological memory, with a clear and significant lag-1 rainfall-malaria association in Limpopo, consistent with habitat persistence beyond the rainfall peak. Third, shock-year reconstructions show compound disruptions, extreme precipitation coupled with sustained atmospheric moisture and sub-seasonal cooling. These open multi-month transmission windows not captured by mean-based climatology (KwaZulu-Natal 2022 floods; Limpopo 2017 Cyclone Dineo) [5, 45].

These climatic signatures intersect with receiver-side mobility. Following the April–May 2022 floods, in-migration from KwaZulu-Natal into Thembelihle and selected parts of Soweto rose markedly, with counts peaking in 2023, while Cyclone Dineo (February 2017) did not produce a comparable inflow into these specific sites. The asymmetry indicates that the scale, reach, and infrastructural disruption of the shock condition determine whether displaced populations move toward these urban receivers. Co-movement analyses reinforce this picture: during malaria tail years (≥ 90th percentile), rainfall and minimum temperature most frequently move in step with malaria peaks, and, in years with mobility data, in-migration rises in tandem (notably Limpopo 2017–2018). This yields a three-way coupling of climate, malaria, and mobility.

These results refine how transmission potential is understood in elimination settings. Long-term warming, especially in minimum temperatures, is evident across provinces, and thermal seasons are lengthening most dramatically in Mpumalanga. Yet the timing and magnitude of incidence fluctuations track humidity plateaus and rainfall pulses far more than linear warming, highlighting moisture-driven contemporaneous windows and lag-driven habitat persistence as the proximate drivers of year-to-year risk.

These patterns are consistent with findings from other studies, which similarly report that rainfall and humidity variability exert a stronger influence on short-term malaria incidence fluctuations than gradual warming trends [46–48]. Prior analyses in South Africa and across the continent show that moisture-driven events, including rainfall pulses and sustained humidity periods, create the most immediate and lagged ecological conditions for vector proliferation [30, 31, 40, 45, 49, 50]. In contrast, long-term temperature increases primarily reshape seasonal thermal suitability rather than year-to-year incidence [51, 52]. At the same time, urban informal settlements in non-endemic provinces function as active nodes within this coupled system: when shocks coincide with mobility surges, odyssean risk and diagnostic delays rise on the receiving side [25]. These findings shift emphasis from seasonal means and static endemic boundaries to event-aware climatology and mobility-integrated readiness. They also acknowledge that non-climatic factors, including lapses in vector control or clinical investigation, can widen vulnerability windows when extremes strike [53].

Moisture-driven transmission windows

Two complementary pathways explain how extremes translate into transmission-relevant windows. Ambient humidity acts contemporaneously on adult anopheline survival and on parasite development within vectors; relative humidity plateaus therefore widen the window during which transmission can proceed even when other conditions are marginal [40]. This is visible in both the diagnostics and the associations: KwaZulu-Natal and Mpumalanga show significant same-year humidity-malaria correlations (Table 1), and multi-month humidity plateaus recur in KwaZulu-Natal (including runs ≥ 3 months), with plateaus concentrated around flood-affected years. The KwaZulu-Natal 2022 profile illustrates the mechanism: an extreme rainfall pulse (April > 250 mm) was followed by sustained high humidity through April, while temperatures dipped below the climatological envelope. The persistence of high moisture, despite declining rainfall, maintained environmental suitability across months when mean-based seasonal expectations would suggest waning risk.

Rainfall pulses operate primarily through lagged hydrological memory. Intense precipitation events replenish and stabilize larval habitats; as surface and near-surface water decays slowly, vector cohorts mature after the meteorological peak, shifting risk into subsequent months [54]. This lag structure is most evident in Limpopo, where the lag-1 rainfall-malaria correlation is significant, and in the 2017 Cyclone Dineo season: February rainfall (> 280 mm) exceeded the upper historical interquartile range, while relative humidity remained elevated from March to May despite a sharp decline in rainfall. Concurrent mid-year cooling did not negate risk; rather, moisture retention sustained suitability, aligning with the observed incidence surge. These profiles demonstrate how rapid-onset shocks initiate multi-month ecological shifts through humidity persistence and habitat carry-over [21].

Thermal structure modulates, but does not dominate, these pathways. Minimum temperatures have risen across provinces, and thermal season lengthening is most pronounced in Mpumalanga, which recorded near-continuous warm seasons in multiple recent years. Yet [42, 43]the operative temperature signals in the incidence series are weak or negative, especially for maximum temperature (significantly negative in Mpumalanga). This indicates that, under contemporary conditions, moisture constraints, not peak heat, are more decisive for outbreak timing [55]. Notably, post-shock cooling in KwaZulu-Natal (April-August 2022) and mid-year cooling in Limpopo (2017) occurred alongside sustained humidity, underscoring that humidity plateaus can offset cooling by preserving adult survivorship and vectorial capacity over the weeks in which cases subsequently rise.

Thus, seasonal structure strengthens these mechanisms. Warm-season lengthening increases the probability that humidity plateaus and rainfall pulses will overlap with thermally permissive periods, whereas dry-season plateaus are rare and short. In KwaZulu-Natal, repeated autumn and summer rainfall extremes co-occur with multi-week humidity plateaus; in Limpopo, warm-season variability is high, but lagged rainfall effects signal hydrological carry-over into late summer and autumn. In Mpumalanga, the tendency toward prolonged warm seasons lowers the threshold for humidity-driven surges, explaining why same-year humidity associates most strongly with incidence there. Across settings, these mechanics clarify why extremes, rather than mean seasonal changes, control interannual fluctuations. Humidity plateaus lengthen the contemporaneous window, and rainfall pulses extend risk through lagged habitat persistence, with temperature acting chiefly through season length and overlap rather than through linear warming alone [56].

Temperature’s supporting role

Although all three provinces exhibit long-term warming, particularly in minimum temperature, the results demonstrate that temperature alone does not reliably shape contemporary incidence dynamics in South Africa’s endemic belt. Minimum temperature trends show highly significant upward trajectories across Limpopo and Mpumalanga, with KwaZulu-Natal also warming in most seasons. These shifts reflect broader regional warming patterns, but their epidemiological effects are neither linear nor primary. Instead, temperature influences transmission through its seasonal structure, the length, continuity, and timing of thermally permissive periods, rather than through annual means. The thermal season length indices highlight this distinction: Mpumalanga shows repeated 12-month warm seasons in recent years, indicating near-continuous suitability for vector development, whereas Limpopo and KwaZulu-Natal exhibit more variable warm-season spans, alternating between extended warm periods and years with prolonged cool seasons. These patterns show that temperature alters the potential envelope of transmission but does not initiate incidence changes unless moisture-related conditions align [57].

The correlation analyses reinforce this nuance. Maximum temperature exhibits predominantly weak or negative associations with malaria, with Mpumalanga showing a significant negative relationship. Minimum temperature correlations are also weak or negative, except for small positive tendencies in KwaZulu-Natal that do not reach significance. These findings indicate that, under current climatic regimes, humidity and rainfall dominate proximate outbreak timing, while temperature modulates background receptivity [58, 59]. This is further illustrated by the shock-year profiles. After the April 2022 floods in KwaZulu-Natal, temperatures dropped sharply below the long-term interquartile range from April to August, yet malaria-relevant environmental conditions persisted because humidity remained elevated. Similarly, in Limpopo, Cyclone Dineo produced early-season rainfall and sustained humidity despite mid-year cooling, supporting conditions for later increases in incidence. In both cases, humidity plateaus offset temperature suppression, allowing environmental suitability to remain high during periods that climatological averages would normally suggest reduced risk.

The findings refine the temperature narrative for malaria elimination contexts. They show that thermal suitability is rarely the limiting factor in the contemporary climate of South Africa’s endemic belt; instead, temperature acts as a contextual amplifier when warm seasons overlap with humidity plateaus and rainfall pulses. The strong, consistent warming signal, especially in minimum temperatures, extends the envelope within which moisture-driven effects operate but does not independently trigger outbreaks.

Seasonal structure and suitability

Seasonality emerges as a central organising feature of malaria receptivity in these endemic provinces, not because transmission ceases, but because the intensity of receptivity fluctuates across the year. Even in settings with persistent low-level transmission, the length and configuration of seasons shape how climatic extremes amplify or extend periods of heightened environmental suitability. Across the three provinces, the seasonal climate trajectories show statistically significant warming of minimum temperatures in most seasons and modest but consistent declines in humidity during warm seasons, particularly in KwaZulu-Natal and Mpumalanga. These conditions reshape the seasonal boundaries of transmission. In Mpumalanga, the maintenance of continuous or near-continuous warm seasons since 2016 compresses the distinction between traditional malaria and non-malaria periods. This aligns with the province’s strong humidity-incidence correlation, as any humidity plateau occurring within an expanded warm season has greater potential to trigger transmission. By contrast, Limpopo shows large interannual swings in warm-season duration, which may buffer or amplify the effects of rainfall pulses depending on their alignment with thermally permissive periods.

The seasonal diagnostics also reveal pronounced intra-annual asymmetries in moisture availability. KwaZulu-Natal displays recurrent autumn and summer rainfall extremes, closely matching its flood-prone climatology, and multi-month humidity plateaus that often span late summer into autumn. This seasonal clustering explains why malaria-relevant anomalies often occur outside conventional peak-season expectations, as climatic shocks can temporarily override the usual seasonal envelope. Importantly, in endemic regions, transmission never falls to zero, and environmental conditions capable of sustaining mosquito survival and parasite development can arise at atypical times when rainfall, humidity, or temperature disturbances shift beyond their normal seasonal patterns. Meanwhile, Limpopo’s lag-1 rainfall effect indicates that late-summer or early-autumn precipitation can influence precipitation months later, highlighting how seasonal timing interacts with hydrological memory. This seasonal offset aligns with the province’s observed peak during the 2017–2018 malaria surge, which followed the cyclone’s early-year rainfall and humidity carryover.

The analysis also provides insight into the changing character of winters. In KwaZulu-Natal and Mpumalanga, winter humidity shows statistically significant downward trends, suggesting progressively drier cool seasons. However, even within these drying winters, specific years, including 2022 in KwaZulu-Natal, exhibited above-IQR humidity anomalies due to shock-related moisture retention. This illustrates how climatic extremes can temporarily override expected seasonal climatology, creating short-lived periods of elevated suitability even in winter. Such episodic departures from long-term averages demonstrate that environmental conditions relevant for transmission can arise outside their typical seasonal envelope [60]. In Limpopo, winters show declining rainfall but variable humidity, allowing hydrological memory from summer rainfall or cyclone disturbances to modulate mid-year receptivity. These findings illustrate why static seasonal boundaries provide limited operational guidance: Winters and summers are not simply becoming warmer or cooler, they are becoming more variable, more asymmetric, and more influenced by extreme events.

These seasonal changes also interact with rainfall-temperature coupling. During the KwaZulu-Natal 2022 floods, autumn rainfall peaks co-occurred with sharp temperature drops, yet high humidity sustained the suitability window beyond the rainfall shock. In Limpopo’s 2017 season, an early summer rainfall extreme, followed by atypical mid-year cooling, nonetheless contributed to extended moisture availability well into autumn. Such patterns demonstrate that the sequencing of seasonal anomalies, when moisture arrives relative to temperature shifts, determines whether events consolidate into high-risk windows [61]. Across provinces, the results show that seasonal transitions have become less predictable and more sensitive to extremes, requiring malaria programs to incorporate season-specific hydrological and humidity thresholds rather than relying solely on calendar-based definitions of peak transmission seasons. Although peak transmission typically occurs within recognised seasons, our seasonal analyses show that climatic disturbances can modify the timing and intensity of suitability in these endemic settings, producing anomalous risk even outside conventional seasonal windows.

Urban receiver vulnerability

The results show that climatic shocks do more than alter environmental suitability in endemic provinces; they also reshape the spatial distribution of malaria vulnerability by directing displaced populations toward structurally fragile urban settlements. In these settings, suitability refers to the combination of sustained humidity, adequate surface-water availability, and temperatures within permissive ranges for mosquito survival and parasite development, conditions that climatic extremes can intensify or prolong beyond their usual seasonal bounds. Following the April–May 2022 floods in KwaZulu-Natal, in-migration into Thembelihle and selected parts of Soweto rose sharply, with arrivals from KwaZulu-Natal more than doubling between 2021 and 2023. This temporal concentration aligns with the period of widespread infrastructural damage and housing loss documented during the floods [24], indicating that climate-linked displacement can create short-lived but consequential mobility surges. These findings contrast with the aftermath of Cyclone Dineo in 2017, which did not generate detectable increases in migration into these specific sites, highlighting that the reach and severity of the shock determine whether mobility pathways extend as far as Gauteng.

Urban informal settlements serve as receiving points within a larger climate-mobility system, and their vulnerability is not solely ecological but structural [17, 62]. Settlements such as Thembelihle are characterised by poor drainage, intermittent water supply, limited healthcare access, substandard housing, and high population density, conditions repeatedly shown to increase malaria exposure when importation occurs. When migration flows rise during or after periods of climatic anomaly, these environmental and infrastructural deficits amplify exposure and complicate timely detection of imported infections. This dynamic is reflected in the national profile of odyssean malaria, where cases frequently present in Gauteng and other non-endemic provinces after vehicular or goods-linked vector transport from endemic regions [25]. The observed migration rise after the 2022 floods, therefore, represents a receiver-side vulnerability pulse, one that is both temporary and operationally significant.

The analysis also shows that mobility responses are episodic rather than continuous, with KwaZulu-Natal consistently dominating the provincial shares of in-migration across seasons. This pattern points to persistent structural drivers, such as inequality, recurrent flooding, and precarious infrastructure, that intensify vulnerability to displacement. Seasonal migration patterns further reveal that autumn carries the highest proportion of migrants from KwaZulu-Natal, mirroring the province’s seasonal exposure to rainfall extremes. These findings connect mobility behaviour directly to climatic seasonality, demonstrating that climatic shocks not only open environmental windows for vectors. They also reshape social exposure pathways by redistributing populations into high-risk urban micro-ecologies during favourable conditions. Urban areas thus emerge as critical but often overlooked nodes within the elimination landscape, where climatic and social dynamics converge to produce temporally concentrated vulnerabilities [63].

Climate-malaria-migration synchrony

The co-movement analysis provides a high-resolution view of how climatic variables, malaria incidence, and mobility interact over time. Across all three provinces, malaria tail years, defined as those exceeding the 90th percentile of annual case counts, coincide with aligned movements in key climate variables, particularly rainfall and minimum temperature. This synchrony is clearest in Limpopo’s 2017–2018 cluster, where malaria, rainfall, and minimum temperature simultaneously reach their upper normalised ranges. During the same period, in-migration into Thembelihle and selected parts of Soweto also rises, producing a three-way coupling among climate extremes, malaria surges, and mobility flows. This convergence should not be interpreted as a numeric driver of incidence, since a single infected individual can cause multiple infections. Rather, it reflects an ecological synchrony in which climatic disturbances, elevated transmission potential, and human movement coincide within the same period.

KwaZulu-Natal exhibits a different pattern: strong co-movement in the early 2000 s tail years, where malaria, rainfall, humidity, and minimum temperature fall and rise in tandem. As migration enters the series (from 2017 onward), new synchronies appear. Between 2017 and 2019, both malaria and migration rise together, despite not being tail years, suggesting that even modest incidence increases may align with changes in population movement. It is important to note, however, that 2019 also coincided with unusually heavy rainfall across large parts of Africa, which may have contributed to elevated climatic suitability in South Africa. This suggests that the observed synchrony reflects overlapping climatic and mobility patterns rather than a direct causal link. A further rise in 2021–2022 shows simultaneous upward shifts in rainfall, humidity, and temperature, reinforcing the province's sensitivity to multivariate climatic anomalies. These patterns indicate that co-movement need not always reach threshold levels to be epidemiologically meaningful; even sub-threshold alignments can represent windows of elevated urban risk when mobility pathways are active.

Mpumalanga’s profile is dominated by early 2000–2004 co-declines across malaria and climate variables, producing the most consistent synchrony in the early period. The province then shows a modest alignment around 2014–2015, followed by synchrony in 2017–2018, when malaria, migration, and some climate variables rise together. After 2019, co-movement fragments: migration increases while malaria declines, illustrating how social dynamics can decouple from climatic structure in later years. These divergent trajectories highlight that mobility is not merely a passive downstream response to climate but an independent pathway with its own drivers, which can accentuate or dilute the temporal alignment with climatic suitability [64].

Collectively, the co-movement results demonstrate that the strongest synchronies emerge during periods of climatic shock, where multiple variables, including migration, shift in the same direction over the same intervals. These synchronies illustrate how climatic extremes can cascade across ecological and social domains, producing multi-layered windows of vulnerability. They also reaffirm that elimination strategies must move beyond climate-only or migration-only models: the epidemiological signal materialises most clearly when both dimensions are analysed together, particularly during tail years and their immediate aftermath.

Reframing elimination assumptions

These findings require a fundamental recalibration of how climatic and mobility drivers are conceptualised within malaria elimination strategies. The evidence shows that transmission risk is not governed by gradual trends in seasonal means or by warming alone. Instead, it is influenced by climatic extremes, humidity plateaus, and rainfall pulses, whose impacts unfold contemporaneously and through lagged hydrological memory. This challenges the common operational assumption that climate-malaria relationships can be adequately monitored through average seasonal indicators. Instead, the results demonstrate that tail events, not means, generate the most epidemiologically consequential windows. In settings where humidity plateaus form quickly and rainfall pulses propagate across months, malaria surveillance must be recalibrated toward event-aware, anomaly-sensitive climate intelligence.

The findings also revise assumptions about temperature. While warming is unequivocal across all provinces, especially in minimum temperature, the weak or negative correlations with incidence indicate that temperature's role is increasingly contextual. It shapes background receptivity rather than acting as a primary driver. This underscores that thermal trends should be interpreted through seasonal length, overlap, and post-shock thermal behaviour, not through linear warming metrics. Similarly, assumptions about static seasonal boundaries require revision, as winters and summers in the endemic belt are becoming more variable, more asymmetric, and more influenced by extreme events, altering the alignment between climatology and transmission windows.

At the same time, the results dismantle the implicit boundary between “endemic” and “non-endemic” zones by showing that urban informal settlements function as active components of the malaria system. Post-shock spikes in in-migration into Thembelihle and selected parts of Soweto reveal how displacement dynamics can reallocate risk into receiver locations lacking routine malaria preparedness. The persistence of odyssean malaria in Gauteng, driven by delayed clinical suspicion and infrastructural constraints, highlights the need to revise assumptions that urban areas are merely endpoints for imported cases [25]. Instead, they form part of an interconnected spatial network where climate shocks, mobility flows, and structural vulnerabilities interact.

The findings also show that non-climatic factors, such as lapses in investigation or vector control, disruptions to health infrastructure during shock years, and constrained surveillance coverage of migration systems, interact with climatic anomalies to shape risk [65]. Their influence is not peripheral; they modulate how climatic extremes and mobility surges translate into incidence patterns. Incorporating these structural determinants into the elimination strategy ensures that climate-linked vulnerabilities are neither overstated nor misinterpreted. These insights compel elimination programmes to adopt tail-sensitive, mobility-integrated, and structurally informed surveillance and response architectures that reflect the realities of a climate-volatile, highly mobile elimination landscape.

Study’s contribution

This study’s novelty and how it advances malaria elimination science is outlined in three ways. First, it establishes that climatic extremes and post-shock mobility, not long-term averages, structure interannual malaria risk in South Africa’s endemic belt and its urban recipients. However, this relationship operates alongside several intervening factors, including vector control coverage, treatment access, and socially network-driven mobility patterns, which mediate how climatic conditions translate into observed transmission dynamics [66, 67].

The analysis demonstrates that humidity plateaus, acting contemporaneously on vector survivorship, and rainfall pulses, operating through hydrological memory, are the proximate climatic drivers of incidence variability. These findings challenge the prevailing emphasis on gradual warming or mean seasonal conditions and establish that tail events are epidemiologically decisive, offering a new lens for anticipating outbreaks.

A second contribution lies in clarifying the relationship between temperature and malaria under contemporary climatic regimes. While the complexity of temperature-malaria interactions is well recognised in the modelling literature [68, 69], our analyses provide empirical insight into how these dynamics manifest under recent climatic variability. Despite robust multi-decadal warming across all provinces, especially in minimum temperatures, the study shows that temperature influences risk primarily through season length, thermal overlap, and post-shock cooling, rather than through linear associations with incidence. This reconceptualisation situates warming trends within their seasonal and moisture contexts, reinforcing the need to interpret temperature dynamically in relation to humidity, hydrological timing, and seasonal boundaries rather than as a standalone driver of malaria dynamics.

A third contribution lies in articulating the climate-mobility nexus as an integral determinant of transmission potential. The evidence shows that post-flood displacement from KwaZulu-Natal in 2022 coincided with marked rises in in-migration into Thembelihle and selected parts of Soweto during months of high environmental suitability. This alignment does not imply that large migrant volumes directly drive case surges, nor that every migrant carries an infection; a single infected individual can generate multiple secondary infections in dense informal-settlements. Instead, the finding highlights how climatic shocks, elevated transmission potential, and human movement can intersect within the same period to create receiver-side vulnerabilities. These dynamics underscore the value of embedding mobility-sensitive indicators in surveillance systems, particularly in settings where climatic volatility and population movement converge at the margins of transmission.

Limitations

A key limitation of this study concerns the migration analysis, which focuses exclusively on selected suburbs in Soweto, and Thembelihle, a single informal settlement within Gauteng. While Thembelihle provides a valuable lens into how climate-linked displacement interacts with urban vulnerability, it represents only one node within a much larger and heterogeneous system of informal settlements across South Africa. As such, the observed post-shock migration patterns cannot be assumed to be representative of broader mobility dynamics within the province or nationally.

Also, since the analysis did not include other settlements in Gauteng or alternative receiver areas in provinces such as Mpumalanga, Limpopo, or the Western Cape, it remains possible that migration from malaria-endemic regions persisted throughout 2017–2021. However, this migration may have been primarily directed toward other urban destinations during those years. Under such a scenario, the apparent increase into selected suburbs in Soweto and Thembelihle in 2022–2023 may reflect a redistribution of flows rather than an absolute increase in mobility from KwaZulu-Natal or other endemic provinces.

Additionally, the SaT-HDSS coverage is site-specific and temporally variable, which constrains the ability to make generalizable inferences about system-level changes in migration patterns. Since SaT-HDSS counts represent events detected within a defined surveillance zone rather than comprehensive migration flows across Gauteng, they may capture local shifts rather than province-wide trends. Furthermore, without parallel migration data from other informal settlements, municipal population registries, health-seeking datasets, or transport usage records, the broader landscape of post-shock mobility remains only partially observable.

Despite these constraints, the findings highlight the mechanistic plausibility and operational relevance of climate-mobility interactions, providing evidence of a potential pathway. The results, therefore, motivate, but do not substitute for broader empirical investigations into how displacement, poverty, and urban spatial form co-produce malaria vulnerability at the margins of elimination.

Future direction

Advancing the malaria elimination agenda requires three practical steps. First, formalise operational thresholds: humidity plateau criteria (duration and magnitude) and rainfall extremes (percentile exceedance with defined antecedent windows) should be tested across endemic provinces and selected urban receivers to confirm predictive reliability. Second, integrate mobility intelligence into malaria alerts. Data on transport volumes, settlement registrations, and humanitarian intake should populate receiver-side dashboards, triggering temporary preparedness in informal settlements during post-shock periods. Third, scale modelling to a weekly or daily resolution by incorporating intervention timelines, entomological counts, and resistance profiles.

Fourth, incorporate community perceptions into early-warning design, as residents’ interpretations of seasonal changes, mosquito nuisance, and perceived vulnerability influence how alerts are received and acted upon [70]. Integrating local perceptions with climatic and mobility indicators improves the relevance, acceptance, and operational performance of early-warning systems [71]. Additionally, evaluate whether humidity and rainfall stacks can predict vector abundance and case clustering at the neighbourhood scale. Parallel implementation research should assess whether tail-aware triggers and urban preparedness reduce post-shock incidence without excessive resource burden, providing evidence for policy adoption.

Policy and governance

Translating these insights into action requires malaria programmes to shift from static seasonal frameworks to responsive, event-aware governance structures. National and provincial systems should institutionalise climatic anomaly triggers, including humidity plateaus and top-decile rainfall months, that activate rapid vector-control and diagnostic escalation. Embedding hydrological memory into intervention calendars would ensure that post-rainfall or post-cyclone risk is proactively managed rather than retrospectively addressed.

Receiver-side preparedness is equally important. Urban informal settlements must be incorporated into malaria policy architectures as legitimate risk zones during periods of climatic displacement. This requires coordination between public health, municipal services, water and sanitation departments, and disaster management to implement micro-drainage maintenance, targeted environmental health patrols, and temporary diagnostic intensification when mobility surges are detected.

Governance reforms should prioritise interoperable data systems that enable timely exchange of climate information, mobility data, and health indicators across institutions. Financing mechanisms must include contingency envelopes for surge operations during shock years, while cross-border coordination remains essential for Limpopo, where lag-1 rainfall patterns in neighbouring countries influence local incidence.

Equity considerations must guide the design of climatic and mobility triggers. Interventions should be prioritised in settlements where insecure housing, inadequate drainage, and limited transport access magnify exposure. By embedding structural vulnerability directly into trigger design, elimination programmes can ensure that climate-linked responses protect the populations least able to absorb environmental and social shocks.

Global implications

The implications extend beyond South Africa. Many regions at the margins of transmission face a similar triad: rising climatic volatility, uneven programme capacity, and growing urban informality. In such settings, reliance on seasonal averages obscures the epidemiological importance of rare but consequential extremes. Event-aware climatology, hydrological memory in planning, and mobility-integrated preparedness constitute a portable architecture for resilience. Reframing malaria control as a dynamic system responsive to episodic shocks, rather than a static seasonal routine, will be central to safeguarding gains where elimination is within reach yet fragile. As climate variability intensifies and displacement patterns evolve, tail-sensitive surveillance and receiver-side readiness will determine whether transient shocks become reversible disruptions or longer-term setbacks.

Conclusion

This study shows that malaria risk at the margins of elimination is shaped not by slow changes in average seasonal conditions, but by climatic extremes and the mobility responses they trigger. Across the three endemic provinces of South Africa, humidity plateaus and rainfall pulses emerged as the most consequential environmental signals, acting through contemporaneous and lagged pathways that widen transmission windows in ways that long-run means cannot capture. These effects were most visible during shock years, when extreme rainfall, sustained atmospheric moisture, and post-event cooling created multi-month ecological shifts, including during the 2022 floods in KwaZulu-Natal and Cyclone Dineo in Limpopo in 2017.

Temperature, though steadily warming over the study period, especially for minimum temperature, did not exert linear control over year-to-year incidence. Instead, it altered the length and overlap of thermal seasons, influencing risk only when favourable temperatures intersected with moisture anomalies. these findings clarify that the decisive climatic mechanisms in contemporary South African settings are moisture-driven, with temperature serving as a structural modifier rather than an independent driver.

The study also demonstrates that climatic extremes reorganize malaria risk through population displacement, particularly where damaged infrastructure and loss of housing intensify mobility. The post-2022 increase in migration from KwaZulu-Natal into Thembelihle and selected parts of Soweto shows how climate shocks can temporarily redirect movement toward vulnerable urban settlements, where diagnostic delay, poor drainage, and constrained access to care heighten the consequences of importation. In such settings, malaria risk becomes a function of both environmental suitability and receiver-side vulnerability, revealing urban informal settlements as active, rather than peripheral, components of the elimination landscape.

By integrating climatic anomalies, thermal structure, and mobility flows, this study reframes malaria elimination as a problem governed by tail risks, hydrological memory, and episodic displacement, rather than by static seasonal expectations. This reconceptualization carries operational implications: surveillance systems must shift toward event-aware and mobility-integrated approaches that detect humidity plateaus, rainfall extremes, and post-shock population movements. Vector-control calendars must encode lagged hydrological processes; and preparedness protocols must extend to high-risk informal urban settlements.

In doing so, the study advances understanding of how climate and mobility combine to reorganize malaria risk in South Africa and provides a framework that can support elimination efforts in similarly climate-volatile and highly mobile settings. As climatic extremes intensify and urban vulnerability deepens, the ability to interpret and act upon these short-lived but influential environmental and social signals will determine whether elimination gains are sustained or eroded in the years ahead.
