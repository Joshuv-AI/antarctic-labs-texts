Intestinal failure-associated liver disease in a child with short bowel syndrome: management issues
Authors: Barkha Dhanjani, Shivangi Tetarbe, Dhruv Gandhi, Ira Shah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntestinal failure (IF) refers to a state where the absorptive surface of the small intestine is inadequate to maintain water and electrolyte balance and to meet nutritional demands for optimal growth in children. We describe a 2-month-old infant with short bowel syndrome (SBS) and intestinal failure-associated liver disease (IFALD) following surgical resection for intestinal malrotation and midgut volvulus. He required prolonged total parenteral nutrition (TPN), which resulted in cholestasis and progressive liver dysfunction. Management included ursodeoxycholic acid, amino acid-based continuous feeds, antibiotics, percutaneous transhepatic biliary drainage, and supportive therapies. The illness course was complicated by recurrent diarrhea, hypoglycemia, suspected small intestinal bacterial overgrowth, and Candida sepsis. Gradual clinical improvement was observed, with the resolution of IFALD and appropriate weight gain. On follow-up at 1.5 years, the child was tolerating full oral feeds and had normal liver function. This case highlights the management challenges of SBS with IFALD associated with TPN.Keywords: bowel resection, intestinal failure, short bowel syndrome, small intestinal bacterial overgrowth, total parenteral nutrition, pediatric nutrition

Introduction

Intestinal failure(IF) is characterized by the inability of the absorptive surface of the small intestine to maintain water and electrolyte balance and meet nutritional demands for optimal pediatric growth [1]. Common causes of pediatric IF include necrotizing enterocolitis, gastroschisis, short bowel syndrome(SBS), malrotation, volvulus, intestinal atresia [1]. Most infants with IF require prolonged total parenteral nutrition(TPN) for optimal growth and development. IF-associated liver disease(IFALD), caused by long-term TPN, may lead to cholestasis, steatohepatitis, fibrogenesis, and progression to end-stage liver disease [2]. We present the clinical course of an infant following intestinal resection and subsequent SBS, complicated by TPN-associated IFALD and discuss management issues.

Case report

A 2-month-old male presented with multiple episodes of large-volume, watery diarrhea, not associated with blood or mucus. He was diagnosed with intestinal malrotation and midgut volvulus for which he underwent surgical derotation with bowel resection, including the ileo-caecal junction, and anastomosis on day 2 of life. He was on TPN until day 40 of life, after which he was transitioned to orogastric tube feeding and discharged on day 45 of life. On presentation to us, weight was 3.25 kg (<3rd percentile as per World Health Organization(WHO) charts) and height was 54 cm (<3rd percentile as per WHO charts). He was dehydrated and had several hypoglycemic episodes, which prompting the initiation of intravenous fluids. Serial investigations are depicted in Table 1. Abdominal ultrasound showed sludge in the gallbladder and common bile duct(CBD), with mild central intrahepatic biliary radicle dilatation, following which the patient was started on ursodeoxycholic acid(UDCA) and fat-soluble vitamins. Stool examination showed 1–2 RBCs/hpf and 1–2 pus cells/hpf, and stool culture grew carbapenem and multidrug-resistant Escherichia coli. Blood culture was negative. He was treated with intravenous colistin and amikacin for 14 days for suspected sepsis. Continuous oral amino acid-based formula and racecadotril were started for diarrhea, arrowroot powder and cornstarch for hypoglycemia, alongwith docosahexaenoic acid(DHA). Stool output continued to remain high, and pantoprazole was added suspecting hypergastrinemia. Despite completion of the antibiotic course, diarrhea failed to improve and loperamide was started. Bilirubin levels persistently rose despite the above measures (Table 1). Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography showed filling defects in gallbladder neck and CBD suggestive of sludge/debris along with mild prominence of CBD and common hepatic ducts. Persistent jaundice required percutaneous transhepatic biliary drainage(PTBD) to be done on day 21 of hospitalization and cholangiogram showed CBD sludge with normal flow into duodenum. Post-PTBD, serial liver function tests(LFTs) showed improvement (Table 1), hence PTBD catheter was removed after 12 days. A central line was placed a day prior to PTBD and the patient developed fever after 5 days of line insertion. Paired blood cultures grew Candida parapsilosis following which the line was removed, and he was given a 14-day course of fluconazole. He also developed abdominal distention and vomiting for which rifaximin was added for suspected small intestinal bacterial overgrowth(SIBO). He gradually improved on continuous oral feeds with decrease in stool frequency and improving LFTs. He was discharged from the hospital after 60 days with a weight of 3.34 kg on UDCA, loperamide, racecadotril, medium chain triglycerides oil, DHA.

At 9 months, continuous oral feeds, racecadotril and loperamide were stopped. He was still continued on amino acid-based formula and oral solid feeds. At 1 year, he was weaned to a regular lactose-based formula but subsequently developed diarrhea. Amino acid-based formula and oral solid feeds were increased. At 1.5-years of age, he had a weight of 10 kg, with normal LFTs and was on full solid diet. At his last follow-up, he was 3.25 years, was tolerating diet well and weighed 12.6 kg.

Discussion

IF in children, following critical reduction in bowel length, poses considerable challenges in maintaining adequate nutrition and gastrointestinal function. In this case, the patient developed SBS after gangrenous bowel resection, which resulted in a significant loss of absorptive surface, impairing the ability to maintain fluid and electrolyte balance, and leading to intestinal failure. TPN was administered for 38 days post-operatively, which while essential in severe IF, can increase the risk of IFALD [2]. IFALD is defined as a spectrum encompassing cholestasis progressing to biliary cirrhosis, steatohepatitis, and gallbladder disease, in patients with IF with prolonged TPN use, excluding other causes of liver injury. The incidence of IFALD secondary to prolonged TPN in children is estimated to be 49.8% [3]. IFALD may stabilize/reverse with promotion of intestinal adaptation, as in our patient with the introduction of continuous oral feeds [3].

Cholestasis is a frequently observed complication of TPN, though its exact cause remains unknown. Proposed mechanisms include disruptions in bile salt metabolism due to prematurity, as well as toxic effects of TPN on the liver [4]. In our patient, UDCA was introduced to manage biliary sludge, as well as for IFALD. It works by competitively inhibiting ileal absorption of toxic endogenous bile salts [5]. In addition to this, UDCA has several beneficial effects on the liver, including cytoprotective, anti-apoptotic, immunomodulatory, and antioxidant properties [5].

Patients with SBS often experience hypergastrinemia, which can lower luminal pH and exacerbate intestinal fluid losses. This pH reduction impairs fat absorption by inactivating pancreatic lipase and deconjugating bile salts [6]. Our patient was treated with pantoprazole as an antisecretory agent for 2 weeks. Prolonged use was avoided as it can worsen SIBO and lead to nutritional deficiencies [7]. SIBO is characterized by the excessive proliferation of specific microorganisms in the small intestine disrupting normal physiological processes. In children, SIBO is a common cause of chronic abdominal pain, bloating, diarrhea, and flatulence [8]. There are several endogenous defense mechanisms which prevent SIBO including gastric acid, intestinal motility, properly functioning ileocecal valve, production of secretory immunoglobulin A, and bacteriostatic properties of pancreatic juice and bile [9]. Due to the difficulty in obtaining duodenal samples for culture, treatment is typically initiated on an empirical basis. In our patient, rifaximin was prescribed empirically. DHA, an omega-3 fatty acid, was added to promote intestinal adaptation. Omega-3 s reduce the risk of initial hepatic injury by minimizing cholestasis, potentially through two mechanisms: decreasing the phytosterol load and directly regulating bile flow via eicosanoid-mediated pathways. By modulating the immune response, omega-3 PUFAs also lower the risk of progressive liver damage, including steatohepatitis and fibrosis, that may follow an initial cholestatic insult [10].

Little data is available on the prognosis and long-term outcomes of IFALD in children. Torres et al. [11] found that out of 89 patients with IFALD, 81% achieved enteral autonomy, 99% normalized serum bilirubin without medical treatment, 2.2% required liver transplant, and overall survival was 97%. Valamparampil et al. [12] found about 33.5%–40.7% of their overall cohort of pediatric IFALD over a period of 30-years were managed by intestinal rehabilitation alone and did not require transplant.
