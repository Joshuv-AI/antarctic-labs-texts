Consensus-based framework for assessing social prescribing schemes in primary healthcare
Authors: A M Carrera-Noguero, M Guilabert Mora, V Pérez-Jover, M Pola-Garcia, J J Mira Solves, M P Astier-Peña, Consortium EvaLRA
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSocial Prescribing Schemes (SPS) incorporate community-based activities (Health Assets, HAs) into primary healthcare (PHC) to address social determinants of health and enhance well-being. Structured evaluation frameworks are needed to ensure equity, accessibility, and measurable outcomes. This study aimed to develop a framework to assess SPS in the Spanish public healthcare system using key stakeholder input.MethodsA mixed-method approach was applied, including five nominal group discussions (NGDs) with 48 national stakeholders (public health officials, PHC teams, HA providers, and patients). Thematic analysis of NGD data identified key challenges and opportunities and with the literature review was the foundation of a two-round Delphi survey with 130 participants. The Delphi process refined 116 initial criteria across three domains (structure, process, outcomes), guided by Donabedian’s quality model.ResultsThe final model contained 91 criteria: 41 structural criteria focused on equity, accessibility, and validation of HAs; 36 process criteria highlighted patient consent, collaboration between PHC teams and HA providers, and adherence tracking; 14 outcome criteria emphasized improvements in quality of life, autonomy, and reduced healthcare use. Key barriers included information health system fragmentation, resource limitations, and regional disparities, emphasizing the need for cross-sector collaboration.ConclusionsThis framework prioritizes equity and patient-centred approaches, emphasizing the value of SPS in PHC. Policy and research efforts should focus on addressing barriers and scaling SPS to strengthen integration and improve population health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-025-02954-3.Keywords: Social prescribing, Primary healthcare, Health assets, Evaluation framework, Social determinants of health, Delphi survey

Introduction

Social Prescription Schemes (SPS) refer to the process of prescribing or referring individuals to local, non-clinical services that address social determinants of health and related issues. While SPS may adopt various models, it is common for Family Doctors (FDs), nurses, and other primary healthcare (PHC) professionals to refer individuals to these services or a link worker who can tailor connections to available resources [1–3]. These services are referred as Health Assets (HAs) which refer to factors or resources that enhance the capacity of individuals, communities, and populations to maintain health and well-being. The idea of health assets is framed within a positive and complementary paradigm focusing on community lead health creation rather than curative approaches [4].The primary objective of SPS is to equip healthcare systems with a mechanism to address social determinants of health, promote self-care, strengthen community connections, and reduce low-value healthcare utilization, such as unnecessary FD appointments, secondary care referrals, and emergency visits [5]. Thus, SPS is a valuable addition to the existing portfolio of PHC services. Implementing of SPS is inherently person-centred reflecting a commitment to tackling social determinants in PHC settings, as noted by Kimberlee [6]. However, every service delivered needs to be assessed and accountable to improve and be funded along the time.

Spanish national healthcare system and primary healthcare organization

The Spanish NHS [7] is publicly funded through taxation and provides universal coverage, with services delivered free at the point of care, except for medications, which require co-payments adjusted by income. The system is decentralized into 17 regional health services—one per autonomous community—and an additional service covering Ceuta and Melilla (North Africa).

Primary healthcare in Spain is structured around multiprofessional teams operating within PHC centres, serving registered populations within defined territories known as Basic Health Zones. These PHC teams typically comprise family doctors, paediatricians, nurses, administrative staff, social workers, physiotherapists, and midwives, delivering longitudinal, comprehensive, and coordinated care while ensuring integration with secondary care services. Additionally, PHC teams are responsible for coordinating preventive and community health initiatives, including vaccination campaigns and health education programs. Most healthcare professionals in these teams are employed as civil servants under regional health authorities.

Social Prescribing Schemes in the Spanish context

The Spanish National Healthcare System (NHS) integrates a community health approach within PHC teams [8] encompassing prevention, health promotion, and family and community care. Recently, the Spanish Health Ministry launched a National Strategy for Community Health [9], now being implemented in some Autonomous Regions and included SPS within the National PHC portfolio of services [10]. This strategy envisions different levels of PHC involvement in Social Prescribing: 1) Individual Level or Social Prescription Schemes: the patient encounter integrates a community health perspective, with activities or HAs prescribed to support health improvements [11]. HAs are commonly available community resources that foster personal skills, enhance self-esteem, and promote individual and collective health empowerment. In a SPS, PHC teams formally prescribe HAs during clinical encounters to support well-being and health management [12]. 2) Group Level: Health education sessions target groups within the community. 3) Collective Level: Community-led actions promote health, effectively transforming the community into a centre for health promotion. HAs providers involved in SPS span a range of stakeholders, including local public social services, regional social and public health authorities, community initiatives, foundations, and private entities.

Although SPS is recognized as an individual health promotion strategy within patient-professional interactions, its implementation reflects the maturity of PHC teams in integrating community health services within basic health zones. Each PHC team maps the HAs available in their territory, identifying community resources and activities tailored to specific health needs. This mapping process raises awareness of community health roles and fosters connections between PHC teams and HA providers [1].

Once identified, HA providers are invited to undergo formal validation by public health (PH) authorities, contributing to the establishment of an SPS repository within the regional health system. In most Spanish regions, PH authorities are responsible for validating and registering HA activities in a regional repository, which can be linked to PHC electronic health records (EHR). This integration allows SPS registrations in the EHR to associate HA interventions with specific health conditions when PHC professionals directly prescribe HA interventions to patients during consultation encounters, aiming to address the underlying social factors contributing to their reason for consultation.

HA providers can be either public or private non-profit organizations. Public HA providers include local or regional public social care services and public sports centres, while non-profit organizations, such as the Spanish Association Against Cancer, frequently represent the private sector.

One significant challenge in implementing and assessing SPS in PHC teams is coordinating the involvement and information of the different stakeholders. Effective collaboration and accountability across these stakeholders – PHC public providers, public health authorities, and HA providers- are often complex to manage. The SPS, widely recognized as individual health promotion activities supported through collaborations between primary healthcare and public health entities, also play a vital role in enhancing patients'quality of life and overall health outcomes. Furthermore, SPS have shown significant potential in strengthening coordination between primary healthcare and social care services, promoting integrative care, and reducing unnecessary medical interventions [13]. It However, measuring these outcomes can be challenging due to different limitations: 1) the time required to observe effects along the time and the complexity of establishing causative relationships between HAs and improved well-being, 2) different funding mechanisms for HAs public providers and private providers, 3) Non interoperability of information systems, even within public providers, 4) Limited global perspective on ensuring governance accountability across regions, with insufficient emphasis on valuing health assets as crucial as medications for community health improvement.

Despite the challenges posed by multistakeholder involvement in evaluating SPS establishing an evaluation framework is essential to ensure appropriate resource allocation and foster SPS development within PHC teams. Given the complexity of SPS, a comprehensive evaluation model should integrate multiple tools and data sources to provide meaningful insights [14, 15]. This paper aims to offer an evaluation framework for SPS in Spanish Primary Healthcare Public Providers Services, considering the different stakeholders’ perspectives and outlining criteria for discussion to establish a strategic evaluation model to solidify SPS as a structured service within the PHC portfolio.

Material and methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Spanish Biomedical Research Law 14/2007 and approved by the Ethics Committee of Clinical Research of Aragón (CEICA) in its meeting on January 13, 2021 (Act No. 01/2021) C.I. PI20/606.

To develop a consensus-based framework for evaluating Social Prescribing Schemes (SPS) within Spain’s public primary healthcare system, we followed a structured, multi-phase approach, as illustrated in Fig. 1. We began with a narrative literature review focused on social prescribing, health assets, primary care, and evaluation frameworks [16, 17]. To guide our evaluation model, we adopted the Donabedian model of healthcare quality assessment, which categorizes indicators into three dimensions: structure, process, and outcomes [18].

Process to define an SPS model in PHC

Building on the literature review, we conducted five nominal group discussions (NGDs) with 48 key stakeholders—including patients, healthcare managers, community activity promoters, public health and health promotion officers, and primary care professionals. Thematic analysis of these sessions led to the identification of 116 preliminary evaluation criteria across the three Donabedian dimensions.

Subsequently, a two-round Delphi study was conducted involving over 101 experts to assess the level of consensus for each item. Participants were recruited from four of the five main stakeholder groups identified in the study. Patients were not invited to participate in this phase, as the evaluation model was primarily professional-oriented and focused on the definition of quality criteria. As shown in Fig. 1, 91 criteria were ultimately validated for use in assessing the implementation and performance of SPS in public primary healthcare settings. Detailed information about the research project can be found in the Supplementary Materials, organized into multiple tables labelled as Table S, followed by their respective numbers.

Literature narrative review

A narrative literature search on SPS was conducted across PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Reviews, and relevant publications from academic institutions and public health authorities. The search focused on the assessment of SPS from the perspective of public health service delivery. However, the specificity of the topic made it difficult to retrieve directly relevant results.

Nominal Group Discussions (NGDs)

To gather insights, nominal group discussions were conducted with four key stakeholder groups: public health administrators, PHCT professionals, HA providers, and patients who had participated in SPS. Professionals representing these groups were invited based on predefined selection criteria. Table S1 provides details on the number of participants, their professional backgrounds, and selection criteria. Additionally, an outline of open-ended questions and visual materials used in the sessions are available.

Five NGDs were conducted via videoconference to accommodate participants from different autonomous communities. The discussions were facilitated using the open-source collaboration platform MURAL (https://app.mural.co), enabling real-time data collection and visualization to support consensus-building. Each session lasted approximately 90 min, and all participants provided informed consent for the recording and transcription of discussions for research purposes. To ensure the quality and transparency of the qualitative research component, we followed the COREQ (Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research) [19] guidelines, which are available in the supplementary material.

Qualitative analysis

Data were analysed using a thematic framework approach [20], as guided by the COREQ checklist. One researcher (MG) transcribed and conducted initial coding. Two additional researchers independently coded the transcripts (VPJ and MPA), and discrepancies were resolved through discussion with the wider research team. Categories were developed iteratively and refined through constant comparison across stakeholder groups. The final analytical framework included: social and community impact of SPS; characteristics of SPS and health assets (HA); criteria for HA assessment (relevance, effectiveness, efficiency, usefulness, sensitivity); and implementation factors such as facilitators, barriers, equity, and gender considerations. These categories supported the development of a structured framework for evaluating SPS in PHC settings.

Delphi study

Based on findings from the literature review and nominal groups, the research team developed key criteria for SPS assessment, which informed the design of a Delphi questionnaire. The questionnaire consisted of 116 items across three domains: structure, process, and outcomes. The questionnaire is available in Table S2. A total of 130 individuals representing the relevant stakeholder groups in this study were invited to participate (public health administrators, PHCT professionals, HA providers and Health System Managers). Additionally, professionals who did not fit into these predefined groups were classified as'Others'(including social workers, occupational therapists, physiotherapists, and academics). It was an extended group of experts, selected according to the same inclusion criteria used for the Nominal Group Discussions, with the exception that patients were not included in this phase. The sample included participants from different autonomous regions, professional backgrounds, age groups, and sexes (Table S3).

Delphi questionnaire structure

Structural Criteria: Ensures accessibility, equity, continuity, healthcare quality and patient safety of HAs. Key considerations include equitable access for vulnerable populations (e.g., ethnic minorities, different age and gender groups), cost-free or low-cost services, continuous availability, and validation by health authorities. Essential professionals identified for implementing SPS include nurses, social workers, and family doctors.

Process Criteria: This examines the alignment of interventions with referral reasons (e.g., loneliness) as well as the consideration of various types of HA. Additionally, it assesses key aspects such as patient satisfaction, provider involvement, and the degree of community engagement.

Outcome Criteria: This evaluates patient-centred outcomes such as improved health literacy, motivation, quality of life, daily autonomy, and reduced medication use. It also monitors decreases in primary care visits and medication frequency to assess health impact and service utilization.

Delphi process and data analysis

The first Delphi round was launched in November 2021 using the Delphi platform of Miguel Hernández University (Elche, Spain). Participants received personal invitations and a reminder after 15 days if they had not yet responded.

Delphi results were analysed based on the following criteria. An item was retained if at least 80% of participants rated it ≥ 8/10. An item was removed if fewer than 25% rated it < 6/10. Items rated 6–8/10 were re-evaluated in the second round using the same retention criteria. For each item, mean scores and coefficient of variation were calculated to support final item selection.

In addition to the structured items evaluated in each round, a set of additional open-ended questions was included in both rounds of the Delphi process. These questions were answered voluntarily by the participants and aimed to explore aspects that might not have been fully captured by the predefined set of criteria. The inclusion of these open-ended items allowed for the collection of complementary qualitative information, which was subsequently analyzed to refine and further define the final model. Results

The literature review informed the development of the nominal group discussion (NGD) questions and provided a deeper understanding of SPS for building up the Delphi questionnaire and analysis.

Results of the nominal groups discussion

A total of five nominal discussion groups (NGDs) were conducted, involving a diverse range of stakeholders. Participants included representatives from regional public health authorities (e.g., Asturias, Aragón, Cataluña, Baleares, Euskadi, and others), healthcare professionals (primary care physicians, nurses, social workers, a physiotherapist, a pharmacist, and a town councillor), and patients engaged in social prescribing activities. The participant cohort represented a balanced gender distribution (17 males and 38 females) and covered a wide geographic and professional spectrum, including both national and UK-based contributors. Patient participants had varied health backgrounds, including diabetes, cardiovascular disease, cancer, memory-related conditions, and autoimmune disorders. Detailed information about participants and the discussion questions is available in Supplementary Table S1. Thematic analysis identified distinct but complementary perspectives across stakeholder groups, which are summarized below. Supporting quotations are provided in Supplementary Tables S4–S6.

Public health authorities

Public health representatives emphasized the need for a standardized, regionally applicable framework for HA validation. Current approaches were reported to be fragmented, lacking uniform criteria for aspects such as cost, evidence of effectiveness, and provider credentialing. Key structural challenges included the absence of integrated information systems, limited intersectoral coordination, and insufficient resources for registering and validating HAs.

Participants recommended the development of a scorecard to monitor SPS implementation, evaluate regional progress, and align HA deployment with population health indicators. Ensuring equity of access was considered a priority, with a preference for HAs that were free or low-cost and accessible irrespective of gender, age, or ethnicity. Public health authorities were identified as the most appropriate entities to oversee HA validation and registration, with periodic credential renewal to ensure quality and safety. Cross-sector collaboration with sports, social, and cultural services was proposed to expand HA availability.

Healthcare Professionals and Health Asset providers

Primary healthcare professionals described their role in operationalizing SPS, including identifying HA providers, conducting field visits, and integrating HA information into clinical workflows. Mapping of community resources was conducted to document available activities, accessibility, and engagement mechanisms. Once validated through the public health authority, HAs were registered in a regional repository linked to the electronic health record (EHR), allowing formal prescription by PHC teams.

Both healthcare professionals and HA providers noted the importance of aligning HAs with specific clinical conditions to ensure targeted application. A list of commonly used diagnostic categories for SPS is provided in the supplementary materials. HA providers reported variability in PHC team engagement; while some teams demonstrated high commitment to SPS, others lacked continuity in prescribing and follow-up practices.

Patient perspectives

All patient participants had experience with prescribed HAs. While initial reactions ranged from scepticism to confusion, most reported positive outcomes after participation, including improvements in physical and emotional health, reduced reliance on pharmacological treatments, and enhanced social connectedness. Patients emphasized the importance of clear communication by healthcare providers regarding the benefits of HA, which was often lacking at the outset.

Results of the delphi study

Participation overview

In the first round of the Delphi process, 101 participants completed the questionnaire (participation rate: 77.7%): 49 PHCT professionals, 26 public health administrators, 9 health asset (HA) providers, 13 health system managers, and 4 classified as ‘Others’ (including social workers, physiotherapists, occupational therapists, and academics). In the second round, 97 participants completed the questionnaire (participation rate 96%): 49 PHCT professionals, 26 public health administrators, 9 HA providers, and 13 health system managers. No participants from the'Others'category took part in the second round. The results were analysed to identify those criteria that reached consensus among experts, as well as items that were not accepted for inclusion in the SPS.

Accepted criteria

The criteria accepted in the Delphi process were characterized by high mean scores (≥ 8.5), low variability among raters (coefficient of variation, C.V. ≤ 20%), and a significant proportion of scores in the ≥ 9 category (≥ 50%). These results indicate broad agreement among experts regarding their significance for the successful implementation and evaluation of RFAs.

The structural criteria focused on equity, accessibility, and professional involvement. Equity principles, such as ensuring access for vulnerable groups (mean: 9.2, C.V.: 17.2%, N ≥ 9: 86.14%), were widely accepted. Additionally, the engagement of key professionals such as nurses (mean: 9.4, N ≥ 9: 87.13%) and social workers (mean: 9.4, N ≥ 9: 86.14%) was strongly endorsed as essential to the implementation and monitoring of SPSs (Table 1).

Non-accepted criteria

Non-accepted criteria generally showed lower levels of agreement, with mean scores below 8.0, higher variability (C.V. > 20%), and fewer than 50% of participants assigning ratings ≥ 9. These criteria were considered either less relevant or less feasible for integrating into the framework.

For structural criteria, items focusing on cost and resources tended to receive lower prioritization. The concept of economic accessibility through low costs scored poorly (mean: 4.8, C.V.: 60.9%, N ≥ 9: 10.89%), likely reflecting practical concerns related to funding and the perceived importance of equity over cost. Similarly, items requiring frequent intermediate evaluations of health assets (mean: 7.6, C.V.: 28.2%, N ≥ 9: 39.6%) were viewed as overly resource intensive and impractical for routine implementation (Table S7).

For the process-related criteria, low consensus was observed for items such as patients not completing the entire program associated with the recommended health asset (mean: 7.6, C.V.: 24.3%, N ≥ 9: 32.32%). Experts also demonstrated limited agreement on the importance of reducing work absence days as a potential outcome criterion (mean: 7.2, C.V.: 28%, N ≥ 9: 26.26%), suggesting that socioeconomic indicators might require further exploration or refinement (Table S8).

Regarding outcome-related criteria, issues with harder-to-measure outcomes, such as reduction in clinical parameters (blood pressure, cholesterol, etc.), showed lower ratings (mean: 7.3, N ≥ 9: 26.26%). Similarly, reduction in medication consumption was not considered a key indicator of success (mean: 8.1, N ≥ 9: 36.08%) (Table S9).

Rejected items typically addressed regional variations in SPS requirements or operational aspects that lacked uniform applicability. For instance, criteria involving highly specific social determinants or region-dependent processes were not retained in the final framework. A full list of rejected items, along with their scores, is available in supplementary material (Table S7-8–9).

Key observations from open-ended responses

Qualitative feedback provided additional insights, helping to contextualize the quantitative findings. Experts emphasized the importance of equity, flexibility, and community-focused indicators, while also highlighting potential challenges. Participants noted the need to minimize stigmatization and bias in evaluations, suggesting systematic methods for integrating family and community support. Additionally, some raised concerns about the efficiency of extensive evaluation procedures during routine implementation and advocated for prioritizing a smaller set of key indicators that are practical and informative (Table S10).

Discussion

This study, rooted in the Spanish context, provides valuable insights into the criteria for implementing SPS and HA in PHC. The Delphi technique and nominal groups were used to identify and prioritize structural, process, and outcome criteria for SPS. SPS is included in the portfolio of primary care services in various European PHC systems. Defining criteria and an assessment model to ensure the quality, equity, and safety of SPS is crucial for its success [21]. We used the model of SPS in PHC professional-patient encounter in the context of the Primary Health Care Services provided free of charge in a Spanish Regional Public Provision Health System as a case study [1]. Our research offers a framework and a set of basic criteria to evaluate the development of SPS in PHC from the perspective of a public healthcare provider with the support of qualitative techniques. The criteria identified are based on the four main stakeholders’ perspectives (public health officers, PHC professionals, health assets providers, and patients) and are categorized using Donabedian’s quality model into structure, process, and outcome criteria [18].

Structure criteria

The Delphi study prioritized equity and accessibility as the most critical structural criteria. Equity ensures access for vulnerable groups, regardless of gender, age, ethnicity, or socioeconomic status, while accessibility emphasizes proximity and affordability. These findings resonate with international studies emphasizing the importance of equity in health promotion programs [22, 23]. Experts consider crucial to have a validation process by health authorities to ensure these HA are safe for patients, like how medications are validated. A recent review also highlights the necessity of validating health assets and ensuring they are accessible to all populations, regardless of socioeconomic status [24].

Another significant issue is the cost. The Spanish Primary Healthcare Portfolio of services is free of charge at the point of delivery, except for medications, which have co-payments based on income [25]. Experts agreed that if SPS is recommended by public PHC team, it should be free or very low-cost. Therefore, HA with associated costs should not be included in the Public HA repository, implying that most HA must be provided by social services, public administrations, local public services, or non-profit organizations. Husk et al. addresses different models of social prescribing, including financial considerations in service delivery, and suggests that free or low-cost access is a key factor for community HA to be effectively used by PHC team [2]. This is consistent with recent international comparisons across high-income countries, which show that financing models and accessibility vary but low-cost access remains a critical factor for successful implementation [26].

Process criteria

Process criteria define the pathway for implementing SPS. The process begins during PHC consultations, where encourage patients to engage with HA as part of their treatment for specific health conditions or as a health-promotion strategy across various life stages or transitions. These criteria emphasize the importance of securing patient agreement with the proposed HA and fostering active participation. To ensure the success of this process, a strong collaboration between HA providers and the PHC team is crucial. HA providers are also expected to report back to the PHC team on patient adherence and address any challenges encountered during the activity. Given that HA providers typically operate outside the healthcare system, establishing agreements with third-sector organizations may be necessary to enable effective information sharing on patient progress. The relevance to care for the ecosystem is highlighted internationally [27]. This process requires regulation, potentially through patient contracts or by integrating mechanisms to exchange information between social and health information systems as Polley et al. suggested3.

The prioritization of loneliness and mood disorders reflects the prevalence of these issues in Spain, particularly among the elderly. SPS has been shown to mitigate loneliness and improve mental health outcomes [28, 29]. The focus on chronic conditions like diabetes and cardiovascular disease underscores the role of SPS in complementing medical treatments. This is consistent with evidence that community-based interventions improve self-management and reduce healthcare utilization [30].

Outcome criteria

The prioritized outcome criteria emphasize patient-centred outcomes, such as improved quality of life, autonomy, and emotional skills. These findings align with the principles of person-centred care, which focuses on empowering patients and addressing their holistic needs. Several HA had evidence supporting their effectiveness in improving chronic conditions, such as physical exercise [31], memory exercises and music therapy for cognitive disorders. However, direct evidence for other HA is lacking due to the complexity of designing studies that account for multiple health-affecting factors and long-term effects. New research methods combining multiple data sources are now on development [32, 33].

Polley et al. apply a wide range of assessment measures, including income, crime, housing, work, and volunteering, which are beyond the health service perspective in this study.

Strengths

This study offers an evaluation framework for SPS with specific items, analysing the barriers and facilitators of SPS in a public primary health care model. Thus, it creates a pathway to establish a robust SPS in primary health care systems.

Limitations

Our analysis focused on SPS in a public PHC regional service in Spain, which offer a common portfolio of services, including community-oriented activities in assigned territories. Only public local providers participated in the nominal groups, coming from town hall social areas, local public sports facilities and sometimes regional social services. The SPS model described is based on a public, free-of-charge PHC model, making international comparisons difficult.

Policymakers in many countries promote collaboration between health care organizations and other sectors as a route to improving population health. Local collaborations have been developed for decades. The collaboration between local health care and non-health care providers might contribute to better population health. But we know little about which kinds of collaborations work, for whom, and in what contexts. The benefits of collaboration may be hard to measure and maybe overestimated by policymakers. Ultimately, local collaborations should be understood as one component within a wider system of factors and interventions interacting to shape population health [34].

Then the questionnaire was developed based on a prior narrative review and a structured nominal group discussion, which provided national and international insights into social prescribing schemes—their components, key actors, and implementation contexts. This process supported the creation of a conceptual model adapted to the Spanish primary care system. While the model aims to be comprehensive and transferable, we acknowledge that the questionnaire reflects the specific characteristics of Spanish system. The formulation of indicators to operationalize and evaluate the model in different settings and health information systems will require a subsequent phase of work, tailored to the diversity of primary care systems. This staged approach is supported by the work of Fixsen A et al. who emphasize the importance of first developing context-sensitive frameworks through stakeholder engagement before establishing evaluation metrics [35].

Regarding “Dimension 2.1, Process Criteria: Reasons for Consultation”, it omits social issues such as housing and food which are clear social determinants of health. These determinants are complex, multifaceted issues that require coordinated action across various sectors of the welfare state, including housing, employment, education, and social services. However, SPS offer a valuable approach within the healthcare system to address these determinants from a community-based perspective. By connecting individuals with non-clinical resources and support services, SPS help to bridge medical care with broader social support, contributing to more integrated and person-centred care.

The resulting Delphi assessment model may have many items. However, all participants agreed that this SPS assessment model is a relevant tool for improving. This comprehensive set incorporates all stakeholder perspectives and can be applied at different evaluation stages as launching SPS, evaluating HA processes, and measuring outcomes after several months.

Conclusions

We produced an SPS assessment model to consolidate SPS as PHC portfolio service in PHC systems. The accepted criteria emphasize the critical role of equity, professional engagement, community-building outcomes, quality and patient safety in the implementation of HAs. By contrast, non-accepted criteria reveal areas requiring further refinement, including feasibility issues and the prioritization of measurable outcomes that directly reflect the impact of HA. These results provide a clear foundation for advancing the SPS process of community HA. Challenges such as building evidence on the relationship between HA and health improvements, the lack of integrated health information systems, and the lack of coordination with social services, sports facilities, local authorities, and PHC teams must be addressed. A new political framework is needed to facilitate SPS for the overall health benefit of communities.
