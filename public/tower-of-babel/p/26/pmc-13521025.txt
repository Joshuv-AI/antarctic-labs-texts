Microbiota contributes to regulates the external genitalia development through gut-testis axis in male geese
Authors: Yating Li, Qin Li, Xin Zhang, Yushi Wang, Guangliang Gao, Donghong Chen, Simeng Qin, Zhifu Cui, Lingbin Liu, Anfang Liu, Haiwei Wang, Qigui Wang, Bincheng Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGeese is one of the few poultry species with complete external genitalia, and the external genitalia abnormal development has become an important factor limiting the reproductive efficiency of the goose industry. Recent studies have shown that the gut microbiota plays an important role in regulating male reproductive processes, but its regulatory mechanisms in male geese's external genitalia development remain unclear. In this study, male geese with normal development (ND) and abnormal development (AD) external genitalia were selected as the research object, and multi-omics were used to investigate the regulatory of the microbe-mediated gut-testis axis on external genitalia development. At the transcriptomic level, we identified key DEGs (KNG1, P2RY4, SSTR5, and HRH3) in the testis and external genitalia between ND and AD groups, which were significantly enriched in the neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction pathway. Metabolomics analysis revealed that DMs in the ND and AD groups were significantly enriched in pathways related to aromatic amino acid metabolism and neural signal transduction. Furthermore, metagenomic results showed that the ND group was identified key bacterial genera g_Blautia and g_Faecousia affecting external genitalia development, which were associated with SCFAs synthesis and neuroendocrine signaling regulation. Integrated with multi-omics data, it was revealed that gut-derived neuroactive metabolic signals may participate in the molecular regulation of external genitalia development in male goose by modulating GPCRs signaling. Our findings not only provide new insights into the gut-testis axis regulates the development of external genitalia in male geese, but also contribute to improving the reproductive performance of male geese.Keywords: Goose, External genitalia, Gut microbiota, Gut-testis axis, Multi-omics

Introduction

In nature, all birds reproduce through internal fertilization, but only 3% of male birds have complete external genitalia (Brennan, 2022). Among poultry, male geese are one of the few species with complete external genitalia, whose development is crucial for both natural mating and artificial insemination. Unlike poultry such as chickens that rely on "cloacal kissing" for sperm transfer, geese achieve internal fertilization via their fully developed external genitalia (Herrera et al., 2013). However, in production practice, we found that male geese exhibit abnormal development of the external genitalia, which leads to decreased fertilization rates during natural mating (Yan et al., 2024). At present, there is still limited information on the phenotype characteristics and genetic mechanisms underlying the development of the external genitalia in male geese. But in mammals, recent studies have found that gut microbes and their metabolites can participate in host endocrine regulation, maintain immune homeostasis, and regulate gonadal development through gut-testis axis (Hao et al., 2022; Li et al., 2022). However, studies on the regulation of the external genitalia development in male geese by gut microbiota have not been reported. Therefore, efforts to elucidate the mechanism by which gut microbes regulate the development of the external genitalia in male geese may thus highlight new approaches to improve male goose reproductive capacity.

Recently, the role of gut microbiota in host reproductive regulation has attracted increasing attention. Increasing research indicates that gut microbiota and their metabolites can influence male reproductive system function by regulating immune homeostasis, energy metabolism, and steroid hormone synthesis via gut-testis axis (Leelani et al., 2023). In mammals, studies have found that gut microbiota can indirectly maintain testis function and testosterone homeostasis by regulating the host's immune status, energy metabolism, and steroid hormone synthesis (Cai et al., 2022; Lv et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024a). Further research indicates that gut microbiota imbalance can cause seminiferous epithelial structural disorders and increased spermatogenic cell apoptosis, ultimately leading to male infertility (Zhang et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2024b). In chicken, altering the structure of gut microbiota through probiotic supplementation or antibiotic intervention can significantly affect testis development and semen quality (Ferenczi et al., 2025). In ducks, the gut-endocrine-testis axis can mediate microbial production of SCFAs, which are significantly correlated with sex hormone levels (Zhu et al., 2024). Furthermore, multi-omics studies have revealed that metabolites derived from gut microbiota can regulate the expression of steroid synthesis and spermatogenesis-related genes, thereby affecting male fertility (Zhao et al., 2021). Recently, comparative transcriptome analysis revealed that the "neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction" pathway regulates external genitalia development in male geese through PLCB1-mediated Wnt signaling (Tang et al., 2022). However, the mechanism by which gut microbiota regulates the development of the external genitalia in male geese remains unknown.

Currently, multi-omics technologies have been widely used to analyze the genetic mechanisms of poultry reproductive behavior across genetic, metabolic, and microbiota levels (Shi et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2023). Based on this, our study aimed to investigate the potential mechanism by which the gut-testis axis regulates the differential development of external genitalia in male geese, through integrating metagenomics, metabolomics, and transcriptome sequencing. In this work, we analyzed the testis and external genitalia phenotypes of male geese between normal development (ND) and abnormal development (AD) groups. Subsequently, transcriptome reveal potential genetic changes in testis and external genitalia. Furthermore, metagenomics and metabolomics revealed differences in the compositional of gut microbiota and their correlation with reproductive organs development in ND and AD male geese. This study provides a novel perspective for elucidating the molecular mechanism underlying reproductive disorders in male geese, and lays a theoretical and practical foundation for improving the fecundity and breeding value of geese through microbial intervention in the future.

Materials and methods

Ethics approval and consent to participate

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Southwest University (Certification No. LAC2024-2-0092) and conducted in accordance with the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics guidelines of Southwest University.

Experimental animals and sample collection

A total of 100 healthy male Sichuan White geese with similar body weight were obtained from the Poultry Breeding Experimental Farm of the Chongqing Academy of Animal Sciences (Rongchang, Chongqing, China). All geese were hatched on the same day and raised under same management conditions. The external genitalia phenotypes of all male geese were as recorded at 360 days of age, including the external genitalia basal diameter (BD; transverse width at the base of the external genitalia), natural length (NL; length from the tip to the base in the naturally extended state), straightening length (SL; maximum length from the tip to the base after manual stretching), spiral number (SN; number of spiral coils in the natural state), and the presence or absence of penile spines. Based on the development of the external genitalia, individuals with well-developed, spiral-shaped external genitalia covered with penile spines were assigned to the ND group, while those with degenerated or absent spiral structures and penile spines were assigned to the AD group. Twelve healthy male geese of similar body weight (5.8 ± 0.31 kg) were randomly selected from each group. After fasting for 12 hours, the geese were euthanized via jugular vein exsanguination. Subsequently, the left testis, intact external genitalia, and cecum contents were collected for histological, transcriptomic, non-targeted metabolomic, and metagenomic analyses. The length and weight of the testis and external genitalia were also recorded. Except for the histological samples, all samples were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and subsequently transferred to a −80°C freezer for storage pending further analysis.

Histological analysis

Testis and external genitalia samples fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde were dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. The sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) according to standard protocol. The stained sections were then observed and imaged under the camera microscope BA410-Digital (MOTIC CHINA GROUP CO., LTD. Xiamen, China) to analyze the characteristics of the testis and external genitalia tissue structures in the two groups, in order to assess inter-individual histological differences.

Transcriptome sequencing analysis

Three biological replicates from both the ND and AD groups were selected for transcriptome analysis of testis and external genitalia tissues. Total RNA was extracted using the MJZol Total RNA Extraction Kit (Majorbio, Shanghai, China). RNA quality was assessed using a Nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and an Agilent 5300 Bioanalyzer (Agilent, USA). Samples with total RNA ≥1 μg, RNA concentration ≥30 ng/μL, OD260/280 ratios of 1.8 - 2.2, and RQN >6.5 were used for library preparation. Libraries were constructed using the Illumina Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, USA). After quality filtering, clean reads were aligned to the Anser cygnoides reference genome (PRJEB75655) using HISAT2. DEGs were identified using DESeq2 with thresholds of |log₂FC| ≥1 and p < 0.05. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed on the DEGs.

Metabolomics sequencing analysis

Cecum contents from four individuals per group were subjected to untargeted metabolomic analysis (Shanghai Meiji Biopharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd.). For metabolite extraction, 100 mg of cecum contents from male geese was placed in 2 mL centrifuge tube, and grinding beads and 800 μL of extraction buffer (methanol: water = 4:1) were added to extract metabolites. The samples were then subjected to cryogenic grinding (−10 °C, 50 Hz, 6 min) and low-temperature sonication (5 °C, 40 kHz, 30 min), followed by standing at −20 °C for 30 min, centrifuged at 4 °C and 13000 g for 15 min, and the supernatant was used for analysis. The supernatant was used for analysis. Equal volumes of all samples were mixed to prepare quality control (QC) samples. One QC sample was inserted every 5-10 samples during the analysis to assess system stability. Metabolomics analysis was performed using a Thermo Fisher Scientific UHPLC—Orbitrap Exploris 240 system (Shanghai Meiji Biopharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd.). Samples were separated using an HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 µm). Mobile phase A was 0.1% formic acid/water/acetonitrile (95/5), and mobile phase B was 0.1% formic acid/acetonitrile/isopropanol/water (47.5/47.5/5). The flow rate was 0.40 mL/min, and the column temperature was 40°C. Raw LC-MS data were processed using Progenesis QI software (Waters, USA) for peak identification, correction, and quantification. Metabolite identification was based on the HMDB, Metlin, and a self-built database from Meiji. PCA and PLS-DA analyses were performed using the R package (version: 1.6.2). Metabolites with significant differences were screened using VIP > 1 and p < 0.05. After differentially expressed metabolites were annotated using the KEGG database, pathway enrichment analysis was performed using SciPy. Stats, and Fisher's exact test was used to identify significantly relevant metabolic pathways.

Metagenomic sequencing analysis

Four individuals were selected from each of the ND and AD groups, and cecum content samples were collected. All samples were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for later use. Total DNA was extracted from the cecum contents of male geese, and DNA quality was assessed using Quantus Fluorometer and agarose gel electrophoresis. Sequencing libraries were prepared and paired-end sequencing was performed on an Illumina platform. Raw reads were filtered using Fastp software to obtain high-quality sequences. Reads were aligned to the host genome sequence using BWA software, and reads with high alignment similarity were removed to obtain contaminant-free reads for subsequent analysis. High-quality reads were assembled using MEGAHIT software, and ORF prediction was performed on contigs within the assembly using Prodigal v2.6.3 software. Genes with a nucleic acid length of 100 bp or longer were selected and translated into amino acid sequences. The predicted gene sequences were clustered using CD-HIT software to construct a non-redundant gene set, and the base sequences of the genes in the non-redundant gene set were obtained. Alpha diversity (Shannon and Simpson method) and beta diversity (principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on the Bray-Curtis method) were analyzed using majorbio tools (https://www.majorbio.com/). Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size (LEfSe) processing was performed using LEfSe software, and microbial functions were predicted based on the KEGG database.

Real-time PCR verification

Four significantly DEGs (KNG1, P2RY4, HRH3, and SSTR5) were selected for qRT-PCR to validate the RNA-Seq results. Previously, total RNA extracted from the testis and external genitalia were reverse transcribed into cDNA using a RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo, MA, USA). Primer 5.0 was used to design the primers (Table 1). A BLAST search against the reference genome was then carried out to confirm primers were specific for the intended target genes. SYBR Green PCR Super Mix (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) and a Bio-Rad CFX96 real-time PCR detection system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) were used for qRT-PCR, and each sample was assayed three times. The reaction was performed at 95°C for 10 s, 60°C for 60 s, and 95°C for 15 s, after which it was slowly heated from 60°C to 99°C. The GAPDH was used as a housekeeping gene. The 2−ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001) was used for normalization of the qRT-PCR results, after which the normalized data was used for statistical analysis, and p < 0.05 was considered significantly different.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 10 and SPSS Statistics 27 software. The means of the external genitalia and testis traits between NE and ED through ANOVA testing. Furthermore, we analyzed significance of different traits between NE and ED through t-tests. A significance level of p < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

Phenotype and histological characteristics analysis of testis and external genitalia between ND and AD group

Our results showed that the external genitalia of the ND group were generally well-developed, while the AD group exhibited significant atrophy and degeneration (Fig. 1a). Phenotypic data analysis indicated that the external genitalia natural length, straightening length, basal diameter, weight, and external genitalia index, as well as the testis transverse diameter, diameter, dorsoventral diameter, weight, and testis index in the ND group were significantly higher than those in the AD group (Fig. 1b-e). Correlation analysis showed that all indicators between the testis and external genitalia were significantly positively correlated, indicating synergistic changes in the development of the two organs (Figure. S1). Furthermore, HE staining analysis revealed that the seminiferous tubules of the testis in the ND group were morphologically intact, structurally clear, and had tightly and orderly arranged cells; and the transverse diameter, diameter of the lymphatic vessels and the CAS of external genitalia were significantly larger than those in the AD group (Fig. 1f and g). The above results indicate that abnormal development of the external genitalia is not only reflected in external phenotype, but also accompanied by differences in tissue structure.

Transcriptome analysis of the testis and external genitalia between ND and AD group

To elucidate the potential regulatory mechanisms underlying the differential development of the external genitalia in male geese, we performed RNA-seq analysis on testis and external genitalia tissues in the ND and AD groups. PCA analysis revealed a clear separation between the testis and external genitalia in the ND and AD groups (Fig. 2a). In this study, 992 (421 upregulated, 571 downregulated) and 1567 DEGs (1057 upregulated, 510 downregulated) were identified in the testis and external genitalia, respectively (Fig. 2b; Additional file 1). KEGG enrichment analysis of the DEGs revealed that the DEGs in both the testis and external genitalia were mainly enriched in neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, and calcium signaling pathway (Fig. 2c and d; Additional file 2). Furthermore, PPI networks were constructed for DEGs in the testis and external genitalia, and key genes including PIK3R1, KNG1, F2, IL6, SOX9, P2RY4, SSTR5, and HRH3 were identified to be associated with the development of the external genitalia in male geese (Fig. 2e and f). Co-expression network analysis of DEGs in the testis and external genitalia revealed that the KNG1 gene may regulate the development of the external genitalia in ganders via the neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction pathway (Fig. 2g and h). Four DEGs (KNG1, P2RY4, HRH3, and SSTR5) involved in the external genitalia development were selected for real-time quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR) validation of our RNA-seq results. The results showed that in the testis and external genitalia, KNG1 and P2RY4 were significantly upregulated in the ND group, whereas HRH3 and SSTR5 were significantly upregulated in the AD group; these findings are consistent with the transcriptome data and support the reliability of the RNA-seq analysis (Fig. 2i).

Metabolomic signatures of cecum contents between ND and AD group

After identifying significant differences in the transcriptomes level of the testis and external genitalia, non-targeted metabolomics analysis was performed on the cecum contents of the ND and AD groups. PCA and PLS-DA analysis showed that significant differences in box plots across both dimensions, further indicating that the data analysis was reliable (Fig. 3a and b). Subsequently, 166 DMs were identified between ND and AD groups, including 140 up-regulated and 26 down-regulated (Fig. 3c; Additional file 3). VIP value analysis based on the PLS-DA model screened key DMs, showing that metabolites with VIP > 1 mainly involved peptide metabolism, aromatic amino acid metabolism, and amino acid metabolism pathways (Fig. 3d; Additional file 4). Furthermore, KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that DMs significant enrichment in Tyrosine metabolism, Phenylalanine metabolism, Tryptophan metabolism, and neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction signaling pathway (Fig. 3e). Subsequently, cross-analysis was performed on the transcriptomic and metabolomic enriched pathways, revealing overlapping pathways such as neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction, tyrosine metabolism, and arginine and proline metabolism, among other amino acid metabolism pathways (Fig. 3g). A metabolism-pathway-enzyme network model constructed based on DMs revealed that gut-derived proteolytic enzymes (such as trypsin and granzyme A) regulate the levels of tyramine and dopamine metabolites through the neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction pathway, thereby indirectly affecting the development of the external genitalia in male geese through neuroendocrine signal transduction (Fig. 3f).

Metagenomic characteristics of cecum contents

Given that the production and transformation of intestinal metabolites depend on the structural and functional characteristics of the gut microbiota, metagenomic analysis was performed on the cecum contents between ND and AD groups. AD influenced the α diversity of microbiota in the cecum (Both the Shannon index and the Simpson index showed significant differences) (Fig. 4a). We also found distinct clustering of microbiota composition in the cecum between ND and AD groups using PCoA and NMDS plot based on Bray-Curtis (Fig. 4b; Additional file 5). Based on this, we further analyzed the composition and differential flora of the gut microbiota in ND and AD groups. Taxonomic variations at phylum, family, and genus levels indicated unique microbial compositions between ND and AD in cecum (Fig. 4c-e). Further screening of differential flora using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test revealed that SCFAs bacteria such as g_Blautia and g_Faecousia were significantly enriched in the ND group, suggesting that the gut microbiota had changed at the level of key functional flora (Fig. 4f). LEfSe analysis results showed that the characteristic bacterial communities of the ND group were mainly enriched in the Firmicutes-related lineage, such as g_Blautia, g_Faecousia, and g_Fusicatenibacter; while the AD group was mainly concentrated in the Actinobacteriota-related branch, such as g_Eggerthella, g_Gordonibacter, and g_Raoultibacter (Fig. 4g and h). Then, KEGG enrichment analysis was then performed to elucidate the functional significance of the characteristic bacterial communities. The results showed that the ND group was significantly enriched neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction, oxytocin signaling pathway, apelin signaling pathway, and arginine biosynthesis-typical neuropeptide signaling and amino acid metabolism pathways. The AD group was mainly enriched in microbial motility, environmental perception, and substance transport (Fig. 4i; Additional file 6).

Integrated multi-omics analysis reveals gut-testis axis regulates the external genitalia development in male geese

To elucidate the relationship between gut microbiota, metabolites, and reproductive organ development in male geese, this study integrated metagenomic, non-targeted metabolomics, and testis and external genital RNA-seq data from cecum contents for joint analysis. As shown in Fig. 5a, neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction, tyrosine metabolism, tryptophan metabolism, and arginine and proline metabolism pathways were identified by both the metagenomic, metabolomics, and RNA-seq analyses. Correlation analysis of DMs-DEGs showed a significant positive correlation between tyramine, dopamine, and key candidate genes in the neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction pathway, such as KNG1 and P2RY4 (Fig. 5b). Furthermore, comparison of the abundance of key DMs revealed that the levels of tyramine and dopamine were significantly higher in the ND group than in the AD group, indicating that aromatic amino acid-related metabolism and its neuroactive derivatives are more active in ND geese (Fig. 5c). Then, correlation analysis results showed that several dominant gut bacterial genera (such as g_Blautia and g_Faecousia) were significantly correlated with neuroactive amine metabolites (dopamine and tyramine), while some conditionally pathogenic or metabolically imbalanced genera showed negative correlations (Fig. 5d). Notably, it was postulated that gut-derived neuroactive metabolic signals may participate in the molecular regulation of the external genitalia development in male geese by modulating GPCRs and the coagulation/kinin-related signaling axis (Fig. 5e). The above results support the neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction pathway as a key integrative hub in the gut-reproductive axis, and this pathway may play an important regulatory role in the development of reproductive organs, from multiple levels including gene expression, metabolites, and microbial functional potential.

Disscusion

The normal development of male reproductive organs relies on the synergistic effects of genetic regulation, hormonal signaling, and both internal and external environmental factors (Otake and Kuroiwa, 2016; Gao et al., 2024). Under both the natural mating and artificial insemination conditions in commercial goose production, whether the male external genitalia development normally directly affects the mating ability. In this study, morphological analysis revealed that some male geese exhibited external genitalia developmental abnormalities among different individuals. Histological evidence further supports this view: in the AD group, the external genitalia showed thinning of the keratogenital epithelium (KE) and loss of normal lamellar structure (OC), indicating that the abnormal developmental state has extended from the macroscopic phenotype to the tissue microenvironment level. Previous studies have shown that an imbalance in the overall microenvironment of the reproductive system can lead to abnormal development of reproductive organs, which is essentially attributed to organ defects (Bao et al., 2025; Dutta et al., 2013; Amann and Veeramachaneni, 2007). In recent years, numerous studies have revealed that gut microbiota can affect the development of male reproductive organs through the "gut-gonad axis" (Cai et al., 2022; Lv et al., 2024). Taken together, regarding the significant differences in the external genitalia phenotypes of male geese in ND and AD groups, RNA-seq was used to further explore the deeper mechanisms.

In the present study, 992 and 1567 DEGs were identified in the testis and external genitalia between BS and n-BS, respectively. Functional enrichment analysis revealed that the DEGs in the testis and external genitalia were significantly enriched in pathways including neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction, ECM-receptor interaction, calcium signaling pathway, and cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction. These pathways collectively target key biological processes such as tissue remodeling, inflammation regulation, and intercellular signaling. Among the shared DEGs, KNG1 in the neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction pathway exhibited high connectivity within the PPI network, suggesting its pivotal position in the regulatory network. As a core component of the kallikrein-kinin system (KKS), KNG1, after cleavage by kallikrein, yields bioactive peptides such as bradykinin, which can regulate vascular permeability, inflammatory responses, and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling through B1/B2 receptors (Colman, 2006; Schousboe and Nystrøm, 2009; Zhang et al., 2025). Previous studies have shown that kinins can promote sperm metabolism and motility, and their receptors (especially B2R) are clearly located in the germ cell and sertoli cell regions of the testis, indicating that the testis have a direct ability to sense kinin signals (Monsees, 2012; Monsees et al., 2002). Accordingly, it can be reasonably inferred that upregulation of KNG1 expression level may affect BTB stability, ECM composition, and MMP-mediated tissue remodeling processes by enhancing kinin production and activating bradykinin receptors, thereby altering the structural development and microenvironment homeostasis of the testis and external genitalia. Notably, previous studies have shown that the neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction pathway is significantly enriched in the development of avian reproductive organs and is linked to signaling molecules such as PLCB1 that drive remodeling of external genital tissues (Yan et al., 2024). Furthermore, in mammals, multiple GPCR-ligand pairs (e.g., GnRH receptors and Kisspeptin receptors) have been shown to be key nodes in gonadal development and hormone regulation, further illustrating the central role of GPCR-mediated neuroendocrine signaling in reproductive system homeostasis (Noel and Kaiser, 2011). These findings strongly indicate that the neuroactive ligand- receptor interaction pathway is closely related to the development of the external genitalia.

Furthermore, metabolomics analysis results showed that a significant separation in the metabolic profiles of cecum contents between the ND and AD groups. Compared with the AD group, the DMs in the ND group were mainly enriched in aromatic amino acid pathways, including tyrosine, tryptophan and phenylalanine metabolism. These pathways are the main sources of neuroactive molecules such as dopamine and tyramine.

Studies in multiple species (mouse, bovine, pig, human, etc.) have shown that dopamine receptors (especially DRD2) are expressed in the testis, spermatogenic cells, and sperm, suggesting their potential involvement in reproductive organ function (Ramírez-Reveco et al., 2017; Kanno et al., 2022; Frungieri and Mayerhofer, 2024). Recent evidence suggests that gut microbiota influence the host endocrine system and male reproductive function through the "gut-testis axis" (Cryan et al., 2019; Cai et al., 2022). Intriguingly, integrated analysis indicated that the neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction was the only pathway significantly enriched in the top 20 of both the testis, external genitalia RNA-seq data and the cecum metabolome, exhibiting a highly consistent cross-omics response pattern. Correlation analysis further revealed that dopamine and tyramine were significantly associated with KNG1, P2RY4, SSTR5, and HRH3, suggesting that neuroactive metabolites from the gut may regulate gene expression in reproductive organs through receptor-mediated signaling pathways. This mechanistic link is consistent with previous findings, indicating that monoamines such as dopamine not only participate in neural regulation but also influence spermatogenesis and male reproductive organ development (Hamamah et al., 2022; Raut et al., 2023). These results indicated that alterations in microbial monoamine metabolism (dopamine and tyramine) may be closely associated with the external genitalia development in male geese, but the specific molecular regulation mechanism needed to be further explored.

In recent years, the effects of gut microbiota on host physiology have garnered increasing interest. It is recognized as a metabolic organ that participates in regulating a variety of physiological and pathological processes of the host, including the immune system(Ze-Hua et al., 2024), metabolic diseases(Yong and Oluf, 2020), and reproductive system(Wu et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2024). Previous studies have demonstrated that gut microbiota can regulate the seasonal development of reproductive organs in male animals via the gut-testis axis(Yu et al., 2024). In our study, we observed a significant difference of gut microbiota between ND and AD groups, and suggesting that disturbances in the gut microbiota may contribute to the abnormal development of the external genitalia in male geese. At the level of bacterial community composition, the ND group was significantly enriched with genera such as g_Blautia and g_Faecousia, which are closely related to the production of SCFAs and showed a significant positive correlation with the levels of dopamine and tyramine, while the AD group was mainly characterized by genera related to the phylum Actinobacteria, such as g_Eggerthella, g_Gordonibacter, and g_Raoultibacter. Previous studies have shown that SCFAs not only provide energy for the intestinal epithelium, but also regulate systemic inflammation, immune tolerance, and endocrine signaling by activating receptors such as GPR41/GPR43, thereby providing a low-inflammatory and high-homeostatic internal environment for gonadal development (Du et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2024). Therefore, the enrichment of SCFAs-related microbiota in the ND group may help maintain a systemic microenvironment conducive to external genitalia tissue differentiation. Furthermore, KEGG functional annotation results further reinforce this inference at the functional level. In our study, the gut microbiota in the ND group was significantly enriched in the oxytocin signaling pathway, the apelin signaling pathway, and multiple pathways related to aromatic amino acid metabolism, which are closely related to neuroendocrine regulation. Notably, the enriched aromatic amino acid metabolism pathway in the ND group, serving as an important upstream source of neuroactive metabolites such as dopamine and tyramine, suggests that the gut microbiota can ensure reproductive organ development by stably supplying neuroactive signaling molecules. Combined with RNA-seq, metabolomic, and metagenomic results, it can be further inferred that under normal developmental conditions, the neuroactive metabolic environment maintained by gut microbiota may participate in the fine regulation of external genitalia development in male geese through the synergistic action of multiple GPCRs in the neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction pathway. To sum up, these findings provide valuable and novel insights into the potential mechanisms underlying the regulation of external genitalia development in male geese via the gut-testis axis mediated by the neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction pathway.

Conclusions

In summary, our comprehensive study elucidates the complexity of external genitalia development in male geese, providing a multifaceted perspective that encompasses phenotypic and molecular analysis between ND and AD male geese. Our metagenomic analysis identified g_Blautia and g_Faecousia as the core dominant intestinal genera affecting external genitalia development in male geese. Metabolomics analysis revealed that neuroactive metabolites such as dopamine and tyramine in the neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction pathway are associated with external genitalia development. Further, combined with testis and external genitalia transcriptome data, it was found that gut-derived neuroactive metabolic signals may participate in the molecular regulation of the external genitalia development in male geese by modulating GPCRs and the coagulation/kinin-related signaling axis. This study provides a detailed genetic regulatory network for the development of external genitalia of male geese, which can help improve the reproductive performance of male geese.
