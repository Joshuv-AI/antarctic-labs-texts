Abundance of atrazine degrading genes trzN versus atzA in soil cultures and in situ degradation rates
Authors: Truphena Akinyi, Damiana Diaz‐Reck, Aman Raj, Anat Bernstein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDAtrazine is a widely used herbicide whose persistence and mobility have led to frequent contamination of soils and water bodies. Atrazine can be degraded in soils through two primary biotic pathways: an oxidative route that produces the relatively persistent metabolites desethylatrazine and deisopropylatrazine, and a hydrolytic route mediated by chlorohydrolases encoded by atzA or trzN, leading to the less recalcitrant intermediate hydroxyatrazine. This study quantified atrazine degradation rates in 17 agricultural soils overlying a regional aquifer and evaluated degradation potential through complementary laboratory experiments.RESULTSThe soils contained hydroxyatrazine and desethyl‐2‐hydroxyatrazine, indicating active hydrolytic transformation in the fields, while varying proportions of dealkylated metabolites suggested concurrent oxidative degradation. Field‐derived half‐lives ranged from 36 to 225 days, with evidence of biphasic kinetics, as younger atrazine residues degraded more rapidly than aged residues. Finally, laboratory enrichment cultures of all soils confirmed the capability of indigenous bacteria to hydrolyze atrazine, with a clear predominance of trzN over atzA across cultures.CONCLUSIONThe findings of the current study indicate that while the predominance of the hydrolytic pathway suggests reduced risk of rapid atrazine migration to groundwater, the observed biphasic degradation behavior indicates that residual atrazine may continue leaching over extended timescales with prolonged risk for groundwater quality. © 2026 The Author(s). INTRODUCTION

Atrazine (6‐chloro‐4‐N‐ethyl‐2‐N‐propan‐2‐yl‐1,3,5‐triazine‐2,4‐diamine) is a widely applied herbicide for controlling broadleaf and grassy weeds. 1 Since its introduction in the 1950s, its persistence and mobility have raised significant environmental concerns, particularly its contamination of soil, surface waters, and groundwater. 2 Exposure to atrazine is associated with detrimental health issues for both humans and aquatic life. 1 , 3 , 4 , 5 This has raised interest in its attenuation in the environment, especially degradation.

Two biotic degradation pathways were reported for atrazine 6 : the first, oxidative degradation, is catalyzed by cytochrome monooxygenases and leads to N‐dealkylation and the formation of desethylatrazine and deisopropylatrazine. These are relatively persistent metabolites that are associated with slower overall attenuation. The second, hydrolytic degradation, is mediated by atrazine chlorohydrolases, which are encoded by either atzA or trzN. This results in dehalogenation to form hydroxyatrazine, which can be further degraded through atzBCEF‐driven reactions to cyanuric acid and finally mineralized to ammonia and carbon dioxide. Since hydrolytic degradation subsequently leads to mineralization, it is a desirable pathway from an environmental health perspective. Oxidative N‐dealkylation, on the other hand, forms weakly sorbed metabolites that are prone to leaching and dominate groundwater contamination signatures. 7

The contribution of hydrolytic degradation to overall atrazine field‐scale degradation depends strongly on the specific atrazine chlorohydrolase genes present in soil microbial communities. Both atzA and trzN encode enzymes that catalyze the initial hydrolytic dechlorination of atrazine to hydroxyatrazine, yet they differ significantly in their substrate range and their environmental adaptation in soils. AtzA is limited to the halohydrolysis of s‐triazine compounds, whereas TrzN exhibits a much broader catalytic range, acting on multiple classes of triazines and pyrimidines. 6 , 8 Besides the differences in substrate specificity, the atzA gene is mostly associated with Gram‐negative bacteria, 6 , 9 , 10 which are often sensitive to soil pH, microbial competition, and other environmental constraints, potentially limiting its effectiveness in situ. In contrast, trzN is broadly distributed among both Gram‐positive and Gram‐negative soil bacteria 8 , 9 , 10 exhibiting greater tolerance to variable soil conditions, including fluctuations in pH, substrate availability, and microbial competition. 8 As a result, soils with a history of repeated atrazine application are more likely to harbour trzN‐expressing communities capable of sustaining hydrolytic activity across varying soil conditions and promoting accelerated degradation shortly after application (e.g., Yale et al. 11 ). Global soil surveys report the occurrence of both trzN and atzA in atrazine‐exposed agricultural soils, and the review by Udiković‐Kolić et al. 6 suggests that trzN is commonly encountered, although its prevalence relative to atzA varies among studies.

Under field conditions, atrazine degradation rates and, consequently, reported half‐lives are highly variable and are governed by factors that control microbial activity and bioavailability. 12 Soil texture, organic matter content, and mineral composition regulate sorption–desorption dynamics and thus the fraction of atrazine accessible for microbial degradation, 13 while soil moisture, temperature, and aeration control microbial metabolism and enzymatic efficiency. 14 Reported field half‐lives in agricultural soils typically range from a few weeks to 2 years, 12 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 reflecting the combined influence of time since application, seasonal conditions, application history, etc. Degradation is generally faster shortly after application, when atrazine is more bioavailable and microbial activity is high, but slows as residues age and become less accessible. Seasonal shifts in temperature and soil moisture further modulate degradation kinetics, producing longer half‐lives during cooler and/or drier periods and shorter half‐lives under warm, moist conditions. In addition, repeated atrazine application promotes microbial adaptation and enrichment of atrazine‐degrading populations, often resulting in accelerated degradation and shorter half‐lives in previously exposed soils compared to unexposed ones. 19 , 20

Recently, groundwater‐focused investigations in a regional coastal aquifer examined atrazine occurrence, metabolite patterns, and inferred degradation processes associated with point and non‐point contamination sources. 21 , 22 These studies have shown a high potential for atrazine degradation in groundwater by atzA‐harboring bacteria, where atrazine was present as a point‐source pollutant with typical concentrations of up to hundreds of μg L−1. On the other hand, no degradation potential was detected in cultures retrieved from groundwater along the aquifer, in which atrazine was present as a non‐point source pollutant with concentrations in the sub‐μg L−1 range. Here, however, dealkylated metabolites were detected at relatively high proportions. Since hydroxyatrazine was not commonly detected in groundwater under these circumstances, it was unclear whether hydroxyatrazine had not prevailed or was not formed in atrazine history in these cases. Further investigation of in situ degradation rates and dominating functional genes in agricultural soils overlying this aquifer is warranted to bridge between these different environments. The present study aimed to quantify atrazine degradation rates across treated agricultural soils overlying the aquifer. It was accompanied by a complementary laboratory investigation to evaluate the degradation potential in these soils and quantify the relative abundance of trzN and atzA (two catabolic genes) in the enrichment cultures to assess which hydrolytic pathway predominates in these agricultural systems.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Field sampling and soil characterization

Agricultural soils were collected from multiple field sites overlying the Israeli coastal aquifer or several kilometers east of the aquifer's recharge zone (Supporting Information, Fig. S1), which lies within a Mediterranean climate. This region is known for its vulnerability to pesticide contamination and frequent detection of atrazine in groundwater. 23 Sites differ in their application histories, including differences in frequency of use and time since the last application.

In total, five sites were sampled, comprising 17 distinct plots. At each plot, the surface ≈1–2 cm layer directly exposed to the sun was first gently removed using a trowel, after which soil was collected from the root zone (up to 30 cm depth). In each plot, one composite soil sample was collected into a 1‐L polyethylene container, along with two additional samples collected in sterile Falcon tubes. Samples were transported to the laboratory and maintained either frozen (−20 or –80 °C) or refrigerated (4 °C), depending on the analysis. The geographical coordinates for each site were recorded, and information on atrazine use history, including time elapsed since the most recent application, was obtained from farmers (Table 1).

In the laboratory, soil moisture content was determined gravimetrically, soil pH following the Food and Agriculture Organization 24 and soil texture was determined by particle‐size analysis after removing aggregates, sieving, and homogenization, as described by Boyd. 25

Atrazine extraction and metabolite analysis

Atrazine and its major transformation products were extracted from soil samples. Ten grams of each soil was mixed with diatomaceous earth (DE; DIONEX corporation, CA, USA) in the ratio of 2:1 (soil:DE) and left to settle for an hour to ensure adequate drying. Then, 30 mL of methanol (HPLC grade; Biolab, Jerusalem, Israel) was added to the mixture, which was incubated on a shaker at 120 rpm for 24 h and subjected to controlled microwave heating using a Microwave Digestion System (Ethos UP, Milestone Srl, Sorisole, Italy), following EPA Method 3546. 26 Extracts were collected and concentrated under a gentle stream of nitrogen gas to a final volume of ~1 mL prior to triple‐quadrupole Liquid Chromatography‐Mass Spectrometry system (LC–MS/MS) Jerusalem, analysis. The recovery of atrazine and its metabolites using this method was tested in two spiked soils with no atrazine history. The two soils were spiked with the target compounds atrazine (Agan Adama‐Ashdod, Israel), hydroxyatrazine, desethylatrazine, and deisopropylatrazine (desethyl‐2‐hydroxyatrazine, PESTANAL®, Sigma‐Aldrich), to a final concentration of 10 μg L−1. The soils followed the same extraction procedure as described above, and the concentrations of the target compounds were analyzed using LC–MS/MS, as described below. The results revealed full recovery for atrazine and variable recoveries for its metabolites, with notably lower recoveries in the clay soil (e.g., 83 ± 7% versus 43 ± 11% for hydroxyatrazine in sandy loam and clay soils, respectively; Supporting Information, Table S2). Due to this variability, field concentrations were not corrected for recovery, and the reported values therefore represent lower‐limit estimates of the actual concentrations.

Quantitative determination of atrazine and selected metabolites was performed using an Agilent Technologies LC–MS/MS system (Agilent Technologies Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with a Kinetex XB‐C18, 3.0 × 100 mm, 2.6 μm (Phenomenex) column. Mobile phase A consisted of Milli‐Q water with 0.1% formic acid and mobile phase B consisted of 0.1% formic acid in 100% methanol (HPLC Supra‐Gradient), at a flow rate of 0.45 mL min−1. Calibration curves were generated using in‐house standards of atrazine (Agan Adama‐Ashdod, Israel), hydroxyatrazine, desethylatrazine, deisopropylatrazine, and desethyl‐2‐hydroxyatrazine (PESTANAL®; Sigma‐Aldrich) over a calibration range of 1–10 μg L−1. Concentrations below the lowest standard of 1 μg L−1 (equivalent to 100 ng kg−1 dry soil) are considered lower than the limit of quantification (LOQ). Compound identification was based on retention times and mass spectral characteristics.

Soil concentrations were used to evaluate the in situ atrazine residual fraction. The atrazine residual fraction, f, was calculated as the ratio of atrazine concentration to the sum of the concentrations of atrazine and its metabolites: f=atrazine∑atrazine+metabolites Since metabolites may have undergone further degradation while only the first key metabolites were monitored, this ratio provides a lower limit for in situ degradation.

In situ degradation rates were evaluated assuming first‐order kinetics. Rate constants (k) were calculated as ln f = −kt, where t is the time elapsed since atrazine was applied in the field until the date of sampling and was provided by the farmers. The half‐lives (t 0.5) for atrazine prevalence in soils were calculated as t0.5=−ln0.5k.

Soil enrichment cultures and microbial degradation experiments

To assess the microbial degradation potential, soil enrichment cultures were established using a liquid mineral growth medium, ATCC 2662 R, prepared according to the American Type Culture Collection formulation, amended with atrazine as the sole nitrogen source. Enrichment cultures were inoculated with soil from all field sites (5.0 ± 0.5 g) and incubated on a shaker (120 rpm) at 25 °C to promote growth of atrazine‐degrading microorganisms. Enrichment was achieved through repeated sub‐culturing (10% v/v transfer) into fresh liquid medium.

Atrazine depletion in enrichment cultures was monitored over time using high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC; Agilent 1100 series) fitted with a Kinetex C18 column (Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA), and a mobile phase composed of 70% methanol (HPLC grade; Biolab, Jerusalem, Israel) and 30% Milli‐Q water operated at a flow rate of 1 mL min−1 at 30 °C. Detection was performed at 220 nm by a diode array detector. Samples were collected at regular intervals and analyzed to determine changes in atrazine concentration. Additionally, bacterial growth was monitored by measuring the optical density at a wavelength of 600 nm using a TECAN Infinite M200 (Tecan, Austria).

Quantification of atrazine‐degrading genes

Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) (Azure Biosystems, Inc., Dublin, CA, USA) was employed to quantify the two atrazine hydrolytic genes atzA and trzN in both soil samples and enrichment cultures. Following the manufacturer's recommendations, genomic DNA was extracted from enrichment cultures using the DNeasy PowerSoil Kit (Qiagen) at the conclusion of the 28‐day incubation period. A NanoDrop spectrophotometer was used to measure the concentration and purity of DNA. Gene copy numbers were determined using specific primer sets (Supporting Information, Table S1) and SYBR Green chemistry. Each 20 μL qPCR reaction included 10 μL of SYBR Green Master Mix, 0.5 μM of each primer, 2 μL of template DNA (diluted 1:10), and nuclease‐free water. The thermal cycling settings were as follows: 5 min of initial denaturation at 95 °C, followed by 35 cycles of 30 s at 95 °C, 30 s at the annealing temperature of 60 °C, and 30 s at 72 °C. Melting curve analysis was used to confirm amplicon specificity. Additionally, 16S rRNA was quantified in all samples and enabled a rough estimation of the total bacterial quantity. 27

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Dealkylated versus hydroxylated metabolites in soil samples

Seventeen agricultural soils (T01–T17) were characterized for soil texture, which spanned from clay‐rich soils (up to 50% clay) to sand‐dominated soils (up to 75% sand) (Supporting Information, Table S3). Concentrations of atrazine and its metabolites in soil extracts of all soils were analyzed by LC–MS/MS. Atrazine and its first metabolites hydroxyatrazine, desethylatrazine, and deisopropylatrazine, as well as desethyl‐2‐hydroxyatrazine, were detected at the hundreds of ng kg−1 range. Hydroxyatrazine was detected in all samples, pointing to the probable presence of atrazine hydrolytic genes atzA or trzN in the soils. The metabolite desethyl‐2‐hydroxyatrazine is a hydroxylated atrazine derivative that has undergone both hydroxylation and dealkylation, and has been previously reported in soils, streams, and laboratory incubation studies. 14 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 In the early study by Mandelbaum et al., 32 dealkylated products were formed only after the formation of hydroxyatrazine. However, based on our data, we cannot determine whether dealkylation or hydrolysis occurred first in the current study.

Soils also presented dealkylated products in differing proportions, implying ongoing dealkylation in soils alongside hydrolysis (Fig. 1). To further evaluate the relative contribution of these transformation pathways, diagnostic metabolite ratios were examined. Comparable abundances of the two dealkylated metabolites were observed, with a slight predominance of desethylatrazine in most soils whose concentrations were above the LOQ (Supporting Information, Table S3).

Concentrations of atrazine and its metabolites in soil samples (A) and the relative abundance of each of the compounds (B). When concentrations were less than the limit of quantification, their relative abundance was set as zero. Hydroxylated denotes the sum of hydroxylated products (hydroxyatrazine and desethyl‐2‐hydroxyatrazine) and dealkylated denotes the sum of dealkylated products (desethylatrazine and deisopropylatrazine).

While the relative ratio of hydroxylated products increases with decreasing atrazine, dealkylated products did not present a similar trend (Supporting Information, Fig. S2). Overall, these data indicate that both transformation pathways can potentially occur in the soils.

Atrazine residual fraction

The atrazine residual fraction, f, was calculated as the ratio of the concentration of atrazine to the sum of the concentrations of atrazine and its metabolites. This ranged between 0.03 and 0.95 and is summarized in Table 1. Since metabolites might have further degraded in the environment, sorbed, or been less efficiently extracted, the calculated fraction, f, sets a lower limit for the degradation extent. The atrazine residual fraction of all soil samples in which atrazine concentrations were higher than the LOQ presented a correlation with time elapsed since the last application (Fig. 2). The residual fraction tended to decline with increasing time since application, implying progressive atrazine degradation in the fields, with scattering that reflects the influence of site‐specific conditions.

In situ half‐lives of atrazine

The in situ half‐lives of atrazine were quantified for the different soil samples assuming first‐order kinetics. Field‐derived half‐lives ranged from approximately 36 to 225 days, with a mean of 100 days (Table 1 and Fig. 2). This range of calculated values sets an upper limit for in situ half‐lives in the studied soils. The half‐lives obtained in this study fall within the broad range reported for agricultural soils, which commonly spans from days to weeks in surface layers to several months or longer in deeper soils (typically ~5 to >280 days). 15 , 16 , 17 , 33 , 34 , 35 This variability reflects seasonal differences, irrigation regime, and atrazine use history. 14 , 33 Degradation kinetics of atrazine are strongly influenced by soil and environmental conditions. 36 Consequently, the values obtained here represent averages over the seasonal variability of these factors and may therefore be considered apparent half‐lives. Although atrazine in situ degradation is commonly described by first‐order kinetics (e.g., Boesten et al. 37 ), the possibility of biphasic behavior should also be considered. This implies rapid dissipation of bioavailable atrazine followed by slower degradation of sorbed residues. 35 Reports indicate that this second phase can extend for months, or even years, resulting in long‐term trace residues in soil and increasing the potential for leaching into groundwater. 12 , 16 , 34 If biphasic degradation is prevalent, samples with young atrazine would yield shorter half‐life estimates, while samples with matured atrazine should yield longer half‐life estimates. Indeed, a linear correlation (P = 0.04) between the half‐life and the time elapsed since application can be seen in the current study (Fig. 2), where samples with young atrazine yielded shorter half‐life estimates, while samples with matured atrazine reflected greater persistence of the residual pool. Yet it should be noted that half‐lives were derived from single sampling points per soil over a wide array of soils, which also differ in their texture, irrigation scheme, application season, etc. As the sampling was of a snapshot nature, biphasic degradation kinetics should be further investigated under more controlled conditions with reduced variability.

The potential of atrazine degradation by the soil enrichment cultures

Soil samples were enriched in ATCC 2662 R growth medium supplemented with atrazine as the sole nitrogen source. This medium is known to promote atrazine‐hydrolyzing bacteria, including Arthrobacter spp., Pseudomonas sp., Nocardioides sp., Pseudaminobacter sp., Variovorax sp., etc. 38 , 39 , 40 These atzA‐ and trzN‐harbouring organisms have previously been shown to utilize atrazine as a sole nitrogen or combined carbon and nitrogen source. 9 , 41

All 17 soil‐derived cultures exhibited atrazine degradation by indigenous microbial communities. First, complete removal of atrazine was achieved within 14 days and gradually decreased to 2–3 days following successive transfers (data are shown only for later transfers; Supporting Information, Fig. S3). The abiotic control (no culture) showed no atrazine loss. The progressively shorter degradation times are consistent with microbial adaptation following repeated exposure to the compound. 19 Overall, the rapid and reproducible degradation observed in the studied soil cultures confirms a strong intrinsic microbial potential for atrazine removal under favorable conditions. Results are comparable to other laboratory‐scale studies in pure and mixed enrichment cultures reporting complete atrazine removal within days to weeks, depending on concentration and microbial composition. 16 , 42 , 43 , 44 , 45

Enrichment cultures from eight selected soils (T01–T08) were analyzed for atrazine and its primary degradation products 3 days after inoculation, coinciding with complete atrazine removal. Hydroxyatrazine was the dominant metabolite in all samples, while only trace concentrations of dealkylated products were detected (Fig. 3). One exception was culture T05, in which both atrazine and its metabolites were detected only at trace levels, suggesting further transformation of the monitored metabolites. Although desethylatrazine and deisopropylatrazine were detected in most cultures, concentrations remained low and only marginally above the limit of quantification (1 μg L−1), suggesting a minor contribution of dealkylation relative to hydrolysis.

Concentration of atrazine and metabolites in the soil cultures (μg L−1) after 3 days of incubation.

The dominance of hydroxyatrazine as a transformation product in all enrichment cultures agrees with previous reports identifying hydrolysis as the principal atrazine degradation pathway in agricultural soils in varying regions. 16 , 32 , 45 , 46 , 47 Similarly, low abundances of desethylatrazine and deisopropylatrazine observed in this study are consistent with previous reports and are generally interpreted as indicating a minor or parallel degradation pathway. 48

Quantities of catabolic genes in soil samples and enrichment cultures

The abundance of atrazine‐degrading genes atzA and trzN was quantified in all soil samples (T01–T17). 16S rRNA gene copy numbers were also quantified and may provide a rough estimate of bacterial quantity in soils (e.g., Lee et al. 27 ). This ranged from 1013 to 1014 copies kg−1 dry soil, with no correlation between 16S rRNA abundance and soil moisture or texture (Supporting Information, Fig. S4). The abundance of atzA and trzN was below the quantification limit (<5.5 × 108 copies L−1 or <1.3 × 109 copies kg−1 dry soil) in all samples, corresponding to four to five orders of magnitude lower than the 16S rRNA copies.

In contrast to the bulk soil samples, in soil enrichment cultures, catabolic genes associated with atrazine dehalogenation were successfully amplified. This indicates that while the degradation potential exists, the active degrading population is small and increases with high substrate availability. The enrichment cultures results showed a clear predominance of trzN relative to atzA across all enrichment cultures (Fig. 4). The gene trzN was detected in all cultures at an abundance ranging from 1012 to 1016 copies L−1, whereas atzA was detected in only 11 of the 17 samples and typically ranged from 1012 to 1013 copies L−1. Generally, atzA was detected at quantities two or more orders of magnitude lower than trzN, with few exceptions. A positive control, spiked with a groundwater culture previously shown to contain abundant atzA, 21 exhibited expectedly high atzA abundance, confirming assay performance.

Several studies report a higher prevalence of trzN relative to atzA in soil microcosms and enrichment cultures. 49 , 50 , 51 Similar patterns have been reported across diverse agricultural soils, where trzN was detected more frequently and persisted across soil depths and over time, suggesting greater ecological stability. 10 , 52 However, other studies present opposite reports, with atzA as the dominant hydrolytic gene in agricultural soils (e.g., De Souza et al. 41 and Siripattanakul et al. 45 ). The higher trzN abundance in the present study may reflect broader environmental tolerance associated with trzN‐harbouring bacteria under varying environmental conditions. 6 Nevertheless, our enrichment cultures reflect selection during the enrichment process and may not fully represent the composition of the native soil community.

Environmental implications

The dominance of trzN over atzA in agricultural soils has important ecological and environmental consequences. The trzN gene is commonly associated with resilient Gram‐positive bacteria such as Arthrobacter and Paenarthrobacter, which are well adapted to stresses like desiccation and nutrient limitation. 10 Their ability to efficiently degrade atrazine via hydrolysis enhances survival in repeatedly treated soils. Environmentally, this pathway is beneficial, as it produces hydroxyatrazine, a less mobile and less toxic metabolite that can be further degraded to cyanuric acid and ultimately mineralized. 6 , 14 This may explain the lower abundance of hydroxyatrazine that is often reported for groundwater. However, biphasic degradation observed in this study indicates that while some atrazine degrades quickly, a persistent fraction remains, slowly releasing into groundwater and posing long‐term contamination risks.

CONCLUSION

Agricultural soils overlying the regional aquifer show clear evidence of in situ atrazine degradation, following the hydrolysis pathway. It is further suggested that trzN is the dominant catabolic gene involved in the process over atzA. The process leads to the formation of hydroxyatrazine, a metabolite that is susceptible to complete mineralization. The fact that hydroxyatrazine was not detected in a former study in the aquifer underneath the studied plots implies that it has likely further degraded over time rather than remaining unformed.

Atrazine degradation rates in the tested agricultural soils vary widely. Biphasic degradation is suggested following an observed correlation between the calculated half‐lives and the time since application. This facilitates continuous leaching of atrazine and its metabolites from agricultural soils to groundwater long after application.
