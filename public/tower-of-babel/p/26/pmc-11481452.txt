Methane pulse spray and irrigation promote seed germination and seedling growth of common vetch
Authors: Yifeng Zeng, Zhiqiang Liu, Weijun Chen, Ketan Qv, Yanxiang Huang, Luji Ade, Fujiang Hou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGrazing livestock emits methane through rumen intestinal activity, however, its impact on plant growth in grassland while grazing still has not been explored in detail. Therefore, the study examined the effects of methane pulse spray (MPS), according to grazing intensity, at four grazing intensities (0, 3.6, 5.0, and 6.5 sheep·hm− 2 yr− 1) on seed germination and seedling growth of common vetch (Vicia sativa), while two irrigation rates (35 and 53 ml d− 1) were employed to simulate the precipitation.ResultsThe study revealed significant interactions between MPS and irrigation rate on seed germination and seedling growth parameters. Under moderate MPS intensities (0.74 and 1.04 mol m− 2), seed germination rate, potential, index, and vigor index improved, especially at higher irrigation rates (53 ml d− 1). Conversely, excessive MPS (1.33 mol m− 2) inhibited particularly at the germination rate and growth,. The seedling growth dynamics fitted a logistic model, with MPS advancing the rapid growth phase and increasing maximum growth rates.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that low to moderate levels of MPS from ruminants can promote seed germination and seedling growth of common vetch, while excessive MPS inhibits these processes. Irrigation enhances plant sensitivity to MPS, with wetter conditions (620 mm yr− 1) facilitating a more pronounced response. The findings introduce a new model elucidating plant responses to external perturbations, which can inform grazing management strategies in diverse ecosystems. In wetter regions, moderate grazing intensities may leverage MPS benefits, while arid regions require careful grazing regulation to maintain grassland-livestock balance.Keywords: Grazing livestock, Methane pulse spray, Irrigation, Feedback mechanism

Introduction

Grazing ecosystems occupy over half of the terrestrial area and contribute significantly to global meat (70%), milk (30%) and protein (26%) supply [1, 2]. Animal husbandry accounts for nearly one-third of anthropogenic methane emissions [3, 4], with ruminant enteric emissions contributing over 90% of this total [5]. Ruminants emit methane primarily through their respiratory and digestive systems, resulting from microbial metabolism [6]. Dairy and beef cattle under feeding conditions discharge 291.6 g d− 1 and 195.8 g d− 1 of methane, respectively [7]. While research has focused on the effects of livestock ingestion, trampling, and excretion on forage growth [8], the potential impacts of enteric methane pulse spray (MPS) from livestock have largely been overlooked.

MPS from ruminants in grazing ecosystems depends on grazing intensity, with MPS being excreted more frequently onto grasslands as grazing intensity increases [9]. Grazing intensity, however, is constrained by water availability, which is a key limiting factor for grassland productivity [10], and surface water primarily derived from precipitation is also a major source of drinking water for grazing livestock [11]. Natural grazing grasslands are primarily found in arid and semi-arid regions, where water availability serves as a significant limiting factor [12, 13]. Therefore, studying the impact of MPS on grassland vegetation germination and growth under different simulated precipitation conditions can enhance the productivity of grazing grassland ecosystems under varying precipitation levels.

However, methane was regarded as an abiotic factor uninvolved in plant physiological activities. While recent studies have revealed that methane exerts both direct and indirect effects on plants [14]. Methane also influences the soil’s physical and chemical properties by altering the microbial community structure [15], thereby indirectly impacting plants. The direct effects of methane are multifaceted; it can stimulate root growth by elevating hormonal levels [16]. Additionally, methane affects plants’ abiotic stress tolerance [17–19]. By adjusting levels of signalling molecules, methane can further promote root morphogenesis and enhance plant resistance [20–22]. Nevertheless, despite these physiological insights, it remains unclear how methane specifically affects the germination and growth of forage plants and how precipitation can modulate those responses.

We postulate that livestock-derived MPS may influence seed germination and seedling growth under varying precipitation conditions. We propose a model to elucidate the plant’s reaction to changes in its environment (Fig. 1, a). As the magnitude of external perturbations increases, plants exhibit a heightened response in the adaptive phase. Nevertheless, once these perturbations reach a critical threshold, the plant’s sensitivity to them diminishes due to saturation, ushering in the reactive phase. The plant’s reaction to these external factors, coupled with its varying sensitivity under different stresses, gives rise to four saturation states: super saturation, surplus saturation, equal saturation, and deficit saturation. When external perturbations exceed a threshold, plant sensitivity decreases progressively until stabilizing, forming an S-shaped curve (super saturation). This occurs under optimal conditions, but excessive perturbations often have contrasting effects compared to moderate ones. As thresholds are exceeded, subtle negative impacts become apparent, weakening positive effects. Continued perturbations can lead to three saturation states: surplus, equal, or deficit saturation. In surplus saturation, plants retain some benefits observed under moderate perturbations. Equal saturation leaves vegetation unaltered. The most prevalent outcome is deficit saturation, where excessive perturbations hinder original vegetation conditions [23]. So, plants’ reactions to external perturbations are influenced by both their inherent adaptability and the intensity of these perturbations. Plant germination and growth responses to MPS also conform to this model, akin to their responses to grazing intensity [24]. In addition to direct regulation of plant seed germination and seedling growth, precipitation may also play a role in modulating the effects of MPS on plants (Fig. 1, b). We postulate that higher precipitation could potentially enhance the sensitivity of plant germination and growth responses to MPS, allowing for a lower level of MPS to maximize the promotion of plant germination and growth, thereby lowering the MPS threshold for reaching the saturation phase.

Materials and methods

Experimental design

Seeds of common vetch (Vicia sativa), an annual legume species, were chosen to evaluate the effects of MPS. The seeds were the ‘Lanjian No.3’ variety from Lanzhou University. ‘Lanjian No.3’ variety of common vetch is a widely cultivated high-quality leguminous forage with strong stress resistance, capable of characterizing plant responses to MPS in arid and semi-arid regions. These seeds were sown in circular pots with a diameter of 20 cm, with 20 seeds per pot and a sowing depth of 5–10 cm. Nutrient soil with uniformity, looseness, and fertility was used, suitable for the germination and growth of common vetch. Physicochemical properties of the soil were as follows: pH 6.3, organic matter content 43.2 g kg− 1, total nitrogen 2.70 g kg− 1, available phosphorus 19.8 mg kg− 1, and available potassium 120 mg kg− 1. To avoid external interference, irrigation started 10 days before sowing to maintain soil moisture and weeds were removed.

Four MPS intensity levels were set, as 0, 0.74, 1.04, and 1.33 mol m− 2, equivalent to 0, 3.6, 5.0, and 6.5 sheep hm− 2 yr− 1 in terms of grazing intensity [25]. The selection of grazing intensities was based on the common grazing rate gradients observed in the Loess Plateau and Qinghai-Tibetan Plateau, ranging from moderate to overgrazing observed in the Maqu Grassland Agricultural Trial Station of Lanzhou University and Huanxian Grassland Agriculture Research Station of Lanzhou University [26, 27]. The MPS was divided into two batches and applied on the 0th and 15th days after sowing, to simulate rotational grazing management on the Qinghai-Tibetan Plateau and the Loess Plateau.

Additionally, two irrigation rates were established based on the Loess Plateau’s and Qinghai-Tibetan Plateau’s precipitation levels, specifically 35 ml d− 1 and 53 ml d− 1, corresponding to annual precipitation of 410 mm and 620 mm, respectively. These specific irrigation amounts were calculated based on daily precipitation conversion for pots with a diameter of 20 cm. Three replicates were set for each treatment, with a total of 24 pots.

The present study was conducted under the regulations of experimental field management protocols of Animal Ethics Committee of Lanzhou University (file No. 2010-1 and 2010-2) in accordance with the Guides for Management of Laboratory Animals in Gansu Province, China (Gansu Provincial Department of Science & Technology, 2005).

Monitoring and measurements

Germination counts and seedling heights of common vetch were recorded daily following sowing. Thirty days post-sowing, seedlings were separated at the rhizome to ascertain the fresh weight of both shoot and root. To determine the biomass (dry weight), fresh samples were dried to a constant weight at 65 ℃ for 48 h. This meticulous monitoring and measurement process ensured accurate data collection for subsequent analysis.

The germination rate (GR) was determined by calculating the percentage of seeds that successfully germinated compared to the initial number sown. Germination potential (GP) was assessed by computing the proportion of seeds that germinated within the first five days after sowing, relative to the total germination count. The germination index (GI) was calculated using the formula GI = Σ(Gt/Tt), where Gt represents the daily germination number on day t after sowing, and Tt denotes the time elapsed after sowing [28]. Additionally, the vigor index (VI) was obtained by multiplying the germination index by the seedling height measured 30 days after sowing. Seedling water content was determined by comparing fresh weight to biomass.

Results

Effects of MPS and irrigation rate on seed germination of common vetch

The effects of MPS and irrigation mutual interaction on germination rate and germination vigor were significant (P < 0.05), whereas germination index and vigor index were only affected by irrigation (P < 0.01) (Fig. 2a-d). Higher irrigation rates raised the sensibility of seed germination on MPS (Fig. 2a-e). Enriching methane increased seed germination of common vetch under 35 ml d− 1 irrigation. While with an irrigation of 53 ml d− 1, the trends represented an increase without MPS and a reduction with high methane enrichment. Specifically, germination potential was equal between those without MPS and high methane. Only the germination rate showed a downtrend with the rise of methane application. MPS explained − 9.3, 16.5, 9.0 and 27.6% variation of germination rate, germination potential, germination index and vigor index, respectively, irrigation contributed − 29.5, -51.8, -84.8 and − 57.5% (Fig. 2f).

Effects of MPS and irrigation rate on growth dynamics of seedlings

Seedling growth dynamic curve of common vetch fitted logistic model (Fig. 3a). MPS and irrigation rate affected seedling length, growth period and maximum growth rate (Fig. 3a-d). MPS put off the gradual increase stage but brought forward the slight increase stage, which shortened the rapid increase stage. MPS also increased the maximum growth rate under both irrigation rates. In general, MPS increase the shoot length of seedlings during all periods under 35 ml d− 1. However, its effect was inhibitory 20 days after sowing but promoter after 30 days.

Effects of MPS and irrigation rate on seedling biomass of common vetch

Interaction between MPS and irrigation was shown on root biomass (P < 0.05) and shoot water content (P < 0.01) (Fig. 4a-f). The single effect of irrigation was powerful on all growth indices (P < 0.05), whereas the single effect of methane was only shown on shoot water content (P < 0.05). Interaction between MPS and irrigation was shown on root biomass (P < 0.05) and shoot water content (P < 0.01) (Fig. 4b and e, respectively). Root biomass was not affected by irrigation when there was no MPS. However, root biomass increased with the increase of MPS and irrigation of 53 ml d− 1. Also, higher irrigation rates increased water content but the extreme level of MPS decreased water content regardless of irrigation rate. Besides, the single effect of irrigation was powerful on all growth indices (P < 0.05). 53 ml d− 1 irrigation, increased shoot biomass (Fig. 4.a), total biomass (Fig. 4.c) and root water content (Fig. 4. f). MPS each contributed 0.6, 22.5, 14.7, 34.8, 20.3 and 28.9% to shoot biomass, root biomass, total biomass, root-to-shoot ratio, shoot water content and root water content, while irrigation rate contributed 76.7, 82.6, 83.6, 49.8, 61.2% and 39.0%, respectively (Fig. 4i).

The influential mechanism of MPS and irrigation rate on common vetch

There were significant differences in seed germination and seedling growth of plants under different MPS and irrigation treatments (PC1 = 45.7%, PC2 = 21.8%; Fig. 5). MPS significantly affected seed germination and seedling growth of plants under the same irrigation treatment, while irrigation altered germination and growth to a greater extent. At higher irrigation rates, increased MPS enlarged the differences in seed germination and seedling growth under the same treatment. Under the regulation of MPS and irrigation, the four germination indices (germination rate, germination potential, germination index, and vigor index) were negatively correlated with biomass, biomass distribution and water content. As seedlings grew, seedling height gradually shifted from being related to germination to being related to biomass.

Structure equation model (SEM) explained 83.7% variance of shoot biomass and 76.8% of root biomass under treatment of MPS and irrigation rate (Fig. 6). Irrigation directly affects belowground biomass (path coefficient: -0.51, P < 0.05), and through its influence on germination and subsequent seedling growth, it enhances aboveground biomass (path coefficient: 0.77, P < 0.001). The impact of MPS on aboveground biomass was mainly manifested as a direct negative effect (path coefficient: -0.30, P < 0.01) and an indirect positive effect through its influence on shoot length of seedlings from 10 to 30 days, which further affects aboveground biomass (path coefficient: 0.30, P < 0.01). Neither the direct effect (path coefficient: 0.14, P > 0.05) nor the indirect effect (path coefficient: 0.10, P > 0.05) of MPS on belowground biomass was significant.

MPS and irrigation rate can significantly predict the index, especially germination index, shoot biomass, root biomass, total biomass and seedling height of 10 days after sowing (P < 0.001). Discussion

Effects of MPS on seed germination and seedling growth

This study reveals that the methane emissions from respiration and the intestines of grazing livestock also play a role in regulating grassland vegetation. The application of MPS at a specific concentration can enhance seed germination and seedling growth. When enriched with 53 ml d− 1 of MPS, various germination and growth parameters—excluding germination rate and root water content, which showed minimal effects—exhibited a positive trend, aligning with the postulated stress effect model [23, 24] and previous research [14]. Methane promotes seed germination through hormonal pathways primarily by inhibiting ABA [32–34] and regulating GA [35–37], thereby influencing the ABA/GA balance and subsequently facilitating seed germination [38–[41]. Additionally, methane can also stimulate germination by modulating auxin and jasmonate levels [16]. Plants use methane as an adaptive strategy, stimulating antioxidant enzyme-related gene expression, enhancing activity, and improving resistance to copper excess [17], salt [18], and osmotic stress [19] by modulating metabolite production and metabolism. These effects can alleviate the inhibition of seed germination under drought stress, waterlogging stress, salt stress, and heavy metal stress [42–45]. Methane, NO, and Ca2+ interact with signaling molecules, promoting adventitious root formation [20, 21]. Methane also stimulates NO synthesis, increases H2S production, and enhances gene expression, further promoting root formation [22]. NO [46, 47] and Ca2+ [48, 49] regulate germination and growth through protein synthesis and hormone interaction.

As the MPS application rate rose, indices such as germination potential, germination index, vigor index, and shoot water content followed an initial increase followed by a decrease, ultimately hitting the saturation phase’s deficit saturation point as predicted by the model (Figs. 1, 2 and 3). Conversely, shoot biomass, root biomass, total biomass, and root-shoot ratio demonstrated a continuous upward trend with increasing methane application. The effect of MPS on root growth, particularly by modulating the production of adventitious roots through other signaling molecules and hormone levels, may directly enhance root biomass, subsequently elevating shoot biomass and total biomass [16, 20–22]. The observed increase in root-to-shoot ratio in our study results further supports this notion. However, we also posit that the biomass and its allocation in vegetation are governed by intricate feedback mechanisms. Hence, even at MPS levels where other indicators have reached saturation and are beginning to be suppressed by excess MPS, the response of biomass and its allocation to MPS remains in the reactive or early adaptive phase. We hypothesize that with further increases in MPS, the biomass and its allocation response will also transition to the saturation phase, similar to more sensitive indicators. Notably, below-ground biomass and root-shoot ratio are already showing early signs of this trend. For instance, under 1.33 mol m− 2 MPS, equivalent to 6.5 sheep·hm− 2 yr− 1 of grazing livestock, both measures are already lower compared to those observed at 1.04 mol m− 2 MPS, equivalent to 5.0 sheep·hm− 2 yr− 1.

The effect of irrigation regulation on MPS in seed germination and seedling growth

Under various simulated precipitation levels, forage crops exhibit differing responses to MPS, and a reduction in irrigation can diminish the crops’ sensitivity to MPS. In the 35 ml d− 1 irrigation treatment, higher MPS led to an increase in germination index, vigor index and shoot water content, but kept constant variables like germination rate, germination potential, root-shoot ratio, as well as root water content. However, it did not significantly inhibit shoot biomass, root biomass, or total biomass (Figs. 1, 2 and 3). The effect of MPS on the root-shoot ratio aligns with the deficit saturation observed in the saturation phase of our model, whereas its influence on other indicators may still be in the reactive or early adaptive phase, as previously mentioned. A comprehensive analysis of the effects of MPS and irrigation rate on plant seed germination and seedling growth reveals that precipitation clearly plays a crucial role in this process, but MPS also influences plants (Figs. 5 and 6). Both high irrigation rates and high MPS enlarged the differences in seeds and seedlings under the same treatment (Fig. 5). Exogenous disturbances may amplify inter-individual differences in plants, which is particularly evident in treatments with high irrigation rates and high MPS. As elaborated in the model proposed earlier, variations among individuals of the same species can lead to changes in the threshold of the reactive phase and the status of the saturation phase [24]. Our experimental findings suggest an interplay between MPS and irrigation volume. We postulate that water may be an important medium for MPS to affect vegetation, and under drier soil conditions, plants are less efficient in methane absorption, leading to a subdued response to MPS when compared to treatments with higher irrigation levels. Notably, past research examining the physiological impacts of methane on plants often employed methane-rich water as a methane delivery method, presumably to aid in methane absorption by the plants [18, 20]. This hypothesis is also supported by the response of seeds and seedlings to MPS and the irrigation rate at different stages (Fig. 6). Irrigation primarily affects seed germination and the initial growth of seedlings, during which MPS has a minor direct effect on plants. As seedlings grow, the role of irrigation gradually decreases, while the effect of MPS becomes more prominent. This is because the response of plants to MPS, as explained in the model, becomes more evident as seedlings grow, especially in terms of enhancing their ability to cope with osmotic stress, reducing the impact of irrigation [19].

The impact of MPS on forage plant height was regulated as seedlings developed. Initially, MPS has a non-significant effect on newly germinated seedlings. However, as these seedlings mature, MPS gradually transitions to promoting growth (Fig. 3). Moreover, as the seedlings grew, seedling height gradually shifted from being regulated by germination to being highly correlated with biomass, biomass distribution, and water content (Fig. 5). This may be attributed to differences in resource allocation between the germination and growth stages of plants as they develop [50]. Additionally, from the model proposed (Fig. 1), in the early post-sowing stages, forage seedlings, with their limited biomass, are highly sensitive to elevated MPS concentrations [51]. During this phase, methane’s effect on forage plants can rapidly reach the deficit saturation stage outlined in the aforementioned saturation model. This observation is reinforced by variations in seedling reactions to MPS depending on irrigation levels. Lower irrigation restricts methane absorption, leading MPS to stimulate the growth of young seedlings. Conversely, ample irrigation enhances methane’s participation in plant physiology, thereby impeding seedling growth. As plants accumulate biomass, their sensitivity to MPS diminishes. Moreover, as forage plants adapt to MPS during their growth cycle, the threshold for entering the saturation phase lowers. Consequently, plants’ response to MPS transitions to reactive and adaptive phases, with MPS then promoting seedling growth. In summary, MPS may initially hinder earlier seedling height, but it later boosts the maximum growth rate and seedling height by day 30, effectively reducing the growth cycle. An optimal level of MPS positively influences forage plant height, a finding relevant to grazing pastures where MPS from livestock may contribute to pasture vegetation after the green-up period, enhancing plants’ tolerance to grazing intensity compared to those before the green-up period [52].

MPS could be the fourth way from which grazing ruminants influence grassland vegetation

Human activities and climatic conditions constitute crucial factors shaping grasslands. Grazing, in particular, stands out as a significant means of grassland utilization. Traditionally, it was assumed that grazing livestock impacted grasslands primarily through feeding, trampling, and excretion [8]. This paper emphasizes the MPS effects on crop growth under various irrigation treatments. It was observed that, under moderate grazing intensity, MPS derived from grazing animals foster seed germination and seedling growth, although its impact on these growth indicators was less effective compared to the direct effects of irrigation.

Based on the aforementioned research, it is evident that grazing ruminants influence grassland vegetation through a fourth pathway, namely MPS. The grassland grazing ecosystem exhibits a robust feedback loop: energy flows from producers to grazing livestock via feeding and is recycled back to the grassland through excretion. Beyond this energy exchange, MPS released during ruminant digestion emerges as a pivotal component of this feedback mechanism. MPS stimulates the germination and growth of forage, thereby preserving the stability of the grassland ecosystem. Nonetheless, methane-related feedback is more pronounced in wetter environments. In arid regions, MPS emitted by grazing livestock struggles to penetrate the vegetation, exacerbating the already challenging environmental conditions and compromising the stability of the grassland, thus making the grassland-livestock balance more susceptible to disruption. Therefore, grazing in arid and semi-arid regions of the Loess Plateau should be conducted based on local vegetation conditions, with appropriate measures such as reducing grazing intensity and adopting rotational grazing to ensure the sustainability of feedback regulation in grassland ecosystems. In the Qinghai-Tibetan Plateau, the grazing intensity can be slightly higher than in the more arid Loess Plateau, but it should still not be excessively high. Keeping the grassland vegetation’s response to grazing disturbance in the reactive phase is crucial for balancing production and ecology. This study sheds light on the grassland-livestock interaction system and introduces a novel pathway through which grazing ruminants impact grassland vegetation via MPS. It’s worth noting that MPS also has the potential to modulate the community structure and physiological functions of key decomposers, such as soil microorganisms, thereby affecting soil physicochemical properties [53]. Nevertheless, these feedback mechanisms remain elusive. Furthermore, existing research on methane’s role in plant physiology primarily focuses on the beneficial effects of moderate methane levels on plant activities. The inhibitory impact of excessive methane on plant germination and growth, as observed in this study, lacks a molecular-level theoretical foundation and warrants further exploration.

Conclusions

Low or moderate levels of MPS, 0.74 and 1.04 mol m− 2, respectively, promoted seed germination and seedling growth of common vetch but MPS at 1.33 mol m− 2 inhibited them. Additionally, plants were more sensitive to MPS at 53 than at 35 ml d− 1 of irrigation rates. This study introduces a model that illustrates plant initial responses to varying intensities of external disturbances, which can be applied to interpret forage responses in grasslands. In this sense, the impact of grazing intensity of 3.6 and 5.0 sheep hm− 2 yr− 1, especially in the wetter grasslands (620 mm yr− 1), would show a more sensitive response to MPS than the more arid one (410 mm yr− 1). Grazing intensity of 6.5 sheep hm− 2 yr− 1 can be detrimental. Other species and communities, on long-term studies, are required to establish how grassland vegetation could be modulated by livestock-derived MPS and precipitation.
