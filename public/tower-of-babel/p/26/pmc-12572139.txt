The effect of different types of drugs on balance and reaction ability of female abstainers
Authors: Jingsong Wang, Lan Zheng, Shen Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aim of this study is to investigate the effects of different types of drug use (anesthetic drugs, sychotropic drugs, and mixed drugs) on abstainers’ balance ability (dynamic and static) and reaction speed. This experiment conducted a random sampling test on drug rehabilitation centers in eight provinces across the country to test the abstainers’ dynamic balance ability and static balance ability. One-legged standing balance test: the abstainers who took the Psychotropic drugs stood longer than the Anesthetic drugs, but the mixed drugs did not differ. Star Excursion Balance Test: The overall appearance of large, small presents as Anesthetic drugs, mixed drugs and Psychotropic drugs. Reaction time: The overall reaction speed is from fast to slow: people who smoke Psychotropic drugs, those who use mixed drugs, and those who use Anesthetic drugs. The destruction of the dynamic balance ability of the human body by new drugs is more severe. Mixed drugs are a combination of new and traditional drugs, and their disruption to the dynamic balance of the human body is only limited to new drugs. Traditional drugs prioritize disrupting the body’s static balance. The reaction rate is the same as above, with mixed drugs ranking second. However, its specific mechanism still needs further research.Keywords: Anesthetic drugs, Psychotropic drugs, Mixed drugs, Balance ability, Reaction abilitySubject terms: Human behaviour, Motor control

Introduction

The 2024 World Drug Report released by the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime shows that the harm of the world drug problem continues to intensify with the expansion of drug use and markets. In 2022, the number of drug users has risen to 292 million, an increase of 20% compared to the previous decade1. In 2022, China is facing an undeniable problem of drug abuse, particularly the abuse of methamphetamine, heroin, and cannabis, which has attracted widespread attention2. Cannabis, opioids, and methamphetamine drugs still dominate the market share, indicating that the drug situation remains severe1.

Although the majority of addicts are male, women are more likely to become addicted3. In some animal experiments, female rats are more sensitive to methamphetamine and amphetamine than male rats and are more likely to resume drug seeking behavior4. In rhesus monkeys, female monkeys are also more sensitive to psychoactive drugs than males5; These all suggest that female individuals are more sensitive, prone to addiction, and relapse to nerve stimulants such as methamphetamine (MA). Although women usually start using drugs later than men, once they start using drugs, they are more likely to develop into severe addicts than men6, and it has rapidly developed into a drug addiction disorder.

There are various types of drugs7,8, mainly divided into two categories: Anesthetic drugs and Psychotropic drugs. Anesthetic drugs are extracted from natural herbaceous plants; The Psychotropic drugs mainly rely on artificially synthesized chemical agents - methamphetamine and its derivatives; Both anesthetic and psychotropic drugs have certain damaging effects on various systems of the human body. A study has found that addicted individuals who use different types of drugs (opioids, heroin, and methamphetamine) exhibit varying levels of neurotoxicity and neurological dysfunction, suggesting that drugs may have different mechanisms of damage to the human nervous system9. However, the specific mechanisms and locations of damage vary, such as in the nervous system: Anesthetic drugs mainly damage the nucleus accumbens, dorsal central gray matter, caudate nucleus, amygdala, nucleus accumbens, hippocampus, and frontal cortex when attacking neurons in the human body, while methamphetamine mainly damages the striatum10. Therefore, it can be concluded that there are differences in the mechanisms of damage between Anesthetic drugs and Psychotropic drugs to the human body; In addition, research in the field of addictive substances has found that the use of addictive substances can have an impact on human motor performance, such as marijuana use causing damage to the body’s balance and nervous system (increased swaying)11. At the same time, patients with alcohol use disorder (AUD) can cause damage to the anterior motor circuit12, altering the neural control of posture and motor function13. However, it is unknown whether different types of drugs can cause varying degrees of damage to the body’s balance ability. Therefore, we speculate that there are differences in the mechanisms of harm to the human body caused by traditional, new, and mixed drug use.

Therefore, this experiment aims to explore the damage of three different types of drug use to balance ability through three tests: standing on one foot with closed eyes (evaluating static balance ability), star offset balance test (evaluating dynamic balance ability), and reaction time (the ability of the nervous system to respond to external stimuli), providing a theoretical basis for future research on the impact of different types of drug use on the nervous system.

Research objects and methods

Participants

2950 participants were recruited from China’s compulsory isolation and rehabilitation centers, and each center selected healthy abstainers who were able to participate in physical examinations according to actual conditions (without underlying cardiovascular and cerebrovascular, metabolic diseases and autoimmune diseases as well as major trauma). A total of 2950 participants participated in this experiment, and 2731 valid test data were obtained. The grouping is shown in Table 1. Informed consent form has been signed before testing. The research participants were divided into three groups based on the type of drug use: Anesthesia drugs group (abuse of opioids, cocaine, marijuana and its products, etc.), Psychotropic drugs group (abuse of central nervous system stimulants, suppressants, and hallucinogens, etc.), and mixed abuse group (mixed abuse of anesthesia and psychotropic drugs).This study follows the Helsinki Declaration, and all participants provided informed consent and voluntarily participated. Research method

Star excursion balance test (SEBT)

The test grid is glued onto the laboratory floor using three standard 140 cm long adhesive tapes. The tape extends from the center point to the front, rear inner, and rear outer sides. The goniometer measures 135 degrees from the front straight line to both sides to determine the rear inner and rear outer sides, with a 45 degree angle between the rear. The star offset balance test refers to the test in which the subject is supported on one leg, maintains body balance, and stands at the center point (with their feet immovable), while the unsupported leg extends to the farthest distance in three directions: forward, right, and left. After reaching the farthest distance, the unsupported leg must return to its starting position each time. The indicator for testing is the average maximum extension distance divided by the length of the lower limb. The larger the indicator number, the better the balance ability14,15.

The maximum distance (centimeters) for each reach direction was recorded. Reach distances were normalized to limb length, which was measured from the anterior-superior iliac spine to the distal tip of the medial malleolus. To calculate the composite score, we added all maximum reach distances, divided the sum by 3 times the limb length, and multiplied the quotient by 100. This composite score was used for statistical analysis16.

One-legged standing balance test

The requirement for standing on a single legged field with closed eyes is simple, and the tester can conduct the test by holding a stopwatch.

The traditional test method for standing with closed eyes and one foot is to use a stopwatch to time. During the test, the subject naturally stands. When the “start” command is heard (and the tester opens the watch to time), close both eyes and stand with the dominant leg on one foot. The non dominant leg is lifted off the ground and cannot be placed against the supporting leg, with both hands akimbo. When the subject moves or lifts their feet to the ground, the tester stops the watch. The accuracy of testing is influenced by human factors, and the workload is high during large-scale testing. Test twice, take the best score, record in seconds, and keep two decimal places.

Reaction time testing

Use JH-2008 reaction time tester. The testing instrument is operated by a dedicated person and only measures the average reaction time of simple red light. Before testing, the testing method is explained to the participants before testing. During the photo reaction, continuously measure 5 times and then calculate the average value. Each data item is automatically recorded and the average value is calculated by the machine.

Statistical analysis

Using the SPSS 22.0 statistical software package for statistical analysis, the significance level of the hypothesis test was set at 0.05, and the Shapiro Walk method was used to perform normality tests on the data. For normally distributed data, one-way ANOVA was performed for group comparisons, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test when significant differences were detected. For non-normally distributed data, the Kruskal-Wallis test was used, with Dunn’s post hoc test for pairwise comparisons. The results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Given our primary aim to compare drug effects within age strata, analyses were conducted separately for each age group. No cross-age comparisons were performed, as age was treated as a stratification variable rather than an independent factor of interest.

Discussion

As can be seen from the above, those who use traditional drugs have better dynamic balance ability than those who use new drugs, and those who mix drugs are in between. Although the results may vary in different age groups, the overall trend is the same; At the same time, those who use new drugs have better static balance ability than those who use traditional drugs, and there is no difference among mixed users; In terms of reaction speed, users of new drugs have the fastest reaction speed, followed by co users, and users of traditional drugs have the worst reaction speed. This is consistent with our proposed research objective, further verifying that there are significant differences in the impact of different types of drugs on balance ability, providing a scientific basis for subsequent intervention strategies.

By reviewing neuropathological literature, it was found that traditional drugs (mainly heroin) are associated with oxidative damage in areas such as the nucleus accumbens and dorsal central gray matter, while methamphetamine is mainly associated with abnormal striatal function10. It should be noted that these literature are based on direct neuroimaging or histological evidence, while the functional tests (balance/response time) in this study only provide behavioral phenotypes. Therefore, the following neural mechanisms belong to explanatory hypotheses and need to be verified through multimodal research. And we also found that the striatum is related to the stability of casual movement, the maintenance of muscle tone, and the regulation of limb posture. In addition, it is also related to the processing of information transmitted by proprioceptors, that is, unconscious motor reflex control. According to clinical and pathological observations, invasion of different parts of the striatum can result in changes in muscle tone and a series of involuntary movements. The change in muscle tone can be an increase or decrease in muscle tone, or an increase or decrease in variability. Uncontrolled movements can include dance like movements, hand and foot movements, abnormal muscle tone, or tremors. The characteristic of this type of involuntary movement is that it disappears during sleep and becomes apparent during emotional arousal. People classify the above symptoms into two major categories in clinical practice: one type mainly manifests as decreased movement and increased muscle tone, such as Parkinson’s disease; Another type mainly manifests as low muscle tone and excessive exercise, such as chorea or athetosis; It can be inferred that the new drug has caused significant damage to the structure of the new striatum, resulting in deficiencies in the body’s dynamic control ability. Li Jianhong et al. used the SNP method to analyze the Kappa opioid (OPRK1) receptors in large white, landrace, and Duroc pigs, and the results showed that the Kappa opioid receptor gene SNP had a certain impact on the resting and standing behavior traits of sows17. Studies have reported that the analgesic effect of the Kappa opioid receptor gene knockout mice on agonists disappears, and the effects of reduced exercise and restlessness are also significantly reduced18. There are also literature showing that compared to heroin addiction, the methylation of the OPRK1 promoter in methamphetamine addicts is significantly increased19. Methamphetamine may significantly reduce the body’s dynamic control ability by increasing the expression of OPRK1. These findings align with clinical reports of movement disorders in drug abstainers, suggesting that functional assessments like SEBT and one-leg standing may serve as proxies for monitoring neurofunctional recovery.

For static balance ability: Some studies have found that spongiform white matter disease caused by long-term heroin use can cause static tremors in drug user20. The reason is that heroin can cause damage to the old striatal structure (globus pallidus) of the striatum, mainly manifested as shrinking21, and damage to the old striatum can also lead to tremor paralysis; Dancing disease caused by damage to the striatum can be alleviated during quiet times; Therefore, new drug users have a relative advantage in static balance. Moreover, through literature review, it was found that the brain structures of methamphetamine addicts were damaged, including severe damage to the cingulate and marginal gray matter, decreased hippocampal volume, white matter hypertrophy, medial temporal lobe injury, and increased striatum. These structural changes can be partially restored after one year of abstinence, but have not returned to normal levels21–24, and some functions recover faster, such as dopamine signal recovery. Perhaps within a period of time after methamphetamine withdrawal, the nervous system or other systems that control static balance have already been restored. Therefore, we speculate that this is also one of the reasons why the static balance ability of new drug user is better than that of traditional drug user. Although these mechanisms can partially explain our findings (such as striatal damage associated with dynamic balance and pallidal changes associated with static control), it must be emphasized that the functional testing in this study cannot directly confirm neural structural damage. The above association is only a reasonable hypothesis based on literature, and further verification through neuroimaging is needed in the future.

In some cases, it has been shown that long-term heroin use can affect the deep tendon reflex of the human body, which may be related to damage to the peripheral nervous system25. The tendon reflex activity can to some extent affect the agility of the human body. Long term use of heroin can cause heroin spongiform leukoencephalopathy, indicating that long-term use of heroin can lead to a decrease in the symmetrical distribution density of bilateral white matter areas, posterior limbs of the inner capsule, and cerebellar dentate nucleus, resulting in some adverse consequences. For example, when onset occurs during drug withdrawal, clinical manifestations often include cerebellar symptoms, delayed response, decreased judgment, and emotional apathy; In addition, heroin can cause damage to the old striatal structure (globus pallidus) of the striatum, mainly manifested as shrinking20, which can lead to delayed body movements. From the above, we speculate that it is precisely because the above pathological manifestations greatly damage the neural agility of drug user, leading to a decrease in reaction speed; Compared to the rapid recovery of new addicts after abstinence, traditional drug user are not the favored ones of fate. Because traditional drug users mainly consume heroin, we boldly speculate that this is greatly related to heroin spongiform leukoencephalopathy. These mechanisms can partially explain our findings, but it must be emphasized that the functional testing in this study cannot directly confirm neural structural damage. The above association is only a reasonable hypothesis based on literature, and further verification through neuroimaging is needed in the future.

In addition, both responsiveness and static balance ability showed stable results; However, there are still a few special cases in terms of dynamic balance ability: (1) In the 10–50 age group, there will be mixed inhalers who are better than the above two, mainly concentrated in the dominant leg (1) and non dominant leg (1) test actions, and in terms of age, they will decrease from 31 to 40 age groups to both sides; Research has shown that the static balance ability decreases with age, with a significant decrease between the ages of 35 and 4426; Reminder: The age of 40 is likely to be a very important turning point. Based on the data results, it can be inferred that the effects of drugs on the human body before the age of 30 are very clear, which can reflect the varying degrees of harm caused by different drug use methods to the human body; In the age range of 31 to 40 years old, the influence of factors such as age maturity slightly reduces the difference in the toxic effects of drugs on the human body, thus blurring the degree of harm caused by different drug use methods to the body; Despite variations in baseline balance ability across age groups, the relative impact patterns of drug types (Anesthetic > Mixed > Psychotropic for dynamic balance) remained consistent in all age strata. This suggests that drug type, rather than age, is the primary factor driving differential impairment of balance control. (2) New drug user in the age range of 51 to 60 are better than mixed drug user, while traditional drug user are between the two; However, throughout this age group, there are only a few differences in motility, and some data shows that the older the age, the poorer the balance and stability ability26. Young people recover faster, while older people do the opposite, so the impact of drugs decreases; It can be inferred that the age factor has a significant impact on the 51 to 60 age group.

Limitations and future directions

It should be noted that this study was not designed to examine age-related differences in balance ability perse. Age stratification was employed solely to control for potential confounding effects of age on balance metrics, ensuring that comparisons between drug types were not biased by age distribution differences across groups. Consequently, formal statistical comparisons between age cohorts were not performed, as this falls outside the scope of our research question. Future studies with balanced age-group sampling may elucidate potential drug-age interactions using factorial designs (e.g., two-way ANOVA). Our interpretations of neural mechanisms remain speculative, as behavioral tests (SEBT, one-leg standing, reaction time) only assess functional outcomes, not structural neural damage. Future studies integrating neuroimaging are needed to validate whether the observed balance/reaction differences directly correlate with specific neuropathological changes. Uncontrolled Standardized rehab-center environments reduced external noise, but key variables (e.g., cumulative drug dose, abstinence duration, polypharmacy history) were not fully quantified. Residual confounding might influence outcomes. Prospective designs with detailed exposure metrics are needed. Although the large sample size (n = 2731) enhances generalizability, subgroup disparities, such as underrepresentation of traditional drug users aged 10–20 (n = 11) and psychotropic users aged 51–60 (n = 11)—may limit statistical power for age-specific analyses. Multi-center collaborations could address demographic imbalances. The single-timepoint assessment cannot establish causality between drug use and functional decline. Longitudinal tracking of abstainers’ recovery trajectories would clarify temporal relationships.

Conclusion

The destruction of the dynamic balance ability of the human body by new drugs is more severe. Mixed drugs are a combination of new and traditional drugs, and their disruption to the dynamic balance of the human body is only limited to new drugs. Traditional drugs prioritize disrupting the body’s static balance. The reaction rate is the same as above, with mixed drugs ranking second. However, its specific mechanism still needs further research.
