Reporting of management and outcomes in the hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn: a systematic literature review
Authors: EJT Verweij, Enrico Lopriore, Marie Fitzgibbon, Jenny Noack, Thomas Wilke, Wim Noel, Jeanine van Klink
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis systematic literature review aimed to identify outcomes and provide a foundation for the development of a core outcome set in HDFN applicable for use in future clinical trials.Study designThe bibliographic databases MEDLINE, Embase, EBM Reviews, and EconLit were searched from 2005 until 2023. Primary research studies were included. Outcomes were systematically extracted from the publications’ abstract and categorized into maternal, obstetric, neonatal, long-term, and patient-reported outcomes (PROs).Results238 included studies reported 104 different outcomes. Outcomes related to HDFN-specific treatments were the most frequently reported across studies. Long-term outcomes as well as PROs were underutilized in the screened literature.ConclusionThis SLR showed that a substantial majority of outcomes reported in HDFN-related studies are clinically-centred. PROs and long-term outcomes beyond the neonatal phase are rarely reported in the current literature emphasizing the need to better incorporate those into future clinical practice.

Introduction

Hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn (HDFN) is a blood disorder in which transplacental passage of maternal antibodies results in the hemolysis of fetal or neonatal red blood cells due to maternal-fetal red blood cell antigen incompatibility [1, 2]. Maternal antibodies can be naturally occurring or develop following a sensitizing event such as incompatible blood transfusion or feto-maternal hemorrhage in pregnancy [1, 3]. Antibodies associated with severe forms of HDFN include anti-Rh(D), anti-K, and anti-Rh(c). Severe manifestations of HDFN can have significant peri- and postnatal consequences including fetal hydrops, the risk of fetal mortality as well as neonatal morbidities, including severe hyperbilirubinemia with the risk of kernicterus [4, 5].

Despite the introduction of adequate antenatal and postnatal anti-D Ig prophylaxis, which has led to a significant decrease in the incidence of maternal D alloimmunization in developed countries, global prevalence of HDFN due to Rh-D antibodies is estimated to be 276 per 100,000 live births [1, 6]. In comparison, prevalence of Rh D disease has been reduced to 2.5 per 100,000 live births in developed countries due to higher-quality perinatal and neonatal care [7]. Due to limited access to anti-D immunoglobulin, RhD hemolytic disease still results in poor fetal and neonatal outcomes in developing countries indicating the need for improved screening and implementation of unrestricted prophylaxis in developing countries [7, 8].

Since no prophylactic immune globulins are available for HDFN caused by other RBC antigens or due to ABO incompatibility, an ongoing alloimmunization due to non-D antigens is observed [9, 10]. While there is no pharmacological therapy for HDFN, current management practices for alloimmunized pregnancies focus on antenatal monitoring techniques such as regular fetal ultrasound and Doppler measurements in the middle cerebral artery (MCA) to detect signs of fetal anemia early. The primary treatment strategy of a pregnancy affected by HDFN usually involves intrauterine blood transfusions (IUT), an invasive procedure often related with severe risks for mother and fetus [11, 12]. To minimize the associated risks, non-invasive treatment strategies such as therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) are proposed to postpone IUT in early pregnancy and to improve perinatal outcomes [12].

This SLR aims to identify outcomes assessed in recent HDFN studies, to address potential research gaps and limitations, and to establish an inventory of outcomes for the development of a core outcome set applicable for the use in future clinical trials.

Materials and Methods

Data sources, search strategy, and study selection

The protocol for this systematic review was registered on PROSPERO (International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews under the registration number CRD42023440097).

The following electronic databases were searched for relevant articles in the English language published between 1 January 2005 and 28 February 2023 using predefined search algorithms (Appendix Table 2 & Table 3): MEDLINE, Embase, EBM Reviews, and EconLit. The search string included disease terms (identifying HDFN), population terms (identifying pregnant women and their fetus, and newborns with HDFN), and study design terms (excluding irrelevant study types). A manual bibliographical check of included articles was conducted at the title and abstract screening stage and all identified review articles, guidelines, SLRs, or meta-analyses were screened for relevant original publications not identified by the electronic database search. Titles and abstracts were assessed independently by two reviewers for inclusion according to predefined population, interventions, comparators, outcomes, study designs, time (PICOS-T) selection criteria. Any disagreement was discussed between reviewers and, if necessary, a mediator [independent reviewer from Cytel] was involved in making a final decision. Contrary to the traditional SLR methodology that demands the examination of publications during an initial title and abstract screening phase followed by a full text examination phase [13], in this modified review, data were directly extracted from the publications’ abstract. Studies that were found not to be in compliance with the eligibility criteria during the stage of title and abstract screening, were not further assessed during a subsequent screening of the respective full text.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

Only primary research studies, including randomized controlled trials, retrospective and prospective cohort studies, cross-sectional studies, and case-control studies. Due to the rarity of the disease, no sample size restrictions were applied.

Studies involving pregnant women and/or their fetuses, infants, newborns, or children experiencing or having experienced HDFN were included. No restrictions regarding study setting, age, comorbidities, or concomitant disease were defined. Further, no limitations were applied to the type of HDFN-mediating antibody.

To avoid missing relevant publications due to variability in terminology used for HDFN, studies with terms similar to HDFN, such as “hemolytic disease of the newborn”, “newborn hemolytic disease”, “rhesus hemolytic disease”, “alloimmune hemolytic disease”, and “erythroblastosis fetalis” were included.

Exclusion criteria

Secondary research studies such as reviews, guidelines, and commentaries as well as animal and in vitro studies were excluded from the search. Additionally, studies were excluded if they exclusively reported on epidemiology-related outcomes or predictors of outcomes.

Outcomes of interest

Primary and secondary outcomes were extracted from eligible studies. Outcome measures were defined as the impact of a health care service or intervention on the health status of a patient, e.g., the overall survival of neonates after receiving IUT. Additionally, outcome measures were extracted that reflected the type of treatment or intervention received by the study population, e.g., “the percentages of neonates receiving phototherapy”. If available, the timepoint of measurement was extracted. Outcome measures were grouped into superior outcome categories, e.g. “the percentage of neonates receiving phototherapy” was grouped as superior outcome “phototherapy”. The number of reported outcomes refers to the number of superior level outcomes.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two reviewers independently extracted data from all studies in compliance with the inclusion criteria following a standardized data extraction sheet. No results were extracted, but only the outcome measure as reported in the publication. Any disagreements were resolved by mutual discussion or, if necessary, via the involvement of a mediator [independent reviewer from Cytel]. Extracted outcomes were allocated into the categories maternal, obstetric, neonatal, and long-term outcomes as well as patient-reported outcomes (PROs).

Maternal outcomes were defined as outcomes concerning the mother, mostly occurring during pre-pregnancy. Obstetric outcomes included outcomes related to the fetus and treatments and interventions received during pregnancy. Outcomes related to the time after birth concerning the neonate were categorized as neonatal outcomes. Any outcome reported beyond the neonatal phase including childhood and adolescence were summarized as long-term outcomes.

Data analysis

Extracted outcomes were summarized in a qualitative and descriptive manner only and no further analysis was conducted. The number and percentage of publications reporting a specific outcome was calculated.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

The Ovid search identified 2387 articles after duplicate articles (n = 527) were removed. Additionally, 25 studies meeting the inclusion criteria were identified from a manual reference check of included studies, resulting in a total number of 2412 studies. After the title and abstract screening, 2174 articles were considered to not meet the inclusion criteria. Reasons for exclusion are depicted in Fig. 1. Finally, 238 studies were included in the final analysis of which n = 221 studies were observational studies (retrospective and prospective cohort studies: n = 160, cross-sectional studies: n = 9, case-control studies: n = 16, chart reviews: n = 16, other: n = 20), and n = 17 were clinical trials. 1/2 (n = 120, 50.4%) of all study abstracts reported the location of data collection with the highest number of studies conducted in the Netherlands (n = 28) with the vast majority of studies (n = 27/28) originating from Leiden University Medical Center (LUMC), followed by India (n = 11), and China (n = 7).Fig. 1PRISMA flow chart.

Outcomes reported across observational studies and clinical for HDFN.

Maternal outcomes

Overall, three outcomes were reported for the time before an affected pregnancy in 4% (n = 9) of all 238 included studies. Identified outcomes included previous fetal loss, history of a previous HDFN-affected pregnancy, as well anti-D prophylaxis administration reported as patients receiving and dosage of prophylaxis.

Obstetric outcomes

In total, 39 outcomes were reported in 54% (n = 129) of all included studies for the phase of pregnancy. The most commonly reported outcome was IUT reported by 36% (n = 85 studies) of all studies and measured as the need for IUT, the gestational age at first IUT, and the number and interval therein of IUTs per pregnancy.

12% of the studies (n = 29) reported fetal mortality. Variations in the definitions reporting time of fetal mortality were observed and included time of death after IUT, gestational week, or unspecified. Definitions included ‘fetal death’, ‘perinatal mortality’, ‘intrauterine death’, and ‘fetal loss rate’. Of the 29 studies reporting fetal mortality, 11 reported fetal and perinatal mortality directly related to treatment. Hydrops fetalis was reported in 7% (n = 16) of the studies. Maternal antibody titers were described in 8% (n = 20) of the studies and 10% (n = 23) reported fetal hemoglobin levels.

Treatment response and treatment-related adverse events (AE) during pregnancy were evaluated in 15% (n = 36) of all studies overall. Other treatment-related outcomes (beyond fetal and perinatal mortality) included fetal survival (4%, n = 9), emergency cesarean section (2%, n = 4), the improvement of laboratory parameters (1%, n = 3), gestational age at birth (1%, n = 2), pre-term birth (2%, n = 4), the type and rate of procedure-related complications (4%, n = 10), development and enhancement of red blood cell antibodies (3%, n = 8), and resolution of hydrops fetalis (2%, n = 4).

Healthcare resource use (HCRU) during pregnancy was infrequently reported in the literature by only 1% (n = 3) of all included studies. HCRU during the pregnancy phase was measured as need for hospitalization, need for referral consultation, and direct treatment costs.

Neonatal outcomes

Outcomes related to the need and use of exchange transfusion were reported by 46% (n = 109) of all studies. It was measured across studies as the proportion of neonates requiring exchange transfusion, the age at first exchange transfusion, and the number and frequency of transfusions per neonate. Other reported treatment outcomes included phototherapy (40%, n = 95), and simple red blood cell transfusion (22%, n = 53).

24% of all studies (n = 56) reported total serum bilirubin (TSB) measurements. A wide variation in the reporting of this outcome was identified, including a variation in definition and timepoint of measurement. Definitions included “maximum level of TSB”, “initial indirect TSB”, “final indirect TSB”, “mean TSB”, “median TSB”, and “time of TSB maximum peak”. 14% (n = 33) of studies reported gestational age at delivery, 13% (n = 31) reported treatment-related adverse events including general procedure-related complications (7%, n = 16), as well as the development of necrotizing enterocolitis (1%, n = 3), and infection (1%, n = 2). 11% (n = 25) of the studies reported neonatal mortality. The reporting of time of neonatal death varied across studies. Outcomes related to treatment response, i.e., reduced need for additional treatment or the improvement of blood variables after having received treatment were reported by only 8% (n = 19) of all studies.

Long-term outcomes

Long-term outcomes, i.e., outcomes beyond the neonatal phase, were reported in 12% (n = 29) of the included studies. Of these, 41% (n = 12/29) of the studies focused on neurological, cognitive, or physical impairment. Other long-term outcomes included the need for long-term support and therapy (17%, n = 5), behavioral problems (14%, n = 4), and long-term mortality (7%, n = 2).

PROs were reported by less than 1% (n = 2) of the studies. All identified PROs are listed in Table 1.

Discussion

This SLR identified maternal, obstetric, neonatal, long-term, and patient-reported outcomes and their reporting frequency in the recent (2005-2023) literature within the context of HDFN. A total of 104 outcomes were identified among the observational and clinical studies evaluated. This was found across different phases of an HDFN-affected pregnancy, including the time before pregnancy through the ante- and postnatal period and up to long-term follow-up.

There were significant variations and inconsistencies in how these outcomes were reported, defined, and measured across studies. For example, TSB, which is an essential indicator for the severity of HDFN and treatment success in the neonatal phase, was reported as “maximum level of TSB”, “initial indirect TSB”, “final indirect TSB”, “mean TSB”, “median TSB”, and “time of TSB maximum peak”, which may complicate the comparison and synthesis of data between studies and restrict the ability to perform meta-analyses. Inconsistencies in outcome selection and reporting have already been discussed by similar studies [14] and previous research in obstetrics and neonatology has also highlighted similar inconsistencies in outcome reporting, suggesting this is a broader issue that extends beyond HDFN [15–18], Additionally, the high number of extracted outcomes indicates that there is a great variation in the type of outcomes that are reported across studies highlighting the need to standardize the process of outcome selection for all studies within this disease area [15].

Most studies focused on the reporting of treatment-related outcomes. Treatment-related outcomes were reported for the antenatal as well as the postnatal period and included the need for treatment, time to treatment, duration of treatment as well as treatment response, and the occurrence of adverse events. The high percentage of studies reporting IUT-related outcomes and the fact that alternative management strategies such as IVIG and TPE have been reported less frequently in the recent literature suggests that to date, IUT remains the cornerstone in the antenatal management of HDFN and the high need for novel, non-surgical treatment options. This coincides with the evidence presented by another systematic literature review conducted by Winter et al. reporting limited evidence for the frequency of use and effectiveness of alternative treatment options such as IVIG, plasmapheresis, and plasma exchange on disease severity and the prevention of fetal anemia [19]. Even though severe cases of HDFN still present a major challenge, RhD prophylaxis, the use of Doppler ultrasound to detect fetal anemia, and treatment with IUT, perinatal survival rates exceed 95% in experienced centers [5, 20]. Simultaneously, this is leading to an increase in surviving children with long-term morbidities requiring an understanding of the long-term effects on neurodevelopment and impairment after severe HDFN and IUT treatment [21]. This review emphasizes that outcomes beyond the neonatal phase are not commonly reported across clinical trials or observational studies with only 13% of all included studies obtaining long-term related outcomes. No increasing trend of studies reporting long-term outcomes was observed over the past twenty years.

Furthermore, this review highlights a significant underreporting of patient-reported outcomes (PROs), as this was only reported in two studies. In a recent nationwide retrospective cohort, they observed the recurrence of women requiring one or more intrauterine transfusions (IUTs) in subsequent pregnancies, as well as the gestational age at the first IUT [22]. Their findings revealed that many women required IUTs earlier in subsequent pregnancies, resulting in a higher risk of recurrent complications. This early recurrence may influence some women to reconsider future pregnancies due to the physical and emotional burden associated with HDFN. Hence, identifying PROs specific to women with pregnancy complications is important in understanding the real-life burden experienced by mothers and families.

Incorporating outcomes that better reflect disease burden, such as PROs, is crucial in the development and validation of a core outcome set for HDFN research. The growing body of literature has emphasized the importance of integrating the patient and family voice into medical research. For example, Dickinson et al. reported that while 14 patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) have been utilized in pregnancy and childbirth studies, most were generic and not specific to pregnancy-related complications [23]. This points to an urgent need for the development of validated, condition-specific tools to capture the lived experiences of women dealing with complex pregnancies, including HDFN. Including PROMs in future HDFN studies is essential for a holistic understanding of the disease’s impact on families.This SLR lays the foundation for the development of a core outcome set (COS) for HDFN. The Core Outcome Measures for Efficacy Trials (COMET) has described three stages for the development and standardizing of core outcome sets that are equally relevant to patients, carers, and health professionals [24]: (1) identifying potential core outcomes, (2) determining core outcomes using robust consensus methods engaging key stakeholders, and (3) determining how core outcomes should be measured. Evidence generated from this SLR has guided an in-depth discussion in terms of the importance and relevance of outcomes in multiple international advisory boards with several stakeholder groups, including healthcare professionals and patient representatives.

Strengths and limitations

The results of this SLR should be interpreted with consideration of several strengths and limitations. The strength of this prospectively registered systematic review includes its comprehensive search strategy and the inclusion of a wide range of study types. To reduce the risk of bias, study screening and data extraction was performed by two independent reviewers.

The results of this SLR are limited by the considerable heterogeneity and variation in the methodology of the studies making it difficult to summarize and compare available data on the management and outcome selection between studies. Nevertheless, as the overall objective of the study was to establish an inventory of outcomes reported for HDFN in the current literature, the chosen study design was adequate to achieve this goal.

Conclusion

This SLR shows that there is a considerable variation in the reporting of outcomes across HDFN publications. It highlights the need to standardize the process of outcome selection for all studies within this disease. The majority of studies focused on treatment-related outcomes, the importance of understanding treatment outcomes and the gaps in treatment for HDFN patients. Additionally, this SLR identified outcome domains that are evidently under-reported in the current literature including long-term effects reporting outcomes beyond the neonatal phase as well as PRO, adding further evidence to the ongoing discussion for the need of the inclusion of PROs into future studies.
