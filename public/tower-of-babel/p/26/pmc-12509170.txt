Antibodies IgA, IgE, IgM, and IgG Reactivities Against Two Species of Anisakis in Three Cases of Anisakis Allergy
Authors: Makoto Kondo, Koji Habe, Keiichi Yamanaka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe current state of research on Anisakis allergy remains insufficiently understood. It is believed that the interaction between the complex combination of Anisakis protein components and the immune regulatory mechanisms of the human body plays a role. However, many studies have focused on a single immunoglobulin. Therefore, we examined the response of IgA, IgE, IgM, and IgG to the constituent proteins of the two Anisakis species. The subjects included a patient who developed Anisakis‐induced urticaria (Case 1), who had a history of urticaria (Case 2), and who had experienced gastrointestinal symptoms caused by Anisakis in the past (Case 3). These cases were comprehensively evaluated using Western blotting analysis. This study found distinct immune response differences between Anisakis simplex ( A. simplex ) and Anisakis pegreffii (A. pegreffii) in three patient cases. All patients showed antigen–antibody reactions to both species, with stronger responses to A. simplex . IgE and IgA responses were more prominent compared to those with IgG and IgM. In Case 1, recurring urticaria corresponded with elevated IgE and IgA levels, with IgE reactions observed at several molecular weights. Intense IgM reactions appeared around 20 kDa for both species. Case 2, with resolved urticaria, showed weaker immune responses. Case 3, despite no intestinal symptoms, displayed reactions similar to Case 2, particularly for IgA and IgM. Even in patients without a long‐standing Anisakis allergy, all immunoglobulins respond to certain Anisakis components. Once developed, the allergy may recur unexpectedly.Keywords: Anisakis allergy, Anisakis pegreffii, Anisakis simplex, immunoglobulins

Introduction

Anisakis allergy predominantly presents with gastrointestinal symptoms and urticaria. Recently, various allergen components of Anisakis have been identified [1, 2, 3]. Multiple antigens, rather than a single component, are believed to be involved in allergic reactions, leading to a wide range of clinical manifestations [4]. We used serum samples from two patients with urticaria and one with gastrointestinal symptoms related to Anisakis to investigate how their IgA, IgE, IgM, and IgG antibodies react with protein components from two species of Anisakis ( A. simplex and A. pegreffii), using the Western blotting method. To our knowledge, no previous studies have evaluated two species of Anisakis and multiple immunoglobulins simultaneously, making this study a valuable contribution to this field. Although previous studies have assessed allergic reactions involving single immunoglobulins, such as IgE, this study provides a more comprehensive evaluation by considering multiple immunoglobulins against both species.

Case Series

Case 1

An 84‐year‐old female presented with a history of episodic urticaria. She had previously experienced severe epigastric pain after eating raw fish; an endoscopic examination revealed an Anisakis larva attached to the gastric wall, which was subsequently removed. Following this event, she began to experience recurring episodes of urticaria. She has no history of autoimmune thyroid inflammatory disease. Raised in a seaside town on a fish‐centric diet, she still consumes raw or cooked fish at nearly every meal. She also reports that urticaria never occurs when she refrains from eating. Although she frequently develops mild urticarial episodes, they are typically so minor that she does not seek medical attention. However, she presented to our clinic with a severe chief complaint of urticaria (Figure 1A). Blood tests revealed an Anisakis‐specific IgE level of 58.4 kU/L (normal range < 0.35) and a total IgE level of 115 IU/L (normal range < 173). She was prescribed antihistamines, which led to an improvement in her symptoms.

Case 2

A 36‐year‐old male presented to the emergency department with urticaria a few hours after eating a tuna bowl. After he was transported and his condition stabilized, various allergen tests were performed. Blood tests revealed only an Anisakis‐specific IgE level of 1.94 UA/mL and a total IgE level of 26 IU/mL, and he was diagnosed with Anisakis allergy and prescribed an epinephrine auto‐injector. Following the strong positive Anisakis‐specific IgE result, the patient began avoiding raw fish entirely and personally researched which species might harbor Anisakis, refraining from consuming anything at risk. He has not experienced further episodes of urticaria and has never needed to use the auto‐injector. These showed an Anisakis‐specific IgE level of 30.4 UA/mL and a total IgE level of 115 IU/mL. The patient continues to avoid raw fish as much as possible.

Case 3

Eight years ago, a 47‐year‐old woman experienced vomiting and diarrhea a few hours after eating raw salmon she had purchased. The patient noted that the salmon had a thread‐like substance on it, and a blood test for Anisakis was performed, revealing an Anisakis‐specific IgE level of 13.2 UA/mL (normal < 0.35) and a total IgE level of 141 IU/mL (normal < 173). The patient mentioned that she always carefully inspects raw fish for parasites before eating. Since that episode, despite continuing to consume raw fish, she has not experienced further vomiting or gastrointestinal symptoms.

Methods

We obtained a mackerel ( Scomber japonicus ) caught in Aomori Prefecture, Japan, and a Japanese sea bass ( Lateolabrax japonicus ) caught in Hokkaido, Japan. Anisakis parasites were extracted using a black light (Anisakis Light Tsumoto Method, Hapyson, Osaka, Japan) (Figure 1B,C). Anisakis proteins were extracted from the Anisakis removed from the mackerel and the Japanese sea bass using RIPA buffer (NACALAI TESQUE, Kyoto, Japan). DNA was then extracted by using a QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen GmbH, Hilden, Germany) from the protein extract solution, and the amplified PCR products using Anisakis‐specific identification primers were sequenced [5, 6]. The sequences identified the parasites as A. simplex (from the mackerel) and A. pegreffii (from Japanese sea bass).

The Western blotting method confirmed the antigen–antibody reactions between immunoglobulins A, E, M, and G from three patients and two types of Anisakis proteins (200 ng/each lane). After SDS‐PAGE electrophoresis of the Anisakis proteins, they were transferred onto a PVDF membrane using the Trans‐Blot Turbo Transfer System (BIO‐RAD, Hercules, CA, USA). Western blotting was performed following the procedures of the iBind Automated Western System (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc. Taito‐ku, Tokyo). The serum from the three patients was diluted 1:100. The secondary antibodies used were Anti‐IgA, Human, Goat‐Poly, HRP (Funakoshi, Bunkyo‐ku, Tokyo), Anti‐IgE, Human, Goat‐Poly, HRP (Funakoshi, Bunkyo‐ku, Tokyo), Anti‐IgM, Human, Goat‐Poly, HRP (Funakoshi, Bunkyo‐ku, Tokyo), and Anti‐IgG (H + L), Human, Goat‐Poly, HRP (Funakoshi, Bunkyo‐ku, Tokyo), all diluted 1:1000. After the antibody reactions were completed, TMB was used for color development to confirm antigen–antibody reactions (Figure 2).

Focusing on IgA, the bands around 40 and 45 kDa are more intensely stained, suggesting that these may be the main proteins involved in the elimination of Anisakis. IgM shows broad staining, indicating constant exposure to Anisakis antigens, although no allergic symptoms caused by Anisakis have been observed.

Results

This study identified noticeable differences in immune responses between A. simplex and A. pegreffii in three patient cases. The Western blotting analysis revealed that all patients exhibited antigen–antibody reactions to both species, but the response was more robust to A. simplex in all cases. Specifically, the IgE and IgA responses were more prominent than IgG and IgM. The study observed that in Case 1, the patient had recurring episodes of urticaria, which corresponded with increased IgE and IgA reactions. For IgE, bands around 20, 30, 37, 45, 60, and 75 kDa showed reactions. In the case of IgM, intense staining was observed at around 20 kDa for both species of Anisakis. Regarding IgA, bands around 30–40 kDa, 60, and 75 kDa were stained. In contrast, in Case 2, where urticaria had subsided for a long time, there was a weaker immune response across all immunoglobulins. Specifically, IgE and IgG showed weak reactions. In Case 3, although no intestinal allergic symptoms were present for a long time, all immunoglobulins reacted. It showed a similar IgA and IgM profile to that of Case 2.

Discussion

In this study, we compared IgE, IgA, IgG, and IgM responses to A. simplex and A. pegreffii proteins in three Japanese patients, all residents of Mie Prefecture—a region where A. simplex overwhelmingly predominates. This geographic context likely contributed to consistently stronger responses to A. simplex across all cases. Patients with urticaria who test positive for Anisakis‐specific IgE may experience flare‐ups related to seafood consumption, but they may also have episodes that are unrelated to meals [7]. It is difficult to determine whether these reactions occur exclusively after eating fish or independently of diet; even when fish is strictly avoided, seasonings or other ingredients could still contain Anisakis components. Therefore, immunoglobulin reactivity against Anisakis—as seen in Cases 1 and 3—can still be elicited.

IgE Responses

IgE is the hallmark of allergic and acute anisakiasis reactions, rising sharply within days of exposure [8].

Case 1 (84‐year‐old woman): Following documented gastric attachment of A. simplex , she developed recurrent urticaria. Her Western blot showed intense IgE bands at ~20, 30, 37, 45, 60, and 75 kDa, corresponding to major allergens Ani s 12 (31 kDa) and Ani s 13 (37 kDa) [1].

Case 2 (36‐year‐old man): He presented with urticaria after a tuna bowl; after avoiding raw fish, his specific IgE initially measured 1.94 UA/mL, but rose to 30.4 UA/mL on renewal. His blot revealed weak but detectable IgE to both species, reflecting partial ongoing exposure.

Case 3 (47‐year‐old woman): Eight years prior, she had salmon‐induced gastroenteritis; casual suspicion prompted testing (Anisakis specific IgE 13.2 UA/mL). She still consumes raw fish—inspecting for parasites—without further symptoms and shows clear IgE reactivity to both species.

IgA Responses

IgA regulates long‐term mucosal immunity rather than immediate hypersensitivity [9].

Case 1 exhibited strong IgA bands at ~60 and 75 kDa, consistent with the 70 kDa excretory/secretory (ES) protein implicated in mucosal responses [10].

Cases 2 and 3 shared similar IgA bands around 40–45 kDa, suggesting these proteins may facilitate asymptomatic antigen clearance.

IgM Responses

IgM rises early in infection and wanes over weeks [11].

In all cases, a strong IgM band was observed at approximately 20 kDa against both Anisakis proteins. This low‐molecular‐weight band reflects the antibody response that rises rapidly upon initial exposure and is noteworthy as an early marker of Anisakis infection—especially in Case 1, which exhibited the strongest signal.

IgG Responses

IgG reflects cumulative or past exposure [12].

IgG reactivity was detected at roughly 30–40 kDa and 60–75 kDa, with varying signal intensities among cases. In Case 1, a prominent IgG band near 60 kDa was observed, corresponding to excretory/secretory (ES) proteins such as Ani s 1 or Ani s 7. In contrast, Cases 2 and 3 showed weaker IgG signals, which likely reflect a history of past exposure or low‐frequency exposure.

Species Differences and Cross‐Reactivity

A. simplex elicited more robust and broader immunoglobulin responses than A. pegreffii in every case, aligning with reports of higher allergenicity and sensitization rates for A. simplex worldwide [13]. Overlapping band patterns at identical kDa regions in A. pegreffii indicate conserved allergenic components, meaning A. simplex ‐sensitized patients still have a risk of reactions to A. pegreffii antigens [14].

Immunological Responses to A. simplex and A. pegreffii

The principal allergens of A. simplex —among which Ani s 1 and Ani s 7 are the most extensively characterized—exceed fifteen in number [15]. A. pegreffii possesses an essentially identical repertoire of allergenic proteins; for instance, the amino acid sequences of Ani s 1, Ani s 2, Ani s 12, and Ani s 13 are conserved at ≥ 99% identity between the two species [16], and no species‐specific allergen has yet been described [17]. Nonetheless, subtle quantitative or contextual differences in allergen expression may engender divergent host responses: in regions where A. pegreffii predominates, clinical observations suggest enhanced eosinophil activation among sensitized individuals. Indeed, in these same regions, allergic patients exhibit stronger basophil activation [18], and clinical case reports of A. pegreffii infection describe eosinophilia accompanied by urticaria and elevated serum IgE—with occasional anaphylaxis—indicating an immunopathological risk equivalent to that of A. simplex infections. Despite this, the overall immunopathological risk—manifested as urticaria, angioedema, or anaphylaxis—appears equivalent for infections caused by either species.

Conclusion

It should be noted that, in the context of allergy diagnostics, discrimination between the two species is frequently impracticable. In terms of treatment and preventive measures, no substantive differences have been identified. Although the predominance of either species varies geographically, rigorous food‐hygiene practices and prompt diagnosis with appropriate therapeutic intervention remain essential irrespective of the infecting species. Collectively, these observations indicate that the human allergic immune responses induced by A. simplex and A. pegreffii are largely congruent, with any disparities attributable chiefly to differences in exposure risk and quantitative variations in antigen expression. Accordingly, clinical management is best approached by regarding both species under the umbrella of “anisakiasis allergy” and applying unified diagnostic and treatment strategies.
