Early detection of positive urine culture in patients with urolithiasis: a machine learning model with dynamic online nomogram
Authors: Di Luo, Jingdong Zhang, Linguo Xie, Chunyu Liu, Rui Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPositive urine cultures are common in urinary stone patients, yet tools for early infection prediction are limited. To address this gap, a user-friendly, dynamic online nomogram was developed to predict the incidence of positive urine cultures in patients with urolithiasis.MethodsA retrospective study was conducted with 3,641 patients with urinary stones at the Second Hospital of Tianjin Medical University. The cohort was split into training and validation sets. Key variables were identified using Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression, while Random Forest and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) methods were applied to assess their importance. Online nomograms were developed and evaluated for performance through metrics such as area under the curve (AUC), calibration curve, decision curve analysis (DCA), probability density function (PDF), and clinical utility curve (CUC).ResultsMultivariate logistic analysis identified four significant predictors—bacteria (BACT), C-reactive protein (CRP), nitrite, and leukocyte esterase (LEU)—which were integrated into the nomogram. The AUC values for the overall, training, and validation sets were 90.53, 91.22, and 89.06%, respectively. Calibration curves confirmed the nomogram’s accuracy, and DCA demonstrated its superior performance over individual metrics. The PDF/CUC method revealed a threshold of 0.168, which effectively distinguished 88.54% of negatives from 78.70% of positives.ConclusionsThis dynamic online nomogram accurately predicts positive urine cultures in patients with urolithiasis, helping clinicians identify high-risk individuals , optimize antibiotic use, and improve patient outcomes. Further validation and biomarker exploration are needed to enhance its generalizability.

Introduction

Urinary tract infection (UTI), particularly culture-confirmed bacterial infections, represents a common complication in patients with urinary stones. Perioperative culture-positive UTIs can prolong treatment and potentially lead to severe complications such as sepsis and septic shock [1,2]. Effective management of infections is paramount for urologists, as early identification of high-risk patients can improve clinical decision-making, reduce sepsis incidence [3], and mitigate unnecessary antibiotic use—an essential consideration in the context of rising antimicrobial resistance [4].

However, early diagnosis of bacterial UTIs is hindered by the 48-hour delay required for urine cultures, the gold standard diagnostic method. Clinicians are often forced to rely on subjective symptom assessments, physical exams, and preliminary laboratory findings [5,6], which may result in inappropriate antibiotic prescribing, either excessive or insufficient.

While previous studies have identified various predictive variables for UTIs or positive urine cultures, few have integrated these factors into comprehensive multivariate models [7–9]. Nomograms effectively address this challenge by offering personalized, visual risk assessments that combine multiple variables. Dynamic online nomograms further enhance this utility with real-time web-based interactivity, improving clinical decision-making and demonstrating strong predictive accuracy for disease occurrence and cancer prognosis [10–13].

Given the limitations of urine cultures and the elevated risk of culture-confirmed UTIs in patients with urolithiasis, an efficient, real-time predictive tool is urgently needed. Existing models lack the integration of dynamic clinical data and machine learning techniques for risk assessment. To fill this gap, a dynamic online nomogram was developed that combines real-time parameters—such as age, stone characteristics, and urinalysis—with machine learning to predict positive urine culture. This tool aims to optimize antibiotic stewardship, reduce healthcare costs, and improve patient outcomes, advancing precision medicine in the management of urolithiasis.

Patients and methods

Patient population

The study was approved by the ethics committee of the Second Hospital of Tianjin Medical University. Written informed consent was waived by the committee due to the retrospective nature of the study, and all data were anonymized prior to analysis. The study adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Data were retrospectively analyzed for patients who underwent flexible ureteroscopic lithotripsy or percutaneous nephrolithotomy for urinary calculi at the Second Hospital of Tianjin Medical University between January 2022 and December 2023.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were as follows: patients aged 18 or older; those diagnosed with urinary calculi through imaging; candidates for surgery related to urinary calculi; patients who had not received preoperative antibiotic therapy (except for empirical antibiotics administered after hospital admission); and patients with complete clinical and laboratory data.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: patients under 18 years of age; those receiving ongoing antibiotic therapy; individuals with anatomical abnormalities, a solitary kidney, missing laboratory values, chronic bacteriuria, or urinary incontinence due to indwelling catheters or stents.

Data collection process

A dedicated medical team, trained to standardize data collection procedures, gathered the data. Clinical information, including age, gender, medical history, and laboratory results, was meticulously recorded in the electronic medical record system using a specially designed data collection form. Data were collected from January 2022 to December 2023, including clinical data and laboratory results (such as urine bacterial quantification and blood tests) obtained at admission before any surgical interventions. Symptoms, including fever and dysuria, were also documented at this time.

Laboratory analysis

Urine BACT quantification

Urinary bacterial counts (BACT) were quantified using Sysmex UF-5000 automated flow cytometry (Sysmex Corporation, Kobe, Japan), with ≥20 bacteria/μL considered clinically significant. This method showed over 90% concordance with urine cultures for Gram-negative infections in validation studies [8].

Blood and urine sample handling

Blood and urine samples were processed within 1 h of collection. Urine cultures were performed using midstream specimens cultured on blood and MacConkey agar at 37 °C for 24–48 h.

Clinical protocol for febrile patients

Febrile patients (axillary temperature ≥38 °C) received empirical antibiotics only after urine culture collection, in accordance with institutional guidelines. Antibiotic regimens were adjusted based on subsequent culture and sensitivity results. This approach ensured research integrity by preventing pretreatment contamination and facilitating timely clinical intervention.

Management of potential biases

Several measures were implemented to minimize potential biases. First, data were collected from all patients admitted to the hospital between January 2022 and December 2023, ensuring sample representativeness. Second, assessors were blinded to patient treatment allocation during data collection and analysis to mitigate assessment bias. Third, double verification of all data was performed, with two independent researchers entering the data separately and comparing results to ensure accuracy. Fourth, missing data were addressed using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE), implemented via the R ‘mice’ package (version 4.3.2). Twenty imputed datasets were generated using predictive mean matching for continuous variables and logistic regression for categorical variables, incorporating all baseline characteristics and outcome measures as predictors. The imputation process adhered to recommended guidelines to ensure convergence, minimizing bias from missing data while preserving statistical power (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2).

Statistical analysis

Continuous normally distributed variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non-normally distributed variables were presented as median (interquartile range). Categorical variables were reported as percentages. To simulate external validation and assess model performance, patients were randomly divided into a training cohort (80%) and a test cohort (20%), with characteristics compared between the two groups. Relevant predictors for the training cohort were selected using ten-fold cross-validation and the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) method. Missing values in the training dataset were interpolated using predictor-mean matching. Multicollinearity among clinical variables was assessed via Spearman’s correlation analysis and variance inflation factor (VIF) methods, with a VIF <5 indicating low multicollinearity, 5–10 indicating moderate, and ≥10 indicating high multicollinearity. The Random Forest method and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) were used to rank the significance of independent variables identified in univariate analysis. Multifactorial logistic regression analyses then selected the clinical variables with the highest discriminatory power to construct predictive nomograms for UTI diagnosis. Discriminability of the nomogram was evaluated using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, calibration was assessed with the calibration curve, and decision curve analysis (DCA) determined the net clinical benefit. The probability density function (PDF) and clinical utility curve (CUC) were utilized to identify the optimal threshold for UTI diagnosis [14,15]. An interactive, web-based dynamic nomogram application was developed using Shiny (version 0.13.2.26) for clinical use. The study design and analysis process are summarized in the graphical abstract. Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 4.3.2 (http://www.r-project.org), with results considered significant at p < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics and laboratory profiles: comparison between training and validation sets

The final analysis included 3,641 patients, stratified by urine culture status according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. This stratification enabled comparisons of characteristics, underlying diseases, clinical signs, stone-related information, and hydronephrosis severity between groups (Table 1). Additionally, laboratory results, including blood and urine tests, next-generation immunoinflammatory markers, and cohort summaries for the training and validation sets, were compared based on urine culture positivity (Table 2).

Among the entire cohort, 572 patients (15.7%) had positive urine cultures. These patients were more likely to be female (59.97 vs. 40.03%, p < 0.001), generally older (57.6 ± 26.1 years vs. 55.3 ± 18.7 years, p = 0.01), and had a higher prevalence of comorbid diabetes mellitus (33.74 vs. 28.15%, p = 0.007). Symptomatically, culture-positive patients experienced a higher incidence of dysuria (9.09 vs. 5.57%, p = 0.001) and fever (9.44 vs. 5.44%, p < 0.001). The degree of hydronephrosis was also significantly more severe in culture-positive patients compared to those without infection (moderate: 19.58 vs. 16.00%; severe: 5.59 vs. 4.11%, p = 0.001) (Table 1).

In terms of laboratory findings, culture-positive patients had elevated levels of urea nitrogen (6.29 ± 2.87 vs. 5.62 ± 2.28 mg/dL, p < 0.001), urea nitrogen/albumin ratio (0.17 ± 0.09 vs. 0.14 ± 0.06, p < 0.001), C-reactive protein (CRP) levels greater than 5 mg/L (53.96 vs. 17.71%, p < 0.001), procalcitonin (PCT) levels exceeding 0.05 ng/mL (48.72 vs. 25.63%, p < 0.001), and six next-generation immunoinflammatory markers: NLR (1.89 [1.43, 3.15] vs. 1.80 [1.39, 2.52], p = 0.001), MLR (0.24 [0.18, 0.35] vs. 0.23 [0.18, 0.30], p < 0.001), PLR (123.11 [90.09, 170.07] vs. 112.55 [90.37, 147.55], p < 0.001), SII (460.37 [299.72, 804.99] vs. 412.04 [298.57, 606.47], p < 0.001), SIRI (0.87 [0.58, 1.68] vs. 0.83 [0.57, 1.25], p = 0.002), and SIIRI (209.36 [123.32, 374.94] vs. 186.70 [124.47, 302.50], p = 0.002). Conversely, albumin levels (40.28 ± 2.76 g/L vs. 40.80 ± 3.95 g/L, p < 0.001) and lymphocyte counts (1.93 [1.40, 2.35]/μL vs. 1.96 [1.56, 2.46]/μL, p = 0.002) were lower in culture-positive patients.

Urine findings revealed significantly higher levels of urinary leucocytes (149 [39, 490]/μL vs. 18 [6, 54]/μL, p < 0.001), urinary erythrocytes (59 [10, 222]/μL vs. 15 [3, 94]/μL, p < 0.001), and bacterial counts (118 [55, 359] bacteria/μL vs. 14 [1, 51] bacteria/μL, p < 0.001). Additionally, culture-positive patients exhibited higher positivity rates for leukocyte esterase (2+: 18.71% vs. 11.83%; 3+: 56.12 vs. 8.05%) and nitrite (36.89 vs. 0.75%) compared to culture-negative patients (Table 2). Other indicators did not show significant differences between the groups. Notably, all variables and urine culture positivity rates were well-balanced between the training and validation sets (all p > 0.05).

Multivariate logistic regression further identified four highly discriminative predictors: BACT, LEU, NIT, and CRP (Table 3). The model was subsequently developed into an easy-to-use nomogram using R software (Figure 3(A)). The DCA for the nomogram is presented in Figure 5(A–C). The high-risk threshold probability represents the point at which model predictions may be significantly biased if used incorrectly for diagnosis and decision-making. The primary objective of the DCA curve is to identify a decision threshold that maximizes net benefit across a range of risk thresholds. This curve aids healthcare decision-makers in selecting the diagnostic tool or prediction model that best aligns with their needs. In this study, the DCA curve demonstrated that the nomogram provides substantial net benefits for clinical application. Additionally, the newly fitted logistic regression model produced a higher net benefit compared to any single predictor.

The term ‘net benefit’ refers to the overall effect after considering the benefits and drawbacks of false-positive and false-negative results. Selection of the optimal threshold probability

Selecting the optimal threshold probability is critical for improving clinical decision-making, a process effectively guided by PDF and CUC. As shown in Figure 6(A), although some overlap exists between the two PDF curves, the distribution for culture-negative patients exhibits a sharp, concentrated peak within the 0–16.8% risk range, in stark contrast to the more evenly distributed curve for culture-positive patients. The CUC (Figure 6(B)) illustrates the model’s performance across various probability thresholds, emphasizing the percentage of true positives for both groups. Notably, accurate identification of culture-negative cases is as crucial as diagnosing culture-positive cases in clinical practice. At the chosen threshold of 16.8%, the model achieves an optimal balance, correctly classifying 88.544% of culture-negative cases while maintaining 78.696% sensitivity for culture-positive cases.

Discussion

This study developed and validated a dynamic online nomogram for predicting culture-confirmed UTIs in patients with urolithiasis using machine learning techniques. The model identified four key predictors: (1) elevated bacterial count (BACT), (2) positive leukocyte esterase (LEU), (3) positive nitrites (NIT), and (4) increased CRP levels, demonstrating excellent discriminative ability (AUC 0.91). Further analysis using the PDF and CUC revealed that a threshold probability of 16.8% effectively distinguished between culture-positive and culture-negative patients, with 88.5% specificity and 78.7% sensitivity. This threshold is recommended for guiding clinical decisions, such as the initiation of antibiotic therapy. In clinical practice, patients with a predicted probability exceeding 16.8% may warrant immediate evaluation and early antibiotic treatment, while those below this threshold could be managed conservatively with close monitoring, thereby optimizing the balance between the risks of under-treatment and over-treatment.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis confirmed the role of established UTI diagnostic markers while highlighting certain limitations. Nitrite, which scored highest in the model, exhibited exceptional specificity (99.19%) but low sensitivity (38.04%), a pattern well-documented in the literature [16–18]. This performance gap reflects nitrite’s exclusive ability to detect Gram-negative bacteria, underscoring the need for complementary predictors to identify non-nitrite-producing organisms. The diagnostic value of combining nitrite with urinary leukocyte esterase has been validated in previous studies, significantly enhancing reliability [19,20]. Additionally, Erdal Yilmaz et al. highlighted CRP’s utility in early detection of bacteremia and bacteriuria following extracorporeal shockwave lithotripsy [21]. Guoqiang Zhang et al. further established that combining leukocyte esterase with BACT yields optimal positive predictive value for bacterial UTI diagnosis [8]. In our study, BACT demonstrated robust performance with 71.52% sensitivity and 80.56% specificity, aligning closely with findings from Nico C. Grossmann et al. who identified a predictive model incorporating CRP, LEU, and NIT for clinical decision-making in patients with symptomatic ureteral calculi [9]. However, the clinical application of such models remains constrained by their reliance on traditional nomogram methods.

Li Yongzhi et al. identified several independent risk factors for culture-confirmed UTI in patients with urolithiasis, including gender, age, site of obstruction, stone shape, and stone multiplicity [22]. In our analysis, culture-positive UTIs were more prevalent among women and older patients, while stone location and number showed no significant association. Diabetes mellitus, a well-established risk factor for infections, was notably more common among culture-positive UTI individuals compared to culture-negative ones. Given that our institution’s prior data reported a 10% urosepsis rate following flexible ureteroscopic surgery (FURS) procedures [23], the development of this preoperative prediction model has significant clinical implications, even though postoperative infections were not the primary endpoint. This context further supports the inclusion of new-generation immuno-inflammatory markers in the analysis. Yusuf Elgormus et al. demonstrated that WBC, CRP, NLR, PLR, and SII serve as independent or complementary diagnostic markers for pediatric UTI [24], consistent with our approach to adult preoperative risk stratification.

Accordingly, these markers were included, along with two additional ones, SIRI and SIIRI, in the analysis. Univariate analysis demonstrated their discriminatory power in detecting culture-confirmed UTI in patients with urolithiasis. Tej K. Mattoo et al. identified blood PCT and urinary neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL) as the most predictive markers for bacterial UTI [25]. Although PCT showed strong discriminatory ability in this study, its performance was limited by significant missing values due to its infrequent use at the Second Hospital of Tianjin Medical University. Hyesun Hyun et al. recognized the urea nitrogen-to-serum albumin ratio (BAR) and lower blood lymphocyte counts as novel predictors for febrile UTI with bacteremia in young children [26]. In this study, both BAR and lymphocyte count exhibited good discriminatory power. Given that urinary stones may cause postrenal renal insufficiency and infections often reduce albumin levels, BAR proved somewhat effective in predicting culture-confirmed UTI in patients with urolithiasis. Despite some discriminatory value from variables such as gender, age, diabetes history, lymphocyte count, BAR, PCT, and new-generation markers, BACT, LEU, NIT, and serum CRP emerged as more effective predictors. Incorporating the robust predictors identified in existing studies is essential; however, identifying novel inflammatory factors that have yet to be applied in bacterial UTI remains critical. Recent advancements in molecular biology and immunology have highlighted the significant role of inflammatory factors in infectious diseases. These factors, central to the body’s immune response, regulate immune cell activation and migration, mediate tissue damage, and facilitate pathogen clearance [27]. During a bacterial UTI, pathogen invasion of the urethra triggers both innate and adaptive immune responses, producing a series of inflammatory factors that influence disease progression and resolution through complex regulatory mechanisms [28,29].

Building on this, future research should include comprehensive Mendelian randomization analyses to explore potential links between systemic inflammatory modulators and UTI risk. Previous GWAS studies, such as those by Zhao JH et al. [30], have created valuable databases for such investigations. Several inflammatory factors warrant specific attention based on existing literature: Macrophage Colony-Stimulating Factor 1 (M-CSF-1), known for its role in macrophage activation and tissue repair [31]; S100-A12, a recognized biomarker for acute infections [32]; and Monocyte Chemoattractant Protein-4 (MCP-4/CCL13), which regulates immune cell migration [33]. These mediators represent promising candidates for identifying early predictors of UTI susceptibility.

Since all the predictive parameters identified in our model are part of routine examinations for patients with urolithiasis, its implementation is both feasible and likely to be well-received by clinicians. This holds significant clinical value, as there are currently no established guidelines or thresholds for predictive parameters in the immediate diagnosis of concurrently positive urine cultures. The personal and subjective interpretation of laboratory markers by clinicians often leads to unnecessary empirical antibiotic treatment, which can increase sepsis-related morbidity and prolong hospital stays due to missed diagnoses of culture-confirmed UTIs. Given the demanding nature of clinical practice, traditional nomograms are often impractical for widespread application. Therefore, a dynamic online column-line diagram has been developed based on traditional predictive models. This technology is accessible via computers, smartphones, and other mobile devices, facilitating more effective predictive analysis of positive urine cultures in patients with urolithiasis and enabling individualized treatment plans.

For patients with urolithiasis undergoing surgical treatment, this model provides a practical tool for preoperative antibiotic administration, postoperative infection monitoring, and anti-infective therapy. Despite the rigor in study design and statistical analysis, several limitations exist. First, the retrospective nature of the study prevents the determination of whether patients were using unreported antibiotics. Second, while internal validation was performed, it is less robust than external validation. Nonetheless, our results indicate that the model is reliable. Importantly, the study endpoint focused on positive urine cultures, and atypical infections or difficult-to-culture microorganisms may have been overlooked. Previous research suggests that urine cultures from the renal pelvis have a higher positivity rate compared to midstream cultures [34]. Lastly, insufficient data on PCT and other potential superior predictors, such as Interleukin-6 (IL-6) and NGAL, were available. Future studies should incorporate PCT, IL-6, and NGAL [25,35], and evaluate M-CSF-1, Protein S100-A12, and MCP-4 to enhance the accuracy and differentiation of UTI prediction models.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the dynamic online nomogram offers an accurate and practical tool for predicting positive urine cultures in patients with urolithiasis. Clinicians can utilize this tool to identify high-risk individuals for culture-confirmed UTIs, optimize antibiotic therapy, and improve patient outcomes. Further validation in larger cohorts and the inclusion of additional biomarkers are necessary to enhance the model’s generalizability.
