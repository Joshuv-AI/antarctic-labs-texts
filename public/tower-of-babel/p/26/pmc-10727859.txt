INTRODUCTION

Kaposi sarcoma (KS) is a vascular and lymphatic neoplasm caused by human herpesvirus 8 (HHV-8; also known as the KS-associated herpesvirus [KSHV]). AIDS-related (also known as epidemic) KS, which is one of the four epidemiologic forms of KS, has variable clinical courses, ranging from mild disease presenting as an incidental finding to severe disease presenting as an aggressively progressing neoplasm that can lead to poor prognosis or even death. Typical clinical manifestation of KS is known as multiple cutaneous lesions on the extremities, trunk, and face with mucosal involvement1. Early KS lesions evolve from macules (patch stage) into plaques (plaque stage) that can eventually grow into larger nodules (nodular stage)2.

CASE REPORT

A 46-year-old male patient was referred to the department of infectious diseases for an erythematous patch present only on the right forearm which appeared 5 months ago. A diagnosis of AIDS was made a year ago. For 1 year, the patient was treated with antiretroviral drugs (bictegravir, tenofovir, and emtricitabine) for AIDS. Physical examination revealed a 2.5-cm solitary erythematous patch with a hyperpigmented area on the right forearm (Fig. 1). Laboratory data revealed human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)-1 RNA of less than 40 copies/ml and CD4 cell count of 264 cells/mm3 (normal range 500~1,200 cells/mm3). Sections from the skin lesions showed deposition of melanin within the keratinocytes in basal layers of the epidermis. There were numerous slit-like spaces and vascular proliferation with primitive blood vessels dissecting between the collagen bundles and the dermis (Fig. 2). Lymphohistiocytic inflammation was observed within the superficial dermis. In some areas, extravasated erythrocytes were also observed. Immunohistochemical staining showed HHV-8 nuclear staining of spindle cells while focal staining identified endothelial cells lining the abnormal vascular spaces (Fig. 3). CD31 and CD34 markers also outlined the endothelial cells of the abnormal vascular spaces. Although the clinical presentation was only a small size of solitary patch, which is unusual in AIDS-related KS, histological features and positive HHV-8 immunohistochemical stain were consistent with the diagnosis of AIDS-related KS. In computed tomography (CT) of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis conducted for systemic evaluation, there were no remarkable findings. The cutaneous lesion regressed with the sustained antiretroviral therapy (ART).

DISCUSSION

As in our case, the incidence of KS is highest as 6.5 events per 1,000 person-years during the first year after the initiation of ART3,4. It is referable to severe immunosuppression and immune reconstitution by ART, which reveal previously subclinical lesions. Moreover, in patients with AIDS-related KS, CD4 cell count appears to be the most important factor related to the incidence of KS3. Therefore, HIV-positive patients who recently started ART, particularly with low CD4 cell counts, should be followed up with close attention for the development of KS.

The clinical manifestations of KS, regardless of the epidemiologic forms, usually present as multiple, pigmented, flat or elevated, painless lesions without blanch1. In particular, AIDS-related KS is typically manifests with numerous disseminated distribution and mucosal involvement at the onset of lesions5. In our case, however, the lesion was solitary only on the forearm, which made it difficult to clinically suspect AIDS-related KS.

Indeed, early patch stage of KS can easily be misdiagnosed as other vascular-appearing mucocutaneous lesions, including capillary hemangioma, stasis dermatitis, acroangiodermatitis, kaposiform hemangioendothelioma, angiosarcoma, and unusual vascular/lymphatic proliferations. On close inspection, however, it can be distinguished from these diseases by its distinctive histologic features and immunohistochemical staining for HHV-86,7.

All the epidemiologic forms of KS have common histologic features: angiogenesis, proliferation, and inflammation. These features include spindle cell proliferations, vascular proliferations with slit-like vascular spaces, and extravasated erythrocytes1. Early patch stage KS, in particular, needs closer inspection because its clues can be subtle to notice. Its lesions subtly infiltrate the upper portion of the dermis through a slight increase in the number of bizarre-shaped, dilated, vascular spaces lined by spindled endothelial cells, as is demonstrated in our case. A sparse perivascular mononuclear cell infiltrate, consisting of lymphocytes and plasma cells, may also be present in the dermis5,8.

Immunohistochemical stains can play a major role in diagnosing KS. CD31, CD34, podoplanin (D2-40), and erythroblast transformation-specific-related gene (ERG) are known as current markers, though none of these markers are ideally sensitive or specific for KS9. HHV-8 immunostaining, however, is identified to have ideal sensitivity and specificity for KS, which makes it a particularly useful method to differentiate KS from other mimickers10,11,12. Therefore, as in our case, it should be performed to recognize early stage of KS or KS with unusual clinical or histological features.

In conclusion, early stage AIDS-related KS can manifest into various forms with subtle clues, which makes it prone to misdiagnosis or neglect without clinical suspicion. Therefore, in cases of HIV-positive patients with clinically or histologically vascular-appearing mucocutaneous lesions, KS should be considered as a possible differential diagnosis.
