Automated Assessment of Nondosimetric Aspects of Prostate Radiation Therapy Treatment Quality in a Large Plan Database
Authors: John A Charters, Melissa Ghafarian, Yasin Abdulkadir, Dishane C Luximon, John Neylon, Drew Moghanaki, Amar Kishan, Michael Steinberg, James M Lamb
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeComprehensive assessment of radiation therapy treatment quality in large-scale multi-institutional contexts remains an outstanding challenge. Human review of retrospective treatment plans is labor intensive and impractical for large data sets. Dosimetric plan quality assessment can be approached with knowledge-based planning, yet a standardized, automated approach is lacking for other aspects of treatment quality, such as target contours, margins, and the use of image guidance with fiducial markers. In this work, we develop novel automated methods for assessing nondosimetric plan quality indicators over large databases of prostate radiation therapy treatments. The purpose of the algorithms is to flag potentially low-quality plans for detailed human review. This study serves as a proof of concept toward scalable quality assessment across multi-institutional databases.Methods and MaterialsA DICOM database of 1395 patients and 1671 prostate radiation therapy plans was analyzed. Automated clinical target volume (CTV) and planning target volume (PTV) identification was based on structure set naming conventions. PTV margins were automatically derived by computing mean Euclidean surface distances between the CTV and PTV boundaries. Fiducial marker presence was determined by intensity thresholding and voxel clustering. Finally, target contour outliers were identified by a V-Net autocontouring model. To validate the accuracy of our models, ground-truth quality measurements were recomputed by hand.ResultsThe models were trained on 1328 patients from our institution and tested on 67 patients from the greater community. PTV identification accuracy was 64/67 (95.5%). Plan type accuracy was 62/67 (92.5%). The models obtained a margin computation agreement of 0.24 ± 0.13 mm and a prescription computation agreement of 5 ± 22 cGy with values manually derived from clinical software. Fiducial marker detection was 67/67 (100%). Autocontouring agreement was 82.4% ± 5.4% on intact prostates and 63.1% ± 11.0% on prostate beds. Statistically significant aggregate differences in PTV margins, fiducial marker usage, and prostate target contours were observed between internal and external plans. These models identified 3/67 (4.5%) plans that were manually reviewed by radiation oncologists and confirmed to be suboptimal.ConclusionsAutomated algorithms for quantitative measurements of plan quality were developed, and high accuracy was achieved on external data sets. Our methodology is pertinent to analyzing large radiation therapy databases for quality control. The relatively small external testing cohort limits generalizability, highlighting the need for future multi-institutional validation.

Introduction

One of the greatest challenges in the broad clinical implementation of intensity modulated radiation therapy (RT) has been implementing objective, quantitative measures of treatment quality. As the number of RT patients has increased and as treatment planning complexity has risen, the need for quality control in RT has become increasingly important. The outcome of RT is known to be correlated with physician experience and institutional volume,1,2 and there exists a range of standards of care in clinical practice. Given the same patient, treatment goals, and delivery technique, one may obtain different optimized treatment plans. Moreover, standards of care have been less widely analyzed in community-based medical centers than in academic centers.3,4 Recent efforts to create large treatment plan databases5 open the possibility of a comprehensive assessment of the quality of community RT treatment, critically informing efforts to improve RT care nationwide.

Existing work on large-scale plan quality assessment has focused on dosimetric plan quality (defined as the degree of optimality of the dose distribution, relative to an absolute standard or Pareto front) using knowledge-based planning (KBP).6, 7, 8, 9 KBP has been demonstrated to identify plans with suboptimal dosimetric quality within multi-institutional treatment planning databases and has been proposed as a tool to regularize plan quality in prospective clinical trials.10, 11, 12 However, other important aspects of plan quality, upstream of those covered by KBP, remain significantly unaddressed challenges in the analysis of plan quality in large-scale data sets.

Human review of retrospective treatment plans is labor intensive and nearly impossible for large data sets on the order of thousands of patients. Therefore, the objective of this study was to demonstrate a proof-of-concept automated treatment quality evaluation methodology that can be used to assess community-based treatments against the standards of our academic institution. Our methodology is based on automated analysis of specific quality indicators that identify potentially low-quality plans for further human review.

This study focuses on intact prostate and prostate-bed RT plans, with or without the inclusion of seminal vesicle and lymph node (LN) targets. In this work, we introduce novel algorithms for quality control that can flag potential low-quality plans based on the following quality indicators: number of days between computed tomography (CT) simulation and treatment plan delivery, clinical target volume (CTV) to planning target volume (PTV) margins, the appropriate use of fiducial markers in intact prostate treatments, planned prescriptions relative to widely accepted guidelines, and agreement with CTV autocontouring as used to flag potentially erroneous target contours. The quality indicators selected for this study were chosen based on their feasibility for fully automated extraction from heterogeneous Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) data sets and their relevance to upstream, nondosimetric components of treatment planning. We prioritized metrics that could be derived directly from target structures, plan metadata, and image intensities without relying on vendor-specific protocols or institution-dependent planning workflows.

Query and retrieval of prostate and prostate-bed plans from clinical databases

To create a database of DICOM files for prostate RT patients, a software application was developed to query, retrieve, organize, and store treatment planning data from clinical databases at our institution. DICOM planning files were retrieved using the pydicom and pynetdicom Python packages. The query was fixed between the years 2012 and 2021. External-beam prostate and prostate-bed patients were identified in our database by matching plan labels containing the strings “prst,” “prost,” and “pbed.”

To ensure plans appearing in our clinical database were distinct, a data-driven approach was used to remove duplicate plans. Such duplicates could occur if, for instance, a patient was anonymized for testing purposes or if an imported patient from an external clinic was relabeled with an internal medical record number.

Identification of plans from external clinics in the database

Plans originating from external radiation oncology departments were used to test our quality review algorithms, and they were removed during algorithm development. To determine which treatment plans were internal, that is, originated from our institution, 2 conditions were checked. First, the patient ID was required to be exactly 7 digits with no alphabetical characters. Second, the planning CT institution name, address, and CT station name DICOM tags were compared against those of our clinics using string comparison functions. If any of the tags did not agree, the treatment plan was labeled as external. External plans represented a heterogeneous collection of prostate RT cases transferred to our institution for continuity of care or replanning purposes. These cases originated from multiple community radiation oncology departments within our referral region.

Identification of target volume structures

Our proposed quality metrics incorporate the prostate CTV and PTV contours as inputs. There was limited consistency in target volume naming conventions, and plans frequently contained multiple target volumes, for example, LN target volumes, as well as target optimization volumes (eg, target cropped out of rectum). The following algorithms were developed to uniquely identify the prostate/prostate-bed CTV and PTV with high confidence without human intervention. A subset of N = 60 randomly selected internal plans was used to determine the accuracy of the algorithms. This internal subset was used only to validate the accuracy of the rule-based quality indicator algorithms.

Identification of PTVs

A subset of candidate PTV contours was acquired by systematically including regions of interest (ROIs) if their names contained substrings “PTV” or “P_” and excluding if their names contained characters indicating organ at risk, rings, and optimization structures. A full list of substring filters is given in Table E1. The resulting list of candidate PTVs was then sorted into 2 groups: 1 containing the prostate or bed exclusively, and 1 containing LNs, if any. If more than 1 candidate PTV remained after the above filters were applied, then the structure with the largest volume was selected as the unique prostate/prostate-bed PTV. This naming-based approach was chosen for its simplicity and reliability within our institutional data set. Refer to Fig. 1 for an example where 2 distinct CTV structures were correctly identified.

Deep learning model to classify prostates versus prostate beds

Plan type classification is an important step in automated quality analysis. For internal cases, plan naming conventions were reliably used to categorize plans as intact prostate or bed. A deep convolutional neural network (CNN) was designed to classify whether a given plan in the data set corresponded to either an intact prostate or a prostate-bed case. The data analyzed for model development were composed of a random sample of internal plans only (290 training, 37 validation, and 37 testing), with approximately equal amounts of intact and bed cases. Ground-truth labels were assigned based on our internal plan naming conventions, where “bed” or “bd” substrings indicated prostate-bed plans.

To evaluate the accuracy of our quality indicator algorithms, a random sample of 60 internal plans, 30 intact and 30 bed, was created. All available internal and testing plans were analyzed to assess and compare treatment planning quality between our institution and clinics in the greater community. The correct plan categories were manually verified for all external testing cases.

Computation of quality metrics

Elapsed days from simulation to treatment

Studies have shown that excessive delays in starting radiation treatment after receiving a diagnosis and CT simulation are correlated with reduced tumor control.13, 14, 15 Date of plan approval was used as a surrogate for treatment initiation. The days between CT simulation and treatment plan approval required the content date DICOM tag in the CT object and the review date DICOM tag in the RT Plan object. These tags were converted to chronological date-time variables, and the difference in dates yielded the desired quality indicator.

CTV to PTV margins

A wide range of recommended PTV margins has been reported in clinical research, and correlations between RT delivery techniques and average PTV margins have been shown to exist.16, 17, 18 However, to our knowledge, no study has attempted to quantify the range of margins that exists in clinical practice. Our present work describes methods to elucidate the range of margins practiced, in comparison between treatment plans from our academic institution and from neighboring community hospitals.

Having identified the PTV and the corresponding CTV or union of 2 CTVs, the PTV margin used for planning was estimated by computing the mean surface distance. Mean surface distance was calculated using Euclidean distance transforms. This surface distance method inherently accounts for nonisotropic expansions, producing margin statistics that reflect the true directional variability of clinical PTV construction rather than any isotropic assumption. Additionally, the SD surface distance was computed as an indicator of nonisotropic margin use.

Fiducial marker identification

The use of fiducial markers has been shown to effectively localize the prostate gland, improve CTV to PTV margins, and improve dose conformality.19, 20, 21, 22 A fiducial marker counting algorithm was developed and trained on the prostate plans from our institution. Voxels corresponding to fiducial markers were identified by thresholding. The search was limited to the CTV identified in step 2.2, and a threshold of 2000 HU was used. If the structure set included fiducial marker contours, determined based on substrings in Table E1, the search space was limited to the union of these contours.

To cluster the high-intensity voxels and count the number of fiducial markers, Euclidean distances between all voxel coordinates were computed. If any pair of voxels was <5 mm, the pair was regarded as belonging to the same marker, and the coordinate with lower intensity was removed. The process was repeated until all such points were at least 5 mm apart. Thus, the number of points remaining was the number of fiducial markers detected.

Assessment of planned prescription

A blue-ribbon panel of prostate cancer experts, organized by the US Department of Veterans Affairs National Radiation Oncology Program, developed updated measures and metrics for prostate RT.23 The recommended dose volume histogram (DVH) quality indicators were used in the present study. Our work fills an important knowledge gap regarding the extent to which RT prescriptions and fractionation schemes implemented in clinical practice follow such guidelines.

The prescription planned for the prostate/prostate-bed PTV was recorded by taking the dose distribution within the PTV, reproducing the DVH curve, and linearly interpolating to extract the D95 point. The number of fractions was found in the RT Plan module. If a patient was treated with initial and boost plans, prescriptions from all plans were analyzed. For each plan, the PTVs were identified, and the D95 prescription doses were computed. Subsets of up to 3 separate doses and fractionations, if available, were accumulated and compared with standard recommended prescriptions from our institution and from recent American Society for Radiation Oncology consensus guidelines.23 Plans were flagged if no match was found for any subset of treatment plans up to a tolerance of 5% compared with consensus guidelines.

CTV autocontouring

Autocontouring models for both intact prostates and prostate beds can demonstrate the range of target contours in clinical practice. Model-predicted CTVs may be used as an automated planning evaluation tool for prostate treatment plans. Intact prostate CTVs (ie, the anatomic prostate) were autocontoured using MIM ProtégéAI software (MIM Software). Prostate-bed delineation for postprostatectomy patients is more subject to interphysician variability.24, 25, 26, 27 Thus, a custom V-Net model was trained on presumed high-quality CTVs from our institution. Training was developed using TensorFlow with the Keras interface and implemented on an NVIDIA RTX A5000 graphics processing unit (Nvidia Corporation). Stacks of 32 slices with an in-plane size of 128 × 128 were used. The ground truth was the CTV binary mask. Training parameters included an Adam optimizer, a batch size of 16, a step of 20 iterations per epoch, and a maximum number of 200 epochs. The loss function was a combination of a Dice loss with a boundary loss regularizer. Volumetric predictions were achieved by running the model on overlapping 32-slice stacks with a stride of 1, followed by a “majority vote” algorithm. After processing included removing small, disconnected regions from the prediction and a morphologic closing operation to smooth the prediction.

Results

Treatment plan data selection

Table 1 shows the number of patients and plans at each stage of selection.

Identification of target volume structures

PTV identification

The accuracy of PTV identification on the internal subset was 100%. For the external cohort, PTV identification accuracy was 64/67 (96%). Among the 3 failures, 2 were because of the presence of an ROI that expands the PTV by 1 mm, which was incorrectly categorized as the actual PTV rather than an optimization structure. The remaining failure was a prostate-bed case where the patient was apparently resimulated for a boost, yet the initial prostate-bed PTV contour was also present in the structure set registered to the boost CT scan.

CTV identification

The accuracy of CTV identification on the internal subset was 100%. On the external plans, CTV identification accuracy was 65/67 (97%). The first failure was because of the presence of an ROI that expanded the CTV by 3 mm. The second failure occurred in a case where the correct CTV was composed of 3 distinct ROIs, whereas our code only accommodated at most 2 ROIs for computational efficiency.

Plan type classification

The CNN plan type classifier obtained 100% testing accuracy on the validation set. When applied to the external cases and compared with manually determined ground-truth category labels, the CNN accuracy was 62/67 (93%). All failures were cases of intact prostates that were incorrectly determined to be prostate beds. Three failures were believed to be related to significant bladder contrast (not present in the training data), and 2 failures occurred for unapparent reasons.

CTV to PTV margins

On the internal cohort, the average difference between margins computed by our proposed automated methods and those derived from clinical software was 0.24 ± 0.13 mm. Figure 3 illustrates normalized histograms of the range of margins for the internal cohort practiced at our institution and for the external cohort in the greater community. All histograms were normalized such that the total area under each curve equals 1, allowing direct comparison between internal and external plan distributions despite differing sample sizes. To evaluate the statistical significance of the differences in mean margins, 2-tailed independent t tests were performed. A Welch’s 2-sample t-test (ie, unequal-variance t test) was used. Equal variance between the internal and external distributions was not assumed because the internal and external groups differed substantially in sample size, and this test is robust to variance mismatch. Using a significance level of 95%, the t tests revealed that P = 8.6 × 10−5 for intact prostate margins and P = 1.1 × 10−2 for prostate-bed margins. Therefore, the differences in mean margins were statistically significant.

Discussion

Our goal in this work was to develop a set of standardized, quantitative, and automatically assessable measures of RT plan quality, and to apply these measures to databases of RT plans internal and external to our institution. Furthermore, we report on the observed aggregate differences between the internal and external data samples. Our results demonstrate the overall high accuracy of our automated methods to compute the quality measures. In our envisioned framework, plans flagged by the automated methods would be manually reviewed. Thus, the automated methods should have high sensitivity to detect “low-quality” plans but also a sufficiently low false positive rate that the number of manually reviewed plans would not be unduly burdensome.

While the present study incorporated a multi-institutional testing data set, the database remains primarily composed of plans from our academic institution. As such, certain parameters, particularly PTV margin conventions, may reflect institutional practice patterns and could limit generalizability. Nevertheless, our inclusion of 67 external community plans demonstrated that the algorithms maintained high accuracy even when applied to data sets with distinct planning conventions. Future iterations of this framework should expand to include additional academic and national repositories to enable cross-institutional harmonization of quality indicators and facilitate model retraining across diverse practice environments.

In our analysis, some outliers were observed, which illustrated the potential value of our large-scale quality analysis approach. To formalize the definition of suboptimal quality, we applied metric-specific thresholds grounded in clinical practice. Plans were flagged when PTV margins were inconsistent with the use or absence of fiducial markers, deviating by more than 1 SD from our institutional distribution. For autocontouring, where moderate variability was expected, a 95% specificity was applied, and the lowest 5% of plans were flagged. For instance, an external intact prostate case was observed with unusually large margins of 8.0 mm despite fiducial markers implanted (Fig. E1a), representing excessive normal tissue irradiation and increased risk of toxicity.16,28 Conversely, another external case was observed with unusually small margins of 3.8 mm despite no fiducial markers (Fig. E1b), raising concern for marginal miss20 and reduced tumor control probability. A third case exhibited the lowest scoring CTV autocontour agreement because of a nonstandard “mini-pelvis” target29 that included the pelvic LN chain (Fig. E2). The relatively modest average Dice score of 63% for prostate-bed targets reflects the inherent uncertainty in delineating the prostate bed following prostatectomy, as described in previous contouring consensus efforts.25,26 Our model was not intended to replace clinical contouring but to serve as an automated screening tool to highlight contouring outliers. In multi-institutional applications, such an approach can help standardize review processes by flagging plans with atypical contour geometries for further human evaluation, even when absolute Dice agreement values are modest. These cases were deemed suboptimal after independent radiation oncologist review. Therefore, 3/67 (4.5%) of external plans indicate deviations from what we believe to be reasonable practice. These cases provide support to our hypothesis that, if applied to large-scale multiclinic databases, our methods could identify variations from the standard of care that could be rectified for future patients.

The present framework focused on nondosimetric, target-related metrics such as margin derivation, fiducial marker usage, and prescription consistency, as these can be standardized and automatically extracted from DICOM metadata without dependence on vendor-specific planning details. Other indicators of quality, such as the accuracy of organ-at-risk contours, beam arrangements, and use of adaptive replanning, are undoubtedly important but remain challenging to automate across multi-institutional data sets because of the wide variability in clinical workflows and treatment planning systems. Future work will seek to extend the framework to include such factors once robust, standardized data representations become available. More broadly, the set of quality indicators used in this study reflects a deliberate focus on parameters that can be extracted consistently and reproducibly across large-scale databases, while deferring characteristics that currently lack standardized representations or automated evaluation methods.

Finally, this analysis identified 1 internal intact prostate case with a previously unnoticed treatment planning error via observation of a dose-prescription outlier. This case did not belong to any of the physician authors on this manuscript, and it was intended to be a stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) plan with 40 Gy delivered in 5 fractions. However, the plan was improperly normalized with a modified treatment percentage of 95% in Eclipse, resulting in a PTV overdose by 2 Gy. This case was reported to our institution’s incident learning system.

The vast majority (approximately 80%) of internal intact prostate prescriptions were hypofractionated, indicating SBRT procedures delivering 40 Gy in 5 fractions. Meanwhile, the majority of external intact prostate prescriptions were conventionally fractionated. Internal cases that did not match any of the prescription categories (up to a 5% tolerance) were reviewed. Reasons for such outliers include urothelial cancers, recurrent cancers, wrong PTV selection (eg, prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) biomarker targets), and clinical trials with experimental doses, such as 24 Gy in 3 fractions for preoperative prostate RT.30 There is a broader range of standards of care among the internal cases than among the external cases, especially for the dose per fraction metric. A few cases with total doses between 40 Gy and 60 Gy were because of factors such as PTV undercoverage and unusual prescriptions (eg, to dominant intraprostatic nodules or gross nodes).

Limitations of our investigation include a few items. First, we often do not have access to physician decision-making or additional clinical variables, especially for cases that are several years old. Hence, we cannot necessarily exclude the fact that deviations from standard practice were justified. Second, our study did not address LN targets, which are often included in prostate plans. Third, based on the way we have designed the quality measures, failure to compute a measure would typically result in “flagging” the plan for human review. Thus, we believe that false negatives would be rarer than false positives. Future improvements of our study could include extending to other anatomic treatment sites, such as LN chains, with physician input. We also foresee extending our methodology to other anatomic sites such as the head and neck.

Another limitation is the relatively modest size of the external testing data set. This reflects the number of community-transferred prostate RT plans available in our institutional archive during the study period. Although small, this cohort was sufficient to evaluate the robustness of our automated indicators when applied to plans generated under different clinical workflows and contouring conventions. Nevertheless, larger multi-institutional data sets will be important for validating and refining these methods. Furthermore, the external plans available to us were imported individually over the study period and lacked consistent metadata describing the originating departments. As a result, we were unable to characterize differences in technology platforms or planning policies between centers. Although this heterogeneity limits our ability to attribute specific practice differences to specific institutions, it also provides a realistic test of algorithm robustness across diverse community workflows.

Our PTV identification algorithm relies on institution-specific naming conventions and simple string-matching heuristics, which may limit generalizability to centers with different contour-labeling practices. Incorporating artificial intelligence (AI)-driven structure classification or standardized nomenclature systems would strengthen cross-institutional applicability in future iterations. Our strategy of selecting the largest remaining PTV also represents a simplification that may not generalize to plans containing multiple boost structures or combined prostate–pelvic nodal targets, and future methods will need more nuanced decision rules to address such cases. Finally, our CTV identification algorithm was chosen to allow unions of no more than 2 ROIs. This design choice was made to maintain computational ease, as the search space and computation time increase combinatorially when evaluating all possible unions of 3 or more ROIs. While this constraint was sufficient for the vast majority of cases in our data set, it may limit performance on more complex plans that define the target volume across 3 or more ROIs. Future work will explore more efficient strategies for handling multistructure unions without incurring significant computational overhead.

Conclusion

Automated algorithms were developed to evaluate treatment planning quality for prostate RT patients. The algorithms were systematically applied to treatment plans from our academic institution and from hospitals in the greater community, which allowed us to compare and assess the existing range of standards of care in clinical practice. Precise cutoff metrics enabled treatment plan outliers to be flagged for retrospective human review. Our study has applications to data-driven standards of care and guidelines for quality improvement in community practice. Future work will focus on extending this framework to larger, multi-institutional data sets to further validate generalizability and harmonize automated plan quality assessment across academic and community settings. As clinical paradigms shift toward greater use of SBRT, adaptive planning, and biologically individualized fractionation, our methodology can be readily adapted to incorporate these evolving standards, enabling continuous and data-driven updates to RT quality benchmarks.
