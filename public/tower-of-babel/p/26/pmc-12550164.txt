Type 2 diabetes remission after initiation of GLP-1 receptor agonists: frequency, characteristics, and outcomes using multiple definitions in an observational study
Authors: Gian Paolo Fadini, Andrea Giaccari, Fabio Broglio, Laura Nollino, Bruno Fattor, Roberto Anichini, Giancarla Meregalli, Angelo Avogaro, Agostino Consoli, Gianluca Aimaretti, Gianluca Aimaretti, Roberto Anichini, Angelo Avogaro, Maria Pompea Antonia Baldassarre, Cristina Barale, Rosella Cau, Agostino Consoli, Gian Paolo Fadini, Bruno Fattor, Stefano Fazion, Gloria Formoso, Mariangela Ghiani, Frida Leonetti, Gaetano Leto, Elena Melchionda, Giancarla Meregalli, Andrea Muscarà, Laura Nollino, Agostino Paccagnella, Giuseppina Russo, Marco Strazzabosco, Marco Zavattaro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundRemission of type 2 diabetes (T2D) is becoming feasible with modern treatments, including GLP-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RA). Here, we explored frequency, characteristics, and outcomes associated with various definitions of remission after initiation of GLP-1RA.MethodsThis was an observational study on new-users of GLP-1RA. We explored 4 definitions of remission: (1) HbA1c to <6.5% persisting ≥3 months in the absence of diabetes pharmacotherapy; (2) As in 1, but allowing GLP-1RA therapy; (3) As in 1, but without new diabetes pharmacotherapy; (4) As in 1, but irrespectively of ongoing diabetes pharmacotherapy.FindingsWe included 14,141 participants initiating GLP-1RA (60% men, 60-year-old, with a diabetes duration of ∼10 years, BMI 32 kg/m2, HbA1c 8.1%). The mean observation was 4 years. Remission frequencies by definition were: (1) 5.8%; (2) 6.2%; (3) 12.2%; (4) 18.3%. Duration was shorter for remission 1–2 (5–6 months) than for remission 3–4 (9–10 months). Factors associated with remission were similar across definitions (shorter diabetes duration, higher BMI, lower burden of complications and medications). Remission was associated with significant and sustained benefits on HbA1c (–1%) and body weight (–2 kg). Microvascular events were reduced by 12–16% in participants with remission 1, 2 or 3. Cardiovascular events were reduced only in remission 3 (HR 0.65; 95% C.I. 0.48–0.88).InterpretationT2D remission is not rare after initiation GLP-1RA, its frequency and duration varying by definition. When achieved, remission is associated with durable metabolic improvements up to 4 years and fewer incident complications.FundingSupported by the Italian Diabetes Society (Società Italiana di Diabetologia).

Research in context

Evidence before this study

This study aimed at evaluating the features, frequency and outcomes associated with remission of type 2 diabetes (T2D) under the free-living conditions of routine care. We reviewed the existing evidence on T2D by searching the English literature in PubMed, between 2000 and 2025, using combinations of the following terms: “type 2 diabetes” AND (“remission” OR “reversal”) AND (“GLP-1 receptor agonists” OR “incretins” OR “liraglutide” OR “semaglutide” OR “dulaglutide” OR “exenatide”) AND (“weight loss”) AND (“outcomes”). We also screened reference lists of relevant reviews and major studies. We included trials and observational studies evaluating T2D remission associated with pharmacological or non-pharmacological interventions. Key findings were: T2D remission is achievable through bariatric surgery, intensive lifestyle changes, or pharmacologic agents, with remission rates highly dependent on the definition. Rates of complete remission was often <10%, particularly in patients achieving <10% weight loss. Most studies focused on highly selected trial populations, often with limited generalizability. The definition of remission was heterogeneous in terms of HbA1c cutoff, minimum duration, and treatment discontinuation requirements. Prior to this study, we found no large-scale real-world cohort that systematically compared the frequency, characteristics, and outcomes of different remission definitions after GLP-1RA initiation.

Added value of this study

This study provides a large, real-world analysis comparing four distinct definitions of T2D remission in over 14,000 individuals initiating GLP-1RA therapy. Herein, we quantify the frequency and duration of remission according to various definitions and identify consistent clinical predictors of remission. These included younger age, shorter diabetes duration, higher BMI, and fewer complication and medication burden. Remission, regardless of definition, was associated with durable improvements in HbA1c, body weight, blood pressure, and triglycerides over four years. Remission definitions influenced the magnitude of observed benefit: stricter definitions correlated with lower remission rates but greater metabolic normalization and reduced incidence of microvascular complications. Notably, one definition of remission (HbA1c <6.5% for at least 3 months without treatment intensification from baseline) was consistently associated with a reduced risk of both microvascular and cardiovascular events.

Implications of all the available evidence

Combined with existing literature, this study underscores that T2D remission is a feasible and clinically meaningful target in people with T2D initiating GLP-1RA treatment. Our data underscore how early pharmacologic intervention can yield sustained metabolic benefits and reduce complication risks, even when remission was transient or partial. We highlight that the urgent need for harmonized remission criteria that balance clinical practicality with prognostic value. The remission definition identified as the best compromise supports continued use of cardioprotective agents despite glycemic normalization. Future research should aim to validate optimal remission definitions prospectively, explore cost-effectiveness, and investigate long-term impacts on quality of life and mortality.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes (T2D) is a metabolic disorder that, without effective intervention, can lead to a high burden due to micro- and macrovascular complications. Its prevalence has reached pandemic proportions and is projected to rise in the coming decades, contributing to substantial healthcare and societal costs.1 The progressive course of T2D typically necessitates long-term pharmacological intervention to maintain glycemic control, often with increasing therapeutic intensity over time.2 With this perspective, strategies to counter or revert progression of T2D are eagerly needed.

The concept of remission in T2D has emerged as a realistic therapeutic goal, particularly with interventions that result in significant weight loss.3 Bariatric surgery has historically provided the most robust evidence for T2D remission.4 Intensive lifestyle intervention in the form of low-calorie diets have also demonstrated potential to induce T2D remission, particularly in seminal works like Look AHEAD and DiRECT5,6

Pharmacological approaches are now increasingly recognized as viable alternatives.7 Among these, glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RA) have demonstrated notable efficacy in lowering glycemia, inducing weight loss, and reducing cardiovascular and renal risk, offering a multifaceted approach to diabetes care.8 The possibility of achieving T2D remission using GLP-1RA has gained attention, especially following the development of more potent agents and dual incretin receptor agonists.9 They can tackle key aspects of T2D pathophysiology, including β-cell dysfunction, insulin resistance and inflammation.

The currently accepted definition of T2D remission, according to international consensus across multiple scientific societies, requires an HbA1c value below 6.5% (48 mmol/mol) measured at least 3 months after cessation of glucose-lowering pharmacotherapy.10 This definition integrates frameworks related to the glycemic threshold, duration, and the continuation of glucose-lowering therapy. The main factors to be considered in clinical practice are: (i) the temporal criterion for defining remission, i.e., how long HbA1c must remain below the threshold; (ii) whether pharmacotherapy must necessarily be stopped. The latter point is particularly relevant in light of recent evidence that tirzepatide discontinuation was followed by a resumption of T2D progression even after years of effective weight management.11,12 An eventual refining of the definition, based on new data, can have critical implications for estimating its frequency, durability, and the prognostic meaning of achieving such a state.

Despite the growing clinical use of GLP-1RA, there remains limited real-world evidence evaluating the frequency and clinical correlates of T2D remission according to different definitions, particularly outside the setting of clinical trials. Moreover, it is unclear whether the long-term benefits associated with remission, such as improvements in intermediate metabolic endpoints and reductions in diabetic complications, vary or are consistent across these definitions.

The present study aims to address these knowledge gaps. We evaluated the frequency, clinical characteristics, and outcomes associated with four distinct definitions of remission in a large, multicenter observational cohort of individuals with T2D initiating GLP-1RA therapy. Our objectives were: (i) to analyze the frequency and duration of remission according to each definition; (ii) to identify baseline characteristics associated with remission; and (iii) to evaluate the impact of remission on intermediate metabolic outcomes and the incidence of diabetic complications during follow-up.

Methods

Study design

GLIMPLES (GLp-1ra for sIMPLification in DiabetES) was a multicenter observational study promoted by the Italian Diabetes Society. It collected baseline data of individuals with T2D who initiated GLP-1RA from January 2010 to January 2022 and were followed up until August 2023 (last data extraction) or last observation. Data were extracted automatically from the same electronic clinical chart system (MetaClinic, Me.Te.Da, San Benedetto del Tronto, Italy) at all participating centers. The original data enter this chart system either automatically (laboratory data and instruments, such as scales and manometers) or through manual input by healthcare personnel (doctors or nurses). This data source has been used for decades to evaluate the quality of diabetes care in Italy.13

Objectives

The primary objective of this analysis was to evaluate the frequency of remission occurrence, based on different definitions, in people with T2D after initiation of a GLP-1RA. Secondary objectives were: (i) to evaluate the clinical features associated with remission; (ii) to compare the outcomes of patients with and without remission with regard to intermediate endpoints and development of complications.

Exposure

The index date was set at the time patients received the first prescription for a GLP-1RA. Remission was evaluated during the post-index observation as an outcome. Four different definitions of remission were used (Supplementary Table S1) extrapolated from the 2021 International Consensus report.10 Remission 1 was the most restrictive and was defined as follows: return of HbA1c to <6.5% that persists for at least 3 months in the absence of usual glucose-lowering pharmacotherapy. Remission 2 was defined as follows: return of HbA1c to <6.5% that persists for at least 3 months in the absence of usual glucose-lowering pharmacotherapy (as in 1) except GLP-1RA (that were allowed). Remission 3 was defined as follows: return of HbA1c to <6.5% that persists for at least 3 months (as in 1) without the addition of new glucose-lowering pharmacotherapy compared to the baseline. Remission 4 was defined as follows: return of HbA1c to <6.5% that persists for at least 3 months (as in 1) irrespectively of the ongoing glucose-lowering medication regimen. The HbA1c threshold and minimum duration remained those suggested by the Consensus report.10 We divided participants into two groups based on occurrence or not of each type of remission and then compared the two groups for the outcomes of interest. We did not examine whether patients experienced multiple remission episodes and only the first episode of remission was considered.

In separate analyses, remission was an exposure, e.g., when evaluating the trend over time in intermediate endpoints and the rates of complications in participants with versus those without remission.

Variables

The following variables were recorded for all patients at baseline as reported in the electronic chart: age, self-reported sex, duration of diabetes, weight and height, body mass index (BMI), systolic and diastolic blood pressure, laboratory data (HbA1c, fasting glucose, lipids, estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR],14 urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio [UACR]), presence or absence of diabetic complications according to ICD-9 codes, prescribed therapy for the management of diabetes and cardiovascular risk factors or conditions. Ethnicity was not reported and no information was available on pharmacy refill rates and adherence. By definition, all patients were alive up to the last observation. For those with a last observation occurring before follow-up closure, we had no information on the reasons (death or lost to follow-up).

Follow-up

After baseline, all available visits in the chart were recorded and fitted into 3 months intervals. If more than one visit was available within a 3-month interval, the ones closest to the outcome of interest were considered. The following information was collected at follow-up: body weight, HbA1c, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, eGFR, UACR, complications.

Endpoints

In the comparison between the two groups, the following intermediate endpoints were considered: HbA1c, body weight, blood pressure, eGFR and UACR. We also evaluated the development of new diabetic complications in those who were free from each complication type at baseline. The following complications were considered: microangiopathy was a composite including a new development of any of CKD, retinopathy or maculopathy, and neuropathy, in people who were free from any microangiopathy at baseline. Macroangiopathy was a composite including a new development of coronary artery disease, cerebrovascular disease, peripheral arterial disease, or asymptomatic atherosclerosis in people who were free from any of these macroangiopathies at baseline. Asymptomatic atherosclerosis was defined as imaging evidence of atherosclerosis with stenosis >30% in any arterial site, in the absence of clinical signs of ischemia. Cardiovascular events were defined as acute myocardial infarction, stroke or transient ischemic attack, or any site revascularization. Data on live status or causes of death were not available.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean (standard deviation) for continuous variables and as number and percentages for categorical variables. Given the large sample size, for variables with a possible skewed distribution, asymptotic normality was assumed for the use of parametric tests. The comparison between two groups at baseline was performed using the Student's t test for continuous variables and the chi square test for categorical variables. SMD was calculated as a standardized metric of between-group difference.15 To evaluate the independent association between individual GLP-1RA type and remission, we used logistic regression models adjusted for covariates and year of cohort entry, to account for the different waves of GLP-1RA marketing. To compare the change over time in continuous variables elected as intermediate endpoints, we used the mixed model for repeated measures (MMRM): the dependent variable was the change versus baseline in the variable of interest; fixed factors were time in trimesters (treated as categorical), group (remission yes/no), and the time-by-group interaction; covariates included the baseline value of the dependent variable and all variables to be adjusted in the model, selected from univariate comparisons between groups. The first-order autoregressive structure of covariance was used.

To compare time-to-event outcomes between groups, we used the Cox proportional hazard regression model, adjusting for confounder variables selected from the univariate comparison between groups. The proportional hazards assumptions were verified using Schoenfeld residuals.16 Due to missing data for a few variables (0.1% for BMI and 24.8% for eGFR), we performed 10 imputations using MICE (multivariate imputation by chained equations) and results were pooled using Rubin's rule.17 No outcome data was imputed.

Assuming a conservative remission rate <10%18 and an 2% yearly rate of cardiovascular events,19 with about 14,000 patients observed for 4 years, the minimum detectable HR would be 0.75 (alpha 55 and power 80%), meaning that smaller effects would have substantially less power.

SPSS ver. 28 was used and statistical significance was accepted at p < 0.05 without adjustment for multiplicity of comparison.

The funding source had no role in study design, data analysis and interpretation, manuscript writing, and decision to publish.

Results

Characteristics of study participants

The study cohort was composed of 14,141 participants with T2D who initiated a GLP-1RA (Table 1). The newly-initiated GLP-1RAs were distributed as follows: 50.5% dulaglutide; 24.9% liraglutide; 12.1% semaglutide (90.2% of which was injectable); 11.0% exenatide; 1.4% lixisenatide. However, 24.7% of patients switched to another GLP-1RA during observation. Overall, the patients were 60% men, 60-year-old, with a diabetes duration of about 10 years, BMI 32 kg/m2, baseline HbA1c 8.1%, 40% with microangiopathy and almost 50% of macroangiopathy. The most common baseline glucose-lowering medications (in addition to the newly-initiated GLP-1RA) were metformin, basal insulin, and sulfonylureas. The proportions (Fig. 1A) of participants who fulfilled the definitions of remission after initiation of GLP-1RA were 5.8% (R1), 6.2% (R2), 12.2% (R3), and 18.3% (R4). Discussion

In this real-world cohort, we found that T2D remission occurred in a non-negligible proportion of individuals initiating a GLP-1RA, with rates varying substantially depending on the criteria used to define remission. The most stringent definition (remission without any glucose-lowering therapy) yielded a prevalence of 5.8%, whereas more permissive definitions allowing ongoing therapy resulted in higher rates, up to 18.3%. These differences were paralleled by varying remission durations, with longer median remission in less restrictive definitions. Importantly, individuals achieving remission had greater improvements in glycemia, weight, blood pressure, lipids, and possibly a lower incidence of chronic complications.

These findings confirm and extend prior evidence that pharmacologically induced remission of T2D is feasible in clinical practice, particularly in the context of substantial weight loss induced by GLP-1RA or dual incretins.9,20,21 Notably, remission rates observed in clinical trials tend to be higher, likely due to patient selection, protocol standardization, and greater adherence. Yet, a recent meta-analysis reports even lower incidence of complete remission (0.7%) and partial remission (5.4%) with weight loss <10%.3 In two recent Italian cohorts, the frequency of remission 1 in people with T2D who lost <10% body weight was 4.1% and 5.5%.18 Our real-world estimates highlight the relevance of remission as a clinical target outside the trial setting, particularly in patients with a favorable baseline profile. Consistent across all remission definitions, individuals more likely to remit were younger, had shorter diabetes duration, higher BMI, preserved renal function, and lower burden of complications. These characteristics align with prior studies showing that earlier disease stages, arguably characterized by better β-cell function, are more amenable to remission.22,23 The association between higher BMI and remission supports the central role of weight as a pathophysiologic driver of glucose homeostasis, and the impact of weight loss, possibly through reduction in hepatic fat and restoration of insulin secretion.24

An important and novel aspect of our study is the differential impact of remission definitions on both observed remission rates and clinical outcomes. As expected, definitions requiring withdrawal of glucose-lowering therapy resulted in lower remission rates and shorter remission duration. We note that the frequency of remission under definition 2 (allowing GLP-1RA monotherapy) was only marginally higher than under definition 1 (requiring discontinuation of all diabetes drugs). This is probably because GLP-1RA was being used as a second or more advanced line of therapy25 and was being discontinued before metformin. These data underscore the challenge of achieving drug-free remission in routine care, where complete medication discontinuation may be clinically discouraged. Nowadays, GLP-1RA and SGLT2 inhibitors may be continued for cardiovascular, renal, or weight-related benefits irrespective of glycemic status.26,27 This is not necessarily a limitation and reflects evolving guidelines that recommend continuing agents with proven cardiorenal protection even in normoglycemic individuals with residual risk.28 These considerations may explain why initiating different GLP-1RA tended to be associated with varying probabilities of remission. Dulaglutide showed the strongest positive association with remission under more restrictive definitions, despite it proved inferior to semaglutide for glycemic control and weight in the dedicated trial.29 This may reflect dulaglutide's uptake in years when end-organ protection was less on focus, and it was more likely to be discontinued than semaglutide.30 In contrast, lixisenatide was inversely associated with remission, possibly due to less potent effects on glycemia and weight.31 However, these results cannot be interpreted as comparative efficacy evidence because intraclass switches occurred frequently during a long period of observation encompassing several waves of GLP-1RA availability and shortage. In the absence of a control group not initiating GLP-1RA, we could not estimate the direct role that this class of molecules played on remission. Furthermore, other therapeutic strategies may yield greater remission rates, such as the dual incretin agonists, or the combination of GLP-1RA with SGLT-2 inhibitors,32 which is rapidly becoming a standard pharmacological management of T2D.

Although the differences in non-glycemic intermediate endpoints between remission and non-remission groups were statistically significant, they may be perceived as clinically modest: ∼2 kg weight loss, and 1–2 mm Hg blood pressure reduction. However, even small improvements in metabolic parameters can be meaningful at the population level and may contribute cumulatively to reduced long-term risk.

The reduction in new-onset microangiopathy among those achieving remission (12–16%) is consistent with evidence that glycemic normalization slows or prevents the development of retinopathy, nephropathy, and neuropathy.33 Interestingly, even transient remission (lasting 6–10 months) was associated with a significant reduction in the incidence of microvascular complications. This observation agrees with the concept of metabolic memory or legacy effect, whereby a period of improved glycemic control confers long-term benefits, even if subsequent glycemic deterioration occurs. This phenomenon, well demonstrated in the UKPDS,34 suggests that early attainment of normoglycemia may initiate protective changes in target organs that persist beyond the remission window,35 mainly involving epigenetic modulation of key disease-modifying pathways.36

In our study, the protective effect against microangiopathy was most evident under the stricter definitions of remission (remission 1–3), and appeared attenuated in remission 4. This gradient likely reflects the progressive dilution of metabolic normalization across definitions: while remission 1 requires normoglycemia without any glucose-lowering therapy, remission 4 permits intensified regimens that may reflect disease progression. As such, the diminishing protection from remission 1 to remission 4 may reflect a lower degree of endogenous glycemic recovery and possibly a lesser impact on underlying disease mechanisms.

In contrast to the effect on microangiopathy, remission was not associated with a lower incidence of a broad macroangiopathy definition incorporating subclinical atherosclerosis, possibly highlighting the long latency and multifactorial nature of atherosclerosis, less sensitive to short-term changes in glycemia. It is in fact expected that HbA1c-based exposures are more strongly associated with micro- than with macroangiopathy.37 Consistently, we recently reported that T2D remission following early weight loss was associated with protection from microangiopathy, but not macroangiopathy.18 Interestingly, here we found that remission 3 was associated with a significant reduction in incident cardiovascular events, possibly reflecting a subgroup with sustained glycemic control, continued cardioprotective therapy, and no disease progression. Remission 3 was also the only definition associated with a significant reduction in UACR by 30%, which is often cited as a valuable surrogate renal endpoint.38,39 Therefore, remission 3 emerges as the best trade-off in terms of frequency, duration, and consistent impact on long-term complications after initiation of GLP-1RA. It should be noted that we varied remission definition only by drug discontinuation criteria and not by HbA1c threshold (e.g., 5.7% versus 6.5%) or duration (e.g., 6 versus 3 months). In addition, other glucose metrics—such as fasting glucose or CGM profiles—may be useful to demonstrate a return to normal glucose regulation. We argue that future studies should systematically address all these variables to identify the best remission definition.

Our study has notable strengths, including its large sample size, multicenter design, and real-world setting, which enhance generalizability. The use of a common electronic health record platform across sites minimized information bias and allowed for uniform data extraction. To our knowledge, this is the largest study to compare multiple definitions of T2D remission after GLP-1RA initiation and to assess their respective associations with clinical outcomes.

Limitations must be acknowledged. Remission was not prospectively targeted or adjudicated, but retrospectively defined based on glycemic records and medication use. The absence of standardized protocols for drug discontinuation introduces bias, particularly for definitions requiring medication withdrawal. The retrospective ascertainment of other outcomes without prospective adjudication is another source of bias. The distribution of the various GLP-1RA mostly reflected their waves of availability in Italy,25 and no formal comparison can be performed within the class. As a limit to the pathophysiological interpretation of remission, we had no data on markers of insulin sensitivity and secretion, both of which can be improved by GLP-1RA and weight loss.40 Third, data on ethnicity, lifestyle factors, medication adherence, and socioeconomic variables were unavailable. Lastly, because follow-up ended with the last available clinical encounter, censoring may have occurred due to death or loss to follow-up, which we could not fully account for. As data were collected retrospectively from electronic health records without linkage to death registries or mortality databases, we were unable to distinguish whether patients lost to follow-up had died or discontinued care for other reasons. This limitation may have introduced informative censoring and potentially biased the estimation of outcomes (complications and remission durability). Furthermore, this precludes any analysis of the relationship between remission and all-cause or cardiovascular mortality, outcomes that are increasingly relevant when evaluating the broader prognostic value of diabetes remission.41

In summary, remission of T2D following GLP-1RA initiation is an achievable outcome in routine clinical practice, though its frequency and duration vary substantially depending on the definition applied. Our findings underscore the importance of early intervention and highlight a consistent clinical profile associated with remission, including shorter diabetes duration and lower burden of complications. While remission was associated with modest but statistically significant improvements in metabolic parameters and reduced risk of microangiopathy, its prognostic implications appear definition-dependent and limited for macrovascular outcomes. These data support the need for harmonized remission criteria and suggest that continuing cardiometabolic therapies may attenuate the distinction between remission and well-controlled diabetes.
