Morphological, Optical, and Dielectric Properties of Chitosan Biopolymer Thin Films Synthesized by Spray Pyrolysis
Authors: Meriem Saadi, Mohsen Erouel, Abdoulaye Tall, Siba Haba, Noureddine Bouguila, Nicole Jaffrezic-Renault, Kamel Khirouni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The development of environmentally friendly devices using natural materials presents a significant challenge for researchers aiming to create more sustainable and eco-friendly technologies. Chitosan has garnered increasing attention in recent years due to its promising applications in transparent and flexible electronic devices, such as transistors and drug delivery systems. This study investigates the preparation of chitosan solutions that are deposited onto glass and indium tin oxide (ITO)-coated glass substrates via spray pyrolysis to produce thin films. These films are thoroughly characterized through optical, electrical, and spectroscopic analyses. The SEM image of the chitosan thin film demonstrates an overall uniform and relatively smooth surface, indicating that most of the chitosan was successfully deposited, forming a continuous layer. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was conducted to examine the structural characteristics of the chitosan thin film, confirming its predominantly amorphous nature. UV–visible spectroscopy reveals that chitosan films display high transmittance in the wavelength range of 500–1500 nm, with a direct optical band gap of 4.14 eV. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy identifies various functional groups in the chitosan powder, confirming its molecular composition. Additionally, at room temperature and a frequency of 1 kHz, chitosan demonstrates a dielectric constant of around 10, suggesting a favorable ability of dielectric energy storage; it shows promising results when used as a dielectric layer in transistor applications. The results also indicate promising sustainability aspects, with minimal solvent use and the possibility of material recovery after the process, addressing the current challenges in sustainable electronics. This study highlights the novelty of using chitosan for eco-friendly, high-performance electronic devices and provides new insights into its integration into next-generation technologies.

Introduction

In the face of the climate crisis and accelerating biodiversity loss, it is essential to rethink our use of materials and manufacturing techniques, particularly in the production of electronic devices. One promising avenue is the adoption of ecological materials for everyday electronics. Natural materials are defined as construction materials that have minimal environmental impact throughout their entire life cycle from production to use and eventual disposal. These materials typically originate from renewable, recyclable, or biodegradable resources. They help reduce greenhouse gas emissions, are free from harmful chemicals, and are therefore safe for human health. Moreover, they contribute to reducing waste and pollution, especially in the construction and manufacturing industries. The use of eco-friendly materials can also improve air and water quality while offering long-term energy savings. In this context, organic and biopolymer-based materials have attracted growing interest for the development of sustainable and environmentally friendly electronics. − Among these, biopolymer-based dielectric materials have been extensively studied in recent years due to their intrinsic properties and environmental compatibility. −

Chitosan, in particular, has emerged as a widely investigated biopolymer thanks to its biodegradability, ability to form uniform thin films, and promising dielectric properties. It has been explored for various electronic applications, such as organic field-effect transistors (OFETs) and capacitive sensors, commonly processed through techniques like spin coating or drop casting. Despite the growing interest in chitosan-based dielectric films, few studies have focused on their processing via spray pyrolysis, a technique known for its simplicity, low cost, and suitability for large-area and flexible substrates. This makes it a compelling option for the development of environmentally friendly and flexible electronics. However, the use of toxic and hazardous solvents can negate the ecological benefits of such materials and hinder the development of truly sustainable electronic technologies. Natural materials also face significant challenges in their integration into electronic devices. A major concern is their limited stability under ambient conditions, as materials like chitosan are often sensitive to humidity and temperature fluctuations. Moreover, the scalability remains a key issue. While laboratory-scale experiments often yield promising results, translating these findings to industrial-scale, reproducible processes is far from straightforward. Additional limitations include performance trade-offs, such as lower conductivity or mechanical durability compared with synthetic alternatives. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of the physical and electrical behaviors of these materials is essential for overcoming their limitations. In this study, we investigate the morphological, optical, and dielectric properties of chitosan thin films, with the goal of contributing to the development of more stable and scalable green electronic systems. This work not only reinforces the ecological compatibility of chitosan but also explores a scalable and industrially relevant processing technique. The dielectric properties of the resulting films are thoroughly characterized, and their potential for use in transistor-based applications is evaluated. The results highlight a novel and sustainable approach for integrating biopolymers into the next generation of green electronics.

Extraction of Chitosan

Chitin is a widely occurring polysaccharide, and its principal derivative, chitosan, is obtained through alkaline deacetylation. Chitosan is a significant copolymer due to its antibacterial properties, biocompatibility, nontoxicity, and biodegradability. Chitin is extracted from shrimp shells (Parapenaeus longirostris, commonly known as deep water rose shrimp). The extraction process involves several steps: demineralization, deproteinization, and discoloration. Initially, the shells are treated with a 0.5 M hydrochloric acid solution at room temperature to remove the mineral content, predominantly calcium carbonate. Then, the solid residue is filtered, rinsed with distilled water, and dried for 24 h. Subsequently, proteins are extracted using a dilute alkaline solution. The resulting chitin is then finely ground to obtain particles approximately 1 mm in size. This process involved adding a sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution to the dematerialized shells. The product obtained after stirring was washed with distilled water and dried in an oven for 24 h. The discoloration step was carried out by using a 0.31% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) solution. After stirring, the chitin was filtered, washed with distilled water until neutral, and then dried in an oven for 24 h. For deacetylation, the chitin obtained after discoloration was treated with NaOH solution under a nitrogen atmosphere and heated to 100 °C for 3 h. After filtration, the resulting chitosan was washed until neutral and subsequently dried in an oven at 50 °C for 24 h to obtain a powder. This temperature was selected to ensure the complete removal of residual moisture while avoiding thermal degradation or chemical alteration of the polymer structure.

Chitosan Film Preparation

Chitosan thin films are prepared by first creating an aqueous solution of chitosan powder in acetic acid. The solution is heated at 60 °C and magnetically stirred at 350 rpm for 2 h to allow the compounds to be completely dissolved in solution. Then, the solution is deposited onto a small piece of glass and ITO-coated glass substrates using the spray pyrolysis technique to form thin chitosan films. The thickness of the layer was measured and found to be approximately 700 nm. XRD Characterization of Chitosan Thin Films

X-ray diffraction (XRD) is a widely used technique for evaluating the crystalline structure of chitosan. Compared to the diffraction patterns of chitosan powder reported in the literature, the XRD analysis of the chitosan thin film reveals a broad and weak peak of low intensity centered around 2θ ≈ 24, indicating that the film is predominantly amorphous, as illustrated in Figure . This loss of crystallinity can be attributed to the interaction between acetic acid and the chitosan chains, which disrupts the regular molecular packing.

The Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectrum of chitosan is presented in Figure . According to the FTIR analysis, in the range of 3500–3000 cm–1, a relatively broad and intense band is observed, indicative of O–H stretching vibrations and overlaps of N–H vibrations. The absorption peaks recorded at 1647 and 1590 cm–1 can be attributed to the amide II band and the C–O stretching vibration of acetyl groups present in chitin. The absorption peaks at 1150 and 1062–1026 cm–1 doublet are, respectively, C–O–C and C–O stretching peaks. , The band at 894 cm–1 corresponds to C–H out-of-plane bending of the β-anomeric configuration of the glycosidic bond. − Based on the method described by Brugnerotto et al., the degree of acetylation (DA) of chitosan was determined by the ratio of the absorption bands at 1320 cm–1 (acetylated amine) and 1377 cm–1 (reference peak). The DA was found to be 80%. The molecular mass, determined using an Agilent 1260 Infinity Steric Exclusion Chromatography System, was 142.7 kDa, with a polydispersity index of 1.57.

For a given investigation temperature, as the frequency increases, the Z′ curve decreases while the Z″ rises until it reaches a peak, and then it decreases. Figure c depicts the Nyquist diagram (i.e., −Z″ versus Z′) of the chitosan thin film. For each temperature, the curve reveals a single circular area, whose radius decreases with increasing temperature. This result indicates that electronic transport is governed solely by the grains or grain boundaries rather than by the presence of impurities or interfacial phenomena. In chitosan-based materials, grain boundaries generally exhibit a higher capacitance than the grains themselves, as they provide more sites for interaction with charges or molecules. Furthermore, studies on similar biosourced materials suggest that the specific capacitance of grain boundaries can range from 10– 3 to 10– 6 F/cm2. In our material, the capacitance reaches approximately 10– 11 F/cm2, which confirms that charge transport is mainly ensured by the grains. Electrical Characterization

Impedance spectroscopy is a technique employed to measure fundamental electrical and dielectric properties of materials. This technique allows the determination of key electrical parameters, including conductance, capacitance, and dielectric permittivity. In this study, we present the characterization of a chitosan thin film. Measurements are carried out using an impedance analyzer (Agilent 4294A) across a frequency range of 40 Hz–110 MHz.

Electrical conductance is a parameter for understanding electrical circuits, as it measures the ability of a material or component to transmit an electric current. This property is fundamental in electronics and electrical engineering. Generally, conductance increases with temperature due to enhanced polarization effects and the variation of the free carrier density. In addition, conductance is frequency-dependent, with an increase in conductance observed with higher frequencies, as represented by the alternating current conductance (σAC). This frequency dependence can be described by Jonscher’s power law, specifying a hopping conduction process: G(ω)=GDC+GAC=GDC+Aωs 2 where G(w) represents a total electrical conductance, G DC and G AC are the conductance at low and high frequencies, respectively, s is the fractional exponent, and A is the temperature-dependent constant. A comprehensive investigation into the conductivity of the chitosan layer involves a temperature-dependent analysis. Figure a presents the variation of the conductance as a function of frequency at different temperatures (80 to 420 K). The curves depict two regions of conductance that follow Jonscher’s universal power law, expressed by eq . At low frequencies, specifically below 1 kHz, the conductance remains relatively constant for a majority of the temperature range, corresponding to the direct current conductance (G DC), which increases with temperature. In Figure a, we plotted the evolution of conductance versus temperature to determine the nature of the G DC conductance mechanism. Figure b shows that G DC follows the Arrhenius law, as described by the following equation: GDC=G0e−Ea/KBT 3 where E a is the activation energy, G 0 is the constant, and K B is the Boltzmann constant. In Figure b, a linear branch appears at high temperatures within the temperature range of 300–420 K. Then, we deduced that the DC conduction mechanism is thermally activated, and the chitosan film exhibits activation energies of approximately 200 meV. Compared to other natural dielectrics, such as cashew gum (730 meV) or almond gum (647 meV), chitosan demonstrates a lower activation energy, indicating a relatively easier charge transport pathway. This advantage may stem from its semicrystalline structure and the presence of proton-conducting functional groups (e.g., −NH2, −OH), which facilitate localized charge movement. Consequently, chitosan not only offers a favorable balance between dielectric stability and limited conductivity but also outperforms several other biopolymers in terms of transport efficiency under ambient conditions. This trend suggests that the compound contains carrier traps, which release free carriers when the temperature reaches 300 K and induce an increase in conductivity. The value of activation energy corresponds to an electronic conduction mechanism. Thus, we conclude that an electron seems to require a significant amount of energy to hop from one site to another. The second region, established at high frequencies, shows that the conductance increases with frequency. This phenomenon is presented by G AC and described by eq , indicating a hopping conduction process: GAC=A(T)×(2πf)s 4 where A is a pre-exponential factor, f is the frequency, and s is the fractional exponent. Different theoretical models correlate the conduction mechanism of AC conductance with the behavior of s(T). Within the studied temperature range, for each temperature, two linear slopes are observed. Consequently, the frequency dependence of the conductance (Figure c,d) exhibits two exponents, s (s 1 and s 2). This phenomenon is known as the double Jonscher power law and can be described by Bruce’s equation: Gac=GDC+Aωs1+Bωs2 5 where G DC, A, and B are constants, and s 1 and s 2 are the frequency exponents.

At low temperatures, charges are often trapped, and their low mobility may lead to a high s 1 exponent. As the temperature increases, hopping events become more frequent, and s 1 decreases, indicating a thermally activated hopping conduction with increased disorder. The increase in s 2 at low temperatures, followed by a decrease as the temperature increases, may correspond to a dipolar relaxation process, where dipoles are more rigid at low temperatures and more free at higher temperatures, leading to a more linear behavior. The exponent s 1 dominant at low frequencies is commonly associated with a hopping mechanism involving localized states. This behavior is consistent with models such as the correlated barrier hopping (CBH) or quantum mechanical tunneling (QMT), where charge carriers are thermally activated to overcome potential barriers between localized sites. In contrast, the exponent s 2, which becomes significant at higher frequencies, is often attributed to dipolar relaxation phenomena or conduction involving more delocalized states. At these higher frequencies, the inability of dipoles or localized charges to follow the rapidly oscillating electric field results in a different dynamic response. Moreover, values of s 2 approaching unity may indicate a transition toward quasi-free charge transport, possibly occurring in structurally less disordered regions of the material. These findings support the presence of multiple coexisting conduction mechanisms within the investigated system.

Dielectric Characterization

The complex permittivity can be expressed using the following eq : ε=ε′−jε″ 6 where ε′ and ε″ are the real and imaginary parts of ε, respectively. ε′ represents the dielectric constant, and ε″ is associated with the dielectric losses (tan δ). The real part of permittivity ε′ (Figure b) can be deduced from capacitance density c d. Figure a presents the variation in capacitance density as a function of frequency at different temperatures. The curve indicates that, at low frequencies, capacitance increases with increasing temperature and decreases with increasing frequency. At low frequencies, the high capacitance values may be attributed to the contribution of different types of polarization. At high frequencies, the capacitance curves tend to converge, suggesting that electronic polarizations predominantly govern the behavior, and the other polarization mechanisms (orientational and interfacial) become negligible. At low temperatures and high frequencies, a “flat loss” type behavior is observed, whereas at high temperatures and low frequencies, dielectric losses increase because more carriers become free and can follow the applied electric field. It is noteworthy that the level of tanδ exhibiting “flat loss” behavior shows a dependence on the temperature. This phenomenon is attributed to the significant increase in dielectric losses with temperature between 300 and 420 K. Indeed, between these two temperatures, the dielectric losses increase by almost 50%, while the real permittivity only rises by 10%. This results in a notable rise in the tanδ value (Figure c). As illustrated in Figure b, the variations in real permittivity with frequency are average (around 3% per decade of frequency), while the temperature dependence of real permittivity is quite pronounced, with a 47% increase between 300 and 420 K at 1 kHz. This behavior, which is a characteristic of electrode polarization, is similar to what is observed in an amorphous dielectric. However, the peak of the loss is not visible due to the measurement being limited to 420 °C.

Conclusions

In this article, we investigated the physical properties of a chitosan thin film using various techniques. The chitosan thin film reveals a generally homogeneous morphology. The XRD pattern confirmed the absence of long-range crystalline order in the chitosan layer, consistent with its amorphous structure. According to the FTIR study, the following vibrations were observed in the chitosan spectrum: O–H stretching vibration, N–H overlap vibration, amide I stretching vibration, and N–H bending of amide II. UV–visible analysis revealed high transparency, with an energy gap of approximately 4.14 eV. Impedance spectroscopy measurements show that the capacitance density and relative dielectric permittivity decrease with increasing frequency and decreasing temperature due to dipolar polarization and a thermal freezing effect. The frequency dependence of the conductivity follows double Jonscher’s universal power law. The AC conductivity is well described by the hopping model based on the behaviors of the angular frequency power. This film can be used as a high-band-pass filter. Besides, these findings are significant and could be optimized or diversified by using other natural products, with the aim of tuning their properties for green electronics applications. As a final remark, the results highlight the need to optimize deposition and drying parameters to improve the material’s integrity. Further characterizations, including recyclability assessment, CO2 fixation studies, porosity analysis, and density functional theory (DFT) calculations, will be conducted in future work to complement this analysis and deepen the understanding of chitosan’s behavior.
