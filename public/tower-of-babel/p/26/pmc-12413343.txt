Exploring the anticancer potential of R. tridentata extracts: a cytotoxicity study against human prostate cancer cell lines (LNCaP and DU145)
Authors: Ali Kudamba, Godfrey S. Bbosa, Allan Lugaajju, Henry Wabinga, Nixon Niyonzima, Moses Ocan, Ali M. Damani, Hussein M. Kafeero, Jamilu E. Ssenku, Shaban A. Okurut, Muniiru Lubowa, Abdul Walusansa, S. Sivagami Sivasundaram, Shobana Shampath, Haruna Muwonge
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the cytotoxic effects of R. tridentata extracts on prostate cancer cells, providing insight into its potential therapeutic benefits and scientific validation for its traditional use in cancer treatment. The cytotoxicity of R. tridentata extracts was evaluated on prostate cancer cell lines (LNCaP and DU 145) using the MTT assay, with doxorubicin as a reference standard. Our findings demonstrated significant concentration- and time-dependent cytotoxic effects of the extracts on both cell lines (p < 0.0024 to p < 0.0002). Notably, the methanol extract exhibited potent cytotoxicity, with IC50 values ranging from 124.07 to 211 μg/mL (DU145) and 100 to 180 μg/ml (LNCaP) over 24–72 h (p < 0.0024 to p < 0.0001), highlighting its therapeutic potential. This study unveils the promising cytotoxic activity of R. tridentata extracts, particularly methanol extracts, against prostate cancer cells, showcasing their concentration- and time-dependent effects. These findings underscore the potential of plant-derived extracts as therapeutic agents for cancer treatment, warranting further research.

Introduction

In the recent times, unlocking cancer’s vulnerabilities hinges on understanding cellular death, where cytotoxicity is a key metric for chemotherapy’s effectiveness [1, 2]. Cytotoxicity refers to the ability of certain treatments to induce cell death, a process that has been extensively studied in the context of cancer research [3]. To counter internal and external threats, cells deploy complex defense strategies against stressors like oxidative damage, DNA disruption, and mitochondrial impairment, all potential triggers for cytotoxicity [4–6]. Furthermore, cells can employ autophagy, a survival mechanism that breaks down and recycles its component parts, but can potentially be cytotoxic under stressful condition [7]. Cytotoxicity and apoptosis share similar cell death mechanisms, involving caspase activation and the release of cytochrome c from mitochondria into the cytosol, which promotes cell death through the formation of the apoptosome [8]. Acute injury triggers cell death through oxidative stress and external signals, such as TNF and Fas ligands, which activate cell surface receptors and caspases [8–11]. In addition, cytotoxic compounds can trigger inflammation by releasing free radicals and cytokines that damage or kill cells [9]. While acute injury and cytotoxic compounds can trigger cell death and inflammation, certain plant compounds like curcumin, artemisinin, ginger, and green tea catechins have shown promise in cancer treatment by modulating ROS/RNS and inducing cell death [9]. The exact mechanisms and therapeutic potential of compounds from medicinal plants like R. tridentata, traditionally used by local communities and herbalists in the Elgon sub-region, are not well understood and warrant further investigation.

Prostate cancer continues to pose a significant global health challenge, with current treatments including chemotherapy, gene therapy, hormonal therapy, surgery, and radiation therapy and these are often limited by severe side effects, less availability among others [12]. According to Dodkins et al., prostate cancer is a disease characterized by the uncontrolled growth and multiplication of abnormal cells in the prostate gland, potentially invading surrounding tissues and spreading to other parts of the body [13]. Due to these limitations, medicinal plants have gained popularity in cancer treatment, including prostate cancer, due to their perceived therapeutic potential and effectiveness compared to conventional medicines [14]. Globally, various medicinal plants have demonstrated potent anticancer properties, such as Canarium schweinfurthii and Dischistocalyx grandifolius in Cameroon, which have shown cytotoxic effects against cancer cells [5, 6, 14]. Medicinal plants contain bioactive compounds, including alkaloids, flavonoids, terpenoids, phenolic compounds, and saponins, which are a major source for the development of conventional anticancer drugs [5, 6, 15, 16]. Furthermore, essential oils from Satureja montana L. demonstrate anticancer properties, harming cervical, leukemia, and breast cancer cells [17, 18, 14, 16]. Several plant-derived anticancer drugs, such as paclitaxel (Taxus brevifolia), vinca alkaloids (Catharanthus roseus), artemisinin (Artemisia annua), etoposide (Podophyllum hexandrum), curcumin (turmeric), and quercetin (tea and fruits), have shown cytotoxic effects against prostate cancer cells [14, 18, 19, 19, 20]. The Vitaceae plant family, to which R. tridentata belongs, including Leea indica, is also known to contain bioactive compounds like quercetin and ursolic acid, which exhibit potent cytotoxic activity against prostate cancer cell lines [5, 6, 19, 20]. Rhoicissus species, belonging to the Vitaceae plant family, have shown potential therapeutic benefits, including facilitating childbirth and treating infection [14, 16]. The Vitaceae plant family contains large quantities of compound including alkaloids, flavonoids, tannins, and terpenoids, which have demonstrated potential as anticancer agents [1, 21]. In the Elgon sub-region, local communities and herbalists traditionally use R. tridentata to treat cancer, citing its effectiveness [22]. As a result, the current study investigated the cytotoxic effects of R. tridentata extracts (aqueous, methanol, and total crude extract) on human prostate cancer cell lines (LNCaP and DU145).

Material and methods

Apparatus and reagents

Apparatus

The current study utilized a range of equipment, including a biosafety cabinet of grade (class 3), carbon dioxide incubator (level 3), water bath, and various culture flasks (25, 50, 75, 100, 125, 150, and 175 mL). Additionally, multi-well plates with 6, 12, 24, 48, and 96 wells were used. The study also employed pipettes, (single and multi) pipette channels, pipette tips of various volumes, filter units (500 mL and 1 L), 96-well plates, cryovial tubes, and falcon tubes (15, 30, and 50 mL). Furthermore, a centrifuge, − 80 °C freezer, nitrogen tank, automated cell counter, cell counter slides, micropipette, and a refrigerator maintained at 2–4 °C were all used during the experimental procedures for data generation in this study.

Reagents

The study used a variety of reagents including Hepes buffer, DMEM, RPMI, fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin–streptomycin solution, hemocytometer, trypsin, l-glutamine, and sodium bicarbonate. In addition, two prostate cancer cell lines (DU145 and LNCaP) were utilized. Furthermore, other key reagents included liquid nitrogen, carbon dioxide gas, phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and the chemotherapy drug doxorubicin, dimethyl sulfoxide, distilled water, methanol, and from R. tridentata tuber extracts were all used.

Plant collection

The plant R. tridentata root tubers used in this experimental study were carefully collected from the slopes of Mount Elgon, a sub-region renowned for its rich biodiversity and unique ecosystem [23]. The plant was identified by a taxonomist at the Makerere University Herbarium laboratory and assigned a voucher number, KA051, which was deposited therein. These were specifically collected from the slopes of Mount Elgon in Sironko and Bulambuli districts. To ensure the integrity and authenticity of the root tubers, they were washed with running tap water, cut into small pieces, air-dried, and ground into a coarse powder using a hammer mill (Model BRONICA).

Serial extraction process and storage

This study employed a serial extraction protocol based on previous work by Ngouana et al. [24] with modifications. The serial extraction used water and methanol (1%) as solvents due to their differing polarities, allowing for the extraction of compounds with varying degrees of polarity [25]. The serial extraction consisted of the following steps: (1) Methanol extraction (1%): 500 g of powdered plant material underwent maceration in 1500 mL of methanol for 72 h at 25 °C. After filtration and concentration via rotary evaporation (50–60 °C), 44 g of extract were obtained, yielding 8.8%. (2) Aqueous extraction: The residue was air-dried and extracted with 2 L of distilled water (previously boiled and cooled) for 24 h. Following filtration and vacuum concentration (60–70 °C), 22.5 g of aqueous extract were obtained, yielding 4.5%. (3) Storage: The dried extracts were stored in a refrigerator at − 20 °C to preserve potency and integrity for later use.

Preparation of extract concentrations

To create the stock solution, aqueous and methanol extracts of 0.5 g each were dissolved in 5 mL of their respective solvents. The resulting solution was diluted by a factor of five to achieve a concentration of 0.1 g/mL, which is equivalent to 100,000 µg/mL (since 0.1 g/mL × 1,000,000 = 100,000 µg/mL). From this solution, subsequent concentrations of drugs used in the study interventions were prepared through serial dilution, based on the formula M1V1 = M2V2.

Spectrophotometric identification and quantification

Principle of spectrophotometry

Phytochemicals in the R. tridentata were identified using spectrophotometry, based on the method previously described by Laxmi et al. [26], with necessary modification. This technique measures the absorbance of light by phytochemicals at various wavelengths, allowing for the detection and quantification of specific compounds. This method facilitated the identification of a diverse range of phytochemicals, including alkaloids, flavonoids, phenols, and tannins, which are known for their potential anticancer properties and contribution to cytotoxic activity. Many of these compounds have been widely utilized in the development of anticancer drugs, highlighting their therapeutic significance.

Optimization of cell concentration for in vitro cell culture tests

To determine the optimal cell concentration, we tested varying concentrations in 96-well plates. Specifically, one plate was seeded at 8.8 × 10^5 cells/mL, while two additional plates were seeded at 4.4 × 10^5 cells/mL. Following incubation in a CO2 incubator under standard conditions, our results showed that 4.4 × 10^5 cells/mL was the optimal concentration for both LNCaP and DU145 cell lines was used.

Cell culturing process and cryopreservation

The cell culturing was performed based on the protocol described by Lin et al. [27], with modifications to suit the current study objectives. The cells were procured from Cytion, a company based in Düsseldorf, Germany, and shipped directly to the Biomedical Research Laboratory (Immunology section) at Makerere University, where cryopreservation and subsequent experiments were performed. For long-term storage, the cells were cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen. To achieve this, the cryotube was first placed in a Mr. Frosty container, which gradually reduced the temperature by 1 °C per minute to − 80 °C, and then the cells were transferred to liquid nitrogen at − 196 °C. This controlled freezing process helped prevent ice crystal formation and preserved cell viability.

Culture media preparation

The RPMI cell culture medium was prepared by supplementing 870 mL of RPMI 1640 with 100 mL of Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS, 10% v/v), 10 mL of l-glutamine (1% v/v), and 10 mL of penicillin–streptomycin (1% v/v), resulting in final volume of 1 L. The medium was then sterilized by filtration using a 0.22 μm filter unit under vacuum pump.

Cell culturing and incubation

The cells were pipetted into a falcon tube washed and centrifuged for 10 min at 1200 rpm after adding 5 mL of the prepared cell culture media. The cell pellets were then resuspended and pipetted into a T25 cell culture flask to facilitate the initial cell signaling. The flask was placed in a carbon dioxide incubator and maintained at 37 °C, 95.5% relative humidity, and 5% carbon dioxide for three days. The cells were monitored daily for signs of contamination and proliferation until they reached 90% confluency.

Sub-culturing and passaging

Once the cells reached the 90% confluency, the old medium was serologically discarded, and the remaining cells were then washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Trypsin was added to the cells, which were then incubated for 2 min to facilitate detachment. The cells were subsequently observed under low power magnification (×10) for morphological changes as indicative of detachment. The cells were counted using a hemocytometer under a microscope or with an automated cell counter. The cells were again sub-cultured in T125 mL flasks, and the process continued until the required number of cells was attained.

Cell treatment and MTT (cytotoxicity) assay

The current investigation for this assay adopted the procedure previously established by Vajrabhaya and Korsuwannawong [28], with modification. Following a 24-h incubation, cells seeded at 1 × 10^4 cells per well were treated with R. tridentata extracts (0–800 μg/mL). Cytotoxicity was evaluated at 24, 48, and 72 h using the MTT assay. The cells were incubated with 20 μL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL) for 3–4 h, and formazan crystals were dissolved with DMSO (0.5%). The absorbance was measured at 590 nm using a microplate reader, and optical density readings were used to compute cytotoxicity.

Calculation of cytotoxicity and inhibitory concentration at 50 (IC50)

Data analysis & presentation

Data analysis

The data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism and Sigma plot14 to determine the IC50 value, which represents the concentration of the test substance required to inhibit 50% of cell growth. The IC50 value was used to evaluate the cytotoxic potential of the test substance.

Data presentation

The data were summarized as mean percentages ± standard deviation (SD) (Mean ± SD) in tables and figures to facilitate interpretation.

Results

Prostate cancer remains a significant global health challenge, particularly in advanced stages where treatment options are limited. Given the growing interest in natural products with anticancer properties, this study investigated the cytotoxic effects of R. tridentata extract on LNCaP and DU145 prostate cancer cells. The findings presented below highlight the extract’s impact on cell viability, offering valuable insights into its potential as a complementary therapeutic approach for prostate cancer treatment.

The RTM at 800 µg/mL exhibits moderate cytotoxicity, with values increasing from 53.38% at 24 h (p = 0.0805) to 79.33% at 48 h (p < 0.0298) and then slightly decreasing to 70.15% at 72 h (p < 0.0048) compared to the negative control. Notably, all RTM values are significantly different from the positive control (p < 0.0001). The RAM at 800 µg/mL shows significant cytotoxicity, ranging from 36.52% at 24 h (p < 0.0114) to 79.55% at 48 h (p < 0.0099) and 70.54% at 72 h (p < 0.0058) compared to the negative control. Furthermore, all RAM values are significantly different from the positive control (p < 0.0001), with an additional significant difference observed at 48 h (p < 0.0008). The RTAM at 800 µg/mL demonstrates substantial cytotoxicity, increasing from 41.51% at 24 h (p = 0.0705) to 81.15% at 48 h (p < 0.0150) and reaching a peak of 81.33% at 72 h (p < 0.0048) compared to the negative control. All RTAM values are significantly different from the positive control (p < 0.0001), including an additional significant difference at 48 h (p < 0.0002). The results of the present study suggest that increasing dosages and treatment durations enhance cytotoxicity, highlighting potential therapeutic applications.

The table presents a comprehensive analysis of the effects of various treatments (RTM, RAM, and RTAM) at different dosages (800, 400, 200, 100, and 50 µg/mL) on cytotoxicity over time (24, 48, and 72 h). At 24 h, the 800 µg/mL treatments exhibit moderate cytotoxicity, with RTM, RAM, and RTAM showing 53.38% ± 0.318 (p = 0.0805), 36.52% ± 9.504 (p < 0.0114), and 41.51% ± 9.645 (p = 0.0705), respectively. In contrast, the positive control reaches 100% cytotoxicity (p < 0.0001). At lower dosages, cytotoxicity decreases; for instance, RTM at 50 µg/mL shows only 1.162% ± 1.398 (p = 0.0805). As time progresses to 48 h, cytotoxicity increases across most treatments and dosages. Notably, RTAM at 800 µg/mL reaches 81.15% ± 0.255 (p < 0.0150), and RAM at 400 µg/mL exhibits 81.33% ± 0.247 (p < 0.0058). The positive control maintains 100% cytotoxicity (p < 0.0001). At 72 h, cytotoxicity further escalates, with RTAM at 800 µg/mL reaching 81.33% ± 0.255 (p < 0.0048) and RAM at 400 µg/mL showing 76.05% ± 0.785 (p < 0.0058). The data suggest that increasing dosages and treatment durations enhance cytotoxicity, although the effects of RTM, RAM, and RTAM vary across different time points and dosages. The findings of this study have significant implications for understanding the cytotoxic effects of these treatments and their potential therapeutic applications.

The findings indicate the cytotoxic effects of R. tridentata extracts at a concentration of 800 μg/mL on LNCaP and DU145 prostate cancer cell lines at various time intervals. Notably, the aqueous extract (RTA) exhibited the most significant cytotoxic effect against DU145 prostate cancer cell lines, achieving a highly significant increase in cytotoxicity at 24 h (p < 0.001), which was sustained at 48 h and 72 h (p < 0.05). Similarly, the methanol extract (RTM) showed significant increase in cytotoxicity at 24 h (p < 0.05), which became more pronounced at 48 h and 72 h. In contrast, the effects on LNCaP cell lines were less pronounced, with only one total crude extract showing a significant increase in cytotoxicity at 24 h (p < 0.05). The findings suggest that R. tridentata extracts, particularly RTA on DU145, hold promise as potential therapeutic agents for the treatment of prostate cancer. These results warrant further investigation into their mechanisms of action and clinical applications to explore their efficacy and safety in prostate cancer treatment.

Discussions

Cytotoxic activity of R. tridentata extract against LNCaP prostate cancer cells at various time points

This study demonstrates that R. tridentata extracts exhibit concentration-dependent cytotoxic effects on cells, with increasing doses leading to enhanced cell death (Table 1 and Figs. 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5). A significant (p < 0.0009) shift in cell death was seen between doses of 200 and 400 µg/mL. The study demonstrated a concentration-dependent increase in cell death across all extracts, with the highest effect observed at 800 µg/mL. The methanol extract stood out for its steady and sustained increase in cytotoxicity up to 800 µg/mL, indicating a potent and consistent cytotoxic effect. The observed cytotoxicity can be attributed to the presence of bioactive compounds in the extracts, including flavonoids, alkaloids, terpenoids, and phenolic acids, which may induce internal stress, including DNA damage and ER stress, activating various signaling pathways [30]. These pathways, including mitochondrial and caspase-dependent TGF-β pathways, are crucial in regulating cell responses, ultimately leading to cell cycle disruption and cell death [31, 32]. The R. tridentata extracts contain bioactive compounds like flavonoids, phenolic acids, and stilbenes that may contribute to the cytotoxic effects of the cell [33–35]. Plant-derived compounds, such as alkaloids (vinblastine, vincristine), flavonoids (quercetin), terpenoids, ursolic acid, saponins, phenolic acids, and quinones, have shown cytotoxic effects by inhibiting cell proliferation and inducing apoptosis [12, 35, 36]. These compounds are known to act through specific receptors, including G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) and Receptor Tyrosine Kinases (RTKs), on various cancer cell lines [37, 38]. They exert their anticancer effects by blocking key signaling pathways, including PI3K/AKT and MAPK/ERK, which ultimately inhibits cell growth and triggers cytotoxicity [12]. Furthermore, they may also induce additional cellular stressors, such as TNF, oxidative stress, and DNA damage, triggering both extrinsic and intrinsic apoptotic pathways, including mitochondrial dysfunction, ultimately leading to cell death [17]. The findings of this study are consistent with previous studies that have reported the anticancer effects of plant extracts [39–41]. The findings of this study demonstrate that the Inhibitory concentration at 50 (IC50) values of all extracts decreased significantly over time, indicating an increase in cytotoxic potency. The total crude extract exhibited the most pronounced effect, with an IC50 value of 258.11 μg/mL, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic agent. The methanol extract and aqueous extract also showed notable cytotoxic activity, with IC50 values of 377.88 μg/mL and 359.37 μg/mL, respectively. Notably, the total crude extract maintained a similar IC50 (p = 0.0557), whereas the methanol extract exhibited a significant decrease in IC50 (p < 0.0001), indicating increased potency. In contrast, the aqueous extract showed a marked increase in IC50, suggesting a decrease in cytotoxic activity. Our findings align with previous research, demonstrating the extracts’ time-dependent inhibition of cell growth, which holds promise for treating various diseases, including cancer [42, 43, 21, 33]. This study underscores the therapeutic potential of the total crude extract and methanol extract, warranting further investigation of their efficacy and safety in animal models and isolate and characterize bioactive compounds and mechanism of action. The findings of the present demonstrate that all extracts of R. tridentata exhibit significant, concentration-dependent cytotoxicity, with a notable increase in cytotoxic effect at higher concentrations (Table 2 and Figs. 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5). The methanol extract displayed the most pronounced cytotoxic effect, outperforming the negative control at 800 µg/L. These results suggest that the extracts have potential therapeutic benefits, particularly in targeting cancerous or damaged cells. The observed cytotoxicity can be attributed to the presence of various bioactive compounds, including flavonoids like apigenin, quercetin, and luteolin, which exhibit anticancer properties by inducing apoptosis and inhibiting cell proliferation [21, 40, 41]. Other plant-based compounds, such as catechins (e.g., EGCG), curcumin, artemisinin, and thymoquinone, also exhibit anticancer properties by inhibiting cell proliferation and inducing apoptosis through modulation of key pathways, including COX-2 and NF-κB [44]. Furthermore, bioactive compounds like phenoxodiol and cardamonin target signaling pathways, including PI3K/Akt/mTOR and Ras/Raf/MAPK, leading to cell cycle arrest and apoptosis [41, 44, 45]. Additionally, compounds like cyanidin, cardamonin, and apigenin modulate the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, inhibiting cancer cell growth and metastasis [46, 47]. These findings are consistent with recent studies that have demonstrated the cytotoxic potential of plant extracts, such as methanol extracts of Catharanthus roseus and Taxus baccata, supporting their promise in cancer research [21, 40]. Overall, this study highlights the therapeutic potential of R. tridentata extracts in cancer treatment and underscores the importance of further research into their bioactive compounds and mechanisms of action. The IC50 values obtained in this study provide valuable insights into the cytotoxic potency of methanol, aqueous, and total crude extracts of R. tridentata (Table 3). Notably, the methanol extract exhibited a significantly lower IC50 value compared to the other extracts, indicating its higher potency in inducing cytotoxicity. This finding is consistent with previous research that highlights the importance of optimizing extraction methods to achieve maximum cytotoxic potency [48–50]. The superior efficacy of methanol in extracting cytotoxic compounds suggests that it is a more effective solvent for this purpose, which is in line with previous studies [40]. The time-dependent reduction in IC50 values across extracts highlights the importance of exposure duration in determining their cytotoxic effects [33]. Interestingly, the cytotoxic effectiveness of the extracts is largely achieved within 48 h, with minimal additional toxicity observed beyond this time point. This finding has significant implications for the development of targeted cancer treatments, suggesting that optimal therapeutic effects can be achieved with controlled exposure durations [51]. These findings support the idea that plant extracts can be optimized for specific therapeutic applications by tailoring the exposure duration and solvent selection to achieve maximum cytotoxic potency [17]. The results are consistent with previous studies, such as Saini et al., which demonstrate the potential of plant extracts in cancer therapy [52, 53]. Further research into extraction method optimization could lead to the development of more potent therapeutic agent [52]. To unlock the full therapeutic potential of these extracts, future studies should focus on identifying the bioactive compounds and elucidating their mechanisms of action. Conclusions

This study demonstrates the potent cytotoxic effects of methanol, aqueous, and total crude extracts, showcasing their potential as therapeutic agents against cancer cells. Notably, methanol proved to be an effective solvent for extracting cytotoxic compounds, while the total crude extract exhibited enhanced activity, likely due to synergistic effects. The concentration- and time-dependent cytotoxicity highlights the importance of optimizing exposure duration and solvent selection. This research provides a foundation for further exploration of plant-based therapeutics, especially for cancer treatment. Future studies should focus on identifying specific bioactive compounds and elucidating their mechanisms to unlock the extracts’ full therapeutic potential and facilitate clinical applications.
