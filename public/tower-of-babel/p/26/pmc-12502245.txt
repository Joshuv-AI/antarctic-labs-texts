HnRNP M expression rescues neurodegeneration in neuronal intranuclear inclusion disease mouse model by restoring dysregulated RNA splicing and transcription
Authors: Yongcheng Pan, Yangping Li, Ying Jiang, Xinhui Wang, Juan Wan, Qiying Sun, Yun Tian, Lu Shen, Hong Jiang, Beisha Tang, Bing Yao, Qiong Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeuronal intranuclear inclusion disease (NIID) is a neurodegenerative disease caused by the expanded GGC repeats in the NOTCH2NLC gene, yet its underlying pathogenic mechanisms remains to be fully elucidated. Previous study suggests that hnRNP M, an RNA-binding protein sequestered into the inclusions, may contribute to RNA processing defects in NIID.ResultsIn this study, we investigated the role of hnRNP M in NIID pathogenesis by utilizing a NOTCH2NLC-98GGC transgenic mouse model that faithfully recapitulates key NIID phenotypes. We found that AAV-mediated hnRNP M expression partially alleviated neuropathological features, such as neuronal loss and gliosis, and improved motor deficits in NIID mice. Transcriptome analysis further revealed that hnRNP M expression restored transcriptional and splicing dysregulation in synapse- and neurodegeneration-related genes, such as Dlg and Smn.ConclusionsOur study established hnRNP M as a key regulator of NIID pathogenesis by modulating RNA transcription and splicing, underscoring the potential of targeting RNA processing abnormalities as a therapeutic strategy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13578-025-01477-9.Keywords: NIID, Neurodegeneration, HnRNP M, RNA transcription, RNA splicing

Introduction

Neuronal intranuclear inclusion disease (NIID) is a rare neurodegenerative disease characterized by widespread intranuclear inclusions in the nervous system as well as multiple visceral organs [1, 2]. The clinical phenotypes of NIID are highly heterogeneous, including cognitive impairment, cerebellar ataxia, parkinsonism, paroxysmal symptoms, autonomic dysfunction, muscle weakness, and more [3–6]. Recently, it was reported that the expanded GGC repeats within the 5’untranslated region (UTR) of the NOTCH2NLC gene was the causative factor for NIID [7–9]. Further studies demonstrated that expanded GGC repeats of NOTCH2NLC produced multiple polypeptides and caused cellular toxicity in vitro and in vivo [10–14]. Although various pathogenic theories regarding the GGC repeat expansion in NOTCH2NLC have been proposed, the molecular mechanisms underlying NIID pathogenesis remain incompletely understood and require further exploration.

It’s worth noting that aberrant RNA processing, such as alternative splicing and transcription, has been identified in various tissues from NIID patients, patient-derived cells, and mouse models [7–9, 12–15]. Heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoproteins (hnRNPs) belong to an evolutionarily conserved and functionally diverse family of RNA-binding proteins with multifarious roles in RNA processing, such as alternative splicing, transcriptional and translational regulation [16–18]. In a recent study, hnRNP M, a spliceosome component and splicing regulator [19–23], was identified to be associated with NOTCH2NLC-derived polypeptide (such as polyglycine, polyG for short) and was sequestered in the inclusions [13]. This suggests polyG inclusions induce hnRNP M functional deficiency, contributing to NIID pathogenesis, but its exact role and underlying mechanism remain unclear.

In this study, we utilized a previously established NIID mouse model expressing NOTCH2NLC-98GGC, which faithfully recapitulates the phenotypes observed in NIID patients, to investigate the in vivo function of hnRNP M and assess the protective effects of its expression in the context of NIID. We found that hnRNP M expression not only partially alleviated the neuropathological and behavioral defects associated with NIID, but also partially rescued dysregulated transcription and splicing of synapse- and neurodegeneration-related genes. These findings underscore the critical role of hnRNP M-mediated RNA transcription and splicing in the development of NIID, highlighting the potential of targeting this pathway as a promising therapeutic strategy for the disease.

Results

Expression of HnRNP M alleviates neurodegeneration in the brain of NIID mice

To investigate the in vivo role of hnRNP M in NIID, we constructed AAV vectors expressing either hnRNP M or GFP (control) under the CMV promoter and packaged them into PHP.eB, a serotype known for its ability to cross the blood-brain barrier and efficiently transduce the central nervous system [24] (Fig. 1A). Given the rapid disease progression in NIID mice, we first performed unilateral stereotaxic injections of AAV-hnRNP M (0.5 × 109 genomic copies, GC) into the cortex at postnatal day 15 (P15), with the contralateral side receiving an equal amount of AAV-GFP as an internal control (Fig. 1A, B). Immunofluorescence analysis at P50 revealed the absence of hnRNP M signal on the GFP-injected side, and the absence of GFP signal on the hnRNP M-injected side, confirming no leakage between the two sides (Fig. S1). More importantly, the unilateral expression of exogenous hnRNP M in the contralateral cortex did not significantly alter the total number of polyG inclusions compared to the AAV-GFP-injected side (Fig. 1C-E). Further analysis revealed that the proportion of polyG inclusions localized within the nucleus showed no significant difference between the AAV-GFP and AAV-hnRNP M-injected regions (Fig. 1F). Additionally, we measured the mean diameter of polyG inclusions and found no significant differences (Fig. 1G). However, hnRNP M overexpression markedly reduced GFAP immunoreactivity, as evidenced by a significant decrease in the intensity of GFAP immunofluorescence (Fig. 1H, I).

Since NOTCH2NLC-98GGC is ubiquitously expressed in the brain of the NIID mouse model, we then delivered 1 × 1011 GC AAV-hnRNP M or AAV-GFP via retro-orbital injection (ROI) at P15 (Fig. 2A). We chose ROI as it allows for efficient and widespread delivery of AAV vectors throughout the brain, particularly when combined with the PHP.eB serotype. One month after injection, mouse brain was collected to check the expression and distribution of GFP and exogenous hnRNP M in the whole brain. Immunostaining demonstrated both GFP and hnRNP M were expressed ubiquitously in most brain regions, with a relatively higher level in the cortex and hippocampus (Fig. 2B). Western blot further verified the expression of hnRNP M or GFP in the brain of injected mouse (Fig. 2C). These results demonstrate that exogenous hnRNP M can be efficiently expressed throughout the brain tissue via ROI.

Given that neurodegeneration has previously been observed in NIID mice [13], we conducted the relevant assays. NeuN (a specific marker for mature neurons) and calbindin (a marker for cerebellar Purkinje neurons) were used for immunostaining, and the results showed that both the immunofluorescence intensity of NeuN in the hippocampus and the number of calbindin-positive Purkinje neurons in the cerebellum were significantly increased in AAV-hnRNP M-injected NIID mice compared to AAV-GFP-injected NIID mice (Fig. 2D, E). Additionally, Nissl staining confirmed that the number of survival neurons in the cortex, hippocampus and cerebellum, was significantly higher in AAV-hnRNP M-injected mice than in AAV-GFP-injected controls (Fig. 2F, G). Similarly, the number of survival neurons in the anterior horn of the cervical spinal cord was increased (Fig. 3A, B). Furthermore, hnRNP M expression attenuated gliosis, including over-activated microglia and astrocytes, indicated by reduced GFAP (Fig. 3C, D) and Iba1 staining (Fig. 3E, F). However, gastrocnemius muscle and cardiac muscle from AAV-hnRNP M-treated mice exhibited pathological features similar to those seen in AAV-GFP-treated controls, including fragmented fibers, multiple internalized nuclei, and centralized nuclei (Fig. S2). Taken together, these findings suggest that hnRNP M overexpression primarily ameliorates neurodegeneration in the brain but does not exert substantial protective effects in peripheral tissues.

Expression of HnRNP M partially attenuates motor deficits in NIID mice

Given that exogenous hnRNP M expression ameliorated neurodegeneration in multiple brain regions, we next performed behavioral tests to determine whether the behavioral deficits in NIID mice could also be alleviated. Motor-related behavioral tests were performed three weeks after AAV injection (P40), a time point at which NIID mice had exhibited significant behavioral deficits [13].

In the Open Field Test, AAV-GFP-injected NIID mice exhibited significantly reduced movement duration and distance compared to control mice. In contrast, AAV-hnRNP M-injected NIID mice showed marked improvement to a level that is indistinguishable to the control group (Fig. 4A–E). Furthermore, in the Wire Hanging Test, AAV-hnRNP M-injected NIID mice demonstrated significantly better performance on the inverted wire compared to AAV-GFP-injected NIID mice (Fig. 4F). In the Rotarod Test, although AAV-hnRNP M-injected NIID mice exhibited a trend toward improved performance, no statistically significant difference was observed compared to AAV-GFP-injected NIID mice (Fig. 4G). Meanwhile, we monitored the overall condition of the mice and found that AAV-GFP-injected NIID mice, consistent with previous reports, showed shorter lifespan with mortality at around postnatal day 60 (Fig. 4H). In comparison, the mortality rate of AAV-hnRNP M-injected NIID mice was not notably improved (Fig. 4H). Similarly, terminal-stage body weight loss in AAV-hnRNP M-injected NIID mice showed no significant improvement (Fig. 4I). Together, these findings suggest that hnRNP M expression can partially rescue motor coordination deficits in NIID mice.

Expression of HnRNP M partially reverses dysregulated gene expression

HnRNP M has been reported to be a component of the spliceosome complex and may be involved in RNA transcription and alternative splicing [19–23, 25]. To explore how hnRNP M alleviates neurodegeneration and motor deficits in NIID mice, we performed RNA-seq on the cortex of P50 NIID mice treated with AAV-GFP or AAV-hnRNP M to identify transcriptomic changes associated with hnRNP M expression. We then cross-compared these results with our previously published RNA-seq dataset from P50 NIID and wild-type control mice [13], in which differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and splicing abnormalities were characterized. As both datasets were derived from the same brain region and processed using the same pipeline, batch effects were minimized. This approach allowed us to identify disease-related transcriptomic changes reversed by hnRNP M, potentially contributing to the observed rescue in neuronal survival and motor function. Notably, exogenous hnRNP M expression level was comparable to endogenous hnRNP M, confirming appropriate and physiologically relevant expression of the transgene (Fig. 5A).

We identified 722 downregulated genes and 1119 upregulated genes in the cortex of NIID mice compared to control mice (Fig. 5B, FDR < 0.05, |log2FC|>0.8). Upon exogenous hnRNP M expression, 657 genes were downregulated and 242 genes were upregulated compared to AAV-GFP injected NIID mice (Fig. 5C; Table S1). Notably, among the 722 downregulated genes in NIID mice, 135 genes (18.7%) were significantly upregulated by hnRNP M expression (Fig. 5D). These genes were highly enriched in synaptic signaling pathways (Fig. 5E). On the other hand, 481 out of 1119 (43%) upregulated genes in NIID mice were repressed by hnRNP M expression, which are significantly enriched in defense response and immune response functions (Fig. 5F). To validate transcriptional dysregulation of synapse-related genes, we select Dlg4, a known hnRNP M target gene [25], that encodes post-synaptic density protein 95 (PSD95). In NIID mouse hippocampal tissues, we observed significant decrease in Dlg4 expression compared to the controls (Fig. 5G). Notably, exogenous hnRNP M expression restored Dlg4 levels in NIID mice to levels comparable to the control group (Fig. 5G).

Expression of HnRNP M partially reverses abnormal alternative splicing

In addition to its role in regulating gene expression, hnRNP M has also been reported to regulate alternative splicing [19–23]. We analyzed splicing events in the cortex of NIID mice and identified a significant number of aberrant alternative splicing events (Fig. 6A), including exon retention and exon skipping. Upon hnRNP M expression, 842 out of 1117 (75.38%) significantly retained exons in NIID mice were rescued, with 254 (22.74%) showing statistically significant exclusion (FDR < 0.05, Fig. 6B; Table S2). Conversely, 3482 out of 4058 skipped exons (85.81%) were restored upon hnRNP M expression, including 1235 (30.43%) with significant exon inclusion (Fig. 6B; Table S2). KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that genes with significantly rescued retained exons and skipped exons were predominantly enriched in pathways associated with synaptic function and neurodegeneration (Fig. 6C, D).

To test whether these synaptic function and neurodegeneration related genes indeed exist abnormal alternative splicing, survival of motor neuron (SMN) gene, a known hnRNP M target gene [22], was selected for further validation. Using RT-PCR, we observed a significant increase of Smn exon 7 exclusion in the spinal cord of NIID mice (Fig. 6E, F). Notably, the expression of hnRNP M significantly restored the inclusion of exon 7 in NIID mice (Fig. 6E, F). It is well-established that the exclusion of exon 7 in the Smn gene during alternative splicing results in a truncated, unstable Smn protein [26–28]. Western blotting results supported that Smn protein levels were significantly decreased in the spinal cord of NIID mice. In contrast, expression of hnRNP M significantly increased Smn protein levels, which were comparable to those in the control group (Fig. 6G, H). Collectively, our data suggest that hnRNP M expression could rescue alternative splicing that are dysregulated in NIID mice.

Discussion

Multiple studies have implicated RNA processing dysregulation in NIID pathogenesis [9, 12–15]. It has been reported that NOTCH2NLC-polyG inclusions interact with and sequester hnRNP M, an RNA processing regulator, potentially leading to its functional deficiency [13]. In this study, we demonstrated that AAV-mediated expression of hnRNP M effectively alleviated neuropathological, molecular, and behavioral abnormalities in NIID mice. Neuropathological analysis revealed that hnRNP M expression reduced neuronal loss, reflected by a significant increase in the number of surviving neurons across multiple brain regions. In addition, glial activation was attenuated, as shown by reduced Iba1 and GFAP staining. Behavioral assessments showed improved motor coordination, evidenced by longer movement duration and greater distance traveled in the Open Field Test and Wire Hanging Test, although no significant improvement was observed in the Rotarod Test. Molecular analysis demonstrated that hnRNP M expression partially rescued dysregulated gene expression and mitigated aberrant alternative splicing. Together with previous findings [13], these results support the hypothesis that polyG inclusions sequester endogenous hnRNP M, leading to its functional insufficiency, and that supplementation with exogenous hnRNP M can restore its function.

Our data suggest that the neuroprotective role of hnRNP M likely arises not from modulating polyG aggregate properties (such as quantity, size, or subcellular distribution), but rather from rescuing transcriptional and splicing dysregulation in synapse- and neurodegeneration-related genes. To further explore this possibility, we examined two reported hnRNP M targets, Dlg4 and Smn, selected based on prior evidence demonstrating their direct regulation by hnRNP M [22, 25]. These genes are well documented to be essential for neuronal function [29–32]. In NIID mice, we observed decreased Dlg4 transcript levels and increased exon 7 exclusion in Smn. Importantly, exogenous expression of hnRNP M significantly rescued these abnormalities in NIID mice. Mechanistically, hnRNP M likely contributes to Dlg4 mRNA stabilization through direct binding to its 3′UTR [25], and promotes exon 7 inclusion in Smn pre-mRNA [22]. Although the precise contribution of Dlg4 and Smn to NIID pathogenesis remains unclear, our findings support the notion that hnRNP M deficiency-mediated transcriptional and splicing dysregulation plays an important role in the molecular pathology of NIID.

Our findings align with the broader understanding that dysfunction of RNA binding proteins disturbs RNA processing, including splicing and transcription, which plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis of various neurodegenerative diseases [33, 34]. For instance, in spinal muscular atrophy (SMA), the loss of SMN1 leads to small nuclear ribonucleoproteins (snRNPs) assembly defects and widespread RNA splicing abnormalities, particularly affecting motor neurons due to inefficient exon 7 inclusion in SMN2 transcripts [32]. In myotonic dystrophy type 1 (DM1), expanded CUG repeat RNAs sequester MBNL1, leading to its functional depletion and widespread mis-splicing of target transcripts [33]. In amyotrophic lateral sclerosis/frontotemporal dementia (ALS/FTD), nuclear clearance and cytoplasmic aggregation of TDP-43 impairs the splicing of multiple neuronal mRNAs, including STMN2, which is essential for axon regeneration [35, 36]. In Fragile X-associated tremor/ataxia syndrome (FXTAS), toxic CGG repeat RNAs bind and sequester hnRNP A2/B1, disrupting normal RNA metabolism and contributing to neurodegeneration [37, 38]. These mechanistic insights have already translated into therapeutic advances. For example, in ALS, therapeutic strategies targeting RNA processing have begun to emerge: ASOs that restore STMN2 splicing (disrupted due to TDP-43 loss) have shown efficacy in preclinical models [35, 36]. In SMA, two FDA-approved drugs, Spinraza (nusinersen) and Evrysdi (risdiplam), enhance exon 7 inclusion in the SMN2 transcript, effectively restoring SMN protein levels and improving clinical outcomes [39, 40]. Together with these examples, our study supports the notion that targeting RNA processing abnormalities represents a promising therapeutic strategy for NIID.

However, there are several limitations to our study. While hnRNP M expression demonstrated neuroprotective effects, it only partially rescued neurodegeneration and motor deficits in NIID mice and did not significantly extend survival. Several factors may contribute to these observations. First, our mouse model exhibits rapid disease progression, with aggregates emerging by P12, behavioral deficits emerging by P30, and mortality occurring by P60. AAV-hnRNP M was administered at P15, but required 1–2 weeks for expression, meaning treatment began relatively late. Furthermore, NIID patients involves not only central nervous system but also peripheral system dysfunction [41, 42]. Previous work on the NIID mouse model demonstrated pathological and functional abnormalities in skeletal muscle and cardiac tissue [13, 43]. Therefore, earlier and systemic therapeutic interventions might yield more pronounced therapeutic benefits. As hnRNP M participates in multiple cellular processes, its expression likely requires fine regulation, as both insufficient and excessive levels could be detrimental. Although AAV-driven hnRNP M expression was comparable to endogenous levels in the CNS, its effects in peripheral tissues remain unclear. The potential long-term side effect of exogenous hnRNP M also warrants further study. Additionally, due to limited access to NIID patient tissues, we were unable to directly validate key transcriptomic changes in hnRNP M target genes, such as Dlg4 and Smn. Further studies exploring the roles of these targets may offer deeper mechanistic insights into NIID pathogenesis.

In conclusion, our study highlights the critical role of hnRNP M in the pathogenesis of NIID and supports its potential as a therapeutic target. Further research is warranted to elucidate the full spectrum of hnRNP M’s molecular mechanism and rigorously evaluate the long-term safety and therapeutic potential of hnRNP M-based interventions for NIID.

Materials and methods

Animals

The NIID mouse model, in which NOTCH2NLC with 98 GGC repeats was ubiquitously expressed from the embryonic stage, was established in our previous study [13]. Heterozygous NIID-Tg mice and the littermate control were used in this study. All mice were bred and maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions in the animal facility of Xiangya Hospital, Central South University. Animal experiments were performed in compliance with the Animal Ethics Guidelines of Xiangya Hospital, Central South University and were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee (Approval No. 202103138).

Constructs and viruses

The full-length coding sequence of human hnRNP M (NM_ 005968) was subcloned from an existing construct into an AAV plasmid driven by the CMV promoter. The AAV-GFP construct was generated using the same backbone as the AAV-hnRNP M construct to ensure consistency. All constructs were verified by bidirectional Sanger sequencing to confirm accuracy. The AAV viruses were packaged into the PHP.eB serotype by PackGene Biotech (Guangzhou, China). Viral titers were determined by quantitative PCR (qPCR) and adjusted to 1 × 1012 GC/mL for stereotaxic injection and 1 × 1013 GC/mL for retro-orbital injections.

Antibodies

Primary antibodies used in this study include: Flag (Sigma, F1804), Calbindin (Sigma, C9848), Vinculin (Sigma, V9131), NeuN (Cell signaling technology, 24307s), GFAP (Cell signaling technology, 3670 S), IBA1 (Wako, 019-19741), GFP (Invitrogen, A-11122), hnRNP M (Santa cruz, sc-20002), SMN (Proteintech, 20451-1-AP), HIS (CST, 12698 S). Secondary antibodies were obtained from Jackson ImmunoResearch.

Stereotaxic injection

Stereotaxic injections were performed as previously described (Liu et al. 2020). Briefly, the mice were anesthetized with 1% isoflurane inhalation and stabilized on a stereotaxic instrument. Meloxicam at 5 mg/kg was given before surgery as an analgesic. The injection site of cortex was determined according to the distance from bregma: anterior-posterior = 0.5 mm, medial-lateral = ± 1.0 mm, dorsal-ventral = − 1.0 mm. Small holes were drilled in the skull, and a 30-gauge Hamilton microsyringe connected to a syringe infusion pump was used to deliver the viruses at a speed of 100 nl per minute. Adeno-associated viruses were injected bilaterally into the cortex as indicated. The microsyringe was left in the injection site for 10 min before it was withdrawn slowly. The mice were placed on a heated blanket to recover from the anesthetic after surgery.

Retro-orbital injection

Retro-orbital injection was conducted following a previously established protocol [44]. Briefly, mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal administration of 1.25% avertin (0.2 mL/10 g body weight), and a drop of ophthalmic anesthetic was applied to the target eye. Gentle pressure was applied to the skin dorsal and ventral to the eye to partially protrude the eyeball, avoiding excessive pressure on cervical vessels or the trachea. Using a syringe with a 30-gauge needle, 100 µL of AAV solution (diluted in PBS) was slowly injected at a 30–45° angle into the retro-orbital sinus. The syringe was held for 5–10 s before slow withdrawal. Post-injection, mice recovered on a heated blanket.

Mouse behavioral tests

Mouse body weight was measured twice per week starting after weaning, and survival was monitored regularly. There was no gender difference in the survival time. Behavioral tests were performed as previously described [13], with at least eight male mice included per group. Briefly, mouse movement in the Open Field Test was recorded for 10 min using an automated system, and total duration, distance, speed, and activity in the central squares were analyzed. For the Wire Hang Test, mice were placed on an inverted wire cage lid, and the latency to fall was recorded over 3 min, with the longest hang time from three trials used for analysis. In the Rotarod Test, mice were trained for 3 min per day for three consecutive days at increasing speeds (5 rpm, 10 rpm, 15 rpm) and tested at 20 rpm on the fourth day, with the latency to fall recorded.

Immunofluorescence staining

Mice were anesthetized and perfused intracardially with 0.9% saline solution, followed by 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer at pH 7.2. Isolated mouse brains were dehydrated in 30% sucrose at 4 °C, and sectioned at 30 μm for subsequent immunofluorescence study: brain sections were blocked in 3% BSA in 0.3% Triton X-100/PBS for 60 min, followed by incubation with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After incubation with the fluorescent secondary antibodies and DAPI, the brain sections were mounted to coated glass slides and examined using a ZEISS Apotom3 microscope.

Histological examination

Paraffin-embedded brain sections were dewaxed in xylene, rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and rinsed with ultrapure water. For Nissl staining, sections were incubated in cresyl violet solution for 15 min, differentiated with Nissl differentiation solution, dehydrated in ethanol, cleared in xylene (5 min, twice), and mounted with neutral balsam. For hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, sections were stained with hematoxylin for 5 min, rinsed in water, differentiated in 1% hydrochloric acid ethanol for 30 s, rinsed again, stained with 0.5% eosin for 1–3 min, dehydrated, cleared in xylene, and mounted. Images were acquired using a ZEISS Apotome3 microscope.

Spinal cord segmentation and neuron counting

To evaluate neuron survival in the spinal cord, tissues were carefully dissected under a stereomicroscope using fine instruments, and specific segments were identified based on vertebral landmarks. Spinal cords were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned transversely at 5 μm thickness. Every 20th section (~ 100 μm apart) was collected for analysis. For each segment, five evenly spaced sections per animal were selected for Nissl staining. Large, polygonal neurons with prominent nucleoli in the ventral horn were manually counted. The average of the five sections was used as the representative value for each segment per mouse.

Western blot

Methods for Western blot were described previously [13]. Brain tissues were lysed in ice-cold RIPA buffer with freshly-added protease inhibitor cocktail. Samples were sonicated and heated at 100 °C for 10 min to denature the protein. Protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE electrophoresis and were then transferred to a PVDF membrane (Millipore). After blocking, blots were probed with the appropriate antibodies overnight at 4℃. The membrane was then incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies in 5% milk/1X PBS for 1 h at room temperature and then developed with ECL Prime (GE Healthcare).

Reverse transcriptase PCR and quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from mice brain tissue using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from total RNA (1 µg) using a RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, K1622) and oligo dT primers. Quantitative real-time PCR was analyzed with the comparative cycle threshold method. Primers used for quantitative real-time PCR were listed as follows: mouse Dlg4_F: TGA GAT CAG TCA TAG CAG CTA CT and mouse Dlg4_R: CTT CCT CCC CTA GCA GGT CC; GAPDH_F: CAT GGC CTC CAA GGA GTA AGA AAC and GAPDH_R: TGT GAG GGA GAT GCT CAG TG. The primers used for detecting skipping of exon 7 of mouse Smn gene are listed as follows: mouse_Smn_F: CTT CCT GCC GTG CTG GAT and mouse_Smn_R: CAG AGC TGA ACT TCT TAA TTT G.

RNA sequencing and analysis

Tissues from P50 mice were used for RNA extraction. An amount of 1 ug of total RNA per sample was processed for library preparation using NEBNext UltraTM RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, USA) following manufacturer’s recommendations. Libraries were sequenced on a HiSeq with a read length configuration of 150 PE, targeting 100 M total reads per sample. All total reads were mapped to hg38 for human samples or mm9 for mouse samples by TopHat2 with default parameters followed by gene expression quantification and differential expression analysis using Cuffdiff. Genes with adjusted P-value < 0.05 and |log2(FoldChange)| >0.8 were assigned as significantly differentially expressed. KEGG pathways and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed by DAVID or PANTHER online. The alternative splicing events were reported by rMATS. Splicing events with FDR < 0.05 were considered significant alternative splicing events. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using R. Heatmaps were generated using the R package “pheatmap”.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.3.1). To eliminate bias, images or animal analyses were either completely automated or blinded. Statistical analyses were performed using one-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test for comparisons involving three or more groups. For comparisons between two groups, a two-tailed Student’s t-test was employed. All quantification data were presented as mean ± SEM. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
