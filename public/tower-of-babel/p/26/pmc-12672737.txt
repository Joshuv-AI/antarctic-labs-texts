Phytochemicals in overcoming venetoclax resistance in acute myeloid leukemia: mechanistic insights and therapeutic potential
Authors: Suresh Prajapati, Mansi Patel, Charmi Jyotishi, Reeshu Gupta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis review critically examines the complex mechanisms underlying venetoclax resistance in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and explores the therapeutic potential of phytochemicals in overcoming this resistance. Emerging evidence suggests that resistance to venetoclax arises through multiple pathways, including the overexpression of anti-apoptotic proteins, such as MCL1 and BCL2L1, mitochondrial adaptations, metabolic rewiring, and genetic mutations in key regulators, such as TP53 and KMT2A. Phytochemicals, including curcumin, quercetin, triptolide, and parthenolide, have demonstrated anti-leukemic and chemosensitizing activities by modulating BCL2 family proteins and associated apoptotic pathways. Furthermore, in silico docking analyses indicated that several terpenoids possess strong binding affinities to BCL2 protein, in some cases outperforming venetoclax, highlighting their potential as alternative or complementary agents. Preclinical studies underscore the ability of phytochemicals to synergize with venetoclax, enhancing apoptosis and reducing leukemic burden while exhibiting minimal toxicity to normal hematopoietic cells. These bioactive compounds exert pleiotropic effects by modulating redox homeostasis, microRNA expression, epigenetic remodelling, and mitochondrial function, offering a multi-targeted approach to overcoming drug resistance. Despite their promise, translational challenges persist, notably poor solubility, limited bioavailability, and insufficient clinical validation of these compounds. Future research should prioritize the development of advanced delivery systems, such as nanoparticle formulations, and initiate well-designed clinical trials to evaluate the safety, pharmacokinetics, and efficacy of phytochemical–venetoclax combinations in AML. Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a biologically heterogeneous hematologic malignancy with treatment strategies influenced by disease subtype, patient performance status (e.g., ECOG ≤ 2), and resource availability. Standard induction therapy involves the “7 + 3” regimen with cytarabine and anthracycline. Post-remission therapy, typically with high-dose cytarabine (HiDAC), aims to eliminate residual leukemic cells and prevent their relapse. In relapsed or refractory cases, re-induction with cytarabine is common, although outcomes are often poor. Emerging therapies for AML aim to overcome the limitations of conventional chemotherapy, especially in relapsed or refractory cases, by targeting specific molecular abnormalities. Agents such as FLT3, IDH1/2, and BCL2 inhibitors have demonstrated promising results, offering personalized and effective treatment options.

Venetoclax (ABT-199) is a potent and selective BCL2 inhibitor that functions as a BH3 mimetic and promotes apoptosis in AML cells by antagonizing the anti-apoptotic protein BCL2. AML blasts and leukemic stem cells heavily rely on BCL2 for survival, whereas normal hematopoietic stem cells are more dependent on MCL1, providing a therapeutic window for venetoclax. Mechanistically, BCL2 inhibits apoptosis by binding to pro-apoptotic proteins, such as BAX and BAK1, thereby preventing mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization, a key step in apoptotic signaling. This process leads to cytochrome c release, caspase activation, and subsequent apoptosis [1]. Acquired mutations in Bax during venetoclax-based therapy represent a novel mechanism of resistance to BH3-mimetics and a potential barrier to the long-term efficacy of drugs targeting BCL2 in AML [2] (Fig. 1). Unlike earlier BH3 mimetics such as navitoclax (ABT-263), venetoclax does not induce significant thrombocytopenia, making it more favorable for clinical use [3].

Mechanism of action of venetoclax in leukemic cells. In venetoclax-sensitive cells, BCL2 inhibition by venetoclax leads to the release of pro-apoptotic proteins BAX and BIM, triggering mitochondrial cytochrome c (Cyt C) release, caspase activation, and apoptosis. In contrast, venetoclax-resistant cells are characterized by the upregulation of MCL1, which sequesters BAX and BIM, preventing Cyt C release and subsequent caspase activation, thereby inhibiting apoptosis and promoting cell survival

In clinical trials, venetoclax-based regimens have shown encouraging results, particularly in elderly or unfit patients when combined with hypomethylating agents or low-dose cytarabine therapy. The FDA granted accelerated approval in 2018 for such combinations in patients aged ≥ 75 years or those who were ineligible for intensive chemotherapy. Notably, most AML patients frequently experience relapse and develop chemotherapy resistance, leading to a poor prognosis. Despite initial responses with an overall response rate of 19% and median response duration of 11.3 months, resistance remains a major concern. Notably, approximately 27% of patients show primary resistance, and more than half relapse after the initial response [4]. These results further support the presence of both intrinsic and acquired resistance to venetoclax therapy. These therapeutic limitations have intensified the search for novel adjunctive agents, including naturally occurring compounds.

A wide array of phytochemicals, including saponins, alkaloids, triterpenes, phenolics, coumarins, polysaccharides, and anthraquinones, have demonstrated anti-leukemic properties. Their efficacy arises not only from their direct cytotoxic effects but also from their ability to modulate immune responses and alter apoptotic signaling. Recent studies have highlighted the potential of these bioactive compounds as chemosensitizers that may enhance the effectiveness of antitumor drugs [5]. Despite the growing interest, a comprehensive analysis of phytochemicals and their hybrid analogs in the context of venetoclax resistance in AML is lacking. This review aims to fill this gap by critically evaluating the mechanistic basis of venetoclax resistance and exploring the therapeutic potential of phytochemicals to overcome it.

Mechanisms of venetoclax resistance in AML

Substantial research has been conducted to determine the molecular mechanisms underpinning venetoclax resistance, and a variety of mechanisms have been identified (Fig. 2).

Mechanisms Underlying Resistance to Venetoclax. This schematic illustrates the multifaceted mechanisms contributing to venetoclax resistance in cancer cells. These mechanisms are broadly categorized into three domains: Mitochondrial and Metabolic Adaptations, which include altered bioenergetics, oxidative phosphorylation, disrupted mitochondrial translation, and integrated stress responses; Genetic and Epigenetic Mechanisms, such as gene mutations, chromatin remodelling, and microRNA regulation; and BCL-2 Family and Apoptotic Resistance, involving BH3 domain competition and overexpression of anti-apoptotic proteins. Additionally, resistance modulation using natural or repurposed compounds is a potential therapeutic strategy

Elevated expression of anti-apoptotic proteins

The primary mechanism of venetoclax involves mimicking the BH3 domain to inhibit BCL2, thereby promoting outer mitochondrial membrane permeabilization and apoptosis. The BCL2 family proteins regulates apoptosis through shared BCL2 homology (BH) domains (BH1–BH4). Anti-apoptotic members (e.g., BCL2, Bcl2L1, and MCL1) prevent cell death by binding to the BH3 domains of pro-apoptotic proteins. The pro-apoptotic group includes sensitizers (e.g., BAD and PMAIP1), activators (e.g., BCL2L11, BID, and BBC3), and the effector proteins Bax and BAK. Although sensitizers cannot directly activate Bax or Bak, they displace activator proteins or effectors from anti-apoptotic proteins, facilitating apoptosis. Once activated, BAX and BAK1 oligomerize, inducing mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization and triggering intrinsic apoptosis via cytochrome c release [6].

Dysregulation and imbalance of BCL2 family members are commonly found in AML, where upregulation of alternative anti-apoptotic proteins, such as the MCL1, BCL2L1, BCL2L2, FOXM1-BCL2A1 axis [7] or BCL2A1, reduces venetoclax efficacy. Targeting these proteins enhances venetoclax efficacy in AML. While numerous reviews have extensively discussed the significance of these proteins in enhancing venetoclax efficacy [4, 8–12], this review will highlight recent findings, with particular emphasis on BAX, MCL1, and BCL2L1, as these proteins have featured prominently in recent studies on venetoclax resistance. For instance, several natural compounds and phytochemicals have shown promise in overcoming venetoclax resistance by modulating these anti-apoptotic targets. For instance, Vitamin K2 [13], Ciclosporin A in FLT-ITD AML [14], Dihydroartemisinin [15] (Semi-synthetic derivative of artemisinin, which is extracted from the plant), 20(S)-protopanaxadiol (saponins derived from Panax ginseng and are the metabolite derived from ginsenosides through acid hydrolysis and gut bacterial de-glycosylation) [16] have demonstrated the ability to inhibit MCL1 or BCL2L1, thereby enhancing the apoptotic response to venetoclax.. Consistent with these findings, molecular docking studies have identified additional phytochemical candidates, such as Gibberellin 1, Zexbrevin B, Ginkgolide A, Phorbol, and 7β,12α-Dihydroxykaurenolide, which bind to key residues of BCL2 [17]. Experimental data suggest that several phytochemicals can modulate BCL2 family proteins and promote apoptosis. For example, piperlongumine induces both apoptotic and autophagic death in primary myeloid leukemia cells, increasing BAX, caspase-3, Beclin-1, and LC3B expression while activating p38 and MAPK8 pathways [18]. Similarly, the flavonoid kaempferol (3,4′,5,7-tetrahydroxyflavone) induces apoptosis in K562 and U937 leukemia cells by decreasing BCL2, increasing BAX, releasing cytochrome c, and activating caspases-3 and −9, while also enhancing SIRT3 expression and inhibiting PIK3CA/AKT1 signaling [19]. Another flavonoid, morin, originally isolated from members of the Moraceae family, has demonstrated dose-dependent cytotoxicity in leukemic cells, particularly U937 cells, where it induced caspase-dependent apoptosis through mitochondrial dysfunction, cytochrome c release, and upregulation of BAX and BAD with concomitant BCL2 downregulation [20]. Friedelin, a triterpenoid, also exhibited potent activity against AML-196 cells, selectively inhibiting their proliferation with minimal effects on normal cells. Mechanistic studies revealed that friedelin triggers apoptosis by upregulating cleaved caspases-3, −8, and −9, along with PARP1 cleavage, while increasing BAX and reducing BCL2 levels. Moreover, Friedelin suppressed migration and invasion in Transwell assays and blocked MEK/ERK and PIK3CA/AKT1 signaling cascades in a dose-dependent manner, further supporting its therapeutic potential [21]. Collectively, these findings highlight that phytochemicals can effectively target BCL2 family–mediated resistance mechanisms, offering promising adjunctive strategies to restore venetoclax sensitivity in AML. However, a major limitation of phytochemicals is their poor pharmacological profile, characterized by low aqueous solubility, rapid metabolism, and poor oral bioavailability, which restricts their systemic exposure and therapeutic utility. To address these challenges, several formulation strategies have been investigated, including biopolymer nanoparticles, emulsions, liposomes, solid lipid nanoparticles, and nanostructured lipid carriers, which can be assembled from food-grade carbohydrates, proteins, and/or lipids [22]. These delivery systems have been shown to improve solubility, enhance stability, and increase bioavailability, thereby supporting more effective translation of phytochemical–venetoclax combinations into clinical settings.

Recent advances have also highlighted the role of epigenetic regulation in venetoclax resistance. In particular, pharmacological inhibition of the methyltransferase METTL3 using STM2457 promotes the ubiquitination and degradation of MCL1 and MYC, two key resistance factors [23]. Moreover, this study identified isoliquiritigenin as a potential novel small-molecule natural product targeting METTL3, which exhibited potential anti-leukemic effects [23]. Another promising approach involves targeting STAT3 activity. Treatment with KT-333, a clinical-stage STAT3 degrader, led to a marked reduction in both STAT3 and MCL1 protein levels within two weeks in a cell line–derived xenograft model of venetoclax-resistant AML. This treatment also significantly improved survival in a patient-derived xenograft model. The observed mitochondrial functional restoration alongside MCL1 downregulation underscores a novel therapeutic mechanism for overcoming venetoclax resistance [24]. These emerging strategies suggest that the integration of small-molecule inhibitors, natural products, and epigenetic or transcriptional regulators holds significant promise for restoring therapeutic sensitivity.

Genomic biomarkers associated with venetoclax sensitivity

Resistance to venetoclax in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) arises from diverse genetic alterations that disrupt apoptotic signaling, mitochondrial function, epigenetic control, and clonal dynamics. Among the key mechanisms, mutations in the BCL2 gene have been reported to diminish drug-binding affinity, especially in chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), with emerging implications in AML. Specific variants such as BCL2 p.Gly101Val, Asp103Tyr/Glu/Val, Ala113Gly, Arg107_Arg110dup, Arg129Leu, Val156Asp, and Phe104Ile have been associated with venetoclax resistance [25]. Of these, the Asp103Tyr mutation has been directly linked to therapy failure in certain clinical cases [26]. Additionally, genetic deletions, such as del(13q14), cause the loss of miR-15 and miR-16. The loss of these miRNAs suppresses BCL2 expression and may influence the treatment outcomes. Beyond BCL2, resistance has been associated with alterations in multiple other genes, such as mutations in TP53 [27], DEAD-box helicase 41 (DDX41)[28], RAS-pathway [29, 30], JAK1, JAK3, STAT5B [31], DNMT3A, GATA2 [32], KIT [33], NF1, PTPN11 [34], FGFR, PDGFRB, and BAX [25]. Additionally, KMT2A abnormalities, such as rearrangements, amplifications, or partial tandem duplications, are present in approximately 15% of AML cases. KMT2A encodes a histone methyltransferase that is vital for gene expression during development and hematopoiesis. In KMT2A-rearranged AML, its association with menin promotes the persistent activation of oncogenic genes, such as HOXA/B clusters and MEIS1 [35]. Prognostic models incorporating mutations in N/KRAS, FLT3-ITD, BCOR, EZH2, and STAG2 [8] indicate that signaling mutations often confer at least an intermediate risk. A recent study stratified patients into prognostic groups of various risks based on mutations in TP53, FLT3-ITD, NRAS, and KRAS, correlating with varying median overall survival [36]. In addition to KMT2Ar, other gene rearrangements involving MECOM (TBL1XR1-MECOM and ETV6_MECOM), BCL11B (BCL11B-GSDMC), CCND2-ETV6 BIRC6-LTBP1, NUP98-NSD1, and CBFB-MYH11 were identified in relapsed cases, suggesting oncogenic roles in resistance [37].

Certain genetic alterations are associated with increased sensitivity to venetoclax. Mutations in IDH1 and IDH2 (including IDH2R140 and IDH2R172) lead to the accumulation of the oncometabolite 2-hydroxyglutarate (2-HG), which inhibits mitochondrial respiration and lowers the apoptotic threshold by increasing cellular reliance on BCL2 [8, 38]. Similarly, mutations in the atypical cadherin gene FAT1 have shown enhanced responses to venetoclax-based induction therapy, as these cells exhibit increased oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid oxidation, making them more vulnerable to mitochondrial-targeting agents such as venetoclax [39]. Spliceosome gene mutations, such as SRSF2 [40], U2AF1, SF3B1 [34], and ZRSR2, also influence the splicing and expression of genes involved in apoptosis, shifting the balance toward Bcl-2 dependence. ASXL1 mutations promote BCL2 overexpression by increasing chromatin accessibility at its promoter through impaired PRC2-mediated repression, thereby enhancing venetoclax and azacitidine sensitivity. Additionally, NPM1 mutations, RAD21, and IDH1/IDH2 have been correlated with improved responses to venetoclax, although the mechanisms remain unclear [41, 42].

Gene fusions also contribute to the venetoclax response. The PICALM::MLLT10 fusion, resulting from the t(10;11)(p12.3;q14.2) translocation, has been associated with favourable outcomes following venetoclax treatment [43]. Conversely, the NUP98-NSD1 fusion, seen in 16.1% of pediatric and 2.3% of adult AML patients with a normal karyotype, is linked to a poor chemotherapy response and frequently co-occurs with FLT3-ITD, further worsening the prognosis. However, the addition of selinexor to a regimen of venetoclax and azacitidine has led to molecular remission in some NUP98-NSD1-positive cases.[44]. Other fusions, such as GATAD2B::MTCP1, which increases MTCP1 expression, have been implicated in disease progression and relapse [45].

These molecular insights have also guided interest in phytochemicals and natural products as potential therapeutic agents in AML. Petromurin C, a metabolite isolated from the marine-derived fungus Aspergillus candidus associated with the sponge Epipolasis sp., induced autophagy followed by apoptosis in FLT3-ITD–mutated MV4-11 cells. This effect was linked to mitochondrial stress and the downregulation of MCL1. Importantly, Petromurin C enhances the efficacy of gilteritinib at sub-toxic concentrations [46]. The plant-derived Silvestrol displayed nanomolar activity in FLT3-wildtype and FLT3-ITD AML cell lines (IC50 values 3.8 and 2.7 nM, respectively) and in patient-derived blasts. Silvestrol promoted apoptosis, abolished colony formation, suppressed FLT3 protein synthesis by nearly 90%, and reduced miR-155 expression, leading to a significant survival benefit in xenograft models [47].

Traditional Chinese medicine has also provided promising leads in this regard. Indirubin and its derivatives, indirubin-3′-oxime (IO) and 6-bromoindirubin-3′-oxime (BIO), demonstrated potent inhibition of FLT3 with IC50 values of 79 nM and 254 nM, respectively. IO strongly inhibited MV4;11 cells harboring FLT3-ITD (IC50 = 30 nM), inducing G1 arrest and apoptosis, whereas wild-type FLT3 cells were comparatively resistant [48]. In addition to FLT3 targeting, the metabolic vulnerabilities of AML are being explored. The green tea polyphenol epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG) inhibits glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH), thereby affecting glutamine-driven energy metabolism and AMPK signaling. By blocking GDH activity, EGCG alters insulin secretion in pancreatic islets and reverses GDH-mediated AMPK suppression in muscle cells, findings that may be relevant for the metabolic adaptation of leukemic cells [49].

Taken together, evidence from marine-derived and plant-based compounds highlights the ability of phytochemicals to interfere with key resistance-associated pathways, including FLT3 activation, mitochondrial apoptosis, and metabolic reprogramming.

In contrast, several synthetic inhibitors targeting the same vulnerabilities have already been tested in preclinical and clinical settings in combination with venetoclax. Phytochemicals that target the same molecular pathways may serve as complementary or alternative candidates; however, further studies are needed to evaluate their synergistic effects with venetoclax.

Mitochondrial mechanisms of venetoclax resistance

Mitochondria are pivotal in regulating cellular respiration and apoptosis, making them crucial determinants of the response to venetoclax (VEN) in acute myeloid leukemia (AML). Using CRISPR/Cas9 knockout screens in BCL2-dependent AML cells treated with venetoclax, several metabolic pathways that cooperate with BCL2 to inhibit apoptosis have been identified. Among these, heme biosynthesis is a significant regulator; its inhibition impairs electron transport chain function and mitochondrial membrane stability, thereby enhancing apoptotic susceptibility. This suggests that targeting heme biosynthesis may offer a novel therapeutic approach in AML [50]. Complementing this, recent studies have shown that inhibition of mitochondrial translation using the antibiotic tedizolid disrupts mitochondrial bioenergetics and activates the integrated stress response (ISR), sensitizing drug-resistant AML cells to venetoclax. The ISR likely serves as a link between metabolic disturbances and apoptotic signaling pathways [51]. In comparison to arsenic trioxide-resistant cells, the distinct metabolic adaptation of cytarabine-resistant cells increased sensitivity to venetoclax, suggesting that different therapies can cause different metabolic changes and that these metabolic dependencies can be used to target chemotherapy-resistant AML [52].

Further insights involve the PIP4K2 family (PIP4K2A, PIP4K2B, and PIP4K2C), which are enzymes responsible for synthesizing PIP4,5P2, a critical molecule with roles in signaling, metabolism, and membrane structure. Elevated expression of PIP4K2A and PIP4K2C correlates with venetoclax resistance in AML, whereas inhibition by the pan-PIP4K2 inhibitor THZ-P1-2 disrupts mitochondrial function and enhances venetoclax-induced apoptosis in AML cells. This combination also modulates the expression of key apoptotic genes, underscoring its potential as a therapeutic strategy [53]. Moreover, chemoresistant AML cells, such as MOLM-14, rely on eukaryotic initiation factor 4 A (ELF4A1), a downstream effector of mTORC1 signaling, which facilitates cap-dependent translation, crucial for cell survival both in vitro and in vivo. eIF4A-driven translation supports metabolic programs that maintain energy homeostasis and redox balance while promoting the production of anti-apoptotic proteins, highlighting its role in adaptive therapy resistance [54]. Lastly, the mitochondrial chaperone gene CLPB, known to interact with the structural protein OPA1 to maintain mitochondrial cristae integrity, is upregulated in AML and further increased upon venetoclax resistance. Notably, CLPB depletion re-sensitizes AML cells to venetoclax, reinforcing its potential as a therapeutic target [55]. These studies suggest that targeting mitochondrial metabolism and translation pathways offers promising strategies for overcoming venetoclax resistance in acute myeloid leukemia.

Phytochemicals as direct BCL2 family inhibitors

There is an urgent need to discover and develop new therapeutic agents for AML that provide improved efficacy, reduced toxicity, and compatibility with existing treatments to enhance the patient outcomes. Phytochemicals have been shown to influence AML therapy through combination approaches via multiple mechanisms. These include modifying chromatin architecture by regulating DNMTs and histone deacetylases (HDACs), altering the expression and function of specific DNA repair proteins, and triggering apoptosis by modulating key signaling pathways [56]. Consequently, there is growing interest in exploring phytochemicals as alternative or adjunctive BCL-2 inhibitors because of their structural diversity, multi-targeted actions, and generally favorable safety profiles.

Among the various classes of phytochemicals, terpenoids are a rich source of bioactive molecules with potential anticancer properties. In silico screening of a large terpenoid library—including carotenoids, diterpenoids, and triterpenoids—identified several compounds exhibiting strong binding affinity to the BCL2 protein. Docking studies revealed that the top candidates, such as Ginkgolide A, Gibberellin 1, Zexbrevin B, Phorbol, and 7beta,12alpha-Dihydroxykaurenolide, bound effectively to the BCL2 active site, with binding energies comparable to or superior to those of the FDA-approved BCL2 inhibitor venetoclax. These compounds demonstrated key interactions with crucial BCL2 residues, including Asp103, Arg107, Tyr108, Gly145, and Phe198, which are essential for ligand binding and anti-apoptotic function. Hydrogen bonding with polar residues, such as Asp103 and Gly145, along with hydrophobic van der Waals contacts with aromatic residues, such as Phe104 and Trp144, contribute to stable and specific binding. For example, gibberellin 1 formed hydrogen bonds with Arg107 and Asp103, while 7beta,12alpha-Dihydroxykaurenolide interacts via hydrogen bonds with Tyr202 and Asp103, highlighting the importance of these residues in effective BCL2 inhibition.

The molecular interactions identified through docking studies suggest that these phytochemicals can competitively inhibit BCL2 by occupying its BH3-binding groove, disrupting its ability to sequester pro-apoptotic proteins such as BCL2L11 and BAX [17]. This displacement may tip the balance towards apoptosis, promoting programmed cell death in AML cells. Moreover, the structural diversity of terpenoids offers the advantage of multiple binding modes and affinities, potentially overcoming the limitations of single-target small molecules, such as venetoclax. This diversity may also facilitate combination therapies, wherein phytochemicals act synergistically with existing drugs to enhance efficacy and reduce resistance.

Curcumin, a polyphenolic phytochemical derived from Curcuma longa, exhibits potent anticancer activity against acute myeloid leukemia (AML) cells, outperforming epigallocatechin gallate, genistein, and resveratrol. It exerts anti-leukemic effects in AML by modulating apoptosis-related proteins, including the downregulation of the anti-apoptotic protein BCL2. By inactivating the AKT signaling pathway, curcumin decreases BCL2 expression, promoting apoptosis in AML cells. This effect was enhanced by AKT1 inhibition and reversed by AKT1 activation, highlighting the role of AKT-mediated regulation of BCL2 in the mechanism of action of curcumin. These findings suggest that curcumin may act as an indirect BCL2 inhibitor, contributing to its cytotoxic activity in AML [57]. Similarly, gallic acid, chlorogenic acid, and other polyphenols downregulate BCL2 expression by inhibiting the WT1 transcription factor, which transcriptionally activates BCL2 [58]. Morin, a flavonol from the Moraceae family, induces caspase-dependent apoptosis in leukemic cells via the intrinsic mitochondrial pathway. It downregulates BCL2 and upregulates pro-apoptotic proteins BAX and BAD, causing mitochondrial membrane potential loss and cytochrome c release. Notably, overexpression of BCL2 attenuates morin-induced apoptosis, underscoring BCL2’s critical role in its mechanism [20]. Furthermore, Sophoraflavanone G (SG), a flavonoid (polyphenolic compound) derived from Sophora flavescens, exerts potent pro-apoptotic effects in HL-60 cells. SG induces caspase-dependent apoptosis, downregulates BCL2 and BCL2L1, upregulates BAX, and activates the mitochondrial intrinsic apoptotic pathway, along with MAPK signaling [59].

In addition, a recent study investigating resveratrol, gallic acid, and piperine (an amide alkaloid found in black pepper) demonstrated their anticancer effects on the HL-60 leukemic cell line, highlighting the modulation of microRNAs (miRs), such as miR-17, miR-92b, miR-181a, and miR-222, which play roles in apoptosis regulation. Treatment with resveratrol and gallic acid significantly increased the expression of pro-apoptotic miRs and the pro-apoptotic protein Bax, while influencing BCL2 expression through these miR pathways. Therefore, these phytochemicals exert their effects, in part, by modulating miR-mediated regulation of BCL2 and apoptosis-related genes, further supporting their potential as indirect BCL2 inhibitors [60]. Additionally, hispolon, a phenolic compound derived from the medicinal mushroom Phellinus igniarius, induced apoptosis in AML cells through caspase-8, −9, and −3 activation, PARP cleavage, and sustained MAPK8/9 activation, which is crucial for its apoptotic effects. In vivo, hispolon significantly inhibited tumor growth in AML xenografts [61]. Collectively, these polyphenolic and alkaloid phytochemicals exhibit significant potential as BCL2 inhibitors, promoting apoptosis and offering promising avenues for the treatment of AML. However, these phytochemicals have inconsistent in vivo efficacy and short half-lives, limiting their application.

Despite this promising evidence, only a limited number of studies have specifically investigated phytochemicals as BCL2 inhibitors in AML, indicating a significant gap and opportunity for further research. However, several phytochemicals have been shown to downregulate BCL2 expression or directly inhibit its function, thereby promoting apoptosis in various cancers beyond AML. Phytochemicals as multi-targeted sensitizers to venetoclax

Although BCL2 inhibition via venetoclax has shown promising pro-apoptotic effects in AML, its clinical efficacy as a stand-alone therapy remains limited. Preclinical studies have explored natural compounds that modulate BCL2 family proteins to sensitize leukemic cells to venetoclax and overcome resistance to it. However, only a few studies have been conducted, and the therapeutic potential of phytochemical–venetoclax combinations remains largely unexplored.

Triptolide (TPL), a diterpenoid triepoxide derived from Tripterygium wilfordii, exerts potent antitumor activity by modulating the BCL2 family. In AML models, TPL has been shown to downregulate MCL1 while upregulating pro-apoptotic BH3-only proteins, such as BBC3, PMAIP1, BID, and BCL2L11. When combined with the BCL2 inhibitor ABT-199 (venetoclax), TPL synergistically induced apoptosis in AML cells, irrespective of p53 status. This synergy is mediated by the enhanced activation of the intrinsic mitochondrial apoptotic pathway. In vivo, this combination not only inhibited leukemia progression more effectively than monotherapies but also significantly extended survival. Mechanistically, while TPL alone can lead to BCL2 upregulation (a resistance mechanism), co-administration with ABT-199 counteracts this, resulting in the dual suppression of both BCL2 and MCL1 and a further increase in pro-apoptotic signals, thereby achieving synthetic lethality [62]. A parallel study further validated the combinatorial efficacy of TPL and ABT-199 in AML, demonstrating selective cytotoxicity toward leukemic cells while sparing normal hematopoietic cells [63]. This selective toxicity enhances the translational potential of this regimen for clinical application. However, its high toxicity and poor solubility limit its clinical use. Various nanoformulations are being investigated.

Parthenolide (PTL), a sesquiterpene lactone derived from Tanacetum parthenium, is another phytochemical of interest. PTL has shown potent anti-leukemic activity, particularly against leukemia stem cell (LSC) populations in childhood B-cell precursor ALL. PTL exerts its effects by inhibiting NF-κB and inducing oxidative stress and apoptosis. When combined with the BCL2 inhibitors ABT-263 or ABT-199, PTL demonstrated synergistic cytotoxicity, significantly reducing the viability of both bulk leukemic cells and resistant subpopulations. Notably, normal cord blood cells were largely unaffected by this combination. In vivo experiments in NSG mice bearing ALL xenografts showed that the PTL-ABT-263/199 combination substantially reduced leukemia burden and improved survival outcomes compared to monotherapies or vehicle controls [64]. However, its poor bioavailability and rapid metabolism hamper systemic delivery.

Quercetin, a naturally occurring flavonoid found in many fruits and vegetables, has emerged as a potential venetoclax sensitizer. In AML cell lines (KG-1 and Kasumi-1), the combination of quercetin and venetoclax significantly enhanced apoptotic cell death, as evidenced by increased PARP1 cleavage and Bax expression. Notably, these effects were not observed in CML cells, underscoring the AML-specific action of this combination therapy. Quercetin was shown to increase BAX levels, suggesting its role in promoting mitochondrial-mediated apoptosis. Importantly, AML cells demonstrate higher BCL2 dependency, providing a rationale for targeting this axis using combination approaches [3]. However, its clinical translation is limited by non-specific effects across cancer types and modest potency. These findings underscore the potential of phytochemicals to enhance venetoclax efficacy by targeting complementary anti-apoptotic pathways and overcoming resistance mechanisms. Through the reciprocal modulation of BCL2 family proteins, these natural compounds synergized with venetoclax to induce robust apoptosis in AML and ALL models, with minimal toxicity to normal cells. These promising preclinical results provide a compelling foundation for future clinical investigations into phytochemical–venetoclax combination regimens to improve therapeutic outcomes and overcome drug resistance in hematologic malignancies (Fig. 3).

Clinical translation and developmental status

Despite promising in vitro and in vivo data, the clinical translation of phytochemicals remains limited owing to challenges in bioavailability, pharmacokinetics, and standardization. Nanoformulations and prodrug strategies may enhance delivery and efficacy in cancer [65–69]. Well-designed clinical trials are essential to evaluate the safety and therapeutic value of phytochemical-venetoclax combinations.

Although numerous phytochemicals have demonstrated promising anti-leukemic activity in preclinical models, their clinical translation, particularly in the context of venetoclax resistance in AML, remains limited. Most compounds are still in the experimental or formulation development stage, with only a few progressing toward clinical evaluation. Curcumin, a polyphenolic compound derived from Curcuma longa, has been extensively investigated in clinical trials for various solid tumors and hematologic malignancies because of its favourable safety profile and pleiotropic anti-cancer properties. In AML, curcumin-loaded nanoformulations, such as chitosan-based nanoparticles, have shown enhanced bioavailability and cytotoxic efficacy in vitro, indicating their potential for therapeutic applications. Parthenolide, derived from Tanacetum parthenium, exhibits selective activity against leukemia stem cells (LSCs). Triptolide, a diterpenoid derived from Tripterygium wilfordii, has shown strong preclinical synergy with venetoclax. Although not yet evaluated in AML patients, its prodrug, Minnelide, is currently undergoing early phase clinical trials for pancreatic cancer, suggesting a viable pathway for repurposing. In contrast, quercetin and resveratrol, although widely studied as dietary supplements and anti-cancer agents in solid tumors, lack AML-specific clinical trials. For effective translation, it is important to align preclinical findings with therapeutic contexts that are already clinically relevant, considering suitable drug partners, solutions to solubility and stability limitations, and the use of biomarkers for patient stratification. However, their potential in combination with venetoclax has not been systematically explored.

Conclusion and future perspectives

Venetoclax has significantly advanced the therapeutic landscape of acute myeloid leukemia (AML), particularly in older or unfit patients. However, resistance to venetoclax remains a major barrier to durable responses, driven by multifactorial mechanisms, including anti-apoptotic protein overexpression, metabolic rewiring, mitochondrial adaptation, and genetic mutations. Phytochemicals, with their multitargeted biological activities, offer a promising avenue for enhancing venetoclax efficacy and addressing resistance pathways. Compounds such as triptolide, parthenolide, curcumin, and quercetin have shown promising results in preclinical AML models by modulating BCL2 family proteins, oxidative stress pathways, and epigenetic regulators. These findings support the integration of phytochemicals as potential chemosensitizers in venetoclax-based regimens.

Despite substantial in vitro and in vivo evidence, the clinical translation of phytochemicals in AML therapy remains in its infancy. Key challenges include poor solubility, limited bioavailability, and a lack of standardized formulations. Advances in drug delivery systems, such as nanoparticles, liposomes, and prodrug strategies, may overcome these limitations and improve the therapeutic index. For instance, a liposomal formulation of triptolide has the potential to enhance bone marrow delivery and synergize with venetoclax in MCL1-high AML patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models. Moreover, well-designed clinical trials are urgently needed to evaluate the safety, pharmacokinetics, and synergistic potential of phytochemical–venetoclax combinations in humans. Future research should explore biomarker-driven patient stratification to identify the subgroups most likely to benefit. To maximize their impact, such trials should prioritize patients with defined resistance biomarkers (e.g., RAS mutations, MCL1 overexpression), thereby aligning these agents with precision oncology approaches. Integrating phytochemicals into precision oncology frameworks holds promise for overcoming venetoclax resistance and improving AML outcomes.
