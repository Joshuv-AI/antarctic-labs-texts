Fine-tuned ResNet34 for efficient brain tumor classification
Authors: Anas Shahin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBrain tumors are among the most fatal diseases, Often leading to a reduction in life expectancy. Early and accurate diagnosis is essential to guide effective treatment and enhance survival rates. Advances in artificial intelligence, particularly deep learning with Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), have revolutionized medical imaging analysis by enabling automated and precise diagnostic tools. This study investigates the effectiveness of deep transfer learning for brain tumor classification using a publicly available 7023 image Brain Tumor MRI Dataset (Figshare, SARTAJ, Br35H), and split into training, validation, and test sets, categorizing four classes : glioma, meningioma, pituitary tumor, and no tumor. The proposed method employs a fine-tuned ResNet-34 model, enhanced with custom classification head, data augmentation techniques, and the Ranger optimizer (combining RAdam and Lookahead for stable convergence). The model achieved an accuracy of 99.66% , surpassing current state-of-the-art approaches.Keywords: Brain tumor classification, Transfer learning, Deep convolutional neural network, MRI scansSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Medical research, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The human brain is among the most vital and complex organs in the body, It is responsible for regulating both voluntary and involuntary functions from cognition to emotion, and decision making.1. Although various conditions can impair brain function, such as infection, and strokes. brain tumors remain among the most severe and life-threatening2. In 2021, approximately 83,570 individuals in the United States were diagnosed with brain tumors, including 59,040 benign and 24,530 malignant cases, with 18,600 deaths attributed to brain cancer3.

Brain tumors are classified as benign (non-cancerous), which grow slowly and are often treatable, or malignant (cancerous), which are aggressive and may be primary (originating in the brain) or secondary (metastasized from organs like the breast or lung). Common subtypes include gliomas, meningiomas, and pituitary tumors, the latter potentially causing hormonal imbalances4.

Early and accurate diagnosis is critical for improving survival rates and guiding treatment strategies such as surgery or radiation therapy. Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) is the preferred imaging modality due to its high-resolution visualization of soft tissues without ionizing radiation5. However, manual analysis of large MRI datasets is time-consuming, costly, and prone to human error, necessitating automated solutions.

Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI), particularly in machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL), have transformed medical image analysis. Traditional ML algorithms, such as Support Vector Machines (SVMs) and Multi-layer Perceptrons (MLPs), have been used for brain tumor detection but rely on manual feature engineering6. In contrast, DL models, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), automatically extract complex features from raw MRI data, achieving superior performance. However, their application in medical imaging is limited by the scarcity of high-quality labeled datasets, which increases the risk of overfitting. Transfer learning (TL) addresses this challenge by adapting pre-trained models, originally trained on large-scale datasets like ImageNet, to domain-specific tasks7, enhancing generalization, and reducing computational costs.

This study proposes an efficient, lightweight deep learning model for brain tumor classification based on a fine-tuned ResNet-34 architecture, augmented with additional layers, data augmentation techniques, and the Ranger optimizer to improve convergence speed and stability8. Using a publicly available Brain Tumor MRI dataset9, the model achieved a classification accuracy of 99.66% outperforming state-of-the-art models, evaluated with metrics including precision, recall, F1-score, and confusion matrix. This performance highlights its potential for clinical diagnostic applications.

Section 4 (Results and Discussion) presents and interprets the experimental results. Finally, Section 5 (Conclusion) summarizes the main findings and outlines potential directions for future research.

Related work

Deep learning has revolutionized feature extraction and predictive modeling in medical imaging, particularly for brain tumor classification using MRI scans. This section reviews recent advances in deep learning techniques for feature extraction and classification, highlighting their performance and limitations.

Early studies on brain tumor classification relied on hand-crafted feature extraction and traditional classifiers. For instance, one study10 employed Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT) and 2D Gabor filters for feature extraction from MRI scans, followed by a neural network trained with backpropagation, achieving 91.9% accuracy on the Figshare dataset (3064 scans). Another work11 integrated Genetic Algorithms with CNNs to classify glioma grades, attaining 90.9% accuracy. In contrast, a deep neural network (DNN) combined with DWT and Principal Component Analysis (PCA)12 achieved 96.97% accuracy across four tumor classes using a smaller dataset of 66 MRI scans, highlighting the potential of hybrid approaches despite limited data.

Custom-designed CNN architectures, independent of transfer learning, have also been explored. For instance, Capsule Networks (CapsNets) were proposed13 to capture spatial hierarchies, achieving 86.56% accuracy. A modified version14 incorporated tumor boundary information, improving accuracy to 90.98%. Additionally, a study15 applied the YOLO architecture for tumor detection and localization, reporting 94% accuracy on a limited dataset of 300 MRI scans, though its small sample size may limit generalizability.

Transfer learning (TL) has become widely adopted for leveraging pre-trained models on large datasets like ImageNet to enhance brain tumor classification. For example, one study16 applied TL using AlexNet, GoogLeNet, and VGG on the Figshare dataset, achieving up to 98.69% accuracy with VGG16. Another work17 utilized ResNet50 with preprocessing techniques such as contrast stretching and histogram equalization, attaining 99.15% accuracy. Additionally, a study18 combined Generative Adversarial Network (GAN)-based data augmentation with ResNet50 and employed Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME) for explainability, achieving 99.53% accuracy on a similar dataset.

Recent large-scale studies have compared multiple pre-trained models for brain tumor classification. One study19 evaluated Xception, MobileNetV2, InceptionV3, ResNet50, VGG16, and DenseNet121 on a dataset of 7023 MRI images, with Xception achieving 98.73% accuracy. Similarly, another work20 identified VGG16 as the most effective model, attaining 98% accuracy. A novel architecture, NeuroNet1921, combined VGG19 with an Inverted Pyramid Pooling Module (iPPM) for multi-scale feature extraction, achieving 99.3% accuracy on a comparable dataset.

Hybrid and multitask frameworks have also been explored to enhance brain tumor classification. One study22 proposed a CNN-LSTM hybrid to capture both spatial and sequential features, achieving 92% accuracy on a dataset of 3264 MRI images. Another work23 combined Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HOG) descriptors with a modified ResNet50, yielding 88% accuracy. Additionally, a 26-layer Graph Convolutional Neural Network (GCNN)24 was introduced, achieving 95.01% accuracy. Multitask frameworks have also gained attention. For instance, one study25 employed a U-Net architecture with ResNet50 as a backbone for brain tumor segmentation, while simultaneously performing multi-class classification using models like NASNet, DenseNet201, and MobileNetV2, with NASNet achieving the highest accuracy of 99.6

Materials and methods

Data

This study used the publicly available Brain Tumor MRI Dataset9, created by Masoud Nickparvar, which integrates three datasets (Figshare, SARTAJ, and Br35H) comprising 7023 brain MRI images across four classes: glioma, meningioma, pituitary, and no tumor. To address the reported inaccuracies in the glioma images within the SARTAJ dataset, these were replaced with reliable images from the Figshare dataset. The dataset was divided into three parts : training, validation, and test sets to support robust model development and evaluation. 5712 images of the data were allocated to the training set, with 1142 images of this training data reserved as a validation set for hyperparameter tuning. The remaining of the overall data : 1311 images made up the test set, used for the final evaluation of the model to ensure an unbiased performance assessment, as illustrated in Table 1. The overall class distribution (glioma: 23.1%, meningioma: 23.4%, no tumor: 28.5%, pituitary: 25%) is illustrated in Fig. 1. Specifically, this split was chosen to provide a substantial training set for learning complex patterns in the ResNet-34 model while ensuring sufficient validation and test data for robust hyperparameter optimization and model evaluation.

Data preprocessing

To prepare the dataset for training, several preprocessing steps were applied to enhance data quality and model robustness. The MD5 hashing algorithm identified and removed 297 duplicate images, reducing the risk of overfitting and improving generalization. The images were normalized using ImageNet’s mean and standard deviation to align with the pretrained ResNet-34 model’s input expectations. All images were resized to 256 × 256 pixels to preserve important anatomical details while maintaining computational efficiency. During training, a random 224 × 224 region was cropped from each 256 × 256 image, introducing variability in scan positioning. This technique helps the model to generalize better to diverse MRI images.

Data augmentation

Before feeding images into the input layer of the ResNet-34 architecture, data augmentation was applied to increase dataset diversity and mitigate overfitting26. The augmentation process was carefully designed to ensure that the augmented dataset remained representative of the original while introducing realistic variations encountered in clinical MRI scans. Proposed model

Convolutional neural network overview

Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) are deep learning netowrks designed to process data, such as MRI images, making them suitable for the classification of brain tumors. CNNs automatically learn hierarchical features, from low-level patterns (edges) to high-level representations (tumor shapes)28, through layers including convolutional layers for feature extraction, pooling layers for dimensionality reduction, and fully connected layers for classification. In this study, a CNN-based approach was employed to capture image-specific features critical to distinguish glioma, meningioma, pituitary, and no-tumor classes, enhancing diagnostic accuracy.

Transfer learning

Training a CNN from scratch requires large labeled datasets and significant computational resources, often challenging in medical imaging due to data scarcity. Transfer learning addresses this by leveraging a pretrained model29, trained on a large dataset like ImageNet, and adapting it to a specific task, such as brain tumor classification. This approach improves performance with smaller datasets and reduces training time. ResNet-34, pretrained on ImageNet, was selected as the base model for its balance of depth (34 layers) and computational efficiency, making it ideal for fine-tuning on the Brain Tumor MRI Dataset9.

ResNet-34 architecture

ResNet-34, introduced by30, is a deep CNN that won the ILSVRC 2015 competition, surpassing human-level performance in image classification. Its key innovation is residual learning, where skip connections allow the network to learn residual functions, mitigating the vanishing gradient problem and enabling effective training of deep architectures. ResNet-34 comprises 34 layers, including convolutional layers, residual blocks, and a fully connected layer, with skip connections that pass activations from earlier layers to deeper ones, facilitating robust feature extraction. These layers were designed to balance complexity and generalization, with Global Average Pooling and Dropout reducing overfitting, Batch Normalization ensuring stable training, and dense layers capturing tumor features. The high accuracy (99.66%) on the test set validates the effectiveness of these modifications for the Brain Tumor MRI Dataset. The architecture is illustrated in Fig. 3.

Modified ResNet-34 architecture for brain tumor classification, showing the custom head with added layers.

Fine-tuning was conducted in two stages to optimize performance. For the first two epochs, the pre-trained ResNet-34 layers were frozen, allowing the new layers to adapt to the dataset using ImageNet weights. Subsequently, all layers were unfrozen, and the network was fine-tuned for 50 epochs with a learning rate of 0.001, selected to balance convergence speed and stability based on validation performance. The Ranger optimizer8, integrating RAdam31, Lookahead32, and Gradient Centralization, was used to enhance training stability and accelerate convergence. The Cross Entropy loss function was employed for the four-class classification task. Training was completed in approximately 37 minutes, demonstrating computational efficiency. Results and discussion

Experimental setup

This study was conducted using Google Colab’s free T4 GPU, equipped with 15 GB GPU RAM and 12.7 GB system RAM. The proposed ResNet-34 model, modified with a custom classification head, was trained for 50 epochs, completing in approximately 37 min. This efficient training time highlights the model’s practicality for clinical deployment, leveraging the lightweight ResNet-34 architecture and transfer learning to minimize computational demands.

Limitation

In this investigation, the model’s potential applicability may be constrained by several factors, such as the dataset. Although it comprises 7023 MRI scans, its moderate size when divided into training, validation, and test sets could limit generalizability. Also, MRI data vairability due to differences in imaging machines, scanning protocols. and patient demographics may affect the performance.

Conclusion

This study presents a fine-tuned ResNet-34 model for brain tumor classification, achieving 99.66% accuracy, surpassing previous studies on the 7023 image Brain Tumor MRI Dataset9. Enhanced by a custom classification head and Ranger optimizer8, it offers a practical solution to reduce diagnostic time and assist doctors in accurately identifying tumors. Future work will explore diverse datasets, interpretability techniques like Grad-CAM, LIME, SHAP, advanced data augmentations techniques, and emerging models such as Vision Transformers (ViT), Swin Transformer, and MixVision Transformers (MiT) to improve robustness and generalization. This study contributes to the growing domain of AI-driven healthcare.
