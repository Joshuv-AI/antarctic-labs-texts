Sudden right ventricular assist device dysfunction: The role of fluoroscopic evaluation in diagnosis
Authors: Salem T Argaw, Thibaut Genty, Pierre Denormandie, Martin Kloeckner, Elodie Mennelet, Benoit Gerardin, Philippe Brenot, Julien Guihaire
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRVAD thrombosis is a well-described complication that requires a high index of suspicion, as it can precipitate irreversible hemodynamic collapse. Here, we present a case of a 51-year-old man with end-stage heart failure who was placed on biventricular support using 2 HeartMate 3 devices. The patient’s postoperative course was complicated by sudden RVAD flow cessation suspicious for inflow obstruction due to pump thrombosis or transient anatomic inflow occlusion. As the patient remained hemodynamically stable, the decision was made to proceed to the catheterization laboratory for fluoroscopic evaluation using a novel catheter-based approach. In addition to providing diagnostic information, this technique offered the potential for immediate thrombectomy if thrombotic obstruction of the RVAD was confirmed.Keywords: RVAD thrombosis, HeartMate 3 RVAD, BiVAD support, right-sided heart failure, dynamic inflow occlusion

Case presentation

A 51-year-old man with nonischemic dilated cardiomyopathy was admitted with acute decompensated heart failure, developed refractory cardiogenic shock on maximal medical therapy, and required venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO). On echocardiography, the patient had an ejection fraction of 20%, left ventricular (LV) end-diastolic diameter of 6.6 cm, global right ventricular (RV) hypokinesis, and no aortic valvopathy. Right heart catheterization demonstrated a right atrial (RA) pressure of 12 mmHg, pulmonary arterial pressure of 41/25 (33) mmHg, RV pressure of 41/9 (21) mmHg, and a pulmonary wedge and cardiac index of 27 mmHg and 1.5 L/min/m2, respectively. He remained on VA-ECMO for 2 weeks without signs of recovery before the decision was made to proceed with bridge-to-transplant biventricular assist device (BiVAD) placement using 2 Heartmate 3 devices (Abbott, Chicago, IL, USA).

Following median sternotomy and cannulation, the LVAD inflow cannula was fixed to the LV apex using 3-0 prolene sutures while the outflow tract was anastomosed to the aorta. To implant the RVAD, the right pleural space was first opened widely. A 2 cm circular atriotomy was then made in the RA and the inflow cannula was propped away from the atrial wall using 6 layers of felt reinforced with Bioglue (CryoLife, Kennesaw, GA, USA). The outflow graft was anastomosed to the pulmonary artery, and both drivelines were tunneled to opposite sides of the abdomen. After devices were deaired, they were started as cardiopulmonary bypass was simultaneously weaned. The left atrial appendage was closed using an AtriClip 45 device (AtriCure Europe, Amsterdam, Netherlands). Bypass time was 181 min and cross-clamp time was 60 min. Patient was transported out of the operating room with RVAD speed of 4,200 rpm and LVAD speed of 5,300 rpm as well as 10 µg/kg/min of dobutamine.

Patient was started on anticoagulation and extubated on day 1. His course was complicated by episodes of massive hemoptysis requiring arterial embolization and frequent interruption of anticoagulation. At week 11, his anticoagulation was held again for a renal biopsy in the setting of progressive renal failure. Three days after the biopsy, the RVAD flows suddenly dropped to 0 lpm with speed at 4,200 rpm (Figure 1). The pulsatility index briefly peaked and remained elevated while the power gradually decreased. LVAD flow and power, however, remained stable. On echocardiography, there was no pericardial effusion appreciated, but given ongoing RV hypokinesis, the patient was restarted on dobutamine of 5 µg/kg/min. Additionally, artifact was noted near the RVAD inflow cannula that showed an anatomical obstruction, possibly a thrombus or occlusion by the interatrial septum (Video 1). Along with gentle resuscitation, the RVAD speed was lowered to better fill the RA with no avail. Since thrombolysis was contraindicated in the setting of recent kidney biopsy, and as the patient remained hemodynamically stable with signs of adequate end-organ perfusion, the decision was made to proceed to the cath lab to assess for thrombus in the inflow cannula under fluoroscopy.

In the cath lab, the right femoral vein was accessed under local anesthesia and a steerable introducer sheath (Agilis NxT, 8.5 Fr; Abbott, Abbott Park, IL) was advanced into the RA. The RVAD was stopped and using an 8 Fr Judkins Right guide catheter (JR4), contrast was injected into the inflow cannula, but no filling defect was appreciated (Figure 2). Subsequently, aspiration was performed with a VacLok 50 cc syringe (Merit Medical, South Jordan, UT); however, there was no thrombus retrieved. On restarting the RVAD, flow recovered to 4 lpm at 4,000 rpm. Pulmonary angiography was performed before closure, and no evidence of pulmonary embolization was observed (Figure 3).

There were no additional related events, and the patient otherwise recovered appropriately. He was transplanted 5 months following his index surgery, and there were no clots retrieved from the device on explantation. After careful review of the cardiac echocardiography, the sudden RVAD failure was likely due to transient occlusion of the inflow cannula by the interatrial septum (Video 2). Patient provided informed consent for publication.

Discussion

When flow suddenly drops to 0 lpm on a RVAD, as in the case presented above, there are several differentials to consider. Given the low-pressure system of the right side of the heart, the RA is less likely to withstand the external pressures exerted by tamponade and tension pneumothorax, both of which require immediate recognition and intervention. Similarly, the perioperative management of BiVAD necessitates meticulous volume optimization and pump speed titration to ensure balanced flows across both systemic and pulmonary circulations.1 When RVAD flows are insufficient to maintain LV preload, or in the setting of global hypovolemia, both LVAD and RVAD flows are expected to decrease. In the present case, LVAD flow remained stable—indicating there was sufficient preload to the LV through the native RV—however, flow through the RVAD had stopped.

Thrombus ingestion into the inflow cannula is a well-described complication feared especially in RVADs due to low-velocity flows and areas of stasis in the right heart.2 In 2024, Marasco et al reported RVAD pump thrombosis in 4 out of 21 BiVAD patients—3 of which due to thrombus ingestion—while no LVAD pump thrombosis was noted.3 While emergent pump exchange is the mainstay of management, especially when patients are hemodynamically labile, other forms of management have also been described. In Marasco et al, 2 out of 4 of these patients underwent pump exchange, while thrombus was recognized and retrieved in the index operation in 1 patient. The last patient was hemodynamically stable and underwent medical management with unfractionated heparin infusion. Other authors have also suggested the use of inferior vena cava filters as preventative measures.4 In all cases, the index of suspicion for RVAD thrombosis should remain high, with a low threshold for further investigation, as hemodynamic collapse may be irreversible.

In the described case, there was a high suspicion for RVAD thrombosis given echocardiographic findings, recent interruption of anticoagulation, and VAD event logfile. However, pump exchange was deferred as LVAD flow and hemodynamics remained unchanged. Following discussion with interventional cardiology, fluoroscopic evaluation was pursued as a strategy with both diagnostic and potential therapeutic benefits. While modalities such as echocardiography and cross-sectional imaging are both useful diagnostic tools, intracardiac thrombi are often difficult to detect with high sensitivity.5 Fluoroscopic evaluation is an additional diagnostic modality, with the added advantage of enabling thrombectomy.

Conclusion

Sudden RVAD dysfunction is a well-known complication seen in BiVAD patients, most of the time related to pump thrombosis. While pump exchange is the mainstay of management, in hemodynamically stable patients with stable LVAD flows, fluoroscopic evaluation of the RVAD may have utility for both the diagnosis of intracardiac thrombus and the potential for concurrent thrombectomy.
