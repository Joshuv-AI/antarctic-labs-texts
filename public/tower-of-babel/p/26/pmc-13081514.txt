Non-invasive prediction of non-tuberculous benign pleural effusion: can we avoid unnecessary pleural biopsies?
Authors: Muzaffer Metintas, Guntulu Ak, Huseyin Yildirim, Semanur Ozyavuz, Fatmanur Korul, Emine Dundar, Nevin Aydin, Selma Metintas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate differentiation between malignant and benign pleural effusions remains a diagnostic challenge, often requiring invasive procedures. This study aims to identify predictive markers and develop a model based on clinical, laboratory, and radiological parameters to distinguish non-tuberculous benign pleural effusions (NT-BPE) in patients with undiagnosed pleural effusion after thoracentesis but before pleural biopsy.MethodsThis study was designed as a retrospective predictive modeling analysis using prospectively collected patient data. Patient cohorts were derived from five studies prospectively conducted between 2008 and 2023, comprising four randomized controlled trials and one observational study, with a total of 684 individuals diagnosed with exudative pleural effusion. For model development, multivariate logistic regression was employed to identify independent predictors of NT-BPE. Model performance and discriminative ability were further evaluated using receiver operating characteristic curve analysis.ResultsNT-BPE was diagnosed in 193 patients (28.2%). Independent predictors included being under 65 years of age, male sex, low pleural fluid albumin, elevated glucose levels, absence of cell balls on direct fluid microscopy, low to moderate effusion volume, and the absence of rind-like pleural thickening, mediastinal involvement, or invasion of adjacent structures on imaging. The final prediction model demonstrated an area under the curve of 0.922 (95% CI: 0.894–0.945), with a sensitivity of 88.0% and specificity of 85.7%.ConclusionThe study findings suggest that clinically and laboratory meaningful predictors of undiagnosed benign pleural effusion after initail thoracosentesis but before biopsy can be identified, highlighting the potential role of predictive modeling in refining diagnostic decision-making. However, future multicenter studies with external validation cohorts and with integration of some other parameters such as thoracic ultrasound are required before definitive conclusions can be drawn.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-026-04222-1.Keywords: Benign pleural effusion, Malignant pleural disease, Diagnosis, Pleura

Introductıon

The global incidence of pleural diseases, which has been reported as 337 cases per 100,000 person-years and is steadily increasing, placing a significant burden in health care systems, is comparable to that of other common respiratory disorders, including bronchial asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [1, 2]. Reflecting their growing clinical impact, pleural diseases now account for up to 25% of consultations in internal medicine and pulmonary services [3]. This substantial burden underscores the urgent need for diagnostic strategies that are not only rapid and reliable but also cost-effective [4].

Despite initial thoracentesis and comprehensive clinical, laboratory, radiological, and cytological evaluations, a definitive diagnosis—such as malignancy or granulomatous inflammation—cannot be established in a considerable proportion of cases. Current clinical guidelines still recommend pleural biopsy with medical thoracoscopy (MT) or image-guided pleural needle biopsy (IG-PNB) in all such cases to obtain a conclusive diagnosis. Patients who remain undiagnosed after IG-PNB are also typically referred for thoracoscopy [5–9].

Following either IG-PNB or MT—particularly when MT is performed after a non-diagnostic IG-PNB—the false-negative rate for conditions such as malignant pleural effusion (MPE) or tuberculous pleurisy (TP) is relatively low. Therefore, pleural biopsy is regarded as a high-yield diagnostic modality in these contexts. Nevertheless, in a substantial proportion of patients—up to or even exceeding 50% in some series—the final diagnosis is a benign pleural condition [2, 6]. In such cases, histopathological examination frequently reveals non-specific pleuritis (NSP), a finding that lacks etiologic specificity and offers limited clinical guidance. This diagnostic uncertainty may lead to unnecessary pleural biopsies, exposing patients to avoidable risks, procedural discomfort, and increased healthcare expenditures. In response to these challenges, several recent studies have focused on identifying clinical, laboratory, and imaging biomarkers to facilitate the diagnosis of MPE or to distinguish malignant from benign effusions [10, 11]. However, because these tools are primarily optimized to detect malignancy, they have limited utility in reducing unnecessary invasive interventions in patients ultimately diagnosed with non-malignant disease.

The primary objective of this study was to determine whether a predictive model could be developed using clinical, laboratory, and radiological parameters to reliably identify patients with non-malignant, non-tuberculous benign pleural effusion (NT-BPE) before pleural biopsy. The ultimate goal was to reduce the need for unnecessary invasive procedures in patients who remain undiagnosed after initial thoracentesis.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study was conducted at the Department of Chest Diseases, Faculty of Medicine, Eskisehir Osmangazi University, and the Lung and Pleural Cancers Research and Clinical Center, between April 2008 and June 2023. Patients were retrospectively identified from five previously conducted prospective studies at our institution, including four randomized controlled trials and one observational study under predefined protocols [9, 12–15]. All trials included in this analysis were conducted by the same research team. Invasive procedures were performed by MM, GA, and HY; radiological evaluations by NA; pathological assessments by ED; and statistical analyses by SM. The same team performed all invasive procedures, and final diagnoses were reviewed and confirmed by the departmental board.

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Eskisehir Osmangazi University (approval number: 2024–41), and the study was conducted following the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent for the use of clinical data had been obtained from all patients in prior studies. Additionally, all patients were asked to provide permission for the use of their data in future research.

Study population and exclusion criteria

A total of 851 patient records were initially screened for inclusion. Three investigators independently extracted relevant data from the original prospective studies.

Patients were excluded based on the following predefined criteria:

(i) lack of written consent for the use of their data in future research, (ii) presence of pleural lesions without accompanying pleural effusion, (iii) follow-up duration shorter than 24 months, and (iv) diagnosis of TP. Accordingly, 167 patients were excluded: 22 did not provide future data use consent, 22 had no pleural effusion, 46 had insufficient follow-up duration, and 77 had TP to avoid confounding effects. The final study cohort consisted of 684 patients with exudative pleural effusions according to Light’s Criteria.

Diagnostic procedures

Among the study population, a total of 938 pleural biopsy procedures were performed: 308 were MT, 587 were IG-PNB (using Abrams or cutting needles), 24 were video-assisted thoracoscopic surgeries (VATS), and 19 were other types of biopsies.

All benign conditions and idiopathic effusions not associated with malignant or granulomatous inflammation were histopathologically classified as NSP [16]. All patients diagnosed with NSP were followed for a minimum of 24 months. Those who developed recurrent effusion or pleuritic chest pain during follow-up underwent re-evaluation and, if necessary, repeat invasive diagnostic procedures. NSP patients whose effusions resolved spontaneously and did not recur during follow-up were classified as having idiopathic pleural effusion.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS (version 15.0), Jamovi (version 2.3), and MedCalc Statistical Software (version 19.1.16; MedCalc Software Ltd., Ostend, Belgium). Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). The normality of distribution was assessed using both statistical tests and visual inspection.

Group comparisons for continuous variables were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test, whereas categorical variables were compared using the chi-squared test.

Univariate logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify potential predictors of NT-BPE, categorized into clinical, laboratory, and radiological domains. In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, NT-BPE was coded as the event category (NT-BPE = 1), whereas MPE served as the reference category (MPE = 0). Continuous variables were dichotomized based on their median values for inclusion in regression analyses. The reference categories for the independent variables were defined as follows: age < 65 years; male sex; non-smoker; absence of fever; hemoglobin ≤ 13.1 g/dL; WBC count ≤ 8,600/µL; ESR ≤ 46 mm/hour; pleural fluid LDH ≤ 431.5 U/L; pleural fluid albumin ≤ 2.8 g/dL; pleural fluid glucose ≥ 100 mg/dL; pleural fluid pH ≥ 7.37; absence of cell ball; small to moderate pleural fluid amount; only pleural effusion (without pleural lesions); absence of rind-like pleural involvement; absence of pleural thickening > 1 cm; absence of fissural nodular involvement; absence of mediastinal invasion; and absence of adjacent organ/structure involvement. Variables with a p-value < 0.05 in univariate analysis were included in multivariate hierarchical logistic regression models. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to evaluate the predictive performance of each model. The area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, and overall accuracy were calculated. A final model was developed by integrating significant variables from each domain.

Discussion

Unlike previous studies, this investigation specifically focused on the feasibility of a non-invasive, parameter-driven approach to distinguish NT-BPE cases early in the invasive diagnostic process. By doing so, it aims to support a more selective, risk-reducing diagnostic strategy that allows for safe clinical follow-up in appropriate patients. Importantly, the present work is a secondary analysis of prospectively enrolled cohorts. At the end of the study, it was observed that the developed model could distinguish 88% of patients with NT-BPE before biopsy. However, false-negative results occurred in 14% of patients with MPE. Therefore, it was concluded that the model largely fulfilled its purpose, but markers should be developed to reduce the false-negative rate in its current form, particularly in patients with diffuse pleural mesothelioma (DPM), as this could affect prognosis.

Unlike mesothelioma, most pleural diseases are secondary to systemic or pulmonary conditions, and their diagnosis often requires a multifaceted approach. A crucial step in the diagnostic pathway is distinguishing exudative from transudative pleural effusions using Light's criteria in conjunction with clinical assessment [20–22]. When exudative effusions remain undiagnosed following cytological evaluation, pleural biopsy is recommended to obtain a histopathological diagnosis, in line with current clinical guidelines [5, 7].

Histopathological evaluation of pleural tissue in patients with cytology-negative exudative effusion typically reveals malignant or granulomatous inflammation, or NSP. As NSP does not provide a definitive etiological diagnosis, its detection may lead to unnecessary invasive biopsy procedures, thereby exposing patients to avoidable procedural risks [23]. At this juncture, the identification and application of reliable predictive markers for BPE in clinical practice could help a substantial proportion of patients avoid invasive diagnostic interventions.

Another important consideration is that, when IG-PNB yields inconclusive results, patients are frequently referred for thoracoscopy—a second, more invasive procedure. In the present study, a total of 938 pleural biopsy procedures were performed, including 193 in patients with BPE and 491 in those with MPE. The mean number of invasive procedures per patient was 1.37.

Our final prediction model, which integrated clinical, laboratory, and radiological parameters, demonstrated good diagnostic performance (AUC: 92.2%; sensitivity: 88.0%; specificity: 85.7%). Retrospective application of this model suggested that 170 out of 193 patients (88%) with NT-BPE could potentially have avoided biopsy.

In our series, the rate of BPE was 28.2% (193/684). Although the proportion of BPE patients in the study cohort appears relatively low compared to the number of MPE patients, the overall cohort size and the number of BPE patients correspond to a high value among similar studies in the literature. In the literature, this proportion varies widely, ranging from 31 to 78%, depending on a country's level of development and local disease epidemiology [1, 6, 24]. The availability and application of predictive markers are likely to be even more valuable in cohorts with higher rates of BPE.

Given the substantial regional variability in the prevalence of TP, cases of TP were excluded from the present study to minimize potential confounding. Tuberculosis incidence varies significantly across geographic regions and has become increasingly uncommon in high-income countries, where it is now primarily observed among immigrant populations. A comprehensive review reported that TP accounts for 23.5% to 82.4% of all pleural effusions in low-income countries [25]. In contrast, the same review documented much lower TP rates in developed countries, ranging from 2.1% to 5.9%. In Türkiye, the prevalence of TP among pleural effusions has been reported to be approximately 5.6% [6]. Notably, some case series from the United States and the United Kingdom have reported no TP cases (0%), underscoring its rarity in high-income settings [21, 25, 26]. Therefore, in such settings, TP should be ruled out independently before applying this model.

Understanding the prognosis associated with BPE is as important as assessing outcomes in MPE. One study reported one-year mortality rates of 50% in patients with heart failure–related effusion, 40% in uremic pleural effusion, and 30% in hepatic hydrothorax [24]. These findings highlight that certain types of BPE may carry a poor prognosis, emphasizing the need to avoid overdiagnosis and overtreatment, particularly in patients with limited life expectancy.

Conversely, most MPEs result from metastatic disease rather than primary pleural malignancies. Median survival after MPE diagnosis ranges from 3 to 12 months, depending on the histological subtype of the underlying cancer [27]. While early diagnosis is crucial for localized malignancies such as mesothelioma, its clinical benefit is limited in the metastatic setting. In this context, the risks, costs, and potential complications of invasive diagnostic procedures must be weighed carefully, especially when the therapeutic goal is palliative rather than curative.

Enhancing diagnostic accuracy while minimizing unnecessary interventions improves both patient outcomes and healthcare resource utilization. Therefore, establishing criteria to identify patients likely to have benign disease is just as important as identifying those with malignancy. A critical clinical question remains: Can benign, exudative pleural effusions be reliably predicted before biopsy? Addressing this would enable a shift away from a "one-size-fits-all" diagnostic paradigm toward a more personalized, patient-centered approach.

In our study, NT-BPE was more frequently observed in younger male patients. This finding may reflect the influence of local epidemiological factors, including high regional rates of environmental asbestos exposure, and women with breast cancer.

Laboratory analysis revealed that patients with NT-BPE had lower pleural fluid albumin levels, higher glucose concentrations, and no cell balls observed on direct fluid smear. Notably, the absence of cell balls was a strong predictor of benign disease. The terminology of “cell balls” has long been used to describe cohesive clusters of mesothelial or atypical cells observed in the direct examination of pleural fluid samples stained by Wright’s stain under light microscopy.

Contrast-enhanced thoracic CT is widely recommended for evaluating undiagnosed pleural effusions [5, 28]. Radiological features suggestive of malignancy include nodular pleural lesions, circumferential ("rind-like") pleural thickening, pleural thickening > 1 cm, and fissural nodularity. While these findings offer moderate sensitivity and specificity, others—such as chest wall or diaphragmatic invasion—demonstrate higher specificity but lower sensitivity [19, 29].

Few studies have investigated CT findings indicative of benign pleural disease. In our cohort, radiological features associated with NT-BPE included small to moderate effusion volume, absence of rind-like pleural thickening, pleural thickening < 1 cm, no mediastinal pleural involvement, fissural nodular involvement, and no adjacent structure/organ involvement. Although thoracic ultrasound (TUS) is a valuable, radiation-free imaging modality that can visualize up to 70% of the pleural surface [30, 31], it was excluded from our final analysis due to incomplete data across the study population.

A definitive diagnosis was reached in all cases in our study, and adequate follow-up was provided for benign diagnoses. While much of the existing research on pleural disease has focused on improving the early detection of malignancy, our findings underscore the equally important need to identify benign cases accurately. Addressing this need is critical for implementing a more personalized, risk-adapted diagnostic strategy.

One of the strengths of our study is that 45% of the patients with MPE in the study group had a diagnosis of DPM, and 38% of the patients with BPE had a diagnosis of benign asbestos pleurisy. The relevant literature indicates that the majority of false-negative cases in biopsy procedures for pleural pathologies are also DPM cases. Similarly, in cases of benign asbestos pleurisy, diagnosis requires a multifaceted methodological study and long-term follow-up of the patients. Another strength of our study is that the data were obtained from four prospective, randomized studies and one prospective case–control study. The modelling itself is retrospective, all source data were collected prospectively under predefined protocols, which mitigates concerns about data accuracy typical of purely retrospective chart reviews.

Limitations

This study has certain limitations. First, the study was conducted at a single center. Second, it did not undergo external validation. Although the models were internally derived from a large, prospectively collected cohort and the sample size is relatively large compared to most reports in the pleural effusion literature, providing a robust basis for hypothesis-generating analyses, the findings performance has yet to be tested in independent populations. Therefore, the predictors identified here should be considered preliminary until validated and also enlarged adding some others in other centers and patient cohorts. Third, the omission of TUS from the predictive model is an important limitation. Fourth, TP were intentionally excluded to avoid confounding, which may limit the model's generalizability in regions where tuberculosis remains endemic. Although ultrasound is now considered a cornerstone in the evaluation of pleural effusions and pleural surfaces, it was not routinely incorporated into our institutional database during the study period. Future models should aim to integrate ultrasound findings into diagnostic prediction tools to improve their clinical relevance.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that predictive markers can be effectively identified to distinguish between NT-BPE and MPEs before invasive diagnostic procedures. By integrating clinical, laboratory, and radiological parameters, the predictive model achieved high sensitivity and reasonably high specificity. This approach has the potential to reduce unnecessary pleural biopsies substantially, minimize procedure-related complications, improve patient comfort, decrease healthcare costs, and alleviate clinical workload. Instead of aiming to eliminate invasive diagnostics, the model seeks to complement clinical judgment by identifying patients with a lower probability of malignancy, thereby supporting a risk-adapted allocation of invasive procedures within clinical practice. Future multicenter, prospective studies incorporating additional diagnostic modalities, such as TUS are warranted to refine further and validate predictive algorithms for clinical use.
