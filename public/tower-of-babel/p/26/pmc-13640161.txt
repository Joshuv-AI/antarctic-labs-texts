Features and management of kidney manifestations of tuberous sclerosis complex: a real-world study in a Brazilian patient cohort
Authors: Aline Grosskopf Monich, Elieser Hitoshi Watanabe, Mariana Faucz Munhoz da Cunha, Luciana Amaral Haddad, Andrei Odevar Cêa, Renan Pizza Terasoto, Gabriela Duarte Neves, Laís Faria Masulk Cardozo, Sérgio Antonio Antoniuk, Luiz Fernando Onuchic, John J. Bissler, Fellype Carvalho Barreto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTuberous sclerosis complex (TSC) is a rare autosomal dominant disorder resulting from pathogenic variants in the TSC1 or TSC2 genes, leading to hyperactivation of the mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway and subsequent development of benign neoplasms in multiple organs. Kidney involvement is frequent, being angiomyolipoma the most common lesion. Angiomyolipoma can occur since childhood and progress throughout life, potentially leading to complications such as hemorrhage and chronic kidney disease (CKD). This study aims to characterize the kidney phenotype in a Brazilian cohort of TSC patients and identify potential risk factors associated with kidney complications.MethodsThis real-world retrospective observational study analyzed data from TSC patients recorded on their first nephrology consultation at two Brazilian university hospitals between September 2019 and August 2024. Clinical, laboratory and imaging data were collected from medical records. Comparative analyses were conducted based on sex, history of kidney hemorrhage, kidney invasive interventions and CKD G3-G5D.Results172 patients were included (28 ± 14.2 years, 62.2% female, 22.7% pediatric). Angiomyolipoma was the most frequent renal lesion (91.7%) with increasing prevalence with age, followed by cysts (45.2%). 41.9% of the patients meeting renal indications were receiving mTOR inhibitor. The prevalence of CKD G3-G5, kidney hemorrhage and interventions was 9.9%, 16.6%, and 27.9%, respectively. Comparative analysis matched by age showed that female patients have slightly lower eGFR (117 [94–127] vs. 126 [97–149] mL/min/1.73m2; p = 0.02) and a trend toward higher prevalence angiomyolipomata ≥ 30 mm, kidney invasive interventions and larger angiomyolipomata size. Logistic regression identified age as the only factor independently associated with a history of kidney hemorrhage [OR 1.06 (1.01–1.11)]. CKD G3-G5D was associated with older age, larger angiomyolipoma, previous kidney hemorrhage and invasive interventions.ConclusionsThis Brazilian cohort study demonstrated that TSC-related kidney involvement may occur early in life, displays a progressive profile and leads to severe kidney outcomes. The high rates of kidney complications and the suboptimal utilization of mTOR inhibitors underscore the need to increase awareness, early referral to nephrologist and timely therapeutic intervention.

Background

Tuberous Sclerosis Complex (TSC) is a rare autosomal dominant disorder caused by pathogenic variants in TSC1 or TSC2 genes [1–4] that encode hamartin and tuberin, respectively, which negatively regulates the mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) pathway. In TSC, the functional inactivation of either TSC1 or TSC2 leads to the hyperactivation of the mTORC1 pathway, resulting in excessive cell growth and proliferation [5–8]. The dysregulated growth leads to hamartomas in various organs, such as facial angiofibroma, subependymal giant cell astrocytoma (SEGA), cardiac rhabdomyoma, and kidney angiomyolipoma. Intellectual disabilities and autism spectrum, named TAND (TSC-Associated Neuropsychiatric Disorders), are also observed [2, 9, 10]. Some manifestations tend to occur earlier in life, such as cardiac rhabdomyomas, which can be diagnosed in utero; and others, tend do occur later, such as renal angiomyolipoma and pulmonary lymphangioleiomyomatosis (LAM) [2]. Recent studies have sought to evaluate the relationships between the most common manifestations in TSC [11, 12]. Renal angiomyolipomata appear to be dynamically linked to development of brain tumors [11], neurodevelopmental disorders and TAND [12], determining a progressive disease trajectory throughout life.

Kidney involvement may manifest early in TSC, affecting even children under the age of two [4, 8]. Angiomyolipomata are the most common lesion, followed by renal cysts and renal cell carcinoma (RCC) [13–16]. As angiomyolipomata enlarge, they distort kidney architecture and become more prone to microaneurysm formation and spontaneous hemorrhage [13, 17]. Chronic kidney disease (CKD), a potential long-term complication of TSC, may result from both kidney encroachment and tissue loss due to kidney invasive intervention, like nephrectomies and transarterial embolization (TAE) [5, 17, 18].

The better understanding of the pathophysiology of TSC has enabled the use of mTOR inhibitors (mTORi) as specific pharmacological therapy to control the growth of angiomyolipomata, reducing the risk of complications [19, 20]. Indeed, mTORi are currently the first-choice therapy for asymptomatic angiomyolipomata larger than 3 cm [4]. Despite these advances, managing TSC remains challenging for nephrologists [18, 21, 22]. While previous Brazilian studies have explored the genetic landscape of TSC [23] and the efficacy of mTORi specifically on angiomyolipomata components [24], comprehensive data detailing the spectrum of kidney involvement, complication rates, and associated risk factors within the Brazilian TSC population remain scarce. This real-world study aims to assess the kidney involvement in a cohort of Brazilian TSC patients and to identify potential risk factors associated to clinical complications. The outcome measures included, among others, the prevalence of prior kidney hemorrhage, interventions and CKD G3-G5D.

Methods

Participants and study design

This real-world retrospective observational study analyzed data from medical records of TSC patients of any age at the first medical appointment with Nephrology Services of Hospital de Clínicas, Federal University of Paraná (Curitiba, Brazil) – pediatric and adult cases – and Hospital das Clínicas, University of São Paulo School of Medicine (São Paulo, Brazil) – only adults. The study period encompassed appointments from September 2019 to August 2024. Data collection included sociodemographic information (ethnicity, sex), use of mTORi, prior diagnosis of hypertension, symptoms (hematuria and back pain), previous kidney hemorrhage, previous kidney invasive interventions (TAE, partial and/or total nephrectomy), serum creatinine, urinalysis, and kidney imaging findings. Patients were stratified according to age, sex and history of kidney hemorrhage, kidney invasive interventions and CKD G3-G5D to better evaluate TSC-related kidney involvement.

Clinical and laboratory definitions

Glomerular filtration rate was estimated (eGFR) based on serum creatinine, using the CKD Epidemiology Collaboration 2021 (CKD-EPI) equation for patients > 16 years and the Schwartz formula for those ≤ 16 years [25, 26]. CKD was classified according to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes guidelines [27]. Proteinuria/albuminuria was defined as a positive urine protein dipstick test (≥ 1+), urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio ≥ 30 mg/g, urine protein-to-creatinine ratio > 0.2 g/g, or 24-hour urinary protein excretion > 150 mg/day.

Kidney imaging

Kidney lesions were evaluated preferably using data from magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). If there was no data from MRI, computed tomography (CT) or ultrasonography (US) were used, in this order of preference. Data extracted from imaging reports included presence of renal cysts, presence of renal angiomyolipomata of any size, presence of angiomyolipomata ≥ 30 mm, and the largest diameter of the largest angiomyolipoma (in mm). The 30 mm cut-off point for angiomyolipoma size was defined based on the latest consensus statement of clinical practice recommendations for kidney involvement in TSC due to the higher risk of bleeding in angiomyolipomata ≥ 30 mm [4].

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation for parametric variables, as median (first and third quartiles) for nonparametric variables, or as number (percentages) for categorical variables. Pearson’s and Spearman’s test were used to analyze the association between parametric and non-parametric variables, respectively. Fisher’s exact test, the chi-square test, t-test, Kruskal-Wallis test or Mann-Whitney test were used for comparing patients’ group, as appropriate. To perform a comparative analysis between sexes with homogeneous ages, propensity score matching was performed using the nearest neighbor method. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were used to identify the threshold of the size of angiomyolipoma and of the patients’ age for kidney hemorrhage. The cutoff points were determined using the Youden index, calculated as sensitivity + specificity − 1; the selected threshold corresponded to the value that maximized this index, representing the best combined sensitivity and specificity for discriminating kidney hemorrhage. Logistic regression models were fitted to evaluate factors associated with kidney outcomes. Univariate analyses were first performed for exploratory purposes. Multivariable models were then built using covariates selected by the investigators based primarily on clinical relevance and biological plausibility, with consideration of the univariate findings. Given the limited number of outcome events, the number of predictors was restricted to reduce the risk of overfitting. The Wald test was used to assess predictor significance, and results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics v.29.0.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

General characteristics

The study included 172 patients, with a mean age of 28 ± 14.2 years, and 62.2% were female. Many variables were not available for all participants, reflecting gaps in data availability in real-world studies. Hypertension and proteinuria/albuminuria were present in 18% and 35.6% of the study population, spanning various age groups. Proteinuria/albuminuria was present in 31.3%, 42% and in 85.7% of patients with CKD G1 (data available for 67 of 95 patients), G2 (data available for 19 of 23 patients) and G3-G5 (data available available for only 7 out of 13 patients), respectively. Interestingly, a substantial fraction of these proteinuric patients were not using mTORi: 30.8% of CKD G1, 71.4% of CKD G2 and 33.3% of CKD G3-G5 patients.

Despite 35.5% of the study population receiving mTORi, either everolimus or sirolimus, 41.9% of the patients with angiomyolipomata ≥ 30 mm were not using these drugs. Notably, 27.9% of patients had undergone at least one kidney invasive intervention. The most performed procedure was partial nephrectomy (58.9%), followed by TAE (21.4%) and total nephrectomy (19.7%). It is important to note that 21.7% of patients underwent more than one kidney invasive intervention.

Pediatric population

Patients under 18 years of age accounted for 22.6% of the study population (56.4% male). Serum creatinine measurement was performed in 41% of pediatric patients and eGFR was estimated in 56.2% of them due to the unavailability of information about height in the medical records, a variable required to estimate eGFR using the Schwartz formula. Although there were no pediatric patients with CKD G3-G5D, hypertension and proteinuria were observed in 12.5% and 15.3% of the sample, respectively, both risk factors for CKD [27].

Regarding renal findings, most pediatric patients underwent US as an imaging examination. It was found that 60% of them, that had undergone imaging examination, had angiomyolipoma (83.3% multiple; 83.3% bilateral; 22.7% ≥ 30 mm) and 50% had renal cysts. Importantly, one female patient (2.6%) had presented an episode of kidney hemorrhage and had undergone TAE and partial nephrectomy, and a male patient was submitted to partial nephrectomy without history of kidney hemorrhage. 23.1% of pediatric patients were using mTORi at the first consultation with the nephrologist, reflecting the prescription of the medication by other specialties, mainly neurology.

Kidney involvement according to sex

The study population included 107 (62.2%) females, who were significantly older than males. Moreover, females presented more severe kidney involvement, evidenced by lower eGFR, higher prevalence of angiomyolipomata (borderline significance), of angiomyolipomata ≥ 30 mm and of previous kidney invasive interventions compared to males (Table 3).

The comparison analysis matched by age showed lower eGFR values (117[94–127] vs. 126[97–149] mL/min/1.73m2; p = 0.02) and a trend toward higher prevalence of use of mTORi (39.6% vs. 23.3%; p = 0.052), angiomyolipomata ≥ 30 mm (58.2% vs. 38.5%; p = 0.07), larger angiomyolipomata size (40[21–79] vs. 27[17–51]mm; p = 0.075) and kidney invasive interventions (32.6% vs. 17.9%; p = 0.057) in females.

Characteristics of patients with angiomyolipomata ≥ 30 mm

Patients with angiomyolipoma were stratified into two groups according to the size of the largest angiomyolipoma (< 30 mm vs. ≥ 30 mm), since angiomyolipomata ≥ 30 mm have an increased risk of bleeding [4]. The group with angiomyolipomata ≥ 30 mm were predominantly female, older, and had lower eGFR. Furthermore, they had a more significant history of hemorrhage and invasive procedures (Table 4). Additionally, it was noted a significant correlation between age and size of the largest angiomyolipoma (ρ 0.40, p < 0.001).

Discussion

Angiomyolipomata represent the most common kidney manifestation associated with TSC, significantly contributing to morbidity and mortality, particularly in adulthood [13, 14]. In our cohort, the prevalence of angiomyolipomata reached 98.4% in adult patients and 60% in the pediatric subgroup, while the prevalence of kidney cysts was approximately 50% in both age groups (44% adults, 50% pediatrics). These prevalence rates are notably higher than those reported in the large international TuberOus SClerosis registry to increase disease Awareness (TOSCA), which documented angiomyolipomata in 48% and cystic disease in 24.6% of adults [17]. This discrepancy likely arises from methodological differences, as the TOSCA registry followed a broad patient enrollment, irrespective of specific clinical manifestations, whereas our study included exclusively patients referred to specialized nephrology services, suggesting a selection bias towards individuals with suspected or known kidney involvement. Furthermore, these differences observed between real-world data and registry-based studies may, at least in part, result from incomplete data availability in real-world datasets, unlike registry studies where data collection is typically more comprehensive. Additionally, the mean age of our cohort (28 years) was higher than the one reported in TOSCA sub-analyses focusing on kidney aspects [14, 17]. Since kidney involvement in TSC is known to be age-dependent [4, 13], a higher prevalence of kidney lesions was expected in our older cohort. Notably, a previous observational study in a North American TSC cohort reported prevalence rates for angiomyolipomata (85.4%) and cystic disease (44.8%) closer to our findings [28].

The TOSCA registry revealed a low prevalence (2.5%) of angiomyolipomata in the youngest patients (≤ 2 years), with progressive increase over time [17]. Renal symptoms such as hematuria, bleeding and hypertension, typically emerge around age nine, while pain and eGFR decline can start even earlier, around age five [17]. Specific studies focusing solely on pediatric TSC populations remain limited [29]. A recent UK study evaluating 182 TSC patients under 19 years of age reported prevalence rates of 53.8% for renal cysts and 60.9% for angiomyolipomata (9% measuring ≥ 30 mm), with no recorded events of kidney hemorrhage [29]. While the overall prevalence of cysts and angiomyolipomata in our pediatric group was comparable to such values, there was a higher proportion of large angiomyolipomata and one case of kidney hemorrhage in our pediatric patients. In conjunction, these findings underscore the need for systematic renal evaluation since childhood, as significant lesions and complications typically seen during adulthood can, albeit less frequently, also occur in pediatric patients [4, 30].

RCC was identified, by histopathological analysis, in 2.9% of our patients, all of them in females. Indeed, TSC patients, particularly females, are exposed to an increased risk of RCC, with an incidence rate two to four times higher than the general population [4, 15, 31, 32]. The TOSCA registry found a prevalence of 2.9% of malignancies and among these patients, 47.7% had RCC [32]. Other studies found RCC prevalence ranging from 0.5% [33] to 2.2% [34], in addition to a mortality rate due to RCC of 2.2% in TSC patients [35]. Confirmation via histopathology is essential, as differentiating RCC from fat-poor angiomyolipoma variants based solely on imaging remains a significant challenge [4, 36].

Females with TSC may exhibit a higher burden of certain disease manifestations, particularly LAM and renal angiomyolipomata, potentially influenced by hormonal factors [17, 28, 37–39]. Our data seems to corroborate to this hypothesis, since there was a trend toward higher prevalence of angiomyolipomata, angiomyolipoma ≥ 30 mm, and prior kidney invasive interventions among females compared to males in age-matched analysis. Moreover, all five cases of RCC occurred in females. Similar to TOSCA registry [17], most of our cohort patients were females (62.2%), which might reflect sex-related disparities in disease severity impacting referral rates between sexes. In contrast to other studies [14], eGFR was significantly slightly lower in females compared to males. This finding might be, at least partially, explained by the fact that females were, on average, significantly older (by ~ 6 years) and had a higher rate of prior invasive interventions, both well-known factors that impair kidney function. Other known risk factors for kidney function decline, such as hypertension and proteinuria, had comparable prevalence between both sexes. Taken together, these findings might suggest a need for closer monitoring in female TSC patients to enable timely initiation of mTORi therapy to mitigate disease progression and long-term complications [4].

Kidney hemorrhage due to angiomyolipoma aneurysms rupture is among the most severe and life-threatening complications of TSC [4, 13, 33, 40]. Risk factors for this complication include angiomyolipoma size ≥ 30 mm, aneurysms ≥ 5 mm, increased tumor vascularity and female sex [4, 13]. The mean age of patients with a history of kidney hemorrhage in our cohort was 37.6 years and the prevalence of prior kidney hemorrhage was 16.6%, occurring more often in patients with larger angiomyolipomata. Even though ROC analysis suggested angiomyolipoma diameter of 30 mm and 30 years age as cutoff points associated with previous kidney hemorrhage, it is important to mention that it does not reflect a true time-to-event or size-to-event relationship. Patient age was recorded at study inclusion rather than at the time of the bleeding event. That said, one may speculate that hemorrhage might have occurred earlier, at least, in some individuals. Similarly, the size of the angiomyolipoma reported in the medical record is not necessarily the same as when the bleeding occurred. Interestingly, age was the only factor independently associated with a history of kidney hemorrhage. This may be explained by the fact that since TSC begins in utero, age may reflect the burden of the disease and its risk of complications, including renal hemorrhage, more powerfully than the size of the angiomyolipoma itself. Additionally, even though size matters, it is important to mention that other factors, such as the size of intra tumoral aneurysms, rich vascularity and low-fat content, have been considered significant risk factors of bleeding in renal angiomyolipoma [41]. The collinearity between age and angiomyolipoma size may also have affected the logistic regression analysis. In fact, it has been recognized that angiomyolipoma prevalence, size and stage are directly associated with age [13, 42]. Importantly, although our data seems to suggest that a subset of TSC patients may have experienced major renal complications at an earlier age, namely renal hemorrhage, further analysis (e.g., stratification by age group, sex, angiomyolipoma size, among other confounding factors) would be necessary to further investigate this hypothesis. Future studies are warranted to identify reliable predictors of early and/or severe complications and to evaluate the impact of early mTORi intervention strategies.

Kidney invasive interventions, including selective arterial embolization and partial or total nephrectomy, are mainly indicated to manage acute, hemodynamically compromising hemorrhage [4], but have been linked to accelerated decline in kidney function and increased risk of CKD progression in TSC [14, 17, 42]. In specific clinical presentations, selective arterial embolization or selective partial nephrectomy may still be appropriate interventions. In our cohort, 27.9% of patients had undergone at least one kidney invasive intervention, with this rate rising to 69.2% among those with CKD. Partial nephrectomy was the most frequent intervention (58.9% of kidney invasive interventions). Patients with a history of invasive interventions exhibited significantly lower eGFR and a higher prevalence of CKD G3-G5D, which might be related to the suboptimal use of mTORi therapy in our cohort: only 35.5% overall (23.1% pediatric, 39.1% adults) were receiving mTORi. It is worth highlighting that among patients meeting the renal indication for mTORi, 41.9% were not receiving this guideline-recommended therapy [4]. This therapeutic gap may reflect a combination of factors prevalent in resource-limited settings, including limited awareness among general healthcare professionals regarding TSC management, restricted access to mTORi due to cost or regulatory hurdles, and delayed referral to specialized nephrology care. Difficulties in the management and follow-up of patients with TSC have been reported worldwide, including among developed countries. A recent real-world study, that included 96 TSC patients from three centers in France found substantial therapeutic inertia, with approximately 60% of patients not receiving mTORi despite the presence of unquestionable indication [43]. A real-world cohort study conducted in the United States of America (n = 180) showed low rates of nephrologist follow-up both prior to the diagnosis of renal angiomyolipoma and one year after the diagnosis of the lesions (11.7% and 22.3%, respectively) [44]. In addition, the absence of multidisciplinary care system with TSC specialists has been proposed as one of the reasons for the delay in the diagnosis of TSC [45]. Taken together, these real-world studies, including ours, suggest that interconnected social, economic and educational factors may create significant barriers to optimal nephron-sparing treatment. Urgent implementation of public health policies aimed at improving access to specialized care and targeted therapies, coupled with broad educational initiatives on TSC and rare diseases, in general, for healthcare professionals are imperative.

Patients with TSC experience an earlier decline in kidney function compared to the general population [13] and may need kidney replacement therapy (KRT) at a younger age [46]. The prevalence of CKD G3-G5D in our cohort was 9.9%. Only one patient (0.8%) required KRT. The CKD prevalence is somewhat lower than the 16.5% reported in a Dutch study of adult TSC patients, which found 2% on dialysis and 2% with kidney transplants [42]. The TOSCA registry reported 1% of patients receiving dialysis [17]. As highlighted by the UK RaDaR study, many patients with rare kidney diseases, including TSC, have relatively preserved kidney function at initial diagnosis, suggesting a substantial therapeutic window for interventions aimed at slowing CKD progression [18]. Angiomyolipoma size, particularly exceeding 5 cm, has been associated with significant CKD progression due to parenchymal disruption and compression [42]. In our study, the median diameter of the largest angiomyolipoma in patients with CKD G3-G5D was 86.5 mm (79.5–109). Moreover, this group of patients had high rates of previous kidney hemorrhage and invasive interventions, further supporting the link between extensive renal architectural damage and function loss.

Beyond angiomyolipomata burden and interventions, traditional risk factors for CKD progression, such as hypertension and proteinuria [47, 48], were observed in our cohort at rates of 18% and 35.6%, respectively. Importantly, 12.5% of pediatric patients already exhibited hypertension, and 15.3% had proteinuria/albuminuria. Although proteinuria/albuminuria was found in 85% of adult patients with CKD G3-G5D, it likely does not accurately reflect the true prevalence due to limited data availability.

While proteinuria in TSC can be a side effect of mTORi therapy [20], 56% of our proteinuric/albuminuric patients were not receiving mTORi. Other potential causes of proteinuria in TSC include hypertension itself, underlying tubular dysfunction, and specific glomerular lesions, clinical conditions previously described in TSC patients [20, 49]. These findings emphasize the need to recognize and manage CKD risk factors beyond angiomyolipomata themselves. In fact, these patients should have their blood pressure, renal function and proteinuria regularly monitored, according to the CKD stage. Appropriate nephroprotection therapy, such as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers and sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors, should be considered to mitigate renal dysfunction progression, whenever indicated [4].

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first real-world study to examine the kidney involvement in a large cohort of Brazilian TSC patients. Some limitations of our study are worth discussing. As it is a real-world retrospective, observational study with data from medical records of TSC patients, shortcomings are widely related to the nature of the database collection, which is particularly affected by missing data. Missing data on age of onset and age at diagnosis of certain clinical manifestations (e.g. age at kidney hemorrhage), as well as missing data on complementary tests (proteinuria/albuminuria, genetic tests and size of angiomyolipomata prior to bleeding and/or intervention), were important limitations. Although a time-to-event analysis using age as the time scale could provide additional insight into renal outcomes, this analysis was not performed because complete time-to-event data were not available. In particular, the age or date of first renal hemorrhage was unavailable, and CKD was assessed at study inclusion without information on the actual age or date of CKD onset. Therefore, the available data were not sufficient to perform a valid survival analysis for these outcomes. Additionally, the current study is derived from data collected at the first nephrological appointment, which likely enriches the sample for patients with more severe renal involvement. This may, at least in part, explain the very high prevalence of angiomyolipoma, high rates of hemorrhage and invasive interventions. In addition, the retrospective and observational design of the study and possible both selection and treatment indication bias, prevent us of establishing any causality, for instance between clinical outcomes and suboptimal use of mTORi. All that said, one should be caution on generalizing our findings. Furthermore, the patients were previously evaluated by other medical specialties that established their own procedures whose indications were unavailable (i.e., indications of invasive interventions). Although the use of cystatin C has been recommended for a more accurate assessment of renal function [4], eGFR was based on serum creatinine levels, which is more widely available in Brazil than cystatin C. Moreover, eGFR by Schwartz formula in pediatric patients depends on height, which was unavailable in some medical records. Regarding the modality of imaging examinations, MRI is the preferred method to evaluate kidney lesions [4, 15]. CT and US are acceptable options, once MRI is not available in all centers and some groups of patients have limitations to perform the examination (i.e., children and patients with claustrophobia) [4]. The limited number of MRI in our cohort reflects the limited access to this imaging examen in Brazil, the difficulty in performing it in young children, lack of knowledge about the management of TSC and the execution of examinations prior to the most recent guideline. No study has compared the accuracy of US, CT and MRI to detect angiomyolipomata in patients with TSC, though [4]. Other studies also have reported low rates of MRI and high rates of US [12, 22, 29]. It has been recommended that, regardless of the imaging method, TSC patients should undergo kidney imaging for detecting and monitoring kidney lesions [4]. Therefore, the use of US and CT does not invalidate our findings. Genetic data was largely unavailable and therefore genotype-phenotype analyses were not performed. Despite all these limitations, this is real-world study reflects clinical practice in resource-limited settings and highlights the importance of studying rare diseases in genetically heterogeneous populations.

In conclusion, our findings indicate that kidney involvement in TSC commences early in life, exhibits a progressive course and might be more severe in females in this Brazilian cohort. The notably high rates of complications such as kidney hemorrhage, CKD G3-G5D, and the need for invasive interventions might reflect a combination of delayed diagnosis, late referral to specialized nephrology care, and underutilization of mTORi therapy. Regardless of selection bias due to the design of the study, it calls attention for the urgent need to close the gap between guideline recommendations and daily clinical practice, especially in resource-limited settings. It is critical to offer regular nephrology consultations for TSC patients starting from an early age to allow close monitoring of kidney lesions and function and timely initiation of pharmacological therapy whenever indicated, while facilitating a structured, well-managed transition from pediatric to adult healthcare. Ultimately, such an approach is expected to improve treatment adherence, reduce loss to follow-up, prevent or delay long-term complications, and thereby enhance the quality of life and long-term survival of individuals living with TSC.
