Surgical resection for sarcoma in the super-elderly: postoperative outcomes and clinical implications
Authors: Masanori Kawano, Yuta Kubota, Ichiro Itonaga, Nobuhiro Kaku, Kazuhiro Tanaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveJapan is one of the most rapidly aging societies worldwide, and the incidence of rare malignancies such as sarcoma is expected to rise among the very elderly. Chronological age often influences treatment decisions, but such bias may deprive patients of optimal management. Clarifying treatment outcome and prognostic factors in this population is essential for evidence-based treatment strategies.Patients and methodsWe analyzed 21 patients aged 85 years or older who were diagnosed with sarcoma at our institution. Clinical characteristics, treatment modalities, and outcomes were reviewed. Univariate analyses were performed for categorical variables. Overall survival was estimated with the Kaplan–Meier method.ResultsThe mean age of the cohort was 87.6 years, and 20 patients (95.2%) underwent surgical resection. Radiotherapy was administered in 6 cases (30%), whereas no patients received chemotherapy. At the last follow-up, 15 patients were alive without disease, 2 were alive with disease, 3 had died of disease, and 1 had died of non–cancer-related causes. No statistically significant associations were identified, probably due to the limited sample size.ConclusionSurgical resection is feasible and can provide long-term benefit in selected patients aged 85 years or older with sarcoma. Analyses focusing on super-elderly patients indicated that refraining from surgery solely due to advanced age could potentially deprive patients of the benefits they may otherwise obtain. Thus, while age remains an important risk factor for surgical complications, individual decisions to perform tumor resection should be made with careful consideration of each patient’s overall condition.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-026-04286-7.Keywords: Sarcoma, the super-elderly, Surgery, Risk factors

Introduction

In Japan, individuals aged 85 years or older are defined as the super-elderly, and efforts are being made to improve healthy life expectancy. Maintaining the health of an expanding elderly population is expected not only to extend healthy life expectancy but also to substantially reduce medical costs [1, 2]. As longevity increases, even rare malignancies such as sarcoma are more frequently encountered in the oldest-old populations [3]. Older individuals are at higher risk of surgical complications, and perioperative care must be carefully managed with strict attention [4–6]. Unfortunately, some patients are deprived of the opportunity for clinical improvement because their health status is not adequately considered and surgical intervention is judged to be unfeasible solely owing to advanced age. Older patients are often regarded as unsuitable candidates for aggressive treatment, even when functional status is preserved. Such age-based bias may lead to suboptimal management and deprive patients of potential therapeutic benefit. Particularly in rare tumors such as sarcomas, identifying which clinical and tumor-related factors truly influence prognosis is crucial.

Surgical resection remains the cornerstone of treatment for localized sarcoma [7, 8]. However, the feasibility and outcomes of surgery in patients aged 85 years or older remain unclear, largely because of the rarity of this population. Evaluating whether surgical resection provides benefits that exceed the risks in super-elderly patients is essential. Demonstrating that age alone should not preclude sarcoma patients from curative treatment could provide insights to guide future individualized therapeutic decision-making.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the clinical characteristics, treatment outcomes, and prognostic factors in patients aged 85 years or older with sarcoma, defined in Japan as the super-elderly. Our findings demonstrate that favorable outcomes can be achieved through active surgical intervention, indicating that surgery should not be withheld solely due to age.

Patients and methods

Ethics

This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Oita University Hospital (Approval No. 3003). Access to medical records for research purposes was obtained on 10 June 2025. All data were anonymized before analysis, and the authors had no access to identifiable personal information.

Study design and patient selection

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of patients aged 85 years or older who were diagnosed with sarcoma at our institution between 2010 and 2025. All sarcoma diagnoses in this study were confirmed based on the original pathology reports issued at our institution. Borderline or intermediate malignant tumors were excluded based on the WHO classification [9]. Twenty-one patients were analyzed, including 20 with primary malignant soft tissue tumors and 1 with a primary malignant bone tumor. Surgical eligibility was determined by multidisciplinary, individualized clinical judgment rather than standardized geriatric scores, as formal frailty or activities of daily living (ADL) tools were not routinely used during the study period. Clinical and pathological data were retrieved from electronic medical records, operative reports, and pathology reports. Patients with incomplete follow-up data were excluded. This study was approved by the institutional review board, and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to its retrospective nature.

Clinical data

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), tumor size, tumor location, histological subtype, performance status (PS), and histological grades were collected. Malignancy grades were assessed using the French Fédération Nationale des Centres de Lutte Contre le Cancer (FNCLCC) grading system for soft tissue sarcomas and the Union for International Cancer Control / American Joint Committee on Cancer (UICC/AJCC) histological grading system for malignant bone tumors. Tumor staging was based on the AJCC system, using the 8th edition for soft tissue tumors and the 7th edition for bone tumors, with tumor extent and depth assessed by MRI. Treatment-related factors such as surgical procedure, surgical margin status (wide, marginal, or intralesional), residual tumor status (R0, R1, R2), and use of radiotherapy or chemotherapy were also recorded. Preoperative surgical planning was performed according to the guidelines of the Japanese Orthopedic Association [10], and resection margins were pathologically classified as R0, R1, or R2. Clinical outcomes, including local recurrence, distant metastasis, and survival status, were assessed at the last follow-up. Outcome categories included no evidence of disease (NED), alive with disease (AWD), died of disease (DOD), and non–cancer-related death (NCD). The median follow-up period was 9 months (range, 2–137 months).

Treatment strategy

All patients were super-elderly; therefore, systemic chemotherapy was not administered. Surgical feasibility was assessed through comprehensive consideration of tumor location and extent, the relationship to surrounding organs, anticipated surgical morbidity, and patient’s preferences. Patients deemed suitable for surgery underwent resection, and the intended surgical margin (wide or marginal) was planned accordingly. One patient whose tumor was deemed unresectable at initial diagnosis, received the best supportive (palliative) care only. In patients with distant metastases, preoperative palliative radiotherapy was performed, and tumor resection was completed using marginal excision to minimize surgical invasiveness. Local tumor resection was generally pursued even in the presence of distant metastases. Reduced-dose preoperative radiotherapy (14–20 Gy) was used solely to support surgical resection and risk reduction, but not for definitive local tumor control. Postoperative complications were assessed in both short-term (0–180 days) and long-term (> 180 days) periods. Complications were categorized by type (infectious, wound-related, mechanical, neurological, vascular, or systemic) and graded according to the Clavien–Dindo classification [11]. Only clinically relevant complications requiring medical or surgical intervention were reported. Recurrence was evaluated exclusively among surgically treated patients. Non–surgery-related events, including non–cancer-related mortality, were documented separately to avoid misinterpretation.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables, including age, BMI, tumor size, and follow-up period, were dichotomized at the median value, and categorical variables, including sex, PS, resection margin status (R0/R1), radiotherapy (yes/no), histological grade, and tumor site, were all evaluated using Fisher’s exact test. Because the number of events was limited and the cohort was small, multivariate Cox regression analysis was not feasible. Therefore, univariate analyses were performed using Fisher’s exact test after dichotomizing continuous variables at the median value. Although dichotomization may lead to loss of information and reduced statistical power, this approach was adopted due to the limited sample size and number of outcome events in the present cohort, which could compromise the stability of multivariable models treating these variables as continuous. In addition, the primary aim of this exploratory study was to evaluate the prognostic impact of clinically interpretable risk stratification rather than to model precise dose–response relationships. Median-based cutoffs were therefore selected as a data-driven and reproducible method that avoids arbitrary threshold selection. As this was an exploratory analysis, variables were dichotomized at the median to minimize subgroup sparsity and imbalance. Overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) were using with the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between groups were evaluated with the log-rank test. OS and PFS were consistently calculated from the date of definitive diagnosis. Statistical significance was set at a p-value of < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software version 25.0. (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Patients and characteristics

A total of 21 patients aged ≥ 85 years were included (Supplementary Material 1). The median age was 87 years (85–94), and the cohort included 8 men and 13 women. The median BMI was 20.4 (15.- 29.6), and the median tumor size was 63.3 mm (14.3–175.3). Radiotherapy was conducted in six patients who consented to and undergo this treatment, mainly because adequate surgical margins could not be obtained for the preservation of critical organs, such as neurovascular bundles. The median radiation dose administered was 35.5 Gy (range, 14–54 Gy). The median follow-up duration was 9 months (2–137 months). The most common histological subtype was undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma (n = 9), followed by leiomyosarcoma (n = 6) and myxofibrosarcoma (n = 3). PS was 0 in 14 patients and 1 in 7 patients; no patients had a PS ≥ 2. Surgical resection was performed in 20 patients, and wide margins were obtained in 15. Wide resection and marginal resection were performed in 15 and 5 patients, respectively, and postoperative margin status was classified as R0 in 17 patients and R1 in 3 cases. Three patients that underwent wide resection and were classified as R0 postoperatively had pathological margins of only 1–2 mm.

Surgical resection was not feasible for the case of pelvic leiomyosarcoma, and both radiotherapy and chemotherapy were contraindicated because of the risk of severe adverse events. Consequently, palliative care was selected as the primary treatment approach. None of the patients received chemotherapy. Based on the AJCC staging system, 2 patients were classified as stage IA, 3 as stage IB, 4 as stage II, 6 as stage IIIA, 3 as stage IIIB, and 3 as stage IV. Distant metastases at presentation were observed in 3 patients and while local recurrence after surgery occurred in 3 patients. At the last follow-up, 15 patients had NED, 2 were AWD, 3 had DOD, and 1 had NCD. (Table 1). Comorbidities were assessed using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), and patients were stratified according to their CCI scores. All patients successfully underwent surgery under general anesthesia. Postoperative complications were evaluated in short-term and long-term periods using the Clavien–Dindo classification (Supplementary Material 2 and 3). Individual-level analysis of ADL using the Barthel Index [12] demonstrated that most patients maintained or improved their functional status at discharge compared with the preoperative baseline status, one subset experienced a modest decline (Supplementary Material 4).

Analysis of prognostic factors

Univariate analyses using Fisher’s exact test showed that age (p = 1.00), BMI (p = 0.582), tumor size (p = 1.00), follow-up period (p = 0.586), sex (p = 0.618), PS (p = 1.00), residual tumor (p = 0.546), radiotherapy (p = 1.00), and malignant grade (p = 1.00), and location (p = 0.498) were not significantly associated with OS. Provided with the limited sample size and small number of outcome events, the odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were interpreted with caution. Although wide CIs reflect statistical uncertainty, the odds ratios with corresponding 95% CIs are reported to enhance transparency (Table 2). Given the limited statistical power, the absence of statistical significance should not be interpreted as evidence of no effect.

OS and PFS

The Kaplan–Meier survival curves for the OS (A) and PFS (B) are shown in Fig. 1. In all cases, the start date was uniformly defined as the date of definitive diagnosis. The 12-month PFS rate was 66%, with 10 patients remaining at risk for 12 months. A small subset of patients achieved prolonged survival with some living more than 5 years after surgery. The median follow-up was 9 months, and censored cases are indicated in the curve. Because fewer than 50% of events were observed, median OS and PFS could not be estimated; therefore, mean survival times were reported as a pragmatic, descriptive alternative. The mean OS was 87.2 months (95% CI, 51.6–122.9), while the mean PFS was 51.9 months (95% CI, 40.4–63.4). Similarly, because the number of events for PFS did not reach half of the total cohort, the mean survival time was evaluated instead of the median PFS. The Kaplan–Meier estimated OS at 1, 3, and 5 years was 95.2%, 95.2%, and 47.6%, respectively, and the corresponding PFS was 81.2%, 81.2%, and 54.1%, respectively (Supplementary Material 5).

Discussion

Japan is one of the world’s leading super-aging societies, which is expected to continue growing [13, 14]. With advances in medical care and diagnostic capabilities, rare malignancies such as sarcoma will inevitably become more common among the very elderly. Thus, age-based bias in treatment decision-making may place patients at a significant disadvantage. Therefore, clarifying the treatment outcome and prognostic factors that affect survival is essential. Even among the oldest-old, active use of surgery or radiotherapy, rather than reliance on systemic therapy alone, can improve prognosis [15, 16]. In our study, most patients had preserved performance status, which enabled surgical intervention in 95% of cases. This finding indicates that surgery may be feasible in carefully selected very elderly patients with preserved functional status; however, this observation should be interpreted cautiously given the potential selection bias.

None of the patients received chemotherapy, reflecting the limited role of systemic treatment in this age group. Among the oldest-old patients, maintaining systemic chemotherapy at the standard dose is challenging owing to frailty and cognitive decline [17, 18]. Moreover, if chemotherapy proves ineffective and tumor progression occurs, surgical resection may become unfeasible. Therefore, an aggressive chemotherapy approach should not be applied in this population. Even when distant metastases are present at diagnosis, surgical resection of the primary lesion may contribute to local tumor control in selected patients. Although these cases are classified as stage IV, primary tumor removal can substantially improve patients’ ADL and quality of life by preventing the tumor-related complications caused by nerve compression, such as progressive pain, deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, and neurological deficits. Furthermore, prior studies have suggested that resection of the primary tumor in selected patients with metastatic disease may be associated with better overall survival. In our cohort, preservation of PS was observed in some patients following primary tumor resection; however, causal inference could not be made.

Sarcomas arising in the trunk, particularly in the retroperitoneum or para-aortic region, are commonly diagnosed at an advanced stage and are frequently considered unresectable [19, 20]. Both surgical and radiotherapeutic approaches are often limited to palliative intent, resulting in poor outcomes. In our study, one patient could not undergo any active treatment and therefore received only palliative care. Additionally, surgery was difficult owing to the location and size. Tumor resection would have required combined total resection of the intestines and bladder, and radiotherapy was considered highly invasive due to bowel involvement within the irradiation field. Following informed consent, the patient and family agreed to the best supportive care and the patient was transferred to a palliative care facility. Excessive or unreasonable treatment may result in harm rather than benefit; thus, therapeutic decisions should be made following careful assessment of individual risk factors.

At our institution, curative-intent radiotherapy is reserved for patients with postoperative R1 margins, whereas reduced-dose preoperative radiotherapy is used only when marginal resection is anticipated, with the aim of facilitating safe surgical dissection rather than achieving definitive local tumor control. Reduced-dose preoperative radiotherapy was intentionally used in this oldest-old cohort to balance surgical priority and perioperative safety, despite recognition that standard preoperative doses are higher [21].

As the number of events did not reach 50% of the total cohort, the median OS and PFS could not be estimated. Because the survival curves were heavily censored, mean OS/PFS estimates could be influenced by a small number of long-term survivors and should be interpreted with caution. Therefore, we additionally presented fixed time-point OS/PFS estimates, while noting that later time-point estimates are less precise because of the small number at risk. Because of substantial censoring and limited numbers of events, long-term OS and PFS estimates should be interpreted cautiously. Therefore, we focused on short-term survival estimates supported by an adequate number of patients at risk. The favorable outcomes observed in this study should be interpreted in the context of this potential selection bias, as patients with poor performance status or unresectable disease were largely excluded from surgical consideration.

Many patients who remained disease-free were transferred to external facilities or long-term care institutions for convenience, making continuous follow-up at our center challenging. The follow-up period was defined as the time from definitive diagnosis to the last clinical visit. Ten patients, excluding those who died, were unable to continue regular outpatient follow-up after discharge or refused follow-up visits.

A major limitation of this study is the substantial selection bias that may have been inherent in the cohort. First, because 95% of patients underwent surgical resection, the analyzed population represents a highly selected group of oldest-old individuals with preserved PS and potentially more favorable clinical characteristics. Therefore, the observed outcomes should be interpreted cautiously and may not be generalizable to all the super-elderly patients with sarcoma. Second, perioperative risk assessment in this study relied on specialist consultations without standardized objective measures. Future implementation of preoperative Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA) [22] may help prevent complications and worsening of geriatric syndromes. Finally, given the exploratory nature of this study and the limited sample size, continuous variables were dichotomized at the median to reduce subgroup sparsity and imbalance. However, this methodological choice may have reduced statistical power and obscured potential dose–response relationships. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted cautiously and considered hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. The potential benefits should be interpreted with caution due to the retrospective design, small sample size, and selection bias. Our findings are exploratory and hypothesis-generating. They suggest that surgery may be considered in carefully selected super-elderly patients.

Previous studies have reported that, even among super-elderly patients, actively performing surgery or radiotherapy, rather than exclusively depending on systemic therapy, can lead to improved prognosis [23–25]. Therefore, chronological age alone may not necessarily preclude surgical consideration, although definitive conclusions cannot be drawn from this cohort.

In conclusion, age-based prejudice may previously have led to cases where individual risk factors were not adequately recognized. Our results suggest that surgery may be feasible and potentially beneficial in carefully selected very elderly patients, particularly those with good functional status. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to the small sample size and retrospective design. In super-elderly patients without distant metastases, we prioritize wide resection under general anesthesia. Ensuring safety requires objective risk assessment using indices such as the Charlson Comorbidity Index [26], Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment, and Clavien–Dindo classification. With the growing super-elderly population, treatment approaches should aim to prevent bias-driven undertreatment while maintaining appropriate risk control.
