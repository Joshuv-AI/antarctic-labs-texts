Integrative analysis of the meibum microbiome in dry eye disease: from dysbiosis and diagnostic biomarkers to immunomodulation by Bradyrhizobium-derived outer membrane vesicles
Authors: Wenjia Zhang, Cong Duan, Xiaoxiao Feng, Liping He, Hai Liu, Hongqin Ke, Yingting Wang, Jie Zhao, Lei Kong, Zhulin Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDry eye disease (DED) is a multifactorial disorder involving tear film instability and inflammation. The ocular surface microbiome (OSM) modulates local immunity, but its functional role in DED remains elusive. We characterized OSM alterations in DED and investigated the immunomodulatory effects of bacterial outer membrane vesicles (OMVs).MethodsWe enrolled 1,262 participants (520 DED, 742 controls). Clinical DED phenotypes were systematically identified using univariate, multivariate, and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression analyses. Meibum samples were subjected to 16S rRNA gene sequencing; amplicon sequence variants were generated using the divisive amplicon denoising algorithm 2 (DADA2) and taxonomically annotated against the SILVA database. Microbial diversity, community composition, and diagnostic biomarkers were evaluated, complemented by functional prediction (PICRUSt2) and co-occurrence network analyses. In vitro, OMVs isolated from Bradyrhizobium were applied to M1 macrophages to assess immunomodulatory effects through cytokine quantification, flow cytometric analysis of surface markers, and polarization-related gene expression profiling.ResultsClinical profiling identified eight independent risk factors associated with DED, including ocular surface disease index scores, tear break-up time, and meibomian gland dysfunction. DED patients exhibited markedly elevated alpha-diversity (P < 0.001) and distinct microbial communities. Taxonomic shifts included reduced Alphaproteobacteria and Bradyrhizobium but enriched Gammaproteobacteria and Ralstonia. A six-genus panel distinguished DED from controls (AUC = 0.793). Functional prediction highlighted upregulated pathways related to inflammation, tight junctions, and PI3K-Akt-mTOR signaling. In vitro, Bradyrhizobium OMVs attenuated pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β; P < 0.001) while enhancing anti-inflammatory mediators (IL-10, TGF-β1). OMVs treatment decreased CD86⁺ and increased CD206⁺ macrophages (P < 0.001), consistent with M2 polarization, supported by downregulated inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and upregulated arginase-1 (Arg-1) expression (P < 0.001).ConclusionThis study establishes DED as a multifactorial condition associated with sleep, systemic disease, meibomian gland blockage, and radiation. These clinical features coexist with ocular surface dysbiosis characterized by depletion of beneficial Bradyrhizobium and expansion of Ralstonia taxa. Notably, Bradyrhizobium-derived OMVs confer protective effects by driving M2 macrophage polarization and resolving inflammation. Collectively, these findings underscore the microbiome-immune axis as a critical node in DED pathogenesis and provide a mechanistic rationale for microbiome-targeted diagnostics and therapeutics. Given the low-biomass nature of meibum and the absence of sequenced negative controls, a contribution of reagent-associated taxa to the observed signal cannot be fully excluded and warrants confirmation in future studies with comprehensive contamination controls.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08327-3.Keywords: Dry eye disease, Ocular surface microbiome, 16S rRNA sequencing, Dysbiosis, Macrophage polarization, Bradyrhizobium

Background

Dry eye disease (DED) is a highly prevalent chronic ocular surface disorder that poses a substantial and growing threat to global visual health. Epidemiological studies indicate that DED affects 5.5% to 33.7% of the population worldwide, with prevalence rates rising steadily [1]. Patients frequently present with symptoms including ocular dryness, foreign body sensation, burning, and visual fatigue-manifestations that markedly impair visual function and quality of life while imposing considerable socioeconomic burdens [2]. Consequently, elucidating the pathophysiology of DED and exploring novel diagnostic and therapeutic targets represent critical priorities with significant clinical and public health implications.

The pathogenesis of DED involves a complex, multifactorial cascade encompassing tear film instability, ocular surface hyperosmolarity, inflammatory activation, and neurosensory dysfunction [3, 4]. Despite considerable research advances, a subset of DED patients responds inadequately to current therapies, suggesting that key pathogenic mechanisms remain incompletely elucidated. In recent years, the ocular surface microbiome (OSM) has emerged as an important modulator of ocular surface homeostasis. Under physiological conditions, the ocular surface harbors a commensal microbial community dominated by Staphylococcus and Streptococcus, which engage in dynamic interactions with host tissues and the local immune system to maintain barrier integrity and immune equilibrium [5, 6]. Accumulating evidence indicates that OSM composition and architecture are altered in DED. For example, a 16S rRNA sequencing study has revealed significant alterations at both phylum and genus levels, characterized by reduced alpha diversity and structural reorganization of the microbial community in DED patients [7]. Subsequent investigations in aqueous-deficient dry eye have corroborated these findings, demonstrating decreased alpha diversity, altered microbiota composition, and a shift toward pro-inflammatory taxa concurrent with reduction in commensal populations [8]. Dysbiosis of the OSM is hypothesized to contribute to DED pathogenesis through several mechanisms: (1) induction of ocular surface inflammation via modulation of local T-cell subsets, including promotion of T helper 17 (Th17) polarization [9]; (2) disruption of tear film stability through enzymatic degradation of lipids and mucins, thereby accelerating tear evaporation [10]. The OSM primarily colonizes the conjunctiva, eyelid margin, and tear film, forming a complex microbial symbiosis network together with the eyelid skin and lacrimal gland system [11, 12]. Among these niches, compositional alterations in the meibum microbiota reflect the functional status of the meibomian glands and are associated with DED related to meibomian gland dysfunction [10]. However, the compositional changes, functional roles, and dynamic interactions with the host immune system of the meibum microbiota in DED remain poorly understood.

Machine learning (ML), a core discipline of artificial intelligence, enables the extraction of informative features from high-dimensional data and has been extensively applied to develop diagnostic and predictive tools across diverse disease contexts [13–15]. Among ML methodologies, univariate logistic regression, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression, and multivariate logistic regression are widely utilized for clinical feature selection and risk prediction. Univariate logistic regression assesses associations between individual clinical variables and outcomes, facilitating rapid identification of candidate risk factors [16]. LASSO regression, a regularization technique, shrinks coefficients of less informative variables toward zero via a penalty term, thereby enabling automated feature selection and dimensionality reduction. This approach effectively mitigates multicollinearity, extracts key variables from high-dimensional clinical datasets, and enhances model parsimony and generalizability [17]. Multivariate logistic regression evaluates independent risk factors after adjusting for confounders, providing robust interpretability and stability for constructing clinically actionable prediction models [18]. In this study, we integrated these three methodologies into a sequential analytical framework for clinical feature selection in DED: univariate analysis for initial screening, LASSO regression for refined identification of core features, and multivariate analysis for final validation of independent predictors and model construction. This integrated strategy establishes a statistically rigorous and clinically interpretable foundation for risk factor identification, patient stratification, and individualized risk assessment in DED.

Accordingly, this study was structured in three interconnected phases. First, we employed ML approaches to identify key clinical features of DED within a clinical cohort. Second, 16S rRNA gene sequencing was utilized to characterize compositional and functional differences in the meibum microbiome between DED patients and healthy controls. Third, in vitro functional assays were conducted to validate the immunomodulatory effects of OMVs derived from priority microorganisms—including Bradyrhizobium species—on macrophage polarization. By integrating these complementary methodologies, we constructed a multidimensional “clinical feature–microbiome–immune response” network. This framework advances understanding of the microbe-immune axis in DED pathogenesis and establishes a conceptual basis for developing microbiome-targeted diagnostic and therapeutic interventions.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical compliance

This cross-sectional study involved participant recruitment and sample collection across six regions in Yunnan Province (2019–2022), followed by 16S rRNA sequencing and data analysis (2023–2024), and then bacterial/cellular experiments with manuscript preparation (2025 onward).

We conducted this study in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The Institutional Review Board of the Affiliated Hospital of Yunnan University (The Second People’s Hospital of Yunnan Province) reviewed and approved the study protocol (Ethics Approval Number: 2019103). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment.

Participant recruitment and regional characteristics

Participants were recruited through a multi-region cluster sampling approach across six geographically and climatically varied areas in Yunnan Province, China: Chuxiong, Diqing, Lijiang, Wenshan, Dehong, and Pu’er. These regions spanned a wide altitudinal gradient (863–3,459 m) and exhibited substantial environmental heterogeneity with respect to mean annual temperature (5.5–19.5 °C), relative humidity (55–80%), wind speed (1.3–7.05 m/s), and solar radiation (110.2–146.5 kcal/cm²). Initial screening included 9,617 permanent residents aged ≥ 40 years from 12 different ethnic groups (Han, Yi, Bai, Naxi, Tibetan, Lisu, Lahu, Hani, Dai, Jingpo, Zhuang, and Miao) to determine study eligibility.

Clinical evaluation and dry eye diagnosis

Participants were enrolled in a comprehensive, standardized ocular surface assessment conducted by trained personnel at community-based screening stations. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire administered by trained investigators, covering demographic information, medical and ocular history, medication use, lifestyle factors, and Ocular Surface Disease Index (OSDI) scores. The OSDI, a validated instrument, was employed to quantify the frequency of dry eye symptoms and their impact on visual function over a three-month recall period. Scores were calculated on a scale of 0–100, with higher values indicating greater symptom severity.

After a 5-minute acclimatization period under dim light, objective measurements were acquired by a single, certified technician using the Keratograph 5 M ocular surface analyzer (Oculus, Wetzlar, Germany). Assessments included tear meniscus height, non‑invasive tear film break‑up time (NIBUT, recorded as first and average values), and infrared meibography for evaluating meibomian gland morphology and obstruction. Meibomian gland dropout was graded on a four‑point scale: Grade 1 (no loss), Grade 2 (< 33% loss), Grade 3 (33–67% loss), and Grade 4 (> 67% loss). Subsequently, a detailed slit‑lamp biomicroscopy examination was independently performed by two experienced ophthalmologists (each with > 10 years of clinical practice) to evaluate ocular surface health. Final diagnoses were established by consensus. DED was diagnosed based on the simultaneous fulfillment of three criteria: (1) presence of at least one typical dry eye symptom (e.g., dryness, grittiness, or burning), (2) an OSDI total score > 20, and (3) an average NIBUT ≤ 5 s.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants were included if they were ≥ 40 years of age, in good physical and mental health, and provided informed consent. Exclusion criteria comprised active ocular surface disease (e.g., infection, inflammation, pterygium, neoplasia), history of ocular surgery or trauma, use of topical ocular medications (antibiotics, steroids, immunosuppressants) within the past month or chronic use of glaucoma medications, systemic conditions affecting ocular surface integrity (e.g., Sjögren’s syndrome, ocular cicatricial pemphigoid), severe systemic comorbidities (cardiac, pulmonary, renal insufficiency), cognitive impairment, or concurrent participation in another clinical trial.

Sample collection and biobanking

Meibum samples were collected under strict aseptic conditions. Participants refrained from using any topical ocular medications for at least one month prior to sampling. The periocular skin and eyelid margins were meticulously disinfected with 75% ethanol, followed by sterile saline irrigation, and allowed to air dry. To minimize contamination from skin and hair follicle-associated microbiota, meibum was expressed by applying gentle digital pressure to the tarsal plate through a sterile gauze pad, using a sterile glass rod to avoid direct contact with the eyelid margin. The expressed meibum was carefully collected from the meibomian gland orifices using a disposable sterile swab, with strict avoidance of contact with the periocular skin and eyelashes. Samples from each eye were processed separately, immediately frozen at − 20 °C, transported on dry ice within 12 h, and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. From an initial pool of 3,067 samples collected from 1,578 participants, 1,831 high-quality samples from 1,211 individuals (672 controls, 539 DED patients) met stringent quality thresholds (sufficient yield, no contamination) and were retained for downstream analysis.

Microbial DNA extraction, quantification, and sequencing

Given the low biomass of meibum samples, which makes them particularly susceptible to exogenous DNA contamination, we implemented a series of stringent contamination-control measures. All extraction reagents and consumables were pretreated with DNase prior to use. Sample processing, DNA extraction, and all pre-PCR procedures were conducted in a dedicated clean bench. Throughout the entire workflow, operators wore sterile gloves, masks, and sleeve covers to minimize contamination risk. We extracted Genomic DNA from meibum samples using the QIAamp DNA Investigator Kit (Qiagen, Germany). DNA concentration and purity were verified using a Zymo Research BIOMICS DNA Microprep Kit (Cat# D4301) and spectrophotometry (OD260/280 ratio 1.8-2.0). To confirm microbial presence and exclude contamination, digital PCR targeting the 16S rRNA gene V4 region (primers 515 F/806R) was performed on a subset of samples. Amplification of the V4 region was performed, and products were verified by gel electrophoresis. Equimolar pools of amplicons were used to construct sequencing libraries with the TruSeq DNA PCR-Free Sample Prep Kit (FC-121-3001/3003). Paired‑end sequencing (2 × 300 bp) was conducted on an Illumina MiSeq platform using Reagent Kit v3 (600 Cycle) [19].

Data processing and cohort definition

Raw sequencing reads were subjected to quality filtering, adapter trimming, and denoising using established pipelines (DADA2) to generate amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Taxonomic assignment was performed against the SILVA database [20]. Following comprehensive clinical and molecular quality control, the final analytical cohort comprised 1,262 participants (742 controls, 520 DED patients) with 1,831 matched meibum samples (1,055 control, 776 DED), all associated with complete phenotypic datasets, enabling robust integrated analysis.

Clinical data processing and baseline analysis

Collected clinical information was systematically integrated and cleaned to establish a standardized baseline characteristics database. Continuous variables conforming to a normal distribution were compared between groups using independent samples t-tests, while categorical variables were analyzed using chi-square tests to identify clinical features with statistically significant differences (P < 0.05) between the DED group and the normal group. The analyzed variables primarily included age, Ocular Surface Disease Index (OSDI), tear film break-up time (BUT) for both eyes, smoking history, alcohol consumption history, ethnicity, dietary habits, environmental factors (temperature, humidity, annual rainfall, wind speed, radiation, annual sunshine duration), and meibomian gland-related indicators (scores for upper and lower eyelid meibomian glands, and meibum quality scores for both eyes).

Screening of key clinical factors in dry eye disease

Using the diagnostic status of dry eye disease (DED group vs. normal group) as the dependent variable, we first performed univariate logistic regression analysis to preliminarily screen for clinical features associated with disease status (P < 0.01). Subsequently, LASSO (least absolute shrinkage and selection operator) regression was applied to further reduce dimensionality and select the most relevant features. Finally, the core features identified by LASSO were incorporated into a multivariate logistic regression model to determine the key clinical features independently associated with DED (P < 0.01) [21–23].

Microbiome sequencing and data analysis

Raw paired-end sequencing data underwent initial processing with Cutadapt software to trim primer sequences, followed by stringent quality control through the DADA2 pipeline. This pipeline integrated quality filtering, error correction, sequence denoising, paired-end read merging, and chimera removal, ultimately generating high-resolution amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) [24]. Taxonomic annotation of representative ASV sequences proceeded within the QIIME2 platform against the Silva database (version 138) using the classify-sklearn plugin. For alpha diversity characterization, the analysis included calculation of the Chao1 richness index, Observed richness index, Shannon diversity index, and Simpson index [25]. Beta diversity patterns were visualized via principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity matrix [26]. Differential abundance analysis employed the Kruskal-Wallis test for inter-group comparisons of microbial relative abundances at various taxonomic levels, followed by Benjamini-Hochberg correction for false discovery rate control (Padj < 0.05). Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) analysis further identified potential biomarkers with discriminatory power between groups, applying a linear discriminant analysis (LDA) score threshold greater than 2.0 [27]. We then constructed a random forest classifier to evaluate the discriminative ability of key differential taxa for disease status.

Finally, based on the 16S rRNA gene sequencing profiles, we predicted the metagenomic functional potential of the microbial communities (annotated to KEGG pathways) using PICRUSt2 [28]. To evaluate the reliability of these functional predictions, we calculated the nearest sequenced taxon index (NSTI) for each sample. The mean NSTI value was 0.075 (range: 0.03–0.12), indicating generally acceptable reference genome coverage, as values below 0.10 are considered reliable for PICRUSt2-based predictions.

Clinical factor-microbiota-pathway association analysis

Multi-dimensional association analysis was further employed to dissect the intricate interrelationships among key clinical features, core differential microbiota, and putative functional pathways. Pearson’s correlation analysis was first applied to quantify correlations between key clinical features and the relative abundances of core differential microbiota. The resulting P values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg method, and an adjusted P value (Padj) < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Subsequently, datasets pertaining to key clinical features, core differential microbiota, and significantly altered predicted microbial functional pathways were integrated to construct an interactive association network, which was visualized using Sankey diagrams [29, 30].

Culture of Bradyrhizobium

Bradyrhizobium sp. reference strain (ATCC 43045) was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA) and maintained at − 80 °C in 25% (v/v) glycerol. For resuscitation, bacteria were streaked onto TY agar (5 g L⁻¹ tryptone, 3 g L⁻¹ yeast extract, 0.88 g L⁻¹ CaCl₂·2 H₂O, 2% [w/v] agar; pH 6.8–7.2) and incubated at 28 °C for 48 h until single colonies appeared. A well-isolated colony was transferred to 5 mL TY broth and cultivated overnight (28 °C, 200 rpm). This pre-culture was diluted 1:100 into fresh TY broth and grown to late-exponential phase (OD₆₀₀ ≈ 2.0). Cells were then harvested by centrifugation (8,000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C) and immediately processed for outer-membrane vesicle isolation.

Extraction and isolation of OMVs

Outer-membrane vesicles (OMVs) were purified from late-exponential-phase cultures of Bradyrhizobium sp. (Section “Culture of Bradyrhizobium”). Cells were removed by centrifugation (4 °C, 4 000 × g, 15 min), and the supernatant was sequentially filtered through 0.8 μm and 0.45 μm surfactant-free cellulose acetate membranes (Millipore). The clarified supernatant was concentrated ~ 100-fold with a 100 kDa molecular-weight-cut-off tangential-flow ultrafiltration cassette (Sartorius) at 4 °C, followed by ultracentrifugation (150 000 × g, 4 h, 4 °C) to pellet OMVs. The translucent pellet was gently resuspended in ice-cold HEPES-buffered saline (10 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.4), snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C until use [31]. Protein concentration was quantified with a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (P0011, Beyotime) using bovine serum albumin as standard. Endotoxin levels were determined by kinetic chromogenic Limulus amebocyte lysate (LAL) assay (L01055-20, GenScript) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Three methods characterized the purified OMVs. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM-1400PLUS, JEOL Ltd) examined OMV morphology after negative staining with 2% uranyl acetate. Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA, ZETAVIEW 23–878, Kdlchina) determined OMV size distribution. 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE, Mini-PROTEAN, Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc.) analysis was performed to determine the protein composition of isolated OMVs following Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining.

Cell culture and treatment

The human monocytic cell line THP-1 (SCSP-567, Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences) offers ease of culture, high reproducibility, and serves as a well-established model for investigating human macrophage differentiation. Accordingly, this study selected this cell line and routinely maintained it in RPMI-1640 medium (11875-093, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator. To induce differentiation into resting macrophages (M0 phenotype), cells were treated with complete medium containing 100 ng/mL phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) (HY-18739, MedChem Express, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) for 48 h, followed by replacement with PMA-free complete medium for an additional 24 h [32]. For M1 macrophage polarization, the M0 cells were stimulated with medium containing 20 ng/mL IFN-γ (HY-P7025, MedChem Express) and 100 ng/mL LPS (ST1470, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 24 h [33]. Four experimental groups were established: control group (M0), M1 model group (M1), OMV treatment group (M0 + OMV), and OMV intervention group (M1 + OMV). We determined the optimal OMV concentration (10 µg/mL) through CCK-8 cell viability assays. In the OMV treatment groups, cells were treated with Bradyrhizobium OMVs at a concentration of 10 µg/mL for 24 h.

Quantitative cytokine profiling by ELISA

Cell culture supernatants from each group were collected, centrifuged at 4 °C, 1000 × g for 10 min to remove debris, and the clarified supernatants were stored at -20 °C. The assays were performed strictly following commercial ELISA kit instructions. Briefly, after gradient dilution and addition of standards, samples were sequentially incubated with biotinylated detection antibodies, horseradish peroxidase-conjugated streptavidin, and TMB substrate for color development. The reaction was stopped with stop solution, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Cytokine concentrations in samples were calculated based on standard curves. The cytokines measured included pro-inflammatory factors TNF-α (PT518, Beyotime), IL-6 (PI325, Beyotime), IL-1β (PI305, Beyotime), IL-23 (PI660, Beyotime) and anti-inflammatory factors IL-10 (PI528, Beyotime), TGF-β1 (PT880, Beyotime), IL-13 (PI543, Beyotime), and G-CSF (PG340, Beyotime).

Double-labeling flow cytometry for macrophage phenotype proportions

After treatment, culture supernatants were carefully aspirated, and cells were digested with an appropriate volume of pre-warmed trypsin solution. Once cells rounded up and detached, digestion was terminated by adding complete medium containing serum. Cell suspensions were collected by gentle pipetting, centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min, and the pellet was resuspended in cold PBS for counting. Approximately 1 × 10⁶ cells were aliquoted, centrifuged, and resuspended in 100 µL of 1× Annexin V binding buffer. Cells were then incubated with 2 µL of CD86-PE antibody (ab77276, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) and 1.5 µL of CD206 antibody (12-2069-42, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), gently mixed, and incubated at room temperature protected from light for 30 min. After incubation, cells were washed with 400 µL of PBS containing 0.5% BSA, followed by staining with 2 µL of Annexin V-FITC (C1062S, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and 1.5 µL of Annexin V-PE (C1065S, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Negative control tubes (unstained) and single-stain tubes for compensation were included. All samples were incubated on ice protected from light for 15 min, resuspended in 400 µL of 1× Annexin V binding buffer, and immediately analyzed on a flow cytometer (BD FACSAria™ Fusion, BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA).

qRT-PCR for detection of M1/M2 macrophage differentiation marker expression

Total RNA was isolated with TRIzol™ reagent (15596018CN, Thermo Fisher) according to the manufacturer’s protocol; RNA integrity (RIN ≥ 8.0) was verified on a 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent). Genomic DNA was removed with DNase I (RNase-free, Vazyme), and 1 µg of RNA was reverse-transcribed using the HiScript III RT SuperMix (RT333-01, Vazyme) with oligo(dT) and random hexamers in a 20µL reaction. Quantitative PCR was performed in technical triplicate with SYBR Green Real-Time PCR Master Mix (QPK-201, Toyobo) on a LightCycler 480 II instrument (Roche) using 96-well white plates. Each 10µL reaction contained 5 µL 2× SYBR Green, 0.2 µM of each gene-specific primer (Supplementary Table 1), and 2 µL of a 1:10 cDNA dilution. Cycling conditions were: 95 °C 1 min; 45 cycles of 95 °C 10 s, 60 °C 20 s, 72 °C 20 s; followed by melting-curve analysis (65–97 °C). GAPDH was used as the endogenous reference; relative gene expression was calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Statistical analysis

R software version 4.5.1 was used for statistical analyses. Between-group comparisons were conducted using the t-test or one-way ANOVA for normally distributed continuous variables, the Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis test for non-normally distributed continuous variables, and the chi-square test for categorical variables. All in vitro experiments (ELISA, flow cytometry, and qRT-PCR) were conducted with at least three independent biological replicates. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

Comparative analysis of baseline clinical data between the dry eye disease (DED) and healthy control groups revealed significant inter-group differences across multiple domains. A total of 46 clinical indicators demonstrated significant differences (P < 0.05), with 27 indicators reaching highly significant levels (P < 0.001). Patients in the DED group had lower body weight than those in the non-DED group. Ethnic distribution also differed markedly between groups: the DED group contained higher proportions of Yi, Lisu, and Lahu individuals. Notably, the proportion of Yi ethnicity in the DED group was approximately 1.7 times that in the non-DED group, suggesting possible ethnic differences in genetic susceptibility to DED. Sampling locations displayed pronounced regional clustering (P < 0.001). Wuding County (Chuxiong Prefecture) and Weixi County (Diqing Prefecture) contributed substantially higher sample proportions to the DED group, whereas the Lijiang site contributed a much higher proportion to the non-DED group. Longitude and latitude analyses further confirmed geographic aggregation (P < 0.001). The DED group concentrated more heavily within the area spanning longitude 98.9°–99.5666°E and latitude 26.8833°–28.0333°N—a region corresponding to the Weixi sampling site—indicating a potentially elevated DED risk in specific geographic zones. Environmental factors also showed notable intergroup differences, including annual mean temperature, altitude, wind speed, solar radiation, and annual sunshine hours (all P < 0.001).

The DED group had a considerably higher proportion of patients with comorbid systemic diseases (P < 0.001), suggesting systemic diseases as important DED risk factors. A history of ocular infection also occurred more frequently in the DED group (P < 0.001), indicating that prior infections may damage the ocular surface and raise DED risk. Daily television viewing time differed substantially between groups (P < 0.001), with a larger proportion of DED patients reporting no television viewing. Combined analysis of reading and television habits revealed that a larger proportion of DED patients engaged in neither activity, possibly because vision impairment limits eye‑intensive behaviors. Sleep duration distribution also showed considerable intergroup differences (P < 0.001). The DED group contained higher proportions of individuals sleeping 3–4 h or 5–6 h, and a lower proportion achieving adequate sleep (7–8 h), suggesting insufficient sleep as a DED risk factor.

OSDI scores reflected distinctly greater symptom severity in the DED group (P < 0.001). Symptom‑specific analyses showed a higher proportion of DED patients reporting foreign body sensation (P < 0.001), higher pain rates across all severity levels (P < 0.001), a much higher proportion reporting blurred vision (P < 0.001), and a higher rate of low vision (P < 0.001). Right‑eye visual acuity grading revealed pronounced intergroup differences (P < 0.001), with a higher proportion of moderate visual impairment in the DED group, suggesting an association between DED and visual impairment.

Meibomian gland function assessment demonstrated a markedly higher positivity rate for meibomian gland obstruction in the DED group (P < 0.001), confirming meibomian gland dysfunction as a core pathological mechanism of DED. Tear film evaluation showed that the DED group had a lower proportion of normal tear meniscus height, a higher proportion of moderate‑to‑severe abnormalities, and exhibited pronounced tear film instability, as reflected by a very low proportion of normal tear break‑up time and a very high proportion of severe abnormalities (Supplementary Table 2).

Identification of key clinical determinants of DED

Based on the baseline characteristics, we performed feature selection to identify clinical features independently associated with DED. Initial univariate logistic regression analysis of bilateral dry eye status yielded 19 clinical features showing pronounced association with disease status at a threshold of P < 0.01 (Table 1). To reduce dimensionality and identify core features, LASSO regression was employed for variable selection. The optimal regularization parameter λ (10⁻⁶), determined via 10‑fold cross‑validation, led to the selection of 14 features: sleep, other systemic diseases (OSD), ocular infection, pain, vague, ocular surface disease index (OSDI), symptom, blocked, tear meniscus height (TMH), tear film break-up time (BUT), temperature, humidity, wind speed, and radiation (Fig. 1A-B). Subsequent multivariate logistic regression incorporated these variables. At a significance level of Pr(>|z|) < 0.01, eight key clinical features were ultimately identified: Sleep, OSD, OSDI, Symptom, Blocked, BUT, Humidity, and Radiation (Table 2).

Composition and diversity analysis of microbial communities

Analysis at the ASVs level revealed distinct meibum microbial community profiles between the DED and healthy control groups. Venn diagram analysis identified 8,017 ASVs shared between the two groups, with 3,812 ASVs unique to the DED group and 4,097 unique to the control group (Fig. 2A), indicating a high degree of group-specific microbial composition. Rarefaction analysis demonstrated that species accumulation curves plateaued at a sequencing depth of 1,000 reads (Fig. 2B), confirming sufficient sequencing coverage to adequately represent microbial community structures.

Alpha diversity analyses revealed significantly higher microbial richness and community diversity in the DED group compared to controls, as evidenced by elevated Chao1 index (P < 0.001, Fig. 2C), Observed ASV count (P < 0.001, Fig. 2D), Simpson index (P < 0.001, Fig. 2E), and Shannon index (P < 0.001, Fig. 2F). Microbial community structure analysis from phylum to genus level

Analysis across five taxonomic levels (phylum to genus) revealed that Proteobacteria dominated the microbial communities in both groups, while other phyla showed relatively low abundance, with no notable inter-group differences appearing at the phylum level. Systematic differences emerged starting from the class level: compared to the normal group, the DED group exhibited a marked increase in the relative abundance of Gammaproteobacteria and a notable decrease in Alphaproteobacteria. This trend remained consistent at finer taxonomic resolutions: at the order level, the DED group showed reduced abundance of Rhizobiales and increased abundance of Betaproteobacteriales; at the family level, Burkholderiaceae abundance increased while Xanthobacteraceae decreased; and at the genus level, Bradyrhizobium abundance declined while Ralstonia abundance rose (Fig. 3A-E). These findings indicate that although the overall dominant phylum structure is similar between DED patients and normal at the phylum level, a consistent pattern of relative expansion of Gammaproteobacteria and contraction of Alphaproteobacteria emerges from the class level onward. Thus, despite conservation of Proteobacterial dominance, DED is characterised by a hierarchical imbalance whereby Alphaproteobacterial lineages (especially Bradyrhizobium) are displaced by Gammaproteobacterial taxa (notably Ralstonia). This reciprocal shift provides a phylogenetically coherent signal that may contribute to meibum dysbiosis in DED pathogenesis.

Functional prediction of differential microbial communities

Functional prediction analysis of the meibum microbiota was performed using PICRUSt2, revealing significant differences in KEGG pathway abundance between the DED and healthy control groups (Fig. 5). According to the KEGG Brite hierarchy system, the main alterations were as follows: Within the Brite Hierarchies category, pathways related to the prokaryotic defense system, polyketide biosynthesis proteins and glycosyltransferases were markedly downregulated in the DED group, whereas glycosaminoglycan binding proteins were markedly upregulated. In the Cellular Processes category, pathways associated with tight junctions, phagosomes, and the cell cycle were substantially upregulated in the DED group, while lysosome activity and Escherichia coli biofilm formation were pronouncedly downregulated. Within Environmental Information Processing, pathways including the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, mTOR signaling pathway, and Apelin signaling pathway were notably upregulated in the DED group, whereas the phosphotransferase system (PTS) and phospholipase D signaling pathway were downregulated. For Genetic Information Processing, pathways such as ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis, the proteasome, mRNA surveillance and basal transcription factors were considerably upregulated in the DED group. In the Organismal Systems classification, pathways related to salivary secretion, prolactin signaling, pancreatic secretion, and carbohydrate digestion and absorption were pronouncedly downregulated in the DED group, while cardiac muscle contraction was substantially upregulated.

Regulatory effects of Bradyrhizobium OMVs on macrophage polarization and inflammatory response

To investigate the functional role of Bradyrhizobium sp.—a commensal bacterium significantly reduced in DED patients—we isolated and purified its OMVs and assessed their effects on macrophage polarization and inflammation. TEM revealed that the purified OMVs exhibited spherical, bilayered vesicular structures, consistent with the typical morphology of OMVs (Supplementary Fig. S1A). Nanoparticle size analysis revealed that the OMVs exhibited a polydisperse distribution, with a median diameter of 89.7 nm, a mean diameter of 108.7 nm, and an original concentration of 6.3 × 10⁸ particles/mL. The predominant population (67.7%) showed a peak diameter of 85.6 nm, which is consistent with the typical size characteristics of OMVs (Supplementary Fig. S1B). SDS-PAGE identified characteristic high-abundance OMV outer membrane protein bands at molecular weights of 34–46 kDa (Supplementary Fig. S1C). BCA assay determined the protein concentration of the purified OMVs (371.55 ± 80.88 µg/mL) (Supplementary Table 3). LAL assay revealed low endotoxin levels (0.04 ± 0.004 EU/mL) (Supplementary Table 4). Collectively, these data demonstrate that the purified OMVs possess intact vesicular structures, homogeneous size distribution, characteristic outer membrane protein, adequate protein yield, and low endotoxin content, ensuring their reliability for subsequent experiments. CCK-8 assay revealed that Bradyrhizobium-derived OMVs exerted dose-dependent cytotoxicity on THP-1. Cell viability was not significantly affected at concentrations of 5 and 10 µg/mL, but decreased markedly at concentrations ≥ 20 µg/mL (P < 0.001 vs. control). Accordingly, 10 µg/mL OMVs was chosen for subsequent experiments to avoid cytotoxic interference (Supplementary Fig. S1D).

ELISA results demonstrated that in unstimulated M0 macrophages, OMV treatment (M0 + OMV group) led only to a significant increase in tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) expression (P < 0.001), while showing no significant effects on the levels of interleukin-6 (IL-6), IL-1β, IL-23, IL-10, transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1), IL-13, or granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) (P > 0.05). These findings suggest that OMVs alone do not directly induce systemic inflammatory activation or reparative polarization in M0 macrophages. However, in lipopolysaccharide/interferon-γ (LPS/IFN-γ)-induced M1 pro-inflammatory macrophages, OMV treatment (M1 + OMV group) exhibited significant anti-inflammatory and reparative regulatory effects. Compared to the M1 model group, the secretion levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, and IL-23 in the supernatant of the M1 + OMV group were significantly downregulated (P < 0.001). Simultaneously, the levels of anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-10, TGF-β1, IL-13, and G-CSF were significantly elevated (P < 0.001) (Fig. 7A–H). These results indicate that Bradyrhizobium-derived OMVs can suppress the pro-inflammatory response of activated M1 macrophages and promote their transition toward a reparative phenotype. Flow cytometric analysis revealed that, compared to the M1 group, the proportion of CD86⁺ M1 macrophages was significantly reduced, while the proportion of CD206⁺ M2 macrophages was significantly increased in the M1 + OMV group (P < 0.001), further confirming that OMV intervention promotes macrophage polarization from the M1 to the M2 phenotype (Fig. 7I–J). qRT-PCR results also demonstrated that mRNA expression of the M1 marker gene iNOS was significantly downregulated, whereas expression of the M2 marker gene Arg-1 was significantly upregulated in the M1 + OMV group, validating the regulatory effect of OMVs on macrophage polarization at the transcriptional level (Fig. 7K).

Discussion

The persistence of dry eye disease (DED) is associated with maladaptation of the ocular surface microenvironment (OSM), yet whether dysbiosis of the resident microbiota within the meibomian glands influences disease progression remains unclear. This study employed an integrated approach combining meibum microbiome analysis and in vitro functional assays to investigate alterations in the meibum microbiome of DED patients and its role in disease pathogenesis. The main findings are as follows: (1) DED is associated with Sleep, Other systemic disease (OSD), Ocular surface disease index (OSDI), Symptom, Meibomian gland block (Blocked), Break-up time (BUT), Humidity and Radiation; (2) The meibum microbiota of DED patients exhibits a significant increase in alpha diversity and undergoes characteristic structural alterations, notably including a decrease in the relative abundance of genera such as Bradyrhizobium; (3) Differential microbiota composition showed notable associations with multiple KEGG pathways involved in immune regulation and metabolism, as well as with key clinical features; (4) Outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) derived from the key differential bacterium Bradyrhizobium can effectively suppress the pro-inflammatory activity of M1 macrophages and promote their polarization towards an anti-inflammatory/repair M2 phenotype. Collectively, these results suggest that meibum microbiota dysbiosis is associated with the pathological process of DED, potentially through effects on local immune homeostasis.

Analysis of baseline data indicates that DED is a typical multifactorial disease, whose occurrence is related to various factors, including the ocular surface microenvironment, external environmental exposures, lifestyle, and individual susceptibility. Using LASSO regression and subsequent multivariate logistic regression, this study identified key clinical features: Sleep, OSD, OSDI, Symptom, Meibomian gland block, Break-up time, Humidity and Radiation. Among these, shortened BUT, reflecting tear film instability, is highly consistent with the core pathological mechanism of DED and serves as a hallmark feature, particularly in evaporative dry eye [34, 35]. Tear film instability can lead to dry spot formation, subsequently causing ocular surface epithelial damage and inflammatory responses [36]. The independent correlation with environmental factors (radiation, humidity) [37, 38] provides epidemiological evidence for the significant impact of environmental exposure on DED development [39, 40], with potential mechanisms possibly involving accelerated tear evaporation and compromised tear film stability. The inclusion of sleep status and systemic disease history suggests that overall health and neuroendocrine rhythms may influence DED pathogenesis by modulating tear secretion quality or altering the ocular surface inflammatory threshold [41]. In addition, meibomian gland ductal obstruction is an independent risk factor for DED, which is highly consistent with the current mainstream international understanding. Meibomian gland dysfunction (MGD) is the primary cause of evaporative dry eye, and meibomian gland ductal obstruction is the most common pathological type of MGD [42]. Under normal conditions, the meibum secreted by the meibomian glands forms the lipid layer of the tear film, which functions to reduce tear evaporation and maintain tear film stability [43]. When the meibomian gland ducts are obstructed, meibum outflow is impeded, leading to tear film instability, altered meibum composition, and a vicious cycle in which ocular surface inflammation exacerbates the obstruction [44].

This study identified altered meibum microbiome structure in DED patients. The DED group showed higher microbial alpha diversity than healthy controls, and beta diversity analysis revealed clear separation between the two groups. However, previous studies on OSM alpha diversity in DED patients have yielded inconsistent results. On one hand, multiple investigations have demonstrated considerably increased alpha diversity in DED patients [45–47]. This increased diversity may be attributed to ocular surface epithelial damage and decreased levels of mucins and antimicrobial substances in tears, resulting in impaired microbial clearance [46]. On the other hand, several studies have reported comparable or even reduced alpha diversity in DED patients [48–51]. Several factors may account for these discrepancies. First, the ocular surface harbors a low-density microbiome, making its composition prone to stochastic variation [52, 53]; second, previous studies were generally limited by small sample sizes (ranging from 10 to 47 subjects), compromising statistical power [9, 45, 51, 54, 55]; third, the OSM is influenced by multiple factors, including demographic characteristics and sampling methodologies [56]. Specifically, age, sex, ethnicity, and geographic environment shape the ocular microbiome composition, while sampling site, collection technique, and precise anatomical location of specimen acquisition also impact the accurate characterization of microbial communities [49–59]. Potential mechanisms underlying reduced alpha diversity in these patients may include: (1) tear film hyperosmolarity triggering antimicrobial peptide release that non-selectively targets commensals; (2) chronic inflammation generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) and S100 proteins that narrow microbial ecological niches; (3) mucin network destruction eliminating bacterial colonization sites; and (4) secretory immunoglobulin A (sIgA)-mediated immune clearance in Sjögren’s syndrome preferentially removing rare strains. The present study enrolled a large clinical cohort (n = 1,262) with all participants recruited from Yunnan Province, utilizing standardized sampling of meibum. The DED group exhibited markedly higher alpha diversity than healthy controls, providing complementary evidence to previous investigations. However, the meibum niche differs from other ocular surface sites such as the conjunctival sac and limbus. Furthermore, the single-center design of this study necessitates cautious interpretation when comparing our findings with those from other populations.

This study identified reduced Bradyrhizobium (Alphaproteobacteria) and elevated Ralstonia (Gammaproteobacteria) in meibum of DED patients. Bradyrhizobium, a Gram-negative bacterium of the Bradyrhizobiaceae family, is ubiquitous in nature and functions as a low-abundance mucosal commensal, including the healthy ocular surface [60]. Its depletion in DED may reflect microenvironmental disruption: tear film instability alters local oxygen tension and elevates antimicrobial peptides, compromising conditions for this microaerophile [9]. Notably, some studies have observed an increase in the abundance of this genus in DED groups and have listed it as a potential biomarker; this inconsistency may be attributable to differences in study populations, DED subtypes, or methodologies [61]. Conversely, Ralstonia encompasses opportunistic pathogens capable of causing disease in immunocompromised hosts, including outbreaks of endophthalmitis [62]. Its enrichment in DED patient meibum—consistent with prior ocular surface findings [63]—suggests barrier dysfunction may enable its colonization and proliferation. Through LEfSe analysis and random forest modeling, Bradyrhizobium and Ralstonia were identified as key microbial biomarkers distinguishing DED from healthy states. A six-genus diagnostic model (including Bradyrhizobium, Ralstonia, the Burkholderia-Caballeronia-Paraburkholderia complex, uncultured archaeon, Aeromonas, and Variovorax) demonstrated good discriminatory performance, suggesting that ocular surface microbiome features may serve as non-invasive auxiliary indicators for DED diagnosis.

Based on 16S rRNA sequencing and PICRUSt2 functional prediction, this study reveals multi-dimensional predicted functional remodeling of the meibomian gland microbiome in DED. Specifically, tight junction pathways were upregulated, potentially reflecting bacterial aggregation via cell wall adhesion proteins and microcolony formation that facilitates quorum sensing [64]. Furthermore, phagosome pathways were upregulated in DED, suggesting pre-activation of intracellular survival mechanisms. Bacteria may secrete effector proteins to modulate phagosomal environments and prevent phagosome-lysosome fusion, thereby establishing intracellular replicative niches [65, 66]. The upregulation of phagosome pathways suggests that DED-associated bacteria face enhanced phagocytic pressure from ocular surface inflammation, preemptively activating immune evasion programs to improve intracellular survival. Proliferation-related pathways (cell cycle, PI3K-Akt, mTOR, and Apelin signaling) were coordinately upregulated, forming a regulatory network that accelerates chromosome replication, senses nutritional signals, promotes protein synthesis, and supports stress adaptation [67–70]. Concurrently, ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis, proteasome, and mRNA surveillance pathways were enhanced, reflecting increased investment in protein quality control and genome maintenance under oxidative and hyperosmotic stress [71, 72]. Together, these predicted pathway alterations suggest a “survival-first” functional remodeling signature in DED meibomian gland microbiota. However, these predictions rely on database annotation completeness and reflect functional potential rather than actual expression; metatranscriptomic or proteomic validation is warranted.

Our correlation analysis revealed potential links among clinical features, ocular surface microbiota, and functional pathways. Tear film break-up time, meibomian gland block, humidity, and radiation exposure were associated with key bacterial genera, which were further related to mTOR, PI3K-Akt, and ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis pathways. These results suggest that clinical and environmental factors may influence the ocular microbiome and related signaling pathways. However, the Spearman correlation coefficients were weak. Despite statistical significance from the large sample, the low effect sizes imply limited individual effects. Therefore, the network represents statistical associations rather than causal relationships.

Ocular surface inflammation in DED involves elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines and immune cell infiltration, with M1 macrophage overactivation playing a key role in exacerbating inflammation and tissue damage [73, 74]. OMVs are naturally produced bacterial nanoparticles that interact with various host cell types, including mucosal epithelial cells and myeloid cells, thereby triggering inflammatory responses or suppressing immune responses [75]. The immunomodulatory effects of OMVs on macrophages have been well documented. For instance, Escherichia coli OMVs induce the production of ROS, iNOS, IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-10, and upregulate CD86 and CD206 [76]. OMVs also stimulate macrophage proliferation and uptake, and trigger the secretion of cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, and TGF-β1. Similarly, Pasteurella multocida OMVs directly interact with macrophages and modulate their immune functions in vitro [77]. In addition to pro-inflammatory effects, OMVs can exert anti-inflammatory activities that benefit bacterial survival or promote secondary infections. For example, Helicobacter pylori OMVs induce IL-10 production in human peripheral blood mononuclear cells, thereby limiting inflammation [78]; Brucella abortus OMVs suppress TLR2, TLR4, and TLR5 responses, inhibit IFN-γ-induced MHC class II expression [79]. The present study demonstrates that 10 µg/mL Bradyrhizobium OMVs exert immunomodulatory effects on macrophages. Unlike the classical pro-inflammatory effects of pathogenic OMVs, Bradyrhizobium OMVs significantly suppress M1-type pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, IL-23) while promoting anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-10, TGF-β1, IL-13) and the repair factor G-CSF. Flow cytometry and qRT-PCR confirmed that OMV treatment downregulates M1 markers (CD86, iNOS), upregulates M2 markers (CD206, Arg-1), and drives M1-to-M2 polarization, revealing the strain-dependent nature of OMV immunomodulation. Our study demonstrated that 10 µg/mL OMVs exhibited no toxicity to THP-1 cells. Previous literature supports that this concentration falls within the effective range for OMVs to exert specific immunomodulatory effects. Kim et al. reported that 10 µg/mL of Lactobacillus plantarum-OMVs induced M2 anti-inflammatory macrophage polarization in THP-1 cells [80]. A recent study by Li et al. demonstrated that probiotic-derived OMVs at 1–16 µg/mL induced IL-10 secretion in RAW264.7 macrophages, with anti-inflammatory responses significantly exceeding pro-inflammatory responses [81]. Additionally, Cecil et al. established a standard experimental protocol using 0.1, 1, and 10 µg/mL as a concentration gradient for Legionella pneumophila OMV stimulation of THP-1 cells [82]. Jung et al. confirmed via MTT assay that 10 µg/mL OMVs incubated with THP-1 cells for 96 h did not significantly reduce cell viability [83]. Nevertheless, this study did not establish a complete dose-response curve. Future studies will include multiple concentration gradients to more precisely determine the concentration threshold for ocular surface cell-specific responses and to distinguish specific biological effects from nonspecific cellular responses. Furthermore, the physiological relevance of 10 µg/mL OMVs remains to be elucidated in this study, emphasizing the necessity of future research utilizing dry eye disease animal models to validate the relationship between OMV concentration and biological effects.

OMVs are compositionally complex, primarily containing lipopolysaccharide (LPS), phospholipids, outer membrane proteins, nucleic acids, and metabolites [75]. The lipid A moiety of LPS exhibits significant strain-specific variation, and its function is highly dependent on lipid A structure. Classical hexa-acylated lipid A potently activates the TLR4/MD-2 complex, recruits MyD88 and TRIF, induces NF-κB and IRF3 activation, drives the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, and promotes M1 polarization [84–86]. In contrast, hypo-acylated lipid A exhibits reduced binding affinity for TLR4/MD-2, fails to effectively trigger receptor dimerization, and leads to attenuated NF-κB activation; such structures may preferentially activate the TRIF-dependent IRF3 pathway, inducing type I interferon and IL-10 expression, thereby favoring M2 polarization [84, 85, 87, 88]. However, the lipid A of Bradyrhizobium is not hypo-acylated but rather possesses a unique hyper-acylated structure. Mass spectrometry analysis of the Bradyrhizobium ORS285 rfaL mutant revealed a hyper-acylated lipid A pattern [89], and the lipid A of Bradyrhizobium BTAi1 strain is primarily a mixture of hexa- and hepta-acylated species [90]. Interestingly, this hyper-acylated lipid A exerts anti-inflammatory effects by antagonizing TLR4 [90], highlighting the distinctness of its mechanism. Therefore, characterizing the acylation status of lipid A in Bradyrhizobium OMVs is of great significance. Furthermore, OMV-derived phospholipids and metabolites may also contribute to immunomodulation [91, 92]. We propose that the immunomodulatory effects of OMVs result from the synergistic action of multiple components. Consequently, elucidating the composition of anti-inflammatory components in OMVs from this strain is crucial for the development of therapeutic agents.

The present study has several limitations. First, meibum collection targets the meibomian gland duct microenvironment in MGD, not the generalized ocular surface. Therefore, direct comparisons with findings from conjunctival swab studies should be made with caution. Additionally, skin swab controls were not included in this study. Second, extraction blanks and no-template controls were not incorporated into the 16S rRNA sequencing workflow; only comparisons between DED patients and healthy control samples were performed. Third, functional predictions were performed using PICRUSt2 rather than metagenomic or metatranscriptomic sequencing. The KEGG pathway enrichment results were derived from database annotations and require independent experimental validation. Fourth, the Bradyrhizobium strain used in the in vitro experiments was a soil-derived model strain (ATCC 43045) rather than a clinical isolate from the ocular surface. Functional phenotypes, including OMV composition and bioactivity, may vary among strains from different ecological niches. Therefore, caution should be exercised when extrapolating the present findings to ocular surface-colonizing strains. Additionally, the lack of a systematic investigation of the dose–response relationship of OMVs represents a limitation of the current study. Fifth, no DED animal model was established to validate the in vivo efficacy of OMVs. Although in vitro experiments have demonstrated their immunomodulatory effects on macrophage polarization, their actual effects within the complex ocular surface microenvironment, pharmacokinetic profiles, and potential adverse effects require further evaluation through in vivo studies. Sixth, while THP-1-derived macrophages are a commonly used cell model in immunological research, they differ from in situ ocular surface macrophages and dendritic cells in terms of phenotype, function, and responsiveness to the microenvironment. Due to resource and time constraints, primary human corneal or conjunctival epithelial cells were not used, nor was an epithelium-macrophage co-culture system established. Future studies should prioritize these more physiologically relevant models to provide evidence with greater translational value.

Conclusion

Clinical cohort analysis revealed that DED is associated with sleep disturbances, other systemic diseases (OSD), Ocular Surface Disease Index (OSDI) scores, symptomatic presentation, Meibomian gland blockage, reduced tear film break-up time (BUT), as well as environmental factors including humidity and radiation exposure. Building upon these clinical insights, this study further identifies meibum dysbiosis in DED, characterized by reduced Bradyrhizobium and enriched Ralstonia. A six-genus panel distinguished DED from healthy eyes with good accuracy. Mechanistically, Bradyrhizobium OMVs promoted an M2 anti-inflammatory phenotype in vitro, suggesting a protective role. These findings position the ocular microbiome as a modulator of local immunity and support further exploration of microbiome-based diagnostics and therapies in DED.
