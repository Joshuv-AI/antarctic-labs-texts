Improving rare-class detection in deep-sea imagery via generative augmentation with stable diffusion
Authors: Junlan Deng, Mi Duan, Dingbang Wei, Wei Song, Xuebao He, Jianxin Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMegabenthos play a critical role in maintaining deep-sea ecosystem stability, making accurate detection important for deep-sea conservation. However, the high cost of deep-sea exploration and the long-tailed distribution of available datasets lead to severe data scarcity for rare species, limiting deep-sea benthos detection. To address this challenge, we propose a data augmentation framework based on Stable Diffusion (SD) and ControlNet. Specifically, we fine-tune a pretrained SD model using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) to synthesize images of rare benthos, and leverage ControlNet to composite the generated targets into deep-sea backgrounds with controllable layouts and automatic bounding-box annotation. We constructed two megabenthos datasets collected using an optically tethered underwater vehicle (OTV) and an autonomous underwater vehicle (AUV), covering 16 biological categories; data augmentation was applied to 7 rare species with the fewest samples. The generated images achieved a Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) of 117.11 and an Inception Score (IS) of 4.97. When combined with real data for RT-DETR training, the augmentation strategy increased the AP50-95 and AP50 on the OTV dataset to 45.2% and 75.2%, representing improvements of 3.7% and 6.1% over the baseline. Similarly, on the AUV dataset, it increased the AP50-95 and AP50 to 36.8% and 64.7%, yielding enhancements of 2.2% and 4.2% over the baseline. Gains were especially pronounced for tail classes, with AP50-95 increased by 23.6% and 21.9% for Octopus and Bryozoa on the OTV dataset, and by 15.1% and 14.6% for Bryozoa and Hydrozoa on the AUV dataset. Moreover, the proposed approach outperforms traditional augmented methods by 1.6% and 0.8% in AP50-95 on the OTV and AUV datasets, respectively, indicating its utility for improving detection in deep-sea megabenthic surveys.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-45732-6.Subject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Ocean sciences

Introduction

Megabenthos are key components of deep-sea ecosystems, and possess unique ecological and genetic value1,2. As deep-sea mining technology matures, the need to detect and conserve deep-sea benthos has become increasingly urgent3,4. Object detection algorithms applied to survey imagery can automate species identification and abundance estimation, thereby supporting ecological monitoring, marine protected area planning and management, and impact assessment and mitigation for mining activities in fragile deep-sea environments5,6. For example, recent works such as UDD-YOLO7, FP-YOLO8, Mussel-YOLO9, and YOLOv10-SEA10 have improved YOLO models for the automated monitoring of targets in aquatic environments, thereby supporting ecological conservation and resource management. However, these data-driven models all require large-scale, diverse, and well-annotated datasets to drive their learning.

Acquiring deep-sea optical imagery remains extremely costly, typically relying on dedicated expeditions and platforms such as an autonomous underwater vehicle (AUV) and an optically tethered underwater vehicle (OTV) to depths exceeding 4000 m11. In addition, megabenthos are relatively sparsely distributed across most deep-sea regions, particularly in specialized habitats such as polymetallic nodule fields12. A small number of common species account for most observations, whereas many taxa are encountered only rarely. As a result, available datasets, including public resources such as FathomNet and the Clarion-Clipperton Zone dataset, exhibit pronounced long-tail distributions and substantial class imbalance13–15.

The scarcity of rare species data substantially limits the performance of deep-sea benthos detectors. During training, class competition biases optimization toward frequent classes, leaving rare species under-optimized and causing rare classes to be misclassified as common classes at inference16,17. Moreover, limited samples heighten the risk of overfitting to specific backgrounds and poses, preventing the model from learning fine-grained features that distinguish rare taxa. Although automated detection of marine megabenthos has progressed, methods explicitly designed to improve the detection of rare species remain relatively underexplored18–21.

Data augmentation, loss reweighting, decoupled training, and contrastive learning are commonly used to address class imbalance and few shots in computer vision18–22. Loss reweighting assigns higher loss weights to rare classes during training, typically by scaling each class’s loss inversely with its sample frequency or prediction confidence23. RT-DETR incorporates focal loss, which downweights easy examples and upweights hard examples24. Therefore, RT-DETR was adopted as the object detection model in this study. Methods such as decoupled training and contrastive learning can partially alleviate these issues, but performance can be further improved by incorporating data augmentation, particularly when rare classes have too few samples to adequately capture object characteristics25–27. Despite the effectiveness of modern augmentation strategies in many application domains, their use for deep-sea biological object detection remains relatively underexplored.

In recent years, data augmentation has received substantial attention as an effective way to improve downstream performance. Data augmentation refers to generating additional samples by applying transformations to existing data, thereby increasing dataset size and diversity without further data collection. Existing approaches can be broadly categorized into traditional augmentation and generative-model-based augmentation. Traditional methods include geometric transformations (e.g., random flipping, scaling, cropping28, random erasing29, and perspective transformation30). They also include photometric adjustments such as changes in contrast, sharpness, blur, hue, saturation, and brightness, as well as sample-mixing operations such as CutMix31 and Mosaic32. Although traditional methods are simple and computationally efficient, the diversity they introduce is inherently limited. Consequently, recent research has increasingly shifted toward generative model-based data augmentation.

Generative model-based data augmentation uses neural networks to learn data distributions and synthesize new samples. Representative generation models include variational autoencoders (VAE), Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)33, and their variants, such as CycleGAN34, as well as diffusion models such as Stable Diffusion (SD)35. GAN models can generate realistic images via adversarial training, but often struggle with stable training and controllable generation of complex scenes. Diffusion models formulate image generation as a Markov chain that progressively denoises data, thereby improving training stability and enabling more controllable convergence.

Image augmentation has become an essential strategy in mitigating data scarcity in specialized domains such as transportation, security, medicine, and remote sensing36–43. For example, Liu et al. applied three augmentation strategies to generate nighttime infrared surveillance data, raising pedestrian detection mAP from 76.0% to 85.4%44. Lee et al. proposed RDAGAN to enhance fire detection with YOLOv545. Yigit and Can synthesized anomalous data under low-light and hazy road conditions using the SD model, which improved road hazard warning in autonomous driving46. Collectively, these works demonstrate the effectiveness of augmentation in enhancing detection performance.

In the field of underwater object detection, data augmentation has been extensively employed to mitigate the issue of insufficient training samples47–51. Regarding data augmentation for underwater backgrounds and imaging conditions, Noh et al. achieved cross-domain data augmentation by translating aboveground images into virtual underwater images52. Huang et al. introduced turbulence perturbations, viewpoint variations, and nonuniform illumination to augment images of sea cucumbers, sea urchins, and scallops, improving the detection accuracy from 49.76% to 59.93%53. Peng et al. developed FocusAugment, which separates foreground and background regions based on the blur induced by underwater light scattering and adjusts their brightness contrast to effectively augment underwater biological datasets54. Teng et al. proposed a UNIT-based cross-domain translation model to mutually transform turbid and clear underwater images55. Collectively, these methods primarily enhance data diversity by modeling variations in background appearance and imaging conditions, while controlled augmentation of biological foreground objects has received comparatively limited attention.

With respect to the generative synthesis of biological foreground objects, Dubrovinskaya et al. utilized the SD model fine-tuned with DreamBooth to generate images of rare fish species, thereby expanding high-quality image datasets for freshwater fish56. Furthermore, Zhang et al. proposed an anemone image generation model based on Diffusion-Stylegan2, addressing the scarcity of anemone datasets57. Although these studies have made progress in improving generation quality, they primarily focus on the foreground object itself, without systematically addressing the issues of foreground–background fusion and spatial layout control for downstream object detection. Regarding the integration of foreground and background data, Liu et al. cropped biological foreground targets from open-sea farm datasets and employed PoissonGAN to composite them into background images, which elevated the mAP50 from 58.1% to 80.8%58. However, this method relies exclusively on real foreground samples, which restricts its capability to generate novel biological poses and expand the feature space. Therefore, there is a need to develop a generative data augmentation framework tailored for rare deep-sea taxa that jointly enables diverse foreground synthesis and controllable deep-sea background composition, and to validate its effectiveness in improving deep-sea object detection performance.

Methods

Dataset description and preparation

The datasets utilized in this experiment comprise single-frame static images captured by the OTV and AUV within the “Beijing Pioneer Corporation” (BPC) polymetallic nodule contract area in the western Pacific Ocean. The OTV navigated along predesigned survey lines at an altitude of 2–5 m above the seafloor with a speed of 1–2 knots, acquiring images at 30 s intervals. Conversely, the AUV operated at a fixed altitude of 5 m above the seafloor at a speed of 2 knots, capturing images at 10 s intervals. The OTV dataset contains 2499 benthic images with a resolution of 4000 × 3000 pixels, of which 1973 images were allocated for training and 526 for testing. The AUV dataset includes 3680 benthic images with a resolution of 4864 × 3648 pixels, with 2928 images assigned for training and 752 for testing. The western Pacific megabenthos data are classified into 16 biological categories, including Holothurian, Sponge, Shrimp, Anemone, Polychaeta, Fish, Isopoda, Sea star, Ophiuroidea, Crinoidea, Coral, Bryozoa, Echinoidea, Hydrozoa, Enteropneusta, and Octopus. The image backgrounds span diverse seafloor environments, including sedimentary substrates, nodule-dense areas, nodule-sparse areas, and basaltic basement.

The datasets were annotated using LabelImg software in the YOLO format59. The taxonomic labeling primarily referenced the benthic fauna identification guides for the Clarion-Clipperton Fracture Zone (CCZ) polymetallic nodule area in the eastern Pacific Ocean, published by Simon et al. and Amon et al.60,61. To guarantee annotation quality, an annotation consistency verification was implemented: two annotators with backgrounds in marine biology conducted the annotations independently. Subsequently, their respective results were cross-verified to rectify erroneous labels caused by operational errors and to supplement omitted target annotations resulting from visual fatigue. Finally, all annotation results were validated and reviewed by experts from the Third Institute of Oceanography, Ministry of Natural Resources, ensuring the reliability of the ground truth labels.

Figure 1 illustrates the sample size distribution of each species for the two datasets. Both exhibit pronounced class imbalance, particularly in the AUV dataset, where the largest-to-smallest class ratio reaches 177:1. Bounding-box statistics indicate that the object sizes range from < 50 pixels to > 500 pixels, and over 90% of targets occupy less than 0.1% of the image area. Given the substantial differences in acquisition conditions and imaging style (e.g., lighting, suspended-particulate turbidity, and imaging altitude above the seafloor), the RT-DETR model was trained separately on the OTV and AUV datasets.

Per-class sample distributions in the OTV and AUV datasets. (a) OTV dataset. (b) AUV dataset.

In this study, the seven categories exhibiting the lowest number of annotated instances in the training set—namely, Crinoidea, Coral, Bryozoa, Echinoidea, Hydrozoa, Enteropneusta, and Octopus—are defined as rare classes. Within the OTV training set, the number of training instances for each of these seven classes is consistently below 50. Furthermore, upon aggregating the OTV and AUV training sets, these seven taxa continue to constitute the least represented classes, with their respective training instance counts all remaining below 100.

Data augmentation

The workflow proposed in this study is illustrated in Figure 2. Given that benthos occupy an extremely small proportion of the imagery, with over 90% of the target areas accounting for less than 0.1% of the total image area, directly generating full images makes it difficult for generative models to capture fine-grained biological details. To address this, the image generation process in this study is divided into two steps: “foreground generation” and “background outpainting”. In the first stage, foreground images of seven rare deep-sea megabenthos are synthesized using an SD model fine-tuned with Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), and hierarchical prompt design is employed to increase sample diversity62. The foreground samples used for LoRA training were cropped from real imagery using the provided bounding-box annotations. In the second stage, leveraging ControlNet-based outpainting under the condition of layout masks, the synthesized foreground objects are seamlessly composited into deep-sea backgrounds with controllable spatial layouts, while object detection annotations are generated automatically63. Specifically, a LoRA model for generating deep-sea backgrounds is trained to support ControlNet-based background outpainting. The background training images cover diverse illumination conditions, varying camera altitudes, and different substrate environments. During outpainting, text prompts are utilized to ensure consistency between the foreground and background substrates. To ensure realistic target scales, bounding-box sizes in the layout masks are sampled from the size ranges of annotated boxes in the real dataset, and positions are assigned randomly. The layout masks precisely control the size and position of the foreground benthos, providing automatically annotated bounding boxes for object detection. In the third stage, the synthesized images are combined with the real images to form an augmented training set, and the resulting performance gains are evaluated using RT-DETR for deep-sea megabenthos detection. Comparative experiments are conducted to assess the effects of different augmentation strategies on the detection performance, demonstrating the effectiveness of the proposed approach. The code and trained models associated with this paper have been made publicly available at https://github.com/dengjunlan/deepsea-benthos-SD-Augmentation.

Overall workflow of the proposed method.

Inspired by the physical process of diffusion from order to disorder, diffusion models formulate image generation as a reverse denoising process that recovers structured content from random noise. The SD model enhances the standard diffusion architecture by integrating a VAE and a CLIP text encoder into the denoising U-Net backbone, as illustrated in Figure 3a. The VAE compresses high-resolution images into a lower-dimensional latent space, substantially reducing computational cost during denoising training. The CLIP Text Encoder encodes input text prompts into textual embeddings. From these embeddings, key (K) and value (V) vectors are derived and fed into cross-attention layers, where they interact with image feature queries (Q) to compute attention scores. This mechanism enables semantic guidance throughout the entire denoising process.

In this study, the SDXL 1.0 model is adopted as the pretrained model to transfer its generalized feature generation capabilities to the deep-sea domain. The SDXL model encompasses approximately 6.6 billion parameters and typically requires at least 8 GB of GPU memory for inference64. This model was originally trained on a large-scale natural image dataset at a resolution of 1024 × 1024, and its full-parameter fine-tuning is inherently contingent upon massive training datasets65. Given the severe scarcity of deep-sea data, executing full-parameter fine-tuning is impractical. Consequently, this research employs LoRA to conduct parameter-efficient fine-tuning on the SDXL model. By updating fewer than 1% of the model parameters, we successfully accomplish the synthesis of rare deep-sea species images alongside deep-sea background images. The fine-tuning of the SDXL model utilizes different resolution configurations across the two stages of the pipeline. Specifically, both the training and inference resolutions for the biological foreground are set to 512 × 512, which adequately satisfies the application requirements. A resolution of 1280 × 960 is adopted for background outpainting to maintain an aspect ratio consistent with the real data, while simultaneously fulfilling the image resolution prerequisites for subsequent object detection training.

Using the bounding-box annotations provided by the real object-detection dataset, 229 foreground instances of rare species were initially extracted. While the SD model can maintain semantic consistency via text prompts, natural language alone offers limited control over the precise spatial structure of generated images. ControlNet mitigates this limitation by adding an additional conditional encoding branch to the SD model, integrating mask images used for layout control into the diffusion denoising process63. This mechanism enables layout-controlled image outpainting while preserving semantic control. Specifically, ControlNet freezes the pretrained U-Net weights of the original SD model to retain its visual priors, and introduces a trainable copy of its encoder as a condition-processing pathway. The features extracted from the layout mask image through this branch are projected through 1 × 1 zero-initialized convolution layers. These features are subsequently concatenated along the channel dimension with the background image features extracted by the original U-Net encoder, and the fused features are then fed into the original U-Net decoder to drive the layout-controlled background image generation.

To ensure a smooth boundary transition between the generated background and the foreground, a feathering operation was applied to the layout masks. This process converts the binary (0/1) hard-edged masks into continuous (0–1) soft-edged masks, thereby achieving smoother foreground–background boundaries. To ensure illumination consistency between the generated background and the foreground, a “fill” strategy was adopted for the initial padding of the masked region, where the background mask area was prefilled using the blurred content of the foreground. Compared to settings without “fill” preprocessing, this strategy enables the generated background to exhibit higher consistency with the foreground in terms of illumination and color tone. Additionally, the "only masked padding" parameter was set to 32, meaning that the mask is dilated outward by 32 pixels to jointly participate in the diffusion denoising process, which enhances the continuity of shadows and textures. The resolution of the synthesized images was uniformly set to 1980 × 960, maintaining the aspect ratio of the original images and satisfying the resolution requirements for subsequent object detection tasks. Other parameter configurations for ControlNet-based image outpainting are detailed in Supplementary Table S3.

Object detection

The object detection model was RT-DETR, with ResNet-18 as the backbone for target localization and category classification. The RT-DETR model was trained with the AdamW optimizer at an initial learning rate of 1 × 10–4. Loss weights were set to 7.5 for box regression, 0.5 for classification, and 1.5 for distribution focal loss.

Results

Data augmentation results

Foreground images for the seven rare categories were generated using the trained LoRA adapters and text prompts, with an average generation time of approximately 2 s per image. During the post-generation stage, invalid images with noticeable artifacts were manually removed. This included filtering out 48 Enteropneusta images with duplicated tail structures and 32 Echinoidea images featuring grayish-black instead of normal white spines. Finally, 200 high-quality foreground images per rare class (1,400 images in total) were retained for the subsequent foreground-background composition. These augmented images were uniformly utilized to expand the OTV and AUV datasets, aiming to boost the sample diversity of rare classes and the model’s generalization ability. Figure 4 shows example images of the seven rare deep-sea megabenthos images generated by the LoRA‑adapted SD model, illustrating clear contours and fine-grained details consistent with the visual traits of deep-sea organisms.

Comparative examples from real and synthesized imagery. (a) Real images. (b) Synthesized images. The additional octopus exemplars showcase the output diversity achievable through varied text prompts.

We computed image quality evaluation metrics, including FID and IS, based on 1,400 synthesized foreground biological images, alongside 196 and 156 real foreground images of rare species from the OTV and AUV datasets, respectively. As presented in Table 1, the KID score between the synthetic dataset and the OTV dataset is 0.028, indicating a high degree of similarity in the feature distributions of the generated and real images. The FID score between the synthetic dataset and the AUV dataset is 160.17, which is comparatively higher than the FID score associated with the OTV dataset. This discrepancy may stem from the tendency of SD models to learn and reproduce more discriminative, visually sharp features during training, whereas AUV imagery—captured at a higher altitude above the seafloor—often exhibits lower effective resolution and blurred fine details. Consequently, this introduces a domain shift in feature sharpness between the synthetic and real images. Regarding semantic consistency, the synthetic OTV dataset yields a CMMD score of 0.73 and a CLIP Score of 0.71, demonstrating strong semantic alignment between the synthesized and real images.

The synthetic images achieve an IS of 4.97, improving over the original datasets (IS = 3.77 for OTV and 3.41 for AUV). This increase indicates that the generative model effectively enhances data diversity. Figure 4 illustrates the diversity of the generated images, showing that the model produces feature combinations absent from the original datasets. For example, the original datasets contained only white octopuses in a contracted state and yellow octopuses in an umbrella spread posture, whereas the synthetic dataset included white octopuses in the umbrella spread posture, yielding novel combinations of pose and morphology. Diversity was further promoted through prompt variation (e.g., “dark environment,” “lateral view,” and “different orientations”), which guided controllable attributes such as coloration, illumination, and camera viewpoint.

While the first-stage outputs can be used directly for image classification, they are less suitable for object detection due to the lack of background and spatial context. Accordingly, in the second stage, layout control and background context were integrated into the 1,400 previously generated benthic images. The training data for this stage included diverse deep-sea background types, including sediment substrate, polymetallic nodule fields, and basaltic substrates. During inference, layout masks, synthetic foreground images, and background‑conditioning text prompts were provided as inputs to guide the generation of ControlNet. To ensure realistic target scales, bounding-box sizes in the layout masks were sampled from the size ranges of annotated boxes in the real dataset, and positions were assigned randomly. This strategy preserves the scale distribution observed in survey imagery.

Figure 5 shows representative results, where megabenthos are placed according to the layout mask and blended into the generated backgrounds. The results indicate that the proposed method can generate images with controllable spatial layouts and visually coherent backgrounds. Lighting, shading, and texture are consistent with real deep-sea observations, and the benthos are seamlessly integrated into seabed environments. For elongated categories such as Coral and Crinoidea, automatically generated bounding boxes often exceed their actual narrow dimensions, encompassing excessive background. To improve detection training, approximately 20% of the bounding boxes were manually optimized to tightly enclose the megabenthos.

Object detection results

The generated images were used to augment the training dataset to evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed method for rare deep-sea benthic detection. As a baseline, RT-DETR was trained using only the original real images to establish the reference performance. For augmentation, we added 1,400 synthetic images for the seven species with the smallest sample sizes to the real training set, while keeping the real test set unchanged to ensure a fair comparison. Deep-learning-based object detection training is influenced by multiple sources of stochasticity, including random initialization and data shuffling71–73. To ensure a fair comparison, we independently repeated the training for both the baseline and the augmented experiments by solely altering the random seed (seed = 0, 1, 2), while keeping the network architecture, hyperparameters, and training strategies strictly identical. Table 2 presents the performance evaluation of the object detection model RT-DETR on the OTV and AUV datasets using the proposed data augmentation method, detailing the mean () and standard deviation () of each metric across the three random seeds. Notably, the Precision and Recall metrics were computed with both the Intersection over Union (IoU) and confidence thresholds set to 0.5. The results demonstrate that the AP50-95, AP50, AP75, and AR50-95 for every individual randomized trial in the augmented experiments consistently surpass the average values of the baseline experiments. On the AUV dataset, paired t-test results based on identical random seeds indicate that the performance improvements of the augmented model over the baseline model are statistically significant (AP50-95: p = 0.016, AP50: p = 0.048, both p < 0.05). This confirms that the performance enhancements yielded by the proposed data augmentation strategy possess strong stability and reproducibility. Specifically, on the OTV dataset, the overall AP50-95 increased from 41.5% to 45.2% (an improvement of 3.7%); the AP50 rose from 69.1% to 75.2% (an increase of 6.1%); and the AR50-95 grew from 55.2% to 58.0% (a gain of 2.8%). Furthermore, the precision improved from 77.4% to 83.8%, while its standard deviation decreased from 4.7% to 1.2%. This indicates that data augmentation can effectively mitigate false positives and that the performance discrepancies among models trained with different random seeds are marginal. For the AUV dataset, the application of SD model-based data augmentation yielded increases of 2.2% in AP50-95, 4.2% in AP50, and 4.3% in AR50-95. However, the improvement in recall on the AUV dataset was relatively limited, showing only a 0.4% increase, which may be attributable to a higher proportion of small objects and increased image blur in the AUV dataset. Furthermore, Table 3 reports the improvements of tail classes in terms of AP50-95, AP50, and AR50-95, with all values averaged over multiple random runs. The results reveal that on the OTV dataset, the most significant improvements are observed for Bryozoa (+ 21.9% AP50-95) and Octopus (+ 23.6% AP50-95). On the AUV dataset, Bryozoa (+ 15.1% AP50-95) and Hydrozoa (+ 14.6% AP50-95) exhibit the most substantial gains. This improvement can be attributed to the fact that the generative model synthesizes additional data to yield a more balanced data distribution within the training set. Consequently, this expands the feature space of the rare categories, enabling the detector to learn more discriminative features of these underrepresented taxa.

Ablation experiment

To investigate the specific contributions of each component within the data augmentation framework, we conducted an ablation experiment on the proposed two-stage data augmentation strategy. As presented in Table 5, the results indicate that both the first-stage LoRA-based foreground generation and the second-stage ControlNet-based background generation exert a positive impact on model performance. This demonstrates that the morphological augmentation of rare-class foregrounds is effective for model learning. Concurrently, the model employing the foreground–background composition strategy outperforms the data augmentation method utilizing solely foreground generation. Compared with the foreground-only strategy, incorporating synthesized backgrounds improves AR50-95 by 0.7% on the OTV dataset and by 1.5% on the AUV dataset, suggesting that the photorealistic backgrounds synthesized by ControlNet enhance the localization quality of the detection bounding boxes. This is likely attributable to the fact that "Copy-Paste" operations using only LoRA-generated foregrounds tend to introduce unnatural edges and illumination inconsistencies, which hinder accurate boundary inference. In contrast, ControlNet-based background generation improves contextual consistency, which facilitates the learning of more precise boundaries.

Comparative experiment

To further evaluate the proposed augmentation strategy, we compared it against traditional data augmentation methods. Traditional single-sample data augmentation was implemented utilizing the imgaug library, which applied rotation, scaling, blurring, color perturbation, and affine transformations to the real image. To ensure a fair comparison, each method expanded the same number of augmented images based on the initial 1,973 training samples; representative examples are provided in Fig. S1. Furthermore, traditional multisample data augmentation methods were implemented based on the Mosaic, Mixup, and Copy-Paste configurations within the Ultralytics library. Specifically, the Mosaic probability was set to 0.5, concatenating four images to enhance background complexity and object scale diversity, while retaining a 50% probability of utilizing authentic images for training. The Mixup probability was configured at 0.1, employing lightweight regularization to maintain sensitivity to real test data. Lastly, the Copy-Paste probability was set to 0.15 to balance between increasing effective training samples and maintaining scene realism.

Table 6 reports the test performance of the RT-DETR model on the OTV dataset under varying data augmentation strategies. The results demonstrate that the proposed method outperforms various traditional data augmentation approaches and their combinations. Among the traditional methods, the "Scale + Shear" strategy yields the optimal efficacy, reaching 43.6% AP50-95 and 73.3% AP50, corresponding to improvements of 2.1% and 4.2% over the baseline, respectively. Mosaic ranks second, improving AP50-95 by 1.2% relative to the baseline. Relative to the optimal traditional scheme, the proposed method achieves further enhancements of 1.6% and 1.9% in AP50-95 and AP50, respectively. This improvement is primarily attributable to the high visual fidelity and diversity of the synthesized samples. The generated samples encompass novel poses, backgrounds, and illumination conditions that are absent from the original training set, thereby expanding the feature space and bolstering the robustness and generalization capabilities of the deep-sea megabenthos detection model.

Among single-sample data augmentation methods, the Blur operation conversely decreased the AP50-95. This degradation is likely attributable to the inherent blurriness of underwater images; applying further Gaussian blur entails the loss of crucial high-frequency texture information, thereby inducing interclass confusion and leading to a decline in model performance. Regarding multisample data augmentation methods, although the Mixup strategy elevated the AR50-95 metric, the overall AP50-95 metrics experienced a decline. This phenomenon can be attributed to the inherent scattering and blurring present in underwater imagery; by fusing two already blurred images via pixel-level weighted combinations, Mixup inadvertently diminishes the discriminative capacity of the model. As indicated in Table 6, combining Scale, Shear, and Saturation, as well as integrating Mosaic, Copy-Paste, and Mixup, caused the AP50-95 to regress to 41.7% in both scenarios. This demonstrates that in underwater scenarios characterized by constrained image quality, applying more augmentation operations does not necessarily yield superior results. Excessive or discordant combinations of transformations can disrupt image semantics and introduce noise. This underlying pattern was similarly corroborated on the AUV dataset. On the AUV dataset, relative to the optimal traditional data augmentation method (Shear), the proposed generative augmentation approach achieved further enhancements of 0.8% and 1.1% in the AP50-95 and AP50 metrics, respectively.

Discussion

Confidence-threshold sensitivity

Figure 8a illustrates the Precision-Recall curves on the OTV dataset before and after the application of the proposed data augmentation strategy. Compared to the baseline model (dashed lines), the curves of the generative augmented model (solid lines) exhibit an overall shift toward the top-right corner, encompassing a larger area under the curve. This indicates that the proposed data augmentation strategy achieves higher precision at the same recall level, realizing a superior trade-off between precision and recall. Figure 8b reports the F1 score across different confidence thresholds, where F1 is defined as the harmonic mean of precision and recall. The results demonstrate that within the threshold range of approximately 0–0.7, the F1 scores of the augmented model consistently surpass those of the baseline model. Furthermore, the F1 curve remains relatively flat within the 0.2–0.7 range, suggesting that the model possesses strong robustness to confidence threshold settings within this interval. This provides a practical reference range for threshold selection during deployment. Figure 8c and d present the variation of precision and recall with respect to the confidence threshold. Within the 0–0.7 range, the augmented model simultaneously attains higher precision and recall, indicating that both false positives and false negatives are reduced under the same threshold setting. However, as the threshold increases further (> 0.7), the recall of the augmented model decreases more rapidly, suggesting a more conservative confidence calibration, whereby a proportion of true positives receive confidence scores below the selected threshold.

Error breakdown

To comprehensively analyze the sources of performance improvement, we utilized the TIDE toolbox to decompose object detection errors into six distinct categories: classification error (Cls), localization error (Loc), both classification and localization error (Both), duplicate detection error (Dupe), background error (Bkg), and missed ground truth error (Miss)74. Specifically, Cls refers to errors where the object is localized correctly but classified incorrectly, whereas Loc denotes instances where the object is classified correctly but the IoU falls below the prescribed threshold. Figure 9 illustrates the error distributions for both the baseline and the proposed data augmentation models across the OTV and AUV datasets. The metric dAP indicates the expected AP improvement if a given error type was eliminated; a larger value indicates a more substantial impact of that error category on overall performance. The results show that generative augmentation substantially reduces Cls. On the OTV dataset, Cls decreases from 16.31 ± 1.46 to 10.68 ± 1.37, and on the AUV dataset, Cls decreases from 24.28 ± 0.46 to 21.45 ± 0.79. This indicates that the overall performance enhancement is primarily attributable to improved classification accuracy; generative data augmentation effectively bolsters interclass discriminability, thereby elevating the model’s capacity for accurate classification. The relatively slight increase in Loc observed on the OTV dataset may result from some of the newly detected bounding boxes lacking sufficient bounding precision under stringent IoU thresholds. Despite this slight increase in Loc, the pronounced reduction in Cls offsets the negative effect of imperfect box alignment, resulting in an overall improvement in detection performance. Future work will focus on improving localization accuracy under deep-sea conditions, where small target sizes and challenging imaging environments remain key obstacles.

Cross-domain experiment

Table 2 and Fig. 6 demonstrate that the performance gains achieved by the data augmentation method on the AUV dataset are significantly lower than those on the OTV dataset. It is hypothesized that this phenomenon can be attributed to the domain shift across disparate datasets. To quantify the extent of domain shift, we conducted cross-domain evaluations, with the results presented in Table 7. The experiments demonstrate that when the model is trained on the augmented OTV dataset and tested on the AUV dataset, its AP50-95, AP50, and AR50-95 metrics decreased by 24.3 points, 33.6 points and 20 points, respectively. Conversely, in the reverse testing scenario, these metrics dropped by 12.2 points, 18.9 points and 8.9 points, respectively. These findings indicate that even when detecting identical target categories within the same marine region, variations in factors such as underwater light scattering degrees and illumination patterns across different imaging platforms introduce significant domain shifts, which consequently lead to a substantial degradation in model performance during cross-domain testing. Furthermore, the generalization observed in the cross-domain experiments exhibits notable asymmetry. The performance drop from OTV to AUV is considerably larger than that from AUV to OTV. A plausible explanation is that models trained on OTV imagery tend to rely more on fine-grained texture cues, which become less reliable in AUV imagery due to longer imaging distances, smaller apparent target sizes, and more severe color distortions. In contrast, models trained on AUV data learn coarser shape and contour features, thereby acquiring relatively stronger cross-domain adaptability. Future work could explore deconvolution75, dilated convolution76, slice-aided inference (SAHI)77, and multiscale feature fusion schemes to improve recall under conditions involving small objects, low image clarity, and interference from suspended particulates78,79.

Effect of augmentation size

To systematically evaluate the impact of the augmented scale on the detection performance, we augmented the original OTV training set by adding 175, 350, 700, and 1,400 synthesized images, corresponding to 1×, 2×, 4×, and 8× the number of rare-class samples, respectively. All experiments were conducted with a fixed random seed (seed = 0) to enable a controlled comparison. The overall AP50-95 metrics for the baseline and the four augmentation settings were 42.5%, 44.2%, 46.5%, 46.9%, and 46.8%, respectively, with the 4× augmentation setting (+ 700 images) achieving the optimal performance. This suggests that the 4× scale may represent a practical trade-off, providing sufficient diversity for most classes while avoiding excessive redundancy or overfitting to contextual patterns introduced by synthetic data. Figure 10 further analyzes the AP50-95 and AR50-95 metrics for rare taxa under different augmentation scales. As the amount of augmented data increases, the AP50-95 and AR50-95 metrics for most categories exhibit an initial upward trend followed by a marginal decline. Notably, Octopus exhibited the most pronounced improvement, with a rapid improvement from the 1× to 2× scale, a plateau from the 2× to 4× scale, and no further benefit at the 8× scale. This phenomenon suggests that an excessive quantity of synthetic images leads to the saturation of data diversity, consequently inducing overfitting to the contextual backgrounds. In contrast, Bryozoa attained its peak performance at a 1× expansion scale. This is likely attributable to the relatively simpler morphological structure of Bryozoa compared to Octopus, which enables its sample diversity to reach saturation more readily.

Future prospects

In this study, to prevent feature confusion and avoid generating unrealistic hybrid samples that mix characteristics of different species, we independently trained a separate LoRA model for each biological category. While training a dedicated LoRA model for each species helps mitigate feature confusion, the additional overhead associated with model switching may increase inference latency. To improve inference efficiency in future work, an approach based on knowledge distillation could be adopted80,81. Specifically, the ensemble of high-quality, class-specific LoRA models can serve as teachers. Through soft labeling or feature alignment, the species-specific knowledge extracted by these models can be distilled into a single, more efficient student model. Additionally, we plan to explore more deep sea biological datasets and extend validation studies on biological object detection to other typical marine regions, thereby further assessing the practical applicability of the proposed framework.

Conclusion

This work applies the SD model to deep-sea megabenthos imagery to address data scarcity and class imbalance arising from the high cost of deep-sea surveys, thereby improving the robustness and generalization of downstream object detectors. Using LoRA fine-tuned SD models, 1,400 images of rare deep-sea benthos were generated that exhibit both diversity and semantic consistency. Compared with the OTV dataset, the FID, KID, and IS values of the generated images were 117.11, 0.028, and 4.97, respectively, and the CMMD and CLIP Score values were 0.73 and 0.71, respectively. Qualitative inspection by domain experts and quantitative evaluation using established metrics jointly indicate that the synthesized images are visually plausible and of adequate quality for data augmentation. The evaluated quality of both the synthetic and AUV datasets is lower than that of the OTV dataset, and the resulting detection performance improvements on the AUV dataset are similarly less pronounced. This demonstrates that a higher consistency in both feature and semantic distributions between the synthetic and real data generally yields more stable detection gains from augmentation.

Based on ControlNet’s conditional guidance mechanism, a conditional background-generation model for deep-sea scenes was developed that supports layout-controlled synthesis, increasing spatial flexibility and reducing manual annotation effort. Compared with the baseline trained only on real images, augmenting the training set with generated data improves performance on the OTV dataset by 3.7% in AP50-95, 6.1% in AP50, and 2.8% in AR50-95, reaching 45.2%, 75.2%, and 58.0%, respectively. On the AUV dataset, the AP50-95, AP50, and AR50-95 increase by 2.2%, 4.2%, and 4.3%, reaching 36.8%, 64.7%, and 49.2% respectively. Error breakdown analysis demonstrates that the performance gains stem mainly from improved classification accuracy, while the bounding box localization precision warrants further improvement in future work. The cross-domain evaluations reveal an inherent domain shift between the OTV and AUV datasets, with the transfer of models from the AUV to the OTV dataset exhibiting better generalization than the reverse transfer.

Compared with traditional augmentation techniques and their combination (e.g., Blur, Scale, Shear,Color perturbation, Mosaic, Mixup, and Copy-Paste), our strategy achieves further gains of 1.6%, 1.9% and 1.4% in AP50-95, AP50 and AR50-95 on the OTV dataset, demonstrating stronger competitiveness over traditional augmentation methods. Evaluations across different augmentation volumes reveal that the performance enhancements initially rise and subsequently saturate as the augmentation scale increases. This suggests that once the feature diversity of the dataset becomes saturated, incorporating additional samples provides limited benefits to the model’s capabilities. Overall, the proposed framework provides a practical approach to increasing data availability for deep-sea benthos detection and demonstrates the potential of generative AI to support deep-sea survey and analysis workflows.
