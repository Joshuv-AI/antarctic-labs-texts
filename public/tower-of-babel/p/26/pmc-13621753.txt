Hypereosinophilic syndrome mimicking gastric malignancy: considerations on the challenging diagnosis—a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Yuzhi Liu, Hailin Yan, Qing Lu, Jinlin Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHypereosinophilic syndromes (HES) are rare disorders characterized by the abnormal accumulation of eosinophils, which can lead to organ damage. The clinical manifestations are highly variable and can sometimes mimic tumors, presenting significant diagnostic challenges. This case report emphasizes the difficulty of diagnosing HES, particularly when it presents as a gastric pseudotumor, and the essential role of pathological examination in guiding diagnosis and treatment.Case presentationA 51-year-old Chinese Han female with a history of elevated eosinophils presented with epigastric pain, weight loss, and fatigue. Imaging suggested a gastric malignancy with possible metastasis. Despite these findings, biopsies revealed eosinophilic infiltration without malignancy. Based on persistent eosinophilia and multi-organ involvement, HES was diagnosed. The patient was treated with corticosteroids, resulting in significant clinical improvement, confirmed by follow-up imaging and endoscopic examination.ConclusionsThis case highlights the importance of distinguishing HES from malignancies to avoid unnecessary surgical interventions. Early recognition and appropriate corticosteroid treatment can lead to significant improvement, underscoring the importance of careful diagnostic evaluation in cases with ambiguous clinical presentations.

Introduction

Hypereosinophilic syndromes (HES) represent a rare spectrum of disorders characterized by persistent hypereosinophilia, classically defined as an absolute eosinophil count exceeding 1500/μL, and/or tissue eosinophilic infiltration, together with eosinophil-mediated end-organ damage after exclusion of relevant secondary and neoplastic causes [1]. Precisely determining the prevalence of each variant is difficult given the infrequent occurrence and diverse clinical presentations [2]. Clinical manifestations of HES exhibit a wide range, spanning from asymptomatic cases to severe, life-threatening conditions affecting organs like the skin, lungs, heart, gastrointestinal tract, and nervous system [3]. Management focuses on reducing eosinophil levels and symptoms, primarily through systemic corticosteroids [2].

Diagnosing HES poses challenges due to its rarity and nonspecific clinical or imaging findings, leading to potential misdiagnosis or inappropriate treatments. An incorrect diagnosis carries the risk of overtreatment, such as unnecessary surgical procedures, or delayed treatment, possibly resulting in severe consequences like thromboembolism, cardiopulmonary dysfunction, and neurological sequelae [4]. This article presents a case of HES mimicking gastric malignancy that responded to oral corticosteroids treatment, emphasizing the complexities of preoperative diagnosis and underscoring the crucial role of pathology and therapeutic assessment. Additionally, we reviewed similar cases, summarizing their clinical, laboratory, imaging, histopathological, and therapeutic features to raise awareness of this rare condition and promote timely, accurate treatment.

Case presentation

A 51-year-old Chinese Han female presented with an 8-month history of severe epigastric pain, a 6-kg weight loss, fatigue, hemoptysis, and elevated eosinophils. The patient and her family had no history of similar illness or autoimmune disease, and she had not taken specific medications such as anticonvulsants, antibiotics, or herbal supplements. Five years prior, she experienced a high fever and right abdominal pain, accompanied by a significant increase in absolute eosinophil count (AEC) of 1147/μL, which accounted for 18% of the white blood cell count (WBC). Multiple low-density masses in the right lobe of the liver were identified as liver abscesses with extensive eosinophil infiltration, without clear evidence of a parasitic infection. She received intravenous ceftriaxone (2 g daily) combined with metronidazole (500 mg every 8 hours) for 14 days. After undergoing treatment for infection control and liver protection, her condition improved, and she was discharged. Since the recurrence of symptoms 8 months ago, she visited local hospitals several times due to recurrent, intolerable epigastric pain. Available laboratory records showed recurrent eosinophilia, with AEC values ranging from 520 to 1520/μL, accounting for 7.4% to 18.6% of the WBC. Representative pretreatment values included AECs of 1110/μL in November 2019, 1520/μL in December 2019, 1320/μL in January 2020, and 1080/μL shortly before corticosteroid treatment. Other available complete blood count parameters, including platelet counts, showed no clinically significant abnormalities; detailed red blood cell indices were not consistently available in the archived records. Gastroscopy and endoscopic ultrasonography (EUS) identified a 4 × 3 cm elevated lesion in the upper stomach, showing congested and swollen mucosa. However, no biopsy was performed due to the lesion's friable appearance and concern for bleeding risk, and it appeared as a hypoechoic solid mass on ultrasound, indicative of a gastric wall malignancy. Subsequent PET/CT imaging showed uneven gastric wall thickening up to 3.5 cm with a maximum SUV of 8.0, and multiple enlarged lymph nodes around the stomach and pancreas, suggesting a primary malignant tumor with potential metastatic involvement. The patient refused the locally proposed surgical intervention and sought further diagnosis and treatment at our hospital.

Upon admission to our hospital, physical examination revealed mild tenderness in the upper abdomen. Laboratory data showed an elevated AEC of 580/μL, which accounted for 9.1% of the WBC. The slightly elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) level was 14 mg/L. The total serum IgE level reached a peak of 1850 IU/mL. Other examinations, including complete blood count, coagulation profiles, parasite identification, hepatic and renal function tests, and urine and protein electrophoresis, were within normal limits. Antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies were negative. Molecular testing for common fusion genes associated with myeloid neoplasms, including BCR::ABL1, BCR::JAK2, ETV6::JAK2, and ETV6::ABL1, was negative. The abdominal contrast-enhanced CT (CECT) showed signs indicative of metastatic gastric carcinoma, including irregular thickening of the gastric walls, diffuse involvement in the lower esophagus and gastric cardia, denser enlarged lymph nodes near the hepatogastric ligament, paraaortic region, and hepatic hilum (Fig. 1a–c). Non-contrast chest CT revealed several irregular nodules with different density ranging from 5–15 mm in the right lung (Fig. 1d). Endoscopic examination showed localized mucosal hyperemia and swelling from the gastric body to the cardia, but gastric biopsies indicated only mild chronic inflammation (Fig. 2a, b). Given the multiple indications of potential gastric malignancy and metastasis, such as significant weight loss, enlarged lymph nodes, irregular lung nodules, and liver calcification, some clinicians initially recommended surgical resection to obtain sufficient tissue specimens for definitive pathological diagnosis, given the discrepancy between malignant imaging features and inconclusive endoscopic biopsies. However, mucosal biopsy pathology did not detect tumor cells (Fig. 2b). EUS-guided fine-needle biopsy (EUS-FNB) core specimen from the gastric wall and an adjacent lymph node revealed extensive eosinophil infiltration without malignant cells (Fig. 2d). Additionally, bone marrow aspiration smear examination, reviewed by a pathologist, showed a mild increase in eosinophils, without morphological evidence of hematologic malignancy or other significant abnormalities. Considering the persistent blood eosinophilia, multi-organ involvement with eosinophil infiltration, and after excluding other diseases, the Multi-Disciplinary Treatment Group advocated for a tentative diagnosis of HES. Instead of surgical therapy, the patient was treated with intravenous methylprednisolone (80 mg/day for 3 days), followed by oral prednisone, tapered from 50 mg/day to 15 mg/day over four months, and monthly cyclophosphamide pulses. This regimen significantly improved her symptoms and lessened lesions in the lungs, stomach, and lymph nodes, as shown in follow-up CT scans and endoscopy (Fig. 3). After intravenous methylprednisolone therapy, the AEC rapidly decreased to 0/μL, accompanied by symptomatic improvement and radiological regression of the gastric wall thickening, pulmonary nodules, and lymphadenopathy. Discussion

HES comprises a group of disorders characterized by hypereosinophilia with eosinophil-mediated organ damage or dysfunction. Classical diagnostic criteria emphasize a peripheral blood AEC above 1500/μL on at least two occasions, whereas refined concepts also recognize tissue eosinophilic infiltration and eosinophil-related organ damage as key diagnostic elements. In the present patient, available records documented one AEC value above 1500/μL, repeated eosinophilia below this threshold, biopsy-proven eosinophilic infiltration in liver and gastric-wall/lymph-node specimens, pulmonary and gastrointestinal involvement, and a rapid hematologic response to corticosteroids. Therefore, although the strict two-sample peripheral-blood threshold was not fully documented in the available records, the integrated clinicopathological findings favored HES after exclusion of alternative secondary and neoplastic causes within the available work-up. The eosinophilia in HES can be primary, secondary, and idiopathic [5]. Primary HES is a result of abnormalities in the myeloid lineage, while secondary eosinophilia is induced by cytokines (such as IL-3, IL-5, and Granulocyte-Macrophage Colony-Stimulating Factor (GM-CSF)) or other mediators produced by cells aside from eosinophils and their precursors [6]. However, most cases are idiopathic. The mechanisms by which elevated eosinophil levels damage tissues include the release of cytokines involved in tissue remodeling and fibrosis, the release of toxic granule products harming epithelial cells and nerves, and the production of lipid mediators mediating smooth muscle contraction and the recruitment of inflammatory cells [7].

Clinical presentations of HES are highly variable, and can involve any organ. In a multicenter series of 188 consecutive patients with HES, the most frequently observed clinical manifestations were dermatologic, pulmonary and gastrointestinal symptoms/signs in 69%, 44% and 38% of patients, respectively [8]. For instance, eosinophilic pneumonias, eosinophilic gastroenteritis, eosinophilic pancreatitis, Löffler’s endocarditis and eosinophilic cystitis could be isolated diseases or served as organ-specific expressions of HES [1]. In very rare cases, HES-related eosinophilic infiltration/damage can mimic tumors or malignancies, resulting in misdiagnosis and inappropriate treatment. Herein, we searched the literature via the PubMed Database for cases with similar situations, and found instances misdiagnosed or suspected as lymphoma in the distal stomach and duodenum, colon cancer with hepatic metastases, pancreatic tumor, and late-stage gastric carcinoma [9–13]. We have summarized the characteristics of these digestive system-involved cases in Table 1, which includes our case as well. Eosinophilic cystitis linked to HES mimicked bladder cancer in a 57-year-old male with severe gait low compliance bladder [14]. The main goal of HES therapy is the sustained reduction of blood and tissue eosinophil levels to prevent end-organ damage. Oral corticosteroids, the first-line treatment, suppress cytokine production and induce eosinophil apoptosis [16]. When intolerable or life-threatening manifestations are present, immediate initiation of high-dose corticosteroid therapy is recommended. The recommended dosage ranges from 1 mg/kg prednisone to 1 g methylprednisolone, depending on the clinical severity. For patients who exhibit obvious signs or symptoms of gastrointestinal involvement, intravenous administration should be considered to ensure absorption [17]. Novel steroid-sparing therapy should be attempted if one encounters steroid failure or relapse. Cyclophosphamide can serve as a cytotoxic and immunosuppressive drug for HES patients by disrupting deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) replication and cellular division, and impairing immune cell functions [17]. Imatinib mesylate, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor, is an effective treatment option especially for patients with Platelet-derived growth factor receptor alpha (FIP1L1-PDGFRA) gene rearrangement by inhibiting abnormal cell proliferation, but should be discontinued if there is no response after 4 to 6 weeks [6]. Antibody therapy targeting cytokines or cell receptors can be used for the treatment of HES. Mepolizumab, an anti-IL-5 agent, reduced HES flares and fatigue severity irrespective of baseline blood eosinophil count and serum interleukin-5 level [18]. Benralizumab, an anti-IL-5 receptor agent, was reported to effectively reduce AEC and relieve symptoms in patients with PDGFRA-negative HES [19]. Secondary-line treatments include interferon-α, hydroxyurea, and other immunosuppressives like azathioprine, cyclosporine A, and methotrexate. The choice of corticosteroids over targeted biologics such as mepolizumab was based on several considerations. First, the rapid symptom progression necessitated immediate intervention, and corticosteroids remain first-line therapy for HES [20]. Second, mepolizumab availability was limited in our regional formulary during the treatment period. However, we acknowledge that biologics may offer steroid-sparing benefits in refractory cases, and this option will be discussed with the patient during long-term follow-up.

Conclusion

We presented a rare case of HES mimicking metastatic gastric cancer with suspected nodal, hepatic, and pulmonary involvement, as indicated by clinical symptoms/signs and findings from abdominal CECT, chest CT, PET/CT, and endoscopic and EUS appearances. The patient was on the verge of undergoing surgical intervention. However, pathological examination of liver abscesses, deep gastric mucosa, and EUS-FNB core specimens deterred this hasty decision. Persistent eosinophilia, tissue eosinophilic infiltration, multi-organ involvement, and the patient’s favorable response to steroid therapy ultimately supported the diagnosis of HES, predominantly affecting the stomach and multiple other organs. This case and the literature review underscore the importance of careful diagnostics in distinguishing HES from malignancies, preventing unnecessary surgery and tailoring the treatment strategy effectively.
