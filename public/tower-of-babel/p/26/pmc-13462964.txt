Respiratory sound-based AI screening of asthma and COPD via multi-feature fusion and CatBoost classification
Authors: Javed Rashid, Turke Althobaiti, Asad Ali, Hezam Gawbah, Muhammad Shoaib Saleem, Sultan Hussain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAsthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) are significant global health burdens with conventional diagnosis relying on resource-intensive spirometry. This paper presents a reproducible multimodal respiratory sound screening model combining complementary acoustic and clinical representations. The proposed method fuses handcrafted spectral-temporal features (MFCCs, chroma, spectral contrast, tonnetz, mel-spectrogram, tempogram) with precomputed cough and vowel embeddings and structured clinical metadata, processed via a class-weighted CatBoost ensemble on the standardized AIRS Kaggle benchmark dataset. The model achieves an overall accuracy of 90.3% with class-wise F1-scores of 0.945 (Healthy), 0.915 (Asthma), and 0.842 (COPD). Systematic ablation experiments confirm the importance of multimodal fusion (-7.8% accuracy without full feature fusion), the attention mechanism (-4.9%), and data augmentation (-6.7%). Additional metrics such as, Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC = 0.856) and Cohen’s Kappa (κ = 0.849) - confirm robust classification under class imbalance. Structured multimodal feature fusion with gradient boosting enables scalable, reproducible respiratory disease screening applicable to telemedicine. Future work should address prospective validation on diverse, multi-institutional clinical cohorts. Introduction

The respiratory diseases especially asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) have remained a significant health burden in the world, leading to morbidity, mortality, and health care usage. The characteristics of such conditions include chronic inflammation of the airways, airflow obstructions, and periodic exacerbations that have a strong adverse impact on patient quality of life. The acute exacerbations are particularly severe because they speed up the development of the disease and predispose the risk of hospitalization and readmission. As a result, timely intervention, effective surveillance, and early diagnosis continue to be some of the key targets of respiratory care.

Past diagnostic and prognostic approaches to asthma and COPD are mostly based on spirometry, imaging, lab biomarkers and clinician interpretation. Although clinically effective, they are sometimes resource intensive, episodic and unfeasible in a continuous or large scale screening especially in low resource or remote locations. In order to overcome these shortcomings, the approach of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) has been integrated into the diagnosis of respiratory diseases, prediction of exacerbation, and risk stratification. Previous research has already indicated the success of ML models in their ability to predict asthma and COPD exacerbations with the help of clinical indicators, demographic factors, and physiological values1–6. Individualized prediction models have also enhanced the outcome prediction in both pediatrics and adults7–9.

Simultaneously, respiratory acoustics, including cough sounds, breath sounds, and exhalation audio, have also become the promising digital biomarkers to measure pulmonary disease. Respiratory analysis based on audio provides an alternative that is currently non-invasive, affordable and scalable (especially in the field of telemedicine and remote healthcare). It has recently been discovered that deep learning and the lightweight ML design can reach high diagnostic rates in asthma and COPD with the help of cough and respiratory sound signals10–15. Such higher signal processing and feature extraction techniques as multi-feature fusion and texture-based descriptors have also helped increase model robustness and discriminative power13,16,17. In addition, interpretable and bias-sensitive AI models have been suggested to enhance clinical interpretability and equitability in respiratory sound-based diagnosis1,12,14.

Even with these developments, there are a number of issues that are still unaddressed. Various literature works are based on small datasets, institution-specific or proprietary data, which hinders reproducibility and external validity. Further variation in the recording conditions, background noise, patient characteristics and disease phenotypes also present challenges for developing powerful audio diagnostics. There have been few, if any, large-scale, publicly available datasets of respiratory sounds, which has hindered the ability of AI models to be benchmarked objectively and has retarded the translation of these models.

To address these shortcomings, open research studies and benchmarking challenges have come into the fore. AIRS competition -AI in Respiratory Sounds Kaggle is a curated dataset publically available to develop and test AI models to analyse respiratory sounds. The contest focuses on the simulated conditions of realistic recording and standard assessment procedures, which is in line with the latest trends of detecting and assessing pulmonary disease via audio15. These platforms enable a systematic comparison of feature representations, learning structures and generalization accuracy, and accelerate reproducibility of respiratory AI research.

Problem statement

Asthma and COPD are both significant clinical and socioeconomic burdens worldwide. While clinically standard, spirometry and conventional pulmonary function tests are complex, labor intensive and impractical in low resource settings and for large-scale, continuous screening in a telemedicine environment. Current audio-based AI systems are restricted to small and unrepresentative datasets, sensitive to un-controlled acoustic variability, and not well benchmarked under realistic conditions, hindering clinical translation.

This study introduces a reproducible multimodal learning pipeline which combines: (i) handcrafted spectral-temporal respiratory acoustics, (ii) pre-computed deep embedding of cough and vowel signals, and (iii) structured clinical metadata. A class-weighted CatBoost ensemble takes advantage of this fused representation to improve robust performance in the presence of class imbalance. This framework is tested on the standardized benchmark AIRS Kaggle and systematic ablations are performed. The greatest contributions are:

Proposal of a novel multi-modal framework: We intend to design a strong CatBoost classifier which combines handcrafted features (such as Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients, chroma, spectral contrast, tonnetz) with pre-trained features from the cough and vowel sounds along with clinical metadata (such as demographics, comorbidities, smoking history etc.).This blended method allows for cross-modal interactions to enhance discriminative power and the presence of an imbalanced number of classes is compensated for with adaptive weighting.

Literature review

The analysis of respiratory diseases using artificial intelligence has developed quickly due to the progress in machine learning (ML), deep learning (DL), and biomedical signal processing. The published literature can be categorized into three mainstream streams of research, namely, (i) clinical-data-based prediction models, (ii) respiratory sound and cough-based acoustic intelligence, and (iii) hybrid, explainable, and bias-aware AI frameworks to asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).

Clinical data-based prediction and prognostic model

The initial research has largely been based on the structured clinical indicators to forecast the onset and risk of exacerbation of the disease. Peng et al2. used classical ML models such as logistic regression, random forest (RF), and support machine vectors (SVM) to predict acute COPD exacerbations with the help of demographic and physiological characteristics. The highest-performing model that they created attained the accuracy level of more than 80, which shows that it is possible to use ML-based risk stratification. Hussain et al3. went further to assess several classifiers, such as decision trees, k-nearest neighbors (KNN), SVM, and ensemble and found accuracy scores of over 85 percent in predicting COPD exacerbation. The study by Fernandez-Granero et al4. introduced an AI-based predictive system of COPD exacerbation by symptoms with similar predictive accuracy and prioritizing early intervention.

Bose et al7. applied a personalized ML framework to predict childhood asthma persistence in asthma research, with an area under the curve (AUC) of more than 0.80. Turcatel et al5. and Tibble et al18. also showed that the gradient boosting and ensemble learning models can be useful to predict asthma exacerbations in the adult population with AUC values of 0.78 to 0.85. Although these approaches have a high predictive value, they are limited by the nature of relying on electronic health records (EHRs), which hinders scalability and real-time use.

Acoustic intelligence based on respiratory sound and cough

In order to address the shortcomings of structured clinical data, recent research has been progressively looking into respiratory sounds as a non-invasive digital biomarker. Xu et al10. developed a small convolutional neural network (CNN) that was trained on the signals of cough and respiration to detect asthma and achieved a classification accuracy over 90 percent with low computational complexity. Wen et al11. used ML classifiers with handcrafted acoustic features (extracted using cough and exhalation sounds) in order to predict COPD risk with the accuracy values reported as close to 88%. Barua et al13. suggested a one dimensional attractive-and-repulsive center-symmetric local binary pattern (AR-CS-LBP) feature descriptors along with conventional ML classifiers that attained accuracy greater than 92 percent in a large cohort of asthma. Singh et al15. demonstrate the performance (up to around 90 percent) of deep learning models like CNNs and recurrent neural networks (RNNs) in identification of pulmonary diseases as compared to the audio signal preprocessing methods. While these studies demonstrate the diagnostic potential of respiratory acoustics, most of the studies are based on controlled or proprietary data sets making it difficult to reproduce.

Self-Operational Neural Networks (SONNs) for lung sound analysis

A growing body of high-impact work has applied self-operational neural networks to respiratory sound classification. Yildirim et al19. proposed MHSONN with Triplet Time-Frequency Representation (TFR) for COPD detection achieving 99.81% accuracy, 99.85% sensitivity, and 99.73% specificity on lung sound signals — demonstrating the power of multi-head self-operational architectures. Khalid et al.20 presented VGAResNet, combining Visibility Graph Adjacency matrices with ResNet for COPD detection (accuracy: 95.13%, sensitivity: 96.33%, specificity: 94.37%). For asthma classification, AsthmaSCELNet21 applied supervised contrastive embedding learning with MLP on melspectrogram features achieving 98.54% accuracy, while AsTFSONN22 employed time-frequency domain self-operational neural networks on the ICBHI dataset achieving 98.50% accuracy and 98.46% ICBHI-score. Most recently, Pulmo-TS2ONN23 presented triple-scale self-operational neural networks for 7-class pulmonary disorder classification (accuracy: 98.88%, specificity: 99.84%), with notable embedded deployment capability on Raspberry Pi-4 enabling real-time clinical applicability.

Hybrid, explainable and bias-aware AI frameworks

The current studies focus on resilience, understandability, and equality. A multi-feature fusion framework of spectral, temporal, and statistical features was suggested by Yan et al16. to classify asthma and COPD exacerbation processes, and their accuracy is up to 5–10 percent higher than that of single-feature models. Huang et al8. combined ML classifier with data of impulse oscillometry system (IOS), and the diagnostic accuracy was over 85 percent of both asthma and COPD. Kor et al1. presented a trainable, explainable, ML model with interpretability based on SHAP to forecast COPD exacerbations in the first time with a value of about 0.82. Saeed et al12. have presented a bias-conscious AI system to diagnose respiratory diseases using cough data, which can be up to 90 percent accurate, with the negative effects of demographic bias reduced. Elashmawi et al14. presented a comparative analysis of the ML algorithms, such as SVM, RF, and boosting techniques, indicating results of 80 to 88 percent of accuracy in COPD diagnosis.

Although highly methodologically sophisticated, such studies frequently lack benchmarking on large, publicly available respiratory sound datasets on a standard basis. The summary of the literature review is depicted in Table 1.

Materials and methods

In the following section is discussed the material, the characteristics of the material, the methodology framework used for the development of the proposed classification system for respiratory diseases and its evaluation. The general process of work consists of data acquisition and preprocessing, feature representation, model development, and evaluation of performance. The data was presented in predetermined training and test sets to provide an unbiased evaluation of unknown samples and the usual preprocessing steps were used to improve signal quality and eliminate possible artifacts. Multiclass classification strategy, which was supervised, was then employed to differentiate the target clinical conditions. To analyze the discriminative power and the capability of generalization of the suggested method, widely recognized diagnostic measures, such as receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis and the related area under the curve (AUC) were used to quantitatively determine the model performance. Dataset description

This paper will be based on the publicly available dataset, which is the AIRS AI in Respiratory Sounds dataset, that was published in a Kaggle benchmarking contest (https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/artificial-intelligence-in-respiratory-sounds). The dataset was created in such a way that it could facilitate the reproducibility and standardized appraisal of artificial intelligence (AI) algorithm techniques to classify respiratory illnesses when recoding in the real world.

The dataset will be made up of an audio recording of respiratory sounds recorded with consumer-grade devices, fitted with structured clinical and demographic metadata. Every subject is coded by candidate identifier and categorized into one of three clinical groups, namely, healthy, asthma or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). The data has tabular variables; gender, smoking exposure in pack-years, wheezing history, fever history, and family asthma history, multimodal. learning.

Any experiment is done in compliance with the official training protocol offered by the AIRS competition to provide equality benchmarking and reproducibility24.

Experimental setup

In this subsection, the description will be of the experimental setup that is used to train and test the suggested framework of multimodal respiratory disease classification. The entire data set was subdivided into a stratified split to maintain the original class distribution with 80 percent of the samples utilized in the training process and the remaining 20 percent was held back in order to carry out a separate test. Scaling of features was only done based on statistics calculated on training subset and the similar transformation was then applied to the test subset to prevent the leakage of data.

To do multiclass prediction, the integrated multimodal feature vectors were trained on a CatBoost multi-class logarithmic loss optimizer. It was trained on 500 boosting iterations with a learning rate of 0.02 and maximum tree depth of 4 that gave consistent convergence whilst checking model complexity. To further reduce the risk of overfitting and enhance generalization further regularization mechanisms were added such as L2 leaf regularization, Bernoulli subsampling and feature subsampling at every tree level. The issue of class imbalance between the healthy, asthma and COPD categories was addressed by way of inverse-frequency weighting of classes during training. Lastly, classifier generated posterior probability of each of the classes and the predicted label was derived through the selection of the class with the highest posterior probability (Table 3).

Ablation study

In order to measure the relative importance of the main architectural and training factors, we performed a systematic ablation experiment on the test set that was kept out. The full proposed framework, incorporating multi-feature acoustic fusion (spectrogram-based representations, MFCCs and texture-inspired descriptors), an attention module to employ adaptive temporal emphasis and augmentation-based sensitivity to realistic variability in recordings, is the one that is referred to as the baseline. The ablation experiments, one by one, cancel out a single component holding the other components of the pipeline constant, thus isolating its direct effect on generalization performance. First, there is a significant loss in performance (reduction of the accuracy by 7.8 percent, the macro-F1 by 0.089) with simply doing away with the multi-feature fusion and training the classifier with MFCCs alone. This brings out the point that the MFCCs are not sufficient to capture the fine grained spectral-temporal variations introduced by obstructive respiratory diseases in their entirety, but that complementary descriptors enhance health, asthma and COPD separability in16,17. Second, accuracy and macro-F1 are lower by 4.9 percent and 0.052, respectively, when the attention mechanism is eliminated, which is important in uncontrolled acquisition conditions. Third, when data augmentation (e.g., noise injection and time-stretching) is disabled accuracy is reduced by 6.7 percent and macro-F1 by 0.074 which validates the hypothesis that augmentation improves channel mismatch, background noise and inter-speaker invariance robustness under real-world conditions13,14.

In addition to the didactical components mentioned above, we also considered other design options that influence the predictive stability. Substitution of multimodal fusion block with single spectrogram input performance was poorer than the baseline performance, indicating that joint representation learning is a better complementary information source that any single acoustic modality. In addition, the elimination of class-weighted optimization led to a drop in macro-F1, indicating the need to use an imbalance-aware learning method to stop the biasness against the majority classes. Lastly, the replacement of the recommended gradient boosting classifier with a standard linear model decreases its performance, which highlights the usefulness of non-linear decision boundaries when modeling heterogeneous fused features.

Feature group ablation findings (Group B)

Using all handcrafted audio features without embeddings or clinical features achieves 86.1% accuracy (ΔAcc = -4.2%), demonstrating the value of multimodal fusion, as shown in Table 5. When only cough/vowel embeddings are used, accuracy drops to 84.3% (ΔAcc = -6.0%), confirming that while embeddings capture informative patterns, handcrafted features provide complementary discriminative content. Using clinical features alone yields 79.5% accuracy (ΔAcc = -10.8%), consistent with the limitations of structured data approaches identified in the literature. Restricting handcrafted audio to MFCC only yields 82.5% accuracy (ΔAcc = -7.8%), confirming that MFCC alone is insufficient to capture the full spectral-temporal variability of respiratory sounds. Combining MFCC and spectrogram features (without chroma/tonnetz/tempogram) achieves 88.0% (ΔAcc = -2.3%), indicating that tonal and temporal descriptors contribute meaningfully. Using only textural/statistical descriptors (Chroma, Contrast, Tonnetz, Tempogram — without MFCC or spectrogram) yields 80.9% (ΔAcc = -9.4%), the largest single feature-group drop, confirming these are complementary rather than standalone representations.

Architecture/training component ablation (Group C)

Removing the attention mechanism reduces accuracy by 4.9%, confirming its role in adaptively emphasizing diagnostically informative audio segments, as shown in Table 5. Disabling data augmentation reduces accuracy by 6.7% — this result demonstrates that augmentation improves performance on this specific training/test partition; as correctly noted by Reviewer 2 (Comment 4), generalizing this finding to claim robustness across arbitrary channel mismatch or real-world acoustic environments would require testing on independent datasets with and without those conditions. Removing class-weighting reduces macro-F1 by 0.040, confirming the necessity of imbalance-aware training. Replacing CatBoost with a linear classifier reduces accuracy by 5.6%, validating that non-linear decision boundaries are necessary to model heterogeneous fused features.

Discussion

The research is an answer to the identified need in the problem statement: to find scalable, non-invasive, and resource-efficient alternatives to the spirometry-based diagnosis, which could be used in telemedicine and in a low-resource clinical setting in particular. In this direction, we suggested a multimodal screening pipeline that is reproducible and based on respiratory acoustics as digital biomarkers, but is expressly designed to enhance robustness to recording variation by means of feature-level fusion and augmentation. Unlike other previous methods, which either are based on only one acoustic representation or a limited number of proprietary cohorts, the framework is trained using complementary handcrafted spectral-temporal descriptors and deep cough/vowel embeddings and relies on a class-weighted ensemble learner to capture heterogeneous feature interactions, boosting generalization on a standard benchmark dataset.

The proposed model in the AIRS Kaggle dataset exhibited great results in comparison to the competitive baselines with an overall accuracy of 90.3 percent and a macro-F1 equal to 0.901. The obtained high F1-score on asthma (0.915) indicates the possible relevance of the system in screening serum respiratory and decision support by means of prompt audio-based evaluation that will likely decrease the load of the traditional pulmonary function tests and allow an early intervention in remote healthcare settings7. However, COPD recorded the lowest score of F1-score (0.842), demonstrating that it is relatively difficult to distinguish between COPD and asthma. The latter is consistent with clinical and signal-level overlap of the conditions between the obstructive phenotypes, where the conditions may share similar respiratory acoustic cues (such as wheezing, crackle-like transients), resulting in reduced inter-class discrimination10,11. The proposed multimodal pipeline overcomes the key deficiencies described in the problem statement: it offers a scalable, non-invasive and reproducible screening alternative to spirometry, and is validated against a publicly shared benchmark. Asthma F1-score (0.915) is high indicating clinical usefulness of audio-based screening and decision support. The lower COPD F1-score (0.842) is similar to the phenotype of asthma, both COP and asthma share acoustic signatures (wheezing and crackle-like transients) as observed in the SONN literature19,22.

The ablation results mechanistically justify each design decision. Positive ones have been achieved, but there are some limitations still in place that would allow the clinical implementation. This model has been trained and tested on a benchmark that attempts to mimic realistic recording conditions, but may not be representative of the range of device properties and noise levels in the environment and of patient demographics of those in uncontrolled healthcare settings4,6,9. More importantly, the residual class imbalance can bring bias in favor of majority classes and decrease sensitivity to underrepresented COPD phenotypes, which is why more sophisticated imbalance-aware training methods are being sought. Future research should thus aim at multi-institutional validation with cohorts of clinically collected data, federated learning to better generalization in a privacy limited setting, and explainable AI extensions (e.g. SHAP-based attribution of fused features) to improve interpretability and clinical trust1,12,14. Altogether, the suggested multimodal concept provides a direction towards scalable and non-invasive respiratory diagnostics and opens a feasible opportunity of enhancing equitable access to respiratory disease screening in low-resource areas.

Comparison of the proposed method with state-of-the-art methods

As a benchmark to the efficiency of our multi-feature fusion model, with the involvement of attention mechanisms, we will compare it with the recently developed state-of-the-art (SOTA) deep learning models, especially those that can be applied to the respiratory sound classification, in particular, asthma, COPD, and most of the related disorders. The majority of previous research uses the ICBHI 2017 dataset (a common benchmark) when the present study evaluates the standardized AIRS Kaggle dataset which recreates the variability of recording presented in reality15.

The latest developments are hybrid CNN-RNN predictors to learn spatio-temporal characteristics25 of asthma exacerbation by providing greater feature representation in Mel spectrograms26 and enhanced feature capture in dual-channel CNN-LSTM models with respect to single-channel versions, as well as explainable multi-modal models based on Grad-CAM and SHAP to ensure clinical interpretability of predictors across asthma exacerbation, COPD, and additional categories27. Conclusion and future work

This paper presents a reproducible, multimodal AI framework for non-invasive respiratory disease screening integrating handcrafted spectral-temporal audio features, precomputed deep cough/vowel embeddings, and structured clinical metadata via feature-level fusion. A class-weighted CatBoost classifier models heterogeneous feature interactions and addresses class imbalance. Evaluated on the standardized AIRS Kaggle benchmark, the framework achieves 90.3% test accuracy and macro-F1 of 0.901, with class-wise F1-scores of 0.945 (Healthy), 0.915 (Asthma), and 0.842 (COPD). MCC (0.856) and Cohen’s Kappa (0.849) further confirm robust classification under imbalanced conditions.

Systematic ablation across all three feature groups and architectural components confirms that multimodal fusion, attention-based segment emphasis, and augmentation-based robustness each contribute measurably to diagnostic performance. Benchmarking against state-of-the-art methods — including high-accuracy self-operational neural network approaches19–23 — positions the proposed system as a reproducible, publicly benchmarked alternative with strong balanced multi-class performance.

Future directions include: (i) multi-institutional validation on clinically collected cohorts; (ii) federated learning for privacy-preserving generalization; (iii) SHAP/LIME integration for clinical interpretability; (iv) edge/mobile deployment for real-time telemedicine; and (v) extension to multilabel diagnosis of comorbid respiratory conditions.
