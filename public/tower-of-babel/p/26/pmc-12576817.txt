Multivalency Enables Signal Processing at Single Protein Level
Authors: Xiaoyu Wu, Yuanqi Jia, Tong Zhang, Zhehao Xiong, Zihang Ye, Ge Yu, Yingjie Lei, Zhuo Mao, Vicky Qu, Bobo Dang, Ziyao Xu, Zibo Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Synthetic signal processing in cells can be achieved through complex networks of molecules or simpler, single-molecule-based mechanisms. The latter approach stands out for its reduced genetic load and ease of delivery. Multivalent protein binders are such single-molecule signal processors that exhibit distinct behaviors when interacting with cells presenting various antigen profiles. However, a quantitative and precise method to design multivalent interactions in the context of cell surface binding is still lacking. Here, we developed Multivalent Antigen Sensing Simulator (MASS) to guide the design of such multivalent proteins. We verified the model through in vitro and cell binding experiments, showing the predictive accuracy of our model in designing multivalent binders to sense cell surface antigen identities and elicit selective killing. Our model also accurately captured, which was verified via experiments, the nonmonotonic interplay between valency and monovalent affinity in designing multivalent binders that sense cell surface antigen densities. Our work suggests practical guidelines in designing multivalent proteins that could simultaneously sense both antigen identities and densities and enables fast and precise in silico prescreening of multivalent protein binders.

Introduction

Cells mainly rely on proteins to process signals. This could occur at the network level, involving circuits of interacting proteins, , or at the single-molecule level, through distinct mechanisms such as allostery and post-translational modifications. Efforts to create protein-based signal processing have mainly focused on the circuit level, making use of modular components that individually contribute to a subset of the circuit function.

Compressing complex signal processing functions from networks of proteins into single protein molecules could result in compact protein modules with easier delivery due to their reduced genetic load. The single component nature also eliminates the need to optimize the component stoichiometry. Thus, recent studies have begun to engineer signal processing at the single protein level. For instance, light-regulated allosteric switches allow tight spatiotemporal control of enzymatic activity for cell signaling, and de novo-designed allostery established a new framework for customizing functions at the single protein level. , However, it has so far been difficult to generalize these methods due to their intrinsic complexity, provoking the question of whether there exists an alternative and more modular approach to engineer signal processing at the single-molecule level.

Multivalent protein interactions offer a versatile and generalizable framework to control the kinetics and thermodynamics of protein–protein interactions. In the absence of confounding factors such as steric hindrance, two or more protein binding domains linked via flexible linkers exhibit tighter binding to their targets than monovalent binders (Figure a, left). This principle has been harnessed to develop multispecific antibodies, cytokines, transcription factors, , and tandem chimeric antigen receptor-T (CAR-T) cell therapeutics. Multivalency has also been demonstrated to be capable of sensing antigen density on the cell surface − (Figure b, left).

Further exploitation of the modularity of multivalent interactions requires accurate models that efficiently search through a large design space to enable fast iterations of design-build-test-learn cycles. Past models of multivalency have mainly focused on either thermodynamic or kinetic approaches. Thermodynamic approaches aggregate the free energy of binding from each domain to derive the overall affinity. However, multivalent interactions in many applications happen far from equilibrium, and require kinetic models to accurately capture these binding events. These models typically deconstruct multivalent interactions into a network of state transitions among individual binding configurations, thus determining the exact dynamics of multivalent binding − and providing a comprehensive and kinetically accurate view of the whole system. The number of unique binding configurations explodes as the valency increases, making kinetic simulations time-consuming. Therefore, a faster modeling algorithm is required to take full advantage of the kinetic approach.

Here, we developed a command-line tool, called Multivalent Antigen Sensing Simulator (MASS), to rapidly capture the complexity of binding between multivalent protein constructs and their targets on a cell surface. We verified the accuracy of our model with both in vitro experiments using Biolayer Interferometry (BLI) and cell binding experiments using cell staining assays. MASS predicted a paradigm where the density of cell surface antigens could be sensed using multivalent proteins, which was verified in Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR) experiments. Based on the simulation outcomes, we provide a guideline for the design of antigen-density-aware multivalent protein drugs.

Results

Multivalency Enables Sensing of Antigen Identity and Density

Multivalency enables the sensing of antigen identities on the cell surface (Figure a, left). For example, a cell coexpressing two distinct types of antigens (A and B) could be distinguished from those expressing either or none of them by a bivalent construct consisting of binding domains against A and B. In this case, we define the binding EC50 as the protein concentration needed to occupy 50% of the antigens on a cell surface. The specificity of a particular protein construct is calculated as the minimum of its EC50 values against cells expressing either antigen A or B, divided by its EC50 value against cells expressing both antigens A and B (Figure a, center). Tuning the respective monovalent binding strength could result in different binding specificities (Figure a, right). Indeed, this principle has been harnessed to develop various applications in precise cell type recognition.

However, cells often do not display antigens in an all-or-none fashion, necessitating the need to distinguish not only antigen identities, but also their densities (Figure b, left). Indeed, previous works have shown that multivalent interactions could be designed to distinguish the densities of surface-expressing antigens. − Here, we define the binding specificity as the ratio of the EC50 values of binding to cells expressing low vs high levels of surface antigens (Figure b, center), which is in principle tunable by the monovalent binding affinity and the valency of the protein construct (Figure b, right).

To predictably design protein constructs with the aforementioned cell recognition capabilities, we built a computational model capturing the binding behavior between a hypothetical multivalent protein construct and its targets on a cell surface. This model takes as inputs valency of binders, monovalent binding kinetics of each protein domain, rigidity and length of each individual linker, and the overall concentration and surface density of protein targets. The model deconstructs the multivalent binding interaction into a network of microstates, with each node representing a unique binding configuration between a binder and its target and each edge representing the transition between its two connected microstates (Figure c, top left; Supplementary Text). Transition rates among microstates are determined by on and off rates of input monovalent binding domains as well as their effective local concentrations, Ceff, calculated as the meeting probability between monovalent binding domains (Figure c, top right; Supplementary Text). Based on these parameters, the model generates a system of ordinary differential equations (ODEs; Figure c, bottom right; Supplementary Text). Solving these equations reveals the kinetics of the multivalent binding (Figure c, bottom left).

The MASS model differentiates from the previously reported MVSim toolkit in two main aspects: 1) in addition to capturing the binding behavior between multivalent constructs in solution, it considers another biologically relevant scenario, in which a multivalent construct binds to antigens on a cell surface, which shows distinct binding behavior against in-solution binding (Figure S1a); 2) it employs a new algorithm to perform three-dimensional convolution, reducing the time complexity of calculating the effective concentration, one of the most time-consuming steps, by one polynomial degree.

Bivalent and Trivalent Constructs Enable 2- and 3-Input AND Effect

To evaluate the accuracy of our model, we first chose Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor (EGFR) and Epithelial Cell Adhesion Molecule (EpCAM) antigens along with their corresponding binders (VHHs) (Figure a) for BLI and cell staining assays. We generated mutants of anti-EGFR and anti-EpCAM VHHs, and we measured their monovalent binding kinetics using BLI or Isothermal Titration Calorimetry (ITC). These mutants, with binding affinities falling in either the weak (KD > 100 nM) or medium (1 nM < KD < 100 nM) categories, were then linked into bivalent constructs for downstream experiments (Table S1, S2).

We first measured the binding kinetics of an EGFRmedium-EpCAMmedium construct against BLI sensors loaded with either EGFR or EpCAM antigens. Using these data (Figure b, top) as inputs to our model, we predicted the expected binding behavior of this bivalent construct against BLI sensors loaded with an equimolar amount of EGFR and EpCAM antigens (Figure b, middle) and found good agreement with actual experimental measurements (Figure b, bottom). Compared with monovalent binding against either antigen, both predicted and measured bivalent binding showed biphasic and much slower dissociation, indicating an AND-gate effect (Figure b). Crucially, the binding kinetics could not be recapitulated if our model ran in the in-solution mode (Figure S1a). We reasoned that in the in-solution model, the effective concentration of available binding sites on the target remains constant during association and dissociation. Therefore, the multivalent effect does not change during the course of simulation. In contrast, our on-surface model treats the entire cell as a multivalent entity. As dissociation occurs, the effective concentration of available binding sites increases, which strengthens the multivalent effect and hinders further unbinding, leading to the observed biphasic dissociation behavior (Figure b). These results offer preliminary verification of the model’s accuracy and provoke the question of whether our model works when antigens are presented on cell surfaces.

To evaluate the ability of our model to accurately predict multivalent binding on cell surfaces, we constructed Chinese Hamster Ovary (CHO) cell lines stably displaying either EGFR, EpCAM, or both antigens. We then quantified the average number of displayed antigens per cell for each cell line (Table S3). Using these measurements, together with ITC- or BLI-measured monovalent binding constants, we first predicted the binding specificity as a function of monovalent KD values, and found a wide and nonmonotonic distribution of specificity values (Figure c, top left). Cell staining assay (Supplementary Text) using bivalent VHH constructs made from different monovalent binding domains revealed their preferential binding to cells expressing both types of antigens. In addition to this expected AND-effect, these bivalent constructs exhibited specificity values and binding behaviors closely aligned with our model predictions (Figure c). These cell staining assays were repeated with constructs of various formats to rule out the impact of affinity tags and linker length. All but one construct  whose binding deviated from a sigmoidal curve thus preventing specificity calculation (Figure S2c, left)  agreed with model predictions (Figure S2). These results demonstrate the versatility of our model in making cell binding predictions.

Our bivalent constructs showed a preference for binding to dual-expression cells. This preference suggests that they should be able to preferentially recognize these cells in a mixed population. To confirm this hypothesis, we applied the EpCAMmedium-EGFRmedium construct to a mixed population of mono- and dual-expression cell lines, and indeed observed a strong preference in its binding to dual-expression cell lines (Figure S3a). We then conjugated this EpCAMmedium-EGFRmedium construct with monomethyl auristatin E (MMAE) to make an antibody-drug conjugate (ADC) (Figure S3b), which specifically killed the dual-expression cell line (Figures d, S3c). These results highlight the practical use of properly designed bivalent constructs.

To evaluate if our model can be generalized to higher order valency binding on cell surface, we selected the human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) as another input to build a 3-input AND gate. We chose two Designed Ankyrin Repeat Protein (DARPin) domains targeting the HER2 antigen with weak or medium affinities (Table S4). These DARPins were genetically fused to our previously described bivalent constructs to generate trivalent binders. However, trivalent constructs containing the HER2medium DARPin could not be purified due to protein aggregation, while those containing the HER2weak domain were successfully purified for further characterization. We then engineered four additional stable cell lines to represent all eight combinations of EGFR, EpCAM, and HER2 expression on the cell surface and quantified their average antigen densities (Table S3). Combining these measurements with previously determined monovalent binding affinities, we predicted the binding behavior of the trivalent construct on cells coexpressing all three antigens. This prediction is validated through another cell staining assay using the EpACMweak-EGFRweak-HER2weak construct, which preferentially bound the triexpression cell line in close agreement with our model (Figures e, S4).

Multivalency Gives Rise to Cell Surface Antigen Density Sensing

In addition to accurately capturing the binding behaviors of bi- and trispecific constructs toward cell surface antigens (Figure a), our model should in principle enable us to design multivalent constructs to sense cell surface antigen densities (Figure b). To evaluate this capability, we designed bivalent to pentavalent tandem repeats of the PDZ domain as multivalent binders, and selected PDZ ligands with a wide range of ITC-measured monovalent binding affinities. Purified PDZ ligands (Figure S5) were fixed onto SPR chips to mimic the cell surface antigens. These SPR chips loaded with PDZ ligands are regarded as “cells” with high expression levels of surface antigens. On cells with low antigen density, multivalent binders should exhibit weak binding due to limited available binding partners on the cell surface. This assumption allows us to simplify comparisons by regarding low-expression cells as those that give rise to the same binding kinetics toward either multivalent or monovalent binders. Under this framework, binding affinities between monovalent PDZ constructs and their ligands are used as proxies to mimic bindings on low antigen density surfaces, and specificity is calculated by dividing overall multivalent affinities with these monovalent affinities.

Revealed by SPR measurements, the monovalent binding affinity and the valency collectively and nonmonotonically affect the specificity (Figure a, top left). This trend was recapitulated by the model (Figure a, bottom left), with a notably good correlation (Figure a, right). This nonmonotonic behavior arises because low monovalent affinity fails to initiate multivalent binding, while high monovalent affinity results in diminished enhancement in overall binding as the valency increases. The observed 10-fold difference in specificity between model predictions and experimental measurements is hypothesized to be coming from the differences in experimental conditions between SPR and ITC techniques. These results indicate the versatility of our model to predict the ability of multivalent binders to sense cell surface antigen densities.

To explore design principles for multivalent binders capable of sensing cell surface antigen densities, we systematically investigated the interplay between valency and monovalent affinity. Resulting binding behaviors were visualized in a series of heatmaps (Figure b), revealing a wide range of overall binding affinities toward various surface antigen density profiles. This wide distribution of overall binding affinities enabled multivalent protein constructs to effectively discriminate cells exhibiting different antigen densities. Scanning these heatmaps along the x-axis revealed that constructs made of weak monovalent binders (Figure c, top) possess bigger fold changes of EC50 values than those made of strong monovalent binders (Figure c, bottom) when used to distinguish cells with high vs low surface antigen expression. Meanwhile, the range of antigen densities that can be sensed by a multivalent construct can also be tuned by changing its valency (Figure c). These results suggest the existence of an optimal multivalent construct to discriminate between a pair of cells with different surface antigen densities.

To understand the performance of multivalent constructs as a function of their valency and monovalent affinities, we define the discrimination window as the range of antigen densities that covers the change in EC50 values from 5% to 95%, and discrimination power as the ratio between fold changes of EC50 values and antigen densities at these boundaries (Figure d). The discrimination power thus quantifies how sensitive a multivalent binder is against changes in cell surface antigen densities. Although higher valency uniformly gives rise to higher discrimination power (Figure e, left), it suffers from diminishing returns (Figure e, right). Additionally, the mid-discrimination antigen density, indicating the location of the discrimination window, is tuned by monovalent binding affinities (Figure f, left), and less so by the valency (Figure f, right). Physiologically, a typical tumor-associated antigen like EGFR is expressed on healthy cells at densities of a few to a few hundred molecules per square micrometer (μm–2) and on malignant cells at densities often exceeding thousands. Our findings suggest that constructs with moderate valency (e.g., trivalent or tetravalent) are sufficient to achieve discrimination within these physiologically relevant antigen density ranges (Figure e, f, left). These findings collectively suggest design principles for multivalent binders that sense cell surface antigen densities: while lower monovalent binding affinities coupled with higher valency leads to better discrimination power, extreme values of both might not result in a suitable binder, since they need to be carefully selected to match the desired detection range of antigen densities. Additionally, high valency suffers from diminishing returns (Figure e, right) and could lead to misfolding or aggregates if the protein becomes too big.

Design Principles for Multivalent Binders

Given that our model could accurately predict binding behaviors of multivalent binders that sense either types or densities of cell surface antigens, we provide design workflows for binders that account for both factors simultaneously. The process begins with measuring antigen densities on both target and nontarget cells (Figure , left). As an example, we designate ″cell line #1″ as the target cell expressing high levels of both A and B antigens and ″cell line #2″ and ″cell line #3″ as nontarget cells, with cell line #2 showing high A and medium B expression and cell line #3 showing low A and high B expression.

A design pipeline for a multivalent construct to maximize its cell surface antigen profile discrimination. The pipeline involves measuring antigen densities on all cells (left), exploring and simulating the selectivity of various multivalent binder formats (middle), and arriving at optimal designs with biologically plausible binding affinities (right). Selectivity here is defined as Min­(Ntarget/Nnontarget), where N is the number of cells bound by multivalent constructs.

Measured antigen density profiles for each cell type, along with an estimated binder concentration (set here at 0.1 μM, based on typical protein drug pharmacokinetic information), are then fed into our model as inputs to simultaneously evaluate various binder configurations, each with distinct valency (e.g., VHHA-VHHB vs VHHA-VHHA-VHHB), topology (e.g., VHHA-VHHB-VHHA vs VHHA-VHHA-VHHB), and monovalent binding affinities (Figure , middle). The model produces, for each binder-cell pair, the number of binders on a cell surface after a fixed time. To quantify a binder’s ability to distinguish target cells, we calculate the ratio of attached binders on the target cell type to that on each of the two nontarget cell types. Binding selectivity is then defined as the smaller of these two ratios (Figure , middle). This metric reflects how well a binder distinguishes target cells from others.

The workflow ends with comparing the best-performing designs from each format and choosing a final candidate, based on the availability of monovalent binders with suitable affinities. In the given example, searching within the biologically feasible binding affinities for monovalent binders (1 nM < KD < 100 μM, dashed lines in Figure , middle) resulted in the VHHA-VHHA-VHHB format as the design that offers the best selectivity (Figure , right). Our model thus provides a practical and systematic pipeline to identify multivalent binders that could simultaneously distinguish cell surface antigen types and densities.

We conducted simulations to explore the extent to which the principles for sensing antigen densities and identities could be combined. Here we designate ″cell line #4″ as the target, expressing high levels (1,000 μm–2) of both A and B antigens (Figure S6a). We define two nontarget cells: “cell line #5”, which expresses A and B at medium (100 μm–2) and high (1,000 μm–2) levels, respectively; and “cell line #6”, which expresses A at a high level (1,000 μm–2) and B at a low level (1 μm–2) (Figure S6a). In this setup, a construct can sense antigen density if it could distinguish cell line #4 from either line #5 or line #6 and can sense antigen identities if it can distinguish cell line #4 from both line #5 and #6.

A VHHA-VHHA construct senses the density of antigen A but fails to distinguish cell line #4 from cell line #6 (Figure S6b, left, middle), thus expectedly could not sense antigen identities (Figure S6b, right). A VHHA-VHHB construct senses both the density and identity of A and B antigens (Figure S6c, left and middle), which gives rise to an AND-gate effect (Figure S6c, right). A VHHA-VHHA-VHHB construct has an improved sensing specificity for antigen A (Figure S6d, left), at the cost of a reduced design space for sensing antigen B (Figure S6d, middle), and only a slight increase in overall selectivity for antigen identities (Figure S6d, right). These results reveal an important trade-off when designing multivalent constructs that sense both antigen densities and identities, where the overall selectivity and design space should be taken into consideration when varying the valency of designs.

Discussion

Circuit-based signal processing carried out by intricate protein networks has long been a cornerstone for cell-type classification. , However, optimizing the stoichiometry of all molecular components often poses challenges when implementing them inside cells. In contrast, single-molecule multivalent proteins present a simpler alternative, eliminating the need for stoichiometric optimization and minimizing the genetic load. This study harnesses multivalency as a signal processing framework and uses single protein molecules for cell type discrimination.

We developed MASS as a biophysical model to accurately predict the binding kinetics of multivalent interactions and verified its accuracy through in vitro and cell binding experiments. These multispecific binders (up to trispecific) can distinguish coexpression from single expression cell lines with specificities closely aligned with our predictions (Figures c, e). This specificity persists in mixed cell populations (Figure S3a) as well as ADC-mediated cell killing (Figure d). Additionally, multivalent constructs can detect antigen densities on cell surfaces. We experimentally verified this with constructs ranging from bivalent to pentavalent, revealing a nonmonotonic effect relative to monovalent affinity, consistent with predictions from MASS (Figure a). The model was further used to inform design strategies for density sensing tasks (Figures b-f). Finally, we suggested a practical design pipeline for creating multivalent constructs that sense both the combination of antigen identities and their densities (Figure ).

Our model reduces runtime by adopting a quick algorithm to calculate three-dimensional convolution (Supplementary Text) and is command line based, allowing parallel simulations using GNU Parallel. The model is designed to be broadly applicable and is not limited to just antibody–antigen interactions. Furthermore, MASS can be applied to scenarios well beyond cell-type discrimination. For example, the in-solution mode can be used to design constructs with desired overall binding affinities and specificities, empowering the construction of complex protein circuits; the on-surface mode can be used to design scaffold proteins that specifically bring different cell-surface proteins into proximity to mediate protein–protein interactions and trigger downstream cellular signaling. While MASS has produced accurate predictions closely aligning with experimental findings, the model has some limitations. First, the performance decreases when the valency goes beyond 7, primarily due to the ODE solver, which could potentially be improved by adopting a faster solver. Second, this study mainly focuses on two key parameters  valency and monovalent affinities  while ignoring other factors that might affect multivalent binding, such as temperature, steric hindrance, and receptor clustering. Incorporating these factors into the analysis requires a more detailed understanding of specific molecules, information that can be challenging to obtain during current design processes. Further research into these parameters, along with reliable design methods for weak yet specific protein binders, could refine our approach into a standardized and quantitative platform, unlocking the full potential of multivalency in single-molecule signal processing.
