Ameliorative effects of Wiryeongtang (herbal medicine) on cardiorenal complications via modulation of inflammatory and fibrotic responses in diabetic mice
Authors: Jung Joo Yoon, Hye Yoom Kim, Ai Lin Tai, Yun Jeong Yang, Sung Joo Park, Ho Sub Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiabetes causes cardiorenal complications characterized by inflammation, fibrosis, and metabolic dysfunction. Wiryeongtang (WRT), a traditional herbal formula, has shown potential in alleviating metabolic disorder-related complications. This study aimed to investigate the protective effects of WRT on cardiorenal complications in diabetic mice.MethodsFour groups were studied: a non-diabetic control (db/m), an untreated diabetic group (db/db), a vildagliptin-treated group (50 mg/kg/day) as a positive control, and a WRT-treated group (200 mg/kg/day). Metabolic parameters, including plasma blood glucose, lipid accumulation, and oral glucose tolerance, were measured. Renal histology was assessed for glomerulosclerosis, mesangial matrix expansion, and fibrosis. Cardiac tissues were examined for hypertrophy, fibrosis, and inflammation. Apoptotic markers (caspase-3, caspase-9, Bcl-2, and Bax) were also measured.ResultsWRT significantly reduced plasma blood glucose levels and lipid accumulation in db/db mice and improved oral glucose tolerance and insulin resistance. WRT reduced glomerulosclerosis, mesangial matrix expansion, and fibrosis markers in the kidneys. WRT also mitigated diabetes-induced cardiac dysfunction and histopathological changes, including hypertrophy, fibrosis, and inflammation. Additionally, WRT downregulated apoptotic markers (caspase-3 and caspase-9) while increasing the Bcl-2/Bax ratio.ConclusionsThe findings suggest that WRT may serve as a potential therapeutic agent for managing cardiorenal complications associated with type 2 diabetes. It alleviated hyperglycemia, improved insulin sensitivity, and reduced inflammation and fibrosis in both cardiac and renal tissues through anti-apoptotic and anti-fibrotic mechanisms.

Introduction

Diabetes profoundly affects both the cardiovascular and renal systems, leading to structural and functional abnormalities that increase the risk of cardiovascular events and renal dysfunction.1 Prolonged hyperglycemia fosters myocardial hypertrophy, apoptosis, and interstitial fibrosis, contributing to diabetic cardiomyopathy characterized by impaired contractility, diastolic dysfunction, and increased susceptibility to arrhythmias and heart failure.2, 3, 4, 5 Dyslipidemia further exacerbates cardiovascular risk by promoting atherosclerosis, hypertension, and renal damage.6,7 These metabolic derangements create a vicious cycle in which cardiac and renal dysfunction mutually reinforce each other, complicating disease management and therapeutic interventions. In the kidney, diabetes induces glomerular hypertrophy, mesangial expansion, and tubulointerstitial fibrosis, hallmark features of diabetic nephropathy.1,8 Transforming growth factor-beta 1 (TGF-β1) is a key mediator of renal fibrosis,8,9 and inflammation plays a pivotal role in disease progression, with pro-inflammatory cytokines perpetuating renal dysfunction.10,11 Insulin resistance and hyperglycemia aggravate cardiorenal complications by promoting sodium retention, fluid overload, and activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system.12, 13, 14 Activation of inflammatory and fibrosis-related pathways, including NF-κB and Smad2/3, results in myocardial hypertrophy and fibrosis, contributing to decreased systolic and diastolic heart function.15, 16, 17 Understanding these intertwined pathological mechanisms is crucial for developing effective interventions to mitigate diabetes-related cardiorenal complications. Traditional herbal formulations such as Wiryeongtang (Wei-Ling-Tang, WRT) have shown potential in ameliorating renal disorders, hyperlipidemia, and chronic edema.18, 19, 20 WRT is composed of twelve medicinal herbs, including Alisma orientalis, Cinnamomum cassia, Atractylodes lancea, Magnolia officinalis, Paeonia lactiflora, and has been historically used to restore renal homeostasis.18,19 However, its effects on diabetes-induced cardiorenal complications remain unclear. Vildagliptin, a prototype DPP-4 inhibitor, is well tolerated and effective in improving glycemic control in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus,21 and was used in this study as a positive control to evaluate a novel antidiabetic therapy. The present study investigates the protective effects of WRT against fibrosis- and inflammation-induced renal and cardiac dysfunction in db/db mice, providing insights into its therapeutic potential in managing diabetic complications and highlighting its promise as an adjunctive therapy for complex metabolic disorders.

Methods

Preparation and quality control of WRT granules

The herbal formula WRT, supplied by Hanpung Pharm & Foods Co., Ltd. (Jeonju, Korea), is approved by the Korea Ministry of Food and Drug Safety and was utilized in this study. WRT was prepared as an extract from a mixture of 12 dried herbs, including Alisma plantago-aquatica (1 g), Ziziphus jujuba (1 g), Zingiber officinale (0.67 g), Poria cocos (1 g), Citrus deliciosa (1 g), Polyporus umbellatus (1 g), Atractylodes lancea (1 g), Cinnamomum cassia (0.83 g), Glycyrrhiza uralensis (0.67 g), Magnolia officinalis (1 g), and Paeonia lactiflora (1 g). Quality control adhered to the standards outlined in the Korean Herbal Pharmacopoeia, and botanical names were validated using The Plant List (www.theplantlist.org) and World Flora Online (www.worldfloraonline.org). HPLC analysis was performed using a C18 reverse-phase column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm, Phenomenex, CA, USA). The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (water with 0.1 % formic acid) and solvent B (acetonitrile) under a gradient elution program. The flow rate was set at 1.0 mL/min, the detection wavelength was 230 nm, and the injection volume was 10 μL. Standard compounds (glycyrrhizin, paeoniflorin, and hesperidin) were used for calibration, and retention times were confirmed by comparison with authentic standards. Glycyrrhizin, paeoniflorin, and hesperidin were selected as marker compounds because they are representative constituents of Glycyrrhiza uralensis, Paeonia lactiflora, and Citrus deliciosa, respectively. Their quantification was performed to ensure batch-to-batch consistency of WRT according to the Korean Herbal Pharmacopoeia and related quality control standards. The chromatogram of WRT showing glycyrrhizin, paeoniflorin, and hesperidin is presented in Supplement 1. The contents of these compounds in WRT extract were determined as follows: glycyrrhizin 4.749 mg/g (as dry extract), paeoniflorin 6.62 mg/g (as dry extract), and hesperidin 10.82 mg/g (as dry extract).

Experimental design and animal treatment protocols

This study was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the National Institutes of Health and was approved by the Animal Experiment Ethics Committee of Wonkwang University (Approval No: WKU 21–18). Eight-week-old male C57BLKS/J db/db and db/m mice were obtained from Clea Japan (Tokyo, Japan) and housed under standard laboratory conditions (22°C, 50–60 % relative humidity, and a 12-hour light/dark cycle). The mice were randomly divided into 4 groups (n=7 per group): (1) control group (db/m mice), (2) diabetes group (db/db mice), (3) positive control group (db/db mice treated with vildagliptin at 50 mg/kg/day; Novartis Pharma, Basel, Switzerland), and (4) WRT treatment group (db/db mice treated with WRT at 200 mg/kg/day). Two samples per group were excluded due to sample loss during processing, resulting in a final sample size of n=5 per group for analysis. Both vildagliptin and WRT were administered via oral gavage for the entire experimental period. The doses were chosen based on prior studies to ensure therapeutic efficacy while minimizing potential adverse effects. WRT and vildagliptin were administered for 8 weeks. The control (db/m) and diabetes (db/db) groups received an equivalent volume of vehicle via oral gavage daily to match the administration procedure of the treatment groups.

Analysis of metabolic and biochemical parameters

At the end of the experimental period, the mice were sacrificed. Blood samples were centrifuged at 4°C for 20 min, and the obtained serum was stored at −80°C until analysis. A fully automated biochemical analyzer (FUJI DRI-CHEM NX700; FUJI FILM Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) was used to measure urine albumin, plasma creatinine, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and serum levels of total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-c). Serum insulin (Shibyagi Co., Gunma, Japan) and KIM-1 (LSBIO., Ltd., Ishihara Shibukawa, Gunma, Japan) levels were measured using ELISA kits. Plasma creatinine levels were determined using a colorimetric method with a spectrophotometer (Milton Roy, Rochester, NY, USA). Prior to the end of the experiment, 24-hour urine samples were collected using metabolic cages. All assays were conducted in triplicate.

Assessment of insulin resistance using HOMA-IR

The Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR) was used as an indicator of insulin sensitivity. The index was calculated using the formula: HOMA-IR = {Fasting glucose (mmol/L) × fasting insulin (μIU/mL)}/22.5. This method provided a reliable estimate of insulin resistance, a key feature of type 2 diabetes.

Blood glucose monitoring and oral glucose tolerance tests (OGTT)

Blood glucose concentrations were monitored every four weeks by collecting tail vein blood samples. A glucometer (Onetouch Ultra, Life Scan Inc., CA, USA) and corresponding test strips were used for measurement. For the oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), a glucose solution (2 g/kg body weight) was administered via oral gavage. Tail vein blood samples were collected at 0, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min post-administration to measure glucose concentrations. This test assessed the ability of WRT to improve glucose tolerance in diabetic mice.

Evaluation of cardiac function by echocardiography

Echocardiographic measurements were performed using an ultrasound system (VINO6, Vinno, Suzhou, China) at the end of the experiment. Mice were anesthetized using isoflurane inhalation anesthesia, and the chest area was shaved to ensure proper contact with the ultrasound probe. Measurements included left ventricular systolic diameter (LVDs), diastolic diameter (LVDd), ejection fraction (EF), and fractional shortening (FS) using M-mode images. Left ventricular systolic function was evaluated by calculating ejection fraction (EF) and fractional shortening (FS) from M-mode echocardiographic measurements. EF was determined as the relative change in left ventricular volume between diastole and systole, expressed as (LVIDd³ − LVIDs³) / LVIDd³ × 100, while FS was obtained as the percentage reduction in diameter during systole, (LVIDd − LVIDs) / LVIDd × 100.

Histological and immunohistochemical analysis of cardiac and renal tissues

Following terminal anesthesia with Avertin, the kidneys and hearts were isolated from mice and fixed in 4 % buffered formaldehyde for 24 hours. Tissues were dehydrated in a graded alcohol series, cleared with xylene (Shanghai, People’s Republic of China), embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5–6 μm thickness. Histopathological comparisons were ascertained by staining with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) or Periodic Acid–Schiff (PAS) and visualizing under light microscopy (EVOS™ M5000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Bothell, WA, USA). Kidney and heart fibrosis were assessed using Masson’s Trichrome stain (BBC Biochemical, USA) and Picrosirius Red stain (Polysciences, USA) kits. For immunohistochemical staining, tissue sections were dewaxed and incubated with 1× target retrieval solution (Dako, Carpinteria, CA) for antigen retrieval. Thereafter, they were incubated with 3 % hydrogen peroxide and 5 % bovine serum albumin for 30 min. Subsequently, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. Primary antibodies included Transforming Growth Factor-beta (TGF-β) (Abcam, Cambridge, MA) at a 1:100 dilution and collagen IV (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA, USA) at a 1:100 dilution. The secondary antibodies (1:300–400 dilutions in PBS) were incubated for 1 hour at room temperature. Sections were then treated with peroxidase substrate 3, 3-diaminobenzidine (DAB; Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA) for coloration and counterstained with hematoxylin. To view the myocyte cross-sectional area, the sections were stained with fluorescein-conjugated Wheat Germ Agglutinin (WGA) stain (Alexa Fluor-488, Invitrogen). Images were viewed under a light microscope (EVOS™ M5000; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Bothell, WA, USA).

Protein expression analysis via western blotting

Protein was isolated from kidney tissue using Radio-Immunoprecipitation Assay (RIPA) Lysis and Extraction Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Bothell, WA, USA). The recovered proteins were subjected to 8 - 12 % sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane to confirm protein changes. Blocking was performed for 1 hour with 5 % Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) powder dissolved in 0.05 % Tween 20-Tris-buffered saline. Antibodies against TGF-β1, Connective Tissue Growth Factor (CTGF), Plasminogen Activator Inhibitor-1 (PAI-1), phosphorylated Smad-2 (pSmad-2), Smad-4, fibronectin, collagen IV, alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), Atrial Natriuretic Peptide (ANP), Brain Natriuretic Peptide (BNP), Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha (TNF-α), Interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), Nuclear Factor-kappa B (NF-κB), Bcl-2-associated X protein (Bax), B-cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2), caspase-3, and caspase-9 were purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology Inc. (Santa Cruz, CA, USA). The blots were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 hour. Immunoreactive bands were analyzed densitometrically using the iBright™ FL1000 Imaging System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Bothell, WA, USA).

Gene expression analysis using RT-PCR

RNA was isolated using Qiagen's RNeasy™ Plus Mini Kit. Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) was performed using the standard optimized SYBR Green 2-step qRT-PCR kit protocol (DyNAmo™, Finnzymes, Finland). mRNA changes were analyzed using Opticon MJ Research equipment (Bio-Rad Inc.). Specific sense and antisense primers used were as follows: Collagen IV, sense: 5´-GGT GTT GCAGGAGTGC CAG-3´, antisense: 5´-GCA AGT CGA AAT AAA ACT CAC CAG-3´; CTGF, sense: 5´-GCA AAT AGC CTG TCA ATC TC-3´, antisense: 5´-TCC ATA AAA ATC TGG CTT GT-3´; TGF-β1, sense: 5′-CAA TTC CTG GCG TTA CCT TGG-3´, antisense: 5´-GAA AGC CCT GTA TTC CGT CTC CTT-3´; Smad-2, sense: 5′-AAC CCG AAT GTG CAC CAT AAG AA-3′, antisense: 5′-GCG AGT CTT TGA TGG GTT TAC GA-3′; Smad-3, sense: 5′-GTC AAC AAG TGG CGT GTG-3′, antisense: 5′-GCA AAG GCT TCT GGG ATA A-3′; and GAPDH, sense: 5′-CGA GAA TGG GAA GCT TGT CAT C-3′, antisense: 5′-CGG CCT CAC CCC ATT TG-3′. PCR data were analyzed using the software provided by the manufacturer.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t-test, while comparisons among three or more groups were conducted using one-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s multiple-comparison test. Owing to the small sample size, formal assumption testing was not performed. Data were analyzed using parametric methods commonly applied in exploratory animal studies. All experiments were repeated at least three to five times, and statistical analyses were carried out using SigmaPlot (version 10) and GraphPad Prism (version 5). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

High-performance liquid chromatography analysis of WRT

The chemical characterization of WRT was performed using High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC). Supplement 1 shows the chromatograms for the analysis of glycyrrhizin from Glycyrrhiza uralensis Fisch., paeoniflorin from Paeonia lactiflora Pall., and hesperidin from Citrus deliciosa Ten. The analysis confirmed the presence of glycyrrhizin (4.749 mg/g), paeoniflorin (6.62 mg/g), and hesperidin (10.82 mg/g) in WRT (Supplement 1).

Effects of WRT on body weight and metabolic parameters

After 8 weeks, the difference in the obesity-related body morphology between the control and db/db groups was confirmed (Fig. 1A). The body weight in the db/db group was significantly higher than that of the db/m group (**P <0.01, Fig. 1B). The body weight of the WRT group was numerically lower than that of the control db/db group, but not statistically significant (Fig. 1B). The corresponding densitometric analysis of waistline in the db/db and db/m mice showed that both the WRT and vildagliptin groups had a significantly reduced waistline compared with the untreated db/db group (P < 0.01, Fig. 1C). At the end of the 8-week period, blood glucose, serum creatinine, and BUN levels were lower in the WRT group than in the db/db group, suggesting a potential improvement in renal function; however, these markers may not fully reflect true GFR in mice (P < 0.05). The albumin levels were lower in the WRT-treated group than in the untreated db/db group (P < 0.01, Table 1). The urinary glucose levels of the WRT and db/db groups did not change significantly (Table 1). The TG levels in the db/db mice were significantly higher than those in the db/m mice (P < 0.01). The WRT group showed a marked decrease in the TG ratio (P < 0.05, Table 1). The TC, VLDL-cholesterol, and LDL-c plasma levels of db/db mice were significantly higher than those in db/m mice. The HDL-c levels did not differ significantly between the groups (Table 1). Effects of WRT on blood glucose regulation and insulin sensitivity

The WRT-treated group showed a decrease in fasting blood glucose levels (P < 0.05) compared to the untreated db/db group (Fig. 1D). The blood glucose level did not differ significantly between untreated and WRT-treated db/db mice for up to 30 min. However, 60 min after the loading dose of glucose, the glucose level in the WRT group was significantly lower than that in the untreated group (Fig. 1E). At the end of the experimental period, serum insulin levels in db/db mice were significantly reduced with WRT treatment (P < 0.01, Fig. 1F). Similar to the WRT-treated group, the vildagliptin-treated group, used as a positive control, exhibited significantly lower blood glucose and insulin levels in db/db mice (P < 0.01 or P < 0.05, Fig. 1D and F). WRT-treated or vildagliptin-treated group exhibited significantly lower HOMA-IR indices than the untreated db/db mice (2.7397 or 2.3488 vs. 6.5573; Fig. 1G).

Renal histopathological changes and biomarkers in response to WRT

Kidney weights showed an increasing trend in the db/db group than in the db/m group. However, there were no significant differences in kidney weights after WRT or vildagliptin treatment (Fig. 2A). WRT treatment did not affect kidney weight (Fig. 2B). To determine the effect of WRT on the histological changes in renal injury, PAS staining was performed. PAS staining demonstrated glomerular basement membrane thickening, mesangial expansion, and ECM accumulation in the db/db mice. Glomerulometric determinations revealed significantly decreased glomerular surface area in the WRT-treated db/db mice, with a tendency for reduction in mesangial area (p < 0.05, Fig. 2C,D). In the db/db group, urinary KIM-1/creatinine showed a decreasing trend after WRT treatment, although the change was not statistically significant. In contrast, the vildagliptin-treated group exhibited a significant reduction in mesangial/glomerular area (%) (P < 0.01), accompanied by a marked decrease in urinary KIM-1/creatinine levels (P < 0.01, Fig. 2D,E). The db/db group showed progressive renal fibrosis with increased total collagen deposition compared to the db/m group (Fig. 3A). Western blotting and RT-qPCR demonstrated that overexpression of TGF-β1, CTGF, PAI-1, pSmad-2, and smad-4 in the renal tissue of db/db mice was markedly attenuated after WRT administration (Fig. 3B). In addition, fibronectin, collagen IV, and α-SMA protein expression were downregulated by WRT treatment (Fig. 3C,D). Improvement of cardiac function by WRT treatment

To define whether WRT protects against diabetes-induced cardiac dysfunction in db/db mice, we examined cardiac function using echocardiography. Echocardiographic data showed that db/db mice had significantly lower EF and FS than db/m mice did (p < 0.01, Fig. 4A,B). WRT administration effectively restored EF and FS to 86.85±1.43 % and 52.82±2.69 %, respectively (p < 0.05, Fig. 4B). Similarly, the vildagliptin-treated group exhibited comparable restoration of EF and FS, indicating a similar improvement in cardiac contractile function. The LVDs, LVDd, and LVPWd were significantly higher in db/db mice than in the db/m mice (p < 0.01, Fig. 4B). The LVIDd, LVIDs, and LVPWd were significantly reduced by WRT and vildagliptin treatment to 3.15±0.05; 3.24±0.13, 1.44±0.17; 1.556±0.06, and 0.67±0.10; 1.14±0.03, respectively (p < 0.05 or p < 0.01, Fig. 4B), suggesting improved cardiac geometry. Histological assessment of cardiac hypertrophy and remodeling

Cardiac hypertrophy and fibrosis are common features of diabetic cardiomyopathy and play an important role in diabetes-induced heart remodeling. Cardiac hypertrophy was evident in db/db mice, as reflected by increased heart weight, but not in db/m mice. These hypertrophic changes were significantly attenuated WRT or vildagliptin treatment (Fig. 5A). The results of H&E staining showed that the hearts of the db/m group had a normal cardiac cell shape and that the structures were clearly and densely arranged. However, it was confirmed that cell hypertrophy was clearly observed in the heart of db/db mice. In the heart of the WRT-administered group, it was found that the shape was restored and the space between cells was reduced, and the arrangement of cells was more regular than that of the non-administered group; similarly, H&E staining of the hearts from the vildagliptin-treated group showed improvements approaching the levels observed in db/m mice (Fig. 5B). Alleviation of cardiac fibrosis by WRT administration

To determine whether WRT protects against diabetes-induced cardiac fibrosis in db/db mice, we performed Masson's trichrome and Sirius Red Staining. The staining results showed disorganized myocardium and perivascular and collagen fiber deposition in db/db mice (Fig. 5D). WRT administration significantly reduced the fibrotic area and lipid deposition in the cardiac tissue. In addition, it significantly reduced the expression collagen IV and TGF-β1, as assessed using immunohistochemistry (Fig. 5E) and western blotting (Fig. 5F). These results suggest that WRT treatment can alleviate cardiac fibrosis in db/db mice. In the vildagliptin-treated group, attenuation of cardiac fibrosis was observed by Masson's staining, although no significant suppression was detected in the protein expression levels of fibrotic factors (Fig. 5D-F).

Anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic effects of WRT in cardiac tissue

The inflammatory response is involved in the development and progression of cardiomyopathy. Changes in the expression of NF-κB and inflammatory cytokines interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) were confirmed by western blot analysis. The protein expression levels of the inflammatory markers were upregulated in db/db mice and were attenuated after WRT administration (p < 0.01 or p < 0.05, Fig. 5G). In addition, the changes in the Bcl2 family proteins levels, including cle-caspase-3, cle-caspase-9, Bax, and Bcl-2, were also measured by western blot analysis. The levels of cle-caspase-3, cle-caspase-9, and Bax proteins were significantly increased in the hearts of db/db mouse (Fig. 5H). However, the upregulation of these apoptotic mediators was significantly reduced with WRT treatment. The effects of vildagliptin on cardiac inflammatory and apoptotic markers were comparable to or slightly greater than those observed in the WRT-treated group (Fig. 5G,H).

Discussion

Summary of key findings

Interest in complementary and alternative approaches to diabetes treatment has been increasing, particularly therapies based on natural products with antiglycemic activity.22,23 While modern antidiabetic agents such as SGLT2 inhibitors, DPP-4 inhibitors, and GLP-1 receptor agonists have expanded treatment options for type 2 diabetes, these therapies still have limitations, highlighting the need for complementary or adjunctive strategies.24,25 Traditional formulations like Wiryeong-tang (WRT), a combination of Pyeong-Wei-San and Wu-Ling-San, have a long history of use for gastrointestinal and urinary disorders, and recent studies suggest potential benefits for nephritis and hyperlipidemia.18, 19, 20

Our study demonstrates that WRT exerts multifaceted protective effects in db/db mice. In the kidney, WRT reduced 24-hour urinary albumin excretion and improved plasma creatinine levels, while ameliorating histopathological alterations such as glomerular fibrosis and mesangial matrix expansion. These renal effects are likely linked to improvements in glucose control and insulin resistance, suggesting that WRT may modulate metabolic pathways that contribute to diabetic nephropathy. Similarly, in the heart, WRT mitigated cardiac hypertrophy and fibrosis, decreased pro-hypertrophy markers (ANP, BNP), suppressed excessive collagen deposition, and regulated pro-inflammatory cytokines (NF-κB, TNF-α, IL-1β) as well as apoptotic mediators (cle-caspase-3, cle-caspase-9, Bax, Bcl-2), resulting in improved cardiac function. These findings suggested that WRT may provide comprehensive protective effects against diabetes-induced cardiorenal complications by simultaneously targeting key molecular pathways in both renal and cardiac tissues.

Importantly, vildagliptin treatment produced similar or slightly greater improvements in both renal and cardiac parameters. These findings indicate that WRT showed measurable protective effects alongside the reference treatment in this model. However, in this study, formal non-inferiority or superiority testing was not performed, and therefore, direct head-to-head studies would be needed to accurately compare the two groups. Collectively, these results suggest that WRT may have potential as a complementary or adjunctive therapy alongside established antidiabetic agents, offering a potential integrative approach for the management of type 2 diabetes and its associated cardiorenal complications.

Comparison with previous studies

The present findings demonstrating WRT’s renoprotective and cardioprotective effects in db/db mice are largely consistent with previous in vitro and in vivo studies.26,27 WRT inhibited the upregulation of fibrosis-promoting growth factors (TGF-β1 and CTGF) and extracellular matrix accumulation in the kidney, in line with prior reports showing suppression of high glucose-induced TGF-β1, collagen, and fibronectin expression in mesangial cells.28, 29, 30 These results extend earlier observations by confirming functional improvement in vivo, including attenuation of glomerular hypertrophy and renal fibrosis, suggesting a mechanistic link to TGF-β/Smad signaling modulation.

Regarding cardiac outcomes, WRT mitigated pathological myocardial remodeling, consistent with previous studies reporting its anti-hypertrophic effects.31,22 Echocardiographic assessment revealed that WRT restored left ventricular function (EF and FS) and reduced ventricular dimensions (LVIDs, LVIDd, LVPWd), reflecting improvements in cardiac contractility and structure. In addition, WRT decreased cardiac fibrosis and suppressed pro-inflammatory and pro-apoptotic mediators, including NF-κB, TNF-α, IL-1β, cleaved caspases, and Bax/Bcl-2, highlighting its multi-faceted cardioprotective mechanisms.17,32 Taken together, these comparisons suggest that WRT’s modulation of profibrotic, pro-hypertrophic, inflammatory, and apoptotic pathways provides integrated protection against diabetic cardiorenal complications, supporting its potential as a multitarget therapeutic agent.

Clinical and scientific implications

The dual renoprotective and cardioprotective effects of WRT highlight its potential as an adjunctive therapy for type 2 diabetes and associated complications. By modulating glucose homeostasis, insulin resistance, fibrotic signaling (TGF-β/Smad) in the kidney, inflammation, and apoptosis, as well as pro-inflammatory/apoptotic pathways (NF-κB, TNF-α, IL-1β, caspases, Bax/Bcl-2) in the heart, WRT may target multiple pathogenic mechanisms underlying diabetic nephropathy and cardiomyopathy. By influencing these pathways, WRT demonstrates potential to mitigate structural remodeling, fibrosis, and dysfunction in both renal and cardiac tissues, which are hallmarks of type 2 diabetes progression.33, 34, 35 While direct clinical extrapolation remains premature, these findings support further translational evaluation of WRT or its active constituents in future clinical studies targeting diabetic cardiorenal complications. From a scientific perspective, the study supports the concept that multitargeted interventions addressing both metabolic control and tissue-specific molecular pathways can provide comprehensive protection against diabetic complications. These findings also offer a mechanistic rationale for further preclinical and clinical investigations into traditional herbal formulations as potential therapeutic agents in diabetes management.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study include the comprehensive assessment of both renal and cardiac outcomes in a clinically relevant db/db mouse model, allowing a thorough evaluation of WRT’s cardiorenal protective effects under diabetic conditions. The experimental design, which compared WRT with vehicle and vildagliptin, enabled clear interpretation of effects on glucose regulation, renal injury and fibrosis, as well as cardiac structure and function.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be noted. WRT is a complex mixture of multiple herbal components, and the specific active constituents responsible for its effects remain unidentified, highlighting the need for further phytochemical characterization and mechanistic studies. As a multi-herbal natural product, WRT also poses intrinsic challenges such as variations in chemical composition due to plant growth conditions, which may affect reproducibility and constitute an important limitation of this study. Additionally, creatinine-based assessment of renal function in mice may be influenced by species-specific handling and excretion differences, suggesting that alternative approaches such as FITC-sinistrin clearance could provide more reliable evaluation of glomerular filtration.

Another limitation is that, although HPLC analysis identified glycyrrhizin, paeoniflorin, and hesperidin as representative markers, the specific constituents directly responsible for the observed effects remain unclear. Each of these compounds has been reported to exert distinct pharmacological activities, including anti-inflammatory, antifibrotic, antioxidative, and lipid-lowering effects,36, 37, 38, 39 suggesting that the protective actions of WRT may arise from their combined and potentially synergistic interactions. However, this study did not perform correlation analyses linking these markers to efficacy outcomes. Future investigations will therefore focus on fractionation and dose–response studies to identify the principal active components of WRT and to clarify possible synergistic mechanisms.

Conclusions and suggestions for future research

This study demonstrates that Wiryeongtang (WRT) significantly ameliorates type 2 diabetes-associated cardiorenal complications in db/db mice through anti-fibrotic, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic mechanisms. WRT improved glucose control and insulin resistance while mitigating key pathological features of diabetic nephropathy and cardiomyopathy, including reductions in 24-hour urinary albumin excretion, improvements in renal function markers, attenuation of renal fibrosis and glomerular hypertrophy, and alleviation of cardiac hypertrophy and dysfunction (Supplement 3). Notably, the renoprotective effects of WRT showed effects of similar direction to vildagliptin, suggesting its potential as an adjunct or alternative therapeutic option. Future research should focus on identifying the active constituents of WRT, elucidating its precise molecular targets, and evaluating its therapeutic efficacy in clinical settings to explore its potential as a complementary strategy for managing diabetic complications. Long-term and large-scale investigations will be essential to further establish its role in the management of type 2 diabetes and its associated cardiorenal complications.
