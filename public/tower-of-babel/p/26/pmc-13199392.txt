Developing a novel index for neighborhood social determinants of cardiovascular diseases in the CARDIA study
Authors: Tao Gao, Yinan Zheng, Brian T. Joyce, Lei Liu, Lili Liu, Catarina Kiefe, Sarah Forrester, Penny Gordon-Larsen, Chunyu Liu, Donald Lloyd-Jones, Kai Zhang, Lifang Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Neighborhood social determinants of health have been shown to influence cardiovascular risk and the development of cardiovascular disease, but few studies have been done about early-life exposure as well as the combined effect of multiple neighborhood social determinants of health measures. Each 1-standard-deviation increase in the neighborhood social determinants of health index is associated with increased risk of coronary artery calcification at the year 7, year 15, and year 20 visits (Odds Ratios range from 1.15 to 1.17). The area under the Receiver Operating Characteristics curve ranges between 0.718 (year 7) and 0.719 (year 20). Compared to White participants, Black participants have a significant association between neighborhood social determinants of health indices and coronary artery calcification at Y15 and at Y20. In conclusion, higher early adulthood neighborhood social determinants of health indices are associated with higher middle-aged coronary artery calcification risk; this association is significant in Black adults at older age compared with White adults.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the foremost cause of mortality in both the United States and worldwide1. Social determinants of health (SDH) have been shown to influence cardiovascular risk and the development of CVD2–4. SDH may be measured at the individual level or at the contextual level by characteristics of the neighborhood in which an individual lives. Individual-level SDH includes economic stability, educational attainment, occupation, and access to healthcare, while neighborhood-level SDH includes neighborhood deprivation measured by factors such as the proportion of residences owned versus rented, the density of fast-food restaurants, or the mean levels of education and income for families residing in the neighborhood. Both the individual- and the contextual-level factors are known to affect health, well-being, and quality of life5, playing a substantial role in the increased risk of CVD6. Neighborhood SES indices using census tract variables comprising neighborhood income, occupation, employment, and wealth have demonstrated that people with lower neighborhood SES levels had up to 3.1 times higher risk of developing CVD compared to those with higher neighborhood SES levels7,8.

Various approaches have been proposed to integrate SDH and evaluate their associations with adverse outcomes9–11. For example, the Area Deprivation Index (ADI) from the Neighborhood Atlas offered a measure of neighborhood disadvantage metrics9. The Social Vulnerability Index (SVI), developed by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention/Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry (CDC/ATSDR), was constructed using 15 variables from the U.S. Census10. A Neighborhood Socioeconomic Deprivation Score (NDS) was also developed to evaluate the association between neighborhood SES and health outcomes11. Factors such as tract-level census indicators of socioeconomic status, including household income and education level, were used in calculating the score. However, all these indices have shown lower effect estimates for specific diseases. Through the combination of conventional regression models and machine-learning methods, the Boosted regression tree (BRT) model might offer a better way to estimate associations for diseases like CVD12. BRT models accommodate complex linear and nonlinear responses to the community-level exposures and covariates while being relatively insensitive to collinearity problems13. Because BRT models are based on a flexible binary-split algorithm, BRT models are able to generate robust estimates despite outliers or missing values commonly observed from community survey data13. A previous study applied BRT models to individual SDH factors and found significant associations with left ventricular mass index (LVMI)14. However, neighborhood SDH captures population-level and contextual influences that are not reflected by individual factors and may be more relevant for informing public health interventions and policy.

The Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults (CARDIA) study, with longitudinal measures of social determinants of health from young adulthood and subclinical atherosclerosis in midlife, offers a unique framework to explore the links between neighborhood social determinants of health (nSDH) and subclinical atherosclerosis15. Thus, we aimed to quantify the association between nSDH factors and midlife subclinical cardiovascular disease – coronary artery calcification (CAC).

Results

Table 1 shows descriptive analyses of participant characteristics at the Y25 visit. CAC measurements were available for 3499 CARDIA participants. The study participants were 56.6% female, 46.9% Black, and the average age was 50 ± 4 (SD) years at the Y25 visit. The participants lived in neighborhoods where, on average, 12% of the population did not have a high school diploma, and 23% had a household income of less than 150% of the federal poverty level. Additionally, over 60% of the population were not employed in professional or management occupations, and 10% were unemployed. We assessed the trend of risk factors as well as the nSDH factors over the repeated years of follow-up. For the nSDH factors, both the proportion of the population with education less than high school and with a non-professional occupation decreased during follow-up. Discussion

We developed nSDH indices by combining measures of neighborhood socioeconomic characteristics, food availability, and physical activity facilities as well as crime rates. The nSDH indices were associated with risk of midlife CVD after adjusting for lifestyle factors, and the associations were significant among the Black population at older age (~40-45 y) compared with White population at relatively younger age (~30 y).

In our study, nSDH indices were associated with mid-age subclinical CVD, especially for those neighborhood SDH indices from the Y15 and Y20 visits in Black Adults. While adverse neighborhood SDH factors at a younger age increased the CVD risk, those SDH factors that were measured close to middle age had higher associations with the risk of CVD. Living in a socially deprived (such as low income, poor living environment, and high crime levels) neighborhood has been shown in the Swedish population to be associated with increased stress and more adverse lifestyle factors such as smoking and low-quality diet16. Socioeconomically disadvantaged neighborhoods also present lower quality living conditions, including poor walkability and limited availability of green spaces to encourage physical activity, which have been shown to be associated with increased risk of CVD17. For middle-aged adults, living in a food desert limits their access to affordable, healthy food, which has been linked to poor CV health in the META-Health (Morehouse and Emory Team up to Eliminate Health Disparities) study and the Predictive Health study18. Residents of food deserts must travel further distances to obtain healthy food options, and tend to have low-quality diets marked by a greater intake of high-energy, nutrient-deficient foods19–21. A former study in CARDIA also showed that living in neighborhoods with greater availability of “unhealthy” food outlets (fast-food chain restaurants and convenience stores) was associated with risk of developing CAC22. On the other hand, another study in MESA found that greater access to neighborhood healthy food resources slowed the development of coronary atherosclerosis23.

We also found that the association of nSDH indices with subclinical CVD was significant in the Black population at older age compared with the White population. In the US, Black adults have lower overall cardiovascular health compared with White adults24. Race differences in environmental, social, and individual exposures have been shown to contribute to health disparities25. Compared to White individuals, Black individuals are more likely to live in high-poverty neighborhoods26. Neighborhood disadvantage, including concerns about crime, and fewer physical resources such as supermarkets and physical activity facilities, all have unfavorable implications for cardiovascular risk among self-reported black race27,28. Also structural policies like residential segregation and redlining (disinvestment in Black neighborhoods and increased neighborhood violence and stress) and environmental racism (higher exposure to pollutants) could explain the racial difference in the association between nSDH factors and cardiovascular diseases29,30.

The findings we report should be interpreted in the context of the study’s strengths and limitations. The strengths of this analysis include the longitudinal follow-up of CARDIA participants from young adulthood through middle age. Furthermore, CARDIA is a balanced cohort by race, which allows for race-specific analyses. However, our study has limitations as well. First, race is a social construct, and so the differences we observed likely relate to a series of unmeasured factors captured by self-reported race. Second, the lack of data related to accessibility to healthcare may also affect the assessment of the association in our study. Further, there are other unmeasured confounding factors like environmental pollution or unmeasured comorbidities, which may affect the associations between neighborhood SDH factors mentioned above and the CVD risk. We did not use causal modeling, so we are only able to study associations. Due to the CARDIA study design, participants were limited to Black and White individuals, which may restrict the generalizability of our findings to other racial and ethnic groups.

In conclusion, in this study, we developed an nSDH index that integrated both neighborhood socioeconomics, food and physical activity facilities as well as crime rate factors. Using this index, we found that higher nSDH indices were associated with increased CAC risk in midlife. The association between nSDH index and CVD was significant in CARDIA participants of Black adults at older age versus White adults. Further studies are warranted to delve into the complex mechanisms that link neighborhood SDH to CVD.

Methods

Study population

The CARDIA Study is a multicenter, longitudinal, population-based cohort of 5,115 Black or White men and women, who were 18 to 30 years at baseline (year: 1985 to 1986) and recruited from 4 urban areas (Birmingham, Alabama; Chicago, Illinois; Minneapolis, Minnesota; and Oakland, California)15. Within each center, the sample was designed to have approximately equal numbers of participants by sex, race (Black vs White), age (18 to 24 years vs 25 to 30 years), and education (high school graduate or less, vs beyond high school). Eight follow-up examinations have been conducted through year 30 (2015 to 2016), with 71% of the surviving cohort attending the year 30 (Y30) examination15,31. To assess the presence of subclinical coronary artery disease, 3499 participants with available chest computed tomography (CT) scan for CAC measures at the year 25 (2010-2011) visit were included in the study. All examinations were approved by the institutional review boards at each participating institution (including University of Alabama at Birmingham, Northwestern University, University of Minnesota and Kaiser Foundation Research Institute), and all participants gave written informed consent.

Neighborhood SDH (nSDH) and personal characteristics measures

nSDH measures

Neighborhood-level SDH (nSDH) factors were measured from each available CARDIA visit (Y0, 7, 10, 15, 20, 25). nSDH factors were obtained and compiled from U.S. Census data (1980, 1990, 2000, and 2010) and two five-year estimates from the American Community Survey (2005-2009 and 2010-2014) using geocoded addresses of CARDIA participants32,33. Various types of variables were extracted: Census tract-level variables: percent population of a race other than white; percent population with less than a high school diploma; proportion with a blue-collar job; proportion older than age 16 in the workforce without a job; proportion below 150% of the poverty level; median household income; median gross rent; median house value and total crime rate per 100,000 people. Historic physical activity and Food facility data were acquired from the Dun and Bradstreet database (a commercial listing of US businesses) and the Environmental Systems Research Institute’s StreetMap product including: 1) count of physical activity facilities located within a 3-km Euclidean buffer including community and public physical activity facilities and public parks; 2) count of food outlets within a 3-km Euclidean buffer including grocery store/supermarket, specialty shop and restaurant34.

Individual characteristics measures

Participants’ BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. Cigarette smoking status was classified as never, ever, or current; The CARDIA Physical Activity History questionnaire was used to estimate participants’ weekly leisure, occupational, and household physical exertion over the past 12 months35.

CT measure of CAC

To measure subclinical coronary artery disease, participants in the CARDIA study underwent a multidetector computed tomography (CT) chest scan at Y25 using a standardized protocol36. A standard protocol for non-contrast CT scan was used to measure CAC36 and a single CT scan was performed at Y25. The CT images were electronically transmitted to the central CT reading center located at Wake Forest University School of Medicine and measured by experienced image analysts. The Agatston score was calculated corrected for slice thickness, with a minimum calcification area of 1.87 mm2 and attenuation threshold of 130 or more Hounsfield units on a dedicated computer workstation (TeraRecon)37. Robustness of the CAC score has been published38–40. Incident CAC was defined as CAC score > 0.

Statistical analysis

Participant characteristics were summarized using mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables and count with percentage for categorical variables. Pearson correlation between nSDH factors was assessed. Boosted Regression Tree (BRT) models were used to regress incident CAC risk status against nSDH to evaluate the contribution of each factor12,13. As a boosted version of the traditional regression tree model, the BRT model was performed using the “gbm” function in the “dismo” package in “R (version 4.2.2)” software. In the BRT model, the learning rate and tree complexity were set to be 0.001 and 5, respectively, as used in previous studies12,13. A contribution was determined for each predictor according to the number of times it was selected as one of the “optimal” predictors. Contribution can also be interpreted as the relative influence (%) of each predictor. The relative influence of each variable is scaled so that the sum adds to 100, with higher numbers indicating stronger influence on the response. The contribution of the nSDH factors was compared for each CARDIA visit. For each nSDH factor, the change of its contribution to the risk of CAC during the CARDIA visits was assessed. Each nSDH factor with its contribution to CAC was then weighted and summed to develop the neighborhood social determinants of health index12. (Appendix A) We standardized the index at each visit using the visit-specific standard deviation to facilitate comparison of effects across study years. We evaluated the accuracy of the index with the Receiver Operating Characteristics (ROC) curve. The models were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR procedure. All models were adjusted for study center, age, sex, race, body‑mass index, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and physical activity. We further adjusted for TC/HDL ratio, systolic blood pressure and diabetes. As a sensitivity analysis, dietary intake was adjusted for. All analyses (with the exception of the BRT models) were performed using SAS (version 9.4, SAS Institute). Two-sided tests were applied throughout, and p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.
