Variants of uncertain significance in precision oncology: nuance or nuisance?
Authors: George S Mellgard, Zoey Atabek, Meredith LaRose, Fay Kastrinos, Susan E Bates
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractVariants of unknown significance cause uncertainty for patients and are a challenge for oncologists. This commentary describes 4 clinical examples illustrating these challenges.

One or more variants of unknown significance (VUS) were detected in this patient’s tumor. These variants may not have been adequately characterized in the scientific literature at the time this report was issued, and/or the genomic context of these alterations makes their significance unclear. We choose to include them here in the event that they become clinically meaningful in the future.1

The above disclaimer appears at the end of a genomic profile for a patient with metastatic pancreatic cancer and a family history of cancer. While this passage is drawn from a FoundationOne CDx report, it could represent a boilerplate disclaimer for any commercial genomic assay.1 Currently, a VUS is defined as a genetic variant for which there is insufficient information to classify as pathogenic or benign.2

VUSs can cause intense uncertainty for patients and are a vexing challenge for oncologists (Figure 1).3 Concerns arise in 3 ways—first, if the VUS occurs in a gene previously established to confer an inherited predisposition for cancer, it could have implications for siblings and offspring, determining who should be screened. Second, if the VUS occurs in a gene for which a targeted therapy might bring benefit, it could have implications for treatment.3 Finally, if the VUS is actually a driver mutation, an underlying biological mechanism might be missed.4-6 Below we provide 4 clinical examples (composite clinical stories) of patients with pancreatic cancer, a family history of cancer, and germline or somatic VUSs, to illustrate the challenges of VUS in clinical practice. Moreover, we use these cases to highlight 2 papers in The Oncologist that discuss ongoing efforts to reclassify VUSs and highlight their impact on treatment choice.7,8

Perplexed in precision medicine: uncertainty upon finding a VUS in the report of yet another patient.

Case 1: The frequency of VUSs and their reclassification

Patient 1 presents with a pancreatic head mass and is subsequently found to have metastatic pancreatic adenocarcinoma. The patient’s family history is notable for a parent with ovarian cancer and a sibling with pancreatic cancer. Germline testing shows a VUS in ATM.

This case illustrates the fundamental challenge of VUSs for the oncologist. How can we reconcile multiple first-degree relatives with cancer and either no or clinically unknown variants in major cancer genes? Are all VUSs truly insignificant and destined to be reclassified as benign or are they hidden targets for therapy and family genetic screening?

The number of VUSs that remain unclassified within major cancer genes underscores this challenge: 62% (474/766) of mutations in ATM, 70% (215/307) in BRCA1, 75% (513/683) in BRCA2, and 68% (101/148) in CHEK2 are currently labeled as VUS in the samples of the Cancer Genome Atlas (Figure 2).9-11 Despite this large number, their reclassification remains challenging due to discrepancies in individual assays and the (reasonably) high threshold for ensuring a variant has clinical significance.

Changing the pathogenicity of a VUS requires a multimodal approach using existing literature, genomic databases (eg, cBioPortal and OncoKB), and computational predictive models.7,8,11 Commercial vendors employ a classification algorithm to reclassify variants. Such systems take into account clinical and in silico studies, mutation type, and VUS frequency in the population when determining pathogenicity.12 Prior retrospective work has approximated that in a 10-year period, 7.7% of individual variants were reclassified with 8.7% of those reclassified as more pathogenic.13 Moreover, VUSs in major cancer genes (eg, BRCA1/2, CDH1, and CHEK2) were included among those upgraded in pathogenicity. As the cost of whole-genome sequencing decreases and more frequent utilization increases the number of identified VUSs, novel strategies for reclassification will be paramount for making accurate decisions regarding the results.

Case 2: VUSs and choice of treatment

Patient 2 is diagnosed with borderline resectable pancreatic adenocarcinoma and a family history of breast and prostate cancer. Germline testing shows no clinically significant variants or VUSs. Patient subsequently undergoes a partial pancreatectomy with somatic sequencing of tumor tissue notable for KRAS mutation and a VUS in BRCA2.

In this case, the determination of pathogenicity of the BRCA2 variants may impact the choice of treatment. Platinum-containing chemotherapy regimens have been shown to be effective in pancreatic cancer bearing loss-of-function mutations in BRCA2, ATM, and other homologous recombination repair genes.14 In addition, olaparib may be considered in the event of metastatic recurrence, given the known susceptibility of cancers harboring pathogenic BRCA mutations to PARP inhibition.15,16 Ongoing efforts to identify additional pathogenic mutations as part of the standard of care have yielded promising results. Germline sequencing in patients with metastatic pancreatic adenocarcinoma reported putatively deleterious germline mutations in 19.5% (26/133) of tumor samples, suggesting susceptibility to PARP inhibitors.17

Case 3: VUSs, family history, and genetic counseling

Patient 3 presents with a new pancreatic head mass with biopsy confirming adenocarcinoma. Notably, a sibling had previously died from pancreatic cancer. Genetic testing revealed a VUS in MLH1 and the patient was advised to seek genetic counseling.

Here, it might appear prudent to ignore the MLH1 VUS as unrelated to the cancer diagnosis and focus on treating the cancer. However, such an approach denies patients the opportunity for shared and informed decision-making regarding genetic counseling and familial screening. It is currently our practice to refer patients with a family history and a newly discovered VUS to genetic counseling. While some may argue this approach can unnecessarily increase anxiety, such a practice not only gives patients the autonomy to opt for genetic screening but also provides the necessary connection such that a future pathogenic reclassification might lead to genetic counseling for family members. Current practices for the management of reclassification of VUSs by companies such as Ambry rely heavily on physician engagement and notify physicians when a reclassification occurs.

Case 4: Reconciling somatic VUS with germline VUS

Patient 4 is diagnosed with pancreatic adenocarcinoma and undergoes pancreaticoduodenectomy. Patient’s family history is notable for a parent with pancreatic cancer and a sibling with prostate cancer. Germline testing shows only an NF1 VUS, while circulating tumor DNA is positive for a pathogenic APC mutation.

Discrepancies between germline and somatic VUS add an additional layer of complexity to VUSs, beyond the recognition that fewer genes are analyzed in germline testing than in somatic testing. Currently, there is no standardized process for interpreting somatic and germline mutations together. Previous attempts have used tumor genomic profiles and tumor immunohistochemistry to determine the pathogenicity of germline VUS.18 In one study of 623 somatic variants from tumor tissue, 5% had a different classification from germline test results and 4 of those variants had differences in pathogenicity altering treatment choice.19

A further consideration

One aspect of this problem is that pathogenic, germline mutations are typically loss-of-function mutations in tumor suppressors, with mutations spread across the entire coding region (see Figure 2 in Fu and Rabadan), as opposed to the hotspot mutations seen in oncogenic drivers.9-11 It is often difficult to reconcile how a mutation in one base pair is pathogenic and an alteration in the very next codon of a tumor suppressor should be innocuous.

On that note, there is relevant data regarding pathogenic TET3 mutations and VUSs. Investigators analyzed genome-wide DNA methylation and identified an episignature that distinguished pathogenic TET3 loss. They then evaluated patients with TET3 VUSs and used the episignature to reclassify several VUSs as pathogenic or partially pathogenic, and reclassified TET3 in 8 patients as being pathogenic. This opens a Pandora’s box for germline mutations—that it may not always be a binary classification—pathogenic vs. nonpathogenic. This non-binary classification is well understood for the Adenomatosis Polyposis Coli (APC) gene, a tumor suppressor, disruption of which is associated with colorectal cancer. APC pathogenic mutations demonstrate variable penetrance. In fact, the location of germline mutations within the APC gene has provided insight into what was previously known as an either “attenuated” or “classic” phenotype of familial adenomatous polyposis syndrome.20 Currently, APC variants are classified based on how much impact the mutation has on protein expression or function and is determined by assigned classification criteria including “pathogenic very strong,” “pathogenic strong,” “pathogenic moderate,” and “pathogenic supporting.”21

In sum, the straightforward story of precision medicine that emerged in 2001 with the approval of imatinib for chronic myelogenous leukemia has expanded to numerous tumor types and blossomed into a far more nuanced and complex entity than imagined 2 decades ago. NGS results often leave both patients and physicians perplexed. And further, questions arise about how interactions between unseen carcinogens and uncertain variants might lead to what was once thought to be a spontaneous malignancy.22 As we look forward, precision medicine paired with ongoing developments in large language models has the potential to continue unraveling the uncertainty of VUS and ensure our patients get the care they deserve.
