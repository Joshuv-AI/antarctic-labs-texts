Defining the bacterial microbiome of ticks in Chongwe and Chisamba Districts of Zambia
Authors: Malala Mulavu, Cynthia Sipho Khumalo, Lavel Moonga, Kyoko Hayashida, Benjamin Mubemba, Katendi Changula, Edgar Simulundu, Walter Muleya, Simbarashe Chitanga
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Microbial communities hosted by ticks in Zambia are diverse.•Illumina high-throughput sequencing to analyze the 16S rRNA gene of both Rickettsia-free and Rickettsia-infected ticks.•Variations due to Rickettsia, location, tick life stages and species of ticks.•Alpha and Beta diversity of tick microbiomes in Zambia were shown.•Differences in Amblyomma, Hyalomma and Rhipicephalus tick microbiomes present in Zambian ticks.

Introduction

Ticks emerge as the primary vectors for transmitting a diverse array of pathogens to humans and domestic animals, surpassing other vector groups in this regard. They stand out as crucial vectors for medically and veterinary-relevant pathogens [1,2]. The catalogue of pathogens conveyed by ticks to mammalian hosts continues to expand, driven in part by climatic changes and advancements in detection methods [3]. Consequently, there exists a pressing need to formulate novel strategies for tick control, infection prevention, and the disruption of tick-borne pathogen transmission [4].

Recent attention has been directed toward investigating the tick microbiome, recognizing its potential to unveil the intricate interactions among various elements within ticks during the transmission of pathogens. In general, studies on the tick microbiome have shown that it is complex and can be influenced by both biotic and abiotic factors such as sex, species, life stage, geographic location as well as host on which it feeds [1,[5], [6], [7]]. The influence of the host on the microbiome has also been ascribed to the influence of the blood meal [8].

The crucial role played by tick-borne pathogens (TBPs) in causing diseases in both humans and animals underscores the paramount importance of understanding their role in the tick microbiome. Among TBPs, Rickettsia stands out as one of the most prominent members of the tick microbiome, with extensive prevalence among ticks [4,9]. Various Rickettsia species, including R. africae, R. aeschlimanni, R. massiliae, and R. conorii, have been consistently reported in numerous studies done in Africa [[9], [10], [11], [12], [13]].

Recent research has shed light on the significant role that specific Rickettsia species, such as R. helvetica, play in shaping the tick microbiome [14,15] and has shown the importance of understanding the effects of Rickettsia on the microbiome in various settings.

Livestock farming in Zambia continues to be a crucial source of income for a significant portion of the population [16]. Consequently, TBPs such as Rickettsia pose a substantial threat to the livelihoods of many. Despite the significance of this issue, there is limited knowledge about the tick microbiome in Zambia and the specific role that Rickettsia plays in shaping it. To address this gap, this study aimed to identify the components within the microbiome of ticks collected from the Chongwe and Chisamba districts of Zambia. Additionally, the study sought to assess the influence of Rickettsia's presence on microbiome composition with regard to the sampling site, life stage, and tick genus.

Materials and methods

Study Setting

After a thorough inspection of the cattle head, ears, teat and perianal region, all visible ticks were removed from the animals using tweezers. Ticks were also collected from the environment by standard dragging and flagging methods using 1m² white cloths [17]. The ticks were placed into a ventilated collection container. The ticks were collected from seven different veterinary camps across Chongwe (Kapete, Kanakantapa, Palabana, Chalimbana) (15.19344°S, 28.40446°E) and Chisamba (Mwomboshi, Lufwambula, Chipembi) districts (14.93701°S, 28.57161°E) in December 2020 and June 2021 respectively. The sampling sites were chosen because both districts are predominantly recognized as livestock farming districts.

Tick identification and DNA extraction

Ticks were identified to genus, sex, and developmental stage morphologically using a dichotomous key described previously by Walker et al. (2003). The ticks were washed for a minute in PBS, 70% alcohol and PBS again for surface sterilisation prior to DNA extraction in order to remove external bacteria.

For molecular analysis, DNA was extracted from each tick using the MN NucleoSpin® tissue kit (Macherey-Nagel, Düren, Germany) following the manufacturer's protocol. Further, PCR was used for tick identification targeting Cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1(COI), the mitochondrial 12S rDNA and the 16S rDNA genes, using the primer pairs in Table 1. Briefly, PCR reactions were performed in 20 µl reactions consisting of 2.5 µl of DNA (5 ng/µl), 0.6 µl of both forward and reverse primers each at 0.3 µM concentration, 4 µl of 5X PrimeSTAR® GXL buffer (Takara Bio Inc, Shiga, Japan), 1.6 µl dNTP mix (2.5 mM), 0.8 µl PrimeSTAR® DNA polymerase and 9.9 µl of nuclease-free water. The thermal cycling profile was as follows; 30 cycles of 98°C for 10 s, 60°C for 15 s and 68°C for 30 s. PCR products were visualized on a 2% agarose gel stained with ethidium bromide. PCR-positive DNA amplicons were purified using the Wizard® Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Promega, Wisconsin, USA). Purified products were then amplicon sequenced using both the forward and reverse primers using Big Dye™ Terminator Cycle Sequencing Kit v3.1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA). Screening for Rickettsia

Genomic DNA extracted from the ticks was tested for Rickettsia infection by means of PCR using primers targeting a 511 bp DNA fragment of the Rickettsia ompB gene [22]. The PCR reaction mixture contained 5 µl of OneTaq® 2X Master Mix with Standard Buffer (New England BioLabs Inc, Massachusetts, USA), 0.48 µM of the forward and reverse primers (10 µM each), 3.04 µL nuclease-free water and 1.0 µL DNA template. The thermal cycling profile was as follows: initial denaturation at 95°C for 1 minute, followed by 35 cycles of 95°C for 30 s, 54°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 1 minute. An elongation step at 72°C for 10 minutes was included. PCR products were then visualised on 2% agarose gel stained with ethidium bromide. Samples that were PCR-positive for Rickettsia were then classified as “Rickettsia-positive” and those that were PCR negative for Rickettsia were classified as “Rickettsia-negative” going forward.

16S rRNA amplicon PCR and library preparation

For sequencing, the 550 bp V3–V4 region of 16S rRNA was amplified using PrimeSTAR® enzyme mix (Takara bio inc, Japan) and the Illumina 16s amplicon primer pair (Table 1).

PCR thermal cycling conditions were: 30 cycles of 98°C for 10 s, 55°C for 15 s and 68°C for 30 s. Electrophoresis on a 1.5% ethium bromide-stained agarose gel was performed to assess the amplification success. The 16S rRNA PCR amplicons were then purified using the MN NucleoMag® Next Generation Sequencing (NGS) beads (Macherey-Nagel, Düren, Germany) according to the manufacturer's protocol. For easy identification of the samples in the bioinformatic analysis, dual indexes were added to individual samples using Nextera XT Index kit (Illumina, California, USA) following the manufacturer's protocol. The resulting barcoded amplicons were again cleaned using the MN NucleoMag® NGS beads and the DNA concentration evaluated using the Qubit® dsDNA HS kit and Qubit® Fluorometer (Thermofisher, Massachusetts, USA). The samples were normalised and pooled equimolar into a single sample with a final concentration of 4 nM. In preparation for cluster generation and sequencing, pooled libraries were denatured with Sodium hydroxide (NaOH), diluted with hybridization buffer and then heat denatured before sequencing on an Illumina MiSeq platform with 300 bp paired end reads using V3 chemistry (Illumina, California, USA).

Statistical Analysis

Sequence Processing and Taxonomic Assignment

Raw 16S rRNA gene sequences were processed using QIIME 2 (version 2022.11) [23]. Prior to denoising, cutadapt was used to trim adapter sequences from the raw reads, ensuring high-quality sequence data of average 285 base pairs. The trimmed sequences were then demultiplexed and quality-filtered using the qiime demux plugin. Denoising was performed with the DADA2 pipeline (qiime dada2 denoise), resulting in amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) [24]. The feature table and representative sequences generated from this process were used for downstream analyses. Taxonomic assignment of ASVs was completed using a Naive Bayes classifier trained on the SILVA database at 99% sequence similarity via the qiime feature-classifier classify-sklearn plugin. Normalization was performed using alpha rarefaction at a sequencing depth of 1000 reads per sample to ensure comparability across samples.

Exporting QIIME 2 Data for Phyloseq Analysis

To leverage the statistical and visualization capabilities of the Phyloseq package in R [25], processed QIIME 2 data were exported as follows: the ASV feature table and taxonomic assignments were exported using the qiime tools export command. Specifically, the feature table was exported in BIOM format (feature-table.biom), while taxonomic assignments were exported as a tab-separated values (TSV) file (taxonomy.tsv). The corresponding representative sequences were exported in FASTA format (rep-seqs.fasta).

Data Import and Phyloseq Object Construction

The exported data were then imported into R (version 4.3.0) for further analysis using the Phyloseq package. The feature table was imported using the import_biom() function from the biomformat package [26], and taxonomic assignments were read into R as a matrix. Sample metadata, originally included in QIIME 2, was imported as a tab-delimited file. These data were combined into a Phyloseq object for downstream analyses. With the constructed Phyloseq object, analyses such as alpha diversity (e.g., Shannon and Simpson indices), beta diversity (e.g., principal coordinate analysis based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity), and differential abundance testing were performed [27,28]. Visualization of results was achieved using the ggplot2 and Phyloseq packages in R, providing clear insights into the structure and diversity of the microbiome [25]. The calculation of alpha diversity involved the utilization of the Shannon diversity matrix, with statistical significance assessed through the Kruskal-Wallis test a p-value of less than 0.05 was used to denote statistical significance.

Tick identification

A total of 94 ticks were utilized for microbiome analysis, sourced from 77 distinct cattle distributed across the 7 veterinary camps. Ticks obtained through environmental dragging comprised three specimens, with two of them being pooled tick larvae. Morphological classification, supplemented by confirmation through COI, 12S rDNA, and 16S rDNA genes (Supplementary Table 1), identified the ticks as Amblyomma (n=28), Hyalomma (n=29), and Rhipicephalus (n=37). The microbial structure in Amblyomma, Hyalomma and Rhipicephalus ticks

The microbiomes of the three tick genera—Amblyomma, Hyalomma, and Rhipicephalus—revealed varying levels of Rickettsia spp (Fig. 3), with Amblyomma and Hyalomma exhibiting a higher abundance compared to Rhipicephalus (Fig. 3 A). The microbiome of Rhipicephalus was predominantly composed of Peptostreptococcaceae and Staphylococcaceae. Regarding tick genus, the microbiomes of Amblyomma and Hyalomma ticks were primarily composed of Staphylococcaceae and Corynebacteriaceae, whereas Rhipicephalus ticks predominantly featured Staphylococcaceae and Peptostreptococcaceae families (Fig. 4 B).

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the microbial communities in both Rickettsia-positive and negative Ixodid ticks from two districts in Zambia. Our specific objectives included identifying differences in the composition and richness of tick microbiomes with respect to various factors, such as the study site, life stage, sex, and tick genus.

The overall prevalence of Rickettsia in ticks in this study was 47.9%, with the segregated prevalence of Chongwe and Chisamba districts being 83.7% and 16.3%, respectively. This discrepancy in prevalence rates may be attributed to the higher number of Amblyomma ticks collected from Chongwe, as they serve as the primary vector for Rickettsia. Both Amblyomma variegatum and Amblyomma hebraeum play a role in maintaining rickettsiae within the tick population through vertical transmission from mother to egg, serving as natural hosts [29], in contrast to the Rhipicephalus ticks predominantly found in Chisamba.

Analyzing the microbiome composition across the three tick genera revealed that the most common bacteria families present in ticks from both districts were Rickettsiaceae, Peptostreptococcaceae, and Staphylococceaea. Previous microbiome studies have reported the predominance of Rickettsiaceae and Staphylococcaceae in the microbiomes of ticks such as Ixodes scapularis, Rhipicephalus microplus, Amblyomma maculatum, and Hyalomma anatolicum [4,[30], [31], [32]].

A noteworthy observation was the substantial amount of Staphylococcus carnosus present in the ticks. This aligns with previous studies finding Staphylococcus in R. annulatus, R. decoloratus, and A. cajennense [33,34]. While the role of S. carnosus in the tick microbiome remains unclear, it may be linked to the horizontal transmission of tick-borne pathogens from the host skin microbiome [35]. Further research is essential to elucidate the role of S. carnosus and other commensal microorganisms in the tick microbiome, especially considering the detection of antibiotic-resistant genes in Staphylococcus cultivated from Ixodid ticks, posing a potential threat [36].

The presence of known tick-borne pathogens Ehrlichia and Anaplasma in the microbiomes of ticks, with instances of co-infection with Rickettsia being noted in some Hyalomma ticks, suggests that ticks in Chongwe and Chisamba districts may serve as reservoirs for these infectious agents. This is particularly significant given the detection of these pathogens in dogs from urban and peri-urban areas of Zambia [37,38].

The identification of endosymbionts such as Francisella and Coxiella in the microbiomes, primarily limited to Hyalomma ticks, underscores the complexity of the tick microbiome. While other studies have found Francisella in Amblyomma spp as well [39], our findings add to existing evidence of these endosymbionts presence in different tick genera. The presence of Candidatus Midichloria-like endosymbionts and Wolbachia further adds to the complexity of the tick microbiome, raising questions about the potential evolution of commensal endosymbionts into vertebrate pathogens [4].

Significant differences in tick microbiome composition were noted based on both alpha and beta diversity analysis, attributable to veterinary camp locations. This aligns with previous studies indicating geographic variations in tick microbiomes linked to local environmental conditions, soil types, and drainage [6,7,40]. The study acknowledges the potential influence of seasonal variations, humidity, temperature, and host blood meal on tick microbiomes. The ticks from the two districts having been collected in different seasons could have confounded the results and led to the further differences observed in the microbiome of ticks in the different geographic locations as studies have shown that temperature has an effect on the microbiome composition in ticks [41,42].

Our investigation revealed a significant difference in the microbial richness and composition of the three tick genera. This consistency with studies from the USA emphasizes the impact of species-specific factors on the tick microbiome [5,43,44]. The low Rickettsia colonization in Rhipicephalus ticks may contribute to the observed higher species richness compared to Amblyomma and Hyalomma ticks, reflecting varying susceptibility to Rickettsia infection. Further variation in tick microbiomes was observed concerning life stage and sex. This concurs with findings from studies in the USA and South Africa, highlighting the influence of hormonal mechanisms and feeding habits on microbial community structure in different sexes and life stages of ticks [1,5,45]. The study emphasizes the need for more research to unravel the intricate mechanisms driving these variations

The study's findings highlighted a substantial difference in microbial richness (alpha diversity) and species composition (beta diversity) of tick microbiomes based on Rickettsia status. Similar shifts in microbiome composition have been observed in response to pathogen infection in other tick species [15,46]. Notably, the presence of a bacterial pathogen like Rickettsia reduced the overall bacterial abundance in the tick microbiome, as evidenced by a lower number of observed features (number of bacterial taxa) in Rickettsia-positive ticks compared to negative ones. The presence of Rickettsia in the ticks influenced the microbiome composition and similar evidence has been found where Rickettsia's presence caused changes in the abundance of several bacterial taxa in the tick microbiome [14,15]. Furthermore, Rickettsia has been shown to be a driver of tick microbial community assembly and to influence the stability of the tick bacterial networks, emphasizing the role it plays in shaping the microbiome in ticks [47].

Further analysis excluding Rickettsia revealed the prominence of families such as Staphylococcaceae, Peptostreptococcaceae, Corynebacteriaceae and Moraxellaceae in the tick microbiome. These families are associated with bovine skin microbiota, emphasizing the potential influence of the host's environment on tick microbial communities. The presence of opportunistic bacteria capable of infecting humans, such as Corynebacterium species, warrants additional investigation into the role of arthropod-associated microbes as potential reservoirs for pathogens.

To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first of its kind to explore the microbiome of Amblyomma, Hyalomma, and Rhipicephalus ticks in Zambia and to compare microbiomes based on Rickettsia infection, location, life stage, sex, and genus. The findings contribute crucial insights to establish a baseline for future microbiome studies and develop pathogen control strategies utilizing microbial elements. The prevalence of Rickettsia across veterinary camps underscores the importance of strengthened tick control measures and community awareness among local farmers, particularly considering the presence of other tick-borne pathogens in the region.

Conclusion

The study underscores Rickettsia prevalence in Chongwe and Chisamba ticks, emphasizing Amblyomma and Hyalomma as key reservoirs. It reveals Rickettsia's impact on tick microbiomes, influenced by factors like location, life stage, and tick genus. While Peptostreptococaceae and Staphylococceae were prevalent, specific microbiome elements inhibiting Rickettsia remain unidentified, emphasizing the need for further research. The study illuminates the tick microbiome's complexity, offering insights for future disease control. In conclusion, it highlights the microbiome's pivotal role in disease understanding, with potential for innovative control measures, stressing the need for further investigations into specific elements influencing Rickettsia for effective disease management.
