Standardizing Normal Pressure Hydrocephalus Diagnosis: The Interdisciplinary Lausanne’s Protocol
Authors: Eric Morel, Jenny C. Kienzler, Giulia Bommarito, Alessandra Griffa, Mirco Nasuti, Noemi Di Fiore, Sabrina Carlier, Andrea Brioschi-Guevara, Gregoire Eberle, Katrien Van Den Keybus Deglon, Giulia Cossu, Mahmoud Messerer, Gilles Allali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIdiopathic normal pressure hydrocephalus (iNPH) is a reversible neurological condition characterized by gait disorders, cognitive impairment, and urinary incontinence with ventriculomegaly at brain imaging. Due to the diagnostic complexity, we developed a structured multidisciplinary protocol to improve iNPH diagnosis. We present here the results of the first year after its implementation.MethodsA multidisciplinary team involving neurosurgeons, physical therapists, neuropsychologists, and neurologists at the Lausanne University Hospitals (Switzerland) organized a diagnostic path with the following steps: first neurological assessment at the Leenaards Memory Center, neuropsychological assessment, CSF tap test with physiotherapeutic evaluation before and 24 h after the CSF tap-test, post-CSF tap test interdisciplinary consultation with a neurosurgeon and a neurologist, and an interdisciplinary board. In case of neurosurgical treatment, interdisciplinary consultations took place at 6 weeks, 6 and 12 months.ResultsThis retrospective analysis included 86 patients (mean age: 76.26 ± 9.08 years; 32.6% female) evaluated between June 2024 and May 2025. Gait disorders were the most common complaint (94.2% of the patients) followed by cognitive (75.6%) and urinary (60.5%) complaints. After initial investigations, the Board evaluated 44 candidates and recommended ventriculoperitoneal shunting for 27 patients. So far, 16 patients have been operated. A total of 92% reported gait improvement at 6 weeks postoperative control. Patients above 80 years of age reported more often gait improvement than patients below 80 years.ConclusionThe Lausanne’s protocol demonstrates a feasible, structured, simplified, and efficient diagnostic pathway for iNPH, especially for the oldest patients.

Introduction

Since its first major publication in 1965 [1], idiopathic normal pressure hydrocephalus (iNPH) has remained a diagnostic challenge for clinicians, despite continuous improvement in identifying affected patients [2]. Its core features includes ventriculomegaly at brain imaging with gait disorders, cognitive dysfunction, and urinary incontinence [3]. As iNPH mainly affects older adults [4], these characteristics are indeed unspecific and found in other neurological as well as non-neurological conditions. Regardless of etiology, ventriculomegaly is found in 32.1% of 70-year-old adults [5]. Gait disorders increases with age with a prevalence of 37.4% after 70 years, reaching 61.7% after 80 years [6]. Cognitive disorders affect 23.7% of older adults worldwide [7]. Finally, urinary disorders in old age affect up to 23.2% of men and up to 63% of women [8]. On the other hand, prevalence of iNPH is 2.1% after 65 years and 8.9% after 80 years, i.e., much less frequent than its associated symptoms [9]. Unfortunately, the combination of these symptoms known as Hakim’s triad is not of significant help for diagnosis as it is found diversely among iNPH patients (between 12% and 60%) [10]. Consequently, clinicians dedicated to iNPH diagnosis are confronted to a triple challenge: (i) identifying iNPH, (ii) distinguishing iNPH from mimicking and/or coexisting pathologies (such as vascular dementia or Alzheimer disease (AD)), and (iii) identifying suitable candidates who might benefit from neurosurgical intervention. Such challenge requires the combination of several competences.

From the first international guidelines [3, 11], several structured diagnostic protocols were published [10, 12–14]. They comprise an exhaustive approach for iNPH with history, clinical examination, brain imaging, and supplemental diagnostic procedures. Additionally, several studies investigated the value of specific components such as radiological features [15], diagnostic tests [16, 17], and neuropsychological characteristics [17] for diagnosis and prognosis. As such, there is a need for a protocol achieving a good trade-off between instrumented evaluation and interdisciplinary evaluation combining expertise from different specialties. To address that need, we developed a diagnostic and therapeutic protocol, called the Lausanne’s protocol, largely inspired by our previous experience with the Geneva’s protocol [12], with a multidisciplinary team involving neurologists, neurosurgeons, neuropsychologists, and physical therapists. The current version of the Lausanne protocol uses a simplified way to assess gait and cognitive disorders; and includes a multidisciplinary board for the decision of neurosurgical treatment. We present here the main findings of the first year after the implementation of this protocol. As iNPH is more common after 80 years [4], we specifically aimed at evaluating whether patients older than 80 years also benefit from investigation and treatment.

Methods

This is a retrospective review of all consecutive patients who were newly integrated in the first year of the Lausanne’s protocol between June 2024 and May 2025.

Structure

The iNPH Lausanne’s protocol consists of a first neurological evaluation at the Leenaards Memory Center to evaluate the suspicion of iNPH (see Fig. 1). Criteria for further investigation were (i) clinical and radiological features compatible with iNPH, (ii) agreement of patients for further investigation, and (iii) absence of contraindication for neurosurgical intervention. Patients undergo a comprehensive neuropsychological examination, then a CSF tap test with physiotherapeutic evaluation before and 24 h afterwards. A post-CSF tap test interdisciplinary consultation takes place 2–4 weeks later in presence of a neurologist and a neurosurgeon to evaluate the effect of the CSF tap test and discuss the intervention. All cases are presented in a multidisciplinary board to elaborate the final diagnosis according to international guidelines [3] and the recommendation for surgery. In case of an operation, a minimum of three interdisciplinary consultations follow at 6 weeks, 6 months, and 12 months, each in presence of a neurologist and a neurosurgeon. The physiotherapeutic evaluation is repeated at 12 months.

Illustration of the steps of Lausanne’s protocol.

First Neurological Assessment at Leenaards Memory Center

Patients are referred for a suspicion of iNPH based on imaging, gait disorders and/or cognitive disorders. The first assessment includes the medical history, the neurological examination including a measure of gait speed, Timed Up and Go (TUG), and Movement Disorder Society – Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS) – part III, the cognitive assessment (at the clinician’s discretion, usually with Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE)), and the review of brain imaging with the iNPH radiological scale [15]. This first assessment aims to determine (i) if the suspicion of iNPH can be retained and (ii) if further investigations are needed. As the aim of the investigations is the validation for neurosurgical intervention, patients who do not want to be operated or who cannot be operated due to other medical conditions are not integrated in the investigation pathway but are scheduled with a routine follow-up at the Leenaards Memory Center.

Neuropsychological Assessment

The neuropsychological assessment aims at evaluating if the cognitive profile is compatible with iNPH and if another neurodegenerative process might be present. It includes a comprehensive evaluation of episodic memory (French version of the Free and Cued Selective Reminding Test, Face Recognition [Rivermead Behavioral Memory Test Third Edition]), verbal short-time and working memory (Digit Span Forward and Backward [Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale-IV]), executive functions (Frontal Assessment Battery, Color Trails Test, semantic and phonemic verbal fluencies), attention (Symbol Search [WAIS IV]), callosal transfer (verbal dichotic listening, interdigital transfer, writing with either hand), and language (naming test [GréMots]) as well as auto-questionnaires if not performed before (Hospital Anxiety and Depression scale, Starkstein Apathy Scale, cognitive questionnaire [subjective self-ratings]). Additionally, and case by case, neuropsychologists may clinically evaluate reading, writing, comprehension, calculus, ideomotor and visuo-constructive praxis, visuo-perceptual and visuospatial gnosis, visuospatial short-time and working memory and other executive functions (inhibition, graphomotor planning).

Physiotherapeutic Evaluation

The physiotherapeutic evaluation consists of TUG, Tinetti Test and assessment of gait speed on a 6-meter distance. Patients are video-recorded to allow a better evaluation of global gait changes. This assessment is performed before the CSF tap test and 24 h later as results remain interpretable till the next day [18]. The following formula quantifies the relative change of gait features post-CSF tap test: ((valuepost-CSF tap test – valuepre-CSF tap test)/valuepre-CSF tap test) × 100. The result is in percentage, and a 10% change is considered significant.

CSF Tap Test

CSF tap test removes a volume of 40 mL via a 19- or 20-gauge spinal needle. When possible, the test is performed in supine position to measure opening pressure with a manometer. CSF analysis includes cell count, differential cell count, glucose, lactate, protein concentration, oligoclonal bands, total tau, phospho-tau, and Aβ42 protein. All CSF tap tests are performed in the morning to avoid bias due to diurnal changes in concentration of AD biomarkers [19]. Patients and their next of kin are instructed to actively observe any changes in gait and balance after the CSF tap test.

Post-CSF Tap Test Consultation

The post-CSF tap test interdisciplinary consultation takes place 2–4 weeks after the CSF tap test. Patients are asked to attend the consultation with a next of kin to provide an external point of view of any changes and to improve understanding of the iNPH neurosurgical procedure. The neurologist and neurosurgeon evaluate the patient’s subjective evaluation of change after the CSF tap test, as this influences postoperative outcomes [20]. After considering the patient’s information and all the medical records at disposal, the neurologist and neurosurgeon emit a provisional recommendation concerning the neurosurgical intervention to the patient and the next of kin. If the intervention is considered, the patient is informed about the advantages and risks as well as about the risk of disease progression and negative prognosis in case the iNPH is not treated with a shunt [21].

Board

The Board consists of neurologists, neurosurgeons, neuropsychologists and physiotherapists. All patients who underwent a CSF tap test are presented. The Board evaluates history, comorbidities, medication, clinical examination, imaging, neuropsychological assessment, CSF analysis, physiotherapeutic evaluation pre/post-CSF tap test if performed and post-CSF tap test consultation if present. International guidelines serve for diagnosis [3]. The Board emits the final recommendation for the neurosurgical intervention.

Neurosurgical Procedure

Neurosurgical intervention consists of ventriculoperitoneal shunt (VPS) placement via a parieto-occipital or trigonal trajectory targeting the posterior horn of the lateral ventricle, typically on the right side. Standard perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis is administered. After making a scalp incision, a burr hole is created and a subgaleal pocket is prepared for the valve. The abdomen is then opened layer by layer down to the peritoneum, where the distal catheter is secured using a purse-string suture. Tunnelization is generally performed from the abdominal to the cranial incision. After subcutaneous passage, the distal catheter is connected to the valve, followed by placement of the ventricular catheter under neuronavigation guidance according to preoperative trajectory planning. Once all components are connected, CSF drainage is confirmed by aspirating the distal catheter with a 5-mL syringe, after which the catheter is placed intraperitoneally, and all wounds are closed in layers. Intraventricular opening pressure is measured using a manometer. We routinely implant a Codman Certas® Plus valve, with an initial setting of at least level 5. In patients with previous abdominal surgery or severe obesity, distal catheter placement is performed laparoscopically by visceral surgeons. Postoperative imaging (CT of the head and plain radiograph of the shunt pathway) is performed on postoperative day 1. Typical postoperative hospitalization lasts 2–3 days.

Postoperative Consultations

Standard postoperative interdisciplinary consultations with a neurologist and a neurosurgeon take place at 6 weeks, 6 months, and 12 months. The consultation includes an evaluation of subjective changes and objective gait evaluation with measure of gait speed. Adaptation of valve setting may happen according to history and clinical examination. A physiotherapeutic evaluation takes place at 12 months, before the last interdisciplinary consultation. Later consultations happen at the Leenaards Memory Center only with a neurologist.

Statistical Analysis

SPSS version 29 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA) served for statistical analysis. Student’s t test, Mann-Whitney U test, and χ2 test were used, as appropriate.

Results

This retrospective study included 86 patients (mean age 76.3 ± 9.1 years old; 32.6% women). Patients were mainly referred from outside the hospital (74.4%). Patients were principally referred by neurologists (36.0%), general practitioners (34.9%), neurosurgeons (9.3%), internists (7.0%), spinal surgeons (3.5%), and psychiatrists (2.3%). The remaining 7.0% of patients were referred by various specialties (oncology, cardiology, pneumology, emergency). Reasons for referral included (i) suspected iNPH on brain imaging alone or together with compatible symptoms (gait disorders, cognitive impairment and/or urinary disturbance) (86.0% of referrals), (ii) assessment of previously diagnosed hydrocephalus (3.5% of referrals), or (iii) the incidental iNPH-compatible imaging in patients initially referred for cognitive complaints to the Leenaards Memory Centre (10.5% of referrals). At first assessment, patients or their next of kin complained mostly of gait impairments (94.2% of patients) and to a lesser extent of cognitive (75.6%) and urinary (60.5%) symptoms. In total, 54.7% of patients reported at least one fall during the last year. A total of 45 (52.3%) patients underwent further investigations after the first assessment; the remaining 41 patients were excluded from the protocol due to severe comorbidities (e.g., heart failure, advanced deconditioning, active alcoholism), previously treated hydrocephalus not requiring shunt revision or absence of iNPH. Among the 17 patients without iNPH, 5 had cognitive disorders (3 due to vascular etiology (stroke and/or leucoencephalopathy) 1 due to frontotemporal dementia and 1 due to AD), 4 had gait disorders (1 due to stroke, 1 due to functional gait disorder, 1 due to traumatic lesions, and 1 due to spinal stenosis), 1 patient had cerebral amyloid angiopathy, 2 had an iNPH-compatible imaging without symptoms, 1 had neither imaging nor symptoms of iNPH; finally 4 had gait and cognitive disorders due to multifactorial reasons (polyneuropathy, drug-induced, fear of fall, arthrosis, deconditioning). A total of 42 patients underwent a CSF tap test (in 3 patients the lumbar puncture was either not possible for anatomical reasons or failed to retrieve the required volume), none of them had Alzheimer pathology. As one of the participants died due to other medical reasons, 44 (51.2%) patients were evaluated by the Board. VPS was recommended for 27 patients. Reasons for not recommending the VPS included lack of objective or subjective improvement after the CSF tap test, refusal to be operated or overwhelming comorbidities that induced a poor risk-to-benefice ratio. A total of 16 patients were operated; the remaining 11 patients are either waiting for VPS, or declined the operation, or their situation deteriorated so that they could not be operated. Among operated patients, 2 (12.5%) had complications requiring reoperation (1 with shunt infection and 1 with abdominal shunt dislocation). A total of 13 patients were seen at the first postoperative consultation at 6 weeks, of them 92.3% reported an improvement of gait.

Compared to patients who did not undergo further investigations after the first assessment, patients who underwent further investigations had higher global cognitive efficiency (MMSE 26.25 ± 3.31 vs. 22.88 ± 3.31; p value = 0.004) and lower anxiety level (HAD-A 5.86 ± 2.92 vs. 7.78 ± 2.17; p value = 0.004) (see Table 1). Among patients presented at the Board, patients with recommendation for VPS had better initial gait speed (0.90 ± 0.22 m/s vs. 0.77 ± 0.16 m/s; p value = 0.044) and baseline TUG scores (10.00 ± 2.49 s vs. 13.28 ± 2.88 s; p value = 0.005) compared to patients for whom VPS was not recommended. We note that patients who were operated were slightly older and had higher apathy level than patients who did not undergo further investigations after the first assessment.

Overall, mean gait speed improved throughout the protocol (see Fig. 2). Among all patients who underwent the CSF tap test (whether they were operated later or not), mean baseline gait speed improved at the evaluation 24 h later (0.87 ± 0.21 m/s vs. 0.96 ± 0.21 m/s; p value <0.001). Among the 12 patients seen at the first postoperative consultation, mean gait speed also improved compared to baseline evaluation at first assessment (0.91 ± 0.24 m/s vs. 1.16 ± 0.27 m/s; p value = 0.013).

Mean gait speed measured among the cohort at first assessment, CSF tap test (pre and post), post-CSF tap test consultation and 1st then 2nd postoperative consultation.

As iNPH prevalence increases after 80 years old, we performed a further comparison of patients aged 80 years and more vs. younger than 80 years (see Table 2). At first assessment, older patients had a worse gait speed (0.77 ± 0.25 m/s vs. 0.95 ± 0.28 m/s; p value = 0.004) and TUG scores (13.57 ± 4.92 s vs. 10.93 ± 3.23 s; p value = 0.025). There was a trend for more falls (73.5% vs. 42.3%; p value = 0.061). Apart from age, other baseline characteristics were not different between the two groups. Older patients had the same access for further investigations after the first assessment (50.0% vs. 53.8%; p value = 0.865). In the physiotherapeutic evaluation before and after CSF tap test, older patients showed similar performances in terms of value before and after the CSF tap test as well as in term of median improvement. Older patients showed an improvement after CSF tap test of gait speed (0.83 ± 0.20 m/s vs. 0.91 ± 0.19 m/s; p value = 0.017), TUG (17.84 ± 5.50 s vs. 16.48 ± 5.77 s; p value = 0.042) and Tinetti test (22.08 ± 3.32 vs. 25.92 ± 2.50; p value = 0.001), whereas patients younger than 80 years only improved at gait speed (0.89 ± 0.22 m/s vs. 0.98 ± 0.22 m/s; p value = 0.002) and Tinetti test (20.48 ± 5.37 vs. 22.89 ± 4.86; p value <0.001) but not at the TUG (18.92 ± 17.43 s vs. 17.79 ± 20.50 s; p value = 0.159). There was a trend for a better subjective improvement after CSF tap test (71.4% vs. 50.0%; p value = 0.182) for older patients. There was no difference in terms of VPS recommendation at the board. Due to the low number of patients in each age subgroup, statistical analysis was not conducted for postoperative consultations. Although gait speed was lower, we note that oldest-old patients reported more often subjective improvement.

Discussion

The interdisciplinary Lausanne’s protocol for iNPH is a structured, feasible, efficient and secure diagnostic and therapeutic protocol, especially for oldest-old patients. The first assessment by neurologists is useful as the differential diagnosis of iNPH includes a wide range of neurological as well as non-neurological disorders [3]. iNPH patients evaluated in a Memory Clinic also benefit from clinical expertise that extends the evaluation beyond the sole diagnosis of iNPH and investigates alternative cognition-impairing conditions according to standard guidelines [22]. On the other hand, patients who are not retained for iNPH investigation may benefit from the standard care of the Memory Clinic. The structure of our protocol defines a clear and standardized pathway that includes the main investigations required for iNPH diagnosis [3]. A comprehensive neuropsychological assessment as well as CSF analysis are part of usual investigations in case of a suspicion of a neurodegenerative process [22]. However, these two diagnostic tools are best used after a thorough first medical evaluation. The CSF tap test is a longstanding [23] and recommended investigation test [10, 11]. The TUG, video-recorded gait examination and walking speed appear to have a good sensibility to identify iNPH patients [24]. From a clinical perspective, we found the video-recordings to be particularly helpful to evaluate patients at the post-CSF tap test consultation and during the postoperative follow-up. The protocol demonstrated efficiency in identifying iNPH by improving the mean gait speed to age-appropriate range among patients who underwent VPS [25]. We do recommend gait speed as easily measured and comparable parameter for follow-ups [6]. Gait speed was also the primary outcome of a recently published randomized trial demonstrating the efficacy of shunting for iNPH [26]. Compared to the Geneva’s protocol [12], a novelty of this protocol is that the interdisciplinary Board decides the operative indication. This multi-eye approach confers more security in the final medical recommendation. The combined postoperative follow-up with a neurosurgeon and a behavioral neurologist for 1 year is also a novelty of this protocol. Most of the postoperative complications happen during the first year and have indeed to be handled by neurosurgery [27]. The presence of a behavioral neurologist is another adding value as iNPH patients often suffer from other comorbid neurodegenerative diseases such as AD [28] and might require such expertise.

A key finding of our protocol is that patients older than 80 years benefit more from these investigations and from the VPS than patients younger than 80 years. These results are in line with a recent prospective study that demonstrated that patients above 80 years show relatively good survival outcome in the middle- to long-term [29]. A retrospective study evidenced improvement of mobility and cognition after shunting in patients older than 80 years [30]. However, data on improvement of cognition among older patients remain limited [31]. Considering that treating iNPH increases life expectancy and quality [32], older patients should not be restricted from shunting. Provided that no other medical conditions contraindicate surgery, clinicians should reassure and encourage older patients to undergo the intervention, given its potential benefits for gait and overall mobility.

Our protocol is not without limitations. First, the diagnostic test relies on CSF tap test, which has a relatively low specificity [24]. It remains unclear whether some of the patients with longer disease duration and no improvement after the CSF tap test might still have improved after the VPS. However, controversy persists in the literature regarding whether such patients benefit more from a repeated CSF tap test, lumbar drainage, or proceeding directly to surgical intervention [10]. Integrating the subjective evaluation of changes after CSF tap test might have alleviated this limitation. As we do not perform neurosurgical intervention other than VPS (e.g., lumboperitoneal shunt), the therapeutic possibilities are restrained for patients at a higher risk of operative complications with VPS. An additional limitation of our study is related to its retrospective nature so that some clinically relevant parameters were not systematically documented (e.g., global health evaluation, autonomy in daily activities, patients’ reasons for operation refusal, etc.).

In conclusion, the Lausanne’s protocol is in line with existing diagnostic protocols for iNPH [12–14] and highlights the importance of an early interdisciplinarity evaluation combining expertise of behavioral neurologists and neurosurgeons. Our results also support to include oldest-old patients to undergo the investigations as they may also benefit from shunting.
