Clinical Outcomes Associated with Single STK11 Mutations and Those Co-occurring with KEAP1 and KRAS Mutations in Metastatic Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer
Authors: Firas Dabbous, Mehmet Berktas, Rajrupa Ghosh, Samuel Huse, Ching-Yu Wang, Ashish Gautam, Kazeem Idowu, Daniel Simmons, Puneeth Iyengar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionTumors with STK11 mutations and those co-occurring with KEAP1 and KRAS mutations commonly occur in patients with metastatic non-small cell lung cancer (mNSCLC). However, real-world evidence on the associated clinical outcomes is limited. This study assessed real-world progression-free survival (rwPFS) and overall survival (rwOS) among patients with mNSCLC and STK11 (co)-mutations.MethodsAdults (≥ 18 years old) diagnosed with de novo stage IV mNSCLC in the USA were identified from the Flatiron Clinico-Genomic Database. The frequencies of STK11 mutation and KEAP1 and KRAS co-mutations were reported. Time-to-event outcomes from mNSCLC diagnosis, including rwPFS and rwOS, were analyzed using Kaplan–Meier estimates and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models assessed associations between STK11 (co)-mutations and rwOS.ResultsAmong 4392 patients with de novo mNSCLC, the frequencies of STK11 mutation and co-mutations in KEAP1, KRAS, and both KEAP1 and KRAS were 16.1%, 6.4%, 8.5%, and 3.7%, respectively. Compared with patients without these mutations, median rwOS was shorter in patients with STK11 mutation (8.6 vs. 12.8 months) and with co-mutations in KEAP1 (6.6 vs. 12.5 months), KRAS (10.1 vs. 12.5 months), and both KEAP1 and KRAS (5.2 vs. 12.5 months). The adjusted risk of mortality was higher in patients with STK11 co-mutations in KEAP1 (hazard ratio [HR] 1.68; 95% CI 1.29, 2.18), KRAS (HR 1.44; 95% CI 1.17, 1.76), and both KEAP1 and KRAS (HR 2.33; 95% CI 1.87, 2.90), compared with those without these mutations. Similar increased mortality risks associated with STK11 co-mutations occurred in patients with PD-L1 tumor proportion score ≥ 1% and non-squamous histology.ConclusionSTK11 mutations with KEAP1 or KRAS co-mutations are associated with significantly worse survival outcomes in mNSCLC compared with patients without these co-mutations. These findings underscore the urgent need for targeted therapies to improve prognosis in this high-risk population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s12325-026-03677-2.

Key Summary Points

Introduction

Lung cancer is the second most commonly diagnosed cancer in the USA [1], with more than 238,000 new cases diagnosed in 2023. It is a heterogeneous disease, and more than 85% of all lung cancer diagnoses are non-small cell lung cancers (NSCLC) [2, 3]. In the USA, approximately 55% of patients have distant metastases at the time of their initial NSCLC diagnosis [4]. The emergence of biomarkers for targeted molecular therapies and the development of immuno-oncology (IO) treatments have enhanced the clinical management of NSCLC. This advancement is attributable in part to the increased use of novel molecular targeted therapies for actionable gene mutations as well as IO treatments targeting programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1). These targeted therapies demonstrate greater efficacy compared with standard chemotherapy, leading to improved survival outcomes [5–7]. Molecularly targeted therapies are now available as first-line (1L) treatments for patients with specific genetic alterations, including epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations, anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) rearrangements, ROS1 rearrangements, BRAF V600E mutations, and neurotrophic tyrosine receptor kinase (NTRK) alterations [8]. For patients without these genetic aberrations, the use of IO, alone or in combination with chemotherapy, was introduced before the availability of targeted therapies and has been associated with improved survival outcomes in the 1L treatment of mNSCLC [9–12]. PD-L1 expression levels on the surface of tumor cells have been studied as a biomarker for predicting the response to IOs, and has both predictive and prognostic properties [13]. Previous studies evaluating the impact of combination IOs on clinical outcomes, including overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS), have yielded varied results across different PD-L1 expression levels [14]. Further, some patients who initially responded to IOs may subsequently develop resistance to these medications, which may lead to disease progression during treatment or after treatment discontinuation [15]. Understanding the mechanisms underlying IO resistance and the factors associated with poor outcomes is thus crucial to improve clinical care for patients with metastatic non-small cell lung cancers (mNSCLC).

Recent studies have shown that mutations in certain genes, including serine/threonine kinase 11 (STK11) and Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (KEAP1), are associated with IO resistance and poor clinical outcomes [16–19], irrespective of the type of treatment received [20]. Mutations in STK11 and KEAP1, both tumor suppressor genes, may frequently co-occur in patients with mNSCLC, and may co-occur with mutations in Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS), a prevalent oncogenic driver in mNSCLC [21–23]. However, few studies have reported the impact of these (co-)mutations on clinical outcomes among patients with NSCLC [23–25], and one study suggested differences in IO resistance induced by different mutations [24]. The overarching aim of the present study was to assess the clinical outcomes, including real-world progression-free survival (rwPFS) and real-world overall survival (rwOS), in patients with de novo mNSCLC with STK11 mutations and KEAP1, KRAS, and both KEAP1 and KRAS co-mutations, and to examine the association between these mutations and clinical outcomes, in a real-world setting. Furthermore, this study reports 1L treatment patterns in patients with de novo mNSCLC.

Methods

Study Design and Data Source

This retrospective cohort study used real-world data from de-identified electronic medical records of the Flatiron Health-Foundation Medicine (FMI) Clinico-Genomic Database (CGDB) to identify patients with mNSCLC in routine clinical practice. The Flatiron CGDB contains processed longitudinal electronic medical records data from patients who received FMI comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) in the Flatiron Health network of over 280 community and academic cancer centers within the USA. Flatiron data include patient demographics, detailed diagnosis information (e.g., staging, histopathology, and biomarkers), treatment, and outcomes (e.g., disease progression, mortality), which are linked with CGP results from the Foundation CORE database of FMI [26]. Genomic alterations were identified via CGP of more than 300 cancer-related genes using the FMI next-generation sequencing test (FMI sequencing platforms: FoundationOne®CDx, FoundationOne®, FoundationOne®Liquid, and FoundationOne®Liquid CDx). The generation and validation of the Flatiron CGDB, as well as methods for clinical data extraction, de-identification of patient data, linkage of the clinical and genomic data, and tumor genotyping (using the FoundationOne platform), have been described previously [26]. The CGDB NSCLC cohort is derived based on predefined clinical and genomic inclusion criteria, additional details are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Access to the data was obtained under appropriate data use agreements, which permit the use of de-identified data for research purposes.

Ethics/Ethical Approval

This study was conducted using de-identified, retrospective data and did not involve direct patient contact; therefore, it was not subject to institutional review board review, in accordance with applicable regulations.

Cohort Selection

We identified adult patients (≥ 18 years old) who had a confirmed diagnosis of de novo mNSCLC (stage IV) between November 1, 2016 and June 30, 2023. We excluded patients who tested positive for EGFR or ALK mutations, had unknown EGFR or ALK status, were enrolled in clinical trials or had a drug record indicating an investigational drug, were diagnosed with other solid or hematologic malignancies before diagnosis with mNSCLC, had evidence of small cell lung cancer or mixed histology, and those without structured data in the Flatiron database within 90 days after diagnosis date of stage IV NSCLC. The index date was the date of stage IV NSCLC diagnosis. Patients were followed up from the index date until the date of the latest valid record in the database, death, or June 30, 2024, whichever occurred first.

Study Outcomes

The frequencies of STK11 mutation and co-occurring KEAP1, KRAS, and both KEAP1 and KRAS mutations were calculated as the percentage of patients with a positive result for each gene (co)-mutation among all patients who were tested for the respective gene aberration. Frequencies were summarized in the overall cohort and stratified by histology (squamous vs. non-squamous) and PD-L1 tumor proportion score (TPS) groups (PD-L1 TPS [< 1%] vs. TPS [≥ 1%]) among patients with known histology and PD-L1 TPS.

1L therapy post-mNSCLC diagnosis was categorized into the following categories (Supplementary Table S2): (1) IO monotherapy; (2) IO + IO combinations; (3) chemo-monotherapy; (4) chemotherapy combinations; (5) IO + chemotherapy combinations; (6) other treatments.

The date and type of progression (actual progression [based on radiographic evidence, pathologic evidence, or clinical assessment], pseudo-progression, or mixed progression) were recorded in the Flatiron database as assessed by the treating physician. rwPFS was defined as the time from the index date until the earliest record of actual NSCLC progression or death. rwOS was defined as the time from the index date to death.

Statistical Analysis

Demographic and clinical characteristics were summarized using mean and standard deviation (for continuous variables) and frequency and percentages (for categorical variables). Frequencies and percentages of patients with STK11, KEAP1, and KRAS mutations and co-mutations were assessed. Treatment patterns of 1L therapy were assessed using frequencies and percentages. The Kaplan–Meier method was used to estimate median (95% confidence interval [CI]) time-to-event clinical outcomes, including rwOS and rwPFS. Cox proportional hazards models were used to assess the association of STK11 mutations and KEAP1, KRAS, and both KEAP1 and KRAS co-mutations with rwOS, adjusted for age, histology, PD-L1 TPS, European Oncology Group performance status (ECOG-PS), and 1L treatment. This association was further stratified by histology and PD-L1 TPS among patients with available data for these variables. Patients with missing covariate data, including ECOG-PS and PD-L1 TPS, were included in the multivariable analyses as an “unknown/missing” category.

Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

The median age among patients with STK11 mutations and co-mutations ranged between 66 and 71 years. Most patients with STK11 and KEAP1 (71.2%) and STK11, KEAP1, and KRAS co-mutations (60.2%) were men, and more than half of the patients with STK11 and KRAS co-mutations were women (55.5%) (Table 1). Patients with STK11-only mutations included a higher proportion of Black patients (13.3%) compared with patients with STK11 co-mutations in KEAP1 (11.0%), KRAS (9.4%), and both KEAP1 and KRAS (3.1%). Three-quarters of the patients had non-squamous mNSCLC. A higher proportion of patients with STK11-only mutation had non-squamous histology (94.5%) compared with patients with KEAP1 (82.2%), KRAS (92.2%), and KEAP1 and KRAS co-mutations (89.8%) (Table 1). More than 85% of the patients with STK11 mutations and co-mutations in KEAP1, KRAS, and both KEAP1 and KRAS did not have ECOG-PS documented within 60 days before the index date. Among the subset of patients with available PD-L1 TPS data, a higher proportion of patients with STK11 and KEAP1 co-mutations (30.2%) had PD-L1 TPS < 1% than patients with STK11-only mutations (25.0%) and co-mutations in KRAS (25.8%) and both KEAP1 and KRAS (25.5%). Notably, PD-L1 TPS data were missing for approximately 65% of patients.

Frequency of Gene Mutations

Among patients tested for STK11 mutation and its co-mutations (N = 2655) with known PD-L1 TPS and histology, 16.1% had an STK11 mutation. The proportions of patients with STK11 co-mutations in KEAP1, KRAS, and both KEAP1 and KRAS were 6.4%, 8.5%, and 3.7%, respectively (Table 2). The highest frequency of STK11 mutation (31.2%) and co-mutations in KEAP1 (12.8%), KRAS (16.6%), and both KEAP1 and KRAS (7.3%) were reported among patients with PD-L1 TPS < 1% non-squamous mNSCLC. Patients with PD-L1 TPS < 1% mNSCLC had a higher frequency of STK11 mutations (23.9% vs. 14.1%) and co-mutations in KEAP1 (10.0 vs. 5.5%), KRAS (12.4% vs. 7.6%), and both KEAP1 and KRAS (5.3% vs. 3.2%) compared with patients with PD-L1 TPS ≥ 1%. Treatment Patterns

1L treatment was administered to 81.2% patients in the overall cohort (Supplementary Table S3). The most frequently used treatments were IO-chemotherapy combinations (49.5%), chemotherapy combinations (21.6%), and IO monotherapy (16.7%). Treatments with IO + IO combinations and chemo-monotherapy were less frequently administered. The proportion of patients receiving IO monotherapy was higher among patients with PD-L1 TPS ≥ 1% compared with those with PD-L1 TPS < 1% (23.8% vs. 3.7%). Discussion

Our study demonstrated that STK11 mutations frequently co-occur with KEAP1 and KRAS mutations and are associated with worse survival outcomes among patients with de novo mNSCLC. Additionally, the frequency of STK11 mutations and co-mutations in KEAP1 and KRAS varied by histology and PD-L1 TPS, occurring more frequently in patients with PD-L1 TPS < 1% and non-squamous histology. This study revealed that patients with STK11 mutation and co-mutations had shorter rwPFS and rwOS. Consistent with these findings, co-mutations of STK11 and KEAP1, KRAS, or both were associated with higher risk of death among patients with mNSCLC compared with those without these mutations. However, the strength of these associations varied by PD-L1 TPS group and tumor histology, indicating heterogeneity in outcomes across these subgroups.

Existing literature suggests that STK11 is one of the most common [27–29] mutations among patients with NSCLC and often co-occurs with KEAP1 and KRAS mutations [30, 31]. Prior studies reported that mutations in the STK11 gene are common, occurring in 8–39% of patients with mNSCLC, and co-occurrence with KEAP1 or KRAS mutations was reported in 28% and 52–58% of patients with NSCLC, respectively [32]. Our findings indicate similar frequencies of mutations in the STK11 gene (16%) among newly diagnosed patients with mNSCLC. Prior observational studies have reported that KRAS mutations frequently co-occur with STK11 and KEAP1 alterations, with a substantial proportion of tumors with STK11 and KRAS co-mutations [33]. Consistent with these findings, KRAS mutations are among the most commonly reported genomic alterations in NSCLC, with a prevalence of 25–30% [34–36]. However, in the present study, a lower proportion (< 10%) of patients with mNSCLC had STK11 mutations with KEAP1, KRAS, and both KEAP1 and KRAS co-mutations. When stratified by PD-L1 TPS and histology of NSCLC, STK11 mutations were observed in approximately 31% of patients with PD-L1 TPS < 1% and non-squamous mNSCLC. Similarly, STK11 mutations with KEAP1 and KRAS co-mutations occurred most frequently among patients with PD-L1 TPS < 1% and non-squamous mNSCLC.

Findings from this study highlight that STK11 mutation with KEAP1 and KRAS co-mutations were associated with worse survival outcomes in patients with mNSCLC. Similarly, previous studies reported that STK11 mutations [31, 37, 38] and co-occurrence with KEAP1 and/or KRAS mutations were associated with poor prognosis [31, 39]. Further, STK11 mutations and/or KEAP1 were identified as independent prognostic factors influencing OS and PFS in patients with mNSCLC [40]. Our results corroborate these findings as patients with STK11 mutation and KEAP1 co-mutation had a shorter time to death and disease progression. Additionally, prior studies have reported that patients with STK11–KRAS co-mutation have worse survival outcomes [18, 29, 41–43] compared with patients without these co-mutations and poorer survival compared with patients with only KRAS mutations [41]. Few studies have assessed the relationship between STK11 mutations and co-mutations in both KEAP1 and KRAS; however, worse survival outcomes have been associated with STK11 mutation and co-mutations in KEAP1 and KRAS [23, 43]. The present results confirmed this finding and indicated that patients with STK11 co-mutations with both KEAP1 and KRAS had the shortest rwPFS and rwOS. This highlights these co-mutations as important prognostic indicators in mNSCLC and potential targets for treatment.

Our findings are supported by biological mechanisms. The tumor suppressor gene STK11 is a negative regulator of the mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway and is involved in key cellular pathways [44]. The tumor-suppressive effects of STK11 are largely mediated through the activation of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) and related kinases, which are pivotal in regulating cellular responses to stress and maintaining cell survival [45]. STK11 mutations are associated with a reduced density of cytotoxic CD8+ T lymphocytes and downregulation of PD-(L)1 expression and have been shown to promote cancer progression, metastasis, and an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [46]. Given its critical role in tumor suppression, STK11 is a key protective factor in patients with cancer, and mutations are associated with poor prognosis. Particularly, STK11 mutations have been identified as potential drivers of malignancy in lung cancer [47]. The effects of STK11 mutations on tumor cell growth are mediated through several important cellular signaling pathways which contribute to tumor progression [48]. Similarly, KEAP1 is a negative regulator of nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NFE2L2) and the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 protein (NRF2), the key transcriptional regulators of the endogenous antioxidant response [49, 50]. The lack of KEAP1-mediated negative regulation or KEAP1 mutation promotes tumor survival, resulting in poor prognosis for patients with NSCLC [32, 50]. Finally, KRAS mutations play a pivotal role in carcinogenesis by driving the expression of inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and signaling pathways that promote tumorigenesis [51, 52]. Overall, these alterations may exert synergistic effects that influence tumor biology. STK11 mutations have been associated with a less immunogenic tumor microenvironment characterized by reduced infiltration of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and decreased PD-L1 expression, which may contribute to reduced responsiveness to immune checkpoint inhibitors [17, 28]. This effect may be further reinforced by co-occurring KEAP1 mutations, which promote tumor survival through activation of NRF2-mediated oxidative stress response pathways [50, 53]. In addition, KRAS mutations drive oncogenic pathways that promote tumor proliferation and inflammation [54]. Collectively, these alterations may contribute to immune evasion and resistance to systemic therapies, potentially explaining the poorer clinical outcomes observed in patients with these co-mutations. Our study also demonstrated that patients with mNSCLC and STK11 co-mutations with KEAP1, KRAS, and both KEAP1 and KRAS have an approximately 1.3–2.3-fold higher risk of mortality compared with patients without these co-mutations. The highest risk was observed in patients who had STK11 mutations with both KEAP1 and KRAS co-mutations, with an approximately two-fold higher risk of mortality observed compared with patients without these co-mutations. This finding is supported by a previous randomized trial where STK11–KEAP1–KRAS co-mutation was associated with shorter OS [55]. Additionally, STK11 mutation with KEAP1 or KRAS co-mutation was associated with a higher risk of death. Prior literature has indicated that STK11 mutations can induce aggressive metastatic tumors [56, 57]. By contrast, in the present study, STK11 mutation alone (independent of co-mutations) was not statistically significantly associated with increased mortality risk, whereas co-occurrence with mutations in KEAP1, KRAS, or both was associated with worse survival outcomes.

A recent study supports our conclusions that patients with mNSCLC harboring STK11 mutations and KEAP1 or KRAS co-mutations represent a high-risk group with poor prognosis [58]. The study highlighted the potential clinical benefit of combining tremelimumab, a cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) inhibitor, with durvalumab and chemotherapy in patients with non-squamous mNSCLC with STK11 and/or KEAP1 mutations, improving overall response rates, PFS, and OS in patients with these mutations [58]. These results highlight the need for novel treatment strategies targeting these biomarkers.

The findings of the present study have important implications for precision medicine in mNSCLC. Patients with STK11 co-mutations represent a high-risk subgroup with poorer outcomes, highlighting the need for more effective therapeutic strategies. Emerging evidence suggests that combination approaches, including dual immune checkpoint blockade and therapies targeting tumor metabolism or oxidative stress pathways, may help overcome resistance associated with these genomic alterations [58]. Advances in molecular profiling and a better understanding of the tumor microenvironment may further support the development of more individualized treatment strategies and improve treatment sensitivity in patients with co-occurring mutations. Further research is needed to better define optimal therapeutic approaches and improve clinical outcomes in this population.

This study has several strengths, including the use of the Flatiron CGDB, which contains data from over 4000 patients, offering a large and reliable sample. The information on treatments received in oncology clinics and on clinical outcomes in the Flatiron data repository is reliable, and the results are generalizable to a broad spectrum of outpatient oncology practice settings across the USA. However, there are certain limitations. The data may not fully represent all oncology practice sites nationwide, as certain types of practices and insurance types may not reflect the entire US population. Additionally, as with other retrospective observational studies, some clinical information of interest was missing, such as PD-L1 TPS and incomplete ECOG-PS and comorbidity information, which could lead to misclassification and unmeasured confounding. Sex-specific analyses were not conducted in this study, as stratification by sex may have resulted in limited sample sizes within subgroups. Additionally, incorporation of other potentially relevant prognostic factors, such as tumor mutational burden or prior treatment history, was not feasible within the available analytic dataset and warrants further investigation. The study specifically focused on patients newly diagnosed with mNSCLC, whereas those who progressed from an earlier stage were excluded as the treatment information on these patients before metastatic diagnosis was incompletely captured in the Flatiron CGDB. Reporting clinical outcomes among patients with mNSCLC who progressed from an earlier stage without sufficiently accounting for their prior treatment may produce misleading results. Furthermore, small sample sizes in certain patient subgroups limited the ability to estimate statistical significance, particularly when stratified by PD-L1 TPS group and histology type. The Flatiron CGDB only includes patients who received next-generation sequencing (NGS) testing during routine clinical care, which may or may not be representative of the broader mNSCLC patient population. Future studies comparing NGS-tested patients with those who did not undergo testing will enhance our understanding of the representativeness of patients in the CGDB. Lastly, services and procedures conducted outside the Flatiron data network may not be captured in the database, which could lead to missing relevant clinical data.

Conclusion

STK11, KEAP1, and KRAS mutations and co-mutations occur frequently in patients with mNSCLC. STK11 mutations with KEAP1 and/or KRAS co-mutations were associated with poor prognosis. The association of STK11 co-mutations with increased risk of mortality was observed in the overall cohort and in patients with PD-L1 TPS ≥ 1% and non-squamous mNSCLC. Therefore, incorporation of molecular testing to assess the presence of these mutations may be crucial in evaluating treatment strategies for patients with mNSCLC. Combination therapies, and particularly anti-PD-(L)1 and anti-CTLA-4 combination, have shown promise in targeting these biomarkers. Identifying other novel drug combination regimens targeting these prognostic biomarkers will be critical to improving outcomes for patients with mNSCLC, including survival and progression-free status, which could lead to improvement in the overall quality of life.
