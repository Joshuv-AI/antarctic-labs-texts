The association of physical activity with urological cancer risk: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Fangfang Xie, Chaoqu Xie, Hongyu Yue, Jiahe Cui, Jianwen Ma, Jiatuo Xu, Fei Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUrological cancer (UC) is associated with significant adverse outcomes and poses a major global health burden. Although physical activity (PA) may reduce the risk of a single cancer site of UC through multiple pathways, whether PA is associated with the whole of UC is largely unknown. This study assesses the reduction in the risk of UC (including bladder, prostate, and renal cancer) associated with PA. We analyze data from 95 studies comprising 176 risk estimates based on 11,255,721 participants. Compared to low PA levels, high PA levels are associated with 9, 13, 6, and 11% lower risks of urological, bladder, prostate, and renal cancer, respectively. This association is stronger in case-control studies (14%) than cohort studies (7%), women experience a greater risk reduction (14%) than men (7%), and occupational PA is more protective than recreational PA (16% vs. 11%). This study provides comprehensive information on the inverse relationship between PA and UC risk. The evidence indicates that PA as prehabilitation may improve physical fitness and reduce UC risk, particularly for certain types of malignancies. International PA guidelines should encourage PA to reduce the risk of UC. However, the highest amount of PA is not always the best. Future studies should determine the maximum beneficial duration and frequency of PA.Subject terms: Bladder, Prostate

Introduction

Urological cancers (UCs), including prostate, bladder, and kidney cancer, are common cancers affecting the genitourinary organs1,2. According to data presented by GLOBOCAN, more than 2 million people worldwide have been diagnosed with a urological tumor, resulting in nearly 0.8 million deaths in 20203. The latest cancer statistics from the American Cancer Society estimate that UC will account for 20.1% of new cancer cases and 11.2% of cancer deaths in the United States4, with prostate, bladder, and kidney cancers being the most common UC tumors worldwide5. In men, prostate cancer accounts for 27% of new cases, while bladder and kidney cancer are among the top 10 cancers causing new cases. In women, kidney cancer is the ninth most common type of cancer5. Problems arising from the treatment of UC include fatigue, decreased physical function, a decreased health-related quality of life (HRQOL)6, and symptoms such as gastrointestinal, urinary, and sexual dysfunction7,8. These challenges may increase the risks associated with UC9.

Physical activity (PA) is closely associated with a reduced risk of UC through the modification of established risk factors10,11. One meta-analysis reports that compared to people with low levels of PA, those with higher levels of PA had 15% lower odds of developing bladder cancer12. Another meta-analysis reports 12% lower odds of developing renal cancer when 37 risk estimates from 19 studies were synthesized using diverse exposure definitions13. A recent review published in 2021 on the association between PA and UC notes that PA can lower the single cancer site, including an improved HRQOL14. A scientific report published in 2018 on PA guidelines in the United States also notes that PA reduced the risk of certain urinary tract cancers, including bladder and kidney cancers. Risk factors for UC include age, smoking, obesity, hypertension, and type 2 diabetes15. Healthy lifestyle habits, such as eating a proper diet16,17, not smoking18, preventing obesity19, and increasing PA20, can reduce the risk of UC. PA is critical for lowering the risk of UC, in part because it helps reduce obesity, blood pressure, and insulin resistance while also lowering lipid peroxidation levels21–24. PA is defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) as any energy-consuming physical movement produced by skeletal muscle25. The WHO and the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM)26,27 recommend at least 150 to 300 min a week of moderate-intensity PA to reduce the risk of UC28. However, there is a growing need to investigate the effects of PA in the development of UC29.

Most existing studies have only explored the association between PA and the risk of individual cancers14,15, including the 2018 U.S. Physical Activity Guidelines Advisory Committee Scientific Report, which summarizes the association between increased PA and a reduced risk of bladder and kidney cancers. However, these studies do not comprehensively evaluate UC as a single, integrated category. The primary objective of this study is to quantify the association between PA and overall UCs (including prostate, bladder, and kidney cancers) through a systematic review and meta-analysis. Furthermore, this study specifically focuses on and systematically analyzes the potential roles of key lifestyle factors—such as smoking, alcohol consumption, and obesity—in modifying the association between PA and the risk of UC, thereby providing comprehensive and integrative insights into its treatment.

Although PA is widely recognized as a modifiable protective behavior, clinical and public health guidelines still lack a clear, comprehensive, and evidence-based framework to promote PA for reducing the risk of UC. By providing overall risk estimates and analyses of key lifestyle factors, this study helps inform health policymakers and clinicians in designing more effective and targeted primary prevention strategies. In this study, we show that the association between PA and UC risk20,30–123, and we also find that the highest amount of PA is not always the best. Although there is still limited evidence for determining the optimal duration and intensity of PA, our results provide valuable information toward improving clinical guidelines to reduce the risk of UC.

Results

Study characteristics

The PRISMA flowchart of the study is shown in Fig. 1. We initially included 12,785 articles (1845 from PubMed, 2009 from Cochrane, 5667 from Embase, 1038 from EBSCO, 508 from CNKI, 1236 from Wan Fang, and 482 from VIP) based on our subject terms combined with free terms. A total of 6897 articles were initially excluded (4423 duplicates, 90 meta-analyses, 287 review articles, 42 commentaries, 6 news reports, 19 letters, 22 editorials, 69 guidelines, 16 animal studies, 577 surviv*, 120 protocols, and 1226 in Chinese). After reviewing their titles and abstracts, another 5506 articles were further excluded, leaving 382 final articles for in-depth evaluation. Ultimately, a total of 95 studies were included in our final analysis20,30–123.

The PRISMA flow chart of the study screening.

Flow diagram summarizes the search strategy and the number of studies excluded at each stage.

The data shown in Supplementary Datasets 1 include 11,255,721 participants and 1,645,708 cases, with 176 risk factors generated from 95 studies20,30–123. We included 61 cohort studies and 34 case–control studies. A total of 22 bladder cancer studies generated 31 risk factors, 31 kidney cancer studies generated 63 risk factors, and 59 prostate cancer studies generated 78 risk factors. There were 76 studies on men, 17 studies on women, and 18 studies that included both men and women. There were 39 studies focused on recreational PA, 28 on occupational PA, and 48 on total PA. Geographically, the studies focused on North America (49 studies), Europe (30 studies), Asia (13 studies), and Oceania (3 studies). In accordance with international PA guidelines, we preferentially extracted studies in which more than 150 min/week of PA was defined as high PA, less than 30 min as low PA, and 30–150 min as moderate PA. We also obtained the adjustment factors for each study, including alcohol consumption (31/95), smoking (49/95), and body mass index (BMI) (51/95).

Main analysis

We summarized the 176 relative risk estimates generated from the 95 studies using a random-effects model, and found that high PA was associated with a 9% reduced risk of UC compared to low PA (RR = 0.91%, 95% CI: 0.88–0.94; I2 = 68.7%; P = 0.000). Figures 2–4, Supplementary Fig. 1 show the risk of bladder cancer (RR = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.80–0.94; I2 = 71.9%; P = 0.001), prostate cancer (RR = 0.94, 95% CI: 0.89–0.99; I2 = 76.7%; P = 0.019), renal cancer (RR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.84–0.95; I2 = 38.3%; P = 0.000) and urinary cancer (RR = 0.86, 95% CI: 0.74–0.99; I2 = 0.0%; P = 0.040), respectively. Supplementary Fig. 2 shows the results of cohort studies (RR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.90–0.97; I2 = 73.2%; P = 0.001) and case-control studies (RR = 0.86, 95% CI: 0.80–0.93; I2 = 59.7%; P = 0.000). As shown in Supplementary Figs. 3–5, PA exhibited a greater protective effect for women (RR = 0.86, 95% CI: 0.77–0.97; I2 = 48.2%; P = 0.013) than for men (RR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.89–0.97; I2 = 72.9%; P = 0.000). Studies of both men and women showed that PA was associated with a 12% reduction in the risk of UC (RR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.81–0.96; I2 = 58.4%; P = 0.016). Furthermore, occupational PA (RR = 0.84, 95% CI: 0.78–0.90; I2 = 56.4%; P = 0.000) (Supplementary Fig. 6) provided greater protection than recreational PA (RR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.84–0.94; I2 = 63.5%; P = 0.000) (Supplementary Fig. 7) and total PA (RR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.91–1.02; I2 = 69.7%; P = 0.233;) (Supplementary Fig. 8). An evaluation of the different levels of PA on the risk of UC found that 82 of the studies systematically evaluated PA in terms of high, moderate, and low levels, resulting in 145 risk estimates indicating that both high and low PA had a protective effect, with moderate PA associated with a 6% reduction in UC risk compared to low PA (RR = 0.94, 95% CI: 0.92–0.97; I2 = 87.9%; P = 0.000) (Supplementary Fig. 9) and high PA associated with a 4% reduced risk of UC compared to moderate PA (RR = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.92–0.99; I2 = 99.9%; P = 0.000) (Supplementary Fig. 10). Limited evidence from 16 of the included studies20,49,52,58,73,76,81,87,88,91,96,97,101–103,105 suggest that compliance with international PA guidelines may reduce the risk of UC by 7% (RR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.86–1.00; I2 = 76.5%; P = 0.050) (Supplementary Fig. 11). In addition, publication bias was examined using a funnel plot (Supplementary Fig. 12A), Begg’s rank test (Supplementary Fig. 12B), and the Egger regression test (Supplementary Fig. 12C), all of which confirmed that the included studies were structurally stable and that there was no publication bias (P > 0.05).

Other factors

Supplementary Datasets 2 shows an analysis of the characteristics of subgroup studies regarding the risk of UC in terms of different PA levels (high vs. low, moderate vs. low, and high vs. moderate). A total of 13 of the studies were from Asia (RR = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.91–1.10), 49 studies were from North America (RR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.83–0.92), 30 studies were from Europe (RR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.87–0.99), and 3 studies were from Oceania (RR = 0.91, 95% CI: 0.88–0.94). We also investigated adjustment factors, including adjusted alcohol consumption (RR = 0.94, 95% CI: 0.90–0.99), smoking (RR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.86–0.94), and BMI (RR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.86–0.94). We pooled the results of these studies and found significant differences in BMI, smoking status, and alcohol consumption after adjustment.

Quality assessment

The study quality ratings are shown in Supplementary Datasets 3. The studies had a mean quality score of 6.44 (SD = 0.648, median = 6). Among the studies, 79 had clear diagnostic guidelines and an explicit definition of UC, and we only included articles with an overall quality rating of 6 or more. Based on the questionnaire, these studies exhibited high quality based on overall scores, although they did not explicitly discuss whether the case groups were consecutive and representative.

Discussion

This study systematically reviews the relationship between PA and UC, finding that high levels of PA are associated with a 9% reduction in the risk of UC compared with low levels of PA. This result is consistent with studies that have investigated the relationship between PA and the risk of a single type of UC, such as bladder124,125, renal13, or prostate126,127 cancer. As more people seek to incorporate exercise into clinical cancer treatments, the ACSM has been tasked with updating its recommendations for PAs with regard to cancer prevention and control based on available evidence. One previous review focused on evidence-based on the exercise method as it relates to health outcomes128. In our meta-analysis, we selected exercise patterns based on the level of PA. Our results also provide evidence that the association between PA and UC varies by study design, gender, intensity, duration, assessment type, adjustment factors, and geographic area.

The PA guidelines proposed by the WHO and the ACSM26,27 advocate for all adults to aim to improve their health by performing at least 150 min of moderate PA per week. Epidemiological studies have shown that increasing PA reduces the risk of malignant cancers by approximately 10%17,129. Two previous meta-analyses investigated the relationship of PA with bladder and kidney cancer: a review of 15 studies12 found that high PA was associated with a 15% lower risk of bladder cancer compared with low PA, while another review comparing high and low levels of PA found a negative association between them, with the risk reduced by 12%13. These sets of results were based on comparisons between high and low PA, and they did not focus on different levels of PA intensity. In light of this, our systematic evaluation was based on a comprehensive analysis of PA at different intensities (high vs. low PA, moderate vs. low PA, and high vs. moderate PA). The results showed that PA was negatively correlated with the prevalence of UC. The largest differences in risk found between high vs. low PA also suggest that maximum benefits were achieved from moving from inactivity to at least some level of exercise, and that even small doses of activity were beneficial. Our findings thus have important implications for lifestyle recommendations by exercise health practitioners, particularly for individuals who do not enjoy exercise and who may find the currently recommended goals unrealistic. However, the quantitative criteria regarding high weekly PA were not consistent with the results of our study, and future studies are needed to further quantify the intensity of PA.

We also examined the effect of PA on the risk of UC based on the type of PA, considering both recreational and occupational PA. The results showed that both occupational PA and recreational PA were associated with a reduced risk of UC. This was consistent with the findings in a study conducted on a Japanese population, which showed that participation in both recreational and occupational PA was inversely associated with the risk of bladder cancer125. However, recreational PA is typically a somewhat discontinuous form of strenuous activity, and as such, it may potentially be affected by recall and potential selection biases. A pooled review130 based on 12 prospective cohort studies that included 1.44 million people from the United States and Europe showed that, compared with a low level of leisure-time PA, a high level of PA was associated with a 13% lower risk of bladder cancer (HR, 0.87 [95% CI, 0.82–0.92]). A Korean study120 used health insurance data to investigate the impact of leisure-time PA on the risk of bladder cancer, and found no significant correlation between the two. These results were inconsistent with our findings, possibly due to the relatively short follow-up period in the Korean study and differences in the definition of PA. Notably, occupational PA was not included in the Korean study. Considering the typically long work hours of the Asian population131, we believe that occupational PA should be included when estimating total daily PA. Previous work has highlighted the “physical activity paradox,” which describes the phenomenon that occupationally driven PA may not have the same health benefits as intense leisure-time PA132. Occupational PA is usually moderate and of a long duration38; however, most long-term occupational PA studies use job titles to assess PA133, which may introduce a degree of misclassification. This also suggests a potential protective effect of long-term activity on the incidence of UC.

Although the biological mechanisms underlying the association between PA and UC are not fully understood, existing evidence suggests there is a potential link between them. High PA reduces the level of insulin, which acts as a mitogenic factor to stimulate cell proliferation and inhibit apoptosis134. Moreover, insulin can promote tumor cell growth by elevating circulating insulin-like growth factor-I levels, which stimulate the growth of premalignant and malignant cells135. Relevant studies have also confirmed that elevated plasma insulin-like growth factor-I levels are associated with an increased incidence of bladder cancer. PA also holds promise for suppressing tumor growth in UC by increasing levels of myokines, which are crucial for regulating immune responses and reducing inflammation136. A 6-month supervised exercise program both increased myokine levels and enhanced the tumor-suppressive effects of serum, indicating that exercise-induced systemic changes may slow disease progression137. Exercise may also improve the efficacy of radiotherapy for treating prostate cancer by enhancing redox signaling and reducing hypoxia in the tumor microenvironment138. Aerobic exercise has been shown to significantly inhibit prostate cancer progression by reducing cell proliferation, enhancing apoptosis, and exerting anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects by modulating the systemic environment139.

Smoking and obesity are the primary lifestyle factors contributing to the development of most types of UC. Kenfield et al. proposed and developed a lifestyle score to help prevent prostate cancer death that included smoking, alcohol consumption, and BMI, and found that patients with higher scores had a 68% lower risk of death from prostate cancer140. Smoking is the leading cause of cancer and premature death and is a major risk factor for bladder cancer141. Some studies have shown that smoke contains a variety of carcinogenic substances that trigger and promote tumor development. These chemicals pass through the kidneys and are directly toxic to the human bladder, and after DNA adduct formation, they contribute to the dysregulation of oncogenes or tumor suppressor genes, resulting in a large proportion of kidney and bladder cancers142. Smoking has also been associated with a decrease in PA levels12,143. Moreover, individuals who exercise consistently or are more active are significantly less likely to be in the highest risk smoking category144, which may be one of the reasons that the protective effect of PA on UC found in this study was more pronounced after adjusting for smoking. BMI is another factor with effects on UC mortality (i.e., kidney cancer) that are comparable to those of smoking. This could be due to an increase in individuals with obesity in the adult population, potentially as a result of poor diets and more sedentary lifestyles145,146. To date, almost twice as many women die of kidney cancer as men due to obesity147, and the increased number of kidney cancer cases due to obesity exceeds that of any other cancer diagnosis148. Therefore, the protective effect of PA on UC may be related to its effect on weight control. Notably, one study found no association between bladder cancer risk and alcohol intake, even at high levels149, which may explain our findings of increased risk after adjusting for alcohol consumption. Thus, our study suggests that PA can reduce the risk of UC by adjusting for smoking and BMI, but not through the strict control of alcohol consumption.

The strengths of our study are as follows. First, the data were more comprehensively analyzed than previous studies; notably, we assessed the overall risk of UC and screened for each type of cancer included in the UC category. Second, our sample size was larger than that of previous studies, and the results were correspondingly more robust and convincing. In addition, subgroup analyses based on the individual types of UC, the study design, PA structural types, sex, and geographic region, with adjustments for BMI, smoking, and alcohol intake, resulted in comprehensive and reliable results. Third, the choice of tools allowed for a particularly rigorous assessment of the literature quality, while Begg’s and Egger's objective treatments comprehensively addressed potential selection, misclassification, and confounding biases and explained the heterogeneity of the results. Finally, to validate the robustness of the results, we extracted data that conformed to international PA guidelines.

Some limitations of this study should also be noted. First, the data on PA were self-reported, which may have led to misreporting. However, a previous study showed that self-reported PA was satisfactory among Japanese subjects150. Second, the detailed types and intensities of different PA and the duration of sedentary behavior were not collected in this study, which prevented us from analyzing the effect of metabolic equivalents of total activity on UC. Future studies can explore these associations more comprehensively150.

This systematic review has provided comprehensive information on the inverse relationship between PA and UC risk. The evidence from this review indicates that PA is a common protective factor. In daily life, even low recreational PA was associated with a reduced risk of UC. This evidence is consistent with international PA guidelines and suggests that high PA is associated with a 7% reduced risk of UC compared to low PA. PA as prehabilitation may improve physical fitness and accelerate recovery, particularly for those with certain types of malignancies. However, the highest levels of activity are not always optimal, and future studies need to further define the most appropriate dose and duration of PA to provide clinicians, public health professionals, and patients with clear and specific guidelines and implementation protocols for using PA to treat and prevent UC.

Methods

This study was performed according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines for systematic evaluations and meta-analyses151. The protocol for our review is available on PROSPERO (ID = CRD42022345538).

Data sources and search

Two authors (FX and FY) independently searched the PubMed, Cochrane Library, EMBASE, EBSCO, CNKI, WanFang, and VIP databases for studies published between database inception and May 2024. To more effectively search the literature and comprehensively quantify the relationship between PA and UC risk, any disagreement between the two authors regarding a study was further discussed until a consensus was reached. The following PA terms were used to search the databases: exercise; exercise trainings; exercise training; isometric exercises; isometric exercise; aerobic exercise; aerobic exercises; physical exercises; exercises; physical exercise; physical activity; and physical activities. We then used the “AND” operator to combine the PA terms with the following UC terms: urologic neoplasms; urinary system cancer; urological neoplasm; urinary tract neoplasms; urologic neoplasm; urological neoplasms; urinary tract neoplasm; urological cancers; urological cancer; cancer of the urinary tract; cancer of urinary tract; urinary tract cancer; urologic cancers; urologic cancer; and urinary tract cancers. Similarly, we used the AND operator to combine the PA terms with the following terms: bladder cancer, kidney cancer, and prostate cancer. Our search strategy focused on obtaining English articles reporting studies with human subjects. We also checked the lists of references that matched the search strategy to identify other relevant studies. We defined PA by duration, field, quality, frequency, and category of activity.

The duration of activity was used to assess the amount of time spent on PA per week. Based on the 2018 Physical Activity Guidelines Advisory Committee Scientific Report, we prioritized studies that recommended the current PA guidelines of 150 to 300 min/week defined as a moderate level of PA, with a low level of PA as less than 150 min/week and a high level of PA as more than 300 min/week. Energy expenditure was assessed in terms of weekly metabolic equivalent tasks (METs). We converted the reported levels of PA into a common metric of PA volume expressed as marginal metabolic equivalent task hours per week (MET-h/wk), which reflected the energy expended above the resting metabolic rate (1 MET)152. Weekly PA durations were then converted into activity volume expressed by multiplying these durations by intensity values of 6.9 MET-h/wk for light PA and 15 MET-h/wk for vigorous PA.

Study selection

We included the following studies in our analysis: (1) studies assessing the associations between PA and UC and the specific types of UC cancer; (2) observational studies using a cohort or case-control design; (3) studies that reported the relative risk (RR), odds ratio (OR), or hazard ratio (HR); and (4) studies that reported risk estimates with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) or sufficient information to calculate these metrics. We excluded studies that combined PA with other interventions (such as diet or pharmacotherapy). We also excluded articles that were reviews, meta-analyses, news reports, letters, commentaries, or practice guidelines.

Data extraction

To fully assess the relationship between PA and UC, the retrieved studies were screened independently by two authors. If a study was assessed as relevant, it was included in this analysis, and a detailed assessment was performed using a standardized eligibility form. If the two screening authors disagreed on the eligibility of the study, consensus was reached through team discussion.

We extracted the first author’s last name, date of publication, study site, sample size, number of cases, subdomains of PA (recreational, occupational, total), sex (men or women), PA category (high vs. low, high vs. moderate, moderate vs. low), type of UC cancer (prostate, bladder, kidney), risk estimate (95% CI), and each adjustment factor in the study. Risk estimates were extracted independently for men and women. If a study reported high, medium, and low categories of PA, estimates were collected for all categories. If authors reported a quantitative PA assessment, we prioritized the number of hours of exercise per week that met the international PA exercise guidelines, as this was the most common component of the PA measurement that could be quantified.

Of the 95 studies that met the inclusion criteria, 10 studies31,33,35,44,71,77,78,87,91,119 reported relative risk estimates (RRi) with high PA levels as the reference, and we extracted them by converting the RRi to their inverse values. For most other studies, with low PA as the reference, we extracted the values directly.

Quality assessment

The Newcastle Ottawa Scale (NOS) was used to assess the study quality of the case-control studies (exposure, selection, and comparability) and cohort studies (comparability, outcome, and selection), with a total score of 9. Studies with a low quality score (<4) were considered low-quality studies, and those with a higher score (>5) were considered high-quality studies153. The NOS was evaluated independently by two investigators (FX and JTX), and if there was a disagreement on the quality of the study, the study was discussed with a third experienced investigator (FY) until a consensus was reached.

Data synthesis and analysis

We investigated whether the summary risk estimate was affected by the type of cancer (bladder, renal, prostate cancer). We also examined potential differences depending on the study design (cohort, case–control), gender (men only, women only, both men and women), geographic region of the study (North America, Europe, Asia, Oceania), PA domain (recreational, occupational), level of PA (light, moderate, or high), adjustments for smoking (yes, no), body mass index (BMI: yes, no), and alcohol intake (yes, no).

Our systematic review included all PA risk estimates for each study. When a study involved both men and women, we reported risk estimates separately for men and women because they were based on independent samples. If the research report covered more than one domain risk estimate, all of the different domain risk estimates were analyzed. In the sub-analysis, we used all 176 risk estimates to investigate the impact of the prespecified and potentially influential methodological factors on the summary risk estimate.

Statistical analysis

The HR and OR were interpreted as the RRi of the association between PA and UC. If RRi values were available, we used the most comprehensively adjusted risk estimates reported in the original literature. If not, we computed the natural logarithms of those risk estimates, log(RRi), with their corresponding S.E.si = (log(upper 95% CI bound of RR)−log(RR))/1.96. We weighted log(RRi)s by wi = 1/(si2 + t2), where si is the error of log(RRi), and t2 represents the restricted maximum likelihood estimate of the overall variance154. In addition, to examine any publication bias, we created a funnel plot and applied Begg’s rank method155 and the Egger test156. P ≥ 0.5 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata version 11.0 software (Stata Corp LP, College Station, Texas).
