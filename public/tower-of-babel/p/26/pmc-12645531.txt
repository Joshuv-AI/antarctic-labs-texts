We arrived at AGS25 from what some people now call the “Semaglutide Kingdom”—a tongue‐in‐cheek reference to Denmark's role in reshaping global metabolic care. But our mission was not at all pharmaceutical. Inspired by our colleagues across the Atlantic, we sought to get first‐hand insights into geriatric research in the United States [1]. As geriatricians and educators, we came to connect, to learn, and to reflect. What we found was both affirming and challenging: familiar principles wrapped in unfamiliar language, shared concerns expressed through different systems, and an unexpected sense of global unity. In this commentary, we reflect on what surprised us, what we learned, and what we share with our colleagues across borders.

What Surprised Us?

As international attendees, we arrived at AGS25 with shared assumptions about the foundational language of geriatric care. One early surprise was that terms we consider core—particularly “Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment” (CGA) and frailty—were not consistently used in the same way across sessions as it would be in our part of the world [2, 3]. Instead, we encountered widespread use of the Geriatric 4Ms/5Ms framework as a shared language for geriatric care, while “frailty” was primarily presented as a clinical and research construct—rather than as a tool for stratifying patients by robustness or guiding referrals to geriatric services [4, 5]. While both frameworks are essential, the variation of interpretations highlighted subtle differences in how we conceptualize geriatric core care across countries. This gave rise to a larger question: How do we ensure global alignment in the principles that guide high‐quality geriatric care?

We were also struck by areas that received relatively limited focus at the conference. While care models were explored extensively, we did not find much focus on, for example, falls and fractures, stroke rehabilitation, secondary prevention, and acute/emergency care of older people. Whether due to parallel tracks or shifting priorities, this absence prompted reflection on how the field distributes attention—and what might be at risk of being underrepresented?

Finally, we noted a productive tension in many sessions between value‐based care and patient‐centered care. While the growing attention to resource stewardship is vital, we found ourselves asking: when is “value” defined from the system's perspective, and when is it defined by what matters most to the patient? Striking this balance remains a central challenge for geriatric medicine globally.

What We Learned

Participation in AGS25 sharpened our awareness of how cultural and structural differences shape clinical practice. For instance, while Choosing Wisely and high‐value care frameworks were recurrent themes, they also revealed a gap in our own universal tax‐funded welfare system: we could not readily name the cost of a CT scan in Denmark [6]. This was more than a trivia question—it was a reminder that clinicians must engage with cost transparency and resource awareness as part of professional accountability. We left with a deeper appreciation for the intersection between clinical decisions and healthcare sustainability.

What truly resonated with us was not just the content of the 4Ms and 5Ms frameworks, but their thoughtful application in clinical practice [7, 8, 9]. Although these frameworks have yet to be adopted in the Danish healthcare system, their structured, person‐centered approach to care offers clear relevance across settings. Seeing them in action—embedded in workflows, training, and system‐level initiatives—prompted us to consider how similar principles might be adapted locally. We are now exploring how this model could inform geriatric training in Denmark, with the goal of strengthening consistent, holistic care for older adults [10].

One of the most unexpected takeaways came from the “Geriatrics Literature Update 2025” session that transformed literature reviews into a dynamic, fun, interactive, and very memorable experience (Figure 1). It reminded us that evidence synthesis can be a powerful educational tool—and it planted the seed for a local initiative and, for example, engaging with the “#ThickenedLiquidChallenge.” Sometimes, the best teaching moments happen when scholarship and storytelling meet.

What We Share

Despite structural differences between the US and Danish healthcare systems, we appreciated how much we have in common as geriatric clinicians and educators.

First, we share a holistic orientation. Whether framed through CGA, 4Ms/5Ms, or other models, the commitment to addressing functional level, cognition, polypharmacy, and social context is universal. Our vocabulary may differ, but our core principles align.

Second, we face shared enormous challenges in building and sustaining the geriatrics workforce [11]. The shortage of trained professionals, the need for interdisciplinary collaboration, and the importance of advocacy were prominent themes throughout the conference—and they echo our own local concerns.

Finally, we were reminded that geriatric medicine attracts people who care deeply about their patients. The dedication, collegiality, and creativity of AGS25 attendees affirmed our belief that this is a field defined not only by expertise but by values.

Where to Go From Here

We were particularly struck by the vision behind the CMS Age‐Friendly Health Systems initiative and how 4Ms is reinforced into routine hospital care. At the same time, we appreciated the candid recognition of the practical challenges such implementation entails. In Denmark, we face similar tensions between ambition and execution. Our own national effort—the DANFRAIL clinical quality database, developed by the Danish Healthcare Quality Institute—aims to embed holistic, person‐centered care for older adults living with frailty across all points of contact with the healthcare system (Figure 2) [12, 13].

AGS25 reminded us that geriatrics is both a specialty and a shared ethos—rooted in complexity, guided by compassion, and shaped by global dialog. While our settings may differ, we are united by a commitment to thoughtful care for older people. As we returned to Denmark with new insights and even a few new teaching experiments, we are reminded that progress in geriatrics is not just built on evidence, but on connection, curiosity, and shared purpose.

“AGS25 reminded us that geriatrics is both a specialty and a shared ethos—rooted in complexity, guided by compassion, and shaped by global dialog. While our settings may differ, we are united by a commitment to thoughtful care for older people.”

Let's continue to learn from one another—and keep the conversations going long after the conference ends. As such, we warmly encourage geriatricians from beyond Europe to participate in the European Geriatric Medicine Society 2025 Conference in Reykjavik, Iceland, in September 2025.
