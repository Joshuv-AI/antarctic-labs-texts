The triple radiologic asthma phenotype is associated with worse disease control
Authors: Rory Chan, Chary Duraikannu, Mohamed Jaushal Thouseef, Brian Lipworth
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT

Introduction

The role of imaging in persistent asthma has expanded over recent years. Efforts have been made to combine radiographic biomarkers such as bronchiectasis or bronchial wall thickness (BWT) for the purpose of identifying asthma subphenotypes and their clinical implications.1 We hypothesized that a composite triple radiologic phenotype, measured by high-resolution computed tomography (CT) (HRCT) and comprising BWT, mucus plug score (MPS), and mediastinal lymph node (MLN) size, might provide a better insight into subphenotypes in persistent asthma.

Results and discussion

A total of 112 patients with moderate-to-severe asthma and manually documented MLN, BWT, and MPS measurements were included in this retrospective observational study. Patients with concomitant moderate-to-severe bronchiectasis or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease were excluded. MLN, BWT, and MPS measurements were all obtained according to previously described protocols.2, 3, 4 Their HRCT scans were read by 2 senior thoracic radiologists who were blinded to all clinical details except for a diagnosis of persistent asthma, with good interrater reliability. All scans were performed for the indication of asthma. Briefly, a binary method was used to classify patients according to median values for the following: pooled MLN size of at least 3.6 mm, averaging lymph node (LN) measurements from stations 2L, 2R, 4L, 4R, 5, and 7; BWT as a pooled wall area of at least 50% of the total airway area, averaging measurements at 4 different bronchopulmonary segments as right apical, right lower lobe posterior basal, left apicoposterior, and left lower lobe posterior basal; and an MPS of 1 or more, with a mucus plug considered positive if complete bronchial obstruction was imaged more than 2 cm from a pleural surface. These previously published cut points have been closely linked to poorer asthma outcomes.2, 3, 4 The HRCT scans were performed in volumetric mode with maximal inspiration, as per the standard department protocol (128-slice CT Revolution EVO, GE Healthcare). CT reconstruction was performed in the lung window with a slice thickness of 1 to 1.25 mm and no interval gap. A composite imaging score was determined for patients with any combination of abnormal measurements, 1 of the 3 (single phenotype), any 2 (dual phenotype), or all 3 (triple phenotype). Patients without any of the 3 radiologic phenotypes were excluded from this analysis.

The Asthma Control Questionnaire (ACQ) was utilized to determine symptom control with a score of 1.5 or higher indicating poor control.5 Fractional exhaled nitric oxide (Feno) level was measured by using the NIOX VERO Feno testing device (Circassia, Oxford, United Kingdom) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society guidelines.6 Feno, peripheral blood eosinophil (PBE), and total IgE levels were obtained before biologic initiation. Spirometry (Micromedical, Chatham, United Kingdom) was performed according to the American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society guidelines.7 Severe annual exacerbations were defined as asthma attacks requiring at least a 5-day course of oral prednisolone, 40 mg daily. Nasal endoscopy (30-degree oblique rigid Hopkins 3.0 mm) was performed in our rhinology megaclinic.

ANOVA and the Kruskal-Wallis tests were used to determine significant differences, with a 2-tailed alpha error of 0.05. Bonferroni correction was then applied to account for multiple testing. Caldicott approval (IGTCAL11952, IGTCAL10810+, and IGTCAL10360) was obtained before all data collection.

The classification of the members of the dual group (n = 46) was as follows: 15 exhibited both an MPS of 1 or higher and a wall area of at least 50%; 17 had both an MPS of 1 or higher and an LN score 3.6 mm or higher; and 14 expressed both a wall area of at least 50% and an LN score of 3.6 mm or higher. Patients with the triple imaging phenotype exhibited significantly worse ACQ scores, exceeding the minimal clinically important difference, a higher prevalence of concomitant chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps (CRSwNP), and a higher total IgE level (Table I).

The absolute mean difference in ACQ score between the triple and dual phenotypes was 1.1 units (95% CI = 0.2-2.1), equating to more than twice the minimal clinically important difference of 0.5. This suggests clinically relevant worse symptom control despite all comparison groups exhibiting poor control (score >1.5). From a clinical perspective, it has been independently shown that each 1-point increase in ACQ is associated with a 50% increase in exacerbation risk.8 Indeed, the triple imaging phenotype was numerically associated with the highest severe exacerbation frequency, although the difference was not statistically different. We have previously demonstrated that the combination of MPS and BWT is associated with greater airway obstruction, a higher frequency of annual exacerbations, and a greater likelihood of nasal polyp disease than are either MPS or BWT alone.9 This has provided the rationale for the present study, which included LN assessment as part of an overall radiologic score.

Additionally, a significantly increased prevalence of comorbid CRSwNP was observed with the triple phenotype versus with the single phenotype. Although greater type 2 (T2) inflammation is associated with concomitant asthma with either mucus plugging or CRSwNP,3,10 there were no differences in Feno or PBE level. One might therefore postulate that there was a reduced differentiating ability owing to all groups exhibiting high levels of PBEs, with the counts being 300 cells/μL or higher. This observation also lends support to the value of radiologic features because the triple phenotype was associated with worse outcomes despite similar levels of T2 inflammation. Our analysis also supports the idea that total IgE level potentially plays an important role in the triple imaging phenotype.

We appreciate that our study should be interpreted in the context of potential limitations, including its retrospective nature and small sample size from a single UK severe asthma center. In our National Health Service Tayside severe asthma center, we perform HRCT chest imaging on all patients with severe or difficult-to-treat asthma to exclude alternative diagnoses before initiation of a biologic. Although radiologic features do improve with therapy,11 we do not typically perform serial imaging to reduce the risk of ionizing radiation, especially as it is unlikely to change clinical management. In the past, it was shown that amalgamating T2 inflammatory biomarkers with lung function raises the likelihood of identifying adverse real-life clinical outcomes. It would therefore be interesting to assess the potentially additive value of incorporating radiologic features as well as ACQ into such analysis for future studies to create an aggregate predictive score for asthma exacerbations.

In conclusion, we have demonstrated an association between poorer symptom control and the triple radiologic asthma phenotype. The triple phenotype is a high-risk group, in which a radiographic phenotype may be particularly helpful if other clinical data are inconsistent but a treatment change is necessary.
