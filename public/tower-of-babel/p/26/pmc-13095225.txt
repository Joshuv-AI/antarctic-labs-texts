The social determinants of health—Rurality and pregnancy
Authors: Madison Palecek, Christine W Hockett, Kimberlee McKay, Tianna Beare, Lacey Arneson McCormack
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis paper examines the distribution of social determinants of health (SDoH) among pregnant individuals, comparing urban and rural populations, with a sub‐analysis of variations within the rural population.MethodsDemographic and SDoH screening data were analyzed from electronic medical records within a large, integrated health system. The sample included 6510 individuals who delivered within the health system over a 1‐year study period. Statistical tests were conducted to determine differences in demographic and SDoH factors at the time of delivery between urban and rural groups (binary classification) and within the rural group (three‐level classification).ResultsRural pregnant individuals were younger, Whiter, and publicly insured more than urban pregnant individuals (all p values < 0.0001). A total of 4.4% of urban individuals experienced one adverse SDoH compared to 5.8% of rural individuals (p = 0.03). Small‐town pregnant individuals were less White, more Hispanic, and more publicly insured and uninsured than large‐town and isolated‐town individuals. Large‐town (10.4%, p = 0.002) and small‐town (11.9%, p = 0.0003) pregnant individuals experienced increased rates of at least one adverse SDoH compared to isolated‐town (7.5%) pregnant individuals. Differences were present among rural pregnant individuals in housing, food, and transportation domains.Conclusion for practiceDemographic and SDoH differences persist between urban and rural pregnant individuals. However, differences are also present within the rural population, noting the heterogeneity of rural areas which must not be overlooked. To understand the amplitude of SDoH experiences and effectively develop interventions to address adverse SDoH, detailed rural groupings must be considered.Keywords: adverse pregnancy outcomes, maternal health, obstetrics, rural, social determinants of health

INTRODUCTION

The World Health Organization defines social determinants of health (SDoH) as nonmedical factors influencing health outcomes that are related to the conditions in which one is born, grows, works, lives, and ages [1]. These factors include, but are not limited to, access to quality food, transportation, neighborhood safety, housing, interpersonal violence, and economic status.

Pregnant individuals are a vulnerable population with increased susceptibility to the effects of adverse SDoH factors [2]. Experiencing consistent societal and community‐level stressors can manifest physiologically and is associated with increased rates of preterm birth, preeclampsia, and other adverse pregnancy outcomes (APOs) [2]. Identification of the needs and barriers experienced by pregnant individuals is crucial to properly support them throughout pregnancy.

Rurality adds complexity to the interplay between SDoH and pregnancy outcomes. Rural pregnant individuals experience a lack in access to perinatal care (both in quality and physical distance) along with rural community disparities (lower socioeconomic status, less access to healthy foods, lack of transportation, etc.) [3, 4]. This results in rural individuals having a 9% higher probability of severe maternal morbidity and mortality compared with their urban counterparts [5]. The rural context is crucial to understanding the SDoH landscape during pregnancy as 20% of the US population live in rural areas [6].

The purpose of this study is to address the gap in knowledge by describing the SDoH landscape of pregnant individuals by rurality, both urban/rural and intra‐rural comparisons. This study will help to inform the need of SDoH screening tools to identify and address adverse SDoH, which in turn will inform future interventions. In conjunction with the use of screening tools, the context of rurality must be considered to ultimately improve maternal and child health outcomes.

METHODS

Study sample

This study includes pregnant individuals who delivered between October 3, 2023 and November 20, 2024 in a large, integrated rural healthcare system servicing primarily eastern South Dakota (SD) with additional locations in four surrounding states (Nebraska, Iowa, Minnesota, and North Dakota). The health system consists of one tertiary care hospital hub, 36 hospitals, and 200 clinics across the 72,000 square mile footprint. The health system has one integrated electronic medical record (EMR) used throughout all facilities within the footprint. Each delivery event within the health system was identified. Individuals were excluded due to a missing or incomplete five‐digit zip code (n = 1), or a state of residence outside the health system footprint (n = 23). The final sample size of delivery events available for analysis was N = 6510. The study obtained ethical approval from the Avera Health Institutional Review Board.

EMR data extraction and management

Data used in this analysis included demographic variables (age, race, ethnicity, insurance coverage, zip code) and SDoH screener responses that were extracted from the health system EMR for each pregnant individual asked at the time of their delivery event.

Race was collapsed into four categories: American Indian/Alaska Native (AI/AN), African American/Black, White, and Other (containing those with Asian, Pacific Islander/Hawaiian Native, other, unknown, and declined race). Ethnicity was collapsed into three categories: Hispanic, Non‐Hispanic, and Unknown (containing those unable to be obtained and those who declined to provide). Insurance coverage was determined using primary and secondary insurance information, with categories including commercial payors, publicly insured, and uninsured/self‐pay. In general, categorization of insurance coverage was made using the primary insurance listed; however, a few exceptions occurred. If Medicaid was listed as primary or secondary insurance, insurance coverage was categorized as “publicly insured.” Because Indian Health Services (IHS) is a health service provider and not an insurance program, if IHS was listed as primary insurance with secondary insurance missing or indicated as self‐pay, it was categorized as publicly insured. If IHS was listed as primary with a commercial payor as secondary, insurance coverage was categorized as commercial. If primary insurance was listed as self‐pay and secondary insurance was listed as self‐pay or missing, insurance coverage was categorized as “uninsured.”

The SDoH screener was developed from the Centers of Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) accountable health communities health‐related social needs screening tool [7]. The full screener contains 26 questions (10 core and 16 supplemental questions) covering 12 topic areas (living situation, food, transportation, utilities, safety, financial strain, employment, family/community support, education, physical activity, substance use, and mental health) and their occurrence over the past year. For pregnant individuals, the health system chose to administer a total of 12 questions, which for reporting purposes have been grouped by topic into the following categories: housing and utilities (question 1, 2, and 6), food security (questions 3 and 4), transportation (question 5), and social support (safety score questions 7–10 and family/community support questions 11 and 12). The screening questions are provided in the Supporting Information. The SDoH screener was administered to all pregnant individuals during their delivery hospital stay and prior to discharge. An indicator variable (yes/no) was created for each question following the recommended scoring method [7]. The sum number of “Yes” responses was calculated to total the number of adverse SDoH experiences. Additionally, a binary variable was created to indicate at least one “Yes” response to any of the questions on the screener.

Rural–urban classification

Zip codes were used to assign 2010 Rural‐Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) codes to the individuals. These RUCA codes take into consideration population density, urbanization, and commuting patterns, and range from 1 to 10 [8]. Individuals were designated as either “urban” (RUCA codes 1, 1.1, 2, 2.1, 3, 4.1, 5.1, 7.1, 8.1, 10.1) or “rural” (RUCA codes 4, 5, 6, 7, 7.2, 8, 8.2, 9, 10, 10.2, 10.3) using a binary categorization scheme outlined by the Washington, Wyoming, Alaska, Montana and Idaho (WWAMI) Rural Health Research Center [9]. A second categorization scheme called RUCA‐4 further broke down rural into “large rural city/town” (RUCA codes 4, 5, 6), “small rural town” (RUCA codes 7, 7.2, 8, 8.2, 9), or “isolated rural town” (RUCA codes 10, 10.2, 10.3) which allowed for exploration of differences among these three rural categories. RUCA coding definitions are provided in the Supporting Information.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 [10]. Descriptive statistics were conducted to compare demographics and SDoH screening results across the rurality continuum. Chi‐squared tests were used for all categorical comparisons for both the binary urban/rural and the three‐level rural comparisons. T‐tests (for binary, urban/rural comparisons) and ANOVA (for three‐level rural categories), followed by a post hoc Tukey test, were used for numerical variable comparisons.

RESULTS

Urban–rural comparison

A total of 6510 pregnant individuals were screened for SDoH from October 3, 2023 to November 20, 2024. The majority of pregnant individuals were rural (72.3%). Rural pregnant individuals were younger (29.4 years vs. 30.4 years), more White (77.6% vs. 68.9%), and were more publicly insured (31.4% vs. 23%) compared to urban pregnant individuals, respectively (all p values < 0.0001, Table 1). Additionally, there was a notable higher percentage of AI/AN individuals from rural locations compared to urban. Completion of SDoH screening was similar among groups, with 97.8% for urban and 97.2% for rural pregnant individuals (p = 0.12).

A summary of any and total “Yes” responses to adverse SDoH among rural individuals is shown in Table 2. Significant differences were found in having experienced any “Yes” adverse SDoH between pregnant individuals from large towns and isolated towns (10.4% vs 7.5%, p = 0.002), and between small towns and isolated towns (11.9% vs 7.5%, p = 0.0003) compared to experiencing none. No significant differences were found among the rural individuals in experiencing one adverse SDoH compared to two or more (p = 0.64).

Differences were found among the three rural classifications for the housing and utilities, food security, and transportation domains (Figure 1B). Individuals from large towns experienced more housing (3.6% vs. 2.0%, p = 0.004) and food security (4.2% vs. 2.7%, p = 0.02) concerns compared to isolated towns. Individuals from small towns experienced more food security concerns (5.6% vs. 2.7%, p = 0.0003) than isolated rural individuals, and more transportation concerns (6.6%) compared to both large‐town (4.0%, p = 0.006) and isolated‐town (4.2%, p = 0.012) individuals. No differences were found among the three rural classifications in relation to the social support SDoH domain.

DISCUSSION

This study highlights how rurality interacts with self‐reported SDoH experiences of pregnant individuals. As indicated in our results, urban–rural and intra‐rural differences in experienced adverse SDoH were found. An urban–rural analysis was warranted because there are inherent differences between urban and rural pregnant individuals, along with having access to different support and resources. The same is true for intra‐rural populations. By moving beyond a binary urban–rural examination and further breaking down the rural group into three more defined classifications, we were able to examine how SDoH factors are experienced differently along the continuum of rurality, thus informing future tailoring of implementation and interventions [11].

Urban–rural comparison

As expected, demographic differences were seen between urban and rural pregnant individuals, with rural pregnant individuals being younger, Whiter, and publicly insured more than urban pregnant individuals (Table 1). Further, AI/AN individuals made up a notably higher percentage of the rural population compared to urban (7.4% and 4%, respectively). Rural individuals being younger and more AI/AN both increase the risk for APOs [12, 13]. Although no differences were seen between urban and rural individuals experiencing any adverse SDoH, differences were seen when comparing having one adverse SDoH versus two or more (Table 2). Urban–rural differences in demographic characteristics and experiencing one or more adverse SDoH factors further emphasize the need for stratification by rurality during analyses to better understand individual challenges, contexts, and potential solutions. Further understanding of urban versus rural differences is gained when looking at individual SDoH domains. Although not significantly different, rural individuals were trending toward experiencing more housing and transportation issues (Figure 1B). These results were expected and are consistent with our current understanding of the urban–rural disparity gap.

Intra‐rural comparison

Rurality in and of itself is an SDoH, which can exacerbate and make difficult other adverse SDoH factors [14]. Examining intra‐rural differences provides crucial context for understanding health in rural communities, as rurality is a spectrum and not all rural areas are the same, despite all being considered “rural.” For example, under the RUCA definition, rural areas span towns of < 2500 people (isolated town) up to towns with 49,999 people (large town) [8]. Individuals who live in an isolated town have much different access to resources and support compared to individuals living in large towns thus facing different challenges with accessing care and achieving a healthy pregnancy.

In the present study, heterogeneity was seen among pregnant individuals from rural areas when the intra‐rural classification was examined. Pregnant individuals from small towns were less White, more Hispanic, and more publicly insured and uninsured than both large‐town and isolated rural individuals (Table 3). This heterogeneity extended to the reporting of adverse SDoH where differences were seen among the rural groups for experiencing any adverse SDoH. Interestingly, individuals from the most rural locations (isolated towns) had the lowest prevalence of adverse SDoH (Table 2). This could be contributed to stigma around answering the questions honestly and not wanting assistance. Another possible factor could be that many of these individuals are part of farming communities and have less SDoH concerns because having a farm provides them access to unique resources not accessible to large‐ and small‐town individuals.

Food insecurity and transportation issues were more prevalent among individuals from small towns compared to those from large towns and isolated towns (Figure 1B). In SD, 24% of pregnant individuals do not have a birthing hospital within 30 min of their home (10% of United States) [15]. Counties with the highest travel time could travel up to 118 miles or 128 min on average to the nearest birthing hospital [15]. This highlights the immense diversity in access to care, transportation, and other SDoH factors within the overall “rural” population, providing context for understanding health and developing interventions to address barriers to achieving healthy pregnancies [15, 16, 17]. Future resource mapping across the three levels of rural will allow for further understanding of resource variability to better inform the trends discovered and future intervention efforts.

Leveraging one of numerous screening tools formatted to assess SDoH vulnerabilities can facilitate proper pregnancy care and management [18, 19]. Previous studies examining SDoH and pregnancy have largely been among primarily urban populations or with no consideration of rurality. For example, in a 2020 systematic review of 83 publications on SDoH and maternal mortality, only seven addressed rurality [19]. Further, the studies that have been conducted in rural settings are primarily states with overall large population densities (like California) or small geographic spaces (like counties) [19]. This study addressed the limited understanding of SDoH factors among pregnant individuals living in geographically large, low density, rural communities, and found SDoH factors to be present among all pregnant individuals regardless of specific demographic characteristics, noting the importance of universal SDoH screening among the pregnant population.

Strengths and Limitations

The strengths of this study include the use of data from a large integrated health system, the large rural sample size, and the use of a standardized screening tool. The healthcare system had a singular, integrated EMR system across all locations allowing for standardized data collection across all health facilities. The large sample included primarily rural individuals which allowed for examination of SDoH across different rural classifications. The use of the CMS SDoH screening tool allowed for standardized collection of responses and alignment with the US Joint Commission requirements for in‐patient SDoH screening. Therefore, these results are comparable to other locations where this screening tool is used. Despite these strengths, this study may not be generalizable to all other rural locations as rurality varies significantly across geographic space and time.

Conclusion

Identification of the needs and barriers experienced by pregnant individuals caused by adverse SDoH factors is important for understanding and addressing barriers to achieving healthy pregnancies and maternal and infant health outcomes. While both urban and rural individuals reported having adverse SDoH factors, differences were seen among rural individuals across the rurality continuum in the domain types and amount of adverse SDoH experienced. Universal SDoH screening should be implemented within healthcare systems for proper pregnancy management, and stratification beyond the binary urban–rural classification is necessary due to the heterogeneity among rural individuals. Future research examining the intersection of reported adverse SDoH and social service utilization should be explored to ensure resources are being used when available and tailor interventions for communities based on what are available. Additionally, future research investigating the association between APOs and SDoH factors should consider multiple levels of rurality to better understand links between rurality, SDoH, and poor maternal outcomes.
