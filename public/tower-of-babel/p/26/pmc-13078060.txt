Tempo-specific complex training improves lower-limb strength and neuromuscular function in male collegiate soccer players: an experimental study
Authors: Mingqi Gao, Enjing Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRepetition tempo may alter mechanical loading and neuromuscular responses, influencing post-activation performance enhancement and chronic adaptations. Evidence for tempo-specific effects within complex training is limited. We compared the effects of three squat tempos on strength, power, and neuromuscular activity in male collegiate soccer players.MethodsThirty players were randomized to slow eccentric–fast concentric (SE; 5-0-X-0), isometric–fast concentric (IC; 1-5-X-0), or accelerated eccentric–fast concentric (AE; 2-0-X-0) complex training for 6 weeks (2 sessions/week), with identical load and volume. Outcomes were 1RM squat, CMJ, SJ, CMJ peak power, RSImod, and V-Drill reactive agility. EMG (vastus lateralis, biceps femoris, medial gastrocnemius) was analyzed using two-way repeated-measures ANOVA and SPM1D.ResultsSignificant improvements over time were observed for 1RM (P < 0.001; η²p = 0.954), CMJ height (P < 0.001; η²p = 0.880), RSImod (P < 0.001; η²p = 0.960), CMJ peak power (P < 0.001; η²p = 0.916), and V-Drill performance (P < 0.001; η²p = 0.859) across all groups. Significant time × group interactions were found for 1RM (P < 0.001; η²p = 0.850), RSImod (P < 0.001; η²p = 0.717), CMJ peak power (P = 0.015; η²p = 0.269), and SJ height (P < 0.001; η²p = 0.577). Specifically, the SE demonstrated the largest 1RM increase (+ 17.6%), whereas SJ height increased in the IC (+ 7.3%) but decreased in the SE (− 6.1%). SPM1D analysis revealed tempo-specific neuromuscular adaptations: SE extensively enhanced biceps femoris recruitment during the concentric propulsion phase; AE primarily augmented vastus lateralis and medial gastrocnemius activation during the terminal concentric phase; and IC elicited widespread increases in medial gastrocnemius activity during the eccentric phase.ConclusionsComplex training effectively enhances lower-limb strength and explosive performance, but distinct squat tempos elicit different neuromuscular adaptations. Tempo manipulation may be periodized to training objectives (e.g., SE/IC for strength-oriented phases; AE for power maintenance).Trial registrationchictr.org.cn. Background

Explosive power is a key determinant of performance in modern soccer [1]. Elite athletes typically demonstrate exceptional explosive capabilities, enabling them to execute high-intensity movements and technical skills during competition [2, 3]. Enhanced jumping ability and faster reactive agility further empower players to gain decisive advantages in critical aspects like dribbling breakthroughs and aerial duels [4]. Consequently, optimizing training strategies to improve explosive performance remains a core objective for coaches and athletes [5].

Complex training (CT) integrates high-load resistance exercises (e.g., squats) with high-velocity plyometric drills to induce post-activation performance enhancement (PAPE) and elevate explosive output [6, 7]. PAPE denotes acute within-session performance gains following maximal or near-maximal voluntary contractions, potentially mediated by transient increases in muscle torque, preferential recruitment of type II motor units, and heightened motoneuron excitability [8, 9]. From a long-term training perspective, CT can also be framed within the force-velocity (F-V) paradigm: heavy-load squats primarily develop maximal strength reserves in the high-force range, while explosive training focuses on achieving rapid force output at higher velocities under stretch-shortening cycle demands [10]. Although training near 1RM correlates with high motor unit recruitment efficiency, improvements in explosive performance also depend on the rate of force development and the ability to generate force efficiently during dynamic tasks [11]. Crucially, acute PAPE must be distinguished from chronic neuromuscular adaptations resulting from repeated exposure to specific load characteristics over weeks. Thus, beyond transient gains, modulating CT variables (e.g., movement tempo) may shape longer-term force production strategies and efficient neuromuscular recruitment.

Most CT research focuses on load intensity and rest intervals between sets [12–14], while the influence of repetition tempo receives relatively less attention. However, tempo significantly impacts force magnitude, contraction speed, and neuromuscular demands [15–17], suggesting that modulating tempo during the heavy-load component of CT may drive unique acute responses and differentiated training adaptations [18]. For instance, slow eccentric tempos prolong muscle tension duration, enhancing neuromuscular excitability prior to subsequent explosive movements. Conversely, isometric contractions maintain high force output at fixed joint angles, potentially reinforcing neural drive and pre-activation effects [19–21]. Additionally, accelerated eccentric phases (≤ 2 S) may increase demands for rapid deceleration and stretch-shortening cycle function, supporting speed-oriented adaptations by recruiting high-threshold fibers to resist rapid elongation [22, 23]. While these mechanisms are often associated with acute enhancement, repeated exposure to specific contraction demands throughout training cycles may foster load-specific long-term neuromuscular adaptations.

Empirical studies partially corroborate tempo-related effects. Stuart et al. [24] found both high-speed and high-load squat protocols induced enhancement effects, though intensity and timing varied. Conversely, Ditch et al. [25] noted that eccentric squats as pre-activation stimuli failed to significantly improve countermovement jump (CMJ) or squat jump (SJ) performance in high school basketball players. By contrast, Kózlenia et al. [26] demonstrated that performing three sets of 4-second isometric squats at 70% 1RM load increased CMJ height in male athletes. Furthermore, Hernández-Davó et al. [27] observed that faster eccentric tempos during loaded CMJ training (30–70% 1RM) produced higher peak velocity, mean propulsive velocity, and power output, supporting the relevance of eccentric tempo regulation to athletic performance. In summary, while previous studies have examined tempo-related factors in isolated contexts, limited evidence has examined tempo variables within a CT training framework.

Therefore, this study aims to determine whether manipulating squat tempo (slow eccentric, isometric, and accelerated eccentric) during a 6-week CT intervention can induce differential adaptations in maximal strength, power, reactive agility performance, and neuromuscular activation among male college soccer athletes. We hypothesize that the three tempo conditions will produce distinct performance changes and phase-specific EMG activation patterns, reflecting tempo-dependent neuromuscular adaptations.

Methods

Study design

This randomized controlled trial followed CONSORT reporting standards (checklist provided in Additional file 2). To minimize baseline differences in strength, participants were stratified by back squat 1RM and then randomly allocated to groups using a sequence generated by an independent researcher (SPSS Statistics v27.0). Allocation concealment was implemented via sequentially numbered assignments, and participants were blinded to group allocation prior to assignment.

This study examined the effects of different squat tempos during complex training on lower-limb maximal strength, explosive power, and reactive agility performance in male collegiate soccer players. Participants were randomly assigned to one of three complex training groups: SE, IC or AE. All other training components were identical across groups. Training intensity was monitored using the rating of perceived exertion (RPE) scale and heart rate (HR) tracking. A mid-intervention reassessment of back squat 1RM was conducted in week 3 to adjust subsequent training loads. Prior to the intervention, participants completed a 1-week familiarization period to standardize technique and testing procedures. Baseline testing included back squat 1RM, SJ, countermovement jump (CMJ with sEMG recording), and SpeedCourt V-Drill performance. Post-intervention testing was performed 3 days after the final training session to evaluate training adaptations.

Participants

An a priori power analysis (G Power 3.1.9.7) for a 2 × 3 mixed-design repeated-measures ANOVA (f = 0.30, α = 0.05, 1 − β = 0.80) indicated that 30 participants were required to detect a time × group interaction. Thirty male collegiate soccer players participated (age: 22.65 ± 2.20 years; height: 178.65 ± 4.98 cm; body mass: 75.21 ± 6.77 kg), each with ≥ 2 years of structured resistance training and a back squat 1RM ≥ 1.5× body mass; none reported lower-limb injury or cardiovascular disorder within the previous 3 months. Written informed consent was obtained. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Central China Normal University (CCNU-IRB-2024-008), and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Habitual training background and preparatory-period training hours are reported in the Supplementary Material (A2).

Training Protocol

Based on the triphasic training framework [28] and evidence-based recommendations for eccentric-emphasis resistance training [29], the present study manipulated squat tempo during the conditioning activity (CA) to emphasize specific contraction phases and time under tension (TUT). This approach is supported by evidence showing that eccentric actions impose distinct mechanical and neural demands and that eccentric-focused methods can enhance strength-power qualities [22, 30].

Accordingly, three groups performed back squats with different tempos as the CA within an otherwise identical complex-training program. The intervention lasted 6 weeks (two sessions per week) and was scheduled on non-soccer days or at least 6 h apart from soccer training to minimize accumulated fatigue. Tempo was prescribed using four-digit notation (eccentric, isometric/transition, concentric, and isometric/transition phases) following Wilk et al. [31]

To ensure tempo compliance, sessions were supervised and guided using an electronic metronome. Participants completed familiarization sessions to standardize tempo perception and execution, and researchers provided real-time cues and corrections. Sets were repeated when substantial tempo deviations occurred.

Each session consisted of one complex-training unit performed for three rounds: back squat, hurdle jumps, loaded squat jump, and a 15-m sprint. A 15-s transition was provided between exercises, and each round ended with a 15-m sprint, after which participants rested for 4 min before starting the next round. Internal load was monitored by RPE and mean HR during each session (Appendix A3). The full training program is summarized in Table 1.

Measurements

Countermovement Jump and Squat Jump

The CMJ test followed the standardized protocol described by Kibele et al. [33] Participants stood quietly on a force plate (9287CA, 1000 Hz; Kistler Instruments AG, Winterthur, Switzerland) for more than 3 s before performing the jump. The hands were placed on the hips to eliminate arm-swing effects. Upon an auditory cue, participants executed a rapid downward movement to approximately 90° of knee flexion, followed immediately by a maximal vertical jump. Each participant performed three maximal trials, with the best result used for analysis. Rest intervals between trials were ≥ 3 min.

Raw force-plate data were filtered using a fourth-order, zero-lag, low-pass Butterworth filter with a 50 Hz cutoff, implemented in Python (version 3.10; Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA) following Smith et al. [34]. The SJ test followed the same procedure as the CMJ but started from a static semi-squat position (90° knee angle) without a preparatory countermovement. Jump height was used as the primary outcome measure.

One-Repetition Maximum Back Squat

Maximal lower-limb strength was assessed using a standardized 1RM back squat protocol. During familiarization, an initial 1RM was estimated from training history (≥ 2 years; inclusion criterion: back-squat 1RM ≥ 1.5× body mass) and observed technique; pre-test warm-up loads were prescribed relative to this estimate. Participants completed a standardized warm-up (light jogging and dynamic stretching) followed by progressive squat sets. They then performed 1RM attempts starting at the estimated load, increasing by 5–10 kg per attempt until failure. The highest successfully lifted load was recorded as 1RM, determined within ≤ 6 attempts with 3-min rest intervals.

To maintain an appropriate training stimulus, 1RM was reassessed at the end of week 3, and updated values were used to adjust warm-up and working loads for weeks 4–6. This mid-test was brief and applied identically across groups, minimizing the likelihood of systematic between-group bias.

Electromyography Recording and Processing

During the CMJ test, sEMG activity was recorded from the dominant leg using a wireless system (Wave Plus Waterproof, 2000 Hz; Cometa Systems s.r.l., Milan, Italy). Following the standardized protocol of Petrigna et al. [37], three muscles were monitored: the vastus lateralis (VL), biceps femoris (BF), and medial gastrocnemius (MG). Electrode placement followed the SENIAM recommendations [38], with skin preparation involving shaving, abrasion, and cleaning with alcohol to ensure low impedance (Table 2; Fig. 1).

V-Drill Test

The SpeedCourt system (GlobalSpeed GmbH, Hemsbach, Germany) is a 5.25 × 5.25 m platform with 12 embedded contact plates and integrated software that delivers visual stimuli and automatically records performance [39]. Reactive agility was assessed using the V-Drill, which requires rapid direction changes in response to external visual cues. Participants started from the center in a standing position and, after an auditory signal, sprinted along a V-shaped path to the left and right front sensors as instructed on the screen for 30 s. Total distance covered was recorded automatically.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± SD. Analyses were performed in SPSS (v27.0) and figures were produced in GraphPad Prism (v10.0). Normality and homogeneity were assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively.

For each outcome, a 2 × 3 mixed-design repeated-measures ANOVA (time: pre, post × group: SE, IC, AE) tested main effects and the time×group interaction. Significant effects were followed by simple-effects analyses. Between-group post hoc comparisons were Bonferroni-adjusted for three contrasts (k = 3; P < 0.017). Effect sizes are reported as partial η² (small ≥ 0.01, medium ≥ 0.06, large ≥ 0.14).

EMG time-series data were analyzed using SPM1D [40]. EMG recorded during standardized CMJ tests (pre/post) was synchronized with force-platform data, time-normalized to 0–100% of the movement cycle, and partitioned into eccentric and concentric phases (transition at v = 0) [41]. For each muscle, a 2 × 3 mixed-design SPM{F} ANOVA was performed, followed by post hoc SPM{t} tests for within- and between-group comparisons where appropriate. Random Field Theory controlled family-wise error across the time domain (α = 0.05), with Bonferroni adjustment for three between-group contrasts (P < 0.017). Significant supra-threshold clusters are reported with their corresponding time intervals and P values [42].

Maximal Strength

The mixed-design ANOVA showed a significant main effect of time on squat 1RM [F(1, 27) = 559.17, P < 0.001, η2p = 0.954] and a time × group interaction [F(2, 27) = 76.45, P < 0.001, η2p = 0.850], with no significant main effect of group [F(2, 27) = 2.71, P = 0.085, η2p = 0.167]. Bonferroni-adjusted simple effects indicated significant pre–post increases in all groups (absolute change, Δ, kg: SE: +24.0 kg; IC: +18.8 kg; AE: +4.5 kg; all adjusted P ≤ 0.0007). Post-test comparisons further showed greater 1RM in the SE and IC groups than in the AE, whereas SE and IC did not differ (Fig. 2A).

CMJ Height

CMJ height showed a significant main effect of time [F(1, 27) = 198.37, P < 0.001, η2p = 0.880], while the main effect of group [F(2, 27) = 0.57, P = 0.571, η2p = 0.041] and the time × group interaction [F(2, 27) = 0.58, P = 0.567, η2p = 0.041] were not significant. All groups demonstrated comparable pre–post improvements, indicating a general enhancement in CMJ height irrespective of squat-tempo condition (Fig. 2B).

CMJ RSImod

RSImod showed a significant main effect of time [F(1, 27) = 652.27, P < 0.001, η2p = 0.960] and a time × group interaction [F(2, 27) = 34.23, P < 0.001, η2p = 0.717], with no main effect of group [F(2, 27) = 0.24, P = 0.792, η2p = 0.017]. Bonferroni-adjusted simple effects indicated significant pre–post increases in all groups (absolute change, Δ, m·s⁻¹,SE: Δ = 0.041, adjusted p < 0.001; IC: Δ = 0.093, adjusted p < 0.001; AE: Δ = 0.093, adjusted p < 0.001), with a smaller improvement in the SE compared with the IC and AE groups (Fig. 2C).

CMJ Peak Power

Peak power showed a significant main effect of time [F(1, 27) = 292.81, P < 0.001, η2p = 0.916] and a significant time × group interaction [F(2, 27) = 4.96, P = 0.015, η2p = 0.269], with no main effect of group [F(2, 27) = 0.42, P = 0.663, η2p = 0.030]. Bonferroni-adjusted simple effects indicated significant pre–post increases in all groups ((Δ, W, SE: Δ = + 217.9, adjusted p < 0.001; IC: Δ = + 291.7, adjusted p < 0.001; AE: Δ = + 346.0, adjusted p < 0.001), with numerically greater gains observed in AE, followed by IC and SE (Fig. 2D).

SJ Height

SJ height showed no main effects of time [F(1, 27) = 1.75, P = 0.197, η2p = 0.061] or group [F(2, 27) = 1.86, P = 0.175, η2p = 0.121], but a significant time × group interaction [F(2, 27) = 18.40, P < 0.001, η2p = 0.577]. Bonferroni-adjusted simple effects showed IC increased (Δ = +2.60, adjusted p < 0.001), SE decreased (Δ = −2.20, adjusted p = 0.0006), and AE did not change (Δ = +0.90, adjusted p = 0.124). Between-group comparisons at post-test further indicated lower SJ height in the SE than in the IC and AE groups (adjusted p = 0.016) (Fig. 2E).

Reactive Agility Performance

In the V-Drill, a significant main effect of time was observed [F(1, 27) = 164.96, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.859], whereas neither the main effect of group [F(2, 27) = 1.54, p = 0.233, η2p = 0.102] nor the time × group interaction [F(2, 27) = 0.85, p = 0.440, η2p = 0.059] were significant. Overall, reactive agility performance improved from pre- to post-test (Fig. 2F).

Neuromuscular Adaptations Across Tempos

SPM analysis of time-normalized EMG envelopes (0%–100% of the movement cycle) revealed distinct adaptation strategies tailored to each training protocol. AE training primarily enhanced activation during the terminal concentric phase (final extension and take-off) for the VL and MG, while also improving early eccentric braking capacity. SE training proved most effective for the posterior chain, driving widespread recruitment improvements in the BF throughout the concentric propulsion phase. The IC showed variable responses: VL and BF adaptations were angle-specific and limited to the amortization phase, in contrast to the MG, which demonstrated enhanced recruitment across the full eccentric phase.

Vastus Lateralis

Within-group Analysis Longitudinal analysis of the VL revealed significant activity increases in both eccentric-based groups, while the IC remained unchanged. Specifically, the SE exhibited elevated activity during the mid-concentric phase (71%–73%) and intermittently throughout the late concentric phase (clusters spanning 88%–100%, P < 0.017). In contrast, the AE showed a concentrated increase confined strictly to the terminal concentric phase (94%–100%, P < 0.001), suggesting enhanced neural drive immediately prior to take-off.

Between-group Analysis Post-intervention comparisons indicated that both eccentric protocols outperformed the IC. The AE elicited greater activation than IC during the eccentric braking phase (20%–23%), the critical amortization transition (47%–52%), and the mid-concentric phase. Similarly, the SE surpassed IC across multiple clusters in the early eccentric (0%–11%) and concentric (58%–77%) regions. When comparing the two eccentric strategies directly (AE vs. SE), AE demonstrated superior efficacy, eliciting significantly higher activity across the early eccentric phase (0%–27%) and the terminal concentric phase (89%–100%, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3).

Biceps Femoris

Within-group Analysis SPM analysis of the BF revealed adaptations specifically favoring the Slow Eccentric protocol. Within-group assessments indicated that the SE achieved substantial post-intervention increases in recruitment, initiating at the amortization point (51.0%–54.0%, P < 0.017) and extending across a vast portion of the concentric propulsion phase (73.0%–97.0%, P < 0.001). The IC demonstrated increased activity confined to the early-to-mid concentric phase (57.0%–79.0%, P < 0.001), while the AE exhibited only a minimal and brief improvement in the late concentric phase (81.0%–82.0%, P = 0.014).

Between-group Analysis Compared to the IC, the SE exhibited widespread superiority post-intervention, with significantly higher activation in the eccentric phase (26%–32%), the transition phase (37%–58%), and effectively the entire concentric phase (60%–83% and 91%–100%; P < 0.001). Direct post-intervention comparison between the two eccentric strategies revealed a significant difference in the concentric propulsion phase. Specifically, the analysis identified a large significant cluster spanning 69.0%–82.0% (P < 0.001) where the recruitment patterns diverged. Given the robust improvements in the SE throughout this timeframe compared to the lack of adaptation in the AE, this suggests that the Slow Eccentric protocol was more effective for enhancing hip extensor recruitment during the primary propulsive phase of the jump (Fig. 4).

Medial Gastrocnemius

Within-group Analysis SPM analysis of the MG revealed a significant Group × Time interaction (P < 0.001). Within-group comparisons indicated that the AE demonstrated a significant post-intervention increase in recruitment primarily during the initial braking phase of the eccentric action (0%–24%, P < 0.001). Specifically, compared to IC, the AE elicited significantly higher MG activity in the early eccentric phase (2%–21%), the transition phase (22%–37%), and extensively throughout the concentric propulsion phase (38%–100%; P < 0.001). Notably, this significant cluster coincides with the critical plantarflexion (“toe-off”) moment, highlighting the specificity of accelerated eccentric loading for maximizing terminal power output (Fig. 5).

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to examine the effects of complex training with different squat tempos on lower-limb strength, explosive power, and neuromuscular activity. After six weeks of training, all groups exhibited significant within-group improvements in back-squat 1RM, CMJ height, RSImod, peak power, and reactive agility performance, with the SE exhibiting the greatest increase in 1RM. EMG results further revealed tempo-specific neuromuscular adaptations: both SE and AE groups showed higher BF activation during eccentric and concentric phases, whereas the IC demonstrated greater MG activation. Notably, only the IC showed a significant increase in SJ height, while SE slightly decreased and AE showed a modest rise.

Maximal Strength Adaptations

The significant increase in squat 1RM confirms the effectiveness of complex training for enhancing maximal strength. The eccentric-dominant exercise has been shown to elicit a stronger acute PAPE response [9], thereby improving the quality of subsequent explosive tasks within a single training session. However, the long-term strength gains observed after the 6-week intervention are more likely attributable to cumulative structural and neural adaptations induced by repeated training, prolonged eccentric phases increase TUT, thereby amplifying mechanical and metabolic stimuli, activating hypertrophic signaling pathways [31].

The IC is characterized by sustained muscle contraction under high tension without joint displacement, requiring continuous motor-unit activation and a high level of neural drive [43]. Oranchuk et al. [44] reported that isometric contractions performed at ≥ 70% 1RM can induce neural and tendinous adaptations at the trained joint angle and its surrounding range, thereby contributing to improvements in squat 1RM. Moreover, recent randomized crossover evidence has shown that split-squat training using overcoming isometric loads can acutely enhance subsequent jumping performance in elite athletes within minutes, supporting the potential existence of an isometric CA–PAPE linkage within a complex training framework [45].

In the present study, no significant difference in 1RM gains was observed between the SE and IC groups, likely due to their shared contribution to increased TUT. In contrast, AE primarily promotes fascicle-length adaptation rather than cross-sectional growth [22], explaining its relatively smaller strength gains under equal training loads and volumes compared with SE and IC.

Explosive Power

As discussed earlier, complex training is widely recognized as an effective strategy for enhancing explosive power [10]. Tempo-specific differences were most evident in SJ performance, where IC improved significantly, SE slightly declined, and AE rose marginally. The IC group’s improvement aligns with training specificity, suggesting that high-tension isometric contractions transfer more directly to purely concentric force generation. Conversely, SJ performance did not improve in the SE; given that post-testing was performed 72 h after the final session, this pattern is more plausibly explained by training specificity and transfer. The SE protocol emphasized prolonged eccentric time-under-tension and lower movement velocity, which may favor maximal force capacity/structural adaptations but provide a less direct stimulus for rapid concentric force initiation in a concentric-only SJ task.

These findings partially differ from those of Shibata et al. [45], who reported significant SJ improvements following both 2-s and 4-s eccentric squat training. This discrepancy likely arises from the integration of plyometrics within our CT framework and a lower dynamic-squat volume, which may have uniquely modulated the transfer of maximal strength in trained athletes. A potential mechanism involves tempo-dependent modulation of muscle-tendon unit (MTU) properties. The superior SJ performance in the IC may be attributed to enhanced neural synchronization and potentially greater tendon stiffness, facilitating more efficient force transmission. Conversely, the SE protocol’s emphasis on prolonged time-under-tension may prioritize structural adaptations at the expense of rapid explosive initiation. Furthermore, AE training, by emphasizing fast eccentric actions and SSC utilization, likely improves elastic energy storage and reflex efficiency. As reported by Earp et al. [46], higher MTU stiffness correlates positively with faster movement initiation, whereas excessive compliance may prolong force development and reduce propulsive impulse.

Neuromuscular Adaptations

All three muscles showed significant post-intervention neuromuscular adaptation, with tempo-specific patterns across the CMJ cycle. During the eccentric phase (0–50%), BF activation was higher in SE and AE than IC, whereas GM activation was greatest in IC. During the concentric phase (50–100%), VL and BF activation remained higher in SE and AE (notably ~ 55–70%), and AE showed an additional BF increase near take-off (notably ~ 85–95%). Overall, SE and AE exhibited greater neural drive around the transition, while IC displayed more stable activation toward the late concentric phase.

Within Hill’s muscle–tendon unit framework (contractile and elastic components) [47], these patterns suggest different strategies of force production and SSC utilization. SE maintained higher activation across the cycle, indicating greater reliance on active contractile work, which may support control but reduce SSC efficiency. IC showed lower overall activation, consistent with more efficient force transmission and greater use of elastic elements [48], allowing similar output with lower neural cost; however, tendon-based interpretations (e.g., stiffness and elastic energy storage–return) should be viewed cautiously because tendon mechanics and muscle–tendon behavior were not directly assessed. AE demonstrated elevated mid-cycle activation and greater BF activation near take-off, reflecting higher global neuromuscular involvement and potentially enhanced SSC-related braking-to-propulsion transfer [27]. Collectively, SE favored active contractile contribution, IC favored elastic efficiency, and AE combined higher neural drive with SSC-oriented propulsion.

Reactive Agility Performance

All three tempo-based CT programs improved V-Drill performance with no between-group differences. Because the V-Drill requires responses to visual cues, it primarily reflects reactive agility rather than a pre-planned change-of-direction task. Reactive agility depends on both physical qualities (e.g., strength, SSC function, leg stiffness) and perceptual–cognitive processes (e.g., stimulus recognition and decision-making) [49]; thus, similar improvements across groups suggest that performance is not determined by maximal strength alone [50]. Supporting this, evidence indicates that once a strength threshold is reached, additional 1RM gains may have limited transfer to direction-change performance, which is more strongly constrained by eccentric braking and reactive-strength qualities [51]. Prior work also shows that greater strength gains from eccentric-focused squats do not necessarily translate to direction-change improvements without SSC- or horizontally oriented stimuli [52]. In the present study, however, the complex-training structure combined heavy resistance with plyometric/SSC-oriented drills, which may have promoted broader task-relevant transfer and produced comparable reactive-agility gains across tempos.

Summary and Limitations

This study showed that manipulating squat tempo within a complex-training framework elicited tempo-specific adaptations in lower-limb strength, power, and neuromuscular activity in collegiate soccer players. All groups improved, but SE produced the greatest gains in maximal strength, IC enhanced concentric jump performance, and AE was associated with greater improvements in SSC-dependent outcomes.

Several limitations should be considered. Because no non–complex-training control condition was included (e.g., resistance-only, plyometrics-only, or passive control), the findings should be interpreted as within-group pre–post changes rather than evidence of superiority over other methods. Participants were male collegiate soccer players, which limits generalizability to females and other populations [53]. EMG was assessed in only three muscles and reflects activation amplitude, precluding inferences about whole-limb coordination or spinal reflex contributions [54]. Future work should include broader muscle coverage, complementary neurophysiological measures (e.g., H-reflex), and longer follow-up to clarify mechanisms and retention of adaptations. Female athletes should be included to explore how periodized adjustments to squat rhythm within combined training effectively enhance specific performance qualities [55].

Future research should adopt a systems/ecological, season-long perspective to examine how tempo-specific complex training interacts with tactical demands, match congestion, and accumulated neuromuscular fatigue, while explicitly accounting for neuromuscular efficiency, fatigue management, and the sustainability of adaptations across the competitive season. Integrating applied indicators of functional stability and injury-related neuromuscular balance with AI-enabled spatiotemporal analytics may further help model how neuromuscular responses evolve under varying loads and support individualized load adjustment in football settings [56, 57].

Conclusion

Within this design, squat tempo was associated with distinct adaptation profiles: slow-eccentric training may favor maximal-strength development, isometric training may support force initiation and transmission, and accelerated-eccentric training may be more closely linked to SSC-related performance. Accordingly, tempo manipulation provides a simple, practical lever to individualize complex training across a season—SE and IC may suit off-season/preparatory phases emphasizing strength and neural adaptation, whereas AE may be preferable during competitive phases to maintain explosiveness while managing fatigue. Transfer can be further enhanced by pairing tempo-specific squats with complementary plyometrics (e.g., SE with weighted jumps, IC with concentric jumps, and AE with short-contact reactive drills).
