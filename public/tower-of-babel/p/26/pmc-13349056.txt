Two-generation Cleidocranial Dysplasia Involving Three Individuals of a Family: A Rare Case Report
Authors: Maroti B. Wadewale, Sanpreet Singh Sachdev, Suleka Ranganath
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cleidocranial dysplasia (CCD) is a rare autosomal dominant skeletal disorder characterized by multiple dental and skeletal abnormalities. While isolated case reports exist, this case is unique due to its clear familial inheritance pattern and the need for urgent surgical dental intervention in a symptomatic young adult, thereby contributing new clinical insights to the limited literature on CCD. A female patient in her early 20s presented with pain and pus discharge in the mandibular left posterior region for 3 months. Extraoral findings included short stature, frontal bossing, hypertelorism, midfacial hypoplasia, and hypoplastic clavicles. Intraoral examination revealed generalized microdontia, malalignment, multiple carious teeth, and a partially erupted mandibular left third molar with pericoronitis. A positive family history of similar skeletal features in her father and sibling raised suspicion of a syndromic diagnosis. Imaging studies showed open cranial sutures, underdeveloped maxilla, and an impacted mandibular molar with surrounding bone resorption. Differential diagnoses such as Down’s syndrome, Gardner’s syndrome, pycnodysostosis, and mandibuloacral dysplasia were ruled out, and a final diagnosis of CCD was established. The patient underwent surgical extraction of the third molar, excision of the associated follicular tissue, and socket curettage. Histopathology confirmed chronic nonspecific inflammation. Healing was uneventful with complete resolution of symptoms. The present case report underscores the importance of early diagnosis and timely dental intervention in patients with CCD. A multidisciplinary approach that includes dental, surgical, and genetic considerations is essential for improving both functional outcomes and quality of life in affected individuals.

Introduction

Cleidocranial dysplasia (CCD) is a rare genetic disorder affecting one in a million individuals on a global scale. It is characterized by abnormalities in the development of the skeletal system, specifically teeth and bones. Involvement of the skull, facial bones, and jawbones is common, while other bones such as the clavicle, spine, bones of the limbs, and phalanges may also be affected. The primary etiological factor behind CCD is the autosomal dominant mutation of the RUNX2 gene, which is responsible for the differentiation of osteoblasts and subsequently the morphogenesis of the skeletal system.[1]

Depending on the degree of mutation, a diverse range of clinical manifestations may be observed in patients with CCD, varying in the severity of symptoms and extent of skeletal involvement.[2] The craniofacial bones are almost invariably affected in CCD, and characteristic features include lack of closure of craniofacial sutures, open fontanelles, and frontal bossing.[3] The orodental structures are also frequently involved, with hypoplastic jaws and irregularities in the eruption pattern of permanent teeth. Over-retained, unerupted, impacted, or supernumerary teeth are commonly reported, while those that do erupt often present with malocclusion. These dental anomalies further compromise oral hygiene and increase the risk of dental caries and periodontal disease.[45]

Early diagnosis of orodental pathologies and timely intervention are essential to reduce associated complications and enhance the quality of life for affected individuals. As such, dental professionals play a pivotal role in the recognition and multidisciplinary management of CCD.[6] Despite its distinct phenotype, CCD often remains undiagnosed until late adolescence or adulthood, particularly in milder familial variants.

The present case report describes the dental diagnosis and management of a young female patient with CCD, highlighting the classical skeletal and dental manifestations along with two-generation familial involvement. This report aims to contribute to the limited literature, which comprises only a few sporadic case reports of this rare skeletal dysplasia, and underscores the essential role of dentists in identifying such conditions early in clinical practice.

Case Report

Patient information

A female patient in her early 20s reported to the outpatient department of our institution with a primary complaint of pain and pus discharge in the mandibular left posterior region persisting for the past 3 months. Although her general medical history was unremarkable, a detailed conversation revealed significant concerns regarding her dental abnormalities and facial esthetics, which had progressively affected her self-esteem, social confidence, and overall quality of life. The patient disclosed a family history of similar facial features, particularly in her father and younger sister, suggesting a possible hereditary condition. No past medical interventions or relevant systemic illnesses were reported.

Clinical findings

On general physical examination, the patient exhibited short stature and a prominent forehead with frontal bossing. These craniofacial features closely resembled those seen in her father and sibling [Figure 1]. Additional extraoral features included hypertelorism, a depressed nasal bridge, and a generally underdeveloped midface, all contributing to a markedly concave facial profile. Examination of the clavicles revealed hypoplasia, enabling the patient to approximate her shoulders anteriorly.

General characteristics of cleidocranial dysplasia including short stature and prominent forehead noted in (a) patient, (b) her father, and (c) her younger sister

Intraoral examination revealed generalized true microdontia with considerable spacing between the teeth. A high-arched palate was present. The mandibular arch showed severe crowding and malalignment, which compromised the patient’s ability to maintain adequate oral hygiene and led to generalized calculus accumulation. Multiple teeth displayed clinical signs of dental caries. In the mandibular left posterior region, a partially erupted mandibular third molar was observed. The associated gingiva appeared severely inflamed, and foul-smelling purulent discharge emanated from the sulcular region surrounding the tooth. Food lodgment was noted between the tooth and the operculum, exacerbating the local infection and contributing to halitosis.

Diagnostic assessment

Considering the presence of multiple skeletal and dental anomalies, and the familial nature of these features, a provisional diagnosis of a developmental disorder or syndrome was established. Differential diagnoses included craniosynostosis syndromes, Down’s syndrome, Gardner’s syndrome, CCD, mandibuloacral dysplasia, and pycnodysostosis. To investigate further, an orthopantomogram was advised, which revealed a horizontally impacted mandibular left third molar associated with an ill-defined radiolucent area suggestive of pericoronal bone resorption. A computed tomography scan of the head and facial bones was performed, revealing open cranial sutures suggestive of delayed closure. The maxilla was underdeveloped with collapsed maxillary sinuses, accounting for the midfacial deficiency. The mandible, in contrast, appeared normal in size but exhibited flattened and divergent condyles [Figure 2]. Because of financial constraints, the patient’s family chose not to opt for genetic testing.

(a) Malaligned dentition with wide spacing, (b) orthopantomogram showing impacted mandibular left third molar associated with an ill-defined radiolucency, (c) computed tomography scan showing open skull suture, hypoplastic maxilla, and clavicles

The radiographic and clinical findings shared overlapping features with several conditions. However, the absence of features such as polypoid soft tissue lesions, short neck, webbed feet, and a protruding tongue made Gardner’s syndrome and Down’s syndrome unlikely. Pycnodysostosis, although phenotypically similar to CCD, is characterized by a generalized increase in bone density, which was not evident in this case. Mandibuloacral dysplasia was also excluded due to the absence of characteristic resorption of the distal phalanges. Based on these findings and the familial context, a final diagnosis of CCD was established.

Therapeutic intervention

Given the chronicity of symptoms and local infection associated with the impacted mandibular third molar, surgical intervention was deemed necessary. The goal was to resolve the acute infectious process and facilitate future dental and orthodontic planning. The patient was prescribed with a 3-day course of preemptive antibiotics (amoxicillin 500 mg + clavulanic acid 125 mg) before the procedures. Under strict aseptic precautions and local anesthesia, a full-thickness mucoperiosteal flap was elevated using a standard Ward’s incision. Buccal guttering was performed to gain sufficient access to the impacted tooth, which was then extracted in toto. The follicular lining surrounding the tooth was carefully excised, and thorough curettage of the extraction socket was carried out to eliminate any residual pathological tissue. Hemostasis was achieved, and the surgical site was sutured using 3-0 black silk suture material. Sutures were planned for removal after 7 days [Figure 3].

The extracted third molar and the excised pericoronal soft tissue were submitted for histopathological evaluation to rule out any underlying cystic or neoplastic pathology. The histopathology report confirmed the presence of chronic nonspecific inflammatory cell infiltrate within a loose edematous stroma, without evidence of cystic transformation or other specific lesions.

Follow-up and outcomes

The patient was reviewed 1 week postoperatively for suture removal. At that visit, the surgical site exhibited healthy granulation and healing without any signs of infection or dehiscence. The patient reported significant improvement in comfort and resolution of previous symptoms, including pain and pus discharge. No adverse events were noted during the perioperative or postoperative period. The patient was educated about the nature of her condition and counseled on the importance of interdisciplinary follow-up care involving orthodontic, prosthodontic, and potentially genetic consultation to support long-term rehabilitation and monitoring of her skeletal dysplasia.

Discussion

The diagnosis and clinical management of CCD pose a unique set of challenges due to the extensive skeletal and dental anomalies associated with the condition. Closely monitoring the oral health status of patients with CCD is of utmost importance to avoid more gruesome sequelae.[4] For instance, in the present case, the infection could have aggravated, spreading to different spaces of the oral cavity or leading to osteomyelitis. This highlights the complex interplay between these anomalies and the resultant functional and esthetic complications, particularly in the context of oral health. These complexities, if not resolved at an early stage, can further affect the patient’s quality of life.

The disturbances in the development and eruption of teeth, as reflected by over-retention of deciduous teeth, delayed eruption of permanent teeth, presence of supernumerary teeth, and microdontia, lead to malalignment and spacing of the dentition.[3] Not only are these of esthetic concern to the patient, but they also predispose them to an increased risk of dental caries and periodontal disease.

The patient in this case presented with multiple carious lesions and a partially erupted third molar complicated by pericoronitis, a common issue in CCD patients due to the delayed and aberrant eruption of teeth and their malalignment. The decision to extract the impacted mandibular third molar was driven by the need to alleviate pain and prevent further infection, which could exacerbate the already compromised oral health. The chronic nonspecific inflammatory tissue, along with areas of granulation observed under histopathology, correlated with the clinical presentation of pericoronitis associated with impacted mandibular molars.[7] This emphasizes the importance of early intervention in preventing the progression of localized infections, which can have systemic implications in CCD patients who are often more susceptible to complications due to their skeletal abnormalities.[8]

Radiographic findings in CCD, such as open cranial sutures and underdeveloped maxillary structures, are well-documented and were consistent with the findings in this patient. These features contribute to the characteristic facial profile of CCD patients, marked by frontal bossing, midface hypoplasia, and a concave profile.[4] In this case, the radiographic evidence of divergent and flattened mandibular condyles further complicated the patient’s occlusion, highlighting the skeletal dysplasia’s impact on both function and esthetics.

The multidisciplinary approach to managing CCD, involving dental, surgical, and orthodontic care, is crucial for addressing the wide-ranging effects of the disorder.[56] Early diagnosis and regular monitoring are essential to plan and execute timely interventions that can mitigate the complications associated with CCD. Surgical management, as demonstrated in this case, plays a pivotal role in addressing the immediate and long-term needs of CCD patients, particularly in alleviating pain and preventing infections.

One of the strengths of this case report is the detailed presentation of a two-generation familial occurrence of CCD, along with its classical clinical, radiographic, and histopathological findings. What makes this case unique is the combination of a syndromic presentation, familial pattern, and surgical dental management in a single visit to relieve symptoms and enable future multidisciplinary care. However, a limitation is the absence of long-term follow-up data, especially with regard to orthodontic or genetic counseling outcomes, which would provide additional insight into comprehensive multidisciplinary care.

Conclusion

The present case highlights the importance of early diagnosis and intervention in CCD to prevent oral infections, functional limitations, and psychosocial distress. The familial pattern of presentation reinforces the need for thorough family history-taking in dental settings. Prompt surgical management can significantly alleviate symptoms and pave the way for successful long-term rehabilitation through interdisciplinary care.

Patient perspective

For a long time, I was very conscious of the appearance of my teeth and facial features. I knew something was different because my father and sister also had similar facial traits, but we had never consulted a doctor for it. Over time, I started to feel more anxious and avoided social interactions due to my facial profile and misaligned teeth. The pain and pus discharge from the lower side of my mouth made things worse and forced me to finally seek help.

When I visited the dental hospital, I was relieved that the doctors listened carefully and explained everything clearly. They told me I had a rare condition called CCD, and for the first time, I understood the reason behind my lifelong dental issues. The surgery went smoothly, and the pain that had been troubling me for months disappeared within a week. I was also happy to know that the procedure would help me get started with other dental treatments in the future. I feel much better now, both physically and emotionally, and I am more hopeful about improving my smile and overall health.
