Laying the groundwork for an inter-ministerial One Health response to antimicrobial resistance in South Korea
Authors: Seok Hyeon Na, Inji Jun, Sangrim Kang, Jung Sik Yoo, Eun-Jeong Yoon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe five-year project to address the threat of AMR, One Health Multi-Ministerial Project on AMR, was implemented in 2019 in South Korea with coordinating action across human, animal, environmental, and food sectors. To describe the design, implementation, and outcomes of the initiative, assess its contribution to AMR preparedness, and identify policy implications for sustaining progress.MethodsA structured evaluation framework was applied integrating quantitative and qualitative indicators across four domains: (i) infrastructure building, (ii) multisectoral surveillance, (iii) containment of AMR spread, and (iv) outcome translation and dissemination.ResultsOver five years, the overall performance rate reached 92.2 %, with completion rates of 96.7 % for infrastructure building, 86.7 % for multi-sector surveillance, 92.5 % for containment of AMR spread, and 92.9 % for outcome translation and dissemination. Standardized annual assessments, based on weighted indicators and capped scores, ensuring transparent evaluation. While most targets were achieved or exceeded, partial attainment in database registration, policy proposals, and innovation outputs highlights priorities for future development. These results demonstrate effective implementation of South Korean One Health AMR strategy. Major achievements included the establishment of standardized laboratories, the acquisition of 20,061 biological resources, the launch of a One Health AMR portal, and expanded surveillance across sectors.ConclusionsThe initiative operationalized the core principles of One Health, i.e., integration, multisectoral collaboration, data sharing, sustainability, and policy integration, while addressing structural gaps in AMR preparedness. Although challenges remain in stewardship, surveillance coverage, and long-term governance, the project provides a durable foundation for the national strategy for future AMR in South Korea.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a global public health challenge, often described as a silent pandemic [1,[2], [3], [4]]. The COVID-19 pandemic further accelerated resistance by increasing antimicrobial consumption, much of it inappropriate [5,6]. The burden of AMR is already substantial: in the European Union/European Economic Area, AMR is responsible for an estimated 33,000 deaths annually [7], and globally, it was associated with 4.95 million deaths in 2019, including 1.27 million directly attributable deaths [8]. International organizations and expert groups have repeatedly warned that, without coordinated global and multi-sectoral action, AMR could cause up to 10 million deaths each year by 2050, along with severe economic losses [9]. In South Korea, AMR remains a critical public health concern, with rising resistance rates and antimicrobial consumption levels among the highest in the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) [10]. Infections caused by multidrug-resistant (MDR) pathogens already result in thousands of deaths annually [11,12]. The emergence and spread of MDR organisms undermine the treatment of infectious diseases in both humans and animals, increasing morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs [[13], [14]].

AMR response in South Korea began with the establishment of the National AMR Control Council in May 2016, followed by the release of the First National Action Plan on AMR (NAP, 2016–2020) in August 2016 (Fig. 1) [15]. In February 2017, inter-ministerial consultations commenced to design a joint initiative, and in July 2017 a formal call for multi-ministerial project proposals was issued [15]. A detailed mid- to long-term strategy was developed by September, accompanied by a national public hearing in November and final reporting to the director-general level in December. The initiative received formal approval in 2018, with the organizational framework and budget allocations secured. Specific project proposals were finalized in May 2018, and the project was officially launched in 2019 with the addition of the Rural Development Administration (RDA) as a participating ministry [15] bringing the total seven participating ministries: the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA), Ministry of Science and ICT (MSIT), Ministry of Agriculture, Food and Rural Affairs (MAFRA), Ministry of Environment (MOE), Ministry of Oceans and Fisheries (MOF), Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (MFDS), and RDA [16].Fig. 1Timeline of the One Health Multi-Ministerial Project on AMR (2019–2023). Globally, recognition of the need for integrated action led to the creation of the Quadripartite alliance, comprising the World Health Organization (WHO), the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), the World Organization for Animal Health (WOAH), and the United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP), which issued its first One Health Joint Plan of Action in 2022 [17]. The late establishment of this alliance underscores the challenges of mobilizing cross-sectoral cooperation against AMR. Against this backdrop, the early launch of a comprehensive multi-ministerial initiative in South Korea represented a timely and proactive step to bridge entrenched silos, harmonize infrastructures of varying maturity, and build consensus on the need for coordinated AMR surveillance and research [11,15,16,18].

This review explores the experiences of the five-year inter-ministerial cooperation project, which strengthened the national capacity in South Korea to address AMR, enhanced research and surveillance infrastructures, and contributed to the development of a sustainable One Health response, by synthesizing both its tangible achievements and intangible yet important outcomes.

Overview of the project

The One Health Multi-Ministerial Project on AMR was launched in 2019 in South Korea as a five-year initiative to address AMR across the human, animal, environmental, and food sectors in an integrated and cooperative manner. The project arose from growing recognition that fragmented efforts across ministries had limited the nation's capacity to manage AMR as a cross-cutting public health crisis.

The overarching vision of the project was to “safeguard the Korean population from AMR”. Its primary goal was to establish a robust national strategy for AMR control by elucidating the mechanisms and transmission pathways of resistance across humans, animals, and the environment within a One Health framework [1,17]. To achieve this, the project defined five priority research domains: (i) Infrastructure Building, (ii) Surveillance Indicators and Metrics, (iii) New Diagnostic Technologies, (iv) Mechanistic Research, and (v) Preventive and Therapeutic Strategies. Implementation of these strategic pillars aimed to close structural gaps, harmonize inter-ministerial efforts, and lay the foundation for sustainable One Health-based AMR governance in South Korea.

Given the project's complexity and the need for scientific rigor, a multi-tiered governance framework of the five-year project was established (Fig. 2). The Project Steering Committee provided high-level oversight and approved strategic directions. The Working-level Consultative Body coordinated research planning, regulatory development, and inter-ministerial collaboration. An independent Scientific Advisory Board, composed of AMR experts, offered technical guidance. Project leadership and administration were undertaken by the KDCA, which managed overall coordination, integration of results, and synthesis of final reports.Fig. 2Governance structure and task allocation of the One Health Multi-Ministerial Project on AMR (2019–2023). The total research and development investment for the five years reached 42.47 billion KRW (326.7 million USD), with annual budgets of 4.96 billion KRW (38.1 million USD) in 2019, 6.36 billion KRW (49.0 million USD) in 2020, 10.95 billion KRW (84.2 million USD) in 2021, 11.12 billion KRW (85.6 million USD) in 2022, and 9.08 billion KRW (69.8 million USD) in 2023. These resources supported the establishment of standardized laboratory infrastructure, the expansion of cross-sectoral surveillance, and the development of collaborative research programs.

Four major domains were defined: (i) infrastructure building, (ii) multi-sector AMR surveillance, (iii) containment of AMR spread, and (iv) outcome translation and dissemination (Table 1). Indicators within each domain were accompanied by quantitative targets and weighted scores, allowing a balanced evaluation of progress across diverse activities. The first domain, infrastructure building, was positioned as a foundational pillar of the framework. Its focus on standardized testing methods, designated laboratories, integrated databases, and culture collections aligns with Objective 2, i.e., strengthen knowledge and evidence through surveillance and research, and Objective 5, i.e., investment in new tools and infrastructure, of the Global Action Plan (GAP). In South Korea, the first NAP introduced critical surveillance platforms such as Kor-GLASS and sector-specific monitoring systems, while the second NAP expanded this to emphasize integrated One Health platforms and formal inter-ministerial coordination. By embedding these activities into a structured and weighted evaluation, the framework extended beyond mere establishment of facilities to measure how infrastructure actively supported AMR control.

The second and third domains, multi-sector surveillance and containment of AMR spread, reflected the dual imperatives of monitoring resistance and translating knowledge into interventions. Surveillance activities, including AMU/AMR monitoring and correlation studies, directly operationalized the GAP's call for harmonized surveillance under Objective 2, and also supported prudent antimicrobial use (AMU) under Objective 4, i.e., optimizing the use of antimicrobial medicines. These priorities were echoed in South Korean first NAP, which developed surveillance across human, animal, and food sectors but struggled with data integration, and were expanded in the second NAP to emphasize One Health-based integration and participation in WHO GLASS. Containment efforts, including discovery of novel targets, development of alternatives and diagnostics, and new therapies, mirrored Objective 4 of the GAP, i.e., reducing infection incidence, and Objective 5, i.e., R&D for medicines, diagnostics, vaccines. While the first NAP largely focused on stewardship and infection control, the second NAP explicitly broadened the scope to R&D investment, placing South Korean strategy in line with global innovation agendas.

Finally, the domain of outcome translation and dissemination emphasized the importance of ensuring that knowledge and innovation lead to measurable societal benefits. This domain included patents, policy proposals, dissemination of findings, and measures of research productivity. Its positioning reflects the GAP's Objective 1, i.e., improving awareness and communication, and Objective 5, i.e., economic and policy support for investment, as well as South Korean first NAP focus on awareness and guidelines, and the second NAP emphasis on policy efficiency, knowledge transfer, and international cooperation. By treating outcome translation as a formal domain within the framework, the project recognized that sustainable AMR control depends not only on scientific discovery but also on effective policy uptake and dissemination of results.

Indicator weighting and calculation

To derive indicator achievement scores, weighting factors ranging from 0.25 to 1.00 were applied to reflect the perceived strategic importance of each indicator. These weights were determined internally by the evaluation team through consensus, rather than based on external references or published frameworks. Higher weights, e.g., 1.00, were assigned to core outcomes such as validated testing standard development, high-impact publications, and commercialization of diagnostic technologies, whereas intermediate weights, e.g., 0.25–0.50, were used for supportive activities like network activation meetings or content development. This approach ensured that critical outcomes exerted proportionally greater influence on the composite performance score while preventing inflation by routine or lower-priority tasks.

For each indicator, the measured value was compared to its quantitative target to determine the percentage of goal completion. This percentage was then multiplied by the pre-assigned weighting factor to generate the indicator's achievement score.

Weighted scores were averaged within each domain to yield field-specific achievement rates, which matched those reported above. The weighted average across all indicators produced an overall program performance rate of 92.2 %, demonstrating that the program not only met but frequently exceeded its quantitative targets while maintaining balanced progress across multiple strategic areas within the One Health AMR framework.

Evaluation framework and overall achievements

The framework assessed performance across the four domains defined above, using both quantitative and qualitative indicators to capture progress (Table 1). Standardized annual evaluations were conducted, with achievements scored against predefined targets and capped at 100 % to prevent inflation from overperformance. Each indicator was weighted according to its strategic importance, and the weighted scores were averaged to generate domain-level performance rates. This approach allowed for a transparent assessment of both immediate research outputs and longer-term systemic impacts [18].

A comprehensive evaluation of the five-year project demonstrated a cumulative achievement rate of 92.2 %, reflecting substantial progress across all domains. Completion rates were 96.7 % for infrastructure building, 86.7 % for multi-sector AMR surveillance, 92.5 % for containment of AMR spread, and 92.9 % for outcome translation and dissemination. These findings indicate that while most objectives were effectively achieved, certain indicators, such as database content registration, policy proposals, and innovation-driven outputs, were partially met and remain as targets for future development.

Although the overall performance rate reached 92.2 %, several indicators did not achieve their full targets. Notably, content registration for the integrated One Health database achieved 66.7 % of its goal (100 of the 150 planned), policy proposal submissions for AMU/AMR correlation research reached 33.3 % (1 of 3 proposals), and some innovation-driven deliverables, such as patent filings for development of novel therapeutic options including antimicrobial alternatives and adjuvants, reached 60.0 % of their respective targets. These gaps primarily reflect longer development timelines, regulatory and intellectual property complexities, and the multi-ministerial coordination required for cross-sectoral AMR efforts.

Addressing these shortfalls provides clear priorities for the next project cycle. Future initiatives should allocate dedicated resources for database curation, strengthen mechanisms for translating research findings into policy proposals, and extend timelines or provide targeted support for technology development and patent processes. By focusing on these areas, subsequent programs can ensure that partially completed tasks are fully realized, thereby reinforcing the sustainability and impact of South Korean One Health AMR strategy.

Key achievements

Infrastructure development

Major achievements of the project included the acquisition of 20,061 biological resources and the establishment of the culture collection for MDR Bacterial Pathogens in October 2022, which enabled active resource distribution [18]. The One Health AMR portal system was launched in 2019 and upgraded annually, facilitating integrated AMR data analysis and public dissemination in collaboration with MAFRA, MFDS, and the Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service (HIRA) [19]. Standardization initiatives encompassed the publication of multi-sector antimicrobial susceptibility testing manuals and the development of standard quality control procedures for whole-genome sequencing of resistant pathogens. Designation of standard laboratories, method validation, and maintenance of ISO 9001:2015 certification strengthened the reliability of research processes. The Korea National AMU Analysis System (KONAS), a stewardship-based feedback platform, was implemented using nationwide antimicrobial usage data from healthcare facilities, in collaboration with the Korean Society of Infectious Diseases and HIRA [20]. Annual reports, operational manuals, and international One Health AMR symposia further promoted cross-sector collaboration [18].

Multisectoral AMR surveillance

National-level surveys of AMR and antimicrobial usage were systematically conducted in the human sector by KDCA and by HIRA, respectively, and in the animal-food sector by MAFRA and MFDS. The resulting data made publicly accessible via the One Health AMR portal system since 2019 [19].

Small-scale studies on AMR transmission have been conducted in various settings, including pig farms and veterinary hospitals, involving animals, workers, and their environments [21,22]. Building on these efforts, a larger multi-ministerial pilot project, “Joint Investigation of Antimicrobial Resistance in Korean Communities through the One Health Approach” (2021−2023), targeted an urban–rural community with specimen collection across human, animal, environmental, and food sectors. Major bacterial pathogens (Escherichia coli, Salmonella enterica, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Acinetobacter spp., Staphylococcus aureus, and Enterococcus spp.) were investigated using antimicrobial susceptibility testing, whole-genome sequencing (WGS), and metagenomics to assess cross-sectoral epidemiological linkages. Findings from this pilot informed protocol revisions, and the study is now being expanded to an urbanized community that includes companion animals and long-term care facilities.

Containment of AMR spread

Research in this domain yielded the identification of novel antimicrobial targets based on resistance mechanisms, the development of antimicrobial alternatives and adjuvants/potentiators [23,24], and the development of rapid diagnostic kits suitable for field application [25]. Advances were also achieved in the development of new therapeutic strategies and in evaluating the quality of AMR control practices [26]. The establishment of a multidisciplinary research network, supported by inter-ministerial collaboration, facilitated knowledge exchange and accelerated technology transfer [18].

Cumulative impact

The project delivered tangible outputs, including interoperable databases, standardized methodologies, culture collection for MDR Bacterial Pathogens [27], and validated diagnostic tools, as well as intangible benefits, such as harmonized governance structures [18], enduring inter-ministerial collaboration, and heightened national awareness of AMR as a public health security threat. These cumulative achievements served as the foundation for designing the follow-up R&D initiative commencing in 2024. Impact and policy implications

The project yielded measurable impacts on AMR preparedness in South Korea. Integration of data from the human, animal, food, and environmental sectors provided a comprehensive understanding of resistance trends. The Korea National AMU Analysis System (KONAS) delivered regular stewardship feedback to hospitals, promoting more rational antimicrobial prescribing [20]. The MDR bacterial culture collections from multiple sectors, which were transferred to the National Culture Collection for Pathogens (NCCP) in January 2024, supplied standardized, high-quality isolates to researchers nationwide, accelerating both academic and clinical investigations. Genomic data generated from multiple sectors enhanced molecular epidemiological analyses, enabling early outbreak detection and supporting more accurate risk assessments.

Rapid diagnostic technologies developed through the project significantly shortened the time required for pathogen identification. Once clinical trials and commercialization are completed, these tools are expected to improve prescribing accuracy and strengthen infection control measures. Although the novel antimicrobial and adjuvant candidates identified in this project are at the early stage of the discovery pipeline, they represent promising leads for future therapeutic strategies in the face of dwindling treatment options [30].

From a policy perspective, institutionalization of inter-ministerial governance facilitated coordinated decision-making, efficient resource allocation, and alignment with international AMR strategies [1]. The project also heightened awareness among policymakers and the public, framing AMR as a national security concern [31]. Collectively, these efforts advanced capacity and visibility in South Korea within the global One Health-based AMR framework, contributing to international data sharing and policy discourse [32,33].

The project effectively operationalized the core principles of the One Health approach. Integration was achieved by consolidating AMR data from human, animal, and environmental sectors into a unified platform for joint analysis and dissemination, enabling a holistic view of intersectoral dynamics [34]. Multisectoral collaboration was fostered through a governance structure encompassing seven ministries, coordinating research planning, regulatory development, and output dissemination, thereby reducing redundancies and improving resource allocation efficiency [34,35]. Data sharing was enhanced through standardized formats and a shared portal enabling real-time exchange of AMR and infectious disease data across sectors, which improved the timeliness of both policy-making and research [32]. Sustainability was reinforced by establishing standardized infrastructure and documented protocols, ensuring that subsequent projects or new participating institutions could apply consistent criteria, thereby strengthening long-term One Health-based AMR response capacity [34,36]. Finally, policy integration was realized as outputs from integrated analyses were incorporated directly into policy recommendations and revisions of the national infectious disease plan, representing a notable example of institutionalizing the One Health approach [37].

In addition, the evaluation framework developed in this project is robust and has strong potential for replication in other settings. Its strength lies in the balance between standardization, through clearly defined domains and a transparent scoring system, and flexibility, by allowing weights and indicators to be tailored to local contexts. If carefully adapted to reflect national priorities and data availability, this framework could serve as a model template for the evaluation of national AMR programs and support harmonized progress tracking under the One Health approach.

Challenges and future directions

Despite notable achievements, South Korea continues to face significant challenges in addressing AMR. Antimicrobial consumption remains high, ranking among the top within OECD member states [[3], [10]]. Antimicrobial stewardship practices in community care settings are still insufficient, contributing to inappropriate prescribing [[38], [39]]. While surveillance coverage in veterinary, aquaculture, and environmental sectors has expanded, it remains uneven, particularly in rural areas and small-scale production systems [40,41].

Sustaining inter-ministerial collaboration beyond the project period will require ongoing political commitment, clearly defined governance structures, and stable funding mechanisms. The COVID-19 pandemic demonstrated how public health emergencies can divert resources from AMR programs, underscoring the need for resilience planning [6,42,[43], [44]].

Future priorities include strengthening stewardship in primary care, expanding One Health surveillance to underrepresented regions and sectors, and translating research outputs into both clinical and policy applications. First, inter-ministerial coordination should be institutionalized through legally mandated governance structures and regular joint planning processes, ensuring continuity beyond project-based funding. Second, antimicrobial stewardship must be reinforced across all healthcare levels, including primary care and community settings, through expanded use of feedback systems such as KONAS, aimed at reducing inappropriate prescribing [45]. Third, AMR and antimicrobial usage surveillance should be broadened to encompass underrepresented rural, aquaculture, and small-scale farming systems, with seamless integration of all data into the national One Health AMR portal. Fourth, translation of research outputs, such as rapid diagnostics, novel antimicrobials, and adjuvant candidates, into regulatory approval, commercialization, and clinical application pathways should be actively promoted. Fifth, sustainable financing mechanisms and workforce development programs are essential to secure stable budgets and retain skilled personnel for AMR-related surveillance, diagnostics, and policy implementation. Sixth, AMR mitigation strategies should be embedded within national health security and emergency preparedness frameworks to safeguard program continuity during crises such as pandemics. Finally, stronger international collaboration, particularly with the Quadripartite Joint Plan of Action and other global initiatives, will help align national policies with evolving international standards and facilitate timely global data sharing [17,35,[46], [47]].

This initiative embodies the core principles of the One Health approach: cross-sectoral cooperation, harmonization of infrastructures, and evidence-based policymaking. While challenges remain in antimicrobial stewardship, surveillance coverage, and sustaining inter-ministerial consensus, the project has laid a strong foundation for the national AMR strategy in South Korea. Its experience offers a valuable model for other nations seeking to operationalize One Health principles in combating AMR, underscoring the importance of durable cooperation, adequate resources, and alignment with global frameworks.
