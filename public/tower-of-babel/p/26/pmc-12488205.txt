Morphometric analysis of spina bifida after fetal repair shows new subtypes with associated outcomes
Authors: L K Mann, S Pandiri, N Agarwal, H Northrup, K S Au, E Grundberg, E P Bergh, M T Austin, R Patel, B Miller, S Zhu, J S Feinberg, D Lai, K Tsao, S A Fletcher, R Papanna
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThe binary classification of spina bifida lesions as myelomeningocele (with sac) or myeloschisis (without sac) belies a spectrum of morphologies. This study aimed to characterize types of spina bifida lesion and correlate them with preoperative presentation and postoperative outcome.MethodsThis was a secondary analysis of a prospective cohort of patients referred for fetal spina bifida who underwent fetoscopic repair between 2020 and 2023 at a single center. We evaluated lesion morphology on high‐resolution intraoperative images and videos to categorize lesions based on placode exposure and nerve root stretching. The reproducibility of lesion classification was assessed using the Kappa statistic (κ) to measure interrater agreement between a primary and a secondary reviewer, and among two additional reviewers. Preoperative characteristics analyzed included largest ventricular diameter, tonsillar herniation level, movement of the lower extremities and lesion dimensions. Outcomes collected included duration of surgery, need for patch‐based skin closure, gestational age at delivery, occurrence of preterm prelabor rupture of membranes (PPROM), length of stay in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) and neonatal cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) diversion.ResultsOf 60 lesions repaired during the study period, 57 had high‐quality images available and were included in the analysis. We distinguished five types of lesion that differed according to the extent of nerve root stretching and neural placode exposure. The primary and secondary reviewers classified lesions with almost perfect agreement (κ = 0.854). Fetal characteristics that differed between lesion types at preoperative evaluation included lesion volume (P < 0.001), tonsillar herniation (P = 0.005) and head circumference percentile (P = 0.001). Lesion level, prevalence of talipes and movement of the lower extremities did not differ according to lesion type. Regarding surgical and perinatal outcomes, need for patch‐based skin closure (P < 0.001), gestational age at delivery (P = 0.002) and length of stay in the NICU (P = 0.001) differed according to lesion type, whereas the rates of PPROM, CSF leakage at birth and CSF diversion in the NICU did not. On linear regression analysis, the severity of ventriculomegaly was associated with lesion type, but not with tonsillar herniation level.ConclusionsThere is a distinct phenotypic spectrum in open spina bifida. The severity of ventriculomegaly is associated with lesion type, rather than tonsillar herniation level. Our findings have expanded the classification of spina bifida to reveal a spectrum that warrants further study. © 2025 The Author(s). INTRODUCTION

Spina bifida develops when the fetal neural tube fails to close within 4 weeks after conception 1 , 2 . The resulting lesion is typically classified as myelomeningocele or myeloschisis, depending on whether a cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)‐filled sac covers the open neural tube 3 . In myelomeningocele, this sac protects the covered portion of the spinal cord from amniotic fluid exposure but stretches the nerve roots. Both lesion types are characterized by CSF leakage, and exposure of the central nervous system to amniotic fluid results in hydrocephalus, hindbrain herniation (Chiari Type‐II malformation) and long‐term morbidity and mortality 4 . However, these two lesion types belie a spectrum of morphologies, because neural tube coverage and nerve root stretching at the lesion site vary in degree 3 . Clinical features also vary, including the severity of ventriculomegaly.

The pathophysiology of spina bifida has been explained by a two‐hit hypothesis, wherein the initial defect that prevents neural tube closure is followed by chemical or mechanical damage to the exposed placode in utero 5 . Reducing the duration of placode exposure in order to minimize intrauterine damage and reverse hindbrain herniation has provided the rationale for repairing spina bifida prenatally 6 . The Management of Myelomeningocele Study (MOMS) showed that patients who underwent prenatal repair needed ventriculoperitoneal shunting less often and had better spinal cord function compared with those who underwent postnatal repair 7 . Since the MOMS trial, prenatal spina bifida repair via open hysterotomy has become widely accepted, and less invasive fetoscopic repair techniques have been tested with promising results 8 , 9 . Nonetheless, the benefits of in‐utero repair vary: in the MOMS cohort, 58% of those who underwent prenatal repair could not walk independently at 30 months, and 68% needed a shunt 7 .

Understanding the factors that contribute to this variability in benefits after in‐utero spina bifida repair is essential to improving outcomes. Although recent studies have shown that fetal and neonatal motor function vary according to type of lesion 3 , 10 , 11 , a detailed typology of spina bifida lesions, with associated characteristics and outcomes, is lacking.

In‐utero repair allows direct visualization of spina bifida lesion morphology at midgestation. We leveraged this opportunity to identify and characterize lesion types and correlate them with preoperative presentation and postoperative outcome.

METHODS

Study design and population

This was a secondary analysis of intraoperative images and videos from a prospective patient cohort who underwent fetoscopic spina bifida repair between 2020 and 2023. Patients referred to UTHealth Houston Fetal Center, Houston, TX, USA, for suspected fetal spina bifida underwent comprehensive ultrasound evaluation, fetal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and multidisciplinary counseling. Fetoscopic repair was offered to those who met the MOMS inclusion criteria 7 , with the upper limit for maternal body mass index increased from 35 kg/m2 to 45 kg/m2, in addition to amniotic fluid alpha fetoprotein > 2.5 multiples of the median and a positive test for acetylcholinesterase to confirm CSF leakage into the amniotic fluid. Eligible patients were invited to participate in an institutional review board (IRB)‐approved spina bifida registry (HSC‐MS‐15‐0168) or a trial approved by the IRB and the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) of the feasibility of laparotomy‐assisted fetoscopic spina bifida repair using a cryopreserved human umbilical cord allograft as a meningeal patch (NCT04243889). Patients were counseled about the risks, benefits and alternatives, and written informed consent was obtained before surgery. High‐resolution images and videos were collected intraoperatively, as per protocol. Patients without available images were excluded from this analysis.

Preoperative evaluation

Patients underwent ultrasound examinations by certified sonographers utilizing GE Voluson E8 and E10 machines (GE Healthcare Ultrasound, Milwaukee, WI, USA) with RAB 4–8 transducers. With the image centered on the spina bifida lesion and with the fetus filling two‐thirds of the screen, the largest measurement of the lesion was taken in three dimensions: anterior–posterior (posterior vertebral body surface to sac surface), transverse (maximum sac width) and vertical (cephalocaudal length). In lesions with no sac, the transverse and vertical dimensions were measured from the skin defect. The anterior–posterior dimension was recorded as zero if there was no sac displacement. Fetal lower extremity movements were evaluated, with normal movements defined as any movement at the hips, knees or ankles over 30 min. The atria of the lateral ventricles were measured in the transventricular (axial) plane at the level of the parieto‐occipital groove and the glomus of the choroid plexus, demonstrating the frontal horns and cavum septi pellucidi, with the cerebral hemispheres appearing symmetrical, when the ventricles were ≤ 12 mm and the choroid plexus was in a normal position. In the case of ventricles > 12 mm with a distorted choroid plexus, the maximum ventricular diameter was measured 12 . Hindbrain herniation, defined as extension of cerebellar tissue below the plane of the foramen magnum, was evaluated by fetal MRI as per established methods 13 , 14 , 15 .

Fetoscopic spina bifida repair

In‐utero spina bifida repair was performed via a laparotomy‐assisted fetoscopic approach, using a cryopreserved human umbilical cord allograft (HUC‐NEOX Cord 1K®; Tissue Tech Inc., Miami, FL, USA) as a meningeal patch over the spinal placode, followed by skin closure (Appendix S1) 16 . A second human umbilical cord patch was used to close large skin defects whose edges could not be approximated together. Videos and images were captured using high‐resolution Hopkins® 45° Telescope 5 mm (Karl Storz Endoscopy America, Inc., El Segundo, CA, USA). Images were acquired with the lesion centered on the screen, providing a panoramic view of the lesion with the fetal lower back visible, so that the placode and lesion elevation could be appreciated.

After surgery, patients recovered in hospital for 2–3 days. Patients stayed in Houston for management at UTHealth Houston Fetal Center or returned home for delivery. Neonates were managed by standard care.

Outcomes

The duration of surgery and need for patch‐based skin closure were recorded. Perinatal outcomes included gestational age at delivery, preterm prelabor rupture of membranes (PPROM) and CSF diversion in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU), which was performed at the discretion of the pediatric neurosurgeon. For patients who delivered at other centers, perinatal outcomes were collected by review of delivery records and contacting patients by telephone.

Lesion classification

In our experience, we have observed five major subtypes of spina bifida lesion that differ based on the extent of nerve root stretching and neural placode exposure. By reviewing intraoperative images and videos, we distinguished these types as follows (Figure 1).

Type 1: Large sac (obscuring visualization of the lower back), with neural placode displaced from the spinal canal and attached to the highest portion of the protruded thecal sac. The central canal has a small opening with skin and meningeal coverage of the sac. There is minimal neural placode exposure to amniotic fluid. The nerve roots are stretched from the spinal canal.

The primary reviewer (R.P.) classified each lesion retrospectively using these criteria. Subsequently, a second reviewer (L.K.M.) independently classified each lesion to assess reproducibility. To further validate the classification, two additional reviewers (F.V.L., J.E.) were trained on a subset of cases before independently classifying the remaining cases in the study population (Figure S1). All reviewers were blinded to pre‐ and postoperative findings.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed and visualized in R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing Platform, Vienna, Austria), using gtsummary, ggpubr and ggplot2 packages. Descriptive and inferential statistics are reported. We derived the semi‐ellipsoid lesion volume using the equation V = 2/3π × A × B × C, where A, B and C are the three lesion dimensions. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, with a threshold of P < 0.001. For normally distributed data, the ANOVA test was performed, and homogeneity of variances was assessed using Levene's test. If the assumption of homogeneity was met (P > 0.05), Tukey's HSD test was used for post‐hoc pairwise comparisons; if the assumption was violated (P ≤ 0.05), the Games–Howell post‐hoc test was applied. For ordinal or non‐normally distributed data, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used, followed by Dunn's test with Bonferroni correction for post‐hoc comparisons. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher's exact test. To address the increased risk of Type‐I error due to multiple testing, we chose eight clinical variables a priori on the basis of prior literature, clinical relevance and impact on patient counseling: head circumference (HC), HC percentile, lesion volume, largest ventricular diameter and tonsillar herniation at preoperative evaluation, as well as need for patch‐based skin closure, gestational age at delivery and NICU length of stay. These outcomes constitute our primary family of hypothesis tests. We applied a Bonferroni correction (α = 0.05/8 = 0.00625) only to this prespecified set, thus preserving full transparency and ensuring the family‐wise error rate was controlled at 0.05. Interrater agreement for lesion types was assessed using the Kappa statistic (κ). The strength of agreement was interpreted according to the benchmarks of Landis and Koch: ≤ 0, no agreement; 0.01–0.20, none to slight agreement; 0.21–0.40, fair agreement; 0.41–0.60, moderate agreement; 0.61–0.80, substantial agreement; and 0.81–1.00, almost perfect agreement 17 . The association of preoperative ventricular diameter with lesion type, lesion level and tonsillar herniation was evaluated via linear regression using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

RESULTS

Of 60 spina bifida lesions repaired fetoscopically at UTHealth Houston Fetal Center between 2020 and 2023, 57 had high‐quality images available. Forty‐eight patients were enrolled in an FDA feasibility study and nine were enrolled in an observational registry. Four patients who underwent fetoscopic repair had cerebellar tonsils slightly above the foramen magnum on fetal MRI; repair was offered at the discretion of the pediatric neurosurgeon, owing to crowding of the posterior fossa, obliteration of the cisterna magna space and effacement of fluid anterior to the brainstem and the fourth ventricle. Nineteen patients delivered outside UTHealth Houston Fetal Center.

Lesion classification

Lesion Types 1–5 accounted for 14%, 39%, 16%, 14% and 18%, respectively, of lesions in our cohort (Table 1). The primary and secondary reviewers classified lesions with almost perfect agreement (κ = 0.854). They disagreed in three cases: Type 4 vs Type 5; Type 2 vs Type 3; and Type 2 vs Type 5. Upon review of the third instance of disagreement, it was determined that the angle of the image of the lesion had affected the second reviewer's interpretation. The primary reviewer's classification was selected for the final analysis in all cases. After being trained on 12 cases, two additional reviewers classified lesion type in the remaining 45 cases: each additional reviewer demonstrated almost perfect agreement with the primary reviewer (κ = 0.879 and κ = 0.910), and agreement between the additional reviewers was likewise almost perfect (κ = 0.849).

Preoperative characteristics

There were no differences in maternal demographic characteristics, fetal extremity movement, lesion level or prevalence of talipes between lesion types (Table 1). Estimated fetal weight (EFW), as an aggregate measure comprising HC, biparietal diameter, abdominal circumference and femur length, did not differ according to lesion type. When considering the component measures of EFW, HC differed by lesion type (P = 0.029) but did not meet the adjusted threshold for statistical significance. However, HC percentile differed significantly according to lesion type (P = 0.001); fetuses with lesion Types 1, 2 and 3 had a higher HC percentile compared to those with a Type‐5 lesion (Figure 2a). The level of tonsillar herniation also differed significantly across lesion types (P = 0.005).

The volume was greater in Type‐1 lesions compared with Types 3, 4 and 5, and greater in Type‐2 lesions compared with Types 4 and 5. Accordingly, lesion dimensions also differed between types. Type‐2 lesions were larger than Types 3, 4 and 5 in the anterior–posterior dimension and larger than Types 4 and 5 in the transverse dimension. Type‐3 lesions were larger than Type‐5 lesions in the anterior–posterior and transverse dimensions.

The largest ventricular diameter differed according to lesion type (P = 0.012), but did not meet the adjusted threshold for statistical significance. Fetuses with a Type‐2 lesion had larger ventricles compared to fetuses with Type‐1 and Type‐4 lesions (Table 1, Figure 2d). The differences in ventricular diameter across lesion types were largely consistent for right (P = 0.010) and left (P = 0.017) lateral ventricles: fetuses with a Type‐2 lesion had a larger right ventricular diameter compared to those with a Type‐4 lesion and a larger left ventricular diameter compared to those with Type‐1 and Type‐4 lesions (Figure 4a,c). Factors associated with ventricular diameter

We evaluated the association of preoperative ventricular diameter with lesion type, lesion level and tonsillar herniation via linear regression. Conditioning on lesion level and tonsillar herniation (foramen magnum or below, mild; C1–C2, moderate; C3–C4, severe), the largest ventricular diameter was associated with lesion type (Table 2). Specifically, the largest ventricular diameter was greater in fetuses with a Type‐2 lesion compared to those with a Type‐5 lesion (P = 0.008). Fetuses with lower lesion levels had smaller ventricles, but this did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.071).

Surgical and neonatal outcomes

All 10 Type‐5 lesions required patch‐based skin closure, which was a significantly higher rate compared with all other lesion types (P < 0.001) (Table 3). Duration of surgery and gestational age at surgery did not differ between groups, nor did the rates of PPROM, CSF leakage at birth, wound revision or CSF diversion in the NICU.

Cortical index from baseline to delivery

HC percentile differed significantly between lesion types at baseline (P = 0.001), but not at delivery (P = 0.117) (Figure 2). By contrast, the largest ventricular diameter at last ultrasound (within 4 weeks before delivery; n = 49) differed between lesion types (P = 0.028): fetuses with a Type‐2 lesion had larger ventricles compared to those with a Type‐1 lesion. Notably, HC percentile increased from baseline to delivery among fetuses with a Type‐5 lesion, from the 2nd to the 75th percentile (P = 0.002). HC percentile also showed an upward trend across lesion Types 2, 3 and 4. Ventricular diameter increased between the two timepoints in fetuses with Type‐2 (P < 0.001), Type‐3 (P = 0.004) and Type‐5 (P = 0.006) lesions.

To determine the extent to which the increase in HC from baseline to delivery reflects cortical development vs ventricle growth, we calculated the cortical index by dividing HC (mm) by ventricular diameter (mm) 18 . Cortical index at baseline differed between lesion types (P = 0.011). Fetuses with a Type‐2 lesion had a lower cortical index compared to those with Type‐1 and Type‐4 lesions. Cortical index before delivery did not differ according to lesion type (P = 0.117). Cortical index increased from baseline to delivery in fetuses with a Type‐5 lesion (P = 0.020), but not in those with other lesion types (Figure 2).

DISCUSSION

Spina bifida is conventionally classified as myelomeningocele or myeloschisis depending on whether a sac covers the open neural tube 10 . However, our observations of spina bifida cases at a high‐volume center suggest that there is a range of lesion morphologies inconsistent with a binary classification. Clinical features, fetal anatomy and postnatal outcome also vary. To understand this variability requires a more complex classification of types of spina bifida lesion and their association with preoperative presentation and postoperative outcome. We evaluated intraoperative images and videos and distinguished five lesion types based on nerve root stretching and neural placode exposure; however, these characteristics do not differ linearly across types of lesion. Baseline fetal characteristics and operative and perinatal outcomes differed between some lesion types. These findings suggest a spectrum of spina bifida that warrants further characterization.

Linear regression analysis showed that ventriculomegaly was associated more strongly with Type‐2 lesions compared with other lesion types, lesion level or tonsillar level. Whether these findings are attributable to anatomical differences (cases with Type‐2 lesions had the largest ventricular diameter), the proportion of Type‐2 lesions in our cohort (39%) or other factors requires further exploration. Notably, largest ventricular diameter was not associated with tonsillar herniation. This differs from the common understanding that hindbrain herniation obstructing CSF flow through the foramina of Luschka and Magendie in the fourth ventricle contributes to ventriculomegaly 19 . These findings should be confirmed by future studies in larger cohorts with subgroup analysis of the relationship between ventricular diameter, lesion level and hindbrain herniation according to lesion type.

Our analysis revealed substantial longitudinal gains in HC percentile among fetuses with a Type‐5 lesion. Considering that these fetuses exhibited an increase in cortical index, reflecting faster growth of HC compared with that of the ventricles, this increase in HC percentile may indicate cortical mass development after in‐utero repair. Danzer et al. 18 reported similar increases in cortical index after in‐utero repair in 50 fetuses. In our cohort, fetuses with lesion Types 2, 3 and 4 also showed increases in HC percentile from baseline to delivery, but these differences were not statistically significant, nor did cortical index change significantly in those groups. Fetuses with Type‐2, ‐3 and ‐4 lesions may have cerebral aqueduct narrowing that does not improve with in‐utero repair; alternatively, the small sample size may explain these findings.

Other studies have reported differences in motor function between different types of lesion. In a review of 404 patients, Oliver et al. 3 associated myelomeningocele lesions, whether repaired pre‐ or postnatally, with fetal talipes and impaired movement of the lower extremities. Corroenne et al. 10 , 11 reported that fetuses with myeloschisis were 3.1 times more likely to have intact motor function prenatally and 11.2 times more likely to have intact motor function at birth compared to fetuses with myelomeningocele. In the MOMS cohort, absence of a sac covering the lesion was associated with independent ambulation at 30 months in the prenatal surgery group 20 . The extent to which differences in presentation and outcomes across lesion types reflect anatomical variation, response to therapy or other factors requires further investigation in larger cohorts.

Gestational age at delivery and length of stay in the NICU differed between patients with different types of lesion. These outcomes may be correlated, as neonates delivered earlier (Types‐1 and ‐4 lesions) stayed longer in the NICU. It is unclear why cases with a Type‐1 lesion delivered earlier compared with other lesion types. Pregnancies with a Type‐1 lesion had the highest rate of PPROM (63%), but this was not statistically significant. Notably, rates of spontaneous vs indicated delivery did not differ by lesion type.

To account for the potential inflation of the Type‐I error rate due to multiple comparisons, we applied a Bonferroni correction to a subset of eight clinical variables deemed most relevant based on prior literature, clinical experience and their potential impact on patient counseling and management. This targeted approach preserves statistical rigor while emphasizing clinically meaningful associations. Under the corrected threshold, 6/8 outcomes remained statistically significant, but HC (mm) and largest ventricular diameter did not. Accordingly, these and other findings should be treated as exploratory and interpreted with caution.

With further validation of our findings in external centers, this spina bifida lesion typology may aid patient counseling. The lesion dimensions and presence or absence of a sac, as determined on preoperative ultrasound, may provide useful information to help patients understand the spectrum of possible outcomes after surgery. This counseling can then be refined after the lesion has been visualized directly during prenatal repair.

Our demonstration of a broader spectrum of spina bifida lesion types than that observed previously may have implications for molecular studies of the genetic and epigenetic mechanisms that contribute to lesion development. Spina bifida is believed to be highly heritable, but its complex etiology, rarity in the population and phenotypic heterogeneity make variant detection and gene/pathway discovery challenging. Studies have identified deleterious genomic variants that may increase the risk for developing spina bifida 21 , 22 . Others have demonstrated the importance of studying the genomic and transcriptomic profiles of spina bifida lesions 23 , 24 , 25 . Nonetheless, further studies are needed to explore the molecular basis for variations in spina bifida lesion formation and their clinical implications.

A major strength of our study is the use of data collected prospectively under a FDA clinical trial protocol. All surgeries were performed at a single center, ensuring procedural consistency. Patients were recruited under broad inclusion criteria comparable to those of the MOMS trial. We confirmed the interrater reliability of our classification among independent reviewers. The study was, however, limited by a small sample size that was not adequately powered to detect differences that were not significant, such as movement of the lower extremities. The lesion characteristics of fetuses not offered prenatal surgery may differ from those analyzed herein. Our fetoscopic repair technique differs from that of other centers, so outcomes after repair may differ; this is under investigation in a clinical trial. Differences in neonatal management across centers may have affected outcomes for patients who delivered outside UTHealth Houston Fetal Center.

In conclusion, direct visualization of spina bifida lesions during fetoscopic repair surgery distinguished five types of lesion that differ based on nerve root stretching and neural placode exposure to amniotic fluid. These types may have implications for baseline function and prognosis. Further research is needed to explore the molecular basis for variations in spina bifida formation and response to in‐utero therapy.
