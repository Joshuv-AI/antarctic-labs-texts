Evaluation of a novel eating disorder prevention programme (Australian diabetes body project) for young women with type 1 diabetes in a clinic setting: A randomized controlled trial protocol
Authors: Neisha D'Silva, Kerri M Gillespie, Christel Hendrieckx, Line Wisting, Tatjana Ewais, Liza Phillips, Simon Denny, Selena Bartlett, Melanie White, Hannah Mackay, Margaret Vitanza, Isabella Magee, Alison Griffin, Lee Jones, Eric Stice
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCo‐occurrence of eating disorders (ED) and type 1 diabetes (T1D) can lead to deterioration in glycaemic control with reduced quality of life, microvascular complications, diabetic ketoacidosis and premature death. The current trial aims to test the effectiveness of an Australian Diabetes Body Project (ADBP) programme, a cognitive dissonance‐based group intervention, on ED risk factors and symptoms, psychometric analysis, quality of life and glycaemia control in young women with T1D.MethodsMixed methods, prospective randomized controlled trial (RCT) using an online, six‐week group intervention facilitated by a clinician and a peer with T1D. A sample of 80 young women with T1D aged 15 to 30 years will be randomized to the ADBP or active control (educational videos) condition. Participants will complete assessments of body dissatisfaction, eating behaviours, quality of life and diabetes distress, at baseline, post‐intervention and at three‐month follow‐up. Medical record data will be extracted to determine changes in glycaemia outcomes. ADBP participants will be invited to provide feedback on the benefits and acceptability of the intervention.DiscussionThe ADBP will be the first clinic‐based RCT to examine the effectiveness of a virtual, cognitive dissonance‐based intervention for reducing ED risk factors and symptoms in young women with T1D. The results of this project will have immediate implications for clinical practice in Australia and globally, given the increasing prevalence of ED in this high‐risk group and limited effective prevention programmes available.Trial RegistrationThe trial is registered with the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry (ANZCTR) (Trial ID: ACTRN12624000555550); accessible at (https://anzctr.org.au/Trial/Registration/TrialReview.aspx?ACTRN=12624000555550).Keywords: adolescent psychiatry, body image, cognitive dissonance, diabetes mellitus, eating disorders, thinness, type 1 diabetes

INTRODUCTION

Type 1 diabetes (T1D) is a chronic, metabolic condition. During adolescence and young adulthood, optimal self‐management is challenging due to significant bio‐psychosocial changes as well as increased rates of mental health conditions such as depression, anxiety and eating disorders (ED). 1 , 2 Adolescents and young adults with T1D are more likely to engage in disordered eating (DE), a significant risk factor for the development of ED, than their peers without T1D, with prevalence as high as 40%.2, 3 Additionally, young women with T1D are 2.4 times more likely to meet criteria for an ED diagnosis. 4 This is partially due to the food‐centric nature of diabetes self management.3 DE/ED onset typically occurs in pre‐adolescence; soon after diagnosis of diabetes or in early puberty.5 A longitudinal study identified that of the 22% of female adolescents with T1D who reported DE at baseline and/or at 1‐year follow‐up, 92% continued to report DE at the 5‐year follow‐up, highlighting its persistent nature. 5

DE/ED in T1D can lead to deterioration in glycaemia control, impaired quality of life and increased risk of diabetes‐related complications including diabetic ketoacidosis, retinopathy and premature death. 6 Insulin omission or restriction is a unique compensatory behaviour used for weight loss in those with T1D. This is partially because insulin deficiency results in a catabolic state inducing lipolysis, glycogenolysis and proteolysis. 5 Evidence suggests a synergetic interaction in 10‐year mortality rates: 35% in women with both Anorexia Nervosa (AN) and T1D compared with 6.5% with AN alone and 2.5% with T1D alone. 7 Prospective studies have identified risk factors that predict the onset of ED, such as drive for thinness, pursuit of the thin appearance ideal, body dissatisfaction, dieting, negative affect and female sex. 8 , 9 , 10 Young people with T1D who report body dissatisfaction were more likely to omit insulin. 11 The Australian Diabetes MILES Youth Study found that, amongst 477 participants with T1D, severe diabetes distress and body dissatisfaction were the strongest correlates of DE. 12

The Body Project is a group‐oriented, co‐led (clinician and peer) ED prevention programme based on cognitive dissonance theory that can be delivered in‐person or virtually. 13 In this programme, individuals with body image concerns collectively critique the thin appearance ideal, which generates cognitive dissonance, prompting them to reduce pursuit of the ideal. The Body Project has produced greater reductions in ED risk factors (thin‐ideal internalization, body dissatisfaction, dietary restriction), ED symptoms, and future ED onset over a 2‐ to 4‐year follow‐up, than assessment‐only control conditions and alternative interventions. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 The Body Project, when implemented virtually, produced larger pre‐to‐post effects (mean Cohen's d = 0.86) 18 than when implemented in‐person (mean d = 0.53). 16 A meta‐analytic review revealed that the Body Project is the only intervention aimed at reducing the risk of ED that has led to a reduction in future onset of ED in multiple trials, with significantly stronger effects when groups were implemented or co‐implemented by peer educators versus when implemented by clinicians. 18

The Body Project has recently been adapted for young women with T1D (age 16–35) and renamed the Diabetes Body Project. A successful feasibility study of a novel 6‐h virtually delivered Diabetes Body Project was conducted in Norway. 13 Immediately after completion of the intervention, participants showed significant reductions in pursuit of the thin ideal (d = 0.81), body dissatisfaction (d = 0.67), dietary restraint (d = 0.63), diabetes‐specific eating psychopathology (d = 0.83) and diabetes distress (d = 0.48). Qualitative feedback sought from participants led to refinements of the Diabetes Body Project. An ensuing controlled pilot study in the US revealed similar results. 19 A recent systematic review found that cognitive dissonance‐based programmes were the most effective among interventions for preventing ED in people with T1D. 20

A clinical audit of young adults with T1D attending the Mater Hospital Diabetes Clinic found that 36% had disturbed eating behaviours. 1 A further publication reveals, 53% attending the Mater Young Adult Diabetes Clinic are female, and 41% of these female report ‘low comfort with body shape’ on the annual psychosocial questionnaire. 21 A higher prevalence for discomfort with weight and eating patterns was observed in females compared with males. 20 Given the promising pilot data for the Diabetes Body Project, we propose to conduct a fully powered randomized controlled trial (RCT) of this ED prevention programme to reduce ED risk factors and symptoms in young women with T1D who are at higher risk for EDs, named the Australian Diabetes Body Project (ADBP).

OBJECTIVES

The overall objective of this trial is to test the effectiveness of the ADBP programme in young women with T1D. More specifically we aim to determine whether, compared to an active control group (educational videos), participants randomized to the ADBP programme:

Have lower post‐programme ED risk factors and ED symptoms.

Have lower post‐programme ED risk factors and ED symptoms.

Have lower post‐programme diabetes distress, quality of life and symptoms of depression/anxiety.

Have lower post‐programme diabetes distress, quality of life and symptoms of depression/anxiety.

Have better post‐programme glycaemic outcomes: HbA1c [mmol/mol and %]; Continuous Glucose Monitor (CGM) data [%Time in Range, %hyperglycaemia] from participants who use CGM devices as part of their diabetes management plan.

Have better post‐programme glycaemic outcomes: HbA1c [mmol/mol and %]; Continuous Glucose Monitor (CGM) data [%Time in Range, %hyperglycaemia] from participants who use CGM devices as part of their diabetes management plan.

METHODS

Trial design and setting

The trial will be reported in accordance with the SPIRIT guidelines for clinical trial protocols. The trial will be a single‐site, pre–post‐follow‐up, randomized controlled trial conducted in a clinic within a tertiary‐referral Diabetes Service at a large metropolitan hospital in Queensland, Australia. The diabetes service operates with a full multidisciplinary team including endocrinologists, endocrine trainees, credentialled Diabetes Nurse Educators (DNEs), dietitians and a young adult support unit consisting of psychologists and a psychiatrist. Social work support is available through internal hospital referral.

The trial design and materials will be reviewed by Australian women with lived experience of T1D via Diabetes Australia and Statewide Diabetes Clinical Network (Queensland), as well as diabetes clinicians and researchers from the multidisciplinary team. This process ensures that all materials are culturally appropriate and adapted to the local context. This review will include promotion materials, consent forms, questionnaires and ADBP intervention. All materials will comply with Diabetes Australia's Position Statement ‘Our Language Matters’. 22

Participants

The study will recruit N = 80 young women aged 15 to 30 years with T1D. All participants will continue to receive usual care regardless of study arm. Eligibility criteria will include young women who respond, ‘yes’ to the question: ‘do you have any body image concerns?’ Participants must also be proficient in English, able to provide informed consent, and have access to a computer capable of hosting the virtual conference software used for the intervention. We will exclude individuals who are medically unstable or have a diagnosed ED that is clinically unstable (required hospital admission in the preceding 12 months according to medical records or self report, or who have a Body Mass Index (BMI) of less than 14 kg/m2).

Recruitment and consent

Young women with T1D and parents of minors (<18 years of age) will be provided with the trial information sheets at their routine clinic appointments. An additional document explaining the trial in simple language will also be provided to eligible participants under 18 years of age. Posters and fliers, which include QR codes for registering interest and research team contact details, will be placed in the clinic waiting areas. Interested women will register their interest via a QR code or can contact researchers directly via email or phone to request further information and to ask questions. The Research Assistant will then email eligible women with a link to online information and consent form.

The Australian diabetes body project

The ADBP will involve weekly, one‐hour virtual sessions over 6 consecutive weeks. Eight participants will be invited to each ADBP group. Between sessions, participants will complete home exercises to be discussed at the next session. They will receive a reminder call the day before. Those unable to attend will be offered a 15‐min make‐up session. The topics for the 6 sessions are as follows:

1. Define the thin ideal and explore its origin.

2. Examine the costs of pursuing this ideal.

3. Explore ways to resist pressures to be thin.

4. Discuss how to challenge our personal body image concerns (including negative social comparison and social media).

5. Learn new ways to talk more positively about our bodies.

6. Discuss how to promote a positive relationship with diabetes and insulin.As part of the final session, intervention participants and facilitators will join separate focus groups to provide qualitative feedback.

The active control group will be asked to watch educational videos. These videos are also divided into six 1‐h blocks to match the intervention group and will cover topics of ED and diabetes education. Links to these videos can be found in Table S1. Facilitators

The ADBP groups will be co‐led by two facilitators. A clinician and a peer with T1D will co‐facilitate groups as this has shown significantly larger ED prevention effects. 16 , 17 , 18 The peer facilitator will be an individual with lived experience of T1D within the age category of the inclusion criteria and a previous history of body image concerns. The clinician could be a psychologist, nurse or dietitian with experience in T1D and the ability to support the management of complex or distressing discussions. All facilitators will receive standardized training of the programme, delivered by co‐author Prof. Eric Stice, and ongoing supervision and support from the Research Team/Principal Investigator.

Participant safety and distress management

Safety is supported through multiple mechanisms. All participants and facilitators receive direct contact details for the research team, including the Principal Investigator and Research Assistant. The trial is embedded within a tertiary Diabetes Service with psychology and psychiatry support, enabling timely assessment, referral and follow‐up within routine care if distress occurs. Facilitators conduct regular check‐ins during sessions to monitor well‐being and provide opportunities for participants to raise concerns or request additional support. Information on accessing care and the option to withdraw at any time is outlined in the participant information sheets provided at consent.

Fidelity testing

Sessions from the first group will be recorded for fidelity and competence ratings by a member of the research team. Key elements of each session will be rated for degree of fidelity (100‐point scale from 0 = No adherence, the section was skipped, to 100 = Full adherence). Facilitator competence will be rated with 12 items (e.g. leader's express ideas clearly) using a 100‐point scale with anchors for each item. Recordings will be deleted for confidentiality and privacy once fidelity testing is complete.

Sample size

Based on a pilot study in a similar population, 19 we expect moderate to large effect sizes for the primary outcome. Group sample sizes of 30 for the DBP and 30 for the education control groups achieve 80% power to detect a moderate effect size between groups (Cohen's d = 0.6) in a design with 3 repeated measures, assuming a compound symmetry covariance structure, within subject correlation of 0.50 and an alpha of 0.05. We aim to recruit 40 young women per group to allow for a 20% dropout rate.

Randomization and blinding

Once participants have been identified, screened and completed the baseline assessment, they will be randomly assigned 1:1 to the virtual ADBP or the active control group via a random number sequence provided by an independent investigator of the research team. Random permuted block randomization with variable block sizes (2 to 6) will be used. Allocation will be concealed until assignment into groups occurs using sealed envelopes. Consenting participants will be assigned a unique identification number on entry into the trial. It will not be possible to blind the ADBP facilitators, Research Assistant in charge of scheduling and disseminating session and education links to participants and the investigator responsible for the fidelity testing. All other research team members will remain blinded to participant allocation. The statistician will not be involved in the randomization process and will be blinded to participant allocation (intervention/active control) by using generic group labels (Group A, Group B).

Compensation

In order to promote participant retention, participants will be provided a $100 voucher upon completion of the intervention and all pre‐ and post‐intervention surveys and focus groups (timepoints 1 and 2). Participants who complete the three‐month follow‐up surveys will be provided an additional $100 voucher (timepoint 3).

Measures

Participants will complete outcome measures at pre‐test, immediate post‐test and at three‐month follow‐up. The primary outcome will be body dissatisfaction at three‐month follow‐up, measured using the Body Dissatisfaction Scale (BDS). Changes in this measure immediately post‐intervention will also be examined. Secondary outcomes will include thin body ideal, ED risks and symptoms, diabetes distress, symptoms of depression/anxiety, quality of life and glycaemia outcomes using the person‐reported outcome measures outlined below.

Body dissatisfaction scale (BDS)

Assesses satisfaction with body parts commonly of concern to women (e.g. stomach, thighs, hips) using 9 items from the Satisfaction and Dissatisfaction with Body Parts Scale. Responses range from 1 (extremely dissatisfied) to 5 (extremely satisfied). Demonstrates strong 3‐week test–retest reliability (r = 0.90) and predictive validity for bulimic symptom onset. 23

The ideal‐body stereotype scale‐revised (IBSS‐R)

A 8‐item measure of appearance ideal internalization, scored on a 5‐point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). Shows good test–retest reliability (r = 0.84). 23

The eating disorder examination – Questionnaire (EDE‐Q)

A 28‐item self report tool derived from the EDE interview, measuring body image and eating pathology on a 5‐point Likert scale (1 = extremely dissatisfied to 6 = extremely satisfied). Demonstrates test–retest reliability (r = 0.66–0.94) and a positive predictive value of 0.56. 24

Diabetes eating problem survey‐revised (DEPS‐R)

A 16‐item self report scale assessing disordered eating behaviours (e.g. insulin omission, vomiting) on a 6‐point Likert scale (0 = never to 5 = always). Scores ≥20 indicate disturbed eating. Exhibits excellent test–retest reliability (ICC = 0.95) and good construct validity (r = 0.52–0.68) with EDE‐Q. 25 , 26

Problem areas in diabetes (PAID)

A 20‐item measure of diabetes‐related psychosocial distress, scored 0 (not a problem) to 4 (serious problem). Total scores are converted to a 100‐point scale. Internal consistency is high (α = 0.95). 27

The patient health Questionnaire‐4 (PHQ‐4)

A 4‐item tool assessing anxiety and depression over the past two weeks using a 4‐point Likert scale (0 = not at all to 3 = nearly every day). Scores classify symptom severity (normal: 0–2; mild: 3–5; moderate: 6–8; severe: 9–12). Demonstrates strong test–retest reliability (ICC = 0.96) and construct validity. 28 , 29

World Health Organization well‐being index (WHO‐5)

A 6‐item scale measuring general well‐being (0 = at no time to 5 = all the time). Raw scores (0–25) are multiplied by 4 for a 0–100 scale. Shows good convergent (r = 0.55–0.69) and discriminant validity. 30

DAWN2 impact on diabetes profile (DAWN‐DIDP)

Assesses diabetes impact on seven life domains using a 7‐point scale (1 = very positive, 4 = no impact, 7 = very negative), with a ‘not applicable’ option. Demonstrates satisfactory convergent and discriminant validity. 31

Glycaemia outcomes

Includes time‐in‐range (percentage of time that glucose values are within the target range), %hyperglycaemia, and HbA1c (mmol/mol and %) collected from medical records.

Statistical methods

Participant characteristics for each group will be described at baseline using frequency (percent), mean (standard deviation) or median (interquartile range) as appropriate. The effects of group (ADBP or educational control) will be estimated for primary and secondary outcomes using mixed effects regression with restricted maximum likelihood estimation (REML). Each outcome will be regressed on intervention group (ADBP or educational control), assessment time (<7 days after programme completion or 3‐months follow‐up), and an interaction between treatment group and assessment time. Participants will be treated as a random effect. The primary analysis will be carried out according to the intention‐to‐treat principle, where all participants are analysed in the group to which they were randomized, regardless of whether they received all or any of the treatments to which they were assigned. Per‐protocol data will also be analysed and presented. Missing data will be examined for patterns using chi‐square and t‐tests. Missing data will be accounted for in longitudinal models using full information maximum likelihood estimation, allowing the inclusion of all available data by estimating model parameters directly from the observed data without the need to impute missing values.

DISCUSSION

The proposed ADBP includes several innovative elements. This trial would be the first to examine the effectiveness of ADBP for preventing ED risk factors and symptoms in young women with T1D compared to a credible active control condition in a sufficiently powered RCT in Australia. It would also be the first project to investigate the effects of the ADBP when implemented in a diabetes clinic‐based setting. The proposed project would be novel as a virtually‐based group intervention model of care, being co‐implemented by a trained clinician and peer with T1D. Virtual implementation represents a novel and promising service provision method to improve access, maximize uptake and reduce cost and participant burden.

Thoughts and behaviours associated with ED are associated with suboptimal glycaemia often through omission of insulin. 32 Consequences of the dual diagnosis of diabetes and ED are serious with increased risk of acute complications such as diabetic ketoacidosis and longer term diabetic microvascular complications (nephropathy, neuropathy and retinopathy) resulting in an associated increase in the burden of illness to the individual, family and hospital system. Conversely, early detection and management of DE may prevent escalation to diagnosable ED, medical consequences requiring hospitalization and reduced mortality, which would assist hospital systems and the community at large.

There is a clear unmet need within the clinical population attending our Diabetes Clinic for an intervention of this kind. The ADBP offers an innovative, scalable solution—delivered virtually and facilitated by peers—that addresses this gap. If proven effective, this trial will have immediate and significant implications for clinical practice, both within our current service and across Young Adult Diabetes Services nationally. By targeting a rapidly growing problem for which no effective prevention programme currently exists, the project will fill critical knowledge gaps in the T1D and ED field and contribute to the broad prevention of future eating disorder onset among high‐risk women with T1D. Study findings (both quantitative and qualitative) will be disseminated through peer‐reviewed publications and national Diabetes and Eating Disorder conference presentations, with translation into practice via existing diabetes service networks and organizations in Australia (State‐based Diabetes Clinical Networks and Diabetes Australia). The virtual delivery model enables implementation from tertiary diabetes services while extending reach to regional, rural and remote settings. Future research will evaluate the intervention in a multi‐centre trial across diverse clinical contexts and expand eligibility to include younger age groups and males, with potential for parent or caregiver involvement to support broader implementation and scalability. Ultimately, this project has the potential to transform national clinical practice and set a new benchmark for preventive care in type 1 diabetes.
