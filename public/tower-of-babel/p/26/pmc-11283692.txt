Assessment of a combined mouthwash on pain relief in pericoronitis: a randomized clinical study
Authors: Neman Arekhi, Nazanin Mortazavi, Emad Bahramnejad, Vahid Khouri, Mahboubeh Tajaldini, Negar Asgari, Ahmad Sohrabi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPericoronitis, an inflammation near wisdom teeth, often occurs when they are partially emerged, especially in the lower jaw. Commonly, the gingiva partially envelops the tooth. Treatments vary from gingival surgery to extraction. Introduction

Pericoronitis refers to the swelling and infection of the gingival tissue surrounding a tooth that has not fully emerged, most frequently observed in the lower jaw’s third molars [1]. Often associated with partially visible or submerged wisdom teeth, this condition predominantly affects young adults, though it can occur in any age group, with a high susceptibility among individuals aged 16 to 30, peaking between 21 and 25 [2]. The incidence rate of pericoronitis is approximately 4.92%, with 95% of cases related to the lower third molar. Multiple studies have indicated that the gender distribution of pericoronitis is insignificant, although there is a slight female predominance [3, 4]. Pericoronitis arises from the buildup of food particles beneath the operculum, fostering the growth of a diverse ecological niche that predominantly harbors anaerobic bacteria, leading to a complex polymicrobial environment [5]. Research indicates a significant presence of facultative anaerobes like Streptococcus milleri, Stomatococcus mucilaginosus, and Rothia dentocariosa, alongside strict anaerobes such as Actinomyces and Prevotella species [6, 7]. Pericoronitis is diagnosed through a clinical evaluation and differential diagnosis. Clinically, pericoronitis appears in two forms: acute and chronic. The acute type is characterized by severe pain, swelling of the gingival flap (operculum), limited jaw opening (trismus), enlarged regional lymph nodes, difficulty swallowing, bad breath (halitosis), fever, and pus discharge. On the other hand, the chronic variant involves mild, intermittent pain and periods without symptoms that can span several months [8]. In treating this condition, early diagnosis is essential for successful management. Dental professionals assess the inflamed gingival tissue around wisdom teeth that have not yet fully emerged. However, there is a prevalent misconception that pericoronitis is equivalent to having impacted third molars, which can result in misdiagnoses and improper treatments, such as unwarranted tooth extractions [9]. Treatment options include localized interventions such as cleaning the affected area, alleviating pain, and using antiseptic mouthwashes, along with antibiotic therapy when necessary. Dentists must carefully balance the need for symptom relief with the potential for developing antibiotic resistance [1, 10]. Amoxicillin or metronidazole are frequently prescribed antibiotics. In more severe cases, the dosage or frequency may be adjusted [11].

Despite the prevalence of pericoronitis-related infections and various effective pain relief methods, there is a lack of high-level clinical studies, such as randomized controlled trials, establishing a standard of care for treating acute pericoronitis. This gap in research may hinder effective treatment and lead to avoidable complications [12]. Health care professionals are concerned about complications from infections linked to dormant third molars in Asian populations, particularly the potential for infection to spread into the neck and chest’s fascial spaces, resulting in conditions like trismus, compromised airways, damage to the mandibular nerve, and severe illnesses including angina and Ludwig’s angina, which can be life-threatening [13]. Pericoronitis extends beyond oral discomfort, impacting work efficiency and overall well-being. Its incidence correlates with the oral microbiome and reflects broader health implications tied to socio-economic status [14, 15]. Regrettably, the segments of the population most impacted by oral health infections are often those experiencing the deepest poverty and possessing the least education, which in turn leads to restricted access to healthcare services [16]. Research has shown a significant correlation between mandibular fractures following third molar extractions and a history of pericoronitis, as well as pathological alterations in the bone structure of males over thirty-five. Despite the rarity of complications following extraction (occurring in less than 0.005% of cases), it highlights the serious consequences of pericoronitis and emphasizes the need for identifying treatment approaches that are both cost-effective and efficacious [17, 18]. Recent research has focused on evaluating the effectiveness of various treatment strategies for acute pericoronitis. One such study aimed to assess the clinical efficacy of a mouthwash formula containing Chlorhexidine, Benzydamine, Nanosilver, as well as the antibiotics Amoxicillin and Metronidazole in managing pain and trismus in patients with acute pericoronitis. The goal was to establish a standard of care that is both effective and affordable, reducing the risk of complications and improving patient outcomes.

The null hypothesis of the study was that, there is no significant difference in pain reduction and improvement of maximum mouth opening (MMO) between patients using the combined mouthwash and those using the chlorhexidine mouthwash alone.

Materials and methods

Trial design

This randomized clinical trial was conducted at the Dental Faculty of Gorgan, with participants assigned randomly. The study spanned from April 2022 to January 2024.

The trial received ethical approval from the Scientific Research Ethics Committee of Golestan University of Medical Sciences, which was identified by the identifier IR.GOUMS.REC.1401.016. Additionally, this study had an IRCT code IRCT20230104057046N1. Written informed consent was carefully obtained from all participants. This randomized controlled clinical study was conducted in accordance with the CONSORT guidelines for reporting clinical trials, ensuring transparency and comprehensive evaluation of the methodology and results.

Participants and sample size

We evaluated the efficacy of a novel mouthwash formulation in 48 patients with pericoronitis, maintaining gender balance (27 women and 21 men) and an average age of 21.56 years. Participants were carefully selected based on predefined inclusion criteria to ensure a representative sample size. Our study aimed to robustly evaluate the therapeutic potential of the mouthwash in treating this common dental condition. To calculate the sample size using the independent t-test approach in the G*Power software, we considered an alpha of 5%, a beta error of 10%, and an effect size of 0.95. With the mean of the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) for the control and case groups being 27 and 23.10, respectively, it was determined that 27 samples were needed in each group [19].

Randomization and blinding

Participants were stratified into two groups—case and control—through random assignment, with neither group aware of the mouthwash variant they received. Participants were assigned to their respective groups through a process using random numbers, ensuring an impartial sample for the clinical study. To eliminate any bias during the treatment and evaluation phases, neither the participants nor the researchers knew the group assignments.

Interventions

The control group utilized a mouthwash solely containing 0.12% chlorhexidine, while the case group used a compounded mouthwash enriched with Chlorhexidine, Benzidamine, Nanosilver, and the antibiotics Amoxicillin and Metronidazole. Each participant was instructed to administer the mouthwash bi-daily over a span of seven days, receiving a 250 ml container for this purpose.

Outcomes

Pain assessment was conducted using a 0-100 Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), with daily recordings initiated a week prior to the intervention. Additionally, participants were permitted to consume 500 mg acetaminophen for pain management, with the stipulation of documenting each intake. MMO was measured by an impartial dentist at two junctures: baseline and post-treatment (day 7). An MMO measurement below 40 mm was indicative of trismus.

Inclusion criteria for the trial were restricted to patients presenting with acute pericoronitis. Exclusion criteria included individuals with a high fever (> 38 °C), facial swelling, chronic systemic illnesses (such as diabetes or kidney disease), recent antibiotic therapy (within the past three months), chronic pericoronitis, smoking or alcohol use, allergies to amoxicillin or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and those who were pregnant or lactating. Additionally, patients with allergies to any components of the mouthwash, dental pain (as it could bias the VAS assessment), and those unable to comply with the study protocol were excluded.

In the conducted research, a novel mouthwash formulation was developed. The formulation entailed the incorporation of Nanosilver at a concentration of 0.25 µg/mL, along with 500 mg each of Metronidazole and amoxicillin, into a base mouthwash solution containing Benzidamine and 0.12% Chlorhexidine. This concoction was thoroughly mixed until fully dissolved. For comparative purposes, the control group utilized a standard mouthwash comprised solely of 0.12% Chlorhexidine, devoid of any supplementary constituents.

Statistical analysis

The acquired dataset was processed using SPSS software (version 20). Initial descriptive analysis encompassed the computation of mean, standard deviation, frequency, percentage, as well as the minimum and maximum values to characterize the dataset. Subsequent inferential analysis involved repeated measures ANOVA and analysis of covariance to examine within-subjects effects and covariate interactions, respectively. The Shapiro-Wilk test was employed to assess the normality of the data distribution. In instances where parametric assumptions were not met, non-parametric analysis was conducted utilizing the Generalized Estimating Equation (GEE). Significance testing for paired comparisons was refined using Bonferroni correction. The threshold for statistical significance was established at a p-value of 0.05.

Examining pain levels

In order to test the hypothesis regarding the average pain levels on different days and between two groups, a repeated measures test was employed. The results indicate that the null hypothesis of variance-covariance matrix uniformity was rejected (p < 0.001) based on the Mauchly’s Sphericity Test to further investigate, the Greenhouse-Geisser non-uniformity test was conducted, revealing an F-value of 77.39 (p < 0.001). Consequently, the assumption of equal means across different days was rejected. Additionally, the between-group test yielded an F-value of 2.74 (p = 0.105), suggesting no significant difference in the mean of this index between the two groups (Fig. 1).

Discussion

Pericoronitis, frequently encountered in dental practices due to inflammation and discomfort around wisdom teeth that have not fully erupted, presents significant treatment challenges. Addressing this condition typically involves a comprehensive strategy aimed at symptom relief and improving the patient’s quality of life [14]. Conventional therapies, such as systemic antibiotics and anti-inflammatory medications, are generally effective but may lead to unwanted systemic reactions and the development of antibiotic resistance. This research suggests that a mouthwash blend, which includes Chlorhexidine, Benzydamine, Nanosilver, and the antibiotics Amoxicillin and Metronidazole, could outperform traditional treatments in reducing pain levels and increasing the range of mouth opening among pericoronitis patients [20, 21]. The goal of this study was to provide solid evidence supporting the combined mouthwash as a viable and potentially more effective treatment option for pericoronitis, thus contributing to the advancement of dental therapeutics and patient care.

Upon application of the mouthwash, participants exhibited marked enhancements in both pain severity and the degree of mouth opening across the study. The variation in pain levels over consecutive days underscored the mouthwash’s efficacy in mitigating pericoronitis-related discomfort. This aligns with the findings of Shahakbari et al. [19], who demonstrated the pain-alleviating properties of green tea mouthwash in acute pericoronitis cases. In a similar study, Magraw et al. [22] highlighted the critical need for potent pain management strategies for pericoronitis sufferers.

In 2019, research by Alalwani et al. [23] compared the influence of oral and topical non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) on the oral health-related quality of life in individuals with symptomatic pericoronitis, revealing the superior performance of topical Benzydamine over oral alternatives such as Diclofenac and Flurbiprofen. Elsadek et al.‘s 2020 [24] study posited that antimicrobial photodynamic therapy could serve as an effective therapeutic approach in pericoronitis, reducing both tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) levels and bacterial counts.

Moreover, the study noted a significant uptick in the range of mouth opening post-mouthwash use in all subjects, which can be credited to the collective action of the mouthwash’s active ingredients. Despite the absence of notable disparities between the groups, the parallel outcomes suggest a neutral impact of the mouthwash on mouth opening capabilities, a conclusion supported by Isola et al. [8], who found no discernible advantage of various drugs over each other in enhancing mouth opening post-wisdom teeth extraction.

In a clinical trial involving 30 patients with periodontal disease, ozonized gels and chlorhexidine were compared as adjuncts to mechanical debridement. Both treatments significantly improved periodontal health. Ozonized gels, which offer similar benefits without chlorhexidine’s side effects like tooth discoloration, may serve as an effective alternative [25]. Additionally, another clinical trial demonstrated that B. lactis HN019 probiotic lozenges, when used alongside traditional gum disease treatment, led to improved patient outcomes. This suggests that probiotics could be a valuable addition to periodontal therapy [26].

It is essential to acknowledge that this research augments the existing literature on pericoronitis treatment by scrutinizing a mouthwash concocted expressly for this ailment. Nonetheless, it is crucial to recognize the study’s limitations, such as its modest participant size, which may limit the broader applicability of the findings. Subsequent studies with more extensive participant pools are warranted to corroborate these results. Furthermore, the study’s narrow focus on pain severity and maximum mouth opening, to the exclusion of other factors like inflammation, microbial presence, and potential mouthwash side effects, suggests that a more holistic examination of these elements could yield a fuller picture of the mouthwash’s efficacy in managing pericoronitis.

In the end, the results of this study show that the combined mouthwash of Chlorhexidine, Benzydamine, Nanosilver and antibiotics Amoxicillin and Metronidazole is effective in reducing pain intensity and improving the maximum amount of mouth opening in pericoronitis patients.

The findings from this investigation reveal that both types of mouthwash effectively increased MMO, yet there was no discernible difference in their ability to alleviate pain between the two cohorts. Consequently, we should accept the null hypothesis, which posits that there is no substantial variance in primary outcomes between the combined mouthwash and the sole use of chlorhexidine mouthwash.

Although there is a need for more research to confirm these results and investigate more aspects of the treatment of pericoronitis, the results of this study provide an important insight into the use of combined mouthwashes as an adjunctive treatment for the management of pericoronitis symptoms. Further studies are required to optimize treatment protocols and determine the long-term effects of this mouthwash in pericoronitis patients.

Conclusion

In this study, we observed that both the combined mouthwash and the Chlorhexidine variant were effective in diminishing pain among the subjects. Despite noticeable fluctuations in pain intensity over various days, the overall pain reduction was comparable between the two groups. Therefore, each mouthwash variety can be considered a practical option for reducing discomfort. Moreover, the utilization of mouthwash was associated with a significant improvement in the extent of mouth opening for both groups, with a similar level of enhancement observed universally. It is advisable to conduct further investigations to fully understand the long-lasting effects and potential adverse reactions associated with these mouthwashes.
