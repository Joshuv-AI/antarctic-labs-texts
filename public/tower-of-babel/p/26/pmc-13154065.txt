The Snubbed Complication: Why Postinfarction Ventricular Septal Defect Management Lacks Evidence-Based Guidance in 2026

Postinfarction ventricular septal defect (PIVSD) complicated by cardiogenic shock and severe aortic stenosis results in a hemodynamic impasse driven by mechanical shunting, fixed afterload, and shock physiology. Surgical repair, mechanical circulatory support, and percutaneous closure each address selected components but are limited by myocardial friability, adverse hemodynamics, or device instability. No strategy currently offers a low-risk solution in this clinical context. AS = aortic stenosis; LV = left ventricle; VSD = ventricular septal defect.

Postinfarction ventricular septal defect (PIVSD) is a rare but devastating complication of acute myocardial infarction, occurring in 0.2% to 0.34% of cases in the reperfusion era.1 Despite its low incidence, PIVSD carries extremely high mortality, historically up to 90% without intervention. Necrosis of the interventricular septum leads to a left-to-right shunt, producing acute volume overload, pulmonary hypertension, and cardiogenic shock. Over the past 2 decades, management has shifted from routine immediate surgery to a more nuanced, multidisciplinary approach integrating mechanical circulatory support and transcatheter techniques.1,2 Nevertheless, decision-making remains guided by low-level evidence and expert opinion, especially in more complex cases. The absence of randomized trials and the tyranny of small sample sizes have created a “snubbed” category of patients for whom the optimal management strategy is a mirage, heavily dependent on local expertise and resource availability rather than definitive guidelines.

Case Summary

A 73-year-old woman with hypertension, type 2 diabetes, and obesity was transferred to our tertiary center in cardiogenic shock. Sixteen hours earlier she had presented to a referring hospital with an anterior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction and received thrombolysis within 4 hours of symptom onset, as primary percutaneous coronary intervention was not available at that time, with resolution of chest pain and ST-segment elevation. Subsequent transthoracic echocardiography revealed a PIVSD. Upon arrival, she was in profound shock requiring high-dose vasopressors (0.2 μg/kg/min of epinephrine and 2.4 U/h of vasopressin), with a mean arterial pressure of 50 mm Hg, tachycardia, oliguria, and a lactate level of 7 mmol/L. Transthoracic echocardiography showed a hyperdynamic left ventricle (LV) with preserved ejection fraction and sequelae of an apical-septal myocardial infarction, and it confirmed the presence of an 8-mm inferoseptal VSD with significant left-to-right shunt and moderate right ventricular dysfunction (Videos 1A to 1C) associated with reduced cardiac output (subaortic VEi: 19.6 mL/m2, velocity time integral: 8 cm, cardiac output: 2.65 L/min). A severely calcified aortic valve with a mean gradient of 26 mm Hg and effective area of 0.58 cm2, consistent with severe aortic stenosis (AS), was surprisingly revealed (Video 1D). Chest x-ray showed an acute pulmonary edema. Invasive hemodynamic assessment was performed under general anesthesia. Coronary angiography revealed a 70% left anterior descending artery (LAD) proximal stenosis with preserved distal flow (Figure 1), consistent with successful thrombolysis. Right heart catheterization demonstrated a significant left-to-right shunt with a Qp:Qs ratio of 5.8, pulmonary hypertension (systolic pulmonary artery pressure: 59 mm Hg, mean pulmonary artery pressure: 40 mm Hg), and elevated biventricular filling pressures (LV end-diastolic pressure [EDP]: 31 mm Hg, right ventricular EDP: 29 mm Hg) (Table 1). The calculated transaortic mean gradient was 18 mm Hg with valve area of 0.7 cm2, confirming severe low-flow, low-gradient AS in the context of critically low cardiac output (2.6 L/min).

Despite maximal medical therapy, the patient's condition deteriorated, with a progressive serum lactate rise to 12 mmol/L. The SCAI (Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions) shock classification was stage E, hemometabolic phenotype, with an extremely high likelihood of short-term mortality.

An urgent multidisciplinary heart team (2 cardiac surgeons, intensivist, interventional cardiologist, 2 heart failure and structural cardiology specialists) discussed 3 distinct management strategies.

Viewpoint 1: Immediate Combined Surgery

Two cardiac surgeons and the interventional cardiologist advocated for immediate definitive surgery. Given the patient's age and lack of significant comorbidities, they proposed a single procedure involving surgical VSD patch repair, aortic valve replacement with a bioprosthesis, and coronary artery bypass grafting (saphenous vein graft to LAD).

Viewpoint 2: VA-ECMO as a Bridge to Delayed Treatment

The clinical and interventional cardiologists proposed venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) to stabilize the patient and serve as a “bridge to decision.” The goal was to allow survival through the acute phase of cardiogenic shock, with the intention of performing delayed treatment (surgical or percutaneous repair) once hemodynamics and end-organ function improved.

Viewpoint 3: Transcatheter Approach

The second cardiac surgeon considered the patient to be at prohibitive risk for immediate complex surgery. VA-ECMO was also deemed high risk given the patient's age and presentation. Intra-aortic balloon pump support was judged insufficient owing to the concomitant severe AS. This team proposed a staged, totally percutaneous approach: initial balloon aortic valvuloplasty (BAV) to improve hemodynamics, followed by transcatheter VSD closure.

Case Resolution

Heart team consensus favored viewpoint 3. Coronary intervention was deferred given the preserved distal LAD flow. To improve coronary perfusion, an intra-aortic balloon pump was placed via the left femoral artery. Under transesophageal echocardiography guidance, BAV was performed first using a 20-mm Nucleus balloon (NuMED) via the right femoral artery (Video 2). This resulted in improved valve opening (Video 3), a reduction in LVEDP (to 14 mm Hg), and enhanced systemic oxygenation. Subsequently, a 20-mm Amplatzer septal occluder (Abbott) was deployed via the right femoral vein to close the VSD (Figure 2A). Initial positioning was satisfactory (Figure 2B). However, a moderate residual shunt was immediately noted. A dissection-like extension through the friable infarcted septum (Video 4) precluded the safe deployment of a larger device. The pre- and postprocedural key hemodynamic changes are summarized in Table 1. Lactate levels briefly decreased to 9 mmol/L. Unfortunately, the patient's condition rapidly deteriorated within hours. Swan-Ganz catheter measurements indicated an increasing pulmonary output, signifying a worsening left-to-right shunt through the residual VSD. This led to a critical reduction in systemic cardiac output, requiring an escalation of vasopressor and inotropic support, and culminating in multiorgan failure. The patient died on postprocedural day 2.

Discussion

PIVSD remains one of the most challenging emergencies in contemporary cardiology. Our case embodies the clinical dilemma that arises when this complication is further compounded by concomitant severe AS and cardiogenic shock, a scenario scarcely addressed in the literature. The tragic outcome, despite a meticulously considered percutaneous strategy, underscores the precarious nature of this condition and serves as a critical platform to discuss the nuances, limitations, and controversies of current therapeutic paradigms.

The challenge of coexisting pathologies: PIVSD and aortic stenosis

The presence of severe AS introduced a unique and deleterious hemodynamic synergism. The PIVSD, with its significant left-to-right shunt (Qp:Qs = 5), robbed the systemic circulation of forward flow, leading to cardiogenic shock. This, in turn, rendered the assessment of the aortic valve challenging, creating a classic “low-flow, low-gradient” severe AS picture (mean gradient: 18-29 mm Hg, aortic valve area: ∼0.6 cm2).3 Crucially, the AS imposed a fixed afterload on the already compromised LV. This combination created a “perfect storm”: The LV could neither generate sufficient forward flow to maintain systemic perfusion nor effectively decompress, leading to persistently elevated LVEDP (31 mm Hg) and fulminant pulmonary edema. This pathophysiology also critically affects hemodynamic calculations. In the presence of a significant VSD, the Fick principle for calculating cardiac output becomes complex. The systemic flow (Qs) is what perfuses the body, while pulmonary flow (Qp) is the sum of Qs and the shunt flow. Our team's use of both echocardiographic methods (velocity time integrals) and invasive methods (Fick with appropriate sampling)4 was essential.

Analysis of the heart team viewpoints: a reflection of current controversies

The 3 proposed strategies accurately reflect the ongoing debates in the field, with each option carrying substantial risks.

•Viewpoint 1: immediate combined surgery. This approach, advocating for definitive repair in a “one-stage” solution, is rooted in the desire to correct all pathologies simultaneously1,5: repair the VSD, replace the stenotic valve, and revascularize the culprit vessel. However, the evidence overwhelmingly highlights the prohibitive risk of operating on friable, necrotic myocardial tissue in the acute phase. The mortality for early surgical repair remains exceptionally high, often quoted between 40% and 60%.1,5 In our patient, the prospect of a triple procedure on a heart in profound shock was deemed by one surgeon as a likely fatal undertaking, a sentiment supported by the high rates of patch dehiscence and failure to wean from bypass in such scenarios.

•Viewpoint 2: VA-ECMO as a bridge to delayed treatment. This strategy represents the modern “bridge-to-recovery” paradigm, aiming to use advanced mechanical circulatory support to stabilize the patient in cardiogenic shock and allow time for organ recovery. In this strategy, ongoing shock is considered the main driver for mortality. The opportunity to stage endovascular and surgical correction is the main advantage.1,6 VA-ECMO provides complete cardiopulmonary support, potentially reversing end-organ hypoperfusion and allowing lactate clearance. The subsequent delayed surgery (after 2-4 weeks) is performed on fibrotic, stronger tissue, significantly lowering the technical failure rate.5,6 However, this approach is not a panacea. VA-ECMO increases LV afterload, which can paradoxically worsen the VSD shunt and pulmonary edema, often necessitating an additional LV venting strategy (eg, an Impella, or an atrial septostomy).7 Furthermore, the risks of major vascular complications, bleeding, and infection during a prolonged ECMO run in an elderly patient are substantial. Observational data suggest that Impella devices might have a lower risk profile.8,9 The team's concern that this might merely exchange one mode of death for another was valid. In the setting of AS, a microaxial pump was not an option. Importantly, the apparent benefit of delayed intervention strategies warrants critical reappraisal. The widely cited survival advantage associated with delayed intervention must be interpreted cautiously. This paradigm is inherently affected by survivor bias, as patients who live long enough to undergo delayed repair represent a biologically selected subgroup. Those who deteriorate rapidly—such as in the present case—are under-represented in delayed-intervention cohorts, reinforcing a narrative that may not reflect causality. The field urgently requires innovative, decentralized, or registry-based randomized strategies comparing early versus delayed intervention, acknowledging that conventional randomized trials are impractical in this rare but lethal condition.

•Viewpoint 3: transcatheter approach. This innovative strategy attempted to circumvent the risks of major surgery and prolonged ECMO by using minimally invasive techniques to rapidly break the vicious cycle of hemodynamic collapse. The rationale was solid: First, relieve the fixed afterload from AS with BAV to improve forward flow and LV decompression; second, close the VSD. BAV, while a palliative procedure, can provide significant acute hemodynamic benefit, as evidenced by the immediate drop in LVEDP in our case. Transcatheter VSD closure, when anatomically suitable, offers a less invasive alternative to surgery.10 However, the efficacy of BAV is transient, and the procedure carries risks of aortic regurgitation, embolism, and circulatory collapse. More critically, transcatheter device closure in the acute phase is technically challenging given the friable, necrotic tissue, which is prone to tear, as occurred in our case, leading to a residual or even worsened shunt. The inability to deploy a larger device highlights the unforgiving nature of the acutely infarcted septum.

Critical lessons from the outcome

The initial success followed by rapid deterioration offers crucial insights. The transient improvement post-BAV and device deployment demonstrated that the physiological rationale was correct. However, the “dissection-like” extension of the VSD and the resulting significant residual shunt were catastrophic. This underscores a fundamental limitation of all acute VSD repair strategies: Devices and patches require a stable rim of tissue for secure anchoring, a condition often absent in the acute phase of PIVSD. The residual shunt, now flowing through a noncompliant, traumatized septum, led to an intractable cycle of low systemic output and multiorgan failure.

This outcome forces a serious reflection: Was the percutaneous strategy too ambitious? Could a more aggressive mechanical circulatory support strategy, such as a proactive “ECPELLA” (ECMO + Impella) configuration to unload the LV and support systemic flow, have altered the course? Or would viewpoint 1—despite its prohibitive risk—have been the only remaining chance of success? Our case provides no definitive answer, but it powerfully illustrates that in such complex scenarios, even a well-reasoned, consensus-driven decision can be thwarted by the inherent lethality of the disease.

Conclusions

This case serves as a stark reminder that the management of PIVSD, particularly when complicated by other significant cardiac pathologies like severe AS and cardiogenic shock, is a race between 2 deteriorating processes: the relentless progression of shock and the urgent need to correct the underlying mechanical lesion. In such scenarios, shock reversal—whether with early ECMO or as rescue in case of procedural failure—may be essential to create the physiological stability needed for definitive options such as delayed surgical repair, hybrid strategies, or even transplant candidacy. Ultimately, shock should be treated as a parallel disease process rather than a consequence of the lesion alone. The heart team debate in this case reflects the current state of evidence-based medicine, where strategies are extrapolated from small series and expert opinion rather than robust clinical trials. Earlier hemodynamic stabilization might have provided a safer platform for subsequent interventions in this patient, and similar future cases will continue to challenge clinicians to balance timing, anatomy, and circulatory support under extreme uncertainty.
