Conical Intersection Governs Ultrafast Photodissociation Dynamics of HO2NO2
Authors: Ya Peng, Hui Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The photodissociation dynamics of peroxynitric acid (HO2NO2) in the atmospherically relevant ultraviolet region are investigated using the complete active space SCF electronic structure calculations combined with nonadiabatic molecular dynamics based on the fewest-switches surface hopping method. Minimum-energy crossing points between the lowest two singlets and between the lowest two triplet states are characterized and classified according to their local topological features in the branching plane. The S0/S1 crossing is identified as a sloped single-path intersection, whereas the T1/T2 crossing exhibits a peaked bifurcating topology. Nonadiabatic dynamics simulations for the photodissociation of HO2NO2 reveal ultrafast S1 → S0 relaxation mediated by the sloped intersection and a dominant NO2 + HO2 dissociation channel, with limited triplet involvement due to weak spin–orbit coupling. The predicted strong preference for the NO2 + HO2 pathway is consistent with updated experimental photolysis measurements in the actinic UV region, and the calculated rotational constants of HO2NO2 show very good agreement with available spectroscopic data. These results elucidate how changes in excited-state molecular geometry govern the photodissociation behavior of HO2NO2, highlighting the structural origin of its dominant reaction pathway.

Introduction

Nitrogen oxides play central roles in atmospheric chemistry, influencing ozone levels, radical cycling, and oxidative capacity throughout the atmosphere. , Reactive nitrogen species include reservoirs such as nitric acid (HNO3), dinitrogen pentoxide (N2O5), and peroxynitric acid (HO2NO2, also denoted PNA or HNO4). Peroxynitric acid is formed from reversible reaction of HO2 with NO2 and is thermally stable only at temperatures characteristic of the upper troposphere and lower stratosphere: HO2+NO2+M⇌HO2NO2+M 1 This reaction serves as a key temporary reservoir that couples the HO x (x = 1, 2) and NO x chemical families. , In these cold regions of the atmosphere, thermal decomposition of HO2NO2 is slow, allowing it to constitute a significant fraction of NO x . −

The continuous nature of the HO2NO2 UV absorption spectrum implies that the molecule photodissociates over the entire band. There are several photolysis channels energetically possible for this reaction as following: HO2NO2+hv→HO2+NO2 2 HO2NO2+hv→OH+NO3 3

The high levels of observed HO x , as compared to a standard photochemical model, result in more active ozone production and a steeper increase in ozone production for a given increase in NO x . Field measurements have reported gas-phase concentrations of HO2NO2 reaching several hundred pptv in the upper troposphere and higher in polar regions. , This reservoir affects the partitioning of HO x and NO x , influencing ozone production rates and the atmosphere’s oxidizing capacity. The fate of HO2NO2 includes thermal dissociation, reaction with OH, heterogeneous uptake and photolysis. − While thermal decomposition (e.g., at higher temperatures), reaction with OH, and heterogeneous uptake (e.g., on ice or aerosols) are relatively well characterized loss processes for peroxynitric acid, ,,− photolysis remains a critical yet uncertain sink, particularly in the actinic UV region. HO2NO2 exhibits a broad UV absorption spectrum extending from ∼190 nm to beyond 330 nm, with absorption cross sections decreasing toward longer wavelengths. Photodissociation in the near-UV tail can contribute significantly in the upper troposphere, where actinic flux penetrates more deeply. Experimental studies at shorter wavelengths (e.g., 193 and 248 nm) have reported quantum yields for the channels producing OH + NO3 (Φ ≈ 0.1–0.2) and HO2 + NO2 (Φ ≈ 0.6–0.9), along with minor NO3 formation. Additionally, near-IR overtone excitation leads to dissociation, primarily yielding HO2 + NO2, with photolysis rates on the order of 10–5–10–6 s–1. Short-wavelength ultraviolet radiation below 280 nm is efficiently absorbed by stratospheric ozone and molecular oxygen and does not penetrate into the troposphere. In contrast, radiation in the UVB and near-UVA range, approximately 290 to 400 nm, partially reaches the troposphere and provides the primary energy source for key photolytic processes, including NO2 photolysis and the dissociation of HONO and HO2NO2, thereby regulating tropospheric oxidative capacity and pollutant cycling. − Despite this importance, the photodissociation dynamics of HO2NO2 in the atmospherically relevant ultraviolet region relevant to the troposphere remain poorly understood. , Available absorption measurements indicate weak but non-negligible cross sections in this spectral window, yet branching ratios, product energy distributions, and the role of nonadiabatic effects are largely unexplored. Accurate characterization of these processes is essential for refining atmospheric models, which currently show discrepancies in predicted HO2NO2 abundances, particularly in cold, low-NO x environments. , Current NASA data evaluations assume that reactions and account for HO2NO2 photolysis in the upper troposphere. Recommended quantum yields are based primarily on 248 nm laboratory measurements by MacLeod et al. and Roehl et al., , giving Φ = 0.56 for reaction and Φ = 0.34 for reaction . These values are typically assumed to apply throughout the actinic region (λ > 290 nm and λ < 220 nm), where direct laboratory measurements are scarce. Subsequent pulsed excimer laser photolysis experiments combined with laser-induced fluorescence detection of OH and cavity ring-down spectroscopy of NO3 demonstrated substantially smaller yields of the OH + NO3 channel than previously assumed. These measurements indicate that HO2 + NO2 is the dominant photolysis channel across both short- and long-wavelength actinic regions, challenging earlier modeling assumptions.

The dissociation of HO2NO2, including its excited states, cannot be accurately investigated due to the inadequacy of single-configuration reference wave functions. A recent study employing machine-learned (ML) potential energy surfaces combined with fewest-switches surface hopping reported a dominant HO2 + NO2 channel from excitation to the higher excited singlet state for a single conformer of HO2NO2, without evidence of O–O bond breaking, based on reduced-dimensional dynamics. The triplet electronic states and surface-crossing topologies were not considered in their work, precluding a complete description of the underlying nonadiabatic photodynamics. Their work overlooked triplet electronic states and the topological features of surface crossings, thereby limiting a comprehensive portrayal of the nonadiabatic photodynamics at play. In contrast, potential energy surface crossings, particularly conical intersections (CIs), are pivotal in photochemical reactions involving excited states or radicals, as they facilitate ultrafast nonadiabatic transitions, funnel population between electronic states, and dictate product branching ratios by channeling nuclear motion along specific topological pathways, often on femtosecond time scales. − For instance, the local geometry and tilt of CIs can bias relaxation toward certain dissociation channels, profoundly influencing quantum yields and atmospheric reactivity. − These omissions underscore the imperative for a topology-centric analysis to address lingering uncertainties in the atmospherically relevant UV photolysis mechanisms and refine predictions of atmospheric photodissociation rates for species like HO2NO2.

Method

Ab Initio Electronic Structure Calculations

Geometry optimizations were performed using state-averaged complete active space self-consistent field (SA-CASSCF) theory with an active space of 10 electrons in 8 orbitals, CASSCF­(10e, 8o), and the aug-cc-pVDZ basis sets, without imposing symmetry constraints. For all SA-CASSCF-optimized geometries, their energies were refined by multistate CASPT2 (MS-CASPT2) calculations to consider both the multireference effect and dynamic electron correlation. The MS-CASPT2 energy corrections were done with the same active space and basis sets as being used in SA-CASSCF calculations. The calculated adiabatic electronic states were labeled according to the energy ordering: S0, S1 for the singlet states and T1, T2 for the triplet states, respectively. In this work, four electronic states X1A­(S0), A1A­(S1), a3A­(T1), and b3A­(T2) were included in the state averaging to provide a balanced description of the potential energy surfaces relevant to both the NO2 + HO2 and NO3 + OH dissociation channels.

The eight active orbitals, represented in Figure , comprise a set of valence orbitals that describe the coupled electronic structure of the HO2 and NO2 moieties. MO18 corresponds to a delocalized in-plane p-type valence orbital predominantly localized on the oxygen atoms of the HO2 moiety and the oxygen atoms of the NO2 group. Its nodal structure and composition indicate nonbonding to weakly bonding character. MO19 is best described as an in-plane π-like bonding orbital centered on the NO2 unit, arising from constructive overlap between nitrogen and oxygen p x /p y orbitals within the molecular plane. Together with the out-of-plane π-like orbital (MO20), these three orbitals constitute the essential π-type valence manifold of NO2 and mediate its electronic coupling to the HO2 fragment. Notably, MO16slightly below the active space frontierexhibits significant N–O bonding character with minor contributions from HO2, underscoring its role in stabilizing the NO2 core. The higher-lying unoccupied orbitals (MO17 and MO21–MO23) provide the requisite π* and σ* character necessary to describe key excited-state processes, including bond cleavage and nonadiabatic transitions along the NO2 + HO2 and NO3 + OH dissociation coordinates. Global minima on the ground- and excited-state surfaces were located for all four states, and harmonic frequency calculations were carried out to confirm their stationary nature. To assess the convergence of the ground-state geometry with respect to basis set and active space size, supplementary optimizations were performed at CASSCF­(10e, mo)/aug-cc-pVnZ levels with m = 8, 9, 10 and n = D, T, Q. Minimum-energy crossing (MEX) points were optimized to identify the relevant nonadiabatic coupling regions and independent crossing seams involved in the reaction pathways. These optimizations were performed using the ORCA 4.0 program as external optimizer to simultaneously compute multiple gradients, which handled the nuclear-coordinate optimization based on electronic energies and gradients evaluated at the CASSCF level for multiple states. The MEX optimization procedure accounts for the energies of the lower and upper states, their energy difference, the corresponding analytical gradients, the gradient difference vector, and the nonadiabatic coupling vector.

To characterize the local topology of the crossing seam, , several key parameters were extracted during the MEX optimizations, including the pitch (σgh), asymmetry (Δgh), relative tilt (σ = s/σgh), and the tilt heading (θs). Within the branching plane spanned by the mass-weighted Cartesian displacements (x, y) around the degeneracy point R deg, the adiabatic energies of the two interacting states can be expressed in the general form E11/22(x,y)=12(E11+E22±|E11−E22|)=Edeg+δgh(xsx+ysy±x2+y2+Δgh(x2+y2)), 4 or, equivalently, in polar coordinates (r, θ) defined by r=x2+y2 and tanθ = y/x, E11/22(r,θ)=Edeg+δghr[σcos(θ−θs)±1+Δghcos2θ] 5

Here, E 11/22 denote the energies of the lower and upper adiabatic states, E deg is the energy at the CI, and θs specifies the direction of the tilt within the branching plane. The energy splitting between the two states is given by |E11−E22|=2x2g∼·g∼+y2h∼·h∼ 6 where g∼ and h∼ are the scaled gradient difference and nonadiabatic coupling vectors, respectively. From these vectors, the pitch and asymmetry parameters are defined as δgh=12(g∼·g∼+h∼·h∼) 7 Δgh=g∼·g∼−h∼·h∼g∼·g∼+h∼·h∼ 8

Based on the topology of the adiabatic surfaces in the branching plane, the intersections can be classified according to two criteria. First, an intersection is termed peaked if the degeneracy point corresponds to a minimum of the upper surface in the branching plane, or sloped if there exist directions along which the upper surface descends below the intersection energy E deg. Second, intersections may exhibit either a single preferred relaxation pathway on the lower surface (single-path intersections) or two competing relaxation directions (bifurcating intersections). These characteristics are most clearly revealed by examining the angular dependence of the adiabatic energies as a function of the polar angle θ at fixed r, which will be discussed in detail in the following section.

All ab initio electronic-structure calculations were carried out using the OpenMolcas V23.02 program suite. Wave functions were visualized using MOLDEN V7.0, which was also used to generate cubic grid data in Gaussian cube format for further volumetric analysis in VMD V1.9.3. Electrostatic potential (ESP) maps were evaluated to characterize charge redistribution and reactive regions, while electron localization function (ELF) analyses were employed to examine bonding patterns localization.

The electrostatic potential V ESP(r) and the electron localization function V ELF(r) are defined as VESP(r)=∑AZA|RA−r|−∫ρ(r′)dr′|r′−r| 9 and VELF(r)=11+(D/Dh)2 10 with D=12∑i|∇ϕi(r)|2−18|∇ρ(r)|2ρ(r) 11 and Dh=310(3π2)2/3ρ(r)5/3 12 here, ϕ i denotes the Kohn–Sham orbital of electron i, ρ­(r) = ∑ i |ϕ i (r)|2 is the electron density, D is the kinetic energy density associated with Pauli repulsion, and D h corresponds to the kinetic energy density of a homogeneous electron gas with the same density. Details of the underlying electron-topology theory can be found elsewhere; only a concise outline is provided here. When evaluated on molecular surfaces, the ESP provides a direct mapping of electrostatic interactions with external charges and has been widely employed to rationalize trends in reactivity, intermolecular interactions, and the directionality of both covalent and noncovalent bonding. Quantitative analyses of high-ELF localization domains, including population (HELP) and basin volume (HELV) descriptors, were extracted from the ELF topology to provide further insight into the electronic structure and bonding evolution along the reaction pathways.

Dynamics Simulation

Nonadiabatic molecular dynamics simulations were performed using the Tully’s fewest-switches surface hopping (FSSH) approach , as implemented in the Surface Hopping Including Arbitrary Couplings program suite, , interfaced with the electronic structure package. The dynamics explicitly included the two lowest singlet states (S0 and S1) and the two lowest triplet states (T1 and T2). In the original surface hopping method, only nonadiabatic couplings are considered, only allowing for population transfer between electronic states of the same multiplicity. The methodology employed in this work is a generalization of standard surface hopping since it allows to include any type of coupling. Beyond nonadiabatic couplings, spin–orbit couplings (SOCs) are also included. The nuclear motion is propagated classically on a single adiabatic potential energy surface, while the electronic wave function evolves according to the time-dependent electronic Schrödinger equation, iℏddtc(t)=Hel(R(t))c(t) 13 where c(t) is the vector of electronic expansion coefficients and H el is the electronic Hamiltonian evaluated along the nuclear trajectory R(t).

The electronic Hamiltonian is expressed in the molecular Coulomb Hamiltonian (MCH) representation, HMCH=HMCHel+HSOC 14 where H SOC contains the SOC matrix elements, allowing for explicit population transfer between states of different spin multiplicities.

Nonadiabatic transitions between electronic states are treated stochastically according to probabilistic fewest switches algorithm, proposed by Tully. The hopping probability from state i to state j during a time step Δt is given by gij=max(0,2Δt|ci|2Re[ci*cjdij·R˙]) 15 where d ij is the nonadiabatic coupling vector and Ṙ denotes the nuclear velocity.

In contrast to conventional surface hopping, which only allows transitions between states of the same spin multiplicity, we treats nonadiabatic and SOCs on equal footing by diagonalizing the total electronic Hamiltonian at each nuclear geometry. Surface hopping is performed in the diagonal basis, while nuclear gradients are evaluated in the MCH representation using appropriate state transformations.

Initial nuclear coordinates and momenta were sampled from a quantum harmonic Wigner distribution constructed from the ground-state normal modes and vibrational frequencies. A total of 2,000 initial conditions were generated. From this ensemble, 260 trajectories were initiated in the S1 state using a vertical excitation-energy window of 3.54–6.20 eV. Nuclear motion was propagated using the velocity-Verlet algorithm with a time step of 0.5 fs. All electronic energies, nuclear gradients, nonadiabatic couplings, and SOCs were evaluated on-the-fly at the CASSCF­(10e, 8o)/aug-cc-pVDZ level. Trajectories that failed to reach the maximum propagation time of t max = 600 fs, for instance due to convergence difficulties in the CASSCF calculations, were treated as follows. If dissociation occurred prior to termination, the separated fragment was propagated to t max using the average velocity obtained after dissociation, and the last valid electronic state was assigned as the final state. Trajectories that had not dissociated before termination were discarded. Using this procedure, fewer than half of the initiated trajectories were retained for statistical analysis.

The data in Table summarize a systematic assessment of HO2NO2 on the ground-state S0 convergence with respect to basis set and active space size. The nine calculations form the planned grid: three basis sets (aug-cc-pVDZ, -pVTZ and -pVQZ) times three active-space sizes (CAS­(10e, 8o), CAS­(10e, 9o) and CAS­(10e, 10o)). For all three active-space choices the CASSCF total energy monotonically lowers (becomes more negative) when the basis sets are enlarged from AVDZ to AVTZ to AVQZ. Similarly, for fixed basis sets the inclusion of more active orbitals (8 → 9 → 10) yields additional correlation lowering. This consistent trend supports the usual expectation that increasing basis size and active space provides increased variational freedom and therefore lower variational CASSCF energies. The energy trends here are fully consistent with standard basis-set convergence analyses and Complete Basis Set (CBS) extrapolation rationale discussed by Helgaker et al. − Bond lengths change only slightly across the nine calculations (typical changes on the order of 10–2 bohr). Angles and dihedrals also show small but virtually shifts with fractions of a degree up to a few degrees. The largest numerical shifts appear between the smallest basis (AVDZ) and the larger bases (AVTZ/AVQZ), and in a few cases there are small further shifts when the active space is enlarged. This pattern is again the expected behavior: total energies, which are global scalar quantities, show more pronounced basis incompleteness error and correlate more strongly with basis size than do optimized equilibrium geometrical parameters which are determined by the shape of the potential energy surface (PES) near its minimum. All three rotational constants of HO2NO2 are reproduced within approximately 10–2, indicating that the optimized equilibrium geometry captures the overall mass distribution with satisfactory accuracy. The slightly larger deviation in the A rotational constant can be attributed to its stronger sensitivity to structural parameters along the principal axis with the smallest moment of inertia, particularly the O–O bond length and the torsional orientation of the HO2 group. Given that the present calculations were performed at the CASSCF level without inclusion of dynamic correlation beyond the active space, the agreement with experiment is excellent. Inclusion of higher-level correlation treatments (e.g., CASPT2 benchmark geometry optimization) would be expected to further reduce the residual deviations. Overall, the agreement between calculated and experimental rotational constants supports the reliability of the equilibrium structures employed for subsequent nonadiabatic dynamics simulations.

Optimized geometries of the stationary points and the MEX points in the photodissociation of HO2NO2 are represented in Figure . The equilibrium geometry of the S0 state is shown in Figure a. It exhibits neither exact symmetry nor a strictly planar configuration. The heavy-atom framework with the O2N5(O3O4) skeleton is approximately planar, whereas the O1–H6 bond is oriented nearly perpendicular to this plane. More specifically, the nitrogen atom (N5) and the terminal oxygen (O1) lie on the same side of the heavy-atom O2–O3–O4 plane defined by O2, O3, and O4, while the hydrogen atom (H6) is located on the opposite side. A central structural feature is therefore the small out-of-plane displacement of N5 relative to the O2–O3–O4 plane. Among the nine calculations, the CAS­(10e, 10o)/AVQZ level yields the smallest N5 plane height 0.0138 bohr, corresponding to the highest degree of planarity. This indicates that the combination of the largest active space and the largest basis set favors a geometry in which N5 is nearly coplanar with the O2–O3–O4 fragment. The degree of planarity of the nitro group is governed by a subtle balance between oxygen lone-pair localization and delocalized π and π* interactions involving the NO3 fragment and the molecular framework. Accurately capturing this balance requires inclusion of the relevant lone-pair orbitals and low-lying π* antibonding orbitals in the active space. In the present calculations, the orbitals most directly associated with the in-plane versus out-of-plane character are the nonbonding oxygen lone-pair orbitals and the π* antibonding orbital. Ensuring that these orbitals are included in the active space is therefore essential for describing the fine energetic competition that determines the small displacement of N5. In routine practice one usually takes the largest active space and largest basis available as the best option for geometry optimization or Intrinsic Reaction Coordinate calculation. If computational budget permits more accurate estimates, one can apply CBS extrapolation and/or perform single-point higher-level corrections. However, the present data demonstrate that the CAS­(10e, 8o)/AVDZ level already reproduces the key structural features of the ground-state minimum with good fidelity, while dramatically reducing computational cost. This balance between accuracy and efficiency makes it particularly well suited for large-scale, multistate nonadiabatic dynamics simulations, where extensive on-the-fly evaluations of energies and gradients are required. The lowest singlet excited state S1 arises predominantly from a single π → σ* excitation: an electron is promoted from the delocalized in-plane π-bonding orbital MO20, which forms part of the core π-system of the NO2 moiety and couples electronically to the HO2 fragment, to the lowest unoccupied σ*-antibonding orbital MO21, which is antibonding between the nitrogen atom and its neighboring oxygen atoms. This transition is delocalized over the O2–O3–O4 framework. In S0, the doubly occupied MO20 exerts a stabilizing bonding interaction that enforces planarity by drawing both N5 and the terminal oxygen (O1) into the plane defined by O2, O3, and O4. Upon excitation to S1, however, partial occupation of the σ* orbital weakens the N5–O bonds and relaxes this planar constraint, thereby facilitating out-of-plane displacement of N5a key nuclear motion associated with the onset of nonadiabatic dynamics along the NO2 + HO2 dissociation coordinate. The O1 atom is similarly affected and moves away from the O2–O3–O4 plane in the same direction as N5. Consequently, the perpendicular distances of both N5 and O1 from the O2–O3–O4 plane increase significantly in S1 relative to S0, consistent with the pronounced pyramidalization at nitrogen in the excited state. This trend is quantitatively confirmed by the data in Table : the height of N5 rises from 0.0181 bohr in S0 to 0.8337 bohr in S1, while that of O1 increases from 0.4892 bohr to 2.0173 bohr.

The lowest two triplet states, T1 and T2, arise primarily from spin-conserving single excitations: T1 corresponds to a promotion from the delocalized in-plane nonbonding orbital MO18, which predominantly composed of oxygen lone-pair character on O2 and the NO2 oxygen atoms, to the σ*-antibonding orbital MO21 (n → σ*), while T2 originates from excitation of the out-of-plane π-bonding orbital MO20 to the same σ* acceptor (π → σ*). In both cases, occupation of MO21-characterized by strong antibonding interactions between the nitrogen atom (N5) and the O2–O3–O4 triad-destabilizes the planar equilibrium geometry, inducing pronounced pyramidalization of N5. This structural response is a direct consequence of σ* population and facilitates access to dissociative pathways in the triplet manifold. Crucially, the symmetry and nodal structure of the parent orbitals govern the correlated out-of-plane motion of the terminal oxygen O1. In T1, removal of electron density from the in-plane, bonding-phase combination on O2 (MO18) reduces electron-mediated cohesion between the HO2 and NO2 fragments, leading to a symmetric displacement in which O1 and N5 move to the same side of the O2–O3–O4 plane. In contrast, the π → σ* excitation in T2 involves MO20 and MO21 with opposite orbital phases on the HO2 and NO2 moieties; this generates an antisymmetric distortion that drives O1 and N5 in opposite out-of-plane directions.

The heights of atoms N5, O1, and H6 to the O2–O3–O4 plane are summarized in Table . The largest N5 displacement occurs in T1, indicating maximal pyramidalization of the nitro nitrogen there. O1 and H6 similarly shift out of plane in the excited states; in particular H6 shows a pronounced deviation in the state with significant O–H σ* character. These geometric changes correlate directly with the nature of the electronic excitations: for example, the strong H6 tilt in one triplet reflects population of an antibonding σ*­(O1–H6) orbital, which weakens and rotates the O–H bond. In contrast, the growing N5 pyramidalization in S1/T2 matches the assignment of π­(NO) to π*­(NO2) excitation on the nitro group. Putting an electron into the NO2 π* manifold increases lone-pair character of N5 atom and reduces N–O bond order, so that N rehybridizes toward sp 3 and puckers out of the plane. Indeed, analogous nitro-containing systems are known to distort their NO2 groups in the excited state, and our calculated shifts of N5, O1, and H6 are entirely consistent with the bonding changes implied by the MOs involved.

In the ground state S0 the N5 lone-pair basin is relatively small, reflecting its sp 2-like environment. Upon electronic excitation, the ELF basin associated with N5 increases significantly, with the most pronounced growth observed in the T1 state. Quantitatively, the nitrogen-centered HELV (the integrated volume of the N–lone-pair basin) increases stepwise from S0 to T1. This signifies that the N5 lone pair becomes more localized and voluminous in the excited states, which is the electronic counterpart of the observed pyramidalization. In other words, as N5 bends out of the molecular plane, more electron density accumulates in its nonbonding region. This trend is clearly visible in Figure : the ELF isosurface around N5 expands and intensifies from (a), (b), (d) and to (c) as clear ascending order. The correlation between increased N–ELF volume and geometric pyramidalization is exactly what one expects when a π to π* excitation on NO2 creates a nonbonding-like orbital on N atom. In this sense, the ELF analysis offers a direct real-space picture of the evolution of the nitrogen lone pair along the photodissociation pathways of HO2NO2, demonstrating that electronic excitation promotes nitro-group pyramidalization accompanied by a concomitant growth of the ELF basin. The ESP maps in Figure e–h provide an independent real-space view of the excitation-induced redistribution of charge, complementing the ELF analysis discussed above. In each panel, the ESP is mapped onto the molecular electron-density surface, allowing the evolution of the local electrostatic environment to be visualized directly. Compared with the ground state (S0), the excited states exhibit a more diffuse N-centered lone-pair region, as reflected by the expansion of the corresponding ELF basin. This enhanced spatial extent indicates a redistribution of electron density in the nonbonding region, which is accompanied by a change in the local ESP around N toward a less positive value. This trend is qualitatively consistent with previous studies, ,, which reported a link between ELF basin evolution and electrostatic response. The ESP behavior is also consistent with the HELV analysis in Table . In this oxygen-rich environment, the nitrogen center is strongly polarized by the surrounding electron-withdrawing oxygen atoms; therefore, even a modest redistribution of electron density can produce a noticeable change in the local ESP. Conversely, the region around H6 also changes upon excitation, reflecting the altered polarization of the O–H bond when H6 moves out of the molecular plane. Overall, the ESP and ELF analyses offer complementary pictures of the same excitation-driven electronic reorganization in the excited states.

Here, r and θ are defined with respect to the MEX point of the potential energy surface CI as the origin. In Figure a, the S0 and S1 energy curves cross the intersection line of the two cylinders, confirming the sloped character of the intersection. The single minimum on the S0 curve identifies the unique relaxation direction on the lower surface, while the corresponding minimum on the S1 curve represents the preferred approach path toward the intersection on the upper surface. In Figure b, the T1 and T2 curves do not cross the degeneracy line E deg, consistent with a peaked intersection. Both curves exhibit two minima, directly reflecting the bifurcating nature of the relaxation on the lower surface. From a dynamical perspective, although the sloped single-path topology of the S0/S1 MEX favors a unique downhill relaxation pathway on the lower adiabatic surface, the strong nonadiabatic coupling in this region leads to highly recurrent bidirectional hopping. As shown in Figure a, the trajectories exhibit frequent recrossing between S0 and S1 (448 hops from S0 → S1 and 387 hops from S1 → S0), resulting in a total of 835 exchanges. The corresponding difference transition matrix in Figure b reveals a net flow of 61 hops. Taken together, these results indicate that the S0/S1 seam acts as a strongly coupled, recurrent nonadiabatic gateway characterized by repeated recrossing events, rather than a simple irreversible decay channel. Transitions involving triplet states (T1 and T2) are significantly less frequent.

The implications for photodissociation depend on where the dissociative coordinate is accessed. If dissociation proceeds predominantly on S1, rapid internal conversion to S0 would tend to suppress photodissociation. However, if the dissociative pathways are also accessible on S0, population transfer back to the ground state can still be productive. Thus, the S0/S1 coupling primarily governs population redistribution rather than acting as a purely inhibiting or promoting channel. In contrast, the peaked bifurcating T1/T2 MEX can redistribute population into two competing relaxation channels. However, as shown in Figure , net transitions involving the triplet states are significantly less frequent, indicating that triplet-mediated pathways make only a limited contribution to the present dynamics. These behaviors are consistent with established theoretical insights into the role of intersection topology in nonadiabatic photochemical dynamics.

The numerical energies in Table show that both MEX points lie high above the S0 minimum (3.4327 and 4.4512 eV, respectively, for S0/S1 and T1/T2 at the CASSCF level), but in photochemical processes the system may access these regions via vertical excitation. CASPT2 single-point corrections lower these values to 3.4050–3.4218 and 4.2542–4.2610 eV, respectively. We note that a small energy splitting appears between the two states after CASPT2 single-point correction. This is expected because the MEX geometries were optimized at the CASSCF level, and CASPT2 energies were computed as single points without further geometry relaxation. In this work, a vertical S0 →S1 excitation of HO2NO2 could reach with photoexcitation energy of 3.54–6.20 eV, making the S0/S1 CI accessible. Once excited, the system is expected to relax on the S1 surface until it encounters the sloped single-path CI, funneling population back to S0. On the triplet side, the T1 minimum lies 2.6304 eV above S0 minimum, so the T1/T2 intersection at 4.4512 eV is quite high; nonetheless, if population is trapped on T1, for example by intersystem crossing with strong SOC constants, it could also find this bifurcating seam. The two-path character of the T1/T2 CI might lead to different T1 → T2 decay products, whereas the S1/S0 CI would likely return the system to a single S0 geometry. These observations are consistent with the general view that CIs are crucial mediators of photochemical reactivity. As Domcke and Guo note, − CI serves as “funnels” connecting excited and ground states and strongly influence product distributions. In the present system, the sloped single-path S0/S1 intersection is expected to promote efficient but topologically constrained relaxation, whereas the peaked bifurcating T1/T2 intersection provides a more structured decay landscape. It is emphasized that these conclusions are strictly local to the immediate vicinity of the MEX points. The ultimate photochemical outcome is expected to depend not only on the local topology captured by the linear branching-plane (g–h) model, but also on the global structure of the potential energy surfaces and on nonadiabatic dynamical effects beyond this local description.

Nonadiabatic Dynamics

On-the-fly ab initio nonadiabatic molecular dynamics simulations were carried out to elucidate the photodissociation dynamics of peroxynitric acid and to assess the dynamical consequences of the CI topology discussed above. As illustrated in Figure , the sampled starting geometries cluster tightly around the S0 equilibrium structure, ensuring a well-defined Franck–Condon region and minimizing bias from excessive initial vibrational excitation. The trajectories were propagated semiclassically with Tully’s fewest-switches surface-hopping algorithm, while electronic structure information was evaluated along the nuclear trajectories. A total of 260 initial conditions were generated by Wigner sampling around the optimized S0 minimum geometry. Reactions occurring on the singlet states dominate the overall photodissociation dynamics. Following vertical excitation into the energy window 3.54–6.20 eV, two distinct photodissociation channels are observed, as summarized in the potential-energy profile shown in Figure .

Trajectory filtering was performed using a set of predefined quality-control criteria. All trajectories were propagated up to a maximum simulation time of 600.0 fs, and only those reaching at least 50.0 fs were included in the statistical analysis. We required the stepwise total-energy change to be below 3.0 eV, the hop-induced active-state energy change to be below 3.0 eV, the stepwise active-state potential-energy change to be below 3.0 eV, the stepwise kinetic-energy change to be below 3.0 eV, and the full-trajectory total-energy drift to be below 3.0 eV. The total population drift was limited to 1 × 10–7, and trajectories exhibiting intruder-state events were excluded. In addition, trajectories had to possess complete output information and terminate normally. The 148 discarded trajectories were removed solely because they violated one or more of these objective criteria, such as abnormal termination, unconverged step, excessive energy drift, or population nonconservation. Since these filters were defined a priori and applied uniformly to all trajectories, the remaining 112 trajectories constitute a statistically consistent ensemble for the observables reported here. Under the present conditions, 110 out of the 112 trajectories dissociate into the NO2 + HO2 channel, whereas only two trajectories evolve toward NO3 + OH. This pronounced branching asymmetry indicates that the NO2 + HO2 channel is strongly favored on the excited-state potential-energy surface accessed by the initial photoexcitation. And it is noteworthy in the context of previous experimental and modeling studies. Specifically, dominated statistically valid trajectories lead to NO2 + HO2, corresponding to a branching ratio of approximately 98%, whereas only two trajectories yield NO3 + OH. This strong preference for the HO2 + NO2 channel contrasts with the quantum yields currently adopted in NASA data evaluations, which assign comparable contributions to both channels based primarily on earlier 248 nm laboratory measurements. ,, However, the present multireference nonadiabatic dynamics results are in closer agreement with the updated experimental findings of Ravishankara and co-workers than with the earlier parametrizations used in atmospheric modeling. From a mechanistic standpoint, this agreement can be rationalized by the topological features of the S0/S1 conical intersection identified above. The sloped single-path topology funnels population efficiently toward geometries that promote N–O bond cleavage leading to NO2 + HO2, while providing limited dynamical access to configurations conducive to O–O bond rupture required for NO3 + OH formation. Moreover, accurate theoretical treatment of this system requires a multiconfigurational description of the excited states and their intersections. Single-reference methods are inadequate to capture the near-degeneracies and nonadiabatic coupling effects that govern branching in HO2NO2 photodissociation. The present CASSCF-based surface-hopping approach explicitly incorporates these effects and thus provides a mechanistically grounded explanation for the experimentally observed dominance of the NO2 + HO2 channel. Taken together, these results suggest that the branching ratio currently employed in some atmospheric modeling frameworks may overestimate the contribution of the OH + NO3 channel in the actinic UV region. While quantitative refinement would require inclusion of wavelength-dependent excitation profiles and dynamic correlation corrections, the present topology-resolved dynamics clearly support a strongly biased photodissociation pathway favoring HO2 + NO2.

Overall, the FSSH simulations demonstrate that the photodissociation of HO2NO2 following S1 excitation is governed by rapid, efficient nonadiabatic relaxation through a sloped single-path CI, which strongly biases the system toward the NO2 + HO2 product channel. Conclusion

In this work, the photodissociation dynamics of peroxynitric acid in the atmospherically relevant ultraviolet region were investigated using a combination of CASSCF calculations and nonadiabatic molecular dynamics simulations within the surface hopping framework. Particular emphasis was placed on elucidating the role of CIs and their local topological characteristics in governing excited-state relaxation and reaction branching. Systematic CASSCF calculations with varying active spaces and basis sets establish that the essential ground- and excited-state geometrical features are well captured at the CAS­(10e,8o)/aug-cc-pVDZ level, providing a computationally efficient yet reliable reference for on-the-fly dynamics. Electronic excitation to the lowest singlet excited state induces pronounced pyramidalization at the nitro nitrogen, accompanied by significant redistribution of electron density, as revealed by both ELF and ESP analyses. These changes reflect the population of antibonding orbitals with substantial NO2 π* character and provide a clear electronic-structure origin for the observed structural distortions.

MEX optimizations identify two qualitatively distinct intersection topologies. The S0/S1 crossing is classified as a sloped single-path intersection, characterized by a continuous downhill direction on the upper surface and a unique relaxation pathway on the lower surface. In contrast, the T1/T2 crossing exhibits a peaked bifurcating topology, with a local minimum on the upper surface and two competing relaxation channels on the lower surface. Angular energy profiles in the branching plane provide a transparent visualization of these differences and directly connect the abstract topological classification to physically intuitive relaxation pathways. Nonadiabatic dynamics simulations using the TSSH simulations demonstrate that photoexcited HO2NO2 undergoes ultrafast relaxation dominated by the S1 → S0 internal conversion mediated by the sloped S0/S1 intersection. Surface hopping events are strongly localized near the MEX region, confirming the central role of the intersection seam in controlling population transfer. Consistent with the weak SOCs obtained along the trajectories, triplet-state populations remain minimal throughout the dynamics. The resulting product branching is overwhelmingly dominated by the NO2 + HO2 channel, with only a minor contribution from NO3 + OH formation. These results align with a recent ML-PES FSSH study that observed high HO2 + NO2 yields from higher excitation in HO2NO2, with no OH + NO3 formation, though restricted to one conformer and two degrees of freedom. While that work demonstrates efficiency in avoiding explicit nonadiabatic couplings, our topology classification of sloped S0/S1 and peaked T1/T2 intersections provides mechanistic insights into relaxation and negligible triplet roles. Future integrations of ML scalability with topological analysis could enhance modeling of multiconformer atmospheric impacts.

Taken together, these results provide a coherent picture in which the local topology of CIs acts as a decisive factor in shaping nonadiabatic relaxation and photochemical outcomes. While the present analysis focuses on the linear branching-plane description in the immediate vicinity of the MEX points, the findings underscore how topological features encoded at these crossings can exert a disproportionate influence on the global photodynamics. The insights obtained here are expected to be broadly relevant to other multifunctional nitrogen oxide systems, where weak absorption in the atmospherically relevant UV and efficient nonadiabatic relaxation pathways play a critical role in atmospheric photochemistry.
