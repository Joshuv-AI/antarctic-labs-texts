Risk of cataract in patients with systemic lupus erythematosus: a retrospective cohort study in Taiwan
Authors: De-Yi Liu, Ching Chung, Ying-Tso Wang, Hsiu-Ming Lee, Ting-An Cheng, Chia-An Lin, Yu-Chen Su, Pei-Ti Yao, Te-Chun Shen, Heng-Jun Lin, Fuu-Jen Tsai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo investigate whether systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) increases the risk of cataract development and to evaluate the impact of corticosteroid use and dosage on this risk.DesignNationwide retrospective cohort study.SettingUsing Taiwan’s National Health Insurance (NHI) database, which covers over 99.9% of the population.ParticipantsThe SLE cohort included 30 501 newly diagnosed adults from 2011 to 2020. For each patient with SLE, four individuals without SLE were selected from the NHI database using frequency matching by age (in 5 year intervals), gender and index year of diagnosis, resulting in a comparison cohort of 122 004 individuals.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcome was incident cataract. Secondary outcomes included risk stratification by age, sex, comorbidities and corticosteroid dose.ResultsSLE patients had a higher incidence of cataracts than non-SLE individuals (adjusted HR (aHR) = 1.73, 95% CI 1.66 to 1.81). Stratified analyses showed elevated risks in women (aHR=1.74, 95% CI 1.66 to 1.83), men (aHR=1.68, 95% CI 1.52 to 1.86), and across age groups 20–49 years (aHR=2.32, 95% CI 2.11 to 2.56), 50–64 years (aHR=1.60, 95% CI 1.51 to 1.69), and ≥65 years (aHR=1.50, 95% CI 1.36 to 1.66). Analysis of corticosteroid exposure revealed that cumulative dose showed a trend towards increased risk at high exposure (adjusted OR (aOR) = 1.14, 95% CI 0.99 to 1.31), while average daily dose demonstrated a dose–response effect: 1–5 mg/day (aOR=1.31, 95% CI 1.13 to 1.52) and ≥5 mg/day (OR=2.48, 95% CI 2.16 to 2.86).ConclusionsAdults with SLE have an increased risk of developing cataracts compared with matched controls, and higher average daily corticosteroid doses are associated with this risk. These findings highlight the need for careful monitoring of ocular complications in SLE patients.

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic autoimmune disease that affects multiple organ systems. It is characterised by organ damage resulting from a dysregulated immune response, initially driven by autoantibodies and immune complex deposits.1 The pathogenesis of SLE is multifactorial, involving genetic predisposition, hormonal factors and environmental triggers.2 SLE predominantly affects women of childbearing age, with a female-to-male ratio of approximately 9:1.3 Its diverse clinical manifestations reflect its systemic nature and the potential for immune-mediated injury to nearly any organ or system, including the cutaneous, musculoskeletal, renal, cardiovascular, haematological, neuropsychiatric and ocular systems. These manifestations contribute to significant morbidity and mortality.1 Ocular complications in SLE are not uncommon, with around one-third of patients experiencing eye involvement, such as keratoconjunctivitis sicca, episcleritis, scleritis and retinal vasculitis. Symptoms include eye pain, dryness, redness and, in severe cases, sight-threatening conditions,4 which can substantially affect the quality of life.

Cataract is a condition characterised by reduced lens transparency and increasing cloudiness.5 It is the leading cause of reversible visual impairment worldwide, accounting for half of all cases of blindness.6 The estimated annual incidence rate is 13.2 per 1000 individuals.5 Cataract formation involves the modification, aggregation and precipitation of crystallin proteins within the lens, leading to increased rigidity and loss of transparency. Common risk factors include advancing age, female gender, non-Caucasian ethnicity, lower socioeconomic status, smoking, diabetes, hypertension, corticosteroid use, ultraviolet B exposure and metabolic disorders such as hypocalcaemia, Wilson’s disease and myotonic dystrophy.7 The immunological impact of rheumatic diseases on cataract formation may result from an imbalance of ocular cytokines and growth factors.4 8

Several studies have reported the association between SLE and cataract formation,913 often attributing it to immune dysregulation and corticosteroid use. However, these studies were limited by small sample sizes, study design and follow-up duration. Therefore, this study aimed to use a large-scale retrospective cohort to investigate the association between SLE and cataract risk, while also assessing the impact of corticosteroid use and dosage on cataract development.

Materials and methods

Data source

The data used in this study were obtained from Taiwan’s National Health Insurance (NHI) database. As a programme that provides universal healthcare coverage, the NHI database includes detailed medical records for all outpatient visits and hospital admissions, encompassing diagnosis codes and prescribed medications. By the end of 2014, NHI covered 99.9% of Taiwan’s population. Disease diagnoses were coded using the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM), and Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM).

Ethics and consent

The Taiwan National Health Insurance database is a secondary healthcare database provided by the Taiwan Ministry of Health and Welfare, containing only de-identified personal data. Written informed consent was waived, and the use of this data was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of China Medical University Hospital (CMUH111-REC2-109-CR-2).

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the study.

Study population

Subjects newly diagnosed with SLE (ICD-9-CM: 710.0; ICD-10-CM: M32) between 2011 and 2020 were identified as the SLE group (figure 1). The date of the initial SLE diagnosis was defined as the index date. For the non-SLE group, individuals were selected from the same database at a 1:4 ratio using frequency matching by age (in 5 year intervals), gender and index year to ensure comparability between groups. Exclusions included individuals with a prior history of cataracts, those under 20 years and those with incomplete basic information.

Main outcome and relevant variables

The primary outcome of interest was the occurrence of cataracts (ICD-9-CM: 366; ICD-10-CM: H25, H26, H28), defined by at least two outpatient claims or one inpatient claim. Patients were followed from the index date until cataract development, death or the end of the observation period on 31 December 2021. Comorbidities considered in the analysis included hypertension (ICD-9: 401–405; ICD-10: I10–I16), diabetes mellitus (ICD-9: 250; ICD-10: E08–E13), hyperlipidaemia (ICD-9: 272; ICD-10: E78), chronic liver disease (CLD; ICD-9: 571; ICD-10: K70–K77) and chronic kidney disease (CKD; ICD-9: 585; ICD-10: N18). Corticosteroid use (Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical Classification System code: H02, defined as use for >28 days) was also analysed.

Statistical analysis

Baseline demographic data for categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages, while continuous variables such as age and follow-up time were presented as means with SD. The chi-squared test and t-tests/Wilcoxon rank-sum test were used to compare categorical and continuous variables, respectively, between the SLE and non-SLE groups. Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan-Meier method to generate cumulative incidence curves, and the log-rank test was used for comparison. The Cox proportional hazards regression model was applied to calculate HR and 95% CI, adjusting for variables such as age, sex and comorbidities. Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS V.9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) and R V.4.3.1, with a p value <0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Table 1 provides an overview of the demographic characteristics of the study population. The study included 30 501 patients in the SLE group and 122 004 individuals in the control (non-SLE) group. Both groups exhibited similar baseline distributions regarding gender and age. In both cohorts, 82.7% of the participants were female, and the average age was 43.5±13.8 years. The most common comorbidities in the SLE cohort were CLD (23.8%), hyperlipidaemia (22.9%) and hypertension (20.6%). The mean follow-up times were 5.31±3.01 and 5.63±2.93 years in the SLE and comparison cohorts, respectively.

Figure 2 shows that patients with SLE had a significantly higher cumulative incidence of cataracts compared with those without SLE (p <0.001). Table 2 presents the risk assessment for cataract development in the entire study population. After adjusting for sex, age and comorbidities, patients with SLE (adjusted HR=1.73, 95% CI 1.66 to 1.81) had a significantly higher risk of developing cataracts than those without SLE. Men had a lower risk of cataracts compared with women (adjusted HR=0.75, 95% CI 0.71 to 0.79). Table 4 displays the risk of cataract development associated with corticosteroid use among SLE patients. While cumulative glucocorticoid dose was not consistently associated with cataract risk, higher cumulative exposure showed a borderline significant trend toward increased risk (adjusted OR=1.14, 95% CI 0.99 to 1.31). In contrast, average daily dose demonstrated a clear dose–response relationship: patients receiving 1–5 mg/day had a significantly higher risk of cataracts (adjusted OR=1.31, 95% CI 1.13 to 1.52), and those receiving ≥5 mg/day had an even greater risk (adjusted OR=2.48, 95% CI 2.16 to 2.86).

Discussion

This population-based retrospective cohort study was conducted to evaluate the incidence of cataracts in individuals with and without SLE. We found that the risk of cataracts was generally higher in older individuals, females and those with comorbidities. The main finding revealed that patients with SLE have a significantly higher risk of developing cataracts compared with those without SLE. This increased risk remained significant even after stratification by age, gender and comorbidities. Furthermore, SLE patients receiving moderate to high doses of corticosteroids had a higher risk of cataract development than those receiving lower doses.

The mechanisms linking SLE to cataract formation remain unclear. In SLE patients, persistent inflammatory activity and autoimmune processes may contribute to ocular complications, including an increased risk of cataract development. Several factors have been proposed to explain this association: (1) immune system hyperactivity: SLE is characterised by an overactive immune response, which can damage ocular tissues; (2) light and oxidative stress: SLE patients may be more susceptible to the harmful effects of light exposure and oxidative stress, which can damage ocular tissues and cause lens degeneration, thereby increasing the risk of cataract formation; (3) corticosteroid use: SLE patients often undergo treatment with anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive agents, particularly corticosteroids. Prolonged corticosteroid use is associated with a heightened risk of cataract development and (4) other ocular conditions: SLE may also be associated with other ocular issues, such as corneal inflammation or retinitis, which could further contribute to cataract formation.4 14 15 In summary, SLE appears to increase the risk of cataract development through mechanisms involving immune dysregulation, inflammation and oxidative stress. However, further research is needed to elucidate the precise mechanisms involved.

Several previous studies have explored the relationship between SLE and cataracts. Alderaan et al9 evaluated 2109 patients with SLE and found 157 cases of cataracts during follow-up. They reported an incidence of 13.2 per 1000 person-years (157/11 887) among SLE patients, which is comparable to our data (19.9 per 1000 person-years, 3713/186 755). Among SLE patients, they found that cumulative corticosteroid dose and high systolic blood pressure were associated with cataract development. In the present study, we also found that corticosteroid dose influenced the risk of cataract development in SLE patients. Dammacco et al10 evaluated 98 patients with SLE and found that 29 patients (29.6%) had ocular disorders, with 20 patients (20.4%) having more than one ocular disorder. The top three ocular disorders reported were cataracts (21.4%), keratoconjunctivitis sicca (11.2%) and glaucoma (4.1%). Dias-Santos et al11 examined 161 SLE patients and found that 51 patients (31.1%) had at least one ocular disorder. The most frequent disorders were dry eye syndrome (12.4%), cataracts (11.2%) and hydroxychloroquine macular toxicity (11.2%). Hsu et al12 evaluated 521 patients with SLE and 5210 patients without SLE, reporting a prevalence of cataracts of 16.3% in SLE patients compared with 8.3% in non-SLE individuals (adjusted IRR=3.18, p<0.001). These findings collectively indicate that cataracts in SLE patients require special attention.

Strength

The primary strength of this study lies in the establishment of a large SLE cohort and a non-SLE cohort for the purpose of tracking cataract incidence. Additionally, the average follow-up period for participants exceeded 5 years, with no issues of loss to follow-up. Taiwan’s NHI system has an exceptionally high coverage rate, with over 99.9% of the population enrolled, ensuring that individuals from all geographic regions and socioeconomic backgrounds are included. This reduces the potential for selection bias.16 Furthermore, this study represents ‘real-world’ practice results, as all disease diagnoses were based on actual clinical visits and medical records.

Limitation

This study has several limitations.17 18 First, the ICD coding algorithm was used to define SLE, cataracts and comorbidities, meaning all diagnoses relied on the clinical competence of physicians. However, in Taiwan’s NHI system, SLE is classified as a special disease, and its diagnosis requires supporting evidence and third-party expert approval. Similarly, other diseases are monitored by experts to ensure diagnostic accuracy and quality of care. To improve diagnostic accuracy, we only included outpatient diagnoses that appeared at least twice or those associated with hospitalisation records.

Second, the NHI database does not provide detailed information on lifestyle factors such as smoking, alcohol consumption or family history, which could serve as potential confounders in this study. In addition, dynamic clinical variables such as inflammatory biomarkers (eg, CRP, serum amyloid A, IL-6) and other laboratory or imaging data are not available, preventing us from assessing the role of systemic inflammation in cataract development. Furthermore, information on SLE disease activity and specific manifestations (eg, lupus nephritis, neuropsychiatric lupus) was not captured in the database. Since these severe forms of SLE often require higher or prolonged glucocorticoid use, their absence may limit our ability to fully address confounding by indication. Future studies incorporating clinical registries or chart-based disease activity measures will be necessary to clarify the influence of systemic inflammation and disease severity on cataract risk.

Third, although we expanded our analysis to include both cumulative glucocorticoid dose and average daily dose, the NHI claims data do not allow us to fully account for treatment duration, dose tapering or fluctuations in daily exposure. These limitations may affect the precision of glucocorticoid exposure assessment, and the findings should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Lastly, the study population is largely homogeneous in terms of race and ethnicity, as more than 95% of NHI enrollees are Taiwanese nationals. Although certain groups of foreigners, such as international students, migrant workers and foreign spouses, are eligible to participate in the NHI programme, short-term visitors and business travellers are not included. As a result, our findings may not be directly generalisable to populations with greater racial or ethnic diversity.

Conclusion

Adults with SLE have an increased risk of developing cataracts compared with matched controls, and higher average daily corticosteroid doses are associated with this risk. These findings highlight the need for careful monitoring of ocular complications in SLE patients, particularly those receiving corticosteroid therapy. Further studies are warranted to clarify the role of disease activity and long-term treatment patterns. Interpretation should be cautious given the inherent limitations of claims data.
