Hierarchical Co–Ni hydroxides integrated with carbon nanotubes via ZIF-67 templates for high-performance supercapacitors
Authors: Mariam Sh Gohr, Mohamed Abdel Rafea, Wael Wazeer, Sara Gad, Diaa A Rayan, Mohamed H Eisa, Huda F Khalil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSupercapacitors but suffer from low specific capacity and poor rate capability due to limited electrical conductivity. Herein, a zeolitic imidazolate framework (ZIF-67) was employed simultaneously as a cobalt source and a sacrificial template to direct the room-temperature hydrolysis of Ni²⁺ ions, leading to the formation of interwoven Co–Ni LDH nanosheets with enhanced electrolyte-electrode interaction and accelerated ion transport. Incorporation of carbon nanotubes (CNTs) markedly improved conductivity and charge-transfer kinetics, with the optimized CNC30 electrode delivering 870 F g⁻¹ at 1 A g⁻¹ and retaining 85% capacitance at 10 A g⁻¹. Notably, CNCZ30 demonstrated excellent durability, maintaining 96.6% of its initial capacitance after 7000 cycles. Introduction

Growing concerns over the environmental impact of conventional energy consumption particularly issues such as climate change and water pollution have accelerated research into advanced nanomaterial technologies for sustainable energy storage and conversion devices1–3. To meet the rising global energy demand, various energy storage technologies, including fuel cells, lithium-ion batteries, and supercapacitors, have been extensively investigated4. Among them, supercapacitors stand out as vital components of green energy systems due to their long cycle life, high power density, and rapid charge–discharge capability5–7. The performance of supercapacitors is largely determined by the properties of their electrode materials, making the development of high-quality electrodes essential8,9. Supercapacitors are generally categorized into two types: electrical double-layer capacitors (EDLCs), which store energy via electrostatic charge accumulation at the electrode–electrolyte interface, and pseudocapacitors, which rely on fast and reversible redox reactions at the surfaces of electroactive materials10,11. EDLCs offer superior cycling stability but limited capacitance, whereas pseudocapacitors provide higher specific capacitance but suffer from poor long-term stability12,13. To overcome these limitations, nanocomposites integrating pseudocapacitive transition metal oxides with carbon-based EDLC materials have been proposed as promising hybrid electrode systems.

Layered double hydroxides (LDHs), a class of inorganic layered materials with large surface areas, abundant redox-active sites, and ion-exchange capabilities, have attracted significant attention as electrode candidates for supercapacitors14. However, their practical application is restricted by sluggish ion diffusion and inherently low electronic conductivity15. To address these issues, LDHs are often combined with conductive additives such as carbon nanotubes (CNTs), reduced graphene oxide (rGO), MXenes, and zeolitic imidazolate frameworks (ZIFs)8–12,16. CNTs are well-established electrode materials due to their excellent conductivity, high specific surface area, and outstanding structural stability11,17. For instance, Tian et al. fabricated a high-performance reusable supercapacitor using PANI/NCNT@CNT fibers, where the porous architecture of N-doped CNTs facilitated efficient electron and ion transport18. Similarly, ZIFs – metal organic frameworks composed of transition metal ions (e.g., Co²⁺, Zn²⁺) and imidazolate ligands exhibit exceptional thermal/chemical stability, high porosity, and tunable structures, making them effective sacrificial templates for producing porous metal oxides11,19. While ZIF-derived Ni-Co oxides have been widely studied for biosensors14, electrochemical sensors20, and catalysis21, their application in supercapacitors remains relatively underexplored.

Recent studies have increasingly focused on engineering hybrid metal oxide/carbon architectures to overcome the intrinsic limitations of single-component electrode materials. For example, Mohanty et al.22 reported that rational structural modulation of transition metal oxide–carbon composites can significantly enhance charge transfer kinetics and long term cycling stability through optimized electron transport pathways and improved interfacial conductivity. Similarly, emphasized the critical role of interfacial electronic coupling and surface chemistry engineering in boosting pseudocapacitive performance, particularly in metal organic framework (MOF)-derived hybrid systems23. In addition, the integration of mixed transition metal oxides with conductive carbon scaffolds promotes hierarchical porosity, accelerates ion diffusion, and improves capacitance retention under high rate conditions24.

These findings clearly highlight that the synergistic integration of redox-active metal centers, conductive carbon matrices, and tunable porous architectures is essential for constructing next-generation hybrid supercapacitors. However, many previously reported MOF-based systems rely on high-temperature calcination to convert ZIF structures into carbonized derivatives, which may compromise structural integrity and increase energy consumption. Therefore, developing alternative strategies that preserve the intrinsic framework characteristics of ZIF-67 while simultaneously enhancing electrical conductivity and electrochemical activity remains highly desirable.

The integration of binary LDHs, conductive CNTs, and ZIF-67 provides a compelling strategy for constructing advanced hybrid supercapacitors. In this work, we present a distinctive low temperature strategy to engineer a ternary Co–Ni LDH/CNTs@ZIF-67 hierarchical architecture. Unlike traditional methods, our approach leverages ZIF-67 as a self-sacrificial template and cobalt source simultaneously, enabling the growth of interwoven LDH nanosheets directly onto a functionalized CNT framework. This unique configuration not only prevents the structural collapse associated with high-temperature processing but also creates a robust “conductive-redox” synergy that significantly accelerates charge-transfer kinetics. The resulting nanostructures were systematically characterized using XRD, TEM (with FFT/IFFT analysis), FTIR, XPS, and N₂ adsorption–desorption analysis to correlate their unique structural merits with their superior electrochemical performance.

Materials and methods

Materials

Multi Walled Carbon Nano Tubes (MWCNTs)

(OD: 10–20 nm, length: 10–30 nm) obtained from central lab, Central Metallurgical Research and Development Institute- Cairo, Egypt. Sulphuric Acid (H2SO4) 98%, Nitric Acid (HNO3) 69%, purchased from Merk Company, Cobalt (II) nitrate hexahydrate (Co(NO3)2.6H2O, 99%), Nikel (II) nitrate hexahydrate (Ni(NO3)2.6H2O, 99%), Urea (CH₄N₂O, 98%), Ethanol (CH3CH2OH, 99%), Methanol (CH3OH, 99% HPLC analytical grade), 2-methylimidazole (C4H6N2, 99%) and Isopropanol ((CH3)2CHOH), 99.8% ) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. The above-mentioned chemicals possess analytical purity and were used without any purification. The entire experiment was performed with deionized water.

Experimental work

Functionalization of commercial MWCNTs, Step 1

Despite the functionalization of the nanotube surfaces with anionic groups, this procedure increases the solubility of CNTs in water. After adding 0.3 g of MWCNTs to 60 mL of a 3:1 combination of concentrated H2SO4 (98%) and HNO3 (69%), the mixture was sonicated for 2 h in a water bath. After removing the acids with distilled water until the water’s pH was neutral, the activated MWCNTs were dried for 12 h at 100 °C and stored in a desiccator until they were ready for use. The goal of the activation process was to create carboxylic acid, carbonyl, and hydroxide groups on their surface. These groups served as linkers to help the carbon nanotubes and Co–Ni LDH bonds.

Synthesize of Co–Ni LDH-CNTs, Step2 (Fig. 1)

Scheme of materials and Experimental work.

In a typical procedure, Co–Ni LDH-CNTs were prepared by adding 15, 30 mg activated CNTs in 120 mL ethanol and sonicated at room temperature for 60 min to ensure the dispersion of CNTs in the alcoholic solution. After that, 1.1 g of Ni(NO3)2·6H2O and 0.6 g of Co(NO3)2·6H2O were added and kept stirring, 1.9 g of urea was dissolved into the resulting solution after 30 min of shaking. The resulting solution was then transferred into a 200 mL Teflon-lined autoclave and heated for 4 h at 120 °C. The products were collected by centrifugation and washed several times with ethanol and deionized water, respectively. The product was then collected for later use after being dried in an oven set at 60 °C overnight. For comparison, the same procedure was used to synthesize Co–Ni LDH without CNTs.

Synthesized of Co–Ni LDH-CNTs/Zif-67 Composites, Step3 (Fig. 1)

For the synthesized of Co–Ni LDH-CNTs/Zif-67 Composites, 1.3 g of 2-Methylimadazole dissolved in 30 ml Methanol (Sol. A). In another beaker, 1.2 g of Co(No3)2.6H2O dissolved in 30 ml Methanol stirred for 15 min then a certain amount of (Co–Ni LDH) was introduced to the above dispersion (Sol. B) and the solution was magnetically kept stirred for another 0.5 h at ambient temperature. Later, Sol. A was added dropwise into Sol. B then stirred for 24 h at room temperature (25 ◦C). The product was centrifuged (6000 rpm, 20 min), washed for three times by MeOH and isopropanol. In addition, dried at 60 ◦C overnight.

Morphology

The structural features of the prepared nanocomposites were investigated by HR-TEM. As presented in Fig. 3 (a–c), ZIF-67 exhibits irregular polyhedral particles, whereas in the Co–Ni LDH/CNTs (15%) and Co–Ni LDH/CNTs (30%) composites, ultrathin Co–Ni LDH nanosheets are clearly distributed over the tubular CNT framework. This intimate interaction between ZIF-67, CNTs, and the layered double hydroxide facilitates charge transport and provides abundant active sites, which are expected to enhance the electrochemical behavior24. The high-resolution TEM images and corresponding SAED patterns Fig. 3 (a′–c′, a′′–c′′) further verify the crystalline nature of the materials. The measured lattice spacings of 0.23 nm, 0.223 nm, and 0.195 nm were indexed to the (235) plane of ZIF-67, the (110) plane of Co–Ni LDH, and the (220) plane of CoS₂, respectively25. These findings confirm the coexistence of multiple crystalline phases within the hybrid nanostructures, highlighting their suitability for high-performance energy storage applications. The EDS spectra of Co–Ni LDH/CNTs (15%)/ZIF-67 and Co–Ni LDH/CNTs (30%)/ZIF-67 reveal the presence of sulfur, which originates from the sulfuric acid employed during the activation of CNT surfaces. Elemental mapping of ZIF-67, Co–Ni LDH/CNTs (15%)/ZIF-67, and Co–Ni LDH/CNTs (30%)/ZIF-67, Fig. 3 (a–c) further demonstrates a uniform distribution of Ni, N, C, O, S, and Co, confirming the homogeneous integration of Co–Ni LDH nanosheets within the CNT framework. The structural periodicity and crystalline integrity were further validated by FFT analysis (Fig. 4b), which reveals distinct diffraction spots. The corresponding IFFT image (Fig. 4c) clearly resolves the lattice fringes, showing a d-spacing consistent with the XRD results. The high-resolution lattice fringes and the corresponding IFFT (inset) shown in (c) illustrate the atomic arrangement and phase purity across a 2 nm scale. This is quantitatively supported by the lattice spacing profile in (Fig. 4d), which measures a d-spacing of approximately 0.223 nm, demonstrating the precise crystalline growth and structural integrity of the optimized CNCZ15 composite. These results provide clear evidence for the successful fabrication of the Co–Ni LDH/CNTs/ZIF-67 hybrid composites. Moreover, quantitative EDS, Fig. 5, analysis indicates a pronounced reduction in the nitrogen content of Co–Ni LDH/CNTs (15%)/ZIF-67 (0.5%) and Co–Ni LDH/CNTs (30%)/ZIF-67 (4.2%) compared with pristine ZIF-67 (16.31%). This decrease is attributed to the consumption of ZIF-67 nanoparticles as a sacrificial template during the in-situ growth of the Co–Ni LDH phase.

The Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectra analysis

FTIR spectroscopy was employed to investigate the chemical structure and surface functionalities of CN, Z67, CNZ, and their composite variations (CNC15, CNC30, CNCZ15, CNCZ30). As shown in the spectra Fig. 6, all samples exhibited a characteristic broad absorption band near 3400 cm− 1, attributed to the O–H stretching vibrations of adsorbed moisture or surface hydroxyl groups. The spectrum of ZIF-67 (Z67) distinctly featured peaks between 1570 and 1420 cm− 126 and 1140–990 cm− 127, confirming the presence of the ZIF framework through the characteristic C = N and C–N stretching vibrations of its imidazolate linkers. For the CN samples, the presence of cobalt-nickel oxides was confirmed by the appearance of metal-oxygen (M–O) stretching vibrations below 700 cm− 128. The successful integration of multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) in the CNC15 and CNC30 composites was evidenced by additional peaks around 1620 cm− 1 corresponding to C = C skeletal vibrations and an intensified O–H band at ~ 3400 cm− 1, suggesting strong interfacial bonding. Furthermore, the hybrid composites (CNCZ15, CNCZ30) displayed characteristic features of both ZIF-67 and MWCNTs, confirming their successful synthesis and integration. Subtle shifts and changes in band intensity indicate electronic interactions among the metal centers, ZIF ligands, and carbon nanotubes, which are crucial for enhancing the structural stability and functional performance of the composites, see Table 1.

The pore structures and specific surface area BET analysis

The N₂ adsorption/desorption isotherms, BET plots, and BJH pore size distributions of the synthesized composites display features of type-I isotherms, consistent with IUPAC classification (Figs. 7 and 8). The sharp increase in adsorption at low relative pressure (P/P₀ < 0.1) indicates the dominance of micropores29. Both micropores and mesopores are crucial in determining the electrochemical behavior of supercapacitor electrodes: micropores provide abundant active sites for charge adsorption, while mesopores facilitate the diffusion and storage of larger electrolyte ions. According to IUPAC, pores are categorized as micropores (< 2 nm), mesopores (2–50 nm), and macropores (> 50 nm)30. The pore structure analysis of the prepared composites confirmed a predominance of microporous and mesoporous architectures, as summarized in Table 2. All synthesized samples exhibited significant surface areas and pore volumes, making them favorable for supercapacitor applications. ZIF-67 demonstrated a very high BET surface area of 1560.93 m²/g, a total pore volume of 0.62 cm³/g, and an average pore diameter of 1.6 nm, confirming microporous characteristics. CN@LDH displayed a BET surface area of 462.92 m²/g, a pore volume of 0.44 cm³/g, and an average pore diameter of 3.8 nm, suggesting a mesoporous framework. CNC30 exhibited a large surface area of 805.36 m²/g, with a pore volume of 0.64 cm³/g and an average pore diameter of 3.2 nm, while CNC15 showed similar values with a surface area of 789.33 m²/g, pore volume of 0.63 cm³/g, and average diameter of 3.2 nm, highlighting uniform mesoporosity.

Electrochemical analysis

Figure 12 (a) shows the CV curves for CN, CNC15, and CNC30 samples at a scan rate of 5 mV/s. They showed an oxidation peak nearly at 450 mV and a reduction peak at 250 mV. Stability results

The specific capacitance is increased during activation cycles at 1 A/g from 790 to 973 F/g after 10 cycles (Fig. S2). This activation is due to the reduction of oxides and the formation of Ni-Co hydroxides during discharge48. Figure 14 shows the stability curves for the CNCZ30 sample. During cycling at 2 A/g, the specific capacitance continues to increase up to 1047 F/g (112%) after 800 cycles, then the capacitance starts to decrease gradually down to 978 F/g (105%) after 3000 cycles. The capacity loss rate increases after about 3000 cycles, reaching 768 F/g (82%) after 5000 cycles at 2 A/g. The cycling at 4 A/g after 5000 cycles showed a relatively lower specific capacitance compared to GCD at 4 A/g capacitance for the fresh electrode (592 F/g compared to 834 F/g). The formation of hydroxides increases the capacitance for a certain limit. After this limit, the electrode resistance increases reducing its rate capability. The capacity retention after the last 2000 cycles (total 7000 cycles) was 96.6%.

Conclusion

The combined structural, textural, electrochemical, and surface analyses confirm that the studied systems represent complementary strategies for constructing hybrid nanostructured electrodes. XRD demonstrated the successful integration of LDH, CNTs, and ZIF-67 into multifunctional composites, while BET analysis revealed that increasing CNT and LDH content systematically reduces surface area and mesoporosity, yet maintains a mesoporous framework suitable for fast ion transport. Electrochemical testing highlighted that CNT incorporation markedly enhances specific capacitance, conductivity, and rate performance, with CNC30 achieving 870 F/g at 1 A/g and 85% retention at 10 A/g. The addition of ZIF-67 further improved porosity and ion diffusion, enabling CNCZ30 to deliver 902.7 F/g at 1 A/g, 72% retention at 10 A/g, and exceptional cycling stability with 96.6% retention after 7000 cycles. XPS confirmed the coexistence of Co²⁺/Co³⁺ and Ni²⁺/Ni³⁺ states, incorporation of N-doped carbon functionalities, and evidence of charge transfer between LDH nanosheets and the conductive carbon framework, which underpins the enhanced electron transport. Collectively, these results establish CNT/ZIF-67-engineered Ni–Co LDH composites as robust and high-performance candidates for next-generation supercapacitor applications.
