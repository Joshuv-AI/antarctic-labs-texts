The Legacy of Seymour Levine
Authors: Michael J Meaney
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis essay assumes an historical perspective intended to highlight the major contributions of Seymour “Gig” Levine to the conceptual scaffold that guides so much of the current research in development and psychopathology. I suggest that perhaps Levine's most important contribution was to establish an experimental framework that served to transition the clinical observations of Freud and the attachment theories of Bowlby into modern Psychology/Psychiatry.

Please forgive as I briefly wax autobiographically. The intent is to historically position the contribution of Levine in the broader context of the developmental sciences. This essay is intended to provide the reader with an opportunity to reflect on the pivotal conceptual importance of the research of Seymour ‘Gig” Levine and the framework it provided for so much of our current research into the nature of human development. There are other papers in this volume (de Kloet, 2026; Schmidt, 2026) that provide a more detailed summary of the scope and impact of Levine's research to which I would add that of the wonderful review of Stern et al. (2010) and that of Cirulli and Suchecki (2010). My ambition is to focus more on the importance of the conceptual advances that, along with Levine's contemporaries, formed the scientific base upon which has emerged so much of the current research on the influence of early experience on brain development and function.

I was at a time an undergraduate student with a devotion to Developmental Biology. Developmental Biology of that era, the 1970's, was a hybrid of Embryology and Genetics. The central quest was to understand the nature and action of the “blueprint” from which emerged the form and function of a species. The material that formed the commanding template was assumed to be entirely genetic in nature and instructional in action. My peers were regaled by studies of amphibian form and its emergence, and the differences across seemingly related species. My wonderment, however, was less what made a salamander a salamander and a frog a frog, and more so why individual members of these species might look and function so differently from one another. Variation in form and function across members of a species was not a topic of interest to developmental biologists of the day. It was a complication, if it was considered at all. My very sympathetic Genetics professor suggested I enroll in courses in Psychology.

I assumed that within the domain of Psychology I would find brethren likewise captivated by individual variation. And to some extent I did. But Psychology itself was plagued by profoundly misguided assumptions concerning the nature of brain development and function. At the heart of the delusion lay the acceptance that certain features of life, including some of profound importance for brain function, were “innate”. A brief historical review of this concept in the developmental sciences of the day underscores the critical importance of the science of Seymour ‘Gig” Levine in advancing the next generation of scientists conceptually beyond this conceptual morass.

According to the Meriam-Webster dictionary, the term innate “describes a quality, ability, or characteristic that you are born with, rather than one you have learned or acquired through experience. It (innate) refers to something that is a natural and essential part of your character or biology.” In its search for the essence of development, Psychology continued to pay homage to the idea that there was indeed merit in an understanding of innately determined traits, including those expressed by humans. At the heart of the confusion was the dichotomy between characteristics that were biologically determined, and thus assumed to be invariant, and those that were acquired through experiential interactions with the environment, thus “learned”. This formulation included elements of personality, including and perhaps especially those considered to be ‘emotional’ in nature. Thus, for example, individual differences in temperament, as so accurately described by Chess and Thomas in the 1970's (e.g., Chess and Thomas, 1984), were apparent in newborns and hence assumed to be “inborn” and entirely heritable. This presumption was based solely on the fact that such variation was present at birth and could therefore not have been “learned”. The origins of individual differences in traits such as shyness were then considered to be of genetic origin. It was as if rigid borders had been drawn to affirm the exclusivity between analyses of development that emphasized “biological” as opposed to “social” levels of explanation. This schism will, I trust, appear anachronistic to many in the field of developmental psychobiology. But I can assure you the confusion continues to linger amongst many social scientists who somehow cannot fathom that a social event has a biological representation. Levine did, and a substantial contribution of his research was to provide clear examples of how social and biological factors represent different levels of analysis, not explanation.

Psychology, Biology and Clinical Medicine have all moved on from the idea that characteristics of an individual present at birth are necessarily “inborn” thus solely genetic in determination. We now appropriately consider the uterine environment as one that varies and bestows variation in structure and function. Indeed Levine and his students contributed substantially to a revised appreciation of prenatal influences on later brain function. But what of the other side of the coin? What of characteristics that were “learned or acquired through experience”? And how might such processes be embedded in biology and sustained over the lifespan?

As luck would have it my professor for Psychology 101 course was a newly minted PhD from Wisconsin, the bastion of Harry Harlow and studies of peer-versus maternally-reared Rhesus monkeys (Harlow and Suomi, 1970). As most of you will know, results of such maternal deprivation were devastating for the young. Coupled to these studies were those of the Hebb-inspired research on the no-less profound effects of environmental enrichment (Hebb, 1949). Experience clearly nurtured variation. But there nevertheless lingered the prevailing notion that, as Merriam-Webster stated, certain physiological functions were “a natural and essential part of your character or biology” and thus immune from the divergency derived from experience. And what could possibly be more ‘essential’ for life that the cardio-metabolic and endocrine responses to conditions that threatened homeostasis?

By the 1950's Walter Canon and Hans Selye had described the nature of the defensive responses to conditions that threatened homeostasis. The nervous system brought catecholamines to bear, forming the basis for cardio-metabolic adaptation. The hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal system furnished glucocorticoids, the ideal complement to cardio-metabolic activation with the added benefit of immunological restraint. Surely nothing could be more fundamental to survival, and thus universal in expression. It was exactly this latter assumption that was so brilliantly challenged by Levine, as well as contemporaries such as Victor Denenberg (Hess et al., 1969). It is with reason that the initial series of scientific papers from these pioneers were routinely published in Science and Nature.

As clearly described in the detailed account provided by Stern, Weinberg and Hennessy (Stern et al., 2010; also see Schmidt, 2026 in this compilation) the very simple neonatal handling studies with rodents very clearly demonstrated that even the most fundamental features of the biological response to stressors were subject to environmental influence and to thus reveal individual variation. In this procedure the mother and then the pups are removed from the nest for a brief period of separation, usually 15 min (Levine, 1957, 1959, 1960). Levine was fully aware that this period mapped onto the duration of normal forays by the mother from the nest and therefore did not represent a period of maternal deprivation. This handling was then repeated daily through to weaning, although Levine later showed that implementation for only the first week of life was sufficient. As adults, the handled animals showed reduced behavioral reactivity to stressors. As adults, the animals handled during infancy were markedly less fearful of novelty and more explorative in new environments (Levine, 1962; Levine et al., 1967, Levine et al., 1967). These behavioral effects were expected as the pup handling aligned with ‘taming’ procedures commonly employed by breeders to reduce fearfulness and aggression across a range of species.

The revolutionary impact of the infant handling studies lay in the enduring effects on HPA responses to stress. Recall that it was widely accepted that the physiological adaptations to stressors were so fundamental to survival as to be invariant across the species. Hence the belief that these traits emerged solely as a function of the developmental blueprint and outside the dominion of environmental influences. Levine's studies falsified such assumptions. Adult rodents handled in infancy showed markedly reduced HPA activation in response to the acute or chronic exposure to a wide range of stressors. The results were successfully replicated across a number of independent laboratories, including the amazing trans-generational studies by Denenberg and colleagues (Denenberg and Whimbey, 1963; Denenberg and Rosenberg, 1967).

An enormous strength of Levine's science, and indeed that of the developmental psychobiological research of the day, was the quest for a deeper level of understanding of the processes reflected in experimental studies of environmental influences. Levine and his students observed that the return to the nest of the mother and pup following the termination of the period of infant handling was followed by an intensified level of maternal care. The altered stimulus features of pups, such as vocalizations, that enhanced the level of maternal care including pup licking (Brown et al., 1977; Hennessy et al., 1980; Levine, 2001). Maternal licking of the pups was a critical feature of the handling-induced enhanced maternal care and, ultimately, for the effect of handling on both behavior and HPA function in adulthood (e.g., Liu et al., 1996; Cameron et al., 2005; Zhang et al., 2006). Indeed, naturally-occurring variations in maternal care, in the absence of any experimental manipulation, reproduced the effect of neonatal handling on both behavior and physiology. But the great strength of the science of this period was to position maternal care within the context of pup physiology and development. Myron Hofer at Columbia (Hofer, 1970, 1981) and Saul Schanberg at Duke added immeasurably to the scope of these findings by showing that mother – pup interactions directly regulated biological processes that underlay growth. For example, Kuhn et al., (1978) showed that the maternal licking directly stimulated the release of growth hormone. The importance of this maternally-regulated alteration in endocrine function can be fully appreciated when one realizes that maternal licking, and thus the increase in growth hormone release, accompanied nursing. A rather propitious time for an increase in anabolic activity.

Collectively the research of these pioneers described the wonderful symbiosis that defined mother – offspring interactions. The result was a remarkable consensus that united the previously disparate fields psychoanalysis, developmental psychology, physiology and the brain sciences. There was now, for example, a clear biological basis for the attachment theory of Bowlby (1969). Social was shown to be biologically embedded, and biology to be socially defined.

Levine extended his research program to examine the physiology of the offspring during the period prior to weaning. These studies ultimately described what came to be referred to as the “stress hyporesponsive period” (Levine et al., 1967; Levine and Dent, 2000). Thus, over the first weeks of life the infant rodent exhibits a dramatically reduced adrenal glucocorticoid response to stressors dependent upon consistent maternal care (Rosenfeld et al., 1991). Levine reasonably assumed that this period of quiescence spared the rapidly growing infant from the inherently catabolic effects of exposure to elevated glucocorticoid levels (Vazquez and Levine, 2005). The elegant studies of Claire-Dominique Walker (Walker et al., 1986a, 1986b) later defined the very distinct nature of HPA function in infancy and its increasing approximation of the adult form at the time of weaning.

The subsequent studies of the mechanism underlying the stress hyporesponsive period attest to Levine's remarkable insight into the role of mother – offspring interactions in maintaining the physiological equanimity essential for the developing organism. Levine and his contemporaries described the intricate process that defined the maternal regulation of pup physiology in the service of the growth and development of her offspring. The vital role of the mother was confirmed in studies by Levine with his students Deborah Suchecki and Patricia Rosenfeld (Suchecki et al., 1993b). The studies showed that prolonged periods of maternal separation, that extended well beyond those of the neonatal handling procedure, triggered an early maturation of HPA function (Stanton et al., 1987, 1988; Suchecki et al., 1993a, 1995; Van Oers et al., 1991). In the repeated absence of maternal care, the offspring was left to physiologically fend for itself, hence the premature activation of defensive responses Levine and his colleagues (Rosenfeld et al., 1991; Suchecki et al., 1993a, Suchecki et al., 1993b) then advanced beyond observation to show that maternal care, such as the tactile stimulation derived from licking, actively maintained the HPA quiescence of the neonate (also see Stanton and Levine, 1990; Stanton et al., 1987). Maternal nursing of the young was likewise essential in maintaining infant hyporesponsivity (Suchecki et al., 1993b). Research from Walker's team then identified the importance of leptin as a mediator for the effect of maternal feeding (Proulx et al., 2001) in the regulation of the infant HPA response.

The resulting image from these analyses was that of the mother as a “buffer” that operated as a countervailing force of protection from the slings and arrows of infant life. The studies confirmed the theoretical framework of Bowlby (1969) who positioned attachment behaviour as an evolutionary strategy for the protection and unimpeded growth of the infant. This formulation was then brilliantly developed in the research of Regina Sullivan (New York University) showing that the premature increase in glucocorticoids advanced the development and activity of the amygdala, forming the basis for a persistently increased fearfulness (e.g., Moriceau and Sullivan, 2006; Barr et al., 2009; Sullivan and Opendak, 2021). Sullivan's research further illuminated the neurobiological basis of infant attachment. Her studies of maternal regulation of amygdala development came to guide the subsequent focus on the effects of early-life trauma.

The critical measure by which we assess the value of research with model systems is the degree to which it can be meaningfully translated into the human condition. Thus, the vital importance of Megan Gunnar, herself a prominent Levine prodigy (Gunnar, 1998, 2021; Hostinar et al., 2014). Gunnar's studies served to effectively translate the major themes from the pre-clinical studies of Levine to research samples ranging from children in generally normal conditions to those experiencing the horrors of the Romanian orphanages. This remarkable research program has very clearly documented the role of maternal care as a countervailing “buffer” against the effects of stress that sustains basal levels of neuroendocrine activity over the period of early childhood. In doing so Gunnar clearly articulated an evidence-based understanding of the social origins of resilience. This research program also reflects Levine's deep interest in the developmental transitions that accompany pubertal development. Gunnar's training with Levine thus inspired the impressively novel conceptualization of adolescence as a period during which stress responses were “recalibrated”, providing an opportunity for the reversal of prior trauma (Gunnar et al., 2019).

The most valuable achievement of Levine, in my view, was an enormous contribution to a conceptual framework for studies of development. Levine's own research was inspired by the conceptual framework developed by Freud concerning the sustained effects of early life trauma on personality development and the later risk for mental disorders (Stern et al., 2010). Levine thus built on the framework constructed by Freud to examine, as he himself put it, to understand “… how the stressful experiences of infancy affect the behavior and physiology of the adult organism later on” Levine (1960), p.80). The use of the term “later on” is telling. Indeed, Levine was a pioneer in the development of an experimental basis for study of the long-term effects of early experience on later health. Levine's science thus long pre-dated what is currently referred to as the developmental origins of adult health and disease (DOHaD) hypothesis. As a result of the pioneers in the study of early experience, and those that followed their path, we now fully appreciate the profound importance of early environmental conditions as a force that shapes phenotypic variation in the biological processes that define vulnerability and resilience.

The eminent geneticist Richard Lewontiin noted that “The difficulty of the problem is not that we lack some crucial bit of knowledge, but that we do not know how to frame the questions” (Lewontin, 2000, p. 74). As reflected in the quotation cited above, Levine knew exactly how to frame the question. As did those who so impressively built on the revised conceptual framework he provided over the lifetime of his scholarship. Levine's essential contribution was to provide experimental flesh to the bones formed by the clinical observations of Freud and the attachment theories of Bowlby (1969).

This brief essay cites only a segment of Levine's research program. It does not include the extensive research with squirrel monkeys examining the developmental origins resilience (e.g., Levine and Mody, 2003) nor the inspired analyses of coping with stress in humans (e.g., Ursin et al., 1978). Instead, the intent is to pay homage to a scientist who deeply affected the way we experimentally analyze and thus understand environmental regulation of developmental processes and the consequences for later health and well-being. We should all aspire to such a legacy.
