Co-delivery of ripasudil and dexamethasone in trabecular meshwork cells for potential prevention of GC-induced ocular hypertension
Authors: Tilahun Ayane Debele, Yong Yuan, Winston Kao, Chia-Yang Liu, Eskezeia Y. Dessie, Yoonjee C. Park
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The purpose of this in vitro study is to investigate whether the co-delivery of Ripasudil (Rip) can suppress the expression of genes related to glaucoma pathogenesis induced by prolonged dexamethasone (Dex) use, which can lead to ocular hypertension and potentially glaucoma formation. The effects of Rip (10 μM) on Dex (100 nM)-treated human trabecular meshwork (TM) cells were tested through co-delivery and sequential treatments using RNA-seq. Genome-wide analysis was performed using gene ontology (GO) enrichment and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis with Enrichr and DAVID gene analysis tools. Gene enrichment and pathway analysis revealed that Rip, when co-delivered or sequentially delivered with Dex, influenced genes involved in glaucoma-related pathways such as focal adhesion, extracellular matrix (ECM) organization, and regulation of the actin cytoskeleton. Rip treatment downregulated genes like ACTA2, COL11A1, ECM2, MBP, and ANGPTL7, which are associated with increased outflow resistance and elevated intraocular pressure (IOP). Additionally, Rip upregulated ITGA11, a gene that promotes actin cytoskeleton reorganization and TM cell relaxation by inhibiting the Rho-ROCK pathway. Overall, co-delivery or sequential delivery of Rip can reverse or prevent Dex-induced ocular hypertension and glaucoma formation by modulating the expression of glaucoma-related genes at the transcriptional level.

Introduction

The prolonged use of glucocorticoids (GC) such as dexamethasone (Dex) poses a high risk of elevated intraocular pressure (IOP) and results in secondary glaucoma in susceptible individuals [1,2]. Literature showed that Dex induced actin cytoskeletal rearrangement in the trabecular meshwork (TM) and formed cross-linked actin networks (CLANs) [3]. CLANS formation is a crucial contributor to increased outflow resistance of aqueous humor (AH) that leads to high IOP formation by creating stiffer TM cells and tissues [4].

Ripasudil hydrochloride hydrate (Rip), a Rho-associated coiled-coil containing protein kinase (ROCK) inhibitor, was approved for the treatment of glaucoma [5,6]. Rip decreases IOP by directly relaxing the TM cells and reduces AH outflow resistance [7]. Hence, a possible mechanism for reducing IOP is by inhibiting ROCK downstream effector proteins that regulate TM cell morphology such as actin cytoskeleton organization [8–11].

Our previous study showed that co-delivery of Rip has the potential to prevent the Dex-induced phenotypic changes in human TM cells [12]. Although Rip is known to be a downstream effector that reverses the effect of Dex on TM phenotype changes, recent studies have found the effect of Rip on regulating mRNA expressions related to ECM formation/degradation in TM cells [13,14]. Therefore, we hypothesize in this study that codelivery of Rip with Dex can regulate genes related to the phenotype changes to offset the effect of Dex and prevent glaucoma formation. Several studies have identified the changes in the expression of genes in response to Dex treatment in cultured TM cells. ANGPTL7, MYOC, OCA2, SAA1, and ZBTB16 have been identified as significantly upregulated genes by long-term Dex treatment in TM cells [15,16]. Especially, ANGPTL7 has been investigated as a target gene that can prevent IOP increase [17].

This study focused on identifying differentially expressed genes (DEGs) across various delivery methods, including Dex alone, Rip alone, co-delivery of Dex and Rip, and sequential delivery of Rip after Dex, in human TM cells. This study can be used to discover a novel therapeutic target by elucidating the signature genes that have a key role in glaucoma formation.

Materials and methods

Materials

Dex was purchased from Fisher scientific (Waltham, MA) and ripasudil was purchased from AdooQ Bioscience (Irvine, CA). Direct-zol RNA Microprep Kits (R2061) were purchased from Zymo Research (Irvine, CA). Phosphate-buffered saline (10 × ), human trabecular meshwork (TM) cell growth medium, and TM cells, which had been isolated from normal healthy human adult eyes, were purchased from Cell Applications (San Diego, CA). Alpha-Smooth Muscle Actin Monoclonal Antibody (1A4) eFluor 660 eBioscience, Product # 50–9760-82, was purchased from ThermoFisher Scientific.

TM cell culture and drug treatment protocols

The TM cells were cultured in a human TM cell growth medium, which was ready to use, as per the manufacturer’s instructions, and they were incubated at 37 °C with 5 % CO2. After 24h, the medium was changed to fresh medium followed by further culture for the indicated times until the cell reached 80–90 % confluences. TM cells from passages 8th to 13th were used in this study. The cells tested in the study (i.e. passage 10) was compared with the cells at passage 2 and exhibit a similar number of doublings and comparable appearance (Fig. S1 in the supplementary data). In addition, there was significant upregulation of the Myocilin (MYOC) gene (FC = 11.3762) following Dex treatment compared to the control group in the RNA-Seq data. These two evidence imply that the TM cells used in the study behave as primary TM cells.

Drug treatment protocol for RNA-Seq

TM cells grown in a 6-well plate (5 × 104 cells/well) were dosed with 100 nM of Dex (Dex-100nM), 10 μM of Rip (Rip-10μM), and co-delivery of Dex-100nM and Rip-10μM (Co-DR) on Day 0, 2, and 4, and incubated for the other two days (6 days total). For the sequential treatment (S-DR), the TM cells were pre-treated with Dex-100nM on Day 0 and Day 2 and then, Rip-10μM with Dex-100nM on Day 4, and incubated for two more days (6 days total). The TM medium was used as a control, and the medium was also replaced every 2 days. Total RNA was extracted from each culture well using a Zymo RNA extraction kit according to the manufacturer’s recommendation. Each condition was done in the duplicate.

Alpha-smooth muscle actin and F-actin staining

The effect of co-delivery and sequential of Dex and Rip on the TM cells cytoskeleton was tested via staining alpha-smooth muscle actin and F-actin following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, TM cells were seeded in 12-well chamber slides at a density of 1.5 × 104 cells per well in the complete TM medium. After 6 days of drug dosing as described in section 2.2, the drug solutions were removed, and the cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The cells were then fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde in PBS for 20 min and washed with PBS (3X). Subsequently, the cells were permeabilized with 0.1 % Triton X-100 in PBS for 20 min at room temperature, washed again with PBS (3X), and then blocked with 2 % BSA in PBS for 1h at room temperature. The cells were labeled with Alpha-Smooth Muscle Actin Monoclonal Antibody (1A4), eFluor 660, eBioscience (Product # 50–9760-82) at 5 μg/mL in 0.1 % BSA, and incubated at 4 °C overnight. After overnight incubation, the cells were washed with PBS-3X. The F-actin was labeled with 0.05 mg/mL Alexa Fluor 555 phalloidin for 1 h at room temperature. After they were washed with PBS (3X), the cells were incubated with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 10 min, washed with PBS (3x) and then mounted with a commercial mounting medium (Invitrogen Slow-Fade Gold Antifade Mountant; Thermo Fisher Scientific). The images were observed using Leica Stellaris 8 confocal microscopy (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) and ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD) was used to analyze α-SMA and F-actin intensity of selected representative images. Briefly, all selected representative images (seven images for each condition) were imported into ImageJ. Then, α-SMA and F-actin mean intensity for each image was measured (analyze → set measurement → measure). The averages and SDs were calculated based on the seven representative images for each condition (n = 7).

RNA sequencing and differentially expressed genes (DEG) identification

Directional polyA RNA-seq was performed by the Genomics, Epigenomics, and Sequencing Core at the University of Cincinnati using established protocols with updates [18,19]. Total RNA samples with high RNA integrity numbers (RIN>8), as determined by a Bioanalyzer (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA), were selected for cDNA library preparation. To enrich polyA RNA for this preparation, the NEBNext Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module (New England BioLabs, Ipswich, MA) was employed using 250 ng of high-quality total RNA. Subsequently, the NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep kit (New England BioLabs) was used for creating unique dual index libraries, with a PCR cycle number of 10. Following quality control and quantification using Qubit (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA), the individually indexed libraries were pooled proportionally and sequenced on a NextSeq 2000 Sequencer (Illumina, San Diego, CA). Sequencing was conducted with paired-end 2×61 bp reads, generating approximately 58 million reads per sample. Upon completion, fastq files for downstream data analysis were automatically generated via the Illumina BaseSpace Sequence Hub and transferred/shared through the Cloud.

To identify differentially expressed genes, bioinformatic analysis was conducted using the BaseSpace RNA-Seq Alignment app v2.0.2, with the “mark duplicates” option left unchecked. This was followed by the RNA-Seq Differential Expression app v1.0.1. Reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg19) using a first-strand setting. The alignment results were then used as input for the RNA-Seq Differential Expression app, which utilized DESeq2 to perform the analysis.

Pathway and process enrichment analysis

For each given gene list (threshold set at the absolute value of FC > 2, P < 0.05), pathway, and process enrichment analysis have been carried out with the following ontology sources: KEGG Pathway, and GO Biological Processes, using Enrichr web-based gene analysis tools [20].

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance between groups was analyzed by using a paired one-tailed Student-t-test in excel or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) in GraphPad Prism 10.0. All data are represented in the mean ± Standard deviation (STD). The results were considered significant at P < 0.05 (*), P < 0.01 (**) and P < 0.001 (***).

Results

Effects of Dex and Rip as co-delivery or sequential on the TM cells phenotype

We have tested the effects of Dex and Rip as a co-delivery or sequential delivery on the TM cells cytoskeleton by observing F-actin and α-SMA actin. As shown in Fig. 1A, treatment with Dex resulted in higher fluorescence levels from F-actin cytoskeletons compared to the control group, consistent with our previous findings [12]. Quantitatively, the F-actin mean intensity was higher (p < 0.001; p = 2.02E-06) in the Dex compared to the control group (Fig. 1B). The addition of 10 μM Ripasudil by Co-DR (p < 0.001; p = 7.98E-07) and S-DR (p < 0.001; p = 4.54E-06) significantly reduced F-actin cytoskeleton formation compared to the Dex. Dex is known to cause cytoskeletal reorganization in cells, including the formation of stress fibers. These stress fibers are composed of actin filaments, which may accumulate and bundle together, leading to more intense, and overlying or overlapping actin staining when visualized through microscopy.

Similarly, the α-SMA mean intensity (Fig. 1C) also significantly increased in the Dex group compared to the control group (p < 0.001; p = 6.06E-07), indicative of increased cell contractility [9]. The α-SMA mean intensity significantly decreased in the Co-DR (p < 0.001; p = 2.24E-10) and S-DR (p < 0.001; p = 1.17E-09) compared to the Dex. Both codelivery and sequential delivery methods showed almost similar effects on the F-actin and α-SMA mean intensity.

Differentially expressed genes (DEG)

RNA sequencing was tested to explore differential gene expressions in the TM cells and to make a connection with the glaucoma phenotypes they display. The relationship between statistical significance and log2 FC in normalized expression between the control vs experimental groups (Dex, Co-DR, S-DR, and Rip) or among experimental groups (Dex vs. Co-DR, Dex vs. S-DR, Co-DR vs. S-DR) was shown in the volcano plot as shown in Fig. 2. Of the total set of 27,914 genes, there were 4954 (2584 up-regulated; 2370 down-regulated) genes that were significantly differentiated (p < 0.05) in Dex compared to the control. The number of significantly differentiated genes with Co-DR and the S-DR were 5194 (2720 up-regulated; 2474 down-regulated) and 1577 (873 up-regulated; 704 down-regulated), respectively, compared to the control group. Interestingly, a greater number of significant DEGs were identified in the co-delivery treatment group as compared to the sequential group, which indicates that co-delivery altered more differential gene expression profiles.

The top ten genes upregulated and downregulated for Dex, Co-DR, S-DR, and Rip compared to control, as well as Co-DR and S-DR compared to Dex are shown in Fig. 2 and Table 1. Out of the top ten genes, five genes, MAGED4 (Melanoma Antigen Family D4), ZBTB16 (Zinc Finger and BTB Domain Containing 16), H19 (H19 Imprinted Maternally Expressed Transcript), OCA2 (Oculocutaneous Albinism II), and NOTCH2NLC (Notch 2 N-terminal Like C) were commonly upregulated by Dex, Co-DR, and S-DR compared to the control groups. However, ZBTB16, H19, OCA2, and NOTCH2NLC genes were also among the top ten upregulated genes by Rip, implying that these genes are not specifically related to the glaucomatous phenotype. There were 4 common genes downregulated by Co-DR and S-DR (NUDT4B, POC1B-GALNT4, KIAA0408 and LINC01541). These genes are understudied, and more research needs to be performed to understand their role.

In addition, our RNA-Seq analysis revealed a significant upregulation of the Myocilin (MYOC) gene in the Dex (FC = 11.3762), Co-DR (FC = 11.76426), and S-DR (FC = 12.73884)-treated groups compared to the control groups. This result confirms that the functional sensitivity of our tested TM cells to glucocorticoid stimulation remains intact, Myocilin (MYOC) gene is a well-established marker of Dex responsiveness in TM cells [21].

Antagonistic effects of Rip as a codelivery or sequential compared to the Dex

To investigate the antagonistic effects of Rip as a codelivery or sequential delivery, a further comparison was made for oppositely expressed genes: (i) up-regulated by Dex and downregulated by Co-DR/S-DR or (ii) downregulated by Dex and up-regulated by Co-DR/S-DR (all genes with threshold were set at absolute FC > 2 and p < 0.05). First, we analyzed DEGs between genes upregulated by Dex and genes downregulated by Co-DR, to identify common genes with opposite trends. There were 63 genes (Dex-UP vs. Co-DR-DW, the yellow circle in Fig. 3A). Then, the genes were further sorted based on the effect of Dex by adding another circle of Control vs. Dex-UP (683 genes, the blue circle). There were 20 common genes between the two. Lastly, 91 genes in the third circle (Dex-UP vs S-DR-DW, the green circle) were added to compare the effect of dosing sequence on gene expression. There are 10 (1.3 %) common elements for the three circles highlighted in the red color in Fig. 3A: COL11A1, ACTA2, ECM2, CACNB2, FOXJ1, KIF5C, TENT5B, CDC20B, MBP, and 1-Mar. The results indicate that Dex significantly upregulated several genes including Extracellular Matrix Protein 2 (ECM2), actin alpha 2, smooth muscle (ACTA2), and collagen type XI alpha 1 chain (COL11A1), that have several biological processes such as muscle contraction, extracellular structure organization, supramolecular fiber organization, external encapsulating structure organization and collagen fibril organization.

As shown in Fig. 3A, there are 10 (1.3 %) genes exclusively present for Co-DR (control vs Dex-UP and Dex vs Co-DR-DW. 27 (3.5 %) genes are exclusively present for S-DR (control vs Dex-UP and Dex vs S-DR-DW). The genes are listed in Table S1A (Supplementary). Those differentially expressed genes upregulated by Dex and downregulated by Co-DR or S-DR have several different biological processes (the details explained in section 3.5).

Similarly, we analyzed DEGs between genes downregulated by Dex and genes upregulated by Co-DR and S-DR to identify common genes with opposite trends. As shown in Fig. 3B, there are 19 (2.8 %) common gene elements (ITGA11, WNT7B, SERPINE2, NR4A2, HHIP, TSPAN7, GPR4, SPOCK3, PART1, PCK1, KALRN, MALL, GAP43, IRF6, STC1, SFRP1, PLAT, TIAF1, HGF), highlighted in the red color. These genes are involved in extracellular structure organization, extracellular matrix organization, positive regulation of focal adhesion assembly, regulation of cell migration, and PI3K-Akt signaling pathway. The detailed pathway analysis in the following sections 3.4 and 3.5. In addition, there are 12 (1.8 %) genes exclusively present for Co-DR (control vs Dex-DW and Dex vs Co-DR-UP). 38 (5.7 %) genes are exclusively present for S-DR (control vs Dex-DW and Dex vs S-DR-UP). The genes are listed in Table S1B (Supplementary). Detailed biological processes of downregulated genes by Dex and upregulated by Co-DR or S-DR are discussed in session 3.5.

Although Co-DR and S-DR shared a common gene that were upregulated or downregulated compared to the Dex, they exhibited different gene expression levels (i.e. fold changes) as summarized in Tables 2A and 2B. Interestingly, H19 was downregulated by Co-DR and S-DR whereas it was highly upregulated by Dex alone and Rip alone. Especially, Co-DR suppressed the genes more than S-DR (FC = 2.4 and 1.5, respectively). The results suggest that the two drugs have an antagonistic effect when they are present together. ANGPTL7 was also highly downregulated by Co-DR and S-DR. Although the FC was less than 2, this downregulation is important because the FC = 196.74 in upregulation was reversed by Co-DR and S-DR. The differences in FC between Co-DR and S-DR are all less than 1, except FOXJ1 (Forkhead box protein J1), ACTA2 (actin alpha 2, smooth muscle), and ACTG2 (actin gamma 2, smooth muscle). FOXJ1 was more downregulated by Co-DR (FC = 8.8) than S-DR (FC = 6.6). On the other hand, ACTA2 and ACTG2 were more downregulated by S-DR (FC = 8.9 and 3.5) than Co-DR (FC = 2.6 and 2.0), implying that actin-related genes are more sensitively responding to sequential delivery than co-delivery. A more detailed DEG comparison between Co-DR and S-DR is shown in section 3.5.

Similarly, as shown in Table 2B, several genes downregulated by Dex were upregulated by Co-DR and S-DR. In this study we found that WNT7B (Wnt family member 7B) is one of the downregulated genes by Dex and upregulated by Co-DR (FC = 2.9) and S-DR (FC = 3.5). In addition, SFRP1 (secreted Frizzled-related protein 1) was also significantly downregulated by Dex, but upregulated by Co-DR and S-DR. However, SFRP1 has been reported to be upregulated by Dex in glaucomatous TM cells and is correlated with IOP elevation [22,23]. The opposite trends indicate that cells respond differently between regular TM cells and Dex-susceptible glaucomatous TM cells. To compare Co-DR and S-DR, the differences in FC are all less than 1, except PCK1 (Phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase 1). The detailed pathway regarding PCK1 will be mentioned in Section 3.4.

Functional enrichment and pathway analysis

For functional enrichment and pathway analysis, 10 common elements (COL11A1, ACTA2, ECM2, CACNB2, FOXJ1, KIF5C, TENT5B, CDC20B, MBP, 1-Mar) upregulated by Dex and downregulated by Co-DR/S-DR, and 19 common elements (ITGA11, WNT7B, SERPINE2, NR4A2, HHIP, TSPAN7, GPR4, SPOCK3, PART1, PCK1, KALRN, MALL, GAP43, IRF6, STC1, SFRP1, PLAT, TIAF1, HGF) downregulated by Dex and upregulated by Co-DR/S-DR were selected for enrichment analysis. Although most uploaded common genes (i.e. intersectional genes) received matched genes and showed associated enrichment terms, some genes (e.g. 1-MAR, and TIAF1) were not associated with any enrichment term.

As shown in Fig. 4A, genes upregulated by Dex and downregulated by Co-DR and S-DR were involved with extracellular matrix organization, positive regulation of voltage-gated calcium channel activity, smooth muscle contraction, and so on. The results imply that the contraction-related genes were suppressed by Co-DR and S-DR. Fig. 4B revealed that genes downregulated by Dex and upregulated by Co-DR and S-DR were involved with the canonical Wnt signaling pathway, and Wnt signaling pathway. There are many studies about the role of canonical/non-canonical Wnt signaling pathway in TM cells related to glaucomatous phenotype [24,25]. Wnt signaling has been also reported to play a crucial role in regulating extracellular matrix (ECM) levels in the TM [22]. The results in Fig. 4B imply that Wnt signaling is regulated by both Dex and Rip in opposite directions.

In addition, an enrichment subnetwork that connects results (terms/pathways) is displayed for common genes upregulated by Dex and downregulated by Co-DR/S-DR in Fig. 5A and downregulated by Dex and upregulated by Co-DR/S-DR in Fig. 5B. COL11A1 and ECM2 are related in the network via extracellular matrix organization, external encapsulating structure organization, and extracellular structure organization. The results imply that Dex and Rip cause changes to the extracellular matrix (ECM) in the TM tissue, resulting in changes to the IOP, in opposite directions. The effect of Dex on ECM in TM cells has been reported [26].

Moreover, MBP (myelin basic protein) is related to chemokine (C-X-C motif) ligand 2, that leads to proliferation of TM cells and decreased aqueous humor outflow [27]. One study has shown that the glaucoma group showed a higher serum MBP level [28]. The results indicate that Dex is causing the chemokine upregulation whereas Rip suppresses this.

Fig. 5B shows that ITGA11 is related to many signaling pathways, including PI3K-AK1 signaling pathway and focal adhesion, and linked to other genes, such as HGF, WNT7B, SFRP1, NR4A2, HHIP, and PCK1. The results imply that Integrin subunit alpha 11 (ITGA11) gene, which encodes the transmembrane protein ITGA11, plays a crucial role in regulating TM cells in response to Dex and Rip. ITGA11 protein contains an I domain, expressed in muscle tissue, dimerizes with beta 1 integrin and appears to bind collagen in this form. Therefore, the protein may be involved in attaching muscle tissue to the ECM. The following KEGG pathways also support the understanding of the effect of ITGA11 on glaucomatous phenotype in TM cells (Fig. 6).

Fig. 6 includes pathways of actin cytoskeleton, focal adhesion, and PI3K-AKT signaling pathways, and ITG or ITGA in the cellular membrane represents ITGA11. The interaction between ITGA11 and FAK, or ITGA11 and c-Src, initiates downstream signaling events that reorganize the actin cytoskeleton by inhibiting the Rho-ROCK pathway, thereby preventing stress fiber formation. This mechanism helps TM cells relax and could prevent high intraocular pressure (IOP). Furthermore, focal adhesion and ECM-receptor interaction (e.g., ECM-ITGA) activate Src downstream, leading to the phosphorylation of RhoGAP, which inhibits the RhoA-ROCK pathway and further triggers actin depolymerization. In addition, ECM-receptor interaction (e.g., ECM-ITGA) also activates the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, impacting various biological processes. Overall, the specific mechanism by which ITGA11 inhibits RhoA activity may vary depending on the cellular context, the presence of other integrins and ECM components, and the specific signaling pathways activated. Hence, further research is necessary to fully understand the intricate regulatory networks involved.

Discussion

Our study was intentionally designed to model the clinically prevalent scenario in which prolonged Dex use leads to ocular hypertension and potentially glaucoma. The primary aim of this in vitro study was to investigate whether co-delivery of Ripasudil (Rip) can suppress the expression of genes associated with glaucoma pathogenesis induced by prolonged Dex exposure. This co-treatment framework represents a potential clinical strategy in which ROCK inhibitors are prescribed concurrently with GC therapy. Nevertheless, future studies could explore the effect of Rip pretreatment prior to Dex exposure on the prevention of GC-induced phenotypic and genotypic changes at different time points.

Literature has shown that cytoskeleton rearrangement is an important process that affects the phenotype and function of TM cells including their contractility, shape, and interactions with the ECM, all of which are crucial for regulating AH outflow [29,30]. Our results showed that Dex-treated TM cells have a high mean intensity of F-actin and α-SMA with higher density compared to the control and Rip-treated groups. Several studies also reported that Dex enhances F-actin stress fiber formation and cytoskeletal reorganization that in turn increases TM cell stiffness and contractility [10,31,32]. On the other hand, Rip, as a Rho kinase (ROCK) inhibitor, reduces stress fiber formation and cytoskeletal tension that reduces TM cells contractility [7,33]. This phenomenon is very crucial in facilitating aqueous humor outflow, reducing AH outflow resistance through the TM cells, and lowering IOP to prevent glaucoma formation.

Our previous study showed that the contractility assay revealed that the addition of Dex to collagen gels containing TM cells induced a significant contraction compared to the control group as shown in Fig. 4 [12]. This indicates that Dex increases the contractile activity of TM cells, contributing to extracellular matrix remodeling and increased outflow resistance [10]. In contrast, the addition of 10 μM-Rip as Co-DR (p < 0.01, p = 0.003) or S-DR (p < 0.05, p = 0.0493) on a Dex-pretreated TM cell–collagen gel matrix significantly inhibited or reversed the collagen gel contraction activity of TM cells. This shows Rip has antagonistic effects with the Dex on the TM cell’s contractility (i.e. Dex induced TM cell contraction whereas Rip reverses Dex-induced contraction or increases TM cell relaxation). Hence, Rip can enhance aqueous humor outflow by reducing TM cell contractility and minimize or prevent the risk of increased IOP induced by Dex when used as a codelivery or sequential delivery [11].

To explore the effects of Rip on Dex-treated TM cells at the genome levels, the differential gene expression profile of human TM cells in response to Dex and Rip as a codelivery and sequential delivery were tested for the first time using RNA-seq. We found expressions of several genes affected both by Dex and Rip as a codelivery and sequential delivery that have numerous biological processes including ECM organization, regulation of cell-cell adhesion, positive regulation of voltage-gated calcium channel activity, vascular-associated smooth muscle contraction, and so on.

The actin alpha 2, smooth muscle (ACTA2) gene provides instructions for making a protein α-SMA, which is part of the actin protein family and important for cell movement and contraction [34,35]. Literature showed that upregulation of ACTA2 can lead to changes in the contractile properties of TM cells which further can reduce aqueous humor outflow, potentially increasing IOP [15]. In this study, we observed Dex upregulated the expression of ACTA2 (FC ~7) that will enhance TM cell contraction. However, Rip, as a codelivery (absolute FC ~2.6) or sequential (absolute FC ~9) delivery, significantly downregulated ACTA2 gene expression and enhanced the relaxation of TM cells, which can further help to increase aqueous humor outflow, and subsequent decreases IOP. ACTA2 is also a characteristic gene found in cells that undergo epithelium-mesenchyme transition (EMT) and fibroblast-myofibroblast transition (FMT). TM cells undergo an EMT or FMT-like phenomenon caused by the ECM ligands of integrins [36–38].

ECM of TM provides amicroenvironment with unique biomechanical properties that are important for its biological function [39]. In this study, we found that Dex significantly upregulated ECM-related genes such as Extracellular Matrix Protein 2 (ECM2), and collagen type XI alpha 1 chain (COL11A1), while these genes were downregulated by Rip both in the codelivery and sequential treatment of TM cells. ECM2 gene encodes ECM protein 2 (ECM2), a member of the secreted protein acidic and rich in cysteine (SPARC) family protein [40]. This protein family secreted to extracellular space and regulated assembly and deposition of ECM influences the activity of extracellular protease and modulate the growth factor/cytokine signaling pathway [41]. Although the role of ECM2 in glaucoma is still underexploited, we observed that Dex upregulated the expression of ECM2 (FC ~2.8), while Rip downregulated its expression both as codelivery (absolute FC ~ 2) or sequential delivery (absolute FC ~2.5). COL11A1 gene encodes one of the two α chains of type XI collagen. Literature showed that common variants in COL11A1 are associated with angle-closure glaucoma in Asian populations [42, 43].

Angiopoietin-like 7 (ANGPTL7) gene encodes Angiopoietin-like 7 proteins, a member of the angiopoietin-like family, which are involved in various biological processes such as regulating angiogenesis, inflammation, and lipid metabolism [44,45]. ANGPTL7 has gained attention in recent years due to its potential role in ocular health, particularly in relation to glaucoma. Literature has shown that ANGPTL7 levels are elevated in the aqueous humor (AH) of glaucoma patients, and several studies have demonstrated that overexpression of ANGPTL7 in TM cells leads to changes in ECM deposition [46,47]. These changes may play a crucial role in increasing AH outflow resistance, contributing to elevated IOP. In our study, we found that Dex induced upregulation of ANGPTL7 gene expression (fold change ~196) in TM cells. This upregulation may be part of the mechanism by which Dex raises IOP, as ANGPTL7 influences ECM regulation and AH outflow resistance in TM cells. Interestingly, we observed that ANGPTL7 expression was significantly downregulated by Co-DR (absolute fold change ~2) and S-DR (absolute fold change ~1.5) in TM cells. This indicates that Rip can reduce stiffness and resistance in TM cells by decreasing ANGPTL7 expression, thereby improving AH outflow and lowering IOP. Overall, our findings suggest that ANGPTL7 plays a key role in the pathological changes in the TM and could serve as a potential target for future therapies aimed at managing IOP, particularly in cases of Dex-induced glaucoma.

The Calcium Voltage-Gated Channel Auxiliary Subunit Beta 2 (CACNB2) gene encodes the β2 subunit of the L-type voltage-dependent calcium channel which plays essential roles in the regulation of calcium ion flow into cells, influencing various cellular processes such as muscle contraction, neurotransmitter release, and gene expression [48]. Literature showed that CACNB2 may be involved in the pathogenesis of proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR) through a pathway by which calcium channels regulate vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) expression and release in the retinal pigment epithelium [49]. In this study, CACNB2 upregulation was observed by Dex whereas downregulated by Rip as a codelivery or sequential treated TM cells. Although there is a limited report on the role of the CACNB2 gene in the TM cells, the upregulation of the CACNB2 gene by Dex might be involved in calcium-mediated cellular signaling and contraction of the TM cells that can influence the outflow of aqueous humor, thus impacting IOP, a critical factor in glaucoma formation. As a result, the CACNB2 gene can be considered as the potential target in regulating aqueous humor outflow resistance and reducing IOP in the TM cells.

Integrin subunit alpha 11 (ITGA11) gene was significantly upregulated by Rip both in the codelivery and sequential treated TM cells compared to the Dex. The ITGA11 gene encodes Integrin subunit alpha 11 (ITGA11) transmembrane proteins that play a great role in transmitting signals via non-receptor tyrosine kinase activities of the FAK and c-Src proteins. ITGA11/FAK or ITGA11/c-Src interaction initiates downstream signaling events that lead to reorganization of the actin cytoskeleton (i.e. prevent stress fiber formation) by inhibiting Rho-ROCK pathways [50]. Several literatures noted ROCK mediates the effects of RhoA on the actin cytoskeleton rearrangement, and changes in the cell shape, motility, and attachment [6,51,52]. Additional studies showed that the RhoA/ROCK signaling pathway has an important role in the regulation of aqueous humor outflow resistance and selective ROCK inhibitor significantly reduced IOP [53,54]. Hence, targeting and regulating ITGA11 genes and ITGA11 proteins signaling pathway might be a good strategy to prevent the pathophysiology of Dex-induced ocular hypertension or to develop novel therapeutics to treat glaucoma.

The significant upregulation of the WNT7B gene was observed in Rip, as the codelivery and the sequential delivery, treated TM cells while WNT7B gene expression decreased in Dex-treated TM cells. Previously, it was shown that prolonged exposure of TM cells to Dex resulted in the inhibition of WNT signaling [55]. As a result, the activation of WNT signaling could prevent the adverse effect of Dex-induced ocular hypertension and glaucoma [56]. Our finding strongly suggests that upregulating the WNT7B gene through Rip could be a potential strategy for treating Dex-induced glaucoma.

Literature showed that transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) regulates the amount and quality of ECM in ocular tissues, and elevated levels of TGF-β are found in the aqueous humor of patients with glaucoma [57,58]. Hence, downregulation of TGF-β, or upregulation of TGF-β inhibitor might be crucial in maintaining TGF-β related glaucoma pathogenesis. In the current studies, the transforming growth factor-β Induced Anti-Fibrotic Factor 1 (TIAF1) gene was upregulated by Rip both in the codelivery and sequential compared to the Dex-treated TM cells. TIAF1 gene encodes a protein that plays a role in modulating the effects of transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), a cytokine that regulates cell growth, and ECM production, and a central mediator of fibrosis and tissue remodeling [59]. Although the effects of TIAF1 on TM cells have not been reported, modulating TIAF1 activity could offer therapeutic options for TGF-β2–induced TM cell fibrosis. Reducing extracellular matrix (ECM) depositions may enhance aqueous outflow and lower IOP, presenting a potential new approach for treating glaucoma.

sFRP1 was downregulated by Dex and upregulated by Rip as codelivery or sequential delivery. However, sFRP1 has been reported to be upregulated by Dex in glaucomatous TM cells and is correlated with IOP elevation [22,23]. The opposite trends indicate that cells respond differently between regular TM cells and Dex-susceptible glaucomatous TM cells. Therefore, it may be crucial to use appropriate cell sources to understand the responses to the drugs for developing drugs and/or understanding underlying pathogenesis mechanisms.

In conclusion, the effects of Rip as a codelivery or sequential on the differential gene expression of the Dex-treated TM cells were investigated using RNA-seq. Our results revealed that Dex upregulated the expression of cytoskeleton genes (ACTA2) and ECM-related genes (COL11A1, ECM2, ANGPTL7) at the transcriptional level which are thought to contribute to the increase in the IOP and lead to glaucoma formation. However, Rip downregulated those mentioned genes, both in a codelivery or sequential delivery, that can reduce IOP and prevent or reverse glaucoma formation. Moreover, Rip as co-delivery or sequential delivery can effectively modulate TM gene expressions, which have various biological processes such as cytoskeleton reorganization and causes TM cells to relax by decreasing actin stress fiber formation that can further enhance aqueous humor outflow and reduce high IOP formation. Future in vivo validation would further strengthen the translational relevance of our findings.
