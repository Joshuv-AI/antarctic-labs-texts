Perioperative patient safety: nurses’ safety perceptions and observed compliance with safety standards in the operating room
Authors: Wiem Aouicha, Mohamed Ayoub Tlili, Bushra Alshammari, Hamdi Lamine, Shaimaa Mohamed Nageeb, Heba Tallah Elashmawy Mohamed Shaban, Samah Ramadan Ibrahim Elrefaey, Rania Abd-Elnaby Mohammed Allam, Nojoud Abdullah Alrashidi, Seham Salamah Alanazi, Maha Sanat Alrashedi, Nick Sevdalis, Aziza Zakaria Faramawy Ali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPerioperative patient safety remains a major concern, as a substantial proportion of adverse events occur in surgical settings. Ensuring safe surgical care requires not only a strong safety culture among healthcare professionals but also good adherence to established safety standards. Nurses play a central role in maintaining perioperative safety in the operating room (OR). This study aims to assess perioperative compliance with established safety standards across all phases of surgical care, evaluate perioperative nurses’ safety perceptions, and identify the factors associated with these perceptions.MethodsAn analytical cross-sectional observational study was conducted in the OR of two university hospitals in Tunisia. An exhaustive sample of all perioperative nurses working in ten OR was targeted. Their perception of patient safety was assessed using a single-item measure adapted from the Hospital Survey on Patient Safety Culture (HSOPSC). Compliance with perioperative safety standards was evaluated through direct observation of a sample size of 266 surgical procedures, using the validated Surgical Patient Safety Observation Tool (SPOT). Descriptive and inferential statistical analyses were performed. All ethical considerations were respected.ResultsThe majority of perioperative nurses rated patient safety in their units as good. Safety training (p = 0.020), regular protocol updates (p = 0.003), and effective communication (p = 0.018) were significantly associated with higher perceived safety. However, observational audit findings demonstrated that none of the SPOT dimensions reached the 90% threshold for good compliance, and the overall compliance with safety standards remained below 60%, indicating persistent weaknesses in perioperative safety practices. Communication and handover demonstrated the highest compliance (59%), whereas infection prevention was the lowest (36.5%).ConclusionsAlthough perioperative nurses reported generally favorable perceptions of patient safety, observed compliance with safety standards remained poor, highlighting a clear perception–practice gap. Key organizational factors, particularly safety training, regular protocol updates, and effective communication, were significantly associated with higher perceived safety. These findings stress the need for targeted, systemic interventions aimed at strengthening training, improving protocol dissemination, enhancing handover processes, and reinforcing infection prevention and surgical counting practices to improve perioperative patient safety.Keywords: Patient safety, Perioperative nursing, Safety perception, Observed compliance, Clinical audit

Introduction

As countries worldwide aspire for universal health coverage by 2030, it has become increasingly evident that providing high-quality care is crucial [1]. Patient safety, a fundamental aspect of quality of care, is recognized by universal health coverage as an essential goal to avert errors and Adverse Events (AEs) related to healthcare [1, 2] and limit the associated economic burden [2]. Yet, no country has fully overcome the challenges related to patient safety [3]. One of the Institute of Medicine’s recommendations to strengthen patient safety is to integrate a safety culture into the daily clinical practice to ensure safe, effective, and reliable healthcare delivery [2].

The perioperative environment is notably susceptible to AEs as patients who undergo surgery are among the most vulnerable to failures and errors in the care process [4]. Approximately one in ten hospitalized patients experiences harm due to an AE [5]. An AE is defined as any incident that results in injury to a patient during preventive, diagnostic, or therapeutic care [2]. Notably, nearly 50% of these AEs are considered preventable, meaning that they would not have occurred if the care provided had met the required standards [6]. Many AEs (40%) that are associated with surgical care occur before or after the surgical procedure, while 15% stem from medication errors [7].

In the operating room (OR), AEs are frequently caused by latent conditions, which are system-level factors that create the circumstances for human error [8]. In the United States alone, these errors are estimated to cause 100,000 to 400,000 deaths annually, typically associated with poor team coordination, inadequate communication, insufficient technical competence, and lack of training in patient safety principles [2, 9]. Furthermore, identification errors such as wrong patient or wrong procedure constitute a major source of AEs in the OR [10]. Equipment-related failures, including issues of availability, configuration, or malfunction, also contribute to OR errors and account for an estimated 23.5% of all incidents occurring in this setting [11].

Admittedly, auditing clinical practices and observing procedures in the OR have been deemed important strategies for improving surgical patient safety [12, 13]. Systematic evaluation of processes and practices allows identifying deviations from established norms and provides essential data on potential perioperative risks [12, 13]. These audits underline areas requiring attention and guide the implementation of corrective measures [12, 13].

Nurses have a crucial role in executing numerous safety-critical tasks assessed in perioperative audits, making their contribution vital for the proper evaluation and improvement of safety performance [14]. Positioned on the frontline of surgical care, perioperative nurses ensure safe practices, identify latent risks, and implement preventive procedures, including the use of the surgical safety checklist, patient identity verification, intraoperative monitoring, and adherence to infection-prevention protocols [15]. Research suggests that effective nursing vigilance can prevent avoidable perioperative complications, highlighting the fundamental role of nurses in patient safety [16]. Several studies have shown that a higher perceived safety level among nurses is associated with better compliance to guidelines and improved patient outcomes [17, 18]. However, several factors have been deemed to hinder nurses’ ability to maintain vigilance, adhere to safety protocols, and detect complications early [16, 19] such as high workload, insufficient staffing, and prolonged shifts [19, 20].

At the organizational level, safety performance is strongly influenced by safety culture. Institutions with a positive safety culture tend to achieve higher levels of safety performance and, consequently, lower rates of AEs and incidents [21, 22]. Yet emerging evidence in healthcare has identified a discrepancy between healthcare professionals’ perceived safety and their actual clinical practices [23–25]. This “perception–practice gap” has been conceptualized as a divergence between safety culture (what professionals believe or report) and safety performance (what is actually enacted in practice). Understanding this gap is particularly important, as high perceived safety does not necessarily translate into safe clinical practice [23], especially in complex and high-risk environments such as the OR.

To date, epidemiological data in Tunisia on the different forms of perioperative risks and perceived safety levels remain scarce [26]. This gap provides the rationale for the present study, which aims to assess perioperative compliance with established safety standards across all phases of surgical care, to evaluate perioperative nurses’ perceived level of patient safety and to determine the organizational and professional factors that influence these perceptions.

Methods

Study design and setting

This is an analytical cross-sectional observational study that was conducted in the OR of two university hospitals in Sousse, Tunisia. The purpose was to assess perioperative compliance and to determine the perceived safety level among perioperative nurses, as well as the factors associated with that perception. Data were collected over 5 months (from October 2023 to March 2024). All four OR (Ear, Nose, and Throat (ENT), ophthalmology, obstetrics and gynecology, and general and digestive surgery) in Farhat Hached University Hospital agreed to take part in the study. At Sahloul University Hospital, six units (neurosurgery, orthopedic, maxillofacial surgery, emergency surgery, general and digestive surgery, and burn and plastic surgery) out of eight OR accepted to participate in our study.

Study population

Participants

The target population consisted of all the perioperative nurses working in the ten participating OR (N = 310), of whom 165 were from Farhat Hached University Hospital and 145 were from Sahloul University Hospital. These nurses were engaged in all stages of the perioperative pathway, including preoperative assessment, intraoperative care, and postoperative handover. They comprised scrub nurses, circulating nurses, OR nurse managers, and nurse anesthetists. We used exhaustive recruitment to ensure maximum representativeness and minimize sampling error. Regarding inclusion criteria, nurses were enrolled in the study if they were actively working in one of the ten OR and voluntarily agreed to participate. Those who declined participation, were absent or on leave during data collection, or were students or trainees were excluded from the study.

Audit sample: surgical procedures

To assess perioperative compliance with established surgical safety standards, direct observations of operations were carried out. Using Kish’s formula for descriptive studies [27], the required minimum number of observations was calculated, assuming a 95% confidence level, a conservative compliance rate of 50% with international safety standards, and a precision of 6%. This calculation yielded a minimum of 266 surgical procedures to be observed. All surgery types performed in these OR were included and the canceled procedures were excluded.

Measures

For data collection, a self-administered questionnaire was used. It consisted of 14 items covering: (1) sociodemographic and professional characteristics, (2) key organizational factors influencing perioperative patient safety drawn from the existing literature [26, 28]. These organizational factors included safety training, availability of protocol updates, communication within the OR, reporting of safety concerns, participation in risk management activities, presence of emergency procedures, perceived workload, logistical challenges, and information transfer during patient handovers, and (3) the perceived patient safety level was adapted from the French-validated version of the Hospital Survey on Patient Safety Culture (HSOPSC) developed by Occelli et al., which has well-established psychometric properties [29]. This French version was selected because it ensures better participant comprehension in our context and has been widely used in previous studies conducted in the Tunisian healthcare setting [26, 28, 30]. The assessment of the perceived safety level was conducted using a single item from the HSOPSC. Prior research by Sorra and Dyer has identified this item as a reliable outcome variable for assessing patient safety perceptions among healthcare professionals [31]. Furthermore, this single-item measure has been widely used in previous studies, supporting its validity and applicability for capturing an overall perception of safety [32, 33]. However, as a single-item indicator, it does not fully capture the multidimensional nature of patient safety culture [34, 35].

Formal content validity assessment was not conducted; however, the questionnaire was reviewed by experts in perioperative patient safety to ensure clarity, comprehensibility, and feasibility. In addition, a pilot test was conducted with seven perioperative nurses working in the private sector prior to data collection.

To rate nurses’ perceived safety level, a 5-point Likert scale was used (1 = failing, 2 = poor, 3 = acceptable, 4 = very good and 5 = excellent) [29]. A good perceived level of patient safety was defined by grouping the three categories excellent, very good, and acceptable, whereas a poor perceived level of patient safety was defined by grouping the categories Poor and Failing.

Perioperative practices observations were conducted using the Surgical Patient Safety Observation Tool (SPOT), which is a validated instrument designed to track perioperative safety and compare performance across healthcare institutions [36]. Additionally, it was tested in eight hospitals and shown to be easy to use and user-friendly [36].

The SPOT comprises 134 items divided into 19 observational themes that correlate to the Joint Commission International (JCI)’s six International Patient Safety Goals [36]. The instrument includes two sections: the first evaluates practices throughout the patient’s perioperative pathway from arrival in the operating suite to transfer back to the ward while the second focuses on observing safety processes within surgical wards. Dimensions and sub-dimensions suggested by the SPOT

To facilitate the analysis of results, operational definitions were established. The compliance rate refers to the extent to which processes involved in a surgical procedure adhere to established safety standards and guidelines in the OR [36]. A high compliance rate suggests that more than 90% of the safety standards recommended by the SPOT were met during the procedure [36]. An acceptable compliance rate indicates that at least 50% of the required safety standards were met during the surgical intervention. This threshold was adopted based on similar studies using observational designs in perioperative settings [37, 38]. It was used as a practical criterion to classify compliance levels for comparative analysis, rather than a standard for optimal performance.

Data collection

Prior to data collection, investigators received training on the SPOT instrument, including detailed review of item definitions and scoring procedures. Several meetings were held to ensure consistent interpretation. They also independently assessed two surgical procedures, after which their observations were compared and discussed until a consensus was reached.

In addition, before initiating data collection, investigators spent time in the OR to allow staff to become familiar with their presence and reduce potential observer-related behavioral changes (Hawthorne effect). Data collection was then initiated, with real-time observations conducted across the preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative phases.

Questionnaire data were collected separately. The investigators visited the participating OR and distributed a self-administered questionnaire to all perioperative nurses in order to collect data on their perceptions of patient safety, as well as their demographic and professional characteristics.

Data analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA) was used to analyze the data. Participant characteristics and perceptions were summarized using descriptive statistics, such as means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages. Chi-square tests, Student’s t-tests, and odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were used to examine relationships between sociodemographic, professional, and organizational factors and the perceived safety level. The threshold for statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

Prior to data collection, the research project protocol was reviewed and approved by the institutional ethics committee of the Faculty of Medicine of Sousse under reference CEFMS 31/2019. Ethical approval was also obtained from the heads of the surgical departments in both participating hospitals. Information about the study’s objectives, methods, voluntary participation, anonymity, confidentiality, and the right to withdraw at any time was provided to all eligible perioperative nurses. Finally, the SPOT developer was conducted to obtain authorization for its use.

Results

Patient safety perceptions survey

Participants’ sociodemographic and professional characteristics

Out of the 310 eligible participants, 286 took part in the study, yielding a 92.2% response rate. Participants’ age ranged from 24 to 59 years (mean = 38.3 ± 8.56). Females accounted for 66.1% of the study population (n = 189), with a male-to-female ratio of 0.51. More than half of the participants were working in Farhat Hached University Hospital (55.6%, n = 159), and the majority (59.4%, n = 170) had over five years of professional experience.

Participants’ perceptions of organizational factors affecting perioperative patient safety

The majority of respondents felt encouraged to voice safety concerns (72.4%, n = 207) and reported that there was sufficient communication within the surgical teams (80.8%, n = 231). However, participants reported limited exposure to surgical safety training (30.8%, n = 88), low participation in risk-management structures (19.2%, n = 55), and a high perceived workload (82.5%, n = 236). Furthermore, insufficient notification of protocol updates and changes (55.6%, n = 159) and information loss during patient transfers (53.5%, n = 153) were reported by more than half of the participants, as shown in Table 2.

Perceived level of patient safety and associated factors

Overall perceived safety level

The majority of perioperative nurses (81.4%, n = 230) perceived the level of safety in their OR as good. This category comprised ratings of “Excellent” (4.5%, n = 13), “Very good” (23.1%, n = 66), and “Acceptable” (52.8%, n = 151). In contrast, 19.6% (n = 56) of participants perceived safety levels as poor, including 17.5% (n = 50) who rated it as “Poor” and 2.1% (n = 6) as “Failing.”

Factors associated with the perceived level of patient safety

No statistically significant associations were observed between perceived safety level and gender (p = 0.99), age (p = 0.622), or years of professional experience (p = 0.696).

As shown in Table 3, several organizational factors were significantly associated with a good perceived safety level, including safety training (p = 0.020), protocol updates (p = 0.003), emergency procedures (p = 0.009), communication within the OR (p = 0.018), and high perceived workload (p = 0.001). In contrast, loss of patient information during transfer was significantly associated with poorer perceived safety (p = 0.003).

Audit results

In total, 266 surgical interventions from nine different specialties (general surgery, obstetrics and gynecology, neurosurgery, ophthalmology, burns and plastic surgery, maxillofacial surgery, ENT, orthopedic, and emergency procedures) were observed during data collection.

The majority of observations were collected at Sahloul University Hospital (60.5%, n = 161), while Farhat Hached University Hospital contributed the remaining 105 procedures (39.5%).

Compliance rates by hospital and surgical specialty

In both hospitals, all 19 SPOT perioperative safety indicators were observed and evaluated. There were notable distinctions between the institutions. At Sahloul University Hospital, 42.2% (n = 68) of observed operations achieved an acceptable compliance level (≥ 50% of SPOT standards were met). In contrast, Farhat Hached University Hospital had only 23.8% (n = 25) of operations achieving an acceptable compliance.

Across surgical specialties, notable differences in compliance were noted. With 87.1% (n = 27) of operations meeting ≥ 50% of SPOT standards, orthopedic surgery had the highest acceptable compliance rate. However, no operations exceeded the 50% threshold in ophthalmology, burns/plastic surgery, and ENT, which recorded zero acceptable compliance. Of the 266 observed procedures, none had a high compliance rate (≥ 90% of SPOT standards) and only 34.9% (n = 93) showed acceptable compliance with SPOT perioperative safety regulations.

Discussion

Research has shown that in order to increase the quality of surgical care, fostering a strong safety climate and ensuring consistent adherence to perioperative safety standards are imperative [39]. In this context, the present study provides an assessment of perioperative patient safety by examining both the nurses’ perceived safety level and the observed compliance with established safety standards using the SPOT tool in two university hospitals.

Perceived level of perioperative patient safety: a nursing perspective

Perioperative nurses make up the largest professional group in the OR [40] and maintain constant contact with patients during the preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative stages [15]. Therefore, their perceptions are a reflection of their daily interactions with team dynamics, care procedures, and system functioning in the surgical setting [14].

In fact, the nursing team plays an important role in safeguarding patient safety and is part of the overall healthcare quality assurance system [41]. Nursing work provides a unique opportunity to discover healthcare errors before they occur [42]. Once the patient is under anesthesia, surgeons focus primarily on performing the intervention. In contrast, perioperative nurses manage instruments, monitor the surgical site, and coordinate OR workflow, placing them in a key position to detect and prevent surgical AEs [43]. As a result, understanding nurses’ safety perceptions is critical for improving the safety climate in the OR [17].

Multiple organizational factors were strongly associated with a better perceived safety level, revealing opportunities to enhance the perioperative nursing practice. For instance, safety training was associated with higher perceived safety (p = 0.02). Indeed, structured training helps perioperative nurses improve important skills like patient identification, checklist implementation, drug safety, and infection prevention [44]. A randomized controlled trial by Faridi et al. found that training increased nurses’ situational awareness, attentiveness, and confidence in risk management, thereby improving compliance with safety protocols [45]. In addition, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis reported that staff training improves patient safety outcomes by strengthening safety culture and reducing errors and AEs [46].

Regular protocol updates were also identified as a significant predictor of a high perceived safety (p = 0.003). Nurses rely heavily on standardized protocols to reduce variability in care and align clinical actions with evidence-based recommendations [47]. Timely dissemination of protocol updates may support nurses’ decision-making, reinforce professional accountability, and promote consistency [48] within surgical teams. In resource-constrained contexts, irregular protocol transmission can lead to ambiguity and dependence on informal methods, potentially jeopardizing safety [2].

Conversely, poor communication and the loss of patient information during transfers was significantly related to a lower perceived safety level (p = 0.003), emphasizing the vulnerability of transitional phases in surgical care [49]. Because nurses are largely responsible for perioperative handovers, they are acutely aware of how incomplete or inaccurate information transfer raises the likelihood of AEs [50].

Interestingly, a high perceived workload was positively associated with perceived safety level (p = 0.001). This finding contrasts with a significant body of literature that has linked high workload to increased errors, reduced compliance, and compromised patient safety [51]. Caution is therefore warranted when interpreting this finding. One possible explanation is that nurses who report high perceived workload may also possess or employ adaptive coping strategies, increased vigilance, or a heightened sense of professional engagement [52], which could positively influence their perception of safety despite objectively demanding conditions. Additionally, this association may be influenced by perceptual bias in self-reported measures, where safety assessments are shaped by individual experiences and contextual factors [53]. Alternatively, this unexpected finding may be due to residual confounding variables not accounted for in the analysis. Given the cross-sectional design, no causal conclusions can be drawn. Overall, this finding may reflect the complex and subjective nature of safety perception, rather than a direct effect of workload on patient safety.

Audit findings: gaps across the four SPOT dimensions

Our findings showed that none of the SPOT dimensions reached the 90% threshold for good compliance [36], and the overall compliance with perioperative safety guidelines remained below 60%, indicating persistent weaknesses in perioperative safety practices.

Communication and handover

Overall, the highest observed compliance was recorded for communication and handover (59%), revealing significant variation across subdimensions. While intraoperative team communication demonstrated very high compliance (91%), substantial deficiencies were identified during handovers, particularly those occurring during team changes (40.6%) and after surgery (41.3%). Compliance was especially low during transfers to recovery (30.8%) and from recovery to the ward (34.6%). These predominantly nurse-led processes, when performed inadequately, increase the risks of information loss, care discontinuity, and AEs [54, 55].

In contrast, higher compliance was observed for patient identification and transfer (73%), preoperative verification (68.4%), and transport from the OR (82%), suggesting that structured, protocol-driven steps are more consistently applied. Strengthening handover processes through standardized tools, clear role delineation, and interprofessional training remains essential to improving continuity of care and patient safety [56].

An apparent inconsistency emerged between the relatively high level of perceived communication reported by the OR staff and the moderate compliance observed in the communication and handover dimension of the SPOT. This discrepancy warrants cautious interpretation, as it may reflect a conceptual ambiguity, where the notion of “effective communication” differs between self-reported perceptions and observational criteria. This difference may also reflect the subjective nature of self-reported perceptions and the potential influence of social desirability bias. As such, perceived communication quality may not fully capture the accuracy required for safe handover practices.

Medical equipment and surgical counting

Overall, compliance in the medical equipment dimension was low (41.4%). Although availability and readiness of OR equipment were considerably better (66.9%), compliance with surgical item counting was critically low (15.4%). Surgical counts are a vital nursing obligation and a key component of perioperative safety [57]. Failure to adhere to surgical counting protocols may increase the risk of retained surgical items and associated preventable complications [58]. One possible explanation is that the complicated and demanding nature of perioperative nursing practice may explain the low compliance observed as they manage multiple concurrent responsibilities (including instrument handling, coordination with the surgical team, documentation, and patient monitoring), which may sometimes lead to counting procedures being overlooked [59]. Although surgical counts are often perceived as a nursing responsibility, effective counting requires active participation from the entire surgical team [60]. In this context, Gomes et al. stressed that multidisciplinary collaboration is highly recommended for effective counting but remains a considerable challenge [60].

Infection prevention

The infection prevention dimension demonstrated the lowest compliance among all SPOT domains, with only 36.5% of participants adhering to recommended practices. General infection control behaviors reached only 50% compliance, indicating that basic infection prevention practices were only partially achieved in routine perioperative care. This low compliance suggests significant variability in the application of standard precautions. Nurses, as frontline infection prevention agents, play a critical role in enforcing aseptic measures, evaluating compliance, and modeling safe practices in the OR [61]. Closing this gap requires ongoing competency-based training, unannounced compliance audits with immediate feedback, and strong nursing leadership that establishes accountability.

The perception–practice gap in perioperative safety

Overall, while perioperative safety was perceived favorably, the audit revealed notable discrepancies in the consistent application of safety protocols, reflecting a clear perception–practice gap, with perceived safety exceeding actual compliance. Similar findings have been reported by several studies [23–25].

To interpret the “perception–practice gap”, our study draws on established patient safety frameworks. Two complementary perspectives help explain why this gap emerges. First, the distinction between Work-as-Imagined (WAI) and Work-as-Done (WAD) highlights the difference between how safety protocols are designed and how frontline nurses may adapt them in response to real-world constraints such as time pressure, staffing shortages, and limited resources [62, 63]. In this framework, perioperative safety standards represent the “imagined” ideal that is rational and resource-intensive, whereas observed practices reflect “work-as-done”, referring to the actual adaptive behaviors used to manage clinical demands [62, 63]. From this perspective, nurses may perceive safety as adequate despite deviations, possibly because they view them as adaptations and unavoidable in practice. However, this explanation was not directly assessed in our study.

Second, normalization of deviance is a theoretical concept that may also explain this gap [64]. This theory suggests that repeated deviations from standards may gradually become routine when they are not associated with immediate harm, thereby weakening compliance while leaving safety perceptions unchanged [65]. The critically low compliance with surgical item counting (15.4%) likely did not emerge suddenly but incrementally. Each time a count was skipped without a retained surgical item, the behavior may have gradually become normalized. Over time, what was once a violation may have been transformed into the unofficial standard, which may help explain how high perceived safety can coexist with low safety compliance [65, 66]. Although this pattern aligns with the normalization of deviance framework [64], the cross-sectional nature of our audit does not allow us to assess changes over time.

Beyond these theoretical mechanisms, the perception–practice gap may be further sustained by cognitive biases such as social desirability bias, which may lead nurses to overestimate their own compliance [67]. However, as cognitive biases were not directly assessed, this interpretation remains speculative.

Finally, a safety culture that emphasizes awareness without sufficient monitoring and feedback may reinforce positive perceptions without ensuring consistent practice [23].

Limitations

This study presents several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. The cross-sectional design limits the establishment of causal relationships between organizational factors and perceived safety level. Additionally, the existence of residual confounding factors cannot be dismissed, as they may have affected the observed associations.

Furthermore, the study was conducted in only two university hospitals, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other settings. Also, the use of a self-administered questionnaire to collect perception data introduces several potential biases. Specifically, self-reported data may be subject to perceptual bias, reporting bias, recall bias, and social desirability bias.

The perceived safety level was assessed using a single-item measure, which, although supported in the literature as a patient safety outcome indicator, does not capture the multidimensional nature of patient safety culture. Consequently, it provides only a global overview of safety perceptions without reflecting key domains such as teamwork, communication, and organizational support. Therefore, this should be considered when interpreting the findings related to safety perception.

Although the questionnaire was reviewed by experts and pilot-tested to ensure clarity and feasibility, no formal content validity assessment was conducted. Also, observational data may have been subject to observer bias, as surgical procedures were assessed by two auditors. Although efforts were made to standardize data collection through training sessions and consensus meetings, formal inter-rater reliability was not assessed. In addition, the presence of observers in the OR may have influenced staff behavior (Hawthorne effect). To mitigate this, investigators spent time in the units before data collection to allow staff to become accustomed to their presence; however, this effect could not be quantitatively measured.

Finally, the study did not allow for analytical integration between the perceived safety level and the observed compliance data. As perception and observational data were collected independently and anonymously without shared identifiers, it was not possible to link perceptions to observed practices at either the individual or unit level. This limits the ability to formally assess the alignment between perceived and actual safety performance and to explore whether variations exist across clinical contexts. Future studies should adopt designs that enable data linkage to better understand the relationship between perceived safety and observed compliance.

Conclusions

Although most perioperative nurses rated patient safety as good, none of the 266 observed procedures met all SPOT standards, revealing a perception–practice gap where perceived safety does not reflect actual performance. Our findings identify three specific high-risk processes requiring targeted attention: surgical item counting, infection prevention practices, and handover transitions, particularly between the OR and recovery unit. To address these gaps, the following system-level interventions tailored to perioperative settings are recommended. First, strengthen surgical counting through dual-verification protocols involving circulating and scrub nurses, establish interruption-free zones, and use a visible counting board shared with the entire surgical team. Second, enhance infection prevention by ensuring that hand hygiene stations and sterile supplies are readily accessible at critical task junctures, combined with unit-specific observed feedback loops. Third, standardize handovers using structured tools such as the Situation-Background-Assessment-Recommendation (SBAR) checklist, with dual sign-off by the sending and receiving nurses to improve continuity of care and reduce information loss.

At the organizational level, implement regular audit and feedback systems where compliance data are shared monthly with frontline nurses rather than only with managers. Promote non-punitive near-miss reporting to reverse the normalization of deviance. Finally, approaches such as the Functional Resonance Analysis Method (FRAM), aligned with Safety-II and Resilient Healthcare perspectives, may support a deeper understanding of WAD and facilitate context-sensitive improvements in perioperative practice.
