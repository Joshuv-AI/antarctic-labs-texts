Comprehensive genomic identification of essential genes required for Brucella melitensis intracellular survival during macrophage infection
Authors: Zhiqiang Jiang, Jiajun Gao, Mengda Liu, Xiangxiang Sun, Fei Qi, Weixing Shao, Wenlong Nan, Lu Chang, Kaihui Zhang, Xin Yan, Mingjun Sun, Shufang Sun, Xiaoxu Fan, Haobo Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Brucella melitensis is a significant zoonotic pathogen transmissible via contact, digestive, and respiratory routes, posing severe threats to livestock economies and human health. While its virulence genes critically regulate intracellular survival and replication, the molecular mechanisms underlying pathogenesis remain elusive. This study employed Transposon Directed Insertion-site Sequencing (TraDIS) technology to identify key genes essential for the survival of Brucella melitensis strain 16 M within RAW 264.7 macrophages, resulting in the discovery of 374 anti-phagocytic associated essential genes. Through GO and KEGG analyses, these genes were categorized into eight functional classes, representative genes from each category were selected to generate respective gene-deletion mutants, with bacterial virulence and phenotypes validated via intracellular survival assays, in vitro stress tests, carbon source utilization experiments, and RT-qPCR. All ten constructed mutants exhibited reduced macrophage survival capabilities. Deletion of membrane-related genes cydDC and BME_RS07715 attenuated stress resistance, membrane stability, and immune evasion, knockout of metabolic gene ptsP impaired carbon source utilization and intracellular proliferation, and disruption of regulatory gene BME_RS00125 diminished TCA cycle activity and fatty acid metabolism, compromising energy supply and structural integrity. In this study, some key genes of Brucella surviving in macrophages were verified, and preliminarily elucidated the function of these genes in regulating bacterial virulence, providing a theoretical basis for exploring their mechanism.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46186-6.

Introduction

Brucella melitensis, a Gram-negative facultative intracellular pathogen, causes brucellosis—a globally signific-ant zoonosis ranked among the most dangerous infectious diseases transmissible between animals and humans1. Geographically, brucellosis hyperendemicity persists in Mediterranean regions, with annual incidence exceeding 50 cases per 100,000 population in North Africa, the Middle East, northern China (particularly Inner Mongolia), and central/western Greece2. While primarily affecting small ruminants, B. melitensis demonstrates broad host tropism, with substantial spillover into cattle populations and humans through occupational or dietary exposure3. Notably, B. melitensis accounts for > 90% of human brucellosis cases worldwide, contrasting sharply with B. abortus (4.6% of isolates), which primarily infects cattle4,5. This species-specific pathogenicity hierarchy positions B. melitensis as the most virulent Brucella species in humans, capable of inducing severe systemic manifestations including reproductive disorders (spontaneous abortions in livestock, infertility in humans), osteoarticular damage, and chronic debilitating infections6. Its dual impact on livestock productivity (through abortion storms and infertility) and human health (via disabling chronic infections) establishes B. melitensis as a critical One Health pathogen with substantial socioeconomic consequences7.

Following bacterial invasion, macrophages initiate antimicrobial autophagy by encapsulating pathogens within double-membrane autophagosomes. These specialized vesicles subsequently fuse with lysosomes to form autolysosomes, executing targeted degradation of entrapped microbes and thereby restricting bacterial proliferation8. However, evolutionarily successful pathogens have evolved sophisticated countermeasures to subvert this defense system—either by intercepting autophagic signaling pathways or co-opting autophagy machinery to create intracellular niches favorable for replication9. As a professional intracellular pathogen, B. melitensis deploys an arsenal of virulence determinants upon host cell entry10–12. These molecular tools orchestrate immune evasion through suppression of antigen presentation, strategic manipulation of apoptotic pathways, and metabolic piracy of host nutrients essential for bacterial energy production and biomass generation13.While current research has identified some critical virulence genes (T4SS) and stress-response regulators involved in intracellular survival, the molecular choreography underlying their precise anti-phagocytic functions remains a critical knowledge gap. Elucidating these mechanisms could reveal novel targets for disrupting Brucella’s stealth pathogenesis strategy14,15.

The high-throughput transposon mutagenesis screening platform TraDIS has evolved into a powerful genomic tool for systematically identifying essential genes under specific environmental stresses16,17. Transposon sequencing technology has been successfully applied to identify genes essential for in vivo survival in multiple pathogens. Although studies have utilized Signature-Tagged Mutagenesis (STM) to investigate genes associated with Brucella 16 M infection, their screening throughput and resolution were constrained by the technology of that era18. In contrast, the TraDIS technology enables parallel analysis of tens of thousands of mutants in a single run, providing near-saturation coverage across the genome.By precisely counting sequencing reads at each insertion site, it quantifies bacterial adaptability with remarkable accuracy, thereby providing a more comprehensive and detailed map of gene functions.

In this study, we focused on the highly virulent B.melitensis strain 16 M. We constructed a genome-wide transposon mutant library using mini-Tn5 transposon mutagenesis and challenged murine macrophages with this pooled mutant library. Comparative analysis of transposon insertion sites pre- and post-infection was performed via TraDIS (Transposon Directed Insertion-site Sequencing). Through integrated statistical and bioinformatics approaches, we identified 374 candidate genes associated with anti-phagocytic mechanism. From these candidates, we selected 10 genes high-priority genes exhibiting fitness defects for further through anti-phagocytosis tests. To sum up, this study revealed some key gene networks that may exist in Brucella’s resistance to macrophage phagocytosis.

Result

High-throughput generation of transposon mutagenesis libraries for screening in cell models

To dissect the molecular mechanisms underlying B.melitensis 16 M’s intracellular anti-phagocytic capacity, we established a saturated mini-Tn5 transposon mutant library using the reference strain 16 M (3.29 Mb genome comprising two circular chromosomes: Chr I with 2,211 genes and Chr II containing 1,139 genes)2,19. Based on computational modeling predicting a minimum requirement of 30,850 mutants for 99.99% genome saturation, we constructed a mini-Tn5 transposon mutant library exceeding this theoretical requirement by generating > 40,000 kanamycin-resistant clones to achieve saturation-level genome-wide mutagenesis.

Prior to phenotypic screening, the library was subjected to triplicate serial subculturing in tryptic soy broth (TSB) to selectively enrich mutants with unimpaired in vitro growth. For screening in cell models, RAW264.7 macrophages were challenged with the input mutant library at an optimized multiplicity of infection (MOI = 100). Following 24-hour intracellular persistence, bacterial populations surviving macrophage-mediated clearance were recovered as output library. Parallel genomic DNA extraction from input and output libraries enabled comparative TraDIS profiling with deep sequencing mapping insertion site dynamics before and after macrophage selection pressure (Fig. 1).

Genome-wide identification of B.melitensis 16 M anti-phagocytic genes through macrophage infection models by TraDIS

The TraDIS datasets were processed through the Bio: TraDIS computational pipeline for transposon insertion sequencing analysis, including rigorous quality control and identification of transposon-specific barcode sequences. Each individual sample matched approximately 22 million high-fidelity single-end transposon-specific sequencing reads. Subsequent alignment revealed that approximately 65% of reads from each sample showed precise alignment to the B.melitensis 16 M reference genome. The input pools of the two biological replicates contained 39,783 and 39,915 unique transposon insertion sites, which were evenly distributed across the genome. The saturation density was equivalent to one insertion event every 83 and 82 base pairs of the entire genome sequence, and the repeatability was high(Supplementary Table S1).

Quality analysis was performed on the input and output library datasets generated by TraDIS sequencing.Histograms revealed a uniform genomic distribution of transposon insertion sites in the input library, with a density of 7–8 insertion sites per 1000 bp. The average read count across all gene loci was 2048, peaking at 5792 reads for highly represented sites. These metrics confirm adequate library saturation and coverage uniformity. Sporadic low-read regions likely reflect the loss of mutants with growth-impairing insertions during cultivation. In contrast, the output library exhibited marked depletion of both insertion sites and sequencing reads, displaying a reduced density 3–4 insertions per 1000 bp (Supplementary Figure S1). The average per-gene read count decreased to 128 reads (maximum 512 reads), with significantly expanded low-read coverage regions. This selective attrition pattern demonstrates that cell-microenvironmental pressures effectively purge mutants impairing cellular viability, thereby validating the biological relevance of the infection model.

Following data normalization, we performed genome-wide comparative analysis of bacterial fitness determinants through standardized read count processing. Briefly, gene-specific read counts from both input and output libraries were normalized using EdgeR software. Subsequent calculation of normalized output-to-input ratios followed by log2 conversion yielded fold change values (designated as log2(FC)) that exhibited a normal distribution across the genome (Fig. 2A). To identify mutants with significant abundance and substantial variation in the output library, we established selection criteria of confidence level above 90% and P < 0.05, ultimately selecting the threshold of log2(FC) ≥ 4.5. We screened 374 candidate essential genes required for B.melitensis 16 M survival during intracellular infection. Functional annotation through integrated GO and KEGG pathway analysis20,21 revealed these virulence-associated genes to be predominantly associated with Metabolism (28%), Cell envelope biogenesis (16%), Transport systems (11%) and Regulatory networks pathways (8%), as shown in Fig. 2B.

Through screening of anti-phagocytic mechanism-associated candidate genes, we identified a proportion of B.melitensis virulence genes that were both cataloged in the Virulence Factors Database (VFDB) and corroborated by independent literature reports, thereby validating the reliability of our screening methodology. These virulence factors include: (1) Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) biosynthesis genes (gmd, wbkC, pmm, per, wbkA, manBcore) critical for membrane integrity22–26; (2) Iron acquisition systems exemplified by dhbA-mediated siderophore synthesis27; (3) Osmoregulatory components including the cgs-encoded cyclic β−1,2-glucan synthase28; (4) ABC transporter constituents (ugpA, pstC) essential for nutrient transport and redox homeostasis29,30; (5) Secretion system elements (secA, secY, secG) governing protein translocation31; and (6) Flagellar assembly components (flgA, flgB, flhB, fliP, fliO, fliQ) involved in motility and host interaction32. This integrated analysis highlights evolutionarily conserved virulence determinants spanning membrane architecture, metabolic adaptation, and host-pathogen interface mechanisms. Based on GO and KEGG annotations, we selected representative genes that exhibited the most significant differences across eight core functional categories—including ‘oxidative phosphorylation,’ ‘ABC transporter systems,’ ‘TCA cycle,’ and ‘two-component systems’—for the construction of knockout Mutants(Supplementary Figure S2) and subsequent functional validation.

Role of membrane structure-associated genes in anti-phagocytosis

The bacterial cell membrane and its associated structures, together with transport-associated proteins, constitute key factors for Brucella’s adaptation to the intracellular environment of the host cell and the maintenance of virulence33,34. These membrane complexes establish a physical barrier essential for nutrient uptake, immune evasion, and virulence factor secretion34–36. Embedded within these structures, the transport system facilitates nutrient acquisition, sustains intracellular homeostasis, and coordinates immune evasion37,38. Synergistic interactions between membrane components and transport machinery collectively enhance bacterial survival capacity and pathogenicity34.

Differential analysis identified 58 cell envelope biosynthesis-related anti-phagocytosis essential genes (16% of core essential genes) and 40 transporter-associated anti-phagocytosis essential genes (11% of core essential genes), including previously validated virulence factors nikB39, secG40, ccoO41, sufC42, omp3133, and plsY43. Select appropriate targets (cydDC, BME_RS11035, BME_RS17320, BME_RS09965, BME_RS07715, and BME_RS00135) from the genes/gene clusters with a log₂(FC) value ≥ 6.4, which are the top 11%, and validate them in a macrophage infection model (Table 1). The results demonstrated that the virulence of all deletion mutants was significantly attenuated compared to the wild-type 16 M strain (Fig. 3AB). Notably, ΔcydDC and ΔBME_RS07715 mutants displayed the most pronounced virulence attenuation.

To elucidate the underlying causes of the significantly attenuated virulence observed in the ΔcydDC and ΔBME_RS07715 mutants.We constructed cydDC and BME_RS07715 complementary strains and performed in vitro stress assays to characterize potential virulence-related pathways44. In acid stress tests, both ΔcydDC and ΔBME_RS07715 mutant strains demonstrated heightened acid sensitivity. At pH 4.0, the survival rates of the ΔcydDC and ΔBME_RS07715 mutants decreased by 79.9% (P < 0.001) and 71.8% (P < 0.001), respectively, compared to the wild-type strain, and by 45.7% (P < 0.05) and 63.1% (P < 0.001), respectively, compared to the complementary strains. At pH 4.5, the survival rate of ΔcydDC decreased by 59.5% and 25.4% (P < 0.05) compared to the wild-type and complementary strains, respectively, while that of ΔBME_RS07715 decreased by 81.4% and 80.1% (P < 0.05), respectively. At pH ≥ 5.5, only ΔBME_RS07715 exhibited a significant reduction in survival rate, with a decrease of 87.2% (P < 0.01) compared to the wild-type strain. (Fig. 3CD).The CVs in the test results ranged from 1.32% to 9.67% (Supplementary Table S7).

In the osmotic stress assay, only the ΔBME_RS07715 mutant strain exhibiting pronounced hypersensitivity. Under hyperosmotic conditions (1 M, 0.5 M, and 0.25 M hyperosmotic solutions), when compared to the wild-type strain, the survival rates decreased by 69.1%, 80.6%, 75.9%, and 69.1%, respectively, compared to the wild-type strain (all P < 0.05),(CVs: 1.99–9.55%) while no significant differences were observed when compared to the complementary strains (Fig. 3EF). These results lead us to hypothesize that the ΔBME_RS07715 mutation may induce structural or functional impairments related to osmotic regulation, which cannot be rescued by plasmid complementation45,46.

In the oxidative stress assay, the ΔcydDC mutant strain exhibited pronounced hypersensitivity to oxidative stress. Following exposure to 1 mM H₂O₂, its survival rate plummeted to levels below the detection threshold (< 0.1%), while the wild-type and complementary strains remained detectable (5.0% and 1.0%, respectively; P < 0.05). Under 2 mM H₂O₂-induced oxidative stress, the survival rate of the ΔBME_RS07715 mutant decreased by 87.7% and 84.4% compared to the wild-type and complementary strains, respectively (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3GH)(CVs: 3.25–9.65%). Collectively, these findings suggest that cydDC and BME_RS07715 may play critical roles in the oxidative stress response, and their deletion can compromise the bacterial capacity to scavenge reactive oxygen species.

Based on the results from the ΔcydDC stress experiments, we postulated that the heightened sensitivity of ΔcydDC to acidic and oxidative environments might potentiate a broad intracellular killing effect. To investigate this, we employed RT-qPCR to examine the dynamic fluctuations of pro-inflammatory cytokines in macrophages at 24 h post-infection. Compared to the uninfected control group, all three strains (16 M, CΔcydDC, and ΔcydDC) significantly upregulated the expression of IL-1β and TNF-α (P < 0.05), while no significant differences were observed in IL-6 and IL-12 levels(CVs: 0.65–9.94%). Notably, the ΔcydDC mutant elicited a more robust inflammatory response: IL-1β levels (9.09 ± 0.45) were 2.8-fold and 2.4-fold higher than those induced by the wild-type strain (3.24 ± 0.21) and the complementary strain (3.75 ± 0.23), respectively (P < 0.01). Similarly, TNF-α levels (6.64 ± 0.32) were 2.4-fold higher than those triggered by both the wild-type and complementary strains (P < 0.001) (Fig. 3I). These observations imply that deletion of cydDC may induce a heightened immune response.

The stress assay results of ΔBME_RS07715 consistently demonstrated sensitivity, however, in certain test outcomes, the complementary strain failed to attain the wild-type strain’s performance level. Integrating with the bioinformatics prediction that BME_RS07715 may encode a membrane structural protein, we hypothesized that deletion of this gene might have induced irreversible damage to the bacterial structure. To verify this, we performed a polymyxin B stress assay on ΔBME_RS07715. The results revealed that at concentrations of 1200 IU/mL, 600 IU/mL, and 300 IU/mL, relative to the wild-type strain, the survival rates of ΔBME_RS07715 decreased by 74.9% (P < 0.01), 60.7% (P < 0.05), and 37% (P < 0.05) respectively (Fig. 3J)(CVs:2.58–9.65%).No significant discrepancies were detected between ΔBME_RS07715 and CΔBME_RS07715, aligning with the osmotic stress assay findings. This further suggests that structural defects induced by gene deletion cannot be rectified through functional complementation.

Metabolic candidate genes of phagocytosis resistance

Metabolic pathways are pivotal in the pathogenic mechanisms of Brucella47. By supplying energy to the pathogen, participating in biosynthesis, and mediating signal transduction, they underpin the survival and pathogenicity of Brucella within the host48,49. Consequently, metabolism-related genes exhibit significant enrichment. A total of 105 metabolism-associated anti-phagocytic essential genes were identified, constituting 28% of shared essential genes. These genes are extensively involved in diverse critical metabolic processes, encompassing the tricarboxylic acid cycle (sucD, mdh), tyrosine metabolism (hpaDEHI), sulfur metabolism (cysQGE), urease synthesis (ureG), purine metabolism (purQ), glutamate metabolism (gltA), and fatty acid-dependent metabolic processes associated with the biosynthesis of type IV secretion system components.

Select genes/gene clusters (ptsP, BME_RS02260, BME_RS00880, BME_RS03140) with log₂(FC) ≥ 7.0, which are in the top 6% (Table 2), and validate them in a macrophage infection model. Results revealed that compared with the wild-type 16 M strain, the intracellular survival rates of deletion mutants for three metabolism-related genes were significantly reduced (Fig. 4A).These findings suggest that these three metabolism-related genes may be critical for the colonization and intracellular survival of Brucella within host cells, and that functional impairment of these genes may compromise the bacterium’s ability to adapt effectively to the intracellular environment. Among these, the ptsP gene has been demonstrated to be essential for the pathogenicity of Escherichia coli and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, yet its function in Brucella remains unclear. Functional predictions from the NCBI database suggest that ptsP may be involved in glucose uptake and utilization50. Thus, we constructed the corresponding gene-deletion complementary strain and assessed its growth dynamics across distinct single carbon sources (including PTS substrates glucose, fructose, N-acetylglucosamine (NAG), and the non-PTS substrate succinate)51.

Experimental results revealed differential growth rates among the three strains across carbon sources, with the most rapid growth observed on glucose and the slowest on succinate. Specifically, compared to the wild-type 16 M strain and CΔ ptsP, the growth rate of Δ ptsP was reduced by 58.9% and 62.7% on glucose (P < 0.05), by 77.1% and 76.5% on fructose (P < 0.001), by 43.6% and 21.2% on NAG (P > 0.05), and by 39.6% and 59.2% on succinate (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4B)(CVs: 0.84–10.09%).

These findings indicate that the protein encoded by ptsP likely serves as a critical component of the phosphoenolpyruvate-sugar phosphotransferase system (PTS), playing a key role in Brucella’s ability to uptake and utilize diverse carbon sources. Disruption of this gene impairs the bacterium’s capacity to normally utilize these carbon sources for growth and metabolism, leading to significant growth inhibition50.

Regulatory Genes Involved in anti-phagocytosis

Genes associated with regulation can dynamically modulate gene expression in response to changes in the host microenvironment, ensuring bacterial persistence under conditions of nutrient limitation, immune pressure, and physicochemical stress52,53. For example, the BvrS/BvrR system is currently recognized as a critical “central regulatory network” governing Brucella pathogenicity54. In our TraDIS analysis, we identified 31 regulatory genes essential for anti-phagocytosis related to environmental adaptation, accounting for 8% of the shared essential genes. These include genes related to two-component systems (such as BME_RS10075, BME_RS04875, BME_RS14065, ychF, BME_RS14360), oxidative stress regulation (such as bcp, ccoO, petA), and transcriptional regulation (sfsA) (Table 3). From the screening results, we selected ΔBME_RS00125 with a log₂(FC) of 8.13 for verification via an intracellular survival assay. Cell survival experiments demonstrated that after 24-hour interaction with macrophages, the bacterial load was only 52.18% of the wild type, with a significant decrease in survival rate. (Fig. 5A).

Based on NCBI and UniProt gene function predictions, BME_RS00125 may encode a protein containing a GMC family oxidoreductase N-terminal domain. The GMC family is involved in multiple carbon metabolism processes, including the tricarboxylic acid cycle and fatty acid metabolic pathways55,56. Since succinate dehydrogenase and acetyl-CoA are key factors participating in the tricarboxylic acid or fatty acid cycles57,58. We measured the expression levels of metabolite-related proteins in the deletion mutant and wild-type strains using RT-qPCR, targeting succinate dehydrogenase and acyl-CoA genes. Compared to uninfected controls, three strains (16 M, ΔBME_RS00125, and CΔBME_RS00125) exhibited increased expression of both enzymes. However, the succinate dehydrogenase activity in ΔBME_RS00125 (2.67 ± 0.18) was only 27.2% of that in the wild-type strain (9.79 ± 0.37) and 34.9% of that in the complementary strain (7.64 ± 1.45). Discussion

Brucella is a critical zoonotic pathogen capable of infecting humans and diverse mammalian species, leading to brucellosis and posing a significant threat to public health security1,2. The virulence genes of this pathogen are pivotal in regulating its intracellular survival and replication9. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying Brucella pathogenesis remain poorly understood, and the functions of numerous genes within its genome await elucidation59.Despite employing a high-throughput TraDIS strategy similar to previous studies60, yet our research subjects and core biological questions differ fundamentally. Brucella’s survival relies more on precise regulation of the intracellular environment, and our findings reveal a unique set of genes supporting its intracellular parasitic lifestyle.These genes are mainly involved in membrane structure, metabolism and regulation, which contribute to the understanding of the pathogenic molecular mechanism of Brucella and offer potential avenues for the analysis of its survival and replication in macrophages.

The ABC protein transport system is widely conserved across bacteria, functioning in nutrient uptake, toxin excretion, and virulence factor secretion, certain genes within this system serve as critical bacterial virulence determinants61. For intracellular survival, studies demonstrate that Brucella’s ABC transporter Cgt facilitates cyclic β-glucan (CβG) transport to form biofilms, protecting bacteria from immune cell-mediated killing62. Similarly, the ABC transporter YejABEF confers resistance to host antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) through active efflux mechanisms63. In our screening results, the cydDC gene exhibited a log2(FC) of 8.1 following macrophage infection. Deletion of cydDC reduced intracellular survival by 79.9%, with phenotypic analysis revealing heightened sensitivity to acid and oxidative stress, alongside enhanced induction of host inflammatory factors. As reported in existing literature, the cydDC complex is essential for cytochrome bd assembly and cysteine/glutathione transport, and plays a role in regulating the periplasmic redox balance64,65. The observed stress sensitivity and immune activation phenotype in cydDC-deficient strains likely stem from its inability to maintain the periplasmic reducing environment, rendering bacteria more vulnerable when encountering phagosomes.

The bacterial cell envelope functions as a critical barrier against diverse environmental stressors. Through sophisticated sensory and regulatory mechanisms, bacteria perceive and adapt to host-derived stress signals, enabling them to evade immune-mediated clearance, sustain nutrient acquisition, and establish intracellular colonization and replication66. In Brucella abortus, the EipB-TtpA protein complex maintains outer membrane integrity via a unique periplasmic β-helix interaction, which is essential for bacterial survival and pathogenicity67. In our screening of membrane-associated genes, BME_RS07715, exhibiting a log2(fold change) ≥ 6.4, was prioritized for functional characterization. Deletion of this gene resulted in 45.7% reduced intracellular survival, accompanied by heightened sensitivity to acidic conditions, osmotic fluctuations, oxidative stress, and polymyxin B. Bioinformatic predictions suggest BME_RS07715 encodes a cell wall hydrolase, potentially regulating peptidoglycan turnover and maintaining structural integrity through controlled cleavage and ligation reactions. Its putative role in cysteine/glutathione transport may further modulate periplasmic redox homeostasis, with disrupted expression triggering pleiotropic stress responses. Notably, BME_RS07715 upregulation during host cell infection likely represents an adaptive mechanism to counteract dynamic intracellular microenvironments. Intriguingly, CΔBME_RS07715 failed to restore wild-type survival levels under hyperosmotic, hypoosmotic, or polymyxin B challenge conditions. These results demonstrate the critical role of BME_RS07715 in stress resistance and intracellular survival of Brucella. Based on bioinformatic predictions, we hypothesize that its encoded cell wall hydrolase may contribute to maintaining peptidoglycan structural integrity and potentially function in periplasmic redox homeostasis, thereby influencing envelope stability. However, the specific enzymatic substrates, regulatory networks, and precise molecular pathways through which this protein leads to the observed pleiotropic phenotypes remain unknown.

The nitrogen-related phosphotransferase system (PTSNtr) plays a significant role in regulating nitrogen and carbon metabolism as well as the pathogenicity of certain Gram-negative bacteria. Although rhizobia lack PTS transport components, their PTSNtr system remains functionally present68. Studies have demonstrated that deletion of PTSNtr enzymes in legume-nodulating rhizobia results in pleiotropic growth phenotypes, dry colony morphology, and post-translational inactivation of ABC transporters69. Our findings revealed that the log2(FC) value of the ptsP gene in the PTSNtr system reached 6.3. Deletion of this gene led to a significant reduction in Brucella virulence and impaired growth ability in single-carbon-source media, indicating that ptsP plays an important role in carbon-source utilization and pathogenic processes, yet its precise mechanistic role remains unclear. However, accumulating evidence highlights the significance of PTSNtr in bacterial virulence. For instance, in Escherichia coli, the PTSNtr system regulates ion homeostasis and nutrient acquisition through the non-phosphorylated PtsN protein70. In Pseudomonas aeruginosa, PtsN within the PTSNtr system influences genes involved in type III secretion, potassium transport, and pyoverdine biosynthesis, thereby modulating bacterial virulence71.In summary, this study reveals the significance of the PTSNtr system in the pathogenicity of Brucella, although further investigation is required to elucidate its underlying mechanisms. These findings provide new insights for the development of novel anti-Brucella strategies.

When confronted with nutrient deficiency, immune challenges, and environmental stressors, bacteria modulate relevant genes to adapt to host environmental changes, thereby preserving their infectivity47. This adaptive regulation represents a key mechanism underlying bacterial intracellular survival and pathogenesis. In Escherichia coli, the ArdA protein functions as an anti-restriction factor. Through a conserved 20-amino-acid motif in its N-terminal domain, ArdA inhibits the activity of the H-NS silencing protein and facilitates horizontal gene transfer of mobile genetic elements, enabling bacteria to overcome restriction barriers and rapidly adapt to novel genomic environments72. In our screening results, deletion of BME_RS00125 gene not only reduced bacterial virulence to 52.18% but also led to 43.3% downregulation in the expression levels of succinate dehydrogenase and acyl-CoA compared to the 16 M wild-type strain. Within the intracellular niche, bacteria must upregulate specific metabolic pathways to sustain survival and replication15.Our validation results for BME_RS00125 demonstrate that this gene can influence bacterial intracellular survival through regulatory mechanisms. Although the specific pathways by which BME_RS00125 modulates metabolic activity remain unclear, further investigation into its molecular mechanisms of action will enhance our understanding of the complex relationship between metabolic adaptation and pathogenicity in Brucella, thereby providing a theoretical foundation for clinical prevention and control strategies.

In conclusion, this study employed an array of strategies, including TraDIS analysis, gene-deletion mutant construction, intracellular survival assay validation, and RT-qPCR detection to identify key genes that mediate the anti-phagocytic activity of Brucella.These findings may reveal the complex and diverse regulatory mechanisms underlying Brucella’s adaptation to environmental stresses. Notably, the study may confirmed that the genes cydDC, ptsP, BME_RS07715, and BME_RS00125 play critical roles in Brucella’s intracellular survival, highlighting the centrality of transport systems, metabolic adaptation, membrane integrity maintenance, and stress response pathways in immune evasion. Beyond deepening our understanding of Brucella’s pathogenic mechanisms, these results provide a theoretical foundation for developing novel antibacterial targets or attenuated vaccine strains. However, this study has limitations. While a large number of candidate genes were identified, only a subset have been functionally validated, and their underlying mechanisms remain elusive. Future research should focus on elucidating the specific functions and regulatory networks of these genes, thereby advancing our comprehension of Brucella’s host adaptation mechanisms and providing a more robust theoretical basis for developing Brucella infection prevention and treatment strategies.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and culture media

The wild-type strain of B. melitensis 16 M, which is preserved by the National Animal Brucellosis Para-Reference Laboratory, was utilized for infection studies, library construction, and stress tests. The Escherichia coli S17 λ pir strain and WM3064 strain were obtained from Biovector Science Lab, Inc. The pUTmini-Tn5km2 (Ampr, Kanr) plasmid was provided by the China Animal Health and Epidemiology Center. All the strains and plasmids used in this study are specifically listed in Table S2 of the supplementary materials. Brucella was cultured in tryptic soy broth (TSB) medium. The two E. coli strains acting as conjugation donors were cultured in different media. Specifically, the E. coli S17 λ pir strain was cultured in Luria-Bertani (LB) medium, while the E. coli WM3064 strain required the addition of 2,6-diaminopimelic acid (DAP) to the LB medium for proper growth. The antibiotics employed in the experiment and their corresponding working concentrations were as follows: ampicillin (100 µg/ml), chloramphenicol (35 µg/ml), kanamycin (50 µg/ml), nalidixic acid (25 µg/ml), and a 10% sucrose medium was also included. All culture operations were carried out under the conditions of a biosafety level 3 laboratory to ensure the biosafety of the experiment.

Cultivation of RAW 264.7 macrophage cells

The macrophage culture medium used was Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco). It was supplemented with GlutaMAX™ and 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco). The cells were incubated in a constant-temperature incubator at 37 °C with 5% carbon dioxide.

Generation of mutant libraries

One milliliter of the overnight-cultured B. melitensis 16 M bacterial suspension was washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Then, it was mixed with 50 µL of the overnight-cultured conjugative E. coli S17 λ pir bacterial suspension carrying the pUTmini-Tn5km2 Kanr plasmid. As this plasmid contains the Tn5 transposase, it can generate a large number of transposon mutants. The transposon mutants of B. melitensis 16 M were screened on tryptic soy agar (TSA) plates supplemented with ampicillin and nalidixic acid18. After incubation, sterile PBS was added, and the Kanr-resistant and Nal-resistant colonies were resuspended in sterile LB medium using a sterile spreader. The total number of colonies was estimated by counting the colony numbers on multiple plates. Each batch was estimated to contain 2,000 mutants. By pooling 20 mutant batches, a total of at least 40,000 mutants were generated. Subsequently, the mini-Tn5 mutants were cultured in tryptic soy broth (TSB) at 37 °C for three rounds, with each round lasting 48 h. The finally stably-growing mutant library was used as the inoculum for cell infection.

Infection of RAW 264.7 macrophages using a transposon mutant library

The stable transposon mutant library was cultured until the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) reached 1. Then, it was diluted in macrophage culture medium to achieve a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 100. The diluted bacterial suspension was added to a 24-well cell culture plate seeded with macrophages at a density of 1 × 106 cells/mL. For each time point, four 24-well plates were prepared. The cell plates were centrifuged at 1000 g for 5 min at 4 °C. Subsequently, they were incubated in an environment containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C for 1 h. After incubation, the medium was replaced with fresh DMEM medium containing 200 µg/mL gentamicin to kill extracellular bacteria. This time was recorded as 0 h post-infection. Then, 50 µg/mL gentamicin was added, and the culture was continued to be incubated in an environment with 5% CO2 at 37 °C for 24 h and 48 h. At each infection time point (0 h, 24 h, and 48 h), the medium was removed, and each well was washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Then, the macrophages were lysed with PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 at 37 °C for 10 min. The resulting macrophage lysates were spread on 100 TSA medium plates supplemented with nalidixic acid and incubated at 37 °C for 4 days to allow colony formation. These colonies were then collected for genomic DNA (gDNA) extraction and sequencing of the Tn5-gDNA junction sites.

TraDIS

The mutants that grew stably following successive sub-culturing and the mutants screened by the macrophage model were used as the input library and output library for TraDIS, respectively. Genomic DNA was extracted from the input and output libraries using a bacterial genomic DNA extraction kit from TaKaRa (Qingdao Madisaisi Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). Five micrograms of genomic DNA was fragmented to generate fragments approximately 150 base pairs (bp) in length by the company (Qingdao Shenggong Bioengineering Co., Ltd., Qingdao, China). DNA end-repair was carried out first. Then, DNA end-adenylation was performed. Subsequently, adapter ligation was conducted, all according to the Illumina TruSeq Nano DNA Library Preparation Reference Guide (Revision D). The PCR enrichment step was carried out under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 98 °C for 30 s, followed by 22 cycles. Each cycle consisted of denaturation at 98 °C for 10 s, annealing at 60 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min. Two replicates were performed for each biological sample group. Paired-end sequencing of the input and output sample libraries was performed by the company (Qingdao Shenggong Bioengineering Co., Ltd., Qingdao, China) on the Illumina HiSeq 6000 sequencing platform.

TraDIS data analysis

BioTraDIS (version 1.4.5) was employed to screen the original sequencing reads at the transposon end (P5 end) from the FASTQ files. Subsequently, these reads were aligned with the genome of the wild-type B. melitensis 16 M strain (the GenBank accession numbers for chromosomes are NC_003317.1 and NC_003318.1, respectively). Next, the raw read counts of each gene and the number of different insertion sites were analyzed and calculated. This analysis was carried out to evaluate the fitness of the corresponding gene mutants and to identify genes essential for intracellular survival73. After that, the normalized read counts were imported into the Bioconductor packages EdgeR (3.22.5) and DEGseq (1.34.1). These packages were used for differential analysis of read counts between the input and output libraries and for visualization of insertion sites.

Recombinant DNA techniques

PCR, electroporation, and DNA gel electrophoresis were all performed following Sambrook and Russell’s methods73. A DNA fragment purification kit from TaKaRa (Qingdao Madisaisi Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) was used to purify plasmids and PCR products. Then, all deletion strains were constructed using the previously described allelic exchange strategy. Details of the primers used to amplify the target genes and their upstream and downstream regions for homologous recombination are listed in Supplementary Table S3. All oligonucleotide primers used in this study were purchased from Qingdao Ruibo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. All mutants were constructed based on the wild-type B. melitensis 16 M strain. Homologous recombination was carried out using purified PCR products containing 500-nucleotide homologous extension sequences. After that, the plasmid was removed on a sucrose medium by transforming the pRE112 plasmid carrying the sacB gene. The mutants were verified by PCR and sequencing. For gene complementation experiments, the coding sequences of the relevant genes and their putative promoter regions were amplified from the wild-type B. melitensis 16 M strain (primers used are listed in Supplementary Table S4). These were cloned into the pBBR1MCS-2 vector using the seamless cloning method and then transformed into the corresponding gene-deletion strains.

Infection of RAW 264.7 macrophages and CFU counting

The procedure for infecting RAW 264.7 macrophages and performing colony-forming unit (CFU) counting was similar to that for infecting RAW 264.7 macrophages with the transposon mutant library described earlier, with the only difference being the inoculum. Here, the inoculum was the recombinant strain that had been cultured overnight. After the infection process, the infected macrophages were broken down (lysed). The resulting lysate was then serially diluted and cultured on tryptic soy agar (TSA) medium. Once the colonies had grown, they were counted to determine the number of colonies per milliliter of the lysate.

In vitro stress tests

When performing in vitro stress tests, the cultured bacterial suspension was initially washed. Subsequently, its optical density at 600 nm (OD600) was measured to estimate the bacterial quantity, and the suspension was then diluted to a concentration of 1 × 106 colony-forming units per milliliter (CFU/mL). The bacteria were then incubated under various stress conditions for 30 min. For each strain, three replicates were set up under each stress condition. After the stress treatment concluded, the samples were subjected to serial dilution and then plated for colony counting to assess the survival rate of the strains under different stress scenarios. Specifically, in the acid stress test, the bacteria were cultured in tryptic soy broth (TSB) liquid media with pH values adjusted to 5.4, 4.5, and 4.0 for 30 min. In the hyperosmotic stress test, the bacteria were incubated in hyperosmotic solutions containing sorbitol and sodium chloride (NaCl) at concentrations of 0.25 mol/L, 0.5 mol/L, and 1 mol/L for 30 min. For the hypoosmotic stress test, the bacteria were incubated in sterile water for 30 min. In the oxidative stress test, the bacteria were treated with hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) solutions at final concentrations of 1 mmol/L, 2 mmol/L, and 4 mmol/L for 30 min. Finally, in the polymyxin B stress test, the bacteria were cultured in TSB liquid media supplemented with polymyxin B at concentrations of 1200 IU/mL, 600 IU/mL, and 300 IU/mL for 30 min.

Determination of the expression levels of macrophage inflammation-related factors

Total RNA was extracted from uninfected cells and cells that had been infected for 24 h using the Trizol method. The extracted RNA was then reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a first-strand cDNA synthesis kit. Subsequently, with the synthesized cDNA serving as a template, real-time fluorescent quantitative PCR was employed to detect the relative mRNA expression levels of pro-inflammatory factors, namely interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-12 (IL-12), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), in macrophages 24 h after infection. The reaction system composition and the cycling procedure for real-time fluorescent quantitative PCR are detailed in Supplementary Table S5 and S6. Supplementary Table S5 presents the components and their respective concentrations in the reaction system, while Supplementary Table S6 shows the temperature and time settings for each step of the PCR cycling. Taking IL-6 as an example, the calculation formula for the ΔΔCt value is as follows: ΔΔCt(sample)=[Ct(IL-6, sample) − Ct(IL-6, control)]−[Ct(Mouse SDHA, sample) − Ct(Mouse SDHA, control)].

Determination of the growth rates of Δ ptsP in media with different carbon sources

The Brucella strains were cultured until the OD600 value reached approximately 1. The bacterial suspensions of different strains were then diluted to ensure the same bacterial quantity. Subsequently, these diluted suspensions were inoculated into media containing different carbon sources, namely glucose, fructose, N-acetylglucosamine, and succinate. Three replicates were set for each strain. After 12 h of cultivation, the bacterial suspensions were serially diluted and plated for colony counting to evaluate the survival rate of each strain under different stress conditions.

Determination of the regulatory gene expression level of BME_RS00125

The total RNA of Brucella that had interacted with macrophages for 24 h was extracted using the Trizol method. Three biological replicates were set to ensure the reliability and reproducibility of the experimental results. Biological replicates are independent samples that capture the natural variation within the experimental population. After the RNA extraction, the extracted RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA. Subsequently, real-time fluorescent quantitative PCR was performed to detect the relative mRNA expression levels of succinate dehydrogenase and acyl-CoA.
