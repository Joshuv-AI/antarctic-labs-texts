Electrostatics in Materials Revisited: The Case of Free Charges Combined with Linear, Homogeneous, and Isotropic Dielectrics
Authors: Dimosthenis Stamopoulos, Patrick Fiorenza
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractHere we revisit the electrostatics of material systems comprising of free charges and linear, homogeneous, and isotropic (LHI) dielectrics. We focus on D(r) suggesting that this is the primary vector field of electrostatics. We show that D(r) is sufficient to conceptually describe all underlying physics and to mathematically accomplish all necessary calculations, beforehand, independently of the secondary vector fields P(r) and E(r) that, if needed, can be easily calculated from D(r). To this effect, we introduce a P-D electric susceptibility, χε, with −1≤χε≤0, that couples linearly P(r) with D(r) (instead of the standard P-E electric susceptibility, χe, with 0≤χe<∞, that couples linearly P(r) with E(r)). This concept restores the somehow misleading causality/feedback between P(r) and E(r) of the standard formulation, captures efficiently the underlying physics, enables electrostatics to obtain a form analogous to that of magnetostatics, and facilitates analytical/computational calculations in relevant systems. To document these claims, we provide technical means, among others, the free scalar potential, Ufr, and clarify the conditions that enable the calculation of D(r) on a standalone basis, directly from the free charge density, ρf, and the electric susceptibility, χε, of the LHI dielectrics. Our concept sets interesting perspectives for the treatment of all dielectrics.Keywords: electrostatics in materials, dielectrics, electric polarization, electric susceptibility

1. Introduction

Electromagnetism is a cornerstone theory of classical physics that ever since its conceptualization [1,2,3] still develops under our need to theoretically explain relevant phenomena and to accurately design useful applications [4,5,6,7]. Maxwell’s equations constitute the fundamental set of relations for the mathematical description of electromagnetism [8,9,10,11,12,13,14,15]. Apart from electrical conductivity that relates to the existence of free charges and currents, another key property that differentiates electromagnetism in matter, from vacuum, is the electric and magnetic polarization, whether this is exogenously imposed (induced polarization) or endogenously established (permanent polarization) [8,9,10,11,12,13,14,15]. In both cases, the concept of bound charges and currents can be employed to model the polarization of matter [16,17,18,19]. Ultimately, matter can be completely replaced by these free and bound charges and currents that, now, exist in vacuum.

In recent decades, advancements in materials science and engineering have expanded the boundaries of what we used to call “matter”. For instance, “matter” traditionally referred to ordinary single/poly-crystalline and amorphous materials consisting of elemental atoms. This ordinary “matter” is produced at the laboratory in various forms (three-dimensional bulk, low-dimensional films, rods and spheres, etc.) by means of a variety of techniques (solid and wet chemistry, sol-gel, thermal evaporation, dc/rf sputtering, etc.). Nowadays, “matter” also includes extraordinary metamaterials consisting of simple/complex building units, of sizes ranging from nanometers to centimeters, that are carefully (self)assembled to form artificial structures. This extraordinary “matter” is produced by means of relatively standard or even highly sophisticated manipulation/patterning techniques (cantilever-based manipulation and lithography, photochemical etching, electron-beam lithography, etc.). It is probably not unexpected that “matter” of such diversity does not ‘behave homogeneously’ in respect to electric and magnetic polarization. Inevitably, depending on the size and the physical properties of the employed constituents and the characteristic length scales of “matter” that they assemble, the distinction between free and bound charges and currents can be quite unclear [20]. Apparently, a variety of physical concepts and mathematical tools should be available to model, both reliably and flexibly, the electric and magnetic polarization of such multifaceted “matter”.

Probably, the most profound endogenous ‘asymmetry’ between electricity and magnetism stems from the constitutive relations that define the respective polarization, electric, P(r), and magnetic, M(r). Specifically, the electric polarization, P(r), is defined through the so-called electric field, E(r), that relates to both free and bound charges, while the so-called auxiliary field, D(r), (else, electric displacement) that relates to (however, not solely depends on) free charges is introduced through the fundamental relation Dr=ε0Er+P(r). On the contrary, the magnetic polarization, M(r), is defined through the so-called auxiliary field, H(r), (else, magnetizing field) that relates to (however, not solely depends on) free currents, while the so-called magnetic field, Br, that relates to both free and bound currents, appears in the fundamental relation Br=μ0(Hr+Mr). Obviously, a complete analogy between electricity and magnetism requires that P(r) should be defined through D(r).

Here we discuss these issues for the electrostatics of systems comprising of free charges and linear, homogeneous, and isotropic (LHI) dielectrics. The latter constitute a wide class of materials with many applications. Also, though many dielectrics are not LHI strictly speaking, they exhibit a quasi linear/homogeneous behavior so that they can be approximated as LHI quite effectively. We introduce a new formulation between the electric polarization, P(r), and the electric displacement, Dr, that are directly coupled in a linear fashion through a P-D electric susceptibility, χε (to be distinguished from the standard P-E one, χe, that couples linearly P(r) and Er). The underlying physics of the direct, linear constitutive relation between P(r) and Dr through χε is discussed in detail. Since Dr is the primary vector field that should be obtained beforehand, independently of the secondary P(r) and Er, we provide thorough technical documentation on its mathematical calculation for any generic system that comprises of free charges and LHI dielectric materials. The introduction of the new formulation of the electric susceptibility, χε, and polarization, P(r), in respect to the primary vector field, Dr, restores mathematical and conceptual flaws which exist in the standard formulation based on the secondary vector field, Er. Also, it establishes the analogy in the definition of electric and magnetic polarizations, Pr and Mr, in respect to the vector fields, Dr and Hr. The foreground mathematics and the underlying physics of the primary vector field, Dr, and of the new formulation, ‘P-D, χε’, are discussed in detail. Representative problems referring to standard geometries met in applications are discussed for the two formulations on a comparative basis, to document their quantitative consistency and highlight the physical advancements and possible technical advantages of the new formulation, ‘P-D, χε’, in respect to the standard one, ‘P-E, χe’.

Interestingly, the linear relation (2) implies that instead of the standard one (1), where χe is the P-E electric susceptibility (0≤χe<∞), an alternative, effective P-D electric susceptibility, could be defined, as well, that couples directly P(r) and Dr. Indeed, from a clearly algebraic point of view, we could define the effective P-D electric susceptibility, χef=χe/(χe+1), through Pr=χefDr, (where 0≤χef≤1, since 0≤χe<∞) and abandon the use of the P-E electric susceptibility, χe, once and for all. Then, based on the fundamental relation Dr=ε0Er+P(r), the vector field Er should be coupled with P(r) and Dr through the linear relations Er=((1−χef)/χef)(1/ε0)Pr and Er=(1−χef)(1/ε0)Dr, respectively. Obviously, before we adopt any strategy of this kind, we should ensure that the effective P-D electric susceptibility, χef, captures consistently the underlying physics of the system in a way equivalent (if not, even, advantageous) to that of the standard P-E electric susceptibility, χe.

The above discussion proves two crucial characteristics which, though inherent in LHI materials, are always overlooked in the standard formulation employed today. First, since the divergence and curl of the primary vector field D(r) are known from the very beginning (relations (7) and (15)–(16)), based on the Helmholtz theorem, D(r) can be calculated beforehand, that is independently of the secondary vector fields P(r) and Er. Technically, D(r) seems to depend exclusively on the free charge density through relation (7). However, the properties of the LHI materials (i.e., χe) can be imprinted onto D(r), as well, through the relevant boundary conditions that should be satisfied at the interfaces of discontinuous dielectric media. This point is clearly elucidated below and in Appendix A.1 of the Appendix A. Second, once D(r) is known, the electric polarization, P(r), is ultimately coupled to it through relation (3). As discussed above, this evidences that there is still some room to express the linear relation between P(r) and Dr in a direct way, Pr=χefDr, without using Er as an intermediate field. Ultimately, this could help us to bypass any conceptually misleading argumentation and mathematical complications on the causality/feedback between P(r) and Er (see [8] pages 68 and 76; [13] page 186) and to restore the “asymmetry” between electrostatics and magnetostatics in respect to the polarization properties.

3. Definition of a P-D Electric Susceptibility, χε, and Constitutive Relations Between the Vector Fields D(r), P(r), and E(r)

Following the above considerations, here we introduce a new formulation between the electric polarization, P(r), and the electric displacement, Dr, that are directly coupled in a linear fashion through a P-D electric susceptibility, χε. We recall that the standard P-E electric susceptibility, χe, couples the electric polarization, P(r), and the electric field, Er, relation (1). To clearly distinguish the two cases, we use the notation ‘P-D, χε’ for the one introduced here and ‘P-E, χe’ for the standard one, currently in use (notice the different subscripts ‘ε’ and ‘e’).

Starting from the fundamental relation Dr=ε0Er+Pr, we introduce the constitutive relation Pr=−χεD(r), else −Pr=χεDr, and easily get Dr=(ε0/(1+χε))Er, where we employ the newly defined P-D electric susceptibility, χε. Likewise, by introducing the reverse electric polarization, P~r=−P(r), relation Pr=−χεD(r) becomes P~r=χεD(r). The use of the reverse electric polarization field, P~r=−Pr, instead of Pr may probably seem as a weird algebraic maneuver. However, the introduction of P~r=−P(r) is based on the physical fact that P~r directly relates to the so-called internal electric field, Eint(r), produced by the bound charges (else, depolarizing field (see [8] pages 93–96), or self-field (see [14] pages 127, 158, and § 6.6.2; [15] page 24)), through P~r=−Pr=ε0Eint(r) [25]. Thus, P~r carries much physical information on a standalone basis (more details are given below). By using the above relations, we easily obtain the electric field through Er=(1/εε)Dr, where we defined εrε=1/(1+χε) the P-D relative permittivity and εε=ε0εrε=ε0/(1+χε) the P-D permittivity.

No matter how we define the scalar coefficients that in LHI dielectrics couple linearly Dr, Pr, and Er, these vector fields should be unaltered between different formulations. Consequently, through a comparison of Dr=(ε0/(1+χε))Er with the standard relation (2) that is based on the P-E electric susceptibility, χe, we see that the newly defined P-D electric susceptibility, χε, relates to χe through 1+χε=1/(1+χe), else χε=−χe/(1+χe). Obviously, the newly defined P-D electric susceptibility, χε, ranges within −1≤χε≤0, thus spanning the entire range 0≤χe<∞ of the standard P-E electric susceptibility, χe, as it should. Notably, the respective P-D relative permittivity, εrε, ranges within 1≤εrε<∞, that is the exact same range, 1≤εr<∞, of the standard P-E relative permittivity, εr.

4. Considerations on the Physics of the P-D Electric Susceptibility, χε, Formulation

4.1. Conceptually Misleading Causality/Feedback Between Pr and Er

In the standard formulation of the P-E electric susceptibility, χe, the electric polarization, Pr, couples to the electric field, Er, through relation (1). Accordingly, the electric field induces an electric polarization that in turn contributes to the electric field and so on. Thus, in the P-E, χe, formulation, the distinction between the cause and causal is somehow obscured by this strange feedback. This is addressed in many textbooks. For instance, in page 68 of [8], this is described as an “…awkward situation: the polarization of the dielectric depends on the total electric field in the medium, but a part of the electric field is produced by the dielectric itself…” (see page 76 of [8], as well). Also, in page 186 of [13], it is mentioned that “…the external field will polarize the material, and this polarization will produce its own field, which then contributes to the total field, and this in turn modifies the polarization, which…Breaking out this infinite regress is not always easy”. In Section 7 below and in Appendix A.2 of the Appendix A we show that, indeed, this “infinite regress” between Pr and Er is mathematically described by an infinite geometric series that, quaintly, seems to converge for the entire range 0≤χe<∞ (though, it should not!) to the standard result obtained by other methods. Our P-D, χε, description is immune to these conceptual and mathematical complications.

Interestingly, to bypass the “awkward situation” described above, in page 186 of [13], it is proposed that “The simplest way is to begin with the displacement, at least in those cases where D can be deduced directly from the free charge distribution”. In the present work, we follow this exact suggestion: by using the P-D, χε, formulation, we focus on the primary vector field of electrostatics, Dr, and provide mathematical means that enable us to deduce it in all cases beforehand, independently of the secondary vector fields, P~r=−Pr and Er, by taking into account both the free charges and the dielectric properties of the LHI materials. Then, P~r=−Pr and Er, can be obtained from Dr through relations (17) and (18) (see Section 7 below and Appendix A.1 and Appendix A.2 of the Appendix A).

4.2. Direct and Concise Description of the Physics of the LHI Dielectrics

The P-D, χε, formulation describes the underlying physics of the LHI dielectrics in a clear way. First, in this scheme, the induced bound charge density, ρbr, is linearly coupled to the free one, ρfr, in a direct way through relation (26). Since −1≤χε≤0 (relation (20)), we see that the induced ρbr is always of opposite sign to ρfr, thus bound charges screen free ones, as expected. In addition, the lowest value that χε can obtain, χε=−1, limits physically the maximum absolute value that the bound charge density can obtain; ρbr should never exceed the free charge density, ρfr, since the electrically neutral state (where ρr=ρfr+ρbr=ρfr−ρfr=0), has the lowest electrostatic energy. Second, the new scheme reveals straightforwardly the well-known fact that dielectrics tend to lower the electric field, Er, in their interior. Indeed, the fact that −1≤χε≤0 and 1≤εrε=(1+χε)−1<∞ (relations (20)–(22)) ensures that the electric field, Er, given by relation (18) is always lower inside a dielectric, ultimately getting Er=0 when χε=−1 (εrε,ε→∞). This fact is also evidenced by the standard P-E, χe, formulation employed today. Indeed, since 0≤χe<∞ and 1≤εr=1+χe<∞ (relations (4)–(6)), it is obvious that Er given by relation (2) is always lower inside the dielectric, eventually becoming Er=0 when χe→∞ (εr,ε→∞).

As already stated above, the introduction of the reverse electric polarization field, P~r=−Pr, is based on the physical fact that it relates to the so-called internal electric field, Eint(r), through P~r=−Pr=ε0Eint(r) [25]. More explicitly, Eint(r) is produced by the bound electric charges that, for the case of the LHI dielectrics discussed here, reside exclusively at the interfaces between distinct media of different dielectric properties. Notably, Eint(r) of the bound electric charges acts against the external electric field, Eext(r), of the free electric charges, thus it lowers the electric field, E(r), inside dielectrics, and tends to destabilize polarization order as well (see Section 7 below and Appendix A.2 of the Appendix A). Interestingly, the divergence of the reverse electric polarization P~r=−Pr is compatible to that of Er and Dr; these vector fields point outwards from a positive charge density/source, while they point inwards to a negative charge density/sink (∇·P~r=ρbr, ∇·Dr=ρfr, and ∇·Er=ρr/ε0).

4.3. Symmetry in the Electrostatics and Magnetostatics of LHI Materials

The P-D, χε, formulation of the LHI dielectrics is based on the consideration that Dr is a primary vector field that can be calculated beforehand, independently of the secondary vector fields Pr and Er (for instance, by using one of the strategies discussed below). Then, Pr and Er can be easily found since they couple linearly to Dr through P~r=−Pr=χεD(r) and Er=((1+χε)/ε0)Dr=(1/ε0εrε)Dr=(1/εε)Dr, (where −1≤χε≤0 and 1≤εrε=1+χε−1<∞). In analogy, in LHI diamagnetic materials, Hr can be considered as a primary vector field that can be calculated beforehand, independently of the secondary vector fields Mr and Br (for instance, by means similar to the ones discussed below for Dr). Notably, in the standard M-H, χm, formulation of magnetostatics, Mr and Br couple linearly to Hr through Mr=χmH(r) and Br=μ0(1+χm)Hr=μ0μrHr=μHr (where −1≤χm≤0 and 0≤μr=1+χm≤1). In this sense, in LHI materials, electricity (Er=1/ε0Dr+(−Pr)=1/ε0(Dr+P~r)) obtains a somehow symmetric formulation to that of magnetism (Br=μ0(Hr+Mr)).

The above analogy between the formulations, P-D, χε, and M-H, χm, of the dielectric and diamagnetic LHI materials is neither coincidental nor artificial. It clearly evidences a basic property that dielectric and diamagnetic materials have in common: they screen their interior from the external stimulus, the electric and magnetic field, respectively. Indeed, the P-D, χε, scheme introduced here for the dielectric materials, P~r=−Pr=χεD(r) and Er=(1/ε0)Dr+P~r=(1/ε0)Dr+χεDr=(1/ε0)1+χεDr with −1≤χε≤0, eventually leads to Er=0 when χε=−1. This is directly analogous to the M-H, χm, scheme of diamagnetic materials where Mr=χmH(r) and Br=μ0Hr+Mr=μ0Hr+χmHr=μ01+χmHr with −1≤χm≤0, that enables Br=0 when χm=−1 [26].

Due to these facts, analogously to the LHI diamagnetic materials, on the basis of the P-D, χε, formulation, the LHI dielectric materials could be termed ‘diaelectric’ (di(a)electric).

5. Technical Considerations on the Calculation of D(r)

From a technical point of view, the introduction of the P-D electric susceptibility, χε, with −1≤χε≤0 is meaningful only if D(r) can always be calculated beforehand (that is independently of E(r)) so that, in turn, P~r=−P(r) can be derived through relation (17), while E(r) can be obtained through relation (18). Once this is proved, D(r) can rightfully be called primary vector field, while P(r) and E(r) can be termed secondary vector fields. To this effect, below, we provide means to calculate D(r) on a standalone basis in systems wherein a known free charge density, ρf(r), coexists with LHI dielectrics of known P-D electric susceptibility, χε. Apparently, except for the methods discussed below, additional ones can also be employed, such as Green’s functions, method of images/inversion, etc., [8,9,10,11,12,13,14].

5.3. Dr and Ufr: Dependence on the Dielectric Properties, Irrotationality and Continuity

Irrespectively of which strategy of the above we will employ to calculate Dr and Ufr, we stress that in the general case, they cannot depend exclusively on the free charges. Under specific circumstances, the properties of the LHI dielectrics can be imprinted onto Dr and Ufr through the relevant boundary conditions discussed above, that should be satisfied at the interfaces of discontinuous dielectric media. Closely related to this issue are the irrotationality and the continuity of Dr and Ufr, respectively, at the interior and at the interfaces of LHI dielectrics. These issues are discussed in detail in Appendix A.1 of the Appendix A.

Here, we clarify briefly these issues that relate both conceptually to the physics and technically to the mathematics of the systems under investigation: First, the conditions under which Dr depends not only on the free charges but also on the properties of the LHI dielectrics. Second, the origin of a probably non irrotational character of Dr. Third, the cause of a possibly non continuous behavior of Ufr.

First, from relation (41) we see that when Dr and P~r=−P(r) are exclusively normal to the interface S/ of the LHI dielectrics, the bound vector potential, Abr, does not exert any influence on both Dr and Ufr; neither through the volume term since at the interior of the LHI dielectrics ∇×Dr=0 (recall relation (31)), nor through the surface term since at the interfaces of distinct LHI dielectrics, the respective boundary condition is trivially satisfied (recall relation (46)). Accordingly, when Dr and P~r=−P(r) are exclusively normal to the interface S/ of the LHI dielectrics, the algebraic expressions of Dr and Ufr should depend solely on free charges, relations (43), (50), (52) and (53), and should not contain any information that relate to the dielectric properties of the LHI materials. On the contrary, when Dr and P~r=−P(r) have at least one component tangential to the interface S/ of the LHI dielectrics, Dr and Ufr will depend on both the free charges and the dielectric properties of the LHI media (e.g., the P-D electric susceptibility, χε) since the respective boundary conditions, relations (47) and (54), inevitably introduce χε in the algebra. Details can be found below and in Appendix A.1 of the Appendix A.

Second, while the electric displacement, Dr, is obviously irrotational at the interior of all LHI dielectrics, it is not necessarily irrotational at the interface S/ of distinct dielectric media having different properties (e.g., different P-D electric susceptibility, χε). Specifically, at an interface S/, Dr should obey the boundary conditions of relation (43) (normal component, D⊥r) and relations (46)–(47) (tangential components, D//r). The latter inevitably imprints on Dr the behavior of P~r=−P(r). Thus, when Dr and P~r=−P(r) have components tangential to interface S/, Dr will attain a non irrotational behavior at S/ due to the inherent discontinuity of the electric polarization, P~r=−P(r), that we apparently experience at the interface S/ as we move between two different dielectric media. On the contrary, when Dr and P~r=−P(r) are exclusively normal to the interface S/, the boundary conditions of relations (46)–(47) are trivially satisfied so that Dr obtains an irrotational behavior at S/. Details can be found in Appendix A.1 of the Appendix A.

Third, the discussion on the continuity of the free scalar potential, Ufr, at an interface S/, follows closely the one made above on the irrotational behavior of Dr, since these two entities couple through relation (52). While the free scalar potential, Ufr, is obviously continuous at the interior of all LHI dielectrics, it is not necessarily continuous at the interface S/ of distinct dielectric media having different properties (e.g., different P-D electric susceptibility, χε). Specifically, it comes out that when Dr and P~r=−P(r) have components tangential to interface S/, the non irrotational behavior of Dr will dictate a non continuous character onto Ufr at S/. On the contrary, when Dr and P~r=−P(r) are exclusively normal to the interface S/, the irrotational behavior of Dr will enable Ufr to preserve a continuous character at S/. Details can be found in Appendix A.1 of the Appendix A.

To conclude, when Dr and P~r=−P(r) are exclusively normal to the interfaces of distinct LHI dielectric media, Dr is irrotational and Ufr is continuous, not only at the interior of these LHI dielectric media but also at their interfaces. Then, the expressions of Dr and Ufr should depend solely on free charges. On the contrary, when Dr and P~r=−P(r) have at least one component tangential to any interface, while still Dr is irrotational and Ufr is continuous at the interior of the dielectrics, they are non irrotational and non continuous, respectively, at the relevant interfaces. Now, Dr and Ufr depend on both the free charges and the properties of the LHI dielectrics (e.g., the P-D electric susceptibility, χε).

Here, we summarize the above information in a ready-to-use technical scheme for both Dr and Ufr: (i) Referring to the electric displacement, Dr, it can be obtained directly through the standard integral and differential equations discussed in Section 5.1, together with the boundary conditions of relations (43) and (47). (ii) Regarding the free scalar potential, Ufr, it can be obtained directly through the standard integral, differential equation, and multipole expansion techniques discussed in Section 5.2, together with the boundary conditions of relations (53) and (54). In this case, Dr is finally obtained through relation (52).

Putting aside the above discussion based on mathematics, below we present a schematic argumentation, as a rule of thumb, for the most efficient calculation of the primary vector field, Dr. Practically, whether Dr will depend solely on the free charges or it ultimately will be affected by the properties of the LHI dielectrics is determined, first, by the geometric morphology of both the free charges and LHI dielectrics and, second, by the relative orientation of their surfaces.

We recall that the system under investigation comprises of free charges and LHI dielectrics. Initially, consider only the free charges existing in the otherwise free space (the LHI dielectrics are removed temporarily) and calculate the relevant external electric displacement, Dextr, based on the most convenient strategy of those reported above (we use the term external to stress the fact that this component originates solely from the free charges in the absence of the LHI dielectrics). Then, introduce the LHI dielectrics at their former positions and check the relative coordination between their interfaces and the ‘frozen’ Dextr. If Dextr is exclusively normal to the interfaces of all LHI dielectrics, the final expression of the total electric displacement, Dr, will not be affected by the properties of the LHI dielectrics. Thus, the total Dr should be identical to the external Dextr, that is Dr=Dextr. On the contrary, if Dextr has at least one component tangential to any of the interfaces of the LHI dielectrics, apart from the original information on the free charges, the final expression of the total electric displacement, Dr, will contain information on the properties of the LHI dielectrics. This will be realized mathematically by means of the boundary conditions that the total Dr should satisfy.

7. Consistency and Advancements of the New P-D, χε, Formulation

The new formulation, P-D, χε (relations (17)–(22)) provides a different and clear viewpoint to the underlying physics of systems comprising of free charges and LHI dielectrics, in comparison to the standard one, P-E, χe (relations (1)–(6)). On quantitative terms, these two formulations should be absolutely consistent with each other. All physical entities such as scalar potentials, vector fields, Dr, Pr, and Er, dipole moments, p, bound charge densities, ρbr, etc., should attain the same form whether we employ the standard P-E, χe, or the new P-D, χε, formulation. Nevertheless, the new formulation advances the understanding of the underlying physics since, among other, it successfully restores the mathematical and conceptual flaws which are inherent in the standard formulation. Here we document these issues through detailed results on some representative problems met in applications (see right below a basic example; more cases are considered in Appendix A.2 of the Appendix A).

The case of a dielectric sphere placed in an externally applied, uniform electric field is considered right below since this is a popular building element employed in many applications, such as scattering, invisibility cloaks, etc., [28,29,30,31,32]. In [28] (pages 136–141) and [29] (pages 74–79), the case of a LHI dielectric sphere with permittivity ε1 embedded in a LHI dielectric host medium of permittivity εm was considered. The system was subjected to an external, uniform electric field. This system is analytically considered quite usually since it is extendedly met in relevant scattering problems of electromagnetic radiation by subwavelength spherical particles; the effective dipole moment and polarizability which describe the complete system (sphere and host medium) are introduced in various formulas of subwavelength scattering such as scattering matrix elements and cross sections [28].

Thus, right below we consider a very relevant problem. A dielectric LHI sphere of P-E/P-D electric susceptibility χe/χε and radius a is placed with its center at the origin of the spherical coordinate system and is subjected to an external, uniform electric field directed along the z-axis, Er=E0z^. The quantitative consistency between the two formulations P-E, χe, and P-D, χε, is evidenced in Section 7.1 and Section 7.2. More importantly, a fundamental mathematical and conceptual flaw of the standard formulation, P-E, χe, and its restoration by the new one, P-D, χε, is documented in Section 7.3.

7.3. Solution Based on the P-E Electric Susceptibility, χe, and the P-D Electric Susceptibility, χε, by Means of Series

Here we analytically present the solution, for both formulations, by means of series. This approach is used to simulate mathematically the “…awkward situation…” described at page 68 in [8] “…the polarization of the dielectric depends on the total electric field in the medium, but a part of the electric field is produced by the dielectric itself…” and at page 186 in [13] “…the external field will polarize the material, and this polarization will produce its own field, which then contributes to the total field, and this in turn modifies the polarization, which…Breaking out this infinite regress is not always easy”. Accordingly, this approach considers the externally applied vector field (Er for the standard formulation P-E, χe and Dr for the new one P-D, χε) as the first term of a series; this term induces an initial polarization to the specimen. In turn, the initial polarization produces a reaction field that adds to the external one. The new total field (superposition of the external and the reaction fields) induces an extra polarization to the specimen which produces an extra reaction field and so on. Each successive term of this iterative procedure is added to the first term (external field) so that ultimately the total field is just the sum of an infinite series. In this procedure a specific point should be carefully considered: in all cases the relevant convergence criterion has to be fulfilled. As we show below, while this is not formally assured for the standard formulation P-E, χe, it is inherently guaranteed for the new one P-D, χε.

7.3.1. P-E, χe, Formulation

Here we employ a series-based approach with the standard formulation of the P-E electric susceptibility, χe, and focus directly on the electric polarization, Pr, and field, Er, to clarify their causality/feedback for the inside space, r≤a, of the LHI dielectric sphere.

Let us consider an experiment wherein we apply an external electric field E0z^ to a specimen, trying to get information on its dielectric properties (susceptibility, χe, relative permittivity, εr, etc.). We focus on the initial stage where the response of the dielectric to the external stimulus, E0z^, still develops (transient state), that is before it eventually attains its permanent form (steady state). One could probably expect that the external electric field, E0z^, applied by the user, would penetrate the specimen and polarize it both permanently and exclusively, in the sense that the following relation should hold, Pin r=χeε0Einr=χeε0Eextinr=χeε0E0z^, where Einr is the total electric field inside the dielectric specimen. Indeed, this is what happens, however, neither permanently, nor exclusively. The above relation will hold only at the first moment of the ‘infinite regress of the P-E polarization process’. An additional electric field of internal origin, Eintinr, will immediately appear, gradually evolve and eventually be established inside the dielectric specimen. Eintinr is produced by the polarization/bound charges of the specimen (see below) and adds to the external electric field Eextinr=E0z^. Following this scheme, the external electric field can be considered as the zeroth-order term, E0inr=E0z^, of the total electric field, Einr=Eextinr+Eintinr, that gradually develops (transient state) and eventually will be established (steady state) inside the dielectric specimen. Accordingly, the zeroth-order term, E0inr=E0z^, will polarize, partially, the specimen. The respective zeroth-order term of the electric polarization, P0inr, induced by E0inr, is P0inr=χeε0E0inr=χeε0E0z^. As we showed in both Section 7.1 and Section 7.2 above, a uniformly polarized sphere of polarization Pr, relates to a bound surface charge density σbr|r=a=r^·Pr|r=a, that produces an internal electric field (depolarizing field/self field) Eintinr=−(1/3ε0)Pinr=(1/3ε0)P~inr at the inside space. Thus, the zeroth-order term of the polarization P0inr will produce a first-order term for the internal electric field given by Eint,1inr=−(1/3ε0)P0inr (notice that the term Eint,0inr does not exist; the only zeroth-order electric field term is of external origin, E0inr=E0z^). In turn, the first-order term, Eint,1inr, will induce a first-order term for the polarization P1inr=χeε0Eint,1inr that subsequently will produce a second-order term for the internal electric field Eint,2inr=−(1/3ε0)P1inr, and so on. Thus, in general, the (i-1)-order term of the induced polarization is Pi−1inr=χeε0Eint,i−1inr, while the (i)-order term of the internal electric field is Eint,iinr=−(1/3ε0)Pi−1inr. Combining these last two relations on Pi−1inr and Eint,iinr, we get Eint,iinr=−χe/3Eint,i−1inr=−χe/3−χe/3Eint,i−2inr=(−(χe/3))(−(χe/3))(−(χe/3))Eint,i−3inr=⋯=(−(χe/3))iE0inr. Accordingly, the total electric field will simply be Einr=E0inr+Eint,1inr+Eint,2inr+⋯+Eint,iinr+⋯=E0inr+(−(χe/3))E0inr+(−(χe/3))(−(χe/3))E0inr+⋯+(−(χe/3))iE0inr+⋯, else Einr=∑i=0∞−χe/3iE0inr. The geometric series results in ∑i=0∞(−(χe/3))i=1/(1−(−(χe/3)))=1/(1+χe/3) so that ultimately Einr=E0inr/(1+χe/3), else Einr=(3/(3+χe))E0z^. This result is identical to the one obtained in Section 7.1 above, as expected.

This ‘infinite regress of the P-E polarization process’ applies, also, to the bound surface charge density, σbr|r=a, that gradually develops (transient state) and eventually will be established (steady state) at the interface, r=a, of the two dielectrics (sphere and vacuum). It should be noted that the bound charge density, σbr|r=a, which is induced by the primary source, the free charge density, acts as secondary source and ultimately produces inside the dielectric the internal electric field, Eintinr. Specifically, the zeroth-order term of the polarization, P0inr=χeε0E0inr=χeε0Eextinr=χeε0E0z^, will induce a zeroth-order term in the bound surface charge density, σb,0r|r=a=r^·P0 inr|r=a=r^·(χeε0E0inr)|r=a=r^·χeε0Eextinr|r=a=χeε0E0cosθ. Then, σb,0r|r=a will produce a first-order term of the internal electric field, Eint,1inr, inside the dielectric sphere, that opposes the external electric field, E0z^, thus reducing it. Then, Eint,1inr will induce a first-order term in the electric polarization, P1 inr, that in turn induces a first-order term in the bound surface charge density, σb,1r|r=a=r^·P1 inr|r=a, responsible for the second-order term of the internal electric field, Eint,2inr, and so on. It can be easily shown that the (i)-order term of the bound surface charge density is σb,ir|r=a=r^·Piinr|r=a. The (i)-order term of the polarization is easily obtained through Piinr=(−(χe/3))iχeε0E0z^. Thus, σb,ir|r=a=(−(χe/3))iχeε0E0cosθ and eventually the bound surface charge density established at the sphere-vacuum interface is σbr|r=a=∑i=0∞σb,ir|r=a=(∑i=0∞(−(χe/3))i)χeε0E0cosθ=(1/(1+χe/3))χeε0E0cosθ. We see that the ‘infinite regress’ on the bound surface charge density results in σbr|r=a=(3/(χe+3))χeε0E0cosθ=3ε0((εr−1)/(εr+2))E0cosθ. This is the exact same result obtained in Section 7.1 above by other means. The internal electric field ultimately produced by σbr|r=a is Eintinr=−((εr−1)/(εr+2))E0z^ (notice that it opposes to Eextinr=E0z^) and relates to the polarization through Eintinr=−(1/3ε0)Pinr.

Summarizing the processes described above, the application of the external electric field Eextinr=E0z^ ultimately induces inside the dielectric sphere a polarization Pinr=3ε0(χe/(εr+2))E0z^ which establishes a bound surface charge density σbr|r=a=3ε0((εr−1)/(εr+2))E0cosθ, which in turn produces an internal field Eintinr=−((εr−1)/(εr+2))E0z^, which opposes the external one, Eextinr=E0z^. Thus, the externally applied electric field, Eextinr=E0z^, inside the dielectric sphere evolves to Einr=Eextinr+Eintinr=E0z^−((εr−1)/(εr+2))E0z^=(3/(χe+3))E0z^. Obviously, the internal electric field, Eintinr, opposes the external one so that Einr<Eextinr=E0z^. In addition, it is evident that Eintinr actually acts toward the depolarization of the dielectric sphere, thus it is commonly termed as depolarizing field or self field (see [8] pages 93–96; [14] pages 127, 158 and § 6.6.2; [15] page 24)). Finally, it should be stressed that these processes take place due to the finite size of the specimen; in an infinite specimen, the ‘lack of external surfaces’ will result in σbr|r=a=0 and Eintinr=0, so that the electric field inside the specimen will simply be equal to the externally applied one, Einr=Eextinr=E0z^.

We note that the distinction between ‘transient’ and ‘steady’ states discussed above under the concept of an ‘infinite regress of the P-E polarization process’ is only schematic. These processes are instantaneous. Nevertheless, the series-based approach of this scheme restores qualitatively the conceptually misleading causality/feedback between Pr and Er which is inherent in the standard P-E, χe, formulation (see [8] pages 68 and 76; [15] page 186, and Section 4 above). However, still, there is a serious quantitative obstacle: in strict mathematical terms, the above geometric series should converge only when |−χe/3|<1 [33], and since by definition 0≤χe<∞, the allowed interval should be 0≤χe<3. Nevertheless, we do not raise any doubts or constraints on the obtained solution of Einr and use it in the entire range, 0≤χe<∞. This is one of the inherent ill-defined points of the standard P-E, χe, formulation.

7.3.2. P-D, χε, Formulation

The alternative P-D, χε, formulation (−1≤χε≤0) introduced here should be immune to the conceptual and mathematical complications of the standard P-E, χe, formulation discussed above. Here we check this issue by using the series approach for the electric displacement inside the dielectric sphere, Dinr, even if we had to. To this end we have to express Dinr in the form Dinr=D0inr+Dint,1inr+Dint,2inr+⋯+Dint,iinr+⋯=∑i=0∞Dint,iinr. First, we recall that in Section 7.2 above, we have found for the inside space that the total electric displacement is Dinr=(3/(2χε+3))D0z^. Due to the superposition principle, the internal electric displacement, Dintinr, produced by the bound charges of the LHI dielectric sphere at the inside space is Dintinr=Dinr−Dextinr=(3/(2χε+3))D0z^−D0z^=(−2χε/(2χε+3))D0z^ (where Dextinr=D0z^ is the external electric displacement applied by the free charges at the inside space). By expressing Dintinr through the reverse electric polarization, P~inr=(3χε/(2χε+3))D0z^, we have Dintinr=(−2/3)P~inr. Now we turn our interest in finding the general term, Dint,iinr, of the series Dinr=∑i=0∞Dint,iinr. Obviously, the zero-order term D0inr is the external electric displacement that originates from free charges, that is D0inr=D0z^. This term will induce the zero-order term of the reverse electric polarization, P~0inr=χεD0inr=χεD0z^. Subsequently, P~0inr will produce the first-order term of the electric displacement, Dint,1inr. By using the general relation, Dintinr=(−2/3)P~inr, found above we have for the particular case, Dint,1inr=(−2/3)P~0inr. In turn, Dint,1inr will induce the first-order term P~1inr=χεDint,1inr that subsequently will produce the second-order term Dint,2inr=−2/3P~1inr, and so on. Accordingly, in the general case, the (i-1)-order term of the electric displacement, Dint,i−1inr, will induce the (i-1)-order term of the reverse electric polarization P~i−1inr=χεDint,i−1inr that in turn will produce an (i)-order term of the electric displacement, Dint,iinr=(−2/3)P~i−1inr. Thus, we easily get the following recurrence relation Dint,iinr=(−2χε/3)Dint,i−1inr. By progressively expanding Dint,iinr down to the zero-order term we have Dint,iinr=−2χε/3Dint,i−1inr=−2χε/3−2χε/3Dint,i−2inr=−2χε/3−2χε/3−2χε/3Dint,i−3inr=⋯=−2χε/3iD0inr. Thus, the electric displacement inside the dielectric sphere is Dinr=∑i=0∞Dint,iinr=∑i=0∞(−2χε/3)iD0inr=∑i=0∞(−2χε/3)iD0z^, else Dinr=∑i=0∞−2χε/3iD0z^. Based on mathematical criteria [33], the series should converge only when |−2χε/3|<1. This condition is fulfilled in the entire range of χε, since −1≤χε≤0 (relation (20)).

We continue with the investigation of the ‘infinite regress’ for the bound surface charge density, σbr|r=a. The bound charge density, σbr|r=a, that is induced by the primary source, the free charge density, acts as secondary source and ultimately produces inside the dielectric the internal electric displacement, Dintinr. Specifically, the zeroth-order term of the polarization, P0inr=−χεD0inr=−χεDextinr=−χεD0z^=−χεε0E0z^, will induce a zeroth-order term in the bound surface charge density, σb,0r|r=a=r^·P0 inr|r=a=r^·−χεD0inr|r=a=r^·−χεDextinr|r=a=−χεD0cosθ. Then, σb,0r|r=a will produce a first-order term of the internal electric displacement, Dint,1inr, inside the dielectric sphere, that opposes the external electric displacement, D0z^, thus reducing it. Then, Dint,1inr will induce a first-order term in the electric polarization, P1 inr, that in turn induces a first-order term in the bound surface charge density, σb,1r|r=a=r^·P1 inr|r=a=r^·(−χεDint,1inr)|r=a, responsible for the second-order term of the internal electric displacement, Dint,2inr, and so on. It can be easily shown that the (i)-order term of the bound surface charge density is σb,ir|r=a=r^·Piinr|r=a. The (i)-order term of the polarization is easily obtained through Piinr=(−(2χε/3))i(−χεD0)z^. Thus, σb,ir|r=a=(−(2χε/3))i(−χεD0)cosθ and eventually the bound surface charge density established at the sphere-vacuum interface is σbr|r=a=∑i=0∞σb,ir|r=a=(∑i=0∞(−(2χε/3))i)(−χεD0)cosθ=(1/(1+2χε/3))(−χεD0)cosθ. We see that the ‘infinite regress’ on the bound surface charge density results in σbr|r=a=−(3χε/(2χε+3))D0cosθ, where D0=ε0E0. This is the exact same result obtained in Section 7.2 above by other means. The internal electric displacement ultimately produced by σbr|r=a is Dintinr=−(2χε/(2χε+3))D0z^. Notice that since −1≤χε≤0 Dintinr is homoparallel to Dextinr=D0z^ so that the total electric displacement, Dinr, inside the LHI dielectric sphere is higher that the externally applied, Dextinr=D0z^, as expected. Most importantly, we see that even for the bound surface charge density investigated here, based on mathematical criteria [33], the above series should converge only when |−2χε/3|<1. This condition is fulfilled in the entire range of χε since −1≤χε≤0 (relation (20)).

The above discussion clearly documents that the P-D, χε formulation is inherently free of any misleading issue and mathematical flaw from which the standard P-E, χe formulation suffers. More cases are presented analytically on the same comparative basis in Appendix A.2 of the Appendix A.

Returning to the issue of quantitative consistency of the two formulations, obviously all standard physical entities should attain the exact same form, irrespectively of the employed one. As an example, in Appendix A.3 of the Appendix A we show that the atomic/molecular polarizability (Clausius-Mossotti relation) attains the same form whether we employ the standard formulation P-E, χe, or the new one P-D, χε.

Finally, long time ago, it was fairly documented that the standard P-E electric susceptibility, χe, (relations (1)–(6)) cannot obtain negative values, relation (4), in systems under thermodynamic equilibrium (see [10] §14 pages 58–59 and [34]). Clearly, the new P-D electric susceptibility, χε, introduced here (relations (17)–(22)) does not violate this principle since as can be seen by the relation χε=−χe/(1+χe), when the P-D electric susceptibility, χε, ranges within −1≤χε≤0, the standard P-E electric susceptibility, χe, spans within 0≤χe<∞ as it should. However, the discussion on “matter” (ordinary, out of thermodynamic equilibrium, and extraordinary, artificial metamaterials) which exhibits negative values of the standard P-E electric susceptibility, −1≤χe≤0, has gained much interest in the recent decades [35,36,37]. Such property would be highly beneficial for applications. The present work of ours on the P-D electric susceptibility, −1≤χε≤0, could provide alternative means to access this issue.

In [30], a radially inhomogeneous core-shell dielectric sphere was considered for utilization in subwavelength scattering. The core was LHI, while the shell was linear and isotropic, however, inhomogeneous with permittivity function ε(r). This core-shell dielectric sphere was embedded in a LHI dielectric host medium and was subjected to an external, uniform electric field. For the radial dependence of the permittivity function ε(r), various cases were considered, such as power-law, linear exponential, and inverse linear exponential [30]. Also, in [32], a core-shell spherical structure was investigated with the permittivity of the core and shell ε1 and ε2, respectively. This building element was embedded inside a host medium of permittivity εm. An external, uniform electric field was applied. The core of the structure was the area to be invisible under the protective action of the shell. All core, shell, and host medium were LHI dielectrics (with the shell having both positive and negative permittivity), while the overall core-shell sphere was obviously inhomogeneous.

Thus, right below, we consider a relevant example. A linear and isotropic dielectric sphere of radius a is radially inhomogeneous with P-D electric susceptibility χε(r) depending solely on the distance, r, from its center, which coincides with the origin of the spherical coordinate system. A free point charge Q resides at the origin, producing the external component, Dextr, of the primary vector field, Dr, which polarizes the dielectric sphere. Here we briefly tackle the problem in the framework of the P-D, χε formulation following the argumentation of Section 5.3 and especially the rule of thumb illustrated in Figure 1 (the simple case of a LHI dielectric sphere with a point charge Q at its center is presented in Appendix A.2 of the Appendix A). First, we realize that the electric susceptibility/permittivity χε(r)/εε(r) depend solely on the radial distance, r. Apparently, the external component of the primary vector field has only radial component and depends solely on the radial distance, r, as well, that is Dextr=Dextrr^=(Q/4πr2)r^. Also, due to the linear and isotropic character of the dielectric sphere, all vector fields Dr, Pr, and Er will exhibit only radial component and will depend solely on the radial distance, r. Under these circumstances, in relation (68), the terms ∇εεr and Dr are parallel so that Dr is irrotational. Accordingly, we are able to calculate the total primary vector field D(r) beforehand (that is, independently of E(r) and P(r)) by using the technical means discussed in Section 5.1 and Section 5.2. More importantly, the calculations can be facilitated by using the detailed outcome of Section 5.3, as summarized in the work-flow diagram presented in Figure 1. In this case, the external component of the primary vector field, Dext(r), is exclusively normal to the dielectric sphere-vacuum interface so that the total primary vector field Dr depends solely on the free charges and coincides with Dext(r). Thus, the total primary vector field reads simply Dr=(Q/4πr2)r^. The total secondary vector fields, Pr and Er, can be obtained directly by using relations (17) and (18), respectively. It should be noted that the above discussion is generic; the procedure followed and the obtained results are the same irrespectively of the functional form of the electric susceptibility/permittivity χε(r)/εε(r). Finally, by using relation (34), we can translate our findings from the P-D, χε notation to the P-E, χe one.

Dielectrics with inhomogeneous properties not necessarily directed along the external component of the primary vector field, Dr, and the more general case of non-linear, inhomogeneous, and anisotropic dielectrics will be discussed in future work.

9. Conclusions

Here we revisited the case of electrostatics in material systems comprising of free charges and LHI dielectrics. We focused on Dr and proved that it is sufficient to describe the underlying physics and to accomplish all algebraic calculations in such systems. We introduced the P-D electric susceptibility, χε, through the constitutive relation P~r=−Pr=χεDr, with −1≤χε≤0. We clearly showed how the primary vector field, Dr, can be calculated directly from the free charge density, ρf, and the electric susceptibility, χε, of the LHI dielectrics, beforehand, without any knowledge of the secondary vector fields, Pr and Er. The latter, if needed, can be easily calculated from Dr. The new P-D, χε, formulation (P~r=−Pr=χεDr, with −1≤χε≤0) advances the understanding of underlying physics in LHI materials and has some technical advantages in respect to the standard P-E, χe, one (Pr=χeε0Er, with 0≤χe<∞). First, the P-D, χε, formulation restores efficiently all conceptual and mathematical flaws which are inherent to the standard P-E, χe, one (misleading causality/feedback between Pr and Er and non-compliance with the necessary convergence criteria). Second, the P-D, χε, formulation unveils the underlying physics of all physical entities of electrostatics in a more direct and consistent way. Third, the P-D, χε, formulation symmetrizes the electrostatics analogously to the M-H, χm, formulation of magnetostatics. Fourth, the P-D, χε, formulation proposes means, such as the free scalar potential, Ufr, that can technically facilitate analytical/computational calculations. Except for the LHI dielectrics discussed here, our concept can possibly be useful for the treatment of non-linear, inhomogeneous, and anisotropic dielectrics.

Appendix A.2.1. Solution Based on the P-E Electric Susceptibility, χe

The standard formulation based on the P-E electric susceptibility, χe, focuses on the electric field, Er, and the respective scalar potential, Ur, that relate through Er=−∇Ur. We can use the integral form of Gauss’s law for Er, Laplace’s equation for Ur, etc. The following boundary conditions should hold on Ur and Er, for the inside (r≤a) and outside (a≤r) spaces, as well as at the interface (r=a) of the two dielectrics (sphere and vacuum): (i) for r=0, both Einr and Uinr should diverge as 1/r2 and 1/r, respectively; (ii) for r→∞, both Eoutr and Uoutr should become zero; (iii) Ur should be continuous at the interface of the two dielectric media, thus Uinr|r=a=Uoutr|r=a; and (iv) the normal component of Er should satisfy the relation Eout⊥r−Ein⊥r|r=a=σr|r=a/ε0, where since σfr|r=a=0 it translates to Eout⊥r−Ein⊥r|r=a=σbr|r=a/ε0. Finally, the extra boundary condition on the continuity of the tangential components of Er (that is Eout//r|r=a=Ein//r|r=a), is trivially satisfied in this case.

The above information results in Uoutr=Q/4πε0r and Uinr=(Q/4πεr) for the scalar potential, Eoutr=(Q/4πε0r2)r^ and Einr=(Q/4πεr2)r^ for the electric field, Poutr=0 and Pinr=(χe/εr)(Q/4πr2)r^ for the electric polarization, and Dinr=Doutr=(Q/4πr2)r^ for the electric displacement.

Depolarizing field/self field: The surface density of bound charge that resides at the interface r=a is σbr|r=a=r^·Pinr|r=a=r^·χeε0Εinr|r=a=(χe/εr)(Q/4πa2), while the volume density of the charge is concentrated at r=0 with ρbr|r=0=−∇·Pinr|r=0=−(χe/εr)(Q/4π)∇·(r^/r2)|r=0=−(χe/εr)Qδ(r). The relevant internal electric field (see [25] of the article) produced by σbr|r=a and ρbr|r=0 at the inside space is simply Eintinr=Einr−Eextinr=(Q/4πεr2)r^−(Q/4πε0r2)r^=−(χe/εr)(Q/4πε0r2)r^. Also, we can easily obtain Eintinr=−(1/ε0)Pinr. We recall that Eintinr is the so-called depolarizing field, or self field, that relates to Pinr, else to the reverse P~inr=−Pinr through Eintinr=(1/ε0)P~inr (see Section 3 of the article and Appendix A.2.2 below).

Dependence of Dr on free charges and dielectric properties: Clearly, since the electric displacement that relates to the external sources (point charge Q placed at r=0 in this case), Dextr=(Q/4πr2)r^, is normal to the interface, r=a, of the two dielectric media (sphere with ε=1+χeε0 and vacuum with ε=ε0), we expect that the total Dr should depend solely on the free charges. Thus, Dr should be identical to Dextr. Indeed, this is the case, since Dr=Dextr=(Q/4πr2)r^ (see above Appendix A.1 of the Appendix A and Section 5.2 and Section 5.3 of the article).

Appendix A.2.2. Solution Based on the P-D Electric Susceptibility, χε

The alternative formulation based on the P-D electric susceptibility, χε, should focus on the electric displacement, Dr, and the respective free scalar potential, Ufr, that relate through Dr=−ε0∇Ufr. Accordingly, we employ the solution of Laplace equation for the (obviously φ-independent) free scalar potential, Ufr, obtained through the method of separation of variables. For the outside space, a≤r, the solution has the form Ufoutr=∑l=0∞Aloutrl+Bloutr−l+1Plcosθ, while the inside space, r≤a, reads Ufinr=∑l=0∞Alinrl+Blinr−l+1Plcosθ. The following boundary conditions should hold on Ufr and Dr, for the inside (r≤a) and outside (a≤r) spaces, as well as at the interface (r=a) of the two dielectrics (sphere and vacuum): (i) for r = 0, Ufinr should diverge as 1/r; (ii) for r →∞, Ufoutr should become zero; (iii) the tangential components of Dr should satisfy the relation Dout//r−Din//r|r=a=−P~out//r−P~in//r|r=a that since P~r=−Pr=χεDr and χεout=0, gets Dout//r|r=a=(1+χεin)Din//r|r=a; and (iv) the normal component of Dr should satisfy the condition Dout⊥r−Din⊥r|r=a=σfr|r=a where since σfr|r=a=0, translates to Dout⊥r|r=a=Din⊥r|r=a. Finally, we recall that in contrast to the inherent continuity of Ur, the free scalar potential, Ufr, is not necessarily continuous at the interface of two dielectric media, as indirectly reflected by the tangential components of Dr in condition (iii) above (see above Appendix A.1 of the Appendix A and Section 5.2 and Section 5.3 of the article).

The above information results in Ufoutr=Q/4πε0r and Ufinr=Q/4πε0r for the free scalar potential, while through Dr=−ε0∇Ufr we get Doutr=Dinr=(Q/4πr2)r^ for the electric displacement. In addition, through P~r=−Pr=χεD(r) we get P~outr=−Poutr=0 and P~inr=−Pinr=χε(Q/4πr2)r^ for the electric polarization and finally through Er=Dr/εε we obtain Eoutr=(Q/4πε0r2)r^, Einr=(1+χε)(Q/4πε0r2)r^ for the electric field.

Depolarizing field/self field: The surface density of the bound charge that resides at the interface r=a is σbr|r=a=r^·Pinr|r=a=r^·−P~inr|r=a=r^·−χεDinr|r=a=r^·(−χε(Q/4πr2))r^|r=a=−χε(Q/4πa2), while the volume density of the charge concentrated at r = 0 is ρbr|r=0=−∇·Pinr|r=0=∇·P~inr|r=0=χε(Q/4π)∇·(r^/r2)|r=0=χεQδ(r). The relevant internal electric field (see [25] of the article) produced by σbr|r=a and ρbr|r=0 at the inside space is given by Eintinr=Einr−Eextinr=(1+χε)(Q/4πε0r2)r^−(Q/4πε0r2)r^=χε(Q/4πε0r2)r^. Also, we can easily obtain Eintinr=(1/ε0)P~inr. We recall that Eintinr is the so-called depolarizing field or self field (see Section 3 of the article).

Dependence of Dr on free charges and dielectric properties: As already discussed above in Appendix A.2.1 for the P-E, χe, formulation, the electric displacement that relates to the external sources, Dextr=(Q/4πr2)r^, is entirely normal to the surface of the dielectric sphere. Accordingly, we expect that the total Dr should depend solely on the free charges, thus should be identical to Dextr. This is expected even for the P-D, χε, formulation discussed here. Indeed, this is observed (see above Appendix A.1 of the Appendix A and Section 5.2 and Section 5.3 of the article).

Non-continuity of the free scalar potential, Ufr, at the interface r = a: As discussed above and in the article, when Pr is entirely normal at an interface, Dr and Ufr should preserve the irrotational and continuous character, respectively, not only in the interior of dielectrics but also at the respective interfaces (see above Appendix A.1 of the Appendix A and Section 5.2 and Section 5.3 of the article). In the present problem, we have P~outr=−Poutr=0 and P~inr=−Pinr=χε(Q/4πr2)r^ that is normal to the interface r=a. Thus, we expect that Ufr should be continuous at this site. Indeed, this is the case since Ufoutr|r=a=Ufinr|r=a=Q/4πε0a.

Comparison between the P-E, χe, and P-D, χε, formulations: The two descriptions, P-E, χe, and P-D, χε, should be equivalent on a quantitative basis. To this effect, it is expected that when we substitute χε=−χe/(1+χe) (relation (34) of the article) in the expressions obtained here in Appendix A.2.2, we should get the exact same relations obtained above in Appendix A.2.1. Indeed, this can be easily confirmed for all electric entities: displacement, Dr, polarization, Pr, field, Er, free scalar potential of the outside space (a ≤r), Ufoutr, and bound surface charge density, σbr|r=a. In addition, we can easily verify that the relation Ur=Ufr+Ubr holds everywhere in space, where Ubr is the bound scalar potential that relates to the reverse electric polarization, P~r=−Pr, through P~r=−ε0∇Ubr. For instance, at the outside space, a≤r, the relation Uoutr=Ufoutr holds, since Uboutr=0. For the inside space, r≤a, we can easily find Ubinr and verify that indeed Uinr=Ufinr+Ubinr (see Section 6 of the article).

Appendix A.2.3. Solution Based on the P-E Electric Susceptibility, χe, by Means of Series

Here we employ a series approach with the standard formulation based on the P-E electric susceptibility, χe, and focus directly on the electric polarization, Pr, and field, Er, to clarify their causality/feedback for the inside space, r≤a, of the LHI dielectric sphere.

Suppose that initially (before the dielectric sphere responds to the external stimuli) the electric field is simply the one applied externally, (Q/4πε0r2)r^. We call it the zeroth-order term of the electric field, E0inr=(Q/4πε0r2)r^. The respective zeroth-order term of the electric polarization is P0inr=χeε0E0inr. As we showed in both Appendix A.2.1 and Appendix A.2.2 above, the sphere of polarization Pr produces an internal electric field (depolarizing field/self field) Eintinr=−(1/ε0)Pinr=(1/ε0)P~inr at the inside space. Thus, the zeroth-order term of the polarization P0inr will produce a first-order term for the internal electric field Eint,1inr=−(1/ε0)P0inr (notice that the term Eint,0inr does not exist; the only zeroth-order electric field term is of external origin, E0inr=E0z^). In turn, the first-order term, Eint,1inr, will induce a first-order term for the polarization P1inr=χeε0Eint,1inr that subsequently will produce a second-order term for the internal electric field Eint,2inr=−(1/ε0)P1inr and so on. Thus, in general, the (i-1)-order term of the induced polarization is Pi−1inr=χeε0Eint,i−1inr, while the (i)-order term of the internal electric field is Eint,iinr=−(1/ε0)Pi−1inr. Combining the last relations on Pi−1inr and Eint,iinr, we get Eint,iinr=−χeEint,i−1inr=−χe−χeEint,i−2inr=(−χe)(−χe)(−χe)Eint,i−3inr=⋯=(−χe)iE0inr. Accordingly, the total electric field will simply be Einr=E0inr+Eint,1inr+Eint,2inr+⋯+Eint,iinr+⋯=E0inr+(−χe)E0inr+(−χe)(−χe)E0inr+⋯+(−χe)iE0inr+⋯, else Einr=(∑i=0∞(−χe)i)E0inr. The geometric series results in ∑i=0∞(−χe)i=1/(1−(−χe))=1/(1+χe) so that ultimately Einr=E0inr/(1+χe)=E0inr/εr. Since E0inr=Q4πε0r2r^, this result is identical to the one obtained in Appendix A.2.1 above, as expected.

The ‘infinite regress of the P-E polarization process’ applies, also, to the bound surface charge density, σbr|r=a, that ultimately will be established at the interface, r=a, of the two dielectrics (sphere and vacuum) even for this case, as discussed analytically for a relevant problem in Section 7.3 of the article.

This series-based approach of the ‘infinite regress of the P-E polarization process’ restores, somehow, the conceptually misleading causality/feedback between Pr and Er that is inherent in the standard P-E, χe, formulation (see [8] pages 68 and 76; [13] page 186). However, the serious obstacle discussed analytically for a relevant problem in Section 7.3 of the article still exists: in strict mathematical terms, the above geometric series should converge only when |−χe|<1 [33], and since by definition 0≤χe<∞, the allowed interval should be 0≤χe<1. Nevertheless, we do not raise any doubts or constraints on the obtained solution of Einr and use it in the entire range, 0≤χe<∞. This is one of the inherent ill-defined points of the standard P-E, χe, formulation. The alternative P-D, χε, formulation (−1≤χε≤0) is free of any misleading argumentation and controversial mathematics from which the standard P-E, χe formulation suffers. This has been assessed analytically for a relevant problem in Section 7.3 of the article.

Boundary condition (iv) gives Eout⊥r−Ein⊥r|ρ=a=σbr|ρ=a/ε0 that since σbr|ρ=a=ρ^·Pinr|ρ=a=ρ^·χeε0Einr|ρ=a=χeε0Ein⊥r|ρ=a, it transforms to Eout⊥r|ρ=a=1+χeEin⊥r|ρ=a, else Eout⊥r|ρ=a=εrEin⊥r|ρ=a. The latter ultimately provides the desired relation −(∂Uoutr/∂ρ)|ρ=a=εr(−(∂Uinr/∂ρ))|ρ=a that gives εrCminam−1=−Cmouta−m+1 for 2≤m<∞, εrDminam−1=−Dmouta−m+1 for 2≤m<∞, εrC1in=−E0−C1outa−2 for m=1 and εrD1in=−D1outa−2 for m=1.

The above set of relations results in Uoutr=−[1−((εr−1)/(εr+1))(a/ρ)2]E0ρcosφ (outside space) and Uinr=−(2/(εr+1))E0ρcosφ=−(2/(εr+1))E0x (inside space) for the scalar potential.

Through Er=−∇Ur we get the respective relations for the electric field Eoutr=[1+((εr−1)/(εr+1))(a/ρ)2]E0cosφρ^−[1−((εr−1)/(εr+1))(a/ρ)2]E0sinφφ^ and Einr=2/(εr+1)E0x^.

Once we have found Er, we easily calculate the respective electric polarization, Pr, and electric displacement, Dr, since they relate to Er through expressions (1) and (2) of the article. Thus, we get Poutr=0 and Pinr=2ε0(χe/(εr+1))E0x^ for the electric polarization, while Doutr=ε0Eoutr=[1+((εr−1)/(εr+1))(a/ρ)2]D0cosφρ^−[1−((εr−1)/(εr+1))(a/ρ)2]D0sinφφ^ and Dinr=εEinr=2(εr/(εr+1))D0x^, where ε0E0x^=D0x^ the external electric displacement.

Depolarizing field/self field: The surface density of bound charge that resides at the cylinder/vacuum interface ρ=a is given by σbr|ρ=a=ρ^·Pinr|ρ=a=ρ^·χeε0Εinr|ρ=a=2ε0((εr−1)/(εr+1))E0cosφ. The relevant internal electric field (see [25] of the article) produced by σbr|ρ=a at the inside space is simply Eintinr=Einr−Eextinr=(2/(εr+1))E0x^−E0x^=−((εr−1)/(εr+1))E0x^. Also, we can easily obtain Eintinr=−(1/2ε0)Pinr. We recall that Eintinr is the so-called depolarizing field, or self field, that relates to Pinr, else to the reverse P~inr=−Pinr through Eintinr=(1/2ε0)P~inr (see Section 3 of the article, and below Appendix A.2.5 of the Appendix A).

Dependence of Dr on free charges and dielectric properties: Here, the electric displacement that relates to the external sources, Dextr=D0x^=ε0E0x^=ε0E0cosφρ^−ε0E0sinφφ^, has component that is tangential to the surface of the dielectric cylinder. Thus, we expect that the total Dr, except for the free charges, should depend on the properties of the LHI dielectric cylinder (see above Appendix A.1 of the Appendix A and Section 5.2 and Section 5.3 of the article). Indeed, this is what we observe here.

Appendix A.2.5. Solution Based on the P-D Electric Susceptibility, χε

The alternative formulation based on the P-D electric susceptibility, χε, should focus on the electric displacement, Dr, and the respective free scalar potential, Ufr, that relate through Dr=−ε0∇Ufr. Accordingly, we employ the solution of Laplace equation for the free scalar potential, Ufr, obtained by means of separation of variables. For the outside space (a≤ρ) the most general solution has the form Ufoutr=A0out+B0outln(ρ)C0out+D0outφE0out+F0outz+∑m=1∞Amoutρm+Bmoutρ−m(Cmoutcos(mφ)+Dmoutsin(mφ))Emout+Fmoutz, while for the inside space (ρ≤a) it is Ufinr=A0in+B0inln(ρ)C0in+D0inφE0in+F0inz+∑m=1∞Aminρm+Bminρ−m(Cmincos(mφ)+Dminsin(mφ))Emin+Fminz. The following boundary conditions should hold on Ufr and Dr, for the inside (ρ≤a) and outside (a≤ρ) spaces, as well as at the interface (ρ=a) of the two dielectrics (cylinder and vacuum): (i) for ρ=0, Ufinr should be finite; (ii) for ρ→∞, Ufoutr should be identical to the external scalar potential that produces Dr=D0z^ (Dr=ε0E0z^), that is Ufoutr|ρ→∞=−E0ρcosφ; (iii) the tangential components of Dr should satisfy the relation Dout//r−Din//r|ρ=a=−P~out//r−P~in//r|ρ=a that since P~r=−Pr=χεDr and χεout=0, gets Dout//r|ρ=a=(1+χεin)Din//r|ρ=a; and (iv) the normal component of Dr should satisfy the condition Dout⊥r−Din⊥r|ρ=a=σfr|ρ=a where since σfr|ρ=a=0, translates to Dout⊥r|ρ=a=Din⊥r|ρ=a. Finally, notice that in contrast to the inherent continuity of Ur, the free scalar potential, Ufr, is not necessarily continuous at the interface of two dielectric media (see above Appendix A.1 of the Appendix A and Section 5.2 and Section 5.3 of the article). Thus, it is meaningless to ask for a boundary condition on the continuity of Ufr at the interface ρ=a. As we will see below, indeed, the Ufr is non continuous at the interface ρ=a. Next, we briefly proceed with the solution.

Boundary condition (i) gives B0in=D0in=F0in=0 and Bmin=Fmin=0 for 1≤m<∞. Thus, for the inside space the solution gets Ufinr=A0in+∑m=1∞ρm(Cmincos(mφ)+Dminsin(mφ)). Here, we have also adopted the obvious fact that for this problem, Ufinr cannot exhibit linear dependence on φ or z.

Boundary condition (i) gives B0in=D0in=F0in=0 and Bmin=Fmin=0 for 1≤m<∞. Thus, for the inside space the solution gets Ufinr=A0in+∑m=1∞ρm(Cmincos(mφ)+Dminsin(mφ)). Here, we have also adopted the obvious fact that for this problem, Ufinr cannot exhibit linear dependence on φ or z.

Boundary condition (ii) gives B0out=D0out=F0out=0, Fmout=0 for 1≤m<∞ and Amout=0 for 2≤m<∞. Thus, for the outside space, the solution gets Ufoutr=A0out−E0ρcosφ+∑m=1∞ρ−m(Cmoutcos(mφ)+Dmoutsin(mφ)). Here, we have also adopted the obvious fact that for this problem, Ufoutr cannot exhibit linear dependence on φ or z.

Boundary condition (ii) gives B0out=D0out=F0out=0, Fmout=0 for 1≤m<∞ and Amout=0 for 2≤m<∞. Thus, for the outside space, the solution gets Ufoutr=A0out−E0ρcosφ+∑m=1∞ρ−m(Cmoutcos(mφ)+Dmoutsin(mφ)). Here, we have also adopted the obvious fact that for this problem, Ufoutr cannot exhibit linear dependence on φ or z.

To proceed with boundary conditions (iii) and (iv), we have to calculate the currently available version of Dr through Dr=−ε0∇Ufr. We easily get Dinr=−ε0∑m=1∞mρm−1(Cmincos(mφ)+Dminsin(mφ))ρ^−ε0∑m=1∞mρm−1(−Cminsin(mφ)+Dmincos(mφ))φ^ and Doutr=ε0Ε0cosφρ^+ε0∑m=1∞mρ−(m+1)(Cmoutcos(mφ)+Dmoutsin(mφ))ρ^−ε0Ε0sinφφ^−ε0∑m=1∞mρ−(m+1)(−Cmoutsin(mφ)+Dmoutcos(mφ))φ^.

Boundary condition (iii) gives (1+χε)Cminam−1=Cmouta−(m+1) for 2≤m<∞, (1+χε)Dminam−1=Dmouta−(m+1) for 1≤m<∞ and (1+χε)C1in=−E0+C1outa−2 for m=1.

Boundary condition (iii) gives (1+χε)Cminam−1=Cmouta−(m+1) for 2≤m<∞, (1+χε)Dminam−1=Dmouta−(m+1) for 1≤m<∞ and (1+χε)C1in=−E0+C1outa−2 for m=1.

Boundary condition (iv) gives −Cminam−1=Cmouta−(m+1) for 2≤m<∞, −Dminam−1=Dmouta−(m+1) for 1≤m<∞ and −C1in=E0+C1outa−2 for m=1.

Boundary condition (iv) gives −Cminam−1=Cmouta−(m+1) for 2≤m<∞, −Dminam−1=Dmouta−(m+1) for 1≤m<∞ and −C1in=E0+C1outa−2 for m=1.

The above set of relations results in Ufoutr=−[1+(χε/(χε+2))(a/ρ)2]E0ρcosφ (outside space) and Ufinr=−(2/(χε+2))E0ρcosφ=−(2/(χε+2))E0x (inside space) for the free scalar potential.

Through Dr=−ε0∇Ufr we get the respective relations for the electric displacement Doutr=[1−(χε/(χε+2))(a/ρ)2]D0cosφρ^−[1+(χε/(χε+2))(a/ρ)2]D0sinφφ^ and Dinr=(2/(χε+2))D0x^, where D0=ε0E0.

Once we have found Dr, we easily calculate the respective reverse electric polarization, P~r, (electric polarization, Pr) and electric field, Er, since they relate to Dr through expressions (17) and (18) of the article. Thus, we get P~outr=−Poutr=0 and P~inr=−Pinr=3(χε/(χε+2))D0x^ for the reverse electric polarization, while Eoutr=Doutr/ε0=[1−(χε/(χε+2))(a/ρ)2]E0cosφρ^−[1+(χε/(χε+2))(a/ρ)2]E0sinφφ^ and Einr=Dinr/εε=Dinr/(ε0εrε)=Dinr/(ε0(1+χε)−1)=(1+χε)Dinr/ε0=2((χε+1)/(χε+2))E0x^ for the electric field.

Depolarizing field/self field: The surface density of bound charges at ρ = a is σbr|ρ=a=ρ^·Pinr|ρ=a=ρ^·−P~inr|ρ=a=ρ^·−χεDinr|ρ=a=ρ^·(−(2χε/(χε+2))D0x^)|ρ=a=−(2χε/(χε+2))D0cosφ, where D0=ε0E0. The relevant internal electric field (see [25] of the article) produced by σbr|ρ=a at the inside space is simply Eintinr=Einr−Eextinr=2((χε+1)/(χε+2))E0x^−E0x^=(χε/(χε+2))E0x^. Also, we can easily obtain that Eintinr=(1/2ε0)P~inr=−(1/2ε0)Pinr. We recall that Eintinr is the so-called depolarizing field or self field (see Section 3 of the article).

Dependence of Dr on free charges and dielectric properties: As already discussed above in Appendix A.2.4 for the P-E, χe, formulation, the electric displacement that relates to the external sources, Dextr=D0x^=ε0E0x^=ε0E0cosφρ^−ε0E0sinφφ^, has component that is tangential to the surface of the dielectric cylinder. Thus, we expect that the total Dr, except for the free charges, should depend on the properties of the LHI dielectric cylinder (see above Appendix A.1 of the Appendix A and Section 5.2 and Section 5.3 of the article). This is expected even for the P-D, χε, formulation discussed here. Indeed, this is observed here.

Non-continuity of the free scalar potential, Ufr, at the interface ρ = a: The existence of a tangential component of Dr and P~r=−Pr at the interface of different dielectric media will result in a non irrotational behavior of Dr, and a non continuous character of Ufr, locally at the interface (see above Appendix A.1 of the Appendix A and Section 5.2 and Section 5.3 of the article). Indeed, here, Dr and P~r=−Pr have a tangential component at the interface ρ=a. By using the expressions found above for Ufoutr and Ufinr, we see that Ufoutr|ρ=a=−2((χε+1)/(χε+2))E0acosφ, while Ufinr|ρ=a=−(2/(χε+2))E0acosφ. The respective discontinuity is Ufoutr|ρ=a−Ufinr|ρ=a=−(2χε/(χε+2))E0acosφ.

Comparison between the P-E, χe, and P-D, χε, formulations: The two descriptions, the standard P-E, χe, employed today and the alternative P-D, χε, introduced here, should be quantitatively equivalent. Thus, all physical entities of electrostatics (scalar potentials, vector fields, dipole moments, bound charge densities, etc.) should be the same irrespectively of which description we use. To this effect, it is expected that when we substitute χε=−χe/(1+χe) (relation (34) of the article) in the expressions obtained here in Appendix A.2.5, we should get the exact same relations obtained above in Appendix A.2.4. Indeed, this can be easily confirmed for all electric entities, displacement, Dr, polarization, Pr, field, Er, free scalar potential of the outside space (a≤ρ), Ufoutr, and bound surface charge density, σbr|ρ=a. In addition, we can easily verify that the relation Ur=Ufr+Ubr holds everywhere in space, where Ubr is the bound scalar potential that relates to the reverse electric polarization, P~r=−Pr, through P~r=−ε0∇Ubr. For instance, at the outside space, a≤ρ, the relation Uoutr=Ufoutr holds, since Uboutr=0. For the inside space, ρ≤a, we can easily find Ubinr and verify that, indeed, Uinr=Ufinr+Ubinr (see Section 6 of the article).

Appendix A.2.6. Solution Based on the P-E Electric Susceptibility, χe, by Means of Series

Here we employ a series approach with the standard formulation based on the P-E electric susceptibility, χe, and focus directly on the electric polarization, Pr, and field, Er, to clarify their causality/feedback for the inside space, ρ≤a, of the LHI dielectric cylinder.

Suppose that initially (before the dielectric cylinder responds to the external stimuli), the electric field is simply the one applied externally, E0z^. We call it the zeroth-order term of the electric field, E0inr=E0z^. The respective zeroth-order term of the electric polarization, P0inr, induced by E0inr is P0inr=χeε0E0inr. As we showed in both Appendix A.2.4 and Appendix A.2.5 above, a uniformly polarized cylinder of polarization Pr produces an internal electric field (depolarizing field/self field) Eintinr=−(1/2ε0)Pinr=(1/2ε0)P~inr at the inside space. Thus, the zeroth-order term of the polarization P0inr will produce a first-order term for the internal electric field Eint,1inr=−(1/2)P0inr (notice that the term Eint,0inr does not exist; the only zeroth-order electric field term is of external origin, E0inr=E0z^). In turn, the first-order term, Eint,1inr, will induce a first-order term for the polarization P1inr=χeε0Eint,1inr that subsequently will produce a second-order term for the internal electric field Eint,2inr=−1/2ε0P1inr, and so on. Thus, in general, the (i-1)-order term of the induced polarization is Pi−1inr=χeε0Eint,i−1inr, while the (i)-order term of the internal electric field is Eint,iinr=−(1/2ε0)Pi−1inr. Combining the last relations on Pi−1inr and Eint,iinr, we get Eint,iinr=−χe/2Eint,i−1inr=−χe/2−χe/2Eint,i−2inr=(−(χe/2))(−(χe/2))(−(χe/2))Eint,i−3inr=⋯=(−(χe/2))iE0inr. Accordingly, the total electric field will simply be Einr=E0inr+Eint,1inr+Eint,2inr+⋯+Eint,iinr+⋯=E0inr+(−(χe/2))E0inr+(−(χe/2))(−(χe/2))E0inr+⋯+(−(χe/2))iE0inr+⋯, else Einr=(∑i=0∞(−(χe/2))i)E0inr. The geometric series results in ∑i=0∞(−(χe/2))i=1/(1−(−(χe/2)))=1/(1+χe/2) so that ultimately Einr=E0inr/(1+χe/2), else Einr=(2/(2+χe))E0inr. Since E0inr=E0x,^ this result is identical to the one obtained in Appendix A.2.4 above as expected.

The ‘infinite regress of the P-E polarization process’ applies, also, to the bound surface charge density, σbr|ρ=a, that ultimately will be established at the interface, ρ=a, of the two dielectrics (cylinder and vacuum) even for this case, as discussed analytically for a relevant problem in Section 7.3 of the article.

This series-based approach of the ‘infinite regress of the P-E polarization process’ restores, somehow, the conceptually misleading causality/feedback between Pr and Er that is inherent in the standard P-E, χe, formulation (see [8] pages 68 and 76; [13] page 186, and Section 4 of the article). However, the serious obstacle discussed above in Appendix A.2.3 of the Appendix A and in Section 7.3 of the article still exists: in strict mathematical terms, the above geometric series should converge only when |−χe/2|<1 [33], and since by definition 0≤χe<∞, the allowed interval should be 0≤χe<2. Nevertheless, we do not raise any doubts or constraints on the obtained solution of Einr and use it in the entire range, 0≤χe<∞. This is one of the inherent ill-defined points of the standard P-E, χe, formulation. The alternative P-D, χε, formulation (−1≤χε≤0) is free of any misleading argumentation and controversial mathematics from which the standard P-E, χe formulation suffers. This has been assessed analytically for a relevant problem in Section 7.3 of the article.

Appendix A.2.7. Solution Based on the P-E Electric Susceptibility, χe

The standard formulation based on the P-E electric susceptibility, χe, focuses on the electric field, Er, and the respective scalar potential, Ur, that relate through Er=−∇Ur. We can use the integral form of Gauss’s law for Er, Laplace’s equation for Ur, etc. The following boundary conditions should hold on Ur and Er, for the inside (ρ≤a) and outside (a≤ρ) spaces, as well as at the interface (ρ=a) of the two dielectrics (cylinder and vacuum): (i) for ρ = 0, both Einr and Uinr should diverge as 1/ρ and ln(ρ), respectively; (ii) for ρ→∞, both Eoutr and Uoutr should become zero; (iii) Ur should be continuous at the interface of the two dielectric media, thus Uinr|ρ=a=Uoutr|ρ=a; and (iv) the normal component of Er should satisfy the relation Eout⊥r−Ein⊥r|ρ=a=σr|ρ=a/ε0 where since σfr|ρ=a=0 it translates to Eout⊥r−Ein⊥r|ρ=a=σbr|ρ=a/ε0. Finally, the extra boundary condition on the continuity of the tangential components of Er (that is Eout//r|ρ=a=Ein//r|ρ=a), is trivially satisfied in this case.

The above information results in Uoutr=(λ0/2πε0)ln(∞/ρ) and Uinr=(λ0/2πε0)ln(∞/a)+(λ0/2πε)ln(a/ρ) for the scalar potential, Eoutr=(λ0/2πε0ρ)ρ^ and Einr=(λ0/2περ)ρ^ for the electric field, Poutr=0 and Pinr=(χe/εr)(λ0/2πρ)ρ^ for the electric polarization, and Dinr=Doutr=(λ0/2πρ)ρ^ for the electric displacement.

Depolarizing field/self field: The surface density of the bound charge that resides at the interface ρ=a is σbr|ρ=a=ρ^·Pinr|ρ=a=ρ^·χeε0Εinr|ρ=a=(χe/εr)(λ0/2πa), while the volume density of the bound charge concentrated at ρ=0 is ρbr|ρ=0=−∇·Pinr|ρ=0=−(χe/εr)(λ0/2π)∇·(ρ^/ρ)|ρ=0=−(χe/εr)(λ0/2π)2πδ(ρ)=−(χe/εr)(λ0/2π)(δ(ρ)/ρ). The relevant internal electric field (see [25] of the article) produced by σbr|ρ=a and ρbr|ρ=0 at the inside space is simply Eintinr=Einr−Eextinr=(λ0/2περ)ρ^−(λ0/2πε0ρ)ρ^=(λ0/2πε0ρ)(1/εr−1)ρ^=(λ0/2πε0ρ)(−χe/(χe+1))ρ^. Also, we can easily obtain Eintinr=−(1/ε0)Pinr. We recall that Eintinr is the so-called depolarizing field, or self field, that relates to Pinr, else to the reverse P~inr=−Pinr through Eintinr=(1/ε0)P~inr (see Section 3 of the article, and below Appendix A.2.8 of the Appendix A).

Dependence of Dr on free charges and dielectric properties: Clearly, since the electric displacement that relates to the external sources (linear charge density λ0 placed at ρ=0 in this case), Dextr=(λ0/2πρ)ρ^, is normal to the interface, ρ=a, of the two dielectric media (cylinder with ε=εrε0=1+χeε0 and vacuum with ε=ε0), we expect that the total Dr should depend solely on the free charges. Thus, Dr should be identical to Dextr. Indeed, this is the case, since Dr=Dextr=(λ0/2πρ)ρ^ (see above Appendix A.1 of the Appendix A and Section 5.2 and Section 5.3 of the article).

Appendix A.2.8. Solution Based on the P-D Electric Susceptibility, χε

The alternative formulation based on the P-D electric susceptibility, χε, should focus on the electric displacement, Dr, and the respective free scalar potential, Ufr, that relate through Dr=−ε0∇Ufr. We can use the integral form of Gauss’s law for Er, Laplace’s equation for Ur, etc. The following boundary conditions should hold on Ufr and Dr, for the inside (ρ≤a) and outside (a≤ρ) spaces, as well as at the interface (ρ=a) of the two dielectrics (cylinder and vacuum): (i) for ρ=0, Ufinr should diverge as lnρ; (ii) for ρ→∞, Ufoutr should become zero; (iii) the tangential components of Dr should satisfy the relation Dout//r−Din//r|ρ=a=−P~out//r−P~in//r|ρ=a that since P~r=−Pr=χεDr and χεout=0, gets Dout//r|ρ=a=(1+χεin)Din//r|ρ=a; and (iv) the normal component of Dr should satisfy the condition Dout⊥r−Din⊥r|ρ=a=σfr|ρ=a where since σfr|ρ=a=0, translates to Dout⊥r|ρ=a=Din⊥r|ρ=a. Finally, we recall that in contrast to the inherent continuity of Ur, the free scalar potential, Ufr, is not necessarily continuous at the interface of two dielectric media, as indirectly reflected by the tangential components of Dr in condition (iii) above (see above Appendix A.1 of the Appendix A and Section 5.2 and Section 5.3 of the article). However, as we will see below, in this case the external electric displacement, Dextr, is absolutely normal to the interface ρ=a of the two dielectrics so that, ultimately, Dr=Dextr.

The above information results in Ufoutr=Ufinr=(λ0/2πε0)ln(∞/ρ) for the free scalar potential, while through Dr=−ε0∇Ufr we get Doutr=Dinr=(λ0/2πρ)ρ^ for the electric displacement. In addition, through P~r=−Pr=χεD(r) we get P~outr=−Poutr=0 and P~inr=−Pinr=χε(λ0/2πρ)ρ^ for the electric polarization, and finally through Er=Dr/εε we obtain Eoutr=(λ0/2πε0ρ)ρ^, Einr=(1+χε)(λ0/2πε0ρ)ρ^ for the electric field.

Depolarizing field/self field: The surface density of the bound charge that resides at the interface ρ = a is given by σbr|ρ=a=ρ^·Pinr|ρ=a=ρ^·−P~inr|ρ=a=ρ^·−χεDinr|ρ=a=ρ^·(−χε(λ0/2πρ))ρ^|ρ=a=−χε(λ0/2πa), while the volume density of the bound charge concentrated at ρ = 0 is ρbr|ρ=0=−∇·Pinr|ρ=0=∇·P~inr|ρ=0=χε(λ0/2π))∇·(ρ^/ρ)|ρ=0=χε(λ0/2π)2πδ(ρ)=χε(λ0/2π)(δ(ρ)/ρ). The relevant internal electric field (see [25] of the article) produced by σbr|ρ=a and ρbr|ρ=0 at the inside space is given by Eintinr=Einr−Eextinr=(1+χε)(λ0/2πε0ρ)ρ^−(λ0/2πε0ρ)ρ^=χε(λ0/2πε0ρ)ρ^. Also, we can easily obtain Eintinr=(1/ε0)P~inr. We recall that Eintinr is the so-called depolarizing field or self field (see Section 3 of the article).

Dependence of Dr on free charges and dielectric properties: As already discussed above in Appendix A.2.7 for the P-E, χe, formulation, the electric displacement that relates to the external sources, Dextr=(λ0/2πρ)ρ^, is entirely normal to the surface of the dielectric cylinder. Accordingly, we expect that the total Dr should depend solely on the free charges, thus should be identical to Dextr. This is expected even for the P-D, χε, formulation discussed here. Indeed, this is observed (see above Appendix A.1 of the Appendix A and Section 5.2 and Section 5.3 of the article).

Non-continuity of the free scalar potential, Ufr, at the interface ρ = a: As discussed above and in the article, when Pr is entirely normal at an interface, Dr and Ufr should preserve the irrotational and continuous character, respectively, not only in the interior of dielectrics but also at the respective interfaces (see above Appendix A.1 of the Appendi A and Section 5.2 and Section 5.3 of the article). In the present problem, we have P~outr=−Poutr=0 and P~inr=−Pinr=χε(λ0/2πρ)ρ^ that is normal to the interface ρ = a. Thus, we expect that Ufr should be continuous at this site. Indeed, this is the case since Ufoutr|ρ=a=Ufinr|ρ=a=(λ0/2πε0)ln(∞/a).

Comparison between the P-E, χe, and P-D, χε, formulations: The two descriptions, P-E, χe, and P-D, χε, should be quantitatively equivalent. To this effect, it is expected that when we substitute χε=−χe/(1+χe) (relation (34) of the article) in the expressions obtained here in Appendix A.2.8, we should get the exact same relations obtained above in Appendix A.2.7. Indeed, this can be easily confirmed for all electric entities: displacement, Dr, polarization, Pr, field, Er, free scalar potential of the outside space (a ≤ρ), Ufoutr, and bound surface charge densities, surface σbr|ρ=a and volume ρbr|ρ=0. In addition, we can easily verify that the relation Ur=Ufr+Ubr holds everywhere in space, where Ubr is the bound scalar potential that relates to the reverse electric polarization, P~r=−Pr, through P~r=−ε0∇Ubr. Appendix A.2.9. Solution Based on the P-E Electric Susceptibility, χe, by Means of Series

Here we employ a series approach with the standard formulation based on the P-E electric susceptibility, χe, and focus directly on the electric polarization, Pr, and field, Er, to clarify their causality/feedback for the inside space, ρ≤a, of the LHI dielectric cylinder.

Suppose that initially (before the dielectric cylinder responds to the external stimuli), the electric field is simply the one applied externally, (Q/4πε0r2)r^. We call it the zeroth-order term of the electric field, E0inr=(λ0/2πε0ρ)ρ^. The respective zeroth-order term of the electric polarization is P0inr=χeε0E0inr. As we showed in both Appendix A.2.7 and Appendix A.2.8 above, a uniformly polarized cylinder of polarization Pr produces an internal electric field (depolarizing field/self field) Eintinr=−(1/ε0)Pinr=(1/ε0)P~inr at the inside space. Thus, the zeroth-order term of the polarization P0inr will produce a first-order term for the internal electric field Eint,1inr=−(1/ε0)P0inr (notice that the term Eint,0inr does not exist; the only zeroth-order electric field term is of external origin, E0inr=E0z^). In turn, the first-order term, Eint,1inr, will induce a first-order term for the polarization P1inr=χeε0Eint,1inr that subsequently will produce a second-order term for the internal electric field Eint,2inr=−1/ε0P1inr, and so on. Thus, in general, the (i-1)-order term of the induced polarization is Pi−1inr=χeε0Eint,i−1inr, while the (i)-order term of the internal electric field is Eint,iinr=−(1/ε0)Pi−1inr. Combining the last relations on Pi−1inr and Eint,iinr, we get Eint,iinr=−χeEint,i−1inr=−χe−χeEint,i−2inr=(−χe)(−χe)(−χe)Eint,i−3inr=⋯=(−χe)iE0inr. Accordingly, the total electric field will simply be Einr=E0inr+Eint,1inr+Eint,2inr+⋯+Eint,iinr+⋯=E0inr+(−χe)E0inr+(−χe)(−χe)E0inr+⋯+(−χe)iE0inr+⋯, else Einr=(∑i=0∞(−χe)i)E0inr. The geometric series results in ∑i=0∞(−χe)i=1/(1−(−χe))=1/(1+χe) so that ultimately Einr=E0inr/(1+χe)=E0inr/εr. Since E0inr=λ0/2πε0ρρ^, this result is identical to the one obtained above in Appendix A.2.7, as expected.

The ‘infinite regress of the P-E polarization process’ applies also to the bound surface charge density, σbr|ρ=a, that ultimately will be established at the interface, ρ=a, of the two dielectrics (cylinder and vacuum) even for this case, as discussed analytically for a relevant problem in Section 7.3 of the article.

This series-based approach of the ‘infinite regress of the P-E polarization process’ restores, somehow, the conceptually misleading causality/feedback between Pr and Er that is inherent in the standard P-E, χe, formulation (see [8] pages 68 and 76; [13] page 186). However, the serious obstacle discussed above in Appendix A.2.3 and Appendix A.2.6 of the Appendix A and in Section 7.3 of the article still exists: in strict mathematical terms, the above geometric series should converge only when |−χe|<1 [33], and since by definition 0≤χe<∞, the allowed interval should be 0≤χe<1. Nevertheless, we do not raise any doubts or constraints on the obtained solution of Einr and use it in the entire range, 0≤χe<∞. This is one of the inherent ill-defined points of the standard P-E, χe, formulation. The alternative P-D, χε, formulation (−1≤χε≤0) is free of any misleading argumentation and controversial mathematics from which the standard P-E, χe formulation suffers. This has been assessed analytically for a relevant problem in Section 7.3 of the article.
