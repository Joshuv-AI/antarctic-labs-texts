Isolated Intracranial Rosai-Dorfman Disease Mimicking Sellar Pituitary Adenoma: a case report and literature review
Authors: Sixian Huang, Congcong Wu, Junjie Ding, Kunwang Han, Guibo Zhang, Shilong Cao, Lunxiao Wang, Shuaifeng Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRosai-Dorfman disease (RDD), also known as sinus histiocytosis with massive lymphadenopathy (SHML), is an extremely rare non-Langerhans cell histiocytosis. The diagnostic histiocytes in RDD are characterized by the immunophenotypic markers S100 (+), CD68 (+), and CD1a (−) (Abla O, Blood 131: 2877, 2018),(Emile JF, Blood 127: 2672, 2016) , and exhibit phagocytosis with varying frequency. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of isolated Intracranial Rosai-Dorfman Disease Mimicking Sellar Pituitary Adenoma, with peripheral facial paralysis as the initial presentation. Pituitary MRI revealed an intrasellar mass with homogeneous enhancement, initially diagnosed as a pituitary adenoma. The patient underwent transnasal transsphenoidal endoscopic resection of the pituitary adenoma. But histopathology and immunohistochemistry (S100+, CD68+, CD1a−) confirmed the diagnosis of RDD. Background

The etiology and pathogenesis of RDD remain elusive, though current research postulates potential associations with infectious triggers, autoimmune dysregulation, and genetic predispositions [1]. Facial paralysis has been reported in patients with hypertrophic meningitis RDD and petroclival meningioma RDD [2], while sellar RDD is rare. To date, only 8 cases of sellar RDD have been reported [3–9], including 4 suprasellar, 1 cavernous sinus, and 3 intrasellar lesions. Among these, only 3 cases demonstrated isolated intrasellar involvement. However, their clinical presentations diverged significantly. To our knowledge, this is the first case of intrasellar Rosai-Dorfman Disease mimicking sellar pituitary Adenoma with peripheral facial paralysis as the initial symptom.

Case presentation

A 50-year-old Han Chinese female patient was admitted to our hospital with left facial paralysis for 2 months. Physical examination showed disappearance of the left frontal wrinkles and nasolabial fold, ptosis and dysraphism of the left eyelid and a single tender lymph node enlargement on the left side of the neck. Preoperative endocrine indicators (sex hormones, insulin-like growth factor 1) were within the normal range, TSH: 10.70 mIU/L (normal range 0.75–5.60) T3: 1.03 nmol/L (normal range 1.30–2.40), FT3: 2.97 pmol/L (normal range 3.85–6.30), COR(8 a.m.): 1.00 ug/dL (normal range 4.82–19.50). Preoperative imaging showed enlargement of the right side of the pituitary gland, with nodular lesions visible inside, isointense signals on both T1WI and T2WI, clear boundaries, and uniform signals, measuring approximately 1.0 cm × 1.5 cm × 0.8 cm (anteroposterior diameter × transverse diameter × vertical diameter). Dynamic Contrast Enhancement (DCE) showed significant and uniform enhancement of the lesion, and the measured time-signal intensity curve (TIC) of the region of interest was a rapid-rise plateau type. The normal pituitary posterior lobe short T1 high signal shadow was clearly visible, the pituitary stalk was shifted to the left, and no compression or displacement of the optic chiasm was observed. The initial MRI diagnosis was pituitary adenoma. The preoperative diagnosis was pituitary adenoma and peripheral facial paralysis. (Fig. 1)

Follow-up course

Postoperative diagnosis confirmed intracranial primary Rosai-Dorfman disease (RDD) with a solitary lesion localized to the pituitary gland, which had been completely resected intraoperatively. Based on existing literature and clinical guidelines, RDD is recognized for its indolent biological behavior, typically characterized by slow progression or even spontaneous regression in some cases. According to the consensus recommendations for the diagnosis and clinical management of Rosai-Dorfman disease in 2018 [10], a strategy of close imaging surveillance was adopted, given the absence of significant neurological deficits. Detailed discussions with the patient and family were conducted, and a follow-up protocol was established. However, the patient exhibited non-adherence to scheduled follow-up evaluations. At the 6-month postoperative assessment, facial paralysis symptoms had improved, but postoperative follow-up MRI showed an enlarged sella turcica, with a nodular lesion similar to an isointense signal in the right part of the pituitary gland, with a uniform signal and a size of approximately 1.2 cm × 1.5 cm × 1.2 cm (anteroposterior diameter × transverse diameter × vertical diameter). DCE scanning and the measured TIC curve showed the same performance as before the operation, suggesting tumor recurrence. (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Rosai–Dorfman disease (RDD) is a rare myeloproliferative disorder of histiocytes with a broad spectrum of clinical manifestations and peculiar morphologic features (accumulation of histiocytes with emperipolesis) [11–13]. According to relevant literature, classic RDD manifests as bilateral painless cervical lymphadenopathy, accompanied by fever, weight loss, and night sweats. In this case, the patient presented with a single tender lymph node enlargement on the left side of the neck, without any of the above-mentioned clinical manifestations. Histologically, the disease is characterized by dilation of the lymph node sinuses and large histiocytes with intact engulfed lymphocytes (emperipolesis or lymphophagocytosis) [4, 14].Central nervous system involvement accounts for approximately 5% of all cases. RDD in sellar region is extremely rare, and its clinical manifestations are diverse and lack of specificity. The common symptoms include headache, visual impairment and endocrine dysfunction. It is easily misdiagnosed as pituitary adenoma, craniopharyngioma or meningioma. Intrasellar RDD is relatively rare. To date, there have been 8 reported cases of sellar region RDD [15], including 4 suprasellar lesions, 1 cavernous sinus lesion, and 3 intrasellar lesions. Among them, only 3 cases presented with isolated intrasellar involvement. The main clinical manifestations of these cases were headache, vision loss, and pituitary hypofunction, and none presented with facial paralysis. Common causes of facial paralysis include idiopathic Bell’s palsy, infection (e.g., herpes zoster, Lyme disease), trauma, and compression by tumors [16]. Facial paralysis is not common in sellar region lesions because the main trunk of the facial nerve does not pass through the sellar region [17]. To our knowledge, this case is the first reported case of intrasellar RDD with facial paralysis as the initial symptom, and its mechanism may involve the following aspects:

Local inflammatory spread and perineural invasion

RDD is characterized by infiltration of a large number of organized cells, lymphocytes, and plasma cells, with local release of inflammatory factors such as IL-6 and TNF-α. These factors may spread to the temporal bone region through the dura mater adjacent to the sella turcica or the periosteum of the skull base, causing inflammatory demyelination around the facial nerve, which leads to nerve dysfunction [17].

Indirect endocrine-immune interactions

Patients exhibit endocrine abnormalities, such as elevated thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) and decreased cortisol, which may exacerbate neural sensitivity to inflammation through metabolic disturbances or immune dysregulation imbalances [18–21].

In this case, facial paralysis symptoms significantly improved after surgical resection of the lesion, supplemented by hormone therapy, suggesting that the nerve damage may be caused by reversible inflammation rather than permanent structural destruction.

Imaging features and differential diagnosis in the sellar region

Typical imaging presentatio

Signal characteristics: T1WI shows an isointense signal, T2WI shows a low to isointense signal, DWI shows no restriction, and enhanced scanning shows uniform enhancement. Skeletal changes: Usually not accompanied by adjacent bone hyperplasia or destruction, which helps to differentiate it from meningioma [4].

Enhanced mode: uniform enhancement, lacking the “snowman sign” or cystic changes commonly seen in pituitary adenomas, also without the eggshell-like calcification of craniopharyngiomas or the “meningeal tail sign” of meningiomas [22].

Principal differential diagnosis

Cavernous Sinus Schwannoma: Originates from the trigeminal nerve, benign lesion, presents with mixed signal intensity on MRI, and shows heterogeneous enhancement after contrast administration [22].

Meningioma: T1WI shows isointense or hypointense signal, T2WI shows mildly hyperintense signal, and uniform enhancement is observed after contrast administration, accompanied by a dural tail sign [23].

Craniopharyngioma: Commonly found in the suprasellar region, cystic or mixed cystic and solid, with “eggshell-like” calcification in the cyst wall, and the solid part shows a “salt and pepper” appearance after enhancement [24].

Limitations

This study has certain limitations. First, this article is a single case report, and its results and conclusions may not be generalizable to all patients with intrasellar Rosai-Dorfman disease. Second, the discussion of the mechanism of facial paralysis in the article is mainly based on literature and pathophysiological speculation, lacking direct evidence to support it. Third, the postoperative follow-up period was short (6 months), and long-term prognosis and recurrence risk were not assessed. Therefore, more case accumulation and long-term follow-up studies are still needed in the future.

Conclusion

The clinical manifestations of intrasellar RDD are insidious and lack specificity, making it easy to confuse with common pituitary space-occupying lesions. Currently, surgical resection remains the gold standard for definitive diagnosis and is the primary therapeutic intervention; long-term follow-up is crucial for monitoring recurrence.
