Status Epilepticus Alters the Function of Brain‐Derived Extracellular Vesicles
Authors: Samantha L Reed, Yilin Wang, Yangping Li, Guoku Hu, Jingjing Yang, Bing Yao, Andrew Escayg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEpilepsy is a neurological disorder characterised by recurrent spontaneous seizures. Approximately 30% of patients are unable to achieve adequate seizure control with available medications, highlighting the need to better understand disease mechanisms and develop improved treatments. Mesial temporal lobe epilepsy (MTLE) is a common subtype of treatment‐resistant epilepsy. Patients with MTLE often have experienced a prior neurological insult, such as a traumatic brain injury, or status epilepticus (SE), that is believed to contribute to epileptogenic changes that lead to MTLE. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying epileptogenesis remain incompletely understood. Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are small membrane‐bound particles released by cells that are important for intercellular communication. Recent studies identified altered microRNA (miRNA) content in brain‐derived EVs (BDEVs) in models of MTLE, but whether BDEV function is altered has not been explored. Using the pilocarpine mouse model of MTLE, we examined the effects of SE on the function and miRNA cargo of hippocampal BDEVs during the epileptogenic period. BDEVs were isolated from hippocampi of control and pilocarpine‐treated mice 24 h after SE. To assess functional changes, we compared gene expression in N2a neuronal‐like cells and BV2 microglial‐like cells exposed to control BDEVs (CON‐BDEVs) or BDEVs collected after SE (SE‐BDEVs). SE‐BDEVs induced distinct transcriptional changes in N2a cells including alterations in the expression of genes related to TGF‐β signalling, amino acid regulation of mTORC1 and neurotransmitter signalling. In BV2 cells, SE‐BDEVs increased the expression of genes associated with inflammatory cytokine release. Analysis of BDEV miRNA content revealed multiple differentially expressed miRNAs between SE‐ and CON‐BDEVs, with predicted targets overlapping with genes uniquely altered by SE‐BDEVs. Notably, SE‐induced miRNA changes were still observed 10 days post‐SE, indicating sustained modulation of BDEV cargo. These findings identify SE‐induced alterations in both the function and miRNA composition of hippocampal BDEVs, suggesting that BDEVs may contribute to epileptogenesis in MTLE.Keywords: epilepsy, epileptogenesis, extracellular vesicles, MTLE, status epilepticus

Introduction

Epilepsy is a common neurological disorder that is characterised by recurrent and unprovoked seizures. Temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) is the most common form of treatment‐resistant epilepsy in adults, and mesial temporal lobe epilepsy (MTLE) is the most common type of TLE (Engel 1996, 2001). In addition to seizures, patients with MTLE experience a range of challenging comorbidities, such as an increased risk for mood and anxiety disorders (Ertem et al. 2017). Patients with MTLE often have experienced a prior neurological insult, such as a traumatic brain injury, neurological infection, or status epilepticus (SE) (Engel 1996, 2001; Falconer et al. 1964). SE is clinically defined as 5 min or more of continuous seizure activity or multiple recurrent seizures without recovery of consciousness (Trinka et al. 2015). Observations from rodent models have revealed that the molecular and cellular changes which occur after SE, including neuronal cell death and neuroinflammation, can contribute to the subsequent development of seizures and behavioural abnormalities (Behr et al. 2017; Mazzuferi et al. 2012; Venceslas and Corinne 2017). However, the epileptogenic alterations that drive disease development are still not fully understood, and a better understanding of the underlying mechanisms is necessary to accelerate the development of more efficacious treatments.

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are cell‐derived particles that facilitate the transfer of contents between cells. They contain nucleic acids, lipids and proteins but are particularly enriched with small RNAs such as miRNAs. In recent years, small EVs (50–200 nm) which include exosomes and some microvesicles, have garnered attention for their important contributions to intercellular signalling (Delpech et al. 2019; Kourembanas 2015; Raposo and Stoorvogel 2013; Reed and Escayg 2021; Yáñez‐Mó et al. 2015; Zappulli et al. 2016). Small EVs are released by all cell types in the brain and play important roles in processes such as neurodevelopment (Bahram Sangani et al. 2024), synaptic plasticity (Korkut et al. 2013; Lachenal et al. 2011) and neuron/glia communication (Ahmad et al. 2022; Bahrini et al. 2015; Frühbeis et al. 2013). There is also increasing evidence that EV content can be altered in disease states, and that such alterations can contribute to neuropathology. For example, in neurodegenerative disorders, toxic, aggregation‐prone proteins can be packaged into EVs and then transferred to other cells, thus contributing to the spread of pathology (Coleman and Hill 2015; You and Ikezu 2019). Furthermore, EVs can exhibit increased levels of proinflammatory proteins and altered miRNA content after neurological insults such as TBI or stroke, suggesting that these alterations might also contribute to disease (Bub et al. 2022; Eyileten et al. 2025). Although several studies have looked at the contents of EVs from biofluids in epilepsy patients (Chen et al. 2020; Ruan et al. 2023; Villena‐Rueda et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2022; Yan et al. 2017; Yang et al. 2024), to date, only two studies have examined the expression of miRNAs in small brain‐derived EVs (BDEVs) isolated directly from the brain tissue of MTLE rodent models. Batool et al. identified multiple upregulated miRNAs in hippocampus‐derived BDEVs from the intra‐amygdala kainic acid mouse model at 24 h and 2 weeks following SE (Batool et al. 2019). In a study of the pilocarpine model of MTLE, Gitaí et al. (2020) identified several differentially expressed miRNAs in BDEVs from rat forebrain 2 months after SE. However, to date, no studies have examined whether the function of BDEVs is altered following SE.

In the current study, we utilised the pilocarpine mouse model of MTLE to establish the effect of SE on the function and miRNA content of hippocampal BDEVs collected during the epileptogenic period. BDEVs were isolated from the hippocampus of control mice or pilocarpine‐treated mice 24 h after SE. To investigate whether SE changes BDEV function, we identified and compared changes in gene expression when N2a neuronal‐like cells and BV2 microglial‐like cells were exposed to hippocampus‐derived BDEVs that were collected from control mice and pilocarpine‐exposed mice. In addition, we assayed miRNA expression in hippocampal BDEVs 24 h following SE to investigate potential mechanisms underlying the observed differences in gene expression. We also assayed miRNA expression in hippocampal BDEVs 10 days after SE to determine if changes in BDEV miRNA expression were transient or sustained.

Methods

Animals

Male C57/Bl6NCr1 mice (strain 027, 9–10 weeks old) were acquired from Charles River and were then maintained on a 12 h light‐dark cycle with standard rodent chow and water available ad libitum. All experiments were approved by the Emory University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

Pilocarpine Model

Male C57/Bl6NCr1 mice (10–11 weeks old) were first injected intraperitoneally (i.p.) with terbutaline hemisulfate and scopolamine methyl bromide (2 mg/kg each) to prevent the peripheral effects of pilocarpine. After 20 min, mice were administered pilocarpine (280 mg/kg, i.p.), and behavioural seizures were scored every 5 min according to the following scale: Stage 0‐normal behaviour, Stage 1‐immobile, staring or curled posture, Stage 2‐automatisms, head bobbing, whisker twitching, Stage 3‐partial body clonus, occasional myoclonic jerks, shivering, Stage 4‐whole‐body clonus, loss of posture, rearing and falling, Stage 5‐SE characterised by continuous Stage 3 and 4 activity. After 1 h of SE (Stage 5), seizure activity was interrupted with diazepam (10 mg/kg, i.p.). Only mice that entered and remained in SE for 1 h were used. Fifty mice were administered pilocarpine, and we observed a mortality rate of 28% (14 mice) while two mice were excluded as they did not enter SE. Control mice (43 mice) were similarly treated but were administered saline instead of pilocarpine. Mice were perfused with PBS at 24 h or 10 days post‐SE and hippocampi were dissected for storage at −80°C.

BDEV Isolation

To prepare each BDEV sample, the hippocampi from three (for miRNA‐sequencing, nanoparticle tracking analysis [NTA], western blot and electron microscopy) or four (for cell treatment experiments) mice were combined (95–130 mg of tissue). To enrich for small BDEVs, samples were processed following the protocol described by D'Acunzo et al. (2022). Tissue samples were cut into small pieces and incubated in 3.5 mL of papain solution (1 mg/mL) in Hibernate A (Gibco, A124754) for 15 min at 37°C. Ice cold Hibernate A (6.5 mL) containing protease inhibitors (5 µg/mL LAP, 1 mM PMSF, 1 µM E64) was then added, and the samples were further dissociated by pipetting up and down 10 times using a 10 mL serological pipette, followed by centrifugation at 300 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was passed through a 5 µm filter and then a 0.2 µm filter, before being centrifuged at 2000 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was removed and then centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 30 min (Beckman Optima XPN‐100, SW28 rotor at 7600RPM, k‐factor = 3346). The supernatant was then collected and further centrifuged for 70 min at 100,000 × g (Beckman Optima XPN‐100, SW28 rotor at 24200RPM, k‐factor = 330). After removal of the supernatant, the pellet containing the BDEVs was washed in 30 mL of PBS before once again undergoing centrifugation at 100,000 × g for 70 min. For further purification, the BDEVs were loaded onto a sucrose density gradient (0.25–2 M) in 100 mM HEPES and centrifuged for 16 h at 200,000 × g (Beckman Optima XPN‐100, SW41Ti rotor at 34600RPM; k‐factor = 175). The BDEV‐containing fractions were then collected (corresponding to sucrose concentrations of 0.25–1.3 M), washed with 30 mL of PBS and centrifuged for 70 min at 100,000 × g (Beckman Optima XPN‐100, SW28 rotor at 24200RPM, k‐factor = 330). Finally, the supernatant was removed, and the BDEV pellet was resuspended in 300 µL of PBS (for NTA, transmission electron microscopy [TEM], fluorescent labelling, cell treatment and miRNA‐seq) or 200 µL RIPA buffer with protease inhibitors (for western blotting protein analysis).

Fluorescent Labelling of BDEVs

Freshly isolated BDEVs from control mice were incubated in a 1:1000 dilution of CellMask Near‐Infrared Membrane Dye (Invitrogen, C56129) for 10 min on ice. Samples were diluted in 30 mL of PBS and centrifuged for 70 min at 100,000 × g (Beckman Optima XPN‐100, SW28 rotor at 24200RPM). After removal of the supernatant, the pellet containing the BDEVs was washed in 30 mL of PBS before once again centrifuging at 100,000 × g for 70 min. Pellets were resuspended in 300 µL of PBS and subjected to NTA.

Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA)

BDEV concentration and size range were assayed using the NanoSight NS300 (Malvern). Samples were diluted 50,000‐fold with ultrapure water and injected with a continuous syringe system for 60 s at a rate of 100 µL/min. Data acquisition was undertaken at ambient temperature and was repeated five times per sample. Data were analysed with NTA 3.2 software to determine particle concentration and diameter.

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

Negative stain TEM was conducted to observe the morphology of BDEV samples (Robert P. Apkarian Integrated Electron Microscopy Core, Emory University). CFlat‐400‐Cu grids were glow‐discharged on a PELCO EasyGlow for 30 s before the samples were applied. Uranyl formate (0.75%) was added after blotting excess solution from the grids. Excess stain was removed, and the grids were air‐dried for 5 min at room temperature and then imaged on a Thermo Fisher Scientific 120 kV Talos L120C transmission electron microscope. Images were collected using a BM‐Ceta camera.

Western Blot

To generate BDEV protein samples, isolated BDEV pellets were resuspended and homogenised in RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors (5 µg/mL LAP, 1 mM PMSF, 1 µM E64). To generate tissue‐derived protein samples, whole hippocampi from saline or pilocarpine‐treated mice were homogenised in RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors. Bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assays were conducted on tissue and BDEV protein samples to determine protein concentrations. BDEV lysates (40–80 µg) or tissue lysates (5 µg) were loaded onto 4%–20% SDS‐page gels, and proteins were separated at 200 V. Samples were transferred to a PVDF membrane overnight at 30 V. The membrane was blocked in 5% milk and incubated overnight in primary antibody (rabbit anti‐Alix 1:1000, Proteintech 12422‐1‐AP; rabbit anti‐calnexin 1:1000, Proteintech 10427‐2‐AP; rabbit anti‐CD9 1:1000, Abcam ab223052). The next day, the blot was washed and incubated in the secondary antibody (donkey anti‐rabbit‐HRP cytiva NA934PS, 1:3000). The membrane was visualised using Advansta WesternBright ECL spray (K‐12049‐D50) and imaged on a BioRad Chemi‐Doc.

Cell Culture and Treatment

Cells were maintained using sterile DMEM/F‐12 (Gibco 11320033) for BV2 cells and sterile DMEM (Gibco 11965092) for N2a cells. Both media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco 26140079) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco 15140122). The cells were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 and passaged using 0.25% trypsin‐EDTA (Gibco 25200072) when 80% confluency was achieved. In preparation for BDEV uptake experiments, 10,000 cells were plated on poly‐D‐lysine‐coated coverslips in 12‐well plates and allowed to grow overnight. Cells were treated with different doses of labelled BDEVs (1 × 108–1 × 1012 particles/mL) or vehicle (PBS) diluted in 500 µL of media. At 2, 12 or 24 h after treatment, coverslips were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and mounted onto slides using VECTASHIELD Vibrance Antifade Mounting Medium with DAPI (Vector Laboratories, H‐1800‐10). For RNA‐sequencing and multiplex immunoassay experiments, 50,000 BV2 or N2a cells were plated in tissue culture‐treated 12‐well plates and allowed to grow overnight. The cells were then treated for 24 h with vehicle (PBS) or with BDEVs (1 × 1012 particles/mL) and collected either 24 h after SE (SE‐BDEV) or from control mice (CON‐BDEV). After treatment, conditioned media was stored at −80°C for analysis of cytokine concentration. Cells were washed with PBS, manually disassociated from the plate and stored at −80°C for RNA isolation and sequencing.

Confocal Imaging and BDEV Uptake Quantification

Images were acquired with a Nikon A1R HD24 fluorescent confocal microscope using a 40X oil objective at 458 nm (DAPI) and 640 nm (CellMask Far‐Red labelled BDEVs), along with transmitted light to visualise whole cells. The camera and laser settings were kept constant throughout the image acquisition process. Three separate images were taken for each treatment condition. Raw image files were opened in FIJI, and the mean fluorescence intensity of the 640 nm channel (labelled BDEVs) was quantified for each image using the measure function.

RNA Isolation

RNA was isolated from N2a and BV2 cell pellets after vehicle‐ or BDEV‐treatment using the ReliaPrep RNA Cell Miniprep System per manufacturer's instructions (Promega Z6011). Samples were resuspended in 30 µL of RNase‐free water and stored at −80°C until further use. BDEV RNA from SE and control mice was isolated using the miRNeasy Micro Kit (Qiagen 21708) with DNase treatment (Qiagen 79254) per manufacturer's instructions. Samples were resuspended in 15 µL of RNAse‐free water and stored at −80°C.

RNA Sequencing

Library preparation and RNA‐sequencing were performed by Admera Health, LLC. Libraries were constructed using the QIAseq miRNA Library Kit (for small RNA‐sequencing from BDEVs) and the NEB Ultra II Directional RNA library Prep kit with PolyA selection (for mRNA‐sequencing from treated N2a and BV2 cells). All libraries were sequenced using the Illumina platform at a read length of 150 bp and a depth of 20 M paired‐end reads for small RNA‐sequencing or 40 M paired‐end reads for mRNA‐sequencing.

Sequencing Data Analysis

PolyA RNA‐sequencing data were mapped to the mouse genome (mm9) using TopHat2 with default parameters followed by gene expression quantification and differential expression analysis using Cuffdiff (FDR < 0.05, see Supporting Information for FKPM and differential expression analysis outputs). Cells treated with SE‐BDEVs or CON‐BDEVS were compared to vehicle‐treated cells. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were subjected to enrichment analysis (Gene Ontology Analysis and Panther classification system, ChEA Transcription Factor [TF] Targets 2022 and RegNetwork) using ShinyGO 0.81 (Ge et al. 2020). Only terms containing 2–5000 genes were examined with redundancy removed (default parameters, FDR <0.05). The background for the enrichment analysis was the genes expressed in either BV2 or N2a cells from our sequence data. For Gene Ontology (GO) analysis, if more than 100 terms were retrieved with FDR < 0.05, only the top 100 terms (ranked by FDR) were considered, and a selection of relevant terms displayed. For PANTHER analysis, all significant terms were displayed. Small RNA‐sequencing data were mapped to the mouse miRNA database with miRge 2.0. Differential expression analysis of miRNAs was performed by DESeq2 (FDR < 0.05). The miRNA expression of the SE‐BDEVs was compared to CON‐BDEVs. See Supporting Information for miRNA raw counts and differential expression analysis outputs.

Protein–Protein Interaction Network Analysis by STRING

DEGs from either SE‐BDEV‐treated cells or CON‐BDEV‐treated cells (compared to vehicle‐treated cells) were input to STRING (version 12.0) to identify the corresponding protein–protein interactions (PPIs) using all active integration sources and a confidence interval threshold of 0.4. Detached nodes were removed from the network figures. Clustering analysis was conducted using the Markov Cluster Algorithm (MCL) with an inflation parameter of 3 and the cluster description assigned by STRING (van Dongen 2008).

Multiplex Immunoassay for Cytokine Release

A multiplex immunoassay was conducted to determine the concentration of cytokines in the conditioned media from vehicle‐treated, CON‐BDEV‐treated and SE‐BDEV‐treated BV2 cells using the Mesoscale Discovery platform (Emory Multiplex Immunoassay Core). We quantified the concentration of seven cytokines (GM‐CSF, IFN‐γ, IL‐16, IL‐1β, IL‐4, IL‐6 and TNF‐α) using a customised U‐PLEX plate (K15069M) per manufacturer's instructions. Analytes were excluded if a concentration could not be calculated for all samples due to values below the detection limit. A one‐way AVOVA followed by post hoc comparison using Tukey's multiple comparison test was used to determine the effect of treatment on the concentration of each analyte.

miRNA Target Identification

Targets of each miRNA of interest were compiled from TargetScan 8.0 and miRDB 6.0 for predicted targets, and miTarBase 8.0 for validated targets (Huang et al. 2022; Liu and Wang 2019; McGeary et al. 2019). The lists of targets were compared to the DEGs with significantly altered expression in only SE‐BDEV‐treated cells (with the direction of change for the miRNA and DEG taken into consideration). This was performed separately for BV2 and N2a cells.

Results

Characterisation of BDEVs From Hippocampal Tissue of Control and SE Mice

BDEVs were isolated (Figure 1A) and characterised based on the MISEV 2023 guidelines from the International Society of Extracellular Vesicles (Welsh et al. 2024). NTA analysis showed that the majority of isolated particles were between 50 and 200 nm in diameter (Figure 1B). TEM imaging revealed vesicles with round and cup‐shaped morphology, indicative of BDEVs (Figure 1C). Finally, consistent with a high‐quality BDEV preparation, western blot analysis of isolated BDEV protein detected the EV protein markers Alix and CD9 and only low levels of the endoplasmic reticulum protein calnexin (Figure 1D). To establish that the isolated BDEVs could be taken up by cells in vitro, the BDEVs were stained with Cell‐Mask Far Red and incubated with either N2a or BV2 cells (108–1012 EV/mL media) for 2, 12 or 24 h. Maximum BDEV uptake was observed with the highest dose and at the 24‐h time point for both cell types (Figure 1E, F). Therefore, this concentration and time‐point were used for further experiments.

Hippocampal BDEV Regulation of Gene Expression in N2a Cells

To determine whether BDEV function is altered following SE, we compared gene expression changes when N2a cells were treated with CON‐BDEVs, SE‐BDEVs or vehicle (PBS) for 24 h (Figure 2A).

CON‐BDEVs Modulate Gene Expression in N2a Cells

When compared to vehicle‐treated cells, CON‐BDEV treatment resulted in 215 DEGs (FDR < 0.05), comprising 76 upregulated and 139 downregulated genes (Figure 2B). PANTHER and GO enrichment analysis of the 215 DEGs revealed significant enrichment for terms related to sterol and cholesterol biosynthesis (Figure 2C). This is supported by transcription factor (TF) target enrichment analysis, which showed that the top TF (ranked by FDR) was SREBF2 (RegNetwork FDR = 1.25E‐10), which is involved in the regulation of cholesterol biosynthesis genes (Hua et al. 1993). Accordingly, SREBF2 expression was also downregulated with in CON‐BDEV‐treated cells (FDR = 0.005). CON‐BDEV treatment was also associated with enrichment for terms related to neurotransmitter signalling, including serotonin and dopamine signalling pathways (Figure 2C). The DEGs within these terms were mostly upregulated (75%) and included increased expression of key enzymes such as Ddc (FDR = 0.009), which is involved in serotonin and dopamine synthesis, and Dbh (FDR = 0.005), which is responsible for norepinephrine synthesis.

To identify interrelated DEGs, a network was constructed using the STRING database of PPIs (version 12.0) and separated into clusters using the MCL algorithm (van Dongen 2008). The network showed enrichment for PPIs (enrichment: p < 1.0E‐16). Thirty‐one clusters were identified with three clusters containing more than five genes (Figure 2D). The largest cluster contained 44 genes and was assigned the description of sterol biosynthesis. Nearly all genes in this cluster were downregulated (42 of 44), suggesting the downregulation of sterol biosynthesis in the N2a cells. The next largest clusters were ‘amino acids regulate mTORC1 and TOR complex’ (seven upregulated, one downregulated) and ‘glycine, serine and threonine metabolism’ (six genes, all downregulated).

SE Alters BDEV Regulation of Gene Expression in N2a Cells

Treatment of N2a cells with SE‐BDEVs resulted in 140 DEGs (FDR < 0.05), comprising 45 upregulated and 95 downregulated genes (Figure 3A). The majority of these DEGs (112 DEGs) were also observed in CON‐BDEV‐treated N2a cells (Figure 3B), yielding a Jaccard similarity index of 46.1% (112/(103 + 112 + 28)) (Jaccard 1901). PANTHER and GO analysis of the 140 DEGs similarly showed enrichment for terms related to cholesterol and sterol biosynthesis (Figure 3C). Likewise, SREBF2 was identified as the top TF in the enrichment analysis (RegNetwork, FDR = 3.2E‐13) and was similarly downregulated (FDR = 0.007). However, in contrast to CON‐BDEVs, SE‐BDEV‐treated N2a cells did not show enrichment for pathways related to neurotransmitter signalling.

Network analysis using STRING showed enrichment for interactions (PPI enrichment: p < 1.0E‐16). MCL analysis identified 21 clusters with the largest containing 37 genes and assigned the description of cholesterol biosynthesis (Figure 3D). Most of these genes overlapped with the sterol biosynthesis cluster in CON‐BDEV‐treated N2a cells (33/37) and were downregulated (36/37). No other clusters contained more than 5 genes.

To further examine functional differences between the two BDEV samples, we analysed gene expression changes that were unique to either the CON‐BDEV (103 genes) or SE‐BDEV (28 genes) treatments (Figure 3B). GO analysis of the 103 genes (47 upregulated, 56 downregulated) highlighted the ability of CON‐BDEVs to regulate neurotransmitter signalling pathways in N2a cells (Figure 3E), while the 28 DEGs unique to SE‐BDEV‐treated cells showed no enrichment for pathways related to neurotransmitter signalling (Figure 3F). In contrast, GO analysis of these 28 DEGs revealed pathways related to the SMAD protein complex and TGF‐β signalling pathway (Figure 3F), suggesting that BDEVs may influence these pathways after SE.

Since BDEVs could exert cell‐type‐specific effects, we next examined the effect of BDEVs on gene expression in microglial‐like BV2 cells. BV2 cells were treated with CON‐BDEVs, SE‐BDEVs or vehicle (PBS) for 24 h, followed by transcriptomic analysis (Figure 4A).

CON‐BDEVs Modulate Gene Expression in BV2 Cells

CON‐BDEV treatment of BV2 cells resulted in 497 DEGs when compared to vehicle treatment (FDR < 0.05, 317 upregulated; 180 downregulated, Figure 4B). Similar to findings with the N2a cells, PANTHER and GO analysis revealed enrichment for terms related to cholesterol biosynthesis. However, enrichment for terms like ‘detoxification’ and ‘response to stress’ were also observed (Figure 4C). TF analysis also identified enrichment for targets of SREBF2 (FDR = 2.16E‐5), However, in BV2 cells, enrichment for targets of the related TF SREBF1 were also observed (FDR = 0.0003). The genes encoding for these TFs were downregulated (FDR = 0.002 for both).

STRING analysis showed significant enrichment of interactions (PPI enrichment: p < 1.0E‐16), and MCL clustering analysis revealed 89 clusters, with 5 clusters containing more than 10 genes (Figure 4D). The largest clusters were related to ‘cholesterol biosynthesis’ (7 upregulated; 29 downregulated), ‘monocyte chemotaxis’ (13 upregulated; 6 downregulated), ‘detection of other organism’ (11 upregulated, 5 downregulated), ‘glutathione metabolism/detoxification’ (1 upregulated; 13 downregulated) and ‘pentose phosphate pathways’ (9 upregulated, 3 downregulated).

SE Increases the Ability of BDEVs to Regulate Inflammatory Pathways

We identified 617 DEGs when SE‐BDEV‐treated BV2 cells were compared to vehicle‐treated cells (FDR < 0.05, 386 upregulated; 231 downregulated, Figure 5A), with 397 DEGs overlapping with CON‐BDEV‐treated BV2 cells (Figure 5B) and a Jaccard similarity index of 55.4% (397/(100 + 397 + 220)). Analyses of these 617 DEGs showed enrichment for some terms (e.g., detoxification, response to stress and cholesterol biosynthesis) that were also observed in CON‐BDEV‐treated BV2 cells (Figure 5C), and we similarly detected enrichment for targets of SREBF1 (FDR = 6.52E‐5) and SREBF2 (FDR = 0.0001). However, SE‐BDEV‐treated BV2 cells also showed enrichment for targets of the TF GATA3 (FDR = 0.01, ChEA TF Targets), which is associated with microglial polarisation and cytokine production, and which was upregulated in SE‐BDEV‐treated BV2 cells (FDR = 0.002). We also found enrichment for targets of STAT5 (FDR = 0.026, ChEA TF Targets), another TF which is involved in inflammatory signalling. Interestingly, we saw downregulation of Jak3 (FDR = 0.004), a kinase that acts directly on STAT5. Notably, Jak3 and GATA3 were differentially expressed in SE‐BDEV‐treated BV2 cells, but not with CON‐BDEV treatment. Overall, while many DEGs overlapped with those seen following CON‐BDEV treatment of BV2 cells (397 shared), 220 genes were unique to SE‐BDEV treatment (Figure 5B).

STRING analysis of the 617 DEGs revealed significant enrichment of interactions (PPI enrichment: p < 1.0E‐16), and MCL clustering analysis revealed 109 clusters, including 4 clusters with 10 or more genes (Figure 5D). The largest clusters were related to ‘positive regulation of cytokine production’ (51 upregulated; 20 downregulated), ‘cholesterol biosynthesis’ (5 upregulated; 29 downregulated), ‘Detoxification/glutathione metabolism’ (1 upregulated; 19 downregulated) and ‘pentose phosphate pathways’ (8 upregulated, 7 downregulated).

To further examine the differences between the effects of the two BDEV treatments on gene expression in BV2 cells, we separately analysed the DEGs that were uniquely altered by either SE‐BDEV (220 genes) or CON‐BDEV (100 genes) treatment (Figure 5B). Enrichment analysis of the 220 DEGs that were only observed in SE‐BDEV‐treated cells revealed pathways related to ‘positive regulation of cytokine production’, ‘cell chemotaxis’ and the ‘response to oxygen‐containing compounds’ (Figure 5E). Furthermore, among the genes uniquely altered in SE‐BDEV‐treated BV2 cells were several well‐characterised proinflammatory cytokines, such as IL‐1β (FDR = 0.002) and IL‐16 (FDR = 0.016). Taken together, these observations identified an upregulation of cytokine signalling with SE‐BDEV treatment that was not seen with CON‐BDEV treatment. GO analysis of the 100 genes unique to the CON‐BDEV‐treated BV2 cells did not yield any significantly enriched terms.

BDEV Treatment Alters Cytokine Release From BV2 Cells

The RNA‐sequencing analysis suggested that BDEVs may regulate cytokine production in BV2 cells. To further investigate this possibility, we quantified the levels of seven cytokines (GM‐CSF, IFN‐γ, IL‐16, IL‐1β, IL‐4, IL‐6 and TNF‐α) in the conditioned media that was collected from BV2 cells treated with SE‐BDEVs, CON‐BDEVs or vehicle. Three of these cytokines were excluded due to readings below the detection limit (GM‐CSF, IFN‐γ, IL‐1β), and four were selected for further analysis (IL‐16, IL‐4, IL‐6 and TNF‐α, Figure 6). When compared to vehicle treatment, we observed a lower concentration of TNF‐α (Figure 6A) and a higher concentration of IL‐4 (Figure 6B) in the conditioned media when BV2 cells were treated with either SE‐BDEVs or CON‐BDEVs. However, the concentration of these cytokines was not significantly different when SE‐BDEV and CON‐BDEV treatments were compared directly, indicating that SE did not affect the ability of BDEVs to alter the levels of these cytokines. While both CON‐ and SE‐BDEV treatments resulted in reductions in IL‐16 when compared to vehicle (Figure 6C), the difference was only statistically significant with SE‐BDEV treatment. Additionally, while CON‐BDEV treatment resulted in lower average levels of IL‐6 (2.65 pg/mL) compared to either SE‐BDEV (4.93 pg/mL) or vehicle (4.27 pg/mL) treatment, these differences were not significant (Figure 6D).

Principal components analysis (PCA) showed that the miRNA expression levels in CON‐BDEVs and SE‐BDEVs collected 24 h after SE are different (Figure 7A). Due to their upregulation in SE‐BDEVs at both time points, we were particularly interested in the potential relationship between miR‐155‐5p and miR‐21a‐5p and the genes that were uniquely downregulated by SE‐BDEV treatment. One of the 28 DEGs unique to SE‐BDEV‐treated N2a cells was a potential direct target of miR‐21a‐5p (Ifrd1), while none were predicted to be direct targets of miR‐155‐5p. Discussion

We demonstrated that BDEVs can alter gene expression in N2a and BV2 cells. Notably, CON‐BDEVs and SE‐BDEVs regulated the transcription of genes related to cholesterol biosynthesis in both cell types. The majority of the DEGs related to cholesterol biosynthesis were downregulated, including transcripts of HMGCR which is the rate‐limiting enzyme responsible for cholesterol synthesis (Tobert 2003), suggesting an overall downregulation of cholesterol biosynthesis pathways. Previous research has shown that the EV membrane is enriched in cholesterol when compared to the total lipid content of cells (Pfrieger and Vitale 2018). Since the expression of cholesterol biosynthesis pathway genes is regulated by the overall concentration of cholesterol present in a cell, it is possible that the administration of exogenous BDEVs resulted in an increase in the level of available cholesterol, causing recipient cells to downregulate endogenous cholesterol biosynthesis. Accordingly, we also observed reduced expression of the TF Srebf2 (in both cell types) and Srebf1 (in BV2 cells), which is expected to occur in the presence of excess cholesterol (Horton et al. 2002). However, the possibility that BDEVs regulate cholesterol biosynthesis through some other mechanism cannot be ruled out. For example, Li et al. found that tumour‐derived EVs increased cholesterol biosynthesis in the liver via miR‐9‐5p modulation of HMGCR (Li et al. 2024).

Interestingly, several pathways that were altered by CON‐BDEV treatment of N2a cells were not changed following SE‐BDEV treatment. For example, MCL analysis of DEGs after CON‐BDEV treatment identified the clusters ‘glycine, serine and threonine metabolism’ and ‘amino acids regulate mTORC1 and TOR Complex’ (Figure 2D), but these clusters, and importantly, the majority of DEGs within these clusters (4/6 and 7/8, respectively) were not observed following SE‐BDEV treatment (Figure 3D). In the brain, mTORC1 is involved in multiple processes, including cell growth, neuron development and synaptic plasticity (Gerasimenko et al. 2023; Panwar et al. 2023). Thus, this finding suggests that, under normal conditions, BDEVs may contribute to these processes via modulation of mTORC1 function. However, following SE, BDEVs show a reduced ability to modulate genes related to mTORC1 signalling. PANTHER and GO analysis also showed that only CON‐BDEV treatment resulted in enrichment for genes related to neurotransmitter biosynthesis and signalling pathways, while SE‐BDEV treatment did not cause significant enrichment for these pathways. These observations suggest the potential loss of important BDEV functions following SE. In contrast, treatment with SE‐BDEVs led to altered expression of genes regulating TGF‐β signalling in N2a cells. It has been shown that there is a substantial increase in TGF‐β signalling after SE, which results in pathological alterations in extracellular matrix proteins (Mercado‐Gómez et al. 2014). Interestingly, the expression of several genes which encode for components or regulators of the extracellular matrix, including Fyn, Olfml2a and Fbxl20, were only significantly altered after SE‐BDEV treatment of N2a cells. This observation further supports the possibility that BDEVs acquire new functions after SE.

In BV2 cells, enrichment analysis showed that CON‐ and SE‐BDEVs altered the expression of genes related to monocyte chemotaxis, detoxification and the stress response. These pathways are not only important for immune function, but also synaptic pruning and plasticity in the hippocampus (Ball et al. 2022). Previous studies have shown that EVs from cultured neurons can increase the expression of complement pathway components in recipient microglia (e.g., C3 and Macro) and this regulates microglial phagocytosis of excess synapses (Bahrini et al. 2015), identifying a connection between EVs and synaptic plasticity. Interestingly, in CON‐BDEV‐ and SE‐BDEV‐treated BV2 cells, we observed upregulation of genes encoding complement pathway components (C3, Macro and C1q) and enrichment for GO terms related to complement pathway signalling (Figures 4C and 5C), suggesting that BDEVs might contribute to the regulation of these pathways under normal conditions. However, due to their immune function, some activation of BV2 cells would be expected when an exogenous substance is applied to the media.

Although both CON‐BDEV and SE‐BDEV‐treatment of BV2 cells altered the expression of immune response genes, the inflammatory response was more pronounced following treatment with SE‐BDEVs. Enrichment and clustering analysis both showed that SE‐BDEV treatment increased proinflammatory pathways in BV2 cells, particularly those related to cytokine release. This included altered expression of cytokine (e.g., Il1b and Il16) and cytokine receptor (e.g., Csf1r and Il6ra) genes. Increased cytokine release and proinflammatory signalling are well documented following SE (Behr et al. 2017; Mazzuferi et al. 2012) and are known to contribute to the neurological damage associated with MTLE. Thus, BDEVs may play a role in these signalling cascades.

To further investigate the effects of BDEVs on immune processes, we compared the concentration of cytokines released into the media when BV2 cells were treated with SE‐BDEVs or CON‐BVEVs. CON‐BDEV and SE‐BDEV treatments similarly altered the levels of certain cytokines (e.g., TNF‐⍺ and IL‐4) (Figure 6A, B). This finding compliments our RNA‐sequencing results which revealed that some immune pathways in recipient BV2 cells were similarly modulated by both CON‐BDEVs and SE‐BDEVs. We observed decreased TNF‐⍺ (proinflammatory) and increased IL‐4 (anti‐inflammatory) levels, suggesting that BDEV modulation of immune pathways might be anti‐inflammatory. However, we also observed differences between the effect of SE‐ and CON‐BDEV treatments. For example, while the difference in IL‐6 levels between groups was not statistically significant, we did observe that CON‐BDEV treatment resulted in a lower concentration of IL‐6, while the level of IL‐6 following SE‐BDEV treatment was slightly elevated compared to vehicle treatment. A reduction in this proinflammatory cytokine by CON‐BDEVs would be consistent with an anti‐inflammatory effect, which could potentially be lost following SE. Thus, these results suggest that, under normal conditions, BDEVs may act to modulate cytokine production and limit neuroinflammatory signalling, and SE could potentially disrupt this normal function of BDEVs.

Although the extent to which BDEVs modulate inflammatory pathways in vivo post‐SE is currently unknown, EVs isolated from microglia after TBI were sufficient to induce neuroinflammation when injected into the cerebral cortex of control mice (Kumar et al. 2017). BDEVs can also cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB) and enter the circulation (García‐Romero et al. 2017). In models of stroke and TBI, BBB damage leads to higher levels of BDEVs in the blood and the induction of peripheral inflammation (Kumar et al. 2017; Zhang et al. 2021a; Zhang et al. 2024). Weakening of the BBB after SE is well documented (Swissa et al. 2019). Thus, it is possible that BDEVs might also contribute to alterations in peripheral inflammation following SE.

As previous studies reported altered BDEV miRNA expression following SE, we speculated that alterations in BDEV miRNA expression might have contributed to the observed changes in gene expression in N2a and BV2 cells. We detected eight and seven differentially expressed miRNAs in BDEVs at 24 h and 10 days after SE, respectively. Interestingly, many of these miRNAs have been implicated in neuroinflammation, gliosis or proinflammatory signalling. In fact, both miR‐132‐3p and miR‐155‐5p have been linked to increased neuroinflammation and hippocampal damage in epilepsy (Huang et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2022; Zhou et al. 2020). These changes in BDEV miRNA expression are therefore consistent with the altered expression of inflammatory genes seen in SE‐BDEV‐treated BV2 cells. However, other differentially expressed miRNAs, such as miR‐21a‐3p, have been shown to have mixed or even anti–inflammatory effects (Ge et al. 2019; Hasan et al. 2025). A previous study looking at the miRNA content of BDEVs from the intra‐amygdala kainic acid mouse model also found that miR‐21a‐5p and miR‐21a‐3p were differentially expressed 24 h after SE (Batool et al. 2019), indicating reproducibility between models and studies.

Interestingly, we found that miR‐155‐5p and miR‐21a‐5p were differentially expressed at 24 h and 10 days following SE, raising the possibility of long‐lasting changes in BDEV miRNA expression and possibly function. Notably, miR‐155‐5p and miR‐21a‐5p have been implicated in inflammatory signalling. While miR‐21a‐5p has been shown to have both anti‐inflammatory and pro‐inflammatory effects depending on the context (Hu et al. 2015; Luo et al. 2023; Xin et al. 2022), miR‐155‐5p is well‐established as a pro‐inflammatory miRNA which likely contributes to pathological changes after SE (Rastegar‐Moghaddam et al. 2023; Wen et al. 2021). The expression of Ikbke was reduced in SE‐BDEV‐treated BV2 cells and is a validated target of miR‐155‐5p (Guo and Zheng 2020). Although Ikbke can promote inflammation under certain conditions, it also important for limiting chronic inflammation (Patel et al. 2015). We speculate that the higher miR‐155‐5p levels in SE‐BDEVs could have led to the observed reduction in Ikbke expression in SE‐BDEV‐treated BV2 cells, thereby contributing to the altered inflammatory signalling.

It is likely that SE‐induced changes in additional BDEV cargo, including mRNAs and proteins, also impact function. Changes in these BDEV components have been observed in other neurological disorders. For example, BDEVs isolated from a mouse stroke model showed increased expression of mRNAs related to immune processes (Bub et al. 2022) and BDEVs from a TBI mouse model showed higher levels of pro‐inflammatory proteins (Keane et al. 2023; Kerr et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2021a). Therefore, expansion of this research to identify changes in other BDEV cargo following SE would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms by which BDEVs contribute to disease pathology following SE.

Overall, this study shows that SE causes alterations in both the miRNA composition and function of hippocampal BDEVs. Importantly, many of the alterations observed, such as increased expression of inflammatory miRNAs (e.g. miR‐155‐5p) and changes in their regulation of immune processes (e.g., cytokine production and release), have previously been shown to contribute to pathological changes following SE. These results suggest that BDEV‐mediated alterations might contribute to epileptogenesis, raising the intriguing possibility that targeting BDEVs could provide a novel strategy to alter the development of MTLE. However, future experiments examining the effects of administering SE‐BDEVs to healthy mice or modulating BDEV release after SE will be needed to further clarify their role in epileptogenesis.
