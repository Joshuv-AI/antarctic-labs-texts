Prognostic significance and tumor cell interactions of tumor-associated neutrophils in gastric cancer
Authors: Soo Kyung Nam, Seo Hwan Yu, Sun-Young Ahn, Yujun Park, Hyeon Jeong Oh, Yoonjin Kwak, Seong-Ho Kong, Do Joong Park, Hyuk-Joon Lee, Han-Kwang Yang, Hye Seung Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to determine the clinicopathologic implications and tumor cell interaction of CD66b-positive (CD66b +) tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs) and CD66b and CD177 double positive (CD66b + /CD177 +) activated TANs in gastric cancer (GC).MethodsSingle immunohistochemistry (sIHC) for CD66b, CD8, HER2, p53, and E-cadherin was performed in 1,060 patients with GC, together with microsatellite instability (MSI) testing and Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) in situ hybridization. Opal multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) for cytokeratin (CK), CD66b, and CD177, and sIHC for ERα was performed on 59 microsatellite-stable (MSS) and 60 MSI-high (MSI-H) GCs. The immune cell densities were quantified using QuPath for sIHC and InForm for mIF.ResultsOn sIHC, a high density of CD66b + TANs was correlated with HER2 positivity, EBV positivity, and MSI-H phenotype (p < 0.001). Combined analysis of CD66b + TAN densities in the tumor center and invasive margin showed a significant association with better OS (p = 0.036), independent of major clinicopathologic factors. TAN densities were significantly associated with OS in female patients (p < 0.001), but not in male patients (p = 0.574). The density of ERα-positive immune cells was inversely correlated with TAN density (ρ = -0.41, p < 0.001). mIF analysis demonstrated that both CD66b + TANs and CD66b + /CD177 + activated TANs were significantly more abundant in MSI-H GC than in MSS GC. Moreover, the distance between TANs and CK + GC cells was significantly shorter in MSI-H GC than in MSS GC. A shorter distance between CD66b + /CD177 + activated TANs and GC cells was also associated with a better OS (p = 0.041).ConclusionA high TAN density in GC is associated with a favorable prognosis, and the spatial proximity of TANs to GC cells, especially in MSI-H GC, provides additional prognostic value. These findings provide basic knowledge for the development of therapeutic targets related to TANs in GC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s10020-026-01555-9.

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is the fifth most common cancer and fourth leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide (Sung et al. 2020). In South Korea, GC is the third most frequent cancer and the fifth leading cause of cancer-related deaths (Park et al. 2022), regardless of sex. Recently, early diagnosis has been increasing mostly because of routine gastroscopic examination, and the 5-year survival of patients with stage IA GC treated with surgery is more than 90% in East Asia (Huang and Hwang 2021). However, patients with stage III or IV GCs still have poor outcomes, with 5-year survival rate of only 18- 50% even after surgery (Zhang et al. 2024). Therefore, new diagnostic and therapeutic approaches are still required.

GC is an inflammation-associated malignant tumor characterized by the infiltration of various immune cells, including macrophages, neutrophils, and different types of T lymphocytes. All these tumor-infiltrated immune cells constitute a complex microenvironment that affects tumor development and progression (Wu et al. 2012). Especially, tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs) represent a predominant population that can influence tumor development and progression through the secretion of cytokines, chemokines, and proteolytic enzymes (Wu et al. 2011; Granot 2019; Gregory and Houghton 2011; Cong et al. 2020). TANs display pro- or antitumor functions according to their polarization state, which is regulated by environmental cues such as interferons and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β). Antitumor neutrophils (N1 phenotype) suppress tumor growth through direct cytotoxicity, antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, and T-cell activation, whereas protumor neutrophils (N2 phenotype) promote tumor progression by enhancing angiogenesis via secretion and activation of proangiogenic factors (Granot and Jablonska 2015).

TANs can be identified in tumor tissue samples by immunohistochemistry (IHC) using the neutrophil-specific biomarker CD66b. Recently, several studies have examined the association between TAN density and various clinicopathologic features in various tumors, using anti-CD66b IHC staining (Granot and Jablonska 2015; Yoon et al. 2007; Ilie et al. 2012). However the prognostic significance of TANs remains controversial, with previous studies reporting conflicting results in several cancer, including GC (Ilie et al. 2012; Carus et al. 2013; Quaas et al. 2021).

In addition to CD66b, the neutrophil activation marker CD177 is highly expressed on activated neutrophils and is well known as a marker of activated TANs (Zhou et al. 2024). Within the tumor microenvironment, CD177-positive (CD177 +) TANs have been reported to exhibit antitumor or immunomodulatory functions (Zhou et al. 2018). In particular, the co-expression of CD177 and CD66b is considered a hallmark of activated neutrophils and is often associated with enhanced antitumor immune responses (Zhou et al. 2018). Consequently, simultaneous evaluation of CD177 and CD66b provides a more robust and precise means of identifying functionally active TAN subsets.

Recent advances in multiplex IHC and immunofluorescence (IF) have enabled the high-resolution, spatially resolved detection of multiple markers, providing powerful approaches to investigate the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) (Boisson et al. 2021). These approaches are particularly valuable for characterizing neutrophil localization, activation states, and interactions with surrounding immune and stromal cells.

In this study, we quantified CD66b + TANs and CD8 + cytotoxic T cells in GC specimens using single immunohistochemistry (sIHC) to characterize intertumoral heterogeneity of TAN infiltration and to examine its relationship with cytotoxic T-cell density. In addition, Opal multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) was performed to further profile an activated TAN subset defined by CD66b and CD177 co-expression and to evaluate the spatial proximity between TANs and tumor cells. We aimed to delineate the clinicopathologic and molecular correlates of TAN infiltration and to assess the prognostic relevance of TAN activation and spatial distribution in patients with GC.

Materials and methods

Patients

A total of 1,060 patients with stage II to IV GC were enrolled from Seoul National University Hospital (cohort 1, n = 732) and Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (cohort 2, n = 328) between 2006 and 2014. All patients underwent radical gastrectomy with D2 lymph node dissection (Park et al. 2021), and patients treated with neoadjuvant chemotherapy and/or radiation therapy were excluded from the study. The clinicopathologic information of the patients was recorded by reviewing their electronic medical charts and pathologic reports. GC was staged according to the 8th edition of the AJCC Cancer Staging Manual (2017). Postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy was broadly classified as fluoropyrimidine-based, fluoropyrimidine/platinum-based, or trastuzumab-based therapy, and palliative chemotherapy was recorded separately. OS was defined as the interval from surgery to death from any cause or or the date of last follow-up, with surviving patients administratively censored at 100 months. Survival data were last updated on June 14, 2021. Follow-up was administratively censored at 100 months, and the median follow-up period was 80.0 months (range, 1.7–100.0 months).

Tissue microarray construction

The CT and IM cores were originally selected from representative areas marked on H&E-stained sections by pathologists during tissue microarray (TMA) construction. Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) surgical specimens were used for tissue TMA construction. Two representative cores with a diameter of 2 mm were selected from both the center (CT) and invasive margin (IM) areas in each case (Yun et al. 2021). TMA blocks were constructed by transferring these tissues, as previously described (Kwak et al. 2025).

sIHC and assessment for CD66b and CD8

IHC for TANs and cytotoxic T cells was performed using serial 4-μm-thick TMA sections. IHC for the anti-CD66b antibody (1:400; Novus Biologicals, Littleton, CO, USA) was performed using a Benchmark XT automated slide staining system (Ventana Medical Systems, Tucson, AZ, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Fig. 1A, B). IHC for the anti-CD8 antibody (pre-diluted; DAKO Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was performed using the Leica Biosystem BondMax Fully Automated IHC and ISH Staining System (Leica Microsystems, Bannockburn, IL, USA) (Fig. 1C, D).Fig. 1Representative images of single immunohistochemistry for CD66b (A, high density; B, low density) and CD8 (C, high density; D, low density)

Representative images of single immunohistochemistry for CD66b (A, high density; B, low density) and CD8 (C, high density; D, low density)

Quantification of CD66b + TANs and CD8 + tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) was performed by scanning of stained slides using the AperioScanScope™ (Leica Microsystems) and calculating the cell densities (positive cells/mm2) in each core using QuPath version 0.3.1 (https://QuPath.github.io/). Immune cell infiltration was classified as low or high using the 25th percentile of the marker-specific density distribution in cohort 1 as the cutoff. Cases with densities ≤ the cutoff were defined as low density, and those with densities > the cutoff as high density. The cutoff values derived from cohort 1 were then applied to cohort 2 analyses. These were 21.2 positive cells/mm2 for CD66b⁺ TANs and 241.9 positive cells/mm2 for CD8⁺ TILs (Supplementary Table S1).

sIHC for ERα and quantification of ERα-positive immune cells

IHC for ERα (1:50; Leica Biosystems, USA) was performed on serial 4-μm-thick TMA sections using a Benchmark XT automated slide staining system (Ventana Medical Systems, Tucson, AZ, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Whole-slide images were acquired using the Aperio-ScanScope™ system (Leica Microsystems). The density of ERα-positive immune cells in each core was quantified using QuPath software (version 0.3.1).

mIF and data analysis

mIF staining was performed using an Opal 6-plex detection kit (Akoya Biosciences, Marlborough, MA, USA) on serial 4-μm-thick TMA sections. Staining was performed using the Leica Biosystems Bond RXm Fully Automated IHC and ISH Staining System (Leica Microsystems), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, TMA slides were dewaxed and rehydrated, followed by heat-induced epitope retrieval (HIER). The slides were washed and blocked, followed by the application of the first primary antibody; Opal-polymer horseradish peroxidase and tyramide signal amplification reagents were then sequentially applied. This process was repeated using HIER for antibody stripping. After the application of each antibody, the slides were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) and mounted. The staining panel included DAPI for nuclear DNA, cytokeratin (CK; 1:300; DAKO Agilent Technologies) as a tumor cell marker, and CD66b (1:400; Novus Biologicals, USA) and CD177 (1:200, Abcam, Netherlands) as TAN markers. The fluorescence signals applied to each antibody were as follows: Opal 520-CD66b, Opal 570-CD177, and Opal 690-CK.

Stained slides were scanned using Vectra Polaris (Akoya Biosciences), and all images were analyzed using InForm version 3.0.0 (Akoya Biosciences) and the R package phenoptr.

IHC for p53 and E-cadherin

IHC for p53 (1:1,000; DAKO Agilent Technologies) and E-cadherin (1:800; BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) was performed using the BenchMark XT automated slide processing system (Ventana Medical Systems). For E-cadherin, strong membranous expression in 20% or more of tumor cells was defined as positive, whereas loss of membranous staining or aberrant cytoplasmic staining was defined as altered expression. Based on our previous study (Hwang et al. 2020) demonstrated that strong p53 expression predicted TP53 missense mutations with high sensitivity and specificity and was associated with worse overall survival in gastric cancer, p53 immunostaining was dichotomized as overexpression versus no overexpression in the present study. Overexpression was defined as strong nuclear staining in more than 10% of tumor cells, whereas cases without this pattern were classified as no overexpression.

HER2 testing

HER2 IHC using an anti-HER2/neu(4B5) antibody (predilutied, Ventana Medical Systems) and HER2 silver in situ hybridization (SISH) using VENTANA HER2 Dual ISH DNA Probe Cocktail (Ventana Medical Systems) were performed. HER2 status was interpreted according to the guidelines for HER2 pathologic diagnosis in GC (Wada et al. 2016). If either IHC 3 + or IHC 2 + and HER2 amplification by SISH was found, the cases were considered HER2-positive.

Epstein-Barr virus ISH

EBV status was determined by automated in situ hybridization for EBV-encoded RNA. TMA sections were processed using a BOND EBER probe (Leica Microsystems) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Nuclear staining in tumor cells was considered positive.

Microsatellite instability testing

Five microsatellite markers (BAT25, BAT26, D2S123, D5S346, and D17S250) were assessed to determine microsatellite instability (MSI) status according to the National Institutes of Health guidelines (Kwak et al. 2026). Tumor and paired normal tissues were macrodissected from FFPE blocks. Genomic DNA was extracted, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification was performed, and the PCR products were run on an ABI 3730 DNA Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). MSI-high (MSI-H) was defined as unstable in two or more markers, MSI-low (MSI-L) as unstable in one marker, and microsatellite-stable (MSS) as having no unstable markers. MSI-L was regarded as the MSS phenotype for statistical analyses.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS Statistics version 18 for Windows (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) or R software version 4.4.3 (R Development Core Team, Vienna, Austria). The associations between each immune cell density and clinicopathologic parameters were compared using Student’s t-test and chi-square tests. Kaplan–Meier curves with log-rank tests were used to compare OS between groups. For multivariate survival analyses, hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using the Cox proportional hazards model. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Relationship between clinicopathologic findings and CD66b + TAN density

Cohort 1 included 476 men (65.0%) and 256 women (35.0%), and cohort 2 included 328 patients with GC, with 217 men (66.2%) and 111 women (33.8%). Laurén intestinal histologic subtype was observed in 264 of 732 (36.1%) of cohort 1 and 124 of 328 (37.8%) of cohort 2. Based on the 8th edition of the AJCC Cancer Staging Manual, 299 cases (40.8%) were stage II and 433 cases (59.2%) were stage III/IV in cohort 1, whereas 133 cases (40.5%) were stage II and 195 cases (59.5%) were stage III/IV in cohort 2.

Prognostic significance of TAN density for OS

We evaluated the association between CD66b + TAN density and patient prognosis using Kaplan–Meier survival curves. CD66b + TAN density was not significantly associated with OS in either the CT or IM area in cohort 1 (Supplementary Figure S2A, B). In cohort 2, high CD66b + TAN density was significantly associated with longer OS in the CT area (p = 0.012), but not in the IM area (Supplementary Figure S2D, E). In the total cohort, high CD66b + TAN density was significantly associated with longer OS in the CT area (p = 0.004; Fig. 2A), but not in the IM area (p = 0.152; Fig. 2B). We further performed a combined analysis of TAN densities in the CT and IM areas of the cohorts. The combined data were divided into three groups: high, median, and low (high group: high density on both CT and IM; median group: high density on CT or IM; and low density on both CT and IM). The high group had the best OS, whereas the low group had the worst OS among all three groups (p = 0.007; Fig. 2C). Discussion

The present study investigated the role of TAN in a large cohort (cohorts 1 and 2). When we defined cutoffs of TAN densities by lower quartiles in cohort 1 and the same cutoffs were applied to cohort 2, the clinicopathologic correlations were verified by most statistical analyses in the two independent cohorts. Therefore, our results suggest an important role of CD66b + TANs in tumor progression. Additionally, we examined whether TAN density served as an independent prognostic factor in multivariate analysis. When TAN density was assessed separately in the tumor center and invasive margin, only the tumor center showed a more pronounced survival difference between high- and low-density groups. However, independent prognostic value was demonstrated only when CD66b⁺ TAN densities from both compartments were combined in the multivariate Cox regression model, which is conceptually analogous to the Immunoscore approach. Between the IM and CT areas, the CT area showed a more significant difference between CD66b-high group and CD66b-low group. Some recent studies on the association between the tumor microenvironment and cancer have shown that TANs within tumors are associated with survival in various tumor types like our study. Shen et al. characterized the infiltration by TANs and their prognostic significance in patients with surgically resected lung adenocarcinoma (Shen et al. 2021). Posabella et al. investigated the prognostic role of CD66b expression, corresponding to neutrophilic infiltration for recurrence-free survival (RFS) and OS in patients with ovarian carcinoma (Posabella et al. 2020). In their studies, CD66b positivity was significantly associated with worse RFS in lung adenocarcinoma (Shen et al. 2021) and better RFS in ovarian carcinoma (Posabella et al. 2020). Carus et al. found that CD66b + TAN count was an independent prognostic factor for short RFS in localized cervical cancer, although the cohort consists of 101 patients, and the prognostic effect of TAN on OS could not be determined (Carus et al. 2013). In addition, studies on GC and CD66b have been conducted in small cohorts, which makes it difficult to determine the true prognostic value of CD66b positivity (Quaas et al. 2021).

In exploratory sex-stratified subgroup analyses, the prognostic association of CD66b⁺ TAN density appeared more evident in female patients than in male patients, with the low-density group showing worse prognosis than the high-density group in female patients. However, a formal interaction test between sex and TAN-density group was not statistically significant (p for interaction = 0.404). Therefore, this finding should be interpreted cautiously as an exploratory observation rather than definitive evidence of sex-dependent effect modification. To explore a possible hormonal contribution to this observation, we evaluated ERα expression by sIHC. Although ERα-positive immune cell density was not directly associated with OS, it showed a moderate inverse correlation with CD66b⁺ TAN density, suggesting a potential association between estrogen signaling and the tumor immune microenvironment. ERα is known to mediate rapid, non-genomic cytoplasmic signaling through interaction with PI3K/p85α and activation of downstream Akt/eNOS pathways (Simoncini et al. 2000). In parallel, ERα/ERβ expression has been detected in human neutrophils, and estrogen has been shown to regulate neutrophil gene expression and functional programs, including cytokine, chemokine, and tissue-remodeling pathways (Molero et al. 2002; Chung et al. 2017). Thus, estrogen-related signaling may provide a biologically plausible link to neutrophil infiltration or function in the tumor microenvironment, although this association remains to be validated in the present cohort. Consistent with this notion, sex-related differences in immune biomarkers and responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors have also been reported in GC (Lee et al. 2025). Collectively, these findings suggest that hormonal regulation may contribute to the sex-specific prognostic relevance of TANs. However, further studies are needed to better understand causal relationship between ERα-positive immune cells and TANs.

Beyond clinicopathologic stratification, the biological effects of TANs in cancer are not homogeneous; they may exert both antitumor and protumor functions. Assessment of the functional heterogeneity of TANs with sIHC for CD66b is difficult. To identify the functional subgroup of activated TANs and their interaction with CK + GC cells, we performed mIF staining using CK, CD66b, and CD177 (Ahn and Lee 2024). CD177 is a well-established marker of activated neutrophils (Kim et al. 2021). In our analysis, MSI-H GC exhibited significantly higher densities of both CD66b⁺ TANs and CD66b⁺/CD177⁺ TANs than MSS GC. Although the density of CD66b⁺/CD177⁺ TANs alone was not significantly associated with OS, this finding warrants further investigation in larger subtype-stratified cohorts. Moreover, the spatial proximity between these activated TANs and GC cells was significantly shorter in MSI-H cases, suggesting more active immune surveillance or interactions between TANs and GC cells in this TAN-rich molecular subtype. Importantly, we found that patients whose tumors had shorter distances between CD66b⁺/CD177⁺ TANs and GC cells showed significantly better OS (P = 0.041), highlighting the importance of not only immune cell density but also spatial relationships within the tumor microenvironment. Notably, in MSI-stratified analyses, the prognostic impact of TAN–tumor spatial proximity appeared more pronounced in MSS GC than in MSI-H GC. This differential pattern may be attributable to the relatively high TAN density and close TAN–tumor proximity already observed in MSI-H tumors, which could limit the ability of spatial localization alone to further discriminate prognosis within this subgroup. In contrast, MSS tumors may exhibit greater variability in TAN–tumor distance, rendering spatial proximity more informative for patient prognosis. Nevertheless, these subgroup analyses are exploratory in nature given the limited sample size, and the independent prognostic value of TAN spatial distribution within MSI-H GC warrants further validation in larger cohorts.

Molecular subtypes were correlated with CD66b + TAN density, and the group with a higher TAN density showed EBV-associated GC more frequently. EBV-associated GC is one of the four subtypes of gastric cancer according to the molecular classification proposed by the Cancer Genome Atlas project (Cancer Genome Atlas Research N 2014) that is caused by EBV (Iizasa et al. 2022). Furthermore, MSI-H GC is likely to show a high density of CD66b + TANs. This molecular subtype of GC occurs predominantly in elderly patients with tubular gastric adenocarcinomas and is characterized by a favorable prognosis (Kwak et al. 2020). However, it has been already known that EBV GC and MSI-H GC provoke immune reaction and are, therefore, associated with high lymphocyte and neutrophil density, and these phenotypes of GC show favorable prognosis than the other (Grogg et al. 2003; Narayanan et al. 2019). We reconfirmed that it was not only TILs but also TANs that showed high density in EBV and MSI-H GC. HER2 status testing revealed that HER2 positivity was higher in the high TAN density group. A previous study showed that HER2 protein overexpression was independently associated with higher OS in GC (Chu et al. 2022) and was significantly related to the Laurén intestinal subtype, which also showed a higher TAN density than the other Laurén subtypes. Furthermore, we investigated relationship between E-cadherin expression and CD66b + TAN density. E-cadherin protein is known as a tumor suppressor and is downregulated in diffuse-type GC (Liu and Chu 2014). Higher TAN density was associated with both intestinal histologic type and membranous expression of E-cadherin, which also supports the reason for the better prognosis of the group with high TAN density. However, the independent influence of HER2 and E-cadherin on TAN density and whether their effects are mediated by the Laurén intestinal subtype remain unclear. Further studies are required to elucidate these relationships. The subgroup analyses performed in this study were exploratory in nature and were not adjusted for multiple comparisons. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted with caution and require validation in independent prospective cohorts.

Our study has some limitations. First, we used TMA slides for analysis, which may not fully represent the spatial heterogeneity of the entire tumor tissue. We attempted to address this limitation by separately analyzing the CT and IM areas. Second, spatial localization was analyzed only in a subset of cases. Future studies using whole-slide spatial analysis and larger subtype-stratified cohorts are needed. In addition, our study was retrospective, which means that we used cohorts consisting of older specimens. Despite these limitations, our study identified a subset of GCs with a high density of TANs, explored their clinicopathologic and molecular features, and suggested that high TAN density may be associated with a favorable prognosis in GC.

In summary, this study assessed TAN infiltration using CD66b IHC and identified its association with the clinicopathologic, molecular, and prognostic features of GC. Especially, we demonstrated prognostic significance of TAN densities in more than 1,000 patients with GC. Furthermore, using mIF, we identified a subset of activated TANs (CD66b⁺/CD177⁺) and demonstrated that a shorter distance between these TANs and tumor cells was significantly associated with improved survival. These findings suggest the potential prognostic relevance of both TAN density and spatial distribution in GC and indicate that TANs, particularly their activated subsets, may play a role in the tumor immune microenvironment.
