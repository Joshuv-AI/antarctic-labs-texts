Influence of pH, ion composition and carbohydrate recognition on the attachment of Cryptosporidium parvum to lamb’s lettuce
Authors: Sophie Kubina, Damien Costa, Isabelle Villena, Loïc Favennec, Stéphanie La Carbona, Romy Razakandrainibe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCryptosporidiosis is a worldwide infection caused by the protozoan parasites Cryptosporidium spp., which are mainly transmitted directly from infected hosts but also indirectly through contaminated food and water. The disease poses significant health impacts, particularly to vulnerable populations such as young children, the elderly, and immunocompromised individuals, including those living with HIV/AIDS. Of the food-borne Cryptosporidium spp. outbreaks reported worldwide, consumption of leafy greens is frequently associated. The different physical and chemical properties of each type of leafy green may affect the parasites’ ability to adhere to plant surfaces. Here, we focus on the attachment of Cryptosporidium parvum oocysts to the leaves of lamb’s lettuce (Valerianella locusta) under different conditions and identify factors that facilitate or inhibit it. We investigated specific interactions through carbohydrate receptors and assessed nonspecific interactions by modulating pH levels and ionic content. The results showed that magnesium ions significantly reduced the attachment of C. parvum oocysts to the leaves of lamb’s lettuce. In contrast, sodium ions and the oocysts’ inherent negative surface charge significantly enhanced attachment. Furthermore, specific carbohydrate interactions involving sialic acid, galactose, mannose, and N-acetyl-glucosamine were essential for attachment. These results highlight the intricate interaction between C. parvum and lamb’s lettuce and suggest that specific molecular bindings and electrostatic forces may facilitate parasite attachment. These privileged interactions could contribute to the durable persistence of oocysts on leaf surfaces, thereby increasing the likelihood of food transmission to humans. Such information is essential for food operators to build hazard analysis in the frame of Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points (HACCP) studies.Keywords: Cryptosporidium parvum, Lamb’s lettuce, Ligand-receptor interaction, Specific and non-specific binding, Food-borne transmission

Introduction

Leafy greens are an essential component of a balanced and healthy diet (Esposito and Giugliano, 2011; Stanaway et al., 2022). More than 20% of food-borne illnesses in the USA caused by pathogens have been traced to leafy greens (Painter et al., 2013). Norovirus, Shiga toxin-producing Escherichia coli (STEC), and Salmonella are known causes of outbreaks associated with leafy greens. There have also been various outbreaks of protozoan infections, including cryptosporidiosis, associated with fresh product (Bujila et al., 2025). The protozoan parasites Cryptosporidium spp., were estimated to be responsible for 8.6 million cases of food-borne illness, 3759 deaths and 47,000 disability-adjusted life years in 2010 (Kirk et al., 2015). Our previous study demonstrated that Cryptosporidium parvum could persist and survive on lamb’s lettuce (Valerianella locusta) leaves throughout growth (under conditions mimicking winter culture). Moreover, the limited effectiveness of the washing process in removing parasites from lamb’s lettuce leaves suggests that C. parvum can persist on lamb’s lettuce (Kubina et al., 2023). However, the interaction involved in the parasite’s attachment to vegetable surfaces remains to be elucidated. Lamb’s lettuce forms dense rosettes that retain moisture, creating microenvironments which may enhance oocyst survival and attachment. These structural characteristics render it a relevant model for studying produce-pathogen interactions.

The oocyst wall is critical in parasite survival, transmission, and infectivity (Dumètre et al., 2012; Freppel et al., 2019). The protective layers provide resistance to environmental stressors such as temperature changes, desiccation, and disinfectants, allowing oocysts to persist in the environment and remain infectious for extended periods (Robertson et al., 1992; Tamburrini and Pozio, 1999; Castro-Hermida et al., 2006; Duhain et al., 2012). The outer wall is a rigid bi-layer made of acid-fast lipids hypothesised to act as a waxy coating that aids environmental survival and protects the oocysts from disinfectants. The inner layer of the oocyst is composed of fibrillar glycoproteins and proteins that appear to be highly crosslinked. This layer contributes to the structural strength and rigidity of the oocyst (Chatterjee et al., 2010; Jenkins et al., 2010; Bushkin et al., 2013). An expanded glycocalyx layer external to the outer bi-layer has also been described on some oocysts, providing immunogenicity and potential attachment possibilities (Dumètre et al., 2012). The leaves of lamb’s lettuce are covered by a cuticle composed mainly of waxes, fatty acids and polysaccharides (Yaron and Römling, 2014). Leaf surfaces are not entirely smooth and often have microscopic irregularities such as trichomes, stomata, or microcracks. These surface features can provide attachment sites for Cryptosporidium oocysts, allowing them to bind more efficiently. It was shown that oocysts can be internalised into the stomata of spinach leaves and can attach to deep natural crevices in the apple exocarp (Macarisin et al., 2010a, 2010b). Oocysts could also be found closely associated with an amorphous extracellular matrix present at the smooth surface of the apple peel. Interaction with the apple’s surface appeared strong, since washing procedures using detergent could not entirely remove oocysts from the surface (Macarisin et al., 2010b).

The surface charge of oocysts has been described as a main factor involved in their adhesion to solid surfaces, dominating hydrophobic effects (Gao and Chorover, 2009). Contrary to monovalent cations (K+), divalent cations (Ca2+, Mg2+) appeared to mediate oocysts’ attachment to different surfaces, including quartz, natural organic matter surfaces, and biofilms (Kuznar and Elimelech, 2005; Janjaroen et al., 2010; Lefebvre et al., 2021). Studies have proposed that the specific binding of calcium ions to the functional groups of oocysts’ surface proteins results in charge neutralisation and conformational changes of surface proteins, which reduce electrostatic repulsion with the quartz substrate. On the other hand, the glycocalyx has been shown to prevent oocysts from adhering to the same substrate (Liu et al., 2010). Opposite impacts of ionic strength on oocysts’ adhesion to surfaces have been described and seem to depend on the electrolyte solutions (Kuznar and Elimelech, 2004; Gao and Chorover, 2009).

Biological processes, such as cell adhesion, involve various specific interactions, including protein-protein, protein-carbohydrate, or carbohydrate-carbohydrate interactions (Khalili and Ahmad, 2015). For Cryptosporidium spp., proteins, glycoproteins, and lectins (carbohydrate-binding proteins specific to different sugar groups) have been described in the attachment of sporozoites to cells and the invasion process (Martínez-Ocaña et al., 2020). However, to our knowledge, specific interactions between the different components of the oocyst wall and other surfaces have not yet been described. This study examines non-specific and specific binding to gain a deeper insight into the interaction between lamb’s lettuce leaves and C. parvum oocysts. Based on previous literature, we focused on: (i) surface charge- and ionic-mediated non-specific interactions; and (ii) carbohydrate-mediated specific interactions.

Materials and methods

Concentration and purification of C. parvum oocysts

Faeces from experimentally infected calves with C. parvum were obtained from the National Institute for Agricultural, Food and Environmental Research (INRAE), Nouzilly, France. Oocysts were purified and concentrated using immunomagnetic separation as previously described and used within three months of receipt (Chalmers et al., 2022). This method enables the production of relatively pure oocysts while preserving the membrane integrity of the oocysts (Supplementary file S1). Purified oocysts were stored at 4 °C in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Gibco™, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Villebon-sur-Yvette, France). Oocysts were counted in triplicate using a counting chamber (KOVA, Thermo Fisher Scientific). At least two batches of C. parvum oocysts were used. Following DNA extraction (Semmani et al., 2023), C. parvum oocysts were molecularly confirmed and characterised by nested PCR targeting the SSU rRNA gene (Koehler et al., 2017) and further genotyped through gp60 gene amplification (Glaberman et al., 2002) and sequencing. Amplicons were purified, sequenced bidirectionally, and analysed using BLAST for species identification and subtyping. All oocysts used throughout the experiments were consistently identified as C. parvum subtype IIaA17G2R1.

Lamb’s lettuce (Valerianella locusta)

Lamb’s lettuce “GALA” is the main cultivated variety in France. Seeds were purchased from a specialist retailer (Hubert, France) and germinated as previously described (Kubina et al., 2023). Lamb’s lettuce plants at the 2-leaf stage were grown in a temperature-controlled chamber simulating winter growing conditions (Kubina et al., 2023) until the 8-leaf stage, the harvest stage in primary production for further consumption (following 2 months of growth).

Preparation of C. parvum oocysts suspension

Purified oocysts (5.104) were pelleted by centrifugation at 3000× g for 30 min (4 °C) and resuspended in 50 μl of test solutions. The test solutions were: (i) PBS solutions prepared at pH ranging from 2 to 10 from a stock solution at pH 7.4 by adding under stirring HCl 6 M or NaOH 2 M (Thermo Fisher Scientific); the pH was controlled by continuous measurement using a H1-2210 pH meter apparatus (Hanna Instruments, Lingolsheim, France); (ii) ion-enriched solutions prepared in UltraPure distilled water (pH = 5.14 ± 0.10; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) by adding 100 mM NaCl (pH = 5.23 ± 0.36), KCl (pH = 5.17 ± 0.21), CaCl2 (pH = 5.93 ± 0.18) or MgCl2 (pH = 8.82 ± 0.37) (Sigma, Saint-Quentin-Fallavier, France); (iii) carbohydrate solutions containing either glucose, galactose, fucose, mannose, N-acetyl-glucosamine (GlcNAc), N-acetyl-galactosamine (GalNAc), or sialic acid (or N-acetyl-neuraminic acid; NANA) (Sigma) at a final concentration of 500 mM in PBS pH 7.4. Following resuspension in a test solution, oocysts were incubated at room temperature for 10 min (pH-PBS and ion-enriched solutions) or 60 min (sugar solutions) before spiking to lamb’s lettuce leaves. If the added carbohydrate is involved in the parasite-leaf interaction, saturation of existing receptors on the surface of Cryptosporidium oocysts may result in reduced or no binding to lamb’s lettuce (Supplementary file S2).

Each condition was tested using at least two distinct oocyst batches (biological replicates). Several oocyst test solutions (up to 6) were independently prepared from these batches, and 3 to 6 leaves were inoculated with these test solutions (technical replicates).

Experimental contamination of lamb’s lettuce

As described previously (Kubina et al., 2021), after incubation in the test solutions (see above), 104 oocysts (10 μl) were inoculated (10 drops of 1 μl) onto lamb’s lettuce leaves just picked from the 8-leaf stage plant (2 × 2 cm at the largest). For each tested solution, separate leaves were inoculated simultaneously (n = 3–6). In parallel, each prepared solution was enumerated (n = 3–6, counting replicates) to determine the mean number of oocysts initially inoculated on the leaf for each experiment. Contaminated leaves were placed under a laminar flow safety cabinet for 2 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C. After incubation, the leaf was washed for 1 min in 5 ml of PBS by vertical rotation (360°; 40 rpm). The supernatant was transferred to another tube, and the leaf was rewashed. The supernatants were pooled and centrifuged at 3000× g (4 °C) for 30 min. The pellet was resuspended in 200 μl PBS for enumeration, representing the unbound oocyst fraction. For each tested condition, three to ten independent experiments (each including inoculation of n leaves with the test condition and n leaves with the control) were performed to consider variabilities due to operator and/or preparation of oocysts suspensions and/or batch oocysts.

Enumeration of C. parvum oocysts and assessment of oocyst attachment on lamb’s lettuce

Five microliters of the oocyst suspension were deposited in slide wells (Teflon printed diagnostic slide, Immuno-cell, Mechelen, Belgium). Oocysts were stained with FITC-labelled anti-Cryptosporidium antibodies according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Crypto Cel FITC; TCS Biosciences, Buckingham, UK). Fluorescent oocysts (FITC+) were counted using an epifluorescence microscope (Diaplan; Leitz, Lenzburg, Switzerland) at a magnification of 400× with a filter system for FITC (excitation: 490 nm; emission: 530 nm). The binding of oocysts was evaluated indirectly by determining the percentage of unbound oocysts for each condition: (Number of FITC+ oocysts in the eluted fraction/Number of oocysts initially inoculated on the leaf) × 100. Mean percentages were calculated from values of all replicates (i.e. including biological and technical replicates).

Identification of carbohydrates on the surface of C. parvum oocysts: Lectin binding analysis and sorting

Table 1 shows the lectins used in this study, their commonly abbreviated names, and the specificity of these lectins for their respective oligosaccharide structure. Lectins were chosen based on their known carbohydrate-binding specificities and their reported or hypothesised roles in parasite-host interactions. For example, Concanavalin A (ConA) was included to detect mannose/glucose residues, previously reported in oocyst walls (Jenkins et al., 2010); Wheat Germ Agglutinin (WGA) was selected for its specificity to GlcNAc, which has been linked to protozoan oocyst wall ageing and modification (Harito et al., 2016); Artocarpus integrifolia agglutinin (AIA) for its high affinity for N-acetylgalactosamine-beta-1,3-galactose (Luo et al., 2011) and Maackia amuresnsis lectin (MAL) was chosen for its recognition of N-acetylneuraminic acid (sialic acid), which is known to be involved in host-parasite adhesion processes (Martínez-Ocaña et al., 2020). After incubation for 30 min in the dark at 37 °C, unbound DAPI was removed by washing the parasites with PBS (centrifugation at 3000× g for 30 min at 4 °C). Fluorescently labelled lectins were then added to the parasite suspension at a concentration of 10 μg/ml, except for AIA lectin, which was used at 1 μg/ml to avoid agglutination of oocysts (glycoMATRIX™, Clinisciences, Nanterre, France), and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Parasites were washed twice to remove unbound lectin, resuspended in PBS, and immediately analysed on an LSR Fortessa™ cytometer (BD Biosciences, Le Pont de Claix, France). Events were gated first based on size (FSC-A) and granularity (SSC-A) to remove particles smaller than a single Cryptosporidium oocyst and large aggregates, and secondly based on granularity and DAPI fluorescence to consider only oocysts with intact nuclei. Once selected, the populations of lectin-labelled oocysts and unlabelled oocysts were analysed for Cyanine-3 (561 nm excitation) or Cyanine-5 (640 nm excitation) fluorescence. Dotplots representing the distribution of events according to size and/or granulometry and/or fluorescence were registered, and corresponding histograms representing the Cy-3 or Cy-5 fluorescence intensity distributions were produced. Flow cytometry data were analysed using FlowJo v10.7 (FlowJo, LLC, Ashland, OR, USA).

Statistical analysis

The percentages of unbound oocysts were compared in all conditions. All data were analysed using XLSTAT statistical and data analysis solution (2023.2.0). The Shapiro-Wilk test was applied to determine if the data were normally distributed. For normal distributions (pH and ion tests), a Levene’s test confirmed homogeneity of variances and, consequently, a Student’s t-test was performed. Data with non-normal distribution (carbohydrate tests) were analysed using the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post-hoc test. A statistical difference was considered when the P-value was < 0.05.

Results

Interaction between C. parvum oocysts and lamb’s lettuce leaves as a function of pH and ion enrichment

To understand the influence of pH on the binding of Cryptosporidium oocysts to lamb’s lettuce, C. parvum oocysts were immersed in several solutions of varying pH. The proportion of unbound oocysts was significantly higher at pH ≤ 3 compared to pH > 3 (Student’s t-test, see Supplementary file S3 for details) (Fig. 1A and Table 2). No statistical difference was observed in the proportion of unbound oocysts between pH 2 and pH 3, and for pH ranging from 4 to 10 (except pH 6 vs pH 7.4 and vs pH 10.0 (Student’s t-test, see Supplementary file S3 for details) (Fig. 1A and Table 2). This suggests that C. parvum oocysts are less likely to attach to leaves at lower pH. The effect of enrichment with monovalent and divalent cations on C. parvum oocyst binding was also investigated. Compared to oocysts not exposed to cation enrichment, Mg2+ significantly increased the percentage of unbound oocysts from lamb’s lettuce (Student’s t-test, alpha = 0.05, df = 22, P = 0.004), whereas Na+ caused a decrease (Student’s t-test, alpha = 0.05, df = 22, P = 0.012) (Table 2 and Fig. 1B). On the other hand, K+ and Ca2+ had no significant impact on oocyst binding, suggesting that the opposite effects observed with Na+ and Mg2+ are due to their characteristics rather than their valence. It is important to note that ionic pH solutions varied within a range that did not show a significant difference in the binding behaviour of oocysts to leaves (5.17–5.93 and 8.82, Fig. 1A).

Discussion

Leafy greens have been identified as a potential transmission route for the protozoan parasites Cryptosporidium spp., which can cause gastrointestinal illness in humans (EFSA Panel on Biological Hazards (BIOHAZ) et al., 2018; Ryan et al., 2018; Zahedi and Ryan, 2020). Studies have shown that various sources, such as irrigation water, animal faeces, and soil, can contaminate leafy greens with Cryptosporidium oocysts (EFSA Panel on NBiological Hazards (BIOHAZ) et al., 2018). This contamination poses a significant public health risk, particularly for individuals with compromised immune systems. Therefore, it is important to understand the factors that contribute to parasites’ contamination of, and attachment to, vegetable matrices. In the phenomenon of microorganisms’ adhesion to inert surfaces, the physicochemical properties of the cells, such as hydrophobicity and surface charge, play important roles. The oocysts’ surface is negatively charged at neutral pH and decreases with pH reduction, approaching neutrality (meaning the net surface charge is zero) at the isoelectric point (pI) of pH 3.3. When modulating the pH of the oocysts’ environment, we observed that C. parvum oocysts interacted more strongly with lamb’s lettuce at pH > 3. These results indicate that the negative charge of the oocyst’s surface facilitates its attachment to the leaves; thus, the surface of the leaves may be positively charged. A decrease in pH has been associated with an increase in the hydrophobicity of oocysts (Hsu and Huang, 2002). Hence, our results suggest that hydrophobicity may be less involved in the oocyst’s attachment to positive leaves than the oocyst’s surface charge. The influence of surface charge on hydrophobicity has already been described for oocysts adhering to positively charged hydrophilic beads (Dai et al., 2004).

Ions may also influence the attachment of Cryptosporidium oocysts to surfaces (Lefebvre et al., 2021). Indeed, Ca2+ ions have been shown to facilitate parasite attachment to quartz (Kuznar and Elimelech, 2005), a negatively charged surface, which increased by 60% and 100% in the presence of Mg2+ and Ca2+ ions, respectively (Luo et al., 2017). They proposed that divalent cations could form bridges between the quartz and negatively charged oocyst surfaces. In the presence of K+ and Ca2+ ions at the same concentration, no discernible difference in attachment was observed in our study. On the other hand, oocyst binding with leaves was favoured and reduced by Na+ and Mg2+ ions, respectively. As previously suggested, if the surface of lamb’s lettuce leaves is positively charged, the ions may act differently than as proposed for the quartz surface. One can hypothesise that Mg2+ ions add positive charges and neutralise some negative charges on the surface of C. parvum oocysts. This change in surface charge could reduce the parasite’s attraction to leaf surfaces due to electrostatic repulsion. However, no change was observed in the presence of Ca2+ ions, suggesting that interaction depends on ion characteristics and charge. Ion specificity depends on ion size and electron layer structures, making ions interact with soil particle surfaces differently (Roux, 2010; Li et al., 2023; Luo et al., 2017). The magnesium ion (1.72 Å) is smaller than calcium (2.23 Å), so the binding sites differ. This feature may also change protein conformation (Luo et al., 2017), explaining the difference in oocysts-lamb’s lettuce interactions in the presence of both divalent cations. Similarly, for monovalent cations (Na+ and K+), specific recognition sites for Na+ but not for K+ (and vice versa) could alter the oocysts-leaf interaction (Roux, 2010). Taken together, we have shown that electrolyte composition influences the attachment of C. parvum to lamb’s lettuce and, depending on ion specificity, could favour (or not) parasite binding. A perspective of this work could be to evaluate the impact of ionic strength on the effect observed with the tested ions.

Proteins and carbohydrates can influence an organism’s ability to interact with surfaces (Pérez and Tvaroška, 2014; Gagneux et al., 2022). Some research suggests that carbohydrates may facilitate the attachment of Cryptosporidium sporozoites to human epithelial cells, confirming the importance of these molecules in cellular interactions (Bhat et al., 2007). Due to the richness of the oocyst wall in carbohydrates, we questioned their role in the interaction of oocysts with leaf surfaces. Our study showed that galactose, mannose, GlcNAc, and NANA reduced the binding of oocysts to lamb’s lettuce leaves. At the same time, fucose and GalNAc appeared to play no role. Using lectins, glycoproteins that can specifically and reversibly bind to specific carbohydrates, we could detect mannose and/or glucose at the surface of the oocyst wall, but neither galactose, nor GlcNAc, nor NANA. Our results confirm the previous analysis by Jenkins et al. (2010), which showed that GlcNAc and NANA were absent from the oocyst wall. This suggests that the reducing effect on oocyst binding due to GlcNAc and NANA was probably indirect, mediated by receptors for these sugars present on the surface of oocysts or leaves. On the other hand, previous GC-MS analysis indicated that galactose and mannose are present in the oocyst wall (Jenkins et al., 2010). Still, only the mannose and/or glucose could be detected in our flow cytometry analyses. The ConA lectin employed in this study can recognise both mannose and glucose; however, to specifically confirm the presence of mannose in the oocysts, another mannose-specific lectin, such as Galanthus nivalis (GNL) lectin, has to be tested. We note that Cy-5, although selected to ensure distinct spectral separation from DAPI, is more susceptible to quenching at high labelling densities, which may have contributed to the weaker signals observed with Cy-5-conjugated lectins. The observed discrepancy regarding galactose may stem from the fact that GC-MS can identify components throughout the entire oocyst wall, but does not clarify their localisation in the inner or outer layers. In contrast, flow cytometry is limited to detecting only those carbohydrates accessible on the oocyst surface under analysis conditions. Consistent with this, the WGA (wheat germ agglutinin) lectin targeting GlcNAc has been shown to bind to the wall of aged T. gondii oocysts but not fresh ones, except if they are previously treated with acidified pepsin (Harito et al., 2016). Hence, we cannot exclude that carbohydrates are differentially exposed at the oocyst’s surface due to the age difference between the used batches (0–3 months). This may explain the intra-batch variations observed in our carbohydrate-enrichment study and the lack of lectin binding in the cytometry assay. Future studies should include enzymatic controls, such as sialidase treatment, to further confirm the specificity of MAL-binding to sialic acid residues on oocyst surfaces.

The vegetable leaves can also exhibit varying carbohydrate content depending on their age, which can influence the attachment of microorganisms. Research by Esseili et al. (2012) showed that Norovirus attached significantly more to older lettuce leaves, by a factor of 1.5–2, than younger ones, highlighting the vulnerability of older leaves and the need for careful handling. In our study, we focused on the 8-leaf stage to simulate the scenario where leaf contamination occurs just before harvesting and consumption. This stage contains old and new leaves. Hence, it would be interesting to perform similar assays with younger leaves (2-leaf stage) to evaluate whether oocysts can attach similarly to those at the 8-leaf stage and if the attachment contributing factors vary. Evaluating contamination at an earlier stage of plant growth would also help to determine if oocysts can durably interact with leaves during primary plant growth. This study investigated oocyst manipulation to characterise the physicochemical parameters underlying adhesion. Although such manipulations are not directly applicable to agricultural practice, they provide a useful framework for understanding oocyst-leaf interactions and for assessing the potential for stable adhesion. Should such adhesion occur, the associated risk of exposure increases, highlighting the importance of control measures. In practice, these measures are most realistically applied downstream in the production chain, once contamination has occurred, and are therefore particularly relevant within the HACCP framework. While direct modification of leaf surfaces (e.g. through ions, carbohydrates, or pH) is not a feasible intervention, further studies could examine how natural variations in leaf surface properties and agronomic conditions influence adhesion and persistence. Such insights would strengthen risk assessment and contribute to a deeper understanding of the factors influencing oocyst adhesion and persistence under field conditions.

A comparative multi-omics analysis of C. parvum oocysts attached to leafy green surfaces versus those remaining unbound could provide critical insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying oocyst adhesion to plant tissues. Integrating transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic approaches may help identify key surface proteins, glycan-binding domains, and metabolic adaptations that facilitate attachment and persistence on produce. Proteomic studies have identified a C. parvum mucin-like glycoprotein, CpClec, containing a C-type lectin domain, which has been characterised and is implicated in host-parasite interactions (Bhalchandra et al., 2013), suggesting a role in adhesion processes. Such investigations represent a promising direction for future research, with potential implications for enhancing food safety and developing targeted strategies to mitigate the transmission of Cryptosporidium spp. through produce.

Conclusions

This study shows that the interaction between lamb’s lettuce leaves and C. parvum oocysts involves both non-specific (ionic and surface charge interactions) and specific (binding to galactose, mannose, GlcNAc, and NANA) mechanisms. Because of the low infectious dose and the strong and long-lasting binding of oocysts to leafy vegetables, these interactions may increase the likelihood of human exposure. Control strategies should focus on agricultural practices that can reduce surface contamination in the field and an effective washing process to remove contamination and prevent parasite contamination of leafy greens.
