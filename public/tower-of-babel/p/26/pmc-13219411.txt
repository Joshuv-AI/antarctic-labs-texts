Multimodal neuroimaging of brain microstructure and hippocampal morphology alterations in type 2 diabetes mellitus
Authors: Mengyuan Li, Lin Yao, Zhen Zhong, Haipeng Huang, Yanglan Li, Haizhu Zheng, Hongfeng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractType 2 diabetes affects the central nervous system, yet the role of glymphatic dysfunction remains unclear. This study investigated alterations in the DTI-ALPS index in T2DM patients and its associations with cognitive function, white matter integrity, and hippocampal morphology. 74 T2DM patients and 32 healthy controls underwent brain T1-weighted and diffusion tensor imaging. The DTI-ALPS index, white matter microstructure, and hippocampal subregion volumes were quantified. Correlation and mediation analyses assessed relationships between the DTI-ALPS index and cognitive assessments, clinical indicators, white matter microstructures, and hippocampus subregions. T2DM patients exhibited reduced DTI-ALPS index and fractional anisotropy, increased axial diffusivity, and decreased global network efficiency. Furthermore, both betweenness centrality and degree centrality in the right superior frontal gyrus were significantly reduced. Hippocampal subfield volumes were significantly reduced. The DTI-ALPS index correlated positively with HDL and MMSE scores, but negatively with fasting blood glucose (FBG). Mediation analysis showed that the DTI-ALPS index partially mediated the relationship between fasting glucose and cognition. T2DM is linked to glymphatic dysfunction, white matter damage, and hippocampal atrophy, contributing to cognitive decline. The DTI-ALPS index may serve as a sensitive biomarker for early glymphatic impairment, emphasizing the role of metabolic clearance dysfunction in diabetes-related cognitive deterioration. Introduction

The global increase in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is multifactorial, reflecting demographic shifts, environmental determinants, and complex socioeconomic drivers that shape dietary patterns and lifestyle risks1. According to the 11th edition of the IDF Diabetes Atlas released by the International Diabetes Federation (IDF), the total number of 589 million adults aged 20 to 79 with diabetes worldwide were living with diabetes in 2024. Among them, about 252 million people have not yet realized that they have diabetes. China currently has the largest number of people with diabetes in the world and the second-highest total diabetes-related health expenditures globally. In 2024, around 148 million adults in China would have diabetes, with projections indicating an increase to 168.3 million by 2050, of which over 95% will have type 2 diabetes (T2DM)2. T2DM is a prevalent, chronic, and progressive metabolic disorder characterized by persistent hyperglycemia resulting from insulin resistance and impaired insulin secretion3. It is associated with a wide range of complications, including microvascular and macrovascular lesions, diabetic neuropathy, nephropathy, retinopathy, cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases4,5. Recently, there is increasing evidence that T2DM is not only a systemic metabolic disorder but also affects the function of the central nervous system, which is mainly manifested by abnormalities in brain structure and function6. Epidemiological studies indicate that individuals with T2DM exhibit an extremely high risk of dementia7, implying that diabetes-associated neurodegenerative alterations may manifest earlier than the onset of clinical cognitive symptoms. Consequently, it is of extreme significance to investigate the mechanism of T2DM’s impact on brain structure and function from the perspective of neuroimaging to facilitate the early detection and intervention of diabetes-associated brain dysfunction.

Currently, numerous studies indicate that patients with diabetes exhibit brain structural abnormalities, including high signals in white matter, increased volumes of white matter lesions, and lacunar cerebral infarctions, all of which suggest that diabetes may lead to changes in brain macrostructure8. In addition, the abnormality of white matter microstructure has been regarded as an important potential marker of diabetes-associated neuropathy9. Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), as a noninvasive imaging technique for evaluating the integrity of white matter microstructure, is widely used in the study of various neurodegenerative diseases. Tract-Based Spatial Statistics (TBSS), which performs statistical analysis on skeletonized white matter tracts, is capable of detecting changes in diffuse features with high sensitivity across major white matter pathways. Specifically, TBSS is highly sensitive in this context because it overcomes the limitations of conventional voxel-based analysis, such as alignment inaccuracies and partial volume effects, by projecting diffusion data onto a common white matter skeleton10. It is particularly suitable for detecting microstructural abnormalities triggered by potential damage to nerve conduction pathways. Previous studies have found that patients with T2DM have abnormalities in several key white matter conduction pathways, suggesting possible myelin degeneration or axonal damage11–13. These microstructural alterations are thought to be closely related to poor glycaemic control and may be involved in the mechanism of diabetes-associated cognitive decline14. The above studies have focused on microscopic abnormalities in static structures, but brain damage is not limited to localized structural disruption. The metabolic disorders and inflammatory responses triggered by diabetes may also interfere with the normal functioning of the fluid metabolism system in the brain, thereby accelerating the onset and progression of neurodegenerative diseases.

Notably, recent studies have discovered that the glymphatic system plays a crucial role in the clearance of waste products from the brain. The glymphatic system, also known as the glial-lymphatic pathway, is a fluid-based transport mechanism that plays a crucial role in removing waste products from the brain15. Moreover, the glymphatic system plays a vital role in determining brain health, particularly in individuals with T2DM, as it is primarily characterized by impaired clearance of metabolic waste from the brain, which has been closely associated with cognitive decline16,17. Therefore, investigating the glymphatic system offers a new avenue for exploring neurobiological changes associated with T2DM. To assess glymphatic system function in the human brain, prior studies have predominantly relied on invasive imaging techniques, such as intrathecal tracer injection followed by contrast-enhanced signal analysis18. However, the invasive nature of these methods presents significant limitations for application in human research. Recently Taoka et al.19 introduced the DTI-ALPS method to assess glymphatic system activity by measuring directional water diffusion in the perivascular regions surrounding deep medullary veins. Since its inception, this imaging method has gained popularity in research on neurological disorders20,21. Due to its non-invasive nature, the DTI-ALPS method has become widely recognized as an effective approach for assessing glymphatic function and is increasingly used in clinical neuroimaging research, although it has not yet been adopted in routine clinical practice22.

Previous studies have linked reduced glymphatic function to microstructural changes in both gray and white matter23. Normal brain function depends not only on the individual brain regions themselves but also on the effective synergy and information transfer of the neural networks between the brain regions, commonly referred to as brain connectivity. Graph theory-based structural brain network analysis methods have been widely used in neurological disease research, which can construct a network of connectivity relationships between brain regions at the macroscopic level and reveal systematic changes in brain connectivity patterns by quantifying network topological parameters such as global efficiency, small-world properties, and node centrality. The human brain can be defined as a complex network24, in which individual brain regions can be defined as nodes and the connections of white matter fibres between brain regions can be defined as edges. Disruption of structural connectivity in the human brain is the neuropathologic basis for a wide range of diseases25. While prior research has established that T2DM is associated with disrupted structural network topology and reduced communication efficiency26, our understanding of how the glymphatic system affects structural connectivity between different brain regions remains limited. Consequently, we hypothesize that alterations in glymphatic system function may be intricately linked to the structural network integrity, potentially offering a new perspective for elucidating the interaction between the two.

Based on the background of the above literature, we explored the relationship between blood glucose, the glymphatic system, white matter microstructure, brain networks, and hippocampal subregions in T2DM patients. In this study, we propose the following hypotheses: (1) T2DM patients exhibit lower fiber bundle integrity and structural network connectivity efficiency; (2)The DTI-ALPS index was significantly reduced in patients with T2DM, indicating impaired glymphatic clearance function in the brain; (3) Hyperglycemia may accelerate cognitive decline in patients with T2DM by reducing the efficiency of glymphatic clearance in the brain. To test these hypotheses, we conducted a systematic exploratory assessment of T2DM patients and healthy controls with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans and simultaneous collection of clinical indicators such as blood glucose, cognitive function, and depression and anxiety. We first analysed the associations between the DTI-ALPS index and clinical indicators, white matter microstructures, and hippocampal subregions. Furthermore, we investigated the potential mediating roles of the ALPS index in the relationship between hyperglycemia and cognitive function, aiming to elucidate the potential mechanistic link between glymphatic dysfunction and cognitive impairment in T2DM.

Materials and methods

Participants

This was a case-control study that included 106 subjects, including 32 HC and 74 patients with T2DM. The study protocol was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2013) and approved by the Ethical Review Committee of Guang’anmen Hospital China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences (Ethical Application Ref: 2019-070-KY-01). All patients signed an informed consent form and volunteered information to participate in this study.

The T2DM inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) In accordance with the diagnostic and classification criteria for diabetes proposed by the American Diabetes Association (ADA) in 201427, with a disease duration of ≥ 2 years; (2) Age 40–75 years old, right-handed; (3) No contraindications to MRI scanning.

Exclusion criteria for the study were as follows: (1) Substantial parenchymal brain lesions (tumours, infarcts, or abnormalities, etc.) detected on MRI; (2) In the past three months, experienced an acute complication of diabetes mellitus, such as diabetic ketoacidosis, or encountered a severe medical emergency, such as moderate to severe anaemia, cardiac insufficiency, liver failure, or renal failure; (3) The history of various other central nervous system diseases includes conditions such as schizophrenia, Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease, and epilepsy; (4) The history of alcohol, tobacco, and drug abuse or dependence; (5) Contraindications to MRI, such as claustrophobia. These criteria were applied to exclude individuals with confounding neurological complexity, ensuring that the observed microstructural and cognitive alterations could be primarily attributed to T2DM.

The healthy control (HC) inclusion criteria were as follows: After MRI scan, no obvious neurologic disease lesions, no organic lesions that can affect the functional activity of the brain; No history of alcohol or drug abuse; age, gender, and education matched to T2DM patients.

Clinical indicator collection

We are collecting demographic information and clinical indicators on all subjects who met the inclusion criteria, including age, sex, education level, fasting plasma glucose (FBG), Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), glycated albumin (GA), triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL) and very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL).

Neuropsychological and behavioral assessment

Cognitive function was assessed using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores. The Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) and the Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS) were used to assess the participants’ psychological status regarding anxiety and depression.

MRI acquisition

MRI scans were performed using a 3.0 Tesla high-field MRI scanner (MAGNETOM Trio, Siemens, Germany). Before scanning, participants were instructed to follow standard preparation procedures and were informed of relevant precautions in accordance with technical operating guidelines. Subjects were instructed to lie flat on their backs, close their eyes, remain awake, and try to correct their posture and keep their heads still while collecting data. 3D T1-weighted structural images (T1WI) were acquired using a magnetization-prepared rapid gradient echo (MPRAGE) sequence. The acquisition parameters were as follows: repetition time (TR) = 2200 ms, echo time (TE) = 1.54 ms, flip angle = 7°, slice thickness = 1 mm, field of view (FOV) = 256 × 256 mm² and matrix = 256 × 256. Multi-directional diffusion information was obtained using 61 non-collinear gradient directions (b = 1000 s/mm2) alongside two b = 0 s/mm2 acquisitions in the diffusion-weighted imaging sequence. The acquisition parameters were set as follows: TR = 5000ms, TE = 105ms, partial fourier = 0.875, slice thickness = 2 mm, FOV = 220 × 220mm3, matrix = 128 × 128, flip angle = 90°。.

TI image preprocessing

T1-weighted images were processed using the automated “recon-all” pipeline provided in FreeSurfer v7.4.1 (https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/). The pipeline included several essential preprocessing steps: motion correction, removal of non-brain tissues (skull stripping), intensity normalization, and non-linear spatial normalization to Talairach space. Subsequently, cortical and subcortical structures were segmented, and surface reconstruction was performed by generating a triangular tessellation at the gray-white matter boundary. Automated topology correction and surface deformation were applied to ensure accurate cortical surface representation.

Hippocampal subfields were derived using the automated segmentation pipeline embedded in FreeSurfer v7.4.1, as illustrated in Fig. 1. This module segmented a comprehensive set of left and right hippocampal subfields, including the hippocampal head, body, and tail; cornu ammonis regions (CA1, CA3, and CA4); the head and body of the subiculum; presubiculum; the granule cell layer of the dentate gyrus (GC-ML-DG); the molecular layer of the hippocampus (HP); as well as the parasubiculum, hippocampal fissure, fimbria, and the hippocampus-amygdala transition area (HATA). All hippocampal subfield segmentations were visually inspected to ensure anatomical accuracy and the absence of distortions or mislabeling.

DTI data preprocessing

DTI data preprocessing was performed using the FSL V6.0.7.17 (FMRIB Software Library, http://www.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl) and MRtrix3 V3.0.5 (https://www.mrtrix.org/). The raw DTI data were first converted from DICOM to NIfTI format, a layer-by-layer quality control to ensure the completeness and integrity of image acquisition. Subsequently, the quality-checked NIfTI data were converted to the MRtrix3 image format (.mif). Then, Marchenko-Pastur Principal Component (MP-PCA) denoising and Gibbs unringing were applied to DTI data to correct for artifacts. Eddy currents and motion corrected, using “dwifslpreproc” tool from MRtrix3, which aligns all diffusion volumes to the b = 0 image and updates the corresponding gradient table accordingly. A threshold of 0.2 was applied in BET (FSL) to exclude non-brain tissues. Following bias field correction, the accuracy of skull stripping and registration precision were manually verified for all data.

Tract-based spatial statistics pipeline

In this study, TBSS was applied for preprocessing of the DTI data. (1) All participants’ fractional anisotropy (FA) images were nonlinearly aligned to the FMRIB58_FA_1mm in Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space template using FSL’s nonlinear registration tool. (2) A mean FA image was generated and thinned to create a white matter skeleton representing the core of major fiber tracts. (3) FA images were projected onto the mean FA skeleton to reduce residual misalignment, and a threshold of FA > 0.2 was applied to focus the analysis on major white matter tracts. (4) Mean diffusivity (MD), axial diffusivity (AD), and radial diffusivity (RD) data were analyzed using TBSS implemented in FSL. (5) Mean diffusion values (FA, MD, AD, RD) were extracted based on the JHU white matter atlas for group-level comparisons.

DTI-ALPS pipeline

DTI-ALPS processing was performed using the FSL software library (FMRIB Software Library, v6.0) based on the methodology described by Taoka et al.28. ALPS was calculated using the preprocessed DTI data. The corrected b = 0 and 1000 s/mm2 DTI shell data were processed with a semi-automated pipeline to derive the DTI-ALPS index. The FA maps of all participants were nonlinearly registered to the JHU-ICBM-FA template within MNI space using the FMRIB’s Non-linear Image Registration Tool (FNIRT). All diffusivity maps were spatially normalized by applying the calculated transformation matrices using “applywarp” command in FSL. For each participant, four 5-mm diameter spherical ROIs were automatically positioned bilaterally in regions corresponding to the projection fibers (superior corona radiata) and association fibers (superior longitudinal fasciculus), centered on predefined coordinates corresponding to the projection (Dxxproj, Dyyproj, Dzzproj) and association (Dxxassoc, Dyyassoc, Dzzassoc) fiber tracts adjacent to the lateral ventricular bodies, as defined within the FA template space. Following the automated placement, all ROIs were visually inspected by an experienced rater to verify their anatomical accuracy, and manual adjustments were performed if the ROIs overlapped with the ventricles or gray matter. ALPS index was calculated independently for each hemisphere, and the average bilateral values were used in the primary analysis20. It should be noted that the DTI-ALPS index, while widely used as a surrogate marker of glymphatic function, represents a composite measure that may reflect both perivascular fluid dynamics and white matter microstructural asymmetry. Topological analysis of brain structure networks

Structural brain networks were constructed for each participant based on the individual FA skeletons derived from the preprocessed DTI data. The brain was parcellated into 90 regions using the Automated Anatomical Labeling (AAL) atlas, with each region defined as a node in the structural connectivity network. Whole-brain white matter tractography was performed using the fiber assignment by continuous tracking (FACT) algorithm29. Fiber tracking was initiated for each participant and continued until either the FA value fell below a threshold of 0.2, or the tracking angle exceeded 45°. The weighted structural connectivity matrix (90 × 90) was constructed by counting the number of streamlines connecting each pair of brain regions. To ensure network reliability, only connections observed in over 80% of subjects were included30. Two regions are considered structurally connected if they have at least three fibres. When the fibres are greater than or equal to 3, the value of the fibre number matrix is set to 1; otherwise, it is set to 0. The weighting matrix of the structural network is then generated by multiplying the binary fiber number (FN) matrix by the FA matrix. Binary graph theory-based topological property analysis of global and local networks in GRETNA Toolbox V2.131. Global network parameters include global efficiency (Eglob), which evaluates the effectiveness of parallel information transfer within the network. It is defined as the inverse of the harmonic mean of the shortest path lengths connecting brain regions in the network. Local efficiency (Eloc), which exhibits the fault tolerance of the network, is averaged by summing the global efficiencies corresponding to the neighboring sub-networks of all nodes in the network. The shortest path length (Lp), which is used to indicate the efficiency of information transfer in a network, is the average distance between the shortest path lengths of any two nodes present in the network. Small-world parameters (Sigma), which are between regular networks and random networks, have high clustering coefficient and short feature path length. Local node parameters include Betweenness centrality (Bc), which reflects the status of a node in the network, is the ratio of the number of shortest paths to the total number of paths in the network at that point in the path. Degree centrality (Dc), which is used to describe the degree of importance of a node in a functional network. Clustering coefficient (Cp), reflecting the information about the local connectivity of the network, is a quantitative revelation of the degree of local connectivity of the network, as well as the degree of clustering and separation of the network. Nodal efficiency (Ne) measures the efficiency of information transfer between a node and all other nodes, responding to the node’s global integration capability. Nodal local efficiency (NLe), which quantifies the efficiency of communication between a node and neighboring nodes when a node is removed, can be used to reflect the average efficiency of information transfer within the functional areas of the brain. The length of the node feature path (NLp), represents the average of the shortest path lengths between a node and all other nodes. Fractional anisotropy (FA), mean diffusivity (MD), axial diffusivity (AD), and radial diffusivity (RD) maps were derived from diffusion-weighted images using the ordinary least squares fitting method implemented in the DTIFIT tool of the FMRIB Software Library (FSL). The bilateral hippocampal subfield masks were generated in T1-weighted space using FreeSurfer.

Statistical analyses

Demographic and clinical data analysis

IBM SPSS 26.0 (https://www.ibm.com/products/spss-statistics), GraphPad Prism 8.0 (https://www.graphpad.com) and MATLAB 2022b software (https://www.mathworks.com) were used to conduct all statistical analyses. Continuous variables are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables are represented as frequencies and percentages. Independent two-sample t-test (for normally distributed variables) or Mann-Whitney U tests (for non-normally distributed variables) were used to compare demographic, clinical indicators, and neuropsychological test between the HC and T2DM groups. Chi-squared tests were used to analyze categorical variables. A p-value < 0.05 (two-tailed) was considered statistically significant for these comparisons.

Neuroimaging data analysis

A mixed-model analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed to examine the overall effect of T2DM on hippocampus subfield volumes. The disease status (T2DM vs. HC) served as the between-subjects factor, whereas the cerebral hemisphere (left vs. right) was regarded as the within-subjects factor. Age, sex, years of education, and estimated total intracranial volume (eTIV) were included as covariates.

For TBSS, group differences were assessed using permutation testing. To control for multiple comparisons, we applied Threshold-Free Cluster Enhancement (TFCE), which enhances the sensitivity of detecting cluster-wise differences while maintaining strict control over the family-wise error (FWE) rate at a significance threshold of p < 0.05. Multiple comparisons for local topological metrics and for measures evaluated across global sparsity thresholds were corrected using the false discovery rate (FDR) method. To specifically address the potential confounding influence of white matter microstructural integrity on glymphatic measures, the mean FA values of the projection and association fibers were included as additional covariates in group comparisons and correlation analyses involving the ALPS index.

To improve the adherence of MRI image data to a normal distribution, a Fisher Z-transformation was performed. Normally distributed data were analyzed using Pearson’s correlation, while Spearman’s correlation was employed for non-normally distributed or categorical data. Linear regression analysis was performed during group comparisons to statistically adjust for the influence of age and gender as covariates. After regressing out the effects of covariates, partial correlation analyses were performed to explore the association between brain imaging indices and neuropsychological assessments. Mediation analyses were conducted using the PROCESS in SPSS (pre-set Model No.4) to determine whether white matter microstructure and glymphatic functioning served as mediator in the association between blood glucose levels and cognitive function. Age, gender and years of education were included as covariates in the statistical models. In all statistical analyses, a p-value < 0.05 (two tailed) was considered statistically significant.

Demographic and clinical data

The demographic data, clinical characteristic, and neuropsychological assessment results are shown in Table 1. A total of 106 subjects participated in this study, comprising 74 patients with T2DM (28 males; mean age 57.89 ± 7.57 years, range 42–68 years) and 32 age- and gender-matched HC (8 males; mean age 55.50 ± 6.52 years, range 40–75 years). There were no significant differences in baseline demographic characteristics (age, sex, years of education) between the two trial groups, indicating comparability. The abnormal clinical indicator values (FBG, HbA1c, GA, TG, LDL, HDL, and VLDL) observed in T2DM patients (p < 0.05), compared to the HC group, were largely attributable to the direct effects of the disease, no difference were found in TC. In terms of neuropsychological assessment scores, although both groups performed within the normal range on the MMSE, SDS, and SAS scales, statistical analysis revealed significant differences between the T2DM and HC groups (p < 0.01). Specifically, patients with T2DM demonstrated lower cognitive performance, along with elevated levels of anxiety and depression, compared to the HC group.

Mediation effect of DTI-ALPS index on the association between FBG and cognitive performance

To examine whether the effect of FBG on cognitive performance is mediated by white matter structural integrity and glymphatic function, we constructed a serial mediation model following the correlation analyses. We hypothesized that the effect of FBG on cognitive performance is mediated serially, with FBG impacting white matter integrity, which in turn affects glymphatic function, ultimately influencing cognitive performance. This is based on the theory that impaired white matter integrity can hinder the efficient clearance of metabolic waste products via the glymphatic system. In this model, DTI-ALPS (mean-ALPS) index was included as potential mediators, with MMSE as the dependent variable and FBG as the independent variable. The results showed a significant total effect of FBG on MMSE (β = −0.472, SE = 0.103, p < 0.001, bootstrapped 95% confidence interval (CI) = −0.678 – −0.266), indicating that higher blood glucose levels were associated with poorer cognitive performance.

After including the DTI-ALPS index as a mediator, the direct effect of FBG on MMSE remained significant (β = −0.389, SE = 0.105, p < 0.001, bootstrapped 95% CI = −0.599 – −0.178). The indirect effect analysis revealed that the DTI-ALPS index significantly mediated the relationship between FBG and MMSE (indirect effect = − 0.082, SE = 0.049, bootstrapped 95% CI = − 0.197 − 0.005). This indirect pathway accounted for approximately 17.40% of the total effect. As shown in Fig. 10, these findings suggest that the impairment of glymphatic clearance (as indexed by DTI-ALPS) is a crucial independent mediator through which hyperglycemia contributes to cognitive decline.

Discussion

In this study, we systematically investigated the associations between the DTI-ALPS, a local measure of glymphatic activity, and clinical indicators, abnormal brain morphology and brain network in patients with T2DM. Firstly, building on our proposed hypothesis, our findings revealed that T2DM patients exhibited significantly reduced DTI-ALPS indices, widespread white matter alterations in TBSS, disrupted structural network efficiency, and atrophy in specific hippocampal subregions. Moreover, the DTI-ALPS indices were associated with global white matter microstructural measures, including FA and AD. Notably, both FA and AD have been shown to be altered in chronic cerebrovascular diseases, even within normal-appearing white matter32. Meanwhile, cognitive performance was also associated with decreased DTI-ALPS indices. These findings suggest that glymphatic dysfunction, as reflected by ALPS, may be closely linked to microstructural degeneration and cognitive decline.

Iliff et al. first proposed the concept of the glymphatic system in 2012. It is composed of aquaporin-4 (AQP4) water channels located on the endfeet of astrocytes and the perivascular space (PVS). This system participates in the clearance of metabolic waste within the brain by facilitating the exchange and flow between cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and interstitial fluid (ISF). This process includes the transportation and distribution of amyloid-beta (Aβ), glucose, lipids, amino acids, and neuromodulators, thereby maintaining brain tissue homeostasis and neuroimmune regulatory functions33,34. Existing research indicates that dysfunction of the glymphatic system is closely linked to brain aging and various neurodegenerative diseases35. Notably, metabolic disorders, particularly T2DM, may impair glymphatic system function through multiple mechanisms. A relevant study have used dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI to observe CSF circulation in the hippocampus of T2DM model rats in order to assess the function of the glymphatic system in the brain. The results showed that the clearance rate of the contrast agent in the brains of T2DM rats was significantly reduced36. Additionally, the experiments using fluorescent tracer injections in the cerebellum showed a significant decrease in tracer removal in the hippocampus of T2DM rats, suggesting that the glial glymphatic system in the brain has been significantly impaired in T2DM36. Although intrathecal injection of gadolinium-based contrast agents is considered a classic method for assessing glymphatic system pathways, the invasive and cumbersome nature of the procedure has restricted its widespread clinical use37. Recently, researchers have developed various non-invasive imaging techniques, providing safer and more convenient means for evaluating the function of the glymphatic system. The DTI-ALPS index, which measures diffusivity along projection and association fibers near the body of the lateral ventricle and perpendicular to the PVS, provides a non-invasive method for evaluating glymphatic system function.

Glymphatic system function and cognitive performance

A higher DTI-ALPS index reflects enhanced diffusivity along the perivascular space, suggesting improved glymphatic system function. In this study, we quantitatively assessed the function of the glymphatic system in patients with T2DM using the DTI-ALPS method. Our findings revealed that the DTI-ALPS indices in the T2DM group were significantly lower than those in the HC group, suggesting a potential impairment in the clearance function of the glymphatic system. This finding is consistent with the findings of previous studies on glymphatic dysfunction in patients with T2DM38. The glymphatic system plays a crucial role in maintaining homeostasis within the brain’s internal environment under conditions of high metabolic demand. Dysfunction of this system may lead to the abnormal accumulation of metabolic waste in the brain and trigger chronic neuroinflammatory responses, ultimately damaging neuronal structures and functions39. While the specific pathological mechanisms are not yet fully understood, increasing evidence suggests that metabolic disturbances under hyperglycemic conditions may disrupt glymphatic clearance pathways. This disruption can lead to the accelerated accumulation of harmful substances in the brain, potentially contributing to cognitive decline and increasing the risk of neurodegenerative diseases40.

Further analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between the DTI-ALPS index and the MMSE scores of patients in the T2DM group. This suggests that a decline in glymphatic system function may be closely related to cognitive dysfunction. Notably, regarding the emotional assessments, although the T2DM group exhibited statistically higher SAS and SDS scores compared to controls, the mean values for both groups remained well within the normal clinical range. This suggests that the cognitive impairments and glymphatic alterations observed in our study were unlikely to be driven by clinically significant depression or anxiety, further strengthening the link between glymphatic changes and cognitive performance in T2DM. This study also revealed a substantial correlation between the DTI-ALPS score and several markers of glucose and lipid metabolism in T2DM patients. It was significantly inversely linked with FBG and HDL, indicating that the glymphatic system’s function may be directly affected by a hyperglycemic condition. Previous studies have shown that chronic hyperglycemia can lead to increased blood-brain barrier permeability, astrocyte dysfunction, and loss of AQP4 polarization. These alterations may impair the exchange efficiency between CSF and interstitial fluid, thereby compromising the clearance function of the glymphatic system41,42. HDL is a biomarker recognized for its anti-inflammatory properties and associated health benefits. However, some studies suggest that metabolic disorders may alter the composition and function of HDL, impairing its protective effects and potentially leading to harmful consequences. These findings indicate that dysregulated glucose metabolism is a core feature of the systemic pathology of T2DM and may also contribute to the development of T2DM-related neurocognitive impairment by affecting glymphatic system function.

Glymphatic system function and structural brain network

DTI can be used to detect microstructural changes in white matter, which have been shown to be associated with alterations in the glymphatic system. These microstructural changes in white matter are associated with the onset and recurrence of T2DM. FA and MD can reflect the integrity of white matter fibers. FA is an important indicator for evaluating axonal integrity and is highly sensitive to changes in the microstructure of fiber bundles. MD is sensitive to cellular structure, edema, and necrosis, and an increased MD also indicates a decline in fiber tract integrity. Cognitive impairment in patients with T2DM has been linked to disruptions in the brain’s white matter structure14. In our study, patients with T2DM exhibited increased AD, particularly in the bilateral anterior thalamic radiation and corticospinal tract, as well as decreased FA, suggesting widespread white matter structural damage. These affected regions are closely associated with high-level cognitive functions, including executive function, attention, and working memory. Furthermore, we found a positive correlation between FA and DTI-ALPS indices, as well as a negative correlation between AD and DTI-ALPS. These findings suggest the presence of white matter damage in T2DM patients, which may contribute to their cognitive impairment.

The human brain consists of various interconnected regions that serve distinct functions, creating a complex network. Network analysis is a valuable approach for investigating the neurobiological mechanisms underlying diseases. Previous studies have analyzed the structural network of brain white matter in T2DM patients and found that it still has small-world properties. This suggests that, regardless of disease status, the structural white matter network of the human brain tends to exhibit small-world properties, which facilitate efficient information transmission and processing43. In our study, we constructed white matter networks using a TBSS approach and quantified network efficiency through graph theory analysis. We found that the Eglob value was significantly reduced in patients with T2DM. Eglob is a metric used to assess the efficacy of information transport within a network, and its numerical irregularities are associated with a deceleration in information processing. Meanwhile, we found that both the Bc and Dc of the right superior frontal gyrus were reduced in T2DM patients, suggesting diminished connectivity and information transfer efficiency of this region within the brain network. Bc indicates the mediating function of a node within information pathways; The more frequently information passes through a given node, the higher its Bc value. In contrast, Dc is a key metric for measuring the importance of a node within the brain network, serving as an indicator of the hub role that a specific brain region plays in the overall network. The occurrence of cognitive impairment in T2DM patients is closely associated with structural abnormalities in the brain network, particularly involving the dysfunction of central hub nodes. Previous studies have indicated that alterations in Dc can serve as a sensitive neuroimaging biomarker for the early detection and monitoring of T2DM-related brain damage44,45. The superior frontal gyrus is an important component of the frontal cortex, which plays a central role in higher cognitive processes such as executive function, attention, working memory, and emotion regulation. Early frontal mild white matter lesions have been linked to various cognitive deficits46.

Glymphatic system function and hippocampal structural damage

The hippocampus is a crucial area in the human brain connected with cognitive functioning, particularly memory recall, learning comprehension, and emotional regulation. Research has demonstrated that glycemic fluctuations are significantly linked to cognitive impairment, which correlates with volume reductions in hippocampal regions47. Volumetric analysis indicated that the overall hippocampal volume in the T2DM group was significantly reduced compared to the HC group, suggesting that hippocampal structural integrity is compromised in the early stages of T2DM48. To further characterize this atrophy, we examined hippocampal subfields and observed significant volume reductions across multiple regions, including the bilateral hippocampal body and tail, the molecular layer, and the subiculum. While some literature suggests that certain subfields may serve as specific early indicators of damage49, our results show a relatively uniform pattern of atrophy across the hippocampal formation. This widespread involvement suggests that T2DM-related metabolic stress exerts a generalized impact on the hippocampus. Such global structural changes likely underpin the observed declines in verbal fluency and early cognitive dysfunction in these patients50,51. By providing a detailed subregional profile, we confirm that the diabetic brain environment leads to comprehensive rather than focal hippocampal neurodegeneration. Given the functional importance of these structures, the well-documented association between hippocampal tail atrophy and memory impairment raises questions about the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms, which remain insufficiently understood. Consequently, further investigation into the correlation between structural brain atrophy and the DTI-ALPS index in patients with T2DM may clarify the underlying mechanisms of neurodegenerative illnesses associated with this condition. Our results indicate that atrophy in hippocampal_tail and hippocampal_body are positively correlated with decreased DTI-ALPS, specifically in T2DM patients. This suggests that glymphatic dysfunction may be linked to early volumetric changes. However, it is important to note that these correlations do not inherently imply a causal relationship. It is possible that hyperglycemia independently exacerbates both glymphatic impairment and structural atrophy through parallel pathological pathways, such as chronic neuroinflammation or microvascular damage. While previous studies have observed that glymphatic dysfunction is associated with volume reductions in the hippocampus52, our findings highlight a significant association in the T2DM population that warrants further longitudinal investigation to clarify the precise interplay between these two factors.

Information exchange within the brain network depends on white matter fiber tracts as its structural foundation. Disruption of white matter microstructure can lead to network disconnection. Furthermore, impaired glymphatic clearance directly affects oligodendrocytes and contributes to demyelination53, thereby compromising information transmission within the network. Our study found that T2DM-induced brain imaging changes included atrophy at both the microstructural and macroscopic volume levels, which were significantly associated with DTI-ALPS. Moreover, the significant association between glycemic indicators and the DTI-ALPS index suggests that hyperglycemia may be a driving factor underlying microstructural changes in the hippocampus. In this study, we constructed a serial mediation model to explore the potential relationships among FBG, DTI-ALPS, and the MMSE. The results indicated that FBG indirectly influenced MMSE scores by affecting the DTI-ALPS index, suggesting that glymphatic system function may play a key mediating role in the mechanism of cognitive impairment in T2DM patients. However, as noted by recent methodological evaluations, the DTI-ALPS index should be interpreted with caution as a composite measure. It incorporates not only glymphatic-related diffusion signals but also reflects white matter microstructural characteristics and physiological flow signals54. Therefore, the mediating effect observed in our model likely represents a synergistic impact of both impaired glymphatic clearance and subtle white matter microstructural alterations. It is noteworthy that in our preliminary analyses, traditional white matter structural indices did not show the same robust mediating effect between FBG and MMSE as the DTI-ALPS index did. This discrepancy highlights the unique value of DTI-ALPS, even as a composite measure, its ability to capture directional diffusivity along the perivascular space allows it to serve as a more sensitive bridge in the hyperglycemic-cognitive pathway than markers of pure structural integrity alone. This finding supports the hypothesis that hyperglycemic states may affect cognitive performance primarily by impairing the brain’s metabolic clearance and the stability of the periventricular microenvironment. Our study suggests that the DTI-ALPS index could offer a valuable non-invasive avenue for exploring the underlying pathogenesis of cognitive decline in patients with T2DM.

Limitation

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged and discussed. First, we only included the MMSE scales to assess cognitive; the MoCA was not administered and we did not conduct a comprehensive neuropsychological assessment across multiple cognitive domains. Consequently, our findings may not fully reflect the complexity of cognitive impairment in patients with T2DM. In particular, the MMSE has relatively lower sensitivity to mild cognitive impairment and provides limited assessment of executive function and processing speed, which are commonly affected in T2DM. Therefore, the associations between glymphatic dysfunction and cognition may have been underestimated in this cohort. Future studies should incorporate more sensitive screening tools and a wider range of domain-specific cognitive assessments to improve the accuracy and interpretability of cognitive function. Second, our sample size was relatively small, and all participants were recruited from a single clinical research center, which may have introduced selection bias. To improve the accuracy and generalizability of our findings, future studies will involve larger sample sizes and multicenter clinical cohorts. Third, specific limitations regarding the DTI-ALPS methodology must be noted. The DTI-ALPS index is an indirect, diffusion-based proxy sensitive to water movement, rather than a direct quantification of glymphatic flow or solute clearance. As such, the index may be influenced by multiple physiological factors, including white matter microstructure, perivascular anatomy, and vascular pulsatility. Furthermore, the DTI-ALPS method is limited to assessing water diffusivity outside the lateral ventricles. Research has shown that the glymphatic system operates differently in various regions, a complexity that DTI-ALPS cannot fully capture. Future studies that integrate contrast-enhanced MRI or multiple methods could offer a more comprehensive understanding of these functions. Fourth, the current study is entirely cross-sectional in design, which precludes the establishment of definitive causal relationships. While we identified significant associations between T2DM, glymphatic system alterations, and cognitive decline, we cannot determine the directionality of these effects. Longitudinal studies are necessary to clarify the temporal sequence of these changes and to confirm the potential mechanistic pathways involved. In addition, we lacked information on long-term glycemic control and detailed antidiabetic medication use, which prevented us from evaluating their potential confounding or modifying effects on the observed associations.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrated that patients with T2DM exhibited significant white matter microstructural damage, reduced DTI-ALPS indices, and cognitive decline, indicating a potential dysfunction of the brain’s metabolic clearance system. Mediation analysis further revealed that FBG indirectly affects cognitive function through DTI-ALPS, supporting the potential mediating role of glymphatic system function in T2DM-related cognitive impairment. Additionally, the function of the glymphatic system along the perivascular perimeter was significantly associated with structural atrophy in the hippocampus and its subregions. These findings emphasize the potential of DTI-ALPS as an imaging biomarker and highlight the importance of exploring early functional changes in the glymphatic system related to neurodegenerative diseases.
