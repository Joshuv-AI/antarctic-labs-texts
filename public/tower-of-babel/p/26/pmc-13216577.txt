Operational deflection shape measurements on rotating bladed disks with tracking continuous-scanning LDV
Authors: Cuihong Liu, Tong Jing, Zhenteng Xu, Longlong Liu, Jincheng He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe high-speed rotation and complex operational environment of rotating bladed disks make it difficult for traditional contact measurement techniques to achieve effective, non-interfering, and continuous monitoring of their full-field dynamic responses under operational conditions. To address this challenge, this paper proposes and validates a tracking continuous scanning method based on laser Doppler vibrometry (LDV), aiming to achieve accurate reconstruction of their operational deflection shapes. The core of TCSLDV lies in synchronizing the laser beam with the disk rotation while performing continuous scanning along a predefined blade path. A multi-beam setup and a phase-offset strategy enable seamless switching between blades without stopping, allowing for simultaneous measurement of responses on multiple blades and fixed reference points. By employing an operational deflection shape frequency response function (ODS FRF) phase identification scheme, the phase relationships between blades are extracted, facilitating the reconstruction of the overall operational deflection shape (ODS) of the entire rotating bladed disk. Experimental validation on a simple bladed disk at 120 r/min demonstrated the method’s accuracy, showing a high modal assurance criterion (MAC) of 0.98 between the reconstructed ODS and the finite element predicted mode shape. Additional tests at higher rotational speeds confirmed the method’s applicability under operational excitation. This work presents a cost-effective, non-contact, and continuous scanning solution for full-field ODS measurement in rotating environments, offering significant potential for in-situ modal analysis and model validation of rotating machinery.Keywords: LDV, Rotating bladed disk, Operational deformation shape, ODS FRFSubject terms: Engineering, Optics and photonics, Physics

Introduction

The vibration of rotating blades is a key factor affecting the safe operation of aero-engines. During operation, gyroscopic effects, centrifugal forces, and varying shear stresses alter the structural-dynamic characteristics of rotating machinery1. The higher the rotational speed, the more pronounced the changes in the structure’s dynamic characteristics. Consequently, accurate measurement techniques for estimating the vibration behavior of bladed disks under rotation are essential. Although measuring the vibration of bladed disks under rotation is inherently challenging, the marked differences in dynamic behavior between rotating and stationary states have driven researchers to continuously advance precise measurement techniques for capturing the dynamic response of rotating machinery. This paper develops a tracking-measurement and ODS reconstruction method for rotating bladed disks based on laser Doppler vibrometry. By synchronizing the scan system so that the laser spot co-rotates with the rotor, a continuous laser scan is performed over the structure’s surface to acquire comprehensive bladed-disk vibration data.

Traditional vibration testing of rotating machinery has primarily relied on resistance strain gauges2. However, problems associated with the use of contact-type sensors in the vibration measurement of rotating blades (e.g. the location selection of the sensors, signal transmission and measurement errors arising from added mass) to the analyzer have been reported. Currently, the Blade Tip Timing (BTT) method is widely applied. In BTT measurement, since the sampling frequency is much lower than the blade vibration frequency (undersampling), frequency aliasing will occur in the signal3,4, making it impossible to directly identify the real frequency. To address this issue, researchers have conducted extensive studies. For instance, some researchers have adopted sparse reconstruction methods, leveraging sparsity assumptions to recover the original signal and thus reconstruct high-quality vibration signatures at low sampling rates5–7. Cao et al.8 developed a series of algorithms such as the SAFE diagram and AWSTFT to resolve the frequency aliasing problem caused by undersampling, thereby obtaining the true vibration frequencies of blades during operation. Xu et al.9 improved the accuracy and reliability of vibration parameter identification for rotating blades under complex working conditions and signal noise interference through the improved blade tip timing technology and dual-factor analysis method. With the development of corresponding data processing technologies, the BTT method has been able to obtain the vibration frequency of rotating machinery relatively accurately, but it still faces great challenges in the separation of adjacent modes and the construction of modal shapes.

Nevertheless, the inherent constraints of BTT fundamentally limit its efficacy in advanced modal analysis. The method’s reliance on sparse tip-mounted probes provides no continuous deformation data along the blade geometry10. Consequently, its mode shape reconstruction heavily relies on prior model assumptions and exhibits limited capability in identifying unexpected modes. Furthermore, its inherent undersampling—dictated by rotational speed and probe count—imposes strict Nyquist limitations that hinder the separation of closely spaced modes and the identification of high-frequency components, often resulting in modal aliasing11. Operationally, BTT captures only low-dimensional tip-timing data, making it susceptible to disturbances from speed fluctuations and manufacturing tolerances12. System configuration also presents challenges, as installation typically requires multiple fixed probes and casing modifications, with performance directly constrained by probe count and offering limited adaptability across different testing scenarios.

In recent years, Laser Doppler Vibrometry has been increasingly adopted as a non-contact measurement technique for vibration analysis in rotating machinery. Compared with BTT, TCSLDV offers several distinct advantages. In terms of spatial resolution and mode shape construction, TCSLDV performs continuous laser scanning along predefined paths on the blade surface, enabling direct acquisition of vibration data with high spatial resolution13. This facilitates model-free, experimental observation of nodal lines and complete vibration patterns, providing a direct means of identifying complex or densely spaced modes and overcoming the indirect inference paradigm characteristic of BTT. Regarding frequency resolution and mode separation, TCSLDV leverages the laser Doppler principle, with a sampling rate limited only by hardware capabilities, significantly exceeding the blade-pass frequency. This allows direct resolution of high-frequency modes and closely spaced adjacent modes, offering a broader and clearer frequency observation window free from the undersampling constraints inherent in BTT.

Castellini et al. pioneered the application of LDV to the vibration testing of rotating components such as naval propeller models and automobile tires14,15, achieving fixed-point tracking measurement. Jang et al.16 further investigated the influence of an underwater environment on rotating-propeller vibration measurements. By employing a feature extraction technique based on principal component analysis (PCA), they effectively eliminated anomalous signals including noise and unrelated components while successfully identifying the natural frequencies of marine propellers. Miao and Ewins et al.17,18 have conducted in-depth research on the Tracking Continuous Scanning Laser Doppler Vibrometry (Tracking-CSLDV, TCSLDV). While maintaining synchronous tracking (i.e., a stationary relative position) with rotating components, this technique performs continuous line- or area-scans over the structure’s surface, thereby obtaining full-field vibration data. Consequently, it not only extracts the vibration frequencies and amplitudes from the complete dataset but also reconstructs the structure’s ODS. During testing, the commercially available scanning laser vibrometer employed in this study utilizes optical mirrors with substantial mass. When driven in continuous scanning mode, the significant rotational inertia of these mirrors induces alignment deviations between the laser beam and the rotor, thereby introducing spurious vibrational signals into the measurements. Addressing this, Rothberg et al.19 took the axis of rotation as their subject, investigated the impact of pseudo-vibrations and instrumental misalignment on laser-tracking measurements, and proposed corresponding correction methods. Taking a simple bladed disk as an example, Liu et al.20 studied the influence of frequency aliasing on the vibration frequency identification of rotating bladed disks, which provides a theoretical basis for the selection of continuous scanning frequency in laser testing.

To obtain the full operational deflection shape (ODS) of a rotating bladed disk, a continuous laser scan must sweep across every blade while the beam co-rotating with the bladed disk, thereby collecting a complete set of vibration response data. In addition to the blade-pass aliasing issue previously addressed, a core challenge lies in reconstructing the disk’s ODS solely based on the response data collected along the scanning path. A further difficulty is switching the laser spot between blades without interrupting rotation. This paper investigates the TCSLDV and ODS reconstruction methods using a simplified rotating bladed disk test specimen. First, the tracking and continuous scanning mechanism of TCSLDV and the switching method for multiple blades without interrupting rotation are elaborated in detail. Subsequently, tracking tests on the rotating bladed disk under different rotational speeds and excitation conditions are carried out. Multiple laser beams are employed to simultaneously perform continuous scanning measurements, fixed-point tracking tests, and reference point response measurements on the blades to obtain comprehensive vibration information. The overall ODS of the rotating bladed disk is successfully reconstructed, and the accuracy of the experimental results is verified by the simulation calculation results under low rotational speeds.

Experimental setup and testing scheme

Experimental apparatus

The rotational tracking test device is mainly composed of a rotor system, a Laser Doppler Vibrometer, and a self-built external galvanometer scanning system, as shown in Fig. 4. Among them, the rotor system directly drives the bladed disk to rotate via a servo motor through a shaft coupling, and the rotational speed of the bladed disk can be directly adjusted and controlled by motor speed controller. The rotating disk is a simplified test specimen fabricated by cutting from 2 mm thick S355 material. The Laser Doppler Vibrometer employed in this study is one of the laser heads from the Polytec PSV-400-3D. The galvanometer scanning system comprises a pair of orthogonally arranged deflection mirrors, namely the X-axis and Y-axis galvanometers. The tracking function of the laser is achieved through the collaboration between the laser vibrometer and the galvanometer scanning system. The laser beam emitted by the LDV initially strikes the geometric center of the X-axis galvanometer. Both galvanometers are controlled by a custom LabVIEW-based software to continuously deflect according to a prescribed pattern, thereby allowing the laser to track and continuously scan the rotating blades along a predefined scanning trajectory. Simultaneously, the LDV captures the blade’s vibration information and converts the photoelectric signals into vibration velocity signals.

Schematic of the TCSLDV test system.

In the present test methodology, a specific mode was selected for experimental validation to maintain controlled variables. Accordingly, an electromagnetic exciter rotating synchronously with the bladed disk was employed to excite a targeted natural frequency of the disk, thereby ensuring the exclusive excitation of a single mode. The electromagnetic exciter is mounted on the co-rotating disk and receives the required voltage signal via a slip ring. To minimize rotor imbalance, two electromagnetic exciters are installed on the co-rotating disk, with only one being activated during the actual experiment.

Testing scheme and data acquisition configuration

To reconstruct the ODS of a rotating bladed disk, it is necessary to simultaneously obtain the ODS of all blades and the vibration phase relationships between each blade. Therefore, a reasonable test scheme should be properly designed. This paper will employ two sets of TCSLDV test systems and a LDV to simultaneously control three laser beams for testing the rotating bladed disk. The specific design of the scheme is shown in Fig. 5. Among them, one set of the TCSLDV test system performs tracking continuous scans on each blade, thereby obtaining complete vibration information for each blade and subsequently reconstructing the operational deformation of each blade. The other two laser beams are used to measure the coupled vibration relationships between the blades (i.e., to determine their vibration phases). One follows the designated measurement points on each blade for fixed-point tracking and collects the response signals of the measurement points. The other is used to measure the response signal of the reference point. To simplify the experimental procedure, the center of the bladed disk is selected as the reference point. This is because the rotation radius at the center of the bladed disk is zero, so the laser does not need to follow the rotation of the bladed disk. Instead, the reference response signal can be obtained through static measurement, significantly reducing the complexity of the experiment.

Schematic of the test scheme.

Based on the response signals from the measurement points and the reference point, the ODS FRF21 for each blade measurement point can be calculated. This function captures both the accurate amplitude of the responses at each measurement point and their phase information relative to the reference point. Through the aforementioned testing scheme, the ODS and vibration phase of each blade can be synchronously acquired, ultimately enabling the reconstruction of the ODS of the entire rotating bladed disk at the specified rotational speed.

To validate the effectiveness of the proposed method, experiments were first conducted at low rotational speeds. So that the bladed disk was operated at a rotational speed of 120 r/min (the actual tested speed was 118 r/min, corresponding to a rotational frequency of 1.97 Hz). The TCSLDV tests were sequentially performed on all 16 blades of the bladed disk. The sampling frequency was set to Fs = 8192 Hz, and the excitation frequency was set to F = 36.93 Hz (preliminary simulation indicated that 36.93 Hz corresponds to the vibration frequency of the 1st -order bending mode with 4-nodal-diameter at this rotational speed), and the test duration to T = 6 s. Phase Identification and ODS reconstruction of bladed disk

The operational deformation of an integral bladed disk is not a simple arrangement and combination of the ODSs of individual blades; instead, the vibration coupling relationship between the blades at this rotational speed determines the overall deformation pattern. To capture the vibrational phase relationship between the blades, a response measurement point is set up on each blade. A laser is used to sequentially track and measure each of these points, while another laser simultaneously measures the response of a reference point. In this experiment, the measurement points for phase identification on each blade were all located at the blade tip, while the reference point was set at the center of the disk. Comparison with finite element predictions

A finite element model was developed and analyzed through simulation to validate the accuracy of the experimental results. The bladed disk employed in the experiments, with detailed dimensions illustrated in Fig. 15a, is a cyclically symmetric structure whose modal characteristics are defined by the mode shape of a single sector and the corresponding number of nodal diameters. A finite element model (FEM) of a single sector was developed using hexahedral elements, as shown in Fig. 15b. The material properties are assigned as follows: density = 7840 kg/m³, Young’s modulus = 206 GPa, and Poisson’s ratio = 0.3. In the finite element model, fixed support constraints were applied to all nodes around the inner diameter boundary of the bladed disk to simulate the rigid bolted connection between the hub hole and the shaft.

The relative error between the calculated modal frequency at this order and the experimentally measured result is 4.4%. This discrepancy may stem from simplifications in the finite element model (such as deviations in material properties and idealized boundary conditions) as well as systematic errors in experimental measurements. To further validate the accuracy of the proposed laser tracking method in measuring operational deflection shapes, the Modal Assurance Criterion was applied to evaluate the correlation between experimental and numerical results. By extracting the nodal coordinate information from the finite element model and aligning it with the continuous laser scanning trajectory on the blade surface, a high level of agreement was achieved, with a Modal Assurance Criterion value of 0.98 obtained between the two. This confirms that the proposed experimental method and ODS reconstruction approach can be reliably used to accurately determine the operational deformation of the rotating bladed disk.

Conclusions

This paper presents a TCSLDV method for ODS of rotating bladed disks. This approach utilizes a self‑built external galvanometer scanning system combined with simultaneous multi‑laser control, offering an alternative and cost‑effective optical solution for vibration measurement of rotating structures.

The technique enables synchronous laser tracking and continuous scanning across blades while allowing seamless blade switching via a phase‑offset strategy. By simultaneously capturing blade responses and fixed reference signals, the vibration phase relationships between blades are identified, facilitating reconstruction of the full‑field ODS. Experimental validation on a rotating bladed disk at 120 rpm shows excellent agreement between reconstructed ODS and finite‑element predictions (MAC = 0.98), confirming the accuracy of the method. Tests across varying rotational speeds and excitation conditions further demonstrate the robustness and potential of TCSLDV for operational modal analysis in rotating machinery. The method overcomes key limitations of blade tip timing by providing direct, high‑resolution, full‑field vibration data under rotation.

This study validates the method’s capability for out‑of‑plane vibration measurement using a simplified flat‑bladed disk. Future work will extend the approach to three‑dimensional vibration measurement to capture torsional and in‑plane displacements of realistic twisted blades. Furthermore, dedicated denoising algorithms will be developed and tested in controlled environments to improve the signal‑to‑noise ratio and measurement accuracy, advancing the technology toward field‑deployable monitoring and diagnostic systems for high‑speed rotating machinery.
