PER1-related inflammatory signaling links night shift exposure to lower lung function in coal miners
Authors: Zikai Liu, Haihong Pan, Fengtao Cui, Qingqing Yang, Zhen Yu, Jiale Chen, Xuan Chen, Yan Lv, Qing Wu, Qi Zhong, Xinping Ding, Wei Gao, Weihong Chen, Xiang Zhang, Tong Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryCumulative night-shift exposure may disrupt circadian homeostasis and adversely relate to respiratory health. In a cross-sectional analysis of 9,464 coal miners, we found that greater cumulative night-shift exposure was associated with lower lung function after accounting for cumulative respirable dust exposure. Mendelian randomization analyses supported a potential causal link between shift work and reduced lung function and further indicated that genetically predicted lower lung function was associated with higher chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) risk. In a coal miner subgroup, lower PER1 expression partly accounted for the associations of cumulative night-shift exposure with forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) and forced vital capacity (FVC). Public transcriptomic and single-cell analyses showed lower PER1 expression in COPD and lung cancer-related datasets and suggested links to NF-κB-related inflammatory signaling. These findings suggest that PER1-related inflammatory signaling may represent a candidate molecular link between occupational circadian disruption and respiratory health impairment.

Introduction

Circadian rhythm disruption induced by shift work represents a critical global public health challenge for the modern workforce.1,2 While its interference with metabolic and cardiovascular homeostasis is well documented,3 the potential association of shift work with respiratory health and its underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. As a major labor-intensive sector, the coal mining industry presents a unique occupational environment where inherent hazards, including high-concentration respirable dust and toxic gases,4,5,6 converge with pervasive and rigorous shift-work schedules.7 Although dust exposure is a recognized primary driver of respiratory pathology,6 whether cumulative night-shift exposure (CNSE) is associated with lower lung function remains unclear after accounting for respirable dust exposure, and genetic epidemiological evidence linking shift work, lung function, and selected respiratory outcomes remains limited.

As a vital peripheral organ, the lung possesses an endogenous circadian clock that orchestrates essential physiological functions, including immune responses and tissue repair.8,9 Such circadian dysregulation has been increasingly implicated in various lung pathological processes.10 Within this complex regulatory network, the expression of circadian genes is fundamental,11 with period circadian regulator 1 (PER1) serving as an important component of the negative limb of the core circadian feedback loop.12,13 Studies in animal models further suggest that aberrant expression of circadian genes is linked to respiratory disease-related processes, including enhanced lung inflammation and alterations in the immune microenvironment.14,15,16 Nevertheless, how shift work modulates PER1 expression, and whether PER1 may represent a molecular link between environmental circadian disruption, lung function impairment, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)/lung cancer-related molecular alterations, remains to be further clarified.

Impaired lung function may occur before clinically overt respiratory diseases and has been considered an important marker of respiratory health.17 We hypothesized that CNSE may be associated with lower lung function and altered PER1 expression, which may provide molecular clues linking occupational circadian disruption to respiratory health impairment. Evaluating PER1 as a potential molecular marker may help characterize circadian-related respiratory alterations in occupational settings. This study first leveraged a large-scale cross-sectional survey of 9,464 coal miners to evaluate the association between CNSE and lung function parameters, while accounting for cumulative respirable dust exposure through covariate adjustment, interaction analyses, and joint association analyses. We then employed Mendelian randomization (MR) to examine potential causal relationships among shift work, lung function, and selected respiratory outcomes. Building upon this, we investigated the potential roles of PER1 expression and single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the association between CNSE and lung function. To explore whether PER1 alterations are observed in disease-related molecular contexts, we integrated public bulk and single-cell transcriptomic data to examine cell-type-specific PER1-related transcriptional patterns in COPD and lung cancer-related datasets. Furthermore, we performed in silico virtual knockout simulations to assess whether PER1 perturbation may be linked to NF-κB-related inflammatory programs. Overall, this study integrates observational, genetic epidemiological, and transcriptomic evidence to characterize the relationship between shift work, lung function, and PER1-related inflammatory signaling, providing evidence for occupational respiratory health risk assessment and future mechanistic studies.

We employed restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression to characterize the exposure-response relationships between CNSE and lung function parameters (Figures 1B–1D). The results indicated that after adjusting for covariates, log10-transformed CNSE had non-linear relationships with FEV1 and FVC (p for nonlinear <0.05), with identified inflection points at 2.98 and 2.92, corresponding to 960 and 828 actual night-shift days, respectively. Association between CNSE and lung function parameters after accounting for respirable dust exposure

To examine whether the association between CNSE and lung function persisted after accounting for cumulative respirable dust exposure, we conducted several sensitivity analyses. First, we compared the estimates for CNSE before and after adjusting for dust exposure. In the model excluding dust adjustment (Figure S1), each unit increase in log10-transformed CNSE was associated with a reduction in FEV1 of −11.78 mL (95% CI: −18.55, −5.01), which was highly consistent with the estimate in the fully adjusted model (Figure 1A, model 3: β = −10.70, 95% CI: −17.48, −3.92). Similar stability in β coefficients was observed for FVC and FEV1/FVC, suggesting that the association between CNSE and lung function was not fully explained by cumulative respirable dust exposure.

Furthermore, interaction and joint association analyses were conducted to further evaluate the relationship between CNSE and lung function across levels of cumulative respirable dust exposure. No statistically significant multiplicative interaction was observed between CNSE and cumulative respirable dust exposure across all lung function parameters (p for interaction >0.05, Figure S2). Notably, in the joint association matrix (Figure S3), participants in the highest CNSE tertile (T3) within the “low dust (T1)” stratum still exhibited significantly lower FEV1 (β = −102.77, p = 0.009) and FVC (β = −83.45, p = 0.047) compared to the reference group (low CNSE and low dust). These findings suggest that the inverse association between CNSE and lung function was observed even within lower dust exposure strata, although residual confounding cannot be fully excluded.

To further assess the consistency of these associations, subgroup and interaction analyses were performed across various covariates, including age, BMI, smoking history, and working years. No significant interactions were identified between CNSE and these stratifying variables (Tables S2–S4). Finally, the robustness of the results was further assessed through three stepwise sensitivity analyses excluding female participants, further excluding individuals with a family history of respiratory diseases, and then additionally excluding those with self-reported respiratory conditions (Tables S5–S7), all of which yielded consistent results.

Multi-step MR analyses of shift work, lung function, and selected respiratory outcomes

To evaluate potential causal associations among shift work, lung function, and selected respiratory outcomes, we performed a multi-step MR analysis, with the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method serving as the primary causal estimator. The genetic instruments utilized for all traits exhibited high validity, with all minimum F statistics exceeding 20.83 (Tables S8, S9, and S10). In the initial stage, genetically predicted shift work was inversely associated with FEV1 (β = −0.13, 95% CI: −0.23, −0.04, p = 0.007) and FVC (β = −0.15, 95% CI: −0.25, −0.05, p = 0.004). In the subsequent stage, genetically predicted shift work was not significantly associated with COPD (p = 0.084), non-small cell lung cancer (p = 0.290), or idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) (p = 0.865). In the final stage, higher genetically predicted FEV1 (OR = 0.54, 95% CI: 0.45, 0.64, p < 0.001) and FVC (OR = 0.70, 95% CI: 0.58, 0.85, p < 0.001) were associated with lower COPD risk (Figures 2 and S4; Table S11). For IPF, genetically predicted FEV1 showed a positive association with disease risk (odds ratio [OR] = 1.61, 95% CI: 1.15, 2.24, p = 0.005), whereas the association for FVC did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.103) (Table S11). Robustness and heterogeneity assessments via Cochran’s Q test, forest plots, funnel plots, and leave-one-out analyses supported the overall stability of these MR estimates (Figures S5–S8; Table S13). Additionally, sensitivity analyses, including MR Egger and MR PRESSO, did not indicate substantial horizontal pleiotropy or outlier bias (Tables S14 and S15). Overall, the MR findings support a potential causal link between shift work and lung function and further indicate that genetically predicted lower lung function is associated with higher COPD risk. The IPF-related results showed a disease-specific pattern distinct from COPD and should be interpreted cautiously.

Role of PER1 expression and PER1 SNPs in the association between CNSE and lung function parameters

Building upon the aforementioned evidence, we investigated the potential role of PER1 in the association between CNSE and lung function parameters. The demographic characteristics of the 228 participants are presented in Table S17. Upon categorizing CNSE into tertiles, PER1 expression levels were lower across higher CNSE tertiles (p = 0.011). Mediation analysis revealed that ln-transformed PER1 expression significantly mediated the association between CNSE and lung function parameters, including FEV1 and FVC (Figure 3 and Table S18). To further examine the potential involvement of PER1 in the association between CNSE and lung function parameters, we genotyped two PER1 SNPs (rs2585405 and rs2253820). After adjusting for covariates, we identified a significant interaction between the PER1 rs2585405 genotype and log10-transformed CNSE on FEV1 (p for interaction = 0.019) (Table S19). Specifically, among carriers of the GG + CG genotypes, each 10-fold increase in CNSE was associated with a mean decrease in FEV1 of 83.57 mL (95% CI: −147.77, −19.38, p = 0.012). Collectively, these findings suggest that the PER1 rs2585405 genotype may modify the association between CNSE and lung function parameters.

PER1 downregulation in COPD and lung cancer-related datasets and its association with inflammatory signaling pathways

Our population-based analysis suggested that lower PER1 expression may partly account for the association between CNSE and lung function parameters. To explore whether PER1-related alterations were also present in disease-related molecular contexts, including COPD and lung cancer, we conducted further analyses using publicly available datasets. We analyzed the expression of PER1 from four independent datasets retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Consistently, PER1 expression was significantly diminished in the COPD group compared to the control group in GSE5058 and GSE8545 (Figures 4A and 4B). A similar pattern was observed in lung cancer datasets, where PER1 levels were significantly lower in patients with non-small cell lung cancer (GEO: GSE1987) and lung adenocarcinoma (GEO: GSE31547) than in healthy controls (Figures 4C and 4D). To explore PER1-associated molecular pathways in these disease-related datasets, we constructed a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network centered on PER1 (Figure 4E). Functional enrichment analysis of the network nodes revealed that, beyond canonical circadian rhythms, PER1-associated network genes were enriched in lung disease-related biological processes (Figure 4F). Cell-type-specific PER1 expression and transcriptional patterns in COPD-related datasets

To further explore cell-type-specific PER1-related patterns in COPD-related datasets, we acquired publicly available datasets (GEO: GSE128033, GSE167295) from the GEO database. Following a series of quality control steps (Figures S11A–S11D), we obtained a total of 21,657 cells. Subsequent cell-type annotation using canonical marker genes (Figure 5A) allowed us to identify 12 distinct cell types (Figure 5B), among which T cells (27.9%), macrophages (27.2%), and natural killer cells (NKCs) (12.5%) predominated. A descriptive comparison of recovered cell proportions suggested differences in relative cell composition between control and COPD datasets (Figures 5C and S11E). Notably, within the PER1-expressing cells, PER1 expression was significantly downregulated in macrophages, T cells, and NKCs from the COPD group compared to the control group (Figure 5D). Further inspection of the recovered proportions of PER1-expressing cells within these three cell types showed no significant difference between control and COPD groups (Figure 5E). We isolated the PER1-expressing cells from these three cell types and visualized their PER1 expression levels in control and COPD groups via violin plots (Figures 5F, S11F, and S11G). To explore transcriptional differences associated with PER1 expression, we stratified cells within each type into high-PER1 and low-PER1 groups (Figures 5G, S11H, and S11I). These findings suggest that PER1 expression differences were associated with more extensive transcriptional differences in macrophages than in T cells or NKCs. Gene Ontology “(GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses of macrophage DEGs revealed that upregulated genes in low-PER1 cells were significantly enriched in “positive regulation of I-kappaB kinase/NF-kappaB signaling”, “myeloid leukocyte activation” and “phagosome” (Figure 5I). Conversely, downregulated genes involved “negative regulation of NF-kappaB transcription factor activity” and “TNF signaling pathway” (Figure 5J). These results suggest a potential link between lower PER1 expression and inflammatory signaling in macrophages.

Single-cell profiling of PER1 expression and transcriptional patterns in lung cancer-related datasets

To broaden our understanding, we analyzed single-cell transcriptomic data from lung cancer patients and healthy controls. Following rigorous quality control steps (Figures S12A–S12D), we profiled a total of 26,279 cells. Cell-type annotation (Figure 6A) identified 9 major cell populations, including T cells (32.7%), epithelial cells (21.8%), and macrophages (21.4%) (Figure 6B). Similar to our observations in COPD, the expression of PER1 within PER1-expressing cells was significantly downregulated across multiple immune and stromal cell types in the lung cancer group, including T cells, NKCs, and fibroblasts (Figure 6C). Furthermore, we examined the recovered proportions of PER1-expressing cells and observed higher recovered proportions of macrophages, monocytes, and T cells, together with a lower recovered proportion of epithelial cells, in lung cancer-related datasets (Figure 6D). These findings should be interpreted cautiously because recovered cell proportions may be influenced by technical and sampling factors. Given the pronounced downregulation, we focused on T cells and NKCs for deeper analysis of their PER1 expression profiles (Figures 6E and 6F). We stratified these PER1-expressing cells into high-PER1 and low-PER1 groups (Figures 6G and S12E). Differential gene expression analysis revealed that PER1 expression variability was associated with marked transcriptomic differences in T cells, yielding 2,011 upregulated and 2 downregulated genes in the low-PER1 group (Figure 6H). Similarly, NKCs exhibited 65 upregulated and 7 downregulated genes (Figure S12F). Enrichment analysis of the upregulated DEGs in T cells revealed pathways intrinsically linked to cancer and inflammation including “positive regulation of I-kappaB kinase/NF-kappaB signaling”, “p53 signaling”, and “intrinsic apoptotic signaling” (Figure 6I). This recurring enrichment pattern suggests a potential link between lower PER1 expression and NF-κB-related signaling. In NKCs upregulated DEGs were enriched in “antigen processing and presentation” and “IL-17 signaling pathway” (Figure 6J). Conversely, downregulated DEGs in NKCs were associated with “cytolysis” and “defense response to tumor cell” (Figure S12G). Collectively, these findings suggest that lower PER1 expression was associated with NF-κB-related inflammatory signatures in both COPD and lung cancer-related datasets, with macrophage-related patterns in COPD and T cell-related patterns in lung cancer.

In silico PER1 perturbation analysis and its association with systemic inflammation

To explore whether PER1 perturbation may be linked to the transcriptional differences observed in COPD and lung cancer-related datasets, we performed virtual knockout analysis using the scTenifoldKnk algorithm. Given that PER1 expression was lower in disease-related datasets, we simulated PER1 knockout specifically in macrophages and T cells from the control group to evaluate predicted PER1-related network perturbations under a non-diseased background (Figure 7). Similarly, virtual knockout in control T cells was also associated with regulatory perturbations that were again significantly enriched in the NFKB signaling cascade, along with secondary impacts on pathways such as “IL6 JAK STAT3 signaling” (Figures 7C and 7D). To evaluate whether the systemic inflammatory profile in our study population is consistent with this proposed regulatory pathway, we measured the serum concentrations of these three cytokines in the coal miner population. Spearman correlation analysis demonstrated that increased CNSE was significantly and positively associated with elevated levels of ln-transformed IL-1β (R = 0.21, p = 0.002) and IL-6 (R = 0.23, p < 0.001) (Figures 8B and 8C). These findings were further supported by GLM adjusting for potential confounders, in which CNSE was significantly associated with both ln-transformed IL-1β (β = 0.07, 95% CI: 0.03, 0.12, p = 0.001) and IL-6 (β = 0.09, 95% CI: 0.03, 0.15, p = 0.003) (Figure 8E). Finally, RCS analysis revealed a monotonic positive trend between CNSE and these markers, further supporting a potential exposure-response relationship (Figures 8F and 8G). Collectively, these results provide population-level evidence consistent with the PER1-related inflammatory signatures identified in the single-cell analyses.

Discussion

Analysis of 9,464 coal miners showed that CNSE was significantly associated with lower lung function parameters after adjustment for covariates, including cumulative respirable dust exposure. This finding aligns with previous reports indicating that shift work elevates asthma risk and that insomnia exacerbates respiratory impairment.18,19,20 Notably, our RCS analysis revealed a non-linear exposure-response relationship, identifying critical inflection points at 960 and 828 night-shift days for FEV1 and FVC, respectively. This nonlinear pattern suggests that the association between CNSE and lung function may become more apparent beyond certain cumulative exposure levels. Biologically, shift work may disrupt internal temporal rhythms and contribute to physiological alterations relevant to lung function.21 The mammalian circadian system operates through a hierarchical regulatory network, in which the central pacemaker in the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) coordinates oscillation frequencies in peripheral organs such as the lungs.22 This regulatory system is highly dependent on synchronization with external environmental signals, notably light-dark cycles and feeding patterns.23,24 Chronic circadian disruption, a characteristic feature of shift work, may promote desynchrony between the central pacemaker and peripheral tissues and has been linked to impaired physiological homeostasis relevant to respiratory health.25,26 Lower FEV1 has been recognized as an important predictor of subsequent COPD development.17 Our multi-step MR analysis using UK Biobank and FinnGen datasets further supported a potential causal association between genetically predicted shift work and lower lung function and indicated that genetically predicted lower lung function was associated with higher COPD risk. Together, these findings suggest that circadian disruption related to shift work may be relevant to respiratory health, particularly through its association with lung function and COPD-related risk pathways.

The MR findings for IPF showed a disease-specific pattern distinct from COPD. Genetically predicted shift work was not significantly associated with IPF, whereas genetically predicted FEV1 was positively associated with IPF risk. This finding should be interpreted cautiously and should not be taken to indicate that higher clinically measured FEV1 increases pulmonary fibrosis risk. IPF is a restrictive fibrotic disease, for which prognostic assessment has mainly emphasized FVC, diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide, oxygen requirement, and longitudinal lung-function decline rather than FEV1 alone.27 Genetic evidence also supports a disease-specific interpretation, as COPD and pulmonary fibrosis may share selected susceptibility loci but show divergent genetic architectures and even opposite risk-allele directions at several loci.28 Consistently, lung tissue transcriptomic evidence indicates that COPD and IPF may show overlapping but directionally divergent gene expression profiles, particularly involving inflammatory and immune-related pathways.29 In addition, genetically predicted FEV1 should not be viewed as identical to clinically measured FEV1, because lung-function-associated variants may capture inherited variation related to lung development, airway structure, epithelial repair, innate immunity, or mechanical stress.30 These findings suggest that MR results across heterogeneous respiratory outcomes require disease-specific interpretation.

In exploratory experiments involving coal miners, lower PER1 expression accounted for approximately 20.42%–20.84% of the association between CNSE and FEV1 and FVC. This finding is consistent with lower PER1 expression observed in COPD and lung cancer-related public datasets. The identification of the PER1 rs2585405 genotype as a significant effect modifier further highlights individual genetic susceptibility, where carriers of the G allele showed a stronger inverse association between CNSE and FEV1. These results suggest that PER1 dysregulation may represent a molecular feature associated with both CNSE-related lung function differences and selected disease-related transcriptomic contexts. PER1 may therefore represent a candidate molecular link between occupational circadian disruption and respiratory health impairment. Nevertheless, circadian disruption is unlikely to be mediated by PER1 alone, and other canonical clock genes may also contribute to shift work-related respiratory alterations. Previous studies have reported important roles of circadian genes in maintaining lung homeostasis and modulating inflammatory processes.31,32 Through PPI and enrichment analyses, we found that PER1 is closely associated with the NF-κB signaling pathway, consistent with previous reports that circadian disruption may promote inflammatory responses involving NF-κB activation.31,33 Furthermore, the identified links between PER1, cellular senescence, and glucocorticoid receptor signaling suggest its potential relevance to lung aging and glucocorticoid-related inflammatory regulation.34

Through single-cell transcriptomic profiling, we observed lower PER1 expression mainly within immune cell populations in COPD and lung cancer-related datasets. In COPD-related datasets, the recovered proportions of PER1-expressing cells were comparable between groups, whereas PER1 expression levels within macrophages, T cells, and NKCs were lower. In lung cancer-related datasets, recovered proportions of PER1-expressing cells differed across several cell types, with higher recovered proportions in macrophages, monocytes, and T cells, and a lower recovered proportion in epithelial cells. These relative composition findings should be interpreted cautiously because scRNA-seq cell recovery can be influenced by technical and sampling factors. Accordingly, our interpretation focused mainly on within-cell-type PER1 expression and PER1-related transcriptional signatures rather than absolute differences in tissue-level cell abundance. Among these populations, macrophages in COPD-related datasets showed the largest number of PER1-associated differentially expressed genes, which is consistent with their role as central coordinators of inflammatory responses in COPD.35,36 These findings are consistent with the concept that cell-intrinsic circadian regulation may be involved in immune and inflammatory processes in the lung.13,37 Our analysis revealed that low-PER1 macrophages showed enrichment of NF-κB-related inflammatory pathways, together with changes in pathways related to negative regulation of NF-κB transcription factor activity.31,35 A similar pattern was observed in lung cancer datasets, where low PER1 expression in T cells was linked to NF-κB and p53 signaling pathways, suggesting that lower PER1 expression may be linked to NF-κB-related inflammatory signaling in both COPD and lung cancer-related contexts.

Sustained NF-κB signaling has been implicated in chronic inflammatory processes in respiratory diseases.38 Our results suggest that lower PER1 expression is associated with NF-κB-related inflammatory signatures, as reflected by a core molecular signature involving RBCK1, TRADD, and SHARPIN. In silico virtual knockout analysis provided additional network-based support for this link, although it should not be interpreted as direct functional validation. Consistent with these molecular findings, systemic inflammatory marker analyses in coal miners showed that increased CNSE was significantly and positively associated with elevated serum levels of IL-1β and IL-6. Previous studies have shown that NF-κB activation can feedback-inhibit core clock genes including PER1,34,39 and clock proteins can also regulate the transcriptional activity of NF-κB.31 These two factors may constitute a mutually reinforcing pathological association, potentially contributing to reciprocal interactions between chronic inflammation and circadian disruption. The exposure-response relationship between CNSE and these cytokines further suggests that systemic inflammatory changes may be one population-level correlate of PER1-related inflammatory signaling in the context of chronic shift work.

This study has several strengths. We leveraged a large-scale occupational population of coal miners and integrated cross-sectional epidemiological analysis with MR, droplet digital PCR (ddPCR)-based absolute quantification of PER1, SNP interaction analysis, public transcriptomics, and single-cell analyses. This multi-layered design allowed us to examine the relationship between CNSE, lung function, and PER1-related inflammatory signaling from complementary analytical perspectives. In addition, cumulative respirable dust exposure, a major occupational respiratory hazard among coal miners, was considered through covariate adjustment, interaction analyses, and joint association analyses.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, the epidemiological analysis was based on cross-sectional baseline data, and the observed associations should not be interpreted as longitudinal lung function decline or disease progression. Some occupational exposure information was self-reported, which may introduce recall bias. Second, detailed information on respiratory medication use was not systematically collected, including bronchodilators, inhaled corticosteroids, and other chronic obstructive pulmonary disease-related treatments. Although self-reported respiratory diseases were adjusted for and related sensitivity analyses yielded broadly consistent results, residual confounding by medication use cannot be excluded. Third, MR analyses and public transcriptomic analyses relied on external datasets from different populations, and potential bias due to phenotype heterogeneity, sample differences, residual pleiotropy, or cross-population extrapolation cannot be fully ruled out. Fourth, the molecular analyses should be interpreted as hypothesis generating. The scRNA-seq cell proportion results may not represent absolute tissue-level cellular abundance, the virtual knockout analysis was based on in silico network perturbation rather than direct functional validation, and the candidate-gene strategy focused on PER1 did not systematically evaluate the full canonical clock-gene network. Future studies using longitudinal designs, broader clock-gene profiling, functional experiments, and validation approaches, such as flow cytometry, immunohistochemistry, or spatial transcriptomics are warranted.

Conclusion

In summary, this study integrates epidemiological analysis, MR, and multi-scale molecular data to characterize the relationship between CNSE, lung function, and PER1-related inflammatory signaling. CNSE was associated with lower lung function among coal miners, while MR analyses supported potential causal links between shift work and lower lung function and between genetically predicted lower lung function and COPD risk. Molecular analyses suggested that lower PER1 expression may be linked to NF-κB-related inflammatory signatures in COPD and lung cancer-related datasets. These findings support PER1 as a candidate molecular marker for future risk assessment and mechanistic studies in occupational populations exposed to long-term night-shift schedules.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human participants

Baseline cross-sectional data were obtained from the China-Northern Anhui Coal Workers Cohort (C-NACWC), which was established between 2021 and 2024. The cohort enrolled employed coal miners aged 18 to 60 years who completed standardized occupational health examinations during this period. Participants were excluded if they refused to participate, had worked for less than one year, or had incomplete critical data required for the present analyses. A flowchart of participant selection is presented in Figure S13. The final epidemiological analysis included 9,464 coal miners.

Information on age, sex, education, marital status, race and ethnicity, family history of respiratory diseases, self-reported respiratory diseases, physical activity, smoking history, alcohol consumption, occupational category, and work history was obtained through structured questionnaires administered face to face by trained investigators. Sex was recorded as reported in the occupational health records and questionnaire. Socioeconomic characteristics were represented by education level and occupational history. The study population was predominantly male because it was based on an occupational coal miner population, and this issue is discussed as relevant to generalizability in the main text.

An exploratory sub-study involving 228 participants was conducted to examine PER1 expression, selected PER1 SNPs, and serum cytokine levels. Peripheral blood samples from this subgroup were used for absolute quantification of PER1 expression and SNP genotyping. Serum samples from the same subgroup were used for cytokine measurements. Baseline characteristics of these participants are provided in Table S17.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Anhui Medical University (approval number 20210647). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before data and sample collection.

Method details

Data collection

Data were collected through face-to-face interviews using a structured questionnaire. Following an overnight fast, each participant underwent anthropometric and clinical examinations conducted by trained physicians, and fasting venous blood samples were collected in the morning.

Education level was categorized as primary or secondary education versus higher education and above. Marital status was categorized as married or unmarried. Smoking was defined as having smoked at least one cigarette per day for more than six months. Smoking cessation was defined as abstinence from smoking for more than six months before the survey date. Both current and former smokers were categorized as having a smoking history.

Night-shift exposure assessment

Night shift work was defined according to International Labor Organization (ILO) standards as work performed during a period of no less than seven consecutive hours, including the interval from midnight to 5:00 a.m. The average frequency of night shifts per month was recorded, and the total duration of night work per year was subsequently calculated. The CNSE was calculated using the following formula:CNSE=∑T=T1+T2+T3+…+Tnwhere CNSE represents the cumulative night-shift exposure (days) for each miner, T denotes the duration of night shift work (days), and n indicates the number of work categories.40 CNSE was log10-transformed for statistical analyses because of its skewed distribution and wide dynamic range.

Respirable dust exposure

Data on respirable dust concentrations across various job positions were systematically collected. A job-exposure matrix (JEM) was developed by integrating respirable dust concentrations specific to both job categories and calendar years. Detailed occupational hazardous exposure data were routinely monitored and provided by the Huaibei occupational disease prevention and control hospital of Huaibei coal mining group. Individual work histories, including occupational categories and the start and end dates for each position, were retrieved from the structured questionnaires. The CDE (mg/m3-years) for each participant was quantified using the following formula:CDE=∑j=1n(Cj∗Tj)Where CDE represents the cumulative respirable dust exposure throughout the participant’s work history (mg/m3-years), Cj denotes the average respirable dust concentration (mg/m3) at the workstation for the jth job category, Tj signifies the duration (years) of employment in the jth job category; n is the total number of distinct job categories held by the participant. CDE was log10-transformed where appropriate before categorization or modeling.

Lung function testing

Before spirometry, participants were asked to sit quietly and breathe calmly for at least 5 min in the lung function testing room. Lung function was then assessed using a portable spirometer (Chestgraph HI-101, Japan) following the guidelines established by the American Thoracic Society (ATS). The instrument was calibrated before each measurement in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Three acceptable volume-time curves were obtained, and the maximal values of FEV1 and FVC were used for subsequent analyses.41,42

MR data sources

A stepwise MR framework was used to examine potential causal associations among shift work, lung function parameters, and selected respiratory outcomes. All genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics were queried and retrieved from the IEU OpenGWAS database (https://opengwas.io/datasets/). A detailed summary of all GWAS datasets used in the MR analyses is provided in Table S20.

Exposure data for shift work were obtained from the UK Biobank (IEU OpenGWAS: ukb-b-1712, N = 263,315). Lung function outcomes included FEV1 (IEU OpenGWAS: ebi-a-GCST007432, N = 321,047) and FVC (IEU OpenGWAS: ebi-a-GCST007429, N = 321,047), both obtained from UK Biobank-derived GWAS summary statistics. Clinical respiratory outcomes included COPD (IEU OpenGWAS: finn-b-J10_COPD, N = 193,638), non-small cell lung cancer (IEU OpenGWAS: finn-b-C3_LUNG_NONSMALL, N = 218,792), and IPF (IEU OpenGWAS: finn-b-IPF, N = 198,014), retrieved from the FinnGen consortium.

The MR framework included three analytical stages. The first stage examined genetically predicted shift work in relation to lung function parameters. The second stage examined genetically predicted shift work in relation to selected respiratory outcomes. The third stage examined genetically predicted lung function parameters in relation to selected respiratory outcomes.

Instrument selection for MR

Instrumental variables (IVs) for shift work and lung function parameters were selected using predefined significance thresholds. A threshold of p < 5 × 10−6 was applied for shift work to retain a sufficient number of genetic instruments. For lung function parameters used as exposures in the final MR stage, a genome-wide significance threshold of p < 5 × 10−8 was adopted. All IVs underwent linkage disequilibrium clumping using a 10,000-kb window and an r2 threshold of 0.001 based on the European ancestry reference panel. F-statistics were calculated to assess instrument strength. The minimum F-statistics for the three analytical stages were 20.83, 20.83, and 28.89, respectively. Details of the final included genetic instruments are provided in Tables S8, S9, and S10. A secondary sensitivity analysis was conducted using a relaxed instrument selection threshold of p < 5 × 10−6 for lung function parameters in the final-stage MR analyses.

Candidate gene selection

The molecular architecture of central and peripheral clocks is governed by a cell-autonomous transcription-translation feedback loop (TTFL), which is present in nearly all tissues and is coupled with cellular metabolism, redox signaling, and oxidative stress.43 At the core of the TTFL, CLOCK and BMAL1 form a heterodimeric transcriptional activator that binds to E-box elements within the promoter regions of Period and Cryptochrome genes to initiate transcription. As PER and CRY proteins accumulate in the cytoplasm, they dimerize and translocate into the nucleus to repress CLOCK-BMAL1 activity. Degradation of PER and CRY proteins relieves this repression and allows the next approximately 24-h cycle to begin.

PER1 was selected a priori as a mechanism-driven candidate gene based on prior literature and biological plausibility. Given its role in the negative limb of the TTFL and previous evidence linking PER1 dysregulation to peripheral clock regulation, inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, immune regulation, and cancer-related pathways, PER1 was prioritized for ddPCR-based absolute quantification. This candidate-gene strategy was not intended to exclude the potential roles of other canonical clock genes, including CLOCK, BMAL1/ARNTL, CRY1, and CRY2.

In addition to PER1, CCL2 was evaluated as an exploratory data-driven candidate gene. However, CCL2 expression did not show a statistically significant change in the study population and was therefore not included in the core mechanistic analyses.

PER1 expression by ddPCR

Absolute expression levels of PER1 in peripheral blood were determined using the QX200 Droplet Digital PCR system (Bio-Rad, USA). Peripheral blood samples were thawed from −80°C and thoroughly homogenized. Total RNA was extracted using the RNAprep Pure Blood Kit (TIANGEN, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentrations were standardized to 400 ng/μL, followed by reverse transcription using the ToloScript RTEasyMix for qPCR Kit (TOLOBIO, China).

Before ddPCR, primer specificity was validated using 2×Q3 SYBR qPCR Master Mix (TOLOBIO, China). The presence of a single sharp melting peak was considered evidence of specific amplification. Primer sequences and amplification procedures are provided in Table S21. For absolute quantification, 1 μL of the resulting cDNA was incorporated into the reaction using QX200 ddPCR EvaGreen Supermix (Bio-Rad, USA). Droplets were generated and amplified according to the standardized Bio-Rad protocol to determine the absolute copy number of PER1 transcripts.

PER1 SNP genotyping

Two PER1 SNPs, rs2585405 and rs2253820, were identified through database mining using the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) dbSNP database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/snp/) and the Han Chinese Genomes Database PGG.Han 2.0 (https://www.biosino.org/pgghan2/). Selection criteria included a minor allele frequency of at least 5% in East Asian populations and relevance to functional annotation.

DNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples collected from coal miners. Primers were designed using Primer3 software. Purified sequencing products were analyzed using an Applied Biosystems 3730xl Genetic Analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s standard operating procedures. Sequencing chromatograms were visualized using Chromas software, and specific base variations were manually verified by two independent investigators. Negative controls were included in all PCR assays, and samples were processed in duplicate to ensure reproducibility.

Serum cytokine measurements

Serum levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-17A were measured in the exploratory subgroup of 228 participants using commercial ELISA kits (Jingmei, China). Thawed serum samples were centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C to isolate supernatants. Supernatant aliquots of 10 μL were added to microplate wells and analyzed for optical density using a microplate reader. Concentrations of these cytokines were calculated using linear regression based on blank-corrected standard curves.

Bulk transcriptomic analysis

Four bulk transcriptomic datasets were obtained from the GEO database (Table S22).44,45,46 The dataset GEO: GSE5058 comprised small airway samples from 12 non-smokers and 6 patients with COPD, and GEO: GSE8545 included 17 non-smokers and 18 COPD samples. GEO: GSE1987 provided transcriptomic profiles from 9 controls and 28 patients with non-small cell lung cancer, and GEO: GSE31547 consisted of 20 controls and 30 lung adenocarcinoma samples.

Data acquisition was performed using the GEOquery R package. Raw intensity data were normalized using the normalizeBetweenArrays function in the limma package. Manual annotation was performed for PER1 expression. When multiple probes mapped to the same PER1 gene, the probe with the maximal signal intensity was retained for downstream analysis.

Protein network analysis

Fifty putative PER1-interacting proteins were identified using the STRING database. Interaction sources were set to text mining, experiments, and database evidence. The minimum required interaction score was 0.400, corresponding to medium confidence, and the maximum number of interactors displayed was set to 50. The PPI network was visualized using Cytoscape version 3.9.1. Functional enrichment analysis of node genes was performed using the clusterProfiler R package.

Single-cell transcriptomics

Publicly available scRNA-seq datasets, including GEO: GSE128033, GSE167295, and GSE198099, were retrieved from GEO. These datasets included peripheral lung parenchymal tissues from healthy individuals and patients diagnosed with COPD or lung cancer.47,48,49 Initial quality control involved filtering cells and genes with low detection counts, followed by removal of abnormal cells based on mitochondrial and hemoglobin gene proportions.

Upon data import, cells with fewer than 300 detected genes were excluded, as were genes detected in fewer than 5 cells. Mitochondrial and hemoglobin genes were annotated and used as quality control metrics. Different filtering thresholds were applied according to disease context. For the COPD datasets, cells with more than 5,000 detected genes, a hemoglobin expression proportion exceeding 0.5%, or a mitochondrial gene proportion greater than 14% were discarded. For the lung cancer datasets, cells with more than 6,000 detected genes, a hemoglobin expression proportion exceeding 0.08%, or a mitochondrial gene proportion greater than 18% were removed.

The expression matrix for each cell was total-count normalized to 10,000 counts per cell and then natural-log transformed. Highly variable genes were identified for centering and variance standardization. Principal component analysis was conducted, and batch integration was performed using the Harmony algorithm with a maximum iteration count of 30. The actual iteration counts were 10 for the COPD datasets and 13 for the lung cancer datasets. A k-nearest neighbor graph was constructed based on integrated principal component representations with k = 15. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) low-dimensional projections were computed based on the adjacency relationships.

Cell clustering was performed using the Leiden graph clustering algorithm implemented in igraph with two iterations and a resolution parameter of 1. Dot plots showing the expression intensity of marker genes within each cluster were generated to facilitate cell type identification. Cell types were manually annotated based on canonical marker genes curated from published literature.50,51 Cell-type proportions were used to describe the relative composition of recovered cells and were not interpreted as absolute tissue-level cellular abundance.

Within each cell type, cells were stratified into high-PER1 and low-PER1 groups based on the top 25% and bottom 25% of PER1 expression, respectively. Differentially expressed genes were then identified for downstream pathway analyses.

In silico PER1 knockout

To assess predicted transcriptional perturbations associated with PER1 knockout simulation, we performed in silico knockout analysis using the scTenifoldKnk R package. Given that PER1 expression was lower in disease-related datasets, PER1 knockout was simulated in macrophages and T cells derived from the control group to evaluate PER1-related network perturbations under a non-diseased background. The input matrix was constructed using the top 3,000 highly variable genes together with the target gene.

To ensure robustness of gene regulatory network reconstruction, 10 independent networks were generated using a subsampling strategy with 300 cells per iteration. The predicted network perturbation after PER1 knockout simulation was quantified by a Z score derived from manifold alignment between the wild-type and pseudo-knockout networks. GSEA was then performed to identify pathways with significant network instability. All detected genes were ranked by perturbation Z-scores and analyzed using clusterProfiler against the MSigDB Hallmark gene set (h.all.v2022.1).

Quality control procedures

Data quality was maintained through standardized procedures for investigator training, data collection, laboratory assays, and data management. All investigators underwent comprehensive training based on a manual developed by field experts. Training covered questionnaire administration, anthropometric measurements, and biological sample management. Only investigators who passed a qualification examination were permitted to conduct fieldwork.

Upon completion of each interview, researchers immediately checked the questionnaire for completeness. Dedicated quality control reviewers then checked all records for missing, erroneous, or illogical data. Discrepancies were resolved by contacting participants promptly for clarification or correction. All physical examination instruments were certified by the National Bureau of Weights and Measures, regularly calibrated, and operated by trained personnel. To minimize measurement error, manual anthropometric measurements were performed in duplicate.

Questionnaire and physical examination data were entered into an EpiData 3.1 database using a double-entry, double-verification method performed independently by two personnel. Discrepancies between entries were resolved by cross-referencing the original records. Before absolute quantification of circadian-related candidate genes using ddPCR, optimal experimental conditions were established, and instrument stability was assessed using negative controls and replicate measurements. Statistical data integrity was verified by checking for outliers and missing values.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics

Descriptive data were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges, means with standard deviations, or numbers with percentages, as appropriate. Continuous variables were compared across CNSE tertiles using analysis of variance or non-parametric tests according to distributional characteristics. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square tests. Variables with skewed distributions and wide dynamic ranges were transformed before analysis. CNSE was log10-transformed, and PER1 expression levels were ln-transformed to improve model assumptions. CNSE was categorized into tertiles (T1, T2, T3), with non-shift-working miners assigned to the lowest exposure group (T1). Baseline characteristics and lung function parameters across CNSE tertiles are presented in Table S1.

Epidemiological models

The epidemiological analyses included 9,464 coal miners, whereas the exploratory molecular analyses of PER1 expression and serum cytokines were conducted in a subgroup of 228 participants. Sample sizes for individual analyses are reported in the corresponding Results sections and figure legends.

Covariates were selected a priori based on previous literature, occupational epidemiological context, biological plausibility, and their potential associations with both CNSE and lung function. The following variables were selected for adjustment: age, sex, body mass index, education, smoking history, alcohol consumption, physical activity, self-reported respiratory diseases, working years, and cumulative respirable dust exposure.

Three models were constructed. Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, body mass index, and education. Model 3 was adjusted for age, sex, body mass index, education, smoking history, alcohol consumption, physical activity, self-reported respiratory diseases, working years, and cumulative respirable dust exposure.

Generalized linear models were applied to assess the associations between CNSE and lung function parameters. Exposure-response relationships were characterized using four-knot restricted cubic spline curves, and nonlinear change thresholds were identified using the segmented package. To assess the potential confounding influence of occupational dust, estimates from generalized linear models were compared before and after excluding adjustment for cumulative respirable dust exposure.

To evaluate whether the association between CNSE and lung function was modified by dust exposure, multiplicative interaction analyses were performed by introducing an interaction term into the adjusted model, and p values for interaction were calculated. A joint association analysis was conducted by categorizing participants into nine groups based on tertiles of CNSE and cumulative respirable dust exposure after log10 transformation. The specific ranges for CNSE tertiles were 0 and 1.40 to 2.29 for T1, 2.31 to 3.19 for T2, and 3.20 to 4.09 for T3. For cumulative respirable dust exposure, the ranges were 0.23 to 1.02 for T1, 1.02 to 1.81 for T2, and 1.81 to 2.60 for T3. The Low CNSE (T1) and Low Dust (T1) group was used as the reference group. Joint association models were adjusted for age, sex, body mass index, education, smoking history, alcohol consumption, physical activity, self-reported respiratory diseases, and working years, with cumulative respirable dust exposure excluded from these joint models.

Subgroup and interaction analyses were conducted to evaluate the associations of CNSE with lung function parameters across strata of age (<40 years versus ≥40 years), body mass index (<24 kg/m2 versus ≥24 kg/m2), education (primary or secondary education versus higher education and above), smoking history (no versus yes), alcohol consumption (no versus yes), physical activity (no versus yes), self-reported respiratory diseases (no versus yes), and working years (<20 years versus ≥20 years). In sensitivity analyses, stepwise exclusion analyses were performed by excluding female participants, then further excluding participants with a family history of respiratory diseases, and then additionally excluding participants with self-reported respiratory diseases.

Mediation and SNP interaction analyses

Mediation analysis was performed to assess the potential role of PER1 expression in the association between CNSE and lung function parameters.52 Statistical associations were required between the exposure X (CNSE) and the outcome Y (lung function parameters), between X and the mediator M (PER1 expression), and between M and Y. When these criteria were met, the mediating proportion of M in the association between X and Y was estimated using a mediation model.

SNP interaction analyses were performed to evaluate whether selected PER1 SNPs modified the association between CNSE and lung function parameters by including multiplicative interaction terms in adjusted models.

MR statistical analyses

The inverse variance weighted method was used as the primary MR estimator. MR-Egger and weighted median methods were used as sensitivity estimators. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test. Directional horizontal pleiotropy was assessed using the MR-Egger intercept test, and MR-PRESSO was used to detect and correct for potential outliers. Steiger directionality tests were used to assess the direction of significant MR associations. MR analyses were conducted using the TwoSampleMR and MRPRESSO packages in R. Primary instrument thresholds and sensitivity thresholds are described in the Method Details section and summarized in Table S20.

Transcriptomic analyses

For bulk transcriptomic analyses, independent Student’s t-tests were used to compare PER1 expression between disease and control groups after checking normality and homogeneity of variance.

For single-cell analyses, differentially expressed genes between high-PER1 and low-PER1 cells were identified using an adjusted p value <0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change >1. Functional enrichment analyses were performed for Gene Ontology, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes, and Hallmark gene sets as appropriate. Genes with a Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p value <0.05 in the scTenifoldKnk analysis were considered significantly perturbed.

Software and significance

Power analysis using PASS version 15.0.5 indicated that the exploratory subgroup of 228 participants yielded a statistical power of 0.83, exceeding the calculated requirement of 210 participants at α = 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.3 with the rms, mediation, ggplot2, TwoSampleMR, MRPRESSO, GEOquery, limma, and clusterProfiler packages. Single-cell transcriptome data processing used Python-based tools including Scanpy, Harmony, UMAP-learn, igraph, Matplotlib, and Seaborn, together with the scTenifoldKnk R package for virtual knockout analysis. Statistical significance was determined using two-tailed tests with p < 0.05 unless otherwise specified. Exact n values, definitions of n, and statistical tests are reported in the relevant figure legends, tables, and results sections.
