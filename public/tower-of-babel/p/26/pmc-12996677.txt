An exosome-biomimetic photothermal nanocarrier for IGF2BP2 siRNA delivery and enhanced ferroptosis in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Mengmeng Li, Die Hu, Congge Tan, Ou Peng, Qian Yang, Yekai Feng, Yuming Zhang, Yonghang Zhang, Bingbing Zhang, Lanjie Lei, Shisheng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHead and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) commonly develops treatment resistance, highlighting the necessity of novel therapeutic strategies. Although ferroptosis has emerged as a promising route, its regulatory determinants and effective gene delivery approaches in HNSCC remain poorly understood. In this study, we determined that the RNA-binding protein IGF2BP2 promotes ferroptosis resistance in HNSCC, at least in part by associating with increased NRF2 mRNA stability and sustaining the NRF2–SLC7A11/GPX4 antioxidant axis. Actinomycin D chase assays further support an IGF2BP2-dependent post-transcriptional regulation of NRF2 under erastin-induced ferroptotic stress. To therapeutically target this pathway, we engineered a biomimetic hybrid nanocarrier (si@PLE) by fusing M1 macrophage–derived exosomes with photothermally responsive cationic liposomes for the targeted delivery of IGF2BP2 small interfering RNA (siRNA). si@PLE exhibited favorable physicochemical properties and stability, and exosome–liposome fusion improved siRNA protection and tumor accumulation compared with that of the matched non-fused control, enabling enhanced tumor-site heating under identical irradiation conditions. In vitro and in vivo, si@PLE combined with near-infrared laser irradiation enhanced ferroptosis relative to either monotherapy, significantly suppressing tumor growth with a favorable safety profile. Collectively, these findings establish a biomimetic gene silencing–photothermal platform to sensitize HNSCC to ferroptosis by targeting the IGF2BP2–NRF2 axis.Keywords: Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, Gene therapy, Photothermal therapy, Ferroptosis

Introduction

Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) is characterized by high incidence, recurrence, and mortality [1,2]. Although standard treatments—including surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy—have improved outcomes in some patients, durable disease control remains limited by insufficient tumor specificity, treatment resistance, and dose-limiting toxicities [3,4]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop effective and highly targeted therapeutic strategies for HNSCC.

Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of lipid-regulated cell death driven by lipid peroxidation, has emerged as a promising vulnerability to overcome therapy resistance in multiple cancers [5]. Small interfering RNA (siRNA)-based gene therapy provides a precision approach to suppress oncogenic drivers via RNA interference [6]. Insulin-like growth factor 2 mRNA-binding protein 2 (IGF2BP2), a key oncogene in HNSCC, is associated with aggressive tumor behavior and poor prognosis [7,8]. Increasing evidence suggests that IGF2BP2 also participates in ferroptosis regulation across cancers [[9], [10], [11]], supporting the rationale of targeting IGF2BP2 to sensitize HNSCC to ferroptosis. Notably, NRF2 (NFE2L2) exhibits a context-dependent role in cancer. While transient NRF2 activation can protect normal epithelial cells from oxidative damage and genomic instability, sustained NRF2 hyperactivation in established tumors may instead promote metabolic adaptation, therapy resistance, immune evasion, and aggressive tumor behavior [12,13]. In HNSCC, alterations in the KEAP1/NFE2L2/CUL3 axis occur in a substantial subset of patients and have been linked to resistance to standard-of-care therapies [14]. In the context studied here, NRF2 activity primarily serves as a ferroptosis-evasion program that supports tumor persistence under stress.

Despite the therapeutic promise of siRNA, its translation is hindered by rapid nuclease degradation, limited serum stability, and inefficient cellular internalization, necessitating safe and efficient delivery vehicles [[15], [16], [17], [18]]. Nanocarrier-based delivery offers practical solutions, and cationic liposomes are widely used because they readily complex nucleic acids through electrostatic interactions and are scalable to manufacture [19]. In addition, photothermal therapy (PTT) enables externally controllable local hyperthermia under near-infrared (NIR) irradiation and can amplify oxidative stress, providing a rational means to enhance ferroptosis-associated lipid peroxidation and therapeutic response [20,21]. However, conventional synthetic nanocarriers often suffer from rapid immune clearance, limited tumor accumulation, and insufficient active targeting, which collectively restrict in vivo efficacy [22,23].

Exosomes are endogenous extracellular vesicles with favorable biocompatibility and low immunogenicity, and their surface proteins can confer intrinsic targeting properties [24]. In particular, M1 macrophage-derived exosomes (M1 Exos) may inherit tumor-homing features and proinflammatory, anti-tumor characteristics from their parent cells [25,26]. However, most previously reported exosome-based delivery systems primarily rely on passive biological targeting and lack external controllability after tumor accumulation, limiting their ability to achieve on-demand therapeutic amplification. Conversely, photothermal nanocarriers offer precise spatiotemporal control but often suffer from rapid clearance, insufficient tumor specificity, and suboptimal intracellular delivery efficiency when used alone. These complementary strengths motivate a hybrid strategy that fuses M1 Exos with photothermally responsive cationic liposomes to integrate biomimetic targeting and immune evasion with externally triggered therapeutic amplification. Importantly, because delivery outcomes are highly sensitive to formulation parameters and evaluation windows, cross-study comparisons across platforms can be misleading; therefore, we designed a composition-matched non-fused control (PTSL) to quantify the incremental contribution of exosome–liposome fusion to pharmacokinetics, tumor delivery, and downstream efficacy.

In this study, we developed a photothermal exosome–liposome hybrid nanocarrier (PLE) to co-deliver the photothermal agent cypate and siRNA targeting IGF2BP2 (si@PLE) for enhancing ferroptosis in HNSCC (Scheme 1). We systematically evaluated physicochemical properties, stability, cellular uptake and targeting, photothermal activation, and gene-silencing efficiency and further assessed antitumor efficacy and biosafety in vitro and in vivo. This study establishes a biomimetic gene delivery strategy integrating IGF2BP2 silencing with photothermal activation to enhance ferroptosis in HNSCC.

Results

IGF2BP2 Knockdown promotes ferroptosis in HNSCC via reduced NRF2 mRNA stability

To investigate the clinical relevance of IGF2BP2 in HNSCC, TCGA-HNSCC transcriptomic data were analyzed using GEPIA. IGF2BP2 was significantly upregulated in tumor tissues (Fig. S1A) and associated with poor prognosis (Fig. S1B). Consistently, western blotting confirmed elevated IGF2BP2 expression in five laryngeal carcinoma tissues (Fig. 1A) and in HNSCC cell lines (CAL27 and FADU) compared with normal controls (Fig. 1B).

To determine the functional role of IGF2BP2, we silenced IGF2BP2 in FADU and CAL27 cells using siRNA transfection, and knockdown efficiency was confirmed by western blotting and qRT-PCR (Fig. S2A–C). IGF2BP2 depletion significantly inhibited proliferation, colony formation, and migration (Fig. S3A–D; Fig. S4A–D), supporting its pro-tumorigenic role in HNSCC cells.

Given the emerging relevance of ferroptosis in therapy-resistant tumors, we next examined whether IGF2BP2 regulates ferroptosis sensitivity in HNSCC. CCK-8 assays showed that ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) partially but significantly restored the loss of cell viability induced by IGF2BP2 knockdown (Fig. 1C). Transmission electron microscopy further revealed ferroptosis-like mitochondrial alterations, including mitochondrial shrinkage and increased membrane density, which were alleviated by Fer-1 (Fig. 1D). We then assessed key anti-ferroptotic regulators and found that SLC7A11 and GPX4 protein levels were markedly decreased upon IGF2BP2 knockdown (Fig. 1E), indicating impaired antioxidant defense and increased vulnerability to lipid peroxidation.

To provide pathway-level support for these phenotypes, we performed RNA-seq in IGF2BP2-knockdown CAL27 cells and identified 3153 differentially expressed genes (2208 and 945 with upregulated and downregulated expressions, respectively; |log2FC| > 1.2, p < 0.05; Fig. S5A). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes analysis highlighted the enrichment of ferroptosis- and metabolism-related pathways, including glutathione and lipid metabolism, alongside signaling pathways such as PI3K-Akt, Wnt, and AMPK (Fig. S5B). GO biological process enrichment analysis indicated that IGF2BP2 knockdown was accompanied by broad changes in inflammatory signaling, extracellular matrix remodeling, coagulation/hemostasis, and regulation of cell death (Fig. S5C). Although the ferroptosis gene set was not significantly enriched by pre-ranked GSEA (NES = 1.09), it displayed a positive enrichment tendency, consistent with a potential shift toward a ferroptosis-related transcriptional state upon IGF2BP2 depletion (Fig. S5D). To assess siRNA specificity, we performed seed-based 3′UTR off-target prediction and integrated it with the RNA-seq dataset; no enrichment of predicted off-target transcripts among genes with significantly downregulated expressions was observed. Among all predicted off-target candidates, only 1.49% (61/4081; excluding IGF2BP2) were significantly downregulated (q < 0.05 and log2FC < −0.5), which was comparable to the genome-wide background downregulation rate (1.41%) and showed no enrichment (p = 0.33), supporting that the observed transcriptomic changes are primarily attributable to on-target IGF2BP2 silencing rather than widespread off-target repression (Fig. S6; Table S1).

NRF2 is a central regulator of antioxidant defense and ferroptosis sensitivity [13,27]. Therefore, we examined NRF2 expression and observed a pronounced reduction of NRF2 protein in IGF2BP2-silenced FADU and CAL27 cells (Fig. 1F). Actinomycin D (ActD) chase assays showed that IGF2BP2 depletion significantly accelerated NRF2 mRNA decay under basal conditions (Fig. 1G and H). This effect persisted and became more pronounced under erastin stress (Fig. 1I and J), supporting an IGF2BP2-dependent post-transcriptional control of NRF2 in the ferroptotic-stress context of HNSCC. Functionally, NRF2 overexpression counteracted the IGF2BP2 knockdown–induced decrease of SLC7A11 and GPX4, indicating that NRF2 is sufficient to restore the SLC7A11/GPX4 antioxidant defense suppressed by IGF2BP2 knockdown (Fig. 1K). For in vivo validation, we established a xenograft model and treated tumor-bearing mice with PBS or a chemically stabilized, in vivo-ready siRNA targeting IGF2BP2 at the same dose. Tumors from the siRNA-IGF2BP2 group showed reduced expression of IGF2BP2, NRF2, and GPX4 compared with controls (Fig. 1L), consistent with the in vitro findings.

Collectively, these results indicate that IGF2BP2 depletion sensitizes HNSCC cells to ferroptosis and downregulates the NRF2–SLC7A11/GPX4 antioxidant axis, at least in part through reduced NRF2 mRNA stability.

Physicochemical characterization and fusion validation of nanocarriers

UV–Vis spectroscopy confirmed cypate loading into liposomes, as indicated by the characteristic absorption peak at 785 nm in free cypate and cypate-loaded photothermal cationic liposomes (PTSL), which was absent in blank liposomes (Fig. S7A). A standard curve showed linearity between cypate concentration (0.5–10 μg/mL) and absorbance (A = 0.3722C + 0.2158, R2 = 0.99; Fig. S7B), and the encapsulation efficiency of cypate in PTSL was 95.3% (ultrafiltration centrifugation).

TEM imaging revealed generally spherical nanostructures (Fig. 2A). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) showed a stepwise increase in hydrodynamic diameter after exosome fusion and siRNA loading: PTSL, 93.46 ± 0.39 nm (PDI 0.242); PLE, 201.9 ± 4.30 nm (PDI 0.389); and si@PLE, 203.8 ± 5.51 nm (PDI 0.437) (Fig. 2B). Consistently, the zeta potential decreased from +33 mV (PTSL) to +27 mV (PLE) and further to +19 mV (si@PLE) (Fig. 2C), supporting successful fusion and siRNA complexation. si@PLE displayed good colloidal stability, with negligible size changes over 7 days at 37 °C in PBS and 10% FBS (Fig. S8A).

The siRNA loading capability of si@PLE was then assessed. The encapsulation efficiency (EE) and loading efficiency (LE) were determined to be 89.56% and 66.98%, respectively, indicating that the majority of siRNA was successfully incorporated into the nanocarrier. In vitro release studies were performed in PBS and 10% FBS to mimic physiological conditions. In PBS, approximately 22% of siRNA was released within the first 24 h, followed by gradual release reaching 67% by day 7. In 10% FBS, the initial release was slightly higher (24% on day 1), with cumulative release of 27–35% over days 2–3, and 68% by day 7 (Fig. S8B). These results demonstrate that si@PLE provides both efficient siRNA loading and sustained, controlled release under physiologically relevant conditions, which is critical for maintaining therapeutic efficacy prior to reaching the target site.

M1 polarization was confirmed by flow cytometry, showing a significantly increased proportion of CD86-positive cells in induced macrophages versus untreated controls (Fig. S9). Exosome–liposome fusion was validated by western blotting, which detected exosomal markers (CD9 and TSG101) and the absence of calnexin in M1 Exos and PLE (Fig. 2D), as well as by Coomassie-stained SDS-PAGE showing similar overall protein profiles between PLE and native M1 Exos (Fig. 2E). Dual-fluorescence confocal microscopy further demonstrated co-localization of DiO-labeled M1 Exos (green) and DiI-labeled PTSL (red), providing visual evidence of effective fusion (Fig. 2F). Collectively, these results confirm successful construction of the M1 exosome–liposome hybrid nanocarrier and retention of key exosome-associated protein signatures.

Assessment of the nanocarrier functionality

By leveraging the near-infrared fluorescence of cypate, cellular uptake of PTSL and si@PLE was assessed in multiple cell lines by confocal microscopy. PTSL (liposome-only, non-fused) served as the primary non-fused control to delineate the contribution of M1 exosome membrane fusion. Representative images showed enhanced uptake of si@PLE in HNSCC cells (CAL27 and FADU) compared with PTSL, whereas uptake in normal 16HBE cells appeared comparable between the two formulations (Fig. 3A). These observations suggest that exosome–liposome fusion promotes cancer-cell-preferential internalization rather than broadly increasing uptake in normal epithelial cells.

In vivo tumor delivery was next evaluated using live small-animal fluorescence imaging. Tumor-associated fluorescence was detectable as early as 2 h after intravenous injection of si@PLE, whereas PTSL showed minimal tumor enrichment under the same conditions (Fig. 3B). The si@PLE signal also persisted at later time points, indicating prolonged in vivo retention relative to PTSL. Ex vivo imaging further confirmed higher tumor accumulation of si@PLE with reduced hepatic signal compared with PTSL (Fig. 3C). Together, these data indicate that M1 exosome membrane fusion enhances tumor delivery and in vivo persistence relative to the composition-matched non-fused liposomal control.

Photothermal performance was then examined. Under 808 nm laser irradiation, si@PLE produced a greater temperature increase than free cypate at matched dye concentrations, consistent with improved dye dispersion in the liposomal formulation (Fig. 3D). In vivo infrared thermal imaging further demonstrated controllable mild hyperthermia at the tumor site following intravenous injection. Under identical irradiation parameters (808 nm, 2.0 W/cm2, 5 min), si@PLE achieved higher tumor heating than PBS and PTSL (Fig. 3E). Dose-selection experiments further showed increased tumor heating with higher si@PLE doses under the same irradiation settings (Fig. S10). To achieve mild hyperthermia without thermal ablation, we selected 2 mg/kg for subsequent in vivo studies, raising the tumor-center surface temperature to 42 °C after 5 min irradiation. To assess thermal safety margins, a multi-ROI analysis was performed in the si@PLE group by comparing temperatures at the tumor center, tumor margin, and adjacent normal skin outside the irradiation field. A clear temperature gradient was observed, with adjacent skin remaining substantially cooler than the tumor region (Fig. 3F). Consistently, the thermal maps in Fig. S11 show that the high-temperature region was primarily confined to the tumor area with limited temperature elevation in surrounding normal skin, in agreement with the temperature–time curves. Together, these results support a tumor-localized mild photothermal effect and suggest a favorable surface thermal safety margin under the irradiation regimen used. Immunohistochemical staining of HSP60 in tumor sections corroborated the in vivo photothermal response (Fig. 3G and H). The photothermal conversion efficiency (η) of si@PLE was further calculated from heating/cooling behavior, yielding an efficiency of 39% (Fig. 3I and J).

An electrophoretic mobility shift assay was used to optimize the complex formation conditions between PLE and siRNA. A nitrogen-to-phosphorus (N/P) ratio of 6, siRNA was largely retained in the loading wells, demonstrating complete complex formation and optimal encapsulation efficiency. However, when the N/P ratio was increased to 8 and 10, the siRNA retention signal in the loading wells significantly weakened, while the signal of free siRNA in the electrophoretic migration channels intensified (Fig. S12). Excessive cationic liposomes disrupt the structural stability of liposome-siRNA complexes, leading to partial siRNA release [28,29]. si@PLE protected siRNA from serum nuclease degradation (Fig. 3K) and enabled cytoplasmic delivery of Cy3-labeled siRNA in CAL27 cells (Fig. S13). Western blot analysis confirmed effective IGF2BP2 knockdown by si@PLE, with silencing efficiency comparable to Lipofectamine 3000 at the same siRNA dose (Fig. 3L).

Combined anti-tumor efficacy of nanocarriers

The biosafety and photothermal cytotoxicity of PLE were first evaluated by CCK-8 assays. PLE showed negligible dark cytotoxicity at concentrations up to 25 μg mL−1 (Fig. S14A), whereas NIR irradiation elicited potent photothermal killing with an IC50 of 10.2 μg mL−1 (Fig. S14B), supporting its suitability for photothermal therapy. Accordingly, subsequent combination experiments were performed under mild photothermal conditions (40–45 °C) to enable enhanced combined effect therapy while avoiding thermal ablation and minimizing nonspecific tissue damage.

Live/Dead staining showed the most apparent cell death in the si@PLE + laser group (Fig. 4A). Consistently, CCK-8 assays indicated that si@PLE + laser produced the strongest growth inhibition among groups (Fig. 4B). Colony formation assays further demonstrated that si@PLE + laser markedly reduced long-term clonogenic survival compared with photothermal therapy alone (PLE + laser) or gene therapy alone (si@PLE) (Fig. 4C and D), and wound-healing assays showed pronounced inhibition of cell migration (Fig. 4E and F).

To evaluate the underlying mechanism, we first assessed oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation. The combination treatment increased ROS levels and lipid peroxidation readouts relative to single treatments (Fig. 4G–K). Consistent with ferroptosis-associated biochemical hallmarks, malondialdehyde (MDA) levels were elevated and intracellular glutathione (GSH) was depleted in the si@PLE + laser group; both changes were partially reversed by Fer-1 (Fig. 4L and M). Moreover, western blotting showed that si@PLE + laser downregulated the expression of the ferroptosis-suppressive proteins SLC7A11 and GPX4 (Fig. 4N). Together, these results support that ferroptosis partially mediates the enhanced antitumor efficacy of si@PLE combined with laser irradiation.

To evaluate the in vivo antitumor efficacy of si@PLE combined with laser irradiation, a CAL27 xenograft model was established in nude mice. Mice were randomized into four groups (Con, PLE + L, si@PLE, and si@PLE + L) and received intravenous injections of PBS (Con), PLE (PLE + L), or si@PLE (si@PLE and si@PLE + L). Laser-treated groups were irradiated with an 808 nm laser (2 W/cm2, 5 min) at 2 h post-injection, and treatments were repeated three times per week; mice were euthanized on day 22 (Fig. 5A). The si@PLE + L group showed significantly reduced tumor growth compared with the other groups (Fig. 5B and C), while body weights remained comparable across groups (Fig. 5D). Immunofluorescence analysis further showed decreased IGF2BP2 signals in the si@PLE and si@PLE + L groups, and reduced Ki67 and GPX4 fluorescence in the si@PLE + L group (Fig. 5E–H), consistent with suppressed proliferation and enhanced ferroptosis-associated responses in vivo.

To address intertumoral heterogeneity, therapeutic efficacy was additionally assessed in a FADU xenograft model using the same dosing and irradiation schedule. Consistent antitumor activity of si@PLE + L was observed in the FADU model, as reflected by reduced tumor growth and comparable body weight profiles (Fig. S15A–C).

At the experimental endpoint, systemic biosafety was assessed via hemocompatibility testing, histology, hematology, and serum biochemistry. Hemolysis assays showed that si@PLE induced negligible hemolysis across the tested concentration range, indicating good hemocompatibility (Fig. S16). Histological examination of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) revealed no overt pathological lesions and showed comparable morphology among groups (Fig. 6A). Complete blood count parameters, including white blood cell (WBC), red blood cell (RBC), hemoglobin (HGB), and platelet (PLT) counts, were similar across groups (Fig. 6B–E), suggesting no apparent hematological toxicity. Serum biochemistry markers, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and creatinine (CREA), were also comparable among groups (Fig. 6F–I), indicating no evident hepatic or renal dysfunction. Collectively, these data support the good in vivo biocompatibility of si@PLE, with or without laser irradiation.

Discussion

Inducing ferroptosis is a promising route to overcome therapy resistance in HNSCC, but achieving tumor-selective induction remains difficult [30]. Nanomaterial-based strategies have therefore been widely explored for cancer therapy [[31], [32], [33]]. In this study, we identified IGF2BP2 as a determinant of ferroptosis sensitivity in HNSCC and developed an M1 macrophage–exosome/photothermal PLE to deliver siRNA-IGF2BP2. Under 808 nm irradiation, si@PLE further enhances antitumor activity, consistent with an enhanced effect of gene silencing and mild photothermal stress.

NRF2 has context-dependent roles in HNSCC. NRF2 activation can protect normal tissues from oxidative injury, whereas sustained NRF2 activity in tumors can reinforce antioxidant capacity and stress adaptation. In our models, the data support NRF2 as a ferroptosis-suppressive node. IGF2BP2 depletion reduced NRF2 abundance and accelerated NRF2 mRNA decay.Although IGF2BP2–NRF2 regulation has been implicated in multiple tumor types, our revision provides evidence that this post-transcriptional control is retained and strengthened under ferroptotic stress in HNSCC cells. Specifically, ActD chase assays performed under erastin-induced stress demonstrated a more pronounced IGF2BP2-dependent destabilization of NRF2 mRNA in both FaDu and CAL27 cells. These findings highlight a stress-contextualized RNA-stability mechanism relevant to ferroptosis sensitivity in HNSCC and support NRF2 as a functionally important node targeted by IGF2BP2 in this setting. Beyond SLC7A11 and GPX4, NRF2 also transcriptionally controls a broader cytoprotective program involved in glutathione metabolism, ROS detoxification, and iron/heme handling (e.g., GCLC/GCLM, NQO1, HMOX1, ferritin subunits), which can collectively shape ferroptosis sensitivity. Systematic profiling of NRF2 target engagement under ferroptotic stress in HNSCC will be an important direction for future work.

An effective siRNA delivery system should be efficient, stable, safe, and specific [34]. si@PLE displayed favorable physicochemical properties, including a uniform size distribution, serum stability, and reduced surface charge after fusion and siRNA loading, which is consistent with improved biocompatibility while maintaining strong nucleic-acid loading. si@PLE protected siRNA from serum degradation and achieved IGF2BP2 silencing comparable to Lipofectamine 3000 at matched siRNA dose, supporting its utility as an RNA-interference delivery platform. In addition to conventional biosafety readouts (body weight, histology, and serum biochemistry), we further evaluated gene-silencing specificity at the transcriptome level. By combining seed-based 3′UTR off-target prediction with RNA-seq, we found no enrichment of significant downregulation among predicted off-target candidates compared with the genome-wide background, supporting a favorable specificity profile for siRNA-IGF2BP2 in this context. Nevertheless, comprehensive assessment of off-target effects across additional siRNA designs and in vivo transcriptomic profiling in treated tumors and major organs will further strengthen translational safety evaluation in future studies.

Delivery efficiency and targeting specificity are major determinants of therapeutic performance [35]. Conventional nanocarriers often show limited tumor accumulation, motivating targeted design [36,37]. In this study, exosome–liposome fusion was quantified using a composition-matched, non-fused control (PTSL). Relative to PTSL, si@PLE showed stronger uptake in HNSCC cells with little apparent change in normal epithelial cells, and improved in vivo tumor accumulation with reduced hepatic signal. These paired comparisons support that exosome membrane fusion primarily improves delivery and tumor-site availability, which in turn strengthens downstream photothermal and gene-silencing outputs. Notably, in contrast to clinically used photothermal agents such as indocyanine green (ICG), which rely primarily on passive tumor accumulation, si@PLE combines tumor-targeted photothermal efficiency with siRNA-mediated gene silencing. This dual function enables mild, controlled local heating for therapeutic efficacy while minimizing nonspecific thermal damage, potentially enhancing both therapeutic specificity and translational applicability. Specifically, membrane-anchored proteins, such as integrins and chemokine receptors, facilitate active recognition and binding to ligands overexpressed on tumor cells, shifting the drug enrichment paradigm from passive accumulation reliant on the EPR effect to receptor-mediated active targeting [38]. In addition, exosomes function as native intercellular communication vehicles and are internalized through evolutionarily conserved endocytic pathways [39,40].

M1 Exos may offer additional biological advantages beyond delivery, including reported immunomodulatory effects in other settings [41,42]. Prior work has demonstrated M1 Exos-based immunotherapy strategies through exosome surface engineering to reprogram immune pathways [25]. In the present study, we confirmed successful fusion and retention of exosome-associated protein signatures, and we observed improved tumor delivery versus PTSL. However, immune remodeling was not directly profiled in the current xenograft setting, and immunological contributions should be addressed in future work.

IGF2BP2 is an oncogenic RNA-binding protein implicated across cancers. By engaging m6A-modified transcripts, IGF2BP2 regulates RNA metabolism and supports malignant phenotypes such as proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis [43,44]. In HNSCC, IGF2BP2 overexpression and prognostic associations have been reported [45], and functional evidence supports its role in tumorigenesis and metastasis [8].Consistent with these reports, IGF2BP2 knockdown in our models suppressed malignant phenotypes and increased ferroptosis sensitivity, at least in part through the NRF2–SLC7A11/GPX4 axis. Transcriptomic analyses further supported broad shifts in redox and metabolism-associated programs after IGF2BP2 depletion, consistent with a network-level role in ferroptosis-related stress handling.

Therapeutically, si@PLE-mediated IGF2BP2 silencing reduced ferroptosis-suppressive defenses. Mild photothermal activation increased oxidative stress. In our study design, the in vivo dose and irradiation conditions were selected to achieve controlled mild hyperthermia rather than ablation. Under these conditions, si@PLE + laser consistently outperformed either monotherapy arm in vitro and in vivo. ROS/lipid peroxidation signals increased, MDA rose with concomitant GSH depletion, and Fer-1 partially reversed these biochemical changes, supporting ferroptosis involvement. In parallel, SLC7A11/GPX4 levels decreased under the combination, aligning the phenotypic benefit with ferroptosis-associated mechanisms. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that a broader ferroptosis profiling panel would further deepen mechanistic resolution. Future work will incorporate iron metabolism and lipid remodeling regulators (e.g., TFRC/FTH1 and ACSL4/LPCAT3) and dynamic ROS measurements to refine the relative contribution of ferroptosis in this setting.

This study has some limitations. First, IGF2BP2 is a multi-target RNA-binding protein, and we therefore cannot exclude contributions from additional stress-responsive transcripts beyond NRF2 in our system. While our data support NRF2 as a functionally important mediator linking IGF2BP2 to ferroptosis resistance in HNSCC, systematic targetome-wide identification of IGF2BP2-associated RNAs under ferroptotic stress will be highly informative and is planned for future investigation. Second, benchmarking against other advanced siRNA delivery platforms was not performed; consequently, our results are anchored to the internal PTSL and current study design. Third, although multi-ROI thermal mapping suggests a favorable surface safety margin under the irradiation regimen used, histological assessment of peritumoral skin/muscle after repeated irradiation will be important to further evaluate potential collateral tissue effects. Fourth, formulation stability was assessed over a short timeframe, and long-term storage/handling and scalable manufacture remain to be established. Future work should focus on improving storage robustness, expanding translational safety profiling (including peritumoral tissue assessment under repeated irradiation), and testing rational combinations in clinically relevant models.

Conclusions

In this study, we engineered a PLE by fusing M1 macrophage–derived exosomes with photothermally responsive cationic liposomes for tumor-directed siRNA delivery. Using this platform, siRNA targeting IGF2BP2 was efficiently formulated (si@PLE) and applied as a combined gene-silencing/photothermal strategy for HNSCC.

Mechanistically, IGF2BP2 depletion sensitized HNSCC cells to ferroptosis and was associated with the suppression of the NRF2–SLC7A11/GPX4 antioxidant program. ActD chase assays further indicated that IGF2BP2 knockdown accelerated NRF2 mRNA decay, and this post-transcriptional regulation remained evident under erastin-induced ferroptotic stress. Under mild 808 nm irradiation, photothermal activation further increased oxidative stress, and si@PLE combined with laser irradiation exerted stronger antitumor effects than either modality alone in vitro and in vivo.

Collectively, the IGF2BP2–NRF2 axis contributes to ferroptosis resistance in HNSCC and supports PLE as a biomimetic platform for integrating targeted gene silencing with controllable photothermal amplification. This strategy provides a modular framework that may be extended to other therapy-refractory tumors by substituting disease-relevant siRNAs.

Materials and methods

Cell lines

Human hypopharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma FADU (RRID:CVCL_1218), tongue squamous cell carcinoma CAL-27 (RRID:CVCL_1107), and monocytic THP-1 (RRID:CVCL_0006) were acquired from Dalian Meilun Biotechnology Co., Ltd in January 2024. Human bronchial epithelial 16HBE14o- (16HBE, normal control; RRID:CVCL_0112) was purchased from Hunan Ourui Biotechnology Co., Ltd. in March 2023. All cell lines were from accredited vendors. Supplier-provided Cell Line Quality Certificates confirmed mycoplasma negativity via PCR prior to shipment.

Preparation of PTSL

PTSLs were prepared using a thin-film hydration method as previously described [46,47]. Briefly, DOTAP cationic lipid, soybean phosphatidylcholine, cholesterol, DSPE-PEG2K, and cypate were weighed at a mass ratio of 50:41:5.5:8:1 and fully dissolved in chloroform. All reagents were obtained from the following suppliers: DOTAP, soybean phosphatidylcholine, cholesterol, and DSPE-PEG2K from Xi'an Ruixi Bio (Xi'an, China), and cypate from MedChemExpress (China). The organic solvent was removed by rotary evaporation at 40 °C for 80 min to form a thin lipid film, which was hydrated with 4 mL deionized water at 40 °C for 60 min. The suspension was intermittently sonicated (20% power, 2 min) and extruded 10 times through 200 nm and 100 nm polycarbonate membranes. The product was dialyzed and adjusted to a final volume of 5 mL. Blank liposomes were prepared similarly without cypate.

Extraction and identification of M1 macrophage-derived exosomes

THP-1 cells during log phase were subjected to centrifugation and then transferred into six-well culture dishes. Phorbol ester (100 ng/mL) was added to induce macrophage differentiation. After 48 h, lipopolysaccharide (100 ng/mL) and interferon-γ (20 ng/mL) were added to polarize the macrophages toward the M1 phenotype, followed by further incubation for 24–48 h. Macrophage polarization was confirmed using flow cytometry. The supernatant of the M1 macrophages was collected and concentrated, and M1 Exos were extracted using ultracentrifugation. Exosomal protein levels were determined via BCA assay kit analysis.

Preparation of PLE

Using established methods, PTSL and M1 Exos were fused using a freeze-thaw approach. PTSL and M1 Exos were mixed in equal volumes. The mixture was frozen at −80 °C for 15 min and then thawed at 37 °C for 15 min. This cycle was repeated thrice. Hybrid nanovesicles (PLE) were formed through lipid membrane fusion between PTSL and M1 Exos.

Preparation of si@PLE

The siRNA-IGF2BP2 was dissolved in DEPC-treated (RNase-free) water and mixed with an appropriate amount of PLE. After incubation for 15 min, the mixture was sonicated for 10 min (5 s on, 5 s off, and 100 W power) to obtain the final si@PLE complex.

Photothermal performance and photothermal conversion efficiency

The photothermal conversion efficiency (η) of si@PLE was calculated using the heating and cooling curves. Following laser irradiation, temperature decay was recorded, and the cooling process was analyzed via linear fitting of ln(θ) vs time to obtain the system time constant (τs). Photothermal conversion efficiency was calculated according to the standard method [48].

Determination of siRNA-IGF2BP2 loading efficiency in PLE

The N/P ratio is a key parameter for evaluating siRNA loading efficiency in cationic liposomes. The stock solution of PLE was serially diluted to concentrations of 1.2, 2.5, 5, 10, 15, 20, and 25 μg/mL in 100 μL volumes. Each dilution was mixed with 100 μL of siRNA (5 μM) to form si@PLE complexes at N/P ratios of 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10. The resulting si@PLE solutions were mixed with loading buffer and analyzed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. After electrophoresis, the gels were imaged and photographed using a gel documentation system.

Determination of working concentration of PLE

CAL27 cells were plated in 96-well plates at a concentration of 5 × 103 cells per well. PTSL and PLE solutions were prepared in complete medium at equivalent concentrations of cypate: 1, 2, 5, 10, and 25 μg/mL. A 100 μL aliquot of each solution was pipetted into designated wells, with triplicate samples for every concentration tested. The control group received an equal volume of PBS. Following a 24 h incubation period, the medium was aspirated and cells were rinsed three times with PBS before being replenished with fresh complete medium. While PTSL and PLE samples underwent another 24 h incubation, the PLE + L group was irradiated to 808 nm laser (2 W/cm2, 5 min) prior to incubation. Following this, the CCK-8 compound was added, and the absorbance was assessed at 450 nm via a microplate reader.

Detection of oxidative stress and ferroptosis-related markers

Intracellular ROS and lipid peroxidation were assessed using commercial assay kits (Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, treated CAL27 cells were incubated with the corresponding fluorescent probes and analyzed by fluorescence microscopy or flow cytometry.

Intracellular MDA levels were measured using a lipid peroxidation assay kit (Beyotime, China), and reduced GSH levels were determined using a commercial GSH assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China), following the manufacturers’ protocols. Absorbance was measured using a microplate reader, and all values were normalized to total protein content determined by a BCA assay.

ActD chase assay under ferroptotic stress

Cells were transfected with si-NC or si-IGF2BP2 (50 nM). To model ferroptotic stress, cells were pretreated with erastin (2 μM, 6 h) or vehicle (DMSO). Transcription was then halted by adding ActD (2 μM) at time 0 while maintaining erastin/DMSO in the medium. Total RNA was collected at 0, 3, and 6 h and subjected to reverse transcription and qRT-PCR to quantify NRF2 mRNA. NRF2 mRNA abundance was calculated relative to the corresponding 0 h sample.

Seed-based off-target prediction and transcriptome integration

Seed-based miRNA-like off-targets of siRNA-IGF2BP2 were predicted using the guide (antisense) seed region (nt2–8) by scanning human 3′UTRs for canonical seed matches (8mer, 7mer-m8, and 7mer-A1). Predicted candidates were aggregated at the gene level using the strongest site type per gene and intersected with RNA-seq differential expression results. Genes with qValue <0.05 and log2FC < −0.5 were identified as those with significantly downregulated expressions. Fisher's exact test was employed to enrich genes with downregulated expressions among predicted candidates relative to the genome-wide background.

In vivo experiments

When CAL27 or FADU xenograft tumor volumes reached approximately 100 mm3, the tumor-bearing nude mice were randomly segregated into four distinct groups, each consisting of six rodents: Control, PLE + L, si@PLE, and si@PLE + L. Mice received 100 μL of PLE or si@PLE at a dose of 2 mg/kg (cypate-equivalent) via tail-vein injection. The PLE + L and si@PLE + L groups received 808 nm laser irradiation (2 W/cm2, 5 min) 2 h after injection, three times per week for 21 days. Body weight and tumor dimensions were recorded every three days. Tumor volume was determined using the formula (length × width2)/2. After 21 days of treatment, the tumor tissues were collected for fixation, paraffin embedding, H&E staining, and microscopic observation. At the experimental endpoint, blood samples were collected for routine blood analysis (WBC, RBC, HGB, and PLT) using an automated hematology analyzer (BC-2800Vet, Mindray, China). Serum was separated for biochemical analysis, including ALT, AST, BUN, and CREA, which was measured using a commercial assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China).

To evaluate in vivo tumor targeting, tumor-bearing mice were injected with 100 μL of PTSL or si@PLE via the tail vein. At 2, 6, and 12 h post-injection, the mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and fluorescence was observed and imaged using an in vivo imaging system. After the final imaging session, mice were euthanized and tumors and organs were extracted for fluorescence analysis. The fluorescence signal intensity was quantified using AniView software.

To evaluate the photothermal effects in live subjects, mice with tumors received intravenous tail vein injections of 100 μL solutions, either PBS, PTSL, or si@PLE. Following a 2-h incubation period, the tumor sites were exposed to an 808 nm laser. Infrared thermal imaging was conducted at regular intervals (0, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 min) during irradiation to monitor temperature changes. After imaging, tumor tissues were collected for HSP60 immunohistochemical staining and quantitative analysis using the ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (v10.0). All experiments were performed in three independent replicates, with data presented as mean ± SEM. For experiments with four groups (PBS, PLE + laser, si@PLE, and si@PLE + laser), one-way ANOVA was performed followed by Dunnett's multiple comparisons test to assess predefined primary comparisons. Two-tailed Student's t-test was used only when comparing two groups. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
