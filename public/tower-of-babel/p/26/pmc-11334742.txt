Humoral immunity and safety of respiratory virus vaccines in systemic lupus erythematosus population: a meta-analysis based on twenty-five observational studies
Authors: Xiao-Zheng Li, Ya-Wei Li, Cheng-Yang Huang, Jia-Ling Liu, Run-Ben Liu, Zhi-Xin Zhang, Jin-Zhu Yan, Chao Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSystemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), an extensive autoimmune disorder, compromises viral resistance and alters immune responses post respiratory virus vaccines. This study aims to assess immune response levels and safety in SLE patients following respiratory virus vaccines.MethodsExtensive searches, until 1 March 2024, were conducted using PubMed, EMBASE, and Cochrane Library. Outcomes, encompassing seroconversion rate (SCR), antibody and IgG titers, neutralizing antibodies, anti-spike antibodies, anti-receptor binding domain (RBD) IgG, and adverse events, were appraised.ResultsSixteen articles, comprising 25 observational studies, were included. SLE patients exhibited lower SCR (OR = 0.42, 95%CI: 0.26 to 0.69), antibody titers (SMD=-2.84, 95%CI: −3.36 to −1.61), and neutralizing antibodies (OR = 0.27, 95%CI: 0.13 to 0.56) compared to the healthy population post respiratory virus vaccines. Notably, differences were statistically insignificant for anti-RBD IgG (OR = 1.75, 95%CI: 0.10 to 29.42), IgG titers (SMD=-2.54, 95%CI: −5.57 to −0.49), anti-spike antibodies (OR = 0.35, 95%CI: 0.08 to 1.53), injection site discomfort (OR = 1.03, 95%CI: 0.52 to 2.06), fatigue (OR = 1.23, 95%CI: 0.74 to 2.03), fever (OR = 1.02, 95%CI: 0.64 to 1.63), localized reactions (OR = 0.69, 95%CI: 0.37 to 1.30), systemic reactions (OR = 1.00, 95%CI: 0.59 to 1.69), allergic reactions (OR = 5.11, 95%CI: 0.24 to 107.10), self-reported vaccination-related adverse events (OR = 1.61, 95%CI: 0.56 to 4.63), and disease flares after vaccination (OR = 1.00, 95%CI: 0.14 to 7.28).ConclusionDespite the reduced immune response and host protection in SLE patients post-Corona Virus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) and influenza vaccines compared to the healthy population, safety profiles are comparable. Therefore, it is recommended that SLE patients receive COVID-19 and influenza viral vaccines to fortify their resistance.

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is an autoimmune inflammatory connective tissue disease primarily affecting young women and involving multiple organs. This condition compromises the body’s ability to resist external pathogens [1–4]. SLE can damage the blood group system, leading to symptoms of anemia. Additionally, it may affect the skin, mucous membranes, kidneys, respiratory system, and cardiovascular system [5]. Notably, infection ranks as the second leading cause of death in SLE patients [6,7]. Being inherently immunocompromised, individuals with SLE have a higher likelihood of infections than the general population. Infections, when they occur, can significantly exacerbate the disease, potentially leading to severe complications. In the context of influenza and Corona Virus Disease 2019 (COVID-19), SLE patients may experience heightened damage to multiple organ systems, elevating the risk of developing severe and life-threatening conditions. Current research indicates that factors influencing the quality of life in SLE patients encompass personal health status, psychological well-being, personal beliefs, social relationships, and environmental factors. These factors collectively contribute to the diminished quality of life experienced by individuals with SLE [8–10].

The work conducted by the Global Alliance for Rheumatism revealed that in COVID-19 and influenza virus infections among patients with SLE, a higher proportion of SLE patients required hospitalization compared to those who were not admitted [11]. Respiratory virus vaccines are recognized as highly effective measures for controlling virus spread and reducing associated morbidity [12,13]. Despite this, concerns arose regarding potential risks such as side effects, exacerbation, and diminished efficacy of these vaccinations in SLE patients due to underlying autoimmunity and the use of immunosuppressive drugs [13]. Several studies have demonstrated that administering virus vaccinations to stabilize the SLE population can reduce the incidence of severe complications caused by viral infections [6]. Investigations on H1N1 and H3N2 influenza vaccinations in the SLE population indicated safety, significantly increasing the seroconversion rate (SCR) [13,14]. Both Comirnaty and CoronaVac demonstrated the ability to significantly enhance SCR in SLE patients [11]. However, one study, focusing on BNT162b2, arrived at an opposite conclusion, failing to show a distinct advantage for vaccination based on their data [15]. This study performs a statistical analysis of the immunogenicity and safety of vaccinations, aiming to identify suitable COVID-19 and influenza vaccines for SLE patients. This information is crucial to address potential barriers that might deter SLE patients from opting for influenza and COVID-19 viral vaccines, thereby mitigating the risk of severe complications associated with viral infections.

With the objective of improving the well-being of individuals grappling with SLE during influenza and COVID-19 outbreaks, this study endeavors to appraise the immune response and safety of influenza and COVID-19 viral vaccines in SLE patients as opposed to the general population. The central metric under scrutiny is the SCR. Positive findings from this analysis have the potential to allay concerns among hesitant individuals, fostering a willingness to undergo vaccination against both influenza and COVID-19 under the guidance of their healthcare provider.

Methods

The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement [16] were employed for this systematic review and meta-analysis.

Search strategy

All studies were obtained by searching PubMed, EMBASE, and Cochrane Library for studies published up to 1 March 2024. Related MeSH terms, including ‘systemic lupus erythematosus’, ‘respiratory virus vaccines’, ‘COVID-19 vaccinations’ and ‘influenza vaccinations’, were searched.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were included for the following reasons: (1) Population: People receiving respiratory virus vaccines, including H1N1 viral vaccine, H3N2 viral vaccine, Hsw1N1 viral vaccine, Hong Kong viral vaccine, B/Malaysia/2506/2004 viral vaccine, B/Jiangsu/10/2003 (type B) viral vaccine, SARS-CoV-2 viral vaccine, BNT162b2 viral vaccine, Comirnaty viral vaccine and CoronaVac viral vaccine. (2) Exposed group: SLE population. (3) Non-exposed group: Healthy population. (4) Outcomes: Humoral responses and adverse events. Humoral responses were defined as SCR, antibody titers, IgG titers, neutralizing antibody, anti-spiking antibody and anti-RBD antibody. Adverse events were defined as injection site pain, fatigue, fever, local reaction, systemic reaction, allergic reaction, self-reported vaccination related adverse events, and disease flares after vaccination. The disease flares after vaccination were defined as an increase in Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index 2000 values (SLEDAI-2k) that patients with higher SLEDAI scores experience more severe forms of disease. (5) Study type: Observational study.

Studies were excluded for the following reasons: (1) Patients with acute infection or fever (>38 °C) or body temperature ≥37.8 °C on the day of vaccination; (2) Previous allergic reactions to vaccination ingredients or eggs; (3) A history of neurological disorders, such as Guillain-Barre syndrome or demyelinating syndrome; (4) Blood transfusion within 6 months; (5) Prior vaccination with any live vaccination for 4 weeks or any live the inactivated vaccination 2 weeks before study entry failed to complete the protocol.

Study selection and data extraction

Two reviewers independently screened the study, and a third reviewer was invited for consultation on uncertain aspects of the included studies. Information extracted from studies that met the inclusion criteria was SCR, antibody titers, IgG titers, neutralizing antibodies, anti-spike antibodies, anti-receptor binding domain (RBD) IgG, number of adverse events after respiratory virus vaccines, disease flares after vaccination and number of persons with adverse events after respiratory virus vaccines. Changes in SCRs by type of respiratory virus vaccines, number of vaccines, therapeutic regimens for SLE, history of COVID-19 and race were analyzed. Continuous variables were expressed as median and interquartile range, and data were extracted by estimating the sample mean and standard deviation according to the approximate formula for optimal weights. Antibody titers and IgG titers were expressed as geometric means.

Quality assessment

The Risk Of Bias In Non-randomised Studies-of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool [17] was employed for quality assessment. The quality of the study was assessed in seven parts: subject selection bias (includes whether study subjects were selected based on individual characteristics observed after the initiation of the intervention), bias of confounding (includes whether there are potential confounding factors in the study that affect the effect of the intervention), bias during randomization (includes whether the subjects are randomly selected and allocation hiding), bias from the intended intervention (included whether the subjects were aware of the intervention they received during the study, whether the researchers were aware of the intervention received by the study subjects during the study, whether there were unusual intervention changes, whether intervention changes were imbalanced between groups and could have affected study outcomes and whether there were study subjects who were not analyzed according to their assigned intervention), bias in outcome measurement (includes outcome assessors were aware of the intervention received and knowing whether the intervention received by the study subjects will have an effect on the outcome measure), bias of missing outcome data (includes whether study outcome data are available for all or most participants, the proportion of local missing data and the reasons for missing data were balanced between groups and whether subjects were excluded due to missing data for certain variables) and bias in the selective reporting (includes whether multiple outcome measures were reported selectively and whether the results of multiple analyses were reported selectively) of the outcome.

Statistical analysis

Dichotomous and continuous outcomes were expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and standardized mean differences (SMDs) with 95%CIs. The heterogeneity of the meta-analysis was measured by calculating I2. When I2 was less than 40%, a fixed-effect model was adopted. When I2 was greater than or equal to 40%, a random-effects model was adopted. Subgroup analyses with SC rate as the primary outcome were performed for possible influencing factors, including race, type of vaccine received, number of vaccinations, therapeutic regimens for SLE, and history of COVID-19 infection conditions. Publication bias was detected using funnel plots. All statistical analyses were conducted using RevMan 5.4.

Results

Study selection

A total of 1386 studies were screened by database search. After exclusion due to population, intervention, outcome and other reasons, a total of 16 literatures [11–15,18–28] with 25 studies were involved in this study. Figure 1 showed the specific screening process.

Study characteristics

A total of 2053 SLE patients and 1813 healthy population were included in the study. All participants were vaccinated with different types of influenza vaccination and COVID-19 vaccination. Participants were from in Brazil, the United States of America, Israel, France, Canada, China, the Netherlands, and Poland. The mean SLE activity index ranged from 0.71 to 5.10. The vaccination types included in the study were H1N1 viral vaccine, H3N2 viral vaccine, Hsw1N1 viral vaccine, Hong Kong viral vaccine, B/Malaysia/2506/2004 viral vaccine, B/Jiangsu/10/2003 (type B) viral vaccine, SARS-CoV-2 viral vaccine, BNT162b2 viral vaccine, Comirnaty viral vaccine and CoronaVac viral vaccine. The racial aspects of the population include White race, Black race, Hispanic race, Asian and mixed race. The population in eight studies received one vaccination, nine studies received two vaccinations, and one study received three vaccinations. The follow-up time of healthy population and SLE patients who were observed after respiratory virus vaccines ranged from 21 days to 1 year. Four vaccination variants, including alpha, β, delta and omicron variants, had been described in only one study in this study. Three of the studies in this study mentioned that the population had COVID-19 before receiving the vaccination. Quality evaluation

The evaluation results of this study were shown in Supplementary Table 1. There was no potential confounding bias observed in any of the outcomes across the 16 articles encompassing 25 studies. The selection of study subjects was not based on the observation after the intervention began, and for most subjects, the time of intervention and follow-up was not consistent. The SLE population was explicitly vaccinated in the study, and the intervention group was not recorded at the beginning of the intervention. The intervention in the intervention group may have been balanced, with the vast majority of subjects adhering to and successfully implementing the assigned intervention. Knowledge of intervention related knowledge did not affect outcome measures, and there were no systematic errors in outcome measures related to intervention receipt. The outcomes of all subjects in the study were obtainable, and there was no selective reporting of multiple outcome measures for a given outcome or of the effect of multiple analyses of the intervention-outcome relationship.

Outcomes of humoral response

Seroconversion rate

A total of 9 literatures [11,13–15,18,22,24,25,28] with 13 studies, including 1322 SLE patients and 543 healthy subjects, were included in SCR. Results of meta-analysis based on all subjects received different vaccinations, including influenza vaccination and COVID-19 vaccination. Compared with healthy population, there was a significantly difference SCRafter respiratory virus vaccines, it was significantly lower than the healthy population level (OR = 0.42, 95%CI: 0.26–0.69) in Figure 2.

Forest plots of seroconversion rate in SLE patients and healthy population. Note. SLE: Systemic lupus erythematosus.

Antibody titers

The results of the meta-analysis of antibody titers were based on all subjects receiving different vaccinations, including influenza vaccinations and COVID-19 vaccinations. A total of 3 literatures [18,23,28] with 4 studies reported antibody titers levels, a total of 1065 SLE patients and 296 healthy population were tested for antibody titers. Compared with the healthy population, there was a significant difference in the antibody titers, it was significantly lower than the healthy population level (SMD = −2.48, 95%CI: −3.36 to −1.61) in Figure 3.

Forest plots of antibodies titers in SLE patients and healthy population. Note. SLE: Systemic lupus erythematosus.

IgG titers

The results of the meta-analysis of IgG titers were based on influenza and COVID-19 vaccinations received by all subjects. Two studies were described IgG titers in 2 studies [23,28] involving 314 SLE patients and 78 healthy subjects. Compared with the healthy population, there was no significant difference in the IgG titers (SMD = −2.54, 95%CI: −5.57 to 0.49) in Figure 4, which was not statistically significant.

Forest plots of IgG titers in SLE patients and healthy population. Note. SLE: Systemic lupus erythematosus.

Neutralizing antibodies

Neutralizing antibody levels were reported in 2 studies [11,28] involving 297 SLE patients and 108 healthy subjects. The results of the meta-analysis of IgG titers were based on influenza and COVID-19 vaccinations received by all population. There was a significant difference in neutralizing antibody levels between SLE patients and healthy population (OR = 0.27, 95%CI: 0.13–0.56), it was significantly lower than the healthy subject level in Figure 5.

Forest plots of neutralizing antibodies in SLE patients and healthy population. Note. SLE: Systemic lupus erythematosus.

Anti-spike antibodies

Only one study [23,26] reported the level of anti-spike antibodies, with 60 SLE patients and 35 healthy population. Results of meta-analysis were based on different vaccinations administered by all subjects, including COVID-19 vaccination. Compared with the healthy population, the level of anti-spike antibody in the SLE patients was no significant difference (OR = 0.17, 95%CI: 0.06–0.49) in Figure 6.

Forest plots of anti-Spike antibodies in SLE patients and healthy population. Note. SLE: Systemic lupus erythematosus.

Anti-RBD IgG

Antibody response was reduced in SLE patients compared with healthy population. Anti-RBD IgG levels were analyzed in two studies [15,26], involving 65 patients with SLE and 36 healthy subjects. There was no significant difference in anti-RBD IgG levels between SLE patients and healthy population after respiratory virus vaccines (OR = 1.75, 95%CI: 0.10–29.42) in Figure 7.

Forest plots of anti-receptor binding domain antibodies in SLE patients and healthy population. Note. SLE: Systemic lupus erythematosus.

Fatigue

A total of 4 literatures [11,14,19,28] described the adverse effects of fatigue after respiratory virus vaccines, including 628 SLE patients and 297 healthy population. Compared with the healthy population, there was no significant difference in the adverse effects of fatigue caused by vaccination injection between patients in the SLE patients and healthy population (OR = 1.23, 95%CI: 0.74–2.03) in Table 2, which was not statistically significant.

A total of 7 studies [12,14,18–20,26,28] reported adverse reactions to fever after respiratory virus vaccines, including responses from 1825 SLE patients and 864 healthy subjects in ten studies. Compared with the healthy population, there was no significant difference in the adverse effects of vaccination-induced fever between SLE patients and the healthy population (OR = 1.02, 95%CI: 0.64–1.63) in Table 2, with not statistically significant.

Local reaction

A total of 3 studies [14,18,28] reported local adverse reactions following respiratory virus vaccines, including responses from 4 studies involving 1100 SLE patients and 367 healthy population. Compared with the healthy population, there was no significant difference in local reactions caused by vaccination injection between SLE patients and the healthy population (OR = 0.69, 95%CI: 0.37–1.30) in Table 2, which was not statistically significant.

Systemic reaction

A total of 3 studies [14,18,28] reported systemic reactions following respiratory virus vaccines, including responses from 4 studies in 1100 patients with SLE and 367 healthy subjects. Compared with the healthy population, there was no significant difference in systemic response caused by vaccination injection between SLE patients and the healthy population (OR = 1.00, 95%CI: 0.59–1.69) in Table 2, which was not statistically significant.

Allergic reaction

Only one study [12] reported allergic reactions caused by respiratory virus vaccines, including reactions from 188 SLE patients and 190 healthy subjects. This was also the first dosage of vaccination administered in this study. Compared with the healthy population, there was no significant difference in allergic reactions caused by respiratory virus vaccines between SLE patients and the healthy population (OR = 5.11, 95%CI: 0.24 –107.10) in Table 2, which was not statistically significant.

Self-reported vaccination related adverse events

Only one study [26] reported self-reported vaccination-related adverse events following vaccination, including responses from 60 patients with SLE and 35 healthy population. Compared with the healthy population group, there was no significant difference in self-reported vaccination related adverse events due to respiratory virus vaccines between SLE patients and the healthy population group (OR = 1.61, 95%CI: 0.56–4.63) in Table 2, which was not statistically significant.

Disease flares after vaccination

In this study, two investigations [11,14] focused on SLEDAI-2K. However, only one [14] of them did not provide pertinent data regarding the SLE population before vaccination, presenting only SLEDAI-2K scores post-vaccination. The other study [11] indicated no discernible change in SLEDAI-2K scores (OR = 1.00, 95%CI: 0.14–7.28) within the SLE population both before and after vaccination in Figure 8, which was not statistically significant.

Stratification analyses

Race

At the same time, a subgroup analysis of the SCRs after respiratory virus vaccines was performed by race. There were 3 studies [20,23,28] reported relevant information, including White, Black, Spanish, Asian and other population. Among different race groups, White (OR = 0.70, 95%CI: 0.45–1.07), Black (OR = 1.62, 95%CI: 0.54–4.81), Hispanic (OR = 0.95, 95%CI: 0.26–3.48), Asian (OR = 0.83, 95%CI: 0.34–2.03), Other (OR = 0.07, 95%CI: 0.00–12.09), SCRs of SLE population after vaccination were not significantly different from those of healthy population after vaccination in Table 3.

Discussion

SLE is an enduring, widespread connective tissue ailment of uncertain origin that can impact numerous body systems. The body generates a substantial number of autoantibodies, leading the immune system to assail its own tissues, resulting in damage to various organs and tissues throughout the body [5,29]. The manifestations of the disease are diverse and may fluctuate in severity. It is more prevalent in women of reproductive age, typically between 20 and 40 years old [3]. Effective disease management can contribute to prolonged survival; however, a poor prognosis may ensue without adequate control. In the face of influenza and COVID-19 infections, the SLE population, when infected with these two viruses, may experience disease exacerbation [30]. In contrast to prior meta-analyses, this study executed a meticulous stratified analysis, observing factors such as race, vaccine types, dosages of respiratory virus vaccines, therapeutic regimens for SLE, and a history of COVID-19 infections, to comprehensively assess the impact on SLE and SCR. This investigation aimed to scrutinize SLE patients in the context of influenza, evaluating the effectiveness and safety of novel vaccinations. Consequently, to bolster the resistance of SLE patients against influenza and COVID-19, it becomes imperative for them to receive both vaccinations.

The SCR, representing the proportion of immunologically active individuals generating protective antibody levels [22,31–33], was lower in SLE patients than in the healthy population. This difference may be linked to the compromised physical fitness of SLE patients. Following respiratory virus vaccines, the immunization prompts the body’s adaptive immune response, stimulating the production of specific protective antibodies. However, the levels of these antibodies in SLE patients were not as robust as those observed in the healthy population, resulting in diminished immunity. Several studies have indicated that the SCR in SLE patients post respiratory virus vaccines was lower compared to their healthy counterparts. Nonetheless, the immunogenicity of the vaccinations approached the levels recommended by CPMP guidelines, signifying the effectiveness of influenza and COVID-19 vaccinations in safeguarding SLE patients [6]. Given the susceptibility of SLE patients to exacerbations and severe complications following influenza infections, opting for respiratory virus vaccines emerges as a judicious choice for protecting SLE patients during the influenza and COVID-19 pandemic.

The antibody titers serves as a quantification of the minimum concentration (maximum dilution) essential for an antibody to identify a specific epitope, typically denoted as the highest dilution still yielding positive results [28,34]. The assessment of antibody titers is commonly conducted using ELISA [15,34]. In this study, no definitive conclusions were drawn regarding IgG titers, representing the concentration of antibodies in IgG, for the benefit of SLE patients. The reduced antibody and IgG titers in SLE patients, compared to healthy individuals, may be attributed to the weakened constitution of individuals with SLE. This compromised physical state can result in a prolonged antibody production time, leading to a lower simultaneous generation of corresponding protective antibodies during vaccination. Consequently, the antibody and IgG titers in SLE patients are not as elevated as those observed in healthy individuals. Contrarily, additional studies have demonstrated an elevation in antibody titers and enhanced immune responsiveness following influenza and COVID-19 vaccinations. This observed increase could potentially be linked to the promptness of administering vaccinations and the ongoing immunosuppressive treatments of SLE patients [6].

Neutralizing antibodies serve as a pivotal defense mechanism generated by the body in response to natural pathogen infections, with initial indications highlighting their correlation with an anti-COVID-19 effect. Guidelines underscore the potential of neutralizing antibodies to impede virus infection of target cells, with monoclonal antibodies emerging as a focal point in this research avenue [35]. This study observed an augmentation in neutralizing antibodies post-respiratory virus vaccinations, enhancing SLE patients’ resilience against COVID-19 infection [11,28]. Presently, research has revealed that the SARS-COV-2 virus utilizes the spike protein, including Flynn’s protease on its surface, to invade infected cells by binding to the ACE2 protein on the surface of human cells, assisted by serine protease TMPRSS2, achieving infection. The immune response to viral stimulation results in the production of corresponding antibodies that bind to antigens, thereby neutralizing the virus. Studies indicate variations in neutralizing antibody activity among different binding epitopes, with different vaccinations generating distinct neutralizing antibodies by binding to different epitopes. For instance, CoronaVac viral vaccine and Omicron viral vaccine exhibited heightened efficacy after the initial dose [36]. The exploration of these key proteins holds promise for unveiling novel avenues in clinical diagnosis and treatment of the novel coronavirus [26,28,30,34]. Nevertheless, this study did not arrive at an ideal conclusion, possibly due to insufficient data supporting definitive findings.

Patients with COVID-19 develop antibodies against the spike protein, blocking SARS-CoV-2 infection. The spike protein, a primary envelope protein of SARS-CoV-2, comprises S1 and S2 subunits, with the NTD forming part of the discernible domain of the S1 subunit. Research has indicated that antibodies directed against specific regions of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein NTD can directly enhance the binding of ACE2 to the spike protein, consequently amplifying the infectivity of SARS-CoV-2 [37]. Therefore, antibodies against the spike protein played a key role in the body’s defense against COVID-19 infection.

RBD is a region on the spike protein on the surface of the coronavirus particle coat, which is where the virus’s specificity lies. Anti-RBD IgG is an antibody produced by the IgG against the novel coronavirus after the virus invades the body. After injection of respiratory virus vaccination, the body produces corresponding antibodies, which can prevent the binding between the receptor binding domain RBD of the S protein of the virus and the human cell receptor ACE2. Studies had shown that controlling the conformation of RBD could enhance the recognition of antibodies to more epitope structures, which played an important role in the production of antibodies to enhance the body’s immunity after respiratory virus vaccines [37]. Nevertheless, this study did not observe an elevation in anti-RBD IgG concentration in SLE patients following COVID-19 vaccination. This discrepancy may be linked to the influence of SLE disease itself on antibody production and the duration of antibody production based on different vaccination types, necessitating additional data to substantiate our conclusion [15,26].

Despite advancements in SLE treatment, adverse outcomes still accompany SLE. Seven adverse reactions induced by respiratory virus vaccines were gleaned from the encompassed studies, encompassing injection site pain, fatigue, fever, local reactions, systemic reactions, allergic reactions, and self-reported vaccination-related adverse events [38,39]. In some studies, adverse effects were more common in patients with SLE than in healthy controls [14]. But all the adverse effects were manageable, and the symptoms were not long-lasting, but for patients with SLE, happiness was diminished [12]. The increased incidence of mild side effects in some reports may be due to the vaccination [36]. Drug-related adverse events were inevitable in patients with SLE, but it would be beneficial if respiratory virus vaccines during influenza and COVID-19 epidemics mitigated or prevented the more severe damage caused by the disease [40].

The study comprised five races, including White, Black, Hispanic, Asian, and mixed races. Regrettably, based on the incorporated data, there is no evidence suggesting that different ethnic groups exhibit a superior SCR after respiratory virus vaccines. In other studies, a higher prevalence [29] had been observed in adults, women, and non-white. In terms of SLE activity index, Hispanics and Africans had higher disease activity compared with white race. The first slow decline in disease activity after disease onset was among Hispanics, followed by African [41], and the decline was more rapid among white [29].

The vaccinations considered in this study encompassed both influenza and COVID-19 vaccinations. Typically, the immune response triggered by these vaccines is humoral in nature. Post-respiratory virus vaccination, the human immune system identifies the invading virus, initiating the production of antibodies. These antibodies, natural proteins generated by the immune system, play a crucial role in disease resistance, swiftly combating bacteria or fortifying the body’s protective mechanisms. It’s noteworthy that certain vaccinations necessitate multiple injections spread over weeks or months. By doing so, it can build a memory of the pathogen to rapidly combat it on future exposure and reduce the likelihood of infection. Some studies had reached different conclusions on respiratory virus vaccines, such as different types of vaccinations or inconsistent opinions, which may be related to the condition of SLE patients and the use of drugs [14,15,24,26,28]. In order to avoid adverse reactions after respiratory virus vaccines or aggravate the disease, it was necessary to evaluate the condition of SLE patients and the drugs they were using before respiratory virus vaccines. In addition, SLE disease activity needed to be assessed to prevent more severe consequences in ineligible patients.

The outcomes of this study suggest that vaccinating SLE patients during influenza and COVID-19 outbreaks is generally safe, although the effectiveness appears to be somewhat lower than in healthy individuals. Furthermore, certain medications taken by SLE patients may influence humoral immunity, although the studies included in this article did not definitively identify which drugs have such an effect. The study covered nine vaccine types. The H1N1 viral vaccine demonstrated short-term safety in SLE patients [13,18,22,25]. In comparison with healthy individuals, SLE patients exhibited a lower humoral immune response to the H1N1 viral vaccine, accompanied by mild adverse reactions. Some studies suggested that antimalarials may enhance the response to H1N1 in SLE patients undergoing immunosuppressive therapy [18]. The Hong Kong viral vaccine was considered safe in SLE patients with quiescent disease, albeit with a therapeutic effect lower than that in healthy individuals [22]. Antibody responses to A/Victoria/3/75(H3N2) after vaccination were lower in both healthy individuals and SLE patients compared to Hsw1N1 after the first vaccination [19]. However, it’s crucial to note that the results may be influenced by the infection of the influenza strain and the presence of antibodies against these strains after vaccination with Hsw1N1 viral vaccine, emphasizing the need to exclude such factors when necessary. The H3N2 viral vaccine did not yield inconsistent results in the study, but some investigations suggested that SLE patients exhibited a weaker response to vaccination than healthy individuals, with no alteration in underlying disease activity [19,22,27]. B/Malaysia/2506/2004 and B/Jiangsu/10/2003 (type B) represent two types of influenza B virus [25,27], but due to the lack of relevant data, no difference in results was obtained in this study. Nonetheless, the two vaccines were mentioned in these two studies as safe. Overall, the immunogenicity and safety of SARS-CoV-2 viral vaccines, including BNT162b2, comirnaty, and CoronaVac, were deemed satisfactory in SLE patients [11,20,23]. However, concerns persist regarding a potentially ineffective immune response, autoantibody production, and increased disease activity.

For children aged 3–9 years who have received influenza vaccination in less than two dosages, two dosages of quadrivalent influenza vaccination were required with a 4-week interval between them. People over the age of 9 years only need to be vaccinated once a year with either a trivalent or quadrivalent vaccination [14,19,22,25,42]. Inactivated vaccinations usually require two dosages, recombinant protein vaccinations require three dosages, and adenovirus vector vaccination only requires one dosage [12,15,43]. The dosage and type of respiratory virus vaccines vary according to age groups among healthy individuals. A study revealed that, initially, a low-immunity group comprising 5% of SLE patients exhibited a lower SCR compared to controls after the first vaccination dose. However, SCR surpassed 80% after the administration of two vaccine doses [11]. However, in some studies, two doses of vaccination did not reach the same conclusion. This may be related to factors such as the type of vaccination, which needed to be supported by detailed research data.

Several drugs commonly used in the included studies include non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, antimalarial drugs, glucocorticoids, and immuno­suppressants. Among antimalarial drugs, chloroquine or hydroxychloroquine were frequently used. Immunomodulatory drugs, such as cyclophosphamide, azathioprine, methotrexate, and cyclosporine A, were common among immunosuppressants. It is noteworthy that other studies have indicated that hydroxychloroquine appears to enhance SCR [28], antimalarial drugs improve vaccination immune activity [18,43], and the use of immunosuppressive drugs leads to better humoral immunity in SLE patients after respiratory virus vaccines [11,24]. This should require the inclusion of studies that study the effects of drugs on SCRs. Previous studies had observed good uptake of the BNT162b2 vaccination but had also shown an inverse association between MMF and MTX use and BNT162b2 antibody responses [26]. Meanwhile, immunosuppressive drugs and high dosages of glucocorticoids had been associated with mortality in SLE population in other studies, but the use of antimalarial drugs had prolonged survival in the SLE population [43]. There was a complex relationship between these drugs and the humoral immunity that arises after vaccination. One possible reason for this discrepancy was ignorance of SLE itself. The relationship between the use of immunosuppressive therapy in patients with SLE and systemic lupus erythematosus disease was ambiguous, so it was not easy to identify factors that may have blunted responses to vaccinations. Further studies are needed to examine the relationship between immunosuppressive therapy, respiratory virus vaccines, and SLE itself.

Following respiratory virus vaccination, SLE patients exhibited distinct variations in seroconversion, antibody titers, and neutralizing antibodies compared to healthy individuals, with values generally lower in the former group. Beyond the influence of the respiratory virus vaccine, certain drugs tailored to the unique conditions of SLE patients, such as hydroxychloroquine [28], have been identified to positively impact humoral immunity. It is noteworthy that this study did not explicitly demonstrate the impact of medications used by SLE patients on humoral immunity. The treatment approach for SLE patients varies based on their SLEDAI score, showcasing differences between humoral immunity and SLEDAI scores. The variability in SLEDAI scores dictates the utilization of different dosages and the selection of distinct immunosuppressants.

Regarding the effectiveness and safety of vaccination in patients with SLE undergoing drug treatment, our study did not show statistically significant results. However, research suggests that immunosuppressive drugs such as glucocorticoids, methotrexate, mycophenolate mofetil/mycophenolic acid, and rituximab may further reduce the efficacy of COVID-19 vaccines [43]. Additionally, in the included articles, the absence of dosage information for some studies prevented us from analyzing the potential impact of these drugs, lacking dosage information, on the final vaccine efficacy. However, from previously published studies [44], it is evident that mycophenolate, tacrolimus, and belimumab significantly diminish immune responses. Maintaining mycophenolate for 1 week can enhance vaccine efficacy, offering clinical benefits in terms of vaccine response without inducing clinical symptoms. Perhaps clinicians and clinical decision-makers can use this data as a basis for clinical diagnosis to alleviate patients’ suffering.

Being infected with COVID-19 provides the body with some natural protection, or immunity, that gives it the chance to avoid being reinfected with the virus that causes COVID-19. Having been infected with COVID-19 and having been vaccinated against COVID-19 were estimated to be associated with a low risk of reinfection with a similar variant for at least six months. Some studies had found that infected with COVID-19, almost all antibody positive samples analyzed that showed low neutralizing ability, indicating that the low affinity antibody also had a protective effect on the body [45]. Given the potential for reinfection and the potential for severe complications associated with COVID-19, coupled with the increased risk posed by the spread of new variants, it is advisable for patients with SLE to receive COVID-19 vaccination, irrespective of prior infection status [43,46].

However, it should be noted that the limitations of this study include the following aspects. Firstly, the data in this study were not comprehensive when discussing the types of vaccinations and the number of vaccination injections. Secondly, there was no single gender study in the included studies, such as whether the participants were all male or all female. There was no date for age in this study. More data were needed to make more references for the respiratory virus vaccines of SLE people of all ages. Thirdly, the scarcity of evidence explicitly specifying drug dosages have a certain impact on the assessment of vaccine efficacy. In addition, when studying the use of drugs after respiratory virus vaccines, some studies did not fully record the data after the use of drugs and could not draw firm conclusions, so it was not very convincing. Therefore, there is a greater need to further explore studies on the respiratory virus vaccines for SLE during influenza or COVID-19 peak periods.

Conclusion

In the context of this study, the SCR, antibody titers, and neutralizing antibody levels in SLE patients following both influenza and COVID-19 vaccinations demonstrated improvement, albeit remaining below the levels observed in healthy controls. This provides valuable evidence supporting the vaccination of SLE patients during the concurrent COVID-19 and influenza epidemics. Consequently, it is recommended that SLE patients receive vaccinations against both COVID-19 and influenza, empowering them to enhance their resilience during this challenging period.
