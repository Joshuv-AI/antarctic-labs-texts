Barriers to access to antihypertensive medicines: insights from the HEARTS initiative in latin American and Caribbean region
Authors: Kathiaja Miranda Souza, Nora Giron, Juliana Vallini, Kemel Hallar, Pedro Ordunez, Andres Rosende, Baudelio Ramírez Loya, David Debrott Sánchez, Christoper Lim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionHypertension significantly impacts the global cardiovascular disease burden, presenting a pronounced challenge within Latin America and the Caribbean. The Pan American Health Organization's (PAHO) HEARTS initiative endeavours to meet this challenge by enhancing comprehensive cardiovascular risk management, inclusive of improved access to antihypertensive medications. This study scrutinises the challenges and barriers in accessing these medications, which are crucial for effective hypertension management in these regions.MethodsThe research employed a two-phase approach: an initial analysis of National Essential Medicines Lists (NEMLs) from 22 countries involved in the HEARTS initiative for the presence of antihypertensive medications, followed by an in-depth pharmaceutical market analysis in six selected countries to evaluate the availability, pricing, and procurement practices of these medications.ResultsThe study revealed notable inconsistencies in the inclusion of recommended antihypertensive medications across NEMLs, particularly the lack of fixed-dose combinations (FDCs). The market analysis brought to light significant limitations in medicine registration and substantial variations in pricing, which adversely impact the accessibility and affordability of essential antihypertensive treatments. Furthermore, an examination of procurement practices identified considerable diversity across countries, highlighting potential areas for optimisation, including the use of the PAHO Strategic Fund.ConclusionsThe barriers to accessing essential antihypertensive medications in Latin America and the Caribbean are multifaceted, stemming from outmoded NEMLs, limited market availability of advised medications, and disparate procurement processes. Leveraging pooled procurement mechanisms such as the PAHO Strategic Fund, coupled with vital updates to NEMLs, stands to markedly improve both the accessibility and affordability of these treatments.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD) are the leading cause of death in the Americas accounting for more than 2 million deaths annually, a high burden of disability, and a huge negative socioeconomic impact. High systolic blood pressure leading population-attributable risk factors for CVD including hemorrhagic stroke (58%), ischemic stroke (50%), and ischemic heart disease (IHD) (55%) (Forouzanfar et al., 2017; Roth et al., 2020). In the Americas around 2019, the age-standardised prevalence of hypertension (≥140/90 mm Hg) in adults aged 30–79 was 35.4% (PAHO, n.d.). Despite this, 37% of people with hypertension had not been diagnosed, 15% of those diagnosed were not receiving treatment, and 47% of those receiving treatment did not have their BP controlled in Latin America and the Caribbean (Martinez et al., 2021, 2022; Zhou et al., 2021).

To address the CVD burden by improving hypertension control population-wide and strengthening primary and secondary cardiovascular prevention in primary health care (PHC), the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) promotes the implementation of HEARTS in the Americas, a regional adaptation of Global Hearts from the World Health Organization (WHO). This initiative, coordinated by the National Ministries of Health, in collaboration with local stakeholders, and with the technical support of PAHO and other partners, is being implemented in 33 countries and territories in Latin America and The Caribbean (Ordunez et al., 2024).

The HEARTS Clinical Pathway is the main implementation tool of the HEARTS initiative in the Americas. It is fully aligned with the WHO hypertension guideline and includes a standardised treatment protocol as its core. This protocol recommends treatment initiation using two antihypertensive medicines from complementary pharmacologic classes, preferably in a single pill/fixed dose combination (FDC), to enhance patient adherence, reduce therapeutic inertia, and improve hypertension control with less adverse effects (Rosende et al., 2022).

Supporting the HEARTS Initiative's implementation, the PAHO Strategic Fund (SF) has initiated various actions to enhance the access and affordability of quality antihypertensive medicines. These include integrating new medicines and FDCs into the medicine list, identifying eligible suppliers of multi-source generic medicines that align with the highest international quality assurance standards, and conducting price negotiations for multiple countries (Giron et al., 2022; PAHO, 2023c). This support facilitates the procurement of antihypertensive medications recommended in the WHO's 22nd edition Essential Medicines List (EML) and the WHO Guideline for the Pharmacological Treatment of Hypertension in Adults (WHO, 2021a, 2021c).

The barriers to accessing antihypertensive medications are predominantly linked to the healthcare delivery system. Such barriers include complex health information systems, insufficient or unavailable information on public procurement and medication prices, fragmented regulatory oversight, and outdated national Essential Medicines Lists (NEML) (Giraldo et al., 2021; Ordunez et al., 2024). In the context of the HEARTS Initiative, while most countries in the region are moving towards this model, few have the accessibility for recommended antihypertensive medications, particularly long-acting medicines and FDCs. This issue also occurs in other countries implementing the HEARTS Initiative worldwide, adversely affecting the quality of healthcare delivery (Rosende et al., 2023; Satheesh et al., 2023). These system-level bottlenecks require a focused approach to identify and address access barriers, select appropriate interventions to resolve these issues and optimise delivery processes within the hypertension treatment cascade.

The study aims to analyze and identify barriers to accessing antihypertensive medicines within Latin American and Caribbean countries. This entails examining the inclusion of these medications in NEML’s and conducting a detailed analysis of the pharmaceutical market in the Region of the Americas. Our focus is to uncover and better understand the specific challenges related to the accessibility of antihypertensive medications in the Region. The methodological approach was chosen to ensure a comprehensive evaluation, starting with an assessment of formal availability through NEMLs, which, while fundamental, does not fully indicate actual access. An in-depth analysis of the pharmaceutical market follows, crucial for identifying practical barriers such as availability, pricing, and procurement practices that directly impact the accessibility of these essential medications.

Methods

This study was structured as a two-phase investigation. The first phase consisted of a cross-sectional analysis of NEMLs from 22 countries implementing HEARTS in Latin American and the Caribbean, focusing on the availability of antihypertensive medicines, including FDCs. The second phase involved a detailed examination of the pharmaceutical market in a subset of six countries, where data were readily available. This two-step approach was designed to provide a comprehensive view of the region's landscape regarding antihypertensive medicine accessibility and specific market dynamics.

Phase 1: cross-sectional analysis of 22 countries

We conducted a cross-sectional analysis focused on the most recently available NEMLs in 2022 from 22 countries across the Americas and Caribbean region: Argentina, Bahamas, Barbados, Bolivia, Brazil, the British Virgin Islands, Chile, Colombia, Costa Rica, Cuba, Dominica, the Dominican Republic, Ecuador, El Salvador, Guatemala, Guyana, Mexico, Panama, Peru, Saint Lucia, Suriname, and Trinidad & Tobago. These NEMLs were obtained from a variety of sources, ensuring a broad and accurate representation. Primary sources included the official websites of the respective Ministries of Health, publicly accessible government documents, Global Essential Medicines database, and MEDLIST database from the PAHO (PAHO, 2023a; Persaud et al., 2019).

From each country's NEML, we selected antihypertensive medicines based on their active ingredients and pharmaceutical class using the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) codes as reference, as delineated in the respective therapeutic group (WHO, n.d.). In cases where the NEMLs were not organised into therapeutic groups we conducted a thorough review of the entire list to manually identify antihypertensive medications.

Subsequently we assessed the antihypertensive medicines listed in the WHO's 22nd edition EML (WHO, 2021c). Within the framework of the WHO EML, we verified the inclusion of selected medicines. Additionally, the presence of these medicines was cross-referenced with the list recommended in PAHO SF/HEARTS Initiative 2021 (PAHO, 2021; WHO, 2018).

For comparative analysis across countries, we calculated similarity scores for each country's NEML, categorising medicines as commonly listed (≥ 50% of countries) or less commonly listed (<50% of countries). The similarity score for each country was determined by the net difference between the number of commonly listed and less commonly listed medicines. A positive similarity score indicated a higher prevalence of commonly listed medications, while a negative score suggested less similarity, in comparison to other NEMLs among the countries (Persaud et al., 2019).

The data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, such as frequency counts, medians, and interquartile ranges (IQRs).

Phase 2: pharmaceutical market analysis

In the second phase, our study focused on the pharmaceutical market in six countries implementing the HEARTS Initiative: Argentina, Chile, Ecuador, the Dominican Republic, Peru, and Mexico. The objective was a thorough collection and analysis of data relating to marketing authorizations, patent situations, public procurement processes, and medication pricing, using the PAHO SF/HEARTS Initiative 2021 list of antihypertensive medicines as a benchmark.

Marketing authorisation information was collected from the online databases of National Regulatory Authorities from April to August 2022. For Mexico, data was sourced from summaries on the drug regulatory agency's website. Patent status was verified using data from the public access systems of the Intellectual Property Institutes in these countries and the Medicines Patent Pool (MedsPal) database (MedsPal, n.d.).

Data on public purchases, including procurement prices, were obtained from online databases of public procurement platforms in the selected countries. These data covered purchases made during 2021 and 2022.

Our analysis utilised descriptive statistics to understand the distribution of marketing authorizations for each medication across the countries. We assessed the availability of these medications in national and institutional lists identified during the market analysis and compared them with the PAHO SF/HEARTS Initiative 2021 List.

Public procurement practices were characterised according to reference medicine lists, public purchasing methods, administrative platforms, and involved institutions. Additionally, a comparative analysis of medicine prices among the countries was conducted. This analysis referenced the prices listed by the PAHO SF, to which a 20% increase was applied. This increment, based on historical trends and data, reflects additional costs like freight, insurance, and a service surcharge. Prices in local currencies were converted to U.S. dollars using the exchange rate published on the respective dates by the central banks. To analyze the statistical association between public purchase prices and quantities acquired, we focused on Amlodipine 5 mg and Losartan 50 mg, employing Spearman correlations with a 95% confidence interval.

While evaluating the inclusion of FDCs in NEMLs, it was observed that less than half of the countries had at least one FDC item included in their lists. Moreover, the concordance analysis of FDCs in national lists with those in the WHO's 22nd edition list showed that only 7 countries have at least one FDC item aligning with the WHO model list.

In assessing the alignment of national medicines lists with the WHO's 22nd EML, we found that 44 out of 70 unique items (63%) align with WHO recommendations. In contrast, 26 items on the national lists do not correspond to WHO recommendations. The deviation of each country’s list from the WHO's 22nd EML ranged from 0 to 36% of items, with a median of 27% (IQR: 18% to 32%). This indicates a median discrepancy of 4.5 items per country (IQR: 3–7), highlighting differences in the medicines recommended, either by medicine name or therapeutic class (Table 1).

In terms of the similarity in medicine lists among countries, 14 out of 70 items (20%) were commonly listed in the formularies of 50% or more countries. Among these, despite being included in the WHO compendium, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACE) such as captopril and enalapril were excluded by the PAHO SF list due to evolving recommendations favouring long-acting medications like those from the HEARTS initiative. Conversely, among the 56 less frequently listed medicines, 37 are categorised as alternative by WHO while 23 do not have specific pharmacological class mentions. The country-specific similarity scores varied significantly from −19 to 11 with a median score of 4 and an interquartile range of −4 to 7. Most countries (68%) had positive similarity scores indicating alignment with other countries’ medicine lists. However, for some countries like Colombia (−19) and Mexico (−13), their negative similarity scores suggested that their listed medicines are less common than those found in other countries’ lists (Table 1).

Regarding the degree of concordance of antihypertensive items in NEML and those in the WHO's 22nd EML and the PAHO's SF list, a significant finding was the limited availability of FDCs in national lists, peaking at 27% for ARB + CCB; ACE + DT; and ACE + CCB combinations, and 32% for ARB + DT combinations. In this context, of the four WHO-recommended FDCs, we identified 17 different FDCs, many of which were present in only one or two countries. Additionally, when comparing NEMLs with the offerings from the PAHO’s SF, we observed a low percentage of countries including FDCs, as well as long-acting ARB and ACE, in their lists (Figure 1). Pharmaceutical market analysis in 6 countries

Marketing authorisation and patent situation analysis

Focusing on the marketing authorisation of core antihypertensive medicines in the PAHO SF/HEARTS Initiative across a selected sample of six countries, it was observed that Argentina, Chile, and Mexico have fewer than six registrations for at least half of the listed medications.

Specifically, chlorthalidone, lisinopril, and the FDCs has limited registrations in several countries. In contrast, medications such as amlodipine, losartan, and telmisartan exhibit a higher number of marketing authorizations in these countries. Hydrochlorothiazide and chlorthalidone show a distribution of registrations suggesting exclusivity in certain countries. While Peru and the Dominican Republic have several registrations for hydrochlorothiazide, there are few for chlorthalidone, with the opposite trend observed in Ecuador (Figure 2). Regarding medications with FDCs, data indicates an absence or minimal market presence in various countries, with registrations ranging from none to six. Another aspect explored in the analysis of the availability of antihypertensive medications was their patent status in the countries studied. We identified that telmisartan + amlodipine and telmisartan + hydrochlorothiazide have related patents in both Chile and Mexico, with these patents being either secondary or related to the manufacturing process. Regarding the number of registrations for these medications, previous patent protection appears to be a significant barrier in Mexico. In Chile there is a higher number of registrations, with at least four registered brands for the telmisartan + amlodipine combination and six brands for telmisartan + hydrochlorothiazide, including options from local manufacturers. This indicates a more diversified situation. In contrast, in the Dominican Republic, Peru, and Ecuador, there are no existing patents for any of the medications analyzed (see Supplemental Table S3).

Public procurement characterisation

Following our analysis of the antihypertensive medication market in selected countries, we conducted a characterisation of public procurement, including the identification of alternative reference medicines lists for the management of public procurement mechanisms used in these countries (see Supplemental Table S4).

Regarding the reference medicine lists for public procurement of antihypertensive medications, it was noted that NEML’s are not always the primary reference in various countries, leading to diverse approaches in managing medication availability. For example, the Dominican Republic has three alternative lists to the national list, each with different active ingredients and concentrations, indicating a broader scope of treatment options (ARS Universal., 2017; Dominican Republic. Ministerio de Salud Pública., 2016, 2019).

In Mexico, considering the autonomy of the States in management, the coordinated approach among the health sector institutions of the States results in a comprehensive and extensive National Compendium of Health Inputs (Consejo de Salud General de Mexico, n.d.). Peru closely aligns its public social security list (EsSalud) with the National Formulary of the Ministry of Health (Ministerio de Salud de Peru, n.d., 2018). Meanwhile, Chile maintains not only their NEML but also a mandatory list for pharmacies serving the public, along with other distinct national lists (CENABAST, 2019; Ministerio de Salud de Chile, n.d.-a, n.d.-b). Ecuador only has the NEML (Ministério de Salud de Ecuador, 2019). Argentina's approach includes both a primary care EML and an extensive list focused on elderly care for retired and pensioned seniors under the New PAMI Vademecum (PAMI, n.d., 2021).

The public procurement of antihypertensives reveals diverse strategies among countries. Electronic platforms and computerised systems are increasingly employed for efficiency and transparency in public procurement processes. National public tenders are common, and in Mexico, specifically, public purchases are also made through international tenders with the support of United Nations Office for Project Services (UNOPS) (Proyecto Salud México, n.d.). In Ecuador, reverse electronic auctions are the preferred modality for medication procurement. The Dominican Republic focuses on purchasing non-patented generics from local suppliers through the public essential medicines programme (PROMESE-CAL) (Ministerio de Salud Pública de Republica Dominicana, n.d.). In Peru, a 2019 decree allows the Ministry of Health to procure medications through PAHO/WHO and other United Nations entities (Ministerio de Salud de Peru, 2019).

Argentina and Ecuador engage multiple generic suppliers, using national/international public tenders or reverse electronic auctions (Ministerio de Salud de Argentina, 2023; Programa Proteger de Argentina, 2023). In Chile, public procurement of medicines mainly occurs within the Supply Center of the National Health Services System (CENABAST) and ChileCompra. CENABAST specialises in centralised purchasing for health services, consolidating demands from entities within the National Health Services System. ChileCompra, serving a broader range of government procurement needs (CENABAST, n.d.; ChileCompra, n.d.).

The institutional coverage of public purchases varies, with some countries limiting procurement to public medicine programmes under health authorities (Dominican Republic and Peru), while others involve a broader range of public institutions beyond the health sector for antihypertensive purchases (Ecuador, Mexico). In four of the analyzed countries, decentralised procurement was evident, using different procurement modalities (national tender, minimal amount, direct purchase).

Public purchases analysis

The public purchases of antihypertensive medicines in the evaluated countries were analyzed and compared with the list from the PAHO SF/HEARTS Initiative.

Amlodipine and losartan, as single-agent medicines, were procured by all the evaluated countries, aligning with many of the current clinical pathways of the countries implementing HEARTS Initiative. Hydrochlorothiazide was acquired by five out of the six countries under review, with Ecuador being the exception.

In contrast, chlorthalidone and telmisartan + amlodipine, despite being included in the preferred HEARTS clinical pathways for all countries in the sample, were procured by a limited number of countries: Ecuador and Mexico for chlorthalidone, and Chile exclusively for telmisartan + amlodipine. Moreover, the Dominican Republic was the only country to procure other FDCs, such as lisinopril + amlodipine and lisinopril + hydrochlorothiazide, alongside the single medication lisinopril. However, these FDCs, though procured, are not widely available in the Dominican Republic, nor are they included in the country's clinical pathways (Figure 3). Prices for the combinations lisinopril + amlodipine, lisinopril + hydrochlorothiazide, and telmisartan + hydrochlorothiazide were excluded from this analysis due to the absence of reference prices in the SF at the time of the study.

In 8 of the 26 evaluated prices, which pertain to the three medications (losartan 50 mg, hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg, and chlorthalidone 25 and 50 mg) under examination, the PAHO SF's benchmark price was higher than the locally achieved price. This occurrence was noted for one medication in Chile, two in Ecuador, two in Peru, and three in Mexico.

On the other hand, in the remaining 18 price points, the local prices were higher than those secured by the SF. This trend was observed across all the medication prices in the Dominican Republic and Argentina, and most prices in Chile.

In examining the impact of purchase volume on medication pricing, we analyzed the correlation between the prices paid by public purchasers in various countries and the quantities acquired in each procurement process. This analysis, detailed in Figure 4 includes data from major buyers in Chile, Argentina, and Mexico, highlighted with green markers, alongside other institutional purchases for Amlodipine 5 mg and Losartan 50 mg. The Spearman correlation coefficients for Amlodipine 5 mg and Losartan 50 mg were −0.744 and−0.765 respectively (p < 0.001). These coefficients indicate a strong negative correlation: as the purchase volume increases, the price per unit tends to decrease.

Notably, the analysis reveals a diversity in purchasing patterns. While many buyers acquire small quantities at varied prices, major buyers tend to procure larger volumes at more consistently lower prices.

Moving towards a synthesis of our findings, we present a concise summary of the key barriers and insights derived from our comprehensive analysis across different aspects of antihypertensive medication availability and procurement. This summary encapsulates the critical points, serving as a bridge to our detailed discussions.

Discussion

In this study, we explored various barriers to accessing antihypertensive medications, focusing primarily on availability and prices (Wirtz et al., 2017). Our research revealed multiple barriers, including outdated NEMLs, limited medicines marketing authorizations, varied dynamics in public procurement, and heterogeneous medicines pricing.

Initially, we assessed the availability of antihypertensive medications in NEMLs across 22 selected countries in LAC region. This was pivotal, as NEMLs guide pharmaceutical care policies in countries, influencing procurement, supply, and utilisation of medications by the public sector (United Nations, 2008). In the subsequent phase, we examined the dynamics of the pharmaceutical market, identifying that pharmaceutical regulations, market economics, and national procurement policies substantially impact medication accessibility.

The barrier of heterogeneous and outdated NEMLs raises questions about whether these lists adequately address the priority health needs of the population, considering best practices for hypertension treatment and regional epidemiological factors. The suboptimal inclusion of essential medicines and the misalignment with evidence-based practice guidelines can result in inadequate patient outcomes and healthcare inefficiencies (Mahmić-Kaknjo et al., 2018; WHO, 2004). Furthermore, this leads to fragmentation in the standard of care within the region, potentially resulting in inconsistent treatment approaches for hypertension across different countries. This inconsistency not only affects the quality of patient care but also poses challenges for policies aimed at fostering cooperation between countries in the field of healthcare and has a negative impact on shaping the pharmaceutical market.

The variation in medicine inclusion across national lists contributes to a fragmented pharmaceutical market. In regions where specific medicines are included, manufacturers can establish a dominant market position. In contrast, in countries where these medications are not listed, their market presence is significantly diminished (Bazargani et al., 2014). Our further research shows that medicines often lack marketing authorizations across countries due to differences in their national lists, a situation compounded by variations in national medicine policies and manufacturers’ strategic and marketing approaches (Mahmić-Kaknjo et al., 2018; Mahmić-Kaknjo & Marušić, 2015).

This disparity in medication listings and resultant market fragmentation underscores the need for unified approaches in developing and updating NEMLs. By re-aligning evidence-based national standard treatment guidelines, the selection and alignment of essential medicines in national formularies are subsequently facilitated. With each country's list aimed at addressing specific population health needs, similar NEMLs are logically expected in countries with comparable demographics, healthcare spending, and health statuses (Persaud et al., 2019). Enhanced coordination could align national formularies with international recommendations, thereby improving healthcare quality, especially in hypertension management. The HEARTS Initiative has been effective in enhancing implementation, blood pressure treatment, and cardiovascular risk management across LAC countries by using a clinical pathway with a simple, straightforward, and standardised treatment protocol, significantly advancing hypertension management in the region (Rosende et al., 2023). Concurrently, PAHO is leading a regional initiative for the harmonisation of NEMLs (PAHO, 2023b). Moreover, the PAHO SF policy provides an essential alternative for countries to import quality-assured medications, even if not registered or not listed in NEMLs, facilitating healthcare access (Giron et al., 2022).

The limited availability of FDCs sheds light on the pharmaceutical market's dynamics. The patent status of these FDCs does not appear to be a barrier, potentially easing the way for generic versions. Nonetheless, the issue of pricing for FDCs becomes apparent, for example, when considering the acquisition costs in Chile, which are about 385% higher than those through the Strategic Fund (SF), underscoring potential concerns regarding excessive pricing.

Economic factors frequently play a critical role in determining whether FDCs are included in NEMLs, a trend evident in countries such as Brazil and Mexico. In these nations, health policy decisions are markedly influenced by economic evaluations (CONITEC, 2023b, 2023a; Gobierno de México, 2020). This economic focus might overlook the long-term benefits of FDCs, such as enhanced patient adherence, improved blood pressure control, and potential effort and cost savings due to streamlined supply chains related to the planning, storage, and transportation of single FDC pills compared to multiple single dose pills. Yet, the market's limited availability and high cost of FDCs restrict their accessibility, perpetuating public health disparities (Cohn et al., 2022; Jahangiri et al., 2022; Ordunez et al., 2024; Rabbani & Alexander, 2008).

Significant fragmentation characterises the procurement of antihypertensive medications in various countries, leading to price disparities. Countries with fragmented demand and smaller purchase volumes face higher costs, whereas consolidated procurement across numerous health institutions yields more affordable prices.

The PAHO SF's medication offerings, aligned with the HEARTS Clinical Pathway, facilitate a standardised treatment approach. This alignment aids in streamlining procurement and allows for more accurate forecasting of medication needs. It also leverages purchasing power more effectively, thereby enhancing the assured quality, efficiency, and affordability of hypertension treatment (Cohn et al., 2022; Ordunez et al., 2024).

Successful equitable procurement strategies, as evidenced by programmes for diseases such as HIV/AIDS, tuberculosis, malaria, diabetes, neglected tropical diseases, hepatitis C, and COVID-19-related products, have been implemented through the SF’s expanded range of medications (PAHO, 2024). Competitive pricing and efficient negotiations have been critical in facilitating this broader access, thereby enhancing the quality and availability of essential medications. The collective procurement strategy has yielded benefits for all participating countries by guaranteeing access to these necessary medicines at negotiated prices. Additionally, the SF's long-term medicine agreements highlight the Fund's dedication to ensuring consistent access to vital treatments. This method exemplifies the SF's pooled procurement model's success in improving the affordability and accessibility of important healthcare treatments and provides valuable insights for the procurement of antihypertensive and other essential medicines (Lal et al., 2022; Vlassoff et al., 2023).

Countries facing access barriers to antihypertensive medications can utilise the PAHO SF as an effective alternative. Operating as a regional pooled procurement mechanism, the SF enhances access to essential antihypertensive medications by consolidating demand across countries, thus improving healthcare quality for hypertension and reducing costs. This method efficiently navigates economic and regulatory challenges in incorporating these medications into NEMLs. Furthermore, increased participation in the SF promises more competitive pricing, easing financial burdens. The potential for joint purchasing further highlights the significance of cooperative efforts to improve the affordability and accessibility of antihypertensive care (PAHO, 2023c).

Our research encountered a significant barrier due to the lack of transparency in medicine pricing. Difficulty in accessing current pricing information for various medications was a major impediment, reflecting a broader issue identified in the literature: non-transparent pricing practices can distort the medicine market by disconnecting demand from price (Rintoul et al., 2020). A study by Médecins Sans Frontières (MSF) revealed a lack of evidence-based information on antihypertensive medicine pricing in Low-and Middle-Income Countries, with prices in some cases being significantly higher than generic equivalents (Resolve to Save Lives, 2023).

Policy efforts aimed at fair access have traditionally focused on pricing and reimbursement, yet often overlook the urgency of improving patient access. This is compounded by a lack of research focusing on real patient affordability and access (Tachkov et al., 2023).

Addressing these transparency issues is crucial and requires collaboration between governments, healthcare systems, civil and patient organisations, and the pharmaceutical industry to share health and medicine information effectively. Overcoming barriers such as confidential pricing agreements and conflicts of interest is necessary. Enhancing transparency involves standardised reporting and the potential development of web-based platforms for price information sharing. These efforts should be complemented by policies that encourage price monitoring, public price information, generic competition, and legislation that supports quality and economic incentives, all aimed at improving healthcare access and affordability (Cernuschi et al., 2020; WHO, 2021b; Wirtz et al., 2017).

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. Firstly, the inclusion of a small number of countries, in the pharmaceutical market analysis, limits the ability to detect broader variations and regional trends in medication access. Additionally, the study predominantly focused on descriptive analysis, confining itself to exploring only the available data from the selected countries. This approach narrowed the investigation to the aspects of medication availability and prices from a national perspective, omitting other crucial factors that impact medication access. As a result, significant elements such as health policy effectiveness, prescribing practices, patient preferences, and other socioeconomic factors, which also play a vital role in medication access, were not covered in this study. These limitations highlight the need for more comprehensive future research that can provide a more holistic and detailed understanding of the challenges in medication access across various national and regional contexts.

Conclusions

Government interventions, accountability and civil society organisation actions are key in addressing access barriers to antihypertensive medicines in Latin America and the Caribbean. Recommendations to improve access include updating national lists and, consequently, clinical protocols, by incorporating WHO-recommended medications, both as single long-acting agents and in FDC’s. Additionally, simplifying medicines registration processes to improve market availability (Ordunez et al., 2024).

The role of international collaboration and entities like the PAHO SF is vital for better access to essential medicines. Public buyers should consolidate demand to secure lower prices, and transparent public procurement pricing is crucial for efficient acquisitions. Policies should focus on reducing fragmentation in purchases and supporting joint procurement for better negotiation and availability.

The study also encourages expanding the HEARTS Initiative beyond the PHC facilities directly depending on Ministries of Health to other private or social security institutions, which are substantial purchasers of antihypertensive medicines. It underscores the need for further research to enhance the application and understanding of these strategies.

Improving access to a core of high-quality and affordable medicines for hypertension is critical to implementing HEARTS in the Americas, the PAHO flagship programme to strengthen primary health care and to reduce the burden of CVD. Unveiling and effectively addressing the access barriers to medicines is critical to building effective pharmaceutical policies, optimising the pharmaceutical market, reducing the prices of medicines, and equitably improving the quality of care.
