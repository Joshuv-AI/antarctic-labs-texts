Effects of Cardiac Resynchronization Therapy on Cardiovascular and Noncardiovascular Hospitalization: A MADIT‐CRT Long‐Term Follow‐Up
Authors: Sabu Thomas, Scott McNitt, Bronislava Polonsky, Jeffrey D Alexis, John D Bisognano, Ilan Goldenberg, Valentina Kutyifa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiac resynchronization therapy with defibrillation (CRT‐D) improves outcomes in heart failure. The long‐term impact of CRT‐D on hospitalizations remains unknown.MethodsWe analyzed the MADIT‐CRT (Multicenter Automatic Defibrillator Implantation Trial With Cardiac Resynchronization Therapy) trial post hoc to assess the effects of CRT‐D versus implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator (ICD) on cardiovascular, heart failure (HF), and noncardiovascular hospitalizations. Hospitalization rates, length of stay, and mortality were compared during extended follow‐up.ResultsPatients receiving CRT‐D had lower rates of hospitalization compared with ICD (37.9 events per 100 patient‐years versus 44.3 events per 100 patient‐years, P=0.033). Rates of cardiovascular hospitalizations (20.8 versus 28.3 events per 100 patient‐years; P<0.001) and heart failure hospitalizations (6.8 versus 11.6 events per 100 patient‐years; P<0.001) were lower with CRT‐D. There was no difference in noncardiovascular hospitalizations in the CRT‐D group compared with ICD (17 versus 16 events per 100 patient‐years, P=0.368). The average length of stay for cardiovascular hospitalizations was shorter in the CRT‐D group versus the ICD group (6.7±0.89 versus 7.7±0.68 days; P<0.001), as was the length of stay for heart failure hospitalizations (4.2±0.79 versus 4.8±0.58 days; P<0.001). No difference was observed in the length of stay for noncardiovascular hospitalizations (8.1 versus 7.0 days; P=0.082). Hospitalization of any type was associated with a markedly increased risk of death (hazard ratio, 8.97 [95% CI, 6.17–13.05]; P<0.0001).ConclusionsAmong patients in MADIT‐CRT, CRT‐D was associated with lower rates and shorter durations of all cardiovascular hospitalizations, including heart failure hospitalizations compared with ICD alone. Hospitalization, regardless of cause, was strongly associated with increased mortality.Registration https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT00180271.Keywords: CRT‐D, heart failure, hospitalizations, ICD, length of stay, mortalitySubject Categories: Cardiomyopathy, Heart Failure, Quality and Outcomes

Cardiac resynchronization therapy with defibrillation (CRT‐D) is an important adjunct to guideline‐directed medical therapy in patients with symptomatic heart failure (HF). CRT‐D reduces HF hospitalizations, improves left ventricular ejection fraction (EF), reduces remodeling and improves survival in patients with left bundle‐branch block (LBBB), left ventricular EF ≤30%, and a QRS duration ≥120 ms in a broad range of patients with HF with different functional classes. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4

Nevertheless, patients remain at risk for hospitalizations even after CRT‐D implantation. Readmissions following a hospitalization are also common, with 27% of patients being readmitted within 30 days and 50% within 6 months. 5 , 6 Readmissions are modifiable and as such the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services financially penalize hospitals for higher than expected readmissions as directed by the 2010 Affordable Care Act. 1 It is important then that we analyze the effects of CRT‐D on hospitalizations to understand its potential role in reducing readmissions.

A prior substudy from the RAFT (Resynchronization‐Defibrillation for Ambulatory Heart Failure Trial) suggested that CRT‐D reduces total hospitalizations, cardiovascular hospitalizations, HF hospitalizations, and length of stay but this was accompanied by an increase in device‐related readmissions. 7 However, the follow‐up period in the RAFT trial was shorter than MADIT‐CRT (Multicenter Automatic Defibrillator Implantation Trial With Cardiac Resynchronization Therapy). Also, the RAFT study included patients with more severe HF symptoms including New York Heart Association (NYHA) class II/III HF. Whether CRT‐D impacts hospitalizations during longer‐term follow‐up and in patients with less severe HF symptoms remains unknown.

In this study, we analyzed the effects of CRT‐D versus implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator (ICD) on cardiovascular (HF and non‐HF related) and noncardiovascular hospitalizations during long‐term follow‐up in patients enrolled in MADIT‐CRT with follow‐up up to 7 years and assessed the association between different types of hospitalizations and overall survival.

METHODS

Data Sharing

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Population

This was a retrospective, post hoc analysis of the MADIT‐CRT database with extended follow‐up up to 7 years after randomization. This was a randomized controlled trial (NCT00180271) approved by the Institutional Review Committee and every patient gave informed consent. A previous post hoc analysis demonstrated benefits only in those with a LBBB; however, this analysis explored the effects on those with and without LBBB. 8 The study design and results of MADIT‐CRT have previously been published. 9

Patients were recruited from 110 hospital centers: 88 centers in the United States with 1271 patients, 2 centers in Canada with 22 patients, and 20 centers in Europe with 527 patients. Enrollment criteria included patients of either sex who were at least 21 years of age with NYHA class I/II ischemic cardiomyopathy or NYHA II nonischemic cardiomyopathy, had sinus rhythm, an EF of ≤30%, and a prolonged intraventricular conduction (QRS duration of ≥130 ms) with a primary or secondary indication for ICD therapy for the prevention of sudden cardiac death. Patients were excluded from enrollment for a variety of reasons, including an existing indication for CRT; already having an implanted pacemaker, ICD, or resynchronization device present; NYHA class III or IV symptoms; previous coronary artery bypass grafting, percutaneous coronary intervention, or an enzyme‐positive myocardial infarction 3 months before enrollment; or atrial fibrillation within 1 month before enrollment.

Eligible patients were randomly assigned in a 3:2 ratio to receive a CRT‐D or an ICD alone. A baseline clinical history, 12‐lead ECG, and an echocardiogram were obtained for each patient. Patients were seen in clinic at 1 month after randomization and then at 3‐month intervals thereafter until the termination of the trial. Clinical evaluation and device testing were carried out at each follow‐up visit. Treating physicians were aware of study‐group assignments.

Outcome Measures

All deaths and hospitalizations were adjudicated according to prespecified criteria by an independent mortality committee and an HF committee whose members were blinded to study group assignments, as previously described. 10 The primary end point of the original trial was a composite outcome of all‐cause mortality or an HF event, whichever occurred first.

Hospitalizations after the index hospital admission were classified as cardiovascular or noncardiovascular. Cardiovascular hospitalizations were further subcategorized as HF or non‐HF related. An HF‐related hospitalization was defined as an admission to an institution for >24 hours with a diagnosis and subsequent treatment of HF including but not limited to intravenous diuretics, nesiritide, or ultrafiltration. The criteria for a HF admission included documentation of signs and symptoms of HF, including dyspnea, progressive peripheral edema, elevated jugular venous pressure, pulmonary edema, chest X‐ray findings of interstitial edema, and the need for intravenous diuretics, as well as vasodilators or inotropic agents. If HF developed during an admission for another medical problem, this was not classified as an HF hospitalization.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were performed according to the intention‐to‐treat principle. Continuous data were presented as means±SD. Differences in baseline demographic variables and hospitalization frequency were compared by Wilcoxon test for continuous variables and the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Baseline characteristics were compared between patients characterized by the category of initial hospitalization during follow‐up. Specifically, the categories were (1) cardiovascular‐HF hospitalization, (2) cardiovascular‐non‐HF hospitalization and (3) noncardiovascular hospitalization and the comparison group included patients who were not hospitalized.

The length of stay in days were also compared between each category of hospitalization. To account for multiple admissions over time, we used counts of events (number of hospitalizations and days in hospital) per 100 patient‐years at risk to compare treatment groups using a negative binomial regression.

The cumulative probability of a cardiovascular hospitalization was determined in a univariable model with the use of the Kaplan–Meier methodology with comparisons by the log‐rank test. Comparisons were stratified according to the presence or absence of a LBBB. The cumulative probability of death among those who had a cardiovascular, noncardiovascular hospitalization and no hospitalization was also determined in a univariate model with the use of the Kaplan–Meier methodology with comparisons by the log‐rank test. A multivariable Cox proportional‐hazards regression was used to estimate the relationship of CRT‐D versus ICD on cardiovascular and non‐cardiovascular hospitalizations stratified by the presence or absence of LBBB. Because of a violation of the proportional hazards assumption for the cardiovascular hospitalization end point within the LBBB patient subgroup, separate hazard ratios were estimated through 6 months and after 6 months reflecting the point at which the CRT‐D cumulative event rate changes slope. Adjustment covariables in the multivariable model were selected using the best subsets procedure for the end point of any hospitalization with the added stipulation of needing to be statistically significant at P<0.05. Adjustment variables included age, NYHA class, prior coronary artery bypass graft, prior hospitalization, diabetes, prior ventricular arrhythmia, glomerular filtration rate, and left ventricular EF.

All tests were 2 sided, and P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted with the use of the SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina).

Rates of Hospitalizations (Cardiovascular and Noncardiovascular Hospitalizations) by CRT‐D Versus ICD

Among patients with LBBB ECG pattern at baseline, CRT‐D was associated with an initial increase in cardiovascular hospitalization in the first 6 months compared with the ICD group, but thereafter, it was associated with a reduction in cardiovascular hospitalizations, in a univariable analysis (log‐rank P=0.002) (Figure 1). However, there was no difference in the Kaplan–Meier cumulative probability of cardiovascular hospitalization among the ICD and CRT‐D groups for non‐LBBB patients (log‐rank P=0.743) (Figure 2).

Multivariable analysis for cardiovascular hospitalizations revealed that among the population with LBBB, CRT‐D was associated with a 42% increased risk of cardiovascular hospitalizations in the first 6 months of follow‐up but a 42% reduction in the risk of cardiovascular hospitalization thereafter. CRT‐D was also associated with a statistically significant reduction in cardiovascular‐HF hospitalization and cardiovascular‐non‐HF hospitalizations in the first 6 months of follow‐up and after 6 months. Among the population without LBBB, CRT‐D was not associated with a statistically significant reduction in cardiovascular hospitalizations (Table 2). We performed further sensitivity testing evaluating the interaction of CRT‐D versus ICD treatment by extended follow‐up time. For cardiovascular‐hospitalization, the treatment with extended follow‐up interaction had a P value of 0.436 for patients with LBBB and 0.90 for patients without LBBB. This analysis demonstrates that extended follow‐up did not have a statistically significant bearing on the respective hazard ratios for cardiovascular hospitalizations.

Recurrent Hospitalizations (Cardiovascular and Noncardiovascular Hospitalizations) by CRT‐D Versus ICD

When assessing the categories and frequencies of recurrent hospitalizations and their respective rates per 100 patient‐years, there was a lower rate of all‐cause hospitalizations per 100 patient‐years in the CRT‐D groups compared with the ICD group (37.9 versus 44.3 hospitalizations per 100 patient‐years; P=0.033) (Table 4). Cardiovascular hospitalization rates were lower in the CRT‐D group compared with the ICD group (20.8 versus 28.3 hospitalizations per 100 patient years; P<0.001) and for HF‐related cardiovascular hospitalizations (6.8 versus 11.6 hospitalizations per 100 patient years; P<0.001). There were no statistically significant differences in hospitalization rates between CRT‐D or ICD groups for noncardiovascular hospitalizations (Table 4).

Risk of Mortality Following Cardiovascular and Noncardiovascular Hospitalizations

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis of the probability of death after any hospitalization showed that compared with all patients, any hospital admission was associated with an increased risk of dying. There was no difference between cardiovascular versus noncardiovascular hospitalization with respect to the probability of death (Figure 3).

Multivariable analysis confirmed that all hospitalizations regardless of cause were associated with a higher risk of death (adjusted hazard ratio, 8.97 [95% CI, 6.17–13.05], P<0.001).

DISCUSSION

This analysis of MADIT‐CRT represents, to date, the largest study with the longest follow‐up comparing CRT‐D therapy with ICD on hospitalizations. This study included patients with depressed LV function (left ventricular EF <30%) and mild HF symptoms (ischemic cardiomyopathy with NYHA class I or II symptoms or nonischemic cardiomyopathy with NYHA class II symptoms). We have extended the previous findings in MADIT‐CRT that CRT‐D was associated with a reduction in HF‐related events, by further classifying the types of hospitalizations, lengths of stay, and their impacts on mortality. CRT‐D was associated with a reduction in all‐cause hospitalization, cardiovascular hospitalization including HF and non‐HF cardiovascular hospitalizations. CRT‐D was also associated with slightly shorter lengths of stay for cardiovascular and specifically cardiovascular/HF‐related hospitalizations.

The reduction in hospitalization rates is limited to those with preexisting LBBB. During the follow‐up period, CRT‐D was associated with an initial increase in cardiovascular hospitalizations in the first year but thereafter was associated with a reduction in cardiovascular hospitalizations. Our study did not have direct information explaining the reasons for this pattern. We surmise that the initial increase in cardiovascular hospitalizations after CRT‐D implantation may be from device‐related complications or admissions for medication titration in the first 6 months after CRT‐D implantation. Finally, after any hospitalization, patients experienced a substantially increased risk of mortality, with a similar risk for both cardiovascular and noncardiovascular hospitalizations.

Our study yielded similar results to the RAFT study 7 in that CRT‐D compared with ICD alone yielded a statistically significant reduction in the number of total hospitalizations (47.18 events per 100 patient‐years versus 52.59 events per 100 patient‐years), any cardiovascular hospitalizations (21.73 events per 100 patient‐years versus 26.75 events per 100 patient‐years) and HF‐specific hospitalizations (12.54 events per 100 patient‐years versus 17.10 events per 100 patient‐years). This was accompanied by an increase in device‐related hospitalizations (8.02 events per 100 patient‐years versus 5.38 events per 100 patient‐years). The RAFT study also showed that the length of hospital stay for any cause was significantly shorter in the CRT‐D group compared with the ICD group (8.83±13.3 versus 9.59±14.4 days), which we also noted in MADIT‐CRT but for cardiovascular/HF‐related hospitalizations only. We did not observe a change in length of stay for total hospitalizations and cardiovascular‐non‐HF related and noncardiovascular hospitalizations.

The follow‐up in MADIT‐CRT was longer than in RAFT (5.6 versus 3.2–4.1 years) and furthermore the functional class of HF in MADIT‐CRT was less severe (NYHA class I or II compared with NYHA II or III in the RAFT study).

Mortality after any hospitalization was increased in our study. The RAFT study also showed that after hospitalization, mortality increased. Both MADIT‐CRT and the RAFT study showed that CRT‐D reduced this mortality risk after hospitalization. Several studies have shown that hospitalization for HF results in worsening functional status and increased mortality with each subsequent admission. 11 , 12

Hospitalizations and rehospitalizations for heart failure continue to be a major problem in US health care. It represents one of the leading causes of hospitalization in older adults with a rate of 18 hospitalizations per 1000 in 2011. 13 One fifth of those who are hospitalized for HF are readmitted within 30 days. 14 Starting in 2009, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services have been publicly reporting rates of HF readmissions. Also, the Hospital Readmission and Reduction Program penalizes hospitals up to 3% of their Medicare reimbursement as part of the Affordable Care Act of 2010. Based on this, our study is particularly relevant in reinforcing the use of CRT‐D in appropriate patients with HF even with mild symptoms to potentially mitigate the risk of recurrent hospitalizations. Unfortunately, there continues to be a broad hospital‐level variation in the rate of CRT‐D use with implantation rates among eligible patients to be as low as 26.7%. 4 This study further supports more widespread use of CRT‐D to reduce hospitalizations in these vulnerable populations.

Limitations

The conclusions of our study need to be interpreted in the context of several limitations. In this post hoc analysis with extended follow‐up, 1281 patients had received ICD and 1150 had received CRT‐D. Compared with the expected ratio of 2:3, this is a noticeable variation. Although we did control for some baseline characteristics including comorbidities, an imbalance in prognosis cannot be excluded entirely. There was also a longer follow‐up in the CRT‐D group because of improved survival. This difference in follow‐up period may also contribute to bias in the rates of hospitalization even with adjustments for patient‐years at risk. Nevertheless, the CRT‐D group still experienced fewer hospitalizations and reduced length of stay overall.

With respect to hospitalization types, data limitations prevented us from categorizing early hospitalizations in greater detail. It is possible that some of these hospitalizations may have been device related or due to early medication adjustment and we could not determine if early hospitalizations were associated with increased risk of mortality.

MADIT‐CRT enrolled only patients with mild HF, therefore, our results may not apply to patients with more advanced HF symptoms. Finally, normalizing data per patient‐years assumes that event rates are constant, which may not be the case.

CONCLUSIONS

This post hoc analysis of the MADIT‐CRT database with extended follow up for 7 years post randomization demonstrates that CRT‐D compared with ICD reduced all‐cause hospitalizations, cardiovascular hospitalizations, and HF‐related cardiovascular hospitalizations. CRT‐D was also associated with slightly shorter lengths of stay for HF‐related cardiovascular hospitalizations. This association was noted only for patients with LBBB. Any hospitalization was associated with increased mortality. Clinicians should ensure patients who meet criteria for CRT‐D receive this therapy, including patients with mild symptoms, to reduce hospitalizations and improve overall survival.
