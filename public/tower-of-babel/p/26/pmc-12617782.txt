Use of mental models to identify one health priorities for wildlife health management in California
Authors: Hannah G. Shapiro, Mark Lubell, Deana L. Clifford, Alex Heeren, Kirsten Gilardi, Will Schmelter, Brandon A. Munk
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Operationalizing a One Health framework for wildlife health requires interdisciplinary collaboration and developing a common vision of the system to prioritize management actions and policies. Mental models are simplified knowledge structures that provide a way to generate common understandings of complex social-ecological systems. We conducted a mental model exercise with wildlife and zoonotic disease experts at a workshop to graphically map the relationships of key factors that influence the emergence and occurrence of disease in California wildlife. We found that experts had similar visions about what determinants of health should be included in the models, but low agreement on how these determinants connect, signaling the need for more system-level research and communication among scientists and decision makers. Participants also identified human expectations components of the system (e.g., policy, collaboration, agency capacity) as important determinants of wildlife health, though these components were not as well connected to the system as ecological components. Our research design and analysis demonstrate how mental models can be used to chart a course from common understanding to policy impact in complex One Health systems where cooperation is the key to effective management and governance.

Introduction

Diseases originating in wildlife have become an increasingly important area of study and management. The frequency and impact of these diseases have increased in recent times, likely due to growing anthropogenic pressures on ecosystems (e.g., habitat loss, climate change) and the global movement of animals [[1], [2], [3]]. These diseases have had detrimental impacts on the health of animals and people as well as economies and people's livelihoods. For example, the recent global outbreak of highly pathogenic avian influenza highlight the system-level feedbacks and impact of diseases originating in wildlife [4].

In response, organizations have increasingly adopted a One Health approach to address these issues. One Health is defined as “an integrated, unifying approach that aims to sustainably balance and optimize the health of people, animals, and ecosystems” [5]. Operationalizing the One Health approach is challenging, as it requires a systems-level perspective to integrate siloed disciplines and institutions, a common vision for prioritizing management actions and policies, and a shared understanding of the complex system surrounding disease emergence and occurrence [6,7]. In fact, one of the key oversights of these initiatives has often been the lack of wildlife health expert representation in One Health networks despite wildlife's well-recognized role in the disease system [7].

Recognizing the need for systems-level collaboration, the California Department of Fish and Wildlife (CDFW) initiated a project in 2023 to establish a wildlife health collaborative that increases the state's collective capacity to identify and respond to emergent diseases [8]. CDFW is aiming to integrate a One Health approach to this collaborative, where experts from diverse organizations with human, animal, and environmental health missions grow current collaborations and cultivate new connections to address critical control points for managing wildlife and zoonotic disease. To start this project, CDFW and UC Davis invited wildlife and zoonotic disease experts from California and adjacent state agencies, institutions, and organizations with either the mandate, need, capacity or expertise to detect, report or study wildlife diseases to participate in three, two-day workshops [8].

A key goal of the first workshop was to use mental models to develop common understanding of the wildlife health system. Mental models are organized, simplified knowledge structures that people form to make sense of complex systems [9,10]. Mental models are explicit representations of experts' implicit knowledge and can enable mutual understanding and acceptance of different perspectives and knowledge sources [11,12]. Mental models can identify needs for additional collaboration and management and research priorities [11,13,14]. Mental models have been applied to a diverse range of natural resource topics, including climate change, forestry, and fisheries management [[15], [16], [17]]. However, few studies have applied mental model methodology in the context of wildlife health and the system surrounding the occurrence and emergence of wildlife disease [13,18].

The objective of this paper was to identify (1) shared understanding and knowledge gaps regarding key factors that influence the occurrence and emergence of disease in California wildlife, and (2) priority health determinants a wildlife health collaborative should initially address. Methods

Workshop participants

The authors selected workshop participants based on their expert knowledge of key members of agencies, non-governmental organizations, and universities with current capacity for wildlife health management. The authors identified laboratories and facilities active in providing diagnostic services for or conducting research and response activities for wildlife diseases in California and neighboring states. Participants represented a variety of organizations, including county, state, and national wildlife, public health, and agricultural agencies; conservation NGOs and wildlife rehabilitation facilities; and academia (Table S1). Most of the participants were from California institutions, but several participants came from federal and adjacent state agencies. There were 33 participants from 12 agencies, organizations, and institutions; two countries; and two states.

Cognitive mapping of mental models

We used cognitive maps to elicit expert mental models on the occurrence and emergence of disease in California wildlife. Cognitive maps and mental models are not synonyms; rather cognitive maps are one methodology for operationalizing mental models [9,10]. Cognitive maps identify different components of a complex system and the causal linkages among them, which can be conceptualized as a network with nodes as system components and edges (connections or links) as causal relationships. Cognitive maps help groups develop a common understanding of the system and identify which components of the system are most important for influencing feedback and causal processes [9,10].

Workshop participants were split into five groups of 6–7 people from different organizations. At least one of the authors was assigned to each group to facilitate discussion, and groups were intentionally designed to include experts from different organization types (e.g., academia, state agency, non-governmental organization [NGO]) and sectors (e.g., wildlife, domestic animal and human health; Table S1). To facilitate social learning, each group was asked to build a single, collective cognitive map rather than individual maps for each person [22,23]. Each group was given printed copies of over one hundred potential drivers of wildlife disease on individual pieces of paper (Table S2), a white-board, magnets, and markers. The initial set of approximately 100 drivers was developed by the authors based on expert opinion and review of the literature. Participants were also encouraged to write new drivers as needed. Supplying the groups with many potential drivers reduces the costs of conceptualizing system components. The groups were instructed to do the following:1.Familiarize yourself with the elements of the system (game pieces).2.Select the most important drivers of the occurrence and emergence of wildlife disease in California and put them on the board with similar drivers close together.3.Write down and place any missing drivers (on sticky notes), for system components not included in the pre-supplied pool.4.Draw arrows for causal linkages between system elements, with negative or positive effects. Arrows could be one-way, or two-way influences.

After the models were created, each participant was given five stickers (stars) to vote for drivers which they felt were influential to the system and could be impacted by a potential wildlife health collaborative in California. The stickers were placed directly on the board, and participants could use multiple votes on a single driver. This allowed us to measure which components of the system participants thought were the most important, independent of any network analysis of the cognitive maps described below.

Network analysis

Description of mental models

The basic outcome of this exercise was the generation of five cognitive maps represented as networks (Fig. 1; Figs. S1, S2). For nodes that were created by the groups, we developed and applied a dictionary to create common node labels for semantically similar nodes across networks. Using the determinants of wildlife health model developed by Wittrock et al. [18], we classified each node in the maps into the following categories: abiotic environment, biologic endowment, direct mortality pressures, human expectations, needs for daily living, or social environment (Table 1). We also calculated degree centrality, or the number of connections, for each node. Hypothesis 1: shared visions

We took the frequency of specific drivers across the cognitive maps ranging from one (unique driver) to five (appeared in all cognitive maps). We also calculated the frequency of the determinants of wildlife health themes in each of the maps. To calculate the frequency of causal links of drivers in the cognitive maps, we created adjacency matrices of the edges in each map conditional on both nodes appearing in those maps and summed them. This conditioning removed any edges connected to unique nodes.

Lastly, we calculated the external-internal index (E-I Index) at the determinants of health level to understand how these themes were connected across the maps. This measure calculates the relative density of internal connections within a group, in this case a determinant of health theme, compared to the number of connections that group has to external groups (Krackhardt and Stern, 1988). This index creates a score from −1 to 1, where −1 indicates that all the connections are made to nodes with the same determinant of health theme, and 1 indicates that all the connections are made to nodes with a different theme. The igraph and ITNr packages were used for this calculation [24,25].

Hypothesis 2: correlation between impact and centrality

To test whether participants perceived they could have an impact on central components of the system, which were nodes with high degree centrality values, we estimated Pearson correlations between degree centrality and participant votes on specific drivers across the full models, as well as within each determinant of health theme. We normalized degree centrality to account for the different model sizes. We visualized these results using a scatter plot.

Hypothesis 1: shared visions

Across the five maps, groups shared more nodes in common than edges between the nodes. We found that 49.3 % of nodes were in at least two maps (Fig. 3). Examples of the most common nodes across cognitive maps include energy development (n = 5 models), economics (n = 4 models), fragmentation (n = 4 models), and habitat quality (n = 4 models; Table S4). The proportion of nodes shared by multiple maps stratified by determinants of health themes was similar to the theme abundance for all nodes (Table S3), with human expectations (n = 14; 41.2 %) and abiotic environment (n = 11; 32.4 %) themes comprising the majority of the shared nodes, followed by needs for daily living (n = 4; 11.8 %), direct mortality pressure (n = 3; 8.8 %), biologic endowment (n = 1; 2.9 %), and social environment (n = 1; 2.9 %).Fig. 3Histograms of the (top) nodes and (bottom) edges shared across multiple models.Fig. 3

In contrast, there were far fewer common edges across networks, with 81.7 % (n = 138) of edges being unique to one model (Fig. 3). There were no common edges across all maps. Approximately 38 % of the 138 common edges across networks (n = 52) were found between nodes with different themes or with one node in the pair being “Occurrence and Emergence of Disease in California Wildlife”, which did not have a theme assigned.

The results of the E-I index, which was used to understand how the determinants of health themes were connected in the cognitive maps, revealed that nodes classified in the human expectations theme generally had more internal ties than the other themes (Average score = −0.13, Table S5). The rest of the themes had positive scores on this index, indicating these nodes had more ties to other themes compared to their own.

Hypothesis 2: correlation between impact and centrality

Lastly, we investigated whether nodes voted as impactful by participants also had high degree centrality in the mental models. Across all groups, we found a positive but marginally statistically significant relationship between impact, measured by number of votes a node received, and degree centrality (rho = 0.151; p = 0.079). However, Group 5's mental model was different from the other four, as many nodes connected directly to “Occurrence and Emergence of Disease in California Wildlife” with fewer edges between other drivers (Fig. S2). Discussion

The goal of our workshops and this paper was to use mental models to develop a common vision of wildlife health as precursor to developing a wildlife health collaborative from a One Health perspective. The discussion focuses on two aspects of the findings with important implications for operationalizing One Health in the wildlife health context.

First, the correlation between impactful nodes and node centrality varied across wildlife health themes. Drivers found in multiple cognitive maps, which are highly connected, and participants believe they can impact, are candidates for priority issue for the emergent wildlife health collaborative in California [14,26]. In our system, the drivers that had a high impact and high level of connectedness were wildlife disease knowledge, policy, habitat quality, and pathogens (Fig. 5). This aligns well with the literature showing that these drivers are important considerations to responding to emerging infectious diseases in wildlife [27]. For example, wildlife disease knowledge has been identified as a critical factor in disease detection and response [28]. Wildlife disease knowledge can come in many forms, including advances in diagnostics, disease ecology, epidemiology, pathology, and modelling [28,29]. Workshop participants echoed the need for improved wildlife disease knowledge through increased agreements and data sharing among California's diagnostic labs and streamlined information sharing protocols between institutions [8].Fig. 5The impact and degree centrality of wildlife disease drivers identified by multiple cognitive maps.Fig. 5

The impact and degree centrality of wildlife disease drivers identified by multiple cognitive maps.

In contrast, drivers that are highly connected to the system but are beyond perceived or actual control of current agencies and organizations may be external constraints on wildlife health management (e.g., climate change, fragmentation, energy development; Fig. 5). A wildlife health cooperative will need to decide whether to operate within these constraints, to develop new expertise in wildlife health themes where constraints are located or expand collaboration networks to include actors who work on these drivers. For example, renewable and nonrenewable energy development can negatively impact wildlife health through direct mortality, increased stress, or indirect effects [30]. However, there were no representatives from institutions that work in this field. Incorporating actors from or training in these fields is likely a long-term goal of a wildlife health collaborative, while short-term “wins” may emerge from focusing on connected parts of the system that actors can impact [31].

Second, our findings reiterate the difficulty of system thinking because while there was substantial agreement across models on the system parts, there was very little agreement on how those parts were connected. In other words, nodes are cognitively cheap, and edges are cognitively expensive [19,32]. This lack of agreement on causality in the system increases the difficulty of reaching any type of consensus, and points to the need for continued engagement among stakeholders along with additional research and models to scientifically adjudicate the importance of different causal pathways and feedbacks.

Furthermore, participants had a greater capacity to conceptualize linkages between wildlife health themes where they have the most expertise [32]. Ecological drivers across multiple themes were connected, showing systems-level thinking and boundary spanning on the ecological components. In contrast, human expectations drivers had more internal connections and fewer connections to the other themes. The clustering of the human expectation nodes may stem from a lack experience and expertise social sciences and policy processes, and the associated challenges of systems thinking for unfamiliar components [33,34]. Within the workshops, there were multiple discussions recognizing the limited disciplinary diversity represented at the workshops and the need to include other disciplines to address this “policy vortex,” a term coined by one participant when describing the clustering of human expectations drivers [8].

Limitations and research considerations

There were several limitations to this study. First, the participants of this study represented a handful of wildlife and zoonotic disease experts in California and the southwest. Additional research should include an increased number and diversity of organizations to understand if and how expert mental models shift. Second, the cognitive mapping approach needs to develop a more precise definition of causal effects to be able to assign direction and valence to the edges. Lastly, it is important to recognize that this study primarily engaged with western science knowledge regarding wildlife health. The literature has expressed growing calls to integrate traditional ecological knowledge into projects and policy trying to leverage One Health.

Conclusion

Mental models are a useful first step towards developing a common vision for integrating a One Health approach to address the occurrence and emergence of wildlife disease. Our mental modelling approach revealed that participants have a reasonably shared understanding of the central drivers of the wildlife health system but felt that they could not influence some of those drivers because of limited expertise and collaboration. An additional challenge was difficulty in recognizing boundary-spanning causal links between wildlife health themes, especially for human expectations drivers that are crucial for the implementation of management and policy solutions. Both these results indicate the need for One Health initiatives to provide training in system-level thinking, integrate multiple disciplines into decision-making, and build collaboration networks that span across boundaries especially into wildlife health themes that appear to be external constraints. The mental modelling approach implemented here can be used to begin the process of establishing a shared vision for One Health initiatives, or any attempt to build collaboration in a complex system.
