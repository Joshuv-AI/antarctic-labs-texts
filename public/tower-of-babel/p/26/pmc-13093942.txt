Frailty in older adults with type 2 diabetes: the importance of metabolic control and treatment adherence
Authors: Özlem Kardaş Kin, Arzu Güngör Tolasa, Ayşegül Çelik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFrailty is frequent in older adults with type 2 diabetes and may be associated with negatively affect treatment adherence and metabolic control.ObjectiveThis study aimed to assess frailty levels in older adults with type 2 diabetes and to examine their associations with treatment adherence and metabolic parameters.MethodsThis descriptive correlational study was conducted with 384 type 2 diabetes. Data were collected using “Personal Information Form”, “Edmonton Frail Scale (EFS)”, “Medication Adherence Report Scale (MARS)”, and “Metabolic Parameters Monitoring Form”. Descriptive statistics, correlation analyses and multiple linear regression analyses were performed.ResultsFrailty levels were significantly higher among women, those with lower education, longer disease duration, insulin or OAD+insulin users, and individuals with complications. Frailty was observed in 78.6% of participants, with 32.8% classified as severe. The mean MARS score was 14.3, indicating moderate adherence, which was lower among those with complications and OAD+insulin therapy. EFS scores showed significant associations with metabolic parameters and a negative correlation with MARS, suggesting reduced adherence with increasing frailty. Multiple regression confirmed the model predicting frailty was significant (F = 16.170, p < 0.001), explaining 28% of the variance (R²=0.28).ConclusionFrailty is highly prevalent among older adults with type 2 diabetes and is associated with lower medication adherence and adverse metabolic profiles. These findings underscore the importance of routine frailty screening and individualized, multidisciplinary care in diabetes management.Implications of the studyEarly identification of frailty in older adults with type 2 diabetes is essential to improve treatment adherence and metabolic outcomes. Integrating frailty screening into routine diabetes care can guide personalized interventions and support multidisciplinary management to reduce complications and enhance overall well-being.

Introduction

Diabetes Mellitus (DM) is a major global public health issue, affecting an estimated 537 million people worldwide, projected to rise to 783 million by 2045. In Turkiye, prevalence was 14.5% in 2021 and is expected to reach 16.2% by 2030 [1]. Such a rapid increase in diabetes prevalence adversely affects the quality of life and well-being of individuals and society, while also creating inevitable economic burdens [2].

Frailty has been defined as the third most common complication following the macrovascular and microvascular complications of diabetes [3]. Frailty is a dynamic syndrome marked by sarcopenia, malnutrition, cognitive decline, and fatigue, leading to metabolic imbalance and worsening the course of diabetes [4]. Moreover, frailty in people with diabetes leads to increased risks of falls, fractures, hospitalizations, and higher medical and healthcare expenditures [3]. Each unit increase in frailty score has been reported to increase long-term mortality risk by 93%, while the risk of complications is 2.62 times higher in individuals with both diabetes and frailty compared to non-frail individuals with diabetes [5].

Diabetes and frailty have a bidirectional relationship, with each exacerbating the other through mechanisms such as inflammation, oxidative stress, sarcopenia, and insulin resistance [6, 7]. While frailty is particularly prominent in older adults, the declining age of diabetes onset highlights its growing importance in younger adults as well [8]. Studies have further shown that frailty significantly increases the risk of moderate and severe hypoglycemia.

Treatment adherence is a key component of diabetes management; however, 20–60% of individuals with type 2 diabetes do not fully adhere to prescribed regimens. Poor adherence is consistently associated with suboptimal glycemic control, including higher HbA₁c and fasting glucose levels, and failure to achieve treatment targets [9–11]. Evidence from a recent systematic review indicates that higher adherence is linked to reduced hospitalization and complication rates [12].

Frailty and treatment nonadherence are frequently observed in older adults with type 2 diabetes and have been reported to be associated with poorer metabolic control and increased disease complexity. Previous studies suggest that frailty may coexist with physical, cognitive, and psychosocial limitations that are related to challenges in treatment adherence and metabolic regulation, while poor metabolic control is also linked to a higher burden of diabetes-related complications [1, 11, 13, 14].

However, despite growing interest in these interrelated factors, studies that examine frailty, treatment adherence, and metabolic parameters together in individuals with type 2 diabetes remain limited, particularly in older populations. Therefore, the aim of this study was to asses frailty levels in older adults with type 2 diabetes and to examine their associations with treatment adherence and metabolic parameters.

Method

Study design and population

This descriptive correlational study was conducted at the Endocrinology and Internal Medicine outpatient clinics of Izmir City Hospital between February and June 2025, targeting the population of patients under follow-up for type 2 diabetes at these units. Assuming a 50% prevalence, a 5% margin of error, and a 95% confidence level, the minimum sample size was calculated using the formula n = (t^2 × p × q) / (d^2). Accordingly, n = (1.96 × 1.96 × 0.5 × 0.5) / (0.05 × 0.05) = 384.16.A consecutive sampling approach was implemented for participant recruitment. Throughout the data collection period, all patients meeting the preliminary criterion of being aged 65 years or older with a documented diagnosis of type 2 diabetes were systematically approached and assessed for eligibility. The inclusion criteria comprised: (1) age ≥ 65 years, (2) diagnosis of type 2 diabetes, (3) ability to communicate verbally in Turkish, and (4) provision of written informed consent. Exclusion criteria were defined as: (1) refusal to participate, (2) presence of a diagnosed neuropsychiatric disorder (e.g., dementia), (3) significant uncorrected visual or auditory impairment hindering communication, or (4) a diagnosis of active malignancy. Data were obtained through researcher-administered, face-to-face surveys requiring approximately 15 min to complete, supplemented by metabolic parameters extracted from the patients’ electronic medical records.

Data collection

Personal information form

Developed by the researchers based on literature review, this form includes 11 questions assessing participants’ sociodemographic characteristics and disease-related conditions [6–8].

Metabolic parameters tracking form

To evaluate whether patients achieved target therapy, data were extracted from medical records, including fasting blood glucose, HbA1c (%), total cholesterol (mg/dl), triglycerides (mg/dl), HDL cholesterol (mg/dl), LDL cholesterol (mg/dl), and systolic/diastolic blood pressure (mmHg). Weight measurements were obtained using the clinic’s scale, and body mass index (BMI, kg/m²) was calculated.

Edmonton frail scale (EFS)

Developed by Rolfson et al. [15] in Canada and validated in Turkish by Aygör [16], the EFS consists of 11 items covering nine frailty domains: cognitive status, general health, functional independence, social support, medication use, nutrition, mood, continence, and functional performance [16]. Scores range from 0 to 17, with higher scores indicating greater frailty. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.75 in the original study and 0.83 in the current study.

Medication adherence report scale (MARS-5 / Turkish adaptation İUBÖ)

Originally developed by Horne and Weinman [17] , this self-report scale includes five items rated on a 5-point Likert scale (“Never” = 5, “Always” = 1), with higher total scores indicating better adherence. The Turkish version has a single-factor structure explaining 54% of variance, with Cronbach’s alpha of 0.78, and shows strong correlation with the Morisky Adherence Scale [18]. In this study, Cronbach alpha was determined as 0.79.

Study limitations

The sample was drawn from a single healthcare institution, limiting generalizability. Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics may not fully represent patients from other regions or institutions. Data collection relied on self-report, which may introduce recall bias or socially desirable responses. A limitation of this study is that medication adherence was assessed using a self-reported scale (MARS), which may be influenced by recall and social desirability bias; therefore, the findings were interpreted with caution. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; observed associations indicate relationships but not causality.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed with SPSS 25.0. Descriptive statistics summarized demographics and clinical features. Group comparisons used t-tests and one-way ANOVA with post-hoc tests. Correlations between EFS/MARS scores and metabolic parameters were assessed using Pearson correlation. Multiple linear regression identified predictors of frailty, explaining 28% of the variance (F = 16.170, p < 0.001). Significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics

The participants had a mean age of 67.7 years (SD = 8.2); 58.6% were female and 69.8% were married. Most (68.2%) had diabetes for 1–10 years. Common treatments were insulin (38.5%) and insulin plus OAD (32.8%). Comorbidities were present in 63.8%, mainly hypertension (19.3%) and heart failure (14.6%). Diabetes complications affected 70.6%, most often retinopathy (39.9%), neuropathy (24.4%), and diabetic foot (15.9%) (Table 1).

Frailty according to participant characteristics

Frailty scores differed significantly by sex, education, disease duration, treatment, and complications (p < 0.05). Women showed higher frailty than men. Frailty decreased with higher education, with the lowest scores among postgraduate graduates and the highest among those with lower education levels.

Frailty scores increased with diabetes duration: patients with < 1 year had the lowest scores, while those with > 10 years had the highest (p < 0.001). Frailty scores were higher in insulin and OAD+insulin users compared to OAD-only or insulin pump users (p < 0.001), with insulin pump users showing the lowest frailty. Participants with complications had significantly higher frailty than those without (Table 2).

Treatment adherence according to participant characteristics

No differences in MARS scores were found by sex, marital status, education, or disease duration (p > 0.05). However, adherence was lower in the OAD+insulin group compared to insulin pump and OAD-only groups, and in patients with complications compared to those without (Table 2).

Correlations between frailty, adherence, and metabolic parameters

EFS scores showed significant positive correlations with HbA1c (r = 0.291), FBG (r = 0.384), total cholesterol (r = 0.305), triglycerides (r = 0.270), LDL (r = 0.243), BMI (r = 0.387), SBP (r = 0.465), and DBP (r = 0.472) (all p < 0.001). A negative correlation was found with HDL (r = − 0.195, p < 0.001), indicating that poorer metabolic profiles are associated with higher frailty.

MARS scores were negatively correlated with FBG (r = − 0.101, p = 0.048), LDL (r = − 0.110, p = 0.031), BMI (r = − 0.129, p = 0.012), SBP (r = − 0.100, p = 0.050), and DBP (r = − 0.118, p = 0.021), and positively with HDL (r = 0.146, p = 0.004). No significant correlations were found with HbA1c (r = − 0.022, p = 0.673), total cholesterol (r = − 0.093, p = 0.068), or triglycerides (r = − 0.086, p = 0.092).

A weak but statistically significant negative correlation was observed between total EFS and MARS scores (ρ = −0.134, p = 0.009) (Table 3).

Predictors of frailty

In the study, a multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to examine the predictors of the EFS score, and the model was found to be statistically significant (F = 16.170, p < 0.001). The explanatory power of the model was at a moderate level (R = 0.53, R²=0.28), indicating that 28% of the variance in EFS scores was accounted for by the independent variables (Table 5). Although HbA1c was positively correlated with frailty, its independent association was attenuated in the multivariate model, suggesting that its effect may be mediated by related metabolic factors. Multicollinearity diagnostics indicated acceptable VIF values.

Discussion

Frailty is associated with poorer diabetes management and outcomes, particularly in older adults. Studies have shown that frailty is related to poor glycaemic control and low treatment adherence in type 2 diabetes [19]. Our findings revealed significant associations between frailty and factors such as gender, education, disease duration, treatment modality, and complications. Women exhibited higher frailty scores compared to men, which is consistent with findings from studies in Vietnam and Korea, where frailty rates were also higher in women [20–22]. These differences are often observed in relation to biological factors such as hormonal changes, muscle and fat distribution, and inflammatory response, as well as social roles and behavioral traits [23–25].

“Our study found that educational level was significantly associated with frailty. Lower education was associated with higher frailty scores, consistent with previous research. Khuc et al. and Rashidi et al. highlighted the role of education in frailty risk [26, 27]. De Lima et al. also showed that low education, particularly when combined with mobility limitations and pain, is associated with a higher frailty risk [28]. Education is linked to health literacy, self-care, and diabetes management, which may be related to resilience to complications and functional losses. Therefore, individuals with lower education levels may be more likely to experience frailty related to diabetes.

Our study found that frailty scores were higher with longer duration of diabetes, consistent with previous research highlighting the impact of chronic disease burden on frailty progression. Golchha et al. found a positive association between diabetes duration and frailty severity [29]. Similarly, Huang et al. showed that frailty prevalence rises with older age at diagnosis and increases over time [30]. This association may reflect the cumulative effects of long-term diabetes, including complications, functional decline, and complex treatment regimens, which are related to increased vulnerability and frailty.

Our study found that individuals treated with insulin or combined OAD+insulin had higher frailty levels compared to those on OADs or insulin pump therapy alone. This suggests that more intensive regimens might be associated with advanced disease and greater comorbidity burden. Similarly, the combined OAD+insulin group exhibited significantly lower treatment adherence scores than the insulin pump and OAD-only groups, indicating that frailty and treatment complexity may be related to adherence. These findings align with prior research showing that frailty complicates diabetes management, is linked to polypharmacy, and may increase the risk of adverse outcomes [31–33]. Additionally, certain antidiabetic agents, such as glinides, SGLT2 inhibitors, and insulin, have been associated with higher frailty levels, while metformin use is linked to better physical function [34]. Together, these results underscore the need for individualized, multidisciplinary approaches, integrating geriatric assessment and deprescribing strategies, to optimize treatment outcomes and adherence in frail older adults with diabetes.

Our study identified diabetes-related complications as a key factor associated with frailty. Frailty is related to nephropathy, microvascular and macrovascular diseases, cardiovascular events, hypoglycemia, and urolithiasis [35]. Participants with complications had lower medication adherence, reflecting the association between disease burden, frailty, and self-management. Frailty scores are related to an increased risk of microvascular complications, with each one-point increase being associated with a 10–20% higher risk [36]. Pre-frailty and frailty are associated with higher risks of nephropathy, neuropathy, retinopathy, morbidity, and mortality [37]. Poor adherence is linked to increased hospitalization risk by 3.14 times [38], while tailored education and family support may improve outcomes [39]. Early frailty screening and individualized care are important for optimizing adherence and preventing complications.

A weak but significant negative correlation was found between frailty and treatment adherence, indicating that higher frailty is associated with lower adherence. Frailty and type 2 diabetes are closely related, influencing older adults’ health and quality of life [40]. Frail diabetic patients often experience reduced physical and cognitive function, as well as increased risks of falls, hospitalization, and mortality [41]. Frailty is also linked to poorer medication adherence, with higher frailty scores associated with lower adherence [19]. The relationship between diabetes and frailty may involve insulin resistance, malnutrition, and complications [42]. These findings highlight the importance of comprehensive geriatric assessments and individualized care plans, with nurses playing a key role in monitoring frailty and supporting adherence through tailored interventions [43].

Our study found significant positive correlations between frailty scores and metabolic parameters such as HbA1c, fasting blood glucose, cholesterol levels, BMI, and blood pressure. HDL cholesterol showed a negative association with frailty. Multiple linear regression identified these variables as significant factors related to frailty, explaining 28% of the variance. These findings suggest that metabolic changes are associated with increased frailty in individuals with diabetes, highlighting the need for multidisciplinary care. The relationship between glycemic control and frailty is well-documented, with both hyperglycemia and hypoglycemia being linked to frailty. Hypoglycemia is associated with a higher frailty risk of 44% and greater dependency [44, 45]. While the protective effect of tight glycemic control is uncertain, maintaining HbA1c levels between 6.5% and 7.9% appears to be less strongly linked to frailty risk [4]. These results emphasize the importance of optimizing metabolic parameters and setting individualized glycemic targets to manage frailty. Nurses play a key role in monitoring metabolic parameters, supporting glycemic control, preventing hypoglycemia, and promoting lifestyle changes.

Our results align with previous studies linking dyslipidemia, obesity, and hypertension to physical decline and frailty. Frailty is associated with increased risks of type 2 diabetes and dyslipidemia [46]. The relationship between dyslipidemia, obesity, and chronic diseases highlights the need for comprehensive risk assessment and tailored interventions [47]. A systematic review found that abdominal obesity, hyperglycemia, and multiple risk factors are related to frailty in older adults, although evidence on dyslipidemia and blood pressure is inconsistent [48]. Frailty complicates diabetes and hypertension management, often contributing to polypharmacy. Early identification and deprescribing, guided by a multidisciplinary team, may improve outcomes and adherence [31, 49].

Conclusion

This study revealed that the majority of individuals with diabetes experienced varying levels of frailty, with particularly higher scores observed among women, those with lower educational attainment, longer disease duration, insulin or OAD+insulin treatment, and the presence of complications. Frailty was also found to be positively associated with metabolic parameters such as HbA1c, fasting blood glucose, lipid profile, body mass index, and blood pressure. These findings suggest that frailty in individuals with diabetes is not only related to aging but is also strongly strongly associated with sociodemographic, clinical, and metabolic factors. Furthermore, treatment adherence was lower at higher levels of frailty.

Based on these findings, considering frailty status may be valuable when evaluating individuals with diabetes, particularly those in higher-risk groups such as women, individuals with lower educational levels, and those with long-standing disease. Approaches that support health literacy and self-management may be relevant in this population. In addition, a multidisciplinary perspective that includes healthcare professionals from different disciplines may help inform individualized care planning and address the complexity of diabetes management in frail individuals.

Although causal inferences cannot be drawn due to the cross-sectional design, the results underscore the potential value of considering frailty status when evaluating older adults with diabetes, particularly those in higher-risk subgroups. Future longitudinal studies are needed to clarify temporal relationships and to determine whether targeted interventions addressing frailty may contribute to improved adherence and metabolic outcomes.
