Salivary assessment of the immune/inflammatory responses and oxidative stress in older adults vaccinated with CoronaVac or ChadOx-1
Authors: Jeniffer Vieira, Thiago Vinícius Vieira de Oliveira, Lorrayne Rithielle Rocha Queiroz, Camila Tussato Soares Camargo, Ariane Nardy, Fernanda Rodrigues Monteiro, Jônatas Bussador do Amaral, Vitória Paixão, Mauro Vaisberg, Gislene Rocha Amirato, Carlos André Freitas dos Santos, Edison Luiz Durigon, Danielle Bruna Leal Oliveira, Andressa Simões Aguiar, Anuska Marcelino Alvares-Saraiva, Débora Heller, Patricia Gabriela Viana Mantoanelli, Michelle Foigel Siqueira, Luiz Henrique da Silva Nali, André Luis Lacerda Bachi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough important information concerning COVID-19 vaccination is available, the effects of the CoronaVac and ChadOx-1 vaccines on immunity and the redox balance in the upper airway mucosa of the aged population are not fully understood. Therefore, the aim of this study was to investigate the impacts of two doses of the CoronaVac or ChadOx-1 vaccine on immune/inflammatory responses and oxidative stress in the airway mucosa of older adults.MethodsSeventy-six older adults of both sexes, with a mean age of 75.1 ± 6.4 years, were separated according to vaccination status into the CoronaVac (n = 52) and ChadOx-1 (n = 24) groups. Saliva samples were collected before (pre) and 30 days after (post) the administration of the second dose of the CoronaVac or ChadOx-1 vaccine to assess the levels of antibodies (sIgA and IgG), antimicrobial peptides, cytokines, and oxidant/antioxidant agents.ResultsThe immunogenicity in the ChadOx-1 group was 37.5% for sIgA and 25% for IgG, while that in the CoronaVac group was 18.9% for sIgA and 13.2% for IgG. Intergroup analysis revealed that (1) lower levels of IFN-α, IFN-γ, and IL-10 and a greater IFN-γ/IL-10 ratio, in addition to a greater IL-6/IL-10 ratio, were found in both the pre- and postvaccination periods, and (2) lower levels of total sIgA, IL-12p70, IL-17A, TNF-α, and the IL-12p70/IL-10 ratio, in addition to higher levels of specific sIgA for SARS-CoV-2 antigens and lysozyme, were observed only in the postvaccination period in the ChadOx-1 group than in the CoronaVac group. Intragroup analysis revealed (1) a significant increase in the salivary levels of total peroxides in the postvaccination period compared to those in the prevaccination period in both volunteer groups; (2) a decrease in the levels of lysozyme and the ratio between total antioxidant capacity (TAC) and total peroxides in the postvaccination period in the CoronaVac group compared with those in the prevaccination period; and (3) decreases in the TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-12p70 levels, and the IL-12p70/IL-10 ratio in the ChadoX-1 group, as well as a higher lactoferrin concentration in the postvaccination period than in the prevaccination period. Several positive and negative correlations between the parameters assessed here were found.ConclusionsIn general, the ChadOx-1 group exhibited improvements in both immune/inflammatory responses and redox balance and greater immunogenicity than did the CoronaVac group.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-024-05357-8.

Background

Since the World Health Organization (WHO) officially declared in March 2020 that the world was facing a pandemic caused by the new coronavirus SARS-CoV-2, which is responsible for coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), several studies have focused not only on defining the major clinical signs and predictors of COVID-19 symptoms but also on the development of a safe and efficient treatment for patients with this disease, mainly for those with severe COVID-19, as well as a vaccine that could be safe and effective in eliciting a robust and protective immune response against SARS-CoV-2, especially through neutralizing antibodies against the spike protein, a molecule found in the SARS-CoV-2 envelope that helps viral infection after it binds to the receptor angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) present in the cell membrane of host cells [1–3].

Despite the estimation that at least 1 in 5 individuals infected with SARS-CoV-2 never present symptoms, some factors, such as preexisting health conditions (particularly chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), cardiovascular diseases, hypertension, and particularly type 2 diabetes), increase the risk of severe COVID-19 infection and mortality [1, 4, 5]. Of interest, sex and an advanced age are also aspects related to different outcomes observed in COVID-19 patients. In this sense, although men and women present a similar prevalence of COVID-19, the male population, regardless of age, and individuals aged 50 years or older presented greater risks of worse outcomes and death from SARS-CoV-2 infection. Moreover, an association between viral load and age has not been reported [1, 4, 6–8].

According to the literature, one of the possible causes of the increases in the rate of infection, severity, and lethality caused by SARS-CoV-2 in the older adult population is the occurrence of immunosenescence, a phenomenon associated with a gradual decline in immune function during aging. In fact, this phenomenon can lead, among other factors, to increased susceptibility to infectious diseases [6, 7]. Importantly, based on the literature, one of the primary cell types affected by the SARS-CoV-2 virus is lymphocytes, which can lead to lymphocytopenia and consequently impair the immune response to COVID-19 [5, 9], particularly in older adults, due to alterations in the lymphocyte profile of this population with advancing age [10].

Beyond these aspects, some information regarding the immunity associated with the mucosa, especially in the upper airways, is worth mentioning. This tissue possesses several host defense mechanisms that are crucial for promoting the removal of inhaled particles and microorganisms, which are represented not only by a mechanical or physical barrier [11] but also by the secretion of saliva and, mainly, its compounds (mucus, secretory immunoglobulin A (sIgA) and antimicrobial peptides, among others) that are pivotal for preventing the occurrence of infections and chronic inflammatory diseases in the upper airways [11, 12]. Both the oral cavity and nasal passages contain higher frequencies of sIgA + B cells, which suggests that these mucosal effector tissues are similar. Therefore, the use of saliva is useful for evaluating not only the occurrence of diseases and health conditions in the oral cavity but also the systemic status of individuals since some compounds, such as IgG, move toward the saliva through the gingival crevicular fluid [13–16]. In fact, while IgG confers systemic protection, secretory IgA is mainly produced on epithelial surfaces to control the presence of the microbiota present in this area and to participate in the clearance of the pathogenic agent through its neutralizing capacity [17].

Concerning aging, researchers have postulated that the increased number of infections in the upper airways of older adults is associated with decreased antibody titers in response to immunization, such as those observed in response to flu vaccination, which leads to this population being more vulnerable to infections with influenza virus [18], even though some studies have shown that salivary sIgA concentrations can increase with age, which would guarantee the defense of the upper airways against infections, including viruses. Furthermore, alterations in or decreased levels of several proteins involved in proliferation, repair, and defense in the epithelial tissues present in the upper airways, combined with the presence of chronic inflammation at this site, can lead to an increased risk of abnormal microbial colonization, leading to loss of microbiota diversity that ultimately could have the potential to alter the protection of the upper airways against infectious agents in aged individuals [19].

In terms of immune/inflammatory responses in the airway mucosa, the presence of a respiratory virus, such as SARS-CoV-2, can trigger the expression of different types of cytokines that can not only assist in the clearance of the pathogenic agent but also generate protective immunity against viral infection [20, 21]. Notably, evidence has shown that nasal immunization is capable of inducing the expression of cytokines in the airway mucosa, which are involved in the activation of protective immunity. For instance, infection with a respiratory virus, such as the coronavirus, triggers the activation of an immune response, and the cytokines released in this situation can be useful both for immunity and as a signature of this infection, thus leading to a broader and unifying definition of functional biomarkers for this infection [20]. However, to date, most studies that aimed to evaluate the role of cytokines in SARS-CoV-2 infection have focused on systemic analyses, since patients, especially those with severe disease, can manifest a systemic inflammatory exacerbation known as a “cytokine storm” [22]. This outcome shows that the inflammatory response elicited by SARS-CoV-2 infection can contribute to worsening COVID-19 outcomes. Moreover, inflammation is closely associated with increased levels of free radicals, such as reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen species (RNS), which can drive oxidative stress if antioxidant defenses are insufficient to inhibit the harmful effects of these oxidant agents on the body's cells and tissues. Thus, oxidative stress is also a key component of the pathogenesis and severity of COVID-19 [23].

In addition to these pieces of information, during aging, the occurrence of a phenomenon called “inflammaging”, together with immunosenescence, can also be observed, which, according to Franceschi and collaborators [24], translates into chronic, sterile, systemic, low-grade inflammation associated with aging. Notably, while proinflammatory cytokines can act to prevent the occurrence of infection in early life, in the senescence period, the same cytokines can play a significant role in increasing the risk of disease and comorbidities [25].

Although most studies have focused on evaluating systemic immune/inflammatory responses in individuals with COVID-19, regardless of age, studies of these responses in the upper airway mucosa, both related to SARS-CoV-2 infection and its vaccination, are fundamental for improving our understanding not only of the natural pathogenesis of this infection but also of methods to achieve better control and the most efficient protection against COVID-19. Therefore, in the present study, we aimed to investigate the immune/inflammatory response and redox indices present in the upper airway mucosa in response to the administration of two doses of the CoronaVac or ChadOx-1 vaccine in older adults by assessing saliva.

Methods

Study population and design

This was a prospective and interventional pre-post clinical trial, involved 76 healthy older adults (mean age 75.1 ± 6.4 years) of both sexes who were vaccinated for COVID-19. All volunteers enrolled in the present study were recruited from the Geriatrics and Gerontology Discipline of the Federal University of São Paulo (UNIFESP). Data and biological samples were collected between January and February 2021 (before the administration of the first dose of the vaccine for COVID-19), between March and April 2021 (30 days after the administration of the second dose of the CoronaVac vaccine) or between May and June 2021 (30 days after the administration of the second dose of the ChadOx-1 vaccine). Importantly, this difference in terms of the collection of the second sample was due to the difference in the vaccination schedule for CoronaVac and ChadOx-1, since for the second dose of CoronaVac was administered approximately 28 days after the first dose was received, and the second dose of ChadOx-1 was administered approximately 120 days after the first dose was received.

Although, in general, we understand that performing research with a naturalistic and exploratory trial study design, in which a sample calculation is performed to establish a minimum number of participants, is important, we evaluated a population by convenience sampling in the present study. In this respect, the majority of clinical trials conducted in this period that aimed to assess immune responses to COVID-19 vaccines focused on systemic aspects. Thus, initially, we performed a sample size calculation to establish a minimum number of participants based on our previous studies involving immune responses in the mucosa of the upper airways not only for SARS-CoV-2 infection [26, 27] but also for influenza virus vaccination [28]. In addition, through the G*Power software program, the sample size and statistical power were estimated based on Student’s t test as an effect size of 0.30 at the α-level (0.05) and a statistical power of 0.95, with two (pre-post) measures. Considering a margin of 30% loss or refusal, a minimum of 74 individuals was predicted. As presented in the flow diagram (Fig. 1), we invited 95 older adults to participate in this study. However, due to the restrictions imposed by the Brazilian government during the COVID-19 pandemic, particularly before vaccination, some older adults declined to participate. In addition, other older adults did not participate based on the inclusion/exclusion criteria described below. Hence, even though we were able to perform the present study with a total of 76 older adults, those volunteers were separated into two different groups following vaccination, as described in the subsection "Vaccination for COVID-19". Therefore, the present study was performed with a convenience sample.

Participant selection criteria

The following inclusion criteria were applied: (1) voluntarily participated; (2) 60 years of age or older; (3) no symptoms or infection in the airways; and (4) received two doses of the vaccines for COVID-19. The exclusion criteria for patients were as follows: (1) had received a diagnosis of HIV infection or even presented with acute or chronic infection; (2) had received a diagnosis of any type of mental or physical illness, neoplasm, or liver or renal disease; (3) had received pharmacological therapy with anti-inflammatory drugs or convalescent plasma during any stage of the study; (4) were currently smoking or had stopped smoking for less than 20 years; and (5) were currently consuming alcohol.

Moreover, all volunteers were oriented to maintain their daily activity routines, and none of them reported being previously infected with SARS-CoV-2 until the study began or presented any symptoms associated with SARS-CoV-2 infection during the development of the present study.

Vaccination for COVID-19

In agreement with the vaccination schedule initially proposed for the Brazilian population by the National Health Surveillance Agency (ANVISA) of Brazil, older adult individuals not only were one of the first populations to receive COVID-19 vaccination but were also exclusively immunized in January–February 2021 with two available vaccines for COVID-19 during this period, namely, CoronaVac or ChadOx-1, which were provided free of charge by the Unified Health System (SUS), Brazil.

These two vaccines were produced using different technologies. In this respect, CoronaVac is a vaccine composed of the inactivated whole SARS-CoV-2 and represents the primary vaccination regimen of two doses with an interval of 2 to 4 weeks [31]. Concerning ChAdOx1 vaccine, also known as the AstraZeneca/Oxford vaccine, is a viral vector vaccine in which a modified chimpanzee adenovirus acts as a vector to deliver genetic material encoding the spike protein, a molecule found on the surface of the SARS-CoV-2 virus, into human cells and represents the primary vaccination regimen of two doses with an interval of 3 to 4 months [32, 33].

All volunteers received two doses of the same vaccine; for CoronaVac (n = 52), the interval between the first and second doses was approximately 28 days, and for ChadOx-1 (n = 24), it was approximately 120 days.

Collection of saliva samples

Saliva samples were collected on two different occasions: before (Pre) and 28 days after the administration of the second dose of the CoronaVac (n = 52) or ChadOx-1 (n = 24) vaccine. Two milliliters (2 mL) of the whole saliva sample from each volunteer was collected directly into sterile 15 mL Falcon® tubes without prior stimulation [34]. After collection, each sample was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 minutes at 4°C, and 500 µL of the supernatant was transferred to 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes and stored at -80°C for later use to measure antibody and antibacterial peptide concentrations. No buffers or preservatives were added.

When collecting saliva samples, the volunteers were asked to refrain from eating and drinking at least 1–2 hours before collection, and the samples were collected from 9:00 am to 11:00 am to decrease circadian effects [35]. Each volunteer was instructed to (1) remain seated and rest for a few minutes, (2) accumulate the saliva on the floor of the mouth, and finally (3) allow it to passively flow from the lip directly into a sterile 15 ml collection tube until it reached a volume of 2 ml. No volunteer reported the presence of any oral or dental disease or was receiving dental treatment; all of them were instructed to report any abnormalities in their buccal health during the study.

Determination of specific antibodies (IgA and IgG) for SARS-CoV-2 Antigens

Saliva aliquots (minimum 500 µL), obtained as previously described and stored at -80°C, were thawed at room temperature. Later, the SARS-CoV-2-specific IgA and IgG concentrations in saliva samples obtained from each volunteer group both before and after vaccination were determined using ELISA. In this assay, we followed the procedure previously described by our group [27], in which 96-well plates (Corning, New York, NY, USA) were coated with the same concentration (0.12 µg/mL) of the N and S antigens of SARS-CoV-2. A blocking step was performed to avoid nonspecific binding of antibodies using the buffer PBS-BSA-T [PBS (1X, pH: 7.3) plus 1% bovine fetal serum (Invitrogen by Thermo Fisher Scientific, Vienna, Austria) and 0.05% Tween 20 (Synth, Diadema, Brazil) at 37°C for 2 h. After the washing step, the saliva samples were diluted 1:2000 for IgA and 1:5000 for IgG in 0.1% PBS-BSA buffer containing 1 M NaCl, added to the plate, and incubated at 37°C for 2 h. After this incubation and a washing step, goat anti-human IgA (1:2000) and goat anti-human IgG (1:10,000), both of which were conjugated with horseradish peroxidase (Sigma‒Aldrich Co., Deisenhofen, Germany), were diluted in PBS-BSA, added to the plate, and incubated at 37°C for 1 h. After this period, a new washing step was performed, and the reaction was evaluated by adding 100 µL of TMB solution (3.3′.5′- tetramethylbenzidine, Thermo Scientific, MA, USA) to each well of the plate. Then, the reaction was stopped by the addition of a 2 N H2SO4 solution, and the reaction was read at 450 nm in a microplate reader (Labsystem Mulitskan MS, Artisan Technology Group: Champaign, IL, USA).

Determination of the salivary the Total Antioxidant Capacity (TAC) and total peroxide content

The salivary total antioxidant capacity (TAC) and total peroxide content were assessed using commercial colorimetric kits (BioAssay Systems, Hayward, CA, USA), QuantiChrom™ Antioxidant Assay Kit and QuantiChrom™ Peroxide Assay Kit, respectively. All analyses were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Determination of the salivary concentrations of total Secretory Immunoglobulin A (sIgA) and antibacterial peptides

The salivary concentrations of total secretory IgA (SIgA) and the antibacterial peptides lactoferrin and lysozyme were determined using commercial ELISA kits (Bioassay Technology Laboratory, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The intra- and interassay coefficients of variance were 2.5-4% and 4–7%, respectively. The values obtained were consistent with our previous reports [28, 36].

Determination of the salivary concentrations of albumin and uric acid

The salivary concentrations of albumin and uric acid were determined using commercial colorimetric kits (Labtest Diagnostics S.A., Minas Gerais, MG, Brazil) according to the manufacturer's instructions, with 3–4% intra-assay and 4.5–6.5% interassay coefficients of variance. The values obtained were consistent with our previous report [15].

Determination of the salivary concentrations of cytokines

Cytokine concentrations (IL-6, IL-10, IL-12p70, IL-17A, TNF-α, IFN-α, and IFN-γ) in the saliva were measured using the LEGENDPlex™ bead-based system for human cytokine kits (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Notably, the concentration of each cytokine was calculated based on the respective standard curve (provided by the manufacturer) that was obtained at the same time as the sample evaluation. The linearity of the multiplex assay was within the 2.4–10,000 pg/mL range, which includes the range of sample determinations, and the lower limit of detection for each cytokine was calculated by comparing it to the reference value provided by the kit’s manufacturer over 2.5 standard deviations from these values. All analyses were performed with a BD Accuri™ C6 Plus Flow Cytometer (BD Biosciences San Jose, CA, USA), and the data obtained were analyzed with LEGENDPlex™ V8.0 software (BioLegend). The values obtained agreed with those of a recent review [37].

Statistical analysis

The data were initially compared with Gaussian curves, and both the normality and homogeneity of the data were determined using the Shapiro‒Wilk and Levene tests, respectively. For the intragroup analysis, paired Student’s t test was applied for the parametric variables presented as the means and standard deviations (SDs), whereas the Wilcoxon test was used for the nonparametric variables presented as the medians and interquartile ranges (25–75%). For the intergroup analysis, one-way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test was used for parametric variables, and the Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s post hoc test was used for nonparametric variables. In addition, Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient test was also performed. Demographics and the ratio between the number of older men and older women in each volunteer group were evaluated using the chi-square test. The effect size (ES) was calculated using Cohen’s coefficient and the following values: (i) between 0.2 and 0.49 indicated a small effect; (ii) between 0.5 and 0.79 indicated a moderate effect; and (iii) greater than 0.8 indicated a large effect [38]. Differences with p values < 0.05 (alpha-risk of 5%) were considered to indicate statistical significance.

Figure 2 shows the results obtained for the salivary levels of sIgA and IgG specific for SARS-CoV-2 antigens in the volunteer groups. First, some volunteers presented specific sIgA (Fig. 2A) and IgG (Fig. 2C) for SARS-CoV-2 antigens at the prevaccination time point, even though none of them reported a previous infection with SARS-CoV-2. In addition, higher levels of specific sIgA for SARS-CoV-2 antigens were detected postvaccination in the volunteers who received the ChadOx-1 vaccine than in those who received the CoronaVac vaccine (Fig. 2A, p = 0.0028, ES = 0.72), whereas a significant reduction in the salivary levels of specific IgG for SARS-CoV-2 antigens was detected in the CoronaVac group postvaccination compared with the prevaccination values (Fig. 2C, p = 0.0045, ES = 0.35). In relation to immunogenicity, the volunteers who received the ChadOx-1 vaccine presented greater immunogenicity, both for salivary levels of sIgA (Fig. 2B, 37.5%) and for IgG (Fig. 2D, 25%), than did the volunteers who received the CoronaVac vaccine [sIgA (Fig. 2B, 18.9%) and for IgG (Fig. 2D, 13.2%)].

Figure S1 shows a comparison of specific antibody responses to COVID-19 vaccination between the volunteer groups stratified by sex to verify whether sex impacted our results. Regarding the salivary levels of sIgA (Figure S1A), both older women (p = 0.039, ES = 0.58) and older men (p = 0.004, ES = 1.33) who received the ChadOx-1 vaccine showed higher levels of this antibody postvaccination than did volunteers who received the CoronaVac vaccine. Moreover, the prevaccination salivary levels of IgG (Figure S1B) in older women (p = 0.018, ES = 0.32) and older men (p = 0.039, ES = 0.35) who received the CoronaVac vaccine were higher than those at the postvaccination time point. Based on these observations, in which similar results were found when separating the volunteers by sex, we opted to maintain the grouping of the participants in the subsequent analysis.

According to the results presented in Fig. 3, the intergroup analysis showed that although the salivary levels of total sIgA were lower (Fig. 3A, p = 0.0007, ES = 0.90), the lysozyme levels observed in the ChadOx-1 group were higher than those in the CoronaVac group at the postvaccination time point (Fig. 3B, p = 0.0014, ES = 0.78). According to the intragroup analysis, although the salivary levels of lysozyme (Fig. 3B, 0.0146, ES = 0.22) and the TAC/peroxide ratio (Fig. 3H, p = 0.0018, ES = 0.38) were significantly lower in the CoronaVac group than in the control group, the salivary levels of lactoferrin (Fig. 3C, 0.0193, ES = 0.37) were higher in the ChadOx-1 group at the postvaccination time point than in the prevaccination time point. Last, both volunteer groups showed a significant decrease in the salivary total peroxide levels postvaccination compared to the data obtained at the prevaccination time point (Fig. 3G; p = 0.0001 and ES = 0.55 for the CoronaVac group; p = 0.0383 and ES = 0.49 for the ChadOx-1 group). Salivary levels of albumin (Fig. 3D), uric acid (Fig. 3E), and TAC (Fig. 3F) were unchanged at the two time points assessed.

Figure 5 shows the ratios of the proinflammatory cytokines IL-6 (Fig. 5A), IL-12p70 (Fig. 5B), IL-17A (Fig. 5C), IFN-α (Fig. 5D), IFN-γ (Fig. 5E), and TNF-α (Fig. 5F) to the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10. According to the intragroup analysis, only a significant reduction in the IL-12p70/IL-10 ratio was observed in the ChadOx-1 group postvaccination compared to that in the prevaccination assessment (p = 0.0147, ES = 0.40). Discussion

In the present study, we were able to determine that some volunteers presented specific sIgA and IgG for SARS-CoV-2 antigens during the prevaccination period, even though none of them had previously been infected with this virus. Therefore, we can suggest that these volunteers were asymptomatic for SARS-CoV-2 infection. Consistent with the literature, most elderly individuals infected with SARS-CoV-2 remain asymptomatic and never progress to severe COVID-19 [39]. Furthermore, although both volunteer groups showed increased levels of specific sIgA and IgG postvaccination, the group that received the ChadOx-1 vaccine presented a prominent increase in the levels of these antibodies compared with the CoronaVac group, indicating that the ChadOx-1 vaccine was able to induce greater immunogenicity than the CoronaVac vaccine in terms of mucosal immune protection.

The mucosa represents an important immunological barrier to the entry of many external pathogenic agents, and, specifically, the immune system present in the mucosa of the oral cavity has the capacity to secrete high levels of antibodies, which are considered the “first line of defense”, providing evident resistance to infections in the upper respiratory tract due to their evident neutralizing ability when directed against pathogens present in the oral mucosa [40]. In particular, sIgA, mainly the IgA1 isotype, is the most abundant immunoglobulin in the airway mucosa, and reduced levels of this antibody lead to an increased risk of developing upper respiratory tract infections (URTIs), especially infections caused by viruses [41]. Important, in the nasal mucosa and salivary glands, which are the main sites of sIgA production in the upper airways, the quantity (mg/mL) of this type of antibody is approximately 70–846 and 194–206, respectively [42]. Although the salivary levels of total sIgA in the ChadOx-1 group were lower in the postvaccination period than the levels in the CoronaVac group, which could suggest a decrease in local immunity, we observed not only higher salivary levels of specific sIgA for SARS-CoV-2 antigens in the postvaccination period but also greater immunogenicity in this group (ChadOx-1). Therefore, even though we cannot explain the difference in the salivary levels of total sIgA between the volunteer groups, the specific protection for SARS-CoV-2, particularly in terms of antibodies, in the upper airway mucosa of the volunteers in the ChadoX-1 group was likely better than that of volunteers in the CoronaVac group.

The corollary action of sIgA against respiratory virus infections in the mucosa of the upper airways has been corroborated, for instance, during a primary natural infection with the influenza virus, the respiratory virus that causes flu. Although different types of antibodies can be detected, the sIgA isotype is the most abundant and provides the greatest protection due to its neutralizing activity. This local response to infection with the influenza virus can last up to 5 months, and after a second exposure, the sIgA levels increase significantly, indicating the presence of local memory cells. Interestingly, the existence of neutralizing antibodies produced locally in response to vaccination against the influenza virus proved to be important in ensuring resistance to infection and disease when a natural challenge occurs with the wild pathogen [40]. In summary, the data collected show that the immunity in the airway mucosa generated by natural infection or even vaccination is effective in promoting resistance to respiratory viral infections.

With respect to aging, both the production and secretion of antibodies in the mucosa, mainly sIgA, can be affected by immunosenescence due to a gradual reduction in the level of this immunoglobulin type with advancing age [43]. In fact, our research group recently found that older adult practitioners of combined exercise training presented higher salivary sIgA levels in response to vaccination for influenza virus than did nonpractitioner older adults, suggesting that the regular practice of physical exercise can minimize the negative effects of aging on immune responses, including those in the airway mucosa, in the older adult population [44].

Specifically, in the context of COVID-19, although significant information concerning immune responses to this disease has been provided, in general, most of these data are associated with systemic immune responses. Thus, limited data related to the role of immune responses in the airway mucosal response to SARS-CoV-2 infection or COVID-19 vaccination are available. In this sense, we previously reported that increased secretion of sIgA varies according to the severity of the disease, since individuals with severe symptoms presented higher concentrations of this antibody than individuals with mild symptoms [44].

In contrast to the studies reported above, the present study aimed to investigate the response of several immune/inflammatory components of the airway mucosa, particularly antibodies, in healthy older adults receiving two doses of COVID-19 vaccines. This finding could help us to understand the immunogenicity in older adults who received the CoronaVac (18.9%) or ChadOx-1 vaccines (37.5%), particularly in terms of salivary sIgA levels. Importantly, the observed immunogenicity in the ChadOx-1 group agreed with the data presented by Sheikh-Mohamed and collaborators, in which 30% of volunteers who received two doses of the mRNA vaccine for COVID-19 were positive for IgA during this period [45].

In addition to the sIgA assessment, we also evaluated the salivary levels of specific IgG for SARS-CoV-2 antigens. Although this immunoglobulin can be detected in salivary samples, its quantity is lower than that of sIgA. In general, the IgG detected in the saliva is mainly derived from the gingival crevicular fluid, an interface between the tooth and the gum, and it can also be used as an important indicator in the evaluation of systemic immunological responses [46–48]. Based on this information, IgG has greater systemic protective activity and, consequently, can be detected at lower levels in the mucosa, mainly in the upper airways [44]. Regardless, both sIgA and IgG can act in conjunction to protect the mucosa against infectious agents. Therefore, our observation of positive correlations between salivary levels of sIgA and IgG in the older adult groups who received the CoronaVac or ChadOx-1 vaccines, both in the pre- and postvaccination periods, can corroborate this proposition.

Although assessments of antibody variations are considered the gold standard for analyzing mucosal immunity, the study of antimicrobial peptides has increased substantially since they are considered an integral part of the innate response present in the airway mucosa of individuals [49]. In this sense, this study also aimed to verify the variation in some antimicrobial peptides, such as lysozyme and lactoferrin, in the saliva of older adults in response to vaccination for COVID-19 using the CoronaVac or ChadOx-1 vaccine.

Lactoferrin is a protein that was first described in 1966, and since then, it has gained greater importance for its anti-inflammatory, antibacterial and antiviral activities. The effects of lactoferrin on chelating iron, a mineral that not only plays important roles in oxidation and reduction reactions and oxygen transport but is also essential for DNA replication and energy generation in microorganisms, have been highlighted due to its immunoprotective effects on iron transport and metabolism. In particular, in a viral context, lactoferrin was reported to protect against HIV infection, rotavirus, herpes simplex virus, human papillomavirus, and cytomegalovirus. More specifically, for coronavirus, this antimicrobial agent was able to inhibit the invasion of the pseudovirus SARS-CoV, which led to the suggestion that lactoferrin could interfere with SARS-CoV-2 infection [50]. Interestingly, an increase in salivary lactoferrin levels was observed in the older adult group that received the ChadOx-1 vaccine, suggesting that an improvement in immunity in the oral cavity could be induced by this vaccine.

Another interesting finding was related to the significant positive correlations observed between lactoferrin and some parameters in both the CoronaVac and ChadOx-1 groups. Concerning the ChadOx-1 group, the positive correlation between lactoferrin and total sIgA or lysozyme levels in the prevaccination period illustrates that a remarkable local immunoprotective status was present and that it was partially maintained in the postvaccination period since a positive correlation was observed only between the salivary levels of lactoferrin and lysozyme. In the CoronaVac group, similar to the findings described above, a significant positive correlation was also detected between the salivary levels of lactoferrin and total sIgA in the prevaccination period, which was maintained after vaccination. However, in contrast to the results observed in the ChadOx-1 group, the volunteers who received the CoronaVac vaccine showed significant positive correlations between the salivary levels of lactoferrin and lysozyme or between specific sIgA and IgG for SARS-CoV-2 antigens, suggesting the existence of a balanced pattern of secretion of these protective agents in the mucosa of this group, even in the absence of SARS-CoV-19 infection.

Lysozyme is a protein that not only has prominent resistance to denaturation [51] but also has broad antimicrobial activity, which offers protection against bacteria, fungi, and viruses. This antimicrobial peptide was first described by Alexander Flemming in 1922 and has shown pivotal activity against viral infections, such as herpes simplex, herpes zoster, and HIV [50]. In accordance with our results, while the salivary lysozyme levels were unchanged in the ChadOx-1 group between the two periods assessed, the CoronaVac group showed lower levels of this peptide after vaccination than in the prevaccination period, and compared with the level in the ChadOx-1 group in the postvaccination period.

However, we cannot affirm that the increased salivary lysozyme levels observed in the prevaccination period for the CoronaVac group could be attributed to the presence of salivary levels of specific sIgA for SARS-CoV-2 antigens since a positive correlation between these immunological parameters was exclusively detected at this time point. Furthermore, these findings do not suggest that these volunteers could have been infected only with SARS-CoV-2 and that their asymptomatic status could have been influenced by the remarkable defensive actions of these two molecules (lysozyme and sIgA) against this viral infection. Consistent with this suggestion, in which the collaborative action of lysozyme and antibodies in saliva may be essential for both inducing and improving mucosal protection against SARS-CoV-2 infection, a positive correlation between the salivary levels of lysozyme and IgG in the ChadOx-1 group was also observed before vaccination.

Beyond the information obtained from the assessments of the salivary levels of antibodies and antimicrobial peptides, studies of oxidative stress and cytokines are also relevant in the context of SARS-CoV-2 infection and vaccination responses. In this sense, according to the literature, SARS-CoV-2 infection can induce a remarkable inflammatory response, in which the increased severity and risk of death in COVID-19 patients are closely related to the manifestation of the “cytokine storm”, a phenomenon characterized by an exaggerated increase in inflammatory cytokine levels in infected individuals [52]. Therefore, this aspect shows that the "dose" of inflammation induced by SARS-CoV-2 infection, which is considered one of the pillars in the protection against this pathogen, can also contribute to the worsening of COVID-19 [27]. Moreover, most studies that aimed to investigate the role of cytokines in the context of COVID-19 have focused on systemic aspects, and their effects on the upper airway mucosa are not fully understood since systemic cytokine levels do not necessarily reflect cytokine levels in the upper airway mucosa [34, 53].

Concerning the role of cytokines present in the upper airway mucosa induced in individuals infected with SARS-CoV-2, our research group previously showed that the increased local production of some proinflammatory cytokines, such as IFNs (types I and II), IL-12p70, and IL-17, as well as of the anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-10 and IL-37, in response to this infection could be useful and pivotal for mediating the immune response against this virus. Furthermore, not only a higher ratio between pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines but also some associations between the cytokines assessed in these studies are important for improving local immune protection against SARS-CoV-2 infection, particularly through the production of specific sIgA against this virus [26, 27].

In contrast to most of the studies, this study assessed the immune/inflammatory responses in the upper airway mucosa, specifically the oral cavity, of older adults in a COVID-19 vaccination context. In this sense, our findings showed that, in general, the ChadOx-1 group presented lower salivary levels of cytokines, both pro- and anti-inflammatory, than did the CoronaVac group, regardless of the period evaluated. At this point, we should mention that the elevated salivary levels of IFN-α and IFN-γ detected in the CoronaVac group in the prevaccination period supposedly reinforce the suggestion that these volunteers had previous contact with SARS-CoV-2 since, consistent with the literature, both type I interferon (IFN-α and IFN-β) and type II interferon (IFN-γ) play crucial roles in coronavirus infections, including MERS-CoV and SARS-CoVs, not only because of their prominent capacity to inhibit the replication of these viruses but also because of their ability to drive viral clearance/elimination in the upper airways [21, 54–56], which could lead to a "satisfactory" clinical outcome, including an asymptomatic state, as observed here. In addition, the exacerbated increase in IFN levels observed in patients with severe COVID-19 can disturb its activity since IFN responses are reduced in those patients [57]. Based on this information, the regulation of IFN levels is clearly essential to guarantee better IFN action in SARS-CoV-2 infection. Thus, the increased salivary levels of IL-10 detected in the same volunteer group (CoronaVac) could be useful for modulating the inflammatory status in the upper airways of these individuals, leading to an optimized immune response to SARS-CoV-2 infection. Consistent with this suggestion, the ratio of IFN-α and IFN-γ to IL-10 was generally regulated, and a significant increase in the IFN-α/IL-10 ratio was observed in the CoronaVac group compared to that in the ChadOx-1 group during the prevaccination period, suggesting that a regulated IFN response was induced and promoted virus elimination without the manifestation of any symptoms. Additionally, the significant negative correlation between the salivary levels of IFN-γ and the immunological parameters (sIgA, IgG, lactoferrin, and lysozyme) observed in both groups, regardless of the period assessed, suggested that the regulation of this proinflammatory cytokine was important for maintaining immune protection in the mucosa of the upper airways.

In this sense, the data obtained from the ChadOx-1 group indicated significant reductions in the levels of some proinflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-12p70, and TNF-α) after vaccination compared to their levels in the prevaccination period, as well as lower salivary cytokine levels and higher IL-6/IL-10 ratios than those in the CoronaVac group. These findings also support our suggestion that a controlled inflammatory status can generate better conditions to induce a protective immune response to SARS-CoV-2 infection or even to vaccination, including in the upper airway mucosa. Moreover, the positive correlation between the salivary levels of specific sIgA for SARS-CoV-2 antigens and IL-6 or IL-10 found in the ChadOx-1 group in the postvaccination period is a remarkable finding since elevated levels of IL-6 in the submandibular glands favored local IgA synthesis [58]. However, these two cytokines are also associated with the Th2 immune response, a well-known type of immune response related to the induction of immature B-cell differentiation into IgA-secreting plasma cells, which leads to increased sIgA synthesis in the mucosa [59]. Considering these data, we suggest that the better immune response to COVID-19 vaccination observed in the ChadOx-1 group could have been generated in association with the development of the Th2 profile.

In conjunction with inflammation, increases in the levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and nitrogen species (RNS), such as peroxides, are involved in the induction of oxidative stress, which, when not controlled by antioxidant defenses, is a key component of the pathogenesis and severity of many diseases, including COVID-19 [23]. Over time, organisms are continuously exposed to exogenous and endogenous oxidant agents, which need to be controlled by intrinsic or extrinsic antioxidant defenses/agents to avoid the harmful effects of these oxidant agents on cells and tissues/organs [60]. However, during aging, constant exposure to oxidant agents associated with a decrease in the protection provided by antioxidant agents favors an imbalance between these agents, leading to marked alterations in some homeostatic systems, especially the redox balance [15, 60, 61]. In this sense, the assessment of oxidative stress through the evaluation of both oxidizing agents, such as total peroxides, and antioxidant agents, such as total antioxidant capacity (TAC), is essential to improve our understanding of the homeostatic conditions of the body to respond to immunological challenges, particularly vaccination, in older adults.

Interestingly, both volunteer groups showed a significant increase in the salivary levels of total peroxides after vaccination, whereas the TAC levels did not change, and the TAC/peroxides ratio decreased significantly in the postvaccination period in the CoronaVac group. Even though these findings could represent an instability of the redox balance, an increase in total peroxide levels, such as the levels of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), is supposedly involved in enhancing the upper airway mucosal protection mechanism against microorganisms, including viruses, since these molecules present prominent antimicrobial activity [62, 63]. Furthermore, the regulated production of peroxides is crucial for the burst of oxidative stress occurring in phagocytes, preferentially polymorphonuclear cells, and these molecules can move toward the outside of these cells [64, 65]; together with antimicrobial peptides, they improve host defenses against pathogens [63]. In this sense, the negative correlation between the salivary levels of TAC and lactoferrin in the CoronaVac group in the postvaccination period may indicate that the elevation of the lactoferrin-derived immunoprotective mechanism in the oral cavity was achieved in association with a reduced TAC. In contrast, the enhancement of the immunity provided by the specific sIgA for SARS-CoV-2 antigens in the ChadOx-1 group in the postvaccination period was closely associated with the presence of TAC, since a positive correlation between these molecules was verified. At this point, we must note that this suggestion is supported by the observation that none of the volunteers presented any discomfort, alterations, or diseases, especially periodontitis, in their oral cavity during the study, which could directly impact our results.

Beyond this information, we did not assess other salivary scavenging systems for oxidizing agents, particularly those involved in the detoxification of peroxides, such as the enzymes peroxidase and myeloperoxidase [66, 67], which can constitute a limitation of the study. In general, the redox balance was maintained in the volunteer groups since the salivary concentrations of uric acid and albumin, two remarkable antioxidant agents present in saliva [68, 69], were unchanged during the study. Finally, the negative correlation between the salivary levels of albumin and lysozyme in the ChadOx-1 group in the postvaccination period corroborates the former observation of the correlation between TAC and lactoferrin levels, in which the increased levels of antimicrobial peptides are associated with a decrease in the levels of antioxidant agents.

Similarly, other limitations of the study can be attributed to (a) the impossibility of comparing the data obtained from our volunteer groups with data obtained from young groups; (b) the lack of information about the systemic antibody response to COVID-19 vaccination, inflammatory status, and redox indices of the volunteers; and (c) the number of participants, as well as the convenience sample. In this respect, we must highlight the following factors: 1) although we were able to initially enroll a minimal number of volunteers to participate in this study (sample size, n = 76), due to the two options of COVID-9 vaccines applied to the Brazilian population during the study period, we had to separate the volunteers into two groups according to the vaccine received, and thus the sample size in each group no longer reached the minimum calculated size; 2) the main findings in this study were obtained by assessing and comparing the data for each volunteer both before and after vaccination for COVID-19 in a duplicate analysis, which can mitigate this drawback.

Although our data can improve our understanding of the immune/inflammatory responses and redox balance present in the mucosa of the upper airways following COVID-19 vaccination, we agree that further studies are necessary to better understand the potential effect of COVID-19 vaccination on this important tissue, especially in the older adult population.

Conclusions

In summary, our results allowed us to conclude that older adults who received two doses of the ChadOx-1 vaccine presented an improved immune response in the oral mucosa against COVID-19, which included specific sIgA for SARS-CoV-2 antigens, antimicrobial peptides, controlled inflammation, and redox balance, as well as better immunogenicity, than older adults who received two doses of the CoronaVac vaccine. Based on these findings, a plausible hypothesis is that using the ChadOx-1 vaccine could be specifically recommended in the context of COVID-19 vaccine strategies for older adults.
