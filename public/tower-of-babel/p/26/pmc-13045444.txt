A Rare Case of Isovaleric Acidemia With Hyperammonemia Caused by Compound Heterozygous IVD Variants: Clinical Features, Molecular Genetics, and Therapeutic Follow‐Up
Authors: Zhi Deng, Guojing Wu, Shangqing Chen, Hui He, Ye Zhang, Xianqiong Luo, Shu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTo improve understanding of isovaleric acidemia (IVA) by analyzing the clinical features, genetic basis, and treatment outcomes of a child with chronic intermittent IVA. A 4‐month‐old infant presenting with lethargy, pallor, and growth retardation was diagnosed with IVA using tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS), urinary organic acid analysis, and genetic testing. MS/MS and urine gas chromatography–mass spectrometry revealed elevated isovalerylcarnitine and isovalerylglycine. Whole‐exome sequencing identified a novel homozygous IVD gene variant (c.899_905delinsCCCACAG) in the proband, with both parents confirmed as carriers. The patient was treated with a leucine‐restricted diet, glycine, L‐carnitine, and arginine. Follow‐up showed reduced metabolite levels and improved neurodevelopment. IVA is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by isovaleryl‐CoA dehydrogenase deficiency, leading to toxic metabolite accumulation. Clinical symptoms are nonspecific, often resulting in misdiagnosis. Early use of MS/MS and genetic testing enables timely diagnosis and targeted treatment, improving outcomes.Keywords: genetic testing, isovaleric acidemia, IVD gene, tandem mass spectrometry

Introduction

Isovaleric acidemia (IVA, OMIM #243500) is an autosomal recessive metabolic disorder caused by deficiency of isovaleryl‐CoA dehydrogenase (IVD) in the leucine catabolic pathway, leading to accumulation of isovaleric acid, isovalerylcarnitine (C5), isovalerylglycine (IVG), and 3‐hydroxyisovaleric acid [1]. Clinically, IVA is classified into acute neonatal, chronic intermittent, and asymptomatic forms [2]. The acute neonatal form presents with feeding difficulties, vomiting, ketoacidosis, a characteristic “sweaty feet” odor, and bone marrow suppression—a feature distinguishing it from other organic acidemias [1]. Chronic intermittent type typically manifests after the neonatal period with non‐specific developmental delay, often precipitated by infections or high‐protein intake [3]. The asymptomatic form is usually identified through newborn screening [4]. Treatment centers on dietary leucine restriction, glycine and L‐carnitine supplementation, and acute management of metabolic decompensation [5]. Here, we report a pediatric patient with chronic intermittent IVA carrying a previously unreported homozygous IVD variant, c.899_905delinsCCCACAG (p.Asp300_Thr302delinsAlaHisSer), and summarize the diagnostic, therapeutic, and follow‐up course.

Case Presentation

The patient was a 4‐month‐28‐day‐old boy admitted with a history of motor developmental delay for over 20 days. He was the first child (G1P1) of non‐consanguineous parents related at the fourth degree of kinship. Delivered vaginally at 36 + 6 weeks of gestation without perinatal asphyxia or resuscitation, the neonate recorded a birth weight of 2.6 kg and a length of 48 cm. There was no family history of similar disorders. The mother denied abnormal pregnancy events or perinatal complications. Postnatally, the infant fed normally without recurrent vomiting or seizures. At presentation, he was able to lift his head but with poor head control and had not yet achieved rolling over. Tandem mass spectrometry performed at a local hospital revealed elevated C5 (isovalerylcarnitine), so the patient was referred to our center for further evaluation.

On admission, the infant exhibited lethargy, poor responsiveness, and pallor. Physical examination revealed: weight 7.3 kg (+0.04 SD), length 70.4 cm (+2.3 SD), and head circumference 40.5 cm (−1.1 SD). He was conscious but poorly responsive, with no characteristic odor spotted. The skin showed no cyanosis, rash, or petechiae. The anterior fontanel was flat and soft. Muscle tone was reduced in all the four limbs, in company with weak primitive reflexes. Nevertheless, cardiopulmonary and abdominal examinations were both unremarkable.

Laboratory findings showed neutrophil count (NEUT) of 0.57 × 109/L and elevated blood ammonia (90 μmol/L; reference: 0–30 μmol/L). Tandem mass spectrometry revealed markedly elevated C5 (20.779 μmol/L; reference: 0.04–0.40 μmol/L) as well as C5/acetylcarnitine (C2) ratio (1.51; reference: 0–0.04). Also, urinary tandem MS indicated significantly increased IVG (12,078.64; reference: 0–0.4). Blood gas analysis and electroencephalogram were unremarkable. Cranial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated widened subarachnoid space in the bilateral frontal and temporal lobes, meanwhile, magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) revealed double lactate peaks (Figure 1). Based on the biochemical and metabolic findings in conjunction with the clinical presentation, the patient was diagnosed with IVA. Neurodevelopmental assessment using the Bayley Infant Development Scales indicated a Mental Development Index (MDI) of 53, which is equivalent to 2.1 months of age, and a Psychomotor Development Index (PDI) of 57, equivalent to below 2 months of age—both suggesting significant developmental delay.

Magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) demonstrates two elevated lactate peaks (indicated by white arrows), suggesting metabolic abnormality.

Treatment

While awaiting whole‐exome sequencing results, the patient was placed on a specialized leucine‐free formula and received supplementation with L‐carnitine (200 mg/kg·d), glycine (450 mg/kg·d), and arginine (dose added) to reduce hyperammonemia. After four days of treatment, the infant showed shortened sleep duration compared with the baseline. Repeated tandem mass spectrometry revealed C5 = 11.103 μmol/L, C5/C2 ratio = 0.44, urinary IVG = 461.53, and blood ammonia = 17 μmol/L. The patient was subsequently discharged.

Thereafter, post‐discharge treatment with L‐carnitine and glycine was continued. Under strict control of natural protein intake, regular formula feeding was gradually introduced at the same time with a progressive reduction in the leucine‐free formula. Regular follow‐up assessments, including plasma C5, C5/C2 ratio, plasma ammonia, and complete blood counts (neutrophil and platelet counts), showed that although occasional fluctuations occurred, C5 levels continued to decline with dietary modifications and medication dose adjustments. Plasma ammonia, neutrophil, and platelet counts remained stable (Table 1 and Figures 2, 3). In addition, urinary organic acid analysis further demonstrated a sustained decrease in urinary isovalerylglycine and 3‐hydroxyisovaleric acid levels (Figure 4).

Genetic Analysis

Although the biochemical findings were highly suggestive of isovaleric acidemia, the nonspecific clinical features warranted a broader genetic approach. Whole‐exome sequencing was therefore performed to simultaneously evaluate other inherited metabolic disorders and to capture potential novel variants in IVD. This strategy led to the identification of a homozygous deletion–insertion variant in IVD, which was subsequently validated by Sanger sequencing. Whole‐exome sequencing revealed a homozygous variant in the IVD gene (NM_002225): c.899_905delinsCCCACAG (p.Asp300_Thr302delinsAlaHisSer). Sanger sequencing confirmed that both parents were heterozygous carriers of this variant (Figure 5). By contrast, no such variant was identified in 50 unrelated healthy controls. Moreover, the variant has not been reported in the gnomAD, HGMD, or ClinVar databases. Bioinformatic analyses predicted it to be potentially pathogenic.

As is known, the IVD gene, which consists of 12 exons containing 1272 nucleotides, encodes a protein of 423 amino acids. The variant identified in this case, p.Asp300_Thr302delinsAlaHisSer, represents a deletion–insertion mutation that replaces the wild‐type amino acid sequence (Asp‐His‐Thr, residues 300–302) with Ala‐His‐Ser. This alteration is located within the acyl‐CoA dehydrogenase C‐terminal domain, thereby likely impairing the protein function. Also, in silico prediction supported its pathogenic potential. The patient was therefore diagnosed with IVA caused by an IVD gene defect. Accordingly, his parents were identified as carriers. Given the autosomal recessive inheritance of the disease, it is comprehensible that only the proband was clinically affected while both parents remained asymptomatic.

Follow‐Up and Outcomes

After four months of treatment, the patient's corrected age was 8 months and 11 days. He demonstrated stable head control as well as the ability to roll over, though he was not yet able to sit with arm support. Neurodevelopmental assessment using the Bayley Scales of Infant Development revealed a Mental Development Index (MDI) of 66, corresponding to an age‐equivalent of 5.3 months, and a Psychomotor Development Index (PDI) of 72, indicating marked improvement in both motor and cognitive development compared with the baseline.

Discussion

We report a case of chronic intermittent isovaleric acidemia presenting with motor developmental delay, lethargy, and hyperammonemia, without the classic acute metabolic crisis or “sweaty feet” odor. This case highlights the diagnostic challenge of IVA in infancy, as its nonspecific neurological manifestations may be easily mistaken for primary encephalopathy or infection. The novel finding of this report is the identification of a homozygous IVD gene variant, c.899_905delinsCCCACAG (p.Asp300_Thr302delinsAlaHisSer), which has not been previously documented in the literature or public databases. This deletion–insertion mutation, located in the acyl‐CoA dehydrogenase C‐terminal domain, expands the mutational spectrum of IVA and provides a molecular basis for genetic counseling and prenatal diagnosis in this family.

Neuroimaging in this patient revealed only mild widening of the frontotemporal subarachnoid spaces on MRI, without the typical symmetric T2/FLAIR hyperintensities in the globus pallidus often described in IVA [6, 7]. However, 1H‐MRS demonstrated elevated lactate peaks, suggesting impaired cerebral energy metabolism due to toxic metabolite accumulation [8, 9]. This finding underscores the value of MRS as a complementary tool to detect early metabolic disturbances before structural changes become apparent on conventional MRI.

The IVD gene, located on chromosome 15q15.1, contains 12 exons encoding a 423‐amino‐acid protein [10]. To date, a total of 198 variants in the IVD gene have been reported through the HGMD database (Professional 2025.4), with missense variants being the most common ones (149 types). In contrast, deletion/insertion variants have rarely been reported, suggesting the uncommonness of this type of variation. In the present family, a c.899_905delinsCCCACAG (p.Asp300_Thr302delinsAlaHisSer) deletion/insertion mutation was identified, which consequently resulted in the replacement of amino acids 300–302 (Asp, His, Thr) with Ala, His, and Ser. Bioinformatic analysis suggested that this mutation might alter the local spatial conformation of the IVD protein; therefore, potentially affecting its biological function. This variant has not been reported in the literature or existing gene mutation databases, nor is it a known polymorphism, suggesting it may represent a novel pathogenic mutation, though functional studies are still needed for further confirmation.

Tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) is a primary tool for newborn screening of IVA within 2–3 days after birth. Key screening markers include elevated isovalerylcarnitine (C5) and an increased C5/C2 ratio. Positive screening results should prompt confirmatory testing, including repeated MS/MS, urinary organic acid analysis by GC–MS, and IVD gene analysis [11, 12, 13, 14].

Once diagnosed, treatment should be initiated immediately. Management usually involves dietary protein restriction, particularly limiting leucine intake, oral supplementation with L‐carnitine and glycine to promote conjugation and urinary excretion of isovaleric acid, in addition to active intervention during acute metabolic crises, such as intravenous fluids, caloric supplementation, and ammonia‐lowering therapy. According to previous studies, timely intervention can prevent neurological damage and allow normal cognitive development in most affected infants [15, 16].

Conclusion

This study is the first to identify a homozygous IVD gene variant, c.899_905delinsCCCACAG (p.Asp300_Thr302delinsAlaHisSer), in an IVA patient presenting primarily with lethargy, reduced responsiveness, pallor, as well as growth and developmental delay. Early detection, precise diagnosis, and timely intervention contributed to the effective control of the patient's condition and his prompt recovery, thus preventing the potentially life‐threatening or disabling consequences associated with delayed diagnosis. Our findings clarify the genetic etiology of the patient's disease and demonstrate that early screening by combined tandem mass spectrometry and genetic analysis plays a vital role in detecting conditions featured by abnormal metabolites before symptom onset, thereby enabling prompt therapeutic intervention and significantly reducing mortality and morbidity. Furthermore, these results provide a solid foundation for personalized treatment of the patient, as well as for future prenatal diagnosis and reproductive counseling within the family.
