How social engagement shapes depressive symptoms in later life: uncovering gendered and asymmetric effects
Authors: Jinho Kim, Keun Young Kwon, Michelle Putnam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesSocial engagement is widely recognized as a protective factor for mental health in later life, yet most studies treat it as a static condition and overlook potential asymmetries in the effects of becoming socially engaged and disengaged. This study examines (1) whether the mental health effects of entering and exiting social engagement are asymmetric, (2) whether these effects differ by the type of engagement (informal vs formal), and (3) whether gender moderates these associations.Research Design and MethodsUsing 7 waves of data from the Korean Longitudinal Study of Aging, we analyzed 6,762 older adults aged 65 and older. Asymmetric fixed effects models were employed to estimate within-person changes in depressive symptoms associated with entry into and exit from social engagement. Gender-stratified models and interaction terms were used to examine differential effects by gender.ResultsTransitions into and out of social engagement were significantly associated with changes in depressive symptoms, but no statistically significant asymmetries were found for either informal or formal engagement in the full sample. However, gender-stratified analyses revealed asymmetric effects for formal social engagement: women benefited more from entering engagement, whereas men were more negatively affected by exiting it. No gender differences or asymmetries were observed for informal social engagement.Discussion and ImplicationsThese findings underscore the importance of distinguishing between types of social engagement and considering gender differences when designing interventions. Policies that support both prevention and re-engagement—particularly those that are gender-sensitive and address formal social roles—may be most effective in promoting mental health among older adults.Keywords: Social participation, Mental health, Gender differences, Informal social contact, Formal social activity

Social engagement plays a vital role in mental health in later life. This study shows that transitions in informal engagement consistently affect depressive symptoms, whereas formal engagement has asymmetric effects that differ by gender—women benefit more from re-engagement, whereas men are more negatively affected by disengagement. These findings highlight the need for gender-sensitive interventions that both maintain and restore social connections. Promoting sustained informal contact and supporting gender-tailored strategies for formal social participation may help reduce depressive symptoms and promote healthy aging across the life course.

Social engagement is widely recognized as a key determinant of mental health, as the formation and maintenance of social ties provide individuals with a sense of meaning and purpose. According to social integration theory, both formal and informal types of engagement play significant roles in reinforcing meaningful social roles and fostering interpersonal attachments, thereby contributing to psychological well-being (Berkman et al., 2000). Studies have found that older adults with low levels of social contact tend to report higher levels of depressive symptoms, increased psychological distress, and lower overall emotional well-being (Cornwell & Waite, 2009; Hawkley & Cacioppo, 2010; Santini et al., 2015). Evidence also indicates that frequent social engagement is linked to better emotional regulation, whereas infrequent interaction with friends or family members contributes to heightened feelings of loneliness and psychological vulnerability (Charles & Carstensen, 2010; Chen & Feeley, 2014). Participation in formal and productive activities has also been linked to improved mental health, whereas disengagement from such activities is associated with an increased risk of depression (Greenfield & Marks, 2004; Park & Kim, 2024). Together, these findings emphasize the consistent association between social engagement and mental health in older adulthood.

Individuals often experience fluctuations in their levels of social engagement over the life course, particularly in later adulthood. Social engagement frequently changes in response to major life events such as retirement, widowhood, health decline, or reduced mobility (Park & Kim, 2023). These transitions underscore the importance of examining within-individual changes in social engagement rather than relying solely on static measures. By focusing on within-individual transitions, researchers can more accurately assess how changes in engagement—such as entering or exiting social roles and relationships—shape mental health outcomes. Some older adults may experience declines in social engagement, whereas others become newly engaged through community involvement, caregiving, or volunteer work. Therefore, it is essential to distinguish between positive and negative within-person changes in social engagement, as entering and exiting may involve distinct psychological processes and consequences (Kim & Hwang, 2024). Understanding these transitions provides more nuanced insight into how dynamic patterns of social engagement influence mental health in later life and informs interventions that target both the prevention of disengagement and the promotion of re-engagement.

Separating the effects of exiting and entering social engagement

Exiting social engagement, or disengagement from social life, can negatively affect mental health through experiences of loss of meaningful social roles and interpersonal connections (Park & Kim, 2025; Song & Kim, 2024). This transition often results in feelings of abandonment, lack of purpose, and diminished self-worth, which heighten vulnerability to depressive symptoms (Courtin & Knapp, 2017; Coyle & Dugan, 2012). Moreover, declines in social engagement frequently coincide with broader deteriorations in physical or cognitive health, thereby amplifying their psychological toll (Shankar et al., 2011). The stress of losing access to social support networks can exacerbate preexisting mental health conditions or trigger new depressive episodes, particularly in the absence of effective coping mechanisms or alternative sources of emotional support (Santini et al., 2015).

In contrast, entering social engagement may benefit mental health by restoring meaningful social roles and renewing interpersonal connections. These changes can enhance emotional support and foster a renewed sense of belonging, which is particularly beneficial for older adults who have lost social ties due to widowhood, divorce, or separation (Jing et al., 2025; Nakagomi et al., 2020). Re-engagement with others—whether through informal social interaction or structured activities—can offer companionship, role fulfillment, and affirming social feedback. These social exchanges help older adults re-establish daily routines and infuse meaning into everyday life, offsetting feelings of purposelessness often associated with prior disengagement (Charles & Carstensen, 2010; Gilmour, 2012). Integration back into social life may also enhance self-esteem, reinforce personal identity, and provide emotional or practical support that improves coping with stress (Park & Kim, 2024). As social ties are rebuilt, individuals may experience improved mood, greater psychological resilience, and enhanced overall well-being, contributing to a reduction in depressive symptoms (Chen & Feeley, 2014; Levasseur et al., 2010).

Hypothesis 1. Exiting social engagement is significantly associated with an increase in depressive symptoms, and entering social engagement is significantly associated with a decrease in depressive symptoms.

The asymmetric effects of social engagement transitions

Given the distinct mechanisms underlying entry into and exit from social engagement, the remaining question is whether these transitions differ in the magnitude of their effects on depressive symptoms. These distinct pathways suggest that entering and exiting social engagement should not be viewed as mirror processes, but rather as transitions with unique and potentially unequal implications for mental health. Conventional regression models typically assume that increases and decreases in social engagement have effects of equal magnitude but in opposite directions. However, this assumption may not hold in reality (Allison, 2019; Kim & Hwang, 2024). The conservation of resources theory provides a valuable framework for addressing this question, postulating that individuals are motivated to preserve valuable resources such as social roles and interpersonal connections (Hobfoll, 1989). In this context, resources may refer to psychosocial assets —such as emotional support, companionship, and a sense of belonging—that buffer against stress and promote psychological well-being (Norris & Murrell, 1984). The loss of social engagement, which represents a depletion of key psychosocial resources, may have a greater negative effect on mental health than the positive effects gained through new social engagement. This pattern corresponds to the scenario illustrated in Figure 1B, where the psychosocial cost of exiting social engagement outweighs the mental health benefits associated with entering.

Hypothesis 2. The effect of exiting social engagement is significantly greater in magnitude than the effect associated with entering social engagement.

Variation by type of social engagement

The possibility that asymmetric patterns in the relationship between social engagement and depressive symptoms are more pronounced for specific types of social engagement warrants careful consideration. This asymmetry may be especially pronounced in the context of formal activities, which often provide structured opportunities for goal-oriented engagement, identity reinforcement, and a sense of belonging (Gilmour, 2012; Levasseur et al., 2010). Exiting from these activities can disrupt daily routines and diminish role-based identity, thereby exacerbating psychological distress (Cattan et al., 2005; Nicholson, 2012). Re-engagement in formal activities may deliver substantial mental health benefits by restoring purpose, structure, and social integration (Charles & Carstensen, 2010; Rowe & Kahn, 1997).

In contrast, informal contact with friends, neighbors, or family members may involve less structured and more spontaneous interactions. These relationships often remain emotionally significant, but the nature of informal contact tends to fluctuate more naturally over time and may not involve the same degree of social role fulfillment or routine integration (Cornwell & Waite, 2009; Hawkley & Cacioppo, 2010). As a result, the mental health consequences of losing or regaining informal contact may be similar in magnitude, as these interactions are often grounded in everyday companionship and emotional closeness rather than social roles or institutional engagement. These distinctions suggest that the nature and function of social interaction—beyond its frequency—play a critical role in shaping how entering and exiting engagement influence mental health in later life.

Hypothesis 3. The asymmetric pattern of entering and exiting social engagement is more pronounced for formal activities than informal social contact.

Gender differences

There are several theoretical and empirical reasons to expect gender differences in the psychological effects of entering and exiting social engagement. Social roles, expectations, and patterns of interaction differ by gender across the life course, influencing how men and women experience and respond to changes in social engagement in later life (Antonucci et al., 1998; Umberson et al., 1996). Social role theory posits that societal expectations and gendered socialization shape men’s and women’s roles across the life course (Eagly & Wood, 2016). Men are more likely to derive identity, purpose, and structure from formal, status-based roles, whereas women are oriented toward relational and caregiving roles (Kim, 2023). The convoy model of social relationships further highlights that men tend to maintain smaller, less emotionally intimate social networks, whereas women typically sustain broader and more supportive social ties (Antonucci et al., 2014). These gendered patterns of socialization and network formation may shape individuals’ vulnerability to social loss and their responsiveness to social gains in later life (Antonucci et al., 1998; Umberson et al., 1996).

Men are often more affected by the negative impact of exiting social engagement, as they tend to rely more heavily on formal roles and structured social environments—such as the workplace or organized groups—for their social integration (Davidson et al., 2003; McLaughlin et al., 2012). According to the social role theory, exiting these roles can lead to a loss of status, purpose, and daily structure, which undermines their sense of self and increases psychological vulnerability. Moreover, because men are less likely than women to cultivate informal support networks for emotional exchanges, they may have fewer alternative sources of emotional regulation when formal or informal engagement declines (Liebler & Sandefur, 2002). They are also less likely to seek help or disclose emotional distress in such situations (Flynn et al., 2010), which may further exacerbate the psychological consequences of disengagement from social life. The combined loss of role-based identity and insufficient emotional buffering renders men more susceptible to the adverse mental health consequences of disengagement from both formal and informal social domains.

In contrast, women often place greater value on emotionally intimate relationships and caregiving roles as central components of their social lives. As suggested by the convoy model of social relationships, they are more likely to maintain emotionally intimate relationships and broader informal networks, which may enhance their ability to benefit from re-engagement in social activities (Fuhrer & Stansfeld, 2002). Given their emphasis on relational interdependence, entering social engagement—whether formal or informal—may lead to stronger psychological gains due to enhanced emotional support, relational affirmation, and opportunities for self-expression. Women also possess greater social and emotional coping resources than men, making them more responsive to the psychological benefits of restored social connections (Kawachi, 2001). At the same time, these sustained support networks and coping resources may buffer the negative psychological consequences of disengagement, potentially resulting in less pronounced effects of exiting social engagement compared to men. Together, these frameworks suggest that gender moderates the psychological implications of social engagement transitions, with men potentially more susceptible to the harms of exiting social engagement and women more responsive to the benefits of entering it.

Hypothesis 4. Men experience a greater increase in depressive symptoms following the exit from social engagement (both formal and informal) compared to women.

Hypothesis 5. Women experience a greater reduction in depressive symptoms following entry into social engagement (both formal and informal) compared to men.

The present study

This study examines the relationship between social engagement and depressive symptoms among older adults, with particular attention to the potential asymmetry in the effects of entering and exiting social engagement. Although prior research has consistently shown that higher levels of social engagement are associated with better mental health outcomes in later life, few studies have explored whether and how the psychological effects of transitioning into versus out of engagement differ in magnitude. To address this gap, we employ asymmetric fixed effects (FE) models that distinguish the mental health impact of becoming socially disengaged from that of re-engaging in social interactions. Additionally, we differentiate between two distinct sources of social engagement—informal social contact and participation in formal activities—recognizing their potentially unique roles in influencing emotional well-being. The study also investigates whether these asymmetric effects vary by gender. Drawing on gender role theory and prior evidence suggesting that men and women differ in their patterns of social engagement and psychological responses to social loss and gain, we estimate gender-stratified models to assess heterogeneity in the mental health effects of changes in social engagement.

Data and methods

Data

This study utilizes data from the Korean Longitudinal Study of Aging (KLoSA), a nationally representative panel survey targeting individuals aged 45 and older across 15 administrative regions in South Korea. Since its inception in 2006, KLoSA has been conducted biennially using computer-assisted personal interviewing (CAPI) to gather comprehensive data on older adults. The survey covers various domains, including family dynamics, socioeconomic conditions, social engagement, physical and mental health, health care utilization, employment, and financial status. The sampling frame was based on enumeration districts provided by the Population and Housing Census of Statistics Korea, and participants were selected through a multistage stratified sampling design, accounting for housing type (apartment vs non-apartment) and geographic location (urban vs rural). This rich and nationally representative data set is well-suited for examining the mental health implications of social engagement among older adults, as it provides longitudinal information on both social interaction and psychological well-being across multiple time points.

For the present analysis, data from Waves 1 to 7 (2006–2018), encompassing a 12-year period, were employed. Among the 29,206 observations (or person-years) for individuals aged 65 and older, 412 cases (1.4%) were excluded due to missing data on key independent variables. An additional 140 cases (0.4%) were removed for missing data on depressive symptoms, and 756 cases (2.6%) were excluded due to missing information on control variables. After these exclusions, the final analytic sample included 27,898 person-years. Given the unbalanced panel nature of the data set, the total number of unique individuals included in the final sample was 6,762, ­consisting of 3,834 women and 2,928 men. All respondents provided informed consent, and the anonymized data set is publicly available. Because this study is based on secondary analysis of de-identified public data, it was exempt from ethical approval.

Measures

Dependent variable

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 10-item short form of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies-Depression scale (CES-D-10), which was administered in each wave of the KLoSA survey. Participants were asked how frequently they experienced specific feelings and behaviors during the past week. Each item was rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (rarely or none of the time) to 4 (most or all of the time), and the two positively worded items were reverse-coded. Following previous studies, responses indicating rare or no occurrence were recoded as 0, while all other responses were coded as 1. Binary scores across the 10 items were summed to create a total depressive symptom score, with higher values indicating greater symptom severity. The internal consistency of the scale in our sample was good (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.81).

KLoSA used two versions of the CES-D-10 across survey waves. From Wave 1 through Wave 4, the Andresen form was used, whereas the Boston form was adopted from Wave 5 onward. The two versions differ slightly in item composition, with seven items shared across both forms: “I felt depressed,” “I felt that everything I did was an effort,” “My sleep was restless,” “I felt lonely,” “I could not get going,” “I felt hopeful about the future” (reverse-coded), and “I was happy” (reverse-coded). The Andresen form included the following three additional items: “I was bothered by things that usually don’t bother me,” “I had trouble keeping my mind on what I was doing,” and “I felt fearful.” In contrast, the Boston form included: “I felt that people dislike me,” “I talked less than usual,” and “I felt sad.” Although the two versions differ in three items and underlying factor structure, both have shown high internal consistency and alignment with the original CES-D-20 scale (Bae et al., 2020). To ensure comparability, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using a harmonized 7-item CES-D score based on items common to both forms. Results were substantively similar to those reported in the main analysis, suggesting that our findings are robust to measurement differences across waves (Supplementary Table 1 [see online supplementary material]).

Independent variable

To assess social engagement, we focused on two widely recognized dimensions (Evans et al., 2019; Kelly et al., 2017): informal social engagement and formal social engagement. Informal social engagement was measured based on participants’ self-­reported frequency of contact with friends, relatives, acquaintances, or neighbors living nearby. We constructed a binary variable to distinguish those who were socially engaged (coded as 1) from those who were socially disengaged (coded as 0). Individuals were categorized as socially engaged if they reported meeting others at least once every 2 months (i.e., five to six times per year or more frequently). This threshold was selected to capture a minimal but meaningful level of ongoing interpersonal interaction, consistent with prior studies. Those who reported social contact only “once or twice a year,” “rarely in a year,” or having “no close contact” were classified as socially disengaged. To assess the robustness of our threshold choice for informal engagement, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using an alternative cutoff (i.e., comparing those with any contact, including “rarely in a year,” to those with “no close contact”). Results of this sensitivity check, presented in Supplementary Table 2 (see online supplementary material), show that our main findings remained substantively unchanged.

Formal social engagement was assessed by asking participants whether they were currently involved in any formal organizations, including religious groups, friendship circles, leisure or sports clubs, reunions, volunteer groups, political associations, or other types of organized activities. A binary variable was created to indicate formal social engagement, with a value of 1 assigned to those participating in at least one formal organization, and 0 to those reporting no involvement.

Control variable

This study incorporated a comprehensive set of time-varying control variables to account for potential confounding factors. Time-varying covariates consisted of age, household size, logged household income, homeownership status (indicating whether the respondent resided in owner-occupied or privately rented housing), economic activity (whether the respondent was currently employed or economically active), and residential location (classified as large city, small city, or rural area). Health-related controls included the number of chronic conditions—derived from a count of ten physician-diagnosed diseases such as hypertension, diabetes, cancer, chronic lung disease, liver disease, heart disease, cerebrovascular disease, psychological disorders, arthritis, or rheumatoid arthritis, and prostatic disease. Additionally, functional status was measured using an activities of daily living (ADL) score, based on whether the respondent required assistance with any of the following seven tasks: dressing, washing one’s face, bathing, eating, leaving the room, using the toilet, and managing urination and defecation.

Statistical analysis

To investigate the association between social engagement and depressive symptoms, we employed standard FE models. Characteristics such as genetics, early life experiences, personality traits, baseline cognitive function, resilience, and cultural background—factors that typically remain unmeasured—can influence both the likelihood of social engagement and the development of depressive symptoms (Klein et al., 2011; Schutter et al., 2020). Although standard FE models offer improvements over pooled ordinary least squares models, they assume that the effects of changes in social engagement are symmetric—that is, the mental health impact of entering into engagement is equal in magnitude (but opposite in direction) to that of exiting it. However, this assumption may not hold in real-world settings, where the mental health impact of social engagement can differ depending on direction and context. To address this limitation, we employed asymmetric FE models (Allison, 2019), which allow for different effects depending on whether individuals transition into or out of social engagement. This terminology aligns with the language used in the asymmetric fixed-effects framework applied to binary independent variables (Allison, 2019). For the first wave of data (t=1), both terms were set to zero due to the absence of prior observations. A similar approach was applied to derive corresponding variables for formal social engagement.

The average score for depressive symptoms was 3.31, with women reporting significantly higher levels than men (3.69 vs 2.81). Approximately 85% of the total sample engaged in frequent informal social contact, with no gender difference. About 67% were engaged in formal activity, with a significantly higher proportion among men (70%) compared to women (65%). In terms of demographic characteristics, 57% of the sample were female. Gender differences were also observed in health status, with men reporting fewer chronic diseases than women (0.83 vs 1.02).

Table 2 presents changes in social engagement across waves for informal contact (Panel A) and formal activity (Panel B). Most respondents reported no change in informal engagement, with stability ranging from 82.20% between Wave 1 and Wave 2 to 92.98% between Wave 3 and Wave 4. Transitions into and out of informal disengagement were relatively infrequent, with the highest rates occurring in earlier waves. Patterns of formal engagement showed slightly more variation, with stability ranging from 71.68% to 87.92%, and the highest transition rates appearing between Wave 1 and Wave 2 and between Wave 2 and Wave 3. We observed a notable drop in within-person change between Wave 3 and Wave 4 across both engagement types. Despite thorough checks, we found no evidence of survey instrument changes, data errors, or cohort shifts that could explain this anomaly. Interestingly, similar patterns of reduced within-person variation during this interval were also observed for physical activity, suggesting the possibility of broader behavioral stasis. Nonetheless, our analytic approach is well-suited to address this irregularity. Importantly, our use of seven waves of data and FE models with wave FE helps ensure that our estimates are robust to such wave-specific anomalies.

Table 3 presents the results from FE models estimating the associations between social engagement and depressive symptoms. Column 1 reports results from the standard FE model. Both types of engagement were significantly associated with lower depressive symptoms, with informal engagement showing a stronger effect (β = −0.436, p < .001) than formal engagement (β = −0.268, p < .001). Column 2 reports results from the asymmetric FE model, which differentiates between entry into and exit from engagement. For informal social engagement, entering engagement was associated with a significant reduction in depressive symptoms (β = −0.394, p < .001), whereas exiting engagement was linked to increased symptoms (β = 0.518, p < .001). Table 4 presents gender-stratified estimates of the asymmetric effects of social engagement on depressive symptoms, revealing two key findings. First, asymmetric effects were observed for transitions in formal social engagement, but the patterns varied by gender. Among women (Column 1), entry into formal social engagement was significantly associated with a reduction in depressive symptoms (β = −0.322, p < .001), whereas exit from formal engagement was not statistically significant (β = 0.089, n.s.). This suggests that re-engaging in formal activities is more beneficial for women than the psychological cost of disengaging. In contrast, among men (Column 2), exit from formal social engagement was significantly associated with increased depressive symptoms (β = 0.428, p < .001), whereas entry into engagement had a smaller effect (β = −0.138, p < .05). This indicates that for men, withdrawal from formal activities has a stronger adverse impact than the benefit of re-engagement. These patterns of asymmetry were statistically significant within each gender group (p = .012 for women; p = .011 for men). Second, the interaction model in Column 3 shows that the effect of exiting formal social engagement was significantly more detrimental for men than for women, as indicated by the significant interaction term (β = 0.338, p < .01). No significant gender differences were observed for transitions in informal social engagement, where both entry and exit were associated with changes in depressive symptoms in expected directions, but the differences between genders were not statistically significant.

Discussion

This study investigated the relationship between social engagement and depressive symptoms among older adults, with a specific focus on whether the psychological effects of transitioning into and out of social engagement are asymmetric. By distinguishing between two sources of social engagement—informal social contact and participation in formal activities—and employing asymmetric FE models, we sought to provide a more nuanced understanding of how transitions in social engagement influence mental health. We found that entering formal and informal social engagement was significantly associated with reductions in depressive symptoms, whereas exiting both types of social engagement significantly increased depressive symptoms, supporting the first hypothesis.

However, contrary to our second and third hypotheses, we found no statistically significant asymmetry in the magnitude of the effects, nor significant differences across types of engagement. The positive mental health effects of entering social engagement did not differ significantly in size from the negative effects of exiting across both informal and formal domains. This suggests that the mental health risks associated with social disengagement—whether from informal contact or formal activities—may be comparable in size, but opposite in direction, to the benefits of social re-engagement. This symmetric pattern suggests that entering both informal and formal forms of social engagement can yield mental health benefits, even when such transitions occur late in adulthood.

Another main finding of this study pertains to gender differences in the association between social engagement and depressive symptoms. As hypothesized (Hypotheses 4 and 5), the effect of exiting social engagement was more pronounced among men, but only in the context of formal social engagement. Conversely, for women, the effect of entering social engagement was more pronounced than for men, again specifically within formal engagement domains. These findings have several important implications. First, in the case of informal engagement, no significant gender differences in asymmetry were observed. Transitions into and out of informal contact produced relatively symmetric changes in depressive symptoms for both men and women, mirroring the results from the full sample. This finding is consistent with prior studies showing that emotionally significant informal ties—such as those with family members, friends, or neighbors—play a critical role in maintaining mental health for both older men and women (Cornwell & Waite, 2009; Hawkley & Cacioppo, 2010). These relationships often provide emotional closeness and everyday companionship, and changes in their availability appear to influence psychological well-being in similar ways across genders.

Second, we observed clear gender differences for asymmetric patterns in formal social engagement, which were not apparent in the full sample analysis. This finding underscores the importance of examining gender-specific patterns, which may be overshadowed in aggregate analyses. Failure to account for this heterogeneity can lead to an underestimation of important gendered dynamics associated with entering and exiting social engagement in later life, limiting the development of effective, targeted interventions. For women, regaining frequent participation in formal activities was associated with a greater reduction in depressive symptoms than the increase associated with losing it, suggesting that re-engagement in structured social roles may be particularly beneficial for their mental health. This pattern aligns with research indicating that women are more likely to derive emotional and psychological rewards from participation in community-based activities, such as volunteering, religious groups, or social clubs (Fuhrer & Stansfeld, 2002; Levasseur et al., 2010). For men, however, the opposite trend was found: exiting formal social engagement led to a substantial increase in depressive symptoms, with an effect size notably larger than the corresponding benefit associated with re-engagement. This finding reflects prior evidence that older men tend to rely more heavily on formal social roles—often tied to occupational or institutional settings—for their sense of identity and social connection (McLaughlin et al., 2012). The loss of these structured forms of engagement may leave men particularly vulnerable to emotional distress.

This study makes both theoretical and methodological contributions to the existing literature on social engagement and mental health in later life. Theoretically, it advances understanding by challenging the common assumption that the psychological effects of entering and exiting social engagement are equivalent in magnitude but opposite in direction—a simplification found in much of the prior research (Cornwell & Waite, 2009; Hawkley & Cacioppo, 2010). By explicitly testing for asymmetric effects, the study underscores the importance of conceptualizing social engagement as a dynamic process, wherein the mental health consequences may differ depending on the direction of transition. Methodologically, the study contributes by applying asymmetric FE models to longitudinal panel data, a technique that enables within-person comparisons over time while controlling for unobserved, time-invariant individual characteristics (Allison, 2019). This approach addresses potential confounding bias that is often present in cross-­sectional or pooled designs and enhances the causal interpretability of findings related to social transitions (Allison, 2009; Brüderl & Ludwig, 2014). Additionally, by disaggregating social engagement into informal and formal components and using gender-stratified models, the study provides a more differentiated framework for examining how diverse forms of social engagement—and their loss or recovery—uniquely influence mental health across subpopulations.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, although FE models account for time-invariant unobserved heterogeneity, they do not control for time-varying confounders that may simultaneously influence both changes in social engagement and depressive symptoms, such as major life events, health shocks, or caregiving responsibilities. Second, the measurement of social engagement relied on frequency-based indicators of informal contact and formal activity, which may not fully capture the quality, emotional depth, or perceived adequacy of social relationships—factors that have been shown to be critical for mental health (Coyle & Dugan, 2012; Hawkley & Cacioppo, 2010). Third, the analysis did not incorporate the duration of social engagement or disengagement, which may moderate its psychological impact (Kim & Park, 2023, 2024). Short-term disengagement may have less impact, whereas prolonged disengagement from social roles or networks may lead to cumulative stress and deeper emotional detachment.

The findings of this study highlight the importance of developing comprehensive, gender-sensitive policies to promote and sustain social engagement among older adults. Given the significant associations between both informal and formal social engagement and depressive symptoms, interventions should not only aim to prevent disengagement but also actively support re-engagement of those who have already withdrawn. Community-based programs that promote participation in formal activities—such as volunteering, senior centers, or lifelong learning—can be especially beneficial, particularly for older men who may be more affected by the loss of structured social roles (Davidson et al., 2003; McLaughlin et al., 2010). For older women, facilitating reentry into social life through relational and emotionally supportive activities may be especially effective (Fuhrer & Stansfeld, 2002). Moreover, screening for social disengagement or withdrawal in primary care and integrating social prescriptions into routine services can help identify and assist at-risk individuals early (NASEM, 2020). These findings underscore the need for coordinated efforts across health, social, and community sectors to strengthen social engagement as a strategy for improving mental health in aging populations.
