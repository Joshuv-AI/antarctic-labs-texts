Impact of isoflurane anesthesia on astrocyte connexin 30 expression in adult rats and its possible implications for postoperative neurocognitive disorder
Authors: Ming-Ying Li, Xiao-Xia Zhang, Xiao-Li Sun, Zhen-Yuan Wang, Jie Li, Xue-Na Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePostoperative neurocognitive disorders (NCD) are common complications following general anesthesia, yet their underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. This study investigates the impact of isoflurane anesthesia on astrocyte connexin 30 (Cx30) expression in the hippocampus.MethodsEighteen 8-month-old male Sprague–Dawley rats were divided into three groups: control, post-anesthesia 1-day, and post-anesthesia 3-day groups. Rats in the anesthesia groups were exposed to 1.8% isoflurane for 2 h. Hippocampal tissues were collected at 1 and 3 days post-anesthesia. Cx30 expression was assessed using Western blot (WB) and immunofluorescence staining. Protein levels were quantified, and statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post hoc tests.ResultsWB analysis revealed a significant reduction in Cx30 protein expression in the hippocampus of rats at both 1 and 3 days post-isoflurane anesthesia compared to controls. Immunofluorescence staining confirmed decreased Cx30 expression in hippocampal astrocytes, consistent with WB findings.ConclusionIsoflurane anesthesia significantly reduces Cx30 expression in hippocampal astrocytes, which might be related with the mechanism of postoperative NCD. These findings highlight the role of astrocyte Cx30 in synaptic function and cognitive processes, implicating its dysregulation in the pathophysiology of anesthesia-induced cognitive impairment. Further research is needed to explore the functional consequences of Cx30 alterations and their contribution to postoperative NCD.Keywords: Astrocyte, Cognitive dysfunction, Connexin 30, Delirium, Isoflurane, Neurocognitive disorders, Postoperative

Introduction

Postoperative neurocognitive disorders (PND) include postoperative delirium, delayed neurocognitive recovery, and postoperative neurocognitive dysfunction [1, 2]. These complications frequently occur after general anesthesia, with a higher prevalence among older adults [3, 4]. Primary symptoms involve memory and attention decline, along with mental status changes, which can persist long after surgery, leading to increased hospitalization costs, mortality, and readmission rates [5, 6]. Research on postoperative cognitive dysfunction (POCD) and postoperative delirium (POD) has continued for many years [7]. In 2018, experts from multiple disciplines collectively classified cognitive impairments occurring before or after surgery as PND, encompassing both preoperative and postoperative NCD [8]. PND development is associated with factors such as age, anesthetic agents, and surgical procedures [9]. Although its precise mechanisms remain unclear, recent studies have highlighted the significance of neuroinflammation, synaptic dysfunction, and disrupted neural connectivity [5].

Astrocytes (AS) play a crucial role in neural circuits and neuronal communication and have emerged as potential targets for enhancing cognitive function [10]. AS can independently induce neuronal plasticity and enhance cognitive performance. They dynamically interact with neurons, actively shaping neural networks, processing specific information, and providing feedback to neural circuits. Additionally, AS networks regulate neuronal activity, and their metabolic processes are essential for hippocampal synaptic transmission [11–14]. Connexin 30 (Cx30), one of the two primary subunits of astrocytic gap junctions, is exclusively expressed in gray matter AS, contributing to the formation of astrocyte networks [15, 16]. According to research, Cx30 is linked to behavior and fundamental cognitive processes. By modulating astrocyte glutamate transport, Cx30 can directly influence synaptic glutamate levels, thereby regulating hippocampal excitatory synaptic transmission. Furthermore, this regulation occurs independently of its gap junction channel function and is mediated by altering the morphology of astrocyte processes within the synaptic cleft [17]. By modulating excitatory synaptic strength, Cx30 plays a pivotal role in long-term synaptic plasticity and hippocampus-dependent contextual memory [17]. Cx30 is essential for facilitating intercellular communication and maintaining central nervous system homeostasis. Both increased and decreased Cx30 expression can impact long-term potentiation (LTP), leading to hippocampal synaptic dysfunction and impaired recognition memory [17, 18].

The expression, perisynaptic localization, and function of Cx30 in the hippocampus are dynamic and influenced by neuronal electrical activity. Neuronal activity bidirectionally regulates Cx30 protein levels through lysosomal degradation. Inhibiting neuronal action potentials rapidly reduces hippocampal Cx30 protein levels [19].

General anesthetics, particularly inhaled agents like isoflurane, exert acute anti-excitatory effects by inhibiting neuronal excitability or action potential conduction [20, 21]. It is hypothesized that the suppression of neuronal activity induced by general anesthesia may alter Cx30 expression in the hippocampus and contribute to postoperative NCD. Although research indicates that isoflurane sustains α5 GABAA receptor activity, leading to persistent impairments in hippocampal memory performance and synaptic plasticity, its specific impact on astrocytes, especially Cx30, and its association with postoperative NCD remain unreported [22].

A preliminary experiment was conducted in this study using Western blot and immunofluorescence staining to assess changes in Cx30 expression in the hippocampus of rats following isoflurane anesthesia. The goal was to identify potential pathways underlying postoperative NCD and explore therapeutic strategies targeting astrocyte function to mitigate anesthesia-induced cognitive impairment.

Materials and methods

Animals

Eighteen 8-month-old healthy male Sprague–Dawley rats were individually housed in a controlled environment with a stable temperature (20 °C–22 °C) and humidity (45–55%). They were maintained on a 12-h light/dark cycle with unrestricted access to food and water. After 1 week of acclimatization, the model was established. All animal experiments were conducted under standardized conditions at Beijing Chaoyang Hospital, Capital Medical University, following institutional animal care regulations (No. 2020-Animal-96).

The rats were randomly assigned to three groups: (A) Control group (n = 6), which breathed air spontaneously; (B) Post-anesthesia 1-day group (n = 6), housed for 1 day after isoflurane anesthesia; and (C) Post-anesthesia 3-day group (n = 6), housed for 3 days after isoflurane anesthesia.

Anesthesia procedure: Rats were anesthetized by placing them in a sealed chamber with 1.8% isoflurane (1.3 times of the minimum alveolar concentration equivalent for rats) at a flow rate of 0.6–0.8 L/min for 2 h [23]. The isoflurane concentration was monitored by sampling the gas inside the sealed chamber, that is, the sample gas was measured with a monitor. Control rats were placed in a room-temperature environment and breathed air spontaneously. To reduce the stress response caused by invasive procedures, only pulse oximetry and carbon dioxide partial pressure monitoring were performed on the rats in the experiment; the maintenance of isoflurane anesthesia was initiated and terminated by the disappearance and recovery of the righting reflex, respectively.

Anesthetized rats were euthanized 1 day and 3 days after anesthesia, respectively, while control rats were euthanized at 3 days after anesthesia. Following cervical dislocation, the brains were rapidly extracted and placed in ice-cold PBS to preserve tissue integrity. The hippocampus was dissected under a stereomicroscope; part of the hippocampal tissue was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and subsequently stored in a − 70 °C ultra-low temperature freezer, while the remaining tissue was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for further experiments.

Immunofluorescence staining

A portion of the hippocampal samples from the rat brains was fixed in paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 4 h, then sequentially immersed in 20% and 30% sucrose solutions (4 °C) until the tissue sank. The tissue was placed on a sample holder, embedded in OCT embedding medium, and pre-cooled on a cryostat (CM1860; Leica, Nassloch, Germany). The tissue was sectioned into 10 μm thin slices, flattened, and adhered to glass slides. After drying, the slides were stored in a − 70 °C freezer.

After drying, the sections were washed three times with distilled water for 5 min each. The slides were then incubated in antigen retrieval solution and heated at high temperature for 10 min. Antigen retrieval was performed using the microwave heat retrieval method without a specific temperature (the retrieval solution was heated to boiling). Information on the buffer: 9 ml of citric acid buffer, 41 ml of sodium citrate buffer, and 450 ml of distilled water were mixed, with a pH value of 6.0. Blocking was carried out with goat serum before the use of primary antibodies, followed by incubation at room temperature for 15 min. Following this, the sections were soaked in 1% phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; HyClone) three times. Primary antibodies, including Cx30 antibody (1:100, DF13225, Affinity, China, validated by the manufacture) and GFAP antibody (mAB (monoclonal antibody), mouse-derived, 1:50, Sc-33673, Santa, USA, validated by the manufacture), were applied to fully cover the tissue and it was incubated overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber. The antibody diluent used was PBS (phosphate-buffered saline). The slides were placed on a slide rack and washed three times with PBS for 5 min each. Fluorescent secondary antibodies, Cy3-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (A0516, Beyotime, China, validated by the manufacture) and FITC-labeled goat anti-mouse IgG (A0568, Beyotime, China, validated by the manufacture), were diluted 1:200 in PBS (protected from light) and added to cover the tissue completely, followed by a 60-min incubation at room temperature. The slides were placed on a slide rack, washed three times with PBS for 5 min each, and then DAPI (D106471-5mg, Aladdin, China) was applied to cover the tissue for nuclear counterstaining. After another round of PBS washes, an anti-fade mounting medium (S2100, Solarbio, China) was added, and the slides were coverslipped. The staining results were observed under a fluorescence microscope (BX53, Olympus, Japan), and images were captured at 400 × magnification using the microscope imaging system (DP73, Olympus, Japan).

Western blot and Cx30 proteomic analysis

Proteins were extracted from the hippocampal tissues of euthanized rats using a lysis buffer containing 1% PMSF. The buffer was applied based on the mass and volume of each sample and incubated on ice for 5 min. The samples were then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm, 4 °C, for 10 min using a refrigerated centrifuge, and the supernatant was collected as the protein extract. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA method (BCA Protein Assay Kit, Wanleibio, WLA004, China). Equal protein amounts (40 μg) were denatured by boiling in a water bath for 5 min. Proteins were separated on a 12% polyacrylamide gel by SDS-PAGE at a constant voltage of 80 V for 2.5 h, and subsequently transferred to a PVDF membrane (Millipore, IPVH00010, USA) at 80 V for 1.5 h using transfer buffer. The PVDF membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h, followed by overnight incubation with the primary Cx30 antibody (pAB (polyclonal antibody), rabbit-derived, 1:1000, Affinity, DF13225, China, validated by the manufacture). After washing the membrane four times with TBST, it was incubated with the corresponding secondary antibody, horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:5000, Wanleibio, WLA023, China, validated by the manufacture), at room temperature for 45 min. Following secondary antibody incubation, the membrane was washed six times with TBST. ECL chemiluminescence reagent (Wanleibio, WLA003, China) was mixed in equal volumes of A and B, applied to the PVDF membrane, and incubated for 5 min. The membrane was then exposed in a dark room.

After the exposure in the dark room, the PVDF membrane was washed twice with distilled water. It was then incubated in a primary and secondary antibody stripping solution (Wanleibio, WLA007, China) for 15 min, followed by two washes with distilled water to remove the antibodies. Subsequently, the PVDF membrane was washed five times with TBST and blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h. The membrane was incubated with the internal reference antibody (β-actin antibody, 1:1000, validated by the manufacture) diluted in 5% skim milk at 4 °C overnight. Following incubation with the internal reference antibody, the PVDF membrane underwent four washes with TBST and was incubated with the secondary antibody (goat anti-rabbit IgG-HRP, 1:5000, Wanleibio, WLA023, China, validated by the manufacture) at room temperature (37 °C) for 45 min. Following incubation with the secondary antibody, the PVDF membrane was washed six times with TBST. ECL chemiluminescence reagent (Wanleibio, WLA003, China) was mixed in equal volumes of A and B, applied evenly to the PVDF membrane, and incubated for 5 min. The membrane was then transferred to a dark box and exposed in a dark room.

The film was scanned, and the optical density values of the target bands were analyzed using Gel-Pro-Analyzer software, a gel image processing system.

Statistical analysis

The immunofluorescence density of brain sections and the fluorescence density of WB proteins were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical comparisons of immunofluorescence density and relative protein expression levels detected by WB were conducted using one-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni post hoc tests, with SPSS version 17.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

WB results indicated that the relative expression of Cx30 protein in the hippocampus of rats significantly decreased on the first and third days after isoflurane anesthesia (p < 0.001) (Fig. 1). Immunofluorescence staining of hippocampal brain sections demonstrated that the expression of Cx30 in astrocytes of isoflurane-anesthetized rats was significantly lower than that of control rats, with a decreasing trend consistent with the WB results (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2).

Discussion

Cx30 plays a vital role in cognitive function. In the adult brain, astrocyte connexins and intact astrocyte networks are essential for maintaining neural homeostasis, plasticity, and spatial cognition [24]. A reduction in Cx30 expression can disrupt the balance of the astrocyte network and lead to cognitive impairment. Studies in transgenic rats have shown that Cx30 deficiency can cause emotional and behavioral abnormalities, while young rats in an enriched environment exhibit increased expression of the Cx30 gene [25, 26]. Additionally, astrocytes are closely linked to sleep regulation. As integrators of sleep need, astrocytes contribute to sleep homeostasis and play a critical role in regulating the sleep–wake cycle [27–29]. Disruption of the glial network can lead to sleep disorders, which are significant contributors to exacerbating postoperative cognitive impairment [30, 31].

We hypothesized that there might be a possibility that general anesthetics may impair postoperative neuronal and synaptic functions by lowering Cx30 expression in the hippocampus. While this may not directly lead to clinically detectable cognitive decline, it could serve as an initiating factor that, in combination with other factors such as inflammation, aging, or central neurodegenerative diseases, exacerbates neurocognitive dysfunction. These factors may also damage astrocytes or disrupt astrocyte function, potentially delaying the recovery of Cx30 levels and extending the negative impact of reduced Cx30 expression on neurocognitive function [32–34].

Results of this study showed that Cx30 expression significantly decreased on the first- and third-days post-anesthesia, and immunofluorescence staining revealed that changes in Cx30 fluorescence intensity were consistent with the WB results. These findings indicates that isoflurane anesthesia affects Cx30, leading to a decrease in its expression in the hippocampus. The reduction in Cx30 expression in the hippocampus did not recover after anesthesia ended. It was observed in this study that, on the third day post-anesthesia, Cx30 levels in the hippocampus remained significantly lower than in the control group, indicating that the impact of anesthesia on the neural network of brain functional areas persists.

According to previously conducted studies, astrocyte Cx30, particularly within the perisynaptic nanodomain, is endogenously regulated by neuronal activity [20]. This regulation also clarifies how isoflurane, as a general anesthetic, affects Cx30 expression. Yin et al. used in vivo techniques to record the inhibitory effect of isoflurane on cortical and hippocampal field potentials, and this inhibitory effect was associated with the concentration of isoflurane [35]. A study by Zhao et al. found that isoflurane at clinical concentrations can directly inhibit the transient and persistent sodium currents of hippocampal CA1 pyramidal neurons [20]. Although isoflurane does not silence neuronal activity in the same manner as tetrodotoxin (TTX), the prolonged inhibition of neuronal activity during clinical isoflurane anesthesia still significantly influences Cx30 expression.

This study provided a preliminary exploration of the potential involvement of Cx30 in postoperative-NCD induced by isoflurane anesthesia, emphasizing Cx30 as a possible therapeutic target. However, there are several limitations in this study: First, the post-anesthesia observation period for the rats was limited to 3 days. Second, rats of varying ages were not separately observed, preventing the determination of age-related differences in Cx30 expression changes in the hippocampus following isoflurane anesthesia. Third, only the effect of isoflurane anesthesia on Cx30 expression in the hippocampus was investigated, and no behavioral assessments were performed to clarify the cognitive changes in rats after anesthesia, hindering the analysis of the relationship between Cx30 expression changes and cognitive and behavioral alterations. Fourth, the mechanism through which isoflurane anesthesia affects Cx30 expression and subsequently influences neuronal function remains unclear. Research addressing these limitations is currently underway. In addition, the sample size lacked a priori power calculation, potentially affecting result robustness and comparisons with other anesthetics were not conducted, limiting conclusions about isoflurane-specific effects on Cx30. Future studies should consider the above factors to further clarify the relevant mechanisms.

In summary, the reduction of Cx30 expression induced by isoflurane anesthesia may be related to key mechanism underlying postoperative-NCD, although further research is required to confirm this. This study offers significant evidence for exploring the role of Cx30 in anesthesia-induced neurocognitive dysfunction and provides new avenues for research focused on the prevention and treatment of postoperative NCD.
