A Comparative Analysis of Maillard‐Derived α‐Dicarbonyl Compounds and Advanced Glycation End Products in Fresh, Wet, Kibble, and Freeze‐Dried Dog Foods
Authors: Tolgahan Kocadağlı, Ciara Clarke, Vural Gökmen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThermal processing of pet foods can lead to the formation of harmful Maillard reaction products (MRPs), which may compromise nutritional quality and pose health risks to companion animals. This study compared the levels of MRPs in 41 commercial dog foods, including fresh (n = 15), kibble (n = 13), wet (n = 10), freeze‐dried (n = 2), and cold‐pressed (n = 1) types. Acrylamide was found in only two kibble samples (40 and 76 µg/kg), while 5‐hydroxymethyl‐2‐furfural and 2‐furfural were not detected in any sample. Fresh foods had significantly higher lysine content (56 ± 13 g/kg dry matter [DM]) and lower lysine blockage (3.2 ± 0.7%) compared to kibble (23 ± 9 g/kg DM; 4.9 ± 1.2%) and wet foods (42 ± 14 g/kg DM; 3.0 ± 1.5%) (p < 0.05). Advanced glycation end products, Nε‐carboxymethyllysine (CML) and Nε‐carboxyethyllysine (CEL), were highest in wet foods (92 ± 36 mg/kg DM and 128 ± 39 mg/kg DM, respectively), indicating a greater thermal input (p < 0.05). α‐Dicarbonyl compounds such as glyoxal and methylglyoxal were also significantly higher in wet samples (7.0 ± 2.5 and 15.8 ± 7.5 mg/kg DM, respectively) compared to fresh foods (2.8 ± 0.9 and 2.6 ± 0.8 mg/kg DM) (p < 0.05). This is the first report on the amounts of α‐dicarbonyl compounds in commercial dog food. These findings emphasize the importance of carefully balancing food safety, nutritional quality, and processing conditions in pet food manufacturing.

Introduction

Thermal processing is used in the food industry to enhance palatability, ensure microbial safety, and improve digestibility. During such treatments, foods are exposed to temperatures ranging from moderate (90°C) to very high (200°C–250°C), triggering complex chemical reactions (Gökmen 2024). Among these, the Maillard reaction plays a crucial role in developing flavor and browning, particularly in thermally processed products such as bakery goods, coffee, meat, and pet food. However, these reactions can also lead to the formation of undesirable heat‐induced contaminants, which raise concerns regarding both nutritional quality and health implications (Gökmen 2024). Heterocyclic amines, acrylamide, furfurals, α‐dicarbonyl compounds, and advanced glycation end‐products (AGEs) are notable thermal process contaminants linked to mutagenic, carcinogenic, chronic inflammatory and degenerative effects on health (Stadler and Lineback 2009). Unlike human diets, dogs typically have a relatively uniform diet and consume the same food throughout their lives. Consequently, regular intake of high thermal processing contaminants may negatively affect their health.

The Maillard reaction is investigated in three primary stages for clarity. The first stage involves the reaction between a free amino group (as free amino acids and mainly lysine and arginine side chains found in proteins) and a reducing sugar (primarily glucose, fructose, and maltose), resulting in an Amadori product. This is referred to as the early or initial stage. It is followed by the intermediate stage, which involves the degradation of the Amadori product into various reactive compounds, such as α‐dicarbonyl compounds. In the final stage, reactions of these carbonyl compounds produce flavor and browning, as well as the AGEs.

Protein glycation during heating and storage contributes to nutritional losses and the accumulation of AGEs, such as Nɛ‐carboxymethyllysine (CML) and Nɛ‐carboxyethyllysine (CEL) (Henle 2008). In pet food, lysine glycation is particularly important, as it affects amino acid bioavailability and may compromise dietary adequacy (Williams et al. 2006). These compounds not only serve as markers of thermal input but are also implicated in the development of chronic diseases in humans. In dogs, excessive dietary AGEs can induce oxidative stress and persistent low‐grade inflammation via AGE–RAGE interactions, contributing to insulin resistance, renal impairment, and other chronic age‐related diseases (Bridglalsingh et al. 2024; Jimenez 2021). The potential adverse health effects of dietary AGEs have been a subject of controversy in humans (Šebeková and Somoza 2007; Henle 2007; Delgado‐Andrade and Fogliano 2018; Hellwig et al. 2024). On the other hand, α‐dicarbonyl compounds (glyoxal, methylglyoxal, and 3‐deoxyglucosone) have been clearly shown to be genotoxic and reactive towards DNA, in vitro, suggesting a possible carcinogenicity (Hellwig et al. 2024). Additionally, some evidence suggests that glyoxal has tumor‐promoting activity (Hellwig et al. 2024).

Over the past century, the production of dog food has evolved through several phases. In the mid‐nineteenth century, James Spratt's baked wheat and beetroot “dog biscuit” inaugurated the first industrial pet food era, delivering shelf‐stable calories to seafaring dogs and, later, urban households (Fraser‐Miller et al. 2021). Post‐World War II meat‐rendering capacity and metal‐can technology fostered the rise of wet diets, followed in the 1960s–1990s by extrusion processes that yielded cost‐efficient, kibble‐style rations (White et al. 2018). These high‐temperature, low‐moisture methods ensured microbial safety but intensified the Maillard reaction and nutrient losses. Since the 2010s, consumer humanization of pets, subscription logistics, and cold‐chain distribution have catalyzed a fourth wave: fresh, lightly cooked, human‐grade formulations prepared at temperatures of 90°C and delivered frozen or refrigerated. The Fresh category of pet foods appears particularly promising due to their nutritional and physiological advantages. Their higher moisture content supports renal hydration (Stevenson et al. 2003; Sires et al. 2025). Fresh foods increase amino acid digestibility (Geary et al. 2023; Kerr et al. 2012), decrease serum triglycerides, and modify gut microbiota and metabolites (Algya et al.2018; Do et al. 2021). While digestibility and nutrient bioavailability are high (Algya et al. 2018), the advantages of such minimal processing, particularly the Maillard reaction products, have not been comprehensively quantified.

This survey study aimed to compare the levels of Maillard reaction products in dog foods produced under low‐thermal processing conditions with high protein and nutrient content to those of some commercially available dog foods. For this purpose, dog food samples were analyzed for acrylamide, furfurals, α‐dicarbonyl compounds, and both early and advanced glycation end products. The levels of highly reactive α‐dicarbonyl compounds in dog foods were reported for the first time. These results offer new insights into how the degree of thermal input and formulation affect the presence of potentially harmful substances and the retention of important nutrients. These results contribute to a deeper understanding of pet food quality, which is essential for informed industry practices and optimal animal health and well‐being.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals and Consumables

Potassium hexacyanoferrate (Carrez I), zinc sulfate heptahydrate (Carrez II), acrylamide (99.5%), lysine (99%), isotope‐labeled amino acid mixture (98 atom% 15N, 98 atom% 13C) containing 15 mM lysine internal standard, HCl (37%), glucosone (98%), quinoxaline (99%), 2‐methylquinoxaline (97%), 2,3‐dimethylquinoxaline (97%), o‐phenylenediamine (98%), diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid (DETAPAC) (98%), sodium phosphate, sodium borohydride (> 98%), 5‐hydroxymethyl‐2‐furfural (99%), 2‐furfural (99%) and methanol (gradient grade) were purchased from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Formic acid (LC‐MS grade) and acetonitrile (LC‐MS grade) were purchased from Carlo Erba (Val de Reuil, France). Furosine standard was purchased from Neosystem Laboratoire (Strasbourg, France). Acrylamide‐d3, CML, CEL, CML‐d4, CEL‐d4, and 3‐deoxyglucosone (> 95% all) were obtained from Toronto Research Chemicals (Toronto, Canada). Deionized water (0.055 µS/cm, Barnstead Smart2Pure, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was used during all analyzes.

Samples

There were fresh (n = 15), freeze‐dried (n = 2), cold‐pressed (n = 1), kibble (n = 13), and wet (n = 10) dog foods available in the UK. Wet diets included canned, tray and Tetrapak carton formats. They were purchased in 2024 before their best‐before or expiry dates. Fresh food samples were purchased frozen and brought to the lab in the same condition. Dry foods were stored at 4°C, and fresh foods were stored − 40°C until sample preparation. Wet and fresh samples were freeze‐dried, except for acrylamide and α‐dicarbonyl analysis. All samples were ground using a coffee grinder (Bosch MKM 6003). The proximate composition of the products is given in Table S1 (see Supporting Information).

Determination of Acrylamide Using Liquid Chromatography With Tandem Mass Spectrometry

A multi‐stage extraction method was used, as previously described (Gökmen et al. 2009). Briefly, 1 g of the ground sample (not lyophilized) was extracted with 10 mM formic acid in water (20 mL in total), containing 20 µg/L acrylamide‐d3 as an internal standard. One milliliter of the extract was filtered through a preconditioned 30 mg Oasis MCX cartridge (Waters, Milford, MA, USA). After discarding the first eight drops of eluent, the remaining drops were collected into an autosampler vial. Acrylamide was analyzed using an Agilent 6475 Triple Quadrupole MS coupled with an Agilent 1290 Infinity II UPLC (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) under the same conditions described earlier (Mogol et al. 2025). Quantification was performed using a linear calibration curve in the range of 1 to 50 µg/L, containing 20 µg/L of acrylamide‐d3. The limit of detection (LOD) was 6 µg/kg sample, and the Limit of quantification (LOQ) was 20 µg/kg sample. The recovery ranged from 93% to 101%, and repeatability and reproducibility values were below 10%.

Determination of Protein‐Bound Lysine Using Liquid Chromatography With Tandem Mass Spectrometry

A 100 mg sample of freeze‐dried and ground material was weighed into a glass tube, to which 5 mL of 8 M HCl was added. The tubes were sealed with Teflon‐lined silicon septa and screw caps after purging the headspace with nitrogen. They were then incubated at 110°C for 23 h to achieve hydrolysis. The resulting hydrolysates were filtered through filter paper and collected into 15 mL falcon tubes. From these, 100 µL of filtrate was transferred to a new 15 mL falcon tube and dried at 50°C using a gentle nitrogen stream. The dried residue was dissolved in 1.5 mL of water. An aliquot of this solution was further diluted 50‐fold with a mixture of acetonitrile and water (8:2, vol/vol), containing 1.5 µM isotope‐labeled Lys, and then transferred to a vial.

Lysine was analyzed using an Agilent 1290 Infinity II UPLC coupled with an Agilent 6475 MS/MS (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) under the same conditions published before (Kocadağlı et al. 2025). Lysine was detected through its specific multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) transitions. The precursor ion of Lys m/z 147 was fragmented to 84 (collision energy 12 V, dwell time 7 ms) and 130 (collision energy 5 V, dwell time 7 ms). Isotope‐labeled Lys precursor ion m/z 155 was fragmented to 90 (collision energy 12 V, dwell time 5 ms). Fragmentor voltage was set at 80 V for all transitions. Both quadrupoles were set to unit resolution. Lysine quantification was performed using a linear calibration curve over a range of 1 to 100 μM, which included 1.5 μM of isotope‐labeled Lys. The recovery ranged from 90% to 106%, and repeatability and reproducibility values were below 10%.

Determination of Furosine Using Hydrophilic Interaction Liquid Chromatography

The same hydrolysate used for lysine analysis was also used to determine furosine. A 1 mL sample of dissolved residue was passed through a preconditioned 30 mg Oasis HLB cartridge (Waters, Milford, MA, USA). After discarding the first eight drops of eluent, the remaining drops were collected into an autosampler vial. Preconditioning involved sequential use of 1 mL of methanol and 1 mL of water. Furosine analysis was carried out using an Agilent 1200 HPLC‐DAD system as described before (Gökmen et al. 2009). Chromatographic separation took place on an Atlantis HILIC column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm; Waters, Milford, MA, USA) with a mobile phase of 1% formic acid, flow rate of 1 mL/min, at 40°C. The injection volume was 10 µL, and detection was at 280 nm. Furosine was quantified against an external calibration curve ranging from 1 to 10 mg/L. Lys blockage refers to the ratio of the amount of Nε‐fructosyllysine to total lysine calculated using the furosine method (Krause et al. 2003). The conversion factor for the bound lysine Amadori product is 2.2 × [furosine] (Krause et al. 2003). The recovery ranged from 95% to 99%, and repeatability and reproducibility values were below 10%.

Determination of CML and CEL Using Liquid Chromatography With Tandem Mass Spectrometry

A 20‐mg sample of freeze‐dried, ground material was placed into a glass tube, to which 100 μL of deionized water, 450 μL of sodium borate buffer (0.2 M, pH 9.2), and 500 μL of 1 M sodium borohydride (in 0.1 M NaOH) were added. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 4 h to convert N‐ε‐fructosyllysine into N‐ε‐hexitollysine. Afterwards, 2 mL of 8 M hydrochloric acid was added, and the tubes were sealed with Teflon‐lined silicone septa and screw caps after purging with nitrogen. The tubes were heated at 110°C for 24 h to hydrolyze the samples. The hydrolysates were filtered into 15 mL falcon tubes using filter paper. A 25 μL aliquot of the filtrate was transferred to another 15 mL Falcon tube and dried at 50°C under a gentle nitrogen stream. The dried residues were reconstituted in 1 mL of water containing 10 µg/L CML‐d4 and CEL‐d4 internal standards. This solution was purified with a preconditioned Oasis HLB cartridge (Waters, Milford, MA, USA); the first 8 drops of eluent were discarded, and the remaining were collected. The cartridge was preconditioned with 1 mL of methanol followed by 1 mL of water. Analysis was performed on a Waters Acquity TQD UPLC–MS/MS system (Waters, Milford, MA, USA). Chromatography was carried out on a SeQuant ZIC‐HILIC column (150 mm x 4.6 mm ID, 3.5 μm, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) using a gradient of 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (B) at 1 mL/min flow rate and 60°C. The gradient conditions: start at 30% A for 10 min, increase to 50% A in 1 min, hold for 2 min at 50% A, decrease to 30% A in 1 min, then maintain at 30% A for 6 min; total run time of 20 min. The autosampler was set at 10°C, with a 5 μL injection volume. MS detection was in positive ion mode, with source temperature at 120°C, desolvation gas flow at 900 L/h and 300°C, collision gas flow at 0.20 mL/min, capillary voltage at 2 kV, extractor voltage at 2 V, and cone gas flow at 100 L/h. MRM transitions for quantitation included: CML m/z 205 to 84 and 130, with collision energies of 18 V and 15 V respectively, cone voltage 25 V; CML‐d4 m/z 209 to 84, collision energy 18 V, cone voltage 25 V; CEL m/z 219 to 84 and 130, collision energies of 18 V and 15 V, cone voltages of 20 V; CEL‐d4 m/z 223 to 84, collision energy 18 V, cone voltage 20 V. Transitions had a dwell time of 35 ms. Quantitation used external calibration curves for CML and CEL, ranging from 1 to 100 µg/L, with 10 µg/mL of CML‐d4 and CEL‐d4 as internal standards. The recovery ranged from 92% to 109%, and repeatability and reproducibility values were below 10%.

Determination of α‐dicarbonyl Compounds Using Liquid Chromatography With Tandem Mass Spectrometry

The extracts from acrylamide analysis were used. A sample of the extract was mixed with an equal volume of methanol to precipitate co‐extracted colloids and then centrifuged at 7000 g for 5 min. The derivatization of α‐dicarbonyl compounds was performed using o‐phenylenediamine based on a published method with slight modifications (Kocadağlı and Gökmen 2014). The quinoxaline derivatives of α‐dicarbonyl compounds were analyzed using a Waters Acquity UPLC‐MS/MS system (Waters, Milford, MA, USA) operated in ESI+ mode, following parameters previously published (Žilić et al. 2021). Chromatographic separation was carried out on an Acquity UPLC BEH C18 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm, Waters, Milford, MA, USA) with a gradient of 0.1% formic acid in water and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile as the mobile phase, at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min and 60°C. Data collection involved monitoring the m/z ratios of quinoxaline derivatives of glucosone: 251, 1/3‐deoxyglucosone: 235, glyoxal: 131, methylglyoxal: 145, and dimethylglyoxal: 159 in SIM mode, with auto dwell time. Calibration curves for the quinoxaline derivatives ranged from 0.02 to 2.0 mg/L. 1‐Deoxyglucosone was semi‐quantified using the calibration curve of 3‐deoxyglucosone. The recovery ranged from 89% to 110%, and repeatability and reproducibility values were below 10%.

Determination of 5‐Hydroxymethyl‐2‐Furfural and 2‐Furfural Using Reversed Phase Liquid Chromatography

The extract obtained for acrylamide was used. A portion of the extract was filtered through a 0.45 µm nylon filter into an autosampler vial. The analysis was performed using an Agilent 1200 HPLC‐DAD system. Chromatographic separation was performed on an Atlantis dC18 column (250 mm × 4.6 mm id., 5 μm, Waters, Milford, MA, USA) using an isocratic mixture of water and acetonitrile (10%) as the mobile phase at a flow rate of 1 mL/min and a temperature of 30°C. The injection volume was 20 µL. Data acquisition involved recording chromatograms at 285 nm and UV spectra between 200 and 380 nm. LOD was 0.1 mg/kg sample for both compounds. The recovery ranged from 90% to 105%, and repeatability and reproducibility values were below 10%.

Statistical Analysis

Box plots were generated using SPSS (version 26, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The whiskers on the box refer to the minimum and maximum values, excluding outliers. The down and upper lines of the box refer to quartiles 1 and 3, respectively; the line in the middle refers to the median, and the cross refers to the average value. The empty circles refer to potential outliers. ANOVA and the Tukey test were used to compare the average values of MRP within a 95% confidence interval, assuming normal distribution and homogeneous variances. The homogeneity of variances was checked using Levene's test, and normality of the data was assessed using Q–Q plots and the Shapiro‐Wilk test. Pearson correlations were conducted in a 99% confidence interval using SPSS. Freeze‐dried and cold‐pressed samples were excluded from the statistical analysis due to their small sample size, although their levels are shown in the box plots.

Results and Discussion

Acrylamide and Furfurals

Among all samples, only two kibble samples had acrylamide concentrations above the limit of quantification (20 µg/kg sample), at 40 and 76 µg/kg (data not shown). VESELÁ and ŠUCMAN (2013) reported the presence of acrylamide in pet foods, as measured by a voltametric procedure. The concentration of acrylamide in dry dog and cat foods ranged from 106 to 358 µg/kg (n = 5) and from 66 to 269 µg/kg (n = 3), respectively, in their study (VESELÁ and ŠUCMAN 2013). KILIÇ and Liman (2022) measured acrylamide in 42 dog and cat foods using an immuno‐enzymatic method. They reported acrylamide in 14 samples, ranging between 45 and 155 µg/kg. The only LC‐MS/MS method used for the determination of acrylamide in pet food was by Sugita et al. (2021). They reported 15–69 µg/kg acrylamide in dry pet foods (n = 30), which is similar to our study. It is essential to note that liquid chromatography coupled with a mass spectrometer is strongly recommended and is usually the primary choice for quantifying acrylamide in foods (Gökmen 2024).

Foods with high water activity values and processed below 100°C generally do not generate acrylamide, except for instances such as prunes and dried pears (Becalski et al. 2011). The formation of acrylamide during pet food processing may be related to the use of cereal and root vegetable sources, which are rich in acrylamide precursors, free asparagine and reducing sugars. In human nutrition, meat dishes are not a significant source of acrylamide exposure, likely due to their low free asparagine and reducing sugar content. These low levels reported in this study are not a concern in human foods. For example, in cereal‐based baby foods, the acrylamide benchmark level is 40 µg/kg (EU Commission Regulation 2017/2158). Sugita et al. (2021) concluded that dogs may be exposed to four times higher levels of acrylamide than humans due to the higher intake per kg body weight. The absorption of acrylamide in beagle dogs was demonstrated to be fast and distributed extensively to tissues, like humans (Ikeda et al. 1987). However, the conversion rate of acrylamide to glycidamide is unknown for dogs, which is responsible for the carcinogenic effect. For example, the conversion rate is higher in rats than in humans (Kocadağlı and Gökmen 2024).

In any of the samples, neither HMF nor 2‐furfural was detected (LOD = 0.1 mg/kg sample). van Rooijen et al. (2014a) reported HMF in extruded dog foods at a range of 0.27–1.92 g/kg and in wet dog foods at a range of 0.34–3.02 g/kg. These levels of HMF can be found only in sugar‐rich, acidic foods (pH < 4.6) or in foods heated to very high temperatures, such as molasses, caramel products or coffee (Morales 2008). We verified that neither the surrounding peaks nor the co‐eluting peaks in a few samples matched the UV spectrum of HMF, and they would not lead to very high concentrations (Figure S1, see Supporting Information). Additionally, Oba et al. (2022) reported levels of up to approximately 30 mg/kg HMF in pet foods, similar to those predominantly found in human foods.

Bound Lysine and its Glycation Products

Fresh diets delivered 56 ± 13 g/kg DM lysine, more than double the kibble value and markedly higher than in wet diets (p < 0.05, Table 1). This indicated a major difference in the formulation of these diets. The lysine levels reported in this study were higher than those reported by van Rooijen et al. (2014b). The products labeled ‘fresh’ in this study are formulated with high‐quality ingredients, including human‐grade meat muscle, heart and liver. Other dog foods were also mostly in the premium category. In Table S2 (see Supporting Information), it can be seen that kibbles in the budget category have Lys levels similar to those reported by van Rooijen et al. (2014b). The higher lysine values in the present study may be related to the product choice, as pet foods range from budget to premium (Supporting Information Table S2). Budget products often include fillers to lower prices. Lysine blockage, as measured by the furosine method, was similar in both fresh and wet samples (p > 0.05), but higher in kibbles (p < 0.05), indicating greater nutritional loss of protein in kibbles, which was already quite low. Williams et al. (2006) were the first to demonstrate significant lysine losses in extruded dog diets. van Rooijen et al. (2014b) also reported similar significant lysine losses in several pet diets.

Based on dry matter, the amount of measured furosine, as an indicator of early glycation, was higher in fresh samples than in kibbles and wet samples (Table 1). On the other hand, based on protein content, kibbles had a higher furosine content than wet samples, and the amount of furosine in fresh samples was not significantly different from that in kibbles and wet samples (Table 1 and Figure S2, see Supporting Information). Oba et al. (2022) also reported that wet pet foods had higher furosine than dry pet foods. It was notable to observe that two freeze‐dried samples demonstrated the highest concentration of furosine, indicating the application of a thermal treatment, likely pasteurization, inadequate drying, or improper storage conditions (Figure 1). The Maillard reaction also occurs at room temperature during storage, leading to quality loss (browning) or nutritional loss (lysine blockage). It is well acknowledged that milk and whey powder are susceptible to the Maillard reaction at room temperature when water activity exceeds 0.3. Elevated water activities decrease the glass transition temperature, resulting in a rubbery state in which lactose and proteins have the mobility necessary for a reaction (Roos and Drusch 2016). There is no information in the literature on the effect of storage on Maillard reaction products in pet foods. Therefore, storage studies are warranted to draw conclusions.

The amount of furosine had a statistically significant, moderately positive correlation with lysine concentration (Pearson r = 0.64, p < 0.01) when freeze‐dried samples were excluded, which are visually outliers (Figure 2). This means that the higher the concentration of lysine in dog foods, the higher the formation of furosine, i.e., the Maillard reaction. This suggests that the impact of ingredients is likely more pronounced on early glycation than the effect of processing. However, it is unknown whether this is a result of a prior thermal treatment of the ingredients or their susceptibility to glycation during pet food processing. van Rooijen et al. (2014a, 2014c) reported a similar observation that even though pelleted pet foods are known to be less thermally treated, they have higher amounts of Maillard reaction products than extruded products. The authors found that ingredient choice was more effective than the steam pelleting process on the occurrence of Maillard reaction products by analyzing the unprocessed ingredient mix (van Rooijen et al. 2014c).

The amount of the advanced glycation end product CML in fresh and wet samples was similar on a dry matter basis (p > 0.05) (Table 1). In both, CML levels were higher than in kibbles (p < 0.05). On the other hand, on a protein basis, CML was higher in wet samples than in fresh samples, indicating greater thermal input for wet samples, while fresh and kibble samples had similar levels (Figure S2, see Supporting Information). The amounts of CML observed in kibbles and wet samples were higher than the levels reported by van Rooijen et al. (2014a). Additionally, they reported that the mean CML levels of wet samples were 2‐3 times higher than those of the extruded samples, which aligns with this study, as the wet samples were found to be 3 times higher than in kibbles, on a dry basis. Oba et al. (2022) did not identify a difference in CML concentrations between dry and wet pet foods.

The concentration of another advanced glycation end product, CEL, in the fresh samples was roughly half that of the kibbles and about one‐quarter of the wet samples (p < 0.05). Bridglalsingh et al. (2024) also found that the CEL content in mildly cooked dog food (processed at under 65°C) was almost one‐quarter of that in wet diets with similar nutrient profiles, based on caloric content. This study also reported higher plasma AGE concentrations in dogs fed wet foods compared to ultra‐processed dry food, moderately processed air‐dried food, and minimally processed mildly cooked food, in decreasing order (Bridglalsingh et al. 2024). The bioavailability of glycation products depends on several factors, including whether they are free or peptide‐bound, their interactions with the gut microbiota, neoformation during digestion, and the nature of the compound (Hellwig et al. 2024). Higher amounts of arginine glycation products might also be present in foods than lysine glycation products (Akıllıoğlu and Lund 2022). However, this study only focused on lysine as an essential amino acid and a marker of heat damage. Further studies are warranted to determine the extent of arginine glycation in pet foods.

CML concentration exhibited a statistically significant, moderate correlation with lysine levels (Pearson r = 0.57, p < 0.01) (Figure 3A), whereas CEL did not (Pearson r = 0.06, p > 0.05) (Figure 3B). However, CEL concentration significantly and strongly correlated with lysine levels when fresh samples were excluded (Pearson r = 0.75, p < 0.01) (Figure 3C). This suggests that, despite the higher lysine content in fresh samples, CEL formation remained limited. CML is formed from the oxidation of the lysine Amadori product (i.e. Nε‐fructosyllysine, measured as furosine) or from the reaction of lysine with glyoxal, a product of sugar fragmentation. On the other hand, CEL is formed by the reaction of lysine with methylglyoxal, another product of sugar fragmentation (Henle 2008). There is no study that has shown the direct formation of CEL from Amadori/Heyn product degradation, although it may be possible. Therefore, it appears that the Maillard reaction in fresh samples remains in the early stage, while in other samples, it proceeds more towards intermediate stages, resulting in the formation of methylglyoxal through Amadori product degradation, and consequently yielding CEL formation.

Oxidation reactions usually have lower activation energies than carbon‐carbon bond breakage. Thus, CML formation from lysine Amadori product might have occurred through oxidation rather than its degradation to α‐dicarbonyl compounds in fresh products. Oxidation reactions are catalyzed by heme and hemin proteins (containing Fe2+ and F3 + , respectively) in cooked meat (Belitz et al. 2009). Therefore, this could explain the oxidation of the Amadori product of lysine to CML, with limited formation of glyoxal. On the other hand, samples with higher thermal input (wet) may exhibit higher sugar fragmentation (carbon‐carbon bond breakage), which could explain the higher CEL formation. The occurrence of these sugar fragments in dog foods is mentioned below.

It is also important to note that lysine glycation products, including Amadori products, CML, and CEL, are generated endogenously within animal tissues, which are incorporated into dog foods. While not directly comparable to pet foods, Eggen and Glomb (2021) documented a threefold increase in CML (3 mg/kg DM in raw) and a 40‐fold increase in CEL (0.9 mg/kg DM in raw) following the grilling of porcine meat. The levels of these compounds reported for pet foods are significantly higher than those found in raw meat. Unprocessed pet food mixtures are likely to contain higher levels of reducing sugars than meat, linked to plant ingredients that facilitate protein glycation. Future research should also include the quantification of pyrraline, a lysine advanced glycation end product produced exclusively during thermal processing.

α‐Dicarbonyl Compounds

Glyoxal and methylglyoxal are formed from the cleavage of the carbon backbone of reducing sugars, and these reactions are predominant over dehydration reactions of sugars through 3‐deoxyglucosone pathway when the pH is over 7. Fresh foods contained 2.8 ± 0.9 mg/kg DM glyoxal, 60% lower than wet (Table 1). For methylglyoxal, the contrast was even starker: 2.6 ± 0.8 mg/kg in fresh versus 15.8 ± 7.5 mg/kg in wet foods. Box‐and‐whisker plots (Figure 4) depict this continuum and corroborate lower thermal load as the principal determinant of α‐dicarbonyl accumulation. There were similar levels of glyoxal and methylglyoxal in fresh and kibble samples (p > 0.05). These findings are likely related to a higher thermal input for wet samples, consistent with the conclusions observed for advanced glycation end products.

Box plots for α‐dicarbonyl compounds based on dry matter (DM).

The dehydration reaction of glucose, via the Amadori products, goes through predominantly the 3‐deoxyglucosone pathway at pH levels below 7 and predominantly the 1‐deoxyglucosone pathway at pH levels above 7. 1‐Deoxyglucosone is an unstable compound and does not accumulate in foods, unlike 3‐deoxyglucosone (Glomb and Tschirnich 2001). There were no statistically significant differences in 3‐deoxyglucosone levels, while 1‐deoxyglucosone was only observed in kibbles. 3‐Deoxyglucosone is formed by the removal of a water molecule from glucose, and its subsequent dehydration reactions yield HMF, via 3,4‐dideoxyglucosone, which was not detected. The levels of 3‐deoxyglucosone are typically several times higher than those of HMF (Degen et al. 2012). Therefore, when these low levels of 3‐deoxyglucosone are compared with those in human foods, it supports the undetectable HMF levels mentioned in the previous section. It should be noted that both freeze‐dried chicken samples contained high levels of 3‐deoxyglucosone and 1‐deoxyglucosone, consistent with the higher levels of glycation products present in them.

On the other hand, glucosone, an oxidation product of glucose, was present at higher levels in fresh samples than in kibble and wet samples. Lower levels of glucosone in the kibble and wet samples could be related to the unstable nature of glucosone, as a highly reactive compound (Glomb and Tschirnich 2001). These samples are stored at room temperature, while the fresh samples are stored frozen, which may explain their higher level in the fresh samples.

Conclusion

This survey study provides a comprehensive and comparative analysis of thermal processing contaminants and Maillard reaction products in different types of commercial dog foods. Acrylamide was detected in only two samples (n = 41), which were kibbles, suggesting minimal concern for most products. The formation of acrylamide in pet foods is probably related to cereal and root vegetable ingredients, which are rich in free asparagine or reducing sugars. Controlled studies focusing on the amounts of free asparagine and reducing sugars in raw formulations, as well as the effect of processing on acrylamide formation, are warranted to maintain acrylamide levels as low as reasonably achievable (ALARA principle) in pet foods. Neither HMF nor 2‐furfural was detected, likely because the samples were not acidic foods and lacked added sugars or extreme thermal processing conditions.

Kibble samples showed greater lysine blockage and elevated levels of CEL when compared to fresh samples. Wet samples demonstrated the highest levels of advanced glycation end products (CML and CEL) and α‐dicarbonyl compounds (glyoxal and methylglyoxal), consistent with a higher thermal processing load. Early glycation of lysine appeared to increase with the increasing amount of bound lysine, suggesting that ingredient composition may play a more significant role in their formation than the degree of thermal processing. Like the unknowns in acrylamide formation in pet foods, the glycation reactions should be monitored under controlled conditions, focusing on the raw materials and their potential for glycation, which depends on processing conditions. This will help highlight critical control points for the industry to address. Another interesting point to note is that there is no correlation between the amounts of Maillard reaction products and the price range of the products (budget, mid‐range, premium; Table S2, see Supporting Information). Some of the highly priced premium kibble/wet products contain the highest levels of unwanted Maillard reaction products.

Overall, the findings suggest that both the composition of ingredients and the processing conditions may significantly impact the formation of Maillard reaction products, which should be examined under controlled conditions. This emphasizes the importance of carefully balancing food safety, nutritional quality, and processing conditions in pet food manufacturing. Further research into the mechanisms behind the Maillard reaction in pet foods is necessary.
