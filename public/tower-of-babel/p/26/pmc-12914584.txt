Clinical Characteristics of Over‐The‐Counter Drug Misusers in Psychiatric Practice in Japan: Comparison Between Codeine Misusers and Dextromethorphan Misusers
Authors: Yuko Tanibuchi, Soichiro Omiya, Takashi Usami, Takuya Shimane, Toshihiko Matsumoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeIn recent years, the misuse of over‐the‐counter (OTC) medications has been increasing in psychiatric clinical settings in Japan. This study aimed to compare individuals who misuse codeine (COD) with those who misuse dextromethorphan (DXM) to clarify the clinical characteristics of DXM misusers and to consider the support measures needed for this population.MethodsWe used data from the 2024 National Survey on Drug‐Related Disorders in Psychiatric Inpatient Facilities. Individuals who primarily misused OTC containing COD (COD group) and DXM (DXM group) were extracted from the database. We examined demographic characteristics, ICD‐10 subcategories, and comorbid psychiatric disorders and compared the two groups using Fisher's exact test. Factors associated with DXM misuse were further analyzed using multivariable logistic regression.ResultsThe study included 160 individuals in the COD group and 72 in the DXM group. Compared with the COD group, the DXM group had higher proportions of females and younger individuals, as well as higher rates of drug use within the past year and a history of self‐harm or suicide attempts. The DXM group also showed significantly higher comorbidity rates of “acute intoxication” and “mood disorders (F3)” based on ICD‐10. In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, younger age (AOR = 2.558, 95% CI: 1.218–5.371), acute intoxication (AOR = 2.73, 95% CI: 1.254–5.942), and comorbid mood disorders (AOR = 2.201, 95% CI: 1.146–4.227) were significantly associated with DXM misuse.ConclusionThese findings highlight the importance of acute‐phase management, assessment of comorbid psychiatric disorders, and interventions that include suicide‐risk evaluation in supporting individuals who misuse DXM. Furthermore, preventive education incorporating perspectives on mental‐health‐focused suicide prevention, as well as regulatory measures for OTC medication sales, are urgently needed.Keywords: addiction, codeine, dextromethorphan, drug dependence, misuse, over‐the‐counter drug

Introduction

In recent years, the misuse of over‐the‐counter medications (OTC) has become a global concern [1, 2, 3, 4]. OTC products are intended for self‐treatment of various symptoms such as common cold symptoms, gastrointestinal complaints, and pain, and they are generally perceived as safe because they can be legally and easily obtained without a prescription at retail stores or online [5, 6, 7]. OTC medications are also expected to contribute to reducing healthcare expenditures under the national insurance system by encouraging individuals to take responsibility for their own health and to actively participate in disease prevention and treatment [8, 9]. Thus, OTC medications are considered to have positive impacts on both individuals and society. However, concerns remain regarding the fact that users bear full responsibility for recognizing and managing the safety of OTC use. This is because some OTC products carry risks of overdose aimed at achieving psychoactive effects on the central nervous system or of concomitant use with other substances [1, 2, 3, 4, 10]. In other words, similar to prescription medications, OTC products can cause adverse events when used inappropriately or in excessive amounts.

The global issue of OTC misuse is also a growing problem in Japan. Indeed, between 2012 and 2024, the number of individuals presenting to psychiatric medical facilities due to inappropriate OTC use increased approximately 11.7‐fold [11, 12], demonstrating a rapid upward trend. Among the substances most frequently misused by OTC misusers in Japan, codeine (COD) [10] and dextromethorphan (DXM) [3] rank as the top two components [12]. Internationally, misuse of these substances is recognized as both a clinical challenge and a significant public health concern [1]. In Japan, inappropriate use of COD‐containing antitussives and cold medications had long been a clinical issue. However, in 2019, the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare prohibited the use of COD‐containing products in children under 12 years of age due to reports of fatal cases and risks of respiratory depression overseas [13]. As a result, COD‐containing products were increasingly replaced by DXM‐containing products, and new OTC formulations with higher DXM content entered the market. Subsequently, psychiatric facilities began to see a marked increase in patients presenting with DXM misuse as their chief complaint [12].

COD is an opioid that exerts antitussive effects primarily by acting on μ‐opioid receptors to suppress the cough center. At high doses, it can induce euphoria and pleasurable sensations and has strong dependence‐forming potential [14]. Moreover, most COD‐containing products in Japan include stimulant precursors such as methylephedrine or pseudoephedrine for their bronchodilatory effects, and the central nervous system effects of these components cannot be overlooked. In contrast, DXM is a non‐opioid antitussive, but at high doses it exhibits NMDA receptor antagonism and serotonergic activity, producing hallucinogenic effects similar to the dissociative anesthetic ketamine [15]. Clinical presentations among DXM misusers are often severe, and cases involving suicide‐related behaviors have been reported [16, 17], indicating that inappropriate DXM use can also lead to dangerous conditions.

Despite these differences in pharmacological properties and mechanisms of action, clinical practice in Japan often treats these cases collectively under the broad category of “OTC misuse.” Furthermore, as the primary OTC products of concern in Japan have shifted from COD‐containing to DXM‐containing formulations, clarifying the clinical characteristics of DXM misusers in comparison with the previously predominant COD misusers is essential for informing responses to Japan's evolving OTC misuse problem. Therefore, this study aimed to provide foundational knowledge to support substance‐specific interventions, preventive education, and policy recommendations by analyzing data from the 2024 National Survey on Drug‐Related Disorders in Psychiatric Inpatient Facilities and identifying the clinical characteristics of individuals who misuse each substance.

Methods

2024 Nationwide Survey on the Actual Conditions of Drug‐Related Mental Disorders in Psychiatric Hospitals in Japan (NPH Survey)

This study was conducted as a secondary analysis of data from the NPH Survey.

The NPH Survey is a component of a multifaceted epidemiological research initiative that aims to understand the actual conditions of drug misuse and dependence in Japan. Since 1987, this survey has been conducted biennially using a largely consistent methodology. It represents the only comprehensive census survey in Japan targeting patients with drug‐related psychiatric disorders. Over the years, the findings of the NPH Survey have served as a critical foundation for the formulation of various drug misuse countermeasures.

The survey was conducted over a 2‐month period from September to October 2024. The survey population included all patients diagnosed with drug‐related mental disorders (i.e., those corresponding to the code F1 of the Tenth Revision of the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems [ICD‐10],8 Mental and behavioral disorders due to psychoactive substance use, excluding alcohol) who were either hospitalized or treated on an outpatient basis at the participating institutions during the survey period. The researchers first contacted the selected institutions to request their participation in the survey. After consent was obtained, questionnaires were mailed to the facilities. Attending psychiatrists at each institution completed the questionnaires by extracting anonymized clinical data from the medical records of patients who met the inclusion criteria.

The survey was conducted using the same method employed in previous years, including demographic data (biological sex, age), educational attainment (high school graduate or above, high school graduate or below), employment status at the time of the survey (employed, unemployed), criminal history (drug‐related crimes, non‐drug‐related crimes, history of incarceration in correctional facilities), the first drug used by the individual in their life (“first‐use drug”), lifetime history of drug use, the drug believed to have the greatest impact on psychiatric symptoms at the time of the survey (“primary drug”), whether the primary drug was used in the past year, diagnosis related to drug use (ICD‐10 classification F1 subcategory), presence of alcohol problems, diagnosis related to drug use (ICD‐10 classification F1 subcategory), presence of any mental disorder, presence of intentional self‐harm or suicide attempts in the past year. The survey also collected detailed information regarding OTC products used.

Participants

From the 2024 NPH Survey, individuals whose primary drug at the time of the survey was an OTC product—specifically those misusing COD‐containing or DXM‐containing medications—were identified as the study population. In selecting the study population, individuals who had used both COD‐ and DXM‐containing products concurrently, those who provided inappropriate responses to survey items, and those with missing or unclear responses were excluded.

Measures

Items Related to Clinical Characteristics

Participants whose primary substance of misuse at the time of assessment was an OTC product containing COD were classified into the “COD group,” while those whose primary substance was an OTC product containing DXM were classified into the “DXM group.” Comparisons between the two groups were conducted based on the following three categories:

(a) The attributes of each group were compared, including biological sex, age group, educational attainment, employment status, history of apprehension or arrest for drug‐related offenses, history of apprehension or arrest for non‐drug‐related offenses, history of incarceration in correctional facilities, presence of alcohol‐related problems at the time of assessment (defined as harmful use or more severe conditions), episodes of deliberate self‐harm or suicide attempts within the past year, and use of the primary substance within the past year.

(b) Subgroup classifications under the ICD‐10 F1 diagnosis were compared between the two groups.

(c) Comorbid psychiatric disorders based on ICD‐10 diagnoses were compared between the two groups.

Items Used in Logistic Regression Analysis (Independent Variable)

Based on prior research indicating associations with DXM misuse and statistically significant differences observed in the group comparisons of this study, the following variables were included as independent variables:

(a) Biological sex: Male = 0, Female = 1 (reflecting findings that DXM‐containing drug misusers are more likely to be female [10, 18]).

(b) Age category: Age 30 and above = 0, Age 10–29 = 1 (reflecting findings that DXM‐containing drug misusers are more prevalent among younger individuals [10, 18]).

(c) Deliberate self‐harm or suicide attempts within the past year: No = 0, Yes = 1 (reflecting the possibility that DXM misusers may exhibit self‐destructive behaviors [16, 17]).

(d) Use of the primary substance within the past year: No = 0, Yes = 1 (reflecting the potential for dependency among DXM misusers and the possibility that abstinence may not be maintained even during treatment [19]).

(e) ICD‐10 F1x.0 (Acute intoxication): No = 0, Yes = 1 (reflecting reports that DXM misusers tend to present with acute intoxication [20]).

(f) Comorbid psychiatric disorder (F3: Mood disorder): No = 0, Yes = 1 (reflecting the reported association between DXM and depression, and the suggested potential antidepressant effects of DXM [21]).

Items Used in Logistic Regression Analysis (Dependent Variable)

A binary variable was used, in which individuals whose primary substance at the time of assessment was a COD‐containing product were coded as “0,” and those whose primary substance was a DXM‐containing product were coded as “1.”

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM, Chicago, Illinois, USA). First, Fisher's exact test was performed to compare the clinical characteristics between the COD and DXM groups, with a two‐tailed significance level set at p < 0.05. Subsequently, multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted to examine factors associated with membership in the DXM group. Prior to the analysis, linear regression was performed to assess multicollinearity among the independent variables, and the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) was calculated. All variables demonstrated VIF values below 5, indicating no issues with multicollinearity. Therefore, all independent variables were entered into the model using the forced entry method (Enter method), and odds ratios (Exp (B)) along with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were calculated. The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (two‐tailed).

Ethical Considerations

This survey was conducted with the approval of the Ethics Committee of the National Center of Neurology and Psychiatry, Japan (approval number: A2024‐041).

Results

Proportion of Individuals Whose “Primary Drug” Was an OTC Product Containing COD or DXM

Figure 1 shows the participant flow diagram illustrating the selection process from the full NPH Survey dataset to the final analysis sample. In the 2024 NPH Survey, responses were obtained from 1098 out of 1525 targeted facilities (response rate: 72.0%). Among the total of 2765 collected cases, 2702 were deemed valid (97.7%). Of these, 409 cases (15.1%) identified an OTC medication as their primary drug. Within this subset, 179 cases reported OTC products containing COD as the primary drug, and 86 cases reported OTC products containing DXM as the primary drug. After excluding cases with incomplete responses, 160 individuals whose primary drug was an OTC product containing COD were classified into the “COD group” (68.97%), and 72 individuals whose primary drug was an OTC product containing DXM were classified into the “DXM group” (31.03%). These two groups comprised the analytical sample for the present study.

Age category showed a significant positive association, with younger individuals having higher odds of belonging to the DXM group (AOR = 2.558, 95% CI: 1.218–5.371). Discussion

This study classified individuals who misuse COD‐containing and DXM‐containing OTC products based on their active ingredients and compared their demographic and clinical characteristics in order to elucidate the clinical profile of DXM misusers in Japan. Multivariable analysis revealed that, compared with the COD group, the DXM group included a higher proportion of younger individuals, more cases classified as acute intoxication under the ICD‐10 F1 category (Mental and behavioral disorders due to psychoactive substance use), and more individuals with comorbid mood disorders under the ICD‐10 F3 category.

One possible explanation for the higher proportion of young women in the DXM group is that drugstores—where OTC medications are purchased—are often places that young women visit routinely to buy cosmetics or daily necessities, lowering the psychological barrier to purchasing OTC products. In addition, for young people experiencing interpersonal difficulties in real life, social media provides a space to connect with peers who share similar struggles, and within these communities, recommendations for overdosing can function as a kind of “in‐group culture.” Social media also enables instantaneous sharing of information about DXM, including which OTC products contain it and how much is needed to achieve euphoria, which may influence their choice of DXM. The higher prevalence of acute intoxication in the DXM group may reflect the psychopharmacological properties of DXM itself, as DXM misuse frequently presents with clinical features such as intoxication and impaired consciousness.

The high rate of comorbid mood disorders, including depression, suggests that some DXM misusers may be using DXM as a form of self‐medication to alleviate emotional distress associated with mood disorders. Although COD has also been reported to be used to manage psychological states such as depression, anxiety, and stress [22], DXM differs from COD in that high doses produce dissociative and consciousness‐altering effects similar to those of ketamine [15]. These effects may temporarily divert attention from emotional pain, producing derealization, hallucinations, and a sense of being “detached from unpleasant reality,” which may strongly resonate with young people seeking escape. Moreover, given recent reports of ketamine's rapid antidepressant effects [23], it is possible that high‐dose DXM produces similar antidepressant effects like ketamine, thereby reinforcing its use as a coping strategy. However, such effects are transient, and maintaining them may require escalating doses or frequency of use, leading to loss of control, acute intoxication, and ultimately presentation to addiction treatment services.

The predominance of young women in the DXM group also raises the possibility of an association with the recent increase in suicides among adolescents in Japan, particularly among junior high and high school girls [24]. Although “female sex” and “intentional self‐harm or suicide attempt” were not independent predictors in the multivariable analysis, the bivariate analysis showed significantly higher proportions of individuals with these characteristics in the DXM group. This suggests that the DXM group may share a profile with high‐risk youth populations for suicide in contemporary Japan. From this perspective, the high prevalence of acute intoxication observed in the multivariable analysis may reflect not only coping with emotional distress but also the use of DXM‐containing products as a means of suicide attempt.

The growing problem of OTC misuse among young people—particularly involving DXM‐containing products—must therefore be examined within the context of suicide prevention. In the United States, suicide attempts involving overdose of OTC analgesics increased by 33.5% between 2000 and 2018, with adolescent females accounting for most cases, suggesting a non‐negligible association between OTC misuse and youth suicide [25]. Particularly noteworthy is an ecological correlation analysis conducted by Tachikawa in Japan using macro‐level data from the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, the Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology, and the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry [26]. The analysis revealed a striking correlation coefficient of 0.780 between the annual number of suicides among teenage girls and the annual domestic sales volume of OTC medications. Although this correlation does not imply causation and must be interpreted cautiously, it underscores that OTC misuse is a critical issue not only for addiction policy but also for suicide prevention.

The findings of this study offer important implications for Japan's drug misuse prevention strategies and health policy. First, regarding prevention education, Japan has long relied on a “zero‐tolerance,” rule‐compliance‐oriented approach symbolized by the slogan “Dame. Zettai.” Such education has tended to overlook the psychosocial backgrounds that lead to drug use and instead emphasize illegality through fear‐based messaging. As a result, it may have inadvertently fostered the misconception among youth that “OTC medications, being legally accessible, are not problematic.” Moving forward, prevention efforts must shift from mere rule enforcement to approaches that address the psychological and social distress underlying drug use and incorporate mental‐health‐informed perspectives. Risk factors associated with OTC misuse—including interpersonal difficulties, living alone, adverse childhood experiences such as parental divorce, unemployment, low self‐esteem, low educational attainment, and low‐income occupations—have been shown to correlate with both DXM and COD misuse [27, 28, 29]. Understanding these psychosocial backgrounds is essential in addition to regulatory measures.

Second, regarding health policy, the findings highlight the mixed consequences of Japan's recent promotion of self‐care and self‐medication. Policies aimed at reducing healthcare expenditures—such as expanding switch OTC products, establishing the registered sales clerk system in 2009 to allow OTC sales without pharmacists, creating risk categories for OTC products without adequately considering overdose or dependence risks, and introducing the self‐medication tax deduction in 2017—have increased public access to OTC medications. However, it is necessary to evaluate whether these policies have truly improved public health or reduced medical costs. Despite the introduction in 2013 of sales quantity limits for high‐risk OTC products designated as “medicines with potential for misuse or misuse,” the number of patients with OTC‐related psychiatric disorders has continued to rise sharply. Furthermore, misuse of DXM expanded rapidly after DXM‐containing antitussives became available as switch OTC products in 2021. Although the category name was later changed to “Designated Misuse‐Deterrent Medicines” and further restrictions will be implemented in 2026, DXM‐containing products have still not been designated under this category, a situation that is difficult to understand. Prompt and effective governmental action is urgently needed.

Limitations of This Study

This study has three methodological limitations, as outlined below:

First, the data analyzed in this study were collected by physicians responsible for treating cases of substance‐related disorders at each participating institution. Therefore, it is not possible to exclude the potential for variability in clinical judgment among individual physicians, nor the possibility of self‐reporting bias on the part of patients during treatment encounters.

Second, there are limitations concerning the study population. The subjects of this study were patients with substance‐related disorders who had visited or been hospitalized at psychiatric medical institutions. Consequently, the findings do not represent the entire population of OTC misusers in Japan. Interpretation of the results should thus be approached with caution, and future research should aim to include individuals who utilize community‐based support services.

Third, although the study categorized subjects into COD and DXM groups, the classification based on ingredient purity is inherently difficult due to the diverse and multi‐ingredient nature of OTC products available in Japan. For instance, commonly misused cough suppressants and cold remedies often contain multiple active ingredients such as dihydrocodeine, methylephedrine, chlorpheniramine, and caffeine. In this study, such products were collectively classified under the COD group. Similarly, OTC products containing DXM often include additional active components. Future research must carefully consider the methodology used to classify OTC products.

Nevertheless, despite these limitations, the present study holds both academic and clinical significance. It is based on the only comprehensive survey data available regarding substance‐related psychiatric disorders among patients treated at psychiatric institutions in Japan. The study elucidates the clinical characteristics of the DXM group and discusses strategies for reducing both the demand and supply of DXM‐containing products.

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicate that DXM misuse among adolescent females has become a significant public health concern in Japan and that these individuals possess psychosocial backgrounds that differ from those of traditional drug misusers. These results suggest the need to reconsider existing drug misuse prevention education and support systems, which have historically focused primarily on illicit substances, in order to more effectively address OTC medication misuse.
