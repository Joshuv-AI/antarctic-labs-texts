TRAF6 integrates innate immune signals to regulate glucose homeostasis via Parkin-dependent and Parkin-independent mitophagy
Authors: Elena Levi-D’Ancona, Emily M. Walker, Jie Zhu, Yamei Deng, Vaibhav Sidarala, Ava M. Stendahl, Emma C. Reck, Belle A. Henry-Kanarek, Anne C. Lietzke, Biaoxin Chai, Mabelle B. Pasmooij, Dre L. Hubers, Venkatesha Basrur, Sankar Ghosh, Linsey Stiles, Alexey I. Nesvizhskii, Orian S. Shirihai, Scott A. Soleimanpour
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Innate immune signaling is activated in immunometabolic diseases, including type 2 diabetes, yet its impact on glucose homeostasis is controversial. Here, we report that the E3 ubiquitin ligase TRAF6 integrates innate immune signals following diet-induced obesity to promote glucose homeostasis through the induction of mitophagy. Whereas TRAF6 was dispensable for pancreatic β cell function at baseline, TRAF6 was pivotal for insulin secretion, mitochondrial respiration, and mitophagy following metabolic stress in mouse and human islets. TRAF6 was critical for the recruitment and function of the ubiquitin-mediated (Parkin-dependent) mitophagy machinery. Glucose intolerance induced by TRAF6 deficiency following metabolic stress was reversed by concomitant Parkin deficiency by relieving obstructions in receptor-mediated (Parkin-independent) mitophagy. Our results establish that TRAF6 is vital for traffic through Parkin-mediated mitophagy and implicates TRAF6 in the cross-regulation of ubiquitin- and receptor-mediated mitophagy. Together, we illustrate that β cells engage innate immune signaling to adaptively respond to a diabetogenic environment.

INTRODUCTION

Innate immune responses serve as the first line of defense against pathogens (1). In immune cells, innate immune signaling pathways are regulated by Toll-like receptors (TLRs), transmembrane receptors that recognize and respond to foreign ligands. Among the most studied innate immune signaling receptors is TLR4, which primarily recognizes components of Gram-negative bacteria but also mediates signaling as a sensor for free fatty acids (2, 3). TLR4 activation leads to a complex signal transduction network via the signaling adaptors myeloid differentiation primary response 88 (MYD88) and Toll/interleukin-1 receptor domain-containing adaptor protein inducing interferon beta (TRIF), which interact with the E3 ubiquitin ligase TNF receptor–associated factor 6 (TRAF6), a nexus for host defense against microbial pathogens (2). While the importance of TLRs is well known in the immune system, the importance of innate immune signaling in nonimmune cells of metabolic tissues, where engagement with microbial pathogens is not as common, is less clear.

TLRs are expressed in numerous cell types associated with systemic glucose homeostasis, including adipocytes, hepatocytes, and insulin-producing pancreatic β cells (4–6). TLR expression and signaling increase during obesity and the subsequent development of metabolic syndrome to drive systemic inflammation (3, 5, 7). Furthermore, TRAF6 expression is elevated in peripheral blood mononuclear cells of individuals with type 2 diabetes (T2D) (8), and TRAF6 polymorphisms are associated with diabetic complications (9, 10). Exposure of mouse or human pancreatic islets to TLR4 ligands, such as endotoxin, impairs β cell function and viability through intra-islet cytokine production and inhibition of insulin gene expression (11–15). While classical TLR4 ligands can be detrimental to β cells, the absence or inhibition of TLR4 signaling components have had contradictory effects. Whole-body deletion of both Tlr2 and Tlr4 was previously reported to improve β cell proliferation following diet-induced obesity (DIO) (6), and inhibition of TLR4 prevented diabetes onset in autoimmune nonobese diabetic (NOD) mice (16), demonstrating that loss of TLR4 signaling is favorable for β cell health and function during diabetes. In contrast, mice null for either Myd88 or Trif develop impaired β cell function (17, 18). The paradoxical findings of these previous studies may relate to their reliance on whole-body knockout models, suggesting that the role of TLR4-mediated innate immune signaling in β cells remains to be clarified.

In immune cells, mitochondria are an integral part of innate immune signaling. Immune TLR signaling regulates oxidative metabolism, rewires metabolic pathways to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) for microbial killing, releases mitochondrial nucleic acids to amplify local and systemic innate immune signals, and engages mitochondrial docking sites for signaling cascades by the viral sensing machinery (19). In β cells, mitochondria provide the energy necessary to support insulin biosynthesis and secretion following glucose stimulation (20–23). Pancreatic β cell mitochondria are highly susceptible to inflammatory or metabolic stress that occurs during the development of diabetes (24, 25), and mitochondrial defects have been observed to precede the development of T2D (26–29). To preserve mitochondrial health, insulin release, and glycemic control following metabolic stress, β cells engage mitophagy (i.e., the selective clearance of mitochondria by autophagy) through ubiquitin- or receptor-mediated quality control pathways that act dependently or independently of the E3 ligase Parkin, respectively (30, 31). Moreover, we recently observed that β cells of human islet donors with T2D have defects in mitophagy (32), and loss-of-function variants of CALCOCO2, which encodes a ubiquitin receptor necessary for mitophagy, have been linked to T2D (33). However, the effect of innate immune signaling on β cell mitochondrial respiration or quality control is unknown.

Here, we show that β cell innate immune signaling via TRAF6 is important for glucose homeostasis during metabolic stress via the regulation of mitochondrial quality control. We found that β cells engage innate immune pathways following DIO or fatty acid exposure that models the lipotoxicity observed in T2D. Further, we found that impairment of TRAF6 function in vivo elicits glucose intolerance and reduced insulin secretion during high-fat diet (HFD)–induced metabolic stress. Using quantitative proteomics, high-resolution imaging, biochemical assays, and transcriptomic profiling, we demonstrate that TRAF6 deficiency leads to an accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria due to impaired recruitment of components of both the ubiquitin- and receptor-mediated mitophagy machinery. We also found that TRAF6 is essential for mitophagy and insulin secretion following metabolic stress in human islets. Notably, impaired glucose homeostasis and defective mitophagy in TRAF6-deficient β cells following metabolic stress is fully reversed by Parkin deficiency, implicating a previously unappreciated role for TRAF6 in the cross-regulation of ubiquitin- and receptor-mediated mitophagy. Our study suggests a model in which TRAF6 deficiency leads to dysfunctional or stalled Parkin-dependent mitophagy, which can be rescued by loss of Parkin to remove this obstruction and allow BCL2 interacting protein 3 (BNIP3)-mediated, Parkin-independent mitophagy to resume unabated. Together, our results illustrate that β cells engage innate immune signaling to promote mitophagy as a vital adaptive response to metabolic stress.

RESULTS

Palmitate activates innate immune signaling responses in pancreatic β cells

We hypothesized that β cells use innate immune signaling in response to diabetogenic stimuli based on studies exploring TLR signaling components in β cells (6, 17, 18). To test this notion, we examined a previously generated RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) dataset from islets from mice fed HFD or regular-fat diet (RFD) for 12 weeks (34). We observed over 770 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) following DIO, including expected changes in lipid and fatty acid metabolism following HFD feeding (fig. S1, A to D). We also observed increases in type I interferon-responsive pathways (fig. S1, A to D), as well as expression of chemokines (i.e., Ccl5 and Cxcl10) and other key response genes activated by innate immune signaling (i.e., Oas2, Irf7, and Isg15) (fig. S1A). We also observed that DIO increased stimulator of interferon genes (STING) expression, consistent with previous reports of increased activation of the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)-STING cytosolic DNA sensing pathway in islets following HFD feeding (fig. S1E) (35–37). These increases in innate immune signaling targets and lipid metabolic pathways are consistent with fatty acid–triggered TLR signaling, and they supported the concept that DIO could induce innate immune signaling in β cells.

To isolate the effects of metabolic stress in β cells without interference from local immune cells, we next performed a screen in Min6 β cells exposed to metabolic stress conditions in culture to identify which members of the innate immune signaling pathways downstream of TLR4 were activated. We transfected Min6 cells with transcriptional reporter plasmids for pathways responsive to innate immune signaling (table S1) (38, 39), including nuclear factor κB [NF-κB; NF-κB and RELB (RELB proto-oncogene, NF-κB subunit)], mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), Janus kinase/signal transducers and activators of transcription (STAT; STAT1 and STAT3), interferon signaling [interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3), IFNB1, type I interferon, and type II interferon], or stress response pathways [p53, activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4), CCAAT-enhancer-binding protein beta (C/EBPβ), and transcription factor EB (TFEB)]. These reporters were then examined in β cells in the context of two exposures of metabolic stress: lipopolysaccharide (LPS), a classical TLR4 ligand that is known to be elevated during obesity due to gut dysbiosis (40, 41), or the saturated fatty acid palmitate. Palmitate both emulates the lipotoxicity observed in T2D and induces innate immune/TLR4 signaling in immune cells (39, 42), but its impact on β cell innate immune signaling was previously unknown. We found that LPS and palmitate elicited similar patterns of reporter activity in β cells, albeit to differing magnitudes (Fig. 1A).

To determine whether the effects of palmitate in β cells were mediated by innate immune signaling, we generated Min6 β cells deficient in TRAF6, an E3 ubiquitin ligase that acts as an innate immune signaling hub (43), by CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing. We also generated Min6 β cells transduced with a scrambled guide RNA control (Fig. 1B, hereafter known as sgTraf6 and sgScram, respectively). Immune-responsive reporter activity was significantly lower in the TRAF6-deficient cells exposed to LPS compared to the LPS-exposed sgScram cells, confirming TRAF6 function within the innate immune signaling pathway in β cells (Fig. 1C). The effects of palmitate were similarly blunted in TRAF6-deficient cells (Fig. 1D). We next found that LPS and palmitate exposure each induced expression of the NF-κB target genes inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and super oxide dismutase 2 (Sod2) (44), which were also TRAF6 dependent (fig. S1, F and G). Together, these data suggest that innate immune signaling is induced in β cells in the setting of metabolic stress.

TRAF6 promotes β cell functional compensation in response to DIO

To clarify the importance of innate immune signaling in β cells in vivo, we generated mice bearing β cell–specific deletion of Traf6 (Traf6loxP/loxP;Ins1Cre, hereafter known as Traf6Δβ; Fig. 2A). Traf6Δβ mice exhibited an ~80% reduction in TRAF6 protein levels in islets and highly efficient loss of TRAF6 within β cells compared to littermate controls without changes in body weight (Fig. 2, B to D). We observed that TRAF6 was dispensable for glucose tolerance following both 15-week and 30-week RFD (Fig. 2E).

To determine the importance of innate immune signaling in β cell responses to metabolic stress in vivo, we fed mice a HFD for up to 30 weeks beginning at weaning. Expression of TRAF6 protein (Fig. 2B), but not mRNA (fig. S1A), was elevated in islets of control mice following DIO. Traf6Δβ mice and littermate controls exhibited a nearly identical trajectory of weight gain following RFD or HFD feeding (Fig. 2D). Of note, Ins1Cre and floxed-only controls were phenotypically indistinguishable from each other and wild-type littermates, exhibiting no differences across genotypes in body weight, fasting blood glucose, and glucose tolerance on RFD or HFD (fig. S2, A to F), and thus were pooled as controls for subsequent analyses, consistent with previous studies from our group and others (34, 45, 46).

Given our observations of palmitate- or HFD-induced innate immune signaling and stress responses (Fig. 1 and fig. S1), we speculated that TRAF6-deficient β cells would exhibit improved metabolic function in vivo following HFD. However, we found that male Traf6Δβ mice were not protected from DIO-related metabolic dysfunction as they exhibited progressive glucose intolerance following HFD feeding (Fig. 2E and fig. S3A). We also observed no differences in insulin tolerance between genotypes following either RFD or HFD feeding, suggesting that alterations in insulin sensitivity were not responsible for the glucose intolerance observed following TRAF6 deficiency (fig. S3B). Rather, HFD-fed male Traf6Δβ mice exhibited less insulin release in vivo after a glucose challenge compared to littermate controls (Fig. 3A). We also observed reduced glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS) in isolated islets of HFD-fed TRAF6-deficient mice compared to islets from HFD-fed control mice (Fig. 3B), yet both in vivo and ex vivo insulin release were unchanged in RFD-fed TRAF6-deficient mice (Fig. 3, A and B). There were no differences in insulin secretion following KCl stimulation of isolated Traf6Δβ islets (Fig. 3C). TRAF6-deficient mice also displayed no changes in insulin content or β cell mass compared to littermate controls and appropriate expansion of β cell mass in response to HFD (Fig. 3, D and E). We also observed similar impairments in glucose homeostasis and insulin release without differences in body weight or insulin sensitivity following DIO in Traf6Δβ females (fig. S4, A to D). Together, these data suggest that reductions in glucose tolerance and GSIS in HFD-fed Traf6Δβ mice were not due to defects in insulin content, β cell number, or insulin granule exocytosis.

To determine whether TRAF6 was also necessary for β cell functional compensation during metabolic stress in human islets, we treated primary human islets with the TRAF6 inhibitor C25-140, which disrupts the interaction between TRAF6 and the E2-conjugating enzyme UBC13 (47). We exposed human islets to glucolipotoxic (GLT) conditions (25 mM glucose and 0.5 mM palmitate) to model the diabetogenic metabolic stress of hyperglycemia and hyperlipidemia observed during T2D (48). We first confirmed that C25-140 inhibits TRAF6 activity by observing a dose-dependent decrease in NF-κB target gene activation following exposure to a proinflammatory cytokine cocktail (fig. S5). Similar to our observations in HFD-fed Traf6Δβ islets, we found that inhibiting TRAF6 function reduced GSIS in human islets following GLT (Fig. 3F), which was not due to a reduction in insulin content (Fig. 3G). Moreover, TRAF6 was dispensable for GSIS in human islets in the absence of metabolic stress (Fig. 3F), again replicating our observations in RFD-fed Traf6Δβ mice. Together, these results demonstrate that TRAF6 is vital for adaptation to metabolic stress by preserving GSIS in both mouse and human β cells.

TRAF6 deficiency results in an accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria following HFD

To understand how TRAF6 maintains β cell insulin secretion during metabolic stress, we evaluated whether TRAF6 regulates mitochondrial function in β cells given the importance of mitochondria for insulin release and TLR signaling (19, 22). We measured mitochondrial respiration in isolated islets from Traf6Δβ mice and littermate controls following 15 weeks of RFD or HFD feeding. We observed significantly lower glucose-stimulated oxygen consumption in islets isolated from HFD-fed Traf6Δβ mice compared to HFD-fed control mice (Fig. 4A). In addition, we saw significant reductions in the glucose-stimulated adenosine triphosphate (ATP)/adenosine diphosphate (ADP) ratio in islets from HFD-fed Traf6Δβ mice (Fig. 4B). However, there were no differences in mitochondrial function between the genotypes following RFD feeding (Fig. 4, A and B).

To clarify the etiology of mitochondrial respiratory dysfunction following TRAF6 deficiency, we next examined mitochondrial mass and ultrastructure. Several measures indicated increased mitochondrial mass in islets from HFD-fed Traf6Δβ mice, including increased mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) content, citrate synthase activity, and expression of oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) subunits, translocase of outer mitochondrial membrane 20 (TOM20), and mitofusin-2 (MFN2) (Fig. 4, C to E), indicating that reduced mitochondrial respiration in TRAF6-deficient β cells was not due to fewer mitochondria. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) demonstrated that TRAF6-deficient β cells from HFD-fed mice exhibited abnormal mitochondrial morphology compared to littermate controls as determined by increases in mitochondria with distorted cristae and spherical appearance (Fig. 4F). In addition, TRAF6-deficient β cells from HFD-fed mice showed reductions in the mitochondrial aspect ratio, suggestive of mitochondrial fragmentation (Fig. 4F) (49). Together, these results indicated that TRAF6-deficient β cells accumulate dysfunctional mitochondria following DIO.

TRAF6 promotes mitophagy in β cells following DIO

Mitochondrial mass is regulated by a balance of mitochondrial biogenesis and clearance (50). Thus, we next assessed whether the accumulation of mitochondria in islets from HFD-fed Traf6Δβ mice was driven by changes in biogenesis and/or mitophagy. We observed no differences in the expression of key regulators of mitochondrial biogenesis following TRAF6 deficiency (fig. S6A) (50–52). Next, we quantified β cell mitophagy via flow cytometry using the pH-sensitive MtPhagy dye to identify mitochondria within the acidic lysosome compartment, with and without exposure to the potassium ionophore valinomycin to elicit mitophagic flux, as previously described (53). In control mice, exposure to HFD led to greater β cell mitophagic flux, but this effect was blunted in β cells from Traf6Δβ mice (Fig. 5A). We observed no differences in β cell mitophagic flux following TRAF6 deficiency in RFD-fed mice, nor did we observe differences in mitophagic flux following TRAF6 deficiency in non–β cells following either RFD or HFD (Fig. 5A). As an orthogonal approach, we assessed mitophagic flux in isolated islets from RFD- or HFD-fed mt-Keima transgenic mice, which express a mitochondrial-targeted dual excitation reporter that shifts its excitation spectra in a pH-specific manner (54). As expected, mitophagic flux was increased in β cells from HFD-fed mt-Keima mice, but this effect was lost upon inhibition of TRAF6 with C25-140, phenocopying our results in β cells from HFD-fed Traf6Δβ mice (Fig. 5B). Pharmacologic inhibition of TRAF6 in human islets also impaired β cell mitophagic flux following GLT, whereas TRAF6 was dispensable for human β cell mitophagic flux in the absence of metabolic stress (Fig. 5C), similar to our observations in RFD-fed mice.

To further clarify these defects in mitophagy, we assessed phosphorylation of ubiquitin at serine 65 (phospho-Ser65-Ub), a key early event in ubiquitin-mediated/Parkin-dependent mitophagy (55, 56). Following palmitate exposure in Min6 β cells, valinomycin stimulated a robust increase in phospho-Ser65-Ub, which was markedly lower in TRAF6-deficient Min6 cells (Fig. 5D). We next measured turnover of the outer mitochondrial membrane protein MFN2 following mitochondrial damage, a target for ubiquitin-mediated clearance in Parkin-dependent mitophagy (57). As expected, MFN2 levels were reduced following valinomycin exposure in islets from both RFD- and HFD-fed control mice (Fig. 5E). However, MFN2 turnover following valinomycin was lower in islets from HFD-fed Traf6Δβ mice compared to HFD-fed control mice, again indicative of impaired Parkin-dependent mitophagy (Fig. 5E). There were no differences in expression of the lipidated form of microtubule-associated protein light chain 3 (LC3-II) in Traf6Δβ islets compared to littermate controls, suggesting that these mitochondrial defects were specific to mitophagy rather than a general defect in macroautophagy (fig. S6B). Together, these results indicated that TRAF6 regulates β cell mitochondrial function during metabolic stress in both human and mouse islets via control of mitophagy.

To next determine whether the E3 ubiquitin ligase activity of TRAF6 is required for its function in β cells, we used a TRAF6 mutant harboring a point mutation in the RING domain (TRAF6-C70A), which abolishes its E3 ligase activity (58). Following reexpression of TRAF6-C70A in sgTraf6 Min6 cells at levels matching endogenous TRAF6 expression, we observed that the ligase-dead TRAF6 mutant failed to restore defects in GSIS or mitophagy flux following palmitate exposure (fig. S6, C to E). Together with studies using the TRAF6 inhibitor C25-140, which also impairs TRAF6 ligase activity (Figs. 3F and 5, B and C), we find that TRAF6 ubiquitin ligase activity is required for β cell adaptation to metabolic stress.

To gain mechanistic insight into how TRAF6 maintains β cell mitochondrial quality control following DIO, we performed RNA-seq on islets from HFD-fed TRAF6-deficient mice and littermate controls. We observed 445 DEGs between HFD-fed Traf6Δβ and control mice (fig. S7A). Pathway analysis of DEGs following TRAF6 deficiency highlighted changes in pathways such as cell migration and cell-matrix adhesion yet did not identify known regulators of mitochondrial quality control (fig. S7B).

ECSIT does not contribute to HFD-induced β cell dysfunction following TRAF6 deficiency

As our transcriptomic studies did not reveal a clear mechanism for impaired mitophagy following TRAF6 deficiency, we next examined potential posttranscriptional regulators of mitochondrial function. We first examined established interacting partners of TRAF6 important for mitochondrial function and innate immune signaling. Evolutionarily conserved signaling intermediate in Toll pathways (ECSIT) is a signaling adaptor that interacts with TRAF6 downstream of TLR activation, preventing excess inflammation in macrophages by operating as a negative feedback regulator (59). ECSIT also regulates mitochondrial complex I stability, mitochondrial ROS, and mitophagy in neuroglioma cells and macrophages (60–62). We noted lower complex I function and higher ROS levels in islets from HFD-fed Traf6Δβ mice compared to HFD-fed littermate controls (fig. S8, A and B), supporting the hypothesis that TRAF6 could act in coordination with ECSIT to regulate β cell mitochondrial function following metabolic stress.

To establish whether TRAF6 and ECSIT interact in β cells following metabolic stress, we immunoprecipitated TRAF6 in Min6 β cells. We found that TRAF6 interacted with ECSIT at baseline and that this interaction increased following palmitate exposure (fig. S8C). ECSIT expression was elevated in islets following HFD and was further increased in the setting of TRAF6 deficiency (fig. S8D). Given that ECSIT resides in both the cytoplasm and mitochondria (60), we assessed whether the increases in ECSIT following metabolic stress and TRAF6 deficiency were localized to the mitochondria. Biochemical subcellular fractionation of Min6 cells showed that both TRAF6 and ECSIT exhibited greater mitochondrial localization following palmitate exposure (fig. S8E). ECSIT localization to mitochondria was further enhanced in TRAF6-deficient cells following palmitate exposure (fig. S8E).

To explore whether elevations in ECSIT levels might contribute to β cell dysfunction in Traf6Δβ mice, we crossed Traf6Δβ mice (or Ins1Cre controls) with mice bearing an Ecsit conditional allele (EcsitloxP). To reduce ECSIT levels but not elicit metabolic dysfunction, we generated mice haploinsufficient for Ecsit in β cells (EcsitloxP/+; Ins1Cre, hereafter known as ECSITβ-het). ECSITβ-het mice exhibited the expected reductions in ECSIT expression and did not develop defects in glucose homeostasis, GSIS, or insulin content during either RFD or HFD feeding (fig. S9, A to J). Islets from Traf6Δβ/ECSITβ-het mice exposed to HFD displayed lower levels of both TRAF6 and ECSIT and no longer exhibited elevations in ECSIT protein found in HFD-fed Traf6Δβ mice (fig. S9B). However, Traf6Δβ/ECSITβ-het mice were phenotypically indistinguishable from Traf6Δβ mice with regard to glucose homeostasis, insulin secretion, and insulin content when fed either RFD or HFD (fig. S9, C to J). These data indicated that elevations in ECSIT per se did not elicit β cell dysfunction following TRAF6 deficiency.

TRAF6 mediates localization of the mitophagy machinery to mitochondria during metabolic stress

To better understand the mechanism by which TRAF6 regulates mitochondrial quality control during metabolic stress, we examined mitochondrial protein expression in β cells by proteomics. Because of the substantial challenge of purifying sufficient mitochondrial protein from primary islets of our genetic mouse models required for proteomic profiling, we instead performed these studies in TRAF6-deficient and scramble control Min6 β cells. Following exposure of TRAF6-deficient (or scramble control) Min6 β cells to palmitate [or bovine serum albumin (BSA), as a control], we performed biochemical fractionation to enrich for mitochondria. We then used tandem mass tag labeling and liquid chromatography (LC)–mass spectrometry (TMT-MS) to examine mitochondrial protein expression (Fig. 6A). We identified 801 differentially enriched proteins in the mitochondrial fraction of TRAF6-deficient β cells compared to controls following palmitate exposure, with pathway analysis implicating alterations related to the proteasome and cholesterol metabolism (Fig. 6B; fig. S10, A to D; and data file S1). To identify factors associated with impairments in mitophagy following TRAF6 deficiency, we overlaid these proteins with a validated list of proteins from mitophagy and autophagy pathways (63). We identified 13 mitophagy and autophagy proteins differentially enriched in the mitochondrial fraction following TRAF6 deficiency after palmitate exposure (Fig. 6, B and C). Of these 13 proteins, 3 were significantly enriched in our TMT-MS studies with both TRAF6 deficiency and palmitate exposure yet not by TRAF6 deficiency or palmitate treatment alone (Fig. 6, B to D, and fig. S10E). This included the ubiquitin receptors TAX1BP1 and p62/SQSTM1, both of which participate in Parkin-dependent mitophagy and are known to interact with TRAF6 (30, 64, 65), and the mitophagy receptor BNIP3, which is a regulator of Parkin-independent mitophagy (66). While all three proteins can be observed in other subcellular compartments, including the cytoplasm, nucleus, lysosome, and endoplasmic reticulum, these proteins are recruited to mitochondria to facilitate clearance during mitophagy (30, 66, 67). We also evaluated the mitochondrial expression of other ubiquitin receptors involved in mitophagy, including CALCOCO2/NDP52, NBR1, and OPTN, as well as other mitophagy receptors, including BCL2L13, NIX/BNIP3L, and FUNDC1, and did not observe differences in mitochondrial enrichment of these proteins following TRAF6 deficiency or palmitate exposure (table S2).

We next confirmed our TMT-MS findings in mitochondrial fractions by Western blotting, observing lower levels of mitochondrial TAX1BP1, p62, and BNIP3 in TRAF6-deficient cells following palmitate exposure (Fig. 6E). As an orthogonal approach to complement our biochemical fractionation, we isolated mitochondria using magnetic sorting with an antibody specific for the mitochondrial protein TOM22 (68). Following anti-TOM22 sorting, we again confirmed reduced mitochondrial recruitment of TAX1BP1, p62, and BNIP3 in TRAF6-deficient cells following palmitate exposure (fig. S10F). Further, we observed lower mitochondrial BNIP3 in β cells of HFD-fed Traf6Δβ mice compared to HFD-fed control littermates, noted by lower BNIP3 colocalization with the mitochondrial marker succinate dehydrogenase A (SDHA) (Fig. 6F).

To determine whether the reductions in mitochondrial TAX1BP1, p62, and BNIP3 were related to reduced overall levels of these proteins, we next examined their expression in whole-cell extracts. No differences in total levels of TAX1BP1 and p62 were observed in Min6 β cells in the setting of TRAF6 deficiency alone or following palmitate exposure (Fig. 6G), indicating that TRAF6 regulates the mitochondrial localization rather than expression of TAX1BP1 and p62. Further, no differences in total expression of TAX1BP1 and p62 were observed in whole-cell extracts of islets from HFD-fed Traf6Δβ mice compared to HFD-fed control littermates (Fig. 6H). Consistent with reports of increased BNIP3 expression in response to metabolic stress (69–72), total BNIP3 protein levels rose both in Min6 β cells and islets following palmitate exposure or DIO, respectively (Fig. 6, G and H); however, no differences were seen following TRAF6 deficiency, indicating that TRAF6 also regulates the mitochondrial localization rather than expression of BNIP3. Moreover, we observed no differences in the mRNA expression of Tax1bp1, p62, or Bnip3 following DIO or TRAF6 deficiency (figs. S1A and S7A). Together, these data suggested that TRAF6 is essential for the proper recruitment of mitophagy machinery to mitochondria during metabolic stress.

Our subcellular fractionation studies, together with impaired phospho-Ser65-Ub and MFN2 turnover in TRAF6-deficient β cells during HFD (Fig. 5, D and E), led us to next explore the connections between TRAF6 and Parkin in β cells. We observed that TRAF6 interacted with both Parkin and the E3 ligase neuregulin receptor degradation protein 1 (NRDP1), which binds to Parkin to tune mitophagy and prevent abnormal mitophagic flux (73), and that these interactions increased upon palmitate exposure (Fig. 7A). Mitochondrial NRDP1 localization was reduced in TRAF6-deficient Min6 cells following palmitate exposure, whereas Parkin localization to mitochondria increased (Fig. 7B and fig. S10G). Again, no differences in total expression of NRDP1 or Parkin were observed following TRAF6 deficiency in Min6 cells or primary islets (Fig. 7, C and D). Together, these results indicate that TRAF6 deficiency alters the recruitment of key regulators of both Parkin-dependent and Parkin-independent mitophagy following metabolic stress.

Loss of Parkin restores β cell function and glucose homeostasis following TRAF6 deficiency

Our observations of numerous defects in the Parkin-mediated mitophagy pathway in TRAF6 deficient β cells following metabolic stress, including impaired phospho-Ser65-Ub, reduced MFN2 turnover, an accumulation of mitochondrial Parkin, and impaired mitochondrial recruitment of the ubiquitin receptors p62 and TAX1BP1, led us to question whether TRAF6 deficiency resulted in glucose intolerance due to abnormalities in Parkin-mediated mitophagy. While Parkin is capable of initiating basal β cell mitophagy (73), its role in adaptive stress responses remains unclear. Previous work has shown that Parkin is essential for GSIS or recovery from palmitate-mediated damage in β cell lines (74). In contrast, we previously demonstrated that Parkin expression in β cells is dispensable for both glucose homeostasis and mitophagy flux at baseline and following HFD (75), indicating that Parkin-independent mitophagy is fully capable of maintaining β cell function following metabolic stress. Our results led us to speculate that TRAF6 deficiency may engage defective Parkin-dependent mitophagy, which then stalls progression through both ubiquitin-mediated and receptor-mediated mitophagy pathways. We therefore hypothesized that removal of Parkin in the context of TRAF6 deficiency could improve β cell adaptation to metabolic stress by restoring Parkin-independent mitophagy.

To test this hypothesis, we crossed Traf6Δβ mice and Ins1Cre controls with mice bearing the Parkin conditional allele (ParkinloxP) to generate both dual Traf6/ParkinΔβ and ParkinΔβ mice, respectively. Traf6/ParkinΔβ islets exhibited the expected reduction of both TRAF6 and Parkin protein levels specifically in β cells, with no respective compensatory changes in the single knockouts (Fig. 8, A and B, and fig. S11A). In line with previous results, Parkin was dispensable for glucose homeostasis following HFD (75); however, deletion of Parkin in β cells completely restored glucose homeostasis in HFD-fed TRAF6-deficient mice to the level seen in HFD-fed controls (Fig. 8C). In addition, GSIS was restored both in HFD-fed Traf6/ParkinΔβ mice in vivo and in isolated islets (Fig. 8, D and E). As expected, Traf6Δβ, ParkinΔβ, Traf6/ParkinΔβ, and littermate controls displayed no physiological differences following RFD feeding (fig. S11, B to D). Further, we found no differences in KCl-stimulated insulin secretion, total insulin content, or β cell mass between Traf6Δβ, ParkinΔβ, Traf6/ParkinΔβ, and littermate control mice following RFD or HFD feeding (Fig. 8, F to H, and fig. S11, E and F). These data suggest that TRAF6 operates through Parkin to promote glucose homeostasis and insulin secretion during metabolic stress.

Loss of Parkin rescues β cell mitochondrial quality control following TRAF6 deficiency

We next asked whether deletion of Parkin could ameliorate mitochondrial dysfunction and stalled progression through mitophagy observed in HFD-fed Traf6Δβ mice. Islets from HFD-fed Traf6/ParkinΔβ mice exhibited a complete recovery of glucose-stimulated oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and ATP/ADP ratio compared to islets from HFD-fed Traf6Δβ mice (Fig. 9, A and B). Combined TRAF6/Parkin deficiency restored mitochondrial mass to the same level as littermate controls, as measured via mtDNA content, citrate synthase activity, and expression of OXPHOS subunits and TOM20 protein (Fig. 9, C to E). Moreover, islets from Traf6/ParkinΔβ mice also displayed rescue of both mitochondrial morphology and mitophagic flux following DIO (Figs. 9F and 10A), indicating that deletion of Parkin ameliorated Parkin-independent mitophagy in TRAF6-deficient β cells. To clarify how concomitant Parkin deficiency could ameliorate mitochondrial dysfunction in TRAF6-deficient β cells, we first examined the mitochondrial recruitment of ubiquitin and mitophagy receptors. Mitochondrial BNIP3 localization was improved following the loss of Parkin in HFD-fed TRAF6-deficient β cells (Fig. 10B). We also used small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting Parkin, achieving approximately a 70% reduction in Parkin protein levels in TRAF6-deficient Min6 cells exposed to palmitate (fig. S12A). Following mitochondrial fractionation, we observed that Parkin knockdown in TRAF6-deficient Min6 cells restored BNIP3 localization to mitochondria, whereas recruitment of TAX1BP1 and p62 remained reduced (Fig. 10C), suggesting that defective Parkin-mediated mitophagy impaired BNIP3-dependent receptor-mediated mitophagy following TRAF6 deficiency.

We next examined whether removal of Parkin would ameliorate proximal defects in innate immune signaling in TRAF6-deficient β cells following metabolic stress. However, concomitant Parkin deficiency did not rescue abnormalities in transcriptional reporter activity of the innate immune signaling pathway in TRAF6-deficient Min6 cells (fig. S12B), nor did it rescue the reduced expression of proteins in the cGAS-STING pathway in islets of HFD-fed Traf6Δβ mice (fig. S12C). Together, these studies suggest TRAF6 integrates innate immune signals to regulate glucose homeostasis and adaptation to metabolic stress through β cell mitophagy. Following TRAF6 deficiency, Parkin-dependent mitophagy is engaged but dysfunctional, stalling progression through both ubiquitin-mediated and receptor-mediated mitophagy pathways. Parkin depletion in the context of TRAF6 loss appears to remove impediments derived from defective Parkin-dependent mitophagy, thus allowing BNIP3-mediated, receptor-dependent mitophagy to resume unabated and restore mitochondrial quality control.

DISCUSSION

Here, we identified a link between innate immune signaling and mitochondrial quality control in pancreatic β cells. We observed that β cells activated innate immune signaling in response to metabolic stress. TRAF6 was dispensable for β cell function under basal conditions, but loss of TRAF6 impaired glucose homeostasis and β cell function following DIO. TRAF6 maintained mouse and human β cell mitochondrial health by promoting mitophagy in the setting of metabolic stress. TRAF6 deficiency led to numerous defects in the Parkin-mediated mitophagy pathway, impaired the recruitment of key machinery within both Parkin-dependent and Parkin-independent mitophagy, and ultimately stalled traffic through mitophagy following metabolic stress. Codeletion of Parkin in HFD-fed Traf6Δβ mice relieved impediments in mitophagy flux, allowing restoration of receptor-mediated mitophagy and, ultimately, glucose homeostasis, suggesting that TRAF6 may regulate communication between ubiquitin- and receptor-mediated mitophagy to promote β cell function (fig. S13). Together, our studies reveal a critical role for TRAF6 in governing adaptive β cell responses to metabolic stressors.

Our studies identify an unexpected intrinsic role for β cell innate immune signaling to preserve insulin secretion in the setting of DIO or elevated free fatty acids by regulating mitophagy. An understanding for the role of innate immune signaling has expanded to nonimmune cells, including studies revealing cross-talk between keratinocytes and osteoblasts with the immune system (76–80). While we cannot eliminate a potentially similar role for TRAF6 in β cell–immune cell cross-talk, our results suggest a β cell–autonomous capacity for TRAF6 in directing mitochondrial quality control. Fatty acid exposure also promoted the interaction of TRAF6 with NRDP1 and ECSIT, both of which are bona fide signaling intermediates engaged during innate immune signaling with connections to mitochondrial health (60, 73, 81). Further, several studies link TBK1 activation to Parkin-mediated mitophagy (82–84), providing additional connections between TRAF6, innate immune signaling, and mitophagy in our work. BNIP3 appears to induce mitophagy independently of TBK1 (85), which is also consistent with the restoration of mitophagy in conditions of TRAF6/Parkin codeficiency where TBK1 remains reduced. Our work led us to revisit past studies of whole-body knockouts of Trif and Myd88, both upstream regulators of TRAF6 and immediately downstream of TLR4 (17, 18, 86), to contextualize the importance of innate immune signaling in β cells. Trif-null mice develop impaired insulin secretion, and Myd88-null mice are sensitized to the β cell toxin streptozotocin (17, 18), phenotypes which bear similarity to those previously observed in models of β cell mitophagy deficiency (87, 88). While β cell mitophagy was not examined in studies of Trif and Myd88-null mice (17, 18), TRIF and MYD88 in macrophages interact with and activate Beclin 1, a key initiator of autophagosome formation that can also facilitate Parkin translocation to mitochondria (89–91). However, unlike Tlr2/Tlr4-null animals (6), Traf6Δβ mice did not exhibit increases of β cell mass following DIO. Thus, future studies will be crucial to consider the β cell–specific relevance of numerous nodes within the innate immune signaling machinery, not only on mitophagy but also on β cell–immune cell cross-talk to more holistically uncover their relevance to metabolic disease.

Our studies identify a unique role for TRAF6 in control of glucose homeostasis through regulation of mitophagy in β cells that is distinct from other tissues. In a model of Parkinson’s disease, Parkin promotes TRAF6 degradation, and Parkin deficiency leads to apoptosis due to stabilization of TRAF6 (92). In neuroblastoma and human embryonic kidney 293 cells, TRAF6 stabilizes PINK1 on damaged mitochondria to recruit Parkin for activation of mitophagy (93). Further, TRAF6 was previously shown to interact with p62 to regulate macroautophagy in the immune system and cancer cells (65, 94) and with TAX1BP1 to regulate NF-κB transcriptional activation in immune cells (64). Despite a similar series of partners, our work suggests that TRAF6 and these partners have distinct functions in β cells during metabolic stress. We observed that TRAF6 deficiency led to impaired mitophagy flux, without overt defects in macroautophagy. Despite a reduction in phospho-Ser65-Ub in TRAF6-deficient β cells, which could be suggestive of impaired PINK1 function, we observed an increase, and not a decrease, of mitochondrial Parkin following TRAF6 deficiency. We also observed that following metabolic stress, TRAF6 was crucial for mitochondrial recruitment of p62 and TAX1BP1, both ubiquitin receptors activated in the Parkin-dependent mitophagy pathway. While Parkin deficiency itself did not yield metabolic dysfunction, loss of β cell mass, or regulate TRAF6 expression, codeletion of Parkin with TRAF6 reversed the glucose intolerance observed in HFD-fed Traf6Δβ mice. These results suggest that TRAF6 is required for successful traffic through Parkin-dependent mitophagy with implications for BNIP3-dependent, receptor-mediated mitophagy. Tight control of Parkin activity has been suggested to be important for β cell function (87, 95); however, the mechanisms underlying the optimization of Parkin action had been previously unclear. Together, our results suggest that TRAF6 plays a critical role in allowing β cells to adapt to metabolic stress by integrating innate immune signals and directing mitophagic flux through a complex network of partners. Additional studies will be valuable to clarify the intricacies of both tissue- and context-specific roles of TRAF6 and its numerous partners in directing mitochondrial quality control following the engagement of innate immune signaling.

Direct mechanisms underlying the cross-talk between ubiquitin- and receptor-mediated mitophagy are emerging yet remain unclear. Our work reveals the importance of both mitophagy pathways in β cells, as well as previously unappreciated connections between Parkin and receptor-mediated mitophagy, which frequently is characterized as Parkin independent. PINK1 and Parkin deficiency are each dispensable for mitophagy in β cells (75, 96), supporting the ability of PINK1/Parkin-independent pathways to maintain mitochondrial quality control. However, our work shows that the dispensable nature of Parkin does not render its function unimportant in β cells and that stalled progression through Parkin-mediated mitophagy ultimately imbalances both mitophagy pathways during mitochondrial adaptation to metabolic stress. Studies of receptor-mediated mitophagy in β cells are more limited but have recently begun to surface. Induction of BNIP3 expression by hypoxia inducible factor 1 subunit alpha (HIF1α) was recently shown to be vital for increases in β cell mitophagy following DIO (69). We similarly observe an increase in BNIP3 expression following DIO but demonstrate that TRAF6 regulates the mitochondrial recruitment rather than the expression of BNIP3, suggesting that HIF1α and TRAF6 may play distinct roles in BNIP3-dependent mitophagy following DIO. While a previous study demonstrated BNIP3-dependent mitophagy is reduced following loss of Parkin in cardiomyocytes (97), it is unclear how impaired Parkin-dependent mitophagy following TRAF6 deficiency regulates cross-talk with the BNIP3 pathway. Restoration of mitophagy flux and BNIP3 recruitment to mitochondria in TRAF6-deficient β cells by concomitant loss of Parkin also appears to be independent of p62 and TAX1BP1, as recruitment of these ubiquitin receptors was not rescued in TRAF6/Parkin double-deficient Min6 β cells. These results further support the potential importance of restoration of BNIP3 localization to mitochondria as crucial to promote mitophagy following metabolic stress. However, future studies will be important to determine whether the restoration of mitochondrial BNIP3 is required for the recovery of mitophagy following combined TRAF6/Parkin deficiency. While we did not observe alterations in the recruitment of other mitophagy receptors following TRAF6 deficiency, including NIX, FUNDC1, or BCL2L13 (98–100), it is also possible that defects in mitophagy (and rescue following concomitant Parkin deficiency) could be mediated by the activity of these receptors, whose roles in β cell mitophagy are unknown. Together, our work provides insights into the cross-regulation of these two distinct pathways underlying mitochondrial quality control by TRAF6.

T2D is a complex, multiorgan disease driven by β cell dysfunction and impaired responses to immunometabolic stress (25, 101). While lipotoxicity is a precipitating factor for β cell damage in T2D (102), our studies suggest that innate immune signaling forms a pivotal protective response to equip β cells with the means to compensate for metabolic stress, rather than as a purveyor of β cell demise. The protective effects of innate immune signaling appear to be due to a noncanonical up-regulation of mitophagy as opposed to classical transcriptional effects, but this remains to be explored in T2D. Further, it will be interesting to determine whether recently reported defects in β cell mitophagy in T2D are due to impairments in innate immune signaling (34). Moreover, the effects of enhancement of β cell innate immune signaling or TRAF6-mediated mitophagy could be of interest for therapeutic benefit in T2D. Together, our studies suggest that activation of innate immune signaling in β cells is a crucial adaptive response to obesity that promotes mitophagy to preserve β cell function.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Genetically modified mouse lines

All mice were maintained in accordance with the University of Michigan Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (protocol number PRO00011233) under specific pathogen–free conditions. Up to five mice were housed per cage and on a 12-hour light-dark cycle. Traf6loxP/loxP mice having loxP sites flanking exon 7 of the Traf6 gene were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory (JAX stock no. 030849) (103). Parkinfl/fl mice having loxP sites flanking exon 7 of the Parkin gene were provided by T. Dawson (Johns Hopkins University) and Lexicon Genetics (104). Ecsitfl/fl mice containing a loxP site flanking exon 3 were previously described (60). To generate β cell–specific deletion, floxed models were intercrossed with Ins1-Cre knock-in mice obtained from the Jackson Laboratory (JAX stock no. 026801) (46). Ins1-Cre–alone and respective floxed-only controls for each study (Traf6loxP/loxP, Parkinfl/fl, Traf6loxP/loxP/Parkinfl/fl, or ECSITfl/fl mice) were phenotypically indistinguishable from each other and combined as controls. Ins1-Cre–alone were also phenotypically indistinguishable from wild-type C57BL/6N controls, consistent with previous reports from our group and others (34, 45, 46, 75, 88). mt-Keima mice were a gift from T. Finkel (University of Pittsburgh) (54). All mouse models were maintained on a 100% C57BL/6N background. All studies and endpoints were completed using both male and female mice.

Human islet samples

All human samples were procured from deidentified donors from Prodo Laboratories or the Alberta Diabetes Institute IsletCore at the University of Alberta in Edmonton (http://bcell.org/adi-isletcore.html) with the assistance of the Human Organ Procurement and Exchange program, Trillium Gift of Life Network, and other Canadian organ procurement organizations. Islet isolation was approved by the Human Research Ethics Board at the University of Alberta (Pro00013094). All donors’ families gave informed consent for the use of pancreatic tissue in research. Human islet studies were approved by the University of Michigan Institutional Review Board. Human primary islets were cultured at 37°C with 5% CO2 in Prodo Islet Media (Standard) [PIM(S)] media (Prodo Laboratories) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gemini Bio), penicillin/streptomycin (100 U/ml; Gibco), antibiotic/antimycotic (100 U/ml; Gibco), and 1 mM Prodo Islet Media (Glutamine/Glutathione) [PIM(G)] (Prodo Laboratories). Islets were used from male and female donors, and donor information is provided in table S3. Drug treatments used for human islet cultures included 1 μM C25-120 [TRAF6-UBC13 inhibitor, 24 hours, MedChemExpress (47)], 250 nM valinomycin (3 hours, MilliporeSigma), or GLT (25 mM glucose + 0.5 mM palmitate [conjugated to BSA as previously described (75), 48 hours], as well as respective vehicle controls [dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) or BSA].

HFD feeding

Control, Traf6Δβ, ECSITβ-het, Traf6Δβ/ECSITβ-het, ParkinΔβ, and Traf6/ParkinΔβ mice were randomized onto a 10% RFD or 60% HFD (Research Diets Inc.) at weaning. Access to food was ad libitum for up to 30 weeks. Mice were then subjected to in vivo metabolic analyses (detailed in the following sections), and pancreatic tissue was harvested at the end of the study for immunohistochemistry, ex vivo islet studies, metabolic phenotyping, or sequencing studies. Physiologic data in male and female mice cohorts were displayed separately to evaluate for potential sexual dimorphic metabolic phenotypes following DIO.

Mouse primary islet isolation and culture

Mouse primary islets were isolated by perfusing pancreata with a solution of collagenase P (1 mg/ml; MilliporeSigma) in 1× Hanks’ balanced salt solution (HBSS; Sigma-Aldrich) into the pancreatic duct. Following excision, pancreata were incubated at 37°C for 13 min, and collagenase P was deactivated by addition of Quench Buffer [1× HBSS + 10% adult bovine serum (Gemini Bio)]. Pancreata were dissociated mechanically by vigorous shaking for 30 s, and the resulting cell suspension was passed through a 70 μM cell strainer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells were centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 2 min, and then the pellet was resuspended in 20 ml Quench Buffer and gently vortexed to thoroughly mix. Cells were again centrifuged at 1000 rpm, 1 min. This wash step was repeated once more. Following washes, the cell pellet was resuspended in 5-ml Histopaque (MilliporeSigma) with gentle vortexing. An additional 5-ml Histopaque was layered on the cell suspension, and 10-ml Quench Buffer was gently layered on top. The cells were spun at 800g for 30 min at 10°C, with the brake off. The entire Histopaque gradient was pipetted off and passed through an inverted 70 μM filter to trap the islets cells. Islets were washed twice with Quench Buffer (10 ml) and once with 10 ml of islet media [RPMI 1640 supplemented with penicillin/streptomycin (100 U/ml), 10% FBS, 1 mM Hepes, 2 mM l-glutamine, antibiotic/antimycotic (100 U/ml), and 10 mM sodium pyruvate]. The filter was inverted into a sterile petri dish, and cells were washed into the dish with 4.5-ml complete islet media. Islets were left to recover overnight, and treatments began the next day. Mouse islets were treated with the following treatments: 100 nM, 10 nM, or 1 μM C25-120 (TRAF6-UBC13 inhibitor, 24 hours, MedChemExpress); valinomycin (250 nM, 3 hours, Sigma-Aldrich); LPSs from Escherichia coli O55:B5 (LPS) (5 ng/ml, 1 or 2 hours, Sigma-Aldrich); proinflammatory cytokine cocktail [interleukin-1β (75 U/ml), tumor necrosis factor–α (750 U/ml), and interferon-γ (750 U/ml), 6 hours, PeproTech]; palmitate (0.5 mM palmitate, 24 or 48 hours, Sigma-Aldrich); or vehicle controls. Islets were isolated from both male and female mice.

Cell culture, cell line generation, treatments, and transfections

Min6 cells (a gift from D. Stoffers, University of Pennsylvania) were cultured as previously described (75). Traf6-deficient Min6 cells were generated using the LentiCRISPRV2 one-vector system as described (105). Traf6 single guide RNA (sgRNA) sequences included (5′-GGAGATCCAGGGCTACGATG-3′, targeting exon 3) and (5′-ATTTGGGCACTTTACCGTCA-3′ targeting exon4) and were cloned into LentiCRISPRV2 as described (106). LentiCRISPRV2 was transfected into 293 T cells along with the lentiviral packaging plasmids REV response element (RRE), vesicular stomatitis virus G (VSV-G), and REV (courtesy of L. Islet respirometry

Islet respirometry was performed using the BaroFuse instrument (EnTox Sciences Inc.) as previously described (112). Setup of the BaroFuse perifusion instrument involved four steps: (i) The system was preheated by filling the media reservoirs with preequilibrated media (1× KRB, hepes-buffered (KRBH) with 3 mM glucose); (ii) the tissue chamber assemblies were inserted into the perifusion module and secured in place on top of the media reservoir to create a gas tight seal; (iii) the headspace of the media reservoirs were purged with the desired gas composition (typically 5% CO2/21% O2 balance N2) to allow the gas in the headspace to equilibrate with the perifusate in the reservoir; and (iv) the purge port was closed to allow the flow to fill up the tissue chambers. When the tissue chambers had almost filled, islets were loaded into six of the eight chambers, and two were left empty to be used as an inflow reference. One hundred islets were transferred into the tissue chamber (ID = 1.5 mm) with a P-200 pipet. Once islets were loaded, the magnetic stirrers, an O2 detector, and a flow rate monitor were started, and the system and the islets were allowed to equilibrate to establish a stable baseline for 90 to 120 min. Subsequently, test compounds (final concentrations of 20 mM glucose, 10 μM oligomycin, and 3 mM KCN) were injected at precise times. Data are presented as fractional change from baseline (at 3 mM glucose containing media) OCRs per sample relative to minimum OCR per sample determined following exposure to 3 mM KCN for baseline normalization using the BaroFuse Data Processing Package software (Entox Sciences Inc.).

TMT labeling mass spectrometry–based quantitative proteomics

Preparation of mitochondrial fractions

Mitochondrial fractions from sgScramble or sgTraf6 Min6 cells were collected as described above and were lysed in RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing phosphatase inhibitors. Mitochondrial lysates were quantified via BCA assay. All samples were diluted to 1 mg/ml in RIPA buffer.

Protein digestion and TMT labeling

Forty microliters of each sample was submitted to the Proteomics Resource Facility at the University of Michigan for processing and mass spectrometry (MS) data acquisition. Briefly, upon reduction (5 mM DTT, for 30 min at 45°C) and alkylation (15 mM 2-chloroacetamide, for 30 min at room temperature) of cysteines in samples, the proteins were precipitated by adding 6 volumes of ice-cold acetone followed by overnight incubation at −20°C. The precipitate was spun down, and the pellet was allowed to air dry. The pellet was resuspended in 0.1 M tetraethylammonium bromide (TEAB), and overnight (~16 hours) digestion with trypsin/Lys-C mix (1:25 protease:protein; Promega) at 37°C was performed with constant mixing using a thermomixer. The TMT 16-plex reagents were dissolved in 41 μl of anhydrous acetonitrile, and labeling was performed by transferring the entire digest to TMT reagent vial and incubating at room temperature for 1 hour. Reaction was quenched by adding 8 μl of 5% hydroxyl amine and further 15-min incubation. Labeled samples were mixed and dried using a vacufuge. An offline fractionation of the combined sample (~200 μg) into eight fractions was performed using high pH reversed-phase peptide fractionation kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Pierce; catalog no. 84868). Fractions were dried and reconstituted in 9 μl of 0.1% formic acid/2% acetonitrile in preparation for LC–tandem MS (MS/MS) analysis. Samples sgScram BSA 1 to 4 were labeled with TMT channels 126, 127N, 128N, and 129N, respectively. Samples sgTraf6 BSA 1 to 4 were labeled with TMT channels 130N, 131N, 132N, and 133N, respectively. Samples sgScram palm 1 to 4 were labeled with TMT channels 134N, 127C, 128C, and 129C, respectively. Samples sgTraf6 palm 1 to 4 were labeled with TMT channels 130C, 131C, 132C, and 133C, respectively.

LC-MS analysis (LC-multinotch MS3)

To obtain superior quantitation accuracy, we used multinotch-MS3 which minimizes the reporter ion ratio distortion resulting from fragmentation of coisolated peptides during MS analysis (116). Orbitrap Ascend Tribrid equipped with high-field asymmetric waveform ion mobility spectrometry (FAIMS) source (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Vanquish Neo ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) was used to acquire the data. Two microliters of the sample was resolved on an Easy-Spray PepMap Neo column [75 μm internal diameter (i.d.) by 50 cm; Thermo Fisher Scientific] at the flow rate of 300 nl/min using 0.1% formic acid/acetonitrile gradient system (3 to 19% acetonitrile in 72 min;19 to 29% acetonitrile in 28 min; 29 to 41% in 20 min followed by 10-min column wash at 95% acetonitrile and reequilibration) and directly sprayed onto the mass spectrometer using EasySpray source (Thermo Fisher Scientific). FAIMS source was operated in standard resolution mode, with a nitrogen gas flow of 4.2 liters/min, inner and outer electrode temperature of 100°C, and dispersion voltage or −5000 V. Two compensation voltages (CVs) of −45 and −65 V, 1.5 s per CV, were used to select ions that enter the mass spectrometer for MS1 scan and MS/MS cycles. The mass spectrometer was set to collect MS1 scan [Orbitrap; 400 to 1600 mass/charge ratio (m/z); 120K resolution; automatic gain control (AGC) target of 100%; max injection time (IT) in auto] following which precursor ions with charge states of 2 to 6 were isolated by quadrupole mass filter at 0.7 m/z width and fragmented by collision-induced dissociation in ion trap [normalized collision energy (NCE) 30%; normalized AGC target of 100%; max IT of 35 ms]. For multinotch-MS3, top 10 precursors from each MS2 were fragmented by higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) followed by Orbitrap analysis (NCE 55%; 45K resolution; normalized AGC target of 200%; max IT of 200 ms, 100 to 500 m/z scan range).

Data analysis

Proteome Discoverer (v3.0; Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used for data analysis. MS2 spectra were searched against SwissProt mouse protein database [Mus musculus (sp_canonical TaxID = 10090) (v2023-09-13), downloaded on 15 May 2023] using the following search parameters: MS1 and MS2 tolerance were set to 10 parts per million and 0.6 Da, respectively; carbamidomethylation of cysteines (57.02146 Da) and TMT labeling of lysine and N termini of peptides (229.16293 Da) were considered static modifications; oxidation of methionine (15.9949 Da) and deamidation of asparagine and glutamine (0.98401 Da) were considered variable. Identified proteins and peptides were filtered to retain only those that passed ≤1% FDR threshold. Quantitation was performed using high-quality MS3 spectra (average signal-to-noise ratio of 8 and <60% isolation interference).

Statistics

In all figures, data are presented as means ± SEM, and error bars denote SEM, unless otherwise noted in the legends. Outlier tests [robust outlier detection (ROUT) method (117)] were routinely performed in GraphPad Prism. Statistical comparisons were performed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t tests and one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s or Sidak’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons, as appropriate (GraphPad Prism). A P value of <0.05 was considered significant.
