Letter to the Editor Regarding “MMP14 is a safe target of CAR-T therapy against liver cancer and metastasis”
Authors: Yiheng Liu, Feifei Dou
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the article by Yu et al. in the Journal of Translational Medicine [1]. This study addresses the critical need for new therapies in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), especially metastatic HCC, by evaluating MMP14 as a target for CAR-T therapy. The authors constructed Peptide G (PG)-based CAR-T cells targeting MMP14 (PG-CAR-T) and systematically demonstrated their antitumor and anti-metastasis efficacy in vitro, in subcutaneous xenografts, and in a spontaneous metastasis model. Importantly, PG-CAR-T cells showed a favorable safety profile in both mouse and nonhuman primate models. This work is the first to establish MMP14 as a safe and effective CAR-T target for solid tumors like liver cancer, offering a promising strategy to overcome current challenges of target scarcity and on-target, off-tumor toxicity.

The study’s major strength lies in its rigorous translational framework and focus on metastasis. The authors confirmed MMP14’s expression and prognostic value in HCC, and demonstrated PG-CAR-T’s specific cytotoxicity against MMP14-high tumor cells, including cancer stem-like cells, in vitro. In vivo, they innovatively used a spontaneous metastasis model to show that PG-CAR-T cells inhibit distant metastasis by eliminating circulating tumor cells (CTCs), directly addressing a key clinical need. Furthermore, the comprehensive safety assessment in immunodeficient mice and immunocompetent nonhuman primates, which revealed no significant toxicity, provides crucial data supporting future clinical trials.

Although the study’s conclusions are encouraging, several key scientific and translational questions warrant further exploration to advance this promising therapy towards the clinic.

First, a deeper understanding of how MMP14-targeted CAR-T therapy inhibits metastasis is needed. While PG-CAR-T cells are shown to eliminate CTCs and suppress metastasis, the detailed mechanisms—particularly regarding immune microenvironment remodeling—require further elucidation. Given MMP14’s multifaceted roles in pre-metastatic niche formation and immune regulation [2], it remains unclear whether PG-CAR-T cells act only by clearing CTCs in the bloodstream, or if they also infiltrate and reshape the immune landscape of pre-metastatic or micro-metastatic sites. Employing advanced techniques like high-resolution in vivo imaging and spatial transcriptomics in relevant models could track PG-CAR-T cell distribution and their impact on local immune cells within metastases, clarifying the full inhibitory mechanism.

Second, exploring strategies to enhance the infiltration and persistence of PG-CAR-T cells in response to the challenges of CAR-T therapy in solid tumors. The study mentions observed tumor infiltration of CAR-T cells in the subcutaneous model, but physical barriers and the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment remain major obstacles to CAR-T efficacy in solid tumors. Given that MMP14 itself is involved in extracellular matrix remodeling, do MMP14-expressing CAR-T cells potentially possess enhanced tissue infiltration capabilities? Future work could compare the infiltration depth and persistence of PG-CAR-T cells versus CAR-T cells targeting other antigens within tumor tissues [3]. Additionally, exploring combination strategies pairing PG-CAR-T cells with therapies targeting the tumor microenvironment (e.g., anti-angiogenic agents, or drugs targeting immunosuppressive pathways like TGF-β) may further enhance their efficacy in HCC, particularly in advanced cases with significant fibrosis.

Third, advancing from preclinical safety to clinical precision requires refined patient stratification. While safety in nonhuman primates is encouraging, potential risks from transient MMP14 expression in human normal tissues (e.g., during wound healing or pregnancy) warrant close monitoring in trials. Future Phase I studies should consider targeted imaging or biopsy of relevant tissues alongside routine safety assessments. Additionally, given the heterogeneous MMP14 expression observed in HCC—reflecting the disease’s high variability—clinical cohorts should explore stratification based on MMP14 levels (e.g., by immunohistochemistry or liquid biopsy) or molecular subtypes with high metastatic potential, to identify patients most likely to benefit [4].

Finally, exploring the positioning and combinatorial potential of PG-CAR-T therapy within the integrated treatment landscape of HCC. Given that CTC numbers may increase after surgery and chemotherapy and correlate with poor prognosis, the potential of PG-CAR-T cells as an adjuvant therapy to eliminate minimal residual disease and CTCs warrants investigation. Future preclinical studies could establish neoadjuvant or adjuvant therapy models to evaluate the efficacy of PG-CAR-T in clearing residual cancer cells and preventing recurrence/metastasis after surgical resection of the primary tumor [5]. Moreover, given the high expression of MMP14 in various solid tumors, the potential applicability of this target and the PG-CAR-T platform could be extended to other MMP14-high malignancies (e.g., triple-negative breast cancer, pancreatic cancer), which deserves validation in corresponding preclinical models.

We sincerely thank the team of Yu et al. for their excellent work and the Journal of Translational Medicine for providing such a significant platform to share our perspectives on this research. In the future, we expect MMP14-targeted CAR-T therapy to be translated into an effective weapon that can improve the survival prognosis of liver cancer patients, especially those with a high risk of metastasis.

Sincerely,

All Authors
