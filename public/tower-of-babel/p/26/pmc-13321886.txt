Evaluating the cost-effectiveness of adjuvanted influenza vaccine in the older adult population in the Netherlands
Authors: Susan de Braak, Joaquin F Mould-Quevedo, Jan C Wilschut, Gerrit A van Essen, Albert Osterhaus, Maarten J Postma, Florian Zeevat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn the Netherlands, older adults are vaccinated with the standard (dose) influenza vaccine (sIV). Enhanced vaccines, such as the adjuvanted influenza vaccine (aIV) and high-dose (HD-IV) influenza vaccine, have demonstrated clinical and economic benefits. This analysis aimed to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of replacing sIV by aIV in adults aged 60y and older and in high‑risk adults aged 50–59y in the Netherlands. A static decision-tree model was adapted to the Dutch setting to assess the associated costs and health outcomes. The model simulated a historic single average influenza season during 2013–2020 from a societal perspective. Influenza-specific data were used to estimate symptomatic incidence, general practitioner (GP) visits, hospitalizations and excess mortality. Recent publications on aIV’s effectiveness that met the inclusion criteria provided a set of non-randomized real-world data. These studies provided a relative vaccine effectiveness (rVE) range that approximated the 10–30% rVE reported by the Dutch Health Council in a recent assessment; this range was analyzed in the economic model. Sensitivity analyses assessed uncertainty, including varying the rVE over a broader range. Applying an rVE range of 10–30%, ~1,571–4,713 GP visits, 498–1,494 hospitalizations, and 347–1,041 deaths could have been avoided. These health benefits translated into €4.3–12.8 million cost savings and 2,220–6,659 quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) gained. The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) was estimated at €2,144–11,903/QALY. Based on economic modeling, replacing sIV with aIV is suggested to be a cost-effective strategy, with ICERs below the commonly accepted willingness-to-pay threshold of €50,000/QALY gained.KEYWORDS: Influenza, vaccination, cost-effectiveness, older adults, adjuvanted vaccine, the Netherlands

Introduction

Influenza is a common seasonal viral infection that poses a significant burden on public health and society each year.1 This viral respiratory disease is transmitted between individuals and causes illness of varying severity, particularly affecting older adults and individuals with underlying medical conditions such as chronic heart disease, diabetes or lung conditions.2 The most common influenza types in humans are type A, specifically the H1N1 and H3N2 subtypes, and type B, comprising Victoria and Yamagata lineages.3 Typically, one or more of these strains dominate each season.

Although many cases are asymptomatic or do not require medical care, the overall impact of influenza remains substantial. In the 2022/2023 season, approximately 837,000 individuals in the Netherlands experienced symptomatic influenza, and around 169,000 individuals consulted a general practitioner (GP for influenza-like illness (ILI).4 In 2022 alone, influenza led to more than 7,000 hospitalizations, and excess mortality during epidemic weeks was 6,683.4,5 These figures highlight the continued pressure of influenza on vulnerable populations and healthcare systems, despite the presence of a national influenza prevention program. Currently within this program, individuals aged 60 y and older, as well as those at increased risk of severe influenza-related complications (high-risk) are invited by their GP to receive vaccination.2 This high-risk group includes individuals with chronic cardiovascular, pulmonary or renal disease, diabetes mellitus or immunocompromising conditions. In 2023, the average influenza vaccination coverage among the invited group was 55.2%.6

The standard vaccine used in this program is the quadrivalent standard-dose influenza vaccine, which offers protection against the two most common influenza A subtypes and both B lineages. Due to the disappearance of the Yamagata lineage in recent years, the trivalent influenza vaccine is reimplemented in 2025.2 While this adjustment reflects adaptability to current epidemiological trends, further optimization of vaccine effectiveness remains necessary, particularly for vulnerable groups. In 2024, the Health Council of the Netherlands (Gezondheidsraad) advised the Ministry of Health to consider the use of enhanced vaccines, either with a higher antigen dose or the addition of an adjuvant, for older adults and individuals at increased risk.7 However, despite this recommendation, enhanced vaccines have not yet been introduced into the Dutch national influenza prevention program.

The implementation of enhanced vaccines is particularly relevant for the older population due to immunosenescence.8 Increasing the antigen dose in the vaccine, or adding an immune-response booster (MF-59) as an adjuvant to the formulation, can improve immunogenicity.9 As a result, for enhanced vaccines, vaccine effectiveness is observed to improve relative to standard influenza vaccines, providing greater protection against influenza, especially in this age group.7,9,10

Severe influenza illness in older adults frequently leads to functional decline, increased frailty, and a higher likelihood of requiring post-care services such as rehabilitation, home nursing, or institutionalization in nursing homes.11 These consequences place considerable pressure on the healthcare system, particularly in the context of recurrent hospital capacity and workforce shortages during the winter months in the Netherlands, underlining the importance of effective vaccination.

In several countries, cost-effectiveness analyses have been conducted to assess the potential value of adjuvanted influenza vaccines (aIV). Two reviews gathering economic evidence worldwide conclude that aIV is a cost-effective alternative to standard vaccines in older adults.12,13 However, no economic evaluation has been performed for the Netherlands. Therefore, the objective of this study is to provide a cost-effectiveness analysis of aIV compared to standard (dose) influenza vaccine (sIV) in the Dutch setting, targeting individuals aged 60 y and older, as well as high-risk individuals aged 50–59 y, as is in line with its registration.14 This target population aligns with the recent recommendations of the Dutch Health Council.7 This study aims to support decision-making on influenza vaccine selection, by providing an economic perspective to complement clinical evidence, within the context of a rapidly evolving landscape of long-established vaccines, more recently registered enhanced vaccines and novel vaccines that are in the pipeline.

Methods

Model overview/design

The analysis used a static decision tree model that was previously applied to Spain in Microsoft Excel®, which was also used to create all figures.15 Country adaptations were conducted to fit it to the Dutch context. A retrospective analysis was performed, simulating an average influenza season over the 2013–2020 period, assuming this average season to be predictive of future seasons. Because individual seasons differ in vaccine match, circulating strain mix and resulting vaccine effectiveness, introducing uncertainty, modeling was performed at the level of an average season rather than a single season or strain-specific scenario. A societal perspective was adopted to capture not only direct healthcare costs but also indirect costs, including productivity losses from disease-related absenteeism. Relevant outcomes and costs of two influenza vaccine strategies were compared: sIV (current strategy) and aIV.

An overview of the model is presented in Figure 1, based on the original modeling study, but adapted to the Dutch context.15 In accordance with the Dutch vaccination strategy and recommendations of the Dutch Health Council, the target population includes individuals aged 60 y and older and those aged 50–59 y with medical conditions. Progression of symptomatic influenza was estimated through incidence rates. Although the model distinguishes between non-medically attended (NMA) cases, GP-attended cases, hospitalizations and potential deaths, these are not mutually exclusive health states. Case numbers were estimated with incidence rates derived from real-world data.16–18 As individuals may have progressed through multiple states, the totals do not sum up to the total number of symptomatic influenza cases.16,19 For both vaccination strategies, outcomes were generated in terms of quality-adjusted life year (QALY) losses, life years (LY) lost and direct and indirect costs. The final outcome of the analysis was the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER), to assess the relative cost-effectiveness of the two strategies. The results were evaluated against a willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold of €50,000 per QALY gained.20

Target population

Age-specific population totals were obtained from Dutch national statistics for the years 2013 to 2020, and the mean value over this period was applied in the model.21

The percentage of individuals aged 50–59 y at high risk for influenza infection was estimated using data from the Dutch influenza monitor, which reports on influenza vaccination within a sample of GP practices, and is considered representative of the Dutch population.22 Based on this report, 8.6% of individuals aged 0–59 y were identified as having a medical condition associated with increased influenza risk. To distribute risk appropriately across ages, age-specific prevalence data of chronic conditions were applied as a proxy, in line with expert opinion.23 Within the 0–59 age group, individuals aged 50–59 y accounted for 39.8% of all chronic conditions. Using population data from 2024, the resulting proportion of high-risk individuals in the 50–59 age group was calculated to be 17.8% (see Supplementary Table S1).21

Clinical input

Symptomatic influenza infections over the 2013–2020 seasons were included in the analysis (Supplementary Table S2).16,19 GP consultations were estimated by multiplying the incidence of seeking a GP while experiencing ILI by the percentage of influenza positive ILI cases at the GP.16,17 An age-dependent average over the years 2011–2017 was applied uniformly for all years in the model. This average was then divided by the average number of symptomatic influenza cases, resulting in consultation rates of 28.2% for individuals aged 45–65 y and 43.7% for those aged 65 y and older (see Supplementary Tables S3–5). Hospitalization and mortality probabilities were based on a modeling study using Dutch national surveillance data, which incorporated epidemiological data on vaccine effectiveness and epidemic size over an 11 y period (between 2003 and 2015).18 Additionally, age distributions derived from this modeling study were used to redistribute symptomatic influenza incidence, as incidence data were only available for the 45–64 and 65+ age groups. Specifically, incidence in the 45–64 age group was reallocated across the 50–59-y-old high-risk subgroup and the 60–64, 65–74, and 75+ age groups (see Supplementary Tables S6–8).

Vaccine characteristics

Only the relative difference in effectiveness between aIV and sIV was modeled explicitly, with the incidence data already implicitly reflecting the effectiveness of the sIV. With various countries already using aIV in the immunization program, real-world data on effectiveness was available, including one pragmatic-randomized and various non-randomized studies. To estimate relative vaccine effectiveness (rVE) for aIV compared to sIV, studies were included according to specific inclusion criteria.

Included studies met the following inclusion criteria: conducted in outpatient-only or various healthcare settings, comparing aIV with sIV (excluding studies comparing aIV with, for example, high-dose influenza vaccine), included influenza of any type, and enrolled a sizable population (defined as >10,000 individuals) (see Supplementary table S9 for an overview of the studies). The first criterium, by excluding hospital-based studies, warrants best alignment of the studies with the model design starting in the non-hospital setting. These studies relied on diagnostic coding rather than laboratory-confirmed influenza, which may introduce outcome misclassification. Studies using laboratory-confirmed outcomes with test-negative designs were based on smaller cohorts and reported wide confidence intervals due to limited sample sizes. Due to substantial heterogeneity in study designs, populations, and outcome definitions, no formal meta-analysis was conducted in previous reviews, which was therefore also not undertaken for this analysis.9

Reported rVE values in the selected studies ranged from 7.5% to 36.3% and were all based on non-randomized real-world evidence. These values approximated the range of 10–30%, as broadly assessed by the Dutch Health Council in a recent report on enhanced influenza vaccines.7 This range for rVE was used in the model on preventing symptomatic influenza cases, with downstream consequences for general practitioner visits, hospitalizations, and influenza-related mortality. Although the included studies enrolled adults ≥ 65 y, the reported rVE evidence was assumed to apply to our full target population, as no rVE evidence was available for younger age groups. Additionally, protection was assumed to last one season, since the underlying rVE studies measured effectiveness over a full influenza season and any within-season decline in protection is therefore already reflected in the reported values.

The price of the sIV is determined through national procurement procedures and remains confidential. For the purpose of this analysis, a unit cost of €8 per dose was assumed, excluding administration costs, based on a previous cost-effectiveness study.24 The price of aIV was set at €20 per dose, as provided directly by the manufacturer.

An average vaccine coverage rate over the 2013–2020 seasons was used, corresponding to the period for which symptomatic incidence data were included in the model (see Supplementary table S10). For the population aged 50–59 y with a medical condition, the same vaccine coverage rate as that in the 60–64 age group was assumed. This assumption was made because only an overall coverage rate for individuals aged 0–59 y with a medical condition was available – except for the year 2013, when the observed coverage in the 50–59 age group with medical condition was higher than that in the overall 0–59 age group and indeed similar to the coverage in the 60–64 age group.25

With regard to adverse events for the different vaccines, the safety profiles of adjuvanted and non-adjuvanted vaccines are generally found to be similar and are therefore not further addressed in this analysis.9,26

Costs and utilities

Costs and utilities can be found in Supplementary tables S11 and S12. All costs were inflated to 2025 euros using the consumer price index.27 No discounting was applied on costs, since they did not extend beyond the one modeled influenza season (week 40 through week 20 the following year).16 Lifetime QALY and LY losses were discounted with an annual rate of 1.5%, in accordance with Dutch guidelines.28

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the influence of individual parameters on the results, a deterministic sensitivity analysis (DSA) was conducted and results were presented in a tornado diagram. Due to the absence of 95% confidence intervals around most parameters, the upper and lower bounds for this analysis were set at ±20% of the input values. In the DSA, a conservative rVE of 10% is applied, and rVE was also varied across the range as reported in Supplementary table S9 within the scenario analyses. Additionally, a probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) was performed to account for uncertainty across all parameters, for both bounds of the 10–30% rVE range. The distributions and corresponding alpha and beta values used to determine the PSA input, are presented in Table 1. A Monte Carlo simulation with 10,000 iterations was conducted. Results of the PSA were presented using a cost-effectiveness plane to visualize the distribution of incremental costs and effects, and a cost-effectiveness acceptability curve (CEAC) to illustrate the probability of the intervention being cost-effective across a range of WTP thresholds.

Scenarios

To assess the robustness of the model and explore the impact of varying epidemiological and economic assumptions, a series of scenario-based sensitivity analyses was conducted for both bounds of the rVE range. These scenarios reflected variations in incidence, population characteristics, vaccine effectiveness, and economic modeling approaches.

The following were evaluated concerning epidemiology: (1.1) Effectiveness on influenza-like illness (ILI): vaccine effectiveness was assumed to apply to all ILI cases, rather than only influenza-specific incidence, as the rVE estimates were based on diagnostic coding rather than lab-confirmed outcomes. For this purpose, ILI incidence, respiratory-related hospitalizations and in-hospital respiratory deaths were used (see Supplementary table S13). (1.2) Mild influenza season: the 2013/2014 season, representing the lowest influenza activity between 2013 and 2020, was used to model symptomatic incidence (see Supplementary table S14)16. (1.3) Severe influenza season: the 2017/2018 season, with the highest recorded influenza activity, was used to reflect a high-burden scenario (see Supplementary table S15).30 (1.4) Case-fatality ratios (CFRs): age-specific CFRs following symptomatic infection were applied, based on a retrospective modeling study estimating influenza-associated mortality in the Netherlands from 2011 to 2021 using surveillance and excess mortality data (see Supplementary S16 and S17).31

Regarding the target group, two scenarios were specified: (2.1) 60+ only: the vaccination target was restricted to solely the population aged 60 y and older, by excluding the high-risk group aged 50–59 y. Hospitalization costs were adjusted accordingly, see Supplementary table S18. (2.2) 50–59 high-risk only: the target population was restricted to high-risk individuals aged 50–59. Hospitalization costs for this age group were assumed to equal the costs of the 45–64 age group, see Supplementary table S18.

For vaccine effectiveness (rVE), beyond the range of 10–30%, two further extreme scenarios were investigated: (3.1) High rVE: a 63% rVE was applied, reflecting the upper bound of laboratory-confirmed effectiveness estimates (see Supplementary table S9). This allows for the assessment of outcomes under more optimistic vaccine effectiveness assumptions. (3.2) Low rVE: a 2.5% rVE was applied, which represents the lowest value of laboratory-confirmed effectiveness estimates (see Supplementary table S9). This allows for exploration of outcomes under less optimistic vaccine effectiveness assumptions.

Finally, specific scenarios were evaluated concerning the economic assumptions and context: (4.1) Payer perspective: costs and outcomes were evaluated from the perspective of the healthcare payer instead of the societal perspective applied in the base case. (4.2) No discounting: future costs and health outcomes were not discounted. (4.3) Indirect medical costs: indirect medical costs associated with extended life expectancy resulting from influenza vaccination were included.32 The discounted medical indirect costs used per age group are available in Supplementary table S19.

Budget impact analysis

In addition to the cost-effectiveness analysis, a budget impact analysis (BIA) was performed to assess the financial implications of replacing sIV with aIV within the population at high-risk aged 50–59 y and the population aged 60 y and older over a 1-y period, specifically in 2026. The BIA assumed 100% market penetration of aIV from the start of implementation, representing a complete replacement of sIV. The BIA was conducted from the payer perspective, following the ISPOR Principles of Good Practice for Budget Impact Analysis.33

Instead of conducting a retrospective analysis, this analysis aimed to project future costs by incorporating forward-looking population estimates. These were based on publicly available demographic projections from Statistics Netherlands for 2026.34 A constant vaccination coverage rate was applied across all years, using the 2023 coverage level as a reference.35 Vaccine acquisition costs, influenza incidence, and rVE were aligned with the assumptions used in the CEA. Specifically, two rVE scenarios (10% and 30%) were modeled. The analysis included direct medical costs related to influenza-associated hospitalizations and GP visits. Total annual costs were calculated for both vaccination strategies, and the incremental budget impact was reported.

Validation

To ensure the reliability and transparency of the adapted cost-effectiveness model, validation was conducted using the TECH-VER checklist – a structured framework for technical verification of health economic models.36,37 This process included verifying model inputs, testing calculation consistency, checking the patient flow in the model, detecting potential errors in the calculation and presentation of outcomes, reviewing the scenario and sensitivity analyses, and assessing overall model performance. All assumptions, inputs and calculations were critically evaluated to ensure that the model outputs aligned with expected results and that the model performed as intended. Key parameters and assumptions were cross-checked against published literature and national data sources. Additionally, expert consultation was used to assess the clinical relevance and consistency of the model structure, input and outcomes. Reporting of this study followed the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS) statement.38

Results

Base case

The results of the base case cost-effectiveness analysis are presented in Table 2. Clinical outcomes, costs and health effects are presented for the current situation under sIV and the alternative scenario under aIV, for both bounds of the applied rVE range. Additionally, incremental effects are presented for each outcome. Replacing sIV with aIV, could avoid a number of symptomatic infections, GP visits, hospitalizations and influenza-related deaths. This results in lower healthcare costs, and less QALYs and LYs lost due to influenza illness in one season. Vaccination costs however increase, due to the higher price of the vaccine. In one average season, incremental costs add up to €26,420,287, and a total of 2,220 QALYs gained for the base case at the lower bound rVE of 10%. This results into an ICER of €11,903 per QALY. The results of the DSA are presented in the tornado diagram in Figure 2. The parameter with the greatest impact on the ICER is the price of aIV. Varying this price by ±20%, the ICER remains below the WTP threshold, indicating that implementing aIV continues to be a possible cost-effective strategy when the price is higher than the estimation applied in the base case analysis. The results of the PSA are presented in a cost-effectiveness plane in Figure 3 and a cost-effectiveness acceptability curve in Figure 4. Scenario analysis

The results of the scenario analyses are presented in Table 3, expressed in terms of the ICER for an rVE of 10% and 30%. As expected, replacing sIV with aIV is more cost-effective in a severe season than in a mild one, due to the larger number of influenza cases that can be prevented. Similarly, assuming broader or higher vaccine effectiveness can prevent influenza cases, improve cost-effectiveness, and may even result in cost savings. Notably, when a scenario is reported as ‘dominant,’ this indicates that the intervention is both more effective and less costly than the comparator. The scenarios on the target population indicate that the greatest benefits are observed in the older population, although the exclusion of the 50–59-y-old age group barely impacts the ICER.

Budget impact analysis

The projected costs for the 2026 influenza season, including both vaccination and medical care (GP-visits and hospitalizations) are presented in Figure 5. When using sIV, total costs (including vaccination and medical care) were estimated at €163.9 million, of which €74.8 million was attributable to vaccination, including vaccine acquisition and administration costs. The use of aIV increased vaccination expenditures to €113.9 million, representing an additional €39.1 million compared to sIV. However, this higher investment led to reductions in medical costs, resulting in net savings of €6.1 million, assuming a 10% rVE, and €18.5 million, assuming a 30% rVE. Ergo, the use of aIV instead of sIV resulted in a net cost difference ranging from an additional €33.0 million (with 10% rVE) to €20.6 million (with 30% rVE).

Discussion

This study provides a cost-effectiveness analysis of aIV vs. sIV in the Netherlands, targeting individuals aged 60 y and older as well as high-risk individuals aged 50–59 y, in line with recent recommendations of the Dutch Health Council.7 The analysis demonstrated that replacing sIV with aIV for these target groups in an average influenza season during 2013–2020, would have likely been cost-effective at a WTP threshold of €50,000 per QALY gained. Specifically, using an rVE between 10 and 30%, the ICERs of €2144 to €11,903 per QALY gained remain well below this threshold. Results from the PSA confirm the robustness of these findings, indicating a 100% probability that aIV is cost-effective at this threshold. Even when lowering the threshold to €20,000 per QALY, the probabilities remain equal to or close to 100%.

The cost-effectiveness outcomes are largely driven by the older population, as illustrated by scenario analyses 2.1 and 2.2. The ICER is more favorable for the 60+ group (€2,125 – €11,334) than for the 50–59-y-old high-risk subgroup (€5,288–€70,701). Including both groups in the base case only has a limited effect on the ICER (€2,144–€11,903). The 50–59-y-old high-risk population represents a small subgroup of the total, with lower uptake than the older population, and therefore proportionally contributes little to the incremental costs and QALYs, leaving the overall ratio largely unchanged.

The more favorable ICER in the 60+ group is largely explained by the assumed influenza-associated mortality rate, particularly in individuals aged 75 y and older. In this study, results from a modeling study based on Dutch national surveillance data covering the period 2003–2015 were applied, where the probability of death following symptomatic infection in the 75 y and older age group was 17.16%.18 This estimate is substantially higher than the case-fatality rate of 1.76% reported in a more recent retrospective modeling study that assessed influenza-associated mortality in the Netherlands between 2011 and 202131. Applying this lower mortality rate (scenario 1.4) resulted in a significant decrease of mortality cases compared to the base case. At an rVE of 10%, mortality was 573 for sIV and 523 for aIV, with the ICER increasing from €11,903 in the base case to €86,148 in this scenario. At an rVE of 30%, mortality was 573 under sIV and 463 under aIV, resulting in an ICER increase from €2,144 base case to €15,783 per QALY gained in this scenario. Despite the uncertainty surrounding mortality estimates, the total number of deaths under sIV in the base case analysis (5,445) aligns with excess mortality data from Dutch influenza seasons between 2013 and 2020, which ranged from 400 to 9,400 deaths.39 This suggests that, while mortality assumptions are a key driver of cost-effectiveness outcomes, the total number of deaths estimated in the base case analysis remains consistent with observed national data.

Another main driver of the outcome is the incremental price of aIV compared to sIV. Given that vaccine prices in the Netherlands are determined through tender procedures and may deviate from the cost estimates applied in this analysis, it was essential to vary this input in sensitivity analyses. Adjusting the incremental price between €8 and €16 in the DSA (€12 as the base case) resulted in ICERs of €7,023 and €16,782 per QALY gained, respectively. These findings show that even at the higher price, the adoption of aIV remains below the willingness-to-pay threshold.

In addition to incidence and vaccine price, rVE is an important driver of the cost-effectiveness analysis. Although the Dutch Health Council has broadly assessed the rVE of enhanced influenza vaccines to be in the range of 10–30%, the underlying evidence is heterogeneous and includes studies with methodological limitations.7,9 This uncertainty should be considered when interpreting cost-effectiveness outcomes. Nevertheless, even with an rVE below 10%, aIV may still be cost-effective in the Netherlands compared to sIV. Specifically, even an rVE as low as 2.5% (scenario 3.2) results in an ICER that only slightly exceeds the commonly accepted Dutch threshold of €50,000 per QALY gained (€55,817/QALY). Conversely, if the rVE is higher than the suggested range, the intervention could become cost-neutral or even cost-saving, as shown in scenario 3.1 with a rVE of 63%.

In the context of enhanced influenza vaccines for adults aged 60 y and older, the high-dose influenza vaccine (HD-IV) is also indicated and recommended by the Dutch Health Council, as it demonstrated effectiveness across multiple studies, in observational research and meta-analyses, as well as randomized trials.9,40–42 Mostly based on non-randomized real-world comparisons, national immunization technical advisory groups (NITAGS), including the Health Council of the Netherlands, the Joint Committee on Vaccination and Immunization (JCVI) in the United Kingdom and the Standing Committee on Vaccination (STIKO) in Germany consider aIV and HD-IV to be broadly comparable in terms of rVE, and to have shown improved effectiveness compared to sIV in this older population.43 With regard to safety, both enhanced vaccines have similar profiles to sIV; the latest ECDC systematic literature review found no significant differences in serious adverse events.9 In this analysis, a comparison between aIV and HD-IV was not performed due to differences in study design, outcome measures and the absence of head-to-head trials, meaning that the available evidence is too heterogeneous to support a direct comparison. However, HD-IV was also recently estimated to be cost-effective in the Dutch setting.24

Cost-effectiveness provides only one component in the health-technology assessment of influenza vaccines, alongside analyses targeting formal evidence synthesis: interpreting and valuing the clinical evidence and societal preferences. Additionally, it is important to note that current economic and other analyses of influenza vaccines occur in a highly dynamic field, including various existing types of enhanced vaccines as well as several emerging vaccines that are still under development.9,44

Based on the performed BIA, the total national budget allocated for the acquisition of sIV in the Netherlands in 2026 is estimated at €26 million. The adoption of aIV would increase this expenditure to €65 million, resulting in an incremental cost of €39 million, which is a 52% increase relative to the current vaccination budget. However, when accounting for medical cost reductions due to reduced influenza complications, the net budget is substantially lower. Depending on the assumed rVE, the remaining difference between sIV and aIV ranges from €33 million (rVE = 10%) to €21 million (rVE = 30%). The additional €39 million required for aIV represents only 0.036% of the total healthcare expenditure in 2026 (€107.4 billion), underscoring the relatively small investment of implementing aIV within the national influenza prevention program for those aged 60 y and older and the 50–59 y olds at high-risk.45 Although the additional investment is not entirely compensated by reductions in medical expenditures, it may still be justified from a public health perspective, given the potential for improved outcomes relative to sIV. Furthermore, vaccine procurement through national tendering processes often results in substantial price reductions, making the actual budget impact significantly lower, and in certain scenarios, the intervention may even be cost-neutral or cost-saving.

The findings of this study are consistent with previous cost-effectiveness studies conducted in Europe.15,46–49 A systematic literature review from 2024 identified six studies comparing aIV and sIV in older populations, all concluding that aIV was cost-effective.12 Another systematic literature review reported that enhanced (both high-dose and adjuvanted) vaccines are cost-effective alternatives to sIV in adults aged ≥ 65 y, based on 31 publications of varying designs, perspectives and modeling approaches.13

Next to the uncertainties regarding the incidence, vaccine price, and rVE, there are some study limitations. First, due to data gaps, certain assumptions had to be made. For instance, no data were available on the duration of illness for NMA cases; therefore, the duration of mild GP-attended cases was used, which may be an under- or overestimation of the actual value. Similarly, no disutility values were available for influenza, so utility decrements from other respiratory diseases (CA-ARTI and CAP) were applied. Second, the results are based on cases averted, disregarding a potential larger societal or public health impact, besides the reduction in influenza-related healthcare utilization. For instance, informal care was not included in the analysis. Due to the lack of Dutch data on this topic, it could not be incorporated, despite its potential relevance from a societal perspective. Evidence from the United Kingdom suggests that informal caretakers take time off work to care for ill elderly individuals, indicating productivity losses not captured in this analysis.50 Additionally, the increased burden on the healthcare system, particularly in long-term care facilities such as nursing homes, resulting from premature functional decline caused by influenza, was not incorporated.11 Finally, herd immunity was not included in this study. A reduction in symptomatic influenza cases can decrease community-level transmissions, thereby indirectly enhancing the overall effectiveness of the vaccine. Incorporating these effects could have improved the ICER even further.51

Conclusion

In the rapidly evolving landscape of influenza vaccines, both clinical and economic evidence is essential for informed decision-making regarding vaccine selection within national immunization programs. This economic analysis indicated that, in the Netherlands, replacing sIV with aIV in individuals aged 60 y and older, as well as in high-risk individuals aged 50–59 y, is likely to be a cost-effective strategy. Using a WTP threshold of €50,000, the probability of being cost-effective was 100%. This study illustrates that, for the case of aIV, implementation of enhanced vaccines in the national vaccination program could prevent a substantial number of influenza-related GP visits, hospitalizations and deaths, as well as provide economic benefits.
