Based on the middle ear negative pressure and multimodal data to construct and externally validate the predictive model for pediatric obstructive sleep apnea
Authors: Simin Zhu, Yewen Shi, Yanuo Zhou, Lina Ma, Yonglong Su, Zitong Wang, Chendi Lu, Zine Cao, Xiaoxin Niu, Yushan Xie, Zihan Xia, Huanhuan Chang, Yuqi Yuan, Jiayi Yang, Rui Lu, Haiqin Liu, Xiaoyong Ren, Wei Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe aim of this study was to analyze negative middle ear pressure in children with obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) and establish and evaluate predictive models according to these findings.MethodsThis retrospective study involved 931 children: 715 with OSA and 216 controls. Demographic, clinical, lateral head radiograph, and tympanometry data were collected. These characteristics of children with OSA were analyzed, with a particular focus on exploring the value of middle ear-related parameters for the diagnosis of pediatric OSA. Additionally, a logistic regression model incorporating optimal indicators was developed to predict pediatric OSA. The model was visualized via a nomogram and evaluated for discrimination, calibration, clinical effectiveness and external validation.ResultsChildren with OSA were younger and exhibited longer soft palates, larger tonsils and adenoids than non-OSA children. Additionally, children with OSA presented higher acoustic admittance (AC) and resonance frequency (RF), lower middle ear pressure (MEP), and narrower pressure gradient (PG) than non-OSA children. The external auditory canal volume (ECV), MEP, and PG were identified as independent predictors of childhood OSA. We constructed a foundational prediction model for childhood OSA (Model 0, AUC = 0.845, 95% CI: 0.813–0.878), and then added each tympanometric indicator to the model individually. After incorporating MEP into the model (Model 4), the AUC increased by 0.022 (p < 0.05).ConclusionsBased on a large sample size and multivariate analysis of factors associated with pediatric OSA, we developed a predictive model incorporating middle ear negative pressure for pediatric OSA, which may assist clinicians in diagnosing pediatric OSA in complex clinical settings.

Introduction

Pediatric obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a common sleep disorder affecting individuals aged 2–18 years, with a prevalence of approximately 1–6% [1]. Unlike adult patients, children with OSA present with distinct symptoms such as hyperactivity, emotional disturbances, decreased academic performance, and poor concentration, therefore, pediatric OSA is often overlooked [2]. Furthermore, pediatric OSA can lead to various serious complications, including growth retardation, cognitive deficits, craniofacial structural abnormalities, and otitis media with effusion (OME) [3, 4].

Risk factors for pediatric OSA include obesity, race, prematurity, craniofacial anomalies, and socioeconomic status [5], among which adenotonsillar hypertrophy is the most common predisposing factor [5]. Chronic infections caused by adenotonsillar hypertrophy may lead to eustachian tube edema and dysfunction, thereby increasing children’s susceptibility to OME [6]. Middle ear pressure reflects eustachian tube function, which shares neuromuscular regulatory mechanisms with upper airway patency through the activity of the tensor veli palatini muscle. Consequently, airway collapse during sleep in children with obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) may be associated with negative middle ear pressure via this shared pathway [7]. Gozal et al. reported a significant association between habitual snoring and recurrent otitis media but did not analyze the relationship between pediatric OSA and middle ear negative pressure or acoustic compliance [8]. Sadia et al. quantified middle ear negative pressure to assess eustachian tube dysfunction and investigated its correlation with OSA; however, their sample size was limited to 34 children with OSA [7]. There remains a lack of large-scale studies on the relationship between pediatric OSA and middle ear function.

Craniofacial disorders are another significant risk factor for pediatric OSA, yet OSA may also be associated with abnormalities in craniofacial development [9, 10]. Previous studies have shown that children who frequently breathe through their mouths often exhibit facial features such as protrusion, thickened lips, and a dull expression commonly referred to as “adenoid facies“ [11]. Our previous studies have also identified craniofacial characteristics associated with pediatric OSA [12]. Additionally, as part of the eustachian tube consists of bony structures, craniofacial structural abnormalities may serve as a common risk factor for both pediatric OSA and ear diseases [13–15].

Currently, pediatric OSA diagnosis relies on polysomnography (PSG), a procedure requiring specialized laboratories, multiple channels, and overnight parental supervision [16]. These requirements make PSG complex, costly, and poorly tolerated by children, limiting its widespread adoption [17]. Therefore, developing a low-cost and convenient diagnostic method for OSA can help alleviate the burden of pediatric OSA on healthcare systems, especially in areas with very limited medical resources [18]. Considerable research has focused on developing alternative methods for pediatric OSA screening. These approaches include questionnaire-based tools, such as the OSA-18 and Pediatric Sleep Questionnaire, which have shown only moderate diagnostic accuracy (AUCs 0.57–0.69) and cannot yet replace PSG. Anatomical assessments, including tonsillar grading and the ColTon index, have demonstrated predictive potential (AUC up to 0.89) but face challenges in clinical applicability due to measurement complexity. More recently, machine learning approaches utilizing physiological signals—such as nocturnal oximetry, heart rate variability, and electrocardiography—have emerged as promising non-invasive screening tools [18]. Despite these advancements, a simple, low-cost, and easily accessible method that integrates multiple clinical parameters for routine pediatric OSA screening remains an unmet clinical need. Predictive models have been widely used to diagnose various diseases and complications. Our team has previously developed predictive models for adult OSA and its complications [19].

Methods

Patients and materials

This study is a retrospective study. From July 2017 to February 2024, 1,565 children aged 2–12 years with suspected OSA were treated at the Department of Otolaryngology—Head and Neck Surgery at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University. Written informed consent, demographic characteristics, and clinical symptoms were obtained from the medical records signed by the children’s legal guardians. After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 931 children were ultimately included in the training set. Additionally, 353 children aged 2–12 years with suspected OSA treated at Xi’an Children’s Hospital from October 2023 to May 2024 were included in the external validation set. All the children underwent PSG, lateral head radiograph examination, and acoustic impedance testing. Prior to testing, informed consent was obtained from each participant’s guardian. The study flowchart is shown in Fig. 1. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) aged 2–12 years and suspected of having OSA; (2) underwent PSG examination; (3) underwent lateral head radiograph examination; and (4) underwent acoustic impedance testing. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) did not have baseline data; (2) had craniofacial diseases; (3) had middle ear diseases other than OME, such as external auditory canal cholesteatoma, tympanic membrane trauma and congenital ear deformities; (4) had muscular or neurological disorders, diagnosed as congenital abnormalities of craniofacial structure or neuromuscular regulation.

Polysomnography evaluation

The obstructive apnea-hypopnea index (OAHI) was used to assess the severity of sleep-disordered breathing. OAHI is defined as the number of obstructive apneas, hypopneas, and mixed apneas per hour during sleep [20]. All participants underwent PSG monitoring at the Sleep Center of the Department of Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University (Computer medics E-series from Computer medics Inc., Abbotsford, Australia). Certified clinical PSG experts evaluated all the records.

Orthophos X-ray equipment

Orthophos X-ray equipment OC-100 (Instrumentarium Imaging Company, Finland) was used to obtain a standardized lateral head radiograph examination, with the instrument fixed at 150 cm from the head while the participant stood naturally. The participant’s line of sight was parallel to the ground, and images were taken while the participant refrained from speaking or swallowing.

Acoustic impedance testing

Acoustic impedance testing was performed using the Titan device (Interacoustics, Denmark) equipped with both conventional tympanometry (IMP440 module) and wideband tympanometry (WBT440 module) capabilities. Conventional single-frequency (226 Hz) tympanometry was first performed using the positive and negative pressure sweep method (+ 200 to -300 daPa) to obtain external auditory canal volume (ECV), middle ear pressure (MEP), and peak-compensated static acoustic compliance (AC). Subsequently, wideband tympanometry was conducted to measure resonance frequency (RF) and pressure gradient (PG). RF was determined as the frequency at which susceptance crossed zero, indicating the transition from stiffness-dominated to mass-dominated reactance. PG was automatically calculated by the system software as the width of the tympanometric peak at half its height. All measurements were performed according to the manufacturer’s protocols, and the software automatically analyzed and recorded the values.

Statistical analysis

The children were divided into the following groups according to their PSG findings: (1) non-OSA group: OAHI < 1; (2) OSA group: OAHI ≥ 1. The following data were collected from the children: (1) basic information: age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and Z score converted according to local reference values for age and sex, the BMI Z-score was calculated using the formula (subject’s BMI - standard BMI) / SD [21]; (2) OSA-related symptoms: snoring, mouth breathing, and sleep suffocation; (3) clinical signs: tonsil grade (Friedman system); (4) acoustic impedance results: ECV, RF, AC, MEP, and PG; and (5) lateral head radiograph examination: width measured at the most protrusive place of the adenoid (A), width of the skeletal upper airway (N), A/N ratio, anterior skull base length (ASL), length of the soft palate (SPL), size of the maxilla (ANS-PNS), length of the body of the mandible (Go-Me), angle of the skull base (N.S-S.Ba), ASL/Go-Me, ANS-PNS/Go-Me, and ASL/ANS-PNS. Data processing was as follows: (1) Text data cleaning: standardizing the record format; (2) Handling missing values: Variables with > 15% missing data were excluded from analysis. For variables with < 15% missing data, mean imputation was performed. Mean imputation was chosen for its simplicity and to preserve sample size, as the proportion of missing data was low and assumed to be missing completely at random (MCAR). However, we acknowledge that this approach may underestimate variability and weaken observed associations; therefore, all results should be interpreted with this limitation in mind; (3) Handling outliers: detecting and processing outliers or extreme values, removing unrealistic values; (4) Handling duplicate data: detecting and deleting duplicate records or samples.

A comparison of the clinical characteristics of children with and without OSA revealed significant differences in multiple candidate variables. With OSA as the outcome variable, univariate logistic regression was performed on the participants’ clinical characteristics, and statistically significant indicators were included in multivariate logistic regression to identify independent risk factors. The primary predictive model for childhood OSA (Model 0) was constructed based on these independent risk factors. Logistic regression was used to explore the diagnostic value of various acoustic impedance indices for OSA and combine them with Model 0 to develop new models (Model 1–Model 5). Delong’s test was used to compare the performance of each model, and a nomogram was constructed based on the best model. The model underwent external validation and evaluation.

All the statistical analyses were conducted using R Studio (version 4.3.2) and SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables are presented as counts (n) and percentages (%), and groups were compared using chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests. The normality of continuous variables was assessed via the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data were analyzed via independent samples t tests and are presented as the means ± standard deviations. Nonnormally distributed data were analyzed via the Mann–Whitney U test and are presented as medians and quartiles. Correlation analysis was performed via the Pearson and Spearman correlation tests. Significant variables were included as predictors in the model construction after data cleaning and feature variable selection from patient clinical records. A logistic regression model was built to construct the nomogram prediction model. The model was visualized via R Studio. The prediction model’s performance was evaluated through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, calibration plots, the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, and a clinical utility curve to assess its performance. The performance of prediction model was evaluated through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The Youden index helped determine the optimal cut-off point for assessing accuracy, sensitivity and specificity. Additionally, the Akaike information criterion (AIC) was used to gauge model fit and complexity, with a smaller AIC indicating a better model due to its penalty for more independent variables. The prediction model was built using the training set, and the validation set was imported for verification. Binary logistic regression was performed using the ‘lrm’ function in R studio, while the model was visualized using the ‘nomogram’ function in the ‘rms’ package. p < 0.05 was considered to indicate significance.

Results

Demographic characteristics, clinical symptoms, physical signs, and lateral head radiographs between the training and external validation sets

Table 1 displays the demographic characteristics, clinical symptoms, physical signs, and lateral head radiograph feature comparisons between the OSA and non-OSA groups in the training and external validation sets. After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, the training group included 931 eligible children, comprising 715 OSA patients (76.80%) and 216 non-OSA patients (23.20%), with severity stratification as follows: mild OSA: 596 patients (64.02%); moderate OSA: 83 patients (9.09%); severe OSA: 36 patients (3.87%). The external validation set included 353 participants, with 274 OSA patients (77.62%) and 79 non-OSA patients (22.38%). Except for ASL/Go-Me (p = 0.043), no significant differences in the parameters were detected between the training and external validation sets. In the training set, there were 544 boys (58.43%) and 387 girls (41.57%), whereas in the external validation set, there were 199 boys (56.37%) and 154 girls (43.63%), with no significant sex differences between the two groups. As compared to non-OSA children, OSA patients in both the training and validation sets were younger. It is noteworthy that although the median age was consistent between the two groups, the age distribution among children with OSA was narrower. Besides, OSA patients had larger tonsils, and had higher reported rates of snoring (74.07% vs. 98.88%) and mouth breathing (82.87% vs. 97.76%) (p < 0.001), these patients also presented a greater A/N ratio (0.71 vs. 0.79) and a greater SPL value (25.39 vs. 26.37) (p < 0.05). However, in the training set, OSA patients had a significantly greater ANS-PNS/Go-Me ratio (p < 0.05), but this difference was not significant in the validation set (p = 0.081). There were no significant differences between the groups regarding the history of mouth breathing, N.S-S.Ba, ASL, BMI, BMI-Z score, ANS-PNS, Go-Me, ASL/Go-Me ratio, and ASL/ANS-PNS ratio (all p > 0.05).

Results of the acoustic admittance measurements

The acoustic admittance indices in the training and the external validation sets were not significantly different. The external auditory canal volume did not significantly differ between the OSA and non-OSA groups in either the training or external validation sets (Table 2; Fig. 3).

Snoring (r = 0.07), tonsil grade, and duration of apnea were positively correlated with the PG (all p < 0.05), as were the OAHI and ODI (all p < 0.001). The PG was negatively correlated with age and LSaO2 (all p < 0.05) (Fig. 4).

Diagnostic value of acoustic admittance indices for pediatric OSA

After multivariate logistic regression, snoring, mouth breathing, tonsil grade, age, the A/N ratio, and SPL were included as predictive variables to construct the baseline model (Model 0) for predicting pediatric OSA (AUC = 0.845). Subsequently, ECV, RF, AC, MEP, and PG were added individually to Model 0 to create Models 1–5 (with AUC values of 0.847, 0.853, 0.849, 0.867, and 0.851, respectively) (Table 4). We selected variables for the final model using univariate and multivariate logistic regression (Tables 2 and 3). We evaluated Models 0 through 5, with Model 4 showing the highest AUC. In terms of sensitivity, Model 2 performed best at 0.884, followed by Model 3 (0.869) and Model 4 (0.864). Sensitivity in the other models was below 0.800. Model 5 showed the highest specificity (0.778), followed by Model 0 (0.773), Model 1 (0.773), and Model 4 (0.736), while the specificity of the other models was below 0.700 (Table 5). DeLong’s test was used to compare the differences between Models 1–5 and Model 0 (Table 6). Model 4 performed best, with an improvement in the AUC of 0.022 compared with that of Model 0 (p < 0.05).

Discussion

PSG as the gold standard for diagnosing pediatric OSA, faces significant limitations in its widespread adoption. Our study uses tympanometry, a non-invasive method commonly used to screen for ear diseases in children with OSA, combined with clinical and imaging data, to develop a prediction model for diagnosing pediatric OSA. The model performs well in both the training and external validation sets. Furthermore, we found that children with OSA have higher acoustic compliance, higher resonance frequencies, and smaller middle ear pressures and tympanograms.

Acoustic impedance testing (tympanometry) is a non-invasive method for examining middle ear function. It provides multiple indicators, such as middle ear pressure and acoustic compliance, and is commonly used to screen for ear disorders in children with OSA. Our study found that OSA patients have lower middle ear pressure than non-OSA children. Zhang et al. reported that children with OSA exhibit more negative middle ear pressure and higher acoustic compliance, which is consistent with our findings [22], while middle ear pressure increases during CPAP treatment [23]. Adenoid hypertrophy, as a significant pathogenic factor in OSA, is associated with fluctuations in upper airway pressure in children with OSA, transmitting negative pressure to the middle ear via the Eustachian tube [7]. Stuckenbrock et al. reported that OSA patients experience upper airway narrowing or even closure, leading to negative airway pressure and Eustachian tube closure, which impedes air replenishment in the middle ear cavity and results in negative middle ear pressure [24]. Negative middle ear pressure may also facilitate the migration of microbial pathogens to the surface of hypertrophic adenoids, triggering otitis media with effusion and further reducing middle ear pressure [25]. A study using a structural model to simulate oral breathing in pediatric OSA found that both adenoid hypertrophy and negative middle ear pressure are associated with pharyngeal negative pressure observed in OSA, and greater severity of pediatric OSA correlates with more pronounced negative middle ear pressure. Furthermore, enlarged adenoids persistently obstruct the pharynx and generate negative pressure. We suggest that the association between pediatric OSA and negative middle ear pressure may extend beyond sleep periods [7]. It is important to note that negative middle ear pressure is not specific to OSA and may arise from various conditions affecting Eustachian tube function, including upper respiratory infections, adenoid hypertrophy, and otitis media with effusion. Therefore, the observed association between MEP and pediatric OSA likely reflects shared upper airway pathology rather than a direct or specific consequence of OSA. Nevertheless, within a multivariable prediction model that already accounts for adenotonsillar hypertrophy and other clinical features, MEP remains an independent predictor, which supports its additive clinical value.

In this study, children with OSA presented greater RF than non-OSA children. This may be attributed to adenotonsillar hypertrophy and associated changes in mucosal swelling or OME, altering the conditions for middle ear vibration and leading to middle ear sclerosis, which manifests as an increase in middle ear resonance frequency [26, 27]. Additionally, negative middle ear pressure may increase stiffness, thereby elevating the resonance frequency. Kourelis et al. identified RF as the sole sign of adenotonsillar hypertrophy and established 899 Hz as the critical value indicating middle ear disease due to adenotonsillar hypertrophy [27]. This study also revealed that tympanogram width was greater in OSA patients than in non-OSA children, which is similar to the findings of previous studies. Research suggests that pressure gradients may assist in detecting middle ear effusion and may be more sensitive to middle ear effusion than acoustic compliance or middle ear pressure values are [10, 28]. Margolis et al. suggested that PG appears sensitive to mechanical changes associated with disease in the middle ear, which other tympanometric pressure measurements or otoscopic examinations may not always detect [29]. Similar to previous studies, our study also revealed that ECV and acoustic compliance were not independent predictors of pediatric OSA between the two groups.

Children with suspected OSA were classified into OSA and non-OSA groups using PSG as the gold standard. The results revealed significant differences between the two groups in terms of symptoms of snoring and mouth-breathing history, similar to the findings of a previous study [30]. Additionally, children with OSA are younger and have larger tonsils, larger A/N ratios, and greater soft palate lengths than non-OSA children. Adenotonsillar hypertrophy obstructs the upper airway, leading to OSA, whereas increased soft palate length affects breathing and airway stability, potentially increasing the risk of airway collapse during sleep, consistent with the conclusion of Pirilä-Parkkinen et al. [31]. Our study provides further support for this association. We found that children with OSA had a significantly smaller ANS-PNS/Go-Me ratio than non-OSA children. Notably, while the absolute lengths of the maxilla (ANS-PNS) and mandible (Go-Me) did not differ significantly between groups individually, their ratio was significantly reduced in the OSA group. This suggests that the proportional relationship between maxillary and mandibular length rather than their absolute dimensions may be more relevant to OSA pathophysiology. This altered maxillo-mandibular proportion could reflect a relatively shorter maxilla, a relatively longer mandible, or a combination of both, potentially contributing to the retrognathic facial phenotype (midface depression) often observed in children with OSA.

Previous research has indicated a negative correlation between mandible length and the apnea hypopnea index (AHI) [32]. However, this study revealed no significant difference between the two groups in the ANS-PNS or Go-Me. We believe that, compared with the lengths of the maxilla and mandible, facial features in children with OSA primarily exhibit retrusion of the maxilla and changes in the relative positions of the maxilla and mandible. Additionally, the maxilla and mandible form a three-dimensional structure, and we speculate that changes in their spatial structure may be more strongly associated with OSA than individual lengths do. Therefore, attention should be given to the three-dimensional structure of a child’s face in clinical practice.

Correlation analysis revealed that pressure is positively correlated with the average SaO2 and minimum SaO2 and negatively correlated with snoring, the longest apnea duration, mouth breathing, bilateral tonsil grade, the OAHI, and the ODI, indicating a relationship between the severity of OSA and pressure. As OSA severity increased, middle ear pressure decreased; pressure was positively correlated with age, possibly because of gradual adenoid and tonsillar shrinkage as children grow. It should be acknowledged that while some correlation coefficients reached statistical significance, several were small in magnitude. More importantly, despite weak univariate correlations, MEP remained an independent predictor in multivariate analysis and modestly improved model performance.

We identified snoring, mouth breathing, tonsil grade, age, the A/N ratio, and SPL as independent predictors of pediatric OSA. Obesity (BMI), a common risk factor for OSA, was not included in the final model, possibly because adenoid and tonsillar hypertrophy have a greater effect on OSA in children. ECV, RF, AC, MEP, and PG were incorporated into the model, with RF, MEP, and PG identified as independent risk factors for pediatric OSA. Furthermore, the model improved with the inclusion of MEP, increasing the AUC by 0.022 compared with that obtained using the base model. There were differences in model performance between the training and validation sets. We believe this may be due to the fact that the training and validation sets were drawn from two different clinical centers, leading to some differences in the included subjects. Bokov et al. employed a machine learning approach (cforest algorithm) incorporating pharyngeal collapsibility and tonsillar hypertrophy (ColTon index) to predict moderate-to-severe pediatric OSA, achieving an AUC of 0.89. However, the ColTon index measurement process remains clinically challenging due to its complexity [33]. Moreover, compared to ColTon index, our study uses tympanometry to predict pediatric OSA, which significantly reduces the complexity of obtaining diagnostic results.

In this study, we developed a pediatric OSA prediction model based on clinical characteristics, lateral head radiographs, and middle ear pressure that demonstrated excellent performance in guiding OSA diagnosis in children. A previous study indicated that the OSA-18 questionnaire can be used to screen OSA severity; however, its screening capability is relatively weak [34]. Anant et al. used the sleep-related breathing disorder scale of the Pediatric Sleep Questionnaire to predict childhood OSA, with an AUC ranging from 0.57 to 0.69, which is insufficient to replace PSG [35, 36]. While electrocardiography-based models have achieved AUCs of 0.759 for mild OSA, their applicability is constrained by signal acquisition complexity [37]. However, its applicability is limited, and obtaining electrocardiogram signals is not straightforward. Our model development process involved a large sample size, extensive feature selection, and external validation from other hospitals, ensuring broad applicability.

Although the addition of MEP to Model 0 yielded only a modest AUC improvement of 0.022, this incremental gain carries both pathophysiological and clinical significance. To our knowledge, this is the first large-scale study to identify middle ear pressure as an independent predictor of pediatric OSA, linking upper airway obstruction to Eustachian tube dysfunction through shared neuromuscular regulatory mechanisms. Unlike Model 0, which relies primarily on symptomatic and anatomical variables, MEP provides an objective physiological parameter free from observer bias and parental reporting error, thereby strengthening model performance in clinically ambiguous cases. Practically, tympanometry is already a routine, low-cost, and non-invasive test in pediatric settings, so incorporating MEP imposes minimal additional burden. Even a modest improvement in predictive accuracy can yield meaningful clinical benefits when scaled, particularly in primary care facilities or regions with limited PSG access, where each incremental gain may help reduce unnecessary referrals. For children with equivocal symptoms or borderline anatomical findings (e.g., tonsil grade II, A/N ratio near the threshold), MEP can serve as a useful adjunct to clarify the need for PSG referral. Thus, the value of MEP extends beyond its modest contribution to AUC, enhancing the model’s biological plausibility, objectivity, and clinical applicability in specific contexts. Future studies should prospectively assess the cost-effectiveness of this approach and identify patient subgroups most likely to benefit from MEP incorporation.

Our study uses easily accessible clinical, imaging, and tympanometry data to predict pediatric OSA. Compared to the expensive and complex polysomnography (PSG), this model can be developed into a simple online diagnostic tool via a web-based platform, potentially serving as a screening alternative for pediatric OSA in regions with limited healthcare resources. We will expand the sample size, utilize multi-center data, and develop a more accurate pediatric OSA diagnostic model. The logistic model used in this study is a traditional predictive model. Its advantages lie in its simplicity, convenience, and the ability to visualize it through a nomogram, allowing for an intuitive understanding of the predictive value of each variable for pediatric OSA. However, this model typically cannot incorporate too many variables. In recent years, emerging machine learning predictive models have been helpful in handling large sample sizes and multi-variable data. In future work, we plan to combine both approaches. From a clinical perspective, the probability cut-off of 0.78 provides a practical reference for decision-making. For children with a predicted probability ≥ 0.78, the high positive predictive value (89%) supports a high likelihood of OSA, warranting prioritized referral for PSG or consideration of treatment in settings where PSG is unavailable. Conversely, for those with a predicted probability < 0.78, the moderate negative predictive value (68%) suggests that OSA cannot be reliably excluded, and clinical judgment, considering symptom severity and risk factors should guide further management. Clinicians may also adjust the threshold based on their specific clinical context: a lower cut-off could be used to maximize sensitivity (avoiding missed diagnoses), while a higher cut-off could be used to maximize specificity (reducing unnecessary referrals). Future studies should prospectively validate this model and its threshold in diverse clinical settings to establish its utility in real-world decision-making.

However, this study is retrospective with several limitations, including the lack of subgroup analysis on the basis of OSA severity. Only retrospective data were collected, so it cannot establish a causal relationship between pediatric OSA and middle ear pressure. Moreover, the enrolled participants in this study demonstrated a notably higher prevalence of mouth breathing compared to the general population, which may limit the generalizability of our predictive model. Mean imputation was used to handle missing data for variables with < 15% missingness. While this approach preserves sample size and is acceptable when data are missing completely at random, it may introduce bias by underestimating variability and attenuating true associations. Although the proportion of missing data was low, this limitation should be considered when interpreting the strength of the observed predictors. Tympanometric measurements were obtained from a single ear (right ear for bilaterally normal subjects, healthy ear for unilateral disease). Although this standardized approach ensures consistency and avoids confounding by overt pathology, it may introduce bias by assuming symmetry of middle ear function. OSA-related Eustachian tube dysfunction could theoretically affect both ears, and unilateral measurement might underestimate the true association between middle ear function and OSA severity. Future studies should consider bilateral tympanometry to assess inter-ear differences and their relationship with OSA. Moreover, transient conditions like upper respiratory tract infections can affect tympanometry and potentially limit how widely the results can be applied. In future studies, we plan to increase the sample size, follow up with subjects, and conduct prospective research to explore the relationship between the two. Additionally, various factors, such as swallowing and infections, influence tympanometric measurements, which could affect measurement outcomes.

Conclusion

Our study established the predictive value of indicators-middle ear negative pressure and resonance frequency-for pediatric OSA, and incorporated these readily available clinical, radiographic, and acoustic impedance variables into a nomogram model. Based on a large sample size, multivariate analysis, and external validation, this predictive model holds significant clinical application value. It is expected to assist clinicians in diagnosing pediatric OSA in complex clinical settings, particularly in cases where polysomnography (PSG) is not readily accessible.
