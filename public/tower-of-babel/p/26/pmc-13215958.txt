Establishment of severe in vitro and in vivo infection models for coxsackievirus B4 and their application in identifying viral virulence determinants
Authors: Haoyi Liu, Xianglin Xuan, Bizhen Tang, Huan Wang, Li Zhang, Shuyan Qiu, Chuncong Mo, Xiao Li, Xingui Tian, Rong Zhou, Qian Liu, Wenkuan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoxsackievirus B4 (CVB4) is a highly pathogenic enterovirus associated with severe neurological disease and mortality. To establish research models that recapitulate severe CVB4 infection, two clinical isolates with distinct neurovirulence—the high-virulence strain GZ-HFM01 and low-virulence strain GZ-R6—were used. An in vitro neurocytotoxicity model with human neuroblastoma SH-SY5Y cells showed that GZ-HFM01 produced significantly larger plaques than GZ-R6, reflecting its increased capacity to damage neuronal cells. Concurrently, an optimized in vivo severe infection model was established in 3-day-old ICR mice through intraperitoneal inoculation, which reproduced key clinical features of severe disease. Compared with GZ-R6, GZ-HFM01 infection resulted in significantly reduced survival, progressive neurological impairment, time-dependent viral accumulation in brain tissue, pronounced histopathological injury, and elevated serum pro-inflammatory cytokine levels. To map genomic determinants of neurovirulence, a chimeric virus panel was generated by replacing individual genome segments (5′untranslated region [UTR], P1, P2, P3, or 3′UTR) of GZ-HFM01 with the corresponding regions from GZ-R6. Evaluation with the established models demonstrated that replacement of the P2 region significantly attenuated both the cytopathic effect in SH-SY5Y cells and pathogenicity in 3-day-old ICR mice. Animal studies further indicated that substitution of the 5′UTR or P1 region also reduced virulence—a phenotype absent from the cell-based model—underscoring the multifactorial regulation of CVB4 pathogenesis. In conclusion, this study provides validated in vitro and in vivo models of severe CVB4 infection and identifies key genomic segments that contribute to neurovirulence, offering a foundation for mechanistic research and the development of targeted interventions against severe CVB4-induced disease.Keywords: Coxsackievirus B4 (CVB4), Severe infection, Infection model, Neurovirulence, Viral virulence determinant

Introduction

Coxsackievirus B4 (CVB4) is a member of the Enterovirus genus within the Picornaviridae family and constitutes a key pathogen in the Coxsackievirus B group (CVB). Virions are icosahedral, approximately 30 nm in diameter; they encapsidate a single-stranded positive-sense RNA genome of around 7400 nucleotides (Zell et al., 2017; Baggen et al., 2018). Consistent with the typical enterovirus (EV) genome organization, CVB4 harbors a central open reading frame that encodes a large polyprotein, flanked by a 5′ untranslated region (5′UTR) and a 3′UTR. This polyprotein is proteolytically processed by the virus-encoded 2A and 3C proteases into three classes of functional proteins: structural proteins (VP1–VP4) derived from the P1 region, which form the viral capsid; and nonstructural proteins produced from the P2 (2A–2C) and P3 (3A–3D) regions, which provide essential enzymatic activities and regulatory functions for viral replication (Jenkins et al., 1987; Baxter et al., 2006; Zoll et al., 2009; Bengs et al., 2015).

CVB4 primarily infects children and spreads via fecal-oral and respiratory routes, posing a risk of large-scale outbreaks (Nekoua and Hober, 2022; Xiao et al., 2023). After infection, the virus can cross the blood-brain and placental barriers, disseminating to multiple tissues in humans or fetuses through lymphatic, circulatory, or vertical transmission. The pathogenicity of EVs is closely associated with their genomic products (Arita et al., 2008; Cordey et al., 2012; Chang et al., 2018; Ang et al., 2021). CVB4 exhibits substantial genetic diversity (Xiao et al., 2023), which contributes to a broad range of clinical manifestations, including aseptic meningitis (Bissel et al., 2014), hand-foot-mouth disease (HFMD) (Hu et al., 2012; Xiao et al., 2021), acute respiratory tract infections (Hierholzer et al., 1972), viral myocarditis (Dong et al., 2018), acute pancreatitis (Hindersson et al., 2004; De Palma et al., 2009), and type 1 diabetes (Nekoua et al., 2020, 2021). Epidemiological studies indicate that CVB4 is one of the most lethal EVs; severe neurological injury is the primary cause of severe illness and mortality (Porres et al., 1985; Khetsuriani et al., 2006; Hu et al., 2012). A recent clinical study of severe HFMD cases in Yunnan Province, China, showed that CVB4 is the second most prevalent EV serotype in the region, highlighting an urgent need for effective prevention and control strategies (Liu et al., 2023). Despite ongoing progress in the development of CVB4-targeted drugs (Liu et al., 2013; Yen et al., 2014; El Kfoury et al., 2017; Arena et al., 2018; Benkahla et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2023) and vaccines (Stone et al., 2020; Gharbi et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024), no approved products are available, leaving limited preventive and therapeutic options. Thus, comprehensive investigations into CVB4 infection mechanisms and countermeasures remain essential.

In our previous work, two CVB4 strains with distinct neurovirulence phenotypes were isolated from clinical samples: GZ-HFM01 (high neurovirulence), derived from a child with severe HFMD complicated by aseptic meningitis, and GZ-R6 (low neurovirulence), isolated from a child with pneumonia (Dai et al., 2023). These clinically relevant strains with divergent pathogenic profiles provide an ideal experimental system for the systematic investigation of mechanisms underlying CVB4-induced severe infections.

The establishment of appropriate in vitro and in vivo infection models is essential for dissecting viral pathogenesis. Although several animal models have been developed for other Coxsackievirus species—such as an ICR suckling mouse model for CVA4 (Zhang et al., 2019); myocarditis (Arola et al., 1995; You et al., 2025) and diabetes models for CVB3 (Di Lorenzo et al., 2007; Tracy and Drescher, 2007); and neural injury models for CVB3 and CVB5 in BALB/c mice (Wang et al., 2014; Mao et al., 2018)—comprehensive data regarding systematic CVB4 infection models, particularly those that recapitulate the pathogenic features of severe neurological injury, remain limited (Dong et al., 2018; Weng et al., 2025). This gap has substantially hindered progress in CVB4 research.

To establish research models suitable for in-depth investigations of severe CVB4 infection, this study characterized the pathogenic profiles of the high-neurovirulence strain GZ-HFM01 and the low-neurovirulence strain GZ-R6 in multiple cell lines and in ICR mice of various age groups, with the aim of developing in vitro and in vivo systems that mimic clinical pathogenic features. Furthermore, given the notably high neurovirulence of GZ-HFM01, we constructed a series of chimeric viruses using its genome as the backbone, by replacing individual genomic segments (5′UTR, P1, P2, P3, and 3′UTR) with the corresponding regions from GZ-R6. Using the established models, we identified genomic segments associated with CVB4 neurovirulence and evaluated the applicability and limitations of these systems in virulence research. The CVB4 infection models and neurovirulence-related genomic segments identified in this study provide a theoretical basis and technical support for future investigations of CVB4 pathogenesis and virulence regulation, as well as the development of targeted prevention and treatment strategies.

Results

CVB4 exhibited typical clinical infection characteristics in in vitro cell infection models

Two days after HeLa cells were infected with the GZ-HFM01 (high neurovirulence) and GZ-R6 (low neurovirulence) strains, no significant differences in plaque size or replication kinetics were observed between the two strains (P > 0.05) (Fig. 1A–C). Based on this observation, viral particles were quantified using the plaque assay in HeLa cells in this study.

In vitro infection characteristics of high- and low-neurovirulence coxsackievirus B4 strains GZ-HFM01 and GZ-R6 in various cell lines. Plaque formation, plaque size distribution, and proliferation curves of the high- and low-neurovirulence coxsackievirus B4 (CVB4) strains GZ-HFM01 and GZ-R6 were analyzed in HeLa (A–C), SH-SY5Y (D–F), and A549 cells (G–I). For plaque assays, cells in six-well plates were infected, incubated, and stained with crystal violet at 2 days post-infection. Plaque sizes are shown as mean values with standard error of the mean (SEM); they were assessed with the Mann–Whitney U test. Viral genome copies were quantified by CVB4 qPCR on various days post-infection (n = 3). Statistical analysis was conducted using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test. ns, not significant; ∗∗∗∗, P < 0.0001.

When SH-SY5Y and A549 cells were separately inoculated with GZ-HFM01 and GZ-R6, the plaques formed by GZ-HFM01 in SH-SY5Y cells were significantly larger than those formed by GZ-R6 (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 1D and E). In contrast, the plaque diameter of GZ-R6 was significantly larger than that of GZ-HFM01 in A549 cells (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 1G and H). No significant differences in replication kinetics were detected between GZ-HFM01 and GZ-R6 in HeLa, SH-SY5Y, or A549 cells (Fig. 1C, F, I). Notably, in the plaque assay, a similar number of plaques were observed when HeLa and SH-SY5Y cells were inoculated with 20 PFU per well, compared with A549 cells (1000 PFU per well). These findings suggest that HeLa and SH-SY5Y cells are considerably more susceptible to CVB4 than A549 cells, a conclusion supported by proliferation kinetics showing that viral loads reached a plateau earlier in HeLa and SH-SY5Y cells than in A549 cells (Fig. 1C, F, I).

High-neurovirulence CVB4 strain induced severe nerve injury in 3-day-old ICR mice

In this study, GZ-HFM01 and GZ-R6 were administered to 3-day-old, 2-week-old, and 3-week-old ICR mice via intraperitoneal injection or intranasal inoculation. Clinical signs, viral tissue distribution, histopathological injury, and cytokine levels were systematically analyzed to establish a severe CVB4 infection model and clarify its biological characteristics (Fig. 2A).

In vivo pathogenic characteristics of high- and low-neurovirulence coxsackievirus B4 strains GZ-HFM01 and GZ-R6 in ICR mice of various ages. A Schematic of the infection protocol for GZ-HFM01 and GZ-R6 in 3-day-old or 2-week-old ICR suckling mice (n = 16 per group). Mice were inoculated with 5 × 104 to 5 × 106 plaque-forming units (PFU) of virus via intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection or intranasal (i.n.) inoculation, respectively. Percent survival (B), clinical scores (C), and body weight of surviving mice (D) were monitored and recorded for 3-day-old ICR mice; the same parameters were analyzed for 2-week-old mice (E–G). Clinical scoring criteria: 0, healthy; 1, lethargy or reduced activity; 2, limb weakness; 3, limb paralysis; 4, moribund or dead. Data are shown as mean values with SEM. Statistical analysis of percent survival was performed using the Gehan-Breslow-Wilcoxon test; clinical score and body weight analyses were conducted using two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test. ns, no significant difference; ∗∗∗, P < 0.001; ∗∗∗∗, P < 0.0001.

When 3-day-old ICR suckling mice were intraperitoneally infected with viral titers ranging from 5 × 104 to 5 × 106 PFU per mouse, the survival rate was significantly lower in the GZ-HFM01 group than in the GZ-R6 group at all titers throughout the 7-day observation period (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2B), indicating that GZ-HFM01 displayed higher pathogenicity. At the highest titer (5 × 106 PFU per mouse), all GZ-HFM01-infected mice died within 3 days (n = 16), whereas no mortality was observed in the GZ-R6 group at the lowest titer (5 × 104 PFU per mouse). To further investigate CVB4 infection characteristics, a moderate titer of 5 × 105 PFU per mouse was selected as the optimal dose for detailed analysis.

At the titer of 5 × 105 PFU per mouse, the survival rate in the GZ-HFM01 infection group was 30% over the 7-day period, significantly lower than the rate in the GZ-R6 infection group (P < 0.0001). Mice infected with GZ-HFM01 exhibited systemic tremors and paralysis, indicative of neurological injury; their clinical scores were significantly higher than those of mice in the GZ-R6 group (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 2C). These findings closely align with the clinical manifestations observed in patients infected with GZ-HFM01 (Dai et al., 2023). Furthermore, GZ-HFM01 infection impaired normal body weight gain in mice more severely than GZ-R6 infection (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 2D).

This study also examined whether a severe CVB4 infection model could be established through the respiratory route by intranasal inoculation. Based on viral behavior in A549 cells, 2-week-old and 3-week-old ICR mice were intranasally inoculated with moderate or high viral titers (5 × 105 to 5 × 106 PFU per mouse). The results showed that even at high titers, neither GZ-HFM01 nor GZ-R6 caused mortality in 2-week-old mice (Fig. 2E). Infected mice primarily exhibited reduced activity without neurological signs (Fig. 2F). Although infection hindered normal growth (P < 0.0001), no significant difference was observed between the GZ-HFM01 and GZ-R6 groups (P > 0.05) (Fig. 2G).

Three-week-old ICR mice displayed almost no disease symptoms after intranasal infection and were therefore not included in subsequent analyses (Supplementary Fig. S1).

High-neurovirulence CVB4 strain caused multi-organ infection and severe nerve tissue injury in 3-day-old ICR mice

Two infection models—3-day-old ICR mice (5 × 105 PFU per mouse, intraperitoneal) and 2-week-old ICR mice (5 × 106 PFU per mouse, intranasal)—were used to evaluate viral distribution and histopathological injury in nine tissues (brain, lung, heart, pancreas, kidney, liver, small intestine, spleen, and hindlimb muscle) at various time points after infection, using CVB4 qPCR and hematoxylin and eosin staining. Within 3-day-old intraperitoneally infected mice, GZ-HFM01 was detected in the brain, lung, heart, pancreas, kidney, and liver tissue. In the pancreas, myocardium, and kidney, GZ-HFM01 was detected on day 1 post-infection and then progressively declined. In brain tissue, viral RNA was first detected on day 3 and subsequently increased, consistent with the severe neurological injury induced by GZ-HFM01. In lung and liver tissues, the virus was detected only on day 3 post-infection. In contrast, GZ-R6 was detected in all nine tissues, but showed no clear temporal pattern (Fig. 3A).

In brain tissue: yellow arrows indicate neuronal edema; black arrows indicate neuronal pyknosis. In lung tissue: red arrows indicate inflammatory cell infiltration in alveolar walls; green arrows indicate vascular edema; orange arrows indicate vascular congestion; blue arrows indicate inflammatory cell infiltration. Histological analysis showed that both GZ-HFM01 and GZ-R6 infection in 3-day-old ICR mice caused damage in multiple tissues; considerable differences between the two infection groups were evident in the brain, lung, and small intestine tissues (Fig. 3B). Groupwise differences were less pronounced in other tissues (Supplementary Fig. S2). In brain tissue, GZ-HFM01 infection led to more severe neuronal injury than GZ-R6 infection, characterized by neuronal shrinkage and edema. Over time, GZ-R6–induced injury gradually improved, whereas GZ-HFM01–induced injury showed no significant improvement, consistent with the disease phenotype observed after GZ-HFM01 infection. In the small intestine, both GZ-R6 infection on day 3 and GZ-HFM01 infection on day 1 resulted in villus edema, mucosal epithelial detachment, and a substantial reduction in villus number. By day 7 post-infection, GZ-R6–infected mice had largely recovered, whereas GZ-HFM01–infected mice continued to exhibit prominent epithelial detachment and edema. In lung tissue, the two viruses produced opposite effects: GZ-R6 caused more severe lung injury than GZ-HFM01, with clear features of interstitial pneumonia that showed minimal improvement over time, whereas GZ-HFM01–induced lung injury improved markedly in later stages. Various degrees of inflammatory cell infiltration were observed in the brain, small intestine, lung, and other tissues (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Fig. S2).

In contrast, both viral detection and tissue injury were less pronounced in 2-week-old mice than in 3-day-old mice. GZ-HFM01 and GZ-R6 were detected only in selected tissues (heart, pancreas, lung, and spleen) on day 3 post-infection. Nonetheless, histopathological analysis on day 3 showed that GZ-R6 caused more severe lung injury than GZ-HFM01, whereas GZ-HFM01 caused more pronounced damage in the brain and small intestine (Supplementary Fig. S3). These findings align with the observation that disease symptoms and clinical scores were most severe on day 3 post-infection in 2-week-old ICR mice.

CVB4 infection induced significant elevation of multiple cytokines in mouse serum

To assess the impact of viral infection on immune responses, nine cytokines—IL-6, IFN-γ, IL-1β, TNF-α, IP-10, RANTES, IL-10, MCP-1, and IL-2—were measured in serum samples collected from 3-day-old and 2-week-old mouse models at 1, 3, 5, and 7 d.p.i. In both models, CVB4 infection resulted in significant increases in the levels of all nine cytokines compared with the mock-infected group (P < 0.05); differences were also noted between the two infection groups (Fig. 4, Supplementary Fig. S4).

Cytokine profiles in serum of 3-day-old ICR mice infected with coxsackievirus strains GZ-HFM01 and GZ-R6. Serum samples were collected from 3-day-old ICR mice at 1, 3, 5, and 7 days post-infection with CVB4 strains GZ-HFM01 or GZ-R6, and the levels of nine cytokines were quantified (n = 3 per group). Data are presented as mean values with SEM. Statistical analysis was performed using two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test. Differences between each infected group and the mock-infected group are labeled above the corresponding bars, and differences between the two virus-infected groups are labeled above the connecting lines. ∗, P < 0.05; ∗∗, P < 0.01; ∗∗∗, P < 0.001; ∗∗∗∗, P < 0.0001.

In 3-day-old ICR mice, compared with the mock-infected group, the GZ-HFM01 group showed significantly elevated secretion of IL-6, IFN-γ, IL-1β, TNF-α, and IP-10 as early as day 1 post-infection (P < 0.05). These cytokine levels were also higher than those in the GZ-R6 group (P < 0.05). Notably, TNF-α and IP-10 remained highly expressed throughout the experimental period, suggesting that GZ-HFM01 induces faster and more severe immune injury. In the GZ-R6 group, eight cytokines (IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, IP-10, IL-2, RANTES, IL-10, and MCP-1) peaked on day 3 post-infection and then declined (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4A), which corresponds with the highest clinical scores observed on day 3, followed by gradual recovery (Fig. 2C).

Cytokine expression levels in serum were generally lower in the 2-week-old ICR mouse model than in the 3-day-old model, likely due to reduced infection efficiency (Supplementary Fig. S4).

In summary, intraperitoneal infection of 3-day-old ICR mice with the high-neurovirulence CVB4 strain induces severe neurological injury and typical manifestations of severe infection. The viral tissue distribution, histopathological injury, and immune characteristics observed in this model align with the clinical features of infected patients, establishing it as a suitable animal model for studies of severe CVB4 infection.

The damage capability of the GZ-HFM01 P2 region–replaced chimeric virus was reduced in SH-SY5Y cells

Differences in CVB4 pathogenicity are closely related to genomic variation. The established in vivo and in vitro infection models provide a foundation for further analysis of viral virulence determinants associated with GZ-HFM01 neurovirulence. Fig. 5A illustrates differences in the 5′UTR, P1, P2, P3, and 3′UTR regions between GZ-HFM01 and GZ-R6. Given the high neurovirulence of GZ-HFM01, we used it as the backbone to replace specific gene segments from the low-neurovirulence strain GZ-R6, generating a series of chimeric viruses for the preliminary localization of virulence-attenuating genomic regions (Fig. 5B). These chimeric viruses—rGZ-HFM01GZ−R6-5'UTR, rGZ-HFM01GZ−R6-P1, rGZ-HFM01GZ−R6-P2, rGZ-HFM01GZ−R6-P3, and rGZ-HFM01GZ−R6-3'UTR—were successfully rescued via cell transfection (Supplementary Fig. S5).

Plaque analysis after chimeric virus infection of SH-SY5Y cells showed that plaques formed by rGZ-HFM01GZ−R6-P2 were significantly smaller than those formed by GZ-HFM01 (P < 0.0001), indicating that replacement of the P2 region attenuates the virus's ability to injure neuronal cells (Fig. 5C, D). In contrast, replacement of the 5′UTR and P3 regions resulted in plaques larger than those of the parental virus (P < 0.0001), whereas plaques produced by P1- and 3′UTR-replacement viruses showed no significant change (P > 0.05) (Fig. 5D). Replication curves of each virus in SH-SY5Y cells showed no significant differences (P > 0.05) (Fig. 5E). In HeLa cells, however, there were no significant differences in plaque size or replication kinetics between all chimeric viruses and the parental virus (P > 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. S6).

The pathogenicity of the GZ-HFM01 P2 region–replaced chimeric virus was significantly attenuated in 3-day-old ICR mice

To determine whether the P2 region serves as a key genetic segment mediating the neurovirulence of the GZ-HFM01 strain and to validate the utility of the established animal model, 3-day-old ICR suckling mice were intraperitoneally infected with each chimeric virus (wild-type GZ-HFM01 and GZ-R6 served as controls) at a dose of 5 × 105 PFU per mouse (Fig. 5F). Over the 7-day experimental period, mice infected with rGZ-HFM01GZ−R6-P2 exhibited a significantly higher survival rate than the GZ-HFM01 group (P < 0.001) (Fig. 5G), accompanied by milder clinical manifestations and lower clinical scores (P < 0.01) (Fig. 5H). Changes in body weight showed a similar pattern across groups (P > 0.05) (Fig. 5I). These results were consistent with findings from the in vitro experiments.

Notably, both rGZ-HFM01GZ−R6-5'UTR and rGZ-HFM01GZ−R6-P1 also showed reduced pathogenicity; in particular, the 5′UTR-replacement virus displayed a survival rate comparable to that of the low-neurovirulence strain GZ-R6. This suggests that CVB4 pathogenicity is regulated by multiple genetic segments. Furthermore, the attenuated pathogenicity of these two chimeric viruses differed from the results of the SH-SY5Y infection assays, and the effect of 5′UTR replacement showed an inverse trend. These discrepancies indicate that the current in vitro model has limitations in recapitulating the in vivo pathogenic features of CVB4.

Discussion

Neurological injury induced by CVB4 infection is a major cause of severe illness and death in affected patients. In-depth studies of its pathogenic mechanisms and preventive or therapeutic strategies heavily rely on appropriate in vitro and in vivo infection models. In the present study, we systematically compared the infection characteristics of the high-neurovirulence strain GZ-HFM01 and the low-neurovirulence strain GZ-R6 in multiple cell lines and in ICR mice of different ages, successfully establishing an in vitro cell model and an in vivo animal model for severe CVB4 infection.

In the in vitro experiments, HeLa cells showed high susceptibility to CVB4. No significant differences in plaque morphology or replication kinetics in HeLa cells were observed between the two strains and the chimeric viruses, making this cell line an ideal platform for the standardized quantification of viable virus titers. SH-SY5Y and A549 cells effectively distinguished the cytopathic effects of the two strains in neuronal and respiratory cells, consistent with the clinical manifestations of the patients from whom the strains were isolated (Dai et al., 2023). These results confirm the clinical relevance of the in vitro model and provide a sensitive system for investigating CVB4 neurovirulence and respiratory virulence. The strain-specific and cell type–specific pathogenic patterns further suggest that CVB4 tissue tropism is determined by viral genomic characteristics, offering important clues for subsequent studies of virulence mechanisms.

In this study, i.p. injection was adopted for 3-day-old ICR suckling mice mainly due to their immature respiratory tract structure and extremely small body size, which make intranasal inoculation technically difficult to perform with accurate control of inoculum volume and deposition site. For 2-week-old and 3-week-old mice with developed respiratory tracts and larger body sizes, intranasal inoculation was used to attempt to establish a severe infection model that mimics the natural respiratory transmission route of CVB4 in humans, one of the main clinical transmission modes of the virus.

In vivo experiments in ICR mice demonstrated that mouse age, infection route, and viral dose strongly influenced the infectious and pathogenic phenotypes of CVB4. Our findings showed that intraperitoneal injection of a moderate viral dose (5 × 105 PFU) into 3-day-old ICR suckling mice resulted in significant differences in survival rate, clinical scores, and inhibition of body weight gain between the high- and low-neurovirulence groups. This model reliably recapitulates the typical neurological injury observed in severe cases caused by high-neurovirulence CVB4 infection (Xiao et al., 2021). Histopathological analysis further showed that GZ-HFM01 exhibited progressive replication in mouse brain tissue, with persistent worsening of neurological injury. In contrast, neurological injury caused by GZ-R6 was self-limiting, but its pulmonary pathogenicity was significantly greater than that of GZ-HFM01. These findings align with the in vitro results and the clinical manifestations displayed by patients infected with GZ-R6 (Dai et al., 2023), indicating that this model is also suitable for studies of CVB4-induced lung injury. In comparison, infection with an excessively high viral dose (5 × 106 PFU) caused rapid death, whereas infection with an excessively low dose (5 × 104 PFU) produced no clear clinical symptoms, making both conditions unsuitable for characterizing disease phenotypes.

In addition to the direct tissue damage caused by viral replication, immune injury is an important pathogenic mechanism in viral infections. Previous studies have confirmed that CVB4 infection induces elevated IL-6 levels in the plasma of infected patients, thereby triggering immune injury (Dai et al., 2023). Analysis of serum cytokines in infected 3-day-old ICR mice showed that GZ-HFM01 induced a rapid increase in pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α during the early stage of infection, with persistently high expression of TNF-α and IP-10 also evident. These changes may lead to a cytokine storm, which is presumably a key factor contributing to GZ-HFM01–induced severe illness. The dynamic cytokine profiles provide in vivo experimental evidence for deciphering the immunoregulatory pathways that underlie CVB4-induced severe disease.

In contrast to the pronounced pathogenicity observed in 3-day-old mice, neither 2-week-old nor 3-week-old ICR mice died after intranasal infection in the present study. However, 2-week-old mice exhibited more prominent pathogenic manifestations and higher clinical scores than 3-week-old mice. While these observations are consistent with the epidemiological characteristics of human CVB4 infection, in which infants and young children represent high-risk populations (Liu et al., 2023), and align with findings that other EV types tend to induce more typical disease phenotypes in younger animals (Jiang et al., 2021), it is important to acknowledge the limitations inherent to cross-species and cross-viral extrapolations. Specifically, differences in host immune responses, viral tropism, and physiological development between mice and humans, as well as variations in viral replication and pathogenic mechanisms across enterovirus subtypes, may restrict the direct translation of these murine model findings to human clinical contexts.

Notably, plaque assay results showed that CVB4 infectivity was much lower in A549 cells than in SH-SY5Y and HeLa cells, suggesting that, within the context of the tested cell lines in this study, CVB4 has a relatively weak ability to infect the respiratory epithelial cell model represented by A549 cells. In contrast, intraperitoneal injection is an artificial method that directly delivers the virus into the peritoneal cavity, thereby bypassing the respiratory mucosal barriers and innate immune defenses of the upper airway—key components of the body's natural first-line defense against the virus. This difference in inoculation routes, together with potential variations in effective inoculum deposition at target sites and intrinsic viral tissue tropism, may explain why intranasal infection demonstrated lower infectivity than intraperitoneal administration. However, this hypothesis requires further verification in age-matched animal models.

Previous studies have shown that the pathogenic characteristics of EVs are closely associated with their genome-encoded products, and virulence-related determinants have been identified in both noncoding and coding regions (Arita et al., 2008; Cordey et al., 2012; Chang et al., 2018). Research focusing on the relationship between the pathogenic features of CVB4 and its genomic differences remains limited. Only two determinants—Thr-129 in VP1 and Arg-16 in VP4—have been confirmed to have close associations with type 1 diabetes mellitus. CVB4 strains harboring these determinants exhibit a greater ability to destroy pancreatic β cells, leading to insufficient insulin secretion and the subsequent development of type 1 diabetes mellitus (Ramsingh et al., 1992; Caggana et al., 1993; Ramsingh et al., 1995; Ramsingh and Collins, 1995). However, no studies have identified neurovirulence determinants associated with severe neurological injury, highlighting an urgent need for further investigation.

Using the established in vitro and in vivo infection models, we constructed chimeric viruses with GZ-HFM01 as the backbone and specific genomic regions replaced by those from GZ-R6, preliminarily identifying the P2 region as a core virulence-attenuating functional segment that regulates CVB4 neurovirulence. The P2 region encodes three nonstructural proteins (2A, 2B, and 2C), which are extensively involved in viral replication, protein processing, and regulation of host antiviral responses (Aldabe et al., 1996; van Kuppeveld et al., 1996, 1997; Gradi et al., 1998; Garmaroudi et al., 2015). Our results showed that replacement of the P2 region significantly reduced the virus's plaque-forming ability in SH-SY5Y cells and its pathogenicity in 3-day-old ICR suckling mice, providing an important direction for subsequent detailed investigation of specific virulence determinants within the P2 region.

Additionally, animal experiments revealed that replacement of the 5′UTR or P1 region significantly attenuated viral virulence—a phenotype not observed in cell models—underscoring the complexity of CVB4 pathogenic mechanisms. As an internal ribosomal entry site, the 5′UTR regulates viral protein translation efficiency, whereas the structural proteins encoded by the P1 region determine receptor binding and cell tropism (Lin et al., 2009). These regions may act synergistically with the P2 region to influence tissue tropism, immunogenicity, or dissemination capacity in complex organisms, collectively shaping the final pathogenic outcome. This finding indicates that CVB4 neurovirulence is not governed by a single gene but instead results from coordinated regulation of multiple functional segments, providing foundational data for deciphering the networked regulatory mechanisms that underlie its virulence determinants.

This study has several limitations. First, although the SH-SY5Y cell model effectively reflects direct viral cytotoxicity toward neural cells, reliance on a single cell type limits its ability to recapitulate complex biological systems. This limitation may explain the absence of phenotypic changes associated with the replacement of the 5′UTR and P1 regions in the cell models. Future studies should incorporate multiple cell types for more comprehensive analysis. Second, viral RNA quantification was used to analyze viral replication kinetics and tissue distribution in infected mice. However, this method cannot distinguish between infectious and non-infectious virions. Additionally, uneven viral distribution in mouse tissues and sampling site variations may lead to inaccurate detection. These factors may result in potential biases in evaluating viral replication, proliferation, and tissue distribution. Third, the developmental stage of suckling mice restricts the selection of inoculation routes. We did not perform parallel experiments using different inoculation methods, which may lead to bias when interpreting infection outcomes across age groups. Fourth, in the analysis of cytokine levels, our use of serum from mock-infected mice at 5 d.p.i. as the negative control may have introduced certain biases. Fifth, this study provides only a preliminary analysis of the contributions of individual gene segments to neurovirulence; further investigations are required to identify specific virulence-associated sites within these segments.

Conclusion

This study established and validated in vitro and in vivo models that recapitulate severe CVB4 infection, providing critical experimental tools for mechanistic investigations of CVB4 pathogenesis. Using these models, CVB4 neurovirulence was found to correlate with multiple genomic regions, including the P2, 5′UTR, and P1 segments. These results advance the current understanding of CVB4 virulence determinants by highlighting the need to evaluate viral pathogenicity within complex biological systems. Future work will build on these models to pinpoint specific molecular motifs within the identified genomic segments, dissect their regulatory mechanisms governing neurovirulence, and develop targeted therapeutic strategies against severe CVB4 infection.

Materials and methods

Cells and virus

HeLa cells are susceptible to CVB4 (Dai et al., 2023), whereas SH-SY5Y and A549 cells are widely used for in vitro studies of EV-induced neural injury (Liu et al., 2019) and respiratory virus–induced respiratory injury (Liu et al., 2022), respectively. HeLa cells (ATCC #CCL-2, USA), human neuroblastoma cells (SH-SY5Y, ATCC #CRL-2266), and human lung carcinoma A549 cells (ATCC #CCL-185) were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Beijing, China) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS; ExCell Bio, Taicang, China) and 1% (v/v) penicillin–streptomycin (P/S; Gibco, NY, USA) at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

The high-neurovirulence CVB4 strain GZ-HFM01 (GenBank ID: MZ540957) and the low-neurovirulence CVB4 strain GZ-R6 (GenBank ID: MZ540958) were used in this study (Dai et al., 2023). CVB4 was inoculated into susceptible HeLa cells at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in DMEM supplemented with 2% FBS and 1% P/S. Inoculated cells were monitored daily and harvested when they exhibited near-complete cytopathic effect. The virus was collected, quantified, and stored in aliquots at −80 °C.

Viral plaque formation assay

HeLa, SH-SY5Y, and A549 cells were seeded in six-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well and cultured overnight at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The medium was removed, and 0.8 mL of diluted virus (GZ-HFM01, GZ-R6) or phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; mock) was added to each well of HeLa, SH-SY5Y, and A549 cells. For chimeric viruses (rGZ-HFM01GZ−R6-5'UTR, rGZ-HFM01GZ−R6-P1, rGZ-HFM01GZ−R6-P2, rGZ-HFM01GZ−R6-P3 and rGZ-HFM01GZ−R6-3'UTR), diluted viral suspensions (0.8 mL per well) were added to HeLa and SH-SY5Y cells for parallel comparison with GZ-HFM01 and GZ-R6. All wells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 2 h, with gentle agitation every 20 min to ensure uniform viral adsorption. The cells were washed with PBS, followed by the addition of 3 mL of agarose medium to each well. The agarose medium was prepared by mixing equal volumes of 2% low-melting agarose (Lonza, Rockland, USA) and 2 × DMEM containing 4% FBS. After the agarose layer had solidified at room temperature, cultures were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 48 h. Following overnight fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde, the agarose was removed, and the cells were stained with crystal violet (0.5% crystal violet, 20% ethanol). Plaque diameters were measured using ImageJ software.

Viral plaque-forming units (PFU) in HeLa cells were also used as a basis for quantifying viral particles.

Replication curve

HeLa, SH-SY5Y, and A549 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1.5 × 104 cells per well and cultured overnight. GZ-HFM01 and GZ-R6 were used to infect HeLa, SH-SY5Y, and A549 cells, while the chimeric viruses (rGZ-HFM01GZ−R6-5'UTR, rGZ-HFM01GZ−R6-P1, rGZ-HFM01GZ−R6-P2, rGZ-HFM01GZ−R6-P3 and rGZ-HFM01GZ−R6-3'UTR) were used to infect HeLa and SH-SY5Y cells. The multiplicity of infection was set to 0.01 for HeLa and SH-SY5Y cells, and 0.1 for A549 cells. After incubation for 2 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2, the cells were washed with PBS and maintained in 0.1 mL per well of culture medium (DMEM + 2% FBS + 1% penicillin–streptomycin). Cells and culture supernatants were collected at 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6 days post-infection (d.p.i.). Viral genome copies were quantified using the established TaqMan fluorescence quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) method for CVB4 (Dai et al., 2023).

Construction of genome segment replacement chimeric CVB4 viruses

Infectious plasmids for genome segment–replacement chimeric CVB4 viruses were constructed using the infectious plasmid pcDNA-CVB4 as the backbone, derived from CVB4 strain GZ-HFM01 (Dai et al., 2023). The pcDNA-CVB4 backbone was linearized by PCR amplification excluding the target genome segment. The corresponding genome segment (5′UTR, P1, P2, P3, or 3′UTR) from the low-neurovirulence strain GZ-R6 was amplified using segment-specific primers. All primers used for backbone linearization and segment amplification are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Linearized backbone plasmids were fused with the respective target segments from GZ-R6 using 5 × In-Fusion HD enzyme premix (Takara, Dalian, China), in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. The constructs were then transformed into SURE 2 competent cells (Stratagene #200152, Agilent, USA) to generate the recombinant plasmids pGZ-HFM01GZ−R6-5'UTR, pGZ-HFM01GZ−R6-P1, pGZ-HFM01GZ−R6-P2, pGZ-HFM01GZ−R6-P3, and pGZ-HFM01GZ−R6-3'UTR, which were sequenced to confirm correct construction.

Chimeric CVB4 viruses rescue

HeLa cells were seeded at a density of 5.0 × 105 cells per well in six-well plates and cultured overnight at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The cells were transfected with the prepared infectious plasmids using Lipofectamine 3000 Transfection Reagent (Invitrogen #L3000015, USA), in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol. The CVB4 2A protease cleaves the host eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4G (eIF4G) (Lamphear et al., 1993), thereby inhibiting host protein synthesis and the translation of viral mRNA derived from the CMV promoter. Thus, infectious CVB4 virions can be generated post-transfection, but with low efficiency. After incubation for 48 h, the cells were harvested and subjected to three freeze–thaw cycles to disrupt them; the resulting lysates were then added to fresh HeLa cell cultures. Cytopathic effect was monitored daily. When complete cytopathic effects were observed, the recombinant viruses—rGZ-HFM01GZ−R6-5'UTR, rGZ-HFM01GZ−R6-P1, rGZ-HFM01GZ−R6-P2, rGZ-HFM01GZ−R6-P3, and rGZ-HFM01GZ−R6-3'UTR—were collected for further analysis.

ICR suckling mouse infection

ICR mice, an outbred strain, serve as a key model for studying EV-induced pathogenic mechanisms and have been widely used in research on myocarditis and neurological injury induced by EVs (Grodums and Dempster, 1959; Khetsuriani et al., 2006; Dong et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2025). ICR mice of different age groups were infected with GZ-HFM01 and GZ-R6 to establish an in vivo severe infection model that recapitulated clinical characteristics of CVB4-induced severe disease. Specifically, 3-day-old ICR suckling mice were intraperitoneally injected with 5 × 104 to 5 × 106 PFU per mouse of GZ-HFM01 or GZ-R6. Mice injected with cell culture lysate supernatant served as mock-infected controls. Two-week-old and 3-week-old ICR mice were intranasally inoculated with 5 × 105 to 5 × 106 PFU per mouse of the same strains. Each group contained 16 mice, with an injection volume of 20 μL per mouse. Mice were monitored daily for changes in body weight, the presence of paralysis, and mortality up to 7 d.p.i. Clinical scores were assigned according to established criteria (Lin et al., 2023; Tang et al., 2023): 0, healthy; 1, lethargy or reduced activity; 2, limb weakness; 3, limb paralysis; 4, moribund or dead. At 1, 3, 5, and 7 d.p.i., mice were euthanized for necropsy. Nine tissues (brain, lung, heart, pancreas, kidney, liver, small intestine, spleen, and hindlimb muscle) were collected to analyze viral tissue distribution (via CVB4 qPCR) and histopathological lesions. Serum samples were also harvested at each time point for cytokine analysis.

For the chimeric CVB4 viruses, 3-day-old ICR suckling mice were intraperitoneally infected with 5 × 105 PFU per mouse. Each group included 16 mice, with an injection volume of 20 μL per mouse. Daily monitoring of body weight, paralysis, mortality, and clinical scores was performed up to 7 d.p.i. to assess the contribution of individual genome segments to viral virulence.

Histological analysis

Tissues were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h and embedded in paraffin. Deparaffinized sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin, scanned, and blindly evaluated by a board-certified pathologist. Images were captured using a Precice 500B slide scanner (Medite, Germany).

Cytokine testing

To characterize the in vivo immune responses induced by GZ-HFM01 and GZ-R6 infection, a MILLIPLEX MAP Mouse Cytokine/Chemokine Magnetic Bead Panel (9-plex, Millipore, USA) was used to quantify key inflammatory cytokines and chemokines in serum samples collected at 1, 3, 5, and 7 d.p.i. Analytes included interleukin (IL)-6; regulated upon activation, normal T cell expressed and secreted (RANTES; C–C motif chemokine ligand 5 [CCL5]); IL-1β; IL-2; IL-10; tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α; interferon (IFN)-γ; monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1; CCL2); and interferon gamma-induced protein-10 (IP-10; C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 10 [CXCL10]). Serum samples from mock-infected mice at 5 d.p.i. served as negative controls. Due to the limited blood volume of suckling mice, 10 μL of serum was collected from each mouse, then diluted with 40 μL of PBS to ensure a consistent sample volume for cytokine quantification. All assays were performed on a Luminex MAGPIX system using MILLIPLEX Analyst V5.1 software (Luminex, R&D Systems, USA), in strict accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. For statistical analysis, the levels of each cytokine in the CVB4-infected groups at different time points were first compared with those in the mock-infected control group to identify infection-induced alterations. Pairwise comparisons of cytokine levels were subsequently performed between the GZ-HFM01-and GZ-R6-infected groups for analytes that exhibited statistically significant differences relative to the mock control, to characterize strain-specific immunomodulatory profiles.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.5 (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA). Differences between groups were evaluated by analysis of variance (ANOVA) and the Mann–Whitney U test. Survival percentages were compared via the Gehan–Breslow–Wilcoxon test. A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.
