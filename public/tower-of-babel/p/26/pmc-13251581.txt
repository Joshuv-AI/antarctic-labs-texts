Quantifying the Influence of Genetic Context on Duplicated Mammalian Genes
Authors: Alexander S Moffett, Andrea Falcón-Cortés, Michele Di Pierro, Russell Corbett-Detig
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGene duplication is a fundamental part of evolutionary innovation. While paralogs frequently exhibit asymmetric evolutionary rates, the extent to which genetic context influences asymmetric evolution remains unclear. In this study, we investigate the role of genetic context in shaping evolutionary divergence within both single-gene and multigene duplications, leveraging microsynteny to differentiate source and target copies. Using a dataset of 193 mammalian genome assemblies and a bird outgroup, we systematically analyze patterns of sequence divergence between paralogs. We find that most paralogs have no detectable difference in their evolutionary rates. When there is rate asymmetry, target copies, those relocated to new genomic locations, exhibit elevated evolutionary rates compared to source copies in ancestral locations. This asymmetry is influenced by the genomic distance between copies, the size of the duplicated region, and the position within multigene duplications. We also demonstrate that the “choice” of the fast-evolving copy in multigene duplications occurs in a collective, block-wise manner more often than expected by random chance. Our findings highlight the importance of genetic context in modulating postduplication divergence, where differences in cis-regulatory elements and co-expressed gene clusters between source and target copies may be responsible. This study presents a large-scale study of asymmetric evolution in duplications of varying size, offering new insight into how genome architecture shapes functional diversification of paralogs.Keywords: gene duplication, genome evolution, mammals

Introduction

Gene duplications play a crucial role in evolution, providing frequent opportunities for functional innovation (Ohno 1970). If a gene duplication fixes in a population, both copies can face relaxed purifying selection, leading to loss of function in one of the copies (Lynch and Conery 2000; Lynch and Katju 2004). Alternatively, several mechanisms can explain long-term maintenance of both copies, including neofunctionalization, subfunctionalization, escape from adaptive conflict, and selection for increased gene dosage (Ohno 1970; Innan and Kondrashov 2010). Most of the possible fates of a duplication are expected to result in asymmetric evolution of the two copies, as has been widely observed (Conant and Wagner 2003; Zhang et al. 2003; Blanc and Wolfe 2004; Zhang and Kishino 2004; Cusack and Wolfe 2007; Han et al. 2009; Jun et al. 2009b; Panchin et al. 2010; Katju 2013; Pegueroles et al. 2013; Pich i Roselló and Kondrashov 2014; Yampolsky and Bouzinier 2014; Holland et al. 2017; Fernández et al. 2020; Jaafari et al. 2024; Lee et al. 2024). While it is not immediately clear which of the two copies will experience an accelerated substitution rate, previous work suggests that the copy in the ancestral genomic location tends to evolve at similar rate before and after the duplication, while the copy inserted into a new location often experiences an accelerated substitution rate. Adopting the terminology of Dewey (Dewey 2011), we refer to the copy in the original location as the source copy and the the relocated copy as the target copy. If a target (source) copy has a higher substitution rate than its corresponding source (target) copy, we say that the paralogs are target-polarized (source-polarized). Evidence for polarization of paralogs has been found in small collections of model mammalian species (Cusack and Wolfe 2007; Han et al. 2009; Jun et al. 2009b; Pegueroles et al. 2013; Pich i Roselló and Kondrashov 2014) and nematodes (Katju 2013). Depending on the specific duplication event, this increase of substitution rate in the source copy can be consistent with positive selection (Han et al. 2009; Pegueroles et al. 2013) or relaxed negative selection.

Substitution rate asymmetry between paralogs is often location-dependent, highlighting differences in the regulatory environment of the source and target copies, where cis-regulatory elements and co-expressed genes can be drastically different depending on the location of a gene (Lee et al. 2024). Polarization has also been found to correlate with local recombination rate in yeast (Zhang and Kishino 2004), suggesting that the relevant genetic context cannot be reduced to only cis-regulatory elements. Regardless of the specific nature of genetic context, both the distance between copies and the size of duplicated regions are factors that might influence polarization of paralogs. Both attributes reflect the similarity of the genetic context across paralogs, where closeby source and target copies are likely to directly share cis-regulatory elements while large duplications may carry cis-regulatory elements and co-expressed genes to the source location. Paralogs resulting from tandem duplications do not seem to evolve asymmetrically (Cusack and Wolfe 2007; Jun et al. 2009b), which is consistent with a model of rate asymmetry caused by differences in regulatory environment. In Caenorhabditis elegans, the genomic distance between target and source copies on the same chromosome has little effect on rate asymmetry, while relocation of the target copy to a different chromosome is associated with accelerated evolution (Katju 2013). In the same study, the length of the duplicated region was inversely related to rate asymmetry, so that smaller duplications tend to evolve more asymmetrically. Additionally, asymmetric evolution in multigene duplications (i.e. duplication of large genomic regions containing multiple genes) is not well-characterized, despite the importance of large duplications (Armengol et al. 2003; Bailey et al. 2004; Jiang et al. 2007; Marques-Bonet et al. 2009a, 2009b; Sudmant et al. 2013; Feng et al. 2017; Vollger et al. 2022, 2023; Aqil et al. 2026), with one study of multigene duplications in Arabidopsis thaliana yielding no significant asymmetric evolution between paralogs (Zhang et al. 2002).

Despite considerable progress in characterizing paralog polarization, more work is needed in order to uncover quantitative relationships between the relative sizes and locations of paralogous genomic regions, substitution rate asymmetry, and polarization. Here, we analyze both single-gene and multigene gene duplication in a broad sample of mammals. We use microsynteny, the local conservation of gene order, to reliably identify source and target copies and to find multigene duplications (Fig. 1a), large duplications that span many genes. Microsynteny forms a basis for comparing genomes of distantly related species and accurately inferring orthology (Jun et al. 2009a; Novichkov et al. 2009; Kristensen et al. 2011; Zhang and Leong 2012; Lechner et al. 2014; Ali et al. 2016; Jahangiri-Tazehkand et al. 2017; Gao and Miller 2020; Kirilenko et al. 2023). We develop an interpretable and statistically conservative algorithm, APES, for identifying microsynteny from comparisons of gene order in pairs of genomes. With source and target copies identified, we perform a quantitative analysis of the relationship between genetic context and paralog polarization. We find strong evidence for target polarization in both single-gene and multigene duplications, although most paralogs display no asymmetric evolution at all. We observe decreasing polarization with the size of duplicated regions and a weak relationship between polarization and the genomic distance between the two copies on the same chromosome, while paralogs on different chromosomes exhibit the strongest polarization. Paralogs near the flank of large duplications also tend to be more target-polarized than those further away. Finally, we observe block-wise polarization for simultaneously duplicated genes. Altogether, these results highlight the importance of genetic context in the evolutionary fate of gene duplications in a broad sampling of mammals.

Results and discussion

Identifying microsynteny

We collected chromosome-length genome assemblies from the DNA Zoo (Dudchenko et al. 2017) and Gencode (Frankish et al. 2019) to form a dataset of 193 mammalian genome assemblies and a single bird genome assembly as an outgroup (Fig. 1b). This dataset represents a broad sampling of mammals (Fig. 1b), allowing us to make general conclusions about mammalian gene duplications. To infer a species tree, orthogroups (i.e. gene families), and corresponding species tree-reconciled gene trees, we used OrthoFinder 2 (Emms and Kelly 2015, 2019) run with amino acid sequences of protein coding genes. The gene tree for each orthogroup fully describes orthology and paralogy relationships between genes and provides an estimated species tree location for each duplication event.

To identify single-gene and multigene duplications and to distinguish between target and source copies, we inferred microsynteny (defined here as conserved gene order) blocks across all pairs of genomes (Fig. 1). In any genome comparison, if a gene belongs to more than one synteny block we can tell that a multigene duplication has occurred (Fig. 2a–d). Duplicated genes that appear in only one synteny block per genome comparison were likely duplicated alone. We can only distinguish source and target copies when comparing organisms inheriting both copies of a duplicated gene and organisms without the corresponding duplication event in their lineage. If we compare two organisms both inheriting a duplication, synteny fails to yield accurate source and target copy assignments (Fig. 2a,b). On the other hand, if we properly choose species comparisons, the source copy is likely to be the paralog belonging to the largest microsynteny block (primary microsynteny) (Fig. 2c,d).

We developed a novel approach to infer microsynteny, designed to be easily interpretable and to achieve a low rate of false positives. Our algorithm, APES (APES Explore Synteny), identifies synteny at the gene level, a significantly easier task as compared with full genomic nucleotide sequences. To achieve this goal, APES first identifies blocks of perfectly conserved gene order, which we call nanosynteny, and then assembles nearby nanosynteny blocks into microsynteny blocks (Fig. 2e). In this way, each microsynteny block contains at least one nanosynteny block. By randomly permuting homology matrices, we show that nanosynteny blocks containing 3 or more pairs of genes (each pair containing a single orthologous gene from each species in the comparison) are highly unlikely to arise by chance (Fig. 2f-e). These results are in close agreement with a simple analytical model (Fig. S1, see Supplementary material for details). The strong statistical significance of nanosynteny blocks with 3 or more putative orthologous gene pairs provides a solid foundation for construction of reliable microsynteny blocks. We chose APES hyperparameters such that the microsynteny results were insensitive to hyperparameter changes (Fig. S2). A complete description of the APES algorithm can be found in the Methods section.

Asymmetric evolution of single-gene duplications

We first investigated rate asymmetry in single-gene duplications. We examined all single-gene duplications for which we could unambiguously assign source and target copies using APES, and which passed a number of filtering steps (Methods). We note that by using APES to identify single-gene duplications, we likely include two-gene duplications in this analysis as well, because the minimum microsynteny block size is 3 genes. Using gene trees generated by OrthoFinder and both amino acid and DNA sequences, we performed dN/dS analysis with PAML (Yang 2007) on each selected orthogroup with a single-gene duplication. We allowed for three different branch-dependent nonsynonymous to synonymous substitution rate ratios, ωS for the source copy branches of each tree, ωT for the target branches, and ω0 for all other branches (Fig. 3a). We then estimated maximum likelihood substitution models under three different hypotheses. H0 assumes that ω0=ωS=ωT, consistent with no change in selection after duplication. H1 assumes ωS=ωT but allows this ratio to differ from ω0, indicating an identical change in selection on both copies as compared with nonduplicated orthologs. In this case there is still no rate asymmetry. Finally, H2 allows ω0, ωS, and ωT to vary independently, so there can be rate asymmetry between source and target copies.

We used the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) (Akaike 1998) to select between the H0, H1, and H2 hypotheses; for each duplication we selected the hypothesis with the lowest AIC value. The plurality of the 308 orthogroups selected for analysis were most consistent with H1 (44.5%), while 38.3% of orthogroups were consistent with H0 and 17.2% with H2 (Fig. 3b). In other words, 61.7% of orthogroups had a detectable change in selection after a single-gene duplication, while 17.2% of orthogroups had detectable asymmetry in selection between paralogs. Rate asymmetry was significantly associated with paralogs located on different chromosomes (Fig. 3b inset).

Of orthogroups consistent with H1, most (96.4%) had an increased ω after duplication (ωS/T>ω0), suggesting a shift from negative selection towards neutral evolution or positive selection (Fig. 3c). Around half (47.4%) of H1 duplications were under positive selection (ωS/T>1, Fig. 3e). For duplications with asymmetric paralog evolution (H2), target copies had a larger ω than source copies (ωT>ωS) and a larger ω than nonduplicated orthologs (ωT>ω0) in 92.5% of orthogroups (Fig. 3d). Target copies experienced positive selection (ωT>1) in 66.0% of all orthogroups, as compared with 5.7% for source copies (ωS>1, Fig. 3e). Distributions of ω values for all three hypotheses are shown in Fig. S3. All together, this suggests that a mixture of relaxed negative selection and positive selection can account for elevated amino acid substitution rates and asymmetric evolution after duplication. When there is rate asymmetry between paralogs, it is overwhelmingly target-polarized and is largely driven by positive selection. This finding is consistent with a dominant role for neofunctionalization (Ohno 1970; Innan and Kondrashov 2010) in maintaining target-polarized gene duplicates.

There was no correlation between the ratio of ω before and after duplication (ωS/T/ω0) and the age of the duplication, measured by dS, within H1 duplications (ρ=0.068, permutation test P=0.415) (Fig. 3f). On the other hand, younger H2 duplications tended to have a larger target to source ratio (ωT/ωS), as suggested by a negative correlation between ωT/ωS and dS (ρ=−0.455, permutation test P<0.05) (Fig. 3f). There was no correlation between ωS/ω0 and dS within H2 duplications (ρ=−0.043, permutation test P=0.765), while there was a negative correlation between ωT/ω0 and dS (ρ=−0.455, permutation test P<0.05). For asymmetrically evolving paralogs (H2 duplications), this is consistent with relaxed negative selection or increased positive selection on target copies soon after duplication, followed by restored negative selection as duplications age, in agreement with previous work (Pegueroles et al. 2013). Interestingly, H0 duplications tended to be younger, while H1 duplications fell in the middle and H2 duplications were overall the oldest, according to distributions of interparalog dS values and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests (Fig. S4). This ordering of duplication classes by age is inconsistent with the possible interpretation that H0 and H1 duplications are simply older H2 duplications that have passed through a phase of paralog rate asymmetry, and in the present day have lost all traces of polarization.

To explore the possibility that target copies evolve faster due to partial gene duplications, we investigated the relationship between ω and the ratio of target to source gene transcript lengths (LT/LS). We found very weak relationships between ωT/ωS (ρ=0.082, permutation test P=0.536), ωS/ω0 (ρ=−0.029, permutation test P=0.817), and ωT/ω0 (ρ=0.045, permutation test P=0.771) each with LT/LS (Fig. 3h), while most source and target paralog pairs had comparable lengths LT/LS, where 74% of duplications had a target to source gene length ratio of at least 0.75 (Fig. S5). It is possible that some cases of rate asymmetry could be due to partial duplication of genes, although changes in gene length could also occur due to postduplication deletions. However, the lack of a clear relationship between LT/LS and source, target, and background ω ratios suggests that partial duplications are not the main cause of rate asymmetry.

To test the correspondence of the dN/dS analysis results with a simpler measure of paralog rate asymmetry, we calculated the normalized Levenshtein distance between each paralog in the source and target copies and the corresponding ortholog in an outgroup genome (which branched off before the duplication occurred), yielding distances in number of amino acid substitutions per site. Referring to the source paralog as A1, the target paralog as A2, and the outgroup ortholog as B1 (as in Fig. 3a), dL(A1,B1) is the Levenshtein distance between the gene A1 and its single syntenic ortholog in species B, and dL(A2,B1) is the same for A2. The difference ΔdL=dL(A1,B1)−dL(A2,B1) directly estimates the difference in evolutionary rates between the source and target paralogs with respect to the shared ortholog B1. ΔdL is negative if the target paralog has experienced a faster substitution rate than the source paralog, positive if the target paralog has experienced a slower substitution rate, and zero if both paralogs have experienced the same substitution rate. This approach focuses only on amino acid substitutions and does not account for multiple substitutions at any one site, but has the advantage of simplicity and ease of interpretability.

Separating out H0, H1, and H2 duplications and calculating ΔdL for each duplication, we were able to see a clear difference in the ΔdL distributions for H2 as compared with H0 and H1 duplications, where H2 duplications had a significantly (one-sided Kolmogorov–Smirnov test P<0.05) left-shifted ΔdL distribution (Fig. 3i). H0 and H1 had statistically indistinguishable ΔdL distributions (one-sided Kolmogorov–Smirnov test P=0.381), with negative, but smaller in magnitude mean ΔdL values as compared with H2 duplications. In H2 duplications, the signs of ωS−ωT and ΔdL agreed for 79.2% of duplications (Fig. S6). All together, our analysis of Levenshtein distances in amino acid sequences in single-gene duplications suggests that ΔdL is a suitable proxy for ω in measuring paralog rate asymmetry over long evolutionary timescales, and we adopt ΔdL for our analysis of multigene duplications.

Asymmetric evolution of multigene duplications

In our amino acid-based analysis of multigene duplications, we took a conservative approach to estimating the duplication time for each multigene duplication through a consensus of two independent methods. Our intent was to minimize the number of times we underestimated the duplication times, to ensure a low false positive rate in determining gene triplets resulting from after-speciation duplications. In the first method, we gathered the duplication times inferred by DLCpar (Wu et al. 2014) for each paralogous pair in the multigene duplication. Within each multigene duplication, we then searched for the paralogous pair inferred to arise from a well-supported duplication (50% or more of species that should have inherited the duplication in the absence of deletions have both copies of the gene) with the earliest duplication time. This duplication time was assigned to the entire multigene duplication. In the case where none of the paralogous pairs were inferred to be the result of a well-supported duplication, we simply chose the earliest duplication time to represent the multigene duplication. Our second method involved searching for the presence of two or more copies of the regions resulting from a multigene duplication in each other genome. We then determined the most recent common ancestor of all species in which we found two or more copies of the duplicated regions. The time to this ancestral species should be an upper bound on the duplication time if we assume there cannot be independent multigene duplication events of the same region. As a consensus rule, we took the earlier of the two calculated duplication times. This process identified multigene duplication timings with a fairly uniform distribution (Fig. S7).

We estimated the amino acid substitution rates of source (rS) and target (rT) copies for all multigene duplications apparent from microsynteny. As mentioned above, in order to calculate ΔdL, rS, and rT, we compared both source and target copies with orthologs from an outgroup species that had branched off before the duplication occurred. We refer to the collection of source paralog, target paralog, and outgroup ortholog as a gene triplet. In order to account for positive Levenshtein distances between paralogs occurring due to random chance rather than differences in substitution rates, we used a Poisson model to calculate the probability of finding the observed ΔdL value with identical substitution rates, and only considered duplications with such a probability of less than 0.05 (Fig. 4a–c, see Supplementary material for details).

When compared with an ensemble of gene triplets randomly assigned to multigene duplications, the probability of observing our original data was low (P<0.05), with excess counts at PSB(ΔdL<0)=0 and PSB(ΔdL<0)=1. This suggests that the fate of multigene duplications is determined collectively more often than expected by random chance.

Of the 22,342 gene triplets in our analysis, 62% had no significant difference in rate. Of the 38% of gene triplets with significantly different rS and rT, 59% had a higher target copy substitution rate than the source copy (target-polarized, rS<rT) while 41% had a lower target copy substitution rate (source-polarized, rS>rT) (Fig. 4a). We calculated these percentages as weighted averages, weighting the contribution of each gene triplet by one over the the product of the number of species inheriting the duplication (under the assumption that neither copy is lost or not detected) with the number of possible outgroup species. This weighting accounts for the phylogenetic interdependence of gene triplets (see Supplementary material for details). When we explicitly averaged over outgroup species, we found that 72% of statistically significant cases of asymmetric paralog evolution were target-polarized (Fig. 4b). In this case, we again computed weighted averages except using only the number of species inheriting each duplication. If we only consider duplications that occurred in terminal branches of the species tree and thus should exist in only one species, while still explicitly averaging over outgroups, we get nearly identical results with 72% of significant cases being target-polarized (Fig. 4c).

In order to examine how robust these results are to the choice of species included in the analysis, we performed bootstrap subsampling of species from the 194 total species. We independently sampled 1,000 sets of species without replacement consisting of 97 species, 49 species, and 25 species each and calculated the unweighted fractions of gene triplets with a faster-evolving target copy (ΔdL<0), given that there was rate asymmetry (ΔdL≠0). This analysis was performed without accounting for the possibility of nonzero ΔdL even with equal rS and rT, in order to examine how ignoring this correction affected our results. We found that the bootstrap mean of target-polarized gene triplets was consistently close to 59%, with only 10.5% of samples in bootstrapping with 25 species having a target-polarized fraction less than 50% (Fig. 4d). This results suggests that our results are robust to both alternative choices of species in the analysis, and to weighting choices intended to account for phylogenetic interdependence of gene triplets. We performed the remaining analyses without weighting gene triplets.

We did not find a strong relationship between rate asymmetry and duplication time. Visually, the probability of a faster-evolving target copy among cases with rate asymmetry (P(ΔdL<0|ΔdL≠0)) had little dependence on duplication time (Fig. 4e). This result is inconsistent with our findings for single-gene duplications and with previous findings of accelerated rates in young duplications (Pegueroles et al. 2013; Pich i Roselló and Kondrashov 2014). However, we did not focus our efforts on estimating fine-grained, branch-specific rates and further work is needed to examine the issue of rate acceleration and deceleration after duplication in mammals.

Next, we examined how coarse-grained differences in the regulatory environments of the source and target copies impact the polarity of asymmetric evolution. We expect that the larger a duplicated region is, the greater the chance that the regulatory environment around a gene is carried along to the target genomic location. Consequently, we expected to see polarization decrease with the size of the duplication if asymmetry in regulatory environment between the source and target copies contributes to polarization. We found that polarity decreased with larger target copies, suggesting that larger duplications lead to lower polarity (Fig. 4f). For small duplications (i.e. when the target region is small) the substitution rate rT is far more likely to be elevated over rS than for large duplications. Notably, in the largest class of duplications, we still found polarization, suggesting that regulatory environments can extend past ∼1 Mb or that other factors influence polarity. Such long-range regulatory activity is consistent with the very largest enhancer-to-transcription start site distances found in the human genome (Gschwind et al. 2023). This suggests that regulatory interactions between elements over 1 Mb in genomic distance from one another may be more common than previously thought. Overall, the inverse relationship we found between polarization and duplication size supports the hypothesis that larger duplications are more likely to carry cis-regulatory elements and important co-expressed genes to the target genomic location, creating a similar regulatory environment to the target location and possibly increasing purifying selection on target copy genes.

We also expected polarity to increase with the genomic distance between copies, reasoning that distant target copies would experience different regulatory environments from the source copy while nearby target copies would experience similar regulatory environments. The dependence of polarization on genomic distance between the two copies is less clear, with polarity appearing more or less insensitive for intrachromosomal duplications (Fig. 4g). However, polarity was maximal for copies on different chromosomes, consistent with previously reported results in C. elegens (Katju 2013). While increasing distance between copies on the same chromosome appears to have a moderate influence on polarity, the novel regulatory environment faced by target copies on different chromosomes from their source copies may explain the polarity found in their evolutionary rates.

The position of a gene within a large, multigene duplicated region should also affect paralog rate asymmetry if the regulatory environment is responsible. Genes in the middle of a duplicated region are surrounded by a regulatory environment similar to the source region, while genes on the flanks are more exposed to the target environment. Our findings confirm this prediction, where the genes closest to the edge of large duplicated regions had the largest target-polarized fraction, which decreased for genes further from the flank (Fig. 4h). The fraction of source-polarized paralogs (P(ΔdL>0)) remained largely constant with distance from the flanks of duplicated regions, while the fraction of paralogs with symmetric evolution (P(ΔdL=0)) increased for genes further from the flanks. This trend disappears after about 8 genes from the duplication edge, although this is likely due to low sample sizes (Fig. S8).

While paralogous pairs within large duplicated regions clearly demonstrate polarity, we sought to determine whether paralogous pairs created simultaneously in a single multigene duplication event share polarities. Since the genes within a single duplicated region share a common regulatory environment, we expect pairs of paralogs created in a single multigene duplication to be have correlated polarities, so that the “choice” of the slower and faster evolving paralog occurs in a block-wise manner. To test this expectation, we defined the within-synteny block probability PSB(ΔdL<0) as the fraction of gene triplets within a duplicated region with ΔdL<0. If all genes on the target copy have evolved at a faster rate than their paralogs on the source copy PSB(ΔdL<0) will be one, while PSB(ΔdL<0) will be zero if the target copy genes have evolved slower than, or at the same as, their source copy paralogs. Intermediate probabilities reflect mixed polarities within duplicated regions. Using a null model that randomly assigns gene triplets to empirically determined duplicated regions, simulating what we would expect if the polarity of paralogous gene pairs was independently determined, we found that the PSB(ΔdL<0) distribution displayed significant enrichment of PSB(ΔdL<0)=0 and PSB(ΔdL<0)=1. This is strong evidence that polarity determination in multigene duplications tends to occur in a block-wise manner rather than independently for each paralogous gene pair, suggesting that the fate of a duplicated gene can be influenced by the other genes and noncoding elements simultaneously duplicated.

Finally, we explored the role of differential epigenetic markings and gene expression on asymmetric evolution of paralogs. We restricted our analysis of epigenetic markings to histone modifications with known associations with gene expression (Karlić et al. 2010; Gerstein et al. 2014; Kundaje et al. 2015; Singh et al. 2016) (Table S1). Unfortunately, experiments probing histone modifications are routinely performed only on a handful of organisms; out of the 194 species in our dataset, experimental data was available only for humans and mice. Out of all multigene duplications in human and mouse, only three could be reliably placed in the after-speciation category. According to our analysis, the human genome presents a single multigene duplication after speciation, which shows significant differences in both epigenetic markings and expression levels between the source and target copies, with higher markings and expression in the source copy (Fig. S9). The distributions of expression levels and epigenetic marks represented in Fig. S9 are for all cell types for which data were available. Thus, a broad distribution (with a wide range of expression or epigenetic marking levels) indicates variability across tissues, while a narrow distribution corresponds to homogeneity across tissues. The first set of duplicated mouse regions had two target copies associated with the source copy, and had significant differences in both epigenetic markings and expression levels between the source copy and both targets. The second multigene mouse duplication was on the X chromosome, in contrast to the previous two duplications which had both copies on autosomal chromosomes. In this duplication, the source and target copies had fewer significant differences in epigenetic markings and gene expression was not significantly different between any pair of paralogs. Overall, these results are consistent with some role of epigenetic markings in polarization and correlate lower expression levels with faster substitution rates, as expected.

Conclusions

Our findings provide substantial evidence for target polarization in a large dataset of mammalian gene duplications, although we find that rate asymmetry itself is relatively rare. In short, our analysis indicates an important role for location in polarizing substitution rates of paralogs: the target copy in the new locus created by the duplication event tends to evolve faster than the source copy in the ancestral genomic location. Around 17% of single-gene duplications are polarized, of which about 93% are target-polarized due more often than not to positive selection. For multigene duplications, we were able to demonstrate that polarization decreases with the size of the duplicated region, yet remains significant for duplications up to the scale of at least 1 Mb. That smaller duplications were more likely to be target-polarized and that target polarization decreases far from the flank of duplicated regions are both consistent with the hypothesis that target regulatory environments are responsible for target polarization. Moreover, we demonstrate that the asymmetry in substitution rate tends to occur in a block-wise manner, so that genes in one copy tend to all evolve slower or faster than their paralogs in the other copy.

The statistical methods we used in synteny detection are central to our approach. Leveraging our strict definition of nanosynteny, we developed a mathematically rigorous estimate of statistical significance for nanosynteny blocks. Using nanosynteny blocks as building blocks for microsynteny, we were then able to identify reliable regions of conserved gene order across a large, representative set of mammals. APES allowed for easy interpretation of synteny blocks, where commonly used dynamic programming algorithms can yield conflicting synteny blocks without any notion of statistical significance. Overall, this innovative approach brings together several previously isolated observations regarding the evolutionary rates of duplicated genes, offering a quantitative analysis of how genetic context influences duplicated genes in mammals.

Methods

Genome assemblies and annotations

We downloaded MAKER2 (Holt and Yandell 2011) genome annotations of 191 mammals and 1 bird (as an outgroup) from the DNA Zoo (Dudchenko et al. 2017), along with the GENCODE human genome annotation v43 and mouse genome annotation v32 (Frankish et al. 2019). Contig N50s, scaffold N50s, and BUSCO (Tegenfeldt et al. 2025) scores are summarized in Fig. S10.

Orthogroup detection

We used OrthoFinder 2 (Emms and Kelly 2015, 2019) to determine orthogroups, using DIAMOND (Buchfink et al. 2015) to identify putative homology relationships between genes. DIAMOND alignment scores are used as the edges of an orthogroup graph, with genes as nodes. The Markov Cluster Algorithm (MCL) (Van Dongen 2008) with inflation parameter 1.5 is then used to find communities in the orthogroup graph, yielding disjoint orthogroups. OrthoFinder also constructs a rooted species tree (Emms and Kelly 2017, 2018) and rooted gene trees for each orthogroup using DendroBLAST (Kelly and Maini 2013). Gene trees are reconciled with the species tree through DLCpar (Wu et al. 2014). DLCpar finds parsimonious gene tree reconciliations considering gene duplication, gene loss, and deep coalescence (also known as incomplete lineage sorting). Our species tree shares 64 species with Foley et al. (Foley et al. 2023) and has a normalized Robinson-Foulds distance of 0.129 as compared with the species tree of Foley et al., when both trees are pruned to contain only shared species. This means that about 87% of bipartitions of species created by removing a single internal tree branch are shared by the two trees.

Microsynteny inference with APES

We developed an algorithm called APES (APES Explore Synteny) in order to reliably infer microsynteny with a low chance of yielding false positive results. APES infers microsynteny in each pair of species by first finding regions of perfectly conserved gene order (nanosynteny), and subsequently piecing together statistically significant nanosynteny into microsynteny.

In order to remove the effects of simple tandem duplications on our microsynteny results, we condensed each set of consecutive genes belonging to the same orthogroup into a single representative gene, which we also call tandem duplication equivalence classes (TDECs). This commonly used strategy (Haas et al. 2004; Wang et al. 2012; Lovell et al. 2022) simplifies the identification of multigene duplications while retaining information about simple tandem duplications (where a single gene has been duplicated one or more times immediately adjacent to the original copy). Unless explicitly stated otherwise, we use “TDEC” and “gene” interchangeably for ease of reading.

Nanosynteny blocks were considered statistically significant if they contained three or more TDEC pairs in order to ensure that the expected fraction of nanosynteny blocks found purely due to chance were less than 0.05 for all genome comparisons (Fig. 2). In order to avoid including regions only representing more complex tandem duplications, we filtered out nanosynteny blocks containing fewer than three unique orthogroups. We then joined nanosynteny blocks based on two hyperparameter choices, controlling the maximum distance between nanosynteny blocks in both genomes, dnanomax, and the maximum distance of gene pairs from each other in the path between the nanosynteny blocks, ddotmax. See Fig. 2, Fig. S2, and SI Methods for details. Python code is available at https://github.com/DiPierroLab/GenomicTools.

All analysis of multigene duplications was performed on the remaining pairs of microsyntenic regions in each genome. We remained intentionally agnostic as to the mechanism producing large duplicate regions.

In order to infer the time of each multigene duplication event, we used the species tree and reconciled gene trees produced by OrthoFinder. Because OrthoFinder does not take synteny into account, the reconciliation process is independent for each orthogroup. However, in multigene duplications we must introduce the constraint that all paralogous pairs were produced simultaneously in a single duplication event. While reconciliation methods exist for this problem (Dondi et al. 2019; Delabre et al. 2020), we took a simpler approach designed to reduce the probability of underestimating duplication times (measured in millions of years from the present). We used a consensus of two independent methods. First, we used the reconciled gene trees from OrthoFinder. Each paralogous pair is assigned to a duplication event at a node in the species tree. We will refer to this as the duplication node. Each duplication node has an associated support, defined as the fraction of descendant leaves (i.e. extant animal genomes in the dataset) of the duplication node that have retained both paralogs resulting from the duplication event. We defined the duplication node of a multigene duplication as the earliest (closest to the root node), well-supported (≥0.5) duplication node out of all the paralogous pairs therein. If none were well-supported, then we simply took the earliest duplication node. Second, we searched for the sequence of orthogroups found in the multigene duplication in all genomes. If two or more copies of those orthogroup sequences were found in a species we added it to a list of multiple-copy species. The duplication node was then simply the MRCA of all multiple-copy species in the species tree.

We then took the earlier of the two duplication nodes from our two methods as the consensus duplication node. Using divergence times from TimeTree 5 (Kumar et al. 2022), the duplication time is defined as the time halfway between the divergence time associated with the duplication node and its parent species tree node.

Inference of amino acid substitution rates

For multigene duplications, we estimate amino acid substitution rates rather than estimating synonymous and nonsynonymous nucleotide substitution rates. Using amino acid substitution rates is justified in our analysis because of the large divergence times between the species in our dataset (such that nonsynonymous nucleotide substitutions may be in danger of saturation), and because we have reasonable estimates of divergence times between paralogs from OrthoFinder 2 and TimeTree. Additionally, we found close correspondence between dN/dS ratios and ΔdL, as seen in Fig. 3i and Fig. S6. We calculate substitution rates by dividing Levenshtein distances by duplication times. See the Supplementary material for details.
