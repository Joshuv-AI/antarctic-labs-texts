The relationship between job burnout and emotional intelligence among employees of teaching hospitals affiliated with Abadan University of Medical Sciences, Iran (2024)
Authors: Fatemeh Aminzadeh Zargar, Mohammad Jahansir, Atefeh Zahedi, Mohammad Mahboubi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundJob burnout and emotional intelligence are critical issues in healthcare organizations, as they directly affect employee performance, quality of care, and patient safety. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between job burnout and emotional intelligence among employees of teaching hospitals affiliated with Abadan University of Medical Sciences in 2024.MethodsThis cross-sectional analytical study was conducted among 325 employees working in teaching hospitals in southwest Khuzestan Province. Data were collected using validated and reliable questionnaires assessing job burnout and emotional intelligence through a mixed and convenience sampling approach. Descriptive statistics, the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, and univariate linear regression analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.ResultsThe majority of participants were female (74.5%), held a bachelor’s degree or higher (83.4%), and were employed in clinical departments (80.9%). Job burnout levels were moderate in 69.8% and high in 30.2% of participants. Emotional intelligence and its domains were significantly higher than the median values. Emotional intelligence demonstrated a significant inverse association with job burnout, such that each one-unit increase in emotional intelligence was associated with a 0.12-unit decrease in job burnout.ConclusionTo enhance emotional intelligence and reduce job burnout, it is necessary for managers and policymakers in the health system, especially in underprivileged areas, to take the necessary measures, particularly by providing welfare facilities and strengthening both process and outcome motivation, especially the impact on hospital accreditation, for hospital staff.

Introduction

Research has demonstrated that one of the significant environmental stressors capable of leading to chronic psychological distress is the type of occupation an individual holds. When work-related psychological pressure becomes excessive, it can result in physical and mental complications, endanger the individual’s health, threaten organizational objectives, and diminish performance quality. Employees who experience such pressures in their workplace often develop job burnout.” [1], Job burnout affects not only the individual nurse or employee but also compromises the quality of patient care, the functioning of healthcare organizations, and the entire health system. Burned-out nurses frequently exhibit disengagement from their responsibilities, which correlates with lower patient satisfaction, a rise in medical errors, and increased nursing turnover rates. Consequently, identifying and addressing the contributing factors to burnout in critical care nursing is crucial not only for safeguarding nurses’ mental and physical health but also for ensuring better clinical outcomes for patients [2].

According to Pelletier, “psychological stress and psychosocial factors play a considerable role in the development of chronic physical disorders” [3]. Hospital employees including physicians, nurses, nurse assistants, operating room technicians, anesthesiology staff, radiology technicians, and other healthcare professionals are directly responsible for safeguarding public health. Numerous studies have demonstrated that the overall health status of a population is closely linked to the physical and psychological well-being of healthcare workers [4]. This relationship arises because the quality of healthcare delivery largely depends on optimal job performance and high job satisfaction among these professionals. Conversely, any condition that compromises the health of hospital staff may reduce work efficiency [5], increase absenteeism [6], and contribute to fatigue and presenteeism [7]. Crucially, these phenomena, absenteeism and presenteeism, are directly conceptually linked to the progression of job burnout. When healthcare professionals consistently withdraw from the workplace (absenteeism) or remain on duty while functioning suboptimally due to psychological or physical distress (presenteeism), the resulting strain depletes their emotional and cognitive resources. This persistent depletion serves as a primary catalyst, accelerating the onset of emotional exhaustion and full-scale job burnout. Ultimately, these consequences lead to a decline in the quality of healthcare services and negatively affect patient safety and satisfaction [8].

Human resources constitute one of the fundamental pillars of organizational success. Employees’ motivation and morale exert a direct influence on job satisfaction and the effectiveness of organizational activities [9]. Occupational stress and job burnout are unavoidable aspects of professional life; however, their intensity varies across occupations. Hospital staff are consistently considered among the most vulnerable occupational groups due to high workload demands, continuous exposure to critical situations, and emotional labor, all of which contribute to elevated levels of burnout and psychological distress [10].

According to a study, the stressful environment of the emergency department is associated with increased job burnout and psychological issues among physicians. However, sensation seeking moderates this relationship: physicians with high levels of sensation seeking view work pressure as a challenge and demonstrate greater resistance to burnout, whereas those with lower levels tend to experience burnout more rapidly. Therefore, preventive interventions for burnout should consider both individual differences in sensation seeking and the reduction of occupational stressors [11]. These findings align with the broader understanding that prolonged exposure to occupational stress and anxiety is a key contributor to burnout symptoms One of the most common adverse psychological outcomes of persistent anxiety and emotional strain is job burnout. Several studies have reported that burnout symptoms typically emerge following prolonged exposure to occupational stress and anxiety [12].

Job burnout is a psychological syndrome associated with occupational stressors, and its manifestations may vary among individuals [13]. According to Maslach and Leiter, burnout comprises three core dimensions: emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and reduced personal accomplishment. Emotional exhaustion refers to feelings of energy depletion, fatigue, and emotional depletion. Depersonalization, also described as cynicism, involves negative, detached, or inappropriate attitudes toward patients and colleagues, accompanied by irritability and loss of idealism. Reduced personal accomplishment reflects diminished productivity, low morale, and a perceived lack of competence and adaptability [14]. Unlike depression, which affects multiple aspects of personal life, job burnout is specifically related to an individual’s professional role and work environment. Prolonged exposure to occupational stressors, unmet job demands, unfavorable working conditions, job dissatisfaction, and insufficient organizational support are key contributors to burnout [13]. Workplace environment and workload intensity are among the most critical determinants of burnout severity [15].

As a multidimensional syndrome, job burnout is associated with numerous adverse outcomes [16], including significant negative effects on employees’ mental health [17]. Psychological distress may initially manifest as anxiety, subsequently progressing to burnout, which in turn exacerbates mental health problems. This cascade of psychological consequences resembles a domino effect, wherein each condition not only precipitates subsequent disorders but also independently reduces work productivity and the quality of job performance [18]. Evidence suggests that creating a fair, peaceful, and supportive work environment for healthcare professionals has a direct and positive impact on patient care outcomes and patient satisfaction [19].

Emotional intelligence (EI), on the other hand, seeks to conceptualize and explain the role of emotions and feelings in human capabilities. It encompasses a set of non-cognitive skills and competencies that enhance individuals’ ability to cope with external demands, pressures, and emotional challenges. Emotional intelligence plays a vital role in organizational settings, and neglecting emotional processes or failing to manage them effectively can impose substantial costs on organizations [20].

Given the highly stressful nature of hospital work environments, and considering that healthcare professionals are entrusted with responsibilities directly related to human health and life, high levels of emotional intelligence are essential for hospital personnel. These individuals interact daily with a large number of patients and service recipients, making emotional regulation and interpersonal competence indispensable. Consequently, emotional intelligence is increasingly recognized as a critical professional competency, and extensive research has been conducted worldwide to examine its role in healthcare settings.

Emotional intelligence comprises skills such as emotional perception, information processing, emotional regulation, and management of one’s own emotions as well as those of others. These competencies have been shown to mitigate the negative effects of occupational stress among nurses and other healthcare workers [21]. Emotional intelligence is defined as the ability to recognize, understand, and utilize emotional information related to oneself and others in ways that facilitate effective performance and improved outcomes [22].

Evidence from a study indicated that nurses with higher emotional intelligence demonstrate greater professional competence compared to their peers. Within hospital environments associated with universities of medical sciences, higher emotional intelligence is linked to improved job performance, increased clinical competence, and enhanced capacity to manage high-pressure situations. Additionally, emotional intelligence exerts a significant positive effect on nurses’ job performance through the mediating role of clinical competence [23]. Therefore, emotional intelligence can be considered one of the most powerful predictors of job performance and a key determinant of leadership effectiveness and professional success.

Job performance refers to activities directly related to organizational goals and is measurable through occupation-specific indicators. It encompasses the knowledge and ability required to perform job-related tasks and demands practical competence, experience in using specialized tools and techniques, and appropriate professional behavior [16]. Given the crucial role of healthcare systems in promoting population health and national development [17], and the fact that no healthcare system can function effectively without a competent workforce [18], strengthening emotional intelligence among hospital staff is essential. Addressing gaps in factors that influence employee performance is therefore a priority [3].

Considering the unique geographical and occupational challenges faced by healthcare workers in southwest Khuzestan Province including harsh climatic conditions, poor air quality, limited access to adequate welfare facilities, insufficient infrastructure, extreme heat, persistent environmental pollutants, and higher living costs compared with more advantaged regions the present study was designed to investigate the relationship between emotional intelligence and job burnout among employees of teaching hospitals affiliated with Abadan University of Medical Sciences in 2024.

Methods

This cross-sectional analytical study was conducted in 2024 among employees working in the 3 teaching hospitals (Ayatollah Taleghani, Shahid Beheshti, and Vali-e-Asr) affiliated with Abadan University of Medical Sciences, located in southwest Khuzestan Province, Iran. The total study population comprised approximately 1,600 healthcare and administrative employees across these centers. Data collection was performed using self-administered questionnaires. Prior to participation, the objectives of the study were explained to all eligible individuals, and informed consent was obtained from those who agreed to take part in the study.

The sample size was calculated based on previous similar studies using the following formula [24]:

σ = 13.15

α = 0.05

d = 1.84

Based on this calculation, a minimum sample size of 196 participants was estimated. To account for the cluster effect (design effect = 1.5) and an anticipated non-response rate of 10%, the final sample size was increased to 325 participants.

Inclusion criteria were having at least five years of continuous work experience, willingness to participate in the study, and current employment in either administrative or clinical departments. Exclusion criteria included less than five years of work experience and incomplete questionnaire responses.

Sampling was conducted using a mixed (combined) and convenience sampling method. Each hospital was considered a cluster, and within each cluster, administrative and clinical departments were treated as strata. The number of participants selected from each hospital and each stratum was proportional to the total number of employees in that unit. Recruitment continued within each stratum until the required sample size was achieved.

Data were collected using three instruments: a demographic questionnaire, the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI), and the Trait Emotional Intelligence Questionnaire (TEIQue).

The demographic questionnaire included items on age, gender, marital status, educational level, number of children, monthly income, presence of chronic disease, work department (administrative or clinical), years of work experience, employment status, weekly working hours, monthly overtime hours, work shift pattern, and organizational position.

Maslach burnout inventory

Job burnout was assessed using the Maslach Burnout Inventory, which consists of 22 items across three dimensions: emotional exhaustion (9 items: 1, 2, 3, 6, 8, 13, 14, 16, and 20), depersonalization (5 items: 5, 10, 11, 15, and 22), and reduced personal accomplishment (8 items: 4, 7, 9, 12, 17, 18, 19, and 21).

Items were rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from very low (1) to very high (5). Item scores were summed and reported as mean scores. Total burnout scores were categorized as low (1–22), moderate (23–66), or high (> 66). Internal consistency reliability of the MBI subscales has been reported with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients ranging from 0.71 to 0.90 in the original validation study by Maslach and Jackson [25]. In Iran, the reliability and validity of the instrument were confirmed by Najafi, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.86 and a significant correlation with job satisfaction (r = 0.76) [26]. In the present study, the internal consistency of the MBI was confirmed with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.82.

Burnout severity for each dimension was classified as follows: emotional exhaustion (low < 25, moderate 26–39, high > 40), depersonalization (low < 6, moderate 7–14, high > 15), and reduced personal accomplishment (low < 36, moderate 37–43, high > 44). To enhance comparability with other studies, burnout scores were analyzed as mean values.

Trait emotional intelligence questionnaire

Emotional intelligence was assessed using the Trait Emotional Intelligence Questionnaire developed by Petrides and Furnham in 2001. This instrument consists of 30 items across four subscales: optimism (items 20 and 24), perception of emotions in self and others (items 2, 6, 11, 17, 19, 21, 23, and 26), self-awareness (items 1, 3, 4, 5, 9, 12, 13, 15, 27, 29, and 30), and social skills (items 7, 8, 10, 14, 16, 18, 22, 25, and 28).

Responses were recorded on a seven-point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (7). Items 2, 4, 5, 7, 8, 10, 12, 13, 14, 16, 18, 22, 25, 26, and 28 were reverse scored. Scores for the subscales and the total emotional intelligence are calculated by summing the respective item scores rather than averaging them. Consequently, the total score ranges from 30 to 210. Because the standard TEIQue instrument does not define strict standardized numerical cut-offs for “low,” “medium,” or “high” severity levels, the theoretical median (the midpoint of the scale, which is a score of 4 per item) is used as a reference threshold. Accordingly, a summed total score significantly higher than the theoretical median of 120 indicates a high level of emotional intelligence. The questionnaire demonstrated acceptable reliability (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.86) and construct validity through exploratory factor analysis in the original study. Its reliability and validity were also confirmed in Iranian populations by Azghandi et al. [27]. In the current study, the internal consistency of the TEIQue was calculated, yielding a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.73.

Collected data were coded and entered into SPSS version 27. Descriptive statistics, including frequency (percentage), mean, and standard deviation, were used to summarize demographic characteristics and study variables. Normality of data distribution was assessed using histograms and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test.

Based on previous literature, gender, age, marital status, number of children, and history of chronic disease were considered potential confounding variables [28]. Univariate linear regression analysis was used to examine the association between background variables, emotional intelligence, and job burnout, as well as between emotional intelligence and demographic variables. Variables with a p-value < 0.20 in univariate analysis were entered into a multivariate linear regression model to evaluate independent predictors of job burnout while controlling for confounders. The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. A One-Sample t-test was used to compare the mean scores of emotional intelligence and its dimensions against their respective theoretical scale medians.

Results

A total of 325 employees from teaching hospitals affiliated with Abadan University of Medical Sciences participated in this study. Of these, 74.5% were female and 62.5% were married. The majority of participants held a bachelor’s degree or higher (83.4%) and were employed in clinical departments (80.9%).

The mean age of participants was 35.87 ± 8.70 years. The mean weekly working hours were 48.63 ± 16.56, and the mean monthly overtime hours were 50.36 ± 40.13. Most participants had no children (48.6%), had 5–9 years of work experience (29.8%), worked rotating shifts (73.5%), earned 10–20 million IRR per month (60.3%), and reported no underlying chronic disease (84.3%). Univariate linear regression analysis

Univariate linear regression analysis was conducted to assess the association between study variables and job burnout (Table 4). Emotional intelligence showed a significant inverse relationship with job burnout (B = − 0.12, p < 0.0001). Monthly overtime hours were positively associated with burnout, such that each additional hour of overtime increased burnout by 0.05 units (p = 0.002).

Participants working in clinical departments reported significantly higher burnout scores than those in administrative departments (B = 3.79, p = 0.02). Rotating shifts were associated with a 3-unit increase in burnout compared with fixed shifts (p = 0.04). Employees without chronic diseases experienced significantly lower burnout than those with underlying conditions (B = − 5.35, p = 0.002). Other demographic variables did not show statistically significant associations.

Multivariate linear regression analysis

Variables with p < 0.20 in univariate analysis were entered into the multivariate linear regression model (Table 5). The final model was statistically significant (p < 0.0001) and explained 14% of the variance in job burnout (adjusted R² = 0.14).

Emotional intelligence emerged as the strongest predictor of job burnout (B = − 0.12, standardized β = −0.25). Female gender, absence of chronic disease, fewer monthly overtime hours, and employment in administrative departments were also significantly associated with lower burnout levels.

Discussion

The present study examined the relationship between job burnout and emotional intelligence among employees of teaching hospitals affiliated with Abadan University of Medical Sciences in southwest Khuzestan Province. The findings revealed that job burnout among hospital staff was at a moderate to high level, while emotional intelligence and its dimensions were higher than the median values. Most importantly, emotional intelligence demonstrated a significant inverse association with job burnout, indicating that higher emotional intelligence is associated with lower levels of burnout.

Working in the specific geographical and occupational context of southwest Khuzestan characterized by extreme heat, persistent air pollution, limited welfare infrastructure, relatively high living costs, and working conditions comparable to more advantaged regions without corresponding compensatory benefits requires substantial psychological resilience and motivation. Continuity of employment in healthcare settings under such circumstances demands not only professional competence but also strong emotional regulation and coping abilities. The moderate-to-high levels of burnout observed in this study are therefore not unexpected and reflect the cumulative burden of environmental, organizational, and occupational stressors.

The inverse relationship between emotional intelligence and job burnout observed in this study aligns with previous research suggesting that prolonged occupational stress can lead to emotional exhaustion, cynicism, reduced personal accomplishment, decreased organizational commitment, absenteeism, impaired concentration, and reduced productivity [29, 30]. Individuals with higher emotional intelligence are better equipped to recognize, regulate, and manage their emotions, thereby reducing emotional dysregulation and vulnerability to burnout.

Mikolajczak et al. emphasized that individuals with high emotional intelligence possess superior emotional regulation skills, which mitigate emotional mismatch and buffer the negative effects of occupational stress [31]. Similarly, Navas (2018) described emotional intelligence as a critical competency that enhances job performance, reduces stress and burnout, and improves organizational commitment and job satisfaction [32]. Given the well-established negative relationship between stress and job performance and the strong positive association between emotional intelligence and performance [33], the findings of the present study further support the protective role of emotional intelligence in demanding healthcare environments.

The results also demonstrated that employment in clinical departments was associated with higher levels of job burnout compared with administrative positions. Additionally, rotating shift work was associated with increased burnout relative to fixed shifts. These findings are consistent with the inherently demanding nature of clinical work, which involves direct patient care, emotional labor, time pressure, and exposure to critical and life-threatening situations. Notably, each one-unit increase in the emotional intelligence score was found to be associated with a 0.12-unit decrease in the job burnout score, underscoring the potential value of emotional intelligence–focused interventions in clinical settings. These findings are in line with the study by Nooryan et al. [34], who reported that training in emotional intelligence components significantly reduced stress and anxiety among physicians and nurses working in intensive care units.

In contrast to some previous studies, demographic variables such as weekly working hours, marital status, number of children, educational level, employment duration, employment status, organizational position, and income did not show significant associations with job burnout in the present study. However, female employees experienced significantly lower levels of burnout compared with male employees. This finding is inconsistent with the results reported by Olanrewaju et al. [35], who found higher burnout levels among female healthcare workers. Differences in working hours, organizational context, geographical conditions, and access to welfare facilities may explain these discrepancies.

Age demonstrated an inverse but non-significant relationship with emotional intelligence in this study. Nightingale et al. (2018) reported that healthcare professionals with higher age, education, and work experience experienced lower burnout when effectively utilizing emotional intelligence [36], which is generally consistent with the trends observed in the present study. However, the unique contextual challenges of southwest Khuzestan may contribute to stagnation or decline in emotional intelligence with increasing age, potentially due to reduced job motivation, limited career advancement opportunities, or insufficient innovation within the work environment.

Consistent with previous domestic studies, higher age, education, and work experience have been identified as protective factors against burnout, while shorter work experience and lower educational levels are associated with increased vulnerability to emotional strain [37]. The present study also demonstrated that each additional hour of monthly overtime was associated with an increase in job burnout, corroborating extensive evidence that excessive workload is a key contributor to burnout among healthcare workers [38–40].

Conclusion

This cross-sectional study demonstrated that job burnout among teaching hospital employees is at moderate to high levels, whereas their emotional intelligence is relatively favorable. The key finding is a significant inverse association between emotional intelligence and job burnout, indicating that employees with higher emotional intelligence experience lower levels of burnout. Given the cross-sectional design, definitive causal relationships cannot be established. Furthermore, while convenience sampling was utilized due to the practical constraints of varying hospital shift schedules and staff availability, this approach may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader healthcare population. However, this pattern was consistently observed across various occupational and demographic subgroups within the hospitals and is likely generalizable to similar healthcare settings, particularly in relatively underserved regions with challenging climatic conditions.

In terms of practical implications, healthcare managers are advised to implement actionable programs to enhance employees’ emotional intelligence (e.g., workshops on emotional regulation and communication skills). Concurrently, it is essential to reduce burnout‑aggravating factors by moderating overtime hours and improving organizational support, particularly in clinical departments. For the future, longitudinal and interventional studies are essential to more confidently confirm the role of emotional intelligence as a preventive strategy against job burnout and to design tailored interventions that fit local contexts. Such an approach can serve as an effective step toward improving staff well‑being and enhancing the quality of patient care.
