Encoding functional edges in graphs to model spatially varying relationships in the tumor microenvironment
Authors: Ashley P. Tsang, Santhoshi N. Krishnan, Reva Kulkarni, Sagnik Bhadury, Marina Pasca di Magliano, Timothy L. Frankel, Arvind Rao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Comprehensive characterization of the tumor microenvironment (TME) is essential for understanding cancer progression and developing effective, patient-specific therapies. Spatial context of the TME is particularly important, and exists across multiple scales—from the molecular to cellular to tissue levels. However, current methods are modality-specific and lack flexibility in effectively modeling the TME. We introduce SPIFEE, a flexible graph deep learning framework designed to model the TME and uncover spatial insights across multiple levels of biological organization. SPIFEE increases the expressivity of graph-based representations by directly encoding spatially varying functional vectors into graph edges. Additionally, it represents graph nodes as unique TME entities (e.g., cell types, phenotypic clusters, molecular pathways). This general formulation is modality-agnostic and also offers cross-modality integration. We demonstrate the versatility of SPIFEE across multiplex immunofluorescence, H&E histopathology, and spatial transcriptomics datasets, enabling rich characterization of cellular, phenotypic, and pathway-level interactions. SPIFEE shows improved performance when leveraging function-based edge representations and outperforms existing spatial modeling approaches. Moreover, by integrating graph attention mechanisms, SPIFEE reveals multi-scale spatial interactions most associated with disease state and patient survival. Overall, SPIFEE enhances the flexibility and representational power of spatial graph modeling, and enables deeper interrogation of the TME, advancing the potential for personalized cancer analysis.

Introduction

The tumor microenvironment (TME) is a complex ecosystem of immune cells, tumor cells, and other supporting cell types, where interactions and signaling dynamics play critical roles in cancer development and progression1. Understanding the spatial organization of the TME is key to uncovering how its diverse components communicate and influence one another. These interactions occur across multiple spatial scales—from molecular pathway signaling, such as the crosstalk between the interferon-gamma and PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint pathways2, to direct cell-cell contacts, exemplified by cytotoxic T cells engaging with tumor cells3, and broader patterns involving the coordinated activities of cellular neighborhoods4–7. Traditional spatial assays include routine hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and immunohistochemistry (IHC). More recently, advances in spatial omics technologies—such as spatial transcriptomics (ST) and multiplexed immunofluorescence (mIF), including co-detection by indexing (CODEX)8–have enabled high-resolution profiling of molecular and proteomics information in situ. These modalities enable characterization of biological patterns and interactions among scales, while preserving their spatial context9.

To extract meaningful insights from these spatial data, many computational approaches employ graph-based modeling, as it provides a natural representation of spatial relationships within the tissue10–13. These approaches are often designed for single modalities, offering limited flexibility across modalities. mIF and CODEX-based methods represent each individual cell as a node, and encode the rich molecular and morphological features into the graphs11–13. In contrast, H&E whole-slide images (WSIs) lack molecular granularity and are typically processed at the patch or tile level, which provides only coarse morphological information. Graph representations developed for cell-resolved data, therefore, do not directly generalize to H&E. Additionally, models built for mIF-scale data do not readily scale to the much larger tissue areas of H&E WSIs, which can exceed tens to hundreds of thousands of patches14,15. In SPIFEE, we represent each slide as a graph of unique TME entities (e.g., distinct cell types), which substantially reduces graph size and complexity. This abstraction enables the framework to generalize seamlessly across spatial modalities with differing resolutions and feature spaces.

Additionally, existing methods are limited in their expressivity. Many approaches assign edge weights solely based on spatial proximity, often using binary indicators to denote whether nodes are neighbors14,16,17 or fall within a distance threshold18–20. Other methods assign scalar weights derived from Euclidean distances11,13. However, proximity-based weighting alone does not fully capture the biological complexity of cell signaling and spatially varying interactions. Scalar distances or adjacency indicators capture only whether cells are near one another, but they cannot represent richer spatial phenomena such as clustering, repulsion, or changes in interaction intensity across distances. Recognizing this limitation, recent frameworks such as Proximogram21 and BiGraph12 have begun incorporating spatial interaction metrics into edge attributes. Proximogram represents graphs with nodes corresponding to distinct cell types, where edges encode spatial statistics. In contrast, BiGraph represents graphs with nodes as individual cells, and edges encode interaction strength that changes with cell-to-cell distance. Yet, these interaction summaries are still ultimately reduced to scalar values, which inadequately represent spatially varying, multi-scale biological processes. Therefore, there is a need for richer edge representations, where edges are defined by functions or structured descriptors rather than single scalar quantities, to more accurately model the complexity of biological interactions across space.

In this work, we present SPIFEE (Spatial Proximogram Integrating Function-Encoded Edges), a framework that models the TME using graph representations that encode functional relationships between entities. Our approach builds upon Proximogram, which first introduced graph construction on the basis of unique cell types rather than individual cells21. SPIFEE relaxes prior constraints enforced in Proximogram, integrates function-encoded edges, and introduces additional graph features that generalize effectively across diverse spatial data modalities.

SPIFEE leverages graph neural networks (GNNs) to learn meaningful representations of these graphs to differentiate disease outcomes. We specifically leverage the power of graph attention networks (GATs) for insights into model decision-making, through the computation of attention weights that assign importance to graph features22. Altogether, this framework enables greater expressivity and interpretability, enhancing our ability to uncover interaction dynamics within the tissue and to identify cellular interactions that contribute to the formation of the TME.

Results

Overview of SPIFEE

SPIFEE models spatial relationships in the TME by representing each sample as a graph, where nodes correspond to TME entities (e.g., cells, clusters, spots) and edges encode functions that describe relationships between entities as a function of spatial distance or other contextual factors. Node features encode phenotypic or functional properties of the entity. These embeddings may be derived from deep learning models or other representation learning techniques that capture biologically meaningful information. Meanwhile, edge features are multi-dimensional feature vectors, representing a sampled function characterizing the spatial relationship between the entities (Fig. 1a). Patient cohorts spanning multiple modalities and scales

We demonstrate our method on two main independent datasets: UM-PD, an mIF dataset from the University of Michigan, representing pancreatic diseases (PD), and TCGA-NSCLC, from The Cancer Genome Atlas, representing non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC).

UM-PD consists of mIF stained tissues. The dataset consists of 198 samples (imaged cores), representing 105 patients diagnosed with either chronic pancreatitis (CP) or pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC). A 6-marker panel was used to phenotype 5 cell types of interest: antigen presenting cells (APC), cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTL), epithelial cells (Epi), T helper cells (THelper), and T regulatory cells (Treg). In addition to imaging data, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) was performed on six organ donors, providing transcriptomic profiles for normal pancreatic tissue.

TCGA-NSCLC is a dataset consisting of H&E-stained WSIs. We leverage this dataset in two ways, generating the following sub-datasets we will refer to as: (1) TCGA-NSCLC-HPC and (2) TCGA-NSCLC-ST. These are TCGA-NSCLC datasets that utilize histomorphological phenotype clusters (HPC) information and have been in-silico stained for ST, respectively. TCGA-NSCLC contains information for lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) versus lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) subtype classification as well as LUAD survival.

TCGA-NSCLC-HPC utilizes HPCs discovered by Quiros et al.23 associated with the subtype classification and LUAD survival objectives. These HPCs, discovered in a self-supervised manner, represent distinct morphological patterns captured across tile patches in the WSIs. For the subtype classification objective, we obtained 607 samples (WSIs), and for the survival regression objective, we obtained 395 samples (patients). These LUAD samples included information on survival time and censorship. Of the 395 patients included in the survival analysis, 199 experienced the event of interest, and 196 were censored.

TCGA-NSCLC-ST uses slides generated with DeepSpot24, a deep learning method that predicts ST directly from H&E images. The authors have validated its application specifically on the TCGA-NSCLC cohort. We leveraged 994 samples (WSIs), representing 898 patients for the subtype classification objective.

The additional datasets, Stanford-CRC and LUAD-LUSC-Visium, are used for generalization and method validation, and are introduced in later sections. SPIFEE enables flexible representation of many functions to model the TME

In this work, we construct multiple sets of graphs, where each set corresponds to a distinct edge type used to model interactions within the tissue. For each edge type, a graph is generated per sample, sharing the same node set but differing in how edges and edge features are defined. Specifically, we implement edge types capturing the following spatial co-localization and interaction relationships: G-cross area under the curve (AUC), G-cross function, and modified geographical weighted regression (mGWR) function (Methods). Briefly, G-cross measures the degree of spatial mixing between two entities over a distance range25,26, and mGWR captures the variation in interaction between entities across fields of view27,28. We use the scalar G-cross AUC as a baseline to compare against the richer, functional edge representations.

SPIFEE predicts PD from single-cell level data and identifies key cell-cell interactions

We constructed graphs from UM-PD such that nodes represent unique cell types in each sample, and node features representing cell communication embeddings derived from the associated scRNA-seq data (Fig. 1b). As such, edges capture spatial relationships between cell types. Graphs corresponding to each edge type are then used as input to our GAT models (Methods). Given the limited sample size, we employ repeated k-fold cross-validation, reporting performance metrics averaged across all 50 validation folds and 95% confidence interval bounds.

We compare SPIFEE against two competitive existing methods: Mew31 and SPACE-GM11. These are both GNN-based approaches designed for mIF data in which nodes correspond to individual cells with features including normalized biomarker expression and cell size, and edges are weighted by cell-cell distances.

To enable fair comparison, while also highlighting SPIFEE’s flexibility, we evaluate three different node feature types: (1) the same feature vector used by Mew and SPACE-GM, (2) cell-type proportion per sample, and (3) our scRNA-seq-derived communication embeddings. Across all node feature types, SPIFEE outperformed both Mew and SPACE-GM. On both UM-PD and Stanford-CRC, SPIFEE achieved gains using the same biomarker expression and cell size node features used by the baseline methods. On UM-PD, scRNA-seq embeddings were the second-best performing feature type, while on Stanford-CRC, cell proportion features were second-best.

For the Stanford-CRC dataset, matched scRNA-seq data were unavailable. To generate scRNA-seq embeddings for this cohort, we leveraged a publicly available dataset from seven primary CRC patients with the closest matching cell types (Methods).

Additionally, SPIFEE hyperparameters optimized on UM-PD were applied directly to Stanford-CRC, demonstrating strong generalizability to independent datasets. These results emphasize the flexibility of our graph representation, which also enables substitution of any available node features: matched scRNA-seq embeddings when possible, related publicly available scRNA-seq data when matched data are absent, or imaging-derived features such as biomarker expression, cell size, or simple cell type proportions.

Functional representations retain predictive information for PD better than scalar reductions

We next sought to investigate: (1) whether high edge attention within each group corresponds to stronger or weaker interactions between entities, and (2) what additional information the full edge function provides beyond the scalar summary. To achieve this, we focused on the top highly attended edges in the G-cross function models (Table 3). We plot the average G-cross functions per group, and compute the AUCs of each function within each group. To assess if the groups are significantly different based on the AUC values, we perform statistical testing using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

We observe that the (Epi, Treg) edge, which was uniquely highly attended to in the PDAC group, corresponds to increased interactions (visualized by the average functions and the AUC in Fig. 2a), as compared to the CP group (p < 0.001). In CP, the (Epi, APC) edge was most highly attended to, and was also highly attended to in PDAC. From Fig. 2b, high interactions of these cell types is indicative of CP, while low interactions characterizes PDAC (p < 0.001). For (Epi, THelper) interactions, the reduction to the AUC of the functions does not provide information on how these patterns influence CP or PDAC (Fig. 2c). However, in visualizing the G-cross functions, there is a clear difference in how the interactions trend over distance. Particularly interesting is the (Epi, CTL) case (Fig. 2d), where we again observe no significant differences between the AUCs. While the average functions also yield similar AUCs (PDAC=2.903, CP=2.716), the shape of the curves reveals a more nuanced distinction. Specifically, the CP curve rises more steeply at shorter distances compared to PDAC, indicating a higher probability of close-range epithelial-CTL interactions. In contrast, PDAC requires longer distances to reach comparable cumulative interaction probabilities. This suggests that, although the overall frequency of interactions may be similar, the spatial dynamics differ substantially–potentially reflecting more immediate or direct immune engagement in CP, versus more dispersed or delayed interactions in PDAC. This highlights the limitations of AUC as a summary metric, and underscores the value of analyzing the shape and features of the G-cross function itself for deeper insight into spatial cellular dynamics.

SPIFEE identifies disease-specific interactions that are uniquely captured using functional metrics

We next analyze edge pairs that are differentially attended to across the CP and PDAC cohorts. In contrast with the previous analysis where we assess top attended edges in isolation, here we identify edges that have been differentially attended to across CP and PDAC, and consequently point to differences in importance assigned to a given edge across cohorts. This was examined for the G-cross AUC, G-cross function and mGWR function edge type models individually. The modified GWR metric captures the variation in spatial interactions across a given field of view, in contrast to the G-function and the corresponding AUC metric, capturing infiltration of cells around a reference point at a fixed distance25,27.

The observed differences in top attended edges (Table 3) and in relative attention scores (Fig. 3), emphasizes that each edge type, as well as the respective metric, offers a unique perspective on the spatial arrangement and interactions of cells in the TME. At the same time, disregarding directionality, we observe that some pairs of interactions occur across all three distinct models, the interactions between Tregs and APCs being an example. Of the top-attended edges, three key edge pairs were differentially attended to between CP and PDAC across all three edge type models, with (APC, Treg), (Epi, Treg), and (THelper, Treg) being highly attended to in PDAC over CP. For context, PDAC has a highly immunosuppressive and hypoxic environment compared to CP, elevating the role and interactions of Tregs with other elements in the TME32–34. From our previous study, it is well-established that Treg interactions with cancerous epithelial cells are elevated in PDAC compared to CP21. Tregs play a critical role in immune evasion for tumor cells, explaining their increased recruitment to tumor regions in PDAC, compared to CP, where there is no protection for the Tregs from cytotoxic immune response35. We observe that there is also an increasingly important role assigned to the (APC, Treg) edges in PDAC compared to CP. In this study, APCs are primarily CD163-expressing cells, a subset of which have been known to exhibit tumor-promoting characteristics together with Tregs36, explaining their heightened importance in the TME.

Similar trends are observed in other interactions with Tregs, indicating that Treg interactions may potentially be the key differentiator between CP and PDAC. This may be explained by several factors. Firstly, Tregs are present at a higher proportion in the pancreatic TME in relation to other immune cells, so their interactions are selectively highlighted more in PDAC. Thus, Treg interactions are greater in PDAC over CP, where other cells have a much more active role in promoting and maintaining various stages of inflammation, and in turn offer protection for the Tregs in the PDAC environment. Secondly, they are also more actively involved in the creation and maintenance of the environment in PDAC37. In contrast, CP is an inflammatory condition compared to PDAC, so the inherent mechanisms and signaling pathways involved may not all be the same29.

Our results on the classification task are competitive with existing works. Jiang et al.38 performed a comprehensive benchmark across eight methods on the same TCGA-NSCLC dataset for the identical LUAD vs. LUSC classification task. Reported accuracies ranged from 0.785 to 0.970, with a mean of 0.876, placing SPIFEE well within the performance range of these existing H&E-based approaches. There are some caveats to this comparison, particularly in different data splits used, as well as resolution of images used. The competitive models were trained on TCGA H&E slides at a higher resolution (10x magnification), whereas our analysis was carried out at a lower resolution (5x), which typically provides less fine-grained morphological detail and therefore represents a more challenging classification setting.

In predicting LUAD survival, the mGWR function edge type slightly outperforms the others according to the concordance-index (C-index) and Akaike information criterion (AIC) (Table 5). To predict survival time, we trained the GAT model on a regression task to predict survival rank, then leveraged embeddings from the model as input to a Cox proportional hazard model39 (Methods). We then stratified patients into low- and high-risk groups based on their true survival rank. Using repeated k-fold cross-validation, the survival model achieved a training C-index of 0.667 ± 0.018, indicating moderate generalization with no evidence of severe overfitting. Further, our results are on par with the previously reported C-index score of 0.6 by Quiros et al. 23. However, our approach enables additional insights and interpretability on cluster interactions most influential on stratifying risk.

On evaluating the attention scores for each sample in our survival model, we identified key edge pairs associated with high-risk and low-risk across the three edge types, with the results summarized in Supplementary Table 1. For the best-performing edge type model based on the G-cross function, we observe that (HPC0, HPC1) and (HPC1, HPC0) are the top-attended edges for high-risk and low-risk patients, respectively, with the results flipped for the mGWR function. (HPC20, HPC22) and (HPC1, HPC16) are the top-attended G-cross AUC edges for high-risk and low-risk cases. The LUAD HPC reference atlas table provided by Quiros et al. provided some histopathological and morphological context, and was used to infer biological features underlying our presented results.

We observe that HPC20, a connective mesenchymal tissue cluster consisting of peribronchial fat with moderate lymphocytic infiltration, is highly correlated with HPC22 in high-risk patients in the G-cross AUC model. HPC22, in turn, is characterized as mesenchymal tissue associated with large vessel lumina, with moderate lymphocytic infiltration23. This can be a sign of strong stromal growth and increased vascularization supporting tumor growth, or it can also indicate vascular invasion, which correlates with a higher risk of metastatic spread40,41.

Stromal HPC16 is associated with elastosis, indicating the presence of inflamed elastic fibers in and around tumor tissues in the lung, thus influencing tumor architecture and remodeling in this tissue42. The interaction of inflamed elastic-fibers-rich stroma (HPC16) and mesenchymal tissue associated with large vessel lumina is quite important, as it may be indicative of an active vascular and tissue remodeling within the TME, indicating potential tumor progression40,43. Additionally, the epithelial-mesenchymal tissue transition has been potentially linked to the proliferation of cancer-associated fibroblasts, which have been identified as an important marker for LUAD transition44. This was an HPC that was not identified as one previously associated with either better or worse survival23, thus highlighting that our framework is able to capture relationships or attributes that may not have been identified using other modeling paradigms. The interaction between diverse, inflamed sparse tumor tissue (HPC9) with highly immune tumor-stromal tissue (HPCs 0,1) across multiple models, points to immune-stromal niches or aggregates that are present in tumor tissue, with high intercellular crosstalk via cytokines and growth factors45. We also observed that (HPC16, HPC1) interaction edges were highly attended to in both low-risk and high-risk patients in the G-cross AUC model. HPC1 clusters consists of mostly inflamed compact stroma-predominant tumor tissue with high lymphocytic infiltration, and was correlated with good outcomes in the reference study23. The presence of immune-rich HPC1 environments in proximity to regions of active tissue modeling can also be potentially indicative of an active immune response being mounted against the tumor, leading to classic markers such as inflammation. This has been alluded to in specific breast cancer cohorts and can potentially explain the elevation of these edges by the model in low-risk patients46. Additionally, the high attention given to edges with HPC0 and HPC1—both correlated with good outcomes in the reference paper, and the highest attending edges across the G-cross function and mGWR models—validates that our framework is able to identify interactions that are clinically significant23.

Surprisingly, all three models pick up interactions between various tumor-stromal HPCs (HPCs 1, 0, 9), which are usually transitory regions within the tissues. Thus, our framework enables the identification of patterns and interactions at the tumor-stroma interface, providing insights into the evolution of the TME. This can facilitate the discovery of markers for treatment efficacy and disease progression through rigorous downstream analyses.

In the G-cross AUC model, we observe that the (JAK-STAT, PI3K), (JAK-STAT, Hypoxia) and (JAK-STAT, EGFR) were identified as key pairs for both LUAD and LUSC. In LUAD, both JAK-STAT and EGFR pathways are heavily involved in tumor remodeling, as both pathways have been implicated in promoting PD-L1 expression in various cells in the TME, promoting immune escape and tumor progression in the lung47–49. In LUSC, observing regions of JAK-STAT and Hypoxia upregulation in close proximity is not a surprising finding, as both pathways have a shared impact on immune suppression and increasing tumor plasticity in the TME. The hypoxia pathway is heavily involved in tumor plasticity through metabolic reprogramming and cancer cell proliferation50,51.

In the G-cross function model, we observe that the (Hypoxia, EGFR) and (TRAIL, Hypoxia) edges were identified as the top attended edges for LUAD and LUSC, respectively. EGFR mutations have been identified as a critical factor influencing LUAD malignancies52. Their long-term moderate interactions with hypoxic regions in the TME have been associated with resistance to EGFR-inhibitor-based therapies53. Integrating clinical context into the workflow can help confirm if these interactions are elevated in patients non-responsive to these therapeutic regimens for further research. On the other hand, regions having high TRAIL pathway scores seem to correlate strongly with high hypoxia activity in LUSC. A hypoxic environment is a classic marker of the worsening tumor environment, with specific hypoxia-promoting markers being induced by the tumor cells increasing aggressiveness and progression to tumor malignancy, specifically in LUSC54. The presence of other death ligand pathways in the surrounding environment, such as TRAIL, induces increased Interleukin-8 (IL-8) expression, which is a marker of cancer progression55.

In contrast, the edge pairs between spots with elevated (Estrogen, TGFb) and (Hypoxia, JAK-STAT) pathways are identified as highly attended to in LUAD and LUSC, respectively for the mGWR function-based model. The proximity of regions with enriched Estrogen and TGFb pathways in LUAD is consistent with literature, as both are involved in promoting cell invasion and metastasis in the microenvironment56. A similar observation can be made about the heightened interactions of regions exhibiting Hypoxia and JAK-STAT pathways in LUSC, both of which are key pathways involved in immune modulation in the TME. Many studies demonstrate a potential cooperative relationship in animal models and other tissue between the hypoxic environment and JAK-STAT activation57. A key point to note is that the Estrogen pathway-associated edges are highly attended to in LUAD, which is supported by earlier studies demonstrating the upregulation of genes and associated pathways in LUAD and other cancer types58,59.

In conclusion, we observe that both the G-cross function and mGWR function based models highlight key features and pathways that are strongly associated with the cancer subtypes and progression in literature, and how regions of high pathway activity interact with each other. Concurrently, our methodology also critically highlights common pathology-specific driver pathways (such as Estrogen and JAK-STAT) across multiple viewpoints of querying both real and simulated biological data. However, due to the imputed nature of the dataset, further validation on a real ST cohort is necessary to establish the full validity of the interactions.

Validation of SPIFEE-nominated pathway interactions on matched bulk transcriptomics and real ST data

In order to validate the findings from our H&E ST pathway analysis, we adopted two main strategies: (1) we performed pathway correlation analysis on associated bulk TCGA-NSCLC transcriptomics and identified pathways pairs that were highly correlated within LUAD and LUSC, and (2) we replicated the pathway analysis on an independent 10x Genomics Visium ST LUAD and LUSC dataset (n = 20) from De Zuani et al.60. Using the G-cross function, we then identified overlap between top pathway pairs nominated by our method and found in the real ST data.

First, we performed pathway analysis separately on the associated LUAD and LUSC bulk trascriptomics samples using PROGENy, and computed pathway enrichment z-scores for all 14 human pathway signatures in the database61. We then performed correlation testing between pathway pairs within the bulk data. Spearman correlation with a p-value threshold p < 0.05 with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction was used to compute pairwise correlations across samples for all pathways. Finally, the top 20 pathway correlation pairs with the lowest FDR scores were selected for comparison against the high attention edge pairs identified from the SPIFEE H&E ST models. The results are presented in Supplementary Tables 6 and 7, where the bolded pathway pairs are those that overlapped with our SPIFEE findings in Table 7. For example, we observed that TRAIL–EGFR pairs are prevalent across both LUAD and LUSC groups in both the bulk data and from the DeepSpot-based results from SPIFEE. This indicates that the SPIFEE top edges identified are not purely spurious, but are identifying biologically relevant signals.

Secondly, we also modeled spatial interactions between regions exhibiting high pathway activity on the real ST dataset (n = 20, nLUAD = 12, nLUSC = 8). Similar to the analysis we performed on our virtual stained ST dataset, PROGENy pathway analysis was performed, and pathways were assigned to each spot according to highest enrichment61. Following this, spatial proximity analysis was done using the G-cross AUC framework previously used in our edge generation process to identify the top interacting pathways in the LUAD and LUSC tissue samples (at a distance of 150 microns). The top ten pathway interactions identified from the real ST dataset, and their corresponding G-cross AUC scores are presented in Supplementary Table 4. We found that edges nominated by SPIFEE overlapped with some of the top edges identified in the real ST dataset, again demonstrating biological significance of our framework.

Notably, JAK-STAT self-interactions and Hypoxia self-interactions show strong overlap in both LUAD and LUSC between model attention patterns and pathway importance scores. Particular, in the LUSC samples where spots with high PI3K, Hypoxia, TGFb, JAK-STAT, and TRAIL tend to co-localize near each other with high proximity scores. When querying interactions between spots enriched with differing pathways, we observed TRAIL and PI3K spots near each other. This interaction was observed as highly enriched for predicting LUAD in the G-cross function based model, which is encouraging even after accounting for potential batch effects such as acquisition modality, tumor stage, and treatment variables.

Discussion

In this work, we presented SPIFEE for graph representation and learning that better preserves the underlying dynamics of the TME and enables the discovery of novel biological insights into disease and progression. We applied the method to multiple spatial and biological resolutions, from single-cell to phenotypic cluster to molecular pathway levels, and multiple modalities, spanning mIF, CODEX, H&E, and ST.

By incorporating function-based edge representations, we observed improved performance in PD classification compared with using scalar summaries of the same spatial G-cross functions. This indicates that richer edge attributes better preserve spatially varying information that is important for distinguishing PD states. We further demonstrated the flexibility of SPIFEE by extending it to H&E and ST data. On these data, function-based edges generally improved performance, although the magnitude of improvement was minimal. We hypothesize that the benefit of function-based edges may depend on dataset characteristics. In the H&E and ST data, the distance-based edge types we employed may face limitations due to data granularity. Our data were on the order of tiles and spots, as opposed to mIF, which is much detailed with cell coordinate information. We observed the strong improvement with the function based edges also on our CODEX-based generalization dataset. Additionally, in settings where node features are highly informative, the model may rely less on edge attributes, diminishing the relative contribution of complex edge functions. Conversely, when node features are limited or less predictive, richer and more descriptive edge representations may play a critical role in improving performance (Supplementary Table 4).

Additionally, we demonstrated an approach to interpreting the model by directly leveraging the attention weights. While explanation methods such as GNNExplainer62 and PGExplainer63 have been developed for GNNs, they come with certain limitations—including the need to retrain or fine-tune explainer models, and the fact that they typically operate on one graph or one instance at a time, limiting scalability and generalization across samples. To address these limitations, we proposed a method to evaluate edge importance by aggregating edge attention weights per node within each graph. By directly leveraging the attention coefficients from trained GAT models, our approach avoids the need for retraining and enables analysis at the dataset or group level. There are, however, implementation invariant methods such as integrated gradients, that may offer more flexibility, and can potentially reconcile biological intuition with model decision making64. These methods could also be integrated with our attention-based implementation, which we leave for exploration in future works.

With our method, we provided context for the biological relationships that were most influential in differentiating the diseases and risk groups. Using mIF data, we identified cell-cell interactions that were significantly enriched in either the CP or PDAC patient groups. Using cluster-level features derived from H&E data, we identified higher-order spatial patterns between clusters that were associated with low- and high-risk LUAD patients. With ST derived from H&E data, we nominated spatial-based pathway interactions that discriminated between LUAD and LUSC patients. We implemented and compared several edge functions representing spatial co-localization, highlighting both shared patterns across functions and the unique insights each one provided. It is important to emphasize that SPIFEE is intended to generate hypotheses regarding potential interactions, which require further rigorous validation. Nonetheless, our validation analyses indicate that the models do not merely identify spurious relationships; rather, they highlight interactions that both corroborate existing literature and warrant further investigation in downstream studies.

A limitation of our analyses lies in the resolution of the imaging data used. With the H&E HPC data, our analysis was limited by the availability of only broad histopathological assessments of tissue clusters, with little to no information on their cellular composition or gene expression profiles. This limited the depth of biologically relevant inferences that could be made about different tissue niches interfacing with each other. Further exploration on a dataset with richer histopathological, genetic, and clinical information would improve our ability to draw more in-depth biological and clinical hypotheses in downstream analyses.

Additionally, while our study leverages DeepSpot-predicted ST to enable large-scale evaluation on TCGA H&E slides, we fully acknowledge that such methods remain imperfect and may introduce biases or hallucinations inherited from the underlying model. DeepSpot represents the current state of the art in ST prediction, yet its accuracy is not equivalent to experimentally measured ST24. For this reason, we performed independent validation using matched TCGA bulk transcriptomics and an external Visium ST dataset. Across both datasets, we observed substantial overlap between SPIFEE-nominated pathway-pathway interactions and those supported by real transcriptomic and spatial measurements, providing empirical grounding for the biological signals identified in our DeepSpot-derived analyses.

Method development in this field of “virtual ST” continues to advance rapidly65. As these predictive ST methods improve, SPIFEE can be readily adapted to incorporate them, enabling corresponding improvements in the biological insights SPIFEE generates. Although our implementation focuses on spot-level ST, SPIFEE is not restricted by data modality or feature dimensionality. The framework is compatible with single-cell spatial data, multi-modal measurements, and tissue-context features, making it readily extensible as higher-resolution and larger-scale ST datasets become available. Together, these considerations reflect both the current limitations of predictive ST technologies and the long-term adaptability and robustness of SPIFEE as a general framework for interrogating spatial molecular interactions.

In summary, SPIFEE is a flexible framework for incorporating functional relationships into graphs and identifying key interactions that influence clinical outcomes and underlying pathologies. The framework is generalizable across data modalities and biological scales. While our study focused on spatial co-localization functions, SPIFEE can be readily extended to capture other types of spatial, biological and temporal relationships and dynamics, enabling the discovery of more complex and nuanced biological interactions within the TME.

Methods

UM-PD dataset

The dataset consists of 198 mIF samples (imaged cores), with 55 from CP cases and 143 from PDAC cases, representing 105 patients—34 with CP and 71 with PDAC. Each core measures 0.6mm in diameter and was taken from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks from patients who underwent surgical resection at the University of Michigan, with the methods previously outlined in Lazarus et al.66.

Tissue slides were first subjected to serial rounds of antigen retrieval, followed by primary and secondary antibody staining. Tyramide signal amplification was applied with a unique fluorophore for individual antigens. Phenotyping for immune and associated cell types was performed using the respective antibodies for CD3, CD8, CD163, PD-L1, pancytokeratin, and FOXP3. DAPI nuclear staining was implemented for nuclear identification and to assign a spatial location to individual cells. The cell location coordinates and corresponding phenotype labels were computed, utilizing user-set thresholds to determine marker expression using Vectra InForm software (Akoya Biosciences). For this study, 5 cell-type phenotypes were selected based on mIF marker expression and a user-defined complex algorithm using the Inform software. For our study, any cell expressing cytokeratin in PDAC is considered to be a tumor but is referenced as an epithelial cell throughout the paper for consistency. Imaging data from normal pancreatic tissue was unavailable for multiplexed analysis. Further details on pre-processing and this cohort can be found in Krishnan et al.21.

We additionally leverage a dataset composed of scRNA-seq tissue samples from 6 Gift of Life (GoL) healthy Michigan donors with normal pancreatic tissue. Normal patient samples obtained from the Michigan branch of GoL, a federally regulated organ and tissue procurement and recovery program, were approved by the GoL research review group. The cell types are annotated according to cell-specific clustering, with further details on acquisition and cell processing explained in Carpenter et al.67 and Krishnan et al.21.

TCGA-NSCLC dataset

The original TCGA H&E collection comprised 1021 WSIs, with 513 from LUAD and 508 from LUSC. A subset of LUAD samples contain additional information on survival, specifically on survival time and censorship.

For the TCGA-NSCLC-HPC data, we utilized data produced by Quiros et al.23, originating from TCGA-NSCLC. We use a subset of these samples that had associated phenotypic cluster information of interest for our method. For the subtype classification objective, we used 607 WSIs, comprising 324 LUAD and 283 LUSC samples, representing 516 patients. For the survival regression objective, we used 395 samples. Additional details on pre-processing and this dataset can be found in the original manuscript23.

For the TCGA-NSCLC ST data, we utilized data produced by DeepSpot24. DeepSpot is a deep learning based method that leverages foundation models and spatial context to predict ST from H&E. For subtype classification, this dataset included 994 WSIs, comprising 524 LUAD and 470 LUSC samples, representing 898 patients. ST data produced is on a spot-level, with full details found in the original manuscript24.

Graph representation for UM-PD

For UM-PD, we leverage both the scRNA-seq and mIF data for graph construction. In these graphs, we represent unique cell types as nodes, and edges represent spatial relationships between cell types.

Each node, corresponding to a unique cell type within the sample, is assigned a feature vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${x}_{v}\in {{\mathbb{R}}}^{128}$$\end{document}xv∈R128, which encodes properties relevant to intra- and inter-cellular communication. Graph representation for TCGA-NSCLC-HPC

For TCGA-NSCLC-HPC, we leverage H&E data only for graph construction. In these graphs, we represent unique morphological clusters as nodes, and edges represent spatial relationships between clusters. Over 40 unique clusters, defined as histomorphological phenotype clusters (HPCs), were discovered via self-supervised learning by Quiros et al.23, and capture morphologically distinct phenotypic patterns. We focus on a subset of the top 20 HPCs of interest, defined as the ten HPCs most strongly correlated with each patient group, determined by the original manuscript.

Each node, corresponding to a unique HPC within the sample, is assigned a feature vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${x}_{v}\in {{\mathbb{R}}}^{128}$$\end{document}xv∈R128, which encodes an embedding that captures the phenotypic characteristics of the HPC. To guarantee that the representative tile embedding corresponds to an actual observed sample, we use the medoid instead of the mean. Graph representation for TCGA-NSCLC-ST

For TCGA-NSCLC-ST, we construct graphs in which nodes represent pathways and edges represent spatial relationships between pathways. Specifically, for each ST spot, we use PROGENy75 (via the decoupler package76) to infer pathway activities. Utilizing the 14 human pathway signatures, we assign to each spot a set of pathway activity weights. Each spot is then associated with the pathway for which it has the maximum activity weight.

Each node in the graph corresponds to a unique pathway and is assigned a feature vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${x}_{v}\in {{\mathbb{R}}}^{135}$$\end{document}xv∈R135. In this vector, each element encodes the gene activity score for the pathway, as obtained from the PROGENy analysis. We select the top 100 genes per pathway and collect the set of genes that are present across all 14 pathways. This resulted in a total of 135 unique genes. For each of the 14 pathways, we then populate the vector with the associated gene activity score. For any pathway that does not include a given gene in its top 100, we assign a value of zero for that gene, indicating neither up- nor down-regulated status. We use a GAT-based model due to its improved performance over traditional graph convolution networks and its ability to support interpretability through attention weights22. Model training, evaluation, and hyperparameters

For model training and evaluation, we use repeated k-fold cross-validation, with k = 5 folds and n = 10 repeats. To prevent data leakage, we ensure that multiple samples from the same patient are assigned to the same fold. Class balance is maintained within each fold based on the distribution in the full dataset. This is implemented using the scikit-learn package80.

We implement Optuna, a Bayesian optimization framework for hyperparameter tuning81. We tune the following hyperparameters for each dataset (except the generalization dataset) over the ranges indicated in parentheses: hidden channels (16, 32, 64, 128, 256), number of layers (2, 3, 4), dropout probability (0.0 to 0.5), learning rate (1e-5 to 1e-2), weight decay (1e-6 to 1e-3), and batch size (16, 32). Optimal hyperparameters per dataset are in Supplementary Table 2.

TCGA-NSCLC-HPC survival prediction model

For the LUAD survival prediction task, the objective is to estimate each patient’s survival time. We compute a survival ranking score for each patient using the GuanRank algorithm82, which incorporates both overall survival time and censoring information. We use the same model architecture described above, but replace the classification head with a regression head and train the model using mean squared error loss.

We then leverage the graph embeddings learned by the GNN model (i.e., the output from the graph-level pooling layer) as input features for a Cox proportional hazards regression model. This allows us to model the relationship between learned graph-level representations and patient survival outcomes. The Cox model is implemented via the lifelines Python package83, incorporating both survival time and censoring information.

Stanford-CRC generalization dataset

The Stanford-CRC dataset is a publicly available cohort introduced by Wu et al.11. Samples were profiled using CODEX with 40 protein markers, and 14 cell types were phenotyped. For benchmarking, we used 8 of these cell types, merging all tumor-related subtypes into a single tumor class. We merge these to enable implementation of the scRNA-seq node embedding, in which only general tumor cell annotation is available. We followed the same data-splitting strategy as used in the Mew31 and SPACE-GM11 manuscripts, which partition the dataset by coverslip.

Biomarker expression and cell-size node features were computed following the procedures described by the existing methods. For the scRNA-seq embedding node features, matched scRNA-seq data were not available for this cohort. Therefore, we obtained a publicly available scRNA-seq dataset from the TISCH2 database84, selecting the dataset (E-MTAB-810785) with conditions and cell-type annotations most closely matching those in the Stanford-CRC cohort. We mapped cell types from the Stanford-CRC dataset to the TISCH2 scRNAseq cell type annotations as follows: B cell was mapped to B, Blood vessel to Endothelial, CD4 T cell to CD4TConv, CD8 T cell to CD8Tex, Granulocyte to Mast, Macrophage to Mono/Macro, Stroma to Fibroblasts, and Tumor to Malignant. This mapping allowed us to derive representative scRNA-seq embeddings for each cell type present in the Stanford-CRC dataset despite the lack of a directly matched scRNA-seq cohort.

TCGA-NSCLC bulk transcriptomics dataset

For the TCGA-NSCLC-ST data validation, we obtained associated bulk RNA-seq data available for a subset of samples (n = 786, nLUSC = 261, nLUAD = 524) from the TCGA Research network database86. Standard QC, normalization and gene identifier mapping were performed on the input counts data for downstream analysis.

LUAD-LUSC Visium validation dataset

The LUAD-LUSC Visium validation data is a publicly available dataset first presented in De Zuani et al.60. The ST dataset was obtained using the 10x Genomics Visium platform, with a spot diameter of 55 microns, with further detailed pertaining to tissue preprocessing, image acquisition and post-processing on SpaceRanger (version 1.1.0)) presented in the original manuscript. The standard Seurat preprocessing (normalization, scaling, PCA and UMAP) was performed on the data, with additional filters for mitochondrial DNA and long non-coding genes.

Pathway analysis on every spot was performed using PROGENy, assigning one of 14 human pathway signatures to each based on top computed pathway scores75. Following this, spatial proximity analysis was done using the G-cross function AUC framework previously explained to identify proximity patterns in pathway prevalence across LUAD and LUSC tissue samples at 150 microns.
