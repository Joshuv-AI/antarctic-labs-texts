Unraveling volcanic impacts in semi-arid climates: machine learning and CMIP6 insights from Krakatau and Santa Maria
Authors: Abdolazim Saman, Gholamreza Roshan, Stefan W. Grab, Muhammad Mubashar Dogar, Tarig A. Ali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Volcanic eruptions are among the strongest natural climate forcings, yet their regional impacts remain poorly resolved, particularly in semi-arid hotspots. Here, we investigate the climatic fingerprints of the Krakatau (1883) and Santa Maria (1902) eruptions on surface temperatures over Iran by integrating CMIP6 multi-model simulations with Random Forest machine learning. This dual framework enables the first systematic assessment of Iran-specific volcanic teleconnection dynamics. Both eruptions triggered prolonged and spatially extensive cooling episodes, characterized by biphasic responses: an intense initial cooling wave followed by a weaker secondary phase linked to aerosol dispersion and decay. Spatial patterns reveal that localized anomalies in northern and western Iran eventually expanded to cover nearly the entire country. Teleconnection analysis highlights the Pacific Warm Pool and Tropical North Atlantic as dominant modulators, with additional contributions from ENSO and the North Pacific, including an anomalous negative TNA phase that amplified Santa Maria’s impact. Our results demonstrate how volcanic cooling is regionally mediated through ocean–atmosphere teleconnections, and reveal systematic model–observation mismatches in intensity and persistence. By bridging physical climate modeling with machine learning, this study provides a novel framework for attributing volcanic impacts in teleconnection-sensitive, semi-arid regions, with implications for climate adaptation, agricultural planning, and regional resilience under future extreme events.

Introduction

Volcanic eruptions are among the most powerful natural forcings of the Earth’s climate system, capable of inducing widespread, multiyear temperature and circulation anomalies at global and regional scales1,2. When explosive eruptions inject large volumes of sulfur dioxide (SO₂) into the stratosphere, these gases form sulfate aerosols that scatter incoming solar radiation, resulting in surface cooling3–6. Such events often trigger cascading impacts on atmospheric and oceanic circulation, disrupt monsoon systems, and influence global teleconnection patterns7–10.

While major volcanic eruptions such as Krakatau (1883) and Santa Maria (1902) have been extensively studied for their global climatic impacts, a persistent knowledge gap remains regarding their region-specific effects. Most global- or hemispheric-scale analyses emphasize broad temperature perturbations and teleconnection responses but often fail to capture localized heterogeneity and the nuanced interplay between volcanic forcing, topography, land–atmosphere feedbacks, and socio-economic vulnerabilities.

The climate of Iran and the broader Middle East is strongly influenced by its geographical position between 25°N and 40°N, placing it largely within the arid and semi-arid climatic zones of the subtropics and mid-latitudes11–13. Large-scale atmospheric circulation regimes such as the subtropical high-pressure belt in summer and the mid-latitude westerly disturbances in winter critically shape temperature and precipitation patterns across the region14. Moisture sources from the Caspian Sea, Mediterranean Sea, and Persian Gulf contribute variably to precipitation regimes, with orographic effects from the Alborz and Zagros Mountain ranges enhancing rainfall on windward slopes and creating significant rain-shadow effects over central and eastern Iran15,16. These mountains act as barriers that prevent moist maritime air masses from penetrating into the interior plateau, leading to sharp spatial gradients in both precipitation and humidity17. As a result, northern coastal regions exhibit humid climates with high annual precipitation, whereas much of central and southern Iran remains dry due to sustained atmospheric stability and dominance of subsidence under the subtropical highs18. Furthermore, teleconnection patterns such as ENSO and the North Atlantic Oscillation modulate these circulation regimes, amplifying or reducing seasonal precipitation and temperature extremes in various parts of Iran19,20. Understanding these prevailing circulation patterns, moisture pathways, and teleconnection influences is essential for interpreting both present-day climate characteristics and variability in the context of regional climate studies. This gap is particularly critical in semi-arid, teleconnection-sensitive regions like Iran, where stratospheric aerosol-induced cooling can alter atmospheric circulation and regional precipitation patterns, exacerbating groundwater depletion, reducing crop yields, and intensifying food insecurity for the nation’s 90 million inhabitants21,22. Such climatic perturbations may also amplify environmental stressors, including desertification and dust storms, further aggravating socio-economic challenges such as rural-to-urban migration, resource-related conflicts, and diminished resilience to compounded climate extremes (Akbari et al., 2021)23,24. Despite these high stakes, quantitative assessments of Iran’s temperature responses to volcanic eruptions remain scarce. To our knowledge, no previous study has systematically combined high-resolution climate model simulations with machine learning techniques and teleconnection-aware analyses to unravel the spatiotemporal mechanisms driving post-eruption temperature variability in this climatically sensitive region.

A growing body of research highlights the critical role of climate teleconnection indices, such as the El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO), Pacific Warm Pool (PWP), North Atlantic Oscillation (NAO), and Tropical North Atlantic (TNA), in modulating the regional climate aftermath of volcanic eruptions25–27.

Large explosive tropical volcanic eruptions inject substantial amounts of sulfur dioxide (SO₂) into the stratosphere, where it oxidizes to form sulfate aerosols that persist for 1–3 years. These aerosols scatter shortwave radiation, cooling the surface, and absorb longwave radiation, warming the lower stratosphere, thereby altering meridional temperature gradients and driving dynamical changes in atmospheric circulation28–31. Stratospheric warming modifies thermal wind balance and planetary wave propagation, affecting the polar vortex, midlatitude jets, and teleconnection patterns such as the NAO, while surface cooling alters sea surface temperature gradients, influencing ENSO through atmosphere–ocean feedbacks32,33. Atmospheric responses occur within months, but slower oceanic adjustments, including the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC), produce lagged and sometimes decadal responses in teleconnection indices34. Therefore, observed changes in NAO, ENSO, and decadal variability reflect the integrated effects of surface cooling, stratospheric warming, and coupled atmosphere–ocean feedbacks, rather than surface cooling alone28,29,35,36.

However, these studies often focus on global or zonal means, leaving the regional-scale teleconnection-volcano interactions underexplored, especially in data-sparse or geopolitically underrepresented regions, such as the Middle East. Understanding how multiple overlapping teleconnection signals combine or interfere following volcanic forcing remains a challenge in climate science given that models have not fully captured their influence.

Moreover, while Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6) models have substantially improved representation of aerosol-cloud-radiation interactions37, (Aubry et al., 2021), most prior studies on volcanic forcing rely on single-model or globally averaged analyses, limiting insight into region-specific dynamics. In parallel, machine learning (ML) methods, especially ensemble approaches such as Random Forest (RF), have emerged as powerful tools to uncover nonlinear and lagged dependencies in climate systems (Barnes et al., 2019; Reichstein et al., 2019), yet their application in volcanic-climate studies remains nascent.

The present study adopts the following conceptual framework: Major tropical eruptions generate direct radiative surface cooling globally, while simultaneously modulating large-scale teleconnection patterns through stratospheric warming and ocean–atmosphere feedbacks (e.g., promoting La Niña-like ENSO states or negative TNA phases).These volcanically modulated teleconnections then regionally amplify or shape the intensity, persistence, spatial distribution, and seasonal timing of temperature anomalies in teleconnection-sensitive semi-arid regions like Iran. Our primary objective is to quantify this regional modulation step—elucidating the relative contributions and synergistic interactions of multiple teleconnections in driving post-eruption temperature variability over Iran—using CMIP6 multi-model ensembles. A key methodological advance is the use of Random Forest analysis, which outperforms traditional linear correlations by capturing non-linear relationships, temporal lags, and interactive effects among indices (e.g., PWP × Niño3.4 or NAO × TNA), thereby providing more robust attribution of event-specific drivers.

We focus on the major tropical volcanic eruptions of Krakatau (1883) and Santa Maria (1902), which occurred during the pre-industrial era with minimal anthropogenic greenhouse gas forcing. This selection allows for a cleaner isolation of the volcanic signal from the strong background warming trend and confounding climate change signals that characterize more recent eruptions. The Pinatubo (1991) eruption is not considered in this study due to its occurrence during the industrial era, where strong anthropogenic warming complicates the isolation of the volcanic signal; investigating it would require a different methodological focus and is left for future work.

We aim to fill these critical gaps by investigating spatiotemporal temperature responses over Iran, following the major tropical volcanic eruptions of Krakatau (1883) and Santa Maria (1902). This is achieved through a combination of CMIP6 multi-model ensembles and teleconnection-aware machine learning analyses. Specifically, we explore how climate indices influence the amplitude, persistence, and spatial footprint of temperature anomalies across Iran, following each eruption.

To our knowledge, previous studies, such as38, investigated volcanic impacts in the Middle East using a high-resolution regional climate model (RCM) with CMIP6 forcings, including volcanic, orbital, solar, and greenhouse-gas changes. Their work highlighted the strong short-term cooling from large eruptions such as Samalas (1255-1264 CE) and provided a framework to evaluate regional climate extremes, bridging the gap between coarse global models and regional climate dynamics. Building on this foundation, this is the first study to integrate CMIP6 multi-model ensembles with Random Forest modeling to systematically evaluate the role of 16 major teleconnection indices, individually and interactively, in modulating Iran’s post-eruption temperature variability. By quantifying lag correlations, feature importance, and synergistic effects among indices (e.g., PWP × NINO3.4; NP × TNA), we provide novel insights into the mechanisms through which volcanic signals are transmitted and amplified across ocean–atmosphere systems into the Middle East.

Materials and methods

The primary objective of this study is to characterize the temporal evolution, intensity, and persistence of climate responses following two major tropical volcanic eruptions: Krakatau (1883) and Santa Maria (1902). To achieve this, we employ simulations from the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6), which provide state-of-the-art representations of aerosol–atmosphere interactions and robust spatial resolution9,39.

From the 57 available CMIP6 models, we implemented a systematic screening procedure to identify ensembles capable of realistically capturing post-eruption temperature anomalies. For each model, baseline variability was defined using the 5th and 95th percentiles of surface temperature over a 60-month pre-eruption window. Models that exhibited anomalies beyond these thresholds in the post-eruption period were retained as suitable candidates. This approach ensures that only models demonstrating a clear volcanic signal were incorporated into the analysis, thereby enhancing the robustness of our ensemble.

Applying this criterion resulted in the selection of 28 models for Krakatau and 34 models for Santa Maria (Table 1). These ensembles form the basis for subsequent evaluations of volcanic climate impacts. This screening procedure also implicitly addresses inter-model agreement and ensemble spread by selectively retaining models that consistently capture the volcanic radiative forcing and its dynamical response, thereby reducing spread from simulations that poorly represent eruption-induced signals or excessive internal variability.

For processing temperature time series, a 20-year period was considered, encompassing 5 years before and 15 years after each eruption. To reduce short-term fluctuations and enhance signals related to the eruptions, a 12-month moving average filter was applied42,43. Subsequently, a bootstrap statistical significance test with 5,000 iterations was conducted on the data. To standardize the data, model outputs were resampled to a spatial resolution of 0.5 × 0.5 degrees, followed by multi-model averaging. Additionally, to assess the heterogeneity of model responses, the interquartile range (IQR) was calculated44. Temperature anomalies were area-averaged over the geographical domain of Iran, defined by longitudes 44°E to 63°E and latitudes 25°N to 40°N.

The random forest (RF) algorithm

To investigate the impact of volcanic eruptions on teleconnection indices and their possible climatic impacts on Iran, temperature anomalies and climatic indices were calculated for a five-year period prior to each eruption, which then provides a baseline for comparing post-eruption conditions. These anomalies were derived by subtracting the baseline mean from the total data and used as inputs for modeling. Data for these indices were sourced from reputable observational and reanalysis climate databases (e.g., HADISST, 20CR, ERSST). This choice is deliberate, as CMIP6 models—despite advancements—often exhibit biases in simulating the amplitude, phase, and regional teleconnections of modes such as ENSO, NAO, and TNA, particularly in the data-sparse late 19th and early twentieth centuries45–47. Using standardized observational/reanalysis indices ensures greater reliability, consistency with the broader literature, and avoidance of model-specific artifacts that could introduce additional uncertainties in regional attribution. Temporal correlation analysis, incorporating various time lags, was performed between temperature anomalies and these indices to identify the role of large-scale patterns in amplifying or mitigating thermal effects following the eruptions.

To detect lagged patterns and uncover hidden relationships between variables, the Random Forest (RF) algorithm, a machine learning method based on ensemble decision trees, was employed due to its robust capability in handling complex, non-linear data. RF enhances model accuracy by generating multiple independent decision trees and combining their predictions through averaging or voting, thereby improving stability and generalizability. In this study, RF was implemented with tuned parameters, such as the number of estimators (n_estimators) and random seed (random_state), to ensure result reproducibility. The primary objective was to model the complex relationships between climatic indices and temperature while accounting for temporal lags, enabling the identification of eruption impacts across different time scales.

Feature importance scores (referred to as importance score in this study) are computed using the standard impurity-based method (mean decrease in impurity) implemented in scikit-learn’s Random Forest Regressor. Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) was applied as part of the modeling process to select the five most influential features affecting temperature. Additionally, to better understand interactions between features, pairwise combinations of indices were calculated, and their importance was assessed using the RF model, facilitating the identification of combined and non-linear effects among variables. Model validation was conducted using training data and feature importance analysis, demonstrating the model’s ability to detect meaningful patterns.

The integration of multiple teleconnection indices was performed to comprehensively capture the climatic dynamics and their interactive effects on temperature. Major volcanic eruptions can simultaneously modulate several large-scale climate modes (e.g., ENSO, NAO, PDO) through stratospheric warming and ocean–atmosphere feedbacks. These modulated teleconnections then shape the regional temperature response over Iran, amplifying or prolonging the direct radiative cooling induced by volcanic aerosols. This multi-index approach allows the RF model to quantify the relative contributions and synergistic interactions of these modes in driving post-eruption temperature variability, identifying significant temporal lags and providing a clearer mechanistic understanding of how volcanic forcing is transmitted from global to regional scales. These results also lay a foundation for future studies in this field.

Results

Spatiotemporal temperature responses to Krakatau

The statistical significance threshold for temperature anomalies was set at -0.15°C, with departures exceeding this value considered as significant. Data indicate that from August 1883 to June 1887, 48 months exhibited significant negative temperature departures, with a mean anomaly of -0.49°C during this period. These temperature changes can be categorized into distinct patterns, including an intense primary phase of cooling, a transitional phase with gradual temperature recovery, and a final prolonged period of slight cooling. The following analysis details these patterns based on the behavior of time series data and provides some explanations.

Significant cooling began in September 1883, with a temperature anomaly of -0.27°C. The cooling trend intensified progressively, peaking in October 1884 with a temperature anomaly of -0.95°C. This 14-month period, from September 1883 to October 1884, is identified as the primary phase of post-volcanic cooling. The mean temperature anomaly during this phase was -0.71°C, reflecting a substantive and sustained influence of aerosol cooling impacts over Iran.

Following peak cooling in October 1884, the temperature pattern transitioned into a new phase that lasted until June 1886. During this period, temperature anomalies remained significant and below the -0.15°C threshold (e.g., -0.35°C in November 1885; -0.19°C in June 1886), but with an overall gradual increase in temperature. This transitional phase indicates the onset of diminishing aerosol effects in the stratosphere given reduced concentration. The mean temperature anomaly during this phase was -0.30°C, suggesting some temperature recovery.

From November 1884 to August 1887, temperature departures remained negative and significant, but not as pronounced as during the primary phase of cooling. The mean temperature anomaly during this period was -0.40°C. The decline in anomaly intensity from -0.95°C in October 1884 to -0.12°C in December 1887, implies a gradual recovery that continued until the end of this period (Fig. 1).Fig. 1CMIP6-derived 12-month moving average temperature anomalies in Iran (1878–1898), indicating the Krakatau (1883) cooling effect.

The autumn maps of 1883 illustrate the onset of initial cooling after the Krakatau eruption. Results indicate that 82.86% of Iran’s territory (particularly western provinces) experienced temperature anomalies ranging from -0.5°C to -0.4°C (Fig. 2). Additionally, 15.70% of the area exhibited anomalies of between -0.4°C and -0.3°C. Only 1.42% of the area records anomalies of between -0.3°C and -0.2°C.

In winter 1883, the pattern shifted, when northern and northwestern regions experienced anomalies of 0.1°C to 0.2°C over 50.39% of the national area, while 48.60% of the territory records anomalies of between 0.2°C and 0.7°C. This temperature recovery may be owing to local warm airflows or a delayed aerosol response, while central and southern regions remained largely unaffected.

The year 1884 marked the peak of cooling, with more than 98.60% of Iran’s land area experiencing temperature anomalies of between -1.2°C and -0.5°C across all seasons. Figure 2 highlights this intense cooling over northern, western, and central regions. Only 1.30% of the area, mainly in the southeast, reported anomalies of between -0.5°C and -0.4°C, indicating more moderate cooling here.

In 1885, varied patterns of temperature recovery emerged. During spring, 90.55% of the area (especially the north and west) maintained anomalies of between -1.2°C and -0.5°C. However, by summer, this percentage dropped to 4.77%, while 71.47% of the land, including central provinces, experienced anomalies of -0.5°C to -0.4°C. In autumn, 45.40% of southern regions recorded anomalies of between -0.4°C and -0.3°C, and 54.42% between -0.3°C and -0.2°C (Figs. 2 & 3).

By 1886, temperature recovery began in central and eastern regions. In spring, 56.86% of the area exhibited anomalies of between -0.3°C and -0.2°C, while 42.44% experienced anomalies of -0.2°C to 0.0°C. During summer, 67.46% of the area still recorded anomalies of between -0.3°C and -0.2°C. In 1887, 89.84% of southern regions returned to near-zero anomalies in summer while the northwest still recorded anomalies of between -0.3°C and -0.2°C across 48.44% of the region.

By 1888, central regions displayed temperature anomalies ranging from -0.2°C to 0.0°C across 46.20% of the area in summer, increasing to 59.41% in autumn. Northeastern regions exhibited anomalies between 0.0°C and 0.1°C, covering 13.91% of the area in summer and expanding to 51.13% in winter (Figs. 2 and 3).

Regional comparisons indicate that in 1884, western and northern parts of Iran exhibited temperature anomalies across > 98% of the area, with cooling observed. In contrast, southern and southeastern regions recorded cooling across ~ 1.30% of the area, with temperature anomalies ranging from -0.5°C to -0.4°C. Temperature changes were first observed in the central regions, followed by the south and east (Figs. 2 and 3).

Among these, the Pacific Warm Pool (PWP) index is identified as the most influential variable, with an importance score of 0.704. This index, representing the extent and intensity of warm waters in the western Pacific Ocean, exhibits strong fluctuations in response to radiative changes induced by volcanic aerosol injection. A strong and positive correlation between PWP and Iran’s temperature anomalies (correlation coefficient 0.56 with a 2-month lag, p < 0.05) suggests that variations in this region significantly contribute to the Iranian thermal regime. The short lag time further underscores the rapid and direct role of tropical dynamics in transferring volcanic effects to mid-latitude regions such as Iran.

Santa Maria: impact on teleconnection indices and Iranian temperatures

Following the massive eruption of Santa Maria in 1902, notable changes emerged in regional and global climate regimes50. These changes had significant impacts on major climate teleconnection indices and, ultimately, on temperature anomalies observed in Iran. By analyzing deviations in climate indices and Iran’s temperature anomalies relative to a five-year pre-eruption mean, and employing the RF model, we evaluate the predictive power and influence of each index.

Results indicate that some indices (especially those related to the Atlantic Ocean and mid-latitude regions) played a critical role in modulating Iranian temperature anomalies after the Santa Maria eruption the Tropical South Atlantic (TSA). Among the indices, the TSA index emerges as the most influential standalone predictor, with an importance score of 0.415. TSA exhibits a statistically significant positive correlation with Iranian temperature anomalies (r = 0.45 at lag 0, p < 0.05), indicating a rapid atmospheric teleconnection pathway linking Atlantic SST variability to Iran. The concurrence of high importance and strong correlation suggests that TSA influences Iranian temperatures through both direct linear mechanisms and nonlinear pathways captured by the RF model.

Discussion

Although the mean Iranian temperature anomaly following Santa Maria (− 0.55 °C) was slightly stronger than that following Krakatau (− 0.49 °C), the duration of statistically significant cooling was longer after Krakatau (48 months) compared to Santa Maria (30 months). The temporal evolution of both events exhibited a two-phase cooling structure: an intense primary phase followed by a more moderate secondary phase. This pattern is consistent with the typical lifecycle of volcanic aerosols, where strong initial stratospheric loading is followed by gradual dissipation5,9. The longer persistence of Krakatau’s signal likely reflects its much larger eruptive volume and broader dispersal of sulfate aerosols (~ 25 km3) compared with Santa Maria (~ 1.3–3.4 km3) (Self et al., 2004).

In comparison, the Pinatubo (1991) eruption induced even stronger short-term cooling across the Middle East, including Iran. Regional mean anomalies reached − 0.4 to − 0.6 °C during 1992/1993, with a peak anomaly of − 0.72 °C in mid-summer 1992, most pronounced in northern Iran and the Arabian Peninsula2,43,52. However, Pinatubo’s effects were shorter-lived, lasting about 3 years. Reports of longer (up to 10-year) anomalies are likely due to non-volcanic factors such as ENSO and NAO variability. In contrast, Krakatau produced more persistent cooling, while Santa Maria’s impacts were briefer and more seasonally confined. Pinatubo’s cooling was also partially masked by rapid anthropogenically driven ocean warming, unlike the pre-industrial context of Krakatau or the shorter seasonal perturbations following Santa Maria (Gleckler et al., 2006)52,53.

Furthermore, teleconnection patterns amplified Pinatubo’s effects: stratospheric warming from volcanic sulfate aerosols, caused by absorption of longwave radiation, strengthened the polar vortex by enhancing zonal winds and reducing wave breaking, which in turn promoted positive NAO phases and intensified cooling across western parts of the Middle East54–56. This mechanism underscores the volcanic initiation of dynamical responses, where stratospheric heating precedes and drives the polar vortex strengthening, rather than the reverse2,28,52.

Spatially, both Krakatau and Santa Maria initially produced cooling concentrated over northern and western Iran, with maximum coverage occurring in 1884 (Krakatau: 98%) and 1903 (Santa Maria: 99.66%). Recovery progressed gradually from central regions outward. However, Krakatau’s impacts were more persistent and geographically stable, which can be attributed to the higher altitude of aerosol injection and more extensive atmospheric dispersion, combined with prevailing circulation patterns57.

Seasonal differences were also evident. Krakatau caused sustained cooling during spring and winter, while Santa Maria’s effects were shorter-lived and concentrated in autumn and early winter. For example, spring 1885 saw widespread anomalies (− 1.2 °C to − 0.5 °C) across 90.55% of Iran, whereas Santa Maria’s influence dissipated more rapidly by 1904. These differences likely reflect both eruption timing (late summer for Krakatau vs. early autumn for Santa Maria) and eruption locality (eastern vs. western hemisphere), which influenced aerosol transport pathways and radiative forcing patterns9,10.

The Random Forest model and correlation analysis highlight the critical role of tropical and mid-latitude teleconnections in modulating regional post-eruption climate responses over Iran. For Krakatau, the Pacific Warm Pool (PWP) index emerged as the strongest explanatory factor (r > 0.5), while for Santa Maria, the Tropical North Atlantic (TNA) index showed the highest correlation (r = 0.51). A significant post-eruption declines in the PWP—most pronounced after Krakatau—was associated with weakened tropical convection and reduced energy transfer to the subtropics and mid-latitudes, driving widespread cooling in semi-arid regions such as Iran2,57.

ENSO-related indices (Niño3.4, SOI, MEI) transitioned into negative phases shortly after both eruptions, consistent with a tendency toward La Niña-like states. While their correlations with Iranian temperature were moderate (r = − 0.2 to − 0.3), their temporal alignment with the post-eruption period likely enhanced the background cooling signal, in line with previous findings25,58. Similarly, mid-latitude indices (NP and TNA) shifted into delayed negative phases: ~ 0 and 12 months after Krakatau, and 12 and 0 months after Santa Maria, respectively. These transitions reinforced the persistence of regional anomalies, with the TNA showing a particularly strong and enduring negative relationship with surface temperatures after Santa Maria.

The Random Forest model also emphasized the importance of interactive effects among indices. For Krakatau, notable combinations included NP × PWP and PWP × Niño3, while for Santa Maria, NAO × TNA and NAO × TSA interactions were more influential. These coupled effects suggest synergistic modulation of Iranian climate, particularly evident after Santa Maria, when simultaneous negative phases of tropical and mid-latitude oscillations intensified cooling.

These findings align with the conceptual framework outlined in the Introduction: the observed cooling over Iran arises primarily from direct radiative forcing by volcanic aerosols, amplified and shaped regionally by volcanically modulated teleconnections. The Random Forest analysis provides robust evidence for this modulation by revealing event-specific dominant drivers (e.g., PWP for Krakatau, TNA for Santa Maria) and synergistic interactions that linear methods would underestimate, thus offering deeper mechanistic insights into how global volcanic signals are translated into regional climate anomalies in semi-arid, teleconnection-sensitive areas.

Overall, both eruptions demonstrate a consistent global-to-regional cascade: initial tropical responses propagated poleward, ultimately shaping regional climate anomalies. This supports the paradigm of volcanic-induced teleconnections, where tropical eruptions initiate atmospheric adjustments that culminate in extratropical impacts (Stenchikov et al., 2006)6. Despite differences in intensity and persistence, the results underscore the high sensitivity of central Middle Eastern climate to remote volcanic forcing through well-defined, lagged teleconnection pathways.

Conclusion

This study provides a novel, region-specific investigation of the climatic impacts over Iran, following the Krakatau (1883) and Santa Maria (1902) tropical volcanic eruptions. Through using CMIP6 model ensembles and machine learning approaches, we identified two distinct cooling phases associated with each eruption, with differences in persistence, intensity, and regional coverage. Krakatau produced a longer-lasting and somewhat more moderate cooling effect, while Santa Maria caused a more intense but shorter-lived response.

The spatial progression of anomalies, initiating in the north and west and recovering from the center outward, was consistent across both events, highlighting shared atmospheric transport mechanisms. Seasonal differences, especially during winters and springs, revealed modulation by regional circulation and timing of aerosol dispersion.

The novelty of our study lies in its integration of Random Forest analysis with climate teleconnection indices. The Pacific Warm Pool (PWP) index and Tropical South Atlantic SST anomalies (TSA) emerged as the dominant modulator of post-eruption temperature variability over Iran, with significant lagged correlations. ENSO-related indices (NINO3.4, MEI, SOI), the North Pacific (NP) index, and indices from the tropical Atlantic and Indian Ocean (TNA and DMI), also played synergistic roles, with negative-phase synchronizations amplifying cooling effects. Notably, stronger teleconnection interactions were found in the Santa Maria case.

Overall, this work advances our understanding of how tropical volcanism interacts with large-scale climate modes to affect regional climates such as over Iran. It emphasizes the importance of including teleconnection dynamics and data-driven methods in future attribution studies and climate impact assessments, particularly for semi-arid and climate-sensitive regions.

Limitations and prospects

This study, utilizing CMIP6 model ensembles and machine learning techniques to assess volcanic impacts on Iran’s climate, is subject to general limitations inherent in climate modeling research. The CMIP6 simulations involve uncertainties in aerosol radiative forcing and stratospheric processes, which may influence the precision of regional temperature anomaly projections. Additionally, resampling model outputs to a 0.5 × 0.5 degree resolution, while enabling multi-model averaging, may not fully capture fine-scale topographic effects in semi-arid regions like Iran.

The analysis of teleconnection patterns is constrained by the complexity of interactions among large-scale climate modes, such as ENSO and NAO, which may not be entirely represented in historical simulations. Furthermore, focusing on Iran as a representative semi-arid region limits the direct applicability of findings to other areas with distinct atmospheric circulation dynamics. Future research could address these limitations by incorporating higher-resolution models and a broader range of teleconnection indices to better understand volcanic-climate interactions across diverse regional contexts.

A major limitation is the lack of observational climate data for the period studied. While historical records could provide valuable validation, they require extensive and time-consuming analysis beyond the scope of this study. Despite this, using climate models to reconstruct spatiotemporal temperature patterns in Iran is a valid and informative approach for periods lacking observational data. These model-based analyses illuminate regional responses to volcanic forcing and provide insights into the timing and magnitude of temperature anomalies, though future studies should aim to validate findings against historical and observational data where available.
