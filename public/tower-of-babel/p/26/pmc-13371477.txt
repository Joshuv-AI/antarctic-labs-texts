Integrative multi-omics profiling identifies a lactylation-associated, metabolically active, and immunosuppressive subtype of colon adenocarcinoma
Authors: Minghui Lin, Xiaolan Huang, Zhiyuan Xia, Haining Chen, Yefu Shen, Xihua Xie, Hao Shu, Sen Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundHistone lactylation, a lactate-derived post-translational modification, bridges metabolic activity and gene regulation. However, its molecular mechanisms and clinical significance in colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) remain poorly characterized.MethodsWe conducted multi-omics analyses on TCGA and GTEx datasets, characterizing lactylation-associated genes via differential expression, enrichment, mutation profiling, and immune deconvolution. Subsequent prognostic modeling, transcriptional network construction, and drug sensitivity predictions assessed their biological and clinical relevance.ResultsWe identified 133 lactylation-associated genes and constructed an eight-gene prognostic signature (CCNA2, CALD1, CSRP2, MSN, RPS23, PSME3IP1, VIM, and SRRM2). High-score tumors exhibited poorer overall survival, enriched glycolytic, cell-cycle, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition pathways, and increased TP53 and KRAS mutations. Single-cell and protein-level analyses localized key genes to endothelial and macrophage compartments. Single-cell and protein-level analyses localized key genes primarily to endothelial and macrophage compartments. Furthermore, high-score tumors displayed reduced cytotoxic T- and NK-cell infiltration, increased M0/M2 macrophages, elevated TIDE scores, and lower immunophenoscores, indicating profound immune suppression. They also demonstrated higher stemness and reduced sensitivity to 5-fluorouracil, oxaliplatin, and sorafenib.ConclusionsLactylation-associated transcriptional programs delineate a metabolically active, immunosuppressed COAD subtype characterized by adverse prognoses and therapeutic resistance. This lactylation-based score serves as a clinically relevant biomarker and a promising target for combined metabolic–epigenetic and immunotherapeutic interventions.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) imposes an escalating burden on global health systems. According to the GLOBOCAN 2022 statistics, CRC ranks as the third most frequently diagnosed malignancy and the second leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide, with both incidence and mortality rates continuing to rise. 1 Despite major advances in early detection and multimodal therapies, clinical outcomes remain unsatisfactory. The overall 5-year relative survival rate is approximately 63%, which drops precipitously to 13% in patients with distant metastases. 2 This stark contrast underscores the urgent need to further elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying CRC progression and to identify reliable biomarkers alongside novel therapeutic vulnerabilities.

Recently, the intersection of metabolic reprogramming and epigenetic regulation has transformed our understanding of tumor biology. Histone lactylation, first described in 2019 as a novel post-translational modification, 3 has emerged as a critical mechanistic link between altered cancer metabolism and chromatin remodeling. Driven directly by lactate accumulation resulting from the Warburg effect, 4 lactylation exerts a broad and profound influence on transcriptional programs, immune evasion, and malignant phenotypes. Transcriptionally, lactylation upregulates YTHDF2 expression, accelerating the degradation of m6A-modified tumor-suppressor mRNAs (such as PER1 and TP53), thereby facilitating oncogenesis. 5 Within the immune microenvironment, lactylation induces the expression of checkpoint molecules, including B7-H3 and PD-L1, impairing CD8⁺ T-cell cytotoxicity. 6-8 It also modifies MOESIN at lysine 72, activating the TGF-β/SMAD3 pathway and fostering Treg differentiation. 9 Concurrently, extensive lactate accumulation acidifies the microenvironment, which impairs dendritic cell function and drives the reprogramming of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) toward an immunosuppressive state. 10 Beyond immune modulation, lactylation sustains oncogenic signaling, such as PI3K/Akt, supporting metabolic adaptability, stemness, invasion, and metastasis. 6 , 11 Collectively, these findings establish lactylation as a central regulatory axis in cancer pathobiology.

In CRC specifically, lactylation drives a malignant self-reinforcing loop that integrates genetic, metabolic, and microenvironmental inputs. Oncogenic KRAS mutations elevate H3K9 lactylation (H3K9la), enhancing chromatin accessibility at the GRAMD1A locus, which activates cholesterol metabolism and accelerates tumor growth and dissemination. 12 Hypoxia in the tumor microenvironment further amplifies lactylation through the HIF-1α/LDHA axis, producing global histone modifications such as H3K18la that preserve stem-like phenotypes. 13 This remodeling enhances aggressiveness: H3K18la upregulates the mitotic kinase AURKB and stabilizes PSAT1 mRNA, driving metabolic reprogramming. 14 Concurrently, the long noncoding RNA STEAP3-AS1 interacts with BRG1 to regulate H3K18la, promoting MMP9 expression and facilitating hepatic metastasis. 15 In advanced disease, immune evasion becomes entrenched: lactylation of PD-L1 directly impairs T-cell responses, 16 while high H3K18la activates RUBCNL/Pacer to promote protective autophagy, jointly reinforcing resistance to immune surveillance and therapy. 17 This cascade reveals lactylation not merely as a metabolic by-product but as a pivotal driver of CRC progression and therapeutic resistance.

Despite these conceptual advances, research mapping the specific role of lactylation in COAD remains fragmented. Current studies often focus narrowly on isolated genes or individual signaling axes, such as KRAS or HIF-1α, 12 , 13 failing to address the broader transcriptional architecture and interaction networks governing lactylation-associated gene clusters. Moreover, the prognostic implications of these comprehensive gene sets have yet to be systematically evaluated, nor have their associations with somatic mutations, copy-number alterations, tumor microenvironmental heterogeneity, or therapeutic responses been fully characterized.

To bridge these knowledge gaps, we conducted an integrative multi-omics analysis of lactylation-associated genes in COAD. Utilizing large-scale transcriptomic datasets, we engineered a lactylation-based scoring model for patient stratification and systematically evaluated its associations with tumor stemness, signaling pathway activity, immune infiltration patterns, and drug sensitivity. Single-cell RNA sequencing was used to delineate the cellular origins of key genes, and protein-level validation was conducted using the Human Protein Atlas. Ultimately, these integrated analyses aim to refine our understanding of how lactylation drives CRC biology, thereby supporting the development of novel prognostic biomarkers and individualized therapeutic strategies that target metabolic‒epigenetic crosstalk.

Methods

Data sources and preprocessing

We obtained mRNA expression (TPM), somatic mutation, and RNA stemness scores (RNAss) for COAD from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA; https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). A total of 327 COAD tumors and 23 matched normal samples were included for subsequent analysis. Additional normal colon expression data (n = 308) were retrieved from the GTEx portal (https://www.gtexportal.org/). Given that all data were retrieved from public repositories (TCGA, GTEx), institutional ethical approval was waived. All expression data were uniformly processed in R (v4.3.0). The TPM values were log2-transformed as appropriate. Batch effects between the TCGA and GTEx datasets were adjusted for downstream analyses requiring combined datasets.

Collection of lactylation-associated genes

A total of 320 lactylation-associated genes – defined as genes whose expression is directly regulated by lactylation or that participate in lactylation regulation – were compiled from the literature 18 and are listed in Supplementary Table S1. To enhance biological specificity, these genes were further classified into five functional categories (writers, erasers, readers, metabolic enzymes, and associated genes) based on literature evidence and database annotations, with detailed classification provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Identification of differentially expressed and lactylation-associated COAD genes

Differential expression analysis between tumor and normal samples (comprising the combined TCGA and GTEx datasets, n = 658) was performed using the limma package. Genes satisfying |log2FC| ≥ 1 and adjusted P < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was used to detect modules correlated with tumor status; modules showing the largest absolute module-trait correlation (P < 0.05) in both positive and negative directions were selected as tumor-related modules. The intersection between (i) differentially expressed genes, (ii) tumor-related WGCNA modules, and (iii) the 320 lactylation-associated genes yielded the set of lactylation-associated COAD genes for further study. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses were conducted using clusterProfiler; terms/pathways with gene overlap ≥ 3 and P ≤ 0.05 were reported.

Identification of prognostic LAPGs and genomic alterations

Univariable Cox proportional hazards regression was used to screen lactylation-associated COAD genes for association with overall survival (P < 0.05). Genes meeting this criterion were defined as lactylation-associated prognostic genes (LAPGs). The somatic mutation frequency of LAPGs was visualized with maftools. Copy number variation (CNV) events (amplification/deletion) were surveyed using TCGA CNV data; chromosomal locations were plotted with RCircos.

Definition of lactylation score and group assignment

A multigene lactylation score model was constructed as a linear combination of the expression levels and Cox regression coefficients of prognostic genes: lactylation score = Σ (exp_Genei × βi), where exp_Genei represents the normalized expression of a genei and βi the corresponding Cox regression coefficient. Patients were stratified into high and low-lactylation-score groups based on the cohort median. Survival differences were assessed by Kaplan–Meier analysis and the log-rank test.

Association analysis with canonical COAD driver genes

Canonical COAD-associated disease genes were retrieved from the GeneCards database. The differential expression of these genes between lactylation-score groups was assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the relationships between the expression of the eight LAPGs and these canonical driver genes.

Functional enrichment and pathway activity analysis

Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) was applied to transform expression data into pathway activity scores using the Hallmark gene sets (MSigDB). A Gaussian kernel was used to compute enrichment scores across 50 Hallmark sets. Differential pathway activity between groups was tested using limma (significance: P < 0.05 and |logFC| > 0.2). For gene-level enrichment, GSEA (clusterProfiler) was performed on limma-ranked gene lists (gene set size 15–500, P < 0.05).

Single-cell RNA-seq analysis

Single-cell RNA-seq data (GEO GSE161277; 3 tumor and 3 paired normal samples) were processed with the Seurat package. The cells were filtered to retain those with 200–7500 detected genes and <25% mitochondrial transcripts. Data from each sample were subsequently log-normalized. After merging, the top 3000 variable features were re‑identified on the combined dataset, followed by scaling and principal component analysis (PCA) on those features. Batch effects across samples were corrected using Harmony on the first 15 principal components. Dimensionality reduction was performed with UMAP, and the cells were clustered using a graph‑based algorithm at a resolution of 0.8. The cell types were annotated with SingleR against the Human Primary Cell Atlas reference. For each cell type, the activity of a lactylation‑related gene set was estimated using gene set variation analysis (GSVA) on log‑normalized expression data.

Transcription factor and ceRNA regulatory network prediction

Trans-regulatory motif enrichment and transcription factor (TF) prediction were performed with RcisTarget using the rcistarget.hg19.motifdb.cisbpont.500bp ranking database. Motif over-representation was evaluated by the normalized enrichment score (NES) derived from the AUC of motif recovery curves. The predicted mRNA–miRNA–lncRNA interactions were obtained from HMDD, miRWalk, ENCORI, and LncBase v3; disease-relevant miRNAs were prioritized. The resultant regulatory pairs were aggregated and visualized with Cytoscape to construct the ceRNA network.

Protein validation

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) data for LAPG proteins were downloaded from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA; http://www.proteinatlas.org) to compare protein expression in colon cancer and normal colon tissues. Protein expression data for the eight LAPGs in COAD tumor and normal tissues were obtained from the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) database. Expression levels were compared between COAD and adjacent normal tissue samples using a two-tailed Student’s t-test.

Tumor mutation burden and immune microenvironment analyses

Per-sample tumor mutation burden (TMB) metrics were derived from TCGA somatic mutation files and visualized with maftools. Immune and stromal scores were estimated using ESTIMATE. Immune cell proportions were inferred using a panel of algorithms, including CIBERSORT, xCell, etc.; concordance across methods was summarized. Pearson correlation analysis was used to relate gene expression or lactylation score to immune cell abundance. TIDE (http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu/) was used to predict tumor immune dysfunction and the likelihood of immune-checkpoint blockade response. The Immunophenoscore (IPS) was obtained from The Cancer Immunome Atlas (TCIA).

Drug sensitivity prediction

Drug response prediction used the GDSC2 dataset and the oncoPredict R package to estimate sample-wise IC50 values via ridge regression models trained on cell-line pharmacogenomic data.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (v4.3.0). Group comparisons utilized the unpaired t-test (for normal data) or Mann–Whitney U test (for non-normal data) for two groups, and one-way ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis test for multiple groups, with post-hoc tests where applicable. Correlations were assessed using Pearson's methods based on data distribution. Survival differences were evaluated by Kaplan–Meier analysis with the log-rank test. The predictive power of the risk score was determined by time-dependent ROC analysis (AUC). The data are presented as the mean ± SD, and a two-sided P < 0.05 defined statistical significance.

Results

Identification and characterization of lactylation-associated genes in COAD

Differential expression analysis integrating the TCGA-COAD and GTEx colon transcriptomes revealed 7500 genes dysregulated in tumors relative to normal colon, including 801 upregulated and 6699 downregulated genes (Figure 1A; Supplementary Table S2). WGCNA identified eight modules associated with tumor status; the green module (369 genes) was most positively correlated (r = 0.85, P < 0.001), while the turquoise module (8391 genes) was most negatively correlated (r = −0.84, P < 0.001) (Figure 1B; Supplementary Table S3). Intersection with the curated lactylation-associated gene set yielded 133 lactylation-associated COAD genes (Figure 1C). Functional enrichment highlighted RNA splicing and chromatin organization, with KEGG pathways including spliceosome, chromatin remodeling, viral carcinogenesis, and transcriptional dysregulation (Figure 1D, E).

Prognostic lactylation-associated genes and genomic alterations

We first applied LASSO regression to the 133 lactylation-associated genes to reduce dimensionality, yielding 20 candidate genes (Figure 2A). Univariable Cox regression on these 20 genes identified eight LAPGs significantly associated with overall survival: C CNA2, CALD1, CSRP2, MSN, RPS23, PSME3IP1, VIM, and SRRM2 (P < 0.05). Their expression patterns confirmed consistent dysregulation in tumors versus normal tissue (Figure S1A). Somatic mutations occurred in 14.5% of tumors, most frequently SRRM2 (~7%), CALD1 (~6%), and MSN (~3%) (Figure S1B). Copy number alterations were observed, including amplifications in CALD1, PSME3IP1, and MSN, alongside deletions in VIM, CCNA2, and RPS23 (Figure S1C, D).

Lactylation score model and prognostic stratification

A multigene lactylation score was constructed from the eight LAPGs, allowing patients to be dichotomized into high- and low-score groups. Kaplan–Meier analysis indicated that the high-score group exhibited significantly poorer overall survival (log-rank P < 0.05; Figure 2B, C), demonstrating the prognostic relevance of lactylation programs in COAD and confirming the clinical utility of this signature. Consistent with the model's design, the expression of six of the eight LAPGs (excluding CCNA2 and RPS23) was significantly elevated in the high-lactylation-score group (Figure 2D), providing a molecular basis for the observed prognostic disparity. The prognostic model exhibited good performance, with a C-index of 0.74 and favorable calibration and decision curve analyses (Figure S2 A–C).

Association with canonical COAD driver genes

Comparisons involving canonical COAD driver genes revealed significant differential expression across multiple oncogenes and tumor suppressors (e.g., APC, ATM, AXIN2, BRAF, CHEK2, EGFR, KRAS, MET, PTEN, RET, and STK11) between the two score groups (Figure 3A). Pearson correlation analysis demonstrated that LAPGs were tightly linked to hallmark COAD genes: CCNA2 with MSH6, NBN, CHEK2; CALD1, CSRP2, MSN with APC, ATM, CDH1; PSME3IP1 with AXIN2, CHEK2; VIM with APC, PTEN; SRRM2 with TSC2, APC, ATM (Figure 3B).

Pathway activity of LAPGs

GSVA and GSEA revealed that LAPGs were enriched in key oncogenic processes (Figure 3C, D). CCNA2, RPS23, PSME3IP1, and SRRM2 were associated with cell-cycle and proliferation signatures, whereas CCNA2, CSRP2, VIM, and MSN were linked to EMT, angiogenesis, and metastasis pathways. CALD1, MSN, VIM, and SRRM2 were enriched in metabolic reprogramming signatures, including oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid metabolism. Additional enrichment included MYC targets (CALD1, RPS23, PSME3IP1, and VIM) and inflammatory/interferon-gamma responses (PSME3IP1, VIM) (Figures S3 and S4), suggesting crosstalk between lactylation and tumor-immune interactions.

Single-cell transcriptomics and cellular localization

Using Seurat, we performed scaling and clustering of single-cell data, with batch correction across samples applied via Harmony. UMAP visualization revealed 19 clusters in tumor tissues and 21 clusters in normal tissues (Figure S5). The cell types were annotated using SingleR (Figure 4A). Differential expression analysis identified six LAPGs that were significantly altered between normal and tumor tissues (Figure 4B). Among the eight LAPGs analyzed in tumor tissues, CALD1 was predominantly expressed in endothelial cells; MSN was mainly detected in endothelial cells and macrophages, whereas VIM was highly expressed in monocytes (Figure 4C). GSVA was employed to estimate enrichment scores of the eight risk-associated genes across cell types (Figure 4D). Notably, endothelial cells and macrophages exhibited the highest lactylation-associated pathway activity among all major cell lineages (Figure 4E, F, Supplementary Table S4). Given the limited cohort size, these exploratory findings suggest that these cell populations may contribute to the prognostic impact of the lactylation-associated signature.

Regulatory mechanisms: TF and ceRNA networks

To elucidate the transcriptional regulatory mechanisms of the eight LAPGs, we performed motif enrichment analysis to identify over-represented TF binding motifs (PSME3IP1 was not identified in the ranking database). The analysis revealed that the top significantly enriched motif was annotated as transfac_pro_M06894 (NES = 7.76; Figure S6A, B), followed by transfac_pro_M07902 (NES = 6.484; Figure S6C) and transfac_pro_M05801 (NES = 5.75; Figure S6D). The genes CSRP2, MSN, SRRM2, and VIM were associated with the transfac_pro_M06894 motif, for which ZFP90 was predicted as the potential upstream regulator. The transfac_pro_M07902 motif was implicated in the regulation of CALD1, MSN, and VIM. The complete results of motif enrichment and corresponding TFs for all LAPGs are provided in Supplementary Material 2. Based on these findings, a comprehensive TF-mRNA regulatory network was constructed (Figure S6E).

To explore post-transcriptional regulation, we retrieved 110 COAD-related microRNAs (miRNAs) from the Human MicroRNA Disease Database (HMDD). Putative miRNA‒mRNA interactions for the eight LAPGs were predicted using the miRWalk database, generating 1527 initial miRNA‒mRNA pairs. This set was refined by retaining only pairs involving the disease-associated miRNAs, resulting in a focused set of interactions comprising 5 mRNAs and 6 miRNAs (Figure S7A). Subsequently, lncRNAs predicted to interact with these 6 miRNAs were predicted using the ENCORI and LncBase v.3 databases. This integrated analysis constituted a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) network consisting of 223 interaction pairs, encompassing 5 mRNAs, 6 miRNAs, and 190 lncRNAs (Figure S7B). Notably, PLAGL2 was identified as a predicted target of hsa-miR-133b within this network. The entire ceRNA network was visualized using the Cytoscape platform. These predicted interactions provide a resource for hypothesis generation and may guide future experimental validation.

Protein expression validation

To validate our findings at the protein level, we examined the immunohistochemical (IHC) staining patterns of the eight LAPGs in normal colon and COAD tissues from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA). Protein expression exhibited distinct dysregulation patterns. Notably, SRRM2 exhibited a clear increase in protein expression in tumors compared to normal tissues. In contrast, proteins such as VIM and MSN were markedly downregulated or lost in COAD, while CSRP2 indicated a trend toward reduced or heterogeneous expression. Other genes, including CCNA2, CALD1, PSME3IP1, and RPS23, displayed persistent or variable expression levels in carcinomas (Figure 5A). The substantial heterogeneity in protein expression among these LAPGs likely underpins the intrinsic biological diversity and contributes to variations in clinical outcomes among COAD patients.

Protein expression validation of LAPGs. (A) Representative immunohistochemistry images of the eight LAPGs in normal colon and COAD tissues from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA). (B) Quantitative protein expression levels of the eight LAPGs in COAD tumor and normal tissues from the CPTAC database. ****P < 0.0001; ns, not significant.

To complement these descriptive observations with quantitative validation, we analyzed protein expression data from the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC). Among the eight genes, CCNA2, RPS23, PSME3IP1, and SRRM2 demonstrated significantly higher expression in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues (all P < 0.0001, Figure 5B), whereas CALD1, MSN, and VIM were significantly elevated in normal tissues (all P < 0.0001, Figure 5B); CSRP2 showed no significant difference. These quantitative results are consistent with the directional trends observed in the HPA data.

The lactylation score delineates an immunosuppressive microenvironment with distinct genomic and therapeutic profiles

To elucidate how lactylation-associated genes influence COAD progression, we comprehensively characterized the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) using seven deconvolution algorithms (Figure S8). The proportion of immune infiltrating cells in each patient was shown in Figure 6A. Given that individual algorithms vary in sensitivity and specificity for different immune cell types, we focused on consensus patterns observed consistently across the majority of methods. This analysis revealed significant disparities between the high- and low-lactylation-score groups. Specifically, the high-lactylation-score group exhibited markedly lower infiltration of antitumor immune cells, including plasma cells, activated CD4+ memory T cells, activated NK cells, and monocytes, but was enriched for M0 and M2 macrophages (Figure 6B, C). Furthermore, the correlation patterns among immune cell subsets were fundamentally distinct. For instance, the correlation between CD8+ T cells and naive B cells shifted from negative in the low lactylation-score group to positive in the high lactylation-score group, indicating a rewiring of cellular interactions (Figure S9). Direct correlation analysis confirmed that the lactylation score itself was negatively associated with beneficial immune cells and positively correlated with immunosuppressive M0/M2 macrophages (Figure 6D). Consistently, the high lactylation-score group exhibited a significantly higher TIDE score (P < 0.001; Figure 6E) – a computational metric predicting an increased likelihood of immune evasion, along with generally lower Immunophenoscores (IPS) across most immunotherapy scenarios (Figure 6F), which serve as predictive models for immunotherapy response rather than direct clinical outcome measures. Additionally, this group exhibited a higher tumor stemness index (RNAss; Figure 6G).

(B, C) Comparative analysis of immune infiltration using CIBERSORT (B) and xCell (C). (D) Interaction network depicting correlations among immune cell subsets. (E) Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) scores, indicating higher immune evasion in high-score tumors. At the genomic level, while the overall TMB was comparable, specific mutation frequencies differed substantially. The high lactylation-score group harbored a higher incidence of TP53 (73% vs. 54%) and DNAH7 (13% vs. 4%) mutations, whereas ATM mutations were more frequent in the low lactylation-score group (21% vs. 5%) (Figure S10A, B, Supplementary Table S5). The translational relevance of these findings was evidenced by drug sensitivity prediction. OncoPredict analyses suggested that tumors with a high lactylation score exhibited elevated estimated IC50 values for several conventional agents, including 5-fluorouracil, oxaliplatin, and sorafenib, thereby indicating potential intrinsic resistance. Conversely, the low lactylation-score group exhibited reduced sensitivity to Dasatinib and AZD6482 (Figure 6H). These predictions are derived from cell-line models and require experimental or clinical validation. Collectively, these results position the lactylation score as a robust biomarker that integrates immunosuppressive TIME features, distinct genomic alterations, and differential therapeutic responses in COAD.

Discussion

In this integrative multi-omics study, we delineated the role of lactylation-associated programs in COAD by identifying a robust prognostic gene signature and linking it to distinct immunological, genomic, and therapeutic features. Our findings highlight that lactylation is not merely a passive metabolic byproduct, but also a pivotal determinant of tumor progression, immune evasion, and therapeutic resistance.

We first demonstrated that a specific subset of lactylation-associated genes is consistently dysregulated in COAD, showing significant enrichment in chromatin organization, RNA splicing, and canonical oncogenic pathways. These findings align with recent reports indicating that histone lactylation integrates glycolytic activity with chromatin remodeling, thereby driving transcriptional reprogramming within cancer cells. 3 , 19 Notably, we identified eight lactylation-associated genes with significant prognostic value, several of which exhibited high expression levels in endothelial cells and macrophages. This cellular distribution suggests that lactylation influences not only tumor-intrinsic processes but also the broader stromal and immune compartments, expanding upon prior studies that focused primarily on cancer cell-intrinsic lactylation. 20 Integrating stromal and immune populations into the lactylation framework offers novel insights into the ecological complexity of tumor progression.

The lactylation score derived from these eight genes demonstrates strong potential as a clinical biomarker, effectively stratifying patients by survival outcomes and delineating an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. High-score tumors were characterized by a reduced infiltration of effector immune cells, such as activated CD4+ T cells and natural killer (NK) cells, alongside an enrichment of M0 and M2 macrophages. This pattern mirrors experimental evidence demonstrating that lactate accumulation drives histone lactylation in macrophages, promoting polarization toward tumor-supportive M2 phenotypes. 3 , 21 Furthermore, the association between high lactylation scores, elevated TIDE values, and reduced immunophenoscores underscores their relevance to immune evasion and suggests a potential resistance to immune checkpoint blockade therapy. Similar immune-refractory states have previously been described in metabolically active CRC subtypes with poor responsiveness to PD-1 inhibitors. 22 Taken together, our data suggest that lactylation contributes to immune escape by reshaping both cellular composition and functional crosstalk within the TIME.

At the genomic level, high-score tumors were significantly enriched for TP53 mutations while displaying a depletion of ATM alterations. This mutational pattern provides a mechanistic rationale for their aggressive phenotypes, as the loss of TP53 has been shown to synergize with metabolic stress, promoting cellular dedifferentiation, stemness, and immune evasion. 23 The convergence of lactylation and TP53 dysfunction may create a particularly aggressive tumor phenotype, posing substantial challenges for clinical management. Moreover, our protein-level analyses revealed marked heterogeneity in the expression of lactylation-associated prognostic genes, underscoring the adaptive plasticity of tumors to microenvironmental pressures and potentially explaining the interpatient variability in clinical outcomes.

From a therapeutic standpoint, our computational predictions revealed that high-score tumors may possess intrinsic resistance to frontline chemotherapeutics, such as fluoropyrimidines and oxaliplatin, as well as to the targeted agent sorafenib. Conversely, low-score tumors displayed a reduced sensitivity to dasatinib and PI3K inhibitors. These observations align with preclinical findings that histone lactylation can promote multidrug resistance via feedback loops involving canonical oncogenic signaling pathways. 24 , 25 Therefore, incorporating lactylation metrics into clinical trial designs could facilitate the development of rational combination strategies that integrate metabolic, epigenetic, and immunotherapeutic modalities.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, our prognostic model and drug sensitivity profiles were derived from retrospective datasets and computational algorithms, necessitating prospective validation in independent clinical cohorts. Second, the limited sample size of the single-cell RNA sequencing cohort may restrict the generalizability of our cellular localization findings; thus, further in vitro and in vivo functional studies are required to establish direct causality for the identified genes. Finally, future studies incorporating spatially resolved multi-omics will be crucial for comprehensively understanding how lactylation orchestrates complex cell-to-cell interactions within the tumor microenvironment.

In conclusion, this study establishes lactylation-associated transcriptional programs as critical determinants of tumor metabolism, immune modulation, and patient outcomes in colon adenocarcinoma. Lactylation acts as a central molecular nexus that couples metabolic reprogramming with immune suppression, thereby defining distinct biological and therapeutic subtypes. These insights not only broaden our understanding of cancer epigenetics but also open new avenues for integrating metabolic and epigenetic targeted therapies with immunotherapy, ultimately striving to improve clinical outcomes for patients with colorectal cancer.

Conclusions

This integrative multi-omics investigation establishes lactylation as a pivotal metabolic–epigenetic regulator in colon adenocarcinoma. Dysregulated lactylation programs shape tumor aggressiveness, immune exclusion, and clinical outcomes by linking glycolytic reprogramming to transcriptional plasticity and immune evasion. The lactylation-based prognostic model effectively stratifies patients by metabolic–immune phenotype, offering potential guidance for individualized immunotherapeutic strategies. Collectively, these findings position lactylation not only as a biomarker of tumor biology but also as a promising therapeutic target capable of transforming immune-deserted colorectal tumors into immune-responsive states.
