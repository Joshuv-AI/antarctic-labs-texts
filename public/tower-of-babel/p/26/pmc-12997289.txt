The mediating role of illness perception between Type D personality and psychological distress in preoperative patients with gynecologic cancers: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Man Ding, Ziyi Cao, Yin Wang, Yuhan Zhang, Qin Xu, Zhuzhu Wang, Jingfang Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the prevalence and influencing factors of psychological distress in preoperative gynecologic cancer patients, and to examine the mediating role of illness perception between Type D personality and psychological distress.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 218 newly diagnosed gynecologic cancer patients from a provincial tertiary hospital in Hefei, China. Self-reported questionnaires were used to assess demographic and clinical characteristics, Type D personality, illness perception, and psychological distress. Mediation analysis was performed using bootstrapping (5000 resamples) to estimate unstandardized path coefficients (B) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsThe prevalence of preoperative psychological distress was 39.9%, which was associated with marital status, primary tumor site, Type D personality, and illness perception. Our results indicated the positive correlations of psychological distress with Type D personality (r = 0.643, P < 0.001) and illness perception (r = 0.723, P < 0.001). Mediation analysis suggested that the total effect of Type D personality on psychological distress was 0.195, while illness perception partially mediated the relationship (B = 0.089, 95% CI: 0.062–0.118), accounting for 45.6% of the total effect.ConclusionsPreoperative psychological distress was prevalent among gynecologic cancer patients. Both Type D personality and illness perception were significantly associated with psychological distress. The study indicated that Type D personality is associated with preoperative psychological distress, and this association may be partially explained by illness perception. Routine screening for Type D personality and targeted cognitive interventions addressing illness perception during the perioperative period may help reduce psychological distress in gynecologic cancer patients.Keywords: Gynecologic cancers, Type D personality, Psychological distress, Illness perception, Mediating effect

Introduction

Gynecologic cancers—including cervical, endometrial, and ovarian cancer—represent a major global health burden among malignancies affecting women. According to the recent Global Cancer Statistics report, there were approximately 1.47 million new cases and nearly 680,000 deaths worldwide, reflecting a notable increase in both incidence and mortality.1 In China, these cancers continue to pose a serious public health challenge, with surveillance data from 2022 indicating about 290,000 new diagnoses and 100,000 deaths, underscoring their persistent priority in women’s health care.2,3

Surgery serves as a primary treatment modality for many gynecologic cancers. However, the anticipation of surgery often precipitates significant psychological distress in patients, driven by uncertainties regarding their diagnosis, fear of operative trauma, and concerns about postoperative outcomes such as recovery, prognosis, and alterations in body image and fertility.4,5 Epidemiological data indicate that the prevalence of psychological distress among gynecologic cancer patients ranges widely from 21.9% to 78.0%,6,7 with particularly heightened levels observed during the preoperative period.4 This psychological distress—a multifaceted negative experience encompassing psychological (cognitive, behavioral, emotional), social, spiritual, and physical dimensions—can create substantial barriers to optimal disease management and treatment adherence.8 Furthermore, preoperative psychological distress is correlated with several negative clinical sequelae, including exacerbated anxiety, increased postoperative pain, and delayed recovery.9 Therefore, understanding the factors contributing to preoperative psychological distress and developing strategies to mitigate it is critical for improving patient care.

The Common-Sense Model of Self-Regulation (CSM) provides a robust theoretical framework for understanding how individuals respond to health threats such as a cancer diagnosis.10 According to the CSM, individuals develop cognitive and emotional illness representations—such as beliefs about illness consequences, controllability, timeline, and emotional impact—based on their interpretation of the threat.10 These representations guide coping responses and strongly influence subsequent emotional outcomes, including anxiety and psychological distress.11 Thus, the CSM supports the notion that illness perception is a key determinant of psychological distress. This association has been empirically established across various cancer populations.12,13 Moreover, the model also posits that stable individual characteristics, including personality traits, shape how these representations are formed.14 Research across chronic illnesses and cancer has shown that individuals with a Type D personality frequently report more negative illness perceptions, including exaggerated consequences and reduced controllability.15,16

While the CSM delineates the specific content of illness representations, Lazarus and Folkman’s Transactional Model of Stress and Coping17 provides the structural framework for understanding mediation. According to Lazarus and Folkman, the impact of antecedent person factors (such as Type D personality) on emotional outcomes is mediated by “primary appraisal”—the individual’s evaluation of the significance of a stressor.17 In the context of preoperative gynecologic cancer, illness perception functions as this specific cognitive appraisal. Type D personality, characterized by a stable tendency towards negative affectivity (NA) and social inhibition (SI),18 acts as a “distal” predisposing factor that biases this appraisal process, predisposing patients to perceive the upcoming surgery as more threatening and uncontrollable (maladaptive illness perception). This negative appraisal then serves as the “proximal” factor that is closely associated with psychological distress. Although this mechanism is theoretically plausible and has received preliminary empirical support in populations such as lung cancer patients,19 it remains unexplored in the context of gynecologic cancers, particularly during the acute stress of the preoperative period—a critical window that merits specific investigation.

Therefore, the primary objectives of this study were to investigate the prevalence of preoperative psychological distress among newly diagnosed gynecologic cancer patients and to examine the interrelationships between Type D personality, illness perception, and psychological distress. Furthermore, the study aimed to clarify the underlying psychological processes by explicitly testing the mediating role of illness perception within this theoretical framework. Based on the integrated theoretical model and prior empirical evidence, we hypothesized that Type D personality and negative illness perception are significantly and positively associated with preoperative psychological distress, and that illness perception mediates the relationship between Type D personality and preoperative psychological distress.

Methods

Participants and procedure

This cross-sectional study was conducted between October 2024 and April 2025 in the gynecology department of a provincial tertiary hospital in Hefei, Anhui Province, China. A convenience sampling method was employed for participant recruitment. Eligible patients met the following criteria: (1) newly diagnosed with gynecologic cancer confirmed by pathological examination and scheduled for surgical treatment; (2) age ≥ 18 years. Exclusion criteria were: (1) presence of psychiatric disorders or impaired consciousness; (2) combined with other primary malignant tumors or other serious life-threatening diseases; (3) currently undergoing psychological treatment or working in mental health-related professions.

Sample size

The sample size was determined a priori using G∗Power 3.1 software. For a linear multiple regression model with an anticipated effect size f2 = 0.15, alpha error probability = 0.05, power (1-β) = 0.90, and 15 associated factors,20 a minimum sample size of 171 participants was required. Accounting for an estimated 20% rate of ineffective responses, the target sample size was set at 214.

Data collection

The trained researchers approached potential participants in the wards the day before their scheduled surgery, following an assessment of clinical stability by physicians and nurses. After being fully informed of the study’s purpose and significance, all participating patients provided voluntary, signed informed consent. They subsequently completed the self-administered questionnaire in a private setting. Researchers collected questionnaires immediately upon completion and performed a preliminary check for completeness. Of the 300 patients initially assessed for eligibility, 230 met the criteria and completed the survey. During data screening, 12 questionnaires were excluded due to incompleteness (n = 2) or invalid responses (n = 10). The primary reasons for refusal included privacy concerns and a lack of interest in participation. The final analytic sample consisted of 218 patients (Fig. 1).

Measurements

Basic characteristics

A self-developed questionnaire was used to collect comprehensive baseline data, including: (1) demographic characteristics: age, educational attainment, marital status, employment status, monthly per capita household income (CNY), and number of children; and (2) clinical characteristics: primary tumor site, pathological stage, surgical parameters (ovarian preservation status and surgical approach), menopausal status, comorbid conditions, and family history of cancer.

Type D personality

The Chinese version of the Type D Personality Scale (DS-14), validated by Yu et al.,21 was used to assess Type D personality. This 14-item scale comprises two 7-item subscales: NA and SI.22 Items are rated on a five-point Likert scale (0–4). Consistent with international and previous Chinese cancer population standards, participants scoring ≥ 10 on both subscales were classified as having a Type D personality.18 In the current study, the scale demonstrated good internal consistency, with Cronbach’s α coefficients of 0.837 for the full scale, 0.827 for the NA subscale, and 0.830 for the SI subscale.

Illness perception

The Chinese version of the Brief Illness Perception Questionnaire (BIPQ), validated by Zhang et al.,23 was used to assess patients’ subjective perceptions of their illness. The questionnaire comprises 8 items (excluding the open-ended causal item) covering cognitive, emotional, and comprehension domains.24 Each item is rated on an 11-point scale (0–10). The total score ranges from 0 to 80, with higher scores indicating more negative or threatening illness perceptions. The Cronbach’s α coefficient in this study was 0.703.

Psychological distress

The Chinese version of the Distress Thermometer (DT), validated by Tang et al.25 for use in cancer populations, was used to screen for psychological distress. This single-item tool asks patients to rate their overall distress level over the past week on a scale from 0 (no distress) to 10 (extreme distress).26 As recommended for Chinese cancer patients, a cutoff score of ≥ 4 was used to identify cases of significant clinical distress. This version has demonstrated high test-retest reliability (r = 0.800, P < 0.001) in previous validation studies.25

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were performed using SPSS 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), the PROCESS macro for SPSS (version 4.2), and GraphPad Prism (version 10.4.1). A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The proportion of missing data for all variables was less than 5%. To address this, missing values were handled using predictive mean matching multiple imputation. Five imputed datasets were generated, a number deemed sufficient given the low fraction of missing information, and the pooled results were used for subsequent analyses.27,28

Normality of distributions was assessed using the one-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Since all continuous variables, including the primary outcome (DT scores), exhibited non-normal distributions (P < 0.05), data were described using medians and interquartile ranges [M (IQR)], and categorical variables using frequencies and percentages (%). Non-parametric tests were employed to compare psychological distress scores across different participant characteristics. Spearman’s rank correlation was used to examine the bivariate associations between Type D personality, illness perception, and psychological distress.

A theory-driven hierarchical linear regression analysis was conducted to identify factors influencing preoperative psychological distress. Prior to interpretation, regression assumptions were rigorously verified. Although the outcome variable showed a non-normal distribution, linear regression was deemed appropriate as the normality of residuals was confirmed by the Shapiro–Wilk test (statistic = 0.989, P = 0.102) and visual inspection of the Normal Q–Q plots (Supplementary Fig. S1). Furthermore, the sample size (n = 218) ensured robustness based on the Central Limit Theorem. Additionally, multicollinearity was ruled out (Variance Inflation Factor [VIF] < 5), and homoscedasticity was confirmed via scatter plot of standardized residuals versus standardized predicted values (Supplementary Fig. S2). Four models were sequentially tested: Model 1 included demographic variables (age, educational attainment, marital status, employment status, monthly per capita household income, and number of children); Model 2 added disease-related factors (primary tumor site, pathological stage, surgical parameters, menopausal status, comorbid conditions, and family history of cancer); Model 3 introduced Type D personality; and Model 4 further incorporated illness perception.

The mediating role of illness perception in the relationship between Type D personality and psychological distress was examined using Model 4 of the PROCESS macro. This analysis controlled for covariates (educational attainment, employment status, monthly per capita household income, number of children, and pathological stage) that showed significant associations with psychological distress in the univariate analyses and have been identified as potential confounders in prior studies.29,30 The significance of indirect effects was tested using bias-corrected bootstrapping with 5000 resamples. The proportion of the mediation effect was calculated as the ratio of the indirect effect to the total effect. To further assess the robustness of the mediation findings, a sensitivity analysis was performed. Influential observations were identified based on Cook’s distance and DFBeta statistics. The mediation model was then re-estimated after excluding these influential cases to verify the consistency of the indirect effects. Unstandardized coefficients (B) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported.

Results

Table 1 presents the sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of the 218 participants. The majority were aged 41–64 years (81.2%), married (89.0%), and postmenopausal (64.2%). Most participants had an educational level of elementary school or below (56.0%), were unemployed (52.3%), had a monthly per capita household income below 1000 CNY (31.2%), and had three or more children (68.3%). Clinically, cervical cancer was the most common diagnosis (54.6%), with 62.4% of patients classified as pathological stage I. Open surgery was performed in 58.3% of cases, and the majority (90.4%) did not undergo ovarian preservation.

Univariate analyses indicated that preoperative psychological distress scores varied significantly by educational attainment, employment status, monthly per capita household income, number of children, and pathological stage (all P < 0.05; Table 1).

Scores and correlations for type D personality, illness perception, and psychological distress

The prevalence of Type D personality in the study cohort was 39.0% (n = 85). The median total score for the DS-14 was 21.0 (IQR: 17.0–27.0), with subscale medians of 10.0 (IQR: 7.0–14.0) for NA and 11.0 (IQR: 9.0–15.0) for SI. The median illness perception (BIPQ) score was 35.0 (IQR: 28.0–45.3). The median psychological distress (DT) score was 3.0 (IQR: 2.0–5.0), with 87 patients (39.9%) scoring ≥ 4, indicating clinically significant distress.

Spearman’s correlation analyses revealed significant positive correlations between Type D personality dimensions and illness perception (NA: r = 0.496, P < 0.001; SI: r = 0.428, P < 0.001). Similarly, both NA (r = 0.611, P < 0.001) and SI (r = 0.381, P < 0.001) were positively correlated with psychological distress. A strong positive correlation was also observed between illness perception and psychological distress (r = 0.723, P < 0.001). Detailed correlation results are shown in Fig. 2.

Hierarchical regression analysis of factors influencing psychological distress

Hierarchical linear regression results are presented in Table 2. Coding schemes for all categorical variables were detailed in Table 3. Model 1, which included demographic variables, explained 13.0% of the variance in psychological distress (R2 = 0.130). The addition of clinical variables in Model 2 increased the explained variance by 8.3% (ΔR2 = 0.083). In Model 3, the inclusion of Type D personality significantly improved the model’s explanatory power, accounting for an additional 30.4% of the variance (ΔR2 = 0.304). The final model (Model 4), incorporating illness perception, explained 67.7% of the total variance in psychological distress (R2 = 0.677). In this fully adjusted model, both Type D personality (β = 0.324, P < 0.001) and illness perception (β = 0.524, P < 0.001) were significantly and positively associated with psychological distress. Among the covariates, being unmarried or divorced (β = 0.168, P < 0.05) and having a primary tumor site of the vulva or fallopian tubes (β = 0.111, P < 0.05) were independently associated with higher levels of preoperative psychological distress.

Mediating effects of illness perception between type D personality and psychological distress

Mediation analysis was conducted to test the hypothesis that illness perception mediates the relationship between Type D personality and psychological distress, controlling for significant covariates (educational attainment, employment status, monthly per capita household income, number of children, and pathological stage). As shown in Fig. 3, Type D personality was significantly associated with more negative illness perception (B = 0.856, 95% CI: 0.666–1.046), which in turn was associated with elevated psychological distress (B = 0.104, 95% CI: 0.083–0.124). The total effect of Type D personality on psychological distress was significant (B = 0.195, P < 0.001). The bootstrapping results confirmed a significant partial mediation effect: the indirect effect of Type D personality on distress via illness perception was 0.089 (95% CI: 0.062–0.118). This indirect path accounted for 45.6% of the total effect. Detailed path coefficients and bootstrapped confidence intervals are provided in Supplementary Table S1. To verify the stability of these findings, a sensitivity analysis was performed by excluding influential observations identified via Cook’s distance (Supplementary Table S2). The mediation effect remained statistically significant in the reduced sample, confirming the robustness of the results.

Discussion

Main findings

This study revealed a 39.9% prevalence of clinically significant preoperative psychological distress among gynecologic cancer patients, which was substantially lower than the 78.0% rate reported in a previous investigation of 60 gynecologic cancer patients undergoing chemotherapy.7 This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in patient characteristics (e.g., more advanced cancer stage), cultural factors, and the limited sample size of the prior study.7,31 Conversely, our rate was notably higher than the 21.9% reported at the 6-month postoperative follow-up in a prior longitudinal study on postoperative gynecologic cancer patients.6 This discrepancy may reflect the critical difference in psychological status across distinct disease phases. The preoperative period constituted a time of particular vulnerability due to the anticipatory uncertainty regarding the diagnosis, surgical invasiveness, and future prognosis.32 In contrast, the postoperative phase, while involving pain and recovery, offered a degree of resolution and certainty.33 For gynecologic cancer patients, these challenges were compounded by threats to feminine identity, reproductive capacity, and sexual function, which may be associated with body image disturbance and diminished self-worth.34,35 Within the Chinese cultural context, the self-stigma associated with gynecologic malignancies may further intensify these psychological burdens.36

Type D personality was identified in 39.0% of participants, a prevalence comparable to rates observed in breast cancer populations but substantially higher than in lung cancer patients.37 This variation may be attributable to both cancer-type differences and the potential role of sex hormones on emotional regulation in women.38 The median illness perception score fell slightly below the scale midpoint, possibly reflecting the use of defensive coping mechanisms, such as reality avoidance, which may be related to a tendency to underreport their subjective perception of disease threat.39

Hierarchical regression analysis identified several significant associations with psychological distress in gynecologic cancer patients. Crucially, Type D personality and illness perception demonstrated the most substantial associations, collectively accounting for a majority of the variance explained in distress.

A pivotal finding of this study was the significant partial mediating role of illness perception in the association between Type D personality and psychological distress, accounting for 45.6% of the total effect. This substantial proportion underscored that the association between this personality disposition and distress was not merely direct but was also substantially explained by patients’ perceptions of their illness. This finding aligned closely with previous research exploring this relationship in lung cancer populations.19 While prior lung cancer studies also highlighted the relevance of coping styles, our results underscore the potential importance of the cognitive appraisal factor in the context of preoperative gynecologic cancer. This mediating association can be understood through the traits that define Type D personality in our study. The persistent NA component is characterized by a heightened experience of negative emotions, which may be associated with the formation of more threatening perceptions of their gynecologic cancer. Such perceptions may include a greater sense of personal threat, more pessimistic beliefs about the consequences and timeline of the illness, and a lower perceived sense of control.40,41 Concurrently, the SI component may impede the natural expression of these fears and concerns in social interactions.42 This inhibition might limit the emotional processing and reality testing that often occurs through sharing with others, thereby potentially contributing to the persistence and intensification of negative illness perceptions without the corrective feedback of social support.43

Moreover, this mediating role demonstrated relevance beyond oncology, supporting the concept of its trans-diagnostic utility. In individuals with inflammatory bowel disease, Type D personality was identified as a factor associated with perceived stress and showed strong associations with emotional outcomes.44 Similarly, studies in depressed populations revealed significant correlations between other personality traits (e.g., neuroticism and openness) and depression severity, with illness-related cognitive appraisals potentially serving as an explanatory pathway.45 These consistent findings across diverse chronic conditions suggest that illness perception plays a representative and fundamental role in mediating between stable personality vulnerabilities and subsequent emotional distress.

Consequently, the mediating role of illness perception indicates a key psychological association: Type D personality was correlated with more negative and threatening interpretations of the illness. This maladaptive cognitive-emotional representation is posited to serve as a core primary appraisal and is likely related to the intensity of preoperative psychological distress. This pattern aligned with the theoretical propositions of both the CSM and Transactional Model of Stress and Coping and was supported by empirical work conducted in other populations.19,44,45

Implications for nursing practice and research

The findings highlighted the potential value of incorporating assessments of personality traits (particularly Type D) and illness perceptions into routine preoperative nursing evaluations for gynecologic cancer patients. Identifying individuals with these risk markers could allow for the timely provision of targeted support. For patients reporting negative illness perceptions, nursing interventions could focus on providing clear information, correcting misconceptions, and fostering a stronger sense of illness control. Establishing streamlined referral pathways to psycho-oncology services for patients with complex needs was also recommended. From a research perspective, these findings provide empirical support for a theoretical framework linking Type D personality and psychological distress, namely the relationship via illness perception. Moreover, by introducing the CSM and Transactional Model of Stress and Coping, they provided theoretical support for this association while extending the frameworks’ application in cancer populations. These insights provided a foundation for future studies to develop and test interventions aimed at modifying maladaptive illness perceptions.

Limitations

However, the interpretation of our findings must be considered in light of several limitations. First, the cross-sectional nature of the data means that the directions of the associations observed cannot be established. Second, the use of a convenience sample from a single center may limit the generalizability of the results. Third, although the sample size was adequate for the primary analyses, a larger sample would provide more stable estimates for complex models. Fourth, despite controlling for several relevant variables, the possibility of residual confounding from unmeasured factors remains. Finally, the reliance on self-reported measures is subject to potential biases. Future research should employ multi-center longitudinal designs with larger samples to validate underlying mechanisms, incorporate a wider range of relevant factors, along with clinical assessments or behavioral measures, and subsequently develop tailored psychological interventions.

Conclusions

This study confirmed that preoperative psychological distress is common among gynecologic cancer patients and highlighted the critical mediating role of illness perception between Type D personality and psychological distress. These findings underscored the potential clinical utility of comprehensive preoperative psychological assessments. They also pointed to maladaptive illness perceptions as a potential target for supportive interventions. Such interventions could aim to alleviate distress, foster more objective and controllable illness appraisals, and ultimately improve the perioperative experience for this patient population.
