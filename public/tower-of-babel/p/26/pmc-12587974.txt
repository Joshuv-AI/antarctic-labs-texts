Introduction

Digital transformation in healthcare holds many opportunities but also risks in medical knowledge management. healthcare is knowledge driven and healthcare professionals as well as patients need current information. Digitalisation enables quick access to search engines or social media platforms, providing a wide range of information, which is often not transparent in terms of its origin and cannot be verified in terms of its trustworthiness.1 2 The introduction of digital applications should be assessed according to the principles of evidence-based medicine and withstand an evidence-based benefit assessment. Trustworthy knowledge as published in evidence-based clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) should be identified and transferred more quickly into practice, whereas less trustworthy knowledge must be discernible. Internationally, the vision of a ‘digital trustworthy evidence ecosystem’36 is being pursued to accelerate the development, critical assessment, implementation and evaluation of medical knowledge by developing uniform data models, terminologies and interfaces that are publicly available. The aim of the digital ecosystem is to ensure that digital applications in medicine are designed and implemented not as stand-alone solutions but as commonly used and content-linked tools to avoid duplication and dead ends. Compliance with and further development of interoperability standards for data exchange based on the ‘Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources’ (FHIR) is essential for a uniform, internationally valid communication standard for medical data and medical knowledge including the certainty of results. In an evidence ecosystem, current results of patient-relevant studies are a basis of high-quality CPG recommendations developed by a representative group of clinical experts including patients weighing benefits and harms. Recommendations will be implemented considering individual scenarios and preferences and will be evaluated using performance measures. Evaluation may lead to new research questions which will enter into the evidence ecosystem.

The CPG registry of the Association of the Scientific Medical Societies (AWMF—Arbeitsgemeinschaft der Wissenschaftlichen Medizinischen Fachgesellschaften e. V.) in Germany is internationally leading in the quality assurance of CPGs.79 The AWMF CPG registry contains different methodological classes of CPGs, called S1-S3, and comprises over 800 CPGs in total. Every CPG development project and every CPG update has to be prospectively registered with information on the designated methodology. Before publication, each finalised CPG is assessed according to its methodology by AWMF research associates. There are about 200 CPGs of the highest development class (‘S3’) which include every state of the art methodological element of CPGs: development by a representative clinical and scientific expert group including patient representatives, criterion-based conflict of interest management, systematic search and appraisal of evidence according to key questions and a formal consensus process. The other CPG classes (S1, S2k, S2e) implement these in part (all: conflict of interest management; S1 and S2k representative CPG group, S2k: formal consensus process, S2e: formally evidence-based with systematic searches and critical appraisal of the evidence). All CPGs are available to the public as comprehensive PDF documents (long version, short version, guideline report, possible further documents: evidence report, patient version(s)). So far, only a few CPGs are also available in a structured data format, for example,10 11 or as applications for smartphones.12

Furthermore, there is a lack of digital formats that allow fast and on-demand access to CPG recommendations at the point of care in inpatient and outpatient settings via standardised interfaces to support decision making. On the other hand, digital applications including those using generative Artificial Intelligence (AI) flood the market of information in medicine. Therefore, there is a need to make a stand for trustworthy information derived from high quality CPGs.

The digitalisation of metadata on CPG to improve identification of trustworthy information, such as the annotation of recommendations, background text and underlying evidence, has been accelerated by the COVID-19 pandemic.13

Healthcare providers as well as patients wish CPG content to be easy to find, up-to-date and suitable for answering individual questions in a timely manner.14 15 Physicians and other healthcare professionals consider the integration of CPG information and recommendations into software systems as useful.16 Therefore, digitalisation of CPGs is necessary.

To date, publications on user experiences with digitalised CPGs are scarce. In a multicentre randomised trial, physicians preferred a digital multilayered CPG format compared with a standard PDF format.17 The digital CPG development tool, which was developed according to this study result, was assessed in a cluster-randomised trial using CPG knowledge as primary endpoint. However, the use of a digital multilayered CPG did not increase CPG knowledge compared with the use of a CPG in PDF format.18 Offering CPG recommendations in a smartphone app was favourable for supporting patient-specific decisions in another trial.19

The aim of our project is the digitalisation of the AWMF guideline registry. To this end, we are developing internationally applicable, interoperable data models (including FHIR-based CPG resources) and metadata standards to structure CPGs and render them machine-readable. Digitalising CPG registries may enable topic-specific access to individual recommendations, not only within a single CPG but also across multiple CPGs.

Considering that a digital multilayered format was preferred by users, we hypothesise that the user-friendliness of the AWMF CPG registry for CPG developers and administrators as well as registry users can be enhanced through digitalisation. Furthermore, we assume that implementing interoperability standards will better meet the needs of the different user groups, leading to increased use of CPG content (eg, recommendations, evidence reports, patient guidelines). In addition, we expect that acceptance and perceived trustworthiness of the AWMF CPG registry will improve as digitalisation encourages developers to adhere to high methodological standards (eg, application of the GRADE methodology (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation)).20 21 Finally, a digital CPG registry may facilitate identification of contradictions within and across CPG recommendations, as well as evidence gaps.

Methods and analysis

Aim

The objective of this study is to evaluate the impact of digitalising a quality-assured CPG registry using the international data standard FHIR. This includes the development of the registry architecture, defining the format of individual CPGs, and the implementation of application programming interfaces (APIs) for importing and exporting guideline content.

The primary outcome is the usability of the digitalised CPG registry for different user groups, including CPG developers and administrators, healthcare professionals, patients and technical users. Secondary outcomes include acceptance of the registry, utilisation of CPG content, perception of trustworthiness of CPGs, promotion of high-quality CPGs (eg, through application of the GRADE methodology), as well as the identification of contradictions across CPGs as well as evidence gaps.

Study design

The study is designed as a prospective, before-after intervention study over a project time of 3 years with semi-quantitative (surveys) and qualitative (focus groups) methods. Partners are CPG methodologists, partners of two German CPG programmes, medical societies and healthcare researchers as well as national and international experts for CPG digitisation, terminology and interoperability (see ‘Dissolve-E-study group’ at the end of this protocol).

The study will start with a 9-month preintervention phase (04/2024—12/2024) investigating the usability of the actual CPG registry and collecting requirements from different user perspectives concerning a digitalised registry. After the development of user-specific questionnaires and focus group guides, the t0 survey will be conducted (09/2024—12/2024). During this phase, the comparative outcomes (usability, acceptance, utilisation and trustworthiness of guidelines) will also be assessed.

Then, the CPG registry is digitalised in a first version using the developed FHIR standard (10/2024—12/2025) (see figure 1). The study will proceed with the intervention of testing the digitalised registry with selected digitised CPGs by the different user groups from t0 and is subsequently surveyed about their experiences (t1, 01/2026—06/2026). A summative evaluation will assess effects of the digitalisation of the CPG registry comparing outcome parameters before digitalisation (t0) and after testing the digitalised CPG registry (t1). In a formative evaluation, the technical realisation of user requirements assessed at t0 will be monitored during the digitalisation process and testing. For the included CPGs, an analysis of contradictions and evidence gaps will be performed. The digital CPG registry will be finalised and discussed in a virtual World Café with all stakeholders. Further research needs will be identified. Figure 2 presents a schematic illustration of the study design. A pre-post design was chosen since a random allocation to a digitalised versus non-digitalised CPG registry was not deemed to be feasible.

Study setting

German national, quality assured CPG registry, run by AWMF with import API for digitalised guidelines and export APIs for use at the point of care. Focus is on CPGs meeting the highest methodological standards (S3) with regard to group members, management of conflict of interests, evidence searches and assessment as well as use of structured consensus methods. Notably, in Germany, scientific medical societies under the umbrella of AWMF are responsible for CPG development.

Study population and sample size

The AWMF CPG registry is used by a broad range of users. This study involves six main user groups: (1) CPG developers, represented by coordinators of ‘S3’-CPGs, (2) CPG administrators of scientific medical societies, that is, employees who support the CPG work, including submitting CPGs to the registry, (3) Registry users, that is, people who directly access quality-assured CPG knowledge from the AWMF registry portal, represented by patient organisations and organisations of different healthcare professionals, (4) Physicians and patients at the outpatient point of care, (5) Physicians at the inpatient point of care and (6) Technical users. Study population and sample size for requirements analysis (t0)

All user groups participate in the status quo and requirements analysis (see preintervention period figure 2). Concerning baseline status (t0), a complete survey of all coordinators of S3 CPGs (approx. n=400) is aimed for. A minimum response rate of 50% is set; this rate will be achieved through multiple reminders if necessary. A participation of at least 50% ensures that the results are quantitatively representative of this group with a 95% CI and a margin of error of <5%. For all other groups, the sample size does not differ from that of the intervention. We will include: (1) A minimum of 15 CPG administrators, (2) About ninety representatives of twenty self-help organisations, fifty representatives of scientific medical societies and twenty representatives of other healthcare organisations, (3) 25 registered physicians at outpatient point of care, (4) 10 senior physicians of intensive care units and (5) 5–10 technical users.

Data collection

Data is collected at baseline (t0) and after the intervention (t1) via online surveys for four of the user groups (CPG developers/coordinators, registry users, physicians and patients at the outpatient point of care) and via focus groups for the remaining two as sample sizes are small (physicians at the inpatient point of care, technical subsequent users). Survey questionnaires will be semi-structured with closed-ended questions (yes/no, Likert scales or multiple choice) and some open-ended questions. Survey participants will be asked for their role, age, education and, if appropriate, profession. Further questions will be formulated on information seeking behaviour concerning CPGs and related documents (eg, CPG report, patient CPG, abridged version of CPG). To assess the primary endpoint usability of the CPG registry at t0 and t1 and additional secondary endpoints (ie, acceptance, utilisation of CPG content, trustworthiness of CPG knowledge), the validated instrument post-study system usability questionnaire will be applied for assessing usability,22 the credibility subscale of the ‘Web-Clic’-questionnaire for assessing trustworthiness23 and questions based on the technology acceptance model for assessing acceptance.24 The instruments were selected considering the object of the study (digitalisation of a CPG registry) and the length of the overall questionnaire suitable for processing. Further questions are formulated for specific aspects or endpoints for which no suitable instrument was found.

Open-ended questions at t0 will allow us to capture users’ ideas and expectations for a digitalised CPG registry, and open-ended questions at t1 will explore perceived needs for further development or research needs. Apart from these differences, questions at t0 and t1 will be the same.

There will be two semi-structured guides for the two focus groups with the aim to ascertain the users’ previous experiences with the current CPG registry according to their role, considering profession, age and education and to get to know what participants want to see from a digitalised CPG registry.

Questionnaires and focus group guides will be shared for comments with all project partners and will be piloted with one to three users per group and finalised accordingly.

Recruitment

Purposive sampling will be applied with regard to topics and experience with CPG development and quality assurance registry processes regarding CPG developers and administrators. For users of the CPG registry, purposive sampling comprises inclusion of different patient organisations and professions in healthcare including employees of self-governmental institutions. Recruitment will take part via email invitation using email lists known to the research consortium—or as member societies of AWMF or as CPG participating organisations.

Appropriate physicians of the outpatient point of care are known to the recruiting partner, the German Society for Wound Healing and Wound Treatment (DGfW) from previous projects. They are practising throughout Germany, specialising in general medicine, angiology, vascular surgery and diabetology and using corresponding CPGs. Also, the senior physicians from the intensive care units (ie, inpatient point of care) are known to the recruiting clinical partner of University of Greifswald from previous projects. Due to the lack of standardised interfaces in the healthcare sector, it is currently not yet possible to achieve statistically representative sample sizes for digitalisation projects at the points of care in Germany. Therefore, partners are necessary who have already certain digital facilities in place. This is due to more than twenty different software systems used by practices as well as hospitals in Germany.

Technical users will be recruited by AWMF and by the participating German Society of Internal Medicine.

Participation is voluntary. All participants will be asked to give consent prior to participation and may withdraw consent at any time. Participants will be informed that analyses are pseudonymised and data are stored according to a data protection plan and deleted after the agreed period (see online E-supplemental 1). Only the evaluation team (of aQua and AWMF, see Dissolve-E study group) will have access to the data. We did not deem a data monitoring team to be necessary for this type of data.

Preintervention period

Status Quo and requirements analysis

At t0, baseline semi-standardised online surveys and focus groups are undertaken with descriptive evaluation of quantitative data, and narrative summary of answers to open questions. A detailed record of the results is prepared for each focus group, and thematic analysis will be done14—for details see ‘summative evaluation’. In parallel, published experiences with digital CPG are systematically collected and analysed in a scoping review.25

Digitalising the AWMF CPG registry (first version)

Decisions on basic technical elements of the FHIR standard used for the digital CPG registry are considered according to the technical requirements analysis with the technical project partners.

Following the results of the user group requirements analysis at t0 and the results of the scoping review, specifications and functions of the digital CPG registry will stepwise be built on the basic technical elements according to international standards. Continuous supervision and quality assurance will be undertaken to meet the technical requirements of data structures and interfaces in the FHIR standard and the use of internationally agreed terminologies and semantics. All cooperating partners assure interoperability. Development will last for about 12 months.

Analysis

Formative evaluation

The formative evaluation starts at t0 and will focus on the process of technical development, including technical requirements analysis and its implementation. Researchers will take part in the technical requirements analysis in an observational role and document all requirements as well as their initial assessment regarding relevance and feasibility. Technical realisation and feedback on experiences or problems during the testing of the digital registry at either time will be recorded in a structured manner. Changes in the requirements and in initial assessment will be documented and incorporated into the technical development process via continuous quality assurance. Feedback, experiences and problems will be analysed quantitatively and descriptively summarised.

Summative evaluation

Summative evaluation of the use of the digital CPG registry is done by repeating the t0 survey for all trial participants after having evaluated the CPG registry during months 19–27 (t1). For the evaluation at t1, the same analysis steps are initially conducted as for the t0: descriptive evaluation of quantitative data, narrative summary of answers to open questions.

Focus group protocols will be analysed by thematic analysis based on qualitative content analysis, according to Kuckartz26 using software (MAXQDA). The category system for analysing the focus groups is initially developed inductively and adapted as necessary during the text work. It may be essential to re-code the protocols. The individual categories will then be summarised narratively. In the last step, differences and similarities between the two target populations will be highlighted. Results of both surveys are compared. Quantitative differences are tested for significance using suitable statistical tests. Differences between the analysed qualitative data of focus group results protocols and survey data are summarised narratively.

Further analyses across CPG and final version of the digitalised CPG registry

To what extent is it possible to identify contradictions between recommendations and evidence gaps across CPG and display them to users will be analysed during and after testing of the digital CPG registry (months 21–30). According to time and resources, further improvements and functionalities will be added before the digitalised registry is finalised.

Public presentation with determining further research needs

All results will be presented and discussed in a final virtual World Café with research partners and all stakeholders (month 34, 02/2027). During this meeting, further research needs for a digital evidence ecosystem, including AI-based solutions, for the content-related networking of CPG knowledge and the identification of evidence gaps will be highlighted and summarised. The World Café will be recorded, and results will be prepared as a thematically sorted table. The final FHIR standard will be published together with the identified further development and research needs. A FHIR Implementation Guide will also be provided, offering technical guidance for data mapping, defining terminologies, data models and profiles, and serving as a roadmap for interoperability between systems in healthcare.

Implications of this research

The innovative potential of this study lies in the combination of comprehensive quality assurance of medical knowledge with comprehensive digitalisation. The development of a widely agreed FHIR standard for the exchange of CPG knowledge with the definition of unambiguous terminologies provides a high utilisation potential for the project. This is a prerequisite for accelerating the development and updating of CPG by using digital tools and applications, including future AI solutions. Furthermore, the recommendation-based utilisation of the entire CPG knowledge in the AWMF CPG registry is made available for both outpatient and inpatient settings of care. A connection to the digital CPG registry, therefore, offers opportunities for exchange with a wide range of participants in the healthcare system. Proprietary interfaces for the digital CPG registry will be avoided.

Patients and public involvement

Patients were not involved in the study design but participated in the requirements assessment and evaluation of the digitalised AMWF CPG registry. The perspective of patients as CPG users will be collected via German patient self-help organisations like the ‘Deutsche Arbeitsgemeinschaft Selbsthilfegruppen’ (DAG-SHG), ‘BAG Selbsthilfe’ and the ‘Paritätischer Gesamtverband’ as well as via the ‘Haus der Krebs-Selbsthilfe’ (House of Cancer Self Help). These self-help groups are regularly participating in German CPG development. Additional thematic groups will be contacted. Patient representatives will also be asked to give feedback during the development of the questionnaires. In the final World Café, patient representatives will take part to discuss results and formulate further development as well as research needs.

Ethics and dissemination

No ethical approval was needed for the Dissolve-E study as it does not comprise medical or epidemiological research. This was confirmed by the Ethics Committee of the Berlin chamber of physicians in accordance with the Berlin chamber of physicians’ medical code of conduct §15 section 1 (Eth-KB-24-11). The study is registered in the German Clinical Trials Registry (registration number: DRKS00034111). Results will be presented at national and international conferences and will be published in relevant peer-reviewed journals and on the website of the funding institution (Innovation Committee of the Federal Joint Committee, in Germany).
