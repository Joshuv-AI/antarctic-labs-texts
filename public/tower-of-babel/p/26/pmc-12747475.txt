Anterior Segment Optical Coherence Tomography and Anterior Segment Optical Coherence Tomography Angiography After Bleb Forming Glaucoma Surgeries: A Systematic Review
Authors: Martin Kallab, Alex S Huang, Matthias Bolz, Clemens A Strohmaier
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe bleb‐forming surgical spectrum in open‐angle glaucoma has recently been expanded by novel surgical approaches. Through a medical device implantation (Xen Gel Stent (XGI) or Preserflo Microshunt (PMI)), these procedures aim at more standardised aqueous humour shunting and an intra‐ and postoperative risk reduction. Postoperatively, however, XEN, PMI and trabeculectomy share their dependence on the proper function of the filtration bleb. This review summarises available literature on anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS‐OCT)‐ and AS‐OCT angiography (AS‐OCTA)‐based bleb parameters with an emphasis on their association with bleb function.MethodsA systematic PubMed search was performed to identify the available evidence.ResultsA total of 52 suitable reports were identified and are presented in a structured manner.ConclusionThe retrieved literature demonstrates that there exists a broad body of evidence for the use of AS‐OCT and AS‐OCTA after TE, XGI and PMI with the potential for AS‐OCT and AS‐OCTA enhanced follow‐up plans. Their implementation in clinical routine is, however, currently impaired by highly variable and impractical study endpoints, lacking transferability of available evidence between different surgical procedures and missing evidence from prospective interventional trials.Keywords: biomarkers, filtering surgery, optical coherence tomography

Introduction

While trabeculectomy (TE) is still the mainstay of filtration surgery once pharmacological treatment, laser trabeculoplasty and/or minimally invasive glaucoma surgery (MIGS) cannot sufficiently control open‐angle glaucoma, TE is undoubtedly associated with significant intra‐ and postoperative risks [1, 2, 3]. Therefore, novel bleb‐forming surgical approaches have been introduced which involve medical device implantation and aim at more standardised aqueous humour shunting and an intra‐ and postoperative risk reduction. To this end, two device implantation procedures are currently available: XEN Gel Stent implantation (XGI, AbbVie Inc., North Chicago, Illinois, US) [4, 5] and Preserflo Microshunt implantation (PMI, Santen Pharmaceuticals, Osaka, Japan) [6, 7]. While they differ in the surgical approach as XGI is commonly performed ab‐interno (but can be used ab‐externo) and PMI ab‐externo, both surgeries are associated with moderate and sustained reduction of intraocular pressure (IOP) [8, 9, 10]. Despite this surgical innovation, IOP reduction after implantation remains highly dependent on the filtration bleb status, and bleb vascularisation, fibrosis and encapsulation still represent significant threats to surgical success. Therefore, close patient follow‐up, especially in the early postoperative phase, is vital for sustained IOP reduction. In an attempt to standardise clinical bleb examination and decision‐making, clinical bleb grading scales such as Indiana Bleb Appearance Grading Scale (IBAGS) [11], Moorfields Bleb Grading System (MBGS) [12], Wuerzburg Bleb Classification Score (WBCS) [13] and Mainz Bleb Appearance Grading System (MaBAGS) [14] have been used for many years. Their subjective nature and the lack of information on the internal bleb structure, however, limit their clinical and scientific value as larger studies on clinical utility are hindered, while individual clinical decision‐making is obviously dependent on the present tools [14, 15]. Recently anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS‐OCT) and AS‐OCT angiography (AS‐OCTA) have been introduced and allow for detailed visualisation of bleb dimensions, internal structure and vascularisation [16]. This review aims at summarising the current literature on qualitative (morphological descriptions of OCT patterns), semiquantitative (categorial metrics) and quantitative (numerical metrics) AS‐OCT‐ and ‐OCTA‐based bleb parameters after bleb forming glaucoma surgery (TE, XGI and PMI) and their association with IOP per se and surgical success endpoints. Emphasis is put on differentiating results from cross‐sectional and longitudinal studies as only the latter offer the possibility to detect predictive bleb features. Moreover, the potential of study results to enhance current routine follow‐up after surgery will be discussed, and existing knowledge gaps shall be highlighted.

Methods

PubMed search (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) was performed in July 2024 and updated in May 2025. Specific search queries can be found in the Supplement.

After abstract screening, full texts of search results were reviewed concerning the following inclusion criteria: full text available in English, surgical procedure: TE, PMI or XGI, postoperative AS‐OCT and ‐OCTA bleb imaging, evaluation of AS‐OCT and ‐OCTA bleb features and IOP/surgical success, inclusion of primary open‐angle glaucoma patients (although additional inclusion of other glaucoma types was allowed). Purely descriptive studies were not included. A Prisma diagram is shown in Figure 1.

Results

A total of 52 studies (AS‐OCT/TE: 34, AS‐OCTA/TE: 7, AS‐OCT/PMI: 5, AS‐OCTA/PMI: 1, AS‐OCT/XGI: 10, AS‐OCTA+XGI: 1) were included in this review. Five studies compare bleb appearances between procedures and were therefore provided as Supplement.

Trabeculectomy

Structural AS‐OCT Findings in Cross‐Sectional Studies

Early cross‐sectional AS‐OCT‐based bleb analyses after TE focused on qualitative and semi‐quantitative bleb features: Hirooka et al. distinguished cystoid‐type, diffuse‐type and layered‐type blebs 12 months after surgery [17]. They found layered‐type blebs to have a lower success rate (75%) than cystoid‐type (94%) and diffuse‐type (97%) while Napoli et al., using the same classification scheme, found the cystoid‐type to have the highest success rate (100%), diffuse‐type a lower (62.5%) and layered‐type the lowest success rate (25%) [17, 18]. Singh et al. ‐ besides qualitative bleb descriptions in successful and failed blebs—semiquantitatively compared bleb wall thickening and found it to be more frequent (87.7%) in successful than in failed blebs (28.6%) [19]. This paved the way for quantitative analysis of bleb wall thickness and was later replicated by Zhang et al. [20] Finally, using conjunctival en‐face reconstructions of bleb OCT images, a semiquantitative measure of microcysts density was shown to be negatively correlated with IOP and higher in functioning blebs [21].

In 2009, Kawana et al., making use of AS‐OCT volume scans, performed quantitative bleb analysis in a cross‐sectional study two to 40 months after TE surgery and found IOP to be negatively correlated with bleb height, horizontal/vertical fluid cavity length, fluid cavity height, maximal bleb wall thickness, volume of the internal fluid cavity, volume of the hyporeflective area and the number of microcysts [22]. All of those parameters were also significantly different between successful and failed blebs in the same study [22], while Tominaga et al. only found bleb wall thickness to be significantly negatively correlated with IOP in a similarly designed study [23]. Differences in OCT devices, scan protocols and in the mean postoperative timepoint of the two cross‐sectional studies could be responsible for the observed disparity. In later studies, bleb height [24, 25, 26, 27, 28] and bleb wall thickness [24, 25, 26, 29, 30] as well as horizontal fluid cavity length [29], height [26, 27, 29] and volume [31] were found to be significantly higher in successful than failed blebs, and the negative correlation of bleb wall thickness [32] and fluid cavity volume [33] with concomitant IOP was replicated.

Quantitative reflectivity analysis was initially performed at the internal fluid cavity only in a study by Pfenninger et al., in which it was significantly correlated with higher concomitant IOP [34]. Napoli et al. were the first to quantitatively measure reflectivity of the whole bleb, and similarly to Pfenninger et al., found it to be significantly correlated with higher IOP [18]. Also, quantitative analysis of the bleb wall reflectivity yielded similar results: it positively correlated with IOP 12 [35] and 6 [33] months after surgery and was lower in successful blebs [24, 25, 28, 30, 31]. Similarly, an earlier semiquantitative analysis of bleb wall reflectivity showed higher values in failed as compared to successful blebs [36].

Evaluating the scleral flap, Tan et al. found visible flaps with free flap margins non‐adherent to the surrounding sclera to be associated with success and with bleb height [37]. Later, flap dimensions were also analysed and flap length, but not width or thickness, was higher in successful than in failing blebs [28].

Structural AS‐OCT Findings in Longitudinal Studies

The predictive value of qualitative bleb parameters was initially evaluated by Nakano et al. [38] They differentiated blebs with multiform and uniform reflectivity, and the multiform appearance was additionally specified as ‘multiple layers’, ‘subconjunctival separation’ and ‘microcysts’. In this analysis, 2 months after surgery, multiform, multiple layered blebs were associated with better bleb function 6 months after surgery [38] This was later shown to hold true for blebs 1 month after TE as well [39, 40] Lately, Choudary et al. found a correlation between 6 month IOP and qualitative bleb reflectivity (multiform/uniform) 3 and 6 weeks after surgery [41].

Similar to these findings, Narita et al. found the so‐called ‘stripping‐phenomenon’, which was described as the appearance of multiple parallel hyporeflective layers in the bleb wall, 2 weeks after surgery to be predictive for surgical success at 12 months [24].

Using a semiquantitative OCT‐based grading scale incorporating bleb height and reflectivity (higher grades = smaller height/higher reflectivity), Wen et al. demonstrated a significant positive correlation with concomitant IOP at 4 and 6 months after TE and with MBGS grading at 4, 6 and 12 months after surgery [42]. In the same study, AS‐OCT‐based bleb grading at 6 months postoperatively was lower (better) in successful blebs at 12 months after surgery [42]. Finally, an OCT‐assisted WBCS (encapsulation and microcysts assessed by OCT) correlated well with concomitant IOP at multiple timepoints in the first postoperative year and at 2, 3, 6 and 12 months after surgery; it was associated with surgical success at 12 months after TE [43].

Kokubun et al. analysed the predictive value of OCT parameters in the early postoperative phase and found mean reflectivity (of all bleb wall pixels in a horizontal B‐scan) after one to 2 week(s) to be correlated with higher IOP 6 months after surgery, while volume parameters (bleb wall, fluid cavity) were not [33]. The same reflectivity parameter differed between successful and unsuccessful blebs (as assessed 12 months after surgery) starting after postoperative week 2 in a larger analysis of the same group and then showed an AUROC of 0.775 [31].

Bleb height and bleb wall thickness 2 weeks after surgery were higher in successful blebs as evaluated 12 months postoperatively by Narita et al. [24] In a longitudinal observation of functioning and encapsulated blebs in the first 12 weeks post TE, bleb wall thickness was lower in future encapsulated blebs as early as 1 week after surgery and the fluid cavity was shallower 2 weeks postoperatively, whereas encapsulation was clinically visible only3 to 44 weeks post TE [44].

Concerning the predictive value of AS‐OCT‐based scleral flap features, Kojima et al. identified a negative correlation between the width of the scleral flap filtration opening 2 weeks after TE and IOP at 12 months postoperatively [45]. Later, they found this to hold true also for a high‐risk population with a history of intraocular surgery [46] and, in another study, also identified a higher filtration opening width after 2 weeks to be predictive for surgical success [47].

AS‐OCTA Finding in Cross Sectional Studies

Concerning OCT‐angiography‐based bleb vessel densities, a cross‐sectional study of colour and brightness densities did not find any correlation with concomitant IOP [48]. A separated analysis of superficial, tenon and deep vessel densities, as well as skeletonised vessel densities by Luo et al. revealed a differential correlation pattern with IOP, and the most pronounced positive correlation was found between skeletonised tenon vessel density and IOP [49] In the latter study, both vessel densities and skeletonised vessel densities of all three evaluated layers were significantly lower in successful blebs [49] Later, the same group used structural bleb OCT parameters (bleb height, microcysts) and bleb OCTA derived bleb vessel densities for a combined bleb score, which showed higher AUROCs for surgical success than IBAGS, MBGS and bleb height alone [50].

AS‐OCTA Findings in Longitudinal Studies

In a longitudinal study by Hayek et al., conjunctival vessel densities 1 and 6 months after surgery positively correlated with IOP at the corresponding timepoints [51].

Yin et al. did not find any correlations between AS‐OCTA measured vessel density and concomitant IOP 1 month after surgery [52]. Vessel density 1 month after surgery was, however, correlated with higher IOP 6 months postoperatively, suggesting predictive potential [52]. Similarly, Seo et al., who did not find a correlation between concomitant IOP and colour/brightness densities in a previous study [48], could show that a lower brightness density 1 week after surgery was associated with final surgical success (≥ 6 months after surgery) both regarding IOP and reoperation rate (needling with 5‐FU) [53]. Complete avascular areas of blebs at various postoperative timepoints, as assessed using AS‐OCTA, had no predictive value for surgical success after 12 months [54].

Preserflo Microshunt Implantation

Structural AS‐OCT Findings in Cross‐Sectional Studies

Ibarz Barbera et al. were the first to analyse AS‐OCT images in the first 3 months after PM implantation: Qualitatively, blebs were mostly multiform at all visits; correlation to IOP was, however, not analysed. Quantitatively, horizontal fluid cavity dimension and fluid cavity height were measured, and no correlation to concomitant IOP was found [55].

Structural AS‐OCT Findings in Longitudinal Studies

In another study, Ibarz Barbera et al. followed PM implantations for 1 year and introduced a novel AS‐OCT‐based parameter: total filtering surface around the fluid cavity (TFS) as calculated from horizontal fluid cavity dimension and fluid cavity height [56]. Bleb wall thickness and TFS were correlated with concomitant IOP after 12 months, and TFS after 6 months was also correlated with IOP after 12 months. While bleb wall thickness, bleb height, vertical and horizontal fluid cavity diameter, as well as TFS were significantly higher in successful blebs after 12 months, none of the AS‐OCT parameters at earlier postoperative timepoints had predictive potential for surgical success 12 months post PM implantation [56].

AS‐OCTA Findings in Cross‐Sectional Studies

We have not identified relevant studies using AS‐OCTA in cross‐sectional study designs.

AS‐OCTA Findings in Longitudinal Studies

AS‐OCTA‐based bleb vessel density was evaluated as a predictive parameter in a longitudinal study by Schneider et al., who assessed bleb vessel density at numerous time points for up to 12 months after PM implantation. This study used custom image processing to overcome the image registration limitations that previously impaired longitudinal study designs with AS‐OCTA [57]. They found bleb vessel density 2 and 4 weeks after surgery to be predictive for surgical success in a 12‐month follow‐up period [57].

Xen Gel Stent Implantation

Structural AS‐OCT Findings in Cross‐Sectional Studies

Introducing a novel AS‐OCT‐based grading scale incorporating conjunctival, Tenon's and episcleral features, Hasan et al. identified differences between the early (mean follow‐up 47 days) and late postoperative phases (mean follow‐up 343 days): While, early on, subepithelial spaces at the conjunctival level and a cavernous or hyporeflective Tenon's were associated with a higher success rate and significantly lower IOPs, later on, only the above‐mentioned Tenon changes were. The presence of an episcleral fluid cavity had no influence on IOP and success rate in the early and late phase [58].

In another cross‐sectional study, Mastropasqua et al. found blebs to be higher in successful blebs 6 months after XEN implantation [59]. Bleb wall thickness and bleb height were not differentiated as fluid cavities around the implant were classified as cysts and therefore as part of the bleb wall [59].

Structural AS‐OCT Findings in Longitudinal Studies

The association between qualitative bleb parameters and IOP after XGI was first longitudinally analysed by Lenzhofer et al. using Nakano et al.'s bleb morphology classifications [60]. They found uniform blebs to have higher mean IOPs until 3 months after surgery, whereas the subconjunctival separation morphology was associated with a lower IOP during the first 12 months [60]. Additionally, multiform microcystic bleb appearance 3 months after surgery and uniform blebs 9 months after surgery were predictive for bleb failure at 12 months [60]. Differentiating uniform and internal cavity bearing blebs, Wy et al., in contrast to Hasan et al. [58], found IOP to be significantly lower in the latter at 6 and 12 postoperative months [61].

Fea et al.‐ in only 12 eyes—analysed quantitative AS‐OCT‐based bleb parameters in the first postoperative year after XGI: Bleb height was associated with concomitant surgical success one, three and 6 months after surgery but not at 12 postoperative months, while bleb wall reflectivity was at all analysed postoperative timepoints. In contrast, no association was found between bleb wall thickness and surgical success [62]. Wy et al.—in the above‐mentioned study—found a negative correlation between bleb height but not bleb wall thickness and concomitant IOP [61].

While Kim et al. found a smaller bleb height 2 months after XGI to be predictive for successful blebs 6 months after surgery [63], it was—as already mentioned above—higher in successful blebs after 6 months when evaluated concomitantly in a study by Mastropasqua et al. [59].

AS‐OCTA Findings in Cross‐Sectional Studies

In the same study by Mastropasqua et al., AS‐OCTA measured bleb vessel density was lower and vessel‐free areas were larger in successful blebs 6 months post XGI [59]. While postoperative bleb vessel density correlated positively with concomitant IOP, vessel‐free areas and bleb height correlated negatively with concomitant IOP [59].

AS‐OCTA Findings in Longitudinal Studies

We have not identified relevant studies using AS‐OCTA in longitudinal study designs.

Discussion

This review summarises the current literature on AS‐OCT‐ and ‐OCTA‐based bleb parameters for surgical success after TE, XGI and PMI. It demonstrates that both qualitative and quantitative markers for concomitant and future IOP/bleb function are available for bleb‐forming surgeries. No interventional clinical trials relying on these parameters for postoperative decision‐making to enhance patient care could be found. Possible reasons for that and future directions to foster such interventional trials are discussed below.

First of all, comparability of studies on the same procedure is impaired by markedly varying surgical success endpoint definitions. Not only do the IOP cut‐offs vary, but there is also pronounced inconsistency regarding permission of IOP‐lowering therapy, secondary interventions (needling, revision) and disclosure of both (Table 1 summarises the different success definitions used). To overcome this, enhanced and more generalizable surgical success endpoints are needed. The guidelines for surgical trial design published by the WGA provide a consensus on how to report surgical success data, but obviously cannot predetermine decisions in the treatment of individual patients [64]. Previously we have used ‘necessity for secondary interventions’ based on pre‐determined clinical criteria (individual target IOP, bleb yes/no) rather than an arbitrary IOP cut‐off as it is clinically relevant, patient‐oriented and incorporates multiple adverse situations. In doing‐so we could recently identify bleb vessel density of the early postoperative phase to be predictive for surgical success in the first postoperative year (after PMI implantation) [57]. When use of necessity for secondary interventions as endpoint is not feasible (e.g., when they are actively triggered or delayed in interventional trials) pre‐determined individual target IOPs or signs of structural/functional disease progression could be an alternative option.

From a technical perspective, comparability of studies might be impaired by the use of numerous different OCT devices relying on different OCT concepts (time‐domain vs. spectral domain vs. swept source devices) and partly being only experimentally adapted to anterior segment imaging (i.e., by add‐on lenses). This results in different scan depths, scan fields/resolutions and susceptibility to imaging artefacts [70]. For a more holistic approach to the analysis of the complex bleb data generated by AS‐OCT and AS‐OCTA, artificial intelligence‐based algorithms—as already used for many other applications in ophthalmology [71]—are promising tools for future studies.

Moreover, AS‐OCT bleb parameters that might be of value in one procedure might not be in the other. For instance, as described above, bleb height is both correlated with concomitant IOP after TE and predictive for surgical success in the early postoperative phase [22, 24, 25, 26, 29]. This relation is less clear after XGI and the evidence for bleb height as a predictive marker for surgical success is—at best—equivocal [59, 61, 62, 63]. For bleb wall thickness this disparity is even more pronounced as it is associated with concomitant and future IOP and surgical success after TE [22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 29, 30, 32, 44] but neither with IOP nor with surgical success after XGI [61, 62]. This differential pattern between TE and XGI might be associated with the lower rate of episcleral fluid in functional blebs after XGI as compared to TE [67, 68] and the lower mean bleb height after XGI [65, 66], reducing the dynamic range of this parameter and, thus, the probability to significantly correlate with success/IOP. It also coincides with our clinical notion that after XGI a larger proportion of clinically flat blebs sustainably retains function.

Chapter S2 in the supplement discusses the structural differences of filtration blebs between TE, PM and XGI.

Similarly, there is currently no evidence for the predictive value of a structural AS‐OCT‐based parameter after PMI. The authors, therefore, acknowledge that further studies after XGI and PMI are necessary to test the broad body of evidence for predictive AS‐OCT‐ and ‐OCTA‐based parameters after TE for its validity in these newer, device‐based procedures.

When looking at AS‐OCTA‐based vessel densities the question arises whether a depth resolved vessel analysis in adjacent structures (conjunctiva, tenon's capsule, sclera) might be beneficial for a better understanding of the relative contribution of these structures in bleb function, that is, it could be well understood that vascularisation in one structure facilitates aqueous humour drainage while, in another structure, it promotes fibrosis and, ultimately, bleb failure. While the currently available evidence on a depth‐resolved vessel density analysis did not find a final answer to this question future larger scale longitudinal studies might be sufficiently powered to analyse these more subtle aspects of bleb vascularisation. Clearly, this will be dependent on standardised and clinically usable layer segmentation which is, to the best of our knowledge, currently unavailable for bleb AS‐OCTA. Federated learning, an implementation of multi‐centre AI‐model training which reduces the amount of data needed at a single centre, might be a future direction to overcome this current limitation [72].

Finally, attention shall be drawn to the necessity for clear differentiation of markers to monitor concomitant bleb function and parameters with predictive value for future bleb function. While the former may be of interest regarding the pathophysiological basis of bleb failure, the clinical value of these associations is limited as they are lacking predictive information on the fate of the bleb. Clinicians are, however, in urgent need of predictive markers for bleb failure in the early postoperative phase to stratify patients, adapt follow‐up intervals and plan interventions accordingly. In an environment of increasingly limited resources on the one hand and persistently high rates of secondary interventions after bleb‐forming surgeries [8, 9, 10] on the other hand, such markers are important for efficient resource allocation and have the potential to foster personalised medicine in postoperative patient care. AS‐OCT‐ and ‐OCTA‐based bleb parameters of the early postoperative phase (until 4 weeks after surgery) showed promise in longitudinal studies to predict the need for further interventions or IOP and thus could allow for bespoke patient follow up based on the risk of failure. Clearly, this approach needs further corroboration in additional studies.

It is noteworthy that the value to monitor concomitant bleb function and the predictive value of a parameter might not at all coincide. For instance, as mentioned in this review, bleb vessel density 1 month after TE was not correlated with concomitant IOP while it was with IOP 6 months after surgery [52] and it was predictive for needling until at least 6 months after TE [53].

In summary, several parameters (bleb height, bleb wall thickness, fluid cavity dimensions, bleb reflectivity, bleb vessel density) have been shown to be correlated with surgical success after filtration surgery, even though differences between procedures exist and are understudied. Up to date, no trial has been performed to investigate AS‐OCT(A)‐based clinical decision making after filtration surgery.

To move the field forward, predictive AS‐OCT and ‐OCTA‐based bleb parameters should be included in protocols of interventional trials. Doing so, two groups of hypotheses could be tested, depending on the aim of the study.

(I) AS‐OCT‐ and ‐OCTA‐based decision‐making leads to earlier identification of necessity for interventions (i.e., needlings) which enhances long‐term bleb function (necessity for revision, target IOP or disease progression).

(II) AS‐OCT‐ and ‐OCTA‐based decision‐making allows for safe early extension of follow‐up intervals while maintaining non‐inferior long‐term bleb function (necessity for needling or revision, target IOP or disease progression).
