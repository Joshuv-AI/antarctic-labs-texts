A Critical Review of YouTube Videos on the Socket-shield Technique: A Content-quality Analysis
Authors: Urvi Echhpal, Subhabrata Maiti, Rajendra Prabhu Abhinav
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Dental students often prefer social media for its accessibility and low cost but must critically evaluate the content before applying it in practice. This study analyzed YouTube content on socket shielding. A new Google account was created to search for “Socket Shield Technique” and “Partial Extraction Therapy.” Eligible videos were assessed for quality using the Global Quality (GQ) tool and the DISCERN tool. Statistical analysis was performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences software. Results showed an average of 28.35 likes per video, no dislikes, and substandard content indicated by a DISCERN score of 25.25 ± 2.4 and a GQ score of 1.82 ± 0.38. The study concludes that video content on socket shielding is not reliable, emphasizing the importance of clinical observation and hands-on practice.

Dental students and practitioners often seek information from social media platforms due to their accessibility and low cost.[1] These platforms allow for free speech and personal opinions, which must be carefully analyzed before use in clinical practice.[2] Recently, treatment objectives have shifted to focus more on the success of implants rather than just their survival. Research shows that the alveolus undergoes several phases of bone remodeling after tooth exfoliation, changing the ridge’s contour and buccal cortical plate thickness.[3] Implants inserted immediately after extraction can either experience resorption of crestal bone or prevent tissue alterations.[4]

The socket-shield technique, which retains part of the tooth root, aims to prevent tissue changes after extraction and ensure long-lasting osseointegration of implants.[5] Many YouTube videos explain this procedure, but clinicians must identify false or misleading information.[6] Social media is popular among dental students and young practitioners, but its reliability is rarely questioned. This study critically analyses YouTube videos on socket shielding, hypothesizing that the information may not be scientifically sound.

This cross-sectional analysis evaluated YouTube videos. Ethical clearance was not needed as only publicly available data were used, with research clearance obtained from the university (SRB/SDC/PROSTHO-2106/21/163). Based on a previous study by Gaikwad et al., a new profile was created to avoid algorithm biases.[7] A search was conducted in January 2024 using the terms “Socket Shielding” and “Partial Extraction Therapy.” Videos were screened for relevance and analyzed, and data were tabulated for statistical analysis [Figure 1].

Flowchart describing the methodology for video selection

A new Google account was used to search YouTube for “Socket Shield Technique” and “Partial Extraction Therapy.” The search was set to “worldwide,” and only the “relevance filter” was applied. Specific video features were not excluded. Based on prior studies, the first 824 videos were selected for review. Videos were added to a playlist for consistent analysis.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) unrelated topic; (2) brand advertisements; (3) non-English languages; (4) duplicate records; and (5) videos mislabeling immediate implants and root shielding as socket shielding. Video features such as upload date, country, duration, and likes were noted. Two evaluators (UE and AP) independently assessed the videos. Demographics, including views, likes/dislikes, duration, upload date, and target audience, were analyzed. Video information quality was evaluated using the Video Information and Quality Index (VIQI), DISCERN tool, and Global Quality Scale (GQS). The reliability of these tools was assessed. VIQI rated overall video quality on a scale from 1 (low) to 5 (high) for coherence, accuracy, quality, and precision. The DISCERN tool evaluated the reliability of information with 16 questions divided into three parts, with scores ranging from 1 (low quality) to 5 (high quality). GQS assessed instructional quality, clarity, relevance for patients, and content quality on a scale from 1 to 5, with higher scores indicating better quality. Disagreements were resolved by re-examination and consensus by a third author (S.M.).

The findings were tabulated, and statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, United States). The KS test assessed data normality, while the Mann–Whitney U test compared video quality. The Spearman correlation coefficient explored relationships in VIQI scores. A third researcher re-evaluated the videos to ensure reliability, with a significance level set at 5%. Cohen’s kappa statistics were used to assess evaluator consistency.

Out of 328 videos, 78 were analyzed. Most videos originated from Asia (44.6%), followed by North America (24.3%) and Europe (20.3%). The highest number were uploaded in 2021 (51.4%) [Figure 2a]. A positive correlation was found between GQS and VIQI scores (r = 0.538; P < 0.001). The mean DISCERN score was 25.72 ± 2.59, with no videos rated as “excellent quality.” Overall, 34 videos (45.9%) were deemed “high quality” [Figure 2b].

The average views per video were 2,814.75 (range, 6–40,000), with a mean duration of 597.71 s. Most videos (90.1%) were uploaded by dental practitioners, targeting primarily dental professionals (98.7%). Many videos focused on straightforward cases and lacked detailed explanations, often uploaded by medical settings and health-care professionals. This prevalence may lead to gaps in crucial implant treatment parameters on social media, resulting in ambiguous information availability [Tables 1 and 2].

Our study found that moderately rated videos had higher VIQI and GQS scores, reflecting smoother information flow and greater accuracy. A positive correlation between GQS and VIQI indicates that using images, animations, and logical structure improves video quality. Despite some issues, YouTube remains a vital platform for information dissemination, and health-care professionals should stay updated with reliable sources to reduce misinformation [Figure 2c].[89] While many videos covered surgical procedures, few addressed definitions, oral hygiene, failures, and contraindications. Research shows that viewers often lose interest in longer videos, suggesting that delivering concise, high-quality content can enhance information flow effectively.[1011]

YouTube users actively express their opinions through comments and likes/dislikes. The DISCERN tool proved reliable and user-friendly, helping clinical teams assess the quality of patient-recommended websites.[12] The Internet’s easy access to information has transformed behaviors, with YouTube being the second most popular social platform, used by nearly 95% of Internet users.[13] However, this study faced limitations due to a short data collection period and the dynamic nature of YouTube, making it challenging to monitor changing viewer preferences and video trends.

Based on the findings of the current study, although a useful tool to understand the methodology of the procedure, it must not be used as the fundamental knowledge of a clinician performing the procedure. Clinical observation, hands-on practice, and the use of simulators to practice procedures are key in order to be able to successfully perform and excel at clinical procedures.[14] It may be an acceptable tool for patients attempting to understand a procedure being recommended to them but would provide incomplete knowledge to practitioners which is dangerous. As Thomas B. Macaulay once said, “Half-knowledge is worse than ignorance.”
