Decrease in Incidence of Diarrhea Due to Cryptosporidium in Bangladeshi Children Is Associated With an Increase in Anti-Cryptosporidium Antibody Avidity
Authors: Carol A Gilchrist, William A O Petri, Biplob Hossain, Mamun Kabir, Hannah H So, G Brett Moreau, Uma Nayak, Jennie Z Ma, Zannatun Noor, Abu S G Faruque, Masud Alam, Rashidul Haque, William A Petri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Cryptosporidium is a cause of diarrhea morbidity and mortality in infants in low- and middle-income countries.MethodsA cohort of children was followed longitudinally in a high-transmission-intensity community in Bangladesh.ResultsDiarrhea attributed to Cryptosporidium (cryptosporidiosis) decreased from a peak of 0.19 episodes per child at 1–2 years to 0.05 episodes per child at 3–4 years of age (P = .0064). Notably, the decrease in cryptosporidiosis was not accompanied by a decline in subclinical infections. Using an episode-based analysis confirmed that the parasite burden declined with repeated infections (P < .0001 from the mixed-effects model included data from all infection frequencies; Cq value of the first and fourth infections (last reinfection with >10 cases): Cq 28.65 ± 5.533 versus 32.42 ± 4.046). There was also a decrease in the time required to clear a parasitic infection: longer infections (>1 month) occurred in 43% of the first infections compared to 24% in the fourth infections (P = .00017 from the mixed-effects model). The avidity of anti-Cp23 and anti-Cp17 plasma IgG increased in older children who had fewer diarrheal infections (ratio of the avidity index after the first infection versus that in the older repeatedly infected children: 1.81 ± 1.02 for anti-Cp23 IgG P > .0001 and 1.14 ± 0.35 anti-Cp17 IgG P = .0056).ConclusionsOur results are consistent with the development of an anti-Cryptosporidium adaptive immune response over repeated infections (average number of previous infections at 4 years, 2.42 ± 1.24) characterized by an increase in anti-Cryptosporidium antibody avidity that is associated with a decrease in cryptosporidiosis but not in subclinical Cryptosporidium infections. Clinical Trials Registration. NCT02764918.

(See the Editorial Commentary by Hanevik on pages e355–8.)

Cryptosporidium infection is a cause of diarrhea and associated with growth faltering in Bangladeshi infants. We are conducting a natural history study of cryptosporidiosis in infants living in a high transmission intensity urban community of Dhaka, Bangladesh, tracking infants’ Cryptosporidium infections by stool surveillance and twice weekly household visits to monitor diarrhea incidence. We report here the natural history of infection from birth through 4 years (48 months) (National Clinical Trial Identifier: NCT02764918) [1–5]. We analyzed the incidence of infections (subclinical and symptomatic) from birth to 4 years of age and correlated that with symptomatic and subclinical infection, infection duration, and the avidity of the anti-Cryptosporidium antibodies.

Antibody maturation, where repeated rounds of somatic hypermutation of the variable regions of the immunoglobulin genes and subsequent clonal selection of the B cells expressing the antibodies with high affinity [6], takes place in the germinal centers of secondary lymphoid organs, such as the mesenteric lymph nodes of the gut [7]. Increasing age or dwelling in a high-transmission area was, for example, associated with the acquisition of antibodies with a high affinity for Plasmodium antigens [8–11].

We previously measured the antibody responses to 1761 Cryptosporidium antigens in 1-year-old children. Antibody responses to only a subset of Cryptosporidium antigens (Gp40/15 [encompassing the Cp17 peptide], Cp23, CpSMP, Gp900, CpMuc8, CpCorA, and CpCCDC1) were associated with partial protection from reinfection through age 3 years [12]. The presence of antibodies against Cryptosporidium antigens Cp17 and Cp23 has also been associated with protection from reinfection in other studies [3, 4, 13–15]. These antibodies could be involved in blocking invasion of the epithelial cells by the parasite or potentially in the other antibody effector functions (antibody-dependent phagocytosis, antibody-dependent complement activation, or antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity) [16].

Here we report that the avidity of the anti-Cp17 and anti-Cp23 antibodies increased as children aged and had repeated infections. We also discuss the associated changes in disease prevalence that occurred in the cohort over this time period (from birth to 4 years [48 months] of age). During this time, episodes of cryptosporidiosis, parasite burden and the duration of parasite carriage declined while the frequency of subclinical Cryptosporidium infections remained constant.

METHODS

Child Cohort

This is a continuation of the study on Cryptosporidiosis and Enteropathogens in Bangladesh (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT02764918) where Mirpur and Mirzapur children were enrolled within 1 week of birth between June 2014 and March 2016. In this work we have focused on the 500 children enrolled in the impoverished urban community, Mirpur Dhaka, Bangladesh (Figure 1). At birth, 20% of children in this community meet the World Health Organization (WHO) malnutrition criteria as assessed by weight for height Z score (Supplementary Table 1). The number of malnourished children increased over the first 2 years of age [1, 17]. These children were monitored for diarrhea through biweekly visits to their homes by trained field investigators. Fecal samples collected from both diarrheal stools and asymptomatic surveillance stools were processed to extract fecal DNA and run a diagnostic quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) assay to detect Cryptosporidium spp. Samples with a quantitative cycle (Cq) value of ≤40 were considered Cryptosporidium positive; however, the analyses reported here were also rerun using the more stringent ≤35 Cq cut off to confirm the robustness of our conclusions.

CONSORT diagram of the Cryptosporidiosis and Enteropathogens in Bangladesh Study.

When most of the children were close to completing the third year of study the protocol was modified, and after a brief pause (June 2018 to January 2019), the follow-up period was extended to 5 years of age. Two different surveillance protocols were followed, in subgroup 1 (children born between 10 June 2015 and 13 March 2016), biweekly diarrheal surveillance and monthly asymptomatic stool collection continued. In subgroup 2 (children born between 8 June 2014 and 22 June 2015) the collection of diarrheal samples continued plus a biannual asymptomatic stool collection. A comparison of key demographics, anthropometry, and socioeconomic indicators of the children in subgroups 1 and 2 is shown in Supplementary Table 1. There was an increase in the probability that a member of subgroup 1 lived in proximity to an open drain compared to those enrolled in subgroup 2 (relative risk 1.25; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.007–1.555). However, growth stunting (anthropometric measurements length-for-age Z score and height-for-age Z score), which is linked to an increased susceptibility to Cryptosporidium infections in our cohort [1, 18], was not significantly different between the 2 groups (Supplementary Table 1). As the year-end clinic visit was used as a surrogate marker of active study participation, 2 additional children who completed their 3-year study visit after our previous publication resulted in a minor adjustment from our previously published numbers. To study the potential role of mucosal and systemic immunity, 59 children (from subgroups 1 and 2) were randomly selected from the children who had at least 1 Cryptosporidium infection prior to 1 year of age. In accordance with WHO polio eradication protocols, stool samples collected prior to May 2016 were discarded and were not available for fecal immunoglobulin A (IgA) analysis.

Human Sampling and Specimen Testing

Stool samples collected in the children's homes were placed on ice and brought to the icddr,b laboratory and frozen at −80°C within 6 hours of collection. Blood samples were collected every 6 months from the children from ages 1–3 years and annually at years 4 and 5. A subset of 59 children identified as infected with the Cryptosporidium parasite prior to 1 year in age were selected for anti-Cp23 and anti-Cp17 IgA and IgG immune profiles using the fecal samples collected after May 2016 and the plasma samples.

ELISA Protocol to Detect Anti-Cp23 and Anti-Cp17 IgG and IgA Antibodies

While 3 Cryptosporidium species were found to commonly infect humans (Cryptosporidium hominis, Cryptosporidium parvum, and Cryptosporidium meleagridis), at this urban site where humans appeared to be the main source of new infections the C. hominis species was predominant [1]. The recombinant Cp23 protein used in the studies described in this article is highly conserved with minimal changes occurring between the Cryptosporidium species (amino acid identity, C. parvum, 97%; C. meleagridis, 92%; Supplementary Material 1) [19–21]. The gp60 gene that encodes the Gp15 protein, which encompasses the Cp17 peptide, is hypervariable [22]. However, Cp17 itself is a relatively conserved peptide and genotype-specific anti-Cp17 antibody differences appear to be minor [23], although they cannot be completely discounted (in 7 out of 10 cases the second gp60 genotype was different than in the preceding case Supplementary Table 2). As amino acid differences do exist between the Cp17 sequences from the different Bangladesh families (Ia, Ib, Id, Ie, If, IIc) we selected as the template for our synthetic gene the most common C. hominis family in Bangladesh children in 2015 and 2016 (Ia, 2015, 44%; 2016, 38%).

These antigens were used in an in-house horseradish peroxidase enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (HRP-ELISA) and results converted to antibody units relative to a pool of positive samples (Supplementary Material 2) [2]. Antibody avidity was determined only if both control and chaotrope-treated wells were within the interpretable range of the assay (Supplementary Material 2). To calculate the antibody avidity index value, the relative antibody values obtained from the wells incubated with ammonium thiocyanate were divided by the companion phosphate-buffered saline-treated well [8].

Statistical Analysis

Analysis of infection frequency and the impact of repeat infections on parasite burden and antibody levels were performed with the Prism 10 computer program using either the Cochran-Armitage χ2 test for trend or the Kruskai-Wallis test with the Dunn test for multiple comparisons of nonparametric data, as appropriate (GraphPad). All other analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.0), with packages tidyverse (version 2.0.0) and readxl (version 1.4.2) used for data organization and visualization (RStudio Team 2015). The strength of the longitudinal antibody associations with time was analyzed using a linear mixed effects model with lme4 package to account for within-child correlations. The diarrheal and subclinical infection rates were summarized in each 6-month interval, and their differences over time were evaluated using Poisson regression with Proc GENMOD in SAS (version 9.4, SAS Institute, Inc).

Study Approval

The study was approved by the Ethical and Research Review Committees of the International Centre for Diarrhoeal Disease Research, Bangladesh (PR-13092) and the Institutional Review Board of the University of Virginia (IRB No. 20388). Informed written consent was obtained from the parents or guardians for the participation of their child in the study.

RESULTS

Cryptosporidium-Associated Diarrheal Disease, But Not Subclinical Infection, Declined Beginning at 2 Years of Age

Five hundred children were enrolled within the first week of birth, and of these 360 completed 4 full years of observation (Figure 1). Although diarrheal samples continued to be collected from all children enrolled over the 4 years (48 months) of the study, monthly surveillance was only continued in years 3–4 for children in subgroup 1, while surveillance samples in subgroup 2 were collected at 6-month intervals (Figure 1). To analyze changes in Cryptosporidium infection frequency in years 0–4, we analyzed the subgroup 1 dataset where the field workers continued to make biweekly study visits, and the collection of both monthly surveillance stool samples and yearly blood samples continued through year 4.

Cryptosporidium qPCR-positive samples were classified as a new infection if the stool samples collected during the preceding 65 days were Cryptosporidium qPCR negative. The median time between new infections was 264 days (95% confidence interval [CI], 242–288 days). If the first Cryptosporidium-positive sample in an infection was from a diarrheal case, this infection was defined as a Cryptosporidium-associated diarrheal infection (cryptosporidiosis). If the first qPCR-positive sample in an infection was from a child with no diarrheal symptoms, it was classified as a subclinical Cryptosporidium infection [1]. The prevalence of the parasite gp60 genotypes in sequential infections in the same child reflected that of the population at that time and was neither necessarily concordant (possibly reflecting a continuing infection below the limit of detection) or discordant (an indicator of genotype-specific immunity) (Supplementary Table 2) [2].

In line with previous observations that cryptosporidiosis significantly declined in the repeat infections (in Kabir et al diarrheal frequency was 0.17 in third infections [5]), the frequency of cryptosporidiosis declined in age 3–4 years (Figure 2A; Poisson regression analysis year 2 [12–24 months] vs year 4 [36–48 months], P = .0064; Supplementary Tables 3 and 4). The frequency of cryptosporidiosis peaked between 1 and 2 years of age when there were 0.19 diarrheal episodes per child. Thereafter it occurred less frequently, falling to 0.05 episodes per child between 3 and 4 years (36–48 months). Subclinical Cryptosporidium infections did not decline over the same time period; in fact, they increased relative to the rate at 2 years of age and remained at 0.7 (SD 0.05) episodes per child year in both 3 and 4 years in age (Poisson regression analysis year 2 vs year 4, P = .0049; Figure 2A and Supplementary Table 5). This suggested that in these children > 6 months of age, exposure to the Cryptosporidium parasite remained constant while the frequency of cryptosporidiosis, but not disease duration, declined (Supplementary Table 6). A decline in cryptosporidiosis also occurred in subgroup 2 (Supplementary Figure 1).

The Duration of Cryptosporidium Stool Positivity Was Shorter in Children With Repeat Infections

We previously discovered that diarrheal frequency and parasite burden declined in repeat infections [5]. We were able to repeat and extend these observations using the 4-year data (Figure 2B). Due to the increased number of repeat infections available for analysis in the year 4 data, we also had sufficient power to examine variations occurring in the asymptomatic carriage of the Cryptosporidium parasite (Figure 2C).

The collection interval in Cryptosporidium-positive diarrheal samples was, of necessity, irregular as these were collected during periods of active diarrhea, and, therefore, the Cryptosporidium-qPCR-positive monthly surveillance samples temporarily linked to an episode of diarrheal cryptosporidiosis did not necessarily represent an equivalent time period. However, the duration of subclinical infections could be examined where the total infection duration was defined by the continuing Cryptosporidium qPCR positivity of monthly surveillance stools. Cryptosporidium-positive infections were classified into cases where only a monthly surveillance qPCR-positive fecal sample was collected or where more than 1 (≥ 2) temporally linked positive samples were collected from the same child [1]. A significant decrease in the proportion of extended infections was evident in repeatedly infected children (P = .00017 from mixed-effects model; Figure 2C and Supplementary Table 7). This suggested that while sterile immunity had not yet developed, prior infection resulted in a protective immune response that decreased the duration of subclinical infections in repeatedly infected children.

Anti-Cryptosporidium Cp23 and Cp17 IgA and IgG Antibody Profiles

Throughout the study period, a significant proportion of subjects remained serologically positive for anti-Cp17 and anti-Cp23 antibodies (Supplementary Table 8). To examine the serological response in conjunction with potential confounders, plasma samples were assayed for anti-Cp17 and anti-Cp23 IgA and IgG antibody levels between years 1 and 4 of age. These samples were collected from children in both subgroups as only 39 children in subgroup 1 had at least 1 Cryptosporidium infection before 1 year of age and had completed 4 years of the study (and so had the potential to be seropositive at age 1 year). An additional 15 previously infected children were added from subgroup 2 (total children, n = 54; plasma samples, n = 345). These children had a median of 3 Cryptosporidium infections over the 4 years tracked, with a range of 1 to 6 infections (Supplementary Figure 2). Surveillance fecal samples collected at < 3 years of age from the same children were also available to analyze fecal IgA; however, due to the discontinuation of some children from the study (Figure 1) and the loss of fecal samples due to the WHO Global Action Plan for the containment of poliovirus infectious and potentially infectious materials [24], only a subset of the collected samples were available in the study database for further analyses (n = 1308; year 1 n = 209, year 2 n = 482, year 3 n = 649). As observed in our previous work, fecal anti-Cp23 and anti-Cp17 IgA were associated with an increase in the time until a Cryptosporidium reinfection occurred (Supplementary Figure 3) [3].

Linear mixed effects modeling was used to examine associations between longitudinal antibody levels and factors previously associated with susceptibility to cryptosporidiosis (household income, proximity to open drains, child height-for-age Z score, and rainy season in Bangladesh [June to October]), as well as the impact of child age and the number of previous Cryptosporidium infections (Figure 3) [1, 5]. Although the percent of antibody-positive children remained high (Supplementary Table 8), the number of new Cryptosporidium episodes was significantly associated with increased fecal anti-Cp17 and anti-Cp23 IgA titers (Figure 3A and 3B) whereas the impact on the systemic antibody level was not significant. In contrast, samples collected during the monsoon exhibited significantly increased anti-Cryptosporidium antibody levels in plasma (Figure 3C, 3E, and 3F) but not in fecal samples. In line with our earlier observations, anti-Cryptosporidium antibody levels continued to decline (fecal anti-Cryptosporidium IgA and plasma IgG) as the children got older (Figure 3A, 3B, 3E, and F; and in the linear regression analysis of fecal anti-Cp23 and anti-Cp17 IgA levels relative to time shown in Supplementary Figure 4) [12].

Anti-Cp17 and Anti-Cp23 Antibody Avidity Increased With Age and With Repeated Infection

We next tested whether an increase in anti-Cryptosporidium antibody avidity after repeated infections could be associated with the decrease in cryptosporidiosis. Antibody affinity maturation increases antibody activity through multiple rounds of somatic hypermutation and selection in the germinal center [25]. To measure antibody avidity (the functional affinity of the antibodies) a modification of the standard ELISA assay was developed that included an incubation with the chaotropic reagent ammonium thiocyanate (NH4SCN) [8, 9, 26, 27]. This assay was then used to compare the avidity of human antibodies in plasma collected from previously infected infants aged 12 or 18 months with the avidity of the IgG plasma antibodies in the samples collected from the same infants at 4 years (48 months) of age. A significant increase in anti-Cp17 and anti-Cp23 antibody avidity was observed in the older children (Figure 4).

DISCUSSION

The key finding of this study was that the frequency of cryptosporidiosis decreased from age 2 to 4 years whereas the frequency of subclinical Cryptosporidium infections remained constant. This decline in diarrhea due to Cryptosporidium was associated with an increase in the avidity of anti-Cp23 and anti-Cp17 antibodies.

After the earliest infections in year 1 antibody levels were high, but thereafter in years 2–4 the level of Cp17 and Cp23 antibodies declined. We do not know if this is related to age or exposure. T follicular helper cells (Tfh) interact with B cells within the germinal centers and are essential for the increase in antibody avidity (the sum of the antibody epitope-specific affinities) [28, 29]. To examine whether it was possible that it was only maturation of the innate immune system rather than repeated exposure to the pathogen that was responsible for the decrease in cryptosporidiosis we separately examined the data from the first collections in younger (0–24 months) and older children (24 to 48 months). Cryptosporidiosis did not significantly decrease if the first infection occurred when the children were older (cryptosporidiosis frequency [0–2 years] 29% of first Cryptosporidium infections [n = 112]; frequency [2–4 years] 21% [n = 43]; P = .32). A limitation in this analysis is, however, the assumption that maturation of the gut immune system is complete at 2 years of age and that environmental factors do not impact this process in Bangladesh infants [30, 31]. In fact, we have observed a defect in the activation of Tfh in Bangladeshi infants associated with the number of diarrheal infections and the presence of malnutrition [30].

In multiple human cohorts that received different vaccines (hepatitis B virus, influenza virus, tetanus toxin, salmonella, or measles), vaccine-specific Tfh17 cells persist and can provide the immunological memory that allows a rapid and effective response to invading pathogens [32, 33]. In controlled human malaria infection models an expansion in the number of T-follicular helper cells was correlated with the magnitude and breadth of antimalarial antibodies [34]. We examined anti-Cp17 and anti-Cp23 antibody avidity in a subset of paired plasma samples collected from the same Bangladeshi infants after the initial Cryptosporidium infection and at 4 years of age when repeated Cryptosporidium infections had occurred. We found that the avidity of the anti-Cp17 and anti-Cp23 IgG antibodies was indeed higher in the samples collected when the children were 4 years old. The development of these antibodies was associated with a decrease in the occurrence of symptomatic disease (cryptosporidiosis) upon infection with the Cryptosporidium parasite.

One limitation of this study was that we did not determine the species or genotypes of the infecting Cryptosporidium parasite. In previous studies, we had determined that at this study site the C. hominis parasite was responsible for most cases of cryptosporidiosis and subclinical Cryptosporidium infections in children and adults and that parasite genotype was not associated with the probability of cryptosporidiosis [1, 2, 35]. A second study limitation was the identification of the cause of a diarrhea episode, children in this community are often concurrently infected with multiple enteropathogens [36]. For most enteropathogens at our study site the incidence of diarrhea was highest between 6 months and 3 years of age [37, 38]. To rectify the attribution issue, cases of cryptosporidiosis were defined as an episode of diarrhea that occurred with a new Cryptosporidium infection, indicated by the preceding stool sample(s) (either from regular surveillance or from a diarrheal episode) testing negative for Cryptosporidium for at least 65 days prior [1]. As this method of linking diarrheal episodes to a Cryptosporidium infection could potentially lead to an overestimation of cryptosporidiosis, the decline may have been even more rapid than is suggested in Figure 2A and Supplementary Figure 1.

The mechanisms involved in the selection of high-affinity antibodies are complex [35] and we noted that anti-Cp23 avidity increased more substantially than anti-Cp17. Differences have also been observed in the timing of the acquisition of increased anti-Toxoplasma gondi antibody avidity to different antigen targets after an infection [39]. Additional work will be required to fully characterize the timing of the functional changes that occurred in antibody affinity after a Cryptosporidium infection [10, 11, 23, 40–43]. A vaccine against cryptosporidiosis could improve child health by reducing the burden of cryptosporidiosis [44]. This parasite has a complex lifecycle with both intracellular and extracellular life stages, and both cellular and humoral immunity are likely to be important components of a protective immune response [3, 4, 45–49]. The data in this article suggest that increases in antibody affinity to key parasite antigens are associated with a reduction in the frequency of cryptosporidiosis and a decline in infection duration. Our findings are consistent with the development of a protective immune response. This work therefore supports a role for adaptive immunity in protecting children from cryptosporidiosis.
