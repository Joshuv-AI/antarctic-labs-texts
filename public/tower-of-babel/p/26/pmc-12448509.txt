Perceived need to prevent stroke readmission: A qualitative study from the perspective of stroke patients and healthcare professionals
Authors: Mohammad Rajabpour, Kavian Ghandehari, Amir Mirhaghi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Stroke readmission poses a significant challenge for both stroke patients (SPs) and healthcare professionals (HCPs). Gaining a comprehensive understanding of the factors influencing stroke readmission is crucial in empowering individuals to leverage supportive resources. This study aimed to explore the perceptions of HCPs and SPs regarding the modifiable factors influencing stroke readmission.MATERIALS AND METHOD:This qualitative study employed in-depth, semi-structured interviews with 30 participants selected through purposeful sampling from October 2023 to January 2024. An interview guide featuring open-ended questions about stroke readmission experiences was utilized for data collection. The interviews were continued until data saturation was achieved. All interviews were recorded, transcribed, and subjected to directed content analysis.RESULTS:The modifiable factors influencing stroke readmission were categorized into five generic categories: (1) knowledge deficit about the disease, (2) self-forgetfulness physical care, (3) food swamp, (4) high-risk behaviors, and (5) psychological distress.CONCLUSION:According to the results of this study, by identifying the modifiable risk factors associated with stroke readmission, health stakeholders can design comprehensive preventive educational programs to address these needs.

Introduction

Stroke stands as the second most prevalent cause of mortality and the third leading cause of disability worldwide.[12] Among the adverse repercussions of stroke, readmission holds particular significance due to its association with heightened morbidity and mortality.[3] Although numerous studies have delineated various causes and risk factors contributing to the readmission of stroke patients (SPs),[34] the majority of these investigations have primarily centered on non-modifiable factors such as age, gender, and race. Furthermore, prior research has overlooked the contextual disparities in healthcare systems across different countries.[56]

Comprehensive follow-up care and preventive programs necessitate a profound understanding of the risks and complications of the disease and its implications on mortality. Kharbach et al. reported that 82.1% of patients were unaware of the risks associated with an inactive lifestyle, 78.9% did not recognize the impact of an unhealthy diet, while 73.3% were oblivious to the link between high cholesterol and stroke, with 70.2% failing to acknowledge the correlation between obesity, overweight, and stroke.[7] In addition, a different study revealed that 17.1% of patients lacked awareness about the specific bodily organs affected by stroke.[8] Notably, possessing knowledge about the risk factors and symptoms of stroke and readmission increased the likelihood of a correct response in the event of a recurrent stroke by 1.82 times.[9]

Investigating patients’ perceptions on preventing stroke readmission can offer valuable insights that go beyond statistical data on patients’ knowledge deficits. Cultural differences may affect patients’ attitudes toward certain aspects, with some individuals being more apathetic while others placing more significance on them. In addition, cultural and social barriers might prevent patients from avoiding certain risk factors, thus diminishing the effectiveness of prevention efforts. In this situation, theories or hypothetical models that can guide the practice of nursing care can be useful.[10] In modifiable risk factors associated with stroke research, so far no specific theory has been developed while analyzing and merging the previous studies of these factors through the hypothetical model deployed in the previous study of the research team [Figure 1].[11] It provides a possible framework for the understanding of the importance of each risk factor. This model is based on the direct and indirect role of risk factors in stroke readmission and the pooled odds ratio (OR) of the associated risk factors. A higher OR indicates more patient persistence in engaging in these risk factors.

Hypothetical model of modifiable risk factors of stroke readmission

How this model is applicable to the care and prevention of stroke readmission is unknown. Qualitative studies can provide an understanding of why patients persist in engaging in these risk factors. The clearest and most effective understanding and explanation of human experiences can be obtained from first-hand resources, namely the individuals who have actually experienced the phenomenon. Therefore, authoritative research sources place great importance on including the voices of these individuals.[12]

Qualitative research, due to its collaborative, comprehensive, and flexible structure, is well-suited for exploring and uncovering complex issues, such as mixed or ambiguous attitudes. Unlike quantitative studies, qualitative research can capture the meaningful experiences and life values of individuals who have been readmitted but whose experiences may not have been captured in previous studies.[13] As a result, qualitative research can provide a range of risk factors based on the unique characteristics of patients. This comprehensive information enables the development of care plans that are more aligned with the specific needs of patients and can lead to greater success. By accurately identifying modifiable risk factors for readmission within the context of a particular society, strategies can be developed that are better suited to that society’s conditions. In addition to identifying modifiable risk factors for readmission, clarifying the perceptions of patients and healthcare professionals (HCPs) by using the content analysis approach can receive greater attention in the education of service providers, with the goal of preventing or reducing readmission. Therefore, the aim of this study is to explore the perceptions of service providers and HCPs regarding the modifiable risk factors for stroke readmission.

Materials and Methods

Study dosing and setting

This qualitative study was conducted from October 2023 to January 2024 at one of the public hospitals affiliated with Mashhad University of Medical Sciences. This university is a leading referral center for SPs in eastern Iran. The choice of the qualitative method was suitable for this study as it allowed the researchers to gather detailed direct descriptions and first-hand descriptions[14] of the specific and intricate topic of readmission and its risk factors in SPs.

Study participants and sampling

The participants in this study consisted of 16 SPs and 14 HCPs who were selected using a purposeful sampling method, with the goal of achieving maximum diversity in terms of demographic characteristics. The inclusion criteria for the patients included having a history of readmission, proficiency in the Persian language, willingness to participate in the study, and confirmation of stroke diagnosis by a neurologist. The inclusion criteria for the HCPs were having at least 1 year of work experience in neurosurgery and willingness to share their experiences. Participants who met the inclusion criteria were invited to voluntarily take part in the study. Sampling continued until data saturation occurred (i.e., no new concepts were extracted from the data).

Data collection tools and technique

At the beginning of the interview, the objectives of the study were stated. Explanations were then given about the readmission of SPs to ensure that the participants understood this concept. In addition, permission was obtained from the participants to record their voices. In-depth, semi-structured interviews were conducted in the educational classroom of the neurology ward, following the interview guide [Table 1]. The interviews lasted for a minimum of 25–55 minutes. To ensure consistency in data collection, a general query interview guide was used. During the interview, probing questions such as “why?” “what?” “Could you tell me more about.?” and “Can you give me an example of.?” were asked to gain a deeper understanding of the dimensions of readmission risk factors.

Example of interview questions

The interviews were recorded using a voice recorder, and after the completion of each interview, they were immediately transcribed, analyzed, and coded. Thus, after the end of each interview, all recorded conversations were fully listened to. Then, the interview content was typed verbatim, along with non-verbal messages, including changes in tone of voice, emphasis, and pauses, and saved in an electronic file format. Analyzing each interview immediately after conducting it was a guide to ask more detailed and complete questions of the next patients. The interviews continued until data saturation (when no new codes were extracted and the ambiguous points of the extracted codes and categories were clear). In the present study, data saturation was obtained after 30 interviews.

Data analysis

Data management was performed using MAXQDA (version 20) software. Elo and Kyngas (2008) content analysis approach (deductive approach)[15] was used to explore the perception of SPs and HCPs regarding the modifiable risk factors of stroke readmission. This approach includes three phases: 1- Preparation (including determining the unit of analysis and finding the logical connection of the data with the whole subject) 2- Organizing (including developing the analysis matrix and extracting data from the content by grouping, categorization, and abstracting), and 3- Reporting.

In the first step of the first phase (determining the unit of analysis), a part of the interview text that was most related to the study question was selected as the most appropriate unit of analysis. During the step of finding the logical connection of the data with the whole subject, the texts were reviewed and read several times to ensure proper communication and real understanding of the data. The researchers sought to answer questions such as “What is happening?” “Who is telling?” “Where is this happening?” “When did it happen?” and “Why?”

In the second phase (organization), an unstructured analysis matrix was designed deductively from the generic categories and subcategories to develop the analysis matrix. This matrix was then integrated into a conceptual framework, which was extracted from the literature review conducted by the research team.[11] During the process of extracting data from the content based on categories, each condensed meaning unit was assigned a label called a code. In the categorization step, the formed groups were categorized into each category of the conceptual framework based on their similarities and differences, and then they were abstracted.

In the third phase (reporting), all phases of directed content analysis and the findings were reported.

Rigor

To ensure the rigor of the study, Lincoln and Guba’s[16] criteria were utilized. Participants were provided with transcripts of several interviews and codes and asked to confirm the consistency between the codes and their experiences, thereby increasing the credibility of the findings. The presence and long-term involvement of M.R.P. and A.M. with the research environment and transcripts contributed to the increased data credibility. To determine confirmability, some interviews were independently analyzed by the study authors. To ensure dependability, the work process and findings were presented to three external peers for the purpose of checking the accuracy of the data analysis. To enhance transferability, interviews, observations, field notes, and the selection of participants with a wide range of readmission experiences were utilized.

Ethical approval

Prior to commencing the study, the objectives of the study, voluntary participation, and the confidentiality of information were explained to all participants. Informed written consent was obtained from each participant. This study has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Mashhad University of Medical Sciences (ethical code: IR.MUMS.NURSE.REC.1402.045).

Emerged main category, generic categories, and subcategories

Knowledge deficit about the diseases

Knowledge deficit refers to full unawareness, inadequate awareness, and the danger of ignorance. This issue is often connected to the complications of diseases, their signs and symptoms, and the methods of treating them. In the analysis of the interviews, four subcategories were categorized into this generic category:

Ignoring the risk of complication of comorbidities

It means not paying attention to the complications of comorbidities (such as hypertension, diabetes, and heart diseases), despite being familiar with them. In most of the interviews, the participants stated that they had information about their comorbidities, but they did not take it seriously. The codes related to this subcategory were not paying attention to the importance of controlling comorbidities, not taking the risks of comorbidities seriously, and neglecting the complications of comorbidities.

For example: “Something was said about the dangers of sugar and my high blood pressure, but I told myself that they were exaggerating.” (SP 13).

“I was talking to the patient, and he said: I know about sugar and blood pressure, but I didn’t know it was so important and that it could affect a person like this.” (HCP 10).

Unawareness of the risk of complications of comorbidities

Unawareness refers to a lack of information and familiarity with the risks and complications of comorbidities. Another significant challenge faced by SPs is their lack of awareness regarding the risks associated with uncontrolled prior diseases. The codes related to this subcategory include ignorance about the dangers of hypertension and diabetes, lack of understanding regarding the importance of controlling hypertension, lack of knowledge about the normal range of blood pressure, lack of awareness about hypertension, and failure to regularly measure blood pressure.

For example: “My blood pressure sometimes rises and falls. However, most of the time it stays around 14 or 15 mmHg, occasionally reaching 16 mmHg. Well, many people in our family are like that and nothing has happened to them. They consider a blood pressure of 15-16 mmHg to be normal.” (SP 11).

“No one has ever provided me with proper training. Sometimes, when I visit my healthcare center, they simply conduct tests or call me and inform me that I need to take a test to complete my medical records.” (HCP 13).

Unawareness of stroke recurrence signs and symptoms

The meaning of this subcategory is a lack of familiarity with the signs and symptoms associated with the risk of stroke recurrence. Although the patients had previously experienced a stroke and it was expected that they would have minimal knowledge of stroke signs and symptoms, a large number of patients were not aware of them. Codes related to this subcategory included not being aware of headache, dizziness, and imbalance as risk symptoms, not knowing that diplopia could be a sign of risk, and not recognizing left/right hemiplegia/hemiparesis as a risk sign.

For example: “Many patients who come here are unaware of the symptoms of a stroke. For instance, one patient had experienced a headache and dizziness a few days ago, but he stated that he didn’t know the reason. Even though this patient had a history of stroke, he still didn’t realize that these symptoms could be related to a stroke.” (HCP 8).

Fluctuating medication adherence

A situation where the patient is aware of the importance of treating his comorbidities but does not follow his treatment plan for various reasons. Despite knowing the importance of taking medications regularly, many patients arbitrarily reduce, stop, or replace them. The codes related to this subcategory include satisfaction with the current situation, being overwhelmed with work, supporting the patient with incorrect information, tiredness of taking medicine, passive role of the patient, changing the dose of medicine due to the fear of side effects, unconscious self-decision making, and using medicine when signs appear.

For example: “I had a patient who said he didn’t take his medicine. I asked him why, and he said, ‘I take the sheep to graze in the morning, I forget to take my medicine. I was fine and I didn’t need to take medicine.’” (HCP 4).

Self-forgetfulness physical care

Self-forgetfulness physical care is defined as a “lack of self-care to an extent that it threatens personal health and safety.” Two subcategories were categorized into this generic category, including:

Ignoring the risk of inactivity

This refers to not paying attention to the potential dangers of physical inactivity, despite being aware of them. The codes associated with this subcategory include lack of physical activity, inadequate time for physical activity, feeling incapable of engaging in physical activity, and underestimating the importance of physical activity. Many patients refrain from physical activity due to reasons such as lack of time, motivation, or physical ability. Some even consider their daily activities, such as housework, to be sufficient exercise.

For example: “When I discuss exercise with many of my patients or their families, they either honestly admit to not exercising or claim that their daily chores serve as physical activity.” (HCP 11).

Inadequate awareness of the importance of physical activity

This subcategory pertains to cases where patients possess limited information about the significance of physical activity, but this knowledge is inadequate to prevent or mitigate risks. Some patients have received information about the importance of physical activity after a stroke, but this information is incomplete and insufficient to assist them effectively. The codes associated with this subcategory include a lack of knowledge regarding permissible physical activities within personal limits, attempting physical activities beyond one’s capabilities, and insufficient understanding of metabolism-boosting activities.

For example: “We had a patient who went home and ended up falling because he didn’t know what alternative activities he could engage in and became stuck.” (HCP 10).

Food swamp

A condition in which a person or a society is surrounded by many unhealthy eating habits and it is not possible to get rid of them and make the right choice (healthy diet) is called a food swamp. The adverse effects of food swamps on individuals and society are usually long-term and relatively deep. Mostly, these habits originate from ignoring the danger and insufficient awareness. Two subcategories were categorized into this generic category, including:

Unawareness of the risks associated with unhealthy eating habits

The codes associated with this subcategory include a lack of perception of the threat, fluctuations in food choices, perceived inefficiency, weak self-control, unpleasant taste, and information confusion.

For example: “I prefer fats over meats, and I always keep a bowl of oil next to me. Wherever I go, they know to bring extra oil to my table.” (SP 14).

“One of the main problems SPs face is their diet. They don’t adhere to it and can’t stop themselves.” (HCP 1).

Ignoring the risks associated with unhealthy eating habits

The codes related to this subcategory include a desire for high-calorie foods, neglecting the harm caused by high-calorie foods, using unhealthy oils and salt, inability to follow a healthy diet in the workplace, adopting harmful eating habits, traditional-family style of cooking, inefficiency in the consumption of soft drinks, fatigue from food restrictions, feeling ineffective in following a healthy diet, not understanding the dangers of consuming excessive salt and fat, finding excuses to eat sweets, and contradictory information.

For example: “.I don’t put sugar in my tea, but I eat baklava and other sweets because they’re delicious. I went home and ate a kilo of baklava.” (SP 7).

“…Sometimes, at work, my colleagues crave sandwiches or pizza, for example, and I join them.” (SP 2).

High-risk behaviors

High-risk behaviors are defined as actions that increase the risk of disease, injury, disability, death, or social problems. One of the most common high-risk behaviors is tobacco use disorder. This generic category can be further categorized into two subcategories, which include:

Harmful recreational habits

Harmful and inappropriate behaviors may occur in recreational programs. Some patients mentioned using hookah and cigarettes at family events as a form of fun and enjoyment. The codes associated with this subcategory were having fun with smoking and having fun with hookah.

For example: “. sometimes when I was with my sister and we went to a party at home, we used to smoke hookah and cigarettes.” (SP 11).

Harmful coping habits

This subcategory pertains to unhealthy ways individuals cope with stress and difficulties. Some patients were unable to adapt to the daily stress in their lives and turned to cigarettes and tobacco as a means to alleviate their problems. The codes associated with this subcategory were taking refuge in cigarettes and using cigarettes as a sedative.

For example: “. I had a patient who even had a history of heart attack, but because of the tensions in his family, after quitting smoking, he returned and started smoking again.” (HCP 5).

Psychological distress

Psychological distress encompasses negative feelings and emotions that can impact a person’s daily activities. Three subcategories were categorized into this generic category:

Occupational stress

Occupational stress refers to the negative psychological and physical effects employees experience due to workplace responsibilities, environment, or other pressures. The codes categorized within this subcategory included job concerns, work environment pressure, and confrontational work environment.

For example: “I was always worried that nothing happened inside the hotel, because he used to find places very difficult and said that if something happens, I will grab your collar, because there is no access to them.” (SP 1).

Family conflict

Family conflict arises when there is active opposition among family members due to differing beliefs and views. The codes associated with this subcategory were economic disagreements within the family and emotional breakdown.

For example: “I had argued with my brother about inheritance and I became like this and had a stroke, otherwise I was fine before and had no problems.” (SP 5).

Empathic concern

Empathic concern refers to emotions directed toward the well-being of someone in need. These emotions include tenderness, sympathy, compassion, and a willingness to intervene in others’ problems. The codes categorized within this subcategory included concern about children’s future life situation and concern about children’s health.

For example: “My stroke this time was triggered by anger and sadness. My eldest son has been having problems with his wife for some time now, and his wife has left him, demanding a divorce.” (SP 12).

Discussion

The present study explores the perceptions of SPs and HCPs regarding the modifiable risk factors for SP readmission. The results indicate a significant lack of knowledge among patients regarding comorbidities and their complications. Readmission is greatly influenced by the health and healthcare services provided by countries. Countries with extensive educational and follow-up programs have significantly reduced the rate of patient readmission of patients.[4]

This indicates the effectiveness of controlling modifiable factors in reducing SP readmission rates. However, to make care and education programs more effective in addressing modifiable factors, it is necessary to adapt training for individuals with lower levels of education. Low-educated or illiterate individuals do not benefit from existing educational programs, and the follow-up process is disrupted.[7] Shahjouei et al. showed that more than 17% of Bushehr’s residents and 48.7% of the SPs admitted to a referral center in Tehran could not name even one stroke risk factor.[17] In the present study, the majority of participating patients had a high school diploma or less and had limited information about the complications of not controlling comorbidities such as hypertension and diabetes.

Another significant aspect of the patients’ knowledge deficit in this study was the limitations of access to health services (population distance from service centers and the ratio of clinics, laboratories, and outpatient centers to the population) in remote areas of Iran.[18] Dong et al. and Sirousinejad et al. showed that the prevalence of patient delay in SPs was 61%.[1920] Thus, access to health services and a positive attitude toward using these services play an important role in promoting healthy behavior in society. Previous studies have also reported a wrong attitude toward the availability, efficiency, and affordability of stroke management.[21]

In addition, healthcare providers in health centers and clinics often lack specialized information about diseases such as stroke readmission, and effective training is not provided to patients.[22] The patients in this study also mentioned this point. Sarbazi et al. showed that the rate of stroke recurrence, severity, disability, and death in rural areas of Iran is higher than in urban areas.[6] Therefore, it is necessary for healthcare providers to be more proactive in this field, and formal training and retraining programs should be considered for them.

Another aspect of the knowledge deficit in the patients of the present study is related to the lack of organized planning in Iran for health promotion education and the implementation of educational campaigns on occasions such as World Stroke Day. In addition, the lack of awareness sessions specifically tailored to the early warning signs of stroke may contribute to the patients’ low level of recognition of these signs. Kharbach et al. demonstrated that a lack of knowledge about warning signs potentially delays the early use of specialized hospital centers for managing SPs.[7]

Another generic category extracted in the present study was self-forgetfulness of physical care. The results indicated that SPs do not make an effort to engage in physical activity and are content with simple daily tasks such as doing household chores. This behavior suggests a lack of awareness regarding the negative complication of not participating in effective physical activities, which in turn increases the risk of stroke recurrence. Ghozy et al. found that individuals who spent more than 4 hours per day watching TV, videos, or using a computer were more likely to be readmitted for stroke.[23] On the contrary, various activities were found to be protective against stroke, such as engaging in moderate-intensity work and vigorous-intensity work activities during the past 30 days, as well as performing muscle-strengthening exercises. Gao et al. believed that this physical activity has a significant effect on improving physical quality of life.[24]

Another generic category identified in this study was food swamp. According to the participants’ experiences, changing their unhealthy eating and dietary habits was extremely difficult, if not impossible for them. Guo et al.’s study also revealed that when SPs have an imbalanced diet and crave unhealthy food or overeat, the risk of stroke and its recurrence increases.[25] Changing eating habits goes beyond simply reducing certain foods and opting for healthier choices. Saulle et al. showed that a strong adherence to the Mediterranean diet is linked to a lower risk of stroke and can also affect the costs associated with managing the condition.[26] In addition, Kang et al. found that several factors influence patients’ dietary choices, including age, family, socioeconomic class, and religion, making it challenging for them to modify their eating patterns.[27] Moreover, food cost and availability are two major determinants of patients’ dietary habits. Considering that most participants in this study had easy access to livestock and livestock products in the long term, it appears that their beliefs and food preferences have been influenced, resulting in negative eating choices.

Another significant risk factor identified based on the participants’ experiences was engaging in high-risk behaviors such as smoking cigarettes and using hookahs. It can be concluded that SPs hold certain beliefs about smoking, perceiving it as non-problematic. However, Pan et al. found that every 5 cigarettes smoked per day increases the risk of stroke and readmission by 12%.[28] Youn and Shin demonstrated that after being discharged from the hospital, SPs view themselves as normal individuals rather than stroke survivors.[29] Consequently, in the presence of their friends, if offered a cigarette, they hesitate to refuse out of fear of losing their social connections. Therefore, it becomes crucial to distract patients from environments that encourage smoking and other high-risk behaviors.

Psychological distress emerged as another significant finding of this study. The nature and process of stroke readmission could be a potential trigger for these distressing outcomes. Pai et al. found that due to the prolonged treatment course and the need for assistance in performing daily activities, SPs experience disruptions in their communication patterns with family members and friends.[30] A study by Gandolfi et al. revealed that the higher the level of disability among SPs, the greater the psychological distress and subsequent problems, such as family conflicts.[31] Therefore, it is necessary to pay special attention to the needs of patients’ families in caring and following up programs.

This study also had some limitations. First, the participants were recruited solely from one public hospital, thus limiting the generalizability of the findings to other populations and regions. Second, being a qualitative study, it was not possible to comprehensively examine all the risk factors influencing stroke readmission. Further research is necessary to explore these factors in a more diverse population and to examine additional risk factors that may contribute to stroke readmission.

Conclusions

This study provides a comprehensive understanding of the modifiable risk factors associated with stroke readmission. Based on the study’s findings, knowledge deficit about comorbidities, diet, and high-risk behaviors are the most modifiable risk factors of stroke readmission; therefore, SPs experience many problems in their lives. These findings have important directions for developing interventions aimed at reducing stroke readmission rates in Mashhad City. These findings can also be considered as a framework for stakeholders to plan effective and culturally sensitive interventions in areas such as preparing more specialized training to provide more effective and improving education and follow-up structures in primary health care systems.
