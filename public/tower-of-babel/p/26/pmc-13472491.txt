Oncological outcomes and treatment characteristics of patients with cT3-4 mesorectal fascia positive rectal cancer operated in 2016: Dutch nationwide cohort with standardized magnetic resonance imaging re-assessment
Authors: Julie E E A Guicherit, Eline G M van Geffen, Tania C Sluckin, Sanne-Marije J A Hazen, Jacobus W A Burger, Joost Nederend, Jan Willem T Dekker, Johannes H W de Wilt, Cornelis Verhoef, Andreas W K S Marinelli, Jarno Melenhorst, Monique Maas, Pieter J Tanis, Dutch Snapshot Research Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRectal cancer with mesorectal fascia involvement (MRF+) requires neoadjuvant downstaging and/or induction therapy, followed by (beyond) total mesorectal excision and/or multivisceral resection (MVR) depending on response. Decisions on the need for MVR and plane of dissection vary. This nationwide cross-sectional study aimed to evaluate treatment and oncological outcomes in MRF+ primary rectal cancer and the impact of MVR types.MethodsUsing collaborative research, extensive clinical data on all rectal cancer resections in 2016 across 67 Dutch hospitals were retrospectively collected. Patients with cT3-4 MRF+ primary rectal cancer and standardized re-assessment of primary and restaging magnetic resonance imaging were selected. Patients were categorized by radiologically involved structures or organs and by MVR type. The primary outcome measures were microscopically positive margin (R1) resection rate, 4-year cumulative local recurrence, and 4-year overall survival.ResultsOf 3178 patients registered, 571 cT3-4 MRF+ were selected resections, 125 of which were MVR. Tumours remained MRF+ on restaging magnetic resonance imaging in 76.4%. The overall R1 rate was 11.8% (9.7% in cT3/4a MRF+ and 17.9% in cT4b tumours). The R1 rate after MVR was 20.8%. Overall, the 4-year local recurrence rate was 15.1% and 4-year overall survival 67.5%. When comparing total mesorectal excision with MVR and/or beyond total mesorectal excision, the local recurrence rate was 12.7% versus 24.2% (P = 0.001), and overall survival 71.3% versus 53.7% (P < 0.001). Patients with radiological anterior involvement seemed to have a higher R1 rate compared with other locations. Anterior resections in both men and women appeared to be associated with relatively low local recurrence and high overall survival, whereas other resections resulted in numerically lower R1 rates, but worse long-term oncological outcomes. However, neither anatomical location of MRF+ and/or ingrowth nor MVR type was significantly associated with the primary outcomes. Pelvic sepsis (hazard ratio 1.533, 95% confidence interval 1.0 to 2.3) and positive margins (hazard ratio 1.837, 1.2 to 2.8) were independent predictors of worse overall survival.ConclusionOncological outcomes of MRF+ rectal cancer were not significantly influenced by the anatomical location of MRF involvement or MVR type.Keywords: rectal neoplasms, mesorectal fascia, multivisceral resection, total mesorectal excision, resection margin, local recurrence, overall survival

Introduction

Approximately 3000 new patients are diagnosed with rectal cancer in the Netherlands annually. Although early-stage tumours are diagnosed more often after introducing screening programmes, a substantial proportion of patients still present with locally advanced disease1. Whereas there is no consensus definition of locally advanced disease, it generally includes tumours with mesorectal fascia involvement (MRF+), with or without involvement of other structures or organs (cT3MRF+ or cT4b)2. Achieving negative resection margins (R0), a well-established predictor of local recurrence (LR) and survival, remains challenging in these cases and may require extensive surgical procedures3–6.

Standard treatment of MRF+ rectal cancer comprises neoadjuvant therapy for downstaging and/or induction therapy, followed by (beyond) total mesorectal excision (TME) and/or multivisceral resection (MVR), depending on response2. Determining the plane of dissection and the need for MVR based on primary and restaging imaging largely depends on local practice, radiology expertise, and individual surgical experience, in the absence of specific evidence-based guideline recommendations. National or regional healthcare infrastructure may facilitate multidisciplinary team discussions with expert centres and support referral of patients for MVR and for additional therapy, such as intraoperative radiotherapy. However, there is scarce literature on this process of surgical decision-making, including patient selection for (tertiary) MVR in an unselected population with baseline MRF+ rectal cancer. Furthermore, it remains largely unknown whether the location of affected organs or the type of MVR influences oncological outcomes. Understanding these factors may improve surgical planning.

Classifications for pelvic tumour invasion have been described in the literature7–9, and are used for both locally advanced primary and locally recurrent rectal cancer. Studies reporting outcomes of MVR or exenterative procedures often combine different pelvic malignancies and primary and recurrent disease. If separate data are reported for the disease entity, they are not further specified by type of organ involvement or MVR.

This is likely due to the heterogeneity of this patient group and between centres, the wide variety of resection types, and the lack of large, unselected cohorts that enable meaningful subgroup analysis with sufficient patient numbers. These factors have limited the systematic evaluation of surgical and oncological outcomes.

This study aimed to investigate treatment characteristics and oncological outcomes in patients with cT3-4 MRF+ primary rectal cancer at a population level, with standardized re-assessment of primary and restaging magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). A secondary aim was to assess the impact of different types of MVR on LR and overall survival (OS).

Methods

In a nationwide cross-sectional study, all patients who underwent surgical resection for primary rectal cancer in 2016 across 67 Dutch hospitals were included. The design of this collaborative research project has been described in detail in previous publications10,11. In short, patients were identified from the Dutch ColoRectal Audit (DCRA). Since 2009, this nationwide registry has prospectively registered the short-term outcomes for all colorectal cancer resections in the Netherlands. For this study, the DCRA data set was expanded with numerous additional variables on disease characteristics, treatment characteristics, and long-term outcomes. A team of residents, supervised by surgeons, retrospectively collected local patient data from between 2020 and 2022, and abdominal radiologists reviewed the MRI following a standardized protocol. The Amsterdam UMC Medical Ethics Committee approved the study and confirmed exemption from the Dutch Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act. Each participating centre received local institutional review board approval.

Patient selection and definitions

This study included patients who underwent intentional curative resection in 2016 for cT3-4 MRF+ rectal cancer. The presence of synchronous distant metastases considered eligible for curative-intent treatment was not an exclusion criterion. Patients who underwent only local excision, R2 resection, or hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy simultaneously with rectal cancer resection were excluded.

Seventy-five trained abdominal radiologists from 60 of the 67 participating hospitals performed MRI reassessments. All primary and restaging scans of patients with cT3-4 tumours were reviewed. Each radiologist reassessed scans from their affiliated centre; inter-observer agreement could therefore not be evaluated. During reassessment, radiologists had access to electronic patient records with treatment details and all available MRI scans. In this study, tumours with involvement of other structures or organs were classified as cT4b (including external sphincter) and involvement limited to the peritoneum or peritoneal reflection was considered cT4a, in accordance with the primary rectal cancer staging guideline of the European Society of Gastrointestinal and Abdominal Radiology12. Tumour response after restaging was classified as MRF—with corresponding ycT stage (1–4a) versus persistent cT3MRF+ or cT4b, reflecting ongoing involvement of the MRF and/or invasion of adjacent organs or structures relevant for surgical planning.

Outcomes of interest

The primary outcome measures were microscopically positive margin (R1) resection rate, 4-year cumulative LR, and 4-year OS. R0 resection was defined as both negative circumferential and distal resection margins, each > 1.0 mm.

To assess the impact of radiologically involved structures or organs on treatment and subsequent outcomes, patients were categorized into four anatomical subgroups. For this purpose, the existing anatomical consensus classification for locoregional recurrence was modified (PelvEx Collaborative guideline)7:

Anterior: vagina, uterus, seminal vesicles, prostate, bladder, ovaries, urethra, peritoneal reflection.

Anterior: vagina, uterus, seminal vesicles, prostate, bladder, ovaries, urethra, peritoneal reflection.

Posterior and/or lateral: coccyx, sacrum, presacral fascia, pelvic sidewall, ureters, internal iliac vessels, obturator region.

Infralevator: pelvic floor muscles, ischioanal fossa, external sphincter.

Other: colon and small intestine combined with one or more of the above compartments.

To analyse different types of MVR for (persisting) MRF+ primary rectal cancer, patients were grouped as follows:

Middle/anterior compartment (women): uterus, vagina, ovaries, bladder, urethra.

Middle/anterior compartment (women): uterus, vagina, ovaries, bladder, urethra.

Anterior compartment (men): prostate, seminal vesicles, bladder, urethra.

Anterior compartment (men): prostate, seminal vesicles, bladder, urethra.

Posterior and/or lateral compartment: sacrum, coccyx, presacral fascia, pelvic sidewall or related structures.

Follow-up and detection of recurrence was performed according to local practice and based on the Dutch guideline recommendations at that time: carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) assessment every 3–6 months and liver and thorax imaging every 6 months during the first 2 years, followed by 6–12 months’ CEA measurement with annual imaging until 5 years, and colonoscopy at years 1 and 4.

Statistical analysis

This was an exploratory analysis of a nationwide, population-based cohort. No formal sample size calculation was performed.

All analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics® version 28.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Baseline characteristics were summarized as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, and as mean(standard deviation) or median (interquartile range) for continuous variables, depending on distribution.

Time to event outcomes (LR and OS) were analysed using Kaplan–Meier curves, with comparisons between groups made using the log rank test. Four-year Kaplan–Meier estimates were reported with 95% confidence intervals (c.i.). R1 resection rates were compared using a 2 × 2 contingency table; depending on sample size, either the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test was applied. Cox proportional hazards regression was used to identify factors associated with LR and OS. The model included variables with P < 0.100 in univariable analysis. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF) and tolerance values, with VIF > 5 and tolerance < 0.2 indicating multicollinearity. A two-sided P < 0.050 was considered statistically significant.

On re-assessment of primary staging, 381 patients (66.7%) had cT3 tumours and 190 (33.3%) cT4, including 140 (24.5%) cT4b. MRF involvement was anterior and/or lateral in 203 patients (53.7%), posterior and/or lateral in 112 (29.6%), lateral in 32 (8.5%), and both anterior and posterior in 31 (8.2%). In those with cT4b tumours, the pelvic floor, seminal vesicles, posterior vaginal wall, prostate, and the external sphincter were most frequently involved (Table 1). Restaging MRI was performed in 467 patients (81.8%). Of these, 109 (23.3%) became mesorectal fascia negative (MRF−) and 357 (76.4%) remained MRF+. In 100 patients, invasion into adjacent structures persisted. The distribution of involved structures or organs after neoadjuvant treatment was similar (Tables 1, 2).

Minimally invasive surgery was performed in 407 patients (71.3%), with conversion to open surgery in 22 patients (5.4%). MVR was performed in 125 patients (21.9%). The most frequently performed extended resections involved the seminal vesicles, posterior vaginal wall, ovaries, and uterus or posterior exenteration (Table 3).

R1 resections

Of the 571 resections of primary MRF+ rectal cancer, 67 (11.8%) were R1. R1 resection occurred in 25 patients (17.9%) initially staged as cT4b and 42 patients (9.7%) with cT3/4a MRF+ tumours. Depending on surgical approach, R1 resections occurred in 33 patients (20.1%) after open surgery and 34 (8.4%) after intentional minimally invasive surgery. MVR resulted in positive margins in 26 patients (20.8%) (Table S1).

Oncological outcomes overall cohort

For the whole cohort, the 4-year Kaplan–Meier estimated LR rate was 15.1% (95% c.i. 11.8 to 18.4), with a 4-year OS of 67.5% (63.4 to 71.6). Stratified by primary staging, the LR rate was 19.5% (12.1 to 26.9) in cT4b tumours and 13.6% (10.1 to 17.1) in non-cT4b tumours (P = 0.100), with corresponding OS rates of 60.6% (52.0 to 69.2) and 69.7% (65.2 to 74.2) (P = 0.051). When comparing TME with MVR, the LR rate was 12.7% (9.2 to 16.2) versus 24.2% (15.8 to 32.6) (P = 0.001), and OS was 71.3% (66.8 to 75.8) versus 53.7% (44.5 to 62.9) (P < 0.001). Among patients who underwent re-staging, LR was 18.4% (9.6 to 27.2) in ycT4b and 14.6% (10.5 to 18.7) in non-ycT4b (P = 0.341), with OS rates of 53.9% (43.5 to 64.3) and 69.5% (64.6 to 74.4) (P = 0.003).

Multivariable analysis demonstrated a higher risk of LR in patients with a higher clinical T category (P = 0.026), those who underwent open or converted surgery (P = 0.040), and those with positive resection margins (P < 0.001) (Table 4). OS was worse in patients of higher age (P < 0.001), synchronous metastases (P < 0.001), poor treatment response (P = 0.042), type of resection (P = 0.010), open or converted surgery (P = 0.036), pelvic sepsis (P = 0.031), and positive resection margins (P = 0.003) (Table 5). No significant multicollinearity was observed.

Oncological outcomes by anatomical radiological involvement

No statistically significant associations were found between the anatomical location of radiological involvement and R1 resection rate, LR, or OS (Table S2). Anterior involvement showed a numerically higher R1 rate, whereas LR and OS were comparable. Posterior and/or lateral involvement, as well as infralevator involvement, likewise showed no differences in outcomes. Other involved structures also had numerically higher R1 rates, but LR and OS remained comparable (Table S2).

Oncological outcomes according to type of MVR

No statistically significant differences in outcomes were observed between the different types of MVR (Table S3). Combined anterior and posterior resections showed the highest rates of R1 resections and LR, whereas posterior and/or lateral resections resulted in the lowest R1 rate. Anterior resections in both men and women appeared to have relatively low LR and high OS, whereas other types of resections showed lower R1 rates but worse long-term oncological outcomes. In multivariable analysis, the type of MVR had no significant effect on LR or OS (Table 4 and 5).

Discussion

This nationwide cohort of 571 patients diagnosed with cT3–4 MRF+ rectal cancer revealed a remarkably high percentage (76.4%) of patients who remained MRF+ on restaging. The overall R1 resection rate was 11.8%, which was 20.8% in MVR. The 4-year LR rate was 15.1% and the 4-year OS was 67.5%. No statistically significant differences in R1 rates, LR, or OS were observed by anatomical location of ingrowth on radiological imaging or by MVR type. However, pelvic sepsis (hazard ratio (HR) 1.533, 95% c.i. 1.039 to 2.263) and positive margins (HR 1.837, 1.225 to 2.754) were independently associated with poorer OS.

Whereas randomized controlled trials such as RAPIDO13, PRODIGE-2314, and PROSPECT15, as well as a recent meta-analysis by Gabbani et al.16 report 5-year OS rates of 80–90% in locally advanced rectal cancer, this unselected, nationwide, multicentre cohort of cT3–4 MRF+ rectal cancers showed a 4-year OS of 67.5%. One of the explanations is related to the lack of a uniform definition of locally advanced rectal cancer, which includes heterogeneity in local and national staging standards. This heterogeneity leads to substantial differences in included patient populations and corresponding outcomes. To illustrate the heterogeneity, the proportion of MRF+ in the RAPIDO trial13 at baseline was 61%, whereas it was only 27% in the PRODIGE-23 trial14. PROSPECT15 excluded cT4 disease and the MRF status was not reported in the baseline characteristics. Furthermore, there might be a gap between outcomes achieved in expert centres under trial conditions and those observed in unselected nationwide practice17. Thereby, the present study provides complementary evidence alongside randomized trials with strictly selected study populations.

LR rates for the different subgroups based on the location of ingrowth and MVR type varied numerically but did not reach statistical significance, whereas OS was similar across subgroups. Although LR is an important adverse event from a clinical perspective (for example, symptoms, need for intensive treatment), these observations suggest that cancer-specific survival in locally advanced rectal cancer is predominantly influenced by tumour biology and systemic disease progression, with probably a less important role for local control. This might also imply that R1 resection reflects a more aggressive tumour biology, and that positive margins might not be directly associated with worse survival.

On restaging MRI, the majority of patients remained MRF+ after neoadjuvant treatment. This is an important observation, as this neoadjuvant treatment aims for downstaging and reduced need for extended resections. Nevertheless, a minority of patients underwent MVR, which illustrates the difficulties in surgical decision-making when trying to balance optimal oncological control versus morbidity related to the extensiveness of surgery. Decision-making is often complicated by treatment-induced fibrosis, which is difficult to distinguish from a viable tumour on MRI. This can lead to an overestimation of residual tumour burden and, consequently, persisting MRF involvement. On the other hand, clear fibrosis might still contain microscopic foci of residual cancer in the case of a fragmented response pattern18. Likely, variability in surgeons’ approaches to deal with this dilemma exists.

Patients undergoing MVR experienced a 4-year LR rate of 24.2%, which is substantially higher than the 5–10% usually reported in rectal cancer series3. This difference is likely caused by the fact that patients with more advanced tumours were those who were deemed candidates for MVR. More advanced tumours lead to higher R1 rates in patients undergoing MVR, which is consistent with the literature and confirms that margin status is an important prognostic factor for LR3–6. However, it also represents a poor response, reflecting aggressive tumour biology with likely other adverse features, such as lymphatic, venous, and perineural invasion. Combined anterior and posterior compartment resections represented the most extensive procedures and appeared to have the poorest outcomes, although these findings were not statistically significant. In the category of other MVRs, LR seemed to occur relatively frequently despite fewer R1 resections, illustrating that margin status alone does not fully account for prognosis.

High-quality, national-level data on outcomes of different MVR types in rectal cancer are lacking. Most available studies19,20 are based on small cohorts or from tertiary referral centres, limiting their generalizability to clinical practice. A study by Mariathasan et al.19 examined patients with pelvic wall infiltration, a subgroup within MVR, and reported higher R1 rates and poorer long-term outcomes in this subgroup19. In that study, pelvic side-wall resections had an R1 rate of 24.5%. In the present cohort, the corresponding broader posterior and/or lateral category (including presacral fascia and the sacrum) showed lower R1 rates. This discrepancy likely reflects differences in compartment definitions. OS was higher in that study, despite higher R1 rates, which may be related to compartment definition, case selection, and specialized perioperative management in tertiary referral centres.

Piozzi et al.20 also studied the impact of different MVR types. Radial beyond TME (adjacent pelvic organs) was significantly associated with poorer survival, a finding consistent with the relatively poor outcomes in the present cohort. By contrast, Piozzi et al. reported the highest LR rates after lateral resections, whereas in the present series recurrent disease occurred more often after combined anterior and posterior resections, as well as in the group of other MVRs, although not reaching statistical significance. Whether observed discrepancies are related to differences in compartment definitions and surgical approach is difficult to judge. Together, these studies19,20 provide some insight into the relationship between MVR types and oncological outcomes, but heterogeneity and rarity of these disease entities make meaningful subgroup analyses difficult.

In contrast to primary rectal cancer, several studies on the association between the type of resection of locally recurrent disease and oncological outcomes have been performed. Some indicated that lateral and sacral recurrences are associated with lower R0 resection rates and poorer survival21,22. Conversely, a study by Kusters et al.23 reported that the site of LR did not influence prognosis. Therefore, evidence remains conflicting regarding the prognostic impact of different compartments of LR21–25. The present series adds valuable data to this literature, as a certain type of ingrowth from a primary tumour cannot be compared with similar ingrowth in LR.

A strength of this study is the unselected nationwide population, in which multidisciplinary treatment steps and outcomes are captured. Furthermore, the standardized reassessment of primary and restaging MRI enhances validity and generalizability. Although this is a large population-based analysis of MVR types, numbers within specific subgroups remained small, limiting the ability to draw firm conclusions regarding individual anatomical patterns or resection types. This retrospective, registry-based study may be subject to misclassification and incomplete data capture. Allocation bias was likely present, with almost one-third of patients undergoing open surgery and over one-fifth undergoing MVR. Some of the expert centres applies intraoperative radiotherapy without standardized indications26. Furthermore, treatment decisions were made using the imaging reports and treatment strategies available in 2016. The current re-evaluation may have produced different results, but the treatment cannot be changed. Therefore, treatment may not have been appropriate in some cases if the current restaging had been used. Furthermore, some patients did not have a restaging MRI. Additionally, the absence of significant differences between MVR types may be because of limited statistical power with the possibility of a type II error. Moreover, surgical decision-making regarding the plane of dissection may have reduced anatomical specific risks, thereby minimizing differences across anatomical sites. Finally, cancer-specific survival could not be assessed due to lack of reliable cause-of-death data.

Taken together, the authors’ results do not identify a clearly defined high-risk subgroup, highlighting the complexity of risk stratification in cT3-4 MRF+ rectal cancer. Future studies should confirm the prognostic role of involved structures and MVR type in larger cohorts and assess the effect of institutional volume and expertise on outcomes.

Overall, in this nationwide cohort of 571 patients with cT3–4 MRF+ rectal cancer, tumours remained MRF+ on restaging MRI in 76.4%. The overall R1 rate was 11.8%, and this was 20.8% among 125 patients who underwent MVR. Overall, the 4-year LR rate was 15.1%, and this was 12.7% after TME and 24.2% after MVR, with corresponding 4-year OS of 71.3% and 53.7%, respectively. Pelvic sepsis and positive resection margins were associated with worse OS. Oncological outcomes did not vary by the anatomical location of MRF involvement or MVR type.
