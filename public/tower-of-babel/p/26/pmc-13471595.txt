Optimal type and dose of exercise for improving executive functions in older adults: a systematic review and Bayesian model-based network meta-analysis of RCTs
Authors: Jialin Quan, Lin Zhu, Ziqi Chen, Zekai Chen, Bing Li, Songyuan Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo identify the optimal type and dose of exercise for improving executive function in adults aged 65 years and older.MethodsWe systematically searched five databases (Cochrane Library, PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and China National Knowledge Infrastructure) from inception to 7 December 2024. Following systematic screening, data extraction, and risk-of-bias assessment, a random-effects Bayesian network meta-analysis and dose-response analysis were performed using R software (version 4.2.0).ResultsA total of 38 studies involving 2,928 participants were included. The effects of exercise modalities on executive function in adults aged ≥ 65 years differed according to baseline cognitive status. In cognitively healthy older adults, aerobic exercise significantly improved all three domains of executive function, whereas mind-body regulation training and resistance training showed no significant effects. In contrast, among older adults with cognitive impairment, all three exercise types significantly improved working memory, while no significant benefits were observed for inhibitory control or cognitive flexibility. Dose-response analysis identified optimal aerobic exercise doses for cognitively healthy older adults: 1,134 MET-min/week for working memory and inhibitory control, and 530.2 MET-min/week for cognitive flexibility. No minimum threshold for cognitive benefit was identified, suggesting that even low-volume of physical activity may confer advantages.ConclusionAerobic exercise is the most effective modality for enhancing executive function in cognitively healthy older adults and for improving working memory in those with cognitive impairment. Although the findings support the World Health Organization’s physical activity guidelines (600-1,200 MET-min/week) for general cognitive benefits, optimal outcomes require dose personalisation according to the targeted executive function domain.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07746-7.

Introduction

Executive functions comprise a set of higher-order cognitive processes that enable coordinated, goal-directed behavior and encompass three core components: inhibitory control, working memory, and cognitive flexibility [1]. Against the background of global population aging and the increasing prevalence of cognitive impairment [2, 3], preventing the decline of executive function and overall cognitive ability in older adults has become a critical public health challenge in the pursuit of healthy aging. Age-related declines in these functions impair daily activities [4] and independence [5]. Impairments in inhibitory control undermine the ability to regulate behavior effectively; declines in working memory contribute to information loss and execution errors; and reduced cognitive flexibility results in impaired environmental adaptation and increased stereotyped behaviors [6, 7]. Collectively, these deficits exert substantial negative effects, thereby transforming otherwise routine daily activities into formidable challenges. Affected individuals face heightened risks of falls [8, 9], disorientation and wayfinding difficulties, as well as marked limitations in navigating complex and dynamically changing environments [10].

Moreover, these declines are inversely associated with executive function performance, and the deficits become more pronounced with advancing age [11]. While cognitive decline is relatively modest before the age of 65 years, an accelerated deterioration becomes more prominent thereafter [12]. Such decline may contribute to physical inactivity [13–15] and eating disorders [16], and is also associated with attention-deficit disorders [17], depression and anxiety [18], as well as cardiovascular disease [19].

Clinical and public health guidelines [20, 21] recommend exercise as a foundational strategy for the prevention and management of cognitive disorders. Beneficial effects on executive function have been demonstrated across multiple exercise modalities, including aerobic exercise [22–25], resistance training, motor games [26–28], multicomponent exercise programs [29, 30], and mind-body regulation training [31–33]. For example, one study reported that the greatest improvements in inhibitory control were observed following 13–24 weeks of low-intensity aerobic exercise, performed for 20–45 min per session, 3–4 days per week [34]. Similarly, open-skill exercise (e.g., tennis) has been shown to elicit greater cognitive benefits than closed-skill exercise (e.g., running) [35–37], and accumulating evidence suggests that aerobic exercise may be more effective than resistance training in enhancing inhibitory control [38]. The mechanisms through which different types of exercise improve executive function vary substantially. Aerobic exercise primarily promotes angiogenesis and neurogenesis, enhances cerebral blood flow, and upregulates the expression of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) [39–41]. Resistance training exerts its central effects mainly via muscle-derived myokines [2, 42]. In contrast, skill-based exercise more effectively engages multiple brain regions and emphasizes synaptic remodeling and the optimization of neural connectivity [39, 43, 44]. In this context, the cognitive benefits of exercise appear to be influenced by both exercise modality [45, 46] and an individual’s baseline cognitive status [47]. However, it remains undetermined which type of exercise is most effective. Existing reviews have typically evaluated exercise modalities in isolation, with few studies comprehensively comparing the relative efficacy of different exercise types. These limitations constrain the development of targeted prevention strategies. Although previous reviews have begun to explore the role of exercise dosage [48, 49], they often categorize dose parameters into broad ranges (e.g., 10–30 min or > 30 min per session), which fall short of providing the precision required for personalized exercise prescription. Evidence regarding precise dose-response relationships therefore remains limited. In addition, few studies have specifically focused on adults aged 65 years and older, a population at particularly high risk of cognitive impairment. Accordingly, this study aims to systematically determine the most effective combinations of exercise type and dosage for improving executive function in adults aged 65 years and older by integrating Bayesian network meta-analysis with dose-response analysis. This approach enables simultaneous comparison of multiple exercise modalities while modeling precise dose-response relationships, thereby bridging the gap between evidence and practical implementation. Our findings are expected to provide a theoretical foundation for mitigating age-related executive decline and to contribute robust evidence for the development of evidence-based, personalized exercise prescriptions.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA checklist [50] and was registered in the Prospective Registry of International Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) database (registration number: CRD420251001673).

Search strategy

We systematically searched five databases (Cochrane Library, PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and China National Knowledge Infrastructure) from their inception up to December 7, 2024. The search strategy was developed based on previously published systematic reviews on similar topics. Additionally, the reference lists of relevant articles and reviews were screened for studies. The search strategy and results are presented in Supplementary File 1. Two independent authors (CZQ and LB) screened the literature based on titles and abstracts, applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Full texts of potentially relevant studies were then retrieved and reviewed. Disagreements were resolved through consensus, with a third author (QJL) acting as an adjudicator.

Selection criteria

The inclusion and exclusion criteria were defined according to the PICOS framework.

Inclusion criteria: (1) participants were aged ≥ 65 years; (2) the study design was a randomized controlled trial; (3) the intervention consisted of a detailed exercise program specifying type, intensity, frequency, and a duration ≥ 4 weeks; (4) the control group was a usual care group, a wait-list group, or another exercise intervention group; (5) at least one of the outcome measures was related to working memory, inhibitory control, or cognitive flexibility.

Exclusion criteria: (1) cross-sectional studies, reviews, non-randomized controlled trials, case reports, trial protocols, animal studies, and conference abstracts; (2) studies with unavailable or incomplete data; (3) duplicate publications with unclear or overlapping data; (4) non-exercise-based interventions, such as those combining exercise with medication.

Data extraction

Two authors (CZQ and LB) independently extracted data using a predefined form, which included details such as the study title, publication year, authors, participant characteristics, intervention details, and outcome data for each included study. A third author (CZK) verified the extracted data. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus. When data were missing, efforts were made to obtain them from the corresponding authors, and studies were excluded if the data could not be retrieved. The mean difference and its standard deviation were calculated using a correlation coefficient of r = 0.50 and r = 0.80, following the guidelines of the Cochrane Handbook recommendations [51, 52]. Subsequently, exercise dosage was estimated based on the type of exercise, its corresponding duration, frequency, and intensity, expressed as metabolic equivalent minutes per week (MET-min/week) [53, 54], which represents the energy expenditure calculated as the product of duration, frequency, and intensity for a specific exercise type.

Assessment of study quality

Two authors (CZQ and LB) independently assessed the risk of bias in the included studies using the Revised Cochrane Risk-of-Bias Tool for Randomized Trials (ROB 2). Any discrepancies in the assessment were resolved through discussion with a third author (CZK). The overall risk of bias for each study was summarized based on the assessments across these domains.

Data synthesis

A random-effects Bayesian network meta-analysis was conducted using the “gemtc”, “ggplot2”, “netmeta”, and “reshape2” packages in R software (version 4.2.0) along with JAGS (version 4.3.0) [55, 56]. Additionally, the “MBNMA dose” package was employed to perform model-based network meta-analysis and dose-response relationship analysis [57]. We further assessed the key assumptions underlying the network meta-analysis, including network connectivity, consistency, and transitivity, and found no evidence of violations [58, 59] (Supplementary File 2). Since all outcome measures were continuous variables, the pooled effect size was calculated using the Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) with 95% credible intervals (CrI) as the effect measure. A Bayesian Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) model was constructed to compare the effects of different exercise types, using four independent chains under a consistency model for network meta-analysis and probability ranking. The model was initialized with a value of 0.5 and ran with a thinning interval of 10 for 50,000 iterations. The first 20,000 iterations were discarded as burn-in to minimize the influence of initial values, and the subsequent 30,000 were used for posterior sampling. Convergence was assessed by examining trace density plots and computing the Potential Scale Reduction Factor (PSRF). Bandwidth values approaching zero, together with PSRF values close to 1 indicated satisfactory convergence and enhanced reliability of the consistency model. Treatment rankings were estimated using the Surface Under the Cumulative Ranking Curve (SUCRA). To assess potential publication bias, funnel plots were constructed and Egger’s regression test was performed. Dose-response relationships were modeled using restricted cubic splines with three knots placed at the 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of energy expenditure, from which the optimal exercise dose was estimated. Sensitivity analyses were re-conducted using standard deviations of differences calculated with a correlation coefficient of r = 0.80. All statistical tests were two-sided, with a significance level at α = 0.05.

Results

Characteristics of the included studies

A total of 36,221 records were retrieved from five electronic databases. After screening titles, abstracts, and full texts, 38 studies met the eligibility criteria and were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1) [28, 29, 33, 38, 60–93]. These studies involved 2,928 participants, with ages ranging from 65 to 86 years. The number of participants was 2,620 for working memory, 1,180 for inhibitory control, and 2,407 for cognitive flexibility. Among the included studies, 18 assessed inhibitory control, 27 evaluated cognitive flexibility, and 33 examined working memory. A total of 10 studies assessed a single executive function outcome, 16 studies assessed two outcomes, and 12 studies assessed all three outcomes. Based on the reported intervention protocols, exercise interventions were categorized into four types: “mind-body regulation training”, “aerobic exercise”, “resistance training”, and “control group”. Regarding intervention types, 7 studies employed mind–body training, 29 studies implemented aerobic exercise, and 10 studies used resistance training. Detailed characteristics of the included studies are provided in Supplementary File 3 and 5.

Influence of exercise types

Working memory

Figure 2 presents the effects of different exercise modalities on working memory in older adults. Among individuals with cognitive impairment, mind-body regulation training (SMD = 0.47, 95% CrI: 0.09 to 0.87), aerobic exercise (SMD = 0.29, 95% CrI: 0.10 to 0.49), and resistance training (SMD = 0.52, 95% CrI: 0.12 to 0.92) were all associated with significant improvements compared with the control group (all P < 0.05). According to SUCRA values, resistance training (SUCRA = 0.86) ranked highest and was identified as the most effective intervention.

In cognitively healthy older adults, only aerobic exercise showed a significant improvement in working memory (SMD = 0.65, 95% CrI: 0.24 to 1.06; P < 0.05), whereas no other pairwise comparisons reached statistical significance. In cognitively healthy older adults, aerobic exercise was associated with a significant improvement in cognitive flexibility compared to the control group (SMD = 0.43, 95% CrI: 0.28–0.59; P < 0.05). Furthermore, aerobic exercise also demonstrated a significant advantage over mind–body regulation exercises (SMD = 0.29, 95% CrI: 0.06–0.50; P < 0.05). Dose-response relationships

Dose-response analyses revealed a significant non-linear association between aerobic exercise dose and cognitive performance in cognitively healthy older adults. For working memory, 28 studies involving 1,655 participants were included. The optimal aerobic exercise dose was estimated at 1134 MET-min/week (χ² = 6.00, P < 0.05; Fig. 5). For inhibitory control, 10 studies involving 634 participants were included. An identical optimal dose of 1,134 MET-min/week was also identified for inhibitory control (χ² = 17.16, P < 0.01; Fig. 6), with both domains exhibiting a positive dose-response relationship. In contrast, cognitive flexibility exhibited an inverted U-shaped association, with the maximal effect occurring at 530.2 MET-min/week (χ² = 28.83, P < 0.01; Fig. 7). A total of 16 studies involving 907 participants were included.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that the effects of different exercise modalities on executive function in adults aged ≥ 65 years vary according to baseline cognitive status. In cognitively healthy older adults, aerobic exercise significantly improved all three domains of executive function, whereas mind-body regulation training and resistance training showed no significant effects. Conversely, among older adults with cognitive impairment, all three exercise modalities were associated with improvements in working memory, while no significant benefits were observed for inhibitory control or cognitive flexibility.

The observed benefits of aerobic exercise are consistent with previous studies [62, 75]. For example, a six-month home-based aerobic exercise intervention produced modest cognitive improvements in older adults with memory complaints [60]. The underlying mechanisms may involve exercise-induced enhancements in neural plasticity [94], increased activation of cognition-related brain regions [95, 96], and favorable adaptations in cortical structure and function [97]. Furthermore, aerobic exercise upregulates key neurotrophic and metabolic factors, such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) [98, 99] and norepinephrine [100], which are known to facilitate cognitive improvement. Aerobic exercise and resistance training improve executive function via distinct underlying mechanisms. Aerobic exercise predominantly targets the hippocampus and cerebrovascular system, enhancing cerebral blood flow and upregulating VEGF and BDNF [40, 41, 101–103]. In contrast, resistance training exerts its central effects primarily through myokines (e.g., irisin, cathepsin B) and insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), and is also associated with structural preservation in specific brain regions, reflecting muscle–brain crosstalk [2, 42, 104]. The present study found that resistance training improved only working memory in older adults with cognitive impairment, which is partially consistent with previous findings [105]. However, no significant benefits were observed for other subcomponents of executive function in this population. This limited efficacy may be attributable to insufficient training dosage and low intervention adherence. The effects of resistance training are inherently dose-dependent [104], and in adults aged ≥ 65 years, maintaining sustained and adequate training intensity and duration is often challenging. Consequently, suboptimal training exposure and poor adherence may ultimately attenuate the overall intervention effects.

Although working memory exhibited selective improvement in cognitively impaired older adults, the absence of corresponding gains in inhibitory control and cognitive flexibility warrants further consideration. Previous evidence suggests that individuals without cognitive impairment tend to derive greater cognitive benefits from exercise interventions than those with cognitive disorders [47]. This differential responsiveness may be explained by prolonged cerebral functional compensation and excessive neural resource consumption in cognitively impaired individuals [106]. Under such conditions, exercise-induced neuroplasticity (e.g., BDNF-mediated synaptic repair) may be preferentially directed toward mitigating compensatory overload rather than enhancing higher-order cognitive processing. Consequently, neural adaptations induced by exercise in this population may primarily contribute to maintaining compensatory balance rather than improving cognitive performance beyond baseline levels.

Regarding mind–body regulation training (MBRT), such as Tai Chi, our analysis identified a significant beneficial effect on cognitive flexibility. Evidence from functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) indicates that Tai Chi promotes functional specialization within the brain and enhances cognitive flexibility [107]. In contrast to previous studies, however, we did not observe significant improvements in working memory or inhibitory control following MBRT [108].

This limited efficacy of MBRT in these domains may be explained by several factors, including the absence of cognitively challenging tasks, limited exposure to unpredictable external stimuli, and relatively simple and repetitive movement patterns, which may be insufficient to robustly engage neural circuits underlying inhibitory control. Additionally, the generally low exercise intensity of MBRT may further constrain its cognitive benefits. Although MBRT demonstrated relatively large SMDs across executive function subdomains, the wide credible intervals (CrI) indicate substantial uncertainty in these estimates. Therefore, additional high-quality, well-designed studies are required to confirm the cognitive effects of MBRT.

Existing evidence indicates that moderate- to vigorous-intensity exercise confers robust benefits on executive function in older adults [78, 109, 110]. Individuals who meet the recommended volume of moderate- to vigorous-intensity physical activity (150 min/week), as outlined in international guidelines, demonstrate significantly better executive performance and greater prefrontal cortical oxygenation [109]. Notably, compared with vigorous-intensity exercise, moderate-intensity exercise is associated with more pronounced improvements in prefrontal cortical activation and cerebral blood flow [78, 111]. Moreover, moderate-intensity exercise is more conducive to long-term adherence among older adults, a critical determinant of intervention efficacy [112]. Given that high-intensity exercise poses greater health risks for individuals aged ≥ 65 years than low- and moderate-intensity activity, moderate-intensity exercise may represent the most appropriate intensity for this population. Moreover, long-term low-intensity exercise may also enhance executive function by improving neural efficiency in the prefrontal cortex [78].

The dose-response analysis identified optimal aerobic exercise doses for executive function in cognitively healthy older adults. The optimal dose for both working memory and inhibitory control was estimated at 1134 MET-min/week, which closely approximates the upper limit of the recommended physical activity level (1,200 MET-min/week). Both cognitive domains demonstrated positive dose-response relationships.

These findings are consistent with previous research. For instance, Yang [113] et al. reported that the effective exercise dose for improving overall symptoms in patients with schizophrenia ranges from 340 to 1,200 MET-min/week, with an optimal dose of approximately 1,200 MET-min/week. Meanwhile, Daniel [114]et al. suggested that exercise doses associated with clinically meaningful global cognitive improvements in older adults range from 724 to 1,800 MET-min/week, with exercise dose and cognitive effects also demonstrating a positive dose-response relationship. Collectively, these results suggest that relatively high levels of regular physical activity could be effective in improving inhibitory control and working memory, potentially through mechanisms such as increased brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) expression [98], hippocampal volume expansion, and neurogenesis within the cerebral and cerebellar cortices [44].

Additionally, cognitive flexibility peaked at an aerobic exercise dose of 530.2 MET-min/week, displaying an inverted U-shaped dose–response relationship. This observation is consistent with the findings of Yuan et al., who identified 660 MET-min/week of aerobic exercise as the optimal dose for improving global cognition in patients with Alzheimer’s disease, also following an inverted U-shaped pattern [115]. Furthermore, Daniel et al. reported a similar inverted U-shaped dose-response relationship for combined aerobic and resistance exercise. The inverted U-shaped association observed for cognitive flexibility suggests that excessive exercise may produce diminishing cognitive returns or even adverse effects. Excessively high levels of physical activity may impose subtle negative influences, potentially attributable to fatigue, increased recovery demands, or disproportionate allocation of cognitive resources to the exercise itself [116].

Although working memory and inhibitory control continued to improve across the observed dose range, these trends may represent only the ascending limb of a broader inverted U-shaped curve, with potential plateaus or declines beyond a certain threshold. However, the limited number of studies examining high-dose exercise restricts definitive conclusions regarding upper-range effects, underscoring the need for further investigation. Notably, baseline cognitive status appeared to moderate the effects of exercise intervention. Compared with cognitively healthy older adults, those with cognitive impairment showed significant improvements only in working memory. This pattern may reflect long-term cognitive compensation in individuals with cognitive deficits, whereby exercise-induced neuroplasticity is preferentially directed toward compensatory processes rather than functional enhancement [117, 118].

In the present study, the optimal exercise dose for improving inhibitory control and working memory was 1,134 MET‑min/week. This target can be achieved through activities such as slow-paced walking (1.0–1.9 mph) or moderate-intensity stationary cycling (30–50 watts) [53, 54], performed for 60 min per session, approximately 5 times per week. For cognitive flexibility, the duration per session may be reduced to 30 min. Integrating these findings with prior evidence [25, 34, 119–121], we recommend moderate-intensity aerobic exercise as the core intervention for enhancing executive function in older adults, supplemented by resistance training. Specifically, a target weekly exercise dose of 600–1,200 MET-min/week is suggested, with a frequency of 3–5 sessions per week, session durations of 20–45 min, and an intervention period of 3–6 months. Nevertheless, to achieve optimal outcomes, exercise prescriptions should be further individualized according to specific executive function subdomains.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the inclusion of only English- and Chinese-language peer-reviewed publications may have introduced publication bias. Second, a substantial proportion of the included studies were rated as having a high risk of bias, primarily due to challenges in blinding participants and personnel, as well as the subjective nature of many cognitive assessments. Future trials should implement double-blinding procedures where feasible and incorporate more objective cognitive outcome measures. Third, owing to the limited number of eligible studies, dose-response models could not be constructed for all exercise modalities. Future research should expand the empirical evidence base to establish such analyses and employ more precise tools for quantifying physical activity exposure. Fourth, although exercise modality classification was refined through multiple internal discussions to minimize subjectivity, residual classification bias cannot be entirely excluded. Finally, while this study identified optimal exercise types and doses, future research should incorporate qualitative dimensions to better inform real-world implementation, such as movement quality, intervention feasibility, adherence, and population-specific adaptations.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that aerobic exercise effectively enhances executive function in cognitively healthy older adults and improves working memory in older adults with cognitive impairment, identifying it as the most effective modality among those evaluated. We further delineated optimal aerobic exercise doses for cognitively healthy older adults: 1134 MET-min/week for working memory and inhibitory control, and 530.2 MET-min/week for cognitive flexibility. While these findings provide actionable guidance for exercise prescription in cognitively healthy older adults, future research is required to establish dose-response relationships in older adults with cognitive impairment. These evidence-based thresholds may assist clinicians in developing individualized exercise prescriptions tailored to an older adult’s cognitive status, targeted executive function domains, and physical capacity.
