DPP-4 inhibitor linagliptin modulates myocardial metabolism in a model of coronary artery disease
Authors: Kelsey C Muir, Dwight D Harris, Christopher Stone, Meghamsh Kanuparthy, Jad Hamze, Frank W Sellke
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveCoronary artery disease (CAD) remains a leading public health concern in the United States. Dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, such as linagliptin, have demonstrated cardioprotective effects in preclinical studies; however, their influence on myocardial metabolic changes remains incompletely characterized. Our study aimed to evaluate the effects of linagliptin, as it influences incretin hormones, on myocardial metabolism and substrate utilization in a clinically relevant model of CAD.MethodsEleven-week-old Yorkshire swine underwent placement of an ameroid constrictor to the proximal left circumflex coronary artery to induce chronic myocardial ischemia. After 2 weeks to ensure ameroid closure, swine were divided into a group that received daily oral linagliptin treatment for 5 weeks (n = 8) and a group that received no drug treatment (n = 8) for 5 weeks. After 5 weeks, the swine underwent a terminal harvest procedure and subsequent immunoblotting and proteomic analysis.ResultsLinagliptin was associated with reduced fatty acid oxidation on proteomic pathway analysis and decreased expression of carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 beta (CPT1β) (P = .0014) and CPT1α (P = .02) on immunoblotting. Treatment resulted in strict regulation of glycolysis and increased expression of proteins involved in ketone metabolism and the glycerophosphate shuttle. Additionally, linagliptin enhanced protein expression of several citric acid enzymes and significantly regulated protein expression relating to oxidative stress within the ischemic myocardium.ConclusionsLinagliptin shifted myocardial metabolism pointing to enhanced ketone utilization, upregulation of the TCA cycle, and reductions in free fatty acid oxidation, glycolysis, and lactate production. These findings suggest a potential cardioprotective role for linagliptin via metabolic manipulation under ischemic conditions, warranting further investigation.Key Words: dipeptidyl peptidase 4 inhibitors, linagliptin, coronary artery disease, myocardial metabolism, mitochondrial oxidation

Metabolic modulation after linagliptin treatment within ischemic myocardium.

Central Message

Linagliptin shifts ischemic myocardial metabolism toward ketone utilization and away from fatty acid and glycolytic pathways.

Perspective

Dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibition with linagliptin modulates myocardial metabolism in the setting of chronic ischemia, enhancing tricarboxylic acid cycle and ketone utilization while reducing fatty acid oxidation and glycolysis. These findings highlight potential novel mechanistic changes in a clinically relevant model with coronary artery disease.

The magnitude and lethality of coronary artery disease (CAD) within the United States continue to present major public health challenges. Despite recent advances, especially procedurally with percutaneous coronary interventions and coronary artery bypass grafting, myocardial ischemia due to CAD remains the leading cause of cardiovascular mortality and disability.1 This persistence is partly related to the nearly 30% of patients for whom mainstay procedural treatments are unsuitable or who undergo incomplete revascularization with these strategies.1 These patients often experience the subsequent sequelae of myocardial ischemia, ranging from myocardial necrosis to maladaptive remodeling, and ultimately heart failure. A majority of these patient receive guideline-directed medical management and either have been ruled out or have exhausted procedural therapies and are deemed “no option” patients.2 Thus, it is imperative to identify therapies to improve cardiovascular mortality and morbidity as well as quality of life for these patients in the setting of this ongoing, late-stage coronary disease.

In this regard, novel antihyperglycemic agents have shown promise by exhibiting cardiovascular protection beyond their systemic metabolic effects. Among this group of agents, dipeptidyl peptidase 4 (DPP-4) inhibitors have demonstrated cardiovascular benefits in both small animal studies and some clinical studies.3, 4, 5, 6 However, large randomized controlled trials with these agents have not shown improvements in cardiovascular outcomes, including cardiovascular deaths, acute myocardial infarcts, and stroke.7, 8, 9 The disparity among these randomized controlled trials and retrospective clinical studies as well as small animal studies may be explained in part by the short follow-up of 2 to 3 years in the clinical trials. Nonetheless, it highlights the necessity of precisely reconciling the inconsistencies in studies on the cardiovascular effects of DPP-4 inhibitors. Our laboratory is well suited for this endeavor by using a clinically relevant large animal model of chronic myocardial ischemia. In fact, we previously demonstrated that DPP-4 inhibition with linagliptin significantly improved cardiac function, increased myocardial survival, and reduced adverse remodeling after induction of CAD.10 We hypothesized that the improved cardiac performance and beneficial molecular changes were related to increased perfusion to the ischemic area resulting from enhanced vascular reactivity. While this study clarified the therapeutic potential of DPP-4 inhibition for CAD, the explanatory power lacks the full breadth of basic molecular mechanisms likely conferred by linagliptin treatment.

Proteomics is becoming an increasingly useful tool for use in comprehensive investigations of myocardial tissue and the therapeutic mechanisms behind DPP-4 inhibition. The use of high-throughput techniques allows for multidimensional analysis of the cellular phenotypes underpinning the pathophysiologic processes of cardiovascular disease and potential therapeutic targets.11 Furthermore, an emerging field of cardiovascular exploration for therapeutic strategies is the altered myocardial metabolism within diseased states and the potential to positively manipulate this process. This is understandable, given that the healthy human heart demands the highest oxygen content per tissue mass and depends on a continuous supply of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) for adequate pump function.12 Consequently, the present study aimed to provide a comprehensive mechanistic account by using highly sensitive throughput techniques to identify any metabolic manipulation likely contributing to the cardioprotective effect of the DPP-4 inhibitor linagliptin. We also explored other proteomic shifts to better understand the myocardial phenotype due to linagliptin treatment in the setting of CAD.

Methods

Overall Animal Protocol

Our model used both female and intact male Yorkshire swine (n = 16) sourced from the Cummings School of Veterinary Medicine at Tufts University. The experiment was designed with 2 foundational principles in mind: the important biological sex-based differences of cardiovascular disease and the ability to accurately model the pathophysiology of CAD in humans. By using Yorkshire swine, which have similar coronary artery anatomy, cardiac physiology, and metabolic and proteomic profiles as humans,13 and by incorporating equal sex representation within our 2 groups, this model is ideal for investigating therapeutic agents and targets for CAD. Furthermore, the use of isolated CAD without metabolic comorbidities allowed us to examine the effects of linagliptin specifically in the context of ischemia.

The surgical protocol began with the arrival of the swine at 10 weeks of age, where they were housed socially and provided a standard laboratory diet. After 1 week, each animal underwent a left mini-thoracotomy for the placement of an ameroid constrictor to the proximal left circumflex coronary artery (LCx) for induction of chronic myocardial ischemia. After a 2-week period, the swine were assigned to either receive 2.5 mg of oral linagliptin daily (n = 8) or no treatment (n = 8) for 5 weeks. The ideal sample size of 8 swine per group was determined using previous data and a β error of 0.10. Finally, the swine underwent a terminal harvest procedure for myocardial tissue collection (Figure 1).

Overview of study methodology. The graphical abstract visually highlights the overall methods for this project, including ameroid placement, treatment, and terminal harvest.

Ameroid Constrictor Placement

The swine received antimicrobial and antithrombotic prophylaxis as described previously.14 They were anesthetized, intubated, and properly positioned supine with a slight tilt to the right. The surgical site was sterilely prepped and draped. A minimally invasive thoracotomy was performed via the third intercostal space. Once within the chest, the pericardium was opened, and the left atrial appendage was retracted. This exposed the LCx and left anterior descending coronary artery (LAD), where the proximal LCx was isolated via blunt and sharp dissection and a vessel loop was placed. The swine were systemically heparinized, after which myocardial perfusion mapping was performed. The LCx was occluded by retracting the vessel loop for 2 minutes, followed by injection of 5 cc of gold microspheres (BioPAL) into the left atrial appendage. The quantification of gold within the myocardial tissue maps coronary artery regional blood flow to identify the most ischemic and nonischemic territories. Then the ameroid constrictor was placed around the LCx, with sizing based on visual inspection. Topical nitroglycerin was applied to the vessel to prevent arterial vasospasm. At this point, the pericardium was closed with absorbable suture, followed by closure of the chest and skin in layers. The swine recovered in a monitored setting with trained veterinary staff.

Linagliptin Treatment

The ameroid constrictor consisted of a titanium ring with an inner casein layer in which the artery sits that closes gradually over 2 to 3 weeks, mimicking the onset of CAD. Treatment with linagliptin began 2 weeks after the surgical procedure, which represents the start of treatment for a patient with a diagnosis of CAD. The swine were then assigned to receive either daily linagliptin (n = 8; 5 females and 3 males) or no drug (n = 8; 4 females and 4 males). Linagliptin dosing at 2.5 mg was determined by the weight in prior swine experiments, approximately 30 to 40 kg, roughly one-half of the ideal human weight of 70 kg. Therefore, the typical human dose of linagliptin at 5 mg/d was halved and given orally to the swine each day over a 5-week period.

Terminal Harvest Procedure

Anesthesia, intubation, and surgical site preparation were as described above. Each animal underwent a median sternotomy, exposing the heart, followed by opening of the pericardium and isolation of both the atria and the left ventricular apex. The entire heart was resected to perform beating euthanasia and subsequently sectioned. The left ventricle was sectioned into 16 segments determined by apical and basal halves, as well as further delineation according to the positions of the LAD and LCx. Tissue was either dried for gold microsphere quantification by BioPal to determine ischemic and nonischemic segments or snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen.

Immunoblotting

Lysates were created via homogenization from the most ischemic area of myocardial tissue as determined by gold microsphere quantification, and lysate total protein concentration was quantified with a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Here 40 μg of total protein was run on 4% to 12% Bis-Tris gels (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and then transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Bio-Rad). Membranes were blocked (5% nonfat milk) and then incubated with 1:1000 dilutions of primary antibody overnight at 4 °C (Table E1). Additionally, separate membranes were incubated with total OXPHOS Antibody Cocktail (Abcam) to assess each complex of the mitochondrial membrane respiratory chain. The next day, membranes were incubated with the appropriate 1:2000 dilution of secondary antibody for 1 hour at room temperature. Membranes were imaged using Chemiluminescence Western Blotting Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and the ChemiDoc Imaging System (Bio-Rad). Repeat probing was accomplished using Restore PLUS Western Blot Stripping Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Band analysis was completed using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health), and all analyzed immunoblot bands were normalized to vinculin run on the same gel. Representative membranes are shown in Figure E1.

Representative immunoblots of several metabolic proteins. Each representative gel is displayed with vinculin, to which the bands were normalized.

Proteomic Analysis

The most ischemic myocardial tissue was transformed into lysates using 10% SDS with 50 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate (TEAB) (n = 6 per group), which were submitted to the Proteomic Core at Brown University for further processing and analysis. The methodology is described in detail in the Appendix E1.

Statistical Analysis

All data were analyzed using Prism 10 (GraphPad Software). Before comparative testing, data normalization was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk method, and outliers were identified using the ROUT method of nonlinear regression set at 2%. Normally distributed data with homogeneous variances were compared using the unpaired Student t tests, and nonparametric data were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. A P value < .05 was deemed statistically significant.

Given the exploratory nature of this study on metabolic manipulation by treatment, as well as the use of discovery-based untargeted proteomic analysis, a lower fold change threshold was chosen, in which log2(FC) >0.1 and <−0.1 were used. This ensured the identification of shifts across multiple metabolic and signaling pathways rather than a focus solely on large-magnitude changes in individual proteins. Pathway enrichment analysis of these proteins was performed with ShinyGo 0.77 (South Dakota State University)15 and KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) pathway diagrams,16 which can partially mitigate overinterpretation of single protein effects but is not a substitute for activity assays.

Results

Overall Proteome in the Ischemic Myocardium

Proteomic analysis identified a total of 5192 proteins. Of these, 707 proteins had significant differences in protein abundance between groups and met the set threshold for fold change, with 369 proteins downregulated and 338 upregulated. Linagliptin Downregulated Key Proteins Involved in Free Fatty Acid Oxidation, Gluconeogenesis, and Lactate Metabolism within the Ischemic Myocardium

Linagliptin treatment was associated with decreased protein expression of CPT1β on immunoblotting (P = .001). This was corroborated by proteomic analysis the identified decreased CPT1β and CPT1α expression in the linagliptin cohort. Furthermore, we found reduced expression of long-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase in the linagliptin-treated group. All of these enzymes play vital roles in fatty acid beta-oxidation. Linagliptin also was associated with reduced expression of Acyl-CoA synthetase short-chain family member 1 (ACSS1), which is involved in the oxidation of short-chain fatty acids (Table 1). On pathway enrichment analysis based on the proteomic data, fatty acid degradation was significantly downregulated (Figure 3, A).

Linagliptin was associated with decreased expression of fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase 1 (FBP1) (log2(FC) = −0.57; P = .01), which catalyzes the key regulatory step in gluconeogenesis, as well as pyruvate carboxylase (log2(FC) = −0.19; P = .009), an important enzyme at the beginning of that pathway. Finally, linagliptin therapy was associated with reductions in both lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) A and LDHB, the 2 main isoforms of LDH involved in lactate metabolism and anaerobic glycolysis. This was corroborated by pathway enrichment analysis demonstrating reductions in gluconeogenesis and lactate metabolism (Figure 3, B).

Linagliptin Modulated Regulatory Proteins Involved in Glycolysis and Entrance Into the Citric Acid Cycle in the Ischemic Myocardium

Linagliptin treatment was associated with decreased expression of hexokinase II determined by immunoblotting, which is the first step in glycolysis and effectively traps glucose within the cell (P = .01) (Figure 3, C). Notably, on proteomic analysis, linagliptin was associated with increased expression of phosphofructokinase (PFKP) (log2(FC) = 0.27; P = .002), the rate-limiting enzyme of glycolysis, as well as increased expression of enolase 2 (ENO2) (log2(FC) = 0.22; P = .02), catalyzing one of the last steps in the pathway. However, this occurs in conjunction with reduced expression of both mitochondrial pyruvate carrier (MPC) 1 and MPC2 (P = .05 and .03; respectively), both of which are essential for transporting pyruvate into the mitochondria (Figure 3, C).

Linagliptin Upregulated Enzymes within the Citric Acid Cycle and Ketone Metabolism

Linagliptin treatment was associated with increased protein expression of multiple enzymes within the citric acid cycle determined by immunoblotting, including the rate-limiting enzymes isocitrate dehydrogenase 2 (IDH2) and citrate synthase, as well as dihydrolipoamide S-succinyltransferase (DSLT) (P < .05 for all) (Figure 3, C). Linagliptin treatment also was associated with increased expression of succinyl-CoA:3-ketoacid coenzyme A transferase 1 (OXCT1) on metabolomic analysis (log2(FC) = 0.3; P = .02), which is the first, rate-limiting step of ketone body utilization in extrahepatic tissue.

Linagliptin Treatment Resulted in Reduction of Several Complexes in the Electron Transport Chain

Linagliptin treatment was associated with an overall decrease in a majority of the complexes composing the electron transport chain (ETC), including complexes I, II, IV, and V (P < .05 for all), as determined by immunoblot analysis (Figure 4). This was observed in conjunction with decreased expression of multiple enzymes related to the assembly of these complexes on proteomic analysis, including multiple cytochrome c oxidase assembly units and the NADH:ubiquinone oxidoreductase assembly unit (Table 1). Additionally, treatment was associated with downregulation of multiple mitochondrial ribosomal proteins (MRPs), including MRPL35, MRPL42, and MRPL54, suggesting reductions in overall mitochondrial biogenesis and transcription of ETC complexes (Log2(FC) <−0.1 and P < .05 for all) (Table 1).

Linagliptin Modulated the Glycerophosphate Shuttle and Multiple Other Proteins Related to the Redox State of the Ischemic Myocardium

We observed an increase in expression of glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase 2 (GPD2) (log2(FC) = 0.21; P = .02), the mitochondrial component of the glycerophosphate shuttle. This occurred in conjunction with a significant reduction in GPD1 (Log2(FC) = −0.91; P = .001), the cytosolic component responsible for converting glycerol-3-phosphate to DHAP and oxidizing NADH to NAD+ (Figure 5). The increase in GPD2 creates more reduced FAD to FADH2 that feeds electrons into the ETC at complex II.

Linagliptin treatment was associated with expression changes in multiple important proteins involved in the redox state of the cell. We observed upregulation of paraoxonase 2 (PON2), heme oxygenase 2 (HMOX2), and microsomal glutathione S-transferase (MGST2) after linagliptin treatment (Table 1). Meanwhile, there was downregulation of several additional redox-related proteins, including glutathione peroxidase (GPX) 3 and GPX7 and NAD(P)H quinone dehydrogenase 1 (NQO1) (Table 1).

Discussion

In this study, we observed significant shifts in metabolic proteins and pathways due to DPP-4 inhibition, marked mainly by reductions in fatty acid oxidation and gluconeogenesis, strict regulation of glycolysis and entrance into the citric acid cycle, enhancement of the citric acid cycle coupled with increased ketone utilization, and an overall reduction in mitochondrial ETC complexes (Figure 6). We also found modulation of the glycerophosphate shuttle that may correspond to increased potential for mitochondrial respiration. Finally, given multiple shifts in myocardial metabolism, we illuminated the redox state within the cell determined by proteome changes of multiple antioxidant enzymes.

Overall demonstration of shifts in ischemic myocardial metabolism following linagliptin treatment. Linagliptin was associated with a reduction in enzymes involved in fatty acid degradation and glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, as well as lactate metabolism. Additionally, linagliptin treatment was associated with upregulation of the TCA cycle and ketone metabolism.

Although a reduction in fatty acid oxidation initially may appear maladaptive, given the multiple investigations demonstrating impaired fatty acid utilization in heart failure, the healthy myocardium readily uses fatty acids in proportion to their arterial concentration, with CPT1 acting as the rate-limiting enzyme.17,18 In the present study, linagliptin was associated with decreased expression of CPT1β and CPT1α, as well as an overall reduction in fatty acid degradation on pathway enrichment analysis. This, along with broad modulation of metabolic pathways, likely reflect a positive metabolic adaptation within the ischemic myocardium. Linagliptin also was associated with decreased hexokinase II expression, indicating a reduction in glucose utilization. Interestingly, we found increased expression of both PFKP and ENO2 after treatment. Several studies have found that an increase in PFK augments overall cardiac function, myocardial remodeling, and electrophysiologic dysfunction in diet-induced cardiomyopathy and even in healthy hearts as well.19,20 These findings suggest tighter regulation of glucose utilization, with decreased glucose entry into glycolysis but enhanced activity of key downstream regulators. Consistent with this, we observed a reduction in both MPC1 and MPC2 after linagliptin therapy, suggesting less pyruvate entering into the mitochondria after glycolysis or fatty acid oxidation. However, this was accompanied by increased expression of citric acid cycle enzymes, implying greater potential for oxidative metabolism.

To summarize, our results point to reduced reliance on fatty acid oxidation and glycolysis, offset by increased mitochondrial metabolic machinery, after linagliptin treatment. One possible explanation for this is our previously reported increase in myocardial perfusion with linagliptin therapy, which may alleviate metabolic stress and reduce the need for high substrate turnover to maintain adequate ATP production.10

Furthermore, linagliptin treatment was associated with increased utilization of ketones rather than lactate. We observed an increase in the expression of OXCT1 and a decrease in the expression of both isoforms of LDH after treatment. OXCT1 is a critical enzyme in ketone body utilization by the myocardium, and this result likely reflects the use of ketones as an alternative fuel by ischemic myocardium in the setting of ischemia and linagliptin treatment. Meanwhile, there was reduced myocardium utilization of lactate after treatment. Ketone bodies provide a more oxygen-efficient substrate for ATP production compared to lactate, which is typically utilized under anaerobic conditions.21 This observed shift away from lactate utilization again may reflect the improved perfusion to the ischemic myocardium following treatment, enhancing oxygen and substrate delivery. As a result, the myocardium becomes less dependent on anaerobic glycolysis and lactate utilization and can preferentially engage in aerobic oxidation of alternative substrates, such as ketones. Moreover, upregulation of ketone oxidation would inherently reduce lactate production, as less pyruvate is generated or diverted to LDH under aerobic conditions.

Linagliptin treatment appears to enhance substrate-level mitochondrial oxidative capacity, despite a concurrent downregulation of oxidative phosphorylation machinery. Specifically, the upregulation of key enzymes in the citric acid cycle suggests increased throughput and improved mitochondrial oxidation of acetyl-CoA–derived substrates, including ketones. This metabolic shift occurred alongside a paradoxical decrease in expression of multiple ETC complexes, their assembly factors, and mitochondrial ribosomal proteins—findings that point to reduced mitochondrial biogenesis and oxidative phosphorylation capacity. We interpret this as a potential shift toward enhanced mitochondrial function, in which improved perfusion and substrate flexibility reduce the need for maximal respiratory capacity. Mechanistically, these adaptations may be mediated by activation of the adenosine monophosphate–activated protein kinase (AMPK) pathway, which we have previously shown to be upregulated in the ischemic myocardium following linagliptin treatment.10 AMPK serves as a key regulator of metabolic homeostasis and mitochondrial quality control, supporting adaptive mitochondrial remodeling under energy stress.22, 23, 24 Thus, the observed improvements in oxidative metabolism may reflect both direct metabolic effects and secondary pleiotropic actions of linagliptin, including previously reported findings of reduced inflammation, myocardial fibrosis, and apoptosis.10,25

In addition to the metabolic modulation, linagliptin also induced compartment-specific adaptations in mitochondrial redox regulation. Notably, we observed nuanced modulation of the glycerophosphate shuttle: upregulation of GPD2, the mitochondrial component, alongside downregulation of GPD1, its cytosolic counterpart. This suggests decreased cytosolic NADH oxidation—consistent with a reduction in glycolysis—and increased FADH2 generation within mitochondria. Enhanced GPD2 expression may facilitate electron flow directly into complex II of the ETC, thereby partially bypassing complex I in the setting of its downregulation. These changes suggest a compensatory shift in electron flow to maintain redox balance and ATP production. Furthermore, alterations in redox-related proteins—upregulation of PON2, HMOX2, and MGST2 and downregulation of GPX3 and GPX7—indicate a shift toward non–glutathione-based antioxidant defenses. Taken together, these results point to linagliptin modulation of redox regulation through both substrate- and compartment-specific mechanisms. Future studies evaluating mitochondrial function ex vivo and delineating the downstream regulation of mitochondrial pathways over time are warranted to better define these effects.

Study Limitations

This study sheds important light on the metabolic manipulation by DPP-4 inhibition; however, it has several limitations worth mentioning. First, our model requires the use of young, healthy swine to allow for reliable ameroid constrictor placement and terminal tissue harvest, which may limit translatability to the clinical setting. Second, this study was exploratory in that it used untargeted proteomic analysis, which results in a lower accuracy of detection. This type of analysis is extremely useful for discovering broad and unbiased proteomic changes but shows more variability in peptide detection and quantification. To combat this, the fold change was lowered to increase the sensitivity to subtle changes, especially in lower-abundance proteins. It is worth noting these small expressional differences might not translate to meaningful biologically relevant changes, and thus multiple findings were corroborated with immunoblotting and pathway enrichment analysis. Nonetheless, targeted proteomic analysis to further validate these metabolic shifts is essential to confirm biological relevance.

Additionally, proteomic analysis reflecting protein abundance might not necessarily reflect enzymatic activity, as post-translational modifications and regulatory dynamics can alter function independent of expression.26 Our use of pathway enrichment partially mitigates this concern; however, this is a single view of complex regulatory interactions, and future studies using “functional” proteomics, enzymatic assays, and metabolomic analysis are essential to corroborate our present findings.

Third, an important element of our model is the use of sex-matched cohorts; however, this current study has a small sample size and likely is underpowered to detect significant sex-specific shifts in myocardial metabolism. Future studies using 7 to 8 animals per sex would allow for a more thorough exploration of these differences.

Finally, our study used a short treatment interval and a single time point to investigate these cellular shifts, which limits the ability to understand long-term treatment and disease progression. These changes observed in this study may shift significantly with a longer treatment interval or with progression of the disease state, which we plan to include in future studies to assess the durability of linagliptin's metabolic manipulation in ischemic myocardium.

Conclusions

In summary, linagliptin treatment induced metabolic modulations of the ischemic myocardium, marked by reductions in fatty acid oxidation and glycolysis and increases in citric acid cycle proteins and ketone utilization. Despite a concurrent downregulation of ETC components and mitochondrial biogenesis, evidence points toward upregulated mitochondrial oxidation and well-regulated redox balance, likely facilitated by the increased myocardial perfusion, AMPK activation, and substrate availability. These findings illustrate potential metabolic pathways manipulated by DPP-4 inhibition that may result in a cardioprotective metabolic state. Future studies with longer treatment durations and “functional” enzymatic analysis are warranted to confirm these mechanistic insights and assess their durability over time.

Appendix E1. Methods: Full Proteomic Analysis

S-Trap Digestion and Desalting

A total of 100 μg of protein was digested using S-Trap Micro columns (Protifi; CO2-micro-40) with minor protocol modifications. Samples were then lysed in SDS/TEAB buffer, reduced with 10 mM dithiothreitol at 37 °C for 1 hour, and then alkylated with 20 mM iodoacetamide at room temperature in the dark for 30 minutes. Following acidification with 2.5% phosphoric acid, samples were mixed with 6 volumes of cold methanol/TEAB buffer and loaded onto S-Trap columns. After centrifugation, samples were washed and digested at 37 °C with trypsin (1:50 enzyme:protein ratio). The next day, peptides were eluted sequentially with TEAB, 0.1% formic acid in water, and 50% acetonitrile. Eluates were dried via vacuum centrifugation and reconstituted in 50 μL of 0.1% formic acid in water. Indexed retention time peptides (Biognosys; Ki-3001-1) were added (1:50 dilution) to monitor ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography analysis.

DIA LC/MS Proteomic Analysis

Peptides were separated on a Vanquish Neo chromatograph using a PepMap C18 trap (7 cm, 3 μm) and an IonOpticks Aurora C18 analytical column (25 cm × 75 μm, 1.7 μm) with a 60-minute gradient (5%-30% B over 50 minutes to 95% B in 5 minutes, then held at 99% B for 5 minutes) at 300 nL/min. Solvent A was 0.1% formic acid in water, and solvent B comprised 80% acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid. Trap and analytical columns were washed and equilibrated between runs using alternating aqueous/organic cycles. MS analysis used electrospray ionization (2050 V) on an Orbitrap in DIA mode: MS1 at 120k resolution (350-1650 m/z), 100-ms maximum injection, and 50% AGC; MS2 with 40 windows (20 m/z, 4 m/z overlap), HCD (28% NCE), Orbitrap at 30k resolution (360-1160 m/z), 50-ms injection, and 1000% AGC.

DDA LC/MS Proteomic Analysis

Peptides were separated using the same Vanquish Neo trap-and-elute setup and gradient as described above. MS1 scans were acquired in centroid mode using wide quadrupole isolation, Orbitrap detection at 120k resolution (300-1500 m/z), 50-ms maximum injection time, 250% AGC target, 30% RF lens, with a 445.12003 lock mass and 275 °C transfer tube temperature. DDA was performed with a 3-s cycle time, selecting precursors (charge 2-7, 375-1500 m/z) using dynamic exclusion (15 s, ±10 ppm), monoisotopic peak selection, and a 20,000-intensity threshold. MS2 scans were acquired in centroid mode using 0.7-m/z isolation width, HCD (30% NCE), and ion trap detection in turbo mode (150-2000 m/z), with a maximum injection time of 35 ms and dynamic AGC (100%).

Quantification and Spectronaut Search Parameters

The .raw files were processed using Spectronaut version 19.6.250121.62635 using 1% peptide spectrum match, peptide, and protein FDR search constraints. Variable modifications included oxidation (M), deamidation (N), phosphorylation (STY), and N-terminal acetylation. Carbamidomethylation (C) was the static modification.
