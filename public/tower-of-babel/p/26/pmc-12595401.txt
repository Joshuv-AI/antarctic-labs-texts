Antagonistic activity of two yeasts against Penicillium expansum in stone fruits
Authors: Paula Tejero, Alicia Rodríguez, Alberto Martín, Sandra Olmo, Alejandro Hernández
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDStone fruits are vulnerable to contamination by Penicillium expansum (PE), a patulin‐producing mould. The use of yeasts as biocontrol agents could be an effective strategy to combat this pathogen. This study evaluated the ability of Metschnikowia pulcherrima L672 (L672) and Pichia kudriavzevii L40 (L40) as biocontrol strategies on peach (Romea), black plum (Black gold), nectarine (Nectavista), and flat peach (Flat Beauty), analysing their impact on incidence and severity of PE damage, patulin production and the expression of the idh gene, a key gene of the patulin biosynthetic pathway.RESULTSThe results indicate that both yeasts reduced the incidence and severity of PE in the four stone fruit types studied, with a more pronounced effect observed for L672. This yeast reduced the presence of patulin in three of the stone fruits where patulin was produced by PE (nectarine, peach and black plum), while P. kudriavzevii L40 limited patulin production in peach by 90%. Regarding the study of the expression of the idh gene, L40 increased its expression compared to the control, although this effect varied among fruits. In contrast, L672 did not produce changes in gene expression in nectarines and black plums – fruits where patulin was not detected.CONCLUSIONThe application of the yeasts L672 and L40 as biocontrol agents was shown to be an effective strategy to reduce the incidence and severity of PE and patulin production in stone fruits, with a greater effect of L672 in most cases. Furthermore, differences were observed in the regulation of the idh gene, suggesting that the efficacy of biocontrol in this aspect depends on both the yeast used and the type of fruit. © 2025 The Author(s). INTRODUCTION

From an economic and nutritional perspective, stone fruit is a significant agricultural commodity. Approximately 40 million tons of stone fruits are produced globally each year from an area of 5 million hectares. 1 Customers really value this kind of fruit, which contributes significantly to the economies of Europe, and particularly Spain. 2 Stone fruits, including peaches, nectarines, plums and cherries, are members of the family Rosaceae, subfamily Prunoidea. 3 , 4 The short postharvest life of stone fruit is primarily determined by the fruit's ripeness upon harvest. Overmaturing stone fruit makes it more prone to rotting and reduces its shelf life, since fungi can easily contaminate it.

Monilia spp., Mucor spp. and Botrytis cinerea are the primary pathogenic fungi at postharvest; however, additional minor pathogens such as Geotrichum candidum, Alternaria spp., Cladosporium spp. and Penicillium expansum can also produce diseases. 1 Penicillium expansum causes a variety of fruits to accumulate mycotoxin and develop blue mould disease. 5 The primary cause of blue rot, which is watery and soft in a wide range of fruits, is the above‐mentioned P. expansum. 6 Numerous stone fruits, such as cherries, plums, peaches and nectarines, have been reported to have this mould lesion. 7 , 8 Although blue mould in stone fruits typically occurs as a secondary infection, it poses a significant risk to consumer health because this Penicillium species can produce patulin. Patulin is a mycotoxin that is associated with neurological, gastrointestinal and immunological effects, and primarily causes liver and kidney damage. 8

Although P. expansum's growth is more prevalent in apples and pears than in stone fruits, patulin has been detected in juices from infected apricot and peach fruits, so it is important not to disregard this risk. 7 , 9 Nevertheless, little is known about P. expansum's ability to colonize stone fruit and produce mycotoxin in these fruits, which will be the basis of juices, a common childhood product, despite the risk that patulin may cause to consumers, and more especially to children's health. 10 , 11 Removing patulin once the fungus has produced it and accumulated inside the fruit will be challenging. Consequently, new and effective strategies should be developed to prevent the growth of P. expansum and the subsequent production of patulin in fruits.

Traditionally, synthetic fungicides have been used to reduce mould infection in the field. However, there are a number of disadvantages to using synthetic compounds, such as the possibility of hazardous residues in food and the emergence of resistant fungi that require larger dosages of fungicides. 12 Alternative control methods are being investigated as a result of growing concern about the drawbacks of synthetic fungicides. Microorganisms or their metabolites have drawn interest as a possible alternative for biocontrol techniques. 13 When used alone or in conjunction with chemical fungicides, moulds, yeasts and their metabolites have demonstrated the ability to biopreserve food, which can help lower the amount of pesticide used to prevent fungal development. 14 , 15

Compared to moulds, yeasts have more rapid growth rates, lower nutritional needs, and non‐toxic qualities that make them suitable for use as biopreservatives. 16 , 17 Several processes, including lytic enzymes, biofilm development, antibiosis, mycoparasitism, competition for resources and the generation of volatile organic compounds (VOCs), have been linked to yeasts' antagonistic ability for biopreservation. 18 , 19 Metschnikowia pulcherrima L672 and Pichia kudriavzevii L40 have both successfully employed this last mechanism of action to inhibit the growth of four common postharvest pathogens (P. expansum, Cladosporium cladosporioides, B. cinerea and Monilia laxa) and Penicillium glabrum, respectively. 20 , 21

The objective of this study was to assess the antagonistic activity of M. pulcherrima L672 and P. kudriavzevii L40 as biocontrol agents against a strain of P. expansum that produces patulin in stone fruits. This was accomplished by testing the impact of both yeasts on fungal growth, patulin synthesis and the expression of the idh gene, a key gene involved in mycotoxin formation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Fruits

To conduct this work, four types of stone fruit were used: peach, black plum, nectarine and flat peach, belonging to the varieties Romea, Black gold, Nectavista and Flat Beauty, respectively. The fruit, with no defects, were purchased from a local shop in Badajoz (Spain). Prior to experimental assays, fruit were stored in cold storage at 1 °C until use.

Fruit characterization

Microbial analysis

Three pieces of each type of fruit were taken. Approximately 10 g of each type of fruit was aseptically taken from different part of the fruit and mixed with 90 mL of 1% (w/v) peptone water (Scharlab, Barcelona, Spain) and homogenized in a Stomacher machine (Seward Ltd, Worthing, UK) at 300 rpm for 1 min. One millilitre of aliquot was used to prepare serial decimal dilutions in 1% (w/v) peptone water. Then, 100 μL of each dilution was spread onto the surface of three different culture media: PCA (enumeration of total mesophilic aerobic microorganism counts, Scharlab), and acidified PDA at pH 3.5 (potato dextrose agar, Scharlab) and RBCA (rose Bengal chloramphenicol agar, Scharlab), both for conducting mould and yeast counts. RBCA and PDA plates were incubated at 25 °C for 5 days, while PCA plates were incubated at 30 °C for 2 days. Later, those plates containing between 30 and 300 colonies were selected for enumeration of total mesophilic aerobic microorganisms, yeasts or moulds. The results were expressed as log10 colony‐forming units (CFU) per gram of sample (log10 CFU g−1).

Physicochemical analysis

For the determination of physicochemical parameters, three pieces of fruit were taken and crushed with a blender.

Total soluble solids (TSS) content was measured for each type of fruit using an RM40 digital refractometer (Hanna Instruments, Eibar, Spain). Each measurement was taken three times. Results were expressed as °Brix.

Titratable acidity (TA) was determined according to the AOAC Official Method 942.15 (AOAC, 2000) and expressed as grams of malic acid per 100 g product. The pH value was recorded after homogenizing 2 g of the crushed fruit with 18 mL distilled water with a digital pH meter (Basic 20+, Letslab, Barcelona, Spain), which was previously calibrated according to the instructions of the device. The analyses were carried out in triplicate.

Microorganisms and microbial inocula preparation

Two yeast strains previously characterized as antagonists against spoilage‐producing moulds by the Food Quality and Microbiology (CAMIALI) research group of the University of Extremadura (Spain) were used in the present work: P. kudriavzevii L40 (L40 21 ) and M. pulcherrima L672 (L672 20 , 22 ).

P. expansum PE‐CECT2280, a patulin‐producing strain, from the Spanish Type Culture Collection (CECT), was used as toxigenic strain.

For the preparation of yeast inocula, both yeast strains were grown for 48 h at 25 °C in PDA. Cells were harvested by sterile distilled water to be transferred to YPD broth (Condalab, Madrid, Spain) before their incubation at 4 °C for 24 h. After this period, cells were harvested by centrifugation at 6000 rpm for 5 min and resuspended in 20 mL of sterile distilled water. Cell concentrations were estimated by bright‐field microscopy using a Neubauer counting chamber and the resulting suspensions were adjusted to 108 cells mL−1.

Mould spore suspensions were prepared from a sporulating culture (10 days old) on PDA at 25 °C. Concentrations were determined by microscopically counting as described above and the suspensions were diluted to 105 spores mL−1 in 0.05% Tween 80.

Experimental settings

The four types of stone fruits (25 fruits of each) were hydrocooled with water containing 100 μg L−1 sodium hypochlorite, at 1–4 °C for 3 min. The fruits were then rinsed in sterile water for 2 min, and completely air‐dried at room temperature. Next, fruits were wounded (4 mm wide × 4 mm deep; three wounds per fruit in the case of peach, nectarine and flat peach, and two wounds per black plum) with a sterilized cork borer. Each wound was then inoculated by using one of three treatments: (i) PE (control) consisting of sterile water (10 μL) followed by 2.5 μL spore suspension (PE‐CECT2280); (ii) PE + L40 consisting of cell suspension (10 μL) of L40, followed by 2.5 μL spore suspension (PE‐CECT2280); and (iii) PE + L672, consisting of cell suspension (10 μL) of L672, followed by 2.5 μL spore suspension (PE‐CECT2280). In the case of treatments L40 + PE and L672 + PE, there was a waiting time of 1.5 h between the yeast and mould inoculation.

The inoculated black plums (six to seven fruits per container) and flat peaches (five fruits per container) were packaged in transparent polyethylene containers (26 × 16 × 5 cm) while the inoculated peaches (two to three fruits per container) and nectarines (two to three fruits per container) were packaged in white polyethylene containers (26 × 16 × 10 cm). The containers were sealed with macroperforated transparent films (ACSA films, Valencia, Spain) with six holes (diameter = 9 mm). All containers were stored at 25 °C, with a relative humidity of 60–65% for a period of up to 6 days. The experiment was repeated three times.

Incidence and severity of P. expansum disease

The incidence and severity of PE‐CECT2280 disease were evaluated every day. All fruits contained in three containers per batch were measured. The disease incidence was expressed as a percentage of fruit with rot symptoms. The disease severity was expressed as millimetres of injured tissue. 22

Extraction and quantification of patulin accumulation

Three samples of each type of stone fruit and batch were collected on day 6 of incubation to determine the patulin amounts produced by P. expansum in the presence and in the absence of both yeasts. Samples of the wounds and their perimeter were aseptically taken with the aid of a sterile knife, weighed and placed in 50 mL conical tubes. The samples were stored at −80 °C for subsequent analysis. For patulin extraction, samples were transferred to a plastic bag and mixed with 100 μL chloroform in a Stomacher Lab machine for 5 min. After 1 h in darkness, the slurry was filtered twice through anhydrous sodium sulfate and evaporated in a rotatory evaporator at 40 °C as described by Sosa et al. 23 The residue was resuspended in 4 mL chloroform, filtered through a 0.45 mm pore size nylon membrane, and evaporated to dryness under a gentle stream of nitrogen. The extracts were stored at 4 °C in the dark until required and resuspended in 200 μL acetonitrile just before analysis. Patulin was quantified using the micellar electrokinetic capillary electrophoresis technique according to the method optimized by Martín et al. 24 An Agilent Technologies 7100 model with a photodiode array detector (Agilent Scientific Instruments, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was used. A fused‐silica capillary of 57 cm length and 75 μm internal diameter was used for separation with 25 mmol L−1 sodium tetraborate and 50 mmol L−1 sodium dodecyl sulfate (pH 9) as running buffer, at 15 kV, maximum current at 150 mA, and a capillary cassette temperature of 25 °C. The absorbance was recorded at 200 and 280 nm wavelengths. For each peak, a spectrum of absorbance between 190 and 600 nm was obtained in the photodiode array detector. Patulin (Sigma Chemical Co., St Louis, MO, USA) was used as standard, and the detection and quantification limits were 0.238 and 3.568 ppb, respectively.

Quantification of the relative expression of the idh gene

Samples for gene expression analysis were taken at days 2, 4 and 6. After each incubation time, mycelium was scraped from the surface and collected under sterile conditions, then flash frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C pending RNA extraction. RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis were conducted as described by Galván et al. 25 Real‐time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) reactions were carried out in a 7300 Real‐Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) using the SYBR Green method, previously optimized by Rodríguez et al. 26 , 27 The amplification of idh, a key gene of the patulin biosynthetic pathway, and β‐tubulin, a housekeeping gene, was performed following the methodology described by Rodríguez et al. 26 , 27 Briefly, the reaction mixture for amplification of each gene consisted of 6.25 μL NZY qPCR Green Master Mix 2× (NZYTech, Lisbon, Portugal), 2.5 μL cDNA template (2 ng μL−1) and different concentrations of each primer. For idh gene the final concentrations of the primers F‐idhtrb and R‐idhtrb were 400 and 700 nmol L−1, respectively. In the case of β‐tubulin gene, the concentration of the primers TubF1 and TubR1 were 300 nmol L−1 each. The thermal cycling conditions for amplification of the two genes were a single step at 95 °C for 10 min, and 40 cycles at 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min, followed by the melting curve analysis of the PCR products, consisting of a slow ramp between 60 and 99 °C. The values of the melting temperatures were checked to ensure the fidelity of the results and specificity of the primers. The quantification cycle – the cycle in which fluorescence reaches a defined threshold – was automatically obtained by the instrument using default parameters of the 7300 Fast System software (Applied Biosystems). The relative quantification of the expression of the idh gene was calculated using the 2−∆∆CT method. 28 The β‐tubulin gene was used as an endogenous control. The calibrator (control conditions) used for calculations were the samples of P. expansum contaminating each of the fruits (nectarine, flat peach, peach and black plum) in the absence and presence of yeasts incubated for 2 days (first sampling day).

Statistical treatment

Statistical analysis was performed using the SPSS statistical program for Windows (v. 22, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). For the analysis of the incidence and severity of P. expansum infections, one‐way and two‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) (treatment × time) was performed. The remaining parameters (patulin production and gene expression) were analysed with one analysis. Mean values were separated for comparison by Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) (P ≤ 0.05).

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Fruit quality

The fruits used in this study had mesophilic aerobic bacteria, yeast and mould counts of 3.27 ± 0.90, 2.74 ± 0.54 and 2.35 ± 0.48 log10 CFU g−1, respectively, according to preliminary microbial assessments (data not shown). According to Khan et al. 29 and Williamson et al., 30 these levels are regarded as low and agree with the efficient postharvest sanitisation procedures commonly used for fresh vegetables. In controlled inoculation experiments, it is important to have such low microbial loads because they reduce background microbial interference, guaranteeing that the inoculated microorganisms, not the native microbiota, are responsible for observed microbial dynamics and patulin production.

Table 1 displays the results for TSS, TA and pH concerning physicochemical characteristics. In terms of TSS, the mean levels ranged from 8.67 ± 0.06 °Brix (nectarine) to 13.57 ± 0.06 °Brix (black plum). The TSS of the four fruit varieties was greater than 8 °Brix, the EU's minimal requirement for commercializing this fruit type as stated in Commission Regulation (EC) 1861/2004. However, it has been reported that TSS > 11 °Brix yields the best fruit quality, 31 , 32 and three of the four fruit kinds examined had TSS levels over this cutoff. High TSS levels also have an impact on fungal physiology, as elevated sugar content can promote P. expansum growth and secondary metabolite production. 33

pH values were within the acceptable physiological range for each fruit type. Nectarine and flat peach had values of 4.25 and 4.10, respectively, which slightly exceed the optimal range for peaches and nectarines (~3.9) as suggested by Cantin et al. 34 Both edibility and microbial interaction investigations are still supported by these values, though, as they fall within the wider consumer‐acceptable range of 2.8–5.5. 35 The average pH of 3.18 in plum aligns with the optimal range for this species (2.8–4.6), supporting its suitability for fungal inoculation studies where acid stress may influence mycotoxin biosynthesis. 36

Regarding TA, values were consistent with expected ranges: plum exhibited 1.36% malic acid, fitting well within the reported optimal range of 1–1.8% for this fruit type. 37 Peach, nectarine and flat peach showed acidity values near the expected 0.50–0.80% malic acid according to previous studies, 38 although flat peach was slightly below this range, at 0.47%.

In terms of incidence results, nectarine (Fig. 1(A)) shows that the percentage of incidence in the control increases rapidly from day 0 of storage and stabilizes near 100% on day 3. In contrast, the increase in incidence in the batches treated with both yeasts occurs gradually, starting on day 2 for those treated with L40 and on day 3 for those treated with L672. When compared to the other batches, the final L672 batch's values were the lowest on each storage day, peaking at about 80% on day 6. These results also match the severity values found in this fruit (Fig. 2(A)), with the control group achieving the highest severity values (up to 30 mm on day 6 of storage) and the L672 batch having the lowest severity (up to 18 mm on the same day), while the L40 batch achieved a value of about 21 mm.

The results obtained for flat peaches are similar to those previously mentioned, although in these batches an increase in both incidence and severity was observed from the beginning of the experiment (Figs 1(B) and 2(B)). The control batch reached 100% incidence on day 5 of storage, while the treated batches did not reach this value on any day, reaching a maximum of 90% on the last day in both batches (L40 and L672). Despite these, higher incidence values (%) were observed during the experimental period in the batch treated with L40 yeast (Fig. 1(B)). On the other hand, when looking at the severity data (Fig. 2(B)), the control similarly had the highest values, reaching about 30 mm on day 6 of storage. There were not many differences in the yeast‐treated batches throughout the course of the days; nevertheless, on the last day, it was noted that the batch treated with L40 stayed at a value near 24 mm, whereas the batch treated with L672 did not reach 22 mm.

In the case of peaches (Figs 1(C) and 2(C)), a significant rise in incidence was noted, reaching 100% in the control on day 3 of storage and in the batch treated with L40 on day 4. On the final day of storage, the batch treated with L672 reached a maximum of 95% (Fig. 1(C)). Regarding the severity results (Fig. 2(C)), the control group also showed higher values, reaching 35 mm severity on the last day of storage. While the batches treated with yeast showed little difference, the batch treated with L40 showed higher results from day 3 of storage, reaching a value close to 32 mm on the last day, while the batch treated with L672 showed a value close to 28 mm.

The incidence of black plums (Fig. 1(D)) increased significantly in the control group on day 2 of storage, reaching about 90% incidence; however, it was not until day 5 that it reached 100%. However, no significant differences were observed in the yeast‐treated batches until day 5 of storage. In contrast to the results obtained for the other stone fruits, the batch treated with L672 had higher incidence values, reaching 100% on day 5, while the batch treated with L40 had an incidence of about 80% on days 5 and 6 of storage. Concerning severity data (Fig. 2(D)), higher values were obtained throughout storage in the control, with the last day's maximum value approaching 40 mm. No significant changes were seen in the yeast‐treated samples, which had a value of about 30 mm on the final day of storage.

The results obtained in this research are expected and very promising. The controls' wounds progressed as predicted because, according to Louw and Korsten's 39 study on P. expansum‐inoculated plums and nectarines, a 100% incidence occurred after 7 days at room temperature and a severity of 45–47 mm.

The results obtained in the yeast‐inoculated batches also followed the expected evolution. Cabañas et al. 21 noted a decrease in the incidence of grapes infected with Pichia kudriavzevii compared to Penicillium glabrum. Delali et al. 40 found that after 9 days of storage at 25 °C, Pichia kudriavzevii reduced the incidence of Penicillium digitatum in oranges by 57% and with a severity of 45 mm, as opposed to 100 mm for the control. In cherry tomato wounds, Liu et al. 41 found that this yeast species also decreased the frequency and intensity of Rhizopus stolonifer, Alternaria alternata, Penicillium spp. and B. cinerea. Metschnikowia pulcherrima was found by De Paiva et al. 22 to reduce the incidence and severity of P. expansum in cherries. Ruiz‐Moyano et al. 20 found that this yeast had the same effect on apples infected with P. expansum and B. cinerea as well as on nectarines inoculated with C. cladosporioides and Monilia laxa. Additionally, Spadaro et al. 42 noted that Metschnikowia pulcherrima had a biocontrol effect on wounds on apples inoculated with P. expansum and B. cinerea, limiting the growth of these moulds and demonstrating control of these moulds on apples inoculated with the yeast for 8 months at 1 °C.

On the four fruit types under investigation, the two yeasts had the intended and favourable impact of reducing the incidence and severity of P. expansum damage. It is noteworthy that, while L672 yeast exhibited a stronger antifungal impact than L40 yeast on three of the four fruits under investigation (nectarine, flat peach and peach), L40 yeast outperformed L672 yeast in the case of black plum. With a pH value of 3.18 and a TA value above 1.30 g malic acid 100 g−1 fruit, black plums had the lowest pH and highest TA values. The reason why Pichia kudriavzevii L40 showed better antagonistic activity against P. expansum on black plum can be based on the fact that Pichia kudriavzevii has been characterized by its ability to survive extremely acidic environments, 43 , 44 despite the fact that Metschnikowia pulcherrima seems to tolerate acidic surroundings well. 45 , 46 , 47

Patulin production

Patulin production by P. expansum with and without yeast inoculated in the four kinds of stone fruits is displayed in Fig. 4. Surprisingly, despite the fact that flat peach and peach belong to the same species (Prunus persica), the behaviour of P. expansum in relation to mycotoxin production was very different. Penicillium expansum was unable to produce this mycotoxin either in the presence or in the absence of any of the two yeasts evaluated when grown on flat peaches (Fig. 4). However, when P. expansum was grown on peaches without yeasts, it was able to produce this mycotoxin in amounts greater than 60 ppm. In the case of the other two fruits, P. expansum alone was able to produce 1.69 and 6.88 ppm in nectarines and plums, respectively. The pH of the fruit, the amount of soluble solids and the combination of nutrients are some of the factors that affect the biosynthetic pathways of this mycotoxin's production, which explains the variations in patulin concentrations observed in the control of the various fruit types. 36 , 48 Although it has been reported that patulin is very stable in low values of pH near 4, and ripened fruits are ideal for their production and biosynthesis, 49 research on the synthesis of patulin by P. expansum in stone fruits is quite rare; studies on other usual fruit hosts, like apples and pears, are far more prevalent. 50 , 51 , 52 , 53 Neri et al. 9 demonstrated that four strains of P. expansum were able to produce patulin in peaches at quantities between 0.2 and 30.1 ppm. Reddy et al. 54 showed that ten strains of P. expansum contaminating peaches and black plums and incubated at 25 °C for 9 days produced patulin at amounts of 0.1 and 1.3 ppm, and 0.15 and 2.3 ppm, respectively.

Amount of patulin (ppm) produced by Penicillium expansum in wounds on nectarine, peach and black plum, inoculated with Pichia kudriavzevii L40, Metschnikowia pulcherrima L672 and in the control (PE).

Considering the impact of the assessed yeasts, L40 demonstrated superior results when applied to peaches, lowering P. expansum's synthesis of patulin by 99% (0.24 ppm). Unexpectedly, the levels of mycotoxins in nectarine and black plum rose by 879% and 231%, respectively, in comparison to the control when this yeast was utilized. It should be pointed out that L40 reduced the incidence percentage of P. expansum disease in black plum. This shows that the behaviour of a mould with respect to a biocontrol strategy does not necessarily correlate with the synthesis of mycotoxins, as previously described. 55 With respect to L672, this yeast strain reduced the amount of patulin that P. expansum produced in the three stone fruits at 100% (nectarine), 63% (peach) and 100% (black plum), in comparison to the control.

Several studies have shown that different yeasts used as biocontrol decreased patulin production in different food matrices. 56 , 57 , 58 , 59 In the case of the application of Pichia kudriavzevii (L40) and Metschnikowia pulcherrima (L672) as a biocontrol strategy in foods, only a few studies about their effect on mycotoxin production have been reported. Helmy 60 found that Pichia kudriavzevii reduced the production of ochratoxin A (OTA) by different strains of P. expansum in vitro. Also, Intanoo et al. 61 observed that this yeast was able to reduce the transfer of aflatoxin B1 from feed to aflatoxin M1 in dairy cows. On the other hand, Settier‐Ramírez et al. 62 described that Metschnikowia pulcherrima reduced patulin produced by P. expansum in apples, and Dikmetas et al. 63 obtained a reduction in the amount of aflatoxin B1 in hazelnuts by this yeast. These investigations support the effectiveness of Metschnikowia pulcherrima and Pichia kudriavzevii as possible food mycotoxin reducers.

According to Commission Regulation (EC) No. 2023/915 of 25 April 2023, 64 which establishes maximum amounts for specific contaminants in foods, fruit juices, reconstituted concentrated fruit juices and fruit nectars may contain up to 50 μg kg−1 patulin. The maximum is 10 μg kg−1 for baby foods, excluding processed cereal‐based diets for newborns and young children. These cut‐off points highlight how crucial it is to control P. expansum growth in fruit. With the exception of batches inoculated with Metschnikowia pulcherrima L672 yeast on nectarine and black plum, the majority of the batches had concentrations far over these limits, according to the data on patulin synthesis. This highlights the need to look for efficient and environmentally friendly strategies to control and even reduce the production of this mycotoxin in stone fruits.

Gene expression studies

The temporal relative expression of the idh gene of P. expansum with and without yeasts was studied in the four types of stone fruits under study, and results are shown in Fig. 5. Samples incubated for 2 days at the different batches (PE, PE + L40 and PE + L672) were used as calibrators. Over the course of the incubation period, the target gene's expression changed, as shown in Fig. 5. These alterations are consistent with recent research showing that mycotoxin‐related gene expression typically fluctuates over time. 65 , 66 , 67 The isoepoxydon dehydrogenase (idh) gene was chosen because it codes for a key enzyme of the patulin biosynthesis in Penicillium spp. 10 , 68 Regarding the control batch (PE), in the case of nectarines and black plums, the idh gene profile was quite similar, with a steady decline in gene expression values followed by an increase at 6 days of incubation. Interestingly, P. expansum produced similar quantities of patulin in these two fruits (Fig. 4). In the case of the flat peach, although the idh gene expression pattern was similar to those found in nectarine and black plum, no increase in the expression of the target gene was found between days 4 and 6 of incubation. This could be the explanation for the absence of this mycotoxin in this stone fruit, despite P. expansum being able to damage 100% of the fruit, and the severity of the damage was similar to that of other stone fruits assessed in this study (Figs 1 and 2). In the case of peaches, the idh gene's expression profile differed from the others; it peaked on day 4 and then began to decline as P. expansum's enhanced production of patulin occurred. There has been past evidence of such temporal peaks in gene expression before toxin production. 66 , 67 , 69 , 70

Relative expression of the idh gene (log2) of Penicillium expansum in nectarine (A), flat peach (B), peach (C) and black plum (D), in wounds inoculated with Pichia kudriavzevii L40 and Metschnikowia pulcherrima L672, throughout storage related to day 2.

Regarding the influence of both yeasts on idh gene expression, it should be noted that in most cases the expression of this target gene when L40 was utilized was higher than that found in the control batch (PE) throughout the incubation time, and the gene expression profile varied between fruits. Comparing gene expression (Fig. 5) and mycotoxin production (Fig. 4) results when this yeast was applied, no relationship could be found. These results are not unusual, as the idh gene is one gene in the pathway, and other key genes in the mycotoxin pathway can be activated, and then the mycotoxin can be produced. In addition, similar to mycotoxin synthesis, gene expression is impacted by nutrients, pH and other abiotic factors. 66 , 67 , 71 , 72

In the case of the L672 yeast, no changes in the target gene expression were found throughout the incubation time in nectarines and black plums, which were the fruits where patulin was not found (Fig. 4). In peach and flat peach, the idh gene patterns in the PE + L672 batch do not link to patulin production in such fruits. It is true that for peaches the idh gene expression is diminished concerning the control throughout the incubation time (Fig. 5). It seems that the application of this yeast can counteract the synthesis of enormous amounts of patulin that P. expansum produces in this fruit host (Fig. 4).

Not much research has been done on how using yeast as a biocontrol affects the relative expression of genes implicated in mycotoxin production. Tejero et al. 70 found that the yeasts Hanseniaspora opuntiae L479 and Hanseniaspora uvarum L793 changed the relative expression of the aflR gene, which is involved in the biosynthesis of aflatoxins, and Peromingo et al. 73 found that the yeast Debaryomyces hansenii had the same effect on the aflR and aflS genes, which are also involved in aflatoxin biosynthesis. Furthermore, Eamlaor et al. 74 demonstrated the capacity of volatiles produced by the antagonistic yeasts Wickerhamomyces anomalus MSCU 0652, Saccharomyces cerevisiae MSCU 0654 and Kluyveromyces marxianus MSCU 0655 to downregulate the expression of AcOTAnrps and Acpks genes associated with OTA biosynthesis in Aspergillus carbonarius. As has been seen, the presence of biocontrol strategies may affect such mycotoxin biosynthetic pathways.

CONCLUSIONS

This study demonstrates the effectiveness of two yeast strains – Pichia kudriavzevii L40 and Metschnikowia pulcherrima L672, in reducing the incidence and severity of Penicillium expansum in stone fruits. Patulin production by P. expansum was markedly suppressed by both yeasts in specific fruit hosts, with L672 showing a complete inhibitory effect in nectarine and black plum. However, a clear correlation between disease suppression and mycotoxin reduction was not always observed, highlighting the complexity of host–pathogen–antagonist interactions. Expression analysis of the idh gene revealed fruit‐ and yeast‐specific modulation patterns, with L672 frequently downregulating gene expression, coinciding with decreased patulin levels. In contrast, L40 often upregulated idh expression, but this did not consistently correlate with patulin synthesis. These findings underscore the potential of both yeast strains as promising biocontrol agents for mitigating P. expansum contamination in stone fruits, while also emphasizing the need for further molecular investigations to elucidate regulatory mechanisms underlying toxin biosynthesis in different fruit matrices.
