From essential thrombocythemia to polycythemia vera (ET-2-PV): rationale and design of a multicenter ELN study
Authors: Tiziano Barbui, Jürgen Thiele, Alberto Alvarez-Larran, Alessandro Rambaldi, Alessandro Maria Vannucchi, Ayalew Tefferi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEssential thrombocythemia (ET) and polycythemia vera (PV) are traditionally viewed as distinct Philadelphia chromosome–negative myeloproliferative neoplasms, yet both frequently harbor the JAK2V617F mutation, suggesting a biological continuum. Occasional reports describe progression from JAK2-mutated ET to overt PV, but this evolution has never been systematically studied. We present the background and design of ET-2-PV, an international, multicenter, retrospective observational study conducted by the Mayo Clinic and European referral centers. Approved by the Lombardy Territorial Ethics Committee and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07203768), it includes patients initially diagnosed with JAK2V617F-positive ET according to 2022 ICC criteria who, after at least one year, newly fulfill diagnostic criteria for PV—minimizing misclassification. The study employs two analytical frameworks: (1) a nested case–control analysis comparing ET-to-PV cases with matched JAK2-mutated ET controls without progression to identify clinical, proliferative, and molecular predictors of progression; and (2) a retrospective cohort comparison assessing outcomes of ET-to-PV patients versus matched individuals with de novo PV. The primary objective is to define the clinical and biological significance of phenotypic evolution from JAK2-mutated ET to PV and its prognostic impact. Secondary aims include identifying risk factors for progression, characterizing clonal dynamics, and determining whether PV arising after long-standing ET differs from de novo PV in vascular risk, disease evolution, and survival. By integrating molecular data with robust epidemiologic design, ET-2-PV aims to define phenotypic evolution from ET to PV as a distinct and clinically meaningful entity, informing risk-adapted monitoring and therapeutic strategies.Keywords: Essential thrombocythemia, Polycythemia vera, Phenotypic evolution, ELN study

Introduction

Philadelphia chromosome–negative myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPN) are traditionally classified into distinct clinical entities — essential thrombocythemia (ET), polycythemia vera (PV), and primary myelofibrosis (PMF) [1, 2] — yet all arise from a common biological substrate driven by mutations in JAK2, CALR, or MPL. Among these, JAK2V617F represents a unique paradigm, being shared by the majority of PV patients and by a substantial proportion of those diagnosed with ET [3]. This overlap has long raised the question of whether ET and PV should be considered strictly separate diseases or rather phenotypic expressions along a biological continuum [4, 5].

Large collaborative datasets, including recent analyses from the Mayo Clinic and the Florence CRIMM centers [6, 7] involving more than 2,000 ET patients classified according to ICC 2022 (International Consensus Classification) and WHO (World Health Organization) criteria [2, 8], have provided a robust description of the natural history of ET in the contemporary era [9]. These studies have clarified risks of thrombosis, fibrotic transformation, leukemic evolution, and overall survival, and have confirmed that driver mutations exert a major influence on phenotype and prognosis. Yet one aspect remains insufficiently explored: the potential for patients diagnosed with JAK2-mutated ET to later develop a phenotype consistent with PV.

This phenomenon has been only sporadically reported in the literature and has never been systematically addressed in a dedicated series. The lack of focused investigation reflects both its apparent rarity and the historical difficulty in distinguishing true phenotypic evolution from misclassification at diagnosis, particularly in the context of so-called masked PV (mPV) [10–15]. In this context it should be underscored that according to clinical, morphological and molecular findings mPV is consistent with an early stage of overt PV [16, 17]. Nevertheless, growing biological insight into clonal dynamics and molecular progression now provides a compelling rationale to revisit this issue in a structured manner.

The clinical study we are describing herein (ET-2-PV) is conceived to address an unmet clinical and biological need, to clarify whether progression from JAK2V617F-positive ET to overt PV represents a real and clinically meaningful trajectory, to identify the patients at risk, and to understand whether PV arising after a long-standing ET course behaves differently from de novo PV. In the current manuscript, we will address the rationale and the aims of the study through two integrated parts. Part A reviews the biological, diagnostic, and clinical evidence supporting the concept of phenotypic evolution and defines the key unresolved questions. Part B presents the design of ET-2-PV, an ongoing international multicenter study specifically developed to answer these questions.

Part A – Biological and clinical rationale

Molecular progression and JAK2 allele burden dynamics

The coexistence of JAK2V617F across ET and PV challenges the notion that these entities are biologically distinct from inception. Patients with PV typically display higher JAK2 allele burdens than those with ET [18], but substantial overlap exists, indicating that allele fraction alone does not strictly determine disease expression.

Lineage-tracing and phylogenetic studies have demonstrated that JAK2-mutant clones may arise decades before clinical diagnosis and expand at highly variable rates across individuals [19]. This variability translates into heterogeneity of phenotypes over time, providing a biological substrate for potential phenotypic shifts. Importantly, experimental and clinical data indicate that erythroid predominance may emerge not through acquisition of new driver mutations but through progressive clonal dominance of an already existing JAK2-mutant stem cell population, influenced by inflammatory milieu, iron availability, and erythropoietin signaling [18].

Within this framework, ET and PV can be conceptualized as dynamic phenotypic states, making evolution from ET to PV biologically plausible, even if it is uncommon in clinical practice.

One of the most compelling arguments in favor of true phenotypic evolution in myeloproliferative neoplasms is the demonstration of longitudinal expansion of the JAK2-mutant clone. Population-genetic modeling of large sequencing cohorts, based on the evolutionary framework developed by Watson [20] and colleagues, has inferred a relative fitness advantage for JAK2V617F clones on the order of ~ 15% per year, albeit with substantial inter-individual variability. In contrast, direct longitudinal observations in individuals reveal much smaller absolute increases in JAK2V617F variant allele frequency, typically in the range of ~ 0.5–1.5% per year, depending on disease stage and study population. Population-based studies from Denmark have shown particularly slow expansion rates in unselected individuals, whereas small clinical series of untreated patients have reported somewhat higher growth in selected settings [21, 22].

Although evolutionary models provide critical insight into the selective advantage of the mutant clone, the clinically observable expansion of allele burden is modest and highly variable as reported by Moliterno [23]. A 2010 study estimated that neutrophil JAK2V617F variant allele frequency (VAF) increases by about 1.4% per year during the first decade after PV diagnosis [24]. Consistently, a 2022 analysis of the hydroxyurea-treated arm of the PROUD-PV trial showed a mean VAF rise of 7% over five years, corresponding to the same annual increase [25]. In PV, higher JAK2V617F VAF is linked to longer disease duration, greater risk of post-PV myelofibrosis, elevated lactate dehydrogenase levels, and more frequent splenomegaly.

To our knowledge, there are no definitive, high-quality studies reporting an exact average rate of JAK2V617F increase over time in ET comparable to the data available in PV; importantly, this gap is consistent with the concept that expansion of JAK2-mutant clones does not invariably translate into erythrocytosis, as the conversion of molecular dominance into a PV phenotype likely requires additional biological or environmental modifiers.

In this regard, recent observations by Tapper et al. [26, 27] are particularly informative. They demonstrated that inherited genetic variation (e.g. at the CTR9 and CHEK2 loci) influences whether the disease manifests as ET or PV among patients with the same JAK2V617F mutation. This suggests that phenotypic evolution reflects not only clonal expansion, but also genetically programmed lineage bias. These findings may offer clinicians a practical perspective. Clearly, germline testing is not currently indicated in routine practice, but clinicians should use this concept to inform their clinical vigilance. They should monitor JAK2-mutated ET patients for rising hemoglobin/hematocrit (Hb/HCT), even within the normal range, and increasing JAK2V617F VAF, in order to evaluate the possibility of early features of PV, and, if clinically indicated, consider further investigation.

Diagnostic pitfall: masked polycythemia Vera and diagnostic boundaries

Any discussion of ET-to-PV evolution must address the diagnostic challenge posed by mPV. It is now well recognized that a subset of patients historically classified as having ET in fact fulfilled the biological criteria for PV [28], despite presenting with Hb and HCT values below the diagnostic range [29–31]. Factors such as iron deficiency, plasma volume expansion, and sex-related differences in erythrocyte mass can obscure overt erythrocytosis, contributing to the under-recognition of PV at initial presentation. These patients often present with isolated thrombocytosis and can appear indistinguishable from ET, yet they usually carry the JAK2V617F mutation and show histopathologic or functional features of PV, such as bone marrow (BM) panmyelosis or endogenous erythroid colony formation, formerly termed “latent” or “inapparent” or “early” or “prepolycythemic phase” PV [13, 14, 18].

Growing awareness of this diagnostic pitfall prompted the WHO to revise the diagnostic criteria for PV in 2016 [1], most notably by lowering Hb and HCT thresholds and emphasizing the integration of molecular and morphologic parameters. Although the fifth edition of the WHO classification published in 2022 retained these criteria, it further underscored the clinical relevance of mPV, the confounding impact of iron deficiency on red cell mass assessment, and the importance of molecular markers in disease classification and prognostication. Contemporary WHO and ICC classifications [4, 8] therefore advocate for an integrated diagnostic approach that incorporates Hb and HCT levels, BM morphology, serum erythropoietin levels, and mutational status.

In this context, longitudinal observation becomes essential. A patient initially classified as having ET may later fulfill full diagnostic criteria for PV not as a consequence of true biological evolution, but rather because a previously masked phase has become clinically apparent. Distinguishing this scenario from genuine phenotypic progression requires careful temporal anchoring of diagnostic criteria and mandates a minimum observation period before labeling a case as ET-to-PV evolution. For this reason, the present study adopts a conservative definition of progression, requiring that PV criteria be met at least 1 year after a well-documented ET diagnosis.

Campbell et al. support the concept of biological continuity between ET and PV, they do not allow for the reliable estimation of transformation frequency [4]. This distinction highlights the importance of distinguishing between incidence data and biological plausibility when interpreting phenotypic evolution.

A pivotal molecularly annotated cohort [32] demonstrated that the risk of polycythemia transformation depends strongly on driver mutation status. No cases were observed among CALR-mutated ET patients, whereas a cumulative incidence of transformation approaching 30% at 15 years was associated with JAK2V617F-positive ET. These observations support the biological plausibility and clinical relevance of an ET-to-PV trajectory in genetically defined subgroups. However, these estimates must be considered within the context of historical diagnostic frameworks preceding the recognition of mPV and the routine use of molecular testing.

Clinical consequences of clonal and phenotypic evolution

An additional clinically relevant question is whether clonal and molecular evolution during the ET phase translates into different outcomes compared with ET patients who do not undergo phenotypic progression. In MPNs, increasing evidence indicates that expansion of the JAK2-mutant clone, reflected by rising variant allele frequency, is associated with higher blood counts and an increased risk of arterial and venous thrombosis [33–36].

Further longitudinal molecular studies suggest that clonal complexity and the acquisition of additional somatic mutations are more prevalent in patients who experience disease progression.

In a sequential mutational analysis of ET and PV, Lundberg and colleagues [37] demonstrated that patients with phenotypic evolution or adverse outcomes were significantly more likely to harbor additional mutations in epigenetic regulators such as TET2, ASXL1, and DNMT3A, supporting a link between molecular evolution and clinical course. Similarly, Grienfeld and coworkers showed that the order of mutation acquisition and the accumulation of secondary mutations influence disease phenotype, progression, and survival, with more complex clonal architectures associated with inferior outcomes [38]. Even in the absence of overt phenotypic progression, molecular features in ET already stratify vascular risk. Large clinical series have consistently shown that JAK2-mutated ET is associated with a significantly higher incidence of thrombosis compared with CALR-mutated disease, a distinction now incorporated into contemporary risk models such as IPSET-thrombosis [39–44]. These observations imply that molecular dominance itself—independent of meeting diagnostic criteria for PV—may carry clinically meaningful consequences.

Against this background, the question of whether PV developing after long-standing ET behaves differently from de novo PV acquires particular importance. Prolonged exposure to chronic inflammation, sustained thrombocytosis, and vascular risk during the ET phase may plausibly condition the subsequent disease course. Although systematic comparative data are limited, preliminary observations suggest that patients with ET to PV evolution may present with a higher symptom burden and possibly an increased rate of vascular complications than those with de novo PV [32].

This gap in knowledge warrants further investigation to clarify its potential clinical implications. In this context, it is also reasonable to consider the possible role of therapy across both the ET and PV phases of disease. In ET, appropriately tailored treatment may influence the time of clonal expansion and could therefore contribute to modulating the risk of subsequent phenotypic progression, although this hypothesis requires confirmation in prospective studies.

Collectively, available molecular and clinical evidence suggests that clonal evolution during ET may have prognostic and therapeutic implications, supporting the need for continued longitudinal molecular monitoring and a more integrated, phase-specific approach to disease management in patients at risk of phenotypic transition.

The need for appropriate studies

These considerations underpin the international, multicenter study presented below. The study is designed to define the frequency and determinants of ET to PV evolution, while rigorously distinguishing true ET from inadvertently mPV. This ensures that phenotypic progression reflects genuine biological change rather than diagnostic reclassification.

Part B – Study design and methods

Overall design and objective

The ET-2-PV study was approved by the Lombardy Territorial Ethics Committee on 01/07/2025 and registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07203768). Ethical approval was also obtained from the institutional review boards of the coordinating center and participating sites.

This is an international, multicenter, retrospective observational study involving the Mayo Clinic and European referral centers specializing in MPNs. The primary objective is to define the clinical and biological significance of phenotypic evolution from JAK2V617F-positive ET to PV and to assess its prognostic implications.

The study is structured around two complementary analytical frameworks (Fig. 1). The first is a nested case–control analysis aimed at identifying proliferative and molecular risk factors associated with progression from ET to PV. The second is a comparative retrospective cohort analysis designed to evaluate whether patients who develop PV after ET experience different outcomes compared with those diagnosed with de novo PV.

Schematic representation of the study design

Eligible patients are those diagnosed with ET according to the 2022 ICC criteria [2] who harbor the JAK2V617F mutation and subsequently fulfill ICC criteria for PV ET to PV case after a minimum interval of 1 year. This temporal threshold was chosen to minimize misclassification due to mPV at baseline.

For the nested case–control component, each ET to PV case is 1:1 matched with a JAK2-mutated ET control who has not progressed to PV over a comparable observation period, matched by age at ET diagnosis, year of diagnosis, and disease duration. For the cohort comparison, each ET to PV patient is 1:1 matched with a de novo PV patient by age at PV dignosis, year of diagnosis, and follow-up duration, ensuring a minimum of five years of observation.

Data collection and variables

All data are derived from routine clinical records and entered into a centralized electronic data capture system managed by FROM - Research Foundation Bergamo Hospital - ETS. Collected variables encompass demographic characteristics, clinical presentation, cardiovascular risk factors, laboratory values, BM findings, molecular profiles including serial assessment of driver mutations, and detailed treatment history.

Longitudinal follow-up includes documentation of thrombotic and hemorrhagic events, progression to myelofibrosis or acute leukemia, occurrence of second malignancies, and survival outcomes. Particular attention is given to changes in JAK2 allele burden over time and to laboratory indices reflecting proliferative activity, such as lactate dehydrogenase levels and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio.

Statistical approach

Patient characteristics are summarized using standard descriptive statistics, with continuous variables reported as means with standard deviation or medians with interquartile range, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Because the study relies on matched designs, inferential analyses account for data dependency. Differences between ET to PV cases and matched ET controls, as well as between ET to PV cases and de novo PV patients, are evaluated using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for continuous variables and McNemar’s tests for categorical variables.

To identify independent predictors of phenotypic evolution, multivariable conditional logistic regression is applied in the nested case–control analysis, yielding odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals. For outcome comparisons between ET to PV and de novo PV cohorts, multivariable Cox proportional hazards models with robust standard errors are used to estimate hazard ratios for composite and individual endpoints. All statistical analyses are conducted using STATA software, with two-sided p values below 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Quality control and data integrity

Data management and quality assurance are centralized at FROM - Research Foundation Bergamo Hospital - ETS, with responsibility for database integrity, consistency checks, and audit trails. Automated validity routines, range checks, and logical consistency algorithms are embedded in the electronic data capture system to minimize entry errors. Visual inspection of case report forms and systematic query resolution ensure adherence to Good Clinical Practice standards. Periodic centralized reports monitor data completeness and performance across participating centers, reinforcing the reliability of the final dataset.

Perspectives

This study integrates recent advances in our understanding of MPN biology with the need to address unresolved clinical challenges, aiming to bridge the gap between emerging biological insights and their practical application in patient care.

Should the study confirm that a definable subset of JAK2-mutated ET patients is prone to evolution toward PV, and that this transition carries distinct prognostic implications, the consequences for clinical practice will be substantial. First, it would support closer molecular and hematologic monitoring of selected ET patients, particularly those exhibiting progressive increases in JAK2 allele burden or proliferative markers. Second, it would legitimize earlier diagnostic reclassification in patients who meet emerging PV criteria, ensuring timely institution of risk-adapted therapy.

From a therapeutic standpoint, these findings would reinforce the need to apply evidence-based PV management strategies, including cytoreductive therapy and antithrombotic measures, in patients evolving from ET once diagnostic thresholds are crossed. In this context, European LeukemiaNet recommendations provide a rational framework for age- and risk-adapted treatment selection.

Ultimately, the integration of biological insight, rigorous epidemiology, and clinical outcomes in this international multicenter effort aims to transform a long-standing anecdotal observation into a well-defined clinical entity. In doing so, it offers a model for how modern hematology can move beyond rigid disease labels toward a more nuanced understanding of clonal evolution and personalized care in myeloproliferative neoplasms.
