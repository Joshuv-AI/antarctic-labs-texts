OsPAL2;1 and OsPAL2;3 are Key Regulators of Phenolic Acid to Modulate Allelopathy and Rhizosphere Microbiome in Rice
Authors: Yujie Gao, Enze Wu, Yiqing Zhang, Xinxin Peng, Jianhe Li, Huabin Zhang, Xue Yan, Jiayu Li, Changxun Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL; EC 4.3.1.5) is encoded by a multigene family in rice (Oryza sativa L.), and its transcriptional abundance is tightly coupled with allelopathic potential. Rice chromosome 2 harbors a tandemly duplicated cluster of four OsPAL paralogs: OsPAL2;1, OsPAL2;2, OsPAL2;3, and OsPAL2;4. To dissect their precise roles in regulating allelopathy, this study generated independent overexpression lines for each OsPAL gene in both the allelopathic rice genotype ‘PI312777’ and the non-allelopathic cultivar ‘Lemont’. Overexpression of individual OsPAL genes significantly enhanced the inhibitory effects of root exudates on barnyardgrass growth, with OsPAL2;1 and OsPAL2;3 exhibiting the most pronounced weed-suppressive phenotypes. Mechanistically, OsPAL overexpression drove distinct tissue-specific metabolic alterations: in transgenic ‘PI312777’, concentrations of protocatechuic acid, p-coumaric acid, ferulic acid, salicylic acid, and cinnamic acid significantly accumulated in both roots and leaves; conversely, ‘Lemont’ overexpression lines displayed selective increases in protocatechuic acid, p-hydroxybenzoic acid, and cinnamic acid. Beyond direct allelochemical mediation, OsPAL overexpression reshaped the rhizosphere microbiome. Transgenic ‘PI312777’ lines displayed reduced alpha diversity and species richness within the root-associated bacterial community. Most strikingly, OsPAL2;1 and OsPAL2;3 overexpression lines showed a marked enrichment of Flavisolibacter, Ohtaekwangia, Lysobacter, and Myxococcota. Collectively, our findings demonstrate that OsPAL2;1 and OsPAL2;3 emerge as prime candidates for engineering next-generation rice varieties with enhanced natural weed-suppressive capacity through integrated metabolic and microbiome engineering.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12284-026-00937-1.Keywords: Allelopathy, Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase, Phenolic acid, Rice, Soil microbes

Introduction

Weeds represent one of the most significant constraints to global agricultural productivity. They aggressively compete with crops for essential resources, including light, water, nutrients, and physical space, thereby impeding crop growth, development, and biomass accumulation (Scarrow 2022; Horvath et al. 2023; Kumar 2024). In fact, yield losses attributable to weed interference often surpass those caused by insect pests and plant diseases combined (Chauhan and Johnson 2011; Dass et al. 2016). Among the most problematic weeds in paddy ecosystems is barnyard grass (Echinochloa crus-galli L.), a notorious companion weed that exhibits striking morphological and phenological resemblance to cultivated rice. This phenomenon, known as crop mimicry, enables barnyard grass to evade detection and mechanical control, making it exceptionally difficult to manage (Ye et al. 2019). Due to its competitive vigor and adaptability, E. crus-galli is widely regarded as one of the most noxious and recalcitrant weeds in rice cultivation systems (Zhang et al. 2021).

Nature has evolved diverse mechanisms for plant-plant interactions, one of which is allelopathy—the process by which plants release bioactive secondary metabolites into the environment to influence the germination, growth, or survival of neighboring organisms (Molisch 1937; Rice 1984). These naturally occurring compounds, termed allelochemicals, play critical roles in mediating ecological relationships and can be harnessed as sustainable tools in agroecosystem management (Blum 2008; Haig 2008). In modern agriculture, exploiting the allelopathic potential of crops offers an environmentally benign alternative to synthetic herbicides. This strategy not only reduces reliance on chemical inputs but also lowers labor and production costs (Ain et al. 2023; Akhtar et al. 2024), contributing to more ecologically resilient farming practices (Kostina-Bednarz et al. 2023) and mitigating the risks associated with herbicide resistance and environmental contamination (Jabran et al. 2015).

The allelopathic properties of rice were first systematically observed during field surveys in the 1980s by Robert H. Dilday from the U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA). He noted distinct growth suppression to ducksalad (Heteranthera limosa) by 191 rice accessions from 5000 donors (Dilday et al. 1989). Subsequent screening of approximately 16,000 rice germplasm lines from global collections revealed that roughly 3% of rice genotypes exhibit strong allelopathic potential under field conditions (Dilday et al. 1994, 1998). Notably, multi-year field trials conducted by Wang et al. demonstrated that plots planted with allelopathic rice cultivars accumulated 32 ~ 60% less weed biomass compared to non-allelopathic controls under natural weed retention. Furthermore, while weed competition reduced grain yields in strongly allelopathic rice by approximately 20%, non-allelopathic varieties suffered yield losses exceeding 50% (Wang et al. 2012a). These findings underscore the agronomic value of allelopathic rice in integrated weed management, highlighting its capacity to reduce dependency on herbicides without compromising productivity.

At the biochemical level, the phytotoxic effects of allelopathic rice cultivars are largely mediated by the rhizospheric secretion of bioactive secondary metabolites, including phenolic acids, diterpenoids, fatty acids, and flavonoids (Iqbal et al. 2019; Li et al. 2022; He et al. 2012; Kato-Noguchi et al. 2010; Berendji et al. 2008). Among these, phenolic acids are among the earliest documented and most extensively studied phytochemicals in rice allelopathy. Structurally, they are aromatic organic acids featuring hydroxyl and carboxyl functional groups attached to benzene rings, conferring high reactivity and biological activity. Widely distributed across plant species, phenolic acids participate in numerous physiological and ecological processes, including defense signaling, rhizosphere modulation, and soil–plant feedback loops (Pu et al. 2025; Kumar et al. 2020).

In the model allelopathic rice line ‘PI312777’, the biosynthesis of phenolic compounds is highly responsive to environmental stimuli. Studies have shown that nitrogen deficiency, high-density co-cultivation with weeds, and exogenous application of salicylic acid (SA) all act as elicitors that upregulate phenolic acid production, resulting in enhanced allelopathic inhibition of neighboring weeds (He et al. 2012; Song et al. 2008; Fang et al. 2009). Phenolic acid biosynthesis originates from the shikimate pathway and proceeds via the phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway, where phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL; EC 4.3.1.5) serves as the entry point and rate-limiting enzyme (Marchiosi et al. 2020). PAL catalyzes the non-oxidative deamination of L-phenylalanine (L-Phe) to produce trans-cinnamic acid, effectively channeling primary carbon flux into the phenylpropanoid network (Tak et al. 2023). As such, PAL activity directly governs the availability of downstream intermediates required for the synthesis of a wide array of phenolic derivatives, including lignins, flavonoids, coumarins, benzoic acid conjugates, and other defense-related metabolites.

The PAL gene family is evolutionarily conserved and typically present in multiple copies within plant genomes, although the number varies significantly among taxa. In rice (Oryza sativa L.), 9 distinct OsPAL genes have been annotated, each exhibiting differential expression patterns and functional specialization (Tonnessen et al. 2015). Comparative studies further confirm that enhanced OsPAL expression and enzymatic activity in rice correlate with improved resistance to pathogens and pests, as well as greater resilience to adverse environmental conditions such as low temperature, saline soils, and heavy metal exposure.

Zeng et al. (2018) revealed that the rice genome encodes nine OsPAL paralogs, each translating into proteins ranging from 681 to 719 amino acids in length. All members possess a highly conserved aromatic lyase domain, a hallmark signature indicative of their functional role in the phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway. Chromosomal localization analysis demonstrated that these nine genes are unevenly distributed across five chromosomes 2, 4, 5, 11, and 12, with notable clustering patterns: four tandemly arranged genes (OsPAL2;1 to OsPAL2;4) reside on chromosome 2, while two adjacent genes (OsPAL4;1 and OsPAL4;2) form a cluster on chromosome 4. The remaining three genes are individually located on chromosome 5 (OsPAL5), chromosome 11 (OsPAL11), and chromosome 12 (OsPAL12).

Under competitive stress imposed by barnyardgrass (Echinochloa crus-galli), transcriptomic profiling showed that eight out of nine OsPAL genes were significantly upregulated in the allelopathic rice cultivar ‘PI312777’, whereas only three exhibited increased expression in the non-allelopathic variety ‘Lemont’ (Fang et al. 2011). Comparative physiological assays conducted by Song et al. (2008) further demonstrated that under low-nitrogen conditions, ‘PI312777’ exerted a markedly higher inhibitory effect on barnyardgrass growth compared to ‘Lemont’. This enhanced allelopathic potential was concomitant with a substantially greater fold induction of OsPAL gene expression in ‘PI312777’, suggesting a nitrogen-responsive regulatory mechanism underlying phenolic acid biosynthesis. Bioinformatics analyses implicated the OsPAL gene cluster on chromosome 2 as a key genomic locus responsible for mediating the elevated production and exudation of phenolic allelochemicals in allelopathic rice. Functional validation through RNA interference (RNAi)-mediated silencing of OsPAL genes in allelopathic rice resulted in downregulation of six downstream enzymes involved in phenolic acid biosynthesis, leading to a significant reduction in root-secreted allelochemicals. Consequently, the allelopathic weed-suppressive capacity was severely compromised, accompanied by a marked decline in rhizosphere microbial population (Fang et al. 2013), underscoring the interdependence between plant secondary metabolism and soil microbiome dynamics.

To dissect the specific contributions of the four OsPAL genes clustered on chromosome 2 (OsPAL2;1, OsPAL2;2, OsPAL2;3, and OsPAL2;4) to allelopathic trait regulation, this study constructed constitutive overexpression constructs targeting each individual gene. These binary vectors were stably transformed into the allelopathic rice accession ‘PI312777’ and non-allelopathic cultivar ‘Lemont’, generating a gain-of-function transgenic lines. By systematically comparing phenolic allelochemical profiles, rhizosphere exudation patterns, and bioassay-based weed inhibition rates among overexpression and wild-type plants, we aimed to identify the key OsPAL paralog(s) most effectively governing allelopathic potential.

Results

Allelopathic Inhibition of Barnyardgrass by Root Exudates from OsPAL-Overexpressing Rice Lines ‘PI312777’ and ‘Lemont’

In our preliminary investigations, the promoter regions of OsPAL2;1, OsPAL2;2, OsPAL2;3, and OsPAL2;4 were individually cloned from the genomic DNA of the allelopathic rice cultivar ‘P3127777’ and the non-allelopathic cultivar ‘Lemont’. Comparative sequence analysis of these promoter regions revealed a high degree of conservation between the two cultivars for OsPAL2;1 and OsPAL2;2. In contrast, the promoters of OsPAL2;3 and OsPAL2;4 exhibited substantial sequence divergence, suggesting potential differences in transcriptional regulation that may underlie varietal differences in allelochemical production (Li et al. 2021). To dissect the specific regulatory roles of these four OsPAL paralogs in rice allelopathy, overexpression transgenic lines of all four OsPAL genes in ‘P3127777’ and in the non-allelopathic ‘Lemont’ background, were generated and genetically identified (Fig. S1), and the target OsPAL gene (OsPAL2;1-OsPAL2;4) was significantly upregulated in the corresponding positive overexpression rice lines. Compared with the WT, target OsPAL gene expression in transgenic ‘PI312777’ lines was upregulated 2.4- to 10-fold in leaves and 2.4- to 9-fold in roots; meanwhile, in transgenic ‘Lemont’ lines, expression increased 5.5- to 12-fold in leaves and 1.5- to 6-fold in roots (Fig. S2).

Root exudates from OsPAL-overexpressing ‘PI312777’ lines significantly suppressed the growth of barnyard grass compared to the control treatment. Among the transgenic lines, overexpression of OsPAL2;1 resulted in the strongest inhibition, reducing plant dry weight of barnyard grass by 65.5%. These inhibition rates were increased 41.3% than those observed with wild-type ‘PI312777’ exudates. Transgenic lines overexpressing OsPAL2;2, OsPAL2;3, and OsPAL2;4 also exhibited enhanced allelopathic activity, with inhibition rates ranging from 61.9 ~ 64.7%, indicating that overexpression of all four OsPAL members contribute to increased allelochemical output or bioactivity in root exudates (Fig. 1A; Table 1).

In the ‘Lemont’ background, overexpression of OsPAL genes similarly enhanced allelopathic potential, albeit to a lesser extent than in ‘PI312777’. Notably, OsPAL2;3-overexpressing ‘Lemont’ displayed the most pronounced effect, inhibiting plant dry weight by 48.5%, markedly higher than the negative inhibition values (− 9.8%) recorded for wild-type ‘Lemont’. The other transgenic ‘Lemont’ lines showed moderate suppression: OsPAL2;1 conferred 18.7% inhibition; OsPAL2;2, 21.4%; and OsPAL2;4, 37.7%, respectively (Fig. 1B; Table 1).

Comparative analysis revealed that overexpression of OsPAL2;1 exerted the greatest improvement in weed-suppressive capacity in ‘PI312777’, while OsPAL2;3 was most effective in enhancing the weak allelopathic trait in ‘Lemont’.

Content of Phenolic Acid Allelochemicals in OsPAL-Overexpressing Rice Lines

Calibration curves were established using serial dilutions of each standard across physiologically relevant concentration ranges. Linear regression analysis demonstrated high linearity (R2 > 0.990) for all target compounds, confirming a consistent detector response and method reliability (Table S1). Peak identities in plant extracts were confirmed by comparing retention times and UV-Vis absorption spectra with those of authentic standards (Fig. S3).

In the leaves of ‘PI312777’ background, all four OsPAL-overexpressing transgenic lines exhibited elevated total phenolic acid content compared to the WT, indicating a consistent metabolic response to increased OsPAL activity. Notably, transgenic lines overexpressing OsPAL2;1 and OsPAL2;3 displayed the most pronounced enhancement in overall phenolic acid accumulation. Detailed profiling revealed significant increases in protocatechuic acid, p-coumaric acid, ferulic acid, and salicylic acid, with cinnamic acid also showing marked upregulation.

In particular, the OsPAL2;3-overexpressing line demonstrated a statistically significant rise not only in these major phenolics but also in p-hydroxybenzoic acid, suggesting paralog-specific regulatory influences on downstream metabolic flux. Among the transgenic ‘Lemont’ lines, overexpression of OsPAL2;1 resulted in the most substantial augmentation of total phenolic acid content. Within this line, p-coumaric acid exhibited the greatest fold-increase relative to its WT counterpart, underscoring its sensitivity to heightened phenylpropanoid pathway flux mediated by OsPAL2;1 overexpression. Specifically, OsPAL2;1 and OsPAL2;3 emerge as particularly influential in boosting phenolic metabolism in ‘PI312777’, whereas OsPAL2;1 alone exerts the strongest effect in ‘Lemont’ (Table 2).

Phenolic acid levels were consistently lower in roots than in leaves; however, total root phenolic acid contents were elevated across all transgenic lines relative to their respective WTs. In the ‘PI312777’ background, the OsPAL2;1 overexpression line showed the most pronounced accumulation, with syringic acid being the most significantly increased. In transgenic ‘Lemont’ lines, OsPAL2;3 overexpression exhibited the most striking effect, significantly increasing root levels of protocatechuic, p-hydroxybenzoic, syringic, p-coumaric, ferulic, salicylic, and cinnamic acids compared to the WT (Table 2).

The profiles of phenolic acids in root exudates from all OsPAL-overexpressing transgenic lines also exhibited a significant increase relative to their WT counterparts. In the ‘PI312777’ genetic background, the magnitude of total phenolic acid accumulation followed a clear hierarchical pattern across the four transgenic lines: OsPAL2;1 > OsPAL2;3 > OsPAL2;2 > OsPAL2;4. Among these compounds, p-hydroxybenzoic acid, syringic acid, and cinnamic acid were consistently and significantly elevated in all four transgenic lines compared to wild-type ‘PI312777’, implying a conserved role of all OsPAL paralogs in enhancing the flux through early steps of the phenylpropanoid pathway. Notably, vanillic acid, ferulic acid, and salicylic acid levels were markedly increased in lines overexpressing OsPAL2;1, OsPAL2;2, or OsPAL2;3, but not in those expressing OsPAL2;4. With respect to individual compound dynamics, p-coumaric acid content was significantly enhanced only in OsPAL2;1- and OsPAL2;3-overexpressing lines, reinforcing the notion of paralog-specific modulation of metabolic branching points within the phenylpropanoid network.

Overexpression of OsPAL2;3 in the ‘Lemont’ led to a broad-spectrum increase in multiple phenolic acids, including protocatechuic acid, p-hydroxybenzoic acid, syringic acid, salicylic acid, and cinnamic acid, resulting in a substantial elevation of total phenolic output. Similarly, OsPAL2;4 overexpression also triggered significant increases, particularly in protocatechuic acid, p-hydroxybenzoic acid, vanillic acid, syringic acid, ferulic acid, and cinnamic acid, culminating in comparably high total phenolic acid levels. Consequently, both OsPAL2;3- and OsPAL2;4-overexpressing ‘Lemont’ transgenic lines displayed significantly augmented phenolic exudation relative to wild-type plants, highlighting genotype- and paralog-dependent nuances in metabolic reprogramming (Table 2).

Variation in Rhizosphere Microbial Diversity Across Rice Genotypes

To investigate the influence of OsPAL2 overexpression on rhizosphere microbial community structure, a total of 10,610 operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were identified across 15 rhizosphere samples encompassing four transgenic ‘PI312777’ lines and their corresponding WT. The wild-type ‘PI312777’ hosted 2378 OTUs, establishing a baseline for comparative analysis. Overexpression of individual OsPAL2 paralogs induced distinct shifts in microbial richness; specifically, the PAL2;2, PAL2;1, and PAL2;3 lines harbored 2252, 2155, and 2147 OTUs, respectively, whereas the PAL2;4 line retained the lowest richness at 1678 OTUs. Notably, each overexpression line possessed unique microbial signatures, with 34, 30, 16, and 29 OTUs exclusively detected in the PAL2;1, PAL2;2, PAL2;3, and PAL2;4 lines, respectively. This strongly implies paralog-specific selective pressures on rhizosphere microbial recruitment (Fig. S4).

Alpha Diversity of Soil Microbial Communities

The Good’s coverage index of all soil samples ranged from 0.958 to 0.971, indicating that the sequencing depth was sufficient to cover the majority of microbial taxa present in the samples. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) revealed that core alpha diversity indices (including Richness, Shannon, Simpson, Pielou, inverse Simpson, Chao1, and ACE indices), which reflect the species richness, diversity, and evenness of soil microbial communities, all differed significantly across treatment groups (P < 0.05).

The BYG-monoculture group displayed the highest levels of alpha diversity across all evaluated indices. The observed improvements in microhabitat conditions and species richness, coupled with increased evenness, contributed to a more resilient microbial community with enhanced ecological functional stability. The PI//BYG co-culture treatment exhibited the second-highest alpha diversity levels, sustaining robust species richness and evenness within the soil microbial communities. This suggests a positive modulatory role in soil microecology. Following above, intermediate alpha diversity indices were observed in the CT and OsPAL-OX (PAL2;1, PAL2;2, and PAL2;3) groups, where the microbial community structure remained relatively stable, showing no significant stimulatory or suppressive shifts in diversity. In contrast, all alpha diversity indices of the PAL2;4 treatment group were significantly lower than those of the other treatment groups (Fig. 2). This treatment not only drastically reduced the species richness of soil microorganisms, but also significantly disrupted the evenness of species distribution within the community, leading to increased community dominance and decreased structural stability.

Beta Diversity of Soil Microbial Communities Based on Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA)

To elucidate the regulatory effects of individual overexpression of OsPAL2 paralogs on the overall structural variation (beta-diversity) of rice rhizosphere bacterial communities, principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) was performed based on the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity matrix generated from the genus-level relative abundance profile of soil bacteria. Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) revealed a highly significant genotypic effect on rhizosphere bacterial community structure (R² = 0.72, P < 0.001), indicating that genetic modification of the OsPAL2 gene family is the dominant factor driving the differentiation of rhizosphere bacterial community assembly.

The PCoA ordination plot showed a clear genotype-dependent clustering pattern of rhizosphere bacterial communities. The CT and PAL2;4 were distinctly separated from all other treatment groups along the first principal coordinate axis, with their rhizosphere bacterial community structure exhibiting highly significant differences from that of the allelopathic donor parent PI (P < 0.01). Specifically, the biological replicates of the PAL2;4 line were tightly clustered within the group, reflecting a strong directional filtering effect of this genotype on rhizosphere bacterial communities, which was fully consistent with the extreme enrichment of Pseudomonas and Azotobacter (phylum Pseudomonadota) observed in our prior taxonomic composition analysis.

In contrast, the rhizosphere bacterial communities of the PAL2;1, PAL2;2, and PAL2;3 overexpression lines clustered closely with the PI parent, with no significant difference in community structure detected between these lines and the PI parent (P > 0.05) (Fig. 3). This result indicated that overexpression of these three OsPAL2 paralogs maintained the rhizosphere bacterial community assembly pattern consistent with the allelopathic rice parent, which was highly concordant with our prior findings from alpha diversity, taxonomic composition, and functional potential analyses. In addition, the BYG-monoculture group (BYG) was clearly separated from all rice rhizosphere samples, reflecting the strong rhizosphere effect of BYG root growth on soil bacterial community assembly.

Further analysis based on the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity matrix quantitatively validated the above findings: the average inter-group dissimilarity coefficient between the PAL2;1, PAL2;2, PAL2;3 lines and the PI parent was only 0.30–0.38, whereas the average inter-group dissimilarity coefficient between the PAL2;4 line and the PI parent reached 0.65–0.68, and that between the CT group and the PI parent was 0.60–0.64. These results further demonstrated the significant functional divergence of different OsPAL2 paralogs in regulating the assembly of rice rhizosphere bacterial communities.

Analysis of Bacterial Community Composition

Based on phylum-level taxonomic annotation data generated via high-throughput amplicon sequencing, this study systematically characterized the compositional and relative abundance profiles of soil microbial communities across different treatments, and the results showed that all soil microbial communities were overwhelmingly dominated by Pseudomonadota and Bacteroidota (collectively contributing over 55% of total relative abundance in all samples), which, together with stably distributed subdominant phyla including Acidobacteriota, Verrucomicrobiota and Actinomycetota, constituted the basal structural framework of soil microbial communities in the study area, while the narrow range of unannotated taxa relative abundance (0.07–2.14%) confirmed the robustness of taxonomic assignment and sufficiency of sequencing depth.

The PAL2;1 and PAL2;3 groups exhibited high homogeneity in soil microbial community structure, maintaining well-balanced dominant taxa abundance (with combined Pseudomonadota and Bacteroidota stabilized at approximately 65% without extreme single-taxon over-enrichment), significant enrichment of functional phyla implicated in organic matter mineralization, nutrient biogeochemical cycling and microbial community assembly regulation (namely Fibrobacterota, Bdellovibrionota and Myxococcota), and strong capacity to sustain rare taxa without marked depletion, a pattern consistent with their moderately high alpha diversity levels; pairwise comparative analyses further identified distinct compositional divergence between the PAL2;1/PAL2;3 groups and other treatment cohorts, as they were entirely distinct from the simplified low-diversity community structure of the PAL2;4 group (defined by extreme Pseudomonadota enrichment at a mean relative abundance of 62.48% coupled with sharp depletion of nearly all subdominant phyla), lacked the unique Cyanobacteriota-enriched compositional signature of the CT group, were characterized by enrichment of copiotrophic functional phyla unlike the oligotrophic taxa-enriched BYG and PI groups, and only shared high structural similarity with the PAL2;2 group with merely subtle variation in rare functional phyla abundance; minor between-group differences between PAL2;1 and PAL2;3 were limited to relative abundance of specific functional phyla, with higher abundance of Verrucomicrobiota and Chloroflexota in the PAL2;1 group and significant advantage in Fibrobacterota, Myxococcota and Gemmatimonadota abundance in the PAL2;3 group, with no significant divergence in overall community structure (Fig. 4).

Collectively, the PAL2;1 and PAL2;3 treatments can effectively preserve soil microbial diversity and maintain the structural balance and functional integrity of soil microecosystems, representing promising strategies for soil microecological regulation, and treatment-mediated shifts in soil habitat conditions are the primary drivers of the observed compositional differentiation of soil microbial communities in this study.

Differential Analysis of Bacterial Genera

Building on the prior phylum-level soil microbial community analysis, this study systematically characterized the genus-level taxonomic composition, relative abundance profiles, and inter-group variations of soil microbial communities across different treatments. Our results showed that the relative abundance of unannotated taxa (‘others’) ranged from 30.95% to 46.70% across all samples, with genus-level community differentiation patterns highly consistent with phylum-level findings: the uncultured genus Incertae sedis was the core dominant genus across all samples (relative abundance: 9.17–38.32%), while Pseudomonas, Lysobacter, Azotobacter, Flavisolibacter, and Sphingomonas were ubiquitous dominant genera with significant inter-group variations in relative abundance.

The PAL2;1 and PAL2;3 treatment groups exhibited high homogeneity in genus-level community structure, maintaining a well-balanced distribution of core dominant genera without single-taxon over-enrichment (mean relative abundance of Incertae sedis: 26.25% and 25.09%, respectively). Both groups were significantly enriched in genera involved in soil organic matter mineralization, nutrient biogeochemical cycling, and community assembly regulation (Flavisolibacter: 2.02% vs. 3.00%; Ohtaekwangia: 2.34% vs. 1.95%; Lysobacter: 3.61% vs. 3.49%; uncultured possible genus 04: 2.44% vs. 4.13%), and had significantly higher Myxococcota abundance (1.56% vs. 2.22%) than the PAL2;4 (0.58%) and CT (1.37%) groups (Kruskal-Wallis test, P < 0.05), alongside stable relative abundances of rare functional genera (e.g., Terrimonas, Ellin6067) without significant depletion, consistent with their balanced phylum-level structure and moderately high alpha diversity (Fig. 5).

Differential abundance analyses further validated phylum-level community divergence: the PAL2;4 group showed extreme Pseudomonas enrichment (mean 9.49%, > 8-fold higher than PAL2;1/PAL2;3), accompanied by > 60% reduction in Incertae sedis and Myxococcota abundance, > 90% reduction in organic matter-related functional genera, and a markedly simplified community structure; the CT group was distinguished by extreme Oleiharenicola enrichment (mean 3.67%, > 30-fold higher than PAL2;1/PAL2;3) and imbalanced functional taxon distribution; the BYG and PI groups were enriched in oligotrophic genera (e.g., Sphingomonas, uncultured genera within Comamonadaceae), while PAL2;1/PAL2;3 favored copiotrophic functional taxa; only the PAL2;2 group showed high community similarity to PAL2;1/PAL2;3, with only minor variations in rare functional taxa. Only minor variations in specific functional genera were detected between PAL2;1 and PAL2;3, with no fundamental divergence in core community structure.

Co-occurrence Network Analysis of Interspecific Interactions in Soil Microbial Communities

A genus-level co-occurrence network of soil bacterial communities was constructed, comprising 34 nodes (bacterial genera) and 85 statistically significant correlation edges (P < 0.05). Among these edges, 53 were positive correlations (62.35%) and 32 were negative correlations (37.65%), indicating that synergistic symbiotic relationships predominate among soil bacterial species, while niche differentiation and antagonistic interactions also exist to a certain extent.

Comprehensive analysis of node topological properties revealed that Roseateles (Proteobacteria, Betaproteobacteria), Azotobacter (Proteobacteria, Gammaproteobacteria), and Buchnera (Proteobacteria, Gammaproteobacteria) were the most critical hub nodes in the network. Roseateles exhibited the highest weighted degree (8.44) and a maximum eigenvector centrality of 1.0, indicating its absolute central position in the network and extensive, tight interactions with other species. Azotobacter and Buchnera had weighted degrees of 7.98 and 8.02, respectively, with eigenvector centrality values exceeding 0.93, further confirming the pivotal roles of these three Proteobacteria genera in maintaining the structural stability of soil bacterial communities.

Betweenness centrality analysis showed that Aromatoleum (Proteobacteria, Betaproteobacteria, 125), Flavihumibacter (Bacteroidetes, Chitinophagia, 78), and Fuscovulum (Proteobacteria, Alphaproteobacteria, 73) had significantly higher betweenness centrality than other nodes. These taxa function as key bridge nodes mediating information and material transfer between phylum-level groups, and their removal could lead to reduced overall network connectivity or even separation of different phylum clusters. The genus Incertae sedis had the highest relative abundance (41.48%) but extremely low topological status in the network (weighted degree = 0.75, betweenness centrality = 0), suggesting that this dominant taxon is primarily driven by abiotic environmental factors rather than biotic interactions with other bacterial groups.

Proteobacteria, the most species-rich and densely connected phylum in the network, contained 15 genera and participated in 58 edges (68.24% of total edges), including 27 intra-phylum positive edges, 11 intra-phylum negative edges, 12 inter-phylum positive edges, and 8 inter-phylum negative edges. Strong positive correlations within Proteobacteria were mainly observed among nitrogen-fixing functional groups, such as between Azotobacter and Roseateles (r = 0.82, P < 0.00001) and between Azotobacter and Buchnera (r = 0.81, P < 0.00001), indicating that these taxa may participate in soil nitrogen cycling through synergistic effects. Bacteroidetes contained 7 genera and participated in 32 edges, including 9 intra-phylum positive edges, 5 intra-phylum negative edges, 10 inter-phylum positive edges, and 8 inter-phylum negative edges. Numerous significant interactions were detected between Bacteroidetes and Proteobacteria, with the most prominent negative correlation between Flavihumibacter (Bacteroidetes) and Aromatoleum (Proteobacteria) (r=-0.64, P < 0.01), suggesting potential niche competition or antagonism between these two groups. Verrucomicrobia contained 3 genera (Oleiharenicola, Opitutus, Ellin6067) and participated in 15 edges, including 2 intra-phylum positive edges, 1 intra-phylum negative edge, 6 inter-phylum positive edges, and 6 inter-phylum negative edges. Acidobacteria was represented by only one genus (Candidatus solibacter) and participated in 8 edges, including 4 negative edges with Proteobacteria and 2 negative edges with Bacteroidetes, indicating strong antagonistic relationships between Acidobacteria and other dominant phyla. Actinobacteria contained 2 genera (MND1, Nocardioides) and participated in 10 edges, mainly showing positive correlations with Proteobacteria and Bacteroidetes. Cyanobacteria was represented by a single genus (Nostoc_PCC-7107) and participated in 9 edges, including 5 negative edges with Proteobacteria and 2 negative edges with Bacteroidetes (Fig. 6).

The soil bacterial co-occurrence network constructed in this study revealed complex interaction patterns among different phylum-level groups. As the most core phylum in the network, Proteobacteria and its internal synergistic symbiotic relationships may make important contributions to the functional stability of soil ecosystems. The antagonistic interactions between Bacteroidetes and Proteobacteria may reflect their competition for resource utilization, while the negative correlations between Acidobacteria/Cyanobacteria and other dominant phyla suggest that they occupy distinct ecological niches.

The identification of core hub nodes and bridge nodes provides important clues for understanding the stability mechanisms of soil microbial communities, and these key taxa may serve as potential targets for regulating soil microbial community structure and function. Furthermore, the low connectivity of the dominant taxon Incertae_Sedis in the network indicates the existence of numerous independent taxa in soil microbial communities, whose distribution is likely primarily influenced by environmental filtering rather than biotic interactions.

Discussion

Rice allelopathy is a heritable quantitative trait governed by multiple quantitative trait loci (QTLs). Previous mapping efforts have identified various allelopathic QTLs across the rice genome, such as those on chromosomes 2, 3, and 8 derived from a ‘IAC 165’ × ‘CO 39’ population (Jensen et al. 2001). Interestingly, our bioinformatic alignment revealed the major chromosome 2 locus flanking RG25 and RZ318 (approximate genomic interval of 22.0–26.5 Mb) precisely co-localizes with the tandemly duplicated OsPAL cluster (OsPAL2;1–OsPAL2;4) identified here (Fig. S5; Table S2). Given that functional divergence between allelopathic and non-allelopathic varieties is highly evident at the regulatory level, this genomic overlap suggests that the OsPAL cluster acts as the primary molecular driver of chromosome 2-mediated rice allelopathy. Promoter activity assays indicate that the OsPAL2;3 promoter in ‘PI312777’ drives significantly higher transcriptional output than its counterpart in Lemont (Li et al. 2021), suggesting cis-regulatory evolution as a mechanism underlying enhanced expression.

In this study, transgenic rice line ‘PI312777’ engineered to individually overexpress four distinct members of the OsPAL gene family exhibited enhanced allelopathic inhibitory activity against barnyard grass (Echinochloa crus-galli). Consistently, within the genetic background of the non-allelopathic cultivar ‘Lemont’, ectopic expression of OsPAL2;3 was sufficient to dramatically augment weed-suppressive capacity, further underscoring its functional significance. These results collectively indicate that different OsPAL paralogs exert differential and context-dependent control over allelochemical biosynthesis in both ‘PI312777’ and ‘Lemont’, with OsPAL2;1 and OsPAL2;3 emerging as key drivers of metabolic reprogramming toward allelopathy.

Overexpression of OsPAL2;1 and OsPAL2;3 led to a marked upregulation in the biosynthesis of several phenolic acid-based allelochemicals in leaf tissues of ‘PI312777’. Crucially, these compounds also accumulated at significantly higher levels in root exudates, indicating not only increased synthesis but also active secretion into the rhizosphere. This dual accumulation pattern supports the hypothesis that elevated PAL activity enhances carbon flux through the phenylpropanoid pathway, thereby boosting both intracellular production and extracellular release of bioactive phenolics. The observed metabolic shifts are consistent with an amplified phenylalanine metabolic flux, likely achieved through coordinated transcriptional regulation of downstream enzymes involved in phenolic metabolism.

The phenylpropanoid biosynthetic cascade is initiated by three consecutive enzymatic reactions: first, PAL catalyzes the deamination of L-phenylalanine to form trans-cinnamic acid; second, cinnamate 4-hydroxylase (C4H) hydroxylates cinnamic acid to yield p-coumaric acid; and third, 4-coumarate: CoA ligase (4CL) activates p-coumaric acid to form p-coumaroyl-CoA, the central branchpoint intermediate feeding into multiple downstream pathways, including lignin, flavonoids, stilbenes, and simple phenolic acids (Dong and Lin 2021). Given its position at the entry point of this network, PAL functions as a rate-limiting and gatekeeping enzyme, effectively controlling the partitioning of carbon from primary to specialized metabolism. Its activity thus has far-reaching implications for plant defense, stress adaptation, and interplant competition via allelopathy.

Transcript abundance of the target OsPAL member genes (OsPAL2;1–OsPAL2;4) was significantly upregulated in their respective positive transgenic overexpression lines relative to the WT. Intriguingly, non-target OsPAL paralogs also displayed divergent transcriptional fluctuations within these overexpression backgrounds. These differential expression dynamics are highly likely attributable to the functional redundancy and complex feedback loops inherent to the OsPAL multigene family (Zhang and Liu 2015). However, the divergent magnitudes of allelopathic enhancement elicited by each paralog, particularly the superior efficacy of OsPAL2;1 and OsPAL2;3, also point to subfunctionalization or neofunctionalization in their regulatory influence over the phenylpropanoid network, possibly due to differences in promoter architecture, protein stability, or interaction partners.

Moreover, OsPAL overexpression functioned as a pathway-wide molecular trigger, accelerating the transcription of downstream phenolic acid biosynthetic genes within the phenylpropanoid pathway. Notably, transgenic ‘Lemont’ lines overexpressing OsPAL2;3 displayed pronounced transcriptional activation of essential pathway components, specifically C4H, 4CL, OMT, CCA, and CAD. Similarly, a parallel phenomenon was observed in the transgenic ‘PI312777’ background, where OsPAL2;1 overexpression consistently upregulated the aforementioned loci in the root system compared with the WT (Fig. S6).

The enhanced allelopathic capacity of rice against co-occurring weeds is mechanistically rooted in the transcriptional activation of genes within the phenolic acid biosynthesis pathway, particularly those embedded in the core phenylpropanoid metabolic network. Xiong et al. (2010) demonstrated that seven genes involved in phenolic acid biosynthesis exhibited a markedly higher magnitude of transcriptional upregulation in ‘PI312777’ than in ‘Lemont’ under abiotic stress, underscoring the superior metabolic plasticity and inducible responsiveness intrinsic to allelopathic accessions. This upregulation drives increased synthesis and root-mediated exudation of phenolic acid allelochemicals into the rhizosphere, thereby elevating their bioactive concentrations in the soil microenvironment.

A key insight emerging from recent studies is the existence of specialized microbial taxa capable of metabolic interactions with phenolic substrates, which can potentiate their allelopathic efficacy. In this study, transgenic overexpression of OsPAL2 resulted in significant transcript accumulation and correspondingly elevated rhizospheric levels of phenolic acid allelochemicals. Among these OsPAL-overexpressing lines, concomitant restructuring of the rhizosphere microbial community, both alpha diversity and overall abundance of dominant microbial lineages were significantly changed, demonstrating that modulation of OsPAL expression can effectively reprogram the rhizosphere microbiome toward a more suppressive and resilient state. Crucially, overexpression of OsPAL2;1 and OsPAL2;3 not only restored but markedly enhanced Myxococcota abundance relative to wild-type controls, coinciding with higher phenolic acid accumulation and superior weed-suppressive ability. Further support for this model comes from prior experimental data showing that co-application of ferulic acid and Myxococcus xanthus results in significantly greater inhibition of E. crus-galli growth than either treatment alone (Fang et al. 2015), highlighting the synergistic nature of allelochemical–microbe interactions. In contrast, overexpression of OsPAL2;2 and OsPAL2;4 had minimal impact on total Myxococcota abundance, suggesting functional divergence among OsPAL paralogs, potentially due to differences in spatiotemporal expression patterns, substrate affinities, or channeling of metabolic intermediates into distinct downstream branches.

Additional experimental evidence derives from exogenous phenolic acid amendment studies, which demonstrate that targeted application of specific compounds can induce a dose- and time-dependent potentiation of allelopathic activity in wild rice accession S37 (Xu et al. 2010). Lin (2011) further reported that phenolic acid inputs enhance multiple indicators of soil biological health, including microbial biomass carbon (MBC), microbial respiration (MBR), population density, and enzymatic activities (e.g., dehydrogenase, urease, phosphatase), while also improving the bioavailability of essential nutrients such as nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium. These edaphic enhancements collectively foster a more favorable biogeochemical environment that indirectly promotes plant vigor and nutrient acquisition, while simultaneously intensifying allelopathic pressure on neighboring weeds, an elegant example of how plant chemistry can engineer its own rhizospheric niche for competitive advantage.

Conclusion

The upregulation of specific PAL paralogs in rice, particularly OsPAL2;1 and OsPAL2;3, enhances allelopathic potential and increases weed growth suppression. The functional divergence of OsPAL2;1 and OsPAL2;3 relative to other family members indicates a specialized role in mediating allelopathy, potentially driven by distinct spatiotemporal expression patterns or substrate utilization efficiencies. These findings demonstrate the feasibility of genetic strategies to bolster natural defense mechanisms in cereals, suggesting that OsPAL-targeted breeding or genome editing represents a potential strategy for developing weed-competitive rice cultivars for sustainable agriculture.

Materials and Methods

Plant Materials

The rice genotypes used in this study included the internationally recognized allelopathic cultivar ‘PI312777’ and the non-allelopathic cultivar ‘Lemont’ (Oryza sativa L.) (Dilday et al. 2000; Fang et al. 2015; Xie et al. 2023). Barnyardgrass (Echinochloa crus-galli L.) was employed as the receptor species for allelopathy bioassays. All plant materials were maintained at the Fujian Key Laboratory of Agroecological Processing and Safety Monitoring (Fujian Agriculture and Forestry University).

Generation of OsPAL Gene Overexpression Lines in Rice

Referring to the method described Li et al. (2021), the CDS of OsPAL2;1 (Os02g0626100), OsPAL2;2 (Os02g0626400), OsPAL2;3 (Os02g0626600), and OsPAL2;4 (Os02g0627100) genes from allelopathic rice ‘PI312777’ were cloned using KOD-Plus high-fidelity DNA polymerase (TOYOBO). The Infusion HD Cloning Kit (Clontech) was employed to ligate each OsPAL member gene into the p2300-35s-eYFP vector, constructing recombinant vectors designated as p2300-35s-OsPAL-eYFP. These constructs were introduced into rice cultivars ‘PI312777’ and ‘Lemont’ via Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain EHA105-mediated genetic transformation (Elakhdar et al. 2021; Luu et al. 2020). Rice lines transformed with the empty vector p2300-35s-eYFP served as the control. Positive transgenic lines were screened, and seeds from these lines were harvested for subsequent analysis.

Identification and Screening of Positive Transgenic Rice Lines

Seeds of the wild-type rice cultivars ‘PI312777’ and ‘Lemont’, along with their respective transgenic derivatives, were subjected to surface sterilization using a 25% (v/v) sodium hypochlorite solution for 30 min, followed by thorough washing with sterile distilled water through ten repeated rinses to eliminate residual bleach. Sterilized seeds were subsequently incubated overnight at 37 °C in a temperature-controlled incubator to promote uniform and accelerated germination via moisture conditioning. Germinated seeds were transferred to hydroponic culture trays supplemented with rice culture solution, which was a minor optimization of the composition of Yoshida et al. (1976), and cultivated in a climate-controlled greenhouse under natural photoperiod conditions (day/night cycle approximately 14/10 h) at 28~ 30 °C. The nutrient solution was renewed every 2 days to maintain optimal mineral availability and prevent microbial contamination.

Rice seedlings were grown for 10 ~14 days until they reached the three-leaf stage (approximately 8~10 cm in height), at which point the penultimate fully expanded leaf from each plant was harvested, immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent genomic DNA extraction. The isolated genomic DNA was used as a template for PCR amplification targeting the transgene cassette, with wild-type ‘PI312777’ and ‘Lemont’ genomic DNA serving as negative controls.

Quantitative Analysis of OsPAL Gene Expression in Transgenic Rice

Total RNA was isolated from the leaves of transgenic and wild-type rice ‘PI312777’ using the TRIzol reagent method, followed by DNase I treatment to eliminate genomic DNA contamination. Approximately 1 µg of high-quality RNA was reverse-transcribed into first-strand cDNA using a PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara Bio.) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

To investigate the transcriptional profiles of the nine members of the OsPAL paralogs and downstream phenolic acid biosynthetic genes, qRT-PCR was performed on cDNA templates derived from OsPAL-overexpressing lines, mutant genotypes, and wild-type controls. The rice OsActin gene (Os03g0718100) was used as an internal reference for data normalization due to its stable expression across tissues and treatments. Gene-specific primers (listed in Table S3) were designed to span exon-exon junctions where possible to avoid amplification of residual genomic DNA. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the comparative 2^−ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001), with biological triplicates and technical duplicates performed for all samples.

Graphical visualization and statistical analysis were carried out using GraphPad Prism 5. Significant differences in gene expression were assessed by one-way ANOVA or Student’s t-test, as appropriate (P < 0.05).

Delayed Co-culture of Barnyardgrass and Rice to Evaluate Allelopathic Potential

Positive rice transformants were selected for hydroponic cultivation in a complete nutrient solution. Pre-germinated transgenic rice seeds and their corresponding wild-type controls (‘P312777’ and ‘Lemont’) were sown in containers at a density of 10 seeds per pot. Upon reaching the three-leaf stage, pre-germinated barnyardgrass (Echinochloa crus-galli) was introduced into the pots to establish a 1:1 rice-to-barnyardgrass co-culture system. Additionally, 20 barnyardgrass plants were grown in isolation as a mono-culture control. The co-culture experiment was conducted at the green house for a duration of 14 days. For each treatment, intact barnyardgrass samples were harvested to measure plant height and root length. To determine biomass, the samples were first subjected to heat treatment at 105 °C for 30 min for enzymatic inactivation, followed by oven-drying at 80 °C for 3 days until a constant dry weight was achieved. The inhibitory rate (IR) of rice exudates on barnyardgrass growth was calculated as follows: IR = (1 - TR/CK) × 100%, where TR and CK represent the growth parameters of the treatment and control groups, respectively. Statistical analyses, including correlation and regression analyses, were performed using DPS software. Significant differences were determined using the Least Significant Difference (LSD) test at a significance level of P < 0.05.

Determination of Phenolic Acid Content in Transgenic Rice Tissues and Root Exudates by HPLC

Transgenic rice lines and their corresponding WT controls were cultivated hydroponically in rice culture solution until reaching the 5- to 7-leaf vegetative stage. At harvest, the penultimate and antepenultimate leaves, root tissues, and continuously collected root exudates (500 mL over a 14-day period) were sampled for phenolic acid analysis.

Eight phenolic acid standards, including protocatechuic acid, p-hydroxybenzoic acid, vanillic acid, syringic acid, salicylic acid, cinnamic acid, p-coumaric acid, and ferulic acid, were employed for calibration and identification. Stock solutions were prepared at 1 mg/mL for seven compounds, while salicylic acid was dissolved at 5 mg/L due to its lower solubility and higher detection sensitivity. Serial dilutions yielded working concentrations across a broad dynamic range, with 10 ~ 1000 µg/mL for salicylic acid and 2.5 ~ 500 µg/mL for other phenolics.

Seven mixed standard solutions spanning these concentration ranges were freshly prepared, filtered through 0.22 μm membranes, and analyzed in triplicate to generate external calibration curves. Peak areas versus concentrations were plotted, and linear regression models (typically R² > 0.995) were established for each compound. The limits of detection (LOD) and quantification (LOQ) were defined by signal-to-noise ratios of S/N = 3 and S/N = 10, respectively, providing evidence for the method’s sensitivity.

These calibration curves were then used to quantify target phenolic acids in rice leaf tissues, root tissues, and root exudates. Compound identification was based on retention time matching with authentic standards ( < ± 0.1 min deviation). All analyses were conducted in biological triplicates to ensure reproducibility and statistical robustness.

Assessment of Rhizosphere Soil Microbial Diversity in Transgenic Rice and Wild-Type Plants

To minimize pre-existing microbial biases, soil without prior rice cultivation was selected, air-dried under sunlight, and passed through a 2-mm sieve to remove debris and large aggregates. An aliquot of pre-sterilized vermiculite was thoroughly mixed with the sieved soil at an optimized ratio to improve aeration and water retention. Basal fertilization was applied according to a standardized nutrient regime equivalent to N: 225 kg/ha, P₂O₅: 29.5 kg/ha, and K₂O: 149.4 kg/ha, with nutrients uniformly incorporated into the soil mixture. The homogenized substrate was then filled into sterilized plastic pots (7.5 cm × 7.5 cm × 12.5 cm) and pre-moistened by maintaining a 1 ~ 2 cm water layer at the bottom via capillary action to ensure uniform hydration without waterlogging.

Rice seedlings from multiple independent transgenic positive lines and the wild-type cultivar ‘PI312777’ were individually transplanted into separate pots (one plant per pot), with each genotype represented by three biological replicates. After establishment, all plants were grown under controlled greenhouse conditions. At the ‘three-leaf and one-heart’ stage (approximately 14 days after sowing), pre-germinated seeds of barnyard grass were sown adjacent to each rice plant to simulate interspecific competition. Co-cultivation was maintained for 14 days before rhizosphere sampling to allow sufficient root exudate-mediated microbial recruitment.

For negative control samples (bulk soil controls), unplanted pots containing identical soil mixtures were prepared in parallel. A total of 21 rhizosphere and control soil samples were collected. All collected samples were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen to preserve microbial community structure and metabolic activity, followed by storage at − 80 °C until further processing. High-throughput amplicon sequencing targeting the V3–V4 hypervariable region of the bacterial and archaeal 16 S rRNA gene was performed by Beijing Allwegene Technology Co., Ltd., using Illumina MiSeq platform technology.

Raw sequencing data were initially filtered to remove reads shorter than 200 bp, those with low-quality scores (≤ 20), and sequences containing ambiguous bases. Reads that did not exhibit exact matches to primer sequences or barcode tags were also excluded. High-quality (qualified) reads were demultiplexed based on sample-specific barcodes and trimmed using the Illumina Analysis Pipeline (v2.6). Subsequently, the dataset was processed using QIIME. Sequences were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at a 97% similarity threshold (Edgar 2013) to generate rarefaction curves and calculate alpha diversity metrics, including richness and diversity indices. Taxonomic classification was performed using the Ribosomal Database Project (RDP) Classifier (Cole et al. 2009) to assign sequences to specific taxonomic groups.

To assess microbial community similarity across samples, clustering analysis and Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) were conducted in R based on the OTU profiles (Wang et al., 2012b)and the Bray-Curtis distances using the “labdsv” package (Roberts 2026). Beta diversity distances between microbial communities were calculated using the Yue & Clayton (θyc) coefficient and visualized via an Unweighted Pair Group Method with Arithmetic Mean (UPGMA) clustering tree, representing inter-sample dissimilarity (1-similarity) (Jiang et al. 2013). A Newick-formatted tree file was generated from this analysis. Furthermore, to compare community membership and structure, heatmaps were constructed for the top 20 OTUs using Mothur (Jami et al. 2013).
