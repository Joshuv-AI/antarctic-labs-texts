Whole-genome sequencing and comparative genomic analysis of a novel Bacillus subtilis strain YE16 isolated from yak dung
Authors: Dixita Chettri, Anil Kumar Verma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, genomic and functional characterization of the previously identified lignocellulolytic Bacillus sp. strain YE16 from an underrepresented ecological niche (yak dung) in the Sikkim Himalayas was performed—thus making it the first report of such a study of a strain from this region. Whole genome sequencing resolved the ambiguity regarding its taxonomic placement where it showed 98.63% ANI and 89.4% dDDH with the reference genome Bacillus subtilis subsp. subtilis 168, definitively assigning YE16 to B. subtilis. This was further confirmed by core-pangenome SNP based phylogenetic analysis, where the YE16 strain forms a separate branch on the phylogenetic tree, with a core-genome SNP distance of 33,807 relative to strain 168, indicating substantial strain-level genomic differentiation. YE16 encodes 43 strain specific genes with nutrient transportation, metabolism and defense mechanisms as the primary functional groups. The isolate possessed a total of 95 GHs, 83 GTs, 38 CBMs, consistent with the previously demonstrated lignocellulolytic phenotype. 18 biosynthetic gene clusters were identified with expanded lipopeptide-related NRPS clusters, of which 4 showed low similarity (< 50%). A single intact prophage related to Brevibacillus phage Osiris was also detected, augmenting its genomic variability. Preliminary biosafety assessment identified no major virulence-associated determinants or evidence of acquired multidrug resistance within the scope of the study. Introduction

Microorganisms, which are ubiquitous in nature, are the most diverse and adaptable life forms on earth and are found in the most extreme environmental niches, where they play a key ecological role1. Particularly, studying microbial diversity from unique and underrepresented niches and their functional characterization has become a major focus of contemporary microbiology due to its significant contributions to our understanding of ecology, evolution, and biotechnological applications2. Selective pressure in distinct environmental niches can catalyze the emergence of microbial lineages with distinct functional, metabolic, and physiological traits. Thus, exploring these unconventional environments can reveal novel gene clusters and rare metabolic pathways that are absent or underrepresented in microbes, even within the same microbial species, from conventional environments3. These discoveries significantly expand our understanding of the microbial tree of life and uncover functional capabilities that have direct relevance to biotechnological and industrial applications4.

One such environment is yak dung, a highly nutrient-rich hotspot amid low-nutrient conditions5,6. Yak (Bos grunniens), found in high-altitude areas with limited vegetation, is a highly specialized ruminant adapted to these extreme conditions. They have evolved their feeding habits to thrive on sparse, low-quality alpine vegetation, consuming grasses, shrubs, and other plants. They have high metabolic efficiency and use fibrous material to meet their nutrient requirements. Accordingly, their microflora has evolved for highly efficient utilization of lignocellulosic biomass7,8. Given the growing significance of biofuels amid rising energy demand and depleting fossil fuels, research focused on identifying highly efficient microbial enzyme systems for the bioconversion of lignocellulose-rich biomass has emerged in recent years9. Metagenomic studies on yak rumen and dung reflects large repertoire of Carbohydrate Active Enzymes (CAZymes), i.e., the group of enzymes that are involved in carbohydrate biosynthesis, transformation, and metabolism of carbohydrates10,11. These studies were mostly restricted to the Tibetan Plateau region, with our group being the first to report isolation and characterization of lignocellulose-degrading bacteria from the Yak dung of the Sikkim Himalayas12. The isolate Bacillus sp. YE16 was confirmed to belong to the genus Bacillus, but its species-level identity could not be resolved by 16S rRNA gene sequencing.

The genus Bacillus is among the most extensively studied bacterial genera, with high environmental resilience and metabolic diversity. They have also been extensively used in industrial applications as well as other biotechnological studies13. In the context of yak dung, they have found to play a vital role in ecological balance by facilitating the biodegradation of lignocellulose for nutrient cycling in the harsh, high-altitude environment14 as well as acting as a reservoir for novel, cold-adapted probiotic strains15. To resolve the species ambiguity of strain YE16 and further understand its functional placement, given its unique source of isolation, this study performed whole-genome sequencing of the isolate, followed by comparative analysis with reference genomes of the same species from other diverse habitats. Further, the existing literature has limited studies on yak-associated microflora, with only one WGS study for yak-rumen-derived B. subtilis isolates16. Our study bridges this gap by providing a comprehensive genomic analysis of the isolate YE16.

Results

Genome assembly, quality assessment and annotation of strain YE16

A draft genome assembly with a total length of 4,200,901 bp and a GC content of 44.08% was obtained from whole-genome sequencing of strain YE16. It consisted of 450 contigs, with the largest measuring 991,981 bp (Fig. 1). Assembly contiguity was supported by an N50 value of 574,390 bp and an L50 of 3, indicating that half of the genome was contained within three contigs. CheckM analyses yielded a genome completeness of 100% while BUSCO (Benchmarking Universal Single Copy Orthologs) reported 99.78% completeness with minimal contamination (0.02%), confirming the assembly’s reliability for subsequent comparative genomic analyses. Detailed assembly and quality statistics are provided in Table 1 and Supplementary Table S1. 4,018 protein-coding sequences, including 3 rRNA, 71 tRNA and a single tmRNA, were predicted from automated genome annotation using the Prokka tool. Of the predicted coding sequences, about 28.8% were annotated to encode hypothetical proteins.

Species identification and taxonomic placement of strain YE16

Genome-based taxonomic classification of strain YE16 revealed that it belongs to the species Bacillus subtilis (Bacillaceae). The B. subtilis reference genome GCF_000009045.1 was identified as the closest reference match based on average nucleotide identity (ANI) of 98.63%. Similarly, independent ANI comparisons between YE16 and Bacillus type strains from the NCBI RefSeq database gave ANI values above the accepted species-level threshold value for B. subtilis species. This was further supported by digital DNA-DNA hybridization (dDDH) analysis, where YE16 showed dDDH values of 89.4%, 89.4%, and 87.1% with the B. subtilis reference strains (B. subtilis 168, NCIB 3610, and NARUSE, respectively), which is well above the accepted threshold of 70% for species delineation. These analyses together confirm the taxonomic assignment of strain YE16, as suggested by the GTDB-Tk classification (Table 2).

Pangenome analysis of Bacillus sp. YE16

To understand its structural position relative to other B. subtilis genomes, we compared the general genomic features of our isolate YE16, with those of the selected 25 reference genomes. The genome structure of strain YE16 was similar to that of other Bacillus subtilis strains with minor differences owing to strain-level differences rather than species-level separation (Table 3). Pan-genome analysis of YE16 was also performed, along with the selected 25 reference B. subtilis genomes, to assess genetic variation within the B. subtilis species. A total of 8,685 gene clusters were identified, demonstrating considerable genomic variation within the species, which were further classified into core (3,370 families, 38.8%), soft core (114 families, 1.3%), shell (1,336 families, 15.4%), and cloud (3,865 families, 44.5%) genes (Fig. 2a). The large number of accessory genes (shell + cloud) relative to core genes in the pan-genome suggests a genetically diverse, open B. subtilis pan-genome that reflects the ecological flexibility of B. subtilis. A large cloud gene composition suggests that gene gain and loss events have been occurring continuously across strains occupying diverse environments. In this context, strain YE16 had the same basic structure as other B. subtilis strains and contributed to the accessory gene pool, supporting its placement within a diverse but coherent species pan-genome. 43 accessory genes were found to be strain‑specific to strain YE16, reflecting strain-specific genomic features that could contribute to adaptation to this strain’s ecological niche (Fig. 2b). However, functional validation (e.g., transcriptomics or gene knockouts) would be required to confirm their ecological roles. Further, Heaps’ law model yielded an estimated exponent (α) of 0.275 with R² = 0.89 (Fig. 2c), confirming the openness of the pangenome and suggesting that additional B. subtilis genomes would continue to reveal novel gene families. This finding is in agreement with known genetic flexibility shown by B. subtilis for adaptation to diverse environments.

Core-genome-SNP based phylogenetic analysis

In order to accurately determine the evolutionary relationships of 26 B. subtilis genomes, a maximum-likelihood phylogeny was created based on core-genome SNP information. A final alignment file was created that used the filtered core-gene alignment of 129,417 parsimony-informative locations. Phylogenomic analysis confirmed that isolate YE16 is B. subtilis but diverged at the strain level from the reference isolates, as evidenced by its placement on a separate terminal branch on the tree with 100% bootstrap support (Fig. 3). Pairwise core-genome comparison between YE16 and the reference strain Bacillus subtilis 168 identified 33,807 SNP differences, further supporting genomic differentiation of YE16 at the strain level despite clear species-level assignment based on ANI and dDDH. YE16 forms a sister lineage with gut/rumen (CRN1 and related) isolates, indicating a closer evolutionary affinity with those strains than with other soil- or plant-associated strains, which are located further along the tree. However, the genetic distance between YE16 and its sister lineages is comparable to other intra-strain genetic distances for other B. subtilis strains, thereby providing additional support for its designation as a distinct strain rather than a separate taxon. Thus, ecological mapping of YE16 as a distinct lineage, with an independent branch relative to other gut-associated B. subtilis lineages, supports the inference that YE16 has some recent common ancestry with, or convergent characteristics relative to, gut– or rumen–associated B. subtilis, while still exhibiting strain‑specific genomic sequence characters.

Functional categorization of core and strain-specific genes

Functional categorization of the three different gene sets, i.e., core, accessory, and strain-specific, from the YE16 genome was done using COG annotations exported from eggNOG-mapper. Distinct functional profiles were observed across the three gene sets, reflecting differing levels of evolutionary conservation and potential for adaptation (Fig. 4). Compared to the other two gene sets, the core genome contained more genes related to vital cellular functions, with amino acid transport and metabolism genes accounting for the largest share (9.63%) among known-function genes. This category, along with genes associated with transcription, carbohydrate transport and metabolism, energy production and conversion, and inorganic ion transport and metabolism, accounts for 38.28% of the core genes, while 26.32% of the genes are of unknown function. Only a small proportion of the core genes (4.46%) have no COG assignment, indicating that many of these core gene functions are highly conserved and well known. In contrast, the accessory genome exhibited far more functional variation than the core genome, with a much higher proportion of accessory genes (14.10%) lacking COG assignments. Accessory genes were enriched for functions involving cell wall, membrane and envelope biogenesis (11.75%), transcription (11.23%), carbohydrate transport and metabolism (8.88), and signal transduction mechanisms (3.92%), indicating roles in both regulatory control and response to environmental stimuli. Among the 43 unique genes, several striking enrichments were observed. Compared to the core and accessory genes, functions related to carbohydrate, amino acid, and lipid metabolism, as well as secondary metabolite biosynthesis and defense mechanisms, were more prominent within the annotated gene set (33.34% and 5.13%, respectively). This may indicate the potential for specialized adaptive traits specific to YE16, particularly the elevated proportion of carbohydrate metabolism genes, which is particularly notable, as it may reflect adaptation to the plant-rich diet of the yak. Similarly, the enrichment of defense and secondary metabolite genes suggests that YE16 may possess enhanced capabilities for competition within the gut microbiome.

The COG-based analysis revealed distinct functional stratification of the YE16 pangenome, with conserved housekeeping functions concentrated in the core genome while adaptive, regulatory and poorly characterized functions increasingly represented in accessory and strain-specific gene sets.

Analysis of CAZymes, biosynthetic gene clusters, and mobile elements in YE16

dbCAN analysis revealed that YE16 encodes a diverse repertoire of carbohydrate-active enzymes (251), including multiple GHs, GTs, and CBMs. A large number of Glycoside Hydrolase (GH) and Carbohydrate Binding Module (CBM) families were identified, which may reflect the ability of Bacillus subtilis YE16 to metabolize a wide variety of complex polysaccharides. In addition, the number of GT families present (83) is among the highest found for YE16, while GH repertoire includes families associated with cellulose (GH5, GH9), hemicellulose (GH11, GH43) and pectin (GH28) degradation, consistent with its isolation from fiber-rich yak dung. Comparison of the CAZyme profile of YE16 with other Bacillus subtilis strains from the pangenome analysis indicated a profile similar to that of strains isolated from plants and the environment, but contrasting with that of host-associated isolates (Fig. 5). This pattern indicates that YE16 retains a substantial polysaccharide-processing potential consistent with nutrient-rich, plant biomass–containing environments such as dung.

antiSMASH analysis identified 18 putative biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) in YE16 (Table 4). Comparative analysis of its BGC content with 25 reference B. subtilis strains revealed a conserved core cluster that included bacillibactin, subtilosin A, bacilysin, and bacillaene (100% similarity). However, YE16 exhibits variations primarily within accessory lipopeptide-associated NRPS clusters, where it harbours a fengycin cluster (80%), surfactin-like regions (43% and 8%), plipastatin fragments (30%, 30%, 15%), and a paenibacterin-like region (60%).

Biosafety assessment of strain YE16

The potential safety concerns of strain YE16 were evaluated using a combination of genomic screening of antibiotic resistance genes and virulence determinants, along with a phenotypic antibiotic susceptibility study.

Genome-based biosafety screening via CARD analysis identified resistance-associated determinants in YE16, predominantly corresponding to intrinsic Bacillus-associated efflux systems and housekeeping resistance-related proteins rather than acquired resistance elements (Table S4). These included aadK, tet(45), vmlR, mphK, FosBx1, tmrB and the small multidrug resistance efflux pump components ykkC and ykkD. Additionally, a class A beta-lactamase gene (BcI, 66.0% identity) and partial glycopeptide resistance gene fragments (vanY, vanW, vanT) with low percent identity (< 40%) were also detected but were not considered definitive evidence of functional resistance. No significant matches to known virulence-associated genes were detected using VFDB screening.

Preliminary phenotypic antibiotic susceptibility profiling was performed using the disc diffusion assay. As no CLSI breakpoints for Bacillus subtilis were available, zone diameters are reported descriptively without categorical interpretation (Table 5). Differential inhibition responses were observed across the tested antibiotics, with comparatively smaller inhibition zones for streptomycin, gentamicin, tetracycline, vancomycin, and ceftazidime, and larger inhibition zones for ampicillin, chloramphenicol, erythromycin, norfloxacin, and ciprofloxacin (Figure S1).

The phenotypic resistances observed were largely consistent with genomic predictions for most antibiotics, particularly for tetracycline- and aminoglycoside-associated determinants. The only discrepancy was the presence of mphK (macrolide resistance gene) with phenotypic susceptibility to erythromycin, which may indicate condition-dependent gene expression, regulatory effects, or non-functional homologous matches. All detected resistance genes are intrinsic to Bacillus subtilis and not associated with mobile genetic elements. No resistance genes were detected for the antibiotics that produced large zones (ampicillin, chloramphenicol, gentamicin, norfloxacin, ciprofloxacin). Vancomycin results were inconclusive because disk diffusion is not reliable for glycopeptide resistance and the detected van fragments had very low identity. Overall, the genomic and phenotypic data indicate that YE16 does not carry acquired antibiotic resistance genes or virulence factors, supporting its safety for biotechnological applications.

Discussion

Yak-associated microflora, including dung, is an underrepresented ecological niche with most studies confined to the Tibetan Plateau. Our previous work showed that strain YE16 isolated from yak dung in Sikkim is a lignocellulolytic Bacillus isolate capable of cellulase production and bioethanol synthesis from lignocellulosic biomass12. However, 16S rRNA sequencing could not resolve its species identity due to limited resolution among closely related Bacillus species17,18. This study resolves the ambiguity regarding the species-level identification of the Bacillus isolate YE16 and further analyzes its functional potential. Though other conserved genes like the gyrB and recA have been shown to provide a higher resolution when used as phylogenetic markers19,20, WGS was preferred in this study since it provides superior resolution by capturing genome-wide variation for a strain from an underrepresented niche21.

Genome assembly and annotation show that YE16 is a high-quality draft genome with genomic characteristics consistent with previously reported environmental and host-associated B. subtilis strains22–24. ANI (98.63%) and dDDH (89.4%) values relative to B. subtilis 168 confirmed species level assignment, resolving the earlier ambiguity. However, the distinct phylogenomic placement of YE16 together with a core-genome SNP distance of 33,807 relative to B. subtilis 168 indicates substantial strain-level genomic divergence despite species-level conservation. This is consistent with the ecological versatility and genomic plasticity known for B. subtilis28, as supported by the observed open pangenome structure (Heaps’ law α = 0.275). It should be noted, however, that the observed phylogenetic proximity of YE16 to gut/rumen isolates may partly reflect sampling bias in public databases rather than definitive ecological specialization. Together, these findings place YE16 within the known genomic framework of B. subtilis while supporting it recognition as a genomically differentiated strain with distinct accessory genomic features.

Functional categorization of core, accessory, and strain-specific genes revealed expected differences in genomic conservation and variability. While core genes were enriched for essential cellular processes, accessory genes, including strain-specific genes, were predominantly hypothetical proteins and genes associated with membrane functions and regulation, transport, metabolism, and defense-related functions. This is consistent with previous reports that accessory genomes contribute to fine-scale ecological and metabolic diversification rather than driving major evolutionary divergence25,26. (Table S2). Comparative genomic analysis indicated that, although YE16 shares the conserved genomic framework of Bacillus subtilis, the presence of strain-specific genes linked to nutrient acquisition and defense may reflect lineage-specific adaptive potential in response to selective pressures encountered across variable environmental habitats, though direct ecological relevance would require experimental validation.

Comparative analysis revealed a more extensive CAZyme profile in YE16 than in other gut-, dung-, or rumen-associated isolates. Notably, YE16 has an expanded CAZymes range compared to yak-associated B. subtilis K35-129, particularly GHs and GTs (Fig. 5). Despite their shared host-associated origin, this divergence suggests strain-level functional specialization in carbohydrate utilization capability, potentially reflecting differences in substrate exposure across host-associated microenvironments. Importantly, these findings align with the previously reported lignocellulolytic potential of YE16 hydrolyzing carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC), xylan, and locust bean gum, along with effective saccharification of lignocellulosic biomass for downstream ethanol production12. The presence of multiple families involved in cellulose, hemicellulose and pectin degradation provides a genotype-phenotype link for lignocellulose conversion.

Detection of multiple highly variable NRPS clusters associated with lipopeptides (e.g., fengycin, surfactin-like, and plipastatin), in addition to the core cluster, suggests the potential for diverse bioactive metabolite production. Detection of a low-similarity (< 50%) cluster suggests biosynthetic divergence or may represent an incomplete biosynthetic cluster; however, experimental validation and manual curation would be required before inferring novelty. Subtilosin A was identified as the primary RiPP-encoded antimicrobial peptide, while the presence of the ComQXPA quorum-sensing system suggests regulatory capacity for secondary metabolism. A single intact prophage (Brevibacillus phage Osiris) contributes to genomic plasticity30, and the absence of degraded prophage remnants indicates a streamlined mobile element landscape compared to laboratory strains such as B. subtilis 168.

The preliminary biosafety assessment of YE16, combining genomic screening and phenotypic antibiotic response profiling, did not reveal major biosafety concerns within the scope of the present study. Genomic screening (CARD) identified resistance determinants commonly associated with the intrinsic Bacillus resistome (e.g., aadK, tet(45), ykkC/D), and no virulence factors were detected (VFDB). For instance, a comparative genomic study of a B. subtilis strain from the Southern Ocean identified a nearly identical set of intrinsic ARGs, including aadK, ykkC, ykkD, tet(45), FosBx1, vanT, and qac family genes27. This concordance across geographically and ecologically distant isolates confirms that the resistome detected in YE16 is not an acquired or strain-specific anomaly but rather a characteristic feature of the B. subtilis species. Phenotypic disc diffusion showed general concordance, with the sole discrepancy being presence of mphK (macrolide resistance) in the genome but phenotypic study showing erythromycin susceptibility, suggesting a non-functional or cryptic gene. The absence of acquired resistance genes and virulence factors indicates no immediate safety concern, although standardized MIC testing would strengthen the assessment for biotechnological applications.

The draft assembly (450 contigs) results from short-read sequencing alone; however, the high completeness metrics (CheckM 100%, BUSCO 99.78%) support the reliability of our comparative analyses, including taxonomic assignment, pangenome analysis, and functional annotation. Ecological interpretations are inferred from genome annotation and comparative bioinformatic prediction; while these were partially supported by prior experimental evidence for lignocellulose degradation, direct validation of gene expression, enzyme contribution, and regulatory behavior under defined conditions remains necessary.

Overall, this study provides the first comparative genomic characterization of YE16, a previously functionally validated lignocellulolytic Bacillus isolate recovered from yak dung in the Sikkim Himalayas. The combination of species-level taxonomic assignment, strain-level genomic differentiation (33,807 SNPs, distinct phylogeny), an expanded CAZyme repertoire relative to a yak-rumen isolate (K35-1), and strain-specific accessory gene content supports the recognition of YE16 as a genomically distinct strain of B. subtilis, resolving the earlier ambiguity. Further integration with prior experimental evidence strengthens the link between genomic functional potential and observed lignocellulosic biomass conversion capability. The genomic resources generated here expand the representation of Bacillus from underrepresented environments and provide a foundation for future functional and biotechnological studies27.

Materials & methods

Isolation, whole-genome sequencing, genome assembly, & annotation

DNA was isolated from a pure culture of Bacillus sp. YE16 via the CTAB method31. The quality and quantity of the isolated DNA were then evaluated by gel electrophoresis and NanoDrop 2000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). The Roche KAPA Hyperplus Kit was used for sequencing library preparation, and the Bioanalyzer (Agilent) and Qubit (Thermo Fisher) were used to evaluate library quality. Finally, Paired-end sequencing (2 × 150 bp) was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using the NovaSeq 6000 S4 reagent kit V1.5.

The FastQC tool (v 0.12.1)32 was used to check the quality of the raw sequence, after which FASTP (v 0.23.4) was used for adapter removal, and low-quality reads removal33. SPAdes assembler (v 4.0.0) was used for de novo assembly of the high-quality reads34. Assembly quality metrics, including genome size, GC content, contig statistics, and N50 values, were assessed using QUAST (v 5.3.0)35 while genome completeness and contamination were evaluated using CheckM (v 1.1.0)36. Genome completeness was additionally assessed using BUSCO (v 5.8.0) with the bacillales_odb10 dataset37 and the Prokka tool (1.14.6) was used for genome annotation38.

Genome-based taxonomic identification and species confirmation

To determine the phylogenetic and taxonomic position of YE16, species identification was performed using the GTDB-Tk toolkit (v 2.5.2)39 with further confirmation by calculating the Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI) of YE16 against Bacillus type strains from NCBI RefSeq using the FastANI (v1.33) tool with default parameters. ≥95% ANI threshold was applied for species delineation40. Additionally, digital DNA-DNA hybridization (dDDH) analysis was performed using the Type (Strain) Genome server (TYGS, DSMZ) (https://tygs.dsmz.de/), in which the Genome-to-Genome Distance Calculator (GGDC) was used with default settings. Since YE16 is a draft genome assembly, the d4 formula recommended for this type of genome was used, with a dDDH threshold of ≥ 70% for species delineation41.

Pangenome analysis of Bacillus sp. YE16 against B. subtilis strains

To further understand the genomic position of the isolate YE16 taken from yak dung within the B. subtilis species, a comparative pangenome analysis was done against other B. subtilis strains. The NCBI RefSeq database was referred to, and a total of 25 high-quality assemblies representing diverse ecological sources were selected to minimize redundancy while retaining phylogenetic breadth within B. subtilis (Table S3). QUAST(v 5.3.0)35 and CheckM (v 1.1.0)36 were run against all genomes to confirm their quality, followed by genome annotation using the Prokka tool with identical parameters and quality filtering was performed to ensure consistency in gene prediction and functional annotation prior to comparative analysis.

Pangenome reconstruction was carried out in strict mode using Panaroo (v 1.6.0) to minimize annotation and assembly artifacts42. Default clustering parameters were used, with a core-genome threshold of 95% to define core genes. Gene clusters were classified into core, soft-core, shell, and cloud categories based on their frequency of occurrence across the analyzed genomes. Summary statistics describing pangenome composition were generated by Panaroo, and the full gene presence–absence matrix was retained for downstream analyses. Heaps’ law analysis was done to evaluate the openness of the pangenome, where the presence–absence matrix was used for the generation of a gene accumulation curve43,44. Genomes were randomly permuted (n = 100) to compute the cumulative number of non-redundant gene families, and Heaps’ law (F = κN^α) was fitted to the mean accumulation curve. An exponent α < 1 indicates an open pangenome, whereas α > 1 indicates a closed pangenome.

Core-genome-SNP based phylogenetic analysis

The phylogenetic placement of strain YE16 among the reference Bacillus subtilis genomes included in the pan-genome analysis was assessed using core-genome single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) from the 3,370 single-copy core genes shared across the genomes, with a core-genome threshold of 99%. The concatenated core-gene alignment generated internally by Panaroo using MAFFT was taken, and SNPs were extracted using the tool SNP-sites (v 2.5.1)45. Phylogenetic analysis using a maximum-likelihood approach was performed via the IQ-TREE tool (v 2.4.0) with automatic model selection46 with 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicate branch support. The resulting tree was midpoint-rooted and visualized to assess the evolutionary relationships among strains.

Functional categorization of core and strain-specific genes

To analyze strain-specific genetic adaptations and compare functional differences between conserved and strain-specific genomic elements, protein sequences for the core, accessory, and strain-specific genes identified in the pan-genomic analysis were obtained from the YE16-annotated genome. Functional annotation of the genes was performed using eggNOG-mapper (v5.0.2), with DIAMOND as the search algorithm, with default settings47. The genes were then assigned to Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COGs) to compare functional differences between conserved and strain-specific genomic elements.

Analysis of CAZymes, biosynthetic gene clusters, and mobile elements in YE16

To place the CAZyme repertoire of YE16 in a species-level context, CAZyme annotation was conducted for all 26 genomes using the dbCAN3 pipeline48. Predicted protein sequences from Prokka were taken, and CAZyme families were identified by HMMER searches against the dbCAN HMM database and DIAMOND searches against the CAZy database using default parameters. Annotated CAZymes were classified into major enzyme classes, including glycoside hydrolases (GHs), glycosyltransferases (GTs), polysaccharide lyases (PLs), carbohydrate esterases (CEs), auxiliary activity enzymes (AAs), and carbohydrate-binding modules (CBMs).

Furthermore, antiSMASH (v 6.1.1) was performed on the genome assembly of all 26 genomes for comparative analysis of putative Biosynthetic Gene Clusters (BGCs) present in YE1649. For screening of bacteriocin and related gene clusters, the YE16 genome was submitted to the BAGEL4 webserver with default parameters50. Areas of Interest (AOIs) containing core peptide sequences and associated biosynthetic genes were identified and classified based on the built-in databases. Similarly, phage detection was done by submission of the genome sequence of YE16 to the PHASTER webserver51.

Genomic and phenotypic biosafety assessment

To evaluate the biosafety profile of strain YE16, both genomic and phenotypic screening approaches were performed. The YE16 genome assembly was taken, and putative antibiotic resistance-associated determinants were identified using the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD) through the Resistance Gene Identifier (RGI) pipeline with default parameters (RGI 6.0.5, CARD 4.0.1 https://card.mcmaster.ca/)52. Open reading frame (ORF) prediction was performed using Prodigal, and homolog detection was performed using DIAMOND with strict significance criteria based on CARD-curated bitscore cutoffs. Virulence-associated genes were screened using ABRicate (v 1.4.0)53 against the Virulence Factor Database (VFDB)54, applying minimum thresholds of 80% sequence identity and 70% query coverage.

To study the preliminary phenotypic antibiotic susceptibility profiling, the disc diffusion method was followed55. A bacterial suspension standardized to an OD600 of 0.08 was uniformly spread onto Mueller–Hinton agar plates, followed by placement of antibiotic discs containing ampicillin (25 µg), chloramphenicol (30 µg), streptomycin (10 µg), gentamicin (10 µg), norfloxacin (10 µg), ciprofloxacin (5 µg), vancomycin (30 µg), ceftazidime (30 µg), erythromycin (15 µg), and tetracycline (30 µg). Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, and inhibition zone diameters were measured in millimeters. Due to the absence of CLSI breakpoints specifically for Bacillus subtilis, zone diameters are reported without categorical interpretation.
