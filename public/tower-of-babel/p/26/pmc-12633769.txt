Deep Learning Algorithm Prognosticating Retinal Tears and Detachments From Optical Coherence Tomography
Authors: Anish Salvi, Yeabsira Mesfin, Leo Arnal, Curtis Langlotz, Vinit Mahajan, Chase A. Ludwig
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeOur image classifier prognosticates future retinal tear/retinal detachment (RT/RD) likelihood from optical coherence tomography (OCT) while providing pixel-level explanations of clinical importance.MethodsRT/RD status (International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revision codes) and surgical status (Current Procedural Terminology codes) were determined for OCTs collected from the Stanford Research Repository. An image positive for future RT/RD-related surgery was defined as no RT/RD or surgery prior to the acquisition date and the acquisition date 90 days prior to RT/RD diagnosis or surgery. A negative image had no patient overlap with the positive class, had no RT/RD or surgery indication at any time, and was positive for plaquenil use without toxic maculopathy. A convolutional neural network, Inception-v4, was fine-tuned in a class-stratified fivefold fashion on the data set containing 433 negative patients (1027 images) and 343 positive patients (1027 images). Each fold contained a separate patient cohort. Heatmaps indicating a model's region of focus were generated using gradient-weighted class activation mapping to verify that the model's intuition was consistent with clinical knowledge.ResultsPerformance metrics were collected by averaging across folds. For the test set, the model achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.87, an average precision score of 0.85, and an accuracy of 0.78. Anatomy highlighted in heatmaps described macular biomarkers for RT/RD, including epiretinal membrane presence, vitreomacular traction, degree of myopic tilt, and choroidal thickness.ConclusionsThe binary image classifier accurately identified future RT/RD development from OCTs.Translational RelevanceOur deep learning algorithm highlights biomarkers for patients at high risk for RT/RDs, providing a window for prophylactic treatment to prevent vision loss.

Introduction

Axial myopia is an elongation of the eye, a deformation that stretches and thins the layers of the globe, which increases risk of retinal tears (RTs). RTs allow fluid from the vitreous cavity to enter the subretinal space.1 The defect can culminate in retinal detachment (RD), or separation of the retina from the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE). Given that the RPE helps maintain the light-sensitive photoreceptors of the retina, such damage can result in permanent vision loss.

A retrospective study in Wenzhou, China, indicated that RDs increased from 7.26 per 100,000 cases in 2015 to 10.00 per 100,000 cases in 2019, an increase of 37.74%.2 Data analyzed from the Health and Social Care Information Centre and the UK's Office for National Statistics unveiled that the incidence of RDs increased from 3447 (7/100,000) in 2000 to 10,971 (19.7/100,000) in 2018, an increase of 181.43%.3 The prevalence of RDs is expected to worsen with the global rise in myopia. By a systemic review and meta-analysis of 145 studies with 2.1 million participants, it is estimated that myopia and high myopia (HM) will impact 4758 (50%) and 938 (10%) million people (of the world population), respectively, by the year 2050.4 Already a study of 23,616 participants revealed that myopia and HM impacted 96.5% and 21.61%, respectively, of 19-year-old men in Seoul, South Korea.5 Early identification and expedited intervention are critical in preventing vision loss and blindness.6 In that regard, optical coherence tomography (OCT) can surprisingly, serve as a screening tool for identifying image characteristics indicating RT/RD. As suggested by retrospectively analyzing OCTs with patient detachments, potential biomarkers of RT/RD include retinal thickness, epiretinal membrane (ERM), macular hole/edema, photoreceptor integrity, and choroidal flow.7

We augment the task of disease prognosis in the ophthalmology domain by applying image classification and model explainability techniques to standard-of-care clinical imaging. The aims of our study were to (1) develop a binary classifier that identifies future RT/RD development and (2) provide heatmaps that indicate the classifier's region of focus. Prior models focus on predicting recurrent instances of disease and relying upon fundus imaging. For example, clinical nomograms rely upon manually defined features (axial length, retinal break diameter, type of surgical repair, etc.) to infer redetachment risk.8 Deep learning methods have been proposed to predict late recurrence from pre- and postoperative ultrawide field (UWF) fundus imaging and to automate RD detection from UWF fundus via cascaded systems.9,10 While there has been recent interest in full cube analysis, the five-line raster offers a different perspective on OCT classification. Raster images (1) require less computational power, (2) can be quickly acquired while minimizing the effect of eye movement, and (3) can have higher resolutions than mac cubes.11 We make two significant contributions to the literature: (1) improving timely identification of high-risk patients for RT/RD to allow for prophylactic interventions and (2) explaining the anatomic evidence behind a finding of future RT/RD within an OCT image.

Methods

Image Classification

Image classification refers to a bevy of computational methods that assign a category to an image. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) serve as the de facto approach to image classification. We opted for a CNN over more recent advancements such as vision transformers (ViTs), owing to our smaller sample size and because ViTs require larger amounts of data for strong performance.12,13 The CNN Inception-v4 was employed for the OCT classification task.14 To prevent overparameterization, the Inception architecture was built upon multiscale processing and the Hebbian principle—neurons that fire together, wire together. Inception modules process visual inputs at multiple scales before aggregation, which enables the next stage to similarly extract features at various scales concurrently. Inspired by embeddings, these modules also apply dimension reduction and projection to lessen computational cost.15 Inception-v2 addressed internal covariate shift through batch normalization, while Inception-v3 introduced factorization to further reduce the need for compute resources.16,17 These efforts culminated in Inception-v4, which had a uniform and simplified architecture with more Inception modules than the previous iteration and had similar performance to variations with residual connections.

Model Explainability

Interpretability provides transparency as to why a deep learning model makes a specific decision (i.e., assigning a category to an image). Visual explanations, indicating an algorithm's region of focus, can bolster trust with deep learning technology, especially in the medical field, which will aid clinicians with decision-making (Fig. 1). Explainability can also help identify modes of failure (i.e., why an image was miscategorized), revealing if a wrong answer has an understandable reason. Therefore, we apply gradient-weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM) to produce heatmaps that highlight the important regions within an image with respect to the predicted category. Within a CNN, the fully connected layers lose spatial information stored within the preceding convolutional layers. Therefore, the last convolutional layer stores significant semantic and spatial information. Grad-CAM leverages the gradient from the last convolutional layer to determine importance values for each neuron with respect to the category of interest. The neurons of this layer have been tuned to identify class-specific semantic information.18

The overall workflow of our image classifier, which infers the likelihood of future RT/RD development prior to diagnosis or surgery from an input OCT. The classifier also generates an interpretable heatmap for clinical validation and explanation of potential early-onset biomarkers of disease.

Data Collection

Institutional review board permission was obtained for data set access. OCTs were stored in the Stanford Research Repository (STARR), a clinical data warehouse, and remained secure in a dedicated storage bucket within a protected computing environment. Sourced from Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Jena, Germany, imaging devices, the collected OCTs were five-line raster scans of size 5 × 1024 × 1024 and 5 × 1024 × 4096.

Labeling

The RT/RD status and surgical status were determined for each image. RT/RD status was determined by International Classification of Diseases (ICD) Ninth and Tenth Revision codes, specifically: (1) retinal detachment with retinal break (361.0*, H33.0*), (2) retinal breaks without detachment (361.3*, H33.3*), and (3) retinal detachment (361.9*, H33.8*). Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes indicative of RT/RD-related surgery were scleral buckle (67107), vitrectomy (67108), pneumatic retinopexy (67110), complex repair (67113), cryopexy (67101), laser retinopexy (67105), prophylactic cryopexy (67141), prophylactic laser retinopexy (67145), and laser treatment of retina (67039). Cases of plaquenil use (Z79.899, V58.69) without toxic maculopathy (362.55, H35.38*) were also identified. An image positive for future RT/RD/surgery was defined as (1) no RT/RD or surgery, or an indication of surgery via silicone oil, prior to the time of acquisition, and (2) time of acquisition 90 days prior to the RT/RD diagnosis or RT/RD-related surgical operation (the first available date). A negative image had (1) no patient overlap with the positive class, (2) no indication for RT/RD or surgery at any time, and (3) positive for plaquenil use without toxic maculopathy. After manually reviewing the OCTs and corresponding fundus images, patients who presented for prior RT/RD were removed as a quality check. Several patients with a condition found under the exclusion criteria were removed (Table 1).

Preprocessing

A smaller number of negatively labeled images were taken from the set of negatively labeled images equal to that of the positively labeled images. Each image was processed by (1) taking the center OCT slice (i.e., if the size of the image was 5 × 1024 × 1024, the third slice would be taken to achieve 1 × 1024 × 1024), (2) cropping to the region of interest within a bounding box by eliminating background, (3) interpolation to a standard size of 1 × 299 × 299, and (4) normalization to a mean of 0.50 and a standard deviation of 0.50. As a quality check, each image was verified to be unique. The final data set used for the experiment contained 433 negative patients with 1027 images and 343 positive patients with 1027 images.

Training

As a form of transfer learning, Inception-v4 was pretrained on ImageNet weights and fine-tuned to the OCT imaging data set.19 The data set was segmented into five clusters based on the difference between the date of acquisition and the date of RT/RD diagnosis or surgery. The data set was stratified by cluster, class label, and supplemental disease, including ERM, age-related macular degeneration (AMD), and so on. The five folds were split into 80% training and 20% testing data, where each fold contained a separate patient cohort (Table 2). Steps to mitigate overfitting included (1) early stopping, (2) learning rate scheduler, and (3) image augmentation. Early stopping ensured that the model would stop training if the training loss did not significantly decrease. The learning rate scheduler ensured that if the training loss did not incrementally decrease, the learning rate would decrease. A bevy of image augmentation methods was applied, including flipping, rotation, cropping, shifting, cut-mix, and mix-up.20,21 In sum, five separate models were trained. Each model was fit according to cross-entropy loss.

Interpretability

Grad-CAM served as the mechanism for explainable heatmap generation. For a test set image, the gradients derived from a model's last convolutional layer were extracted and pooled across the channels. The activations were taken from the last convolutional layer. The activations were weighted according to the corresponding pooled gradients by channel. The channels of the activations were averaged to form the image-specific heatmap. The rectified linear unit, a nonlinear activation function defined as f(x) == max(0, x) (ReLU) function was applied to the heatmap to delineate the most valuable semantic and spatial contributions with respect to either the positive or negative class. The heatmap was normalized to a maximum value of 1 and interpolated to the dimensions of the input image. For visualization purposes, the heatmap was passed through a Gaussian filter for smoothing.

Results

Inference

The five separate binary classifiers predicted class probabilities for the positive and negative classes from the OCT images, with the class prediction being the maximum of the probabilities. Performance metrics were collected across each fold at the image level, including precision, negative predictive value, recall, specificity, F1-score, accuracy, average precision score (AP), and receiver operating characteristic area under the curve (ROC-AUC).

Recall measures the ratio of all truly positive cases being correctly predicted as positive, with a higher recall minimizing false negatives to capture all instances of future RT/RD development Equation (3).

Groups

Labels were also grouped by their maximum value within each group for further evaluation. The grouping policies were (1) patient-laterality-acquisition-date, with multiple images specific to the same patient, laterality (right or left eye), and acquisition date (the label shall be the maximum value of all images on the acquisition date and the maximum positive class probability for the same); (2) patient-eye, with multiple images specific to the same patient and laterality (the label shall be the maximum value of all images for that eye across all acquisition dates and the maximum positive class probability for the same); and (3) patient, with multiple images specific to the same patient (the label shall be the maximum value of all images for both eyes across all acquisition dates and the maximum positive class probability for the same) (Fig. 2). As before, training and test set metrics were collected for each binary classifier by averaging across each of the folds (Table 3).

Discussion

The model demonstrated strong classification performance for the test set at the image level despite higher precision and specificity with a comparatively lower negative predictive value and recall. Higher precision and specificity imply lower false positives, and lower negative predictive value and recall implied more false negatives. The model favored the normal class, suggesting it was slightly more cautious when predicting future RT/RD. Despite augmentation strategies and selecting Inception-v4 as per the aforementioned reasons, the model was moderately overfit, as indicated by the training set AP of 0.91 and ROC-AUC of 0.91, while the test set achieved an AP of 0.85 and a ROC-AUC of 0.87. We specifically chose the CNN architecture because CNNs are less prone to overfitting with smaller sample sizes as compared to ViTs. During prototyping, we observed that a RetFound model, underpinned on a ViT, experienced difficulty fitting to our OCT data set for the same image interpretation task.22 This may be a result of CNNs having a stronger inductive bias to extract local spatial features of neighboring image pixels as compared to ViTs, underpinned by finding global, long-range relationships between image patches.12 Utilizing self-supervised learning as a pretext task of a CNN or ViT encoder on our STARR data set for the downstream task of classification may bolster performance. Given the variability of coding practices and scanning technology across institutions, our data set may be subject to bias and limited generalizability because our data, as well as ICD and CPT labeling, were solely obtained from STARR. While the algorithm's performance on OCTs acquired outside of Stanford's clinics is unknown, we hope to obtain an OCT set from an outside institution for external validation.

When reviewing the grouped predictions, the ranking of groups, in terms of test performance of ROC-AUC, was (1) image with a ROC-AUC of 0.87 and an AP of 0.85, (2) patient-laterality-acquisition-date with a ROC-AUC of 0.87 and an AP of 0.85, (3) patient-laterality with a ROC-AUC of 0.86 and an AP of 0.79, and (4) patient with a ROC-AUC of 0.84 and an AP of 0.81. For the patient-laterality-acquisition-date group, the model achieved higher precision and specificity, with a lower negative predictive value and recall for the test set, akin to the image-level performance. For the patient-laterality group, the model achieved a higher negative predictive value and specificity, with lower precision and recall for the test set with fewer false positives and more false negatives. Under this paradigm, the model predicts the normal class well but struggles with future RT/RD. For the patient group, the model achieved a higher negative predictive value and recall, with lower precision and specificity for the test set, which reduces false negatives and increases false positives. This prediction strategy favors future RT/RD cases at the expense of normal cases. Therefore, aggregating predictions across images taken for the various groupings performs objectively worse than the de facto approach of interpreting a single image independently. We suspected that grouping predictions would account for the frame of reference altering slightly (i.e., the depth of the OCT changes between acquisitions) and thus future RT/RD feature discovery. An increase in false positives is expected due to taking the maximum probability prediction per group. While aggregating predictions had no performance improvement, the model's cautiousness toward a specific class can be increased or decreased by grouping.

After computing the algorithm's recall (i.e., true-positive rate) for the positive class of the test set at the image level, we observed that the recall declined as the time to the RT/RD-related diagnosis or related surgery increased, indicating a negative correlation. This observation is expected because the presence of RT/RD biomarkers increases as the time to RT/RD finding decreases, as demonstrated by the recall rates of 0.76 at 0.86 years and 0.65 at 7.35 years. When reviewing the subgroups of disease, the ranking of disease, in terms of test performance of ROC-AUC, was (1) myopia with a ROC-AUC of 0.85 and an AP of 0.83, (2) PD with a ROC-AUC of 0.83 and an AP of 0.96, (3) HM with a ROC-AUC of 0.79 and an AP of 0.93, (4) AMD with a ROC-AUC of 0.77 and an AP of 0.95, and (5) ERM with a ROC-AUC of 0.64 and an AP of 0.92. There were few images positive for diabetes with ophthalmic complications and SRL, which made analysis difficult; however, results indicated that ophthalmic complications performed well for the positive class, while SRL performed well for the negative class. The AP was higher than ROC-AUC because images positive for the disease are also positive for future RT/RD diagnosis or related surgery and because AP favors the positive class. The algorithm performed best for the myopia subgroup, considering precision, recall, and so on, which is expected given that myopia increases RT/RD risk, but for other subgroups, like AMD, performance degradation may be due to a small sample size.

We observed that the model largely focused on four anatomic structures upon review of the generated test set heatmaps, alluding to four biomarkers of RT/RD that the algorithm was potentially attending to. These structures include (1) myopic tilt, (2) thinness/thickness of the choroid, (3) hyaloid with vitreomacular traction, and (4) ERM presence. Posterior staphyloma, a characteristic of myopia that leads to posterior scleral thinning, can lead to the appearance of tilting within OCTs.23 Axial elongation, which accompanies myopia, stretches retinal layers and weakens their integrity.24 As axial length increases, the thickness of choroidal layers decreases, especially in HM eyes.25 As suggested by the algorithm's regions of attention, these synergistic hallmarks of myopia predispose patients to RT/RD. RD is an outcome of abnormalities of the vitreoretinal interface.26 Vitreomacular degeneration and the presence of traction foreshadow posterior vitreous detachment (PVD). PVD separates the hyaloid membrane from the retina, as evidenced by a significant portion of eyes with PVD having had RD.27 PVD can also cause idiopathic ERMs.28 While ERMs are established as complications following RT/RD, our results suggest that ERMs could be harbingers of RT/RD. Additionally, ERMs have been associated with myopia.29 We recognized that heatmaps may have highlighted ERM because the patients underwent membrane peeling surgeries (CPT code 67041), which would predispose them to RD. Upon review, only seven were noted to have undergone such operations (i.e., these patients had RT/RD during an ERM-related operation in the future). While these may in fact be mislabeled as internal limiting membrane detachments in the setting of pathologic myopia with or without ERM abnormalities, the association again links myopia to RT/RD. The model's focus shifted across different combinations of anatomy for negatively and positively predicted images. The variability suggested that the model was not solely identifying biomarkers as departures from normality but also searching for specific features. In the heatmap set, positive predictions were observed to focus on hyaloid, ERM, and retinal nerve fiber layer, whereas both negative and positive predictions focused on myopic tilt, choroid, and occasionally retina.

Saliency maps, including the heatmaps generated by Grad-CAM, can have poor performance compared to localization models, including consistency regarding the repeatability of randomly initialized intra-architecture models and the reproducibility of interarchitecture models.30 Because our data set did not contain segmentation masks or bounding boxes of annotated biomarkers, the explainability technique was nevertheless a useful tool for providing guidance on identifying potential biomarkers and modes of failure. We noted several factors that may contribute to the reduced likelihood of the positive class from the sample of true-positive OCTs approaching the RT/RD discovery date. This includes (1) the frame of reference missing the majority of the ERM, (2) the region of attention missing the ERM, and (3) low image quality.

Unique to the literature, we determine if evidence of RT/RD is present in standard-of-care OCTs prior to diagnosis or surgery. The image classifier showed high prognostic accuracy, achieving excellent performance when determining RT/RD status from OCTs.31,32 The model performs best for images indicating myopia or approaching the RT/RD diagnosis or related surgery date. Further, the heatmaps provide explanations by highlighting potential biomarkers of disease development. Our prognostic algorithm could serve as an interpretable screening tool for identifying eyes at high risk of future RT/RD, which did not have prior evidence of RT/RD.
