Making regional comparisons policy-ready: clarified, standardised methods and region-wide RSV hospital discharge estimates from Emilia-Romagna, Italy
Authors: Marco Montalti, Gabriella Frasca, Simona Di Mario, Enrica Perrone, Roberta Giroldini, Christian Cintori, Giuseppe Diegoli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRespiratory syncytial virus (RSV) prophylaxis with nirsevimab was introduced in Italy from the 2024–2025 epidemic season. The preprint “Impact of Regional heterogeneity of RSV infection prophylaxis on bronchiolitis in Italy” provides timely information for clinicians and health-system planners; however, several methodological specifications require clarification to ensure reproducibility and valid cross-regional comparability, particularly for the Emilia-Romagna Region. In Emilia-Romagna, programme choices were supported by a retrospective analysis of routinely collected hospital discharge and birth registry data (2015–2023), documenting that most of the RSV-associated hospital-admission burden is concentrated in infancy and follows a marked seasonal pattern, with severe outcomes largely confined to the first year of life. This evidence informed a risk-stratified approach during the initial roll-out. Using region-wide hospital discharge records from all Emilia-Romagna hospitals, we observed larger reductions between October 2023–March 2024 and October 2024–March 2025 than those presented as “regional” estimates in the preprint: total hospitalisations decreased from 1384 to 607 (−56.1%) among children aged < 14 years and from 1131 to 364 (−67.8%) among infants aged < 1 year; paediatric intensive care unit admissions decreased from 136 to 32 (−76.5%) and from 126 to 23 (−81.7%), respectively. To maximise the policy value of cross-regional comparisons and to support a more uniform, evidence-based national strategy in future seasons, key methods should be reported in a clear and standardised way.Keywords: Respiratory syncytial virus, RSV, Bronchiolitis, Nirsevimab, Sentinel surveillance, Hospital discharge records, Pediatric intensive care, Emilia-Romagna, Italy

Dear Editor,

The introduction of nirsevimab for respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) prophylaxis in Italy from the 2024–2025 epidemic season provides an important opportunity to quantify changes in bronchiolitis-related hospital burden under routine conditions. In this context, the Research Square preprint “Impact of Regional heterogeneity of RSV infection prophylaxis on bronchiolitis in Italy” by Badolato et al. addresses a timely and policy-relevant question for paediatric care and health-service planning [1].

Any interpretation of between-region differences during the first season of roll-out should, however, be grounded in the operational context in which programmes were implemented. In Italy, the initial deployment of nirsevimab occurred while national indications were still evolving, alongside heterogeneous regional delivery pathways and constraints related to procurement and organisational sustainability. In Emilia-Romagna, 2023–2024 programme priorities were defined through a structured retrospective assessment of RSV epidemiology and determinants of hospitalisation severity, with the aim of identifying the subgroups most likely to benefit from prophylaxis.

Specifically, we conducted a retrospective observational analysis integrating routinely collected hospital discharge records (SDO) and birth registry data (CeDAP) for the period 2015–2023, including paediatric admissions (0–14 years) with RSV-related ICD-9-CM codes 466.11 (acute RSV bronchiolitis), 480.1 (RSV pneumonia), or 079.6 (RSV). Over the study period, 8357 RSV-related paediatric hospitalisations were recorded; 82% occurred in infants aged < 1 year, with a pronounced seasonal concentration in December–February. Most admissions were managed in ordinary wards (94%), while intensive care involvement was largely confined to infancy, affecting approximately 7% of infants < 1 year and <1% of older children. Among 4817 hospitalised infants < 1 year, males accounted for 55.5%, and births clustered in the autumn–winter months, consistent with heightened vulnerability during peak RSV circulation.

Multivariable modelling showed strong and consistent associations with markers of vulnerability. Multiparity was associated with substantially higher odds of hospitalisation and intensive care (odds ratio [OR]: 3.25 for ordinary admission and OR: 3.50 for intensive care; p < 0.001). Low birth weight (<2,500 g) was also associated with increased risk (OR up to 1.84 for ordinary admission and up to 5.50 for intensive care; p < 0.001), as was prematurity (30–35 weeks’ gestation) (OR: 1.82 for ordinary admission and OR: 2.80 for intensive care; p < 0.001). The most salient finding concerned individual-level seasonality: winter birth (November–January, compared with May) was associated with the highest risk (OR up to 6.97 for ordinary admission and up to 87.60 for intensive care; p < 0.001). Maternal educational level was associated with intensive care admission (OR:1.40; p = 0.031), whereas maternal age, body mass index, smoking during pregnancy, and citizenship did not show meaningful associations.

These quantitative findings underpinned a risk-stratified regional strategy during the first roll-out season, prioritising infants at higher risk of severe outcomes while maintaining feasibility within existing delivery platforms.

Within this framework, the policy value of cross-regional comparisons depends critically on methodological transparency and on an explicit distinction between sentinel-based estimates and region-wide measures. For Emilia-Romagna, several core analytic elements in the preprint warrant clearer specification to ensure reproducibility and comparability with routine administrative flows.

The preprint does not report the ICD-9-CM codes or the full ascertainment algorithm used to identify RSV/bronchiolitis admissions, which precludes independent replication and limits assessment of case-definition performance. The age boundaries of the analysed population are not explicitly stated, despite the dependence of expected effect sizes on age and target cohort. The observation window is described inconsistently, with reference to both November–March and October–March for the same seasons.

Moreover, the Emilia-Romagna sentinel network is described in a non-standardised manner, alternating between health authorities and individual hospitals, which hinders inference on catchment and territorial coverage, and the unit of analysis (admissions versus unique patients) is not specified.

These issues are not merely technical, because the preprint reports for Emilia-Romagna a 41.2% reduction in “neonatology & pediatrics admissions” between 2023 and 2024 and 2024–2025 and a 54% reduction in paediatric intensive care unit (PICU) admissions, and these are discussed as regional outcomes.1

To support a coherent interpretation, we conducted an independent analysis using Emilia-Romagna hospital discharge records (SDO) covering all regional hospitals, analysed exclusively in aggregated form using source-assigned anonymised progressive identifiers. Using ICD-9-CM codes 466.11, 480.1, and 079.6 within ward types corresponding to those described in the preprint, and comparing October 2023–March 2024 with October 2024–March 2025, we observed larger reductions at the region-wide level. Total hospitalisations decreased from 1384 to 607 (−56.1%) among children aged < 14 years and from 1131 to 364 (−67.8%) among infants aged < 1 year; PICU admissions decreased from 136 to 32 (−76.5%) and from 126 to 23 (−81.7%), respectively.

Importantly, Badolato et al. base much of their interpretation on a threshold-based classification of Italian regions into performance groups according to percentage reductions in admissions. When our region-wide estimates reported above are mapped onto the authors’ own thresholds, Emilia-Romagna would fall into a different performance group than that assigned in the preprint, which would materially affect any comparative interpretation built on these categories.

Comparative evaluations of the first nirsevimab season remain valuable for informing future immunisation strategy and supporting more uniform, evidence-informed approaches at national level. Their credibility and usefulness for decision-makers, however, depend on standardised reporting of key methods and on explicit differentiation between partial-coverage sentinel estimates and population-based data. We submit these observations in a collaborative spirit and with the aim of strengthening the interpretability and policy relevance of the study.
