Spatial synergy in the digital era: a geo-analytics framework for detecting regional health patterns in urban smart city development
Authors: Ritu Chauhan, Mehak Jena, Eiad Yafi, Megat F Zuhairi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWith enhanced digitization of large-scale health and socio-economic data, it has generated new opportunities and challenges in the processes and planning of smart cities in the cities and public health. It is increasingly becoming necessary to have combined analytical frameworks that are able to work out these data into actionable information in dealing with health disparities. The paper hypothesizes a geospatial analytical model, the Spatial Health Information Tool (SHIT), which can be used to study health inequity at the county level in the United StatesMethodsThe framework was constructed with the help of the CDC PLACES 2024 data that comprised 3,142 counties and 40 health measures. To determine the spatial autocorrelation and geographic hot spots of unfavorable health outcomes, spatial statistical tools, such as Global Moran I, Local Moran I and Getis-Ord statistics were used. DBSCAN was used to perform density-based clustering to identify spatial groups of counties that were similar in their risk profiles. Besides, a random forest regression was also used to assess predictive relations between identified risk factors and health outcomes, which allowed considering the relative significance of determinants that led to health disparities. In an attempt to further encode spatial relationships among neighboring regions, a Graph Neural Network (GNN) was used to encode spatial relationship across counties. Moreover, a composite Health Vulnerability Index was computed by summing up the choice of health indicators into a normalized score in order to determine counties with high cumulative health risk. To enhance the explanatory power of the analytical findings, the Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) methods were included to determine the most significant determinants of health inequalities. This element enables the framework to connect the spatial health patterns with their socioeconomic or behavioral risk factors. The framework has been equipped with interactive visualization modules, such as choropleth maps, scatterplots, spatial queries, and three-dimensional prevalence surface maps as support of the exploratory spatial analysis.ResultsThe presented framework was able to find statistically significant spatial clusters of health disparities at the county-level in the U.S and found specific geographic patterns of high disease burden and high prevalence of risk factors. The clustering based on density also demonstrated clusters of counties with comparable vulnerability patterns. The random forest analysis revealed that there are significant predictive relationships between socio-economic and behavioral risk factors and unfavourable health outcomes and some of the major determinants are consistently at the top of feature importance. The predictions of the Graph Neural Network also demonstrated patterns of spatially correlated patterns of health risk between neighboring counties, proving the existence of regional health disparities. Besides that, an index of Health Vulnerability helped identify the counties where several health risk factors are combined, which is a convenient measure to inform the priorities of specific public health interventions. Explainable AI analysis also demonstrated the most important determinants and linked with unfavorable health outcomes and offered a way to interpret the reasons behind the regional health disparities. The interactive modules allowed the stakeholders to visually examine these patterns and relationships in an easy and user-friendly way.ConclusionsThe SHIT framework is a powerful decision-support system that can be used to investigate the issue of health inequities because it combines open health data, spatial statistics, and clustering methods with machine learning in a single and interactive system. The method produces policy-relevant details that might be used to inform specific interventions, equal distribution of resources and infrastructural planning. The present work illustrates the positive value of spatial big data analytics in enhancing smart city management plans and helping to advance the quality and health of the population.Keywords: Geo-spatial Analytics, Spatial Autocorrelation, DBSCAN, Random Forest Regressor, Data in Healthcare, GIS-based Decision Support

Introduction

With the emergence of big data analysis, urban system design, monitoring and management has fundamentally changed [1]. The integration of evidence-based practices in the framework of smart cities is considered a decisive engine of resilient governance, efficient provision of resources, and health equity for people [2]. Of these technologies, geospatial analytics is one of the most important as it allows us to identify spatial patterns and relationships and use the resulting insights to understand where to intervene. Whereas traditional statistical methods would do nothing to highlight the clusters, hotspots and differences it generates, spatial statistics use geography as a purposeful context, providing us with great insight into the locations of various incidents. Spatial analytics has found a new application domain in public health [3]. Obesity, diabetes, and lack of access to healthcare are just some examples of chronic conditions that are far from uniformly distributed among populations, yet display very strong regional clustering that is mediated by socioeconomic and environmental determinants. This makes it especially relevant to intelligent-city design, where the distribution of healthcare facilities, the provision of living conditions conducive to physical activity and the targeting of preventive measures depend upon a proper understanding of local inequalities in health [4]. This is because the recent advancements of new geographic information system (GIS) platforms using big-scale health surveillance data offer new opportunities for data-driven policy-making.

The geospatial analysis that involves analysis of spatial relationships and patterns in geographic data is an effective way of hurdling these obstacles [5]. Geospatial analysis can help discover hidden patterns in health outcomes, including prevalence of a disease in a region cluster or healthcare shortage areas using spatial autocorrelation (e.g., Global Morans I), clustering (e.g., DBSCAN) and hot spot analysis (e.g., Getis-Ord G*). These results are particularly relevant to the intelligent cities, the public health of which is a primary source of city sustainability. Indicatively, knowledge of the geographic concentration of obesity or lack of insurance can be used to locate health facilities, to plan walkable cities, or to implement specific interventions with regard to public health. Further, geospatial analysis enables the integration of diverse layers of information, such as health prevalence and other types of socioeconomic factors such as income or education level to create a visual image [6].

This multi-pronged strategy corresponds to the smart city vision of an interdependent system, in which the resources are to be optimized and the welfare of citizens is to be maximized. In the sphere of the field of public health, there is a plethora of data, like the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention PLACES dataset, which are informative in terms of model-based estimates of health at different geographic levels within the United States, including counties, census tracts and ZIP Code Tabulation Areas. An example is the Data Warehouse of PLACES 2024, which contains 40 health measures of 3,142 counties including data such as obesity, diabetes, and the presence of health insurance as well as demographic and geospatial data such as centroid coordinates and county FIPS code [11]. These datasets are particularly convenient to smart cities because they can be used to find the imbalance in health which directly influences the economic productivity, social justice, and urban planning. However, both the size and complexity of large spatial data are associated with their own set of problems such as flaws in data modelling, computationally intensive specifications, and the necessity of having custom analytical models to extract any meaningful information.

In this paper, the term of Spatial Synergy is used to refer to the combination of various spatial analytic elements such as spatial statistics, clustering methods, predictive modelling, and composite vulnerability assessment to create a more holistic image of geographic health inequalities. Conventional spatial analysis tools tend to analyze the result of each analytical process individually e.g. hotspot areas or statistical correlation. Nevertheless, more often than not, complex issues in public health are because of the interplay of various spatial factors in geographic regions.

The suggested framework operationalizes spatial synergy by integrating multiple approaches that are complementary to each other into a single geospatial platform. The spatial statistics of Moran I, LISA, and GetisOrd statistics are used to determine the patterns of spatial autocorrelation and clustering. Clustering methods also identify the geographically close clusters of counties which have comparable profiles of health risks. Graph neural networks can be used to predict the relationships across space between adjacent areas, whereas the Health Vulnerability Index represents various health variables into one interpretable measure of health risk in a region. A combination of these elements of analysis produces insights that would otherwise be hard to achieve through one form of analysis. Such synergistic combination of spatial analytics can enable the researcher and policymaker to gain a clearer insight into the complicated geographic processes of health disparities and be able to implement more effective and coordinated interventions in the domain of public health.

Such a framework is valuable not only in terms of technical innovation to achieve broader societal goals in smart cities but primarily in this case, in the area of public health. Long-term effects of health inequities among city dwellers are diabetes, obesity, and health insurance, which have effects on the economic productivity and the social justice in the society as well as on health [7]. Health disparities across regions are normally amplified to a further extent by socioeconomic factors, e.g. poverty and lack of education, which, in their turn, are often locally concentrated. These issues should be answered in the light of the data-based approach that will not only be able to identify where such issues have proliferated, but also where these issues originated, and where they have been localized. Through geospatial analysis and big spatial data, smart cities can now plan place-based solutions such as community health programs or city infrastructure investment that can solve the underlying causes of health inequalities that eventually create more inclusive and more resilient cities [12].

In the past decade, they have always identified the trends of massive data using statistical methods. Nonetheless, the intricacy and high-dimensionality of urban smart city data have compelled scholars and scientists to create intervention technology to manage data with augmented nature in the future prediction [8]. Comprehensively, the Big data analytics incline is more likely to be new era of computing to manage both structured and unstructured databases to generate knowledge in the real world application domain in developing urban smart cities [33]. The present methodology is based on the cognition of diverse parameters and complexities in medical databases where an integrative strategy is sacramentalised on big data through the GIS to find concealed information and trends in big databases to develop urban smart cities. Specifically, GIS is the new way of advancing data availability and computing capacity to identify trends [9]. The relationship between geographic information system (GIS) and spatial data can be long and fruitful to exhibit invariable results available to wide spectrum of users that can be useful in effective and efficient decision making in case of medical-scientific research [10].

In our study, the Framework of Spatial Health Information Tool (SHIT) in Fig. 1 represents a holistic geospatial analytical pipeline targeting health disparities in Smart Cities. In our research, we present a geospatial health analytics model in the form of an interactive Streamlit dashboard that combines state-of-the-art spatial statistics, machine learning, and visualization to help guide evidence-based decision-making in the creation of smart cities. The model incorporates spatial autocorrelation measures (Global and Local Moran I) to find the quality of health outcome clustering, hotspot analysis (Getis-Ord Gi) to find statistically significant areas of high prevalence, and density-based clustering (DBSCAN) for finding clusters of irregular shapes beyond the administrative limits. In addition, again, Random Forest regression is employed to evaluate predictive correlations between risk factors and outcomes, interactive spatial queries and 3D mapping enable policymakers to explore health inequities independently. To further enhance this form of analytical integration, the framework has integrated Graph Neural Network modelling to reflect spatial dependencies between counties and has introduced a Health Vulnerability Index that summarises a variety of health indicators into one policy-relevant health risk metric of a region. To increase the interpretability of these components of analysis, Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) techniques are also included in the framework, which determine the most important determinants related to spatial health disparities. Such integration empowers the framework to discern geographical trends of health disparities along with being able to give insight into the factors behind such trends. By integrating these methods, the system not only quantifies spatial disparities, but also converts them into interactive policy-relevant findings, demonstrating how open health data and spatial big data analytics might be applied to support geographically targeted and socially equitable interventions, as per the goals of urban resilience and inclusivity.

Literature review

Spatial analytics and health informatics together, nowadays, is the specialty of modern epidemiological studies, in particular, in the context of urban smart cities. Broadly, the concept of spatial analysis can be viewed as application of statistical and computer related tools to geographically indexed data to allow the investigator to reveal patterns, cluster and anomalies that would otherwise be unidentified under aspatial statistics [13]. With the work of projects such as the CDC PLACES project which released the fine-grained county level indicators of health, more rigorous and interactive forms of spatial epidemiology are now possible. The focus of geography of health remains to be spatial autocorrelation techniques [14]. The distribution of health outcomes on a global basis whether clustered, dispersed or randomly distributed was determined using the Moran I which was initially introduced by Moran (1950). It is expanded by Local Indicators of Spatial Association (LISA) that identifies both hotspots (High-High) and coldspots (Low-Low), which is very significant in terms of monitoring of the public health [15]. More recent applications in cancer epidemiology, heart diseases, and diabetes, have demonstrated that local autocorrelation techniques are able to detect micro-regions of high risk otherwise obscured by aggregate models [16]. These methods could be particularly applicable to policy targeting of smart city infrastructures, where the interventions are required at a more geographically accurate level. In addition to autocorrelation, the Getis-Ord Gi* statistic has become an effective tool of hotspots analysis that identifies areas where prevalence is unusual compared to the spatial neighbours. This is essential in the case of diseases like obesity and diabetes that have a heavy socio-economic and geographic gradient. This power to differentiate between clustering on the global level and the occurrence of hot zones has been demonstrated to enhance effectiveness of regional health planning [17].

During the last few years, data mining and machine learning methods have enriched spatial analysis. Non-contiguous clusters of disease burden are more and more identified in density-based clustering algorithms like DBSCAN, especially where patterns are no longer limited by administrative borders [18]. DBSCAN can detect irregularly shaped epidemiological clusters that could be overlooked by autocorrelation techniques by directly working on geocoordinates [32]. Socio-economic and behavioural determinants of disease prevalence have been widely used to predict disease prevalence using machine learning models, particularly, Random Forest regressors [19, 31]. Random Forests are predictive in spatial epidemiology, as well as having rankings of importance, which aids policymakers to prioritize determinants of interest in intervention strategies (e.g., obesity, smoking, access to healthcare) [20]. Researchers have however also warned that standard train-test splits can induce spatial leaks, and that split cross-validation specific to spatial data needs to be used [21]. The interactive visualization features of 3D surface maps, choropleth comparisons, and query interfaces are becoming common in modern spatial health dashboards [22].

These systems facilitate exploratory analysis, in which the decision-makers are able to probe into the health disparities [23]. These spatial queries (such as neighbourhood analysis, threshold exceedance, and cluster identification) are not restricted to the viewing and processing of maps, but they allow users to interactively engage with the dynamically changing nature of health indicators with space [24]. This interactivity is also consistent with the vision of smart cities of governance, where the construction of the policy as collaboration, with emphasis on transparency, participation and co-construction, is framed on data [25]. It is a revolution in the study of the health of populations to integrate GIS, further developments of spatial statistics, clustering algorithms and predictive models [26]. New systems, unlike traditional epidemiological surveys, which are only accessible to a limited degree due to privacy implications, are powered by petabytes of open health information and spatial big data infrastructure [27]. It can be deployed to detect the differences in real-time, combine them with socio-economic layers and provide actionable insights in the shape of user-friendly dashboards [28]. It has been suggested that these integrated systems are not only able to make informed decisions regarding healthcare delivery, but also the overall urban planning, which is in fact a reaction to social determinants of health implicit in the smart city plan [29].

The recent research has examined how geospatial analytics and machine learning can be used to examine and investigate healthcare access and spatial health disparities [34–36]. Individually, as an example, one study implemented hybrid machine-learning and clustering methods to assess the spatial access to healthcare services in urban environments through KMeans and KNN approaches to clustering data [a]. That work, however, was mainly concerned with access to healthcare facilities but not the spatial distribution of health outcomes or disease prevalence, and did not make use of the spatial statistics to measure spatial autocorrelation or geographic clustering. A different research examined spatial differences in healthcare features in rural communities by applying machine-learning algorithm to examine the links between healthcare indicators and regions features [b]. Although this study had valuable information on spatial variation of the healthcare systems, it fails to explicitly model spatial dependence among the neighboring regions, and it fails to establish statistically significant clusters of health disparities. Furthermore, more recently developed models of analysis in the study of a healthcare system have mostly been concerned with the approach of optimization or statistical modeling, which is typically not coupled with sophisticated geospatial analytics or interactive analytical models [c]. As a result, an expert research gap is that integrated geospatial frameworks combining spatial statistics, spatial clustering and spatially aware approaches to deep learning have not yet been developed to capture more intricate geographic patterns of health disparities. To fill this gap, this paper introduces a spatial health analytics framework, involving the combination of spatial statistical tools (Moran I, LISA, and Getis Ord hotspot analysis), spatial clustering tools, and Graph Neural Network models to predict and analyse health patterns in the region based on large-scale data on public health.

Regarding these gaps, this research comes up with three main contributions. To begin with, it combines both spatial clustering and spatial statistical methods to determine important geographic configurations of health inequalities. Second, it employs the use of a Graph Neural Network model to integrate spatial dependencies between neighboring counties and complement predictive analysis. Third, it creates an interactive geospatial analytics system enabling exploratory spatial analysis and decision informed on smart city health planning.Hence, the overall illustration is discussed in Table 1.

Methods

The field of spatial data research in smart city development is aimed at discovering the patterns that can be used in knowledge discovery with the help of both spatial attributes (location-based) and non-spatial attributes (socioeconomic and demographic factors). A thorough GIS based research merges these dimensions, as shown in Fig. 1, which lists the procedures to draw patterns out of large spatial databases to aid informed urban planning.

Big spatial databases

The foundation of this methodology is the PLACES dataset (2024 release) [11], a big spatial database providing model-based health estimates at multiple geographic levels across the United States, including counties, census tracts, and ZIP Code Tabulation Areas. At the county level, the dataset encompasses 3,142 counties across 50 states and the District of Columbia, covering 40 health-related measures derived from the Behavorial Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) 2021 and 2022, Census Bureau 2022 population estimates, and American Community Survey (ACS) 2018–2022 estimates. This database offers two broad elements, which include demographic data that include state statistics and county-level population projections and health prevalence data that include crude and age-adjusted prevalence rates with 95% confidence intervals. The main characteristics are demographic (e.g., state, county, population estimates of the total and 18 + populations), health prevalence (e.g., crude and age-adjusted prevalence of such conditions as obesity, diabetes, and high blood pressure) and geospatial (e.g., County FIPS codes and centroid coordinates). The data has a GIS-friendly format that allows seamless incorporation of spatial boundary files particularly U.S. Census 2022 county shapefiles, thus it is the best resource to conduct mass spatial analysis. Its broad area coverage and fine-granularity facilitates the discovery of health differences which is an important part of the smart city planning, as Public health defines both the resilience levels of the urban environment and the manner in which resources are allocated.

The CDC PLACES dataset delivers model-driven estimates of health outcomes and risk factors at different geographic levels of counties, census tracts, places and ZIP Code Tabulation Areas. These estimates are created with the help of small-area estimation methods which involve a combination of the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) survey data with demographic and socioeconomic data provided by the U.S census bureau and the American Community Survey. The method facilitates producing uniform health estimates on small geographic areas in the whole United States, which allows the researcher and policymaker to examine regional health disparities at a small spatial scale. Among the strong aspects of the PLACES data is that it has a national coverage and an equal approach to measurement which facilitates homogenous comparison of health indicators across geographical areas. Moreover, the data is renewed once a year, and it is possible to observe the trends in the field of people health over time.

PLACES is especially applicable in space epidemiology research and is also well-suited to supporting geographically-focused public health planning. Nonetheless, there are a number of restrictions that need to be taken into consideration, as well. The estimates are based on a model instead of direct survey measurements and thus there is a possibility of uncertainty in other areas where the survey sample is small. The data also incorporates aggregate population level estimates and thus can be biased with ecology in making inferences on the relationship of variables at the individual level. Moreover, some structural determinants of health (local healthcare infrastructures, environment, and characteristics of built environment, etc.) are not directly reflected by the data. The further socioeconomic or environmental data sets may be included in the future work to enhance the analytical power of the proposed framework.

Geospatial data preprocessing

The process of spatial data cleaning was performed to bring the dataset to a consistent, complete, and usable state with U.S. county shapefiles. To easily join CountyFIPS with the shapefile GEOID attribute, the field was trimmed down to a 5-digit string to denote County identifiers. The health measures, whose termination was of the nature CrudePrev, were converted to numbers and missing data substituted with a mean, as one method of maintaining data integrity. The spatial layer, loaded in GeoPandas, was reprojected to EPSG:4326 (WGS84) so it could be used with geospatial libraries and web-based visualization. To trade-off between computation efficiency and rendering speed in the interactive dashboard, topology-preserving simplification of the county geometries was done with a tolerance threshold of 0.05. Besides, centroid coordinates (latitude and longitude) were obtained per county polygon to facilitate clustering, distance-based computations, and plotting on a map. The cleaning of the data, georeferencing, and optimizing of the data to be further processed into spatial autocorrelation, hotspots, clustering, and machine learning occurred in a preprocessing pipeline that was necessary in the dashboard as well.

Geospatial insights

Geographic information system (GIS) forms the basis where researchers can organize, manipulate and analyze spatial data in such a manner that relationships, trends and patterns, of invaluable significance, are discovered to support the development of smart cities. Spatial analytics is highly valuable in the field of public health because prevalence and risk factors of a disease may be geographically non-randomly distributed. Health data is now in very large amounts due to automated data collection and open datasets like CDC PLACES 2024, and therefore, it is possible to use sophisticated spatial statistics to make useful discoveries of knowledge.

In the context of Moran’s I, the z-score provides a measure of statistical significance, indicating whether the observed spatial pattern differs meaningfully from a random distribution of values across features as shown in Eq. 1 [30]. A positive z-score suggests clustering of high values, whereas a negative z-score indicates clustering of low values (dispersion). When the z-score is close to zero, the spatial distribution is effectively random. Statistical inference is further supported by the p-value, which assesses the likelihood that the observed pattern could occur under the null hypothesis of spatial randomness. In our framework, Global Moran’s I was calculated for each health indicator, with 9999 random permutations to generate empirical significance levels. The null hypothesis, i.e., that health outcomes occurred randomly among the counties, was rejected based on both large z-scores and the p-value smaller than traditional levels (e.g., p < 0.05). These results were included in the interactive dashboard that allows the user to not only view the I value of the Moran but also the statistical context of the same when evaluating the spatial clustering in public health outcomes.

Along with global indicators, local indicators of spatial association (LISA) are also employed to decide the number of particular counties to assist with the clustering, distinguishing between High-High, Low-Low, High-Low and Low-High patterns. Similarly, Getis-Ord Gi+ hotspot analysis identifies the statistically significant high or low prevalence areas. Through their integration, it is possible to acquire a large picture and detailed data regarding space health inequalities. Our study have spatial query capability in our system, in addition to which we can query the data on-the-fly. Spatial queries enable the user to filter or explore areas based on threshold (ex. counties with obesity over 35%), geographic proximity (ex. neighbors within 100 km), or statistical significance (ex. clusters identified by Local Moran I). The dashboard enables the user to pose geographic inquiries about the data by combining questions and interactive maps, choropleths and scatter plots to inform healthcare planning and resource allocation. The site goes one step further in dynamic mapping by applying the integration of Moran I, LISA clusters, Gi + hotspots as well as spatial queries to provide evidence-based and dynamic information. This excerpt demonstrates that evidence-based smart city governance can be redesigned by integrating spatial analysis and big health data to reveal where inequalities reside and how to implement policy to make cities more equitable and resilient.

Besides the conventional spatial statistical analysis, the framework includes a Graph Neural Network (GNN) model as the one capturing the spatial dependencies among adjacent counties. Under this method, the counties are modeled as nodes in a spatial graph that is defined by geographic relationships that are built based on a k-nearest neighbor spatial adjacency structure. The characteristics of the node include the choice of health indicators used by the CDC PLACES dataset. Bringing together the information of neighbors counties via the use of graph convolutions operations, the GNN is able to use the information to learn spatially sensitive representations of the health trends in the regions. This method allows predicting the health outcome based on the local county-specific characteristics and the spatial interaction of one region with another, which offers greater insights into geographic health inequity, other than the standard statistical analysis. n order to further aid in the identification of high risk areas, a composite Health Vulnerability Index was created to describe several health indicators in one interpretable measure. The index combines the risk factors that are deemed to cause adverse health outcomes through the use of weighted by feature importance of a Random Forest regression model. All the indicators were normalized and added together to form a weighted vulnerability score of every county. The resulting index was brought to a 0-100 scale in order to make it easier to interpret and compare across regions. Counties with greater index values are an indication of regions with greater health vulnerability and allow policymakers and urban planners to make interventions in the regions with the greatest health disparity priority. Alongside signaling the high-risk areas, the index offers a policy-related parameter that can be used to facilitate resource allocation and specific public health interventions.

Spatial health information tool (SHIT)

The Spatial Health Information Tool (SHIT) is an interactive geospatial analytics platform created to assist in exploring the health disparities of the region using the CDC PLACES data. The system was created in Python with popular libraries, such as Streamlit to create the user interface, Plotly to create interactive visualization, GeoPandas to process spatial data. It combines the spatial data layers like the county lines, geographic location, and health indicators to facilitate dynamic visualization and analysis of the spatial health trends. The dashboard offers various analytical modules in thematic tabs which simplify various aspects of spatial health analysis. The Overview tab introduces the summary statistics and interactive spatial maps, with the help of which one can investigate the geographic distribution of the chosen health indicators. The Spatial Analytics module applies the spatial statistical techniques in the form of Global Moran I, Local Indicators of Spatial Association (LISA), and Getis Ord G + hotspots analysis to demonstrate statistically significant clusters of health outcomes.

Besides classical spatial statistics, the framework uses Graph Neural Network (GNN) predictions to obtain the spatial dependencies between adjacent counties. The GNN model can be used to predict health outcomes which are spatially aware by using the counties as nodes in a spatial graph and aggregate information of nearby regions to make predictions. This allows identification of complicated relationships in space, which would otherwise not be found using traditional statistical tools. In addition to facilitating the decision-making, the system contains a Health Vulnerability Index, a composite measure of the health risk in the region, which is a combination of several health indicators. The index is calculated based on feature importance scores of machine learning models and gives a normalized vulnerability score that can be used to locate counties with the highest health disparity. More modules are used to assist comparative spatial analysis, and further graphical representation, such as choropleth maps, scatter plots and spatial clustering results. The platform allows policymakers, researchers, and urban planners to explore spatial health trends and generate insights to support evidence-based smart city planning through a user-friendly interface and by filtering the interactive map and exploring maps.

Big spatial working pipeline for spatial health information tool (SHIT)

The Spatial Health Information Tool is based on a built-in geospatial analytics pipeline, which combines spatial data processing, statistics, machine learning and interactive visualization. The system can be seen starting with the importation and preprocessing of the CDC PLACES data and county-level spatial boundary files as shown in Fig. 2. Standardized geographic identifiers, missing values, and pre-analysis of the spatial dataset are performed through data cleaning and preprocessing processes. Spatial statistical tools are used to analyze the geographic trends of health indicators after preprocessing. The I of Global Moran is applied to evaluate the overall spatial autocorrelation, whereas the I (the Localized Spatial Autocorrelation) of Local Moran and Getis Ord G are applied to determine local cluster and hotspots of health disparity at counties level. These tests present a statistical basis of identifying non-random spatial disease distribution trends and risk variables. Spatial clustering and predictive modeling methods are then used on the framework to generate greater depth of analytical information.

Illustration of working pipeline for spatial health information tool (SHIT)

Specifically, the Graph Neural Network (GNN) module will create a spatial graph, where counties will be the nodes, and the connection between them is made based on geographic closeness. The model acquires both local and spatial interaction of neighbor regions and generates a prediction of health outcomes using both local and spatial interactions. Besides that, the system also calculates a Health Vulnerability Index, which is a composite score that takes various risks indicators into one. The index shows counties that have a high level of vulnerability to health and assists in prioritization of intervention strategies. The ensuing analysis products are incorporated into an interactive visualisation setting in which users can interact with spatial patterns, contrast indicators and discover areas that may need specific interventions by the general public health. The pipeline facilitates the stakeholders to convert the massive spatial health data into actionable information to support the urban planning and the decision-making of the population through integrating spatial statistics, predictive modeling, and interactive geospatial visualization.

Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) methods were added to the framework to enhance the interpretability of the predictive modelling findings. Specifically, the contribution of various socioeconomic and behavioral risk factors to health outcomes was determined with the help of feature importance analysis based on the Random Forest model. This method gives the predictions of the model interpretations of the relative role of the individual predictors instead of considering the model as a black-box system. The XAI component allows researchers and policy makers to gain a more adequate insight into the underlying causes of spatial health inequalities and facilitates more informed decision making in terms of the health of the population. Besides that, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values were included to give a coherent measure of feature contribution and allow visualizing the contribution of individual variables to model predictions in each county.

Spatial health information tool (SHIT)

The Spatial Health Information Tool (SHIT) combines CDC PLACES 2024 county-level health data with U.S. Census TIGER/Line spatial boundary files to allow the examination of geographic health disparities throughout the United States as shown in Fig. 15. The framework is not a visualization platform only, but it is rather an analytical platform aimed at detecting spatial clustering, uncovering associations among health indicators and creating predictive insights that can be used to make data-driven decisions in the field of public health. The analytical functionality of the structure enables the researcher and policy makers to analyze how health findings differ across geographical areas and identify those regions that are high concentration of health disparities. The statistical spatial techniques, such as Global Moran I, Local Indicators of Spatial Association (LISA), and Getis Ord hotspots, identify statistically significant disease clusters in counties, which allows one to determine regions with higher prevalence of certain diseases or scarce access to healthcare. These space patterns indicate the non-random patterns of geographic distributions of health risks and indicate where specific health-related public interventions might be needed. In addition to conventional spatial statistical analysis, the structure also includes predictive modelling methods in order to give more information about the regional health dynamics.

Illustration of the whole user interface of spatial health information tool (SHIT)

The random forests regression machine learning models are employed in determining the most significant predictors of health outcomes, which allows evaluating the role played by behavioral and socioeconomic risk factors in increasing health disparities by geographic location. Besides that, Graph Neural Network (GNN) modelling is utilized to model the geographical dependence between adjacent counties so that health outcomes can be predicted taking into consideration the geographical interaction among the regions. It is a spatial predictive modelling technique offering information beyond the usual statistical techniques. To further facilitate policy-based analysis, the framework is calculated to come up with a Health Vulnerability Index that consolidates various health indicators into a single score to depict the overall health risk of each county. This index also identifies areas where the various health challenges are co-occurring and offers a readable measure that can be used to inform there prioritization of where there are public health resources and intervention measures. The proposed framework converts big data on public health into geographic actionable information through the incorporation of spatial statistics, clustering analysis, predictive modelling and composite vulnerability assessment, thus addressing large scale public health data. These analytical functions show how spatial data analytics have the potential to help generate smarter urban planning, interventions in healthcare, and better health equity in the context of smart city development.

Discussion

The Spatial Health Information Tool (SHIT) design, illustrates how geospatial analytics can deliver actionable outcomes to the digitalisation of the smart cities and how it can be enhanced. The 42 counties, 35 counties, and 28 counties were identified using the number of counties around Atlanta with incidence of diabetes of over 15, Chicago with uninsurance of over 20, and Houston with obesity of over 30, respectively, which enabled the proper allocation of resources and formulation of policies. Such results and the correlations such as the 0.68 correlation of inactivity and diabetes demonstrate the promise of the Spatial Health Information Tool (SHIT) to aid data-driven city health policy, such as targeted wellness or increased healthcare access in communities with high prevalence.

The Health Vulnerability Index allows recognizing those counties in which several health risks are co-morbid. These areas might need specific intervention measures such as extended health care insurance, screening prevention programs, and health promotional activities on the community level. As an example, the counties that will be highly vulnerable based on the vulnerability score may be considered to receive preventive measures of obesity, diabetes, and cardiovascular risk factors. Moreover, the Graph Neural Network model emphasizes the role of spatial relationships between adjacent counties that imply that health outcomes in regions are not determined by local determinants only, but also by broader geographic areas. This observation shows that regional policy measures, as opposed to local interventions, can be more effective to eliminate endemic health disparities. Combined with the insights from other analytical products, they can identify the potential of spatial data analytics to aid decision-makers in better inventory of high-risk jurisdictions, prioritizing interventions, and distributing available public health resources in more favorable prospects of smart city health planning.

The dynamic nature of SHSI support stakeholder engagement, which is an essential element of smart city government, in real-time because planners can view the trends and amend the policies in real-time. The dashboard will, however, be restrained by its assumption of user expertise in geospatial terms, which may need to be trained to more general contexts of application, and its computationally expensive nature will limit scalability to real-time operation on large datasets. The improvements in the future would involve IoT information to support real-time health monitoring and more scalable algorithms with the SHSI more aligned with the efficiency, equity, and sustainability goals of the smart city to provide a scalable health management model to the city.

Conclusion

Our study was capable of designing and implementing the Smart Health Spatial Interface (based on Streamlit), built around the CDC PLACES 2024 dataset to establish the essential health inequalities in 3,142 counties in the United States so that additional digitalization could be promoted in the smart cities in the United States based on geospatial analytics. The SHSI found strong spatial clustering, e.g., a Global Moran I of 0.804 of uninsurance (ACCESS2_CrudePrev) and strong correlations such as 0.72 between obesity and diabetes, by imposing a formal pipeline of spatial data cleaning, selection, analytics, and knowledge discovery, to provide stakeholders with interactive visualizations and spatial queries to inform targeted health interventions. Combining Graph Neural Network modelling and Health Vulnerability Index also increases the analytical potential of the framework by considering the spatial dependencies between areas and counties with a high concentration of health risks. The components enhance the framework capacity to facilitate the use of data-driven public health planning processes and intervention strategies in the smart urban settings. The findings indicate the usefulness of spatial synergy in which incorporation of spatial statistical analysis, spatial clustering, predictive modelling and vulnerability assessment offers a better representation of geographic health inequalities than any analytical approach separately. The framework is also reinforced by the integration of Explainable Artificial Intelligence, which gives insights into the determinants of spatial health disparities, which can be interpreted. The framework can assist in making more informed and targeted public health planning in the smart city setting through associations between the geographic patterns and the risk factors underlying them. The framework helped to enhance the development of smart cities through offering insights for practical recommendations on how to distribute the resources equally, how to construct the infrastructure, and how to shape the public health policy as shown in the finding of the high-prevalence clusters of the Southern states, and in the possibility to take interventions directly on the cities like Atlanta and Chicago. Although it has been successfully achieved, in the future methods can be considered to incorporate real-time IoT data to enable dynamically health monitoring, increase in computationally efficiency towards scalability, and integration of user training modules to increase its accessibility, augmenting the role of Spatial Health Information Tool (SHIT) in the construction of sustainable, data-driven urban spaces.

Appendix 1: methodological details

Health vulnerability index (HVI)

The Health Vulnerability Index (HVI) is a way of giving each county a single number to show how much health risk is building up, and it does this by combining lots of different health-related factors. To start with, all the health things we looked at (like how many people are obese, have diabetes, don’t get enough exercise, or don’t have health insurance) were adjusted to be on the same scale – we call this min-max scaling. Then, a Random Forest regression model gave us scores for how important each of those things is in predicting bad health. We used these importance scores as weights to show how much each factor affects health outcomes. The weighted factors were added together with a simple sum to get a basic vulnerability score for each county. And lastly, this overall score was changed to fit between 0 and and 100, which makes it easier to understand; a higher number means a county is more vulnerable to health problems. So, the index doesn’t just show how many risks are present, but also how well those risks predict bad health, giving those setting policy a useful tool to find areas with the biggest issues and decide where to focus public health work.

Graph neural network (GNN) configuration

A Graph Neural Network ( GNN ) model was used by the framework to capture spatial relationships between counties situated nearby. In this formulation, each county is represented as a node in a spatial graph, while edges are constructed based on geographic proximity using a k-nearest neighbors (k-NN) approach. The dataset’s health indicators and socioeconomic variables are included in the node features. The GNN model learns spatially contextualized representations of health patterns by iteratively combining information from neighboring nodes using graph convolution operations. At each layer, node embeddings are updated by combining their own characteristics with those of nearby counties, which are weighted by the graph structure. The model was then trained to predict selected health outcomes, thus assuring both local and regional influences on health disparities. The spatially aware learning mechanism’s ability to recognize inter - regional interactions improves predictive performance and offers more comprehensive knowledge about geographically linked health risks, beyond conventional models.

Random forest with SHAP (RF–SHAP) interpretation

The Random Forest ( RF ) model was used to establish nonlinear relationships between health outcomes and socioeconomic and behavioral predictors. The model was equipped with a decision tree training set that involved randomly selecting features from a bootstrapped sample of data to build each tree, which boosted its robustness and lowered overfitting. The contribution of each feature to model predictions was quantified by computing the values of SHAP to improve interpretability. To each feature, SHAP values assign an importance score by measuring its marginal contribution across all possible feature combinations. At the local level, with a focus on explaining predictions for individual counties and the study employed shap analysis to locate crucial predictors both globally. The use of dual - level interpretability facilitates a clear understanding of how risk factors affect health outcomes across different regions. Moreover, the Health Vulnerability Index was constructed using the feature importance scores extracted from the RF model, which provided a level of uniformity between predictive modeling and composite risk assessment.
