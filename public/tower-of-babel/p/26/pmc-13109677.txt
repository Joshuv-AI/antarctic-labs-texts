Therapeutic Effects of Fermented Soy Extract on Osteoporotic Changes in Ovariectomized Mice as a Model of Menopause
Authors: Nisansala Chandimali, Seon Gyeong Bak, Seong‐Min Hong, Ji Young Hwang, Hyuck Se Kwon, Jaehoon Bae, Sun Hee Cheong, Seung‐Jae Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe objective of this study was to evaluate the effects of fermented soy extract (FSE) on bone metabolism and neuroendocrine regulation in an ovariectomized (OVX) mouse model of postmenopausal conditions. FSE administration improved bone microarchitecture and modulated key markers associated with bone turnover, including reduced CTX‐1 and RANKL levels and increased OPG expression, indicating suppression of osteoclastogenesis. In addition, FSE enhanced estrogen receptor–related activity and upregulated genes involved in bone metabolism. Notably, FSE also increased estradiol, serotonin, and norepinephrine levels, suggesting a beneficial role in neuroendocrine regulation. Collectively, these findings indicate that FSE may serve as a promising natural intervention for alleviating postmenopausal bone loss and neuroendocrine imbalance, supporting its potential application in functional nutrition.Keywords: bone health, estrogen receptors, fermented soy, menopause, osteoporosis

Introduction

Menopause is a natural biological transition that typically occurs in women between the ages of 45 and 55, marking the end of their reproductive phase due to the cessation of ovarian function [1]. This transition is characterized by a significant decline in estrogen and progesterone levels, leading to various physiological and psychological symptoms, such as hot flashes, night sweats, mood disturbances, and vaginal dryness [2]. One of the most concerning health issues during menopause is osteoporosis, a metabolic bone disease resulting from an imbalance between bone resorption and formation [3]. The decline in estrogen levels during menopause contributes to osteoporosis by enhancing osteoclast activity and promoting bone resorption while inhibiting osteoblast function and bone formation. As a result, postmenopausal women face an increased risk of bone fractures, reduced bone mineral density, and compromised bone integrity, all of which significantly affect their quality of life [4, 5].

Estrogen replacement therapy (ERT) has traditionally been used to alleviate symptoms and prevent osteoporosis in menopausal women [6]. However, the use of ERT is associated with potential side effects, including an increased risk of breast cancer, cardiovascular diseases, and thromboembolic events [7, 8]. This has prompted ongoing research into safer and more effective alternatives, particularly natural compounds with estrogen‐like activities, to manage menopausal symptoms without the associated risks of conventional hormone replacement therapies.

Fermented soy extract (FSE), also known as a fermented soy bioconversion product, is a natural compound derived from soybeans through fermentation and has attracted attention as a potential functional ingredient. Previous studies have indicated that FSE possesses phytoestrogen properties and can modulate estrogen receptor (ER) signaling pathways, which are involved in menopause‐associated physiological changes, including alterations in bone metabolism [9, 10]. FSE has been shown to influence bone metabolism, enhance bone mineral density, and regulate osteoclast activity, suggesting its potential relevance for menopause‐related bone health [11, 12]. In addition, FSE has demonstrated modulatory effects on neuroendocrine‐related markers, which may be associated with neuroendocrine changes observed during menopause [13, 14, 15]. Despite these promising findings, the full scope of the impact of FSE on menopause‐related health issues remains underexplored.

This study aims to investigate the potential therapeutic effects of FSE, focusing on its impact on osteoporosis and neuroendocrine function in menopausal conditions. Using both in vitro models, such as T47D human breast cancer cells, and in vivo models like ovariectomized (OVX) mice, commonly used to simulate postmenopausal osteoporosis, we examined the effects of FSE on estrogen receptor expression, bone microarchitecture, and markers of bone resorption. Additionally, we assessed it impact on serum hormone levels, including estradiol, serotonin, and norepinephrine, which are critical in bone health and mood regulation. Through this comprehensive approach, we aim to contribute to the understanding of FSE as a natural compound for managing menopausal symptoms, specifically its effects on bone health and neuroendocrine regulation. Our findings offer valuable insights into the potential of FSE as a safe and effective alternative to traditional hormone replacement therapies.

Material and Methods

Preparation of Fermented Soy Extract (FSE)

Renoflavone‐D10 was obtained from Vitech Co., Ltd. (Wanju, Korea) and originally sourced from Hughes Biotech Co., Ltd. (Taipei City, Taiwan), after which it was subsequently used in the experiments [16]. The compound was produced by cultivating Bacillus subtilis BCRC80517 in a nutrient broth medium supplemented with soybean at 37°C for 48 h. This fermentation process resulted in the bioconversion of approximately 95% of genistein into genistein‐phosphate derivatives [17]. Estrogenic Activity Assay

T47D‐Kbluc cells, which stably express estrogen response element (ERE)‐luciferase (Luc), were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Rockville, MD, USA). The cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco BRL, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 50 U/ml penicillin, 50 µg/ml streptomycin, and 0.2 U/ml bovine insulin. For the estrogenic activity assay, the culture medium was replaced with RPMI 1640 containing 10% charcoal‐stripped FBS, along with 50 U/ml penicillin and 50 µg/ml streptomycin, followed by incubation for 24 h. After incubation, the cells were plated in 96‐well plates and exposed to FSE for 24 h. Luciferase activity was then assessed using a commercially available luciferase assay kit (Promega Corp., Madison, WI, USA), following the manufacturer's protocol.

MTT Assay

T47D‐Kbluc cells (3 × 104 cells/well) were seeded into 96‐well culture plates and exposed to FSE (10, 30, 60 and 100 µg/mL) for 24 h. The concentration range was selected based on preliminary cytotoxicity experiments showing no significant reduction in cell viability. Following the treatment of the different concentrations of FSE, 10 µL of thiazolyl blue tetrazolium bromide (5 mg/ml in PBS) was added to each well and incubated for 3 h at 37°C. After incubation, the supernatant was discarded, and the resulting formazan crystals were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). The absorbance was measured at 540 nm using a microplate reader (Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA, USA). All experiments were performed in triplicate and repeated independently at least three times.

Animals

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Korea Food Research Institute (KRIBB‐AEC‐23276). A total of 54 female C57BL/6 mice (5 weeks old, weighing 15–20 g) were obtained from Raon Bio, Inc. (Yongin, South Korea) and randomly assigned to six groups (n = 9 per group). The experimental groups were as follows: (1) normal control group (non‐OVX, orally administered saline), (2) OVX control group (OVX, orally administered saline), (3) β‐estradiol (E2)–treated group (OVX, orally administered E2 at 0.5 mg/kg body weight (BW)/day), and (4–6) three FSE‐treated groups (OVX, orally administered FSE at doses of 20, 40, or 80 mg/kg BW/day, respectively). All treatments were administered once daily by oral gavage for the entire experimental period. Animals were randomly allocated to each group after OVX surgery, and all outcome measurements were performed using predefined experimental protocols. These doses were chosen to evaluate dose‐dependent effects and to establish a pharmacologically relevant range. The doses of 20, 40, and 80 mg/kg in mice correspond to approximately 1.6, 3.2, and 6.5 mg/kg in humans, respectively, which fall within the range achievable through dietary supplementation of flavonoid‐rich extracts.

Microcomputed Tomography (CT) Analysis

Detailed procedures were adapted from a previous study [19]. Briefly, femurs were harvested from the mice and fixed in 10% formalin for 24 h. The samples were subsequently stored in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) at 4°C. Bone morphology and microarchitecture were analyzed using a SkyScan 1076 micro‐CT scanner (Bruker, Kontich, Belgium). The femurs were placed in the sample chamber and automatically rotated along the axis (angular step: 0.42°, rotation range: 360°). For high‐resolution imaging (9 µm), the scanning parameters included a 0.5 mm aluminum filter, a source voltage of 88 kV, and a source current of 112 µA. Morphometric parameters and 3D reconstructions were obtained using NRecon, CTAn, and CTVol software (Bruker), with the region of interest defined as 0.5 to 2.5 mm below the proximal femoral growth plate.

Biochemical Analysis of Serum and Brain

Blood samples were collected from the mice via cardiac puncture and centrifuged to obtain serum. The whole brain was homogenized and lysed using a lysis buffer. Total brain protein levels were quantified using a detergent‐compatible (DC) protein assay kit (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) and used to normalize the estradiol and norepinephrine levels. Serum concentrations of serotonin, C‐terminal telopeptide (CTX), osteoprotegerin (OPG), receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa B ligand (RANKL), and osteocalcin (OCC) as well as brain levels of estradiol and norepinephrine, were measured using commercially available ELISA kits according to the manufacturer's protocols. The ELISA kits were sourced from Abcam for OPG and RANKL, Immunodiagnostic Systems (Boldon, UK) for CTX, Enzo Life Science (Farmingdale, NY, USA) for serotonin, MyBioSource (San Diego, CA, USA) for norepinephrine, and R&D Systems (Minneapolis, MN, USA) for estradiol.

Statistical Analysis

The data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). For in vitro experiments, data represent three independent biological experiments (n = 3). For in vivo experiments, data were obtained from nine animals per group (n = 9). For cell‐based assays, technical replicates were averaged within each independent experiment and were not treated as independent observations.

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 5 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Dunnett's post hoc test for multiple comparisons against the appropriate control group. For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired Student's T‐test was applied. The specific statistical test used for each dataset is indicated in the corresponding figure legends.

Results

FSE Exhibits Estrogenic Activity Without Cytotoxicity in Estrogen Receptor‐positive Cells

To evaluate the effects of FSE on cell viability, luciferase activity, and gene expression in T47D cells, we conducted several assays. First, cell viability was measured using three different concentrations of FSE (10, 30, and 60 µg/mL). The results showed no significant reduction in T47D cell viability at any FSE concentration compared to the control group (Figure 1A). Next, we assessed luciferase activity to determine if FSE could influence estrogen receptor (ER) activity. β‐estradiol was used as a positive control. The luciferase activity increased slightly in the estradiol‐treated group, and an increase in luciferase activity was observed with rising concentrations of FSE, indicating a modest activation of ER‐dependent transcriptional activity under the experimental conditions (Figure 1B).

(a) Cell viability of T47D cells treated with various concentrations of FSE, assessed using the MTT assay. (b) Luciferase reporter assay showing the activation of estrogen‐responsive elements in cells treated with FSE, without FSE, and with E2 as a positive control. (c) Relative ESR1 gene expression and (d) relative ESR2 gene expression in cells treated with FSE, determined using qRT‐PCR. Data are presented as mean ± SD from three independent experiments. Statistical analysis was performed using one‐way ANOVA followed by Dunnett's post hoc test. Differences were considered statistically significant at *p < 0.05.

We then analyzed the relative expression of ESR1 and ESR2 genes. The expression of ESR1 decreased with estradiol and, increasing concentrations of FSE (Figure 1C). ESR1 is a key estrogen receptor involved in estrogen signaling, and changes in its expression reflect alterations in ER‐related cellular responses. In the present study, FSE treatment was associated with a reduction in ESR1 mRNA expression in T47D cells, as observed under these in vitro conditions. In contrast, ESR2 expression was significantly increased with estradiol treatment, and FSE also increased ESR2 expression, though to a lesser extent than estradiol (Figure 1D). ESR2 is another estrogen receptor subtype, and its expression is known to vary depending on estrogenic stimuli and cellular context. The observed increase in ESR2 expression indicates that FSE modulates ER subtype expression in T47D cells.

Together, these results suggest that FSE exerts a mild estrogen receptor–related effect in vitro, as evidenced by modest changes in ER‐dependent luciferase activity and alterations in ESR1 and ESR2 gene expression. These findings describe an association between FSE treatment and estrogen receptor–related molecular responses in T47D cells, without implying specific downstream functional or pathological outcomes.

FSE Improves Bone Microarchitecture and Structure in OVX Mice Through Estrogenic Activity

To examine the effects of FSE on osteoporosis, a condition that often arises due to decreased estrogen levels during menopause [20], we conducted micro‐CT analysis of femur bone microarchitecture in OVX and control mice administered with estradiol and different concentrations of FSE. The three‐dimensional images showed that the bone structure in the 40 and 80 µg/mL FSE‐treated groups was significantly more complex and preserved compared to the OVX group, which exhibited marked bone degradation (Figure 2A). This suggests that FSE treatment may help restore bone integrity by maintaining or enhancing the bone structure, likely through estrogenic modulation. In addition, bone volume fraction (BTV%) was significantly reduced in OVX mice compared to control. Treatment with FSE increased BV/TV in a dose‐dependent manner, with the highest concentrations of FSE showing a marked improvement (Figure 2B). This enhancement in bone volume fraction could be attributed to the mild estrogenic activity of FSE, which may influence osteoblast function and bone formation processes. Similarly, trabecular thickness (Tb.Th.) was significantly reduced in OVX mice but increased with increasing concentrations of FSE, again in a dose‐dependent manner (Figure 2C). These results suggest that FSE promotes bone preservation by improving trabecular bone thickness, which is a key indicator of bone density and strength. Trabecular number (Tb.N.) followed the same trend, with FSE treatment reversing the OVX‐induced decrease (Figure 2D). This indicates that FSE could potentially enhance trabecular structure, which is essential for maintaining bone strength and preventing fractures.

These findings highlight the potential of FSE as a therapeutic agent for managing osteoporosis in postmenopausal conditions. The dose‐dependent improvements in bone volume and trabecular structure suggest that FSE may exert its effects through estrogen receptor modulation, contributing to enhanced bone preservation and a reduced risk of bone loss associated with menopause.

FSE Treatment Reduces Bone Resorption Markers and Modulates Osteoclastogenesis in OVX Mice

Next, we evaluated the effects of FSE on bone resorption markers in OVX mice. as monitoring these markers is crucial for understanding the impact of decreased estrogen levels during menopause on bone metabolism and the progression of osteoporosis. C‐terminal telopeptide of type I collagen (CTX‐1) levels were significantly higher in OVX mice compared to control. However, treatment with FSE decreased CTX‐1 levels in a dose‐dependent manner, with the highest FSE concentration showing the greatest reduction (Figure 3A). This suggests that FSE may reduce bone resorption by modulating osteoclast activity, likely through estrogen receptor signaling. Similarly, osteoprotegerin (OPG) levels were reduced in OVX mice but increased with increasing concentrations of FSE, further supporting the potential of FSE to mitigate bone resorption (Figure 3B). OPG is a decoy receptor that inhibits RANKL and osteoclastogenesis, and the increase in OPG suggests that FSE may help counteract excessive bone resorption. Additionally, receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa‐B ligand (RANKL) levels were elevated in OVX mice, and FSE treatment decreased RANKL expression in a dose‐dependent manner (Figure 3C). RANKL is a key factor that promotes osteoclastogenesis, and the reduction in its expression indicates that FSE might inhibit osteoclast differentiation, contributing to bone preservation. The ratio of RANKL/OPG, which is a crucial determinant of osteoclastogenesis, was significantly increased in OVX mice, but treatment with FSE decreased this ratio in a dose‐dependent manner (Figure 3D). This indicates that FSE may help maintain a favorable balance between RANKL and OPG, thus preventing excessive osteoclast activation and bone resorption.

Furthermore, osteocalcin (OCC) was significantly increased in OVX mice, indicating heightened bone resorption activity. Treatment with FSE effectively reduced OCC in a dose‐dependent manner, with the highest FSE concentration leading to the most significant decrease (Figure 3E). This aligns with the observed reductions in RANKL and CTX‐1 levels, further supporting the notion that FSE may suppress osteoclastogenesis and bone degradation. These findings suggest that FSE treatment could be effective in reducing bone resorption by influencing key signaling pathways involved in osteoclastogenesis. The dose‐dependent effects of FSE on these bone resorption markers further support its potential as a therapeutic agent for osteoporosis, possibly through estrogen receptor‐mediated pathways.

FSE Treatment Increases Estradiol, Serotonin, and Norepinephrine Levels in OVX Mice

We evaluated the effects of FSE treatment on estradiol, serotonin, and norepinephrine levels in OVX mice. Estradiol levels were significantly lower in OVX mice compared to control, but FSE treatment significantly increased estradiol concentrations in a dose‐dependent manner, with higher doses leading to greater increases (Figure 4A). Norepinephrine levels were significantly reduced in OVX mice compared to controls, but FSE treatment effectively increased norepinephrine levels in a dose‐dependent manner (Figure 4B). Additionally, Serum serotonin levels were reduced in OVX mice, but FSE treatment significantly increased serotonin levels. Interestingly, this increase was not dose‐dependent, as all concentrations of FSE resulted in a similar enhancement of serotonin levels (Figure 4C).

These results suggest that FSE may have potential in modulating neuroendocrine biomarkers, which are crucial for managing menopausal symptoms. The increase in estradiol, serotonin, and norepinephrine levels following FSE treatment points to its potential to alleviate symptoms commonly associated with menopause, including mood disturbances and bone loss. Further investigation is needed to explore the full range of FSE's effects on neuroendocrine and hormonal regulation in the context of menopausal health.

Discussion

Menopause represents a pivotal phase in a woman's life, characterized by the cessation of menstruation and a marked decline in estrogen levels [21]. This hormonal transition induces a wide range of physiological changes, including bone loss, mood alterations, and metabolic disturbances, all of which substantially affect quality of life [22]. Among these, osteoporosis is one of the most prominent health concerns, arising from an imbalance between bone resorption and bone formation under estrogen‐deficient conditions [3, 23]. In parallel, reduced estrogen levels negatively influence neuroendocrine function, contributing to mood instability, anxiety, and cognitive decline [24]. Given these multifaceted challenges, therapeutic strategies that can modulate estrogen‐related pathways without the adverse effects associated with conventional hormone replacement therapy are of considerable interest. In this context, the present study explored the effects of fermented soy extract (FSE), a compound suggested to interact with estrogenic pathways, on both skeletal and neuroendocrine alterations associated with menopause.

To investigate the estrogenic properties of FSE, we first examined its effects in T47D cells, an estrogen receptor–positive breast cancer cell line commonly used to evaluate estrogenic activity. FSE did not significantly affect cell viability at the tested concentrations, indicating an absence of overt cytotoxicity. This finding is relevant, as compounds targeting estrogen pathways must demonstrate cellular safety at biologically active doses to be considered viable therapeutic candidates.

Further assessment using an ER‐responsive luciferase reporter assay revealed that FSE induced a modest, dose‐dependent increase in luciferase activity. Compared with β‐estradiol, which elicited a strong estrogenic response [25], FSE produced a substantially weaker effect. This pattern is qualitatively similar to previously reported transcriptional responses induced by several phytoestrogenic compounds, such as genistein and daidzein, which activate estrogen receptors with lower potency than endogenous estrogens [26, 27, 28]. These findings suggest that FSE exhibits mild estrogen receptor–related transcriptional activity under the conditions tested. In interpreting estrogen‐related endpoints measured in vivo, caution is warranted. Although commercially available ELISA kits were used according to the manufacturers’ instructions, ELISA‐based measurements reflect immunoreactive signals rather than definitive molecular concentrations. These observations highlight the need for further validation using complementary analytical approaches, such as mass spectrometry–based methods.

In addition to functional ER activity, FSE treatment altered the expression of estrogen receptor subtypes. ESR1 expression decreased following treatment with both estradiol and increasing concentrations of FSE, whereas ESR2 expression was enhanced by estradiol and modestly increased by FSE. Such differential regulation of ESR1 and ESR2 suggests that FSE may influence estrogen signaling in a receptor subtype–dependent manner.

ESR2 has been reported to play a role in maintaining bone homeostasis and exerting protective effects on skeletal health [29]. The observed increase in ESR2 expression following FSE treatment may therefore be relevant to its bone‐related effects observed in vivo. However, given the absence of mechanistic assays in the present study, these findings should be interpreted as descriptive observations rather than evidence of causal signaling pathways. Further investigation will be required to clarify the molecular mechanisms underlying the receptor‐specific actions of FSE.

Estrogen deficiency–induced osteoporosis is characterized by increased osteoclast activity and accelerated bone resorption [30, 31]. The ovariectomized (OVX) mouse model is widely used to mimic postmenopausal bone loss and related hormonal changes [32]. Using this model, we demonstrated that FSE treatment significantly improved bone microarchitecture, as assessed by micro‐computed tomography.

Three‐dimensional reconstruction of femoral bone revealed that FSE‐treated OVX mice exhibited more preserved trabecular structures compared with OVX controls, which showed marked trabecular deterioration. Quantitative analyses further indicated dose‐dependent improvements in bone volume fraction, trabecular thickness, and trabecular number. Preservation of bone microarchitecture is particularly important, as it directly correlates with bone strength and fracture resistance in postmenopausal women.

Beyond skeletal effects, menopause‐associated estrogen deficiency also disrupts neuroendocrine regulation, contributing to mood disorders and cognitive impairment [35, 36]. In the present study, FSE treatment significantly increased circulating estradiol levels in OVX mice, with higher doses producing more pronounced effects. This observation suggests that FSE may partially compensate for estrogen deficiency under OVX conditions. Moreover, FSE influenced key neurotransmitters involved in mood regulation. Serotonin and norepinephrine levels, both of which were reduced in OVX mice, were restored following FSE administration. Although the serotonin response was not dose‐dependent, the overall normalization of these neurotransmitters suggests that FSE may modulate neuroendocrine biomarkers associated with emotional and cognitive function [37, 38]. These findings provide preliminary evidence that FSE may influence both hormonal and neurotransmitter systems affected during menopause. Collectively, FSE exhibited mild estrogen receptor–related activity in vitro and improved bone microarchitecture and neuroendocrine biomarkers in an OVX mouse model, suggesting its potential as a functional agent for managing menopause‐related conditions. Further studies are needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms and to evaluate its long‐term efficacy and safety.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the findings are based on an ovariectomized (OVX) mouse model, which may not fully recapitulate the complexity of postmenopausal osteoporosis in humans. Second, while estrogen receptor–related responses were evaluated, the mechanistic insights remain limited, and the present data do not allow direct identification of the active constituents or precise molecular pathways involved.

Third, potential cross‐reactivity or interference in immunoassay‐based measurements cannot be fully excluded, as ELISA methods detect immunoreactive signals rather than definitive molecular concentrations. Therefore, further validation using orthogonal analytical approaches, such as mass spectrometry, is required.

Fourth, the relatively short duration of the experimental period may not adequately reflect long‐term efficacy and safety. Finally, although the selected doses demonstrated biological activity, further studies are necessary to confirm their translational relevance and clinical applicability in humans.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrates that FSE exhibits mild estrogen receptor–related activity and improves bone microarchitecture and neuroendocrine biomarkers in an OVX mouse model. These findings suggest that FSE may serve as a promising functional agent for alleviating menopause‐related conditions, including osteoporosis and mood disturbances.

Given its ability to modulate estrogen‐related pathways while influencing both skeletal and neuroendocrine systems, FSE may represent a potential alternative to conventional hormone‐based therapies. Further studies, including clinical investigations, are required to elucidate its molecular mechanisms and to confirm its long‐term efficacy and safety in humans.
