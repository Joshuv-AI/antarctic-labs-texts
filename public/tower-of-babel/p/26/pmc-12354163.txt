Role of biliverdin reductase, a heme degradation pathway enzyme, in the development of vasospasm after subarachnoid hemorrhage
Authors: Zahra Hasanpour-Segherlou, Hunter Hutchinson, Haiyan Xu, Shawna Amini, Elizabeth Klaas, Melanie E Martinez, Brandon Lucke-Wold, Koji Hosaka, Brian Hoh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSubarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) is a life-threatening condition with high mortality and significant neurological morbidity, often complicated by delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI) and vasospasm. Heme metabolites such as biliverdin (BV) are implicated in SAH-induced vascular dysfunction, yet the role of biliverdin reductase-A (BVR-A), an enzyme that reduces BV to bilirubin, remains underexplored. This study investigates the contribution of BV and oxidative post-translational modifications of BVR-A to vasospasm development.MethodsWe used a murine model of BV injection into the subarachnoid space and analyzed its effects on vasospasm, microthrombosis, neuronal apoptosis, and microglial activation. Additionally, human plasma and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples from patients with SAH with and without vasospasm were evaluated for BVR-A expression and oxidative modifications using immunoprecipitation and western blot techniques.ResultsBV injection in the murine model induced significant vasospasm, increased microthrombosis, neuronal apoptosis, and a reactive morphological shift in microglia. In human samples, oxidative modifications of BVR-A were significantly raised in plasma from patients with SAH with vasospasm compared with those without, despite similar BVR-A expression levels. No significant differences in oxidative modifications were observed in CSF samples.ConclusionThis study shows a novel role of BV in vasospasm development and identifies oxidative modifications of BVR-A as potential modulators of SAH pathology. These findings suggest that BV and altered BVR-A activity may serve as biomarkers or therapeutic targets for improving outcomes in patients with SAH.Keywords: Aneurysm, Cerebral Vasospasm, biliverdin, Subarachnoid Hemorrhage

Introduction

Subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) is a critical and potentially fatal medical condition with an annual incidence of nine cases per 100 000 individuals.1 SAH typically results from aneurysmal rupture or traumatic head injury.1 Prehospital mortality rates for aneurysmal SAH range from 22% to 26%.2 The mortality rate for patients receiving conservative treatment in the initial months is between 50% and 60%. Approximately 50% of survivors from the initial hemorrhagic event experience a secondary ischemic phase, attributed to delayed arterial vasospasm in the perihematomal area.3 Vasospasm typically begins between 3 and 21 days after SAH, with the highest incidence occurring between days 5 and 7 following the hemorrhage.3 Delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI) affects approximately 30% of patients within the first 2 weeks following SAH, potentially leading to significant disability and mortality, and has the strongest correlation with outcomes at 3 months post-SAH.4

Activation of several pathways after SAH leads to DCI, including vascular dysfunction due to sympathetic nervous system activation, microthrombosis and thromboinflammation, and involvement of the glymphatic and meningeal lymphatic systems, as well as systemic inflammation.5 Decades ago, researchers suspected that hemoglobin (Hb) released from lysed erythrocytes into the subarachnoid space, along with its breakdown products in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), played a role in causing DCI.6 Heme is metabolized by heme oxidase into biliverdin (BV), carbon monoxide, and iron7 (figure 1). Free iron released from heme metabolism or breakdown can interact with H₂O₂ to generate hydroxyl free radicals, which oxidize heme, BV, and bilirubin; these oxidized metabolites may contribute to vasospasm following SAH.8 BV is a water-soluble tetrapyrrolic compound produced from the breakdown of heme.7 The conversion of BV to bilirubin is catalyzed by the enzyme biliverdin reductase (BVR) (figure 1).7 The timeline of BV presence in CSF, peaking within 3–5 days and fading after a week post-SAH, roughly aligns with the onset of delayed vasospasm. However, not all patients with SAH experience vasospasm after surviving the initial ictus, even with BV present in all cases. This suggests that other factors, such as the BV degradation enzyme BVR, may also have a role.

This study aimed to explore the role of BV in developing vasospasm, as well as the role of BVR enzyme and its post-translational oxidative and nitrosative modifications in both plasma and CSF samples of subjects affected by SAH. To our knowledge, the role of varying BVR enzyme expression in contributing to vasospasm has not been thoroughly investigated previously.

Methods

Animals

All animal experiments adhered to the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments guidelines and received approval and support from the University of Florida Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC202100000059). The study used C57BL/6 wild-type female mice aged 12–16 weeks. The mice were housed under standard conditions with unrestricted access to food and water and maintained on a 12-hour light/dark cycle. All mice were sourced from Charles River Laboratories (Wilmington, Massachusetts, USA), and cages were randomly assigned to experimental groups to ensure balance before surgical procedures. A detailed overview of the selection process, number of mice used, random group assignment, and measurements performed is provided in online supplemental figure 1.

Experiment model

The animal model used the injection of BV into the basal cisterns near the right anterior circulation.9 To summarize, the mice were anesthetized using a combination of xylazine (10 mg/kg) and ketamine (100 mg/kg). While maintaining the body temperature at 37°C, the head was immobilized in a stereotaxic frame. A sagittal incision was performed, followed by the creation of a burr hole located 5.0 mm anterior to the bregma and 0.5 mm to the right of the midline. Using a Hamilton syringe, 15 µL of BV hydrochloride (cat# 30891, Sigma-Aldrich) at a concentration of 10 mg/mL, dissolved in a 50:50 mixture of dimethylformamide (DMF) and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), was drawn. At a 30° caudal angle, the syringe needle was inserted until it contacted the skull base (approximately 7.2 mm). The BV solution (15 µL) was manually injected rapidly (~5 s). In the control group, the same protocol was followed, but the vehicle solution (50:50 DMF and PBS) was injected instead of BV solution.

Vasospasm measurement

To assess vasospasm, the diameters of the middle cerebral artery (MCA) and anterior cerebral artery (ACA) were evaluated based on a previously described method.10 At 24 hours after the experiment, the mice were deeply anesthetized using ketamine and xylazine. Serial perfusion was performed through the left ventricle using a solution protocol consisting of 5 mL PBS, 15 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), and 20% India ink dissolved in 5% gelatin. All solutions were pre-warmed to 37°C to maintain thermal consistency for the vasculature. The solutions were infused at a controlled flow rate of 15 mL/min to replicate cardiac output.11 Following perfusion, the mice were kept at 4°C overnight to allow the gelatin to solidify. The brains were then extracted, imaged, and vessel diameters were measured by an observer blinded to the group classifications. The narrowest vessel diameters, within the first 2 mm of the MCA and ACA branching from the terminal internal carotid artery, were recorded to analyze vasospasm.

Immunohistochemistry

For immunohistochemistry analysis, 5–7 mice per group were included. At 24 hours post-experiment, deep anesthesia was induced in the mice using ketamine and xylazine. Cardiac catheterization was conducted, followed by perfusion with 15 mL of 4% PFA. The brains were extracted and fixed overnight in 4% PFA. They then underwent dehydration in an 18% sucrose solution for 48 hours, were embedded in plastic cassettes with OCT, and submerged in −65°C isopentane. The blocks were stored at −80°C. Thin sections of the OCT-embedded tissue, 7 µm in thickness, were sliced and mounted on glass slides. These slices were then stained as outlined in previous protocols.12 Primary antibodies used included the Iba1 monoclonal antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA5-27726) for microglia detection and the fibrinogen beta chain antibody (Proteintech, 16 747–1-AP) for identifying microthrombosis. Detection was performed using donkey anti-rabbit IgG conjugated with Alexa Fluor 488 and Alexa Fluor 594 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA).

TUNEL assay method

The Elabscience One-step TUNEL In Situ Apoptosis Kit (E-CK-A32) was used to detect apoptosis in tissue samples, following the manufacturer’s instruction. Briefly, the sections were fixed with 4% PFA and permeabilized using a 1× Proteinase K working solution for 20 min at 37°C. For labeling, the slides were incubated with TdT equilibration buffer, followed by the addition of a labeling working solution containing Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT) enzyme and fluorescein-labeled Deoxyuridine triphosphate (dUTP). The samples were incubated at 37°C for 60 min in a humidified chamber, protected from light. Following labeling, the slides were washed with PBS. The slides were mounted using an antifade medium containing (4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole) DAPI (VectaShield, Vector Labs, Burlingame, California, USA) and visualized with an Olympus IX71 fluorescent microscope (Olympus America, Center Valley, Pennsylvania, USA). Two blinded observers analyzed all images across five representative 40× microscope fields.

Microthrombosis quantification

The slides were washed in 1× Tris-buffered saline with 0.05% Tween-20, then incubated with anti-fibrinogen goat IgG (Proteintech, 16 747-1-AP) overnight. Red fluorescent Alexa Fluor 594 donkey anti-goat secondary antibody was then used to detect the primary antibody. Five representative 40× microscope images for each mouse were analyzed with the ImageJ software. Images were filtered with a brightness threshold of 80 in the red color channel. The percentage of red in each image was averaged and compared between groups.

Microglia quantification and morphology analysis

The slides were washed in 1× Tris-buffered saline with 0.05% Tween-20, then incubated with anti-IBA1 rabbit IgG overnight. Green fluorescent Alexa Fluor 594 donkey anti-rabbit secondary antibody was then used to detect the primary antibody. Five random 40× images of Iba1-stained sections from the ipsilateral cerebral cortex were captured from each tissue sample by a blinded observer. Iba1+ cells with distinct cell bodies were then analyzed using ImageJ software following established protocols13 to generate the number of process endpoints and average length of process for each cell. The images were skeletonized with the ImageJ Analyze Skeleton (2D/3D) tool and FracLac for morphology data collection. The data from ImageJ were extracted to an Excel sheet and analyzed using GraphPad Prism to quantify the total microglia burden.

Human samples

We evaluated samples from 19 patients at a tertiary care center from May 2023 to September 2024. Subjects were included if they were aged 18–89 years, were diagnosed with SAH on a non-contrast head CT, and consented to have their data and biospecimens collected for the Neurosurgery Bank. The determination of vasospasm was based on a subject having radiological evidence of large cerebral vessel contraction confirmed by a radiologist and symptoms attributed to vasospasm by neurologists in patient charts. Five milliliters of blood were collected from the peripheral blood (radial or femoral access, representing systemic circulation). Plasma and CSF were stored at −80°C for subsequent immunoprecipitation and western blot analysis.

Immunoprecipitation

On day 1, 10 µL of protein A/G slurry was washed with 1 mL 1× PBS in a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube. After washing, 450 µL of 1× PBS and either 50 µL of human plasma or 75 µL of CSF were added to the tube. The mixture was rotated at room temperature for 1 hour to pre-clean the sample, which was then transferred to a new tube. Next, 1 µg of BLVR-A antibody was added and the sample was incubated overnight at 4°C with rotation. On day 2, an additional 10 µL of pre-cleaned protein A/G slurry was introduced to the immunocomplexes, followed by 1–2 hours of rotation at room temperature. After incubation, the sample was washed five times with 1 mL of PBST (PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20). Following the washes, 30 µL of 1× sample treatment buffer was added and the sample was heated at 100°C for 5 min. Finally, western blotting was performed.

Western blot

Fifteen µL of the sample was loaded onto a 4–20% Tris/Glycine gradient gel for protein separation and a Polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane was used for transfer. After incubation in 5% non-fat milk at room temperature for 1 hour, the membrane was incubated overnight at 4°C with either anti-HNE (protein-bound 4-hydroxynonenal) (Alpha Diagnostic International, Cat #HNE11-S) diluted 1:500 or anti-DNP (2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazone) (EMD Millipore, Cat #MAB2223) diluted 1:1000. On the following day the membrane was washed three times for 5 min each with PBST washing buffer (0.1% Tween-20). The corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were then incubated with the membrane at room temperature for 1 hour. After three additional washes, ECL was applied to the membrane for imaging using the BIO-RAD ChemiDoc MP system. The same membrane that was initially probed with anti-DNP was stripped using Restore PLUS Western Blot Stripping Buffer (Thermo Scientific, Cat #46430) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. It was then re-blotted with anti-human BV reductase A (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat #B8437) diluted 1:1000, followed by secondary antibody incubation, Enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) application, and imaging.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean±SE of the mean (SEM) or median±IQR, unless stated otherwise. Western blot, TUNEL, immunohistochemistry, age, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) scores, Hunt and Hess scores, modified Fisher scores, and vasospasm were evaluated with the Mann–Whitney U test to determine differences between groups, and for qualitative data the χ2 test was used. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA). A power analysis was conducted using G-Power with an alpha of 0.05, power of 0.80, and a two-tailed test. The sample size was determined based on a previous study published by our laboratory.14

Results

BV causes cerebral vasospasm and microthrombi formation in animal model

The effect of BV on cerebral vasospasm was assessed by measuring the diameters of the ipsilateral MCA and ACA 24 hours after the experiment. We observed that the BV group induced a reduction of approximately 12% in the MCA lumen diameter and a reduction of approximately 15% in the ACA lumen diameter compared with the control group (MCA: control group=24.57±0.74 μm, BV group=21.45±1.02 μm, P<0.05; ACA: control group=39.50±0.74 μm; BV group=34.35±1.46 μm, P<0.01) (figure 2).

The terminal internal carotid artery (ICA), anterior cerebral artery (ACA), and middle cerebral artery (MCA) are labeled in the control image. Scale bars=100 µm. (C) Plot of the minimum diameter along the M1 segment and (D) plot of the minimum diameter along the A1 segment (n=12–13 per group). (G) Plot of the percentage area with a positive signal for microthrombi (n=5–6 per group). *P<0.05, **P<0.01 (Mann–Whitney test).

The accumulation of microthrombi within cerebral arterioles and capillaries contributes to decreased cerebral blood flow and the development of DCI.15 16 Brain samples from 5–6 randomly selected mice were used for staining. We performed immunofluorescence staining 24 hours after the experiment for fibrinogen in the BV and control groups to evaluate microthrombi formation. The BV group had an increased number of microthrombi compared with the control group (0.41±0.12% of area positive for the fibrinogen/high-power field (HPF) vs 0.18±0.06%, P<0.05) (figure 2).

BV causes neuronal apoptosis and microglial activation in an animal model

To evaluate BV toxicity on apoptosis in the delayed phase of SAH pathology, we performed TUNEL staining 24 hours after the experiment (figure 3). Brain samples from 5–6 randomly selected mice were used for staining. A significantly increased number of TUNEL+ cells/HPF was observed in the BV group compared with the control group (0.09±0.02 TUNEL+ cells/HPF vs 0.0±0.0 TUNEL+ cells/HPF, P<0.01) (figure 3).

BV also causes microglial morphology shift and burden in the cerebral cortex 24 hours post-experiment. (G) The average branch length of microglia is significantly reduced in the BV-treated group compared with controls (n=6–7); *P<0.05 (Mann–Whitney test). (H) The total microglial burden, measured as Iba1+ cells/HPF, remains unchanged between groups (n=6–7); ns (not significant), Mann–Whitney test.

Microglia are known to undergo a morphological transition from a ramified to an amoeboid state in response to stroke injury.17 The effect of BV on microglial morphology was evaluated by automatically counting the number of endpoints of Iba1+ cell bodies and measuring the average branch length of each microglia in the cerebral cortex 24 hours post-experiment. BV caused a significant decrease in endpoints (control: 16.97±0.92 vs BV: 11.15±0.89, P<0.01; number of endpoints/cell) and in the average branch length of each microglia (control: 3.52±0.10 µm vs BV: 3.16±0.13 µm, P<0.05) (figure 3F,G). The total microglial burden in the ipsilateral cerebral cortex was unchanged in all groups (control: 7.38±0.38 vs BV: 7.70±0.49 Iba1+ cells/HPF; NS) (figure 3H). These results show that BV causes a microglial morphology shift to the amoeboid form without affecting the total number of microglia in the tissue.

Protein carbonyls and HNE levels of BVR-A in plasma and CSF of patients with SAH with and without vasospasm

The characteristics of the groups were similar, with no significant differences in sex distribution (88% female vs 70%, P=0.83, χ2 test of independence), GCS scores (14.0±4.5 IQR, n=7 vs 10.0±7.5 IQR, n=8, P=0.47), Hunt and Hess scores (2.0±0.0 IQR, n=9 vs 2.0±0.0 IQR, n=10, P=0.19), or modified Fisher scores (3.0±0.0 IQR, n=9 vs 3.5±1.0 IQR, n=10, P=0.31) in vasospasm-negative and vasospasm-positive groups, respectively, except for age which was significantly lower in the vasospasm-negative group (52.56±2.80 SEM, n=9 vs 65.10±2.95 SEM, n=10, P=0.01) (see online supplemental table 1). Vasospasm was determined based on documented vasospasm on imaging and either a new focal neurologic deficit or a decrease in GCS by ≥2 points.18 Following immunoprecipitation with specific anti-BVR-A antibody, the levels of protein carbonyls (DNP) and oxidative (HNE) on BVR-A were measured by western blot analyses. As shown in figure 4, the protein oxidative modification of BVR-A was significantly increased by about 14.47% compared with the vasospasm-negative group (vasospasm-negative group: 1.33±0.06; vasospasm-positive group: 1.56±0.06, P<0.05), whereas no significant changes were found for DNP levels in the plasma of subjects with SAH regardless of the vasospasm status (vasospasm-negative group: 7.10±0.63; vasospasm-positive group: 5.63±0.50, NS). As shown in figure 4, no changes were observed for BVR-A levels in the plasma of patients with SAH with or without vasospasm (vasospasm-negative group: 0.23±0.01; vasospasm-positive group: 0.24±0.01, NS) (figure 4).

After immunoprecipitation with an anti-BVR-A antibody, levels of protein carbonyls and oxidative modification on BVR-A were measured by western blot analysis in CSF samples. As shown in figure 4, there was no significant difference in BVR-A oxidative modification (vasospasm-negative: 1.25±0.21; vasospasm-positive: 0.95±0.08, NS) or carbonyl modification (vasospasm-negative group: 4.09±0.76; vasospasm-positive group: 3.30±0.51, NS) between patients with SAH with and without vasospasm. Similarly, BVR-A levels in the CSF did not differ between the two groups (vasospasm-negative group: 1.37±0.09; vasospasm-positive group: 1.43±0.01, NS) (figure 4).

Discussion

Brain injury following SAH presents a biphasic progression characterized by two distinct pathological phases.19 The initial phase, termed early brain injury, encompasses transient global cerebral ischemia during the ictal event, cerebral edema, disruption of the blood–brain barrier, and microthrombi formation.19 The subsequent delayed phase involves cerebral arterial vasospasm and neuronal apoptosis, culminating in the development of DCI.19 DCI is a well-recognized clinical phenomenon associated with considerable morbidity and mortality.20 Its underlying pathophysiology is highly complex and remains not fully elucidated. Various factors have been proposed to account for delayed neurological deterioration including genetic predisposition, inflammatory responses, large vessel vasospasm, dysfunction of cerebral autoregulation, microcirculatory failure, and spreading cortical depolarizations.20

The hypothesis that blood or its metabolites induce vasospasm is compelling as it explains the propensity for vasospasm to occur adjacent to substantial blood clots around the basal cerebral vessels.21 22 Metabolites of Hb, particularly those derived from heme or globin, have been implicated in delayed vasospasm due to the time required for their formation post-SAH.23 While Hb may contribute to both acute and chronic vasospasm following SAH, attributing delayed vasospasm solely to Hb is problematic because delayed vasospasm peaks approximately 1 week after SAH when CSF levels of Hb are low.8

Heme released from Hb is metabolized into bilirubin (see figure 1).7 The appearance of BV and bilirubin in the CSF after SAH is time dependent. BV is hydrophilic, excreted via bile and urine without further conjugation, and cannot cross the blood–brain barrier.24 Notably, the temporal profile of BV in the CSF—emerging within a day, peaking at 3–5 days, and disappearing several weeks post-SAH—correlates with the occurrence of delayed vasospasm.25 This correlation suggests that BV may contribute to vasospasm development. Furthermore, the neurotoxicity of heme metabolites, including bilirubin, is well documented in infants with jaundice. Unconjugated bilirubin, unlike water-soluble conjugated bilirubin or BV, can cross the blood–brain barrier and, if untreated, lead to kernicterus.26 In our murine model, direct injection of BV into the subarachnoid space induced significant vasospasm and microthrombi formation, resulting in neuronal apoptosis and cerebral ischemia.

If BV is considered the primary agent causing vasospasm in patients with SAH, the fact that BV is detectable in almost all SAH cases while vasospasm occurs in only about half challenges this hypothesis. This discrepancy highlights the potential significance of other modulatory factors, such as BVR activity, which may influence the extent or impact of BV.

Two isoforms of heme oxygenase (HO)—namely HO-1 and HO-2—are responsible for bilirubin production (see figure 1).7 HO-1, also known as heat-shock protein-32, is inducible by stimuli such as heat shock, heme, free radicals, and iron and is expressed in various cells including vascular smooth muscle cells, brain microglia, and macrophages.8 23 In contrast, HO-2 is constitutively expressed in numerous cells including vascular endothelial cells and neurons.23 27 28 Similarly, BVR exists in two isoforms, BVR-A and BVR-B.29 30 Both enzymes produce bilirubin; however, only BVR-A reduces BV to the potent antioxidant molecule bilirubin, while BVR-B preferentially acts on other BV isoforms.29 30 Post-translational modifications of BVR-A, such as autophosphorylation of specific serine residues, regulate its reductase activity.29 31 Changes in BVR activity due to differential phosphorylation at serine/threonine/tyrosine residues have been observed in the brains of individuals with Alzheimer’s disease or mild cognitive impairment.32 Additionally, oxidative and nitrosative modifications have been reported to alter BVR-A function in Alzheimer’s disease.32

These findings prompted us to explore the possibility of post-translational modifications of BVR-A and their potential role in vasospasm progression. We measured BVR-A levels in the CSF and plasma of patients with and without vasospasm. While the expression levels of the BVR enzyme were similar, the degree of oxidative post-translational modification was significantly higher in the plasma of patients experiencing vasospasm. In summary, our study is the first to demonstrate the role of BV in inducing vasospasm in a mouse model and to show that oxidative post-translational modifications of the BVR-A enzyme are elevated in patients with SAH with vasospasm compared with those without. Ongoing research in our laboratory is further exploring this topic, and it could potentially serve as a biomarker for vasospasm development and a therapeutic target in patients with SAH.

Limitations

There are some important limitations to consider when drawing conclusions from this study. We used only female mice due to the higher incidence of subarachnoid hemorrhage in women.33 This study involved 12–16-week-old mice, which represent a younger age group compared with most patients with SAH. The effect of age on the severity of vasospasm is recognized as a limitation.

Additionally, the P value comparisons for human samples may be limited by low power, and the study was restricted to measuring only one time point in humans. Future research could improve by tracking CSF analysis over time, particularly during the first 14 days of SAH.

Conclusion

This study provides novel insights into the role of BV in vasospasm following SAH. We demonstrated that BV induces vasospasm and neuronal injury in a mouse model and found that oxidative post-translational modifications of BVR-A are significantly higher in patients with SAH who develop vasospasm. These findings suggest that BV and altered BVR-A activity contribute to the pathogenesis of vasospasm, offering potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for improving outcomes in patients with SAH.
