Health outcomes of COVID-19 in immigrants versus native Spaniards
Authors: Jose Manuel Ruiz Giardin, Nieves Mesa Plaza, Almudena Escribá Bárcena, Juan Victor SanMartín López, Sonia Gonzalo Pascua, Luis Antonio Lechuga Suárez, Santiago Prieto Menchero, Maria Jesús Domínguez García, Sergio Serrano Villar, Santiago Moreno Guillén, on behalf FUENCOVID group
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundDuring the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic, the differential impact on ethnic groups has been widely debated. This study examines the immigrant population of Fuenlabrada, Spain, analyzing their infection, hospitalization, ICU admission, and mortality rates compared with the Spanish population.MethodsThis ambispective observational study (March 2020–January 2023) analyzed infection rates, hospitalizations, ICU admissions, and deaths among 215,000 inhabitants (10 % immigrants). Propensity score analysis was conducted to ensure comparability between groups.FindingsAmong 14,688 patients with 18,724 infections, 4102 (21·9 %) infections occurred in immigrants. Of 5429 hospitalized infections, 989 (19·2 %) involved immigrants. Immigrants had more than double the hospitalization rates of Spaniards (fourfold among Latin Americans). Spaniards were older (by 16 years), had higher comorbidities, longer hospital stays, and higher one-month mortality (4·9 % vs. 1 %). Vaccination rates were lower among immigrants (Spaniards 77 %, Latin Americans 70 %, other immigrants ∼50 %). Adjusted analyses revealed no significant differences in mortality between groups.InterpretationElevated infection rates in immigrants, particularly Latin Americans, likely explain their higher hospitalization and ICU admission rates. Mortality, adjusted for demographics and clinical factors, did not differ significantly. Future research should explore the socio-demographic and genetic contributors to these disparities.Keywords: Ethnic groups, SARS-CoV-2, COVID-19, Infections, Hospitalization, Death

Introduction

Ethnic minority groups have experienced disproportionately higher rates of infection, hospitalization, ICU admission, and mortality due to SARS-CoV-2 compared with majority populations. These disparities have been widely documented, though it remains unclear whether they result from a higher risk of infection or worse outcomes after infection. (Pareek et al., 2020; Rentsch et al., 2020) This uncertainty may stem from complex interactions between socioeconomic factors, healthcare access, and pre-existing health conditions that vary across different ethnic groups.

Evidence before this study

To explore the impact of SARS-CoV-2 infections on ethnic groups, we conducted a comprehensive search in PubMed from March 2020 to September 2024. Our search terms included: “ethnic groups” and “SARS-CoV-2 infections,” “hospitalization,” and “death.” We filtered results to focus on reviews, systematic reviews, clinical trials, meta-analyses, and observational studies.

A total of 658 manuscripts were identified, with 86 published in the last year, including 5 clinical trials, 8 meta-analyses, and 35 systematic reviews. These studies reveal that minority ethnic groups are disproportionately affected by SARS-CoV-2, with higher rates of infection, hospitalization, ICU admission, and death compared with majority populations. A systematic review by Irizar P. et al., analyzing data from 77 studies involving over 200 million patients, highlighted that Black, South Asian, and other minority groups have an increased risk of testing positive for COVID-19. Furthermore, Black, Hispanic, and Indigenous populations experience higher risks of hospitalization and ICU admission, while mortality risk is particularly elevated among Hispanic, mixed, and Indigenous populations. Contributing factors include health disparities, socioeconomic challenges, unequal access to healthcare, underrepresentation in clinical trials, crowded living conditions, and occupations with higher exposure risks.

Genetic factors, such as variations in genes like ACE2 and TMPRSS2 (key to viral entry into cells), may also play a role, potentially explaining susceptibility disparities among ethnic groups. However, the certainty of the evidence is variable, emphasizing the need for more robust and comprehensive research.

A systematic review by Irizar P. et al. (Irizar et al., 2023), analyzing data from 77 studies involving over 200 million patients, found that Black, South Asian, and other minority groups had a higher risk of testing positive. Black, Hispanic, and Indigenous groups also faced increased hospitalization risk, while ICU admission was more likely for Black, Hispanic, South Asian, East Asian, mixed ethnicity, and Indigenous populations. Mortality risk was higher for Hispanic, mixed ethnicity, and Indigenous groups. The quality of evidence ranged from very low to moderate, suggesting that disparities are likely driven by differences in exposure risk and post-hospitalization outcomes.

Health disparities play a significant role in these outcomes. Racial and ethnic minorities are more likely to have underlying health conditions such as diabetes, hypertension, and obesity, all of which are known to worsen the severity of COVID-19. (Raharja et al., 2021) These pre-existing conditions place individuals at heightened risk for more severe outcomes following infection. Socioeconomic factors further exacerbate risks. Lower socioeconomic status limits healthcare access. Crowded living conditions and essential public-facing jobs increase exposure. Harris et al. reviewed over 6000 studies and found that factors such as minority status, low socioeconomic standing, and occupation significantly increased infection risk. Most of these studies were conducted during the early pandemic phases and had biases related to selective testing and limited laboratory capacity. (Harris et al., 2023; Harding et al., 2023; Ogedegbe et al., 2020; Mude et al., 2021; Magesh et al., 2021)

Unequal healthcare access also contributes to worse outcomes. Mackey et al. found that African American and Hispanic populations had higher infection and mortality rates, suggesting that disparities in healthcare quality play a significant role. (Mackey et al., 2021) Similar trends have been observed among Native American, Australian Aboriginal, Alaskan Native, and South African Black populations, which have also reported higher mortality rates. (Griffee et al., 2023)

Another contributing factor is the underrepresentation of ethnic minorities in clinical research. Xiao et al.'s review of 122 clinical trials involving over 172,000 participants found that women, Black, and Asian individuals were underrepresented in prevention trials, while Hispanic participants were overrepresented in treatment trials compared with their expected representation in the general U.S. population. (Hill et al., 2023; Murali et al., 2023; Xiao et al., 2023) This underrepresentation limits the applicability of clinical trial findings and hinders the development of equitable healthcare strategies.

Here, we aimed to determine whether immigrant status influences infection rates, hospitalization, ICU admission, hospital stays, and mortality compared with the Spanish-born population. By focusing on birthplace, this research aims to provide a clearer understanding of how immigration status contributes to health disparities observed during the COVID-19 pandemic, ultimately helping to shape more equitable healthcare policies.

Methods

This observational cohort study employed an ambispective approach, incorporating retrospective data from March 1, 2020, to January 2021, and prospective data collection from January 2021 to January 2023. All-cause mortality was documented up to February 2024. The study adhered to the 22 STROBE criteria for observational research.

Study population

All patients aged over 15 years in the Fuenlabrada area, in the Community of Madrid, who visited the Emergency Department and were diagnosed with SARS-CoV-2 infection were included, irrespective of hospitalization status. The Fuenlabrada University Hospital, serving as the sole referral center for the Fuenlabrada area, covers over 200,000 residents, approximately 10 % of whom are foreign-born.

Data collection

All patients evaluated in the Emergency Department of the Hospital and diagnosed with SARS-CoV-2 infection were recorded. This included patients who did not require immediate hospitalization or hospitalization within 30 days, as well as those who required hospitalization (nearly 5000 patients). Patient data were recorded in the FUENCOVID dataset, designed to create a comprehensive repository including epidemiological, clinical, analytical, and therapeutic information for analysis.

The collected data were transferred to a platform based on the i2b2 system, managed by the Hospital Systems Unit. Queries were conducted, and the responses provided necessary information. Vaccination data were obtained from the RUV register of the Community of Madrid. Comparative analyses were performed globally and by the period of diagnosis and hospital admission to reflect evolving SARS-CoV-2 strains. Only patients with a confirmed microbiological diagnosis and compatible clinical presentation were included in the analysis.

Variables

The variables analyzed included patient history, age, sex, place of birth, underlying conditions, SARS-CoV-2 vaccination status, worst oxygen therapy requirement, blood pressure, heart rate, lowest lymphocyte count upon admission, and highest recorded levels of IL-6, CRP, ferritin, and D-dimer during hospitalization. Additional variables included treatments received, ICU admission status, outcome (recovery or death), duration of hospital and ICU stay, days from hospitalization to ICU admission, and time from diagnosis to death for deceased patients. Furthermore, readmission rates by ethnic groups and revisits to the Emergency Department were evaluated.

Mortality data

Mortality data in FUENCOVID were obtained from records available in the SELENE work program at the Fuenlabrada University Hospital, which also incorporated health card deactivation data from the SIP-CIBELES system. The Population Information System of the Community of Madrid (SIP-CIBELES) covers all individuals utilizing the Madrid Health Service, including health card holders and reasons for deactivation.

Mortality analyses encompassed all hospital admissions, including patients with multiple hospitalizations and Emergency Department visits, as well as recurrent cases. Long-term mortality (beyond the first month) was assessed based on the first COVID-19 admission.

Statistical analysis

Qualitative variables were summarized using absolute frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between qualitative variables were made using Chi-square tests or Fisher's exact tests, depending on data availability and sample sizes. Proportions were compared using the SPSS macro 'Confidence interval for the difference between two independent proportions of the Odds Ratio, 1998© J.M. Domenech,' implemented in SPSS® statistical software.

Quantitative variables were assessed for normality using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Normally distributed variables were summarized using means and standard deviations, while non-normally distributed variables were described using medians and interquartile ranges (IQR). Comparisons of normally distributed quantitative variables were performed using Student's t-test for two groups or ANOVA for more than two groups. For non-normally distributed variables, the Mann-Whitney U test was used for two groups, while the Kruskal-Wallis test was employed for comparisons across more than two groups.

An initial survival analysis was conducted using an unadjusted Cox regression model to assess the influence of nationality (Spanish vs. foreign) on time-to-event, defined as death, with right-censoring. Patients were enrolled progressively from March 1, 2020, and followed until either death or the end of the observation period (February 2, 2024), whichever occurred first.

We performed a two-step analysis to explore the association between nationality and mortality. First, a univariable Cox regression was fitted in the unmatched sample (n = 5311), followed by a propensity score matching approach to control for confounding. The propensity score was estimated using logistic regression with age, sex, number of vaccine doses received prior to hospitalization, Charlson comorbidity index, and epidemic wave at admission as covariates. Nearest neighbor 1:1 matching without replacement was applied. The matched sample consisted of 987 Spanish and 987 foreign-born patients, with balanced distributions of covariates across groups. A second Cox regression model was then applied to the matched cohort to assess mortality differences.

For the hospitalized patient subsample, homogeneity of key potential confounding covariates—including epidemic waves, prior vaccination status, categorized Charlson index (0, 1, 2, ≥3), age at admission, and sex—was evaluated using Chi-square tests for categorical variables and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for age, given the assumption of non-normality. When homogeneity was not achieved, propensity score analysis was applied to estimate the probability of classifying as Spanish or foreign, based on observed covariates. This matching aimed to create balanced groups to allow for subsequent unbiased comparative analyses.

All data analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0.

Hospital visits by population group

During the study period, 6.6 % of the Fuenlabrada population visited the hospital for SARS-CoV-2, with 5.8 % of the Spanish population and 13.7 % of the immigrant population seeking care. Among immigrants, 26.7 % of Central and South American and Caribbean residents, 10.5 % of African residents, 7.3 % of European residents, and 4.9 % of Asian residents visited the hospital. Overall, immigrants visited the hospital 2.3 times (95 % CI 2.2–2.4) more frequently than Spaniards. Emergency department visits and outcomes

There were 13,295 Emergency Department (ED) visits corresponding to 9817 patients who did not require hospitalization. Immigrants accounted for 23.9 % of ED visits (3113 infections involving 2186 individuals), representing 9.3 % of the foreign population compared with 3.8 % of the Spanish population. The most frequent visitors were from Morocco (3.9 %), Ecuador (2.9 %), and Colombia (2.3 %). A total of 5429 infections led to hospitalizations for 4786 individuals, of whom 18.5 % were immigrants. Hospitalized Spaniards were, on average, 16.7 years older than immigrants (95 % CI 14.6–16.9). The proportion of hospitalized patients over 60 was 67 % among Spaniards and 23.7 % among immigrants. The highest hospitalization rates were among Central and South Americans and Caribbean residents, with rates consistently 2 to 8 times higher than those of Spaniards across all epidemic waves Table 5, Fig. 2.

ICU admissions

Of the hospitalized patients, 416 (315 Spaniards and 101 foreigners) required ICU admission. Immigrants had a higher ICU admission rate at 10.1 % compared with 7.2 % for Spaniards. ICU admission rates were highest for Asians (15.3 %) and Latin Americans (11.2 %). The length of stay at the ICU was 15.3 days for Spaniards compared with 10.3 days for the non-spanish population Table 6, Fig. 3.

Vaccination and mortality

Vaccination rates were highest among Spaniards (77 %) and Latin Americans (70.2 %), while North Africans and Asians had the lowest rates, just over 50 %. Mortality was significantly higher in Spaniards (9.6 %) compared with immigrants (2.3 %), with a hazard ratio of 3.97 (95 % CI 3.0–5.1). In the unmatched cohort, foreign-born patients exhibited significantly lower mortality than Spanish-born patients (HR: 0.25; 95 % CI: X–Y; p < 0.001). However, after matching, the mortality difference was no longer significant (HR: 0.94; 95 % CI: X–Y; p = 0.56). This change was largely explained by age differences between groups. In the unmatched cohort, Spaniards had a median age of 67 years compared to 49 years in the foreign-born group (p < 0.001). After matching, the age distribution was balanced (median 51 vs. 49; p = 0.35). In univariable Cox analyses of individual predictors, age at admission had the strongest association with mortality (HR per year increase ≈ 1.06; p < 0.001), followed by Charlson index and vaccination status. These findings indicate that the initially observed mortality difference was confounded primarily by age.

Discussion

This study highlights that infection, hospitalization, and ICU admission rates were significantly higher among the immigrant population compared with the Spanish population, with infection and hospitalization rates up to four times higher. This trend was particularly notable among Latin American populations, followed by those from Africa. Notably, these higher rates occurred despite the immigrant population being significantly younger and having fewer comorbidities than the Spanish population. When analyzing all-cause mortality up to February 2024, we observed that mortality was four times higher among Spaniards compared with immigrants. However, after adjusting for covariates including the Charlson index, age, epidemic wave, and vaccination status using propensity score analysis, there was no significant difference in mortality between Spaniards and immigrants, suggesting that origin alone did not influence mortality.

Our findings align with those reported by Mackey et al., who found that African American/Black and Hispanic populations experienced disproportionately higher rates of SARS-CoV-2 infection, hospitalization, and COVID-19-related mortality compared with non-Hispanic white populations, though no higher in-hospital fatality rates were observed. The authors suggest that these disparities may stem from differences in healthcare access and exposure rather than susceptibility due to underlying conditions. (Mackey et al., 2021) Some studies have suggested the influence of genetic differences, indicating that American populations have a higher genetic risk score for COVID-19 severity, while others suggest that Sub-Saharan Africans may have genetic adaptations that reduce the risk of severe COVID-19. (Mozaffari et al., 2024; Hou et al., 2020; Esteban et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2024)

In the Fuenlabrada population, increased exposure to SARS-CoV-2 among immigrants likely contributed to the higher infection and subsequent hospitalization rates. This exposure may have been related to crowded living conditions and greater social interaction, particularly during the second epidemic wave of 2020. Anderson S. et al. also reported differences in behaviors by race and ethnicity, with racial and ethnic minorities being more likely to attend social gatherings, which may have contributed to increased exposure. (Anderson et al., 2022)

Further investigation into the socioeconomic and sociocultural factors underlying higher exposure rates among immigrants could provide a clearer understanding of these high infection rates. Genetic susceptibility might also contribute to higher infection rates among immigrants. Recent studies suggest that genetic factors, such as variations in the ACE2 and TMPRSS2 genes, may influence the differing susceptibility to SARS-CoV-2 infection among ethnic groups. Additionally, lower vaccination rates among the immigrant population, particularly among African and Asian residents, may have played a role. The study by Keegan G. et al. found that areas with lower vaccination rates had a higher percentage of African American residents and higher poverty rates, underscoring the impact of social determinants on vaccine uptake. (Keegan et al., 2024) In the Fuenlabrada area, significantly lower vaccination rates were observed among African and Asian immigrants compared with the Spanish population. Addressing barriers such as language differences and mistrust in the healthcare system could improve vaccination coverage in these groups.

Another significant finding was the higher number of emergency department visits by immigrants compared with Spaniards, which could reflect more frequent use of primary healthcare services by Spaniards versus greater reliance on emergency care by immigrants.

Regarding clinical characteristics, no significant differences were found between immigrant and Spanish patients in markers of inflammation, such as C-reactive protein or interleukin-6. Similarly, no disparities were observed in access to treatments or ICU admissions, which were actually higher among immigrants. This contrasts with findings from Mozaffari E. et al., who reported that white patients were more likely to receive COVID-19 treatments compared with black patients. (Asgari and Pousaz, 2021) Mortality analysis indicated no differences in short- or medium-term all-cause mortality between immigrants and Spaniards with prior SARS-CoV-2 infection.

These results are consistent with findings by Ayala IL et al. (Ayala et al., 2024) who found no significant differences in-hospital mortality or length of stay between Hispanic and non-Hispanic populations after adjusting for relevant covariates. (Ayala et al., 2024) Our results suggest that the healthcare system in Fuenlabrada, despite its shortcomings, has managed to provide equitable care to immigrant populations, at least in terms of hospital care.

Added value of this study

This study offers a unique contribution as a population-based analysis of all hospitalized patients in the Fuenlabrada area, part of Madrid, Spain—a region significantly affected by SARS-CoV-2. Covering more than three years of the pandemic, our study includes comprehensive data on infections, hospitalizations, treatments, and mortality among a population of 220,000 inhabitants, 10 % of whom are immigrants.

Key data sources include the Population Information System of the Community of Madrid (SIP-CIBELES), which tracks individual health cardholders, including reasons for deactivation (e.g., death). Additionally, vaccination records were obtained from the RUV register of the Community of Madrid. We compared epidemiological, clinical, and analytical data, vaccination status, and treatments received, focusing on potential inequities in treatment access despite Spain’s universal healthcare system.

Using propensity score matching, we analyzed two comparable groups: approximately 1000 Spanish patients and 1000 immigrant patients who required hospitalization during the pandemic. Immigrants had significantly higher infection rates (fivefold among Latin Americans) and hospitalization/ICU needs (fourfold among Latin Americans). Adjusted mortality analyses showed no significant differences between groups, despite Spaniards having a higher crude mortality rate (9·6 % vs. 2·3 %). No disparities in clinical characteristics (e.g., inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein or interleukin-6), access to treatments, or ICU admissions were observed between groups.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our findings suggest that higher SARS-CoV-2 infection rates among immigrant populations, particularly Latin Americans, account for their increased hospitalization and ICU admission rates. However, after adjusting for age, vaccination status, and comorbidities, mortality rates were similar between immigrant and Spanish patients. Immigrants exhibited lower vaccination rates, except for Latin Americans, whose rates closely matched Spaniards.

Future efforts should aim to investigate the underlying causes of higher infection rates among immigrant populations, which likely extend beyond socioeconomic factors. Strategies should focus on improving access to preventive measures, such as vaccinations, and ensuring equitable treatment for all affected individuals.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The immigrant population analyzed may not fully represent the characteristics of their regions of origin, as our cohort was relatively young and had fewer comorbidities. Additionally, biases could exist in mortality data for patients who died outside the hospital and did not have a health card, as such deaths might not have been recorded. Infection rates among immigrants might also be overestimated compared with Spaniards if the latter were more likely to seek care through primary healthcare, whereas immigrants might have relied more on hospital services. Conversely, hospitalization rates are reliable and can provide a solid estimate of infection rates.

Conclusions

In Fuenlabrada, immigrants make up 10 % of the population, and 13.6 % of the immigrant population sought hospital care for SARS-CoV-2, compared with 5.8 % of the Spanish population. Immigrants accounted for 18.5 % of hospitalized patients. The Latin American population had infection rates five times higher, and hospitalization and ICU needs were four times higher compared with the Spanish population. Despite these disparities, there were no significant differences in adjusted mortality. These findings underscore the need for further research into the demographic, socioeconomic, and genetic factors contributing to higher infection rates among immigrants, particularly Latin Americans. Efforts should focus on improving access to preventive measures, such as vaccinations, and ensuring that infected individuals receive appropriate care and support.
