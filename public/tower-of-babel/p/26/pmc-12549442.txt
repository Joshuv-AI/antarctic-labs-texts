Network Meta-analysis of 1.5% Ruxolitinib Cream Versus Systemic Agents in the Treatment of Moderate Atopic Dermatitis
Authors: Melinda J Gooderham, H Chih-ho Hong, Di Wang, Becky Hooper, Avery Hughes-Martin, Heather Cameron, Laura Pastor, Alicia Pepper, Ping Wu, Ali Mojebi, Grace Wong, Rafael Marino, Marie-Lise Dion, Mehdi Larbi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAtopic dermatitis (AD) is a chronic, highly pruritic, relapsing inflammatory disease associated with high quality-of-life burden. Topical 1.5% ruxolitinib cream is a selective Janus kinase (JAK)1/JAK2 inhibitor that is well tolerated and effective in improving itch and lesion clearance in patients ≥ 12 years old. This analysis estimates comparative efficacy for 1.5% ruxolitinib cream relative to systemic therapies for treatment of patients ≥ 12 years with AD.MethodsA systematic literature review (SLR) identified randomized controlled trials evaluating 1.5% ruxolitinib cream, oral JAK inhibitors, monoclonal antibodies, phosphodiesterase 4 inhibitors, and systemic immunosuppressants. A feasibility assessment evaluated whether indirect treatment comparison (ITC) was appropriate and determined appropriate ITC methods. This network meta-analysis (NMA) assessed the comparative efficacy of 1.5% ruxolitinib cream against systemic therapies in a “systemic-eligible moderate AD” subgroup defined as ≥ 12 years old and eligible for topical and systemic therapies (Investigator’s Global Assessment [IGA] = 3, Eczema Area and Severity Index [EASI] ≥ 16, and body surface area ≥ 10%); an ITC with the full study populations was not feasible. A frequentist framework using a penalized likelihood NMA included outcomes of IGA 0/1 with at least a 2-point improvement from baseline, EASI-75, and Itch Numerical Rating Scale 4 (NRS4).ResultsThe SLR identified 25 studies, of which 12 reported outcomes for the relevant subgroup for four interventions: 1.5% ruxolitinib cream, dupilumab 300 mg, upadacitinib (15 mg, 30 mg), and abrocitinib (100 mg, 200 mg), which were compared against placebo or placebo + topical corticosteroids. There were no statistically significant differences between active comparators for IGA 0/1, EASI-75, and Itch NRS4, although point estimates numerically favored 1.5% ruxolitinib cream for IGA 0/1 and EASI‑75.ConclusionFor patients with moderate AD who are eligible for systemic therapies, 1.5% ruxolitinib cream might provide disease control comparable to systemic treatments, such as dupilumab, regarding IGA 0/1, EASI-75, and Itch NRS4.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13555-025-01503-1.Keywords: Atopic dermatitis, Itch, JAK inhibitor, Ruxolitinib, Systemic treatments, Topical

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? This systematic literature review (SLR) and network meta-analysis (NMA) aimed to identify the evidence and assess the comparative efficacy and safety of 1.5% ruxolitinib cream relative to relevant comparator therapies for the treatment of patients ≥ 12 years of age with systemic-eligible moderate AD. What was learned from the study? Across analyses for the systemic-eligible moderate AD subgroup, 1.5% ruxolitinib cream provided comparable efficacy to systemic comparators in terms of key measures of disease severity, namely IGA 0/1, EASI-75, and Itch NRS4.This study provides what we believe to be the first estimates of comparative efficacy for topical 1.5% ruxolitinib cream with systemic therapies.For patients with moderate AD who may be eligible for systemic therapies, 1.5% ruxolitinib cream appears to offer disease control comparable to the available systemic treatments for these effectiveness outcomes, providing a potential topical option prior to systemic therapy.

Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a chronic relapsing inflammatory skin disorder characterized by intense itching, xerosis, and recurrent eczematous lesions, which can vary in severity from mild to severe and significantly impact quality of life [1, 2]. Topical corticosteroids (TCS) and topical calcineurin inhibitors (TCI) are currently recommended for frontline treatment of AD [3, 4]. In patients who do not achieve disease control with these therapies, phototherapy, or systemic treatments such as immunosuppressants (i.e., methotrexate, cyclosporine, azathioprine), monoclonal antibodies (i.e., dupilumab, tralokinumab, lebrikizumab) or oral Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors (i.e., upadacitinib, abrocitinib) may be recommended, depending on disease severity [3, 4].

Ruxolitinib cream, a new topical therapy that acts by selectively inhibiting JAK1 and JAK2, providing both anti-inflammatory and antipruritic effects, was assessed through two identically designed phase III, double-blinded, vehicle-controlled randomized controlled trials (RCTs) known as the Topical Ruxolitinib Evaluation in Atopic Dermatitis Study 1 (TRuE-AD1 [NCT03745638]) and Study 2 (TRuE-AD2 [NCT03745651]) in patients ≥ 12 years old [5, 6]. In the intent-to-treat (ITT) population with mild to moderate AD (Investigator’s Global Assessment [IGA] = 2 or 3), 1.5% ruxolitinib cream was effective in reducing disease severity and extent and provided a rapid and significant improvement in itch, which is the top treatment goal of patients with AD [5, 6]. Efficacy and safety were consistent across a range of subgroups. In a subgroup of patients who are typically eligible for both topical and systemic therapies (IGA = 3, Eczema Area and Severity Index [EASI] ≥ 16, and body surface area [BSA] ≥ 10%, hereafter referred to as the “systemic-eligible moderate AD subgroup”), substantially more patients treated with 1.5% ruxolitinib cream achieved an IGA score of 0 or 1 with a ≥ 2-grade improvement (defined as IGA 0/1 hereafter) from baseline at week 8 versus vehicle (59.4% versus 0%, respectively); results were consistent with the ITT population [7]. There is currently a lack of direct evidence comparing 1.5% ruxolitinib cream with systemic treatments for AD. This systematic literature review (SLR) and network meta-analysis (NMA) aimed to identify the evidence and assess the comparative efficacy and safety of 1.5% ruxolitinib cream relative to systemic therapies for the treatment of patients ≥ 12 years of age with systemic-eligible moderate AD.

Methods

SLR

Excerpta Medica dataBASE, Medical Literature Analysis and Retrieval System Online, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials were searched from inception to February 22, 2023, without language restrictions, for published RCTs evaluating 1.5% ruxolitinib cream, other treatments for AD, or placebo, at any dose or frequency that included outcomes of interest (Supplementary Material Search Strategies). Therapies included oral JAK inhibitors (abrocitinib, upadacitinib), monoclonal antibodies (dupilumab, tralokinumab), phosphodiesterase 4 (PDE4) inhibitors (crisaborole, roflumilast), and systemic immunosuppressants (cyclosporine, methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil). At the time of this study these were considered potentially relevant comparators. To capture all relevant data, electronic searches were supplemented with hand-searching of the gray literature. This involved keyword searching the US National Institutes of Health clinical trial registry, health technology assessment (HTA) websites, including National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) committee papers, Canada’s Drug Agency recommendation reports, and the European Medicines Agency European public assessment reports. Recent proceedings of the following five conferences were searched: American Academy of Dermatology Annual Meeting (2020–2023), European Academy of Dermatology and Venereology (2020–2022), International Society of Atopic Dermatitis (2021–2022), Revolutionizing Atopic Dermatitis (2021–2023), and World Congress of Dermatology (2015, 2019).

Study eligibility criteria were prespecified in terms of the population, interventions, comparators, outcomes, and study design (Supplementary Material Table 1). Abstract and full-text selection of studies were performed by two independent investigators, with any discrepancies resolved by a third investigator to reach consensus. Data extraction of included studies was performed by a single investigator and reviewed for quality by a second investigator. Risk of bias (RoB) was assessed using the NICE RoB assessment tool [8].

Feasibility Assessment: Heterogeneity and Inconsistency

A feasibility assessment was conducted to determine the appropriateness of conducting an ITC using NMA. Three key assumptions underpinning NMAs are exchangeability, homogeneity, and consistency. The exchangeability assumption means that trials in a network are similar and that all evaluated interventions could have been included in a trial and are truly competing interventions. The appropriateness of this assumption was evaluated qualitatively by comparing study design, inclusion and exclusion criteria, distribution of potential treatment effect modifiers (TEMs), outcome definitions, and assessment timepoints to ensure similarity across RCTs. Expert opinions from dermatologists were sought to assess potential bias related to these aspects and to identify relevant TEMs.

The homogeneity assumption indicates that there is no relevant heterogeneity between the results of trials within each pairwise comparison, while the consistency assumption indicates that direct and indirect evidence is aligned [9]. Inconsistency applies to closed networks and can be assessed using methods outlined in the NICE Decision Support Unit (DSU) Technical Support Document (TSD) 2 [10]. Since there is no direct evidence connecting 1.5% ruxolitinib cream and relevant comparators, consistency was not assessed.

NMAs

A flexible evidence synthesis approach was considered because of the small number of comparators and potential for sparsity of the resulting evidence network, which was anticipated to present possible challenges in model fitting and convergence. Consequently, frequentist NMA was used as the main analysis. While Bayesian NMA is more commonly used, global HTA guidelines do not specifically favor Bayesian over frequentist NMA. Furthermore, the results and conclusions of Bayesian and frequentist NMA typically align, provided that the correct statistical model has been used [11–14].

Frequentist NMA using a penalized likelihood NMA (PL-NMA) as described in Evrenoglou [15] was used to attempt to reduce the bias of the maximum likelihood estimate known to occur in the presence of rare events such as IGA 0/1 in certain treatment arms [5]. PL is a regularization method that applies a penalty term to the likelihood function, reducing bias compared with the standard likelihood function.

Fixed effect Bayesian NMA was also conducted where feasible according to conventional diagnostics, including model convergence (Rhat < 1.01) and effective sample size (ESS) per chain (> 400 per parameter). Where these diagnostic measures were inadequate, models were re-fit using more iterations and burn-ins. In cases where a Bayesian NMA was deemed infeasible, results are not presented.

Standard ITC methodology based on NICE DSU TSD 2 was followed [10]. Frequentist PL-NMAs were performed using R statistical software with R packages brglm (version 0.7.2) and AICcmodavg (version 2.3–2). A binomial logit link function was used to compare interventions and odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Fixed effect Bayesian NMAs were performed using R statistical software with R package rjags (version 4–8) to interface with Just Another Gibbs Sampler (JAGS) software (version 4.3.0) for both model specification and Bayesian estimation. The code for rjags was adapted from the WinBUGS code provided by the NICE DSU TSD 2 and 3 with some minor modifications to address the difference in syntax between JAGS and WinBUGS. A default seed was specified to ensure that results can be reproduced. The Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling process used to construct the posterior distribution for all model parameters involved discarding a burn-in of 20,000 iterations before accepting a subsequent 20,000 iterations for NMA estimation. A binomial logit link function was used to compare interventions and ORs with 95% credible intervals (CrIs). Across both approaches, an OR greater than 1 corresponded to a favorable treatment effect for the treatment of interest (1.5% ruxolitinib cream) as compared with the reference treatment. Conclusions around statistical significance can be drawn for frequentist NMAs based on the 95% CI (i.e., a result is statistically significant if the 95% CI excludes one); however, such conclusions cannot be drawn from 95% CrIs presented with Bayesian NMAs.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the impact of a trial which was limited to adolescent patients and was excluded from the main analysis and pooling doses of upadacitinib and abrocitinib (Supplementary Material Sensitivity Analyses). This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Results

SLR

The search identified 2578 unique records, with 119 relevant records reporting 25 unique RCTs selected for inclusion (Fig. 1). Approximately half of the trials evaluated topical (ruxolitinib) and oral (upadacitinib and abrocitinib) JAK inhibitors, with the other half evaluating monoclonal antibodies (dupilumab and tralokinumab). Twelve studies reported outcomes for a systemic-eligible moderate AD subgroup (IGA = 3, EASI ≥ 16, BSA ≥ 10%) (Supplementary Material Table 2).

Study selection flow diagram. SLR systematic literature review

Although 25 trials were included in the evidence base, two phase III studies (AD-301, AD-302) evaluated crisaborole and were excluded from further review since they had mixed-age populations (2–18 years old) but did not provide baseline characteristics for the subgroup ≥ 12 years old, which comprised less than 40% of the population of those trials [16]. Two of the conventional immunosuppressants (methotrexate and cyclosporine) were only assessed in a single trial, while azathioprine and mycophenolate mofetil were not evaluated in any of the included trials. Two phase III studies (INTEGUMENT-1 [NCT04773587], INTEGUMENT-2 [NCT04773600]) evaluated roflumilast; however, results were not published as a peer-reviewed full-text publication at the time of this SLR and thus were not captured in the evidence base. During manuscript development, the INTEGUMENT-1/2 trials were published but did not report outcomes for relevant subgroups [17].

Trials were generally of good quality, with low RoB (Supplementary Material Fig. 1).

Feasibility Assessment

A feasibility assessment was conducted to determine the most appropriate approach to derive comparative estimates for efficacy outcomes of IGA 0/1, EASI-75, Itch Numerical Rating Scale (NRS)4, and the safety outcome of treatment-emergent adverse events. As a result of a lack of connectedness and significant differences in ITT populations with respect to trial eligibility criteria, prior treatment requirements, baseline AD duration, and disease severity in the comparator trials (Supplementary Material Table 3), an ITC was not feasible for the full ITT population (Supplementary Material Fig. 2).

Among the 25 studies identified, 12 presented results for a systemic-eligible moderate AD subgroup (IGA = 3, EASI ≥ 16, BSA ≥ 10%) (Table 1) and included 4 interventions: 1.5% ruxolitinib cream, dupilumab, upadacitinib, and abrocitinib compared against placebo or placebo + TCS. These trials reported outcomes for patients with similar disease severity and had similar trial eligibility criteria in terms of BSA and EASI scoring as the moderate AD subgroup (IGA = 3, EASI ≥ 16, BSA ≥ 10%) of the TRuE-AD trials. Baseline characteristics, when reported, were more similar to the TRuE-AD subgroup of moderate severity (Table 2).

Baseline risk was assessed by comparing placebo response for efficacy outcomes. A larger placebo effect was typically seen in comparator trials where placebo was combined with TCS or other prescription topicals across all considered efficacy outcomes, whereas the response in placebo-only arms was typically below the average across all placebo comparators. Therefore, it was deemed inappropriate to combine or to compare placebo and placebo + TCS/prescription topicals comparators. Trials not connected through the common placebo arm were subsequently excluded. The main network included two satellites; seven trials were connected to 1.5% ruxolitinib cream through a common placebo comparator (Fig. 2). An NMA using the systemic-eligible moderate AD subgroup data was deemed feasible for efficacy outcomes but not for safety outcomes, as active comparator trials did not report adverse event rates in sufficient detail.

NMA for the Systemic-Eligible Moderate AD Subgroup

Frequentist NMAs were conducted as the primary analysis for all outcomes (Supplementary Material Table 4). Bayesian NMAs were conducted as sensitivity analyses for EASI-75 and Itch NRS4 as they passed convergence checks (Rhat < 1.01) and had adequate ESS (> 400) per parameter, suggesting the MCMC algorithm adequately sampled the parameter space (Supplementary Material Table 5). Bayesian NMAs are not presented for IGA 0/1 (Rhat ≥ 1.01 and ESS < 400 per parameter), as diagnostics were not improved by increasing the number of iterations or burn-ins.

For IGA 0/1, all active treatments were statistically superior to placebo (Table 3). Upadacitinib 30 mg was statistically significantly better than upadacitinib 15 mg (OR 1.71; 95% CI 1.22–2.40) and dupilumab 300 mg (OR 3.19; 95% CI 1.67–6.09); 1.5% ruxolitinib cream was not statistically different than any other active treatment.

Discussion

The SLR captured evidence on all relevant comparators, including oral JAK inhibitors, monoclonal antibodies, PDE4 inhibitors, and systemic immunosuppressants; however, PDE4 inhibitors and systemic immunosuppressants were excluded from further review because of limited evidence. The objective of the NMA was therefore to estimate comparative efficacy and safety of 1.5% ruxolitinib cream relative to systemic therapies for the treatment of patients ≥ 12 years of age with systemic-eligible moderate AD (IGA 3, EASI ≥ 16, BSA ≥ 10%). Among the 25 studies included in the SLR, 12 reported outcomes for this subgroup. These included six trials evaluating dupilumab, three evaluating upadacitinib, two evaluating 1.5% ruxolitinib cream, and one evaluating abrocitinib. Frequentist NMAs were conducted for IGA 0/1, EASI-75, and Itch NRS4; analyses were not possible for safety outcomes as a result of data reporting limitations. The NMAs for IGA 0/1 and EASI-75 estimated that 1.5% ruxolitinib cream was statistically superior to placebo and not statistically different versus dupilumab, upadacitinib, or abrocitinib, although point estimates numerically favored 1.5% ruxolitinib cream. Results for Itch NRS4 were similar across active comparators, although 1.5% ruxolitinib cream was not statistically different compared with placebo; this was likely due to the smaller sample size, which informed estimates of 1.5% ruxolitinib cream, resulting in wider CIs. However, a significantly greater number of patients achieved clinically relevant improvements in itch as measured by NRS4 versus vehicle in the ITT TRuE-AD population.

This NMA evaluated the efficacy of 1.5% ruxolitinib cream compared with systemic therapies as a result of its use in patients who do not achieve disease control with conventional topical therapies prior to treatment with systemic therapies. This analysis contributes robust evidence to an existing body of literature on the comparative effectiveness of 1.5% ruxolitinib cream and therapies for AD, with new evidence for systemic-eligible moderate AD. The majority of published NMAs compare topical therapies in patients with mild to moderate AD or systemic therapies in a moderate to severe patient population, with few investigating the efficacy of topicals and systemics in a patient population eligible for both classes [14, 19, 20]. This study provides what we believe to be the first estimates of comparative efficacy for topical 1.5% ruxolitinib cream with systemic therapies. Important differences in the patient populations across trials for topical and systemic therapies have previously limited robust analyses for patient subgroups that may be overlapping across trials; however, the recent publication of efficacy estimates for subgroups from several pivotal trials enabled such estimates in the present analysis. For patients with moderate AD who may be eligible for systemic therapies, this NMA showed that 1.5% ruxolitinib cream appears to offer disease control comparable to systemic treatments (i.e., dupilumab, upadacitinib, abrocitinib) to achieve IGA 0/1, EASI-75, and Itch NRS4 in a shorter time period.

A strength of this analysis was that the NMAs were based on a comprehensive SLR and trial heterogeneity was thoroughly assessed to determine the feasibility to conduct robust NMAs and identify appropriate methods. While it was deemed infeasible to compare the full trial populations, all 12 trials in the NMA included disease severity as a stratification factor at the time of randomization, further supporting the decision to incorporate their moderate AD subgroup data into the NMA. Additionally, clinical validation was sought on methods to ensure assumptions were valid and to mitigate potential bias. Statistical analyses and NMAs were performed according to well-established methods per NICE DSU TSDs.

There were limitations associated with this analysis. Specifically, as the analysis was conducted in a subset of the ITT populations, sample sizes were reduced (e.g., n = 31 for systemic-eligible moderate AD subgroup relative to n = 499 for the 1.5% ruxolitinib arm of the ITT population) and some heterogeneity remained between trials (e.g., baseline BSA, disease severity, etc.). Heterogeneity in trial design for differing treatment regimens led to variability in the timing of the endpoint assessment; 1.5% ruxolitinib cream was assessed at week 8 while comparator interventions were assessed at week 12 or 16 (Supplementary Material Table 18), potentially biasing the results against ruxolitinib cream (i.e., results favoring ruxolitinib may be understated because of the shorter time period for which outcomes were assessed compared to other included treatments). Moreover, frequentist PL-NMAs were used to reduce bias, as some estimates were associated with considerable uncertainty due to limited data and few events, making certain Bayesian NMAs infeasible. Pooled outcome data were used for some trials with identical study designs (SOLO 1 and 2, Measure Up 1 and 2) based on data availability, and outcome data for TRuE-AD 1 and 2 were pooled to align with this approach and to further reduce bias and uncertainty due to low event counts, particularly in the placebo arms. Finally, not all potentially relevant systemic comparator therapies were included, which may limit applicability. Specifically, the comparators identified in the SLR reflected relevant therapies accessible to patients in the Canadian public healthcare setting, and therapies included in the NMA were limited to those where data were available.

Conclusion

Across analyses for the systemic-eligible moderate AD subgroup, 1.5% ruxolitinib cream demonstrated efficacy comparable to systemic comparators in terms of key measures of disease severity, namely IGA 0/1, EASI-75, and Itch NRS4 in a shorter time period. For patients with moderate AD who may be eligible for systemic therapies, 1.5% ruxolitinib cream appeared to be at least as effective as the available systemic treatments, such as dupilumab, for these effectiveness outcomes, providing a potential topical option prior to systemic therapy.
