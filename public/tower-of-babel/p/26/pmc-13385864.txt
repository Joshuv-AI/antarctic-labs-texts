Connexin 43-dependent mesenchymal stem cell mitochondrial transfer activates PGC1α disrupting oxidative-inflammation axis in ischemia-reperfusion injury
Authors: Abantika Ganguly, Jing Wang, Shobha Regmi, Rosita Primavera, Shashank Chetty, Steven Levitte, Ramasamy Paulmurugan, Avnesh S. Thakor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ischemia-Reperfusion Injury-related Acute Kidney Injury (IRI-AKI) is a significant clinical challenge. Despite extensive research into the underlying mechanism for IRI-AKI, the development of effective therapeutic interventions remains challenging due to self-perpetuating positive-feedback loops between oxidative stress and inflammation pathways. Mitochondrial dysfunction has been acknowledged as a significant player in the early stages of IRI in causing cellular damage. However, antioxidant therapy has only yielded modest improvements in outcomes. Interestingly, Mesenchymal Stem/Stromal Cells (MSCs) have shown potential in the treatment of AKI through their anti-inflammatory properties, though their ability to disrupt the oxidative stress-inflammation axis remains incompletely understood. In a clinically relevant surgical model of IRI-AKI, we investigated precision delivery of MSCs directly into the kidney, via its arterial blood supply, to ensure spatial in vivo coupling of these cells with injured renal cells. Here, we observed that MSCs, specifically BM-MSCs, mitigated mitochondrial dysfunction during the early stages of IRI-AKI, leading to reduced inflammation, preventing cellular injury and recovery of renal function. Mechanistic studies demonstrated the importance of Connexin 43 (Cx43)-mediated gap junctions in facilitating mitochondrial transfer from MSCs to damaged renal epithelial cells, resulting in activation of PGC1α signaling, a reduction in mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS), and promotion of mitogenesis. These findings highlight the mechanism by which spatially coupled MSCs can restore cellular mitochondrial homeostasis by disrupting the oxidative stress-inflammation axis and paving the way for the development of disease etiology-based therapeutics for treating IRI-AKI.

Introduction

Ischemia-Reperfusion Injury-related Acute Kidney Injury (IRI-AKI) occurs when blood flow to the kidneys is transiently restricted (ischemia) and then restored (reperfusion),1,2 initiating a cascade of hemodynamic, metabolic, and inflammatory events that rapidly compromise kidney function. It is a leading cause of perioperative AKI, especially following major cardiovascular surgeries,3 kidney transplantation,4 sepsis,4,5 shock, and trauma; resulting in significant patient complications, including prolonged hospital stays, predisposition to the development of chronic kidney disease,6 and higher mortality rates.7 Despite the high prevalence and severity of IRI-AKI, there are no targeted pharmacologic therapies in routine use for IRI-AKI,8 and conventional supportive care has only modestly improved patient outcomes over the past four decades.9

At the cellular level, IRI-AKI is driven by a biphasic process consisting of an initial ischemic phase followed by a subsequent reperfusion phase. During ischemia, reduced blood flow causes cellular hypoxia and disruption of oxidative phosphorylation, resulting in ATP depletion and metabolic reprogramming towards less efficient pathways. In addition, ischemia leads to membrane depolarization, impaired mitochondrial biogenesis, and reduced cellular antioxidant capacity. Together, these changes place substantial stress on Renal Proximal Tubular Epithelial Cells (RPTECs), which possess the highest energetic demands among all renal cell types and rely heavily on oxidative phosphorylation to meet their energy requirements. Following reperfusion, the sudden influx of oxygen and nutrients triggers a surge in metabolic activity. Although necessary to prevent irreversible injury from prolonged ischemia, this metabolic surge also promotes the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and dysregulated mitophagy, further exacerbating IRI through loss of mitochondrial mass and deterioration of mitochondrial quality and function.1,10,11 The resulting dysregulation of mitochondrial dynamics and homeostasis can impair various energy-intensive active transport mechanisms in the kidney, leading to AKI.12 Furthermore, oxidative stress induced cellular damage can activate pro-inflammatory signaling, creating a self-reinforcing oxidative stress-inflammation axis which further exacerbates IRI-AKI.13–15 Although antioxidant strategies and mitochondria-targeted delivery systems have been developed to address mitochondrial dysfunction and excessive ROS generation in IRI,16 the clinical translation of these broadly acting antioxidant biologics has remained largely disappointing, due to limited efficacy, non-specific biodistribution, drug accumulation, and concerns regarding nephrotoxicity. This persistent therapeutic gap highlights the need to explore alternative approaches that restore mitochondrial mass and function while simultaneously disrupting the oxidative stress-inflammation axis to mitigate IRI-AKI.

Advances in regenerative medicine, including Mesenchymal Stem/Stromal Cells (MSCs), have gained prominence due to their demonstrated efficacy in restoring cellular bioenergetic deficits, accelerating tubular regeneration, and promoting kidney function in AKI.17,18 Most studies on the reno-protective effects of MSCs focus on their migration19 and anti-inflammatory, anti-apoptotic paracrine effects.20 These indirect mechanisms can secondarily improve mitochondrial integrity by reducing inflammatory stress and apoptosis in AKI.20 Recent accumulating evidence shows that recipient cell damage can also promote unidirectional transfer of mitochondria from MSCs to damaged cells, which can directly restore mitochondrial integrity in various disease states.21,22 This form of intercellular organelle transfer has brought renewed focus to the signaling mechanisms that permit mitochondrial donation. Connexins, a family of transmembrane proteins, have been identified as playing a crucial role in facilitating intercellular mitochondrial transfer by activating signaling pathways between donor and recipient cells to create a favorable environment for mitochondrial donation.23–25 While several connexins (Cx37, Cx40, Cx43, and Cx45) are expressed in the kidney,26 and have been shown to be crucial in propagation of cellular apoptotic signaling following IRI-AKI,27–29 their role in facilitating mitochondrial transfer from spatially coupled MSC cell therapy, and its impact on mitochondrial dynamics has been incompletely investigated. Furthermore, despite the promising attributes of MSCs, their molecular heterogeneity arising from their source of origin has been associated with diverse “regenerative signatures”,30 and differential mitochondrial transfer ability.31 Hence, determining the ability of source-specific MSCs to effectively transfer functional mitochondria in IRI-AKI is essential to disrupting the oxidative stress-inflammation axis and developing disease etiology-based therapy for IRI-AKI.

In the present study, we established a clinically relevant IRI-AKI model that is also amenable to locoregional delivery of MSCs as an early preventive therapy immediately following IRI-AKI induction. This model also enabled spatial coupling between MSCs and damaged RPTECs in vivo to investigate the therapeutic role of Connexin 43 (Cx43) in facilitating mitochondrial transfer from MSCs in IRI-AKI. We undertook a transcriptome-based temporal profiling of IRI-AKI disease progression to identify the critical timepoints and overlaps between mitochondrial dysfunction and activation of immune signaling, which was used to identify the optimal therapeutic window for early intervention in IRI-AKI. We also compared the renoprotective potential of adult Bone Marrow-derived MSCs (BM-MSCs) and neonatal Umbilical Cord-derived MSCs (UC-MSCs), with emphasis on their relative ability to transfer functional mitochondria and thereby disrupt the oxidative stress-inflammation axis, for the development of a disease etiology-based therapy. Using both in vivo preclinical model and in vitro cellular injury model, we explored the effect of mitochondrial transfer therapy in restoring mitochondrial dynamics and cellular bioenergetic deficits via PGC1α signaling at the systemic, cellular, and molecular levels.

Results

Time-course analysis reveals distinct transcriptomic signatures during the progression of IRI-AKI

Preclinical model of IRI-AKI provides insights into inflammatory and metabolic pathways

Creating a clinically relevant model of IRI-AKI, involving temporary disruption of blood supply to both kidneys,32 is essential for uncovering molecular signatures of disease progression and defining the optimal therapeutic window for early intervention. In the current study, we utilized a surgical bilateral IRI-AKI model (BIRI-AKI), involving ligation of both renal arteries to induce warm ischemia in each kidney for 30 min followed by reperfusion (Supplementary Fig. 1, Supplementary Information). Transcriptomic profiling of kidney tissue at different timepoints was used to determine the molecular signatures associated with the progression of BIRI-AKI. Principal component analysis (PCA) of all protein-coding genes displayed a sequential temporal trajectory during the acute phase (6–72 h), with variance emerging at 24 h and increasing through 72 h (Fig. 1a); over 95% of the variance among differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was retained in the first six Principal Components (PC1–PC6) (Supplementary Fig. 2a). The top 200 transcripts contributing to each of the six PC axes (Supplementary Fig. 2b) were used to develop a time-course analysis using unsupervised hierarchical clustering to study the progression of BIRI-AKI, identifying seven significant temporal modules with correlation coefficients of 0.3 ≤ R ≤ 1 (Fig. 1b). Five of the modules showed an upregulation of mRNA expression compared to healthy animals, while two modules showed a downregulation of mRNA expression compared to healthy animals (Fig. 1c). Pathways associated with inflammatory responses, such as TNFα, IL-6 production, and Type II interferon signaling (m1, m2, m7), were upregulated as early as 6 h. However, significantly more inflammatory pathways corresponding to a stronger inflammatory response, like Neutrophil, Monocyte and Lymphocyte chemotaxis, response to IL-1, TNFα and IL-17, Type I interferon responses (m5) were upregulated between 48–72 h. In contrast, pathways related to angiogenesis, extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, and blood vessel formation were upregulated at 6 h (m1, m2), but not sustained at later timepoints. Pathways related to oxidative stress, mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) depolarization, and cellular apoptosis were significantly upregulated at 24 h (m4). Concomitantly, cellular metabolic functions including mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC) assembly, ATP biosynthesis, and fatty acid β-oxidation (m6) were downregulated starting at 24 h (Fig. 1c). Interestingly, mRNA expression for early tubular injury markers, NGAL (Lcn2) and KIM-1 (Havcr1), was found to peak at 24 h (Supplementary Fig. 2c) while mRNA expression for genes associated with mitochondrial biogenesis (Ppargc1α) and mitochondrial ROS scavenging (Sod2) were downregulated (Supplementary Fig. 2d). mRNA expression of the “master regulator” of inflammation, Tnfα, remained elevated from 6–48 h, while specific chemokines such as Il18 (6 h) and Cxcl1 (24–48 h) were elevated at specific timepoints (Supplementary Fig. 2e). BM-MSCs show enhanced efficacy over UC-MSCs in mitigating damage from BIRI-AKI

MSCs from various sources have been investigated in several preclinical studies for treating IRI-AKI with promising results. However, research that can match source-specific MSCs to the molecular mechanisms driving the disease etiology of IRI-AKI is still lacking.

Precision delivery of MSCs enhances survival at a lower dose in BIRI-AKI

To mitigate loss of MSCs in the lung vasculature following conventional intravenous (IV) delivery and to promote spatial coupling of MSCs within the kidney microenvironment, we developed a microsurgical technique for intra-arterial (IA) delivery of MSCs directly into the kidney via the renal artery, which can be performed in the same surgical event as BIRI-AKI induction (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. 3a). Biodistribution studies with luciferase-expressing BM-MSCs confirmed the higher localization and persistence of BM-MSCs at 24 h in the kidney following IA delivery compared to IV delivery (Supplementary Fig. 3b). Dose-response analysis following IA delivery of MSCs identified 0.75 × 106 cells/animal as the optimal dose, where survival was the highest compared with BIRI-AKI mice injected with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) only (Supplementary Fig. 3c). When comparing survival outcomes between IA and IV delivery of MSCs at the optimal dose, IA delivery resulted in improved survival outcomes compared to IV delivery (Supplementary Fig. 3d). At the optimized dose, no significant alterations were observed on healthy kidney biochemical parameters (Supplementary Fig. 3e, f). Additionally, qualitative histopathological analysis of kidney tissue of mice with sham surgery receiving optimized BM-MSC dose did not show renal cast formation (Supplementary Fig. 3g). Interestingly, mice undergoing sham surgery with IA delivery of a higher BM-MSCs dose (≥1.5 × 106 cells/animal) showed a high aggregation of damaged renal epithelial cells (Supplementary Fig. 3g). BM-MSCs improve renal function and prevent cell damage following BIRI-AKI

To determine the comparative efficacy of different sources of MSCs for BIRI-AKI, we performed a comparative evaluation of MSCs from two different sources, BM-MSCs and UC-MSCs, for treating BIRI-AKI. All animals receiving IA infusion of MSCs showed improved survival compared to untreated controls, but Kaplan–Meier analysis showed statistically significantly higher survival rate for BM-MSCs only compared to BIRI (Fig. 2b). Although no statistically significant difference was observed between BM-MSCs and UC-MSCs, at 48 h the survival rate of mice receiving UC-MSCs declined to 60%, while mice receiving BM-MSCs maintained a survival rate of 80%. Notably, over a 7-day period, mice receiving BM-MSCs maintained a survival rate exceeding 80%, while those receiving UC-MSCs declined to 30%. Histopathological analysis revealed a statistically significant reduction in renal cast formation only in the BM-MSC group; however, no significant difference was observed between the BM-MSC and UC-MSC groups (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Fig. 4a). This lack of significance may be attributed to both types of MSCs secreting immunomodulatory factors, which could impact cellular debris aggregation and the subsequent activation of inflammatory signaling pathways necessary for cast formation. Analysis of kidney biochemical parameters showed a consistent decrease in both serum BUN and creatinine levels compared to the BIRI group, with only the BM-MSCs group (Fig. 2d, e). However, although we observed a significant difference between the ability of BM-MSCs and UC-MSCs to reduce serum BUN, no statistical difference was observed for serum creatinine levels. This lack of significance may be attributed to the higher sensitivity of BUN to renal perfusion and acute changes, while serum creatinine is dependent on changes in skeletal muscle mass and may show lower sensitivity. Interestingly, the mRNA expression for molecular markers of early tubular injury, NGAL and KIM‑1, was consistently decreased in the BM-MSCs group compared to the BIRI group, while also showing a statistically significant difference with the UC-MSCs group (Fig. 2f, g). Together, these findings indicate a superior efficacy of BM-MSCs compared to UC-MSCs in rescuing from BIRI-AKI.

Given the kidney’s complexity and diverse cell types, we investigated the ability of different MSC sources to protect specific renal cell populations from damage. For epithelial cells lining the Proximal Convoluted Tubule (PCT), treatment with BM-MSCs partially restored the expression of PCT marker (Kap), and genes associated with water reabsorption (Aqp2, Atp6v1b1) and solute transport (Slc22a12, Slc16a18), affected by BIRI-AKI (Fig. 2h). Podocytes, which constitute the glomerular filtration barrier, also exhibited a decrease in mRNA expression of critical filtration barrier and nutrient absorption genes (Umod, Slc27a2, Lrp2), which was partially restored following treatment with BM-MSCs (Fig. 2i). Among the podocyte differentiation markers, expression of Podxl and Nphs1 remained unchanged while Nphs2 expression was significantly reduced following BIRI-AKI. The BM-MSCs treated group also showed an increase in both Nphs1 and Nphs2 expression. In contrast, genes in the Distal Convoluted Tubule (DCT) and Collecting Duct (CD) were not affected by BIRI-AKI (Supplementary Fig. 4b, c). Interestingly, BIRI-AKI also affected mRNA expression of VEGF receptors (Vegfr1, Kdr) in endothelial cells, which was restored by BM-MSCs (Fig. 2j). Concomitantly, BIRI-AKI increased mRNA expression of fibroblast-associated markers (Col4a2, Col4a1, Vimentin), which was significantly reduced following BM-MSC treatment (Fig. 2k). Together, these findings indicate that BM-MSCs can effectively protect various renal cell populations following BIRI-AKI.

BM-MSCs show enhanced efficacy over UC-MSCs in mitigating activation of the oxidative stress-inflammation axis

PCA analysis of DEGs between the different MSC treated groups at 24 h indicated lower variance between BM-MSCs treated and healthy groups, as opposed to variance between UC-MSC treated and healthy groups (Supplementary Fig. 5a). Unsupervised hierarchical clustering of 1578 DEGs (Supplementary Data 2) identified nine significant temporal modules with correlation coefficient of 0.1 ≤ R ≤ 1 (Supplementary Fig. 5b). Gene Ontology (GO) pathway analysis of DEGs associated with BM-MSCs treatment compared to BIRI-AKI (m2, m5, and m8), indicated downregulation of apoptosis and inflammatory pathways, while upregulating β-fatty acid oxidation and long-chain fatty acid metabolism that occurs in mitochondria (Fig. 3a). In addition, subcellular structures associated with these modules indicated that 19% of mRNA changes occurred within the mitochondria (Fig. 3b), validating the effectiveness of BM-MSCs in restoring mitochondrial dysfunction that helps to address any cellular bioenergetic deficits. In contrast, DEGs associated with treatment with UC-MSCs (m4 and m6) highlight the ability of UC-MSCs in mitigating innate immune responses and promoting DNA damage repair in the nucleus (Fig. 3c). Notably, subcellular structures associated with UC-MSC treatment indicated mRNA changes related to cell division machinery, but no changes related to the mitochondrion (Fig. 3d). In addition to these modules, hierarchical clustering also identified four additional modules (m1, m3, m7 and m9) associated with mRNA changes in the renal tissue due to UC-MSCs (Supplementary Fig. 5c). Although no significant pathways (FDR < 0.05) were observed with these modules, analysis of the subcellular structure revealed these mRNA changes affected the external side of plasma membrane, apical plasma membrane and basolateral membrane, which are associated with ion transport (Supplementary Fig. 5d). BM-MSCs prevent oxidative stress and mitochondrial damage following BIRI-AKI

To investigate the role of MSCs in mitigating oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction during BIRI-AKI, we developed an in vitro cellular injury model, where human RPTECs (HK-2) were subjected to a cycle of hypoxia followed by normoxia, effectively simulating the cellular response to IRI. The superior efficacy of BM-MSCs in comparison to UC-MSCs in preventing oxidative stress and mitochondrial damage from IRI was also confirmed in our in vivo model. BM-MSCs can regulate inflammation following BIRI-AKI

To understand the role of oxidative stress in activating pro-inflammatory signaling, we investigated the correlation between tubular injury markers, mitochondrial function, and inflammation. In our in vivo BIRI-AKI model, we found a strong negative Pearson correlation between tubular injury biomarkers Lcn2, Havcr1, and representative genes involved in mitochondrial biogenesis (Ppargc1α), mitochondrial β-oxidation (Acadm), and mitochondrial ROS scavenging (Sod2). Conversely, the mitochondrial damage sensor and mitophagy activator Pink1, as well as canonical mediators of inflammation, including Cxcl1, Ccl11, Ccl2, Il1β, and Il6, exhibited positive Pearson correlation with Lcn2 and Havcr1 (Fig. 4i). A temporal Pearson correlation of Lcn2 with mitochondrial function and inflammatory mediators during the progression of BIRI-AKI (6–72 h), further validated the inverse relationship between compromised mitochondrial function and inflammation (Fig. 4j). In vitro studies demonstrated that both BM-MSCs and UC-MSCs significantly reduced the cytokine expression of master regulators of inflammation and apoptosis (TNFα, IL1β) (Supplementary Fig. 5e, f), however BM-MSCs showed greater efficacy in suppressing TNFα expression compared to UC-MSCs. In addition, only BM-MSCs were able to significantly attenuate IL6 and IL8 expression, which are transcriptionally regulated by oxygen-sensing Hif1α (Fig. 4k, l). The differential ability of source-dependent MSCs in regulating different chemokine signaling was also confirmed in our in vivo model. Although BM-MSCs and UC-MSCs were able to significantly reduce the cytokine expression of TNFα and IL18, only BM-MSCs were able to dampen the expression of chemokines specific for neutrophil-recruitment (IL6, CXCL1) and neutrophil-mediated immune signaling (CCL1, CCL22) (Supplementary Fig. 5g). Flow cytometric analysis of kidney immune cells also confirmed an increase in the infiltration of CD11b+Ly6G+ neutrophils following BIRI-AKI; however, this population was significantly reduced following BM-MSC treatment (Fig. 4m). In addition, levels of Myeloperoxidase (MPO), an enzyme released by active neutrophils, were significantly lower following BM-MSC, but not after UC-MSC, treatment (Fig. 4n). In contrast, macrophage infiltration into kidney tissue was not significantly affected by BIRI-AKI (Supplementary Fig. 5h). Collectively, these findings suggest a crosstalk between mitochondrial damage, oxidative stress and the activation of neutrophil-mediated pro-inflammatory signaling following BIRI-AKI, which cannot be ameliorated by anti-inflammatory properties of MSCs alone.

BM-MSCs communicate via Cx43 gap junctions to disrupt the oxidative stress-inflammation axis following BIRI-AKI

Our data consistently demonstrated the superior efficacy of BM-MSCs compared to UC-MSCs in disrupting the oxidative stress-inflammation axis during BIRI-AKI to prevent renal damage. Therefore, we aimed to investigate the specific molecular signaling pathways activated during BIRI-AKI, which can contribute towards the differential efficacy between MSCs and their subsequent development in disease etiology-based therapeutics for BIRI-AKI.

Cx43 expression is elevated in renal tissue following IRI-AKI

Among the signaling molecules upregulated in the renal transcriptome following BIRI-AKI, we observed an increase in mRNA expression of connexins Cx43 (Gja1) and Cx31 (Gjb3) (Fig. 1c). A comprehensive comparison of the transcript levels of various gap junction family of genes in renal biopsy samples from 39 AKI patients (GSE139061),33,34 from multiple causes revealed that various connexins were consistently upregulated in patients with AKI compared to healthy individuals (Fig. 5a). In addition, among the various connexins identified in our in vivo BIRI-AKI model, we observed a significant increase in only Cx43 (Gja1) mRNA levels, which was downregulated following treatment with BM-MSCs, but not following treatment with UC-MSCs (Fig. 5b). Interestingly, hypoxic stimulation was also found to upregulate Cx43 expression levels in BM-MSCs, but no such increase was observed in UC-MSCs under the same conditions (Fig. 5c). Disrupting Cx43 signaling reduces the protective effect of BM-MSCs against BIRI-AKI

To validate the role of Cx43-mediated signaling in enhancing the efficacy of BM-MSCs in treating BIRI-AKI, we selectively silenced Cx43 in BM-MSCs using a targeted pool of siRNA (Supplementary Fig. 6a), without affecting viability (Supplementary Fig. 6b) or increasing mitoROS (Supplementary Fig. 6c). Loss of Cx43 did not impair the ability of BM-MSCs to generate ATP (Supplementary Fig. 6d) or alter cAMP mediated signaling pathways (Supplementary Fig. 6e), responsible for intracellular paracrine signaling and intercellular communications. However, in our in vitro IRI model, Cx43si-BM-MSCs showed a reduced capacity to improve cellular ATP production (Fig. 5d), restore MMP (Fig. 5e), and limit mitoROS production (Fig. 5f) compared with BM-MSCs. Our in vivo IRI-AKI model similarly confirmed the reduced ability of Cx43si-BM-MSCs, compared to BM-MSCs, to lower mRNA expression of NGAL (Lcn2) (Fig. 5g), PRKN (Parkin) (Fig. 5h), and restore expression for Sod2 (Fig. 5i). Interestingly, conditioned media from BM-MSCs exposed to hypoxic stimuli provided negligible therapeutic benefit both in our in vitro and in vivo IRI models, further validating a critical role of Cx43-mediated signaling in the superior therapeutic efficacy of BM-MSCs in BIRI-AKI. Consistent with these findings, flow cytometric profiling showed an increase in Epithelial-to-Mesenchymal Transition (EMT) phenotype following BIRI-AKI in RPTECs co-expressing the renal epithelial marker, Prominin-1 (CD133), and mesenchymal marker, CD44. The quasi-mesenchymal population (CD133+CD44+) was decreased following treatment with BM-MSCs, but not when treated with Cx43si-BM-MSCs (Fig. 5j). Analysis of serum BUN (Supplementary Fig. 6f) and creatinine (Supplementary Fig. 6g), together with infiltration and activation of CD11b+Ly6G+ neutrophils were also found to be higher in the group treated with Cx43si-BM-MSCs compared to animals treated with BM-MSCs (Supplementary Fig. 6h, i). Together, these findings underscore the critical role of Cx43-dependent gap junction signaling in facilitating coordinated cellular communications between spatially coupled MSCs and damaged kidney cells that subsequently can modulate the oxidative stress-inflammation axis.

Mitochondrial transfer via Cx43 gap junctions prevents mitochondrial dysfunction by PGC1α activation

Intercellular signaling mediated by Cx43 between damaged renal cells and BM-MSCs emerged as a pivotal factor in influencing the disease etiology-based therapeutic efficacy of BM-MSCs in BIRI-AKI. Considering the high mitochondrial density, energy-intensive activity of RPTECs, and their vulnerability to oxidative stress during IRI-AKI, we aimed to investigate the role of Cx43 signaling in facilitating therapeutic mitochondrial transfer to damaged RPTECs.

Cx43-mediated signaling facilitates mitochondrial transfer to injured RPTECs

BM-MSCs and Cx43si-BM-MSCs were labeled with MitoTracker Deep Red dye, which selectively binds to functional mitochondria. Labeled BM-MSCs were co-cultured with HK-2 cells in our in vitro cellular injury IRI model. Stressed HK-2 cells co-cultured with BM-MSCs exhibited a significantly higher mitochondrial signal compared to those co-cultured with Cx43si-BM-MSCs (Fig. 6a, Supplementary Fig. 7a). Flow cytometry-based quantification in the same model also confirmed higher mitochondrial signal in HK-2 cells co-cultured with BM-MSCs compared with Cx43si-BM-MSCs (Fig. 6b). To exclude overlap between signals from labeled BM-MSCs and mitochondrial fragments associated with apoptotic cells, HK-2 cells were negatively gated for viability dye and CD90+CD105+ BM-MSCs (Supplementary Fig. 7b). Interestingly, flow cytometric profiling of these injured HK-2 cells co-cultured with BM-MSCs also showed increased protein levels of the transcriptional factor NRF2 (Fig. 6c). Mitochondrial transfer promotes mitogenesis via PGC1α signaling

To determine how mitochondrial transfer influences NRF2-regulated pathways controlling mitochondrial biogenesis and homeostasis, we examined the downstream targets of NRF2. Relevant mitochondrial genes affected by IRI-AKI were identified using renal AKI biopsies (GSE139061)33,34 from multiple etiologies (Supplementary Fig. 7c). The genes were then ranked by their variance in our preclinical mouse transcriptomics dataset, identifying PGC1α (Ppargc1α) as the gene with the highest variance (Fig. 6e). In our in vitro cellular injury model, PGC1α protein levels decreased after IRI but were restored following co-culture with BM-MSCs, but not with Cx43si-BM-MSCs (Fig. 6f). However, the increase in PGC1α protein following co-culture with BM-MSCs was abolished with the mTORC1 inhibitor - Rapamycin, which aids in NRF2 translocation to nucleus and PGC1α regulation following high cellular ATP activity. We also observed a lower ratio when comparing protein expression for two subunits of the mitochondrial respiratory chain - succinate dehydrogenase subunit A (SDHA) and Cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COX-1), in injured HK-2 cells following co-culture with Cx43si-BM-MSCs compared to BM-MSCs (Fig. 6g). While SDHA is encoded by nuclear DNA and hence has a relatively stable expression, COX-1 is encoded in mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) and hence its expression levels are more susceptible to changes in mitogenesis. Hence, this reduced COX-1/SDHA ratio indicated impaired mitochondrial biogenesis with Cx43si-BM‑MSCs. Furthermore, the increase in mitogenesis observed after co‑culture with BM‑MSCs was abolished by PGC1α silencing. Together, these findings demonstrate that therapeutic mitochondrial transfer from BM‑MSCs can promote mitogenesis in RPTECs via PGC1α activation involving mTORC1 and NRF2-coupled signaling cascades.

Discussion

Kidneys are organs with a high metabolic rate that possess a dense microcirculatory system and a unique countercurrent exchange system, essential for efficient filtration of a large volume of blood.35 However, these features also create low intrinsic oxygen tension, making the kidneys highly susceptible to IRI following transient blood flow disruptions. In this study, we used temporal profiling of IRI-AKI in a small-animal model of BIRI to show that early disruptions in mitochondrial homeostasis and cellular metabolism (i.e., within 24 h post-IRI) are directly linked to subsequent activation of inflammatory signaling and loss of renal function, and that targeted delivery of MSCs can interrupt this trajectory via Cx43‑mediated mitochondrial transfer and PGC1α activation. Current IRI-AKI treatment strategies have notable gaps, particularly in targeting the mutually exacerbating oxidative stress and inflammatory pathways without adverse effects. Systemic antioxidants like levosimendan36 and ascorbic acid37 have shown disappointing clinical outcomes due to issues with organ specificity and potential nephrotoxicity. While cytokine inhibitors can temporarily mitigate systemic inflammation,38,39 their effects on restoring redox balance are unclear. To address this early interplay between oxidative stress and inflammation in the progression of IRI-AKI, we implemented a preventive therapy by delivering MSCs directly into the renal vasculature during the same surgical event as IRI induction. Our findings revealed that mitochondrial transfer from MSCs to damaged RPTECs via Cx43 gap junction signaling, which was activated in the kidney following IRI, could indeed rescue cellular bioenergetic deficits at early timepoints and promote subsequent molecular and functional recovery through the PGC1α transcriptional regulator pathway.

Despite promising therapeutic attributes of MSCs in preclinical models of AKI,40 there are currently only three registered clinical trials investigating the use of MSCs in treating IRI-AKI: NCT00733876,41 NCT04388761, and NCT02563366. In a recent meta-analysis of preclinical studies, the efficacy of MSCs from different sources in treating IRI-AKI showed that while MSCs could improve renal functional indices, their ability to reduce oxidative stress and pro-inflammatory cytokines was difficult to infer with high confidence.42 Previous work from our group has demonstrated that MSCs from different sources exhibit unique “genomic and proteomic signatures”30 that should be aligned with specific disease etiologies. Indeed, in prior work assessing cisplatin-AKI, we identified UC-MSCs as the optimal candidate to address the associated strong inflammation encountered in this setting.43 For treating IRI-AKI, although both BM-MSCs and UC-MSCs comparably reduced systemic inflammation and improved renal function and survival, BM-MSCs consistently showed superior recovery of cellular bioenergetic and metabolic deficits and maintenance of redox homeostasis, including limiting oxidative stress-driven neutrophil recruitment. This underscores the need to align the regenerative signature of source-specific MSCs with the disease etiology to maximize therapeutic benefit.

Interestingly, we found that upregulation of gap junction protein Connexin 43 (Cx43) in MSCs plays a crucial role in their differential protective effects in IRI-AKI, a benefit that diminishes when Cx43 is silenced. Previous research has extensively explored the ability of Cx43 transmembrane proteins to form intercellular channels for direct transfer of small signaling molecules following cellular response to tissue injury. However, the utility of these direct cell–cell communication channels to promote selective therapeutic cargo transfer and thereby enhance MSC efficacy in a disease etiology-based manner has not been previously explored. Unlike prior studies that predominantly targeted Cx43 to blunt propagation of ROS and DAMP signals,28,29,44 our data suggest that injury-induced Cx43 upregulation can be leveraged to enhance therapeutic mitochondrial transfer from MSCs to RPTECs, thereby restoring metabolic homeostasis in AKI.

Although several in vitro studies have explored the ability of spatially coupled MSCs to transfer mitochondria to various cells, including cardiomyocytes, neural stem cells, lung epithelial cells, T cells, and macrophages,45 their in vivo validation has been restricted due to MSCs becoming trapped in the lungs - the “pulmonary first-pass effect”,45 following IV delivery.46 Indeed, to date, the only study exploring the effects of horizontal mitochondrial transfer between MSCs and damaged cells in vivo has been conducted in the ALI lung disease model.47,48 Our group has previously developed locoregional delivery of BM-MSCs into the mouse pancreas, which was shown to improve glycemic control compared to IV delivery of the same dose of MSCs.49 Building on the success of precision delivery, we have developed a similar innovative delivery technique for IA administration of MSCs directly into the kidney. Given that IA renal access is routinely achieved in interventional radiology, these data support the feasibility of perioperative precision MSC delivery for patients at high risk of IRI-AKI. This approach has provided crucial evidence for the importance of spatially coupling MSCs at the injury site to effectively investigate the role of Cx43-based gap junction signaling in mitochondrial transfer following IRI-AKI.

Investigation into the mechanism by which integration of exogenous mitochondria regulates the endogenous mitochondrial network has not been fully resolved. Most studies exploring mitochondrial therapy have provided evidence for the functional effects of mitochondrial transfer in terms of recipient cell metabolism maintenance, systemic wound healing, and immune cell deactivation. However, exogenous mitochondrial transfer alone cannot fully account for the recovery of mitochondrial mass and function. Our findings suggest that exogenous mitochondrial transfer may augment cellular resilience to oxidative stress in IRI-AKI at least in part by boosting ATP-dependent mTORC1 and NRF2 signaling, leading to mitochondrial biogenesis via PGC1α activation, as this effect was attenuated when Cx43-mediated mitochondrial transfer was disrupted. Although the upregulation of the PGC1 family has been shown to provide protection in AKI, no study to date has specifically investigated the impact of exogenous mitochondrial transfer on PGC1α and its co-activators. Using both our preclinical data, as well as human metadata analysis, we highlight the therapeutic role of PGC1α in IRI-AKI.

While this study provides critical data on the mechanism by which MSCs can be used as a specific therapy to regulate cellular bioenergetics of RPTECs in IRI, limitations include inferring the cellular composition and heterogeneity of a sample based on aggregated bulk measurements, which may mask the gene expression of low-abundance cell types, leading to underrepresentation of cellular interactions. In addition, data derived from changes in mRNA transcriptome levels may not directly correlate with protein abundance due to post-translational modifications and differences in half-life between mRNA and proteins. Future studies will focus on high-throughput multi-omics analyses that enable simultaneous analysis of protein markers with gene expressions (mRNA) within the same cell to directly map affected cell types with protein expressions, which will help to distinguish changes between various subsets of epithelial and immune cell populations, as well as rare endothelial cells. Mechanistically, the current study provides evidence that Cx43-mediated signaling in IRI establishes stable cell–cell contacts that facilitate mitochondrial transfer and enhance the efficacy of MSC therapy, but the relative contribution of distinct Cx43-dependent trafficking routes remains unresolved. Future studies will therefore focus on quantitative partitioning of mitochondrial trafficking among Cx43-dependent mechanisms, including regulation of mitochondrial ATP production and resistance to oxidative stress when Cx43 localizes to mitochondria,50 and facilitation of TNT formation and mitovesicle/extracellular vesicle uptake when Cx43 resides at the plasma membrane,51–53 by using live-cell imaging and ultrastructural EM. From a translational perspective, the current study only focused on male mice for identifying the molecular progression of IRI-AKI since males are at higher risk of IRI-AKI compared to females,54 and future studies will profile the molecular progression of IRI-AKI in females to define sex-specific responses to BM-MSC therapy. Finally, to avoid model-specific bias, future work will also evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of IA delivery of MSCs in other AKI models, such as cisplatin-AKI and folic acid nephropathy.

Together, our study provides strong evidence supporting BM-MSCs as an etiology-informed therapy for IRI-AKI that can prevent mitochondrial dysfunction and cellular metabolic collapse associated with IRI. We also establish a robust precision intra-arterial delivery technique for locoregional administration of therapeutics into the kidney while minimizing systemic exposure and toxicity, an approach that can be readily implemented by interventional radiologists using imaging-guided minimally invasive procedures. By enabling MSC delivery during high-risk perioperative periods and transplant surgeries, this strategy holds promise for early intervention protocols in patients at high risk for developing IRI-AKI.

Materials and methods

Cellular model of IRI

Cell culture protocols for BM-MSCs and HK-2 cells are included in Supplementary Methods. HK-2 cells were cultured to 70% confluency, then subjected to hypoxia (1–3% O2) for 48 h, following which they were returned to normoxic conditions with fresh media and co-cultured with BM-MSCs in a ratio of MSC:HK-2 (1:10) for an additional 24 h. For flow cytometry-based quantitative assessment of mitochondrial transfer, HK-2 cells were co-cultured with MitoTracker Deep Red-labeled BM-MSCs. Cells were stained with fluorochrome-labeled anti-human antibodies for CD90, CD105 (Biolegend, San Diego, CA, USA), for 1 h at 4 °C, washed with PBS, fixed, and permeabilized using Fixation buffer (Biolegend, San Diego, CA, USA) and Perm Wash Buffer (Biolegend, San Diego, CA, USA) for 1 h at RT. Intracellular cell staining was performed with NRF2 Rabbit Monoclonal Antibody (Cell Signaling, Danvers, MA, USA) in 1x Perm Buffer for 1 h at RT, followed by incubation with secondary anti-NRF2 antibody for 1 h at RT. The cells were washed 2x with PBS and resuspended in FACS staining solution prior to analysis with FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). Data were analyzed using FlowJo v10.2 (TreeStar, Ashland, OR, USA). All cellular biochemical assays were carried out according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Supplementary Methods), and measurements were taken using a microplate reader (Infinity 1000, Tecan, Mannedorf, Switzerland). In all assays, wells with only BM-MSCs were used as an internal control to subtract the signal contribution from the MSCs co-culture.

Cell imaging

BM-MSCs at 70–80% confluency were labeled with MitoTracker Red (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for 1 h. HK-2 cells were separately stained with TubulinTracker Green (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The labeled BM-MSCs and HK-2 cells were co-cultured in a ratio of MSC:HK-2 (1:5) on cell culture chamber slides (Nest Scientific, Woodbridge, NJ, USA) under in vitro cellular injury model conditions for 24 h. Before imaging, cellular nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33342 dye (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Cells were imaged using a Leica TSC SP8X White Laser Confocal Microscope (Wetzlar, Germany).

Transfection

BM-MSCs were transfected using a mixture of four small interfering RNAs targeted against Cx43 and PGC1α using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) in serum-free media with a siRNA:Lipofectamine ratio of 1:3 w/v to a final concentration of 10 nM for Cx43 and 20 nM for PGC1α. siRNA was designed based on SMARTpool ON-TARGETplus technology (Horizon Discovery, Waterbeach, UK). ON-TARGET non-targeting scrambled siRNA was used as a control.

Murine model of BIRI with locoregional delivery of MSCs

All animal experiments were performed under approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Stanford University. Male C57BL/6 mice at 6–8 weeks of age were acquired from Charles River Laboratories (Wilmington, MA, USA). For induction of bilateral ischemia reperfusion injury (BIRI), the right renal artery and left renal artery were occluded using a reversible ligation knot with #6 silk suture for 30 min, at which time the knot was reversed to allow renal perfusion. Following induction of BIRI, select mice received IA delivery of MSC injection with 0.75 × 106 MSCs in 100 µl of PBS. The mouse aorta was ligated just above and below the renal artery branches, and MSCs were delivered into the kidneys via a direct aortic injection using a 36-gauge NanoFil needle with an inner diameter of 25 μm (WPI, Sarasota, FL, USA). The injection rate was slow and steady, not increasing to more than 2 μl/sec. At the end of the infusion, the needle was removed and light pressure applied to the injection site using a cotton tip for 1 min to attain hemostasis. Mice in the BIRI group were also injected with only 100 µl of PBS, and healthy controls did not undergo any surgical intervention or treatment. Cells were stained with Trypan blue (1:1 ratio) and counted using the Countess 3 Cell Counter (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and only live cell populations were considered for injection into mice.

BM-MSC biodistribution

Luciferase-expressing BM-MSCs (Luc+BM-MSCs) were isolated from C57BL/6 mutant mice. 1 × 106 Luc+BM-MSCs were injected by IV or IA method in healthy C57BL/6 mice. PBS was used as a control. 24 h post-injection, the animals received an intraperitoneal injection of luciferin at a dosage of 150 mg/kg. Following a 5 min interval to allow for optimal bioluminescent signal development, bioluminescence imaging was conducted using a Lago Optical Imaging System (Spectral Instrument Imaging, Tucson, AZ, USA).

Cell profiling

Single cell suspensions were obtained from harvested kidney tissue following digestion with 1 mg/mL collagenase (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 30 min at 37 °C. Cell suspensions were filtered with 70 µm strainers, centrifuged at 500 g for 5 min, and single cells devoid of RBC were stained with Fc antibody for 20 min at 4 °C to prevent non-specific binding and then stained with DEAD cell staining (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Next, cells were stained with fluorochrome-labeled anti-mouse antibodies for CD11b, Ly6G, CD86 and CD206, CD45, CD13, CD133, and CD44 prior to analysis with FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). Data were analyzed using FlowJo v10.2 (TreeStar, Ashland, OR, USA). All antibodies were purchased from BioLegend (San Diego, CA, USA).

Histopathology

Harvested kidney tissue was fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin. Histopathological changes were analyzed using hematoxylin and eosin stain, and tubular injury was scored by assessing the number of renal casts formed per 1 mm2 area of renal parenchyma, using a NanoZoomer slide scanner 2.0-RS (Hamamatsu, Hamamatsu City, Japan) with subsequent analysis performed using ImageJ.55

qPCR and transcriptomics

Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). qRT-PCR was performed using iTaq Universal One-Step RT-qPCR kit (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) by the SYBR GREEN method. Primers were synthesized at Integrated DNA Technologies (Coralville, IA, USA) (Supplementary Data 1) with GAPDH used as an internal control. For transcriptomics, paired-end 150 bp sequencing was performed using the HiSeq 4000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), reads were aligned against the mouse reference genome GRCm39, and DESeq256 was used for differential gene expression analysis. DEGs were defined with a significance threshold of absolute log2(fold change) > 1.5 and p.adj < 0.05 (Supplementary Data 2). Further data analysis was done in R 4.4.3 (R Core Team, 2025). Different kidney cell populations were identified by deconvoluting our bulk transcriptomic data using single-cell genomics datasets (GSE107585).57

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed in GraphPad Prism 10 (San Diego, CA, USA). Variables among multiple independent groups were compared using a non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test to account for non-normal data distribution. If the Kruskal–Wallis test indicated significant differences, a post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the Two-stage linear step-up procedure of Benjamini, Krieger, and Yekutieli,58 to identify specific pairwise differences between the groups. For time-dependent variables across multiple groups, a mixed ANOVA was applied, followed by Sidak’s post hoc correction for pairwise comparisons. A P value (adjusted p value for non-parametric and mixed ANOVA), P ≤ 0.05, was considered statistically significant.
