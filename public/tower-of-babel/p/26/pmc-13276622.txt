Mexico-Based Accompaniment Collectives for Abortion in the US After Dobbs
Authors: Alexandra Wollum, Sofia Braunstein, Sandra H. Cardona Alanís, Verónica Cruz Sánchez, Sofia Filippa, Sarah E. Baum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionWhat are the characteristics of requests in the US for self-managed medication abortion support through Mexico-based accompaniment collectives?FindingsThis cross-sectional study using records for 2850 requests from 2 Mexico-based accompaniment collectives in 2023 found that use of accompaniment collective support was associated with living in a state with a complete abortion ban and living in areas with higher poverty levels, levels of uninsurance, and larger Spanish-speaking populations.MeaningThese findings suggest that accompaniment collectives supporting people to self-manage their abortion using evidence-based protocols are important care sources for people with limited access to abortion clinics, with fewer resources, and who face language barriers.

Introduction

Legal abortion care is difficult, if not impossible, to access in many US states. Following the Dobbs v Jackson Women’s Health Organization (hereafter, Dobbs) ruling that overturned the constitutional right to abortion in June 2022, as of September 2025, 17 states banned abortion completely or early in pregnancy with few exceptions.1 Even prior to Dobbs, access to abortion was curtailed by cost, logistical and information barriers, stigma, and policy restrictions, which disproportionately impact communities marginalized by systems of oppression, including racism, classism, and xenophobia.2,3,4 Navigating these barriers and abortion bans means that people seeking abortion in states without legal access must travel to receive in-person care, seek abortion care through telemedicine, or self-manage their abortion. People may also prefer telemedicine or self-managed abortion models of care for a variety of reasons over clinic-based care, including being in the privacy and comfort of their homes, convenience, autonomy, avoiding travel and associated logistics, affordability, or a desire to avoid medical institutions.4,5

Self-managing an abortion is defined as having an abortion without clinical support.6 Self-managing an abortion with either mifepristone and misoprostol or misoprostol alone is a safe and effective way to end a pregnancy; however, it may entail legal risk to the pregnant person.7,8,9 Self-managed medication abortion has increased dramatically since Dobbs, and community networks have facilitated access for abortion seekers. In a recent study,10 an estimated additional 28 000 people accessed self-managed abortions in the 6 months following the Dobbs decision. However, little is known about those who access abortions through community networks such as accompaniment collectives. Accompaniment is a model of abortion care that supports people to access safe abortion through community-based strategies, often through trained lay feminist activists who share information and support to people mainly outside of clinical contexts.7,11 In 2021, in direct response to the shifting legal climate in the US, initially following Senate Bill 8 in Texas,12 activists in Mexico expanded their capacity to offer abortion accompaniment to people in the US by text message or telephone call, providing counseling and support, and sending medications to any US address at no cost. In this study, we sought to describe the individuals who accessed self-managed medication abortion in the US through 2 accompaniment collectives located in Mexico.

Methods

Data Sources

This cross-sectional study was designed and implemented as a partnership between Ibis Reproductive Health, a global nonprofit organization working to conduct rigorous research to advance sexual and reproductive autonomy, choices, and health, and 2 abortion accompaniment collectives based in Mexico. We used records of support requests in 2023 to characterize people from the US seeking accompanied abortion care from these collectives. Records did not include names or contact information of people requesting support.

Accompaniment collective 1 received most individual requests for support through 2 primary telephone lines, and some additional requests through email. In addition, people in the US occasionally requested multiple packs of pills at one time. We gathered all available records for direct requests from individuals requesting 1 to 3 packs of pills from the 2 primary telephone lines, which we estimate to capture 75% of the records during the study period (bulk orders of >3 packs were included in the denominator). We had no way to know how many packs of pills were requested for each record. For accompaniment collective 2, we gathered information on all individuals requesting single packs of pills in 2023 who contacted the organization via WhatsApp and by telephone. We did not receive information to identify individuals within the records from either collective; if an individual requested support more than once, they appear more than once in the data.

Both accompaniment collectives asked a limited set of questions to those they supported, including age, pregnancy duration, and geographic information. Pregnancy duration primarily reflected self-report of weeks since the first day of the last menstrual period, but a small proportion reported according to ultrasonography dating. Where this information was missing in accompaniment records, the person requesting support either did not provide the information or it was not recorded. One collective also recorded the language of the interaction and where people had learned about the collective. Because the information about the individuals supported by the 2 collectives was limited, we also relied on data from the 5-year 2022 and 2023 American Community Survey about the zip code tabulation area (ZCTA; identified on the basis of zip code) provided. Finally, we used information on clinic locations from the Myers Abortion Facility Database13,14 to calculate the distance from the nearest abortion clinic.

This study was approved by the WCG Ethical Review Committee. Informed consent from study participants was not obtained given this study involved a retrospective review of existing records, and no direct contact with study participants occurred. We report our findings in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines for cross-sectional studies.

Variables

We assigned each request to a state-level abortion policy category according to the state reported at the time of contacting the accompaniment collective. Categories reflected state abortion bans or gestational limits: states with complete abortion bans, states with a 6-week or embryonic cardiac activity ban, states with a 12- to 15-week ban, and states where abortion was available past 15 weeks. Because laws changed during our study period, this variable was time varying. We also created a binary variable categorizing states into 2 policy categories: states with complete abortion bans and states without these bans.

Within the records of those who requested support from the 2 accompaniment collectives, we created categories of age (<20 years, 20-24 years, 25-34 years, ≥35 years, and missing) and pregnancy duration at the time of contact (≤6 weeks, 7-9 weeks, 10-11 weeks, 12-13 weeks, ≥14 weeks, and unknown). We also created categories of ZCTA-level variables based on variable quantiles, applying rounded cut points rather than the exact quantile values for ease of interpretation, including the proportion of people aged 5 years or older who speak Spanish in the ZCTA (0%, >0%-2%, 3%-6%, or ≥7%), the percentage of people with incomes at or below the poverty level in the ZCTA (≤5%, 6%-9%, 10%-16%, ≥17%, or unknown), and the percentage of female individuals uninsured in the ZCTA (≤2%, 3%-5%, 6%-10%, ≥11%, or unknown).

We used the Myers Abortion Facility Database13 to calculate the driving time from the population-weighted centroid of every ZCTA to the nearest abortion facility in operation for each month in 2023 using the Open Street Map package in R. We calculated ZCTA population–weighted centroids using the ZCTA TIGER shapefile and rasterized census block American Community Survey population estimates at the 1000-by-1000 m resolution.15 We created a categorical variable to group ZCTAs based on driving time to the nearest abortion facility averaging over the 12 months of 2023: 30 minutes or less, 31 to 60 minutes, more than 1 hour to 2 hours, more than 2 hours to 4 hours, more than 4 hours to 8 hours, and more than 8 hours. We used data from the 5-year 2023 American Community Survey that captured how many female individuals aged 15 to 49 years lived in each ZCTA as the best approximation of a denominator for the population who may obtain an abortion, recognizing that people who do not identify as women also need and access abortions.

Statistical Analysis

We described the frequency and proportion of requests for support to the accompaniment collectives by sociodemographic and pregnancy characteristics overall and stratified by state-level abortion policy category. We tested differences in request characteristics by our state-level abortion policy category using χ2 tests. Statistical significance was defined as 2-sided P < .05.

To describe how the overall rate of people being supported by these accompaniment collectives varied by ZCTA-level characteristics within the contiguous US, we calculated the rate of requests per 100 000 female individuals aged 15 to 49 years within categories of ZCTA-level characteristics using estimates from bivariable negative binomial models with the number of people requesting support from the accompaniment collectives as the outcome and the number of female individuals aged 15 to 49 years living in the ZCTA as an offset. We included a random effect on state to model correlation between ZCTAs in the same state. The mean rate in each ZCTA strata and the Wald 95% CIs were calculated using model results. We excluded 6 ZCTAs from our analysis that were unpopulated. ZCTAs that were missing ZCTA-level variables were excluded from analyses of those characteristics. We also ran these models stratified by our binary measure of the state abortion policy context (states with complete abortion bans at any point in 2023 vs states that never had a complete abortion ban in 2023). We used a crosswalk of zip codes to ZCTAs from UDS Mapper through the package zippeR in R to map the zip codes from accompaniment requests to a corresponding ZCTA. The analysis was conducted in R statistical software version 4.5.1 (R Project for Statistical Computing).

Results

We analyzed 2850 records from people in the US requesting support from 2 Mexico-based accompaniment collectives in 2023. The majority of requests were from states with complete abortion bans (2363 requests [82.9%]) (Table 1). Four percent (115 requests) lived in states with 6-week or embryonic cardiac activity bans, 4.5% (129 requests) lived in states with 12- to 15-week bans, and 8.5% (243 requests) lived in states where abortion was available past 15 weeks. Although most people were aged 20 to 34 years (700 [24.6%] were aged 20-24 years and 995 [34.9%] were aged 25-34 years), 192 (6.7%) were younger than 20 years; however, age was unknown for 782 requests (27.4%). Approximately one-half of requests reported pregnancies of 6 weeks or less (1441 requests [50.6%]), and 677 (23.8%) reported pregnancies of 7 to 9 weeks. A small proportion of pregnancies were 12 weeks or more (160 requests [5.6%]). For the accompaniment collective that documented language (2528 requests), 2090 people (82.7%) communicated with the accompaniment collective in English. The most common channel through which people learned about this accompaniment collective was either their social network of friends, family, and/or partner (510 requests [20.2%]) or a hotline (317 requests [12.5%]); however, the information channel was unknown for more than one-half of requests (1481 requests [58.6%]).

Pregnancy duration, language spoken, and how requesters learned about the collective differed by state abortion policy (Table 1). In states with less-restrictive abortion policies, a larger proportion of the people requesting support from the Mexican accompaniment collectives were further along in pregnancy; 36 requests (14.8%) in states where abortion was available past 15 weeks, 13 requests (10.1%) in states with 12- to 15-week bans, 13 requests (11.3%) in states with 6 week bans, and 98 requests (4.2%) in states with complete bans had pregnancies that were 12 or more weeks (P < .001). As the abortion context became more legally permissive, a larger proportion of people communicated with the accompaniment collective in Spanish (65 [38.0%] in states where abortion was available past 15 weeks, 35 [33.0%] in states with 12- to 15-week bans, 22 [20.4%] in states with 6-week bans, and 282 [13.2%] in states with complete bans; P < .001). More people reported hearing about the accompaniment collective through their social network in states with abortion bans compared with states where it was available past 15 weeks (469 requests [21.9%] vs 10 requests [5.8%]; P < .001).

Overall, the mean rate of people requesting support from the 2 accompaniment collectives per ZCTA in 2023 was 2.44 (95% CI, 1.81-3.08) per 100 000 female individuals aged 15 to 49 years (Table 2). The rate of people requesting support was highest in ZCTAs with higher percentages of people with incomes at or below the federal poverty level, with higher percentages of female individuals who were uninsured, and with higher percentages of the population speaking Spanish. In ZCTAs with up to 5% of the population with incomes at the federal poverty level or below, 0.35 (95% CI, 0.23-0.53) people per 100 000 female individuals aged 15 to 49 years requested support compared with 1.63 (95% CI, 1.14-2.34) requests per 100 000 female individuals in ZCTAs with 17% or more of the population with incomes at or below the poverty level. Considering language, the rate of request for support showed a monotonic pattern, with higher rates in ZCTAs that had successively larger shares of Spanish-speaking residents. In ZCTAs with 7% or more of Spanish-speaking residents, we observed a rate of 1.56 (95% CI, 1.03-2.39) people requesting support per 100 000 female individuals aged 15 to 49 years, whereas ZCTAs where 0% of the population spoke Spanish had rates of 0.25 (95% CI, 0.12-0.54) requests per 100 000 female individuals aged 15 to 49 years. The rate of requests for support was highest among ZCTAs more than 8 hours driving distance from an abortion facility (2.54 [95% CI, 1.58-4.09] requests per 100 000 female individuals aged 15 to 49 years) and relatively similar within ZCTAs that were closer to an abortion facility.

Stratified by whether there was a complete abortion ban in place at any point in 2023, states with complete abortion bans had markedly higher rates of requests for support than states without (6.64 [95% CI, 4.68-8.60] vs 0.47 [95% CI, 0.33-0.60] requests per 100 000 female individuals aged 15 to 49 years) (Table 2). We observed similar patterns in rates of requests by ZCTA-level poverty, uninsurance, and Spanish speaking in our stratified analysis as we did in the overall sample. In both states without complete abortion bans and states with complete abortion bans, ZCTAs with higher percentages of residents with incomes at or below the federal poverty line, higher proportions of uninsured female individuals, and larger Spanish-speaking populations had successively higher rates of requests for support from Mexican accompaniment collectives. We saw no pattern in rates of requests by driving distance to the nearest abortion facility within states that did not have complete abortion bans. In states with complete abortion bans in 2023, we observed the largest rate of requests within ZCTAs over 8 hours driving from the nearest abortion facility (7.85 [95% CI, 4.53-13.61] requests per 100 000 female individuals aged 15 to 49 years).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study, we describe the volume and geography of requests for self-managed medication abortion with accompaniment support from collectives in Mexico after Dobbs, as well as available demographic characteristics of those who requested support. Little research has examined self-managed abortion supported by accompaniment collectives after Dobbs. In this study, people who accessed medication abortion from accompaniment collectives lived predominantly in states where abortion was banned, and rates of request for accompaniment collective support were associated with living in areas with higher poverty levels, larger Spanish-speaking populations, and living far from an abortion facility in states where abortion was banned. This demonstrates the importance of this model of care in helping to fill gaps in care following Dobbs and the resulting state-level abortion bans. We know from past research that people who hold historically marginalized identities may face the most barriers to accessing abortion care.4,16,17 Recent research18 found that use of shield-law telemedicine abortion was highest in counties with complete abortion bans, far from abortion clinics, and with higher levels of poverty, similar to the results from the present study. Facility-based care may be particularly inaccessible for Hispanic and Latine people in states with abortion bans as the immigration environment becomes increasingly hostile and dangerous. Adolescents and young adults have also been shown to face disproportionate barriers to accessing facility-based care in states with bans.19 Abortion accompaniment offers a safe, effective, affordable, and person-centered option in a landscape where in-clinic care may be inaccessible.4,20,21

Although accompaniment collectives are practiced at navigating restricted legal contexts, people self-managing their abortion with accompaniment support face potential legal risk.22,23,24 Our findings show that those who have marginalized identities, such as those with economic insecurity and who speak Spanish, may be more likely to use this model. Thus, those requesting support from accompaniment collectives may be at heightened risk of criminalization on the basis of intersecting axes of social and economic inequities.25,26 Access to accurate legal information and risk assessments and research exploring people’s perception of legal risk is important to ensure people are equipped to navigate a volatile and ever-changing legal landscape.27

Our data also highlight the persistent barriers people face in states where abortion is legal. In states with legal abortion, rates of requests to the accompaniment collectives increased in areas with more uninsured people, people with incomes below the federal poverty guideline, and Spanish speakers, and a larger proportion of people spoke Spanish with the accompanier in more permissive contexts. Our findings suggest that this model may specifically address cost and language barriers. Furthermore, as the abortion policy environment became more permissive, the proportion of people requesting support after 12 weeks of pregnancy also increased. Many states have gestational limits on abortion that force abortion seekers with pregnancies beyond the legal limit to travel to find care.28 As options for individuals with pregnancies beyond 12 weeks narrow, some people may not be able to travel or afford in-person care, especially among marginalized populations, and turn to shield law telemedicine to mail pills29 or to self-manage their abortion with support from nonclinical care options.30,31,32 The free, person-centered, Spanish language concordant care provided by Mexico-based accompaniment collectives may have helped address these challenges after Dobbs.32,33,34

Limitations

Our analysis has several limitations. First, although it offers some of the most in-depth data currently available on self-managed abortion with Mexico-based accompaniment support in the US, the overall number of observations remains small relative to the broader population of individuals likely engaging in self-managed abortion. As such, our results should not be broadly generalized to people self-managing their abortions. Second, we relied on ZCTAs to analyze contextual-level factors that may be related to utilization of self-managed abortion; however, ZCTAs are heterogeneous and may obscure important variation in social and economic conditions within a given area. In addition, our dataset includes a limited number of individual-level sociodemographic variables, some with high levels of missingness, and does not capture race or ethnicity, which constrains our ability to examine how intersecting forms of marginalization and oppression may shape self-managed abortion experiences and access. We cannot identify individuals in the accompaniment records analyzed or quantify the number of pills packs requested. Third, our analysis cannot speak to how preferences for abortion models influence patterns of utilization. People may prefer to self-manage their abortion for a range of reasons regardless of the legal status of abortion in their context.

Conclusions

With the fall of Roe v Wade and increase in state bans on abortion, support from Mexico-based accompaniment collectives was one model of abortion care that emerged among others to increase access for those who no longer had clinic-based services or preferred abortion pills by mail. In 2023, Mexico-based accompaniment support was requested at higher rates in states with abortion bans and among people living in areas with higher rates of poverty, higher rates of uninsurance, and where more people spoke Spanish, suggesting this care model served those potentially facing cost and language barriers. Quality abortion care from community networks, along with access to accurate information, will continue to be an important option.
