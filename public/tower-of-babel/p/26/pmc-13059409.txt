Introduction

The widespread application of the organophosphorus flame retardant Tris(1,3-dichloro-2-propyl) phosphate (TDCPP) has established it as a global pollutant. As a primary replacement for the phased-out polybrominated diphenyl ether (PentaBDE), TDCPP is extensively used in household furniture, office supplies, and electronic products, leading to its detection in indoor dust at concentrations reaching the microgram per gram (µg/g) level [1]. This widespread environmental exposure has led to its extensive accumulation within the human body; its metabolite, BDCPP, can be detected in the serum and urine of over 90% of the general population [2–4]. An increasing amount of epidemiological evidence suggests that TDCPP exposure is associated with developmental toxicity, endocrine disruption, and adverse health outcomes [5–8]. Although direct epidemiological evidence linking TDCPP exposure to ovarian cancer is currently lacking, a critical clue has emerged: TDCPP exposure is significantly correlated with several core risk factors for ovarian cancer, such as hormonal imbalance and infertility [9–12]. This suggests that TDCPP may indirectly increase cancer risk by disrupting reproductive endocrine homeostasis and creating a microenvironment conducive to tumorigenesis. However, the potential molecular bridge connecting this “exposure–risk factor–malignant outcome” paradigm—i.e., a hypothetical biological model of exposure-associated disease progression—remains an unexplored “black box.” This knowledge gap not only limits the precision of risk assessment but also poses significant challenges in clinical practice: it is difficult for physicians to provide effective risk counseling for highly exposed populations based on current evidence, and public health authorities are constrained in their ability to formulate targeted preventive interventions due to the lack of definitive biomarkers, leaving large segments of the potentially at-risk population facing heightened health risks.

Concurrently, significant progress has been made in understanding the molecular pathology of ovarian cancer. A group of genes represented by MMP3, CYP3A4, and CYP3A43 have been confirmed as key prognostic factors for ovarian cancer invasion, metabolism, and hormonal balance [13–17]. Therefore, the core scientific question is: through which molecular pathways do environmental chemicals (such as TDCPP) affect these molecules closely associated with clinical outcomes to promote tumor progression? Traditional methods have consistently struggled to systematically decipher the key links from environmental exposure to core disease genes.

To address this gap, we propose an innovative “computational bridge” strategy: a multi-level, multi-dimensional framework integrating computational toxicology and network biology, aimed at exploring potential molecular links between TDCPP and ovarian cancer development. The implementation of this strategy draws upon established analytical methods that identify key molecules through the intersection of toxicological targets and disease-associated genes [18–20]. We extend and deepen this approach by integrating multi-omics data—including toxicity predictions, gene expression profiles, and miRNA regulatory networks—to identify key factors connecting TDCPP with experimentally validated key prognostic genes in ovarian cancer, thereby supporting the biological plausibility of our analytical strategy. On this basis, we further dissected the potential synergistic regulatory networks among these genes and their upstream regulatory mechanisms, ultimately constructing a hypothetical model of the systematic mechanism of action. Through a parallel comparison with other halogenated pollutants, we not only identified their unique toxicological pathways, but for the first time, this study identified a candidate miRNA signature and its molecular network associated with the co-regulation of genes related to tumor invasion and chemoresistance in the context of TDCPP exposure. Accordingly, we proposed a testable “liver-to-ovary crosstalk” model to interpret the potential mechanisms of this remote tumor progression.

The core value of this study lies in its dual contribution to “replicating the known” and “exploring the unknown.” First, by successfully predicting the association between TDCPP and known key molecules in ovarian cancer, we support the reliability of the integrated computational strategy. Second, building upon this foundation, we propose a hypothetical model based on liver-to-ovary distal crosstalk, which predicts a novel synergistic regulatory mechanism and provides a new perspective for understanding how the environmental pollutant TDCPP systematically and remotely affect human health. Specifically, multi-omics analysis identified a miRNA signature (miR-3677/3135/4436 families) closely associated with the co-regulation of genes related to tumor invasion and chemoresistance in the context of TDCPP exposure. This work provides a testable molecular framework (bioinformatics evidence) for exploring the pro-tumorigenic molecular mechanisms of TDCPP, and also offers a valuable reference strategy for investigating the complex health risks of other environmental chemicals.

Materials and methods

Chemical selection and computational toxicological assessment

This study selected nine organophosphate and halogenated organic compounds (Table 1), with the organophosphorus flame retardant TDCPP as the primary subject of investigation. The other compounds were chosen to facilitate systematic comparisons: Bis(1,3-dichloro-2-propyl) phosphate(BDCPP, a TDCPP metabolite), Bis(1-chloro-2-propyl) phosphate(BCPP), Tris(1-chloro-2-propyl)phosphate(TCPP), Tris(2-chloroethyl)phosphate(TCEP), Triphenyl phosphate(TPHP), and 2-Ethylhexyl diphenyl phosphate(EHDPP) (structural analogs or substitutes) for intra-class comparison within the phosphate group; and 2,2’,4,4’-Tetrabromodiphenyl ether(BDE-47) and 2,2’,4,4’,5,5’-Hexachlorobiphenyl(CB-153) (different classes of halogenated pollutants) for cross-class comparison of toxicological profiles. This strategy aims to systematically evaluate the unique toxicological characteristics of TDCPP and explore its structure-activity relationship. Using the ProTox 3.0 and ADMETlab 2.0 platforms, we systematically assessed the acute toxicity, organ toxicity, endocrine-disrupting effects, and ADME/T properties of all compounds [21, 22] to delineate their macro-toxicological profiles and provide a prioritized basis for subsequent target screening.

Systematic identification and screening of key targets: the foundation for constructing the “computational bridge” strategy

To comprehensively identify the potential targets of the compounds, we integrated three complementary strategies: PharmMapper (pharmacophore matching), the STITCH database (text mining), and SwissTargetPrediction (ligand similarity) [23–29]. The union of prediction results from all three methods was taken and filtered by a confidence threshold to obtain the potential target set for each compound.

To underpin the “computational bridge” strategy, we employed a “triple-evidence-chain” strategy to screen for key target genes: (1) broad-spectrum ovarian cancer-related genes retrieved from the GeneCards, OMIM, and CTD databases [30–36]; (2) differentially expressed genes in ovarian cancer derived from a differential analysis of the TCGA dataset (|log2FC| ≥ 0.585, adj. p ≤ 0.05); and (3) the compound’s potential target set. A Venn analysis was performed on these three gene sets, and their intersection was taken to obtain the core genes that simultaneously possess three lines of evidence: being “acted upon by the compound,” “associated with ovarian cancer,” and “aberrantly expressed in clinical samples.”

Notably, the core targets identified in this study (MMP3, CYP3A4, and CYP3A43) are all key prognostic molecules closely associated with ovarian cancer progression, as previously confirmed by wet lab studies [13–17]. These results provide support for the reliability of our computational screening strategy and a solid biological basis for subsequent mechanistic analysis, thereby laying the foundation for the application of the “computational bridge” research paradigm.

Multi-omics regulatory network analysis of core targets: constructing potential synergistic regulatory networks

To investigate the potential synergistic associations among the identified core targets (MMP3, CYP3A4, and CYP3A43), this study performed multi-omics data mining based on public databases, aiming to construct a logically coherent regulatory network model.

Transcriptional and Protein-Protein Interaction (PPI) analysis

The mRNA co-expression relationships among MMP3, CYP3A4, and CYP3A43 were assessed using the cBioPortal platform (containing 3262 cancer samples) [37–39]. A protein-protein interaction (PPI) network for their encoded proteins was constructed using the STRING database (confidence score > 0.7) to infer potential signaling pathways and functional modules [40].

miRNA regulatory network analysis

The miRWalk 4.0 database was used to predict miRNAs targeting the three aforementioned genes. A reliable miRNA-target gene interaction network was constructed by screening for miRNAs predicted by at least two databases and possessing good binding affinity [41].

Epigenetic and genomic variation analysis

The DNA methylation levels (β values) in the promoter regions of the core target genes were analyzed via the cBioPortal platform to investigate their epigenetic silencing status [42, 43]. Concurrently, copy number variations (CNVs) of these genes were analyzed in ovarian cancer cohorts from TCGA, CPTAC, and others to assess their genomic instability [37–39].

miRNA-based pathway-phenotype integrated analysis: inferring downstream biological significance

Given the limitations of traditional functional enrichment analysis in interpreting the interaction patterns of core target genes, this study adopted an miRNA-based integrated analysis strategy [44, 45]. First, a set of miRNAs capable of simultaneously regulating MMP3, CYP3A4, and CYP3A43 was screened. Subsequently, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed on this miRNA set using the TAM 2.0 tool [46]. This analytical logic of “target genes -> shared miRNAs -> shared pathways” aims to interpret, from a computational perspective, how these three target genes potentially synergistically influence downstream biological pathways and their association with the clinical phenotypes of ovarian cancer.

Cell type localization analysis and molecular docking simulation: exploring cellular origins and assessing potential interactions

Cell type localization: providing clues for the “liver-to-ovary crosstalk”

To explore the cellular origins of the core target genes and provide support for the hypothesis of “liver-to-ovary crosstalk,” this study searched the PanglaoDB database to analyze the expression distribution patterns of MMP3, CYP3A4, and CYP3A43 across various human cell types, with a focus on liver and reproductive system-related cells [47]. Although these genes are not exclusive to the ovary, this analysis provides clues at the cellular level by identifying the specific cell subtypes involved. Specifically, the analysis highlights the role of the liver as the primary source of CYP enzymes and confirms the expression of effector genes in ovarian cells, thereby providing a basis for the biological plausibility of inter-organ communication.

Molecular docking simulation: predicting potential molecular binding at the atomic level

To explore the theoretical feasibility of the potential molecular interaction between TDCPP and the target proteins, we employed the CB-Dock2 platform to perform molecular docking simulations [48–51]. The three-dimensional structures of MMP3 and CYP3A4 were obtained as crystal structures from the Protein Data Bank (PDB). All structures underwent pretreatment, including energy minimization, residue completion, and the removal of water molecules and cofactors, after which ligand binding pockets were automatically predicted using CB-Dock2. Molecular docking was executed with TDCPP as the ligand; the algorithm considered the conformational flexibility of both the protein and the ligand, and a scoring function based on the principles of AutoDock Vina was used to evaluate binding affinity. Finally, the binding affinity between TDCPP and the target proteins was assessed by analyzing the magnitude of the binding free energy, providing theoretical clues at the atomic level for understanding its potential toxicity mechanism. It should be noted that for CYP3A43, molecular docking was not performed due to the lack of a crystal structure in the PDB database. Given the high homology between CYP3A43 and CYP3A4, we referred to the molecular docking results of CYP3A4 to infer the potential interaction pattern of CYP3A43. This simulation aims to generate a theoretical model at the atomic level to predict the potential protein binding characteristics of TDCPP and to provide targets for subsequent wet lab validation (e.g., fluorescence polarization, SPR).

Results

Computational Toxicological Predictions Suggest Differentiated Toxicity Profiles of TDCPP and its analogues

Systematic virtual screening of nine target compounds using the ProTox 3.0 and ADMETlab 2.0 platforms indicated that they generally possess carcinogenic and endocrine-disrupting potential (Table 2). Notably, TDCPP, TCEP, BDE-47, and CB-153 exhibited very high predicted carcinogenicity scores. In terms of endocrine disruption, the computational results suggested a complex, compound-dependent regulatory pattern: TDCPP was predicted to be an aromatase activator, whereas its metabolite BDCPP and most other compounds were predicted as inhibitors. The coexistence of activation and inhibition suggests that these compounds may participate in the pathological processes of estrogen-dependent tumors (such as ovarian cancer) by interfering with common pathways of estrogen homeostasis. These data provide preliminary mechanistic clues regarding the ovarian cancer risk of TDCPP and lay the foundation for subsequent research into its potential pathways of action.

Systematic target identification suggests a unique target profile of TDCPP/BDCPP in ovarian cancer

To precisely pinpoint the targets of TDCPP in ovarian cancer, this study integrated multi-strategy target prediction with multi-source ovarian cancer gene data. Venn analysis was then used to screen for core genes satisfying a triple-evidence criterion: being “acted upon by the compound,” “associated with ovarian cancer,” and “aberrantly expressed in clinical samples” (Fig. 1 A-E, Supplementary Tables 1–2). The analysis indicated a key finding: the target spectra of TDCPP and its metabolite BDCPP demonstrated high consistency and uniqueness (Supplementary Fig. 1). Both shared three core targets: MMP3, CYP3A4, and CYP3A43. In contrast, the predicted targets for the other seven related compounds were largely limited to MMP3 (Supplementary Figs. 2–8). This distinction suggests that TDCPP/BDCPP may participate in the pathological processes of ovarian cancer through a unique pathway involving CYP3A4/43, which is closely related to the disruption of drug metabolism and hormonal homeostasis, and the underlying molecular-structural basis warrants further investigation.

Multi-omics Analysis Exploring the Synergistic Regulatory Network of MMP3, CYP3A4, and CYP3A43

To explore the synergistic effects of MMP3, CYP3A4, and CYP3A43 in ovarian cancer, we conducted a systematic analysis at the transcriptional, protein, miRNA, epigenetic, and genomic levels (Fig. 2). An analysis of 3,262 samples confirmed that MMP3, CYP3A4, and CYP3A43 exhibit significant positive co-expression at the transcriptional level (Log2 Odds Ratio > 1, p < 0.05) (Supplementary Table 3). However, the STRING database indicated that no physical interactions were detected among their protein products (Fig. 2A). This phenomenon of “transcriptional co-expression but no protein interaction” suggests that their functional association may stem from upstream indirect regulatory mechanisms. miRNA network analysis identified several miRNAs (e.g., miR-4436) capable of simultaneously targeting these genes (Supplementary Table 4), indicating that shared miRNAs act as key hubs connecting these independent genes, forming a functionally synergistic network through post-transcriptional regulation.

DNA methylation analysis showed that, compared to normal tissues, the promoter regions of MMP3, CYP3A4, and CYP3A43 are significantly hypermethylated in ovarian cancer tissues (p ≈ 0) (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Table 5). This widespread promoter hypermethylation represents an epigenetic feature associated with dysregulated expression and holds potential as a prognostic marker for ovarian cancer progression. At the genomic level, copy number variations (CNVs) were detected in all three genes (Fig. 2 C, Supplementary Table 6). “Gene amplification” was the primary type of variation, with a frequency as high as 10.79% in the Firehose Legacy cohort, whereas the frequencies of mutations and deep deletions were low (< 1%). This suggests that genomic amplification is a potential factor contributing to the aberrant overexpression of these genes, and together with epigenetic silencing, it constitutes the complex regulatory landscape of these genes.

miRNA-Related Pathway-Phenotype Associations Support the “Liver-to-Ovary Crosstalk” Hypothesis and Indicate Potential Multi-Organ Toxicity

Given the lack of evidence for protein-protein interactions among the target genes, we analyzed the biological functions of a set of miRNAs co-regulating these genes to explore their synergistic regulatory mechanisms. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis (Fig. 2D, Supplementary Table 7) indicated three key findings. First, regarding cellular origin, these miRNAs were most significantly enriched in liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (P = 7.12e-3), providing indirect computational support for the “liver-to-ovary crosstalk” hypothesis. Second, at the miRNA family level, the miR-3677 cluster, miR-3135 family, and miR-4436 family were all significantly enriched (P < 0.05), suggesting that these miRNAs coordinate to regulate downstream pathways as synergistic clusters. Finally, at the disease association level, these miRNAs were also significantly enriched in pediatric glioma (P = 0.0219), suggesting that the toxicity of TDCPP may possess systemic risk potential involving multi-organ networks.

Cell Type Localization and Molecular Docking Provide Multi-Scale Clues for “Liver-to-Ovary Crosstalk”

To support the aforementioned network analysis at the cellular and atomic levels, we performed single-cell expression profiling analysis and molecular docking simulations. Single-cell RNA sequencing data analysis (Fig. 3, Supplementary Table 8) indicated that the expression of MMP3, CYP3A4, and CYP3A43 presented distinct tissue distribution preferences and cell type specificity. MMP3 was highly expressed in mammary basal cells, which is consistent with its potential role in tumor invasion (Fig. 3 A). The expression of CYP3A4 was almost entirely confined to hepatocytes, supporting the hypothesis in the “liver-to-ovary crosstalk” model that the liver serves as a potential origin of signals (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, CYP3A43 presented high expression in testicular germ cells (Fig. 3 C), suggesting functional diversification of the CYP3A family in reproductive tissues and indicating that TDCPP may pose broad potential risks to the reproductive system through similar pathways. These cellular distribution patterns provide important clues for understanding the remote effects of TDCPP, but further research is needed to confirm their specific roles in ovarian pathology.

Molecular docking simulations (Fig. 4, Supplementary Table 9) indicated that, under simplified modeling conditions, TDCPP and BDCPP could theoretically interact with the key domains of MMP3 and CYP3A4 proteins. Specifically, the binding free energies of TDCPP with MMP3 and CYP3A4 were − 5.5 and − 5.4 kcal/mol, respectively, while the binding energies of BDCPP with these two targets were − 5.5 and − 5.4 kcal/mol, respectively. These results provide theoretical clues at the atomic level for the hypothesis that TDCPP/BDCPP may affect target protein functions through molecular interactions. Notably, CYP3A4 exhibited a substantial binding pocket volume (12,068 Å³), and the binding pocket of BDCPP with MMP3 was also relatively large (3,163 Å³), suggesting that these proteins may possess complex interaction modes. Although the molecular interaction with CYP3A43 was not simulated due to the lack of a crystal structure, we speculate that CYP3A43 may possess similar binding characteristics given its high homology with CYP3A4.

Discussion

By integrating computational toxicology and multi-omics analysis, this study proposes a potential molecular mechanism hypothesis suggesting that the environmental pollutant TDCPP may play a role in ovarian cancer progression through specific inter-organ communication. Our work provides potential mechanistic clues for understanding the association between TDCPP and ovarian cancer risk. More importantly, by identifying TDCPP exposure-specific miRNA signatures, it suggests a new perspective on how the environmental pollutant TDCPP remotely affect human health through systemic effects, offering a new research strategy for understanding complex environment-disease relationships.

Evaluation of the computational strategy: linking TDCPP to Known ovarian cancer-related molecules

Through integrated computational analysis, this study preliminarily suggests potential molecular links between the environmental exposure factor TDCPP and core molecules associated with ovarian cancer. The core targets we identified—MMP3, CYP3A4, and CYP3A43—are important functional molecules regulating ovarian cancer invasion, metastasis, and chemoresistance, and their biological roles have been extensively validated by previous experimental evidence [13–17]. The computational strategy of this study successfully identified these verified molecules, providing a reference basis for the scientific rigor and reliability of the analysis workflow.

Based on these assessment results, this finding contributes to mitigating concerns regarding the predictive reliability of purely computational studies, and advances the understanding of TDCPP health risks from general toxicological concerns to a mechanism-informed hypothesis. Specifically, this study provides bioinformatics evidence for the potential molecular mechanisms linking TDCPP to ovarian cancer risk. It is important to clarify that the core targets we identified are primarily closely related to tumor progression characteristics (such as metastasis and chemoresistance), and this regulatory association was derived in the specific context of TDCPP exposure. This finding suggests that TDCPP may exert its effects by influencing known tumor progression-related genes, while also implying that other genes involved in the tumor initiation stage may need to be further captured through broader screening criteria.

Suggesting an innovative regulatory mode: potential mechanism of synergistic regulation of tumor progression-related genes by miRNA clusters

Based on the evaluation of the aforementioned computational strategy, this study further proposes the potential mechanism by which TDCPP regulates tumor progression-related genes. Through multi-omics analysis, we identified a specific miRNA signature (miR-3677/3135/4436 families) in the context of TDCPP exposure, which is closely associated with the co-regulation of genes related to tumor invasion and chemoresistance. This finding primarily locates the biological effects of TDCPP at the tumor progression stage (e.g., metastasis and chemoresistance), with functional characteristics consistent with the specific roles of the identified targets (MMP3, CYP3A4/43). It also suggests that other genes involved in the tumor initiation stage may need to be further elucidated through broader screening criteria.

Moving beyond the traditional toxicological perspective that often focuses on single targets [52, 53], our systems biology analysis predicts that TDCPP may exhibit a synergistic regulatory relationship with MMP3, CYP3A4, and CYP3A43 through the specific miRNA clusters mentioned above. For instance, miR-4436 is capable of targeting both CYP3A43 and MMP3, thereby functionally linking two distinct pathways: extracellular matrix remodeling and drug metabolism. This finding not only contributes to understanding the pleiotropy of TDCPP but, more critically, proposes a potential analytical perspective: that the environmental pollutant TDCPP may achieve multi-target, multi-pathway synergistic regulation via “miRNA hubs.” This provides a new theoretical reference for understanding the systemic effects of environmental exposure and opens new avenues for environmental health risk assessment.

Proposing the “liver-to-ovary crosstalk”: a potential new model of TDCPP-associated tumor progression

This study proposes a novel conceptual model, “liver-to-ovary crosstalk,” suggesting that TDCPP may be involved in remote tumor progression via this unidirectional pathway. Interestingly, a recent review by Weiskirchen and Lonardo formally introduced the concept of the “Ovary-Liver Axis”, elucidating how ovarian function (e.g., in PCOS, menopause) influences liver health (e.g., MASLD/NAFLD) through hormonal, metabolic, and immune crosstalk [54]. However, the review also noted that ‘reverse’ communication—where liver-derived signals affect ovarian pathophysiology—remains an underexplored area within this bidirectional relationship. Our study operates precisely within this framework, focusing on this underexplored “reverse” perspective. Our data analysis indicates that the key TDCPP-regulating miRNA clusters are significantly enriched in liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs) [55–57], supporting the hypothesis that TDCPP may act first on the liver, inducing LSECs to release exosomes carrying specific miRNAs. These exosomes may subsequently reach the ovary via the circulatory system, synergistically regulating downstream target genes by delivering their miRNA cargo.

Specifically, these liver-derived miRNAs can simultaneously target MMP3 (which plays a role in invasion and metastasis) and CYP3A4/43 (which affect drug metabolism) [58–60]. This shared regulatory mechanism suggests that TDCPP may not only play a role in tumor progression but also influence the chemoresistance of ovarian cancer by interfering with drug metabolism, thereby increasing the complexity of clinical treatment. This model proposes a specific and testable hypothesis regarding the mechanism of inter-organ communication for the environmental pollutant TDCPP, transcending the traditional “toxicant-target organ” paradigm and pointing out a new direction for understanding the systemic and distal effects of environmental TDCPP exposure. Furthermore, the affected miRNAs are also significantly enriched in glioma [61], indicating that the toxicity of TDCPP may operate through a multi-organ synergistic network involving the liver, brain, and reproductive system. Such systemic toxic effects highlight the breadth and depth of its health risks.

Clinical relevance of “liver-to-ovary crosstalk”: insights from rare cases

The “Liver-to-Ovary Crosstalk” model proposed in this study possesses theoretical plausibility, a concept that receives indirect support from recently reported rare cases. These cases suggest the existence of a deep pathophysiological connection between the liver and the ovary that extends beyond mere anatomical proximity. For instance, a case of hepatoid carcinoma of the ovary responded favorably to specific targeted therapies [62], indicating that the biological behavior of ovarian tumors may be influenced by host systemic factors. Another case involved a hepatocellular carcinoma patient who developed isolated ovarian recurrence 12 years after radical resection [63]; although this pattern of long-interval recurrence is rare, it is conceptually consistent with the hypothesis that “the liver sends signals to the ovary” proposed by this model, suggesting that the ovarian microenvironment may be regulated by distal organs.

It is important to emphasize that while these two cases align conceptually with functional communication in the “liver-to-ovary” direction, they do not constitute direct validation of the model. Our study provides specific molecular-level clues for this potential inter-organ communication from the perspective of environmental toxicology: environmental pollutants like TDCPP may participate in regulating “Liver-to-Ovary Crosstalk” via the LSEC-exosome-miRNA pathway, thereby simulating the aforementioned clinicopathological processes at the molecular level. The current findings remain predictive hypotheses based on bioinformatics analysis, and future research is urgently needed to experimentally verify this mechanism, for example, by tracking the migration of liver-derived exosomes to the ovary and their specific impact on tumor phenotypes in in vitro and in vivo models. This not only provides a new perspective for understanding these rare clinical phenomena but also offers new research avenues for exploring the potential role of environmental TDCPP exposure in regulating inter-organ communication.

Relevance to IARC hazard identification, limitations, and future perspectives

By integrating computational toxicology with multi-omics analysis, this study provides a mechanism-based hypothesis for the potential ovarian cancer risk of TDCPP. Given that the IARC emphasizes the critical role of mechanistic evidence in hazard identification and has introduced the “Key Characteristics of Carcinogens” (KCCs) as a standard assessment framework, we adopt this systematic evaluation system to discuss the study’s findings, limitations, and future validation paths [64].

The computational findings of this study are consistent with several key characteristics defined by the IARC, primarily involving mechanisms of tumor promotion and progression. Specifically, TDCPP is predicted to modulate the activity of aromatase and CYP3A4, suggesting that it may affect estrogen homeostasis by interfering with receptor effects. Analysis based on TCGA data further indicates that core targets (MMP3, CYP3A4, CYP3A43) exhibit significant promoter hypermethylation patterns and high-frequency gene amplification in ovarian cancer, which relates to inducing epigenetic alterations and genomic instability. Of particular importance, part of the above mechanistic evidence is derived from human clinical samples (TCGA); according to IARC assessment principles, such data with human relevance are generally weighted more heavily than purely experimental animal or in vitro models. The current findings represent preliminary mechanism-based clues, offering a new systemic perspective for understanding the potential hazards of TDCPP. Future studies are still required to accumulate more sufficient data through functional validation experiments to elevate the level of evidence to meet the rigorous standards required for IARC assessment.

We acknowledge the limitations of this work with a rigorous scientific attitude. First, the study relies on computational analyses and public databases, lacking wet lab validation for the core hypothesis of “liver-to-ovary crosstalk” (particularly the exosome-dependent communication process), and the identification of cell types is derived from indirect inference based on databases. Second, regarding the screening strategy, this study focused only on miRNAs that simultaneously regulate all three target genes (MMP3, CYP3A4, CYP3A43), which may overlook other important regulatory miRNAs that control only one or two of them. Although this intersection approach aims to suggest potential association patterns among these three targets, it may also sacrifice the breadth of the regulatory network; future research needs to employ broader screening criteria to fully elucidate the unique networks regulating each gene. Regarding cellular localization, limited by public database resources, the lack of available ovarian single-cell datasets in the PanglaoDB database prevented us from visualizing the expression distribution of CYP3A43 in ovarian cells at the single-cell level. However, these existing cellular distribution patterns have provided important clues for understanding the remote effects of TDCPP. Furthermore, the fact that molecular docking simulations cannot fully replicate the complex in vivo epigenetic environment conversely underscores the necessity of wet laboratory validation such as ChIP-qPCR, for which our computational results provide valuable theoretical hypotheses and potential targets [65–67].

Despite the aforementioned limitations, this computational screening strategy reflects the value of this study as “exploratory research”—that is, to screen potential targets from a vast array of possibilities and avoid blind experimentation [68]. Considering the widespread environmental exposure of TDCPP and its potential threat to the female reproductive system, and given that the “liver-to-ovary crosstalk” inter-organ communication mechanism involved in this study represents an emerging direction in environmental health risk research, submitting TDCPP to the IARC Monographs program for assessment has potential value. This helps to incorporate the perspective of TDCPP’s systemic toxicity into authoritative consideration. Based on this, this study provides a clear roadmap for experimental validation for future research: at the mechanistic level, qPCR and Western Blot can be used to verify expression changes in key molecules (e.g., CYP3A4, MMP3) under TDCPP exposure; exosome co-culture experiments can be employed to track the transport process of liver-derived exosomes to the ovary; and functional rescue experiments can be utilized to observe the impact on malignant phenotypes (e.g., invasion, metastasis) after blocking this crosstalk [69–72]. Future studies could establish TDCPP-exposed animal or organoid models combined with single-cell sequencing technology to precisely track the true cellular origins and dynamic processes of crosstalk in vivo [73–75]. At the translational level, the miRNAs identified in this study (e.g., miR-4436) can serve as candidate biomarkers, while blocking strategies targeting this crosstalk offer new avenues for the prevention and treatment of ovarian cancer.

Finally, the findings of this study suggest that the current risk assessment paradigm may require a transformation. Existing toxicological risk assessment models primarily rely on traditional target organ toxicity data, which may underestimate the potential of chemicals like TDCPP to associate with distal organ damage through systemic effects, such as inter-organ communication. Our results propose that future risk assessment models may need to incorporate new dimensions, including inter-organ communication and remote health effects, to reflect the true health risks of environmental chemicals more comprehensively and accurately. Although current mechanistic evidence is insufficient to directly guide clinical protection, given TDCPP’s exposure characteristics and the potential risks suggested by the mechanistic hypothesis of this study, based on the precautionary principle, individuals (especially women planning pregnancy, pregnant, or lactating) can take general measures to reduce exposure, such as: paying attention to ingredient labels of household products and choosing products labeled “TDCPP-free” or “no flame retardants added”; and cleaning indoor environments regularly to reduce dust intake. These measures aim to reduce exposure burden and align with general environmental health protection principles, making them reasonable choices for mitigating potential risks.

In conclusion, by combining a computation-based strategy with mechanistic exploration, this study provides data-driven potential clues linking environmental exposure to disease mechanisms. Although the inherent limitations of computational predictions indicate that the conclusions require validation by wet lab experiments, this exploratory strategy serves as a vital link connecting computational predictions and experimental verification, offering new testable targets and perspectives for environmental health and oncology research.

Conclusion

This study proposes an analytical strategy integrating computational toxicology with multi-omics analysis. Derived from human epidemiological observations, this strategy aims to provide clues for unraveling the links between environmental exposure and core disease mechanisms, thereby offering new perspectives for research in this field. Applying this strategy, we inferred that the environmental flame retardant TDCPP may synergistically regulate key genes involved in tumor progression through specific miRNA clusters, and based on this, proposed a “liver-to-ovary crosstalk” hypothesis model to explain distal tumor progression. This model suggests that TDCPP may remotely influence the ovarian microenvironment by inducing the release of liver-derived exosomes. In conclusion, this study not only offers a mechanistically informed, hypothesis-driven framework for the potential health impacts of TDCPP, but more importantly, demonstrates the potential of mechanism discovery based on population observations to provide a basis for public risk prevention and health promotion. The methodological approach and theoretical model we constructed provide clear targets for subsequent experimental validation and clinical translation, facilitating a shift from single toxicity assessment to complex risk assessment paradigms based on inter-organ communication mechanisms, and promoting the process from public health issues to understanding potential mechanisms through basic research, and ultimately forming evidence-based public health solutions.
