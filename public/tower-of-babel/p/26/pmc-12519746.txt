Establishment of a rabbit model for microscopically assisted posterior lumbar fenestration
Authors: Lei Jiao, Yi Dai, Yao Yuan, Wei Li, Fu Dai, Jianhua Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMicroscopically assisted posterior lumbar fenestration plays a critical role in spinal surgery, yet no animal model currently replicates this procedure.ObjectiveThis study aimed to rigorously simulate clinically relevant microscopically assisted posterior lumbar fenestration in New Zealand white rabbits, establishing a stable animal model for investigating postoperative recurrence and pathophysiological mechanisms, while evaluating its feasibility.Materials and methodsForty-eight 4-month-old male New Zealand white rabbits (weight: 2.0–2.5 kg) were randomly assigned using a number table. Preoperative assessments included general nutritional status and response to stimuli. Under microscopic guidance, partial laminectomy and ligamentum flavum resection were performed using 2.0-mm rongeurs, with successful exposure of the dura mater and nerve roots defined as procedural success. Anesthetic depth was evaluated using the Definition of the Anesthetic States and Attributed Numerical Scale. Vital signs (Peripheral Oxygen Saturation, respiratory rate, temperature, heart rate), intraoperative blood loss, surgical duration, and anesthesia time were monitored. Background and introduction

Microscopically assisted laminotomy represents a minimally invasive spinal procedure utilizing small incisions coupled with high-magnification microscopy systems, primarily employed in the management of lumbar disc herniation (LDH) and lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) [1–3]. The conceptual foundation was established by Williams in 1978 through the description of open lumbar microdiscectomy (OLM) [4], which remains the current gold standard for LDH treatment. This posterior approach necessitates resection of posterior structural elements including partial lamina, ligamentum flavum, and facet joints [5, 6]. Therapeutic strategies for LSS include decompressive procedures (most clinically prevalent), spinal fusion techniques, and non-fusion stabilization methods, among which spinal canal decompression is the most commonly used in clinical practice [7–9]. Currently, common anterior spinal canal decompression procedures include laminar decompression and microendoscopic decompression. Laminar decompression encompasses total laminectomy, hemilaminectomy, and laminar fenestration. Notably, microscopically assisted laminotomy has emerged as the preferred intervention for LSS [10–12], involving precise resection of pathological tissues including hypertrophic ligamentum flavum, partial lamina, and facet joints. The procedure’s success depends on two critical parameters: adequate osseous and ligamentous decompression as well as complete visualization of the affected dural sac and nerve roots. In general, both open lumbar microdiscectomy (OLM) and microscopically assisted laminotomy share the fundamental surgical principle of fenestration technique.

Ohtaki et al. [13] stated in their review that long-term follow-up data demonstrate a reoperation rate of 7.58 ± 5.29% following laminotomy procedures over 8.28 ± 6.26 years [13]. Postoperative scar formation significantly complicates revision surgery while increasing risks of dural tears and neural injuries [14, 15]. These clinical challenges underscore the need for pathophysiological investigation of post-surgical complications and development of animal models for mechanistic studies.

The scarcity of human postoperative specimens and the prohibitive costs associated with large animal models necessitate alternative approaches. Recent advances support the utility of rabbit models in spinal research: Yao et al. established mechanical stress-induced lumbar disc degeneration models [16]; Li et al. developed reliable lateral lumbar interbody fusion (LLIF) models for cage evaluation [17]; Kato et al. documented laminectomy protocols for adhesion prevention studies [18]. In summary, rabbits provide a reliable animal choice for studying animal models of lumbar spine surgery. Furthermore, a safe and effective anesthesia method is crucial for the successful establishment of the model. Common pre-anesthetic analgesic and sedative drugs include Buprenorphine, Medetomidine, and Xylazine; induction anesthetics include Ketamine, a combination of Ketamine and Xylazine, and Propofol; and postoperative medications include Buprenorphine and Meloxicam [19–21]. Propofol-based anesthesia in rabbits has gained widespread acceptance over the past few decades [22, 23]. Studies have shown that Xylazine is widely used in rabbit anesthesia and exhibits mild analgesic effects in rabbits [24]. In this study, Xylazine and Propofol were selected for analgesia and anesthesia, with Meloxicam used for postoperative analgesia, providing a stable anesthetic option for the successful establishment of the model.

This investigation pioneers the development of a New Zealand white rabbit model for microscopically assisted posterior lumbar laminotomy, addressing three critical research gaps. This study aims to establish a reliable animal model of posterior lumbar laminectomy in New Zealand white rabbits with the assistance of a microscope. The model is intended to serve as a stable platform for clinical research into the pathological mechanisms underlying the recurrence and postoperative complications associated with posterior lumbar laminectomy, thereby evaluating the feasibility of this approach.

Animal model establishment protocol

Preoperative evaluation

A comprehensive anesthetic evaluation was performed on the experimental rabbits, including assessments of body weight, general nutritional status, and responsiveness to external stimuli, to ensure the effectiveness and safety of the anesthesia process.

Anesthesia protocol

First, the experimental rabbits were fixed in a dedicated experimental box. The ear marginal vein was located, and local skin preparation with alcohol disinfection was performed to fully expose the vein. A 24G yellow indwelling needle was used for ear marginal vein puncture and catheterization. After successful establishment of the ear marginal venous access, 3 mg/kg of veterinary 1% propofol was slowly injected intravenously to induce anesthesia. Once induction anesthesia was successful, the rabbits were fixed in a prone position on the animal experiment table. A micro-infusion pump was connected to continuously infuse 3 mg/kg of 1% propofol through the ear marginal vein at a rate of 150 mg/h. Meanwhile, 1.5 mg/kg of veterinary xylazine injection was slowly injected intravenously for auxiliary analgesia, with additional doses administered intraoperatively based on the rabbits’ pain responses. It was ensured that the rabbits remained in an anesthetized state throughout the surgery, without spontaneous movement or episodes of excitement and restlessness.After induced anesthesia and throughout the surgical procedure, the experimental rabbits were provided with oxygen support using an oxygen generator (ROC-5 A, RWD, China) at an oxygen flow rate of 1–1.5 L/min. A benchtop multi-parameter monitor (Vetal 3, Mindray, China) was used for continuous monitoring, and their vital signs were closely monitored and recorded. A constant-temperature operating table set at 37 °C was used for maintaining body temperature to ensure the safe conduct of the surgery.

Surgical procedure

Following successful induction of general anesthesia and analgesia, anatomical localization was achieved by palpating the iliac crest as the primary osseous landmark to identify the L5-L6 interlaminar space. The local area was prepared using an animal hair clipper, followed by standard disinfection three times and draping with 2 layers of sterile surgical towels. A longitudinal incision approximately 4 cm in length was made parallel to the long axis of the L5-6 spinous processes, centered between the L5-6 spinous processes. The skin, fascial layer, muscular fascial layer, and the muscle layer adjacent to the left edge of the L5-6 spinous processes were incised sequentially. The local muscle tissue was bluntly dissected to expose the unilateral laminar space. A 2.0 mm laminectomy forceps was used to remove parts of the upper and lower laminae and the ligamentum flavum, allowing clear visualization of the white dura mater and its distributed blood vessels. At the end of the surgery, the surgical site was irrigated with normal saline three times. The incision was sutured layer by layer, including the muscular fascial layer, fascial layer, and skin layer. After suturing, the skin was disinfected with iodophor, an appropriate amount of erythromycin ointment was applied, and the incision was dressed (Fig. 1).

Postoperative management

Animals were allowed to recover spontaneously in a temperature-controlled recovery chamber (20 ± 2 °C). Postoperatively, intramuscular injection of ceftiofur (30 mg/kg) in the gluteal region for anti-infection and subcutaneous injection of meloxicam (1 mg/kg) [25] were administered daily for 3 consecutive days. Additionally, close observation was conducted on the rabbits’ pain conditions, mental status, dietary intake, incision healing, and lower limb mobility, with timely detection and management of any potential complications.

Monitoring indicators

The anesthetic state was evaluated using the definition of the anesthetic states and attributed numerical scale [21] (Supplementary Materials 1). Preoperative and intraoperative oxygen saturation (%), respiratory rate (breaths/min), body temperature (°C), and heart rate (beats/min) were monitored to ensure stable vital signs. Intraoperative blood loss (ml), surgical duration (min), anesthesia duration (min), and postoperative awakening time (min) were assessed and recorded, and a learning curve for surgical duration was plotted.

Methods for model validation

Intraoperative microscopic observation: The surgical procedure was considered successful if parts of the lamina were resected, the ligamentum flavum was removed, and the dural sac and nerve roots were fully exposed. Postoperatively, the lower limb mobility and incision healing were observed. Preoperatively and 1 day postoperatively, the experimental rabbits underwent lumbar Micro-CT scans, and the area of the laminar window was measured in the coronal plane to further verify the success of the fenestration (Fig. 2).

Statistical methods

Data processing and graphing were performed using SPSS (v27.0, USA) and R statistical analysis software. Normality tests were conducted for continuous data. For data that conformed to a normal distribution with homogeneous variance, the t-test was used for analysis; for data that did not conform to a normal distribution or with heterogeneous variance, the Mann-Whitney U test was applied, and the results were expressed as median (interquartile range). For continuously measured data, two-way repeated measures analysis of variance was used, and the results were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Categorical data were expressed as counts and rates (%), and the chi-square (χ²) test was used for comparison. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The success rate of anesthesia for the model was 93.75%; the surgical success rate was 95.56%; and the overall model success rate was 89.58% (Table 1). Two experimental rabbits died during induction of anesthesia, which may be related to respiratory depression caused by propofol. One rabbit died during anesthetic analgesia, possibly due to hypotension and respiratory depression following xylazine injection. During the surgical procedure, one rabbit was found to have a fractured facet joint, and another had dural sac and spinal cord injury. These complications, attributed to anatomical differences between rabbits and humans as well as surgical manipulation, resulted in model establishment failure.

Comparison of pre- and post-operative micro-CT 3D images revealed a significant enlargement of the left L5-6 laminar window (Fig. 2A-C). Post-operative axial, sagittal, and coronal views all demonstrated the successful resection of parts of the superior and inferior laminae (Fig. 2D-F). The red arrows indicate the sites of L5-6 laminar bone defects, confirming the successful radiographic resection of the laminae.

The R language-based statistical analysis software was utilized to plot the operative time learning curve of the Experimental Group (EG) (Fig. 3). Key findings included: the learning time of operation time was missing in EG-3, EG-9, and EG-12 rabbits due to anesthesia failure caused by respiratory depression induced by anesthetic induction and analgesia. The average slopes of the operation time learning curves for rabbits from EG-1 to EG-20 and from EG-21 to EG-48 were − 0.84 and − 0.23, respectively; a significant downward trend was observed when comparing the curve slopes. These results indicate that with the increase in the number of modeling cases, the operation time was significantly shortened and the surgical operation became more skilled.

Discussion

The microscopically assisted posterior lumbar laminotomy technique described in this study demonstrates significant advantages over conventional open laminectomy approaches. Our findings indicate that this microsurgical method preserves posterior column integrity while achieving adequate neural decompression, consistent with clinical reports in human patients [26–29]. While rabbit models offer distinct advantages for spinal research, including cost-effectiveness, high reproducibility, and suitability for large-scale studies, existing models have failed to accurately replicate microsurgical laminotomy techniques. Kato [19] et al. reported the rabbit laminectomy in the evaluation study of hyaluronic acid membrane for preventing adhesions after laminectomy. The scope of the rabbit laminectomy and the specific method were not specified in the article.

Therefore, considering the anatomical characteristics of experimental rabbits and the technical features of microscope-assisted lumbar posterior laminotomy, this study aims to establish a rabbit experimental model by strictly simulating the clinical operation process of microscope-assisted lumbar posterior laminotomy. This model has a high surgical success rate, a relatively gentle surgical learning curve, and is suitable for studying the local pathological changes and their occurrence and development mechanisms after posterior surgery for lumbar disc herniation (LDH) and lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) following laminotomy.

This study employed a propofol-xylazine anesthesia regimen. During induction, two rabbits died following intravenous administration of 1% propofol (3 mg/kg). Propofol acts on the central nervous system, particularly by depressing the brainstem respiratory centers, leading to respiratory suppression [30, 31]. It potentiates inhibitory neurotransmission via γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) receptors [32], reducing respiratory rate and tidal volume, and may even induce apnea. This respiratory depression likely results from direct inhibition of the respiratory center and blunted hypercapnic response, ultimately causing respiratory acidosis [33].

One additional rabbit died after slow intravenous injection of xylazine for analgesia following successful induction. Xylazine, an α-2 adrenergic agonist, was initially investigated as a potential anesthetic and sedative-hypnotic agent. However, early human trials were discontinued due to severe hypotension and CNS depression, leading to its approval for veterinary use in 1972. Known adverse effects include cardiac conduction suppression, bradycardia, CNS depression, respiratory depression, miosis, and hypothermia [34].

Although anesthesia was administered by a professional anesthesiologist, individual variability and unforeseen complications may have contributed to these outcomes. The induction failures were likely related to propofol-induced respiratory depression, while the post-analgesia mortality may have resulted from xylazine-associated hypotension and respiratory suppression.

Limitations: This study did not investigate molecular-level alterations in local tissues following posterior lumbar laminotomy, which could provide deeper insights into postoperative pathological changes. Future Perspectives: With advancements in spinal endoscopic techniques, minimally invasive procedures such as: Percutaneous endoscopic lumbar discectomy (PELD), Unilateral laminotomy for bilateral decompression (ULBD), Endoscopic lumbar over-the-top decompression (Endo-Love), and Unilateral biportal endoscopy (UBE) are increasingly being employed clinically for managing lumbar disc herniation and spinal stenosis [35–40]. Building on these developments, we plan to refine our surgical approach to establish an even safer and more reliable animal model for clinical translation.

Conclusion

This animal model demonstrates a high success rate with a relatively gentle learning curve, providing a stable experimental platform for investigating the pathological mechanisms of spinal disorders treated with posterior lumbar laminotomy. The model offers a novel approach for related research. In subsequent studies, we will further explore local pathological changes following laminotomy to provide new insights into the histopathological mechanisms of tissue changes after clinical microscopic posterior lumbar laminotomy.
