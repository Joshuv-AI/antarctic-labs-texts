Diagnosis and management of multiple-site heterotopic pregnancy with acute appendicitis: Case report
Authors: Jiaying Shen, Yedi Huang, Rujing Fang, Yeping Wang, Jianqiong Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHeterotopic pregnancy (HP), characterized by coexisting intrauterine (IUP) and ectopic pregnancy (EP), poses diagnostic challenges due to overlapping symptoms with conditions like acute appendicitis in pregnancy (AAIP). This case highlights the complexities of managing HP with concurrent AAIP and bilateral tubal pregnancies following ovulation induction.Case presentationA 32-year-old female with a history of clomiphene-induced ovulation presented at 9 weeks’ amenorrhea with vaginal bleeding and right lower abdominal pain. Transvaginal ultrasound revealed an IUP, a left adnexal mass with embryonic cardiac activity, and a right lower abdominal mixed-echoic lesion suggestive of appendicitis. Laparoscopic left salpingectomy and appendectomy were performed in the first operation, and the diagnosis was confirmed by postoperative pathology. Postoperatively, β-hCG remained elevated (161,134 IU/L), and a right adnexal mass enlarged progressively. A second laparoscopic surgery was performed to remove the right fallopian tube and successfully preserve intrauterine pregnancy, and a healthy male baby was delivered by cesarean section at 38 weeks and 5 days of gestation.ConclusionThis case underscores the diagnostic complexity of overlapping HP and AA symptoms, emphasizing the role of serial biomarker assessment and early surgical intervention in high-risk populations. Bilateral tubal ectopic pregnancies, though rare, warrant heightened vigilance in assisted reproductive technology (ART) patients. Dynamic imaging and intraoperative exploration are critical to avoid missed diagnoses. Timely laparoscopic surgery, coupled with threatened abortion management, can optimize maternal and neonatal outcomes.

Introduction

Heterotopic pregnancy (HP) is a special condition in which intrauterine pregnancy (IUP) and ectopic pregnancy (EP) coexist.Due to atypical early symptoms, such as abdominal pain and vaginal bleeding, HP is highly susceptible to underdiagnosis or misdiagnosis, and rupture of the EP can lead to fatal bleeding if not intervened in time. Notably, acute appendicitis in pregnancy (AAIP) with its clinical manifestations, such as right lower abdominal pain, nausea and vomiting, has significant overlap with EP, further increasing the complexity of differential diagnosis [1]. In this article, we report a case of a patient who developed HP after ovulation induction therapy and successfully managed bilateral tubal pregnancy and appendiceal lesions through two laparoscopic surgeries in the setting of coexisting acute appendicitis in pregnancy and ultimately achieved delivery of a live infant at term.

Case presentation

Medical history and examination

A 32-year-old female patient was admitted to our hospital on May 16, 2024, due to “9 weeks of amenorrhea and intermittent vaginal bleeding for 30 days.” The patient had irregular menstruation and received clomiphene citrate for ovulation induction due to ovulatory dysfunction for more than 4 months. Last menstrual period was 25 February 2024; the urine β-hCG test was Positive at 6 weeks of amenorrhea. On May 5, 2024 (at 8 weeks’ amenorrhea), the patient was admitted to another hospital for vaginal bleeding and received progesterone for continuation of pregnancy. On the following day, she had sudden lower abdominal pain. Transvaginal ultrasound revealed (1) an intrauterine Gestational sac consistent with 8 weeks’ amenorrhea and (2) a mixed echoic mass identified in the left adnexal region, measuring approximately 45 × 36 × 40 mm (Fig. 1A-B). Within the mass, a Gestational sac echo is visualized, with dimensions of approximately 20× 17 × 21 mm. An embryonic Pole measuring approximately 12 mm in length is observed, along with visible fetal cardiac activity; (3) a mixed echogenic mass in the right lower abdomen and possible appendicitis. First surgery and postoperative management

On May 6, 2024, the patient developed right lower quadrant pain without obvious inducement, and emergency surgery was performed. During the operation, the left ampulla of the fallopian tube showed significant spherical enlargement, approximately 5 × 4 × 4 cm in size, purple-blue, unruptured, oozing at the fimbria of the left fallopian tube. The right fallopian tube is dark red and congested, with blood clots attached to the fimbria of the right fallopian tube. The left ovary was enlarged approximately 5 × 4 cm in size, the right ovary was enlarged approximately 4 × 4 cm in size. Appendix was slightly thickened and congested, no obvious adhesion of periappendiceal tissues was observed during the operation, left salpingectomy and appendectomy were performed. Postoperative pathology confirmed left tubal pregnancy and uncomplicated acute appendicitis (containing fecalith in the lumen) (Fig. 4A-B). The patient underwent clean-contaminated surgery and needed to be alert to the occurrence of infection, so cefuroxime sodium injection (1.5 g bid) was given to prevent infection for 5 days, combined with phloroglucinol, progesterone, and dydrogesterone supplementation to maintain pregnancy. On May 10, 2025, reexamination of ultrasound showed hypoechogenicity in the right adnexal region (34 × 22 × 35 mm), β-hCG showed 161,134 IU/L, considering the possibility of right EP, and the patient refused further surgical treatment.

Secondary surgery and pregnancy outcomes

The patient was transferred to our hospital from another hospital on May 16, 2024, with a β-hCG level of 197,300 IU/L. On May 17, 2024, ultrasound examination showed a Gestational sac in the uterine cavity, with an embryo length of 28 mm and the presence of a primitive heart beat. A mixed echo mass measuring 23 × 35 × 68 mm was observed in the right ovarian area, and blood flow signals could be seen within the mass. She refused surgical treatment and requested expectant management. The patient was monitored for abdominal pain, vaginal bleeding, and hemodynamic stability, and combined phloroglucinol, progesterone, and dydrogesterone supplementation to maintain pregnancy.

Discussion

This case highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of compound intrauterine and extrauterine pregnancies, especially when complicated by acute appendicitis in pregnancy, a clinical scenario with this dual pathology that is extremely rare. More notably, EP in this case presented as bilateral tubal EP, an extremely rare form of EP, which occurs in only approximately 1 in 200,000 pregnancies, further increasing the complexity and diagnostic difficulty of the case [2]. The pathogenesis of HP is not yet fully understood, and its occurrence is strongly associated with a number of factors, and the risk factors for spontaneous bilateral EP are the same as those for unilateral EP, including tubal factors, such as tubal inflammation and history of previous tubal pregnancy; ovulatory and endocrine factors HP is associated with a variety of factors, including tubal factors such as inflammation of the fallopian tubes and history of previous tubal pregnancies; ovulation and endocrine factors such as ovulation abnormalities and the use of ovulation stimulants; and other factors such as age and smoking. Although HP is extremely rare in nature, with an incidence of approximately 1/30,000 to 1/15,000, the incidence of HP has increased significantly with the widespread use of assisted reproductive technology (ART), such as in vitro fertilization (IVF), embryo transfer, and ovulation stimulation therapy [3]. The incidence of EP after IVF-ET ranged between 2.8% and 6.2%, which was significantly higher than that observed after spontaneous conception [4]. At the same time, it has been suggested that clomiphene citrate can induce apoptosis of epithelial cells in the tubal isthmus, slow down the passage of embryos in the fallopian tube, and lead to multiple follicular development, thereby increasing the risk of EP [5]. The incidence of acute appendicitis in pregnancy as an obstetric emergency is approximately 1/1000 to 1/2000, and its occurrence is directly associated with risk factors such as fecalith obstruction, lymphoid hyperplasia, parasitic infection, and tumor compression. The risk of appendiceal perforation during pregnancy is 2 to 3 times higher than during non-pregnancy, significantly increasing the risk of sepsis, abortion and maternal-fetal death.

The most common type of combined intrauterine and extrauterine pregnancy is intrauterine combined tubal pregnancy, which usually presents as a unilateral occurrence, in addition to other types such as intrauterine pregnancy combined with uterine horn pregnancy, cervical pregnancy, and uterine scar pregnancy. However, the present case is unique in the presence of both bilateral tubal pregnancies and AA, which makes the diagnosis very challenging. Ultrasound is an important screening tool for HP [6].It is able to diagnose both intrauterine and EP. However, the sensitivity of ultrasonography varies greatly. Similarly, ultrasound has a sensitivity of only 52–86% and specificity of 81–86% in the diagnosis of acute appendicitis, and approximately 45–82% of abdominal ultrasound findings are inconclusive in the diagnosis of acute appendicitis [7]. Usually we can combine β-hCG with B ultrasound to improve the detection rate of EP, but there is no significant difference in β-hCG values between intrauterine and extrauterine compound pregnancy and normal pregnancy [8, 9].Therefore, in this case of complex intrauterine and extrauterine compound pregnancy with acute appendicitis, the limitations of ultrasound diagnosis are particularly significant. Comprehensive laboratory tests, imaging, and clinical assessment play a crucial role.

The clinical manifestations of both HP and AA have acute abdominal pain as the core symptom, but the pathogenesis and red flags of the two are very different. Abdominal pain in HP mostly stems from the risk of EP rupture, which may be accompanied by vaginal bleeding or hemorrhagic shock, while AA typically presents with migrating pain localizing to the right lower quadrant, and laboratory tests show leukocytosis, elevated CRP and bilirubin [10]. However, physiologic leukocytosis during pregnancy and the anatomical upward shift of the appendix with uterine enlargement often mask AA features [11]. In contrast, ruptured EP or hemorrhagic corpus luteum cysts may be mistaken for appendicitis masses on ultrasound. In this case, although B ultrasound successfully identified the left tubal mass and intrauterine pregnancy, there was ambiguity in judging the nature of mixed echoes in the right iliac fossa and appendiceal morphology, and MRI should be performed during pregnancy if ultrasonography was uncertain, and studies showed that non-contrast MRI was safe during pregnancy and had high specificity and sensitivity for AAIP (100% and 89%, respectively) [12, 13]. Meanwhile, dynamic monitoring of biomarkers is essential, and serial serum β-hCG measurements can indicate pregnancy viability rather than location and are not direct EP biomarkers. Zhang et al. found that the median serum levels of serum protein calmodulin 2 (CNN2) in IUP and EP patients were 25.68 ng/mL (range: 7.49–98.86 ng/mL) and 91.54 ng/mL (range: 34.05–139.78 ng/mL), respectively, with a higher sensitivity of 90.48% (95% CI: 82.09–95.8%) and specificity of 90.12% (95% CI: 81.46–95.64%) [14], with good performance for distinguishing EP from IUP. The CNN2 of EP patients is higher than that of IUP patients. For HP patients, extrauterine pregnancy in HP may affect the absolute level of CNN2, so it can be combined with the dynamic changes of β-hCG and imaging examination to improve the diagnostic accuracy. Other biomarkers, including pregnancy-associated plasma protein A (PAPPA) [15], microRNA markers (e.g., miR-517c, miR-515-3p) [16], and metabolite combinations of lactate and acetic acid [17] all showed high potential in distinguishing extrauterine pregnancy from intrauterine pregnancy. Sezİklİ et al. defined pregnancy index (PGIndex) as PGIndex = NE x CRP/MPV based on the relationship between Neutrophil count (NE), C-reactive protein (CRP), Mean platelet volume (MPV) and appendicitis in pregnancy. And those with a PGIndex above 10.62 were 78.75 times more likely to belong to the appendicitis group than patients below this threshold [18], which greatly improved the accuracy of the diagnosis. When faced with abdominal pain in early pregnancy, especially after ART, clinicians must integrate serial clinical assessments and new biomarkers and judiciously use MRI when ultrasound is equivocal; a single diagnostic modality is not sufficient.

The treatment of HP combined with AA needs to take into account the risk of EP rupture, intrauterine pregnancy protection and appendicitis control. Expectant treatment, drug treatment and surgical treatment can be adopted. Expectant treatment is mainly suitable for patients with stable conditions and slow disease progression. In this case, if the development of the embryo of the EP stops naturally and the impact on the intrauterine pregnancy is small, the patient can be closely monitored by the doctor for temporary observation without taking active treatment measures [19]. Drug therapy is another important treatment modality for combined intrauterine and extrauterine pregnancies. Single-dose or multi-dose methotrexate (MTX) is widely utilized in the treatment of such cases, but studies have demonstrated the teratogenicity of MTX. Therefore, when using MTX, the patient’s need for continued pregnancy needs to be fully considered, and the treatment regimen chosen carefully. For ectopic pregnancy with visible fetal heartbeats or When continuation of intrauterine pregnancy is required, ultrasound-guided intracapsular injection of potassium chloride and embryo aspiration may be used as treatment options. In the report by Ugurlucan et al., 2 ml of 2 mg/ml KCl was injected directly into the cardiac region of the embryo, and the fetal cardiac activity disappeared, while amniotic fluid in the gestational sac was aspirated to increase the success of the treatment [20].Surgery is the main treatment for combined intrauterine and extrauterine pregnancy. Depending on the patient’s specific situation and surgical conditions, open surgery, laparoscopic surgery or hysteroscopic surgery can be chosen. Open surgery is mainly suitable for emergency situations such as fainting and shock in patients with ruptured extrauterine pregnancy. Laparoscopic surgery is often the preferred modality, including salpingectomy and salpingotomy. Salpingectomy is mainly indicated in patients with no fertility needs and severe tubal destruction (> 4 cm in diameter or repeated EP) to reduce the risk of recurrent EP but may affect female fertility. Salpingotomy may improve fertility in women with contralateral tubal damage if fertility requirements are met and the gestational sac is not ruptured (< 4 cm in diameter and β-hCG < 5000 IU/L), but β-hCG follow-up is required after surgery, and the risk of persistent EP is higher than the former [21, 22]. In cervical pregnancies or cesarean scar pregnancies [23, 24], hysteroscopic surgery provides more intuitive and accurate surgical treatment, and once intraoperative bleeding is detected, it is easy to locate the source and ligate the bleeding vessels; however, there is an increased risk of miscarriage, preterm delivery, and cervical insufficiency in intrauterine pregnancies due to cervical dilatation [25].

Combined with this case, preoperative ultrasound of the patient’s initial surgery showed embryonic heart tube pulsation in the mass in the left adnexal area, and intraoperative findings of tubal ampullary enlargement, purple-blue with oozing, which may rupture at any time and endanger life, and laparoscopic left salpingectomy was performed. At the same time, the patient had persistent right lower abdominal pain, which progressively worsened. Ultrasound revealed a mixed echogenic mass in the right lower quadrant. A thickened and congested appendix was found during surgery, and the risk of perforated appendicitis during pregnancy was extremely high, which could lead to severe infection or abortion, and an appendectomy was performed. Considering that surgical trauma increases the risk of miscarriage, and progestogen-mediated PR-B signaling can reduce pro-inflammatory gene expression and inhibit myometrial contractility [26], at the same time, studies have shown that phloroglucinol is a highly selective drug released in abnormal spastic smooth muscle and can effectively relieve abnormal uterine contraction without causing fetal malformations [27], so phloroglucinol, progesterone, and dydrogesterone supplementation are used to maintain pregnancy. Postoperative review revealed a right adnexal zone mass (34 × 22 × 35 mm), which was treated expectantly in combination with the absence of fetal cardiac pulsation of the mass and the patient’s lack of severe abdominal pain and signs of internal bleeding. However, the progressive enlargement of the mass with right-sided abdominal pain during follow-up was considered to be a risk of rupture if the pregnancy continued, and a second laparoscopic surgery was finally performed. It is worth noting that the intraoperative observation of the right tubal jugular abdomen was only mildly thickened (1.0 × 0.5 cm), which was inconsistent with the preoperative ultrasound suggestion of a 70-mm mass, and was presumed to be a mechanized hematoma formed by repeated rupture of the small vessels on the surface.

The key to the successful management of this case was (1) early recognition of the risk of rupture of EP and timely intervention, as the literature shows that the maternal mortality rate for HP is 8 times higher than that of tubal EP [28].The importance of early ultrasound screening and laparoscopic exploration of high-risk pregnancies is emphasized; (2) The contralateral adnexal mass was found 4 days after the first operation, and although the possibility of missed diagnosis was not excluded, it was more suggestive that ovulation induction and IVF-ET patients needed to strengthen the vigilance of multiple sites of ectopic pregnancy; (3) Finally, the patient maintained pregnancy with phloroglucinol, progesterone, and dydrogesterone despite two major surgeries. It is worth reflecting that the right fallopian tube congestion has been found to be dark red with fimbrial clots attached at the first operation, and if linear salpingotomy is performed to explore the villous tissue at the suspicious site during the operation, the second emergency operation may be avoided even if no lesions are seen on ultrasound. This case highlights the need to pay attention to clinical practice: the possibility of multiple sites of pregnancy should be systematically investigated in patients conceived by assisted reproductive technology; intraoperative imaging and fine exploration under direct vision should be combined, especially for suspected tubal morphological changes; and a high degree of vigilance should be maintained. Clinicians need to perform systematic intraoperative exploration beyond initial imaging findings. Even if ultrasound finds an ectopic lesion, the clinician must carefully examine the contralateral adnexa. Intraoperative linear incisions in the suspicious tubal area (even if there is no obvious mass) can prevent missed diagnoses, and as shown in this case, initial intraoperative exploration was not ideal. Although ultrasound remains the usual modality of examination for HP, its sensitivity to acute appendicitis is not ideal, and inconclusive results require MRI, especially when appendiceal morphology is equivocal. At the same time, we need to distinguish appendicitis from periappendicitis. Appendicitis is mainly acute inflammation caused by obstruction of appendiceal lumen (such as fecalith, etc.) or bacterial infection; periappendicitis mainly refers to inflammation around the appendix [29], inflammation involves the serosal layer of the appendix, but does not involve the mucosal layer of the appendix, this patient found congestion and thickening of the appendix during surgery, no adhesion to the surrounding tissues, postoperative fecalith was observed in the lumen, postoperative pathology suggested uncomplicated acute appendicitis, considering acute appendicitis caused by fecalith obstruction.

Conclusions

This case exemplifies the severe challenges and management strategies of EP combined with acute appendicitis. The diagnostic dilemma posed by the overlapping symptoms of HP and AA highlights the limitations of conventional imaging and the importance of integrating dynamic biomarker assessment (e.g., CNN2, PGIndex, etc.) to improve diagnostic accuracy. Expectation therapy failed in this case, as evidenced by rapid enlargement of the adnexal mass and elevated β-hCG, so we should be highly vigilant for HP in the IVF, pro-implantation population and prioritize early surgical intervention when biomarkers or imaging suggest a high risk of rupture. Both ultrasonographers and clinicians, especially clinicians, should consider the possibility of combined bilateral tubal EP when they see an abnormal presentation and confirm the diagnosis early to minimize patient suffering and cost.
