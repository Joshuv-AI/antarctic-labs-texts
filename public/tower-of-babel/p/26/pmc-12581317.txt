Esophageal atresia with concomitant hypertrophic pyloric stenosis: a case series and literature review
Authors: Zhaozhou Liu, An Wang, Dingding Wang, Yong Zhao, Yanan Zhang, Shuangshuang Li, Junmin Liao, Kaiyun Hua, Yichao Gu, Dayan Sun, Jinshi Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEsophageal atresia (EA) with concomitant hypertrophic pyloric stenosis (HPS) is a rare condition, and its diagnosis can be challenging due to overlapping postoperative complications. Comprehensive clinical characterization remains limited.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed five patients with EA with concomitant HPS treated at our center between 2018 and 2025. A systematic literature search of PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and CNKI identified 48 additional cases. Clinical features, timing of diagnosis, treatment strategies, and outcomes were analyzed.ResultsAmong 132 neonates with EA in our institution, five (3.8%) developed HPS. Four had Gross type C EA, and three were male. All underwent EA repair in the first week of life and later developed HPS, diagnosed at a median age of 45 days (range: 20–145). All patients underwent laparoscopic pyloromyotomy with uneventful recovery and were alive at a median follow-up of 50 months. Literature review revealed 48 reported cases, predominantly male (41/48) and Gross type C (42/48). Combined analysis of 53 patients showed HPS onset typically between 2 and 15 weeks, with a median age of 4 weeks (IQR: 2.5–6 weeks). Overall survival was 90.6%, with deaths mainly due to infection or severe associated anomalies.ConclusionsHPS may coexist with EA, with an incidence of approximately 3.8%. It often presents later in the disease course. Its diagnosis can be delayed by postoperative complications or altered feeding. Long-term follow-up and multicenter studies are warranted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06248-6.Keywords: Esophageal atresia, Hypertrophic pyloric stenosis, Congenital anomalies, Pediatric surgery

Introduction

Congenital esophageal atresia (EA) is a serious malformation of the digestive tract that is often associated with tracheoesophageal fistula (TEF), with an incidence of approximately 1 in 2,500 to 4,000 live births [1]. Advances in neonatal intensive care, surgical techniques, and perioperative management have significantly improved the survival of EA patients in recent decades [2]. However, a recent 30-year, population-based study reported the incidence trends, associated anomalies, survival, and long-term outcomes in neonates with EA, and found that comorbidities and postoperative complications remain persistent challenges [3]. A significant proportion of infants with EA also present with other congenital anomalies, among which gastrointestinal defects account for approximately 20%, including imperforate anus and duodenal obstruction [4, 5]. Congenital hypertrophic pyloric stenosis (HPS) is a relatively common cause of gastric outlet obstruction in infants, characterized by hypertrophy and hyperplasia of the pyloric circular muscle. Its incidence ranges from 4 to 5 per 1000 live births, with a marked male predominance [6].

In recent years, the coexistence of EA and HPS has garnered increasing clinical attention. Earlier studies reported an incidence of HPS in 1% to 10% of patients with EA, which is significantly higher than that in the general population, suggesting a potential pathophysiological link [7, 8]. However, the typical symptoms of HPS, such as non-bilious projectile vomiting, may be obscured by conditions frequently observed after EA repair, such as anastomotic stricture and gastroesophageal reflux. This overlap may contribute to diagnostic delays or misdiagnosis [9–11]. Management of EA with coexisting HPS requires both esophageal reconstruction and timely surgical relief of pyloric obstruction. In most centers worldwide, laparoscopic pyloromyotomy has become the standard treatment because of its safety, minimal invasiveness, and outcomes comparable or superior to those of open surgery [12]. The long-term prognosis depends largely on early recognition and appropriate intervention strategies [13]. Although case reports on this rare association have been published, most are small, single-center series, and there is a lack of systematic analysis regarding the clinical characteristics, timing of diagnosis, and optimal treatment approaches.

In this study, we retrospectively reviewed five cases of EA with concomitant HPS admitted to our center, and analyzed them in combination with 48 previously reported cases. We aim to summarize the clinical features, diagnosis and treatment patterns of this rare coexisting anomaly, and to provide insights for optimizing its clinical management.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical approval

This study consisted of a single-center retrospective case review and a systematic literature review. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review board (2019-k-333), and all data were anonymized before analysis.

Patients and data collection

We retrospectively reviewed all neonates diagnosed with EA who were treated at our institution during the study period (January 1, 2018-January 1, 2025). Among these patients, a subset was subsequently identified with concomitant HPS. EA was diagnosed based on failure to pass a nasogastric tube and radiographic evidence of a blind-ending pouch. HPS was diagnosed by typical clinical signs (projectile non-bilious vomiting), ultrasonographic criteria (pyloric muscle thickness > 3 mm and length > 15 mm), or upper gastrointestinal series (showing the “string sign”).

The extracted clinical data included gender, gestational age, birth weight, EA type (Gross classification), age at EA surgery, age of HPS symptom onset, age at HPS diagnosis, age at pyloromyotomy, and outcomes.

Literature search strategy

This study followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. A comprehensive search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) databases to identify relevant case reports and case series published from January 1, 1965 to March 1, 2025. Search terms included “esophageal atresia,” “oesophageal atresia,” “tracheoesophageal fistula,” “hypertrophic pyloric stenosis,” “infantile pyloric stenosis,” and “pyloric stenosis.” No language restrictions were applied. Two independent reviewers (initials: ZZL and AW) screened titles, abstracts, and full texts to identify eligible studies. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion with a third reviewer (DDW).

Eligibility criteria

We included case reports and case series of neonates or infants diagnosed with EA with concomitant HPS. Studies without original patient data, reviews, and conference abstracts were excluded.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of included case reports and case series was evaluated using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Case Reports/Case Series. For one non-randomized study, the Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies (MINORS) was additionally applied. The assessment process was conducted independently by two reviewers (ZZL, AW) and results are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

Data extraction and analysis

We extracted demographic characteristics, timing of HPS diagnosis, clinical presentation, treatment, and outcomes from each included study. For descriptive synthesis, categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were presented as median (interquartile range, IQR) and range. To illustrate overall survival, a descriptive Kaplan–Meier curve was generated by pooling institutional cases with those identified in the literature review.

Results

Institutional cases

During the study period, a total of 132 neonates with EA were treated at our center. Among them, five patients (5/132, 3.8%) were subsequently diagnosed with HPS. Their clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Of these five patients, three were male and two were female. Four patients had Gross type C EA, and one had type D. One patient was born preterm, while the others were full-term. The birth weights ranged from 2010 to 3600 g. None of the patients had perinatal or postnatal exposure to macrolide antibiotics such as erythromycin or azithromycin.

Clinical characteristics of five cases of EA complicated with HPS

All patients underwent EA repair within the first week of life (median age: 4 days; range: 3–6 days). A jejunal feeding tube was placed intraoperatively in all patients, and enteral nutrition was initiated on postoperative day 3. Two patients received formula feeding, while the other three received mixed feeding. The median duration of jejunal tube placement was 10 days (range: 7–108 days). Case 1 and Case 2 developed severe postoperative esophageal strictures and were initially unable to tolerate oral feeding. After endoscopic balloon dilation, the jejunal feeding tubes were removed on postoperative days 108 and 44, respectively. Notably, Case 1 required prolonged reliance on jejunal tube feeding, which contributed to a delayed manifestation of HPS-related symptoms.

Typical symptoms of HPS, such as projectile, non-bilious vomiting, appeared at a median age of 34 days (range: 18–111 days), and the diagnosis was confirmed at a median age of 45 days (range: 20–145 days). The median interval between symptom onset and final diagnosis was 9 days (range: 1–34 days). Ultrasound was the primary diagnostic modality, with two patients requiring additional upper gastrointestinal contrast studies for confirmation.

All patients underwent laparoscopic pyloromyotomy at a median age of 46 days (range: 20–147 days). The median time to first oral feeding was 1 day (range: 1–2 days), and the median time to achieve full enteral feeding was 3 days (range: 2–4 days). None of the patients experienced any HPS-related complications following surgery. The median postoperative hospital stay was 7 days (range: 5–10 days). At a median follow-up of 50 months (range: 6–90 months), all patients were alive, although two remained under follow-up for esophageal stricture–related issues.

Literature review

A total of 278 records were identified from four databases (PubMed: 103, Embase: 78, Web of Science: 60, CNKI: 37). After removing duplicates (n = 125), 153 records were screened by title and abstract. Of these, 111 records were excluded, and 42 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Finally, 17 studies (including 16 case reports/case series [8, 10, 13–26] and 1 non-randomized study [7]) were included in the review. The selection process is summarized in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

The methodological quality of the included studies was generally moderate. The quality assessment indicated that most case reports and case series fulfilled the JBI criteria in terms of clear case description, confirmed diagnosis, and adequate reporting of clinical management. However, as most studies were single-patient case reports, they lacked continuity and completeness in case inclusion. Detailed quality assessment results are presented in Supplementary Table 1. The single non-randomized study was evaluated using the MINORS tool and achieved a score of 20 out of 24, indicating high methodological quality and reliable findings.

A total of 48 cases were identified from 17 published studies (Table 2) [7, 8, 10, 13–26]. Of these, 41 were male, and 7 were female. Gross type C was the most common EA subtype (42/48, 87.5%), followed by types A (3 cases), E (2 cases), and D (1 case). The median age at EA surgery was 3 days (range: 1–10 days). HPS was diagnosed at a median age of 4 weeks (range: 2–14 weeks), with pyloromyotomy performed shortly afterward in most cases. Among the 48 literature cases, 43 patients survived, while 5 deaths were reported: one due to aspiration pneumonia, three due to sepsis, and 1 died due to severe associated anomalies.

Combined analysis

We combined our institutional series with cases from the literature, for a total of 53 patients. Most were male (44/53, 83.0%) and classified as Gross type C EA (46/53, 86.8%). The typical course included early EA repair in the neonatal period. HPS symptoms appeared later, usually between 2 and 15 weeks of age. The median age of onset was 4 weeks (IQR: 2.5–6 weeks). All patients underwent sequential surgical correction. We constructed a Kaplan–Meier survival curve by combining institutional and literature cases (Fig. 2). The overall survival rate was 90.6%. Most deaths were associated with severe anomalies or infection. Most patients achieved good survival during follow-up.

Discussion

This study reviewed five cases of EA with concomitant HPS treated at our institution, along with 48 previously reported cases from the literature. This combined dataset represents one of the most comprehensive case series published to date on this rare congenital anomaly. Our findings indicate that HPS typically presents in a delayed manner following EA repair. With timely diagnosis and appropriate surgical intervention, the overall prognosis for affected patients is favorable.

The incidence of HPS among neonates with EA in our center was 3.8%. This rate appears higher than the expected incidence of isolated HPS in the general population [6]. A similar trend has been observed in previous case series, which reported frequencies of HPS ranging from 2.5% to 7.5% among EA patients [7, 15].Consistent with previous reports, most patients with EA and HPS in this study were male, and Gross type C was the predominant EA subtype [7]. EA repair was typically completed within the first week of life, while the onset of HPS usually occurred between the 1 to 3 months after birth, mirroring the natural course of isolated HPS [27]. In our cohort, all patients received intraoperative placement of a jejunal feeding tube, and early enteral nutrition was initiated postoperatively, which may have helped prevent further nutritional deterioration before HPS was diagnosed.

In our cohort, symptoms of HPS appeared between 18 and 111 days of age, with a median age at diagnosis of 45 days. This was later than the typical onset of isolated HPS. A possible reason is that many EA patients begin oral feeding late due to complications such as anastomotic stricture, severe gastroesophageal reflux, or prolonged jejunal feeding. These altered feeding patterns reduce exposure to postprandial vomiting, thereby masking the classical presentation of HPS and delaying its recognition [9, 28]. One patient (Case 1) experienced a marked delay in both symptom onset and diagnosis, likely due to prolonged jejunal feeding after severe esophageal stricture. These findings emphasize the need for high clinical suspicion of HPS in EA patients who require long-term enteral nutrition, even before regular oral feeding is initiated, especially when signs such as feeding intolerance or gastric retention are present.

Differentiating HPS from EA-related complications requires careful evaluation. Reflux-related vomiting is usually intermittent and associated with feeding, whereas HPS often presents as progressively forceful, projectile, non-bilious emesis. Persistent vomiting despite adequate dilatation of anastomotic strictures should raise suspicion of HPS [29]. Imaging is critical for diagnosis. Ultrasonography is the first-line modality and allows direct measurement of pyloric muscle thickness and length. Upper gastrointestinal contrast studies are supplementary and provide functional information such as delayed gastric emptying. In our study, ultrasound was the main diagnostic method, consistent with current guidelines [30]. While ultrasound effectively measures pyloric muscle thickness and length, contrast studies provide additional functional assessment such as delayed gastric emptying. This complementary approach improves diagnostic accuracy.

In our cohort, the age at pyloromyotomy ranged from 20 to 147 days, which is slightly older than the operative age reported in the literature [31]. However, all procedures were performed before the onset of significant metabolic derangements. All patients underwent laparoscopic pyloromyotomy with uneventful recovery. This supports the safety and efficacy of minimally invasive surgery in this population. Previous studies also confirmed that laparoscopic pyloromyotomy provides definitive treatment for HPS with minimal trauma and fast recovery [32]. Our literature review also identified several earlier reports of open pyloromyotomy [8, 18, 22]. These patients generally achieved satisfactory outcomes in terms of symptom resolution and survival. Taken together, timely diagnosis and surgical intervention usually lead to a good prognosis, as demonstrated by the complete survival in our five institutional cases. However, cases complicated by severe infections such as sepsis, complex anomalies, or significant diagnostic delay may still have a higher risk of mortality [13, 22, 26]. Two patients in our cohort required additional management for esophageal strictures, including repeated balloon dilatation under endoscopy. This highlights the need for long-term multidisciplinary care focused on both esophageal and pyloric function.

Although our institutional series was limited in size, the addition of a comprehensive literature review provided a broader understanding of this rare dual anomaly. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our review was not prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), which may introduce reporting bias. Second, heterogeneity in case reporting and the retrospective design of both our cohort and the included studies limit the strength of causal inferences. Third, many of the included studies were single case reports or small case series, which represent low-level evidence and may be affected by reporting or publication bias. Larger multicenter prospective studies are needed to validate these findings, clarify clinical patterns, and optimize management strategies.

Conclusion

HPS may coexist with EA, with an incidence of approximately 3.8% in our institutional cohort. It often presents later in the disease course. Its typical symptoms may be masked by postoperative complications such as anastomotic stricture and prolonged use of jejunal feeding tubes, leading to delayed diagnosis. Early recognition and timely laparoscopic pyloromyotomy usually result in favorable outcomes. Long-term follow-up is recommended, and further multicenter studies are needed to establish optimal management strategies.
